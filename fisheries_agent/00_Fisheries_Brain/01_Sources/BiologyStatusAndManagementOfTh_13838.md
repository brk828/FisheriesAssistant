# Biology, Status, and Management of the Timber Rattlesnake (*Crotalus horridus*): A Guide for Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #viperidae

## Executive Summary

This SSAR Herpetological Circular, written by William S. Brown (Skidmore College) and prepared from an Element Stewardship Abstract for The Nature Conservancy, is a synthesis of the biology, status, threats, and management of the [[Timber Rattlesnake]] (*Crotalus horridus*). The species is the only wide-ranging woodland rattlesnake of the deciduous forest biome of eastern and central North America. The document combines published literature with unpublished data, field observations, and expert opinion, and it states explicitly that it is provisional.

The central conclusion is that human exploitation, especially the removal of reproductive females through bounty hunting, commercial collecting, and sport hunting, has depleted or collapsed many populations. Combined with habitat loss from development and vegetative change, this has made recovery slow, because the species matures late, reproduces infrequently, and has little harvestable surplus. The author recommends full legal protection, den and summer-range protection within about 1.5 miles of dens, secrecy about den locations, and an end to sport hunting, especially in [[Pennsylvania]].

## Background & Description

The species is distinguished from harmless look-alikes by a short thick tail with a rattle, a single row of subcaudal scales, a [[Facial Pit]], vertically elliptical pupils, and small dorsal head scales. Its skin is coarse and dull, with keeled scales.

Coloration varies geographically. Conant and Collins (1991) recognize four variations: yellow, black, southern ([[Canebrake Rattlesnake]]), and western. Martin (1992a) recognizes eastern, western, and southern forms. Northern populations often contain both yellow and black [[Color Morphs]]. The taxonomy is unresolved, with some workers recognizing a northern subspecies *C. h. horridus* and others not recognizing subspecies (see [[Pisani et al. 1972]]).

Adult body size: males average 111 cm total length (n=200) and females 97 cm (n=150) in northeastern New York. Newborns average 31 cm and 20–25 g. Sexual dimorphism is present, with males having longer tails. The largest verified sizes are much smaller than historical claims. The largest male in the author's 13-year New York study measured 137.5 cm and the largest female 120 cm. Reported maxima of 6 ft or more (for example, Ditmars' 74-inch and 8-ft southern records) are regarded as unreliable, and the author recommends a standardized "squeeze box" method to avoid stretching bias.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The species uses three distinct habitats: the den (hibernaculum), transient habitat, and summer range. Dens in northeastern New York are typically rocky fissures, talus, or fallen rock on south-facing slopes, at 150–390 m. In the Pine Barrens of New Jersey, dens are in white cedar swamps. Transient habitat, within about 200 m of a den, consists of outcrop knolls and shelf clearings used for migration and gravid-female gestation. Summer range is mainly canopied deciduous forest. Study data from New York showed mean canopy closure of 67% at snake locations.

**Spatial biology.** Snakes migrate seasonally between dens and summer range. Mean maximum migratory distance is about 4.07 km for males and 2.05 km for nongravid females in New York. Maximum recorded migration is 7.2 km (4.5 miles). Home ranges average 160–500 acres for males and 40–100 acres for nongravid females. Gravid females have the smallest ranges.

**Seasonal cycle and communal denning.** The species is ectothermic and hibernates communally. Hibernation lasts about 7.4 months in northeastern New York, with a 4.6-month active season from early May to late September. In the south the active season is longer.

**Reproduction and demography.** Females in northern populations usually reproduce every three or four years, with first reproduction at about 9–10 years. Brown (1991) found 0% of sampled females reproducing on a two-year cycle. Litter sizes are 9.3 (New York) to 12.6 (South Carolina). Maximum captive longevity is 30 years and estimated wild longevity is about 25 years. Adult survivorship is high and juvenile mortality is high, so the population has little harvestable surplus.

**Threats.** Dodd (1987) identifies collection for pets and malicious killing as major threats. Other threats include habitat destruction (including rock moving and blasting), human incursions from second-home and housing development, bounty hunting, commercial collecting, sport hunting and organized round-ups, behavioral disturbance, shading-over of dens by succession, logging, mining, and gas wells.

## Key Findings

- **Population declines are widespread.** Stechert (1992) reports that 26% of historically known dens in New York are extinct and 76% of active dens are in various stages of depletion. Vermont has fallen from about 25 historical sites to two viable colonies. The species has been extirpated from Maine and Rhode Island.
- **Pennsylvania is a key case.** A Martin et al. (1990) survey found that 75% of Pennsylvania dens are below viability thresholds (fewer than 45 snakes or fewer than 8–10 reproductive females). Reinert (1990) found that 84% of females turned in by snake hunters were gravid. The author concludes that the licensed sport hunt, which was based on a management model, has failed and should be stopped.
- **Regulatory changes were weakened.** Proposed Pennsylvania changes (a four-week season, a one-snake limit, and a possession limit) were revised after public objections to a season from the second Saturday in June to 31 July and a two-snake per-hunt allowance for organized hunts. The author argues this increases harvest of gravid females.
- **Bounties and collectors caused deep damage.** Bounty systems in New York and Minnesota were dominated by a few individuals. One collector took more than 4,000 snakes over 30 years, and in one New York bounty decade a single hunter took thousands.
- **Protection guidelines are recommended.** Protecting an area with a 1.5-mile radius around a den (about 7 square miles, or 18 km²) is recommended, with a further 1.0-mile buffer suggested. The author also recommends that logging be restricted to November–March near dens.
- **Recovery is expected to be slow.** A minimum viable population is estimated at 30–40 snakes with at least four or five mature females. Recovery of a protected den may take 20–30 years. One den in Pennsylvania rose from 50–60 snakes in 1968 to 120–130 in 1983.
- **Translocation is uncertain.** Dodd and Seigel (1991) found only 19% of amphibian and reptile translocations succeeded, so translocation should be done cautiously and monitored.
- **Management is contested.** Experts disagree on whether human disturbance is significant, and on whether tree removal to reduce shading helps or harms dens (see [[Reinert]] and [[Stechert]]).
- **Research priorities** include long-term population trends, mark-recapture estimates, age-specific survivorship, reproductive biology, and newborn den-finding.
