## Executive Summary
This study investigates the ecological aftermath of a significant fuel oil spill (approx. 13,000 liters of No. 2 fuel oil) into Plum Creek, a 4th-order tributary of the [[Clinch River]] in southwestern Virginia, in May 1973. Contrary to expectations based on similar incidents (e.g., [[Bury (1972) Oil Spill Study]]), the spill resulted in surprisingly minimal direct mortality to fish and benthic macroinvertebrate populations, despite causing significant immediate mortality in vertebrate wildlife (waterfowl and water snakes). Researchers monitored 8 stations over a 5-month period, concluding that while diversity indices showed high variability downstream, the overall biological impact on the fish community was less severe than anticipated, though chronic effects from residual containment materials (straw booms) were flagged as a concern.

## Background & Description
*Note: This paper is a broad ecological survey and does not focus on specific morphological or anatomical descriptions of the fish. The data below represents taxonomic classification and community composition rather than individual morphology.*

- **Total Fish Sampled**: 3,556 individuals.
- **Taxonomic Diversity**: 21 species representing 5 distinct families were identified.
- **Dominant Family**: [[Cyprinidae]] (Minnows) was the most speciose family observed, including *Campostoma anomalum*, *Notropis cornutus*, and *Rhinichthys atratulus*.
- **Other Families Represented**:
    - [[Catostomidae]] (Suckers): *Catostomus commersoni*, *Hypentelium nigricans*.
    - [[Cottidae]] (Sculpins): *Cottus carolinae*.
    - [[Centrarchidae]] (Sunfish/Bass): *Ambloplites rupestris*, *Micropterus punctulatus*.
    - [[Percidae]] (Darters/Perch): *Etheostoma blennioides*, *Etheostoma flabellare*, *Etheostoma simoterum*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The study area, [[Plum Creek]], serves as a habitat characterized by riffle-pool complexes with substrates ranging from bedrock and rubble to sand and organic detritus. The surrounding drainage basin is largely utilized as pasture land, contributing to baseline organic pollution independent of the oil spill (specifically noted at Station 1 due to cattle disturbance).

- **Habitat Structure**: Researchers specifically selected stations to include both riffle and pool habitats to capture a representative sample of the [[Lotic Ecosystem]].
- **Community Interactions**: The confluence with the Clinch River (Station 7) showed fish diversity patterns skewed by an upstream secondary sewage treatment plant, indicating that municipal effluent had a more measurable impact on diversity indices at that specific site than the oil spill itself.
- **Population Dynamics**: A significant natural increase in fish numbers observed in the final sampling period (September) was attributed to the natural recruitment of Young-of-Year (YOY) juveniles spawned that spring, rather than a recovery from spill impacts.
- **Macroinvertebrate Context**: The fish community existed alongside a diverse benthic macroinvertebrate community (13,395 specimens, 93 taxa), including [[Ephemeroptera]], [[Plecoptera]], and [[Trichoptera]] (EPT taxa), which are typically sensitive indicators of water quality.

## Key Findings
- **Low Direct Fish Mortality**: Despite the large volume of fuel oil, immediate fish kills were low. Only 3 dead *Campostoma anomalum* (Stoneroller), 1 *Hybopsis amblops*, and 1 *Hypentelium nigricans* (Northern Hogsucker) were specifically documented dead during the initial collection periods.
- **Contrast with Vertebrate Mortality**: While fish and invertebrate mortality was low, the spill caused high immediate mortality in other vertebrates, including 32 dead wild ducks (likely [[Mallards]] - *Anas platyrhynchos*) and multiple [[Water Snakes]] (*Natrix sipedon*).
- **No Significant Population Decline**: Statistical analysis using the [[Willm and Dorris Diversity Index]] showed that fish did not exhibit significant decreases in the number of taxa or specimens collected at the spill site throughout the 5-month survey period.
- **Contradicts Prior Literature**: This finding conflicts with a similar tank-truck oil spill incident studied by Schultz and Tebo (1974), where an estimated 90% immediate fish mortality was observed in a comparable small stream.
- **Chronic Risk from Cleanup Methods**: A critical finding was that the straw booms used to contain the oil settled to the streambed and acted as a long-term reservoir for hydrocarbons. Stations immediately downstream of these dams (Stations 4 and 6) exhibited the highest variability in macroinvertebrate diversity, correlating with the highest levels of hexane-extractable oil residues found in sediment (per companion study [[Hoehn et al. 1974]]).
- **Recommendation**: The study recommends against the prolonged use of straw containment booms, advising rapid removal to prevent chronic leaching of oil fractions into the substrate long after the initial surface slick has been addressed.
