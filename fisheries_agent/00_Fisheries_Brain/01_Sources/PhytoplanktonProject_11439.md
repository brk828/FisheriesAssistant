# Phytoplankton Project: Final Report on Ohio River Algae at Louisville, Kentucky (1960)

#phycology #freshwater-biology #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This final report, issued by the [[Potamological Institute]] at the [[University of Louisville]] for the [[Ohio River Valley Water Sanitation Commission]] (ORSANCO), documents daily phytoplankton sampling of the [[Ohio River]] at Louisville, Kentucky, from July to November 1960. The study examined algal composition, abundance, chemistry, taste and odor problems, and the causes of algal blooms. It found that [[Melosira]] dominated the community, that algal counts varied up to 500-fold within July, and that no clear correlation existed between phytoplankton, routine water chemistry, and taste-and-odor events. The authors attribute this partly to the surface-only sampling design and to the large share of dead cells. They conclude that nutrient loading from sewage, detergents, and agriculture, combined with the river's conversion into a series of pools, will likely increase bloom frequency.

## Background & Description

The report opens with a 1823 account by E. James, who described a greenish coloration of the Ohio River that may have been caused by Aphanizomenon, the first recorded evidence of algae in the river. The authors define a bloom as a visible change in surface water caused by millions of free-floating phytoplankton. Blooms are linked to drinking-water tastes and odors, livestock deaths, fish kills, and human gastrointestinal and skin disorders, citing a range of earlier studies.

The present study sampled [[Phytoplankton]] daily at three stations on a transect across the river at mile 603 (Louisville) from July 4 to October 4, 1960. Additional samples were taken from October 5 to November 5 at mile 600.6. The work produced 618 water samples and 3,090 analyses. Full data were not published; they are available on loan from the Institute.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Community composition.** A checklist of 72 species was compiled: 12 blue-green algae, 39 green algae, 17 diatoms, and 4 flagellates. Green algae were the most numerous group in the checklist, but the daily samples were dominated by a single diatom.

**Seasonal succession.** Melosira made up more than 90% of the daily community on all but three days in July. Coccoid green algae were the next most abundant group. Oscillatoria became the third dominant form in August and rose through September. Anacystis formed 0 to 15% of the community in October. On October 15 no Melosira were found, and coccoid greens made up 68% of the sample.

**Nutrients and bloom conditions.** The report describes the Ohio River as eutrophic. Although 95% of cities treat their sewage, effluents still enter the river and supply phosphates and nitrates. The river's total alkalinity is described as suitable for photosynthesis. Pool conditions created by impoundment or low flow were identified as favorable for algal reproduction. A rise in green algae density from 1958 to 1959 was attributed to either increased nutrients or reduced toxic industrial wastes.

**Vertical and horizontal structure.** The river at Louisville appeared to form horizontal layers. The middle of the river differed noticeably from the surface 1000 feet from either shoreline.

**Taste and odor.** The [[Louisville Water Company]] reported taste or odor problems in treated water only on September 10, 11, 19–24, 27, and 28. Raw-water odor thresholds ranged from 2.8 to 8.0 each month, with 8 occurring most often in September.

## Key Findings

1. **Dominance of Melosira:** Melosira exceeded 90% of the total daily community on most days, and July populations ranged from 0.04 to 21.14 × 10⁶ cells/L (a 500-fold change).
2. **Station differences:** Station II had lower counts than the other stations, which the authors suggest may relate to higher mid-channel current velocity. July monthly totals were 92.2 (Station I), 77.9 (Station II), and 101.8 (Station III) × 10⁶ cells/L. Counts declined through August and September.
3. **No chemistry correlation:** Daily [[Water Chemistry]] (free carbon dioxide, total alkalinity, total hardness, pH, chlorides, sulphates) showed no correlation with phytoplankton counts.
4. **No taste-odor correlation:** Bad-taste days could not be linked to any species or to cell-size composition from screening.
5. **Dead cells:** In a parallel study at the same institute, less than 40% of the algae at the surface were alive in September and October. The authors argue that total cell counts may therefore say little about the taste-and-odor problem.
6. **Benthic source hypothesis:** The taste source may lie in the bottom-dwelling (benthic and epipelic) algae, since drinking-water intakes draw from depth while samples came from the surface. Dense Oscillatoria splendida growths were found on shoreline rocks in September. Earlier work (Goryunova, 1949) showed that senescent Oscillatoria splendida releases volatile aldehydes that cause strong tastes.
7. **Stratification:** The horizontal layering of the river has major implications for representative sampling and water-quality interpretation. The authors call for detailed study of currents and laminar flow.
8. **Predictive capacity:** No method for predicting phytoplankton blooms had been developed.
9. **Future outlook:** The authors expect more blooms as detergents, organic compounds, and inorganic salts accumulate in effluents and as the river is converted into pools.

**Recommendations:** The report calls for long-term basic research into the ecological mechanisms of algal blooms, study of bottom-dwelling algae and their extracellular products, and the effects of specific pollutants on algal physiology. Such work requires expensive equipment and trained staff. The authors state that the Ohio River has shifted from a clean stream that supported occasional blooms, to one with waste-driven inhibition, to one that now supports blooms again because of modern nutrient inputs.
