## Executive Summary

This study (Sowka & Brunkow 1999) investigated the feasibility of **cage culturing juvenile [[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*), the most endangered endemic fish of the [[Colorado River]] system, as a conservation aquaculture technique. Fish hatched at Dexter National Fish Hatchery were reared for four months in floating mesh cages at Office Cove (Lake Havasu, Arizona) under a two-factor experimental design testing three **stocking densities** (low: 18 fish/enclosure, medium: 30, high: 60) crossed with three **size-variability treatments** (low, medium, high). No supplemental feeding was provided. Results showed that low-to-medium stocking densities and higher initial size variability among individuals produced the best growth and survivorship outcomes, informing practical recommendations for cage-based rearing of this critically endangered cyprinid as an alternative to predator-free pond rearing.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Gila elegans* (Bonytail), family Cyprinidae — an endemic Colorado River minnow.
- **Initial size-classes at stocking** (mean TL and weight):
  - Small: 53 mm TL, 1.2 g (n = 516)
  - Medium: 67 mm TL, 2.7 g (n = 750)
  - Large: 93 mm TL, 6.0 g (n = 358)
- **Growth over 4-month study** (best-performing treatment — medium density × medium size variability):
  - Length: ~68 mm TL → ~92 mm TL (up to 40% increase in mean length across treatments)
  - Weight: ~3.3 g → ~6.1 g (up to 85% increase in mean weight across treatments)
- **Predation-escape size threshold**: Juveniles must reach approximately **250 mm TL** before being safely released into open water (Lake Mohave/Lake Havasu) to avoid predation by introduced fishes.
- Coefficient of variation in length (CV) was used as a quantitative metric of size variability; low-variability treatments maintained significantly lower CV than medium/high-variability treatments throughout the study (ANOVA: F = 98.6, df = 2,15, P < 0.001).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic range**: Once distributed throughout the [[Colorado River]] main stem and tributaries; now the rarest endemic Colorado River fish.
- **Current status**: Small remnant adult population persists in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona, with negligible natural recruitment for over 30 years.
- **Primary threats**:
  - Habitat alteration from reservoir/dam construction (loss of riverine habitat, altered flow/temperature regimes).
  - Predation by introduced non-native fishes, particularly on larval and juvenile life stages.
- **Study habitat**: Office Cove — an 18.2 × 10³ m³ earthen-berm cove (5-m max depth) adjacent to Lake Havasu, functioning as a predator-free acclimation site for captive-reared fish prior to release.
- **Trophic context**: Cages permit small invertebrates (zooplankton/benthic prey) to migrate through mesh into enclosures, potentially supplementing diet without artificial feeding — linking caged rearing to natural [[Trophic Cascades|trophic dynamics]] of predator-dominated water bodies (per Carpenter et al. 1985 cascading trophic interaction theory).
- **Intraspecific interactions**: Evidence suggests size-based aggression/competition and possible **cannibalism** (larger individuals preying on smaller conspecifics) especially in high-density, high-size-variability cages, contributing to unexplained mortality.

## Key Findings

1. **Stocking density effects**: Growth (length and weight) and survivorship were negatively related to stocking density; fish at high density (60/enclosure) grew less and suffered higher mortality than those at low (18/enclosure) or medium (30/enclosure) densities.
2. **Size variability effects**: Fish in **low size-variability** populations (all same size-class) grew significantly less (length: P < 0.01–0.09; weight: P < 0.03–0.06) than those in medium- or high-variability populations, likely due to reduced aggression or exploitative competition when size heterogeneity was present. Initial size variability did **not** significantly affect overall survivorship (P > 0.73).
3. **Best-performing treatment**: Medium density × medium size variability yielded the fastest growth (68→92 mm TL; 3.3→6.1 g).
4. **Survivorship**: Density significantly affected survivorship (ANOVA, F = 3.43, P < 0.059); high-density cages combined with high size variability showed a notably negative survivorship relationship (Tukey's P < 0.05).
5. **Overall recovery rate**: 64% (510 of 792 fish recovered on 16 December), considered a conservative survivorship estimate since undetected escapes were possible; 218 of 282 missing fish had unknown fate (presumed death/decomposition or cannibalism).
6. **Practical recommendation**: Stock juvenile bonytails in cages at **~18–22 fish/m²**, using a **wide range of body sizes**, to maximize both growth and survivorship.
7. **Cage culture viability**: Even without supplemental feeding, cage culture proved a viable rearing method — comparable or superior in cost/logistics to predator-free pond rearing, since cages are portable, inexpensive, low-maintenance, and can be sited in waters with high predator densities (which often also host abundant invertebrate prey).
8. **Design recommendations**: Nylon mesh (vs. fiberglass screening) attached to PVC pipe frames is recommended for future cage designs — more resistant to algal fouling, inexpensive, durable, and easier to disassemble/transport/store.
9. **Broader relevance**: Findings reinforce the importance of individual size variability as an ecological variable in captive rearing/[[Aquaculture]] contexts, paralleling similar effects documented in larval amphibians (Brunkow & Collins 1996, 1998) and other taxa.
