# Feasibility of Developing and Maintaining a Sport Fishery in the Salt River Project Canals

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae #Salmonidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department Research Branch technical report (Technical Report No. 18, 1995, by [[Brian R. Wright]] and [[Jeff A. Sorensen]]) assesses whether the [[Salt River Project]] (SRP) canal system in the Phoenix metropolitan area can support a harvestable sport fishery. Monthly electrofishing, stocking experiments, contaminant testing, water quality and biotic sampling, and two telephone public opinion surveys were conducted, focused mainly on the 61.4 km [[Arizona Canal]]. The study concludes that a put-and-take fishery is feasible, with channel catfish and rainbow trout stocked seasonally, while liability concerns must be resolved first.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Urban fishing demand has grown (urban fishing licenses rose from 2,500 in 1983 to 25,679 in 1994; urban lake angler-days rose to about 400,000 by 1994). Canal fishing occurs informally but SRP discourages it over liability concerns.
- **System:** The SRP canal system includes 277 km of main canals and 1,487 km of lateral canals and ditches, with 8 major canals. Water enters at [[Granite Reef Diversion Dam]].
- **Existing management:** About 18,000 sterile [[White Amur]] had been stocked into 119 km of canals by March 1994 for aquatic vegetation control, reducing herbicide use.
- **Prior regional context:** The report reviews canal fisheries in California (e.g., [[Central Valley Project]], [[California Aqueduct]], [[All-American Canal]], [[Coachella Canal]]) and the [[Central Arizona Project]].
- **Funding:** Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration Project F-14-R (the Dingell-Johnson/Wallop-Breaux program) and the Arizona Game and Fish Department Heritage Fund.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study period and sites:** Monthly electrofishing from October 1992 to July 1994 at 5 collection sites (Sites 1, 2, 3, 5, 7) along the Arizona Canal, plus 3 alternate sites. Site 3 was shallow and fast-flowing; Site 7 and Site 5 were deep, free-flowing reaches.
- **Electrical barrier:** A barrier below Granite Reef Dam limits upstream fish movement from the Salt River into the canals.
- **Flow regimes:** The Arizona Canal was estimated at about 44 km of run, 13 km of pool and 4.4 km of riffle habitat.
- **Water chemistry:** Water temperature peaked in August; dissolved oxygen was highest February through August. Alum sludge discharges from water treatment plants affected Sites 1–3.
- **Primary production:** Mean chlorophyll a to pheophytin a ratio of 1.3 in the Arizona Canal indicated moderate food base quality.
- **Benthos and zooplankton:** 18 benthic macroinvertebrate taxa and 18 true zooplankton taxa were recorded.
- **Vegetation control:** SRP applies herbicides (endothall, acrolein, copper sulfate, glyphosate) and uses demossing bridges. Endothall and acrolein are lethal to fish and are not used where white amurs are stocked.

## Key Findings

### Fish community
- 13,353 fish were collected from electrofishing, representing 20 species in 10 families (17 introduced, 3 native).
- Dominant species were Sonora sucker, desert sucker, threadfin shad, red shiner, white amur and largemouth bass.
- Site 3 yielded the most fish (35%); Site 7 the fewest (9.5%). Species richness ranged from 12 (upstream) to 18 (downstream at Skunk Creek Drain).
- Native suckers made up about 58% of the catch; forage fish about 36%; game fish about 3%.
- Largemouth bass showed evidence of natural reproduction; rainbow trout were rare as residents (n = 11 excluding stocked fish).
- Most resident fish were in good condition (mean K > 1.00), except threadfin shad, roundtail chub, channel catfish and rainbow trout (mean K > 0.80).

### Fish immigration
- Barrier surveys (1991–1995) recorded 17 species above the barrier, with desert sucker (19.7%) and tilapia (16.5%) most common; rainbow trout comprised over 7% of fish sampled.
- Two stocked white amurs passed the barrier in 1994 during a power outage.

### Experimental stockings
- **Channel catfish:** 1,500 stocked in June 1993 and 500 in July 1993. Recapture was highest in the first 3 weeks; growth was not substantial and condition declined.
- **Rainbow trout:** 2,200 stocked in November 1993. Recapture was highest within 6 weeks; 347 (15.8%) were caught, and 132 were reported harvested by anglers. No substantial growth occurred and condition declined, likely due to summer water temperatures.
- **Retention:** 99.4% of channel catfish and 95.5% of rainbow trout remained in the stocking reach. Downstream movement was about 0.8–4.0 km per week.
- **Losses to lateral canals:** 0.17% of stocked rainbow trout and 0.47% of channel catfish were lost to lateral canals.

### Contaminants
- Of 129 EPA priority pollutants tested in composite samples (small sample sizes: 3 control and 5 recaptured composites), most were not detected or were well below FDA Action Levels.
- Pesticides, mercury and PCBs were below FDA Action Levels. Dioxin was not detected in rainbow trout.
- AGFD explicitly states it cannot declare canal fish safe or unsafe for consumption.

### Public opinion
- Surveys in May 1994 covered 300 licensed anglers and 600 general public respondents; sampling errors were ±5.8% (anglers) and ±4.1% (public), per the report's stated values.
- 68% of licensed anglers and 51% of the non-angling public said they would be somewhat or very likely to use a canal fishery.
- Bass was the top species preference among anglers (25%), followed by catfish and trout; the non-angling public favored catfish.
- About 80% of interested respondents were willing to buy a special license; median willingness to pay was about $11.25.

### Economic projections
- Estimated annual use: 753,100 person-use days.
- Estimated new anglers: 129,538.
- Estimated new license revenue: about $1.55 million at $12.00 per license.

### Management recommendations
- **Administration:** Options include keeping the program under the statewide fishing program, folding it into the Urban Fishing Program, or creating an independent program funded by a special license or stamp.
- **Enhancement:** Physical improvements (access sites, parking, restrooms, safety railings) and habitat additions (tire reefs, corrugated pipe catfish shelters) are recommended.
- **Stocking:** Winter put-and-take rainbow trout and summer channel catfish stocking, modeled on the Urban Fish Program.
- **Monitoring:** Creel surveys, angler satisfaction surveys and seasonal electrofishing.
- **Liability:** Resolving liability and public safety issues, through agreements between SRP, AGFD and cities, is identified as the first priority.
- **Future research:** Study the remaining canals, the effects of alum sludge, lateral canal losses across the full canal length, and expanded contaminant testing.

### Conclusions
The report concludes that a put-and-take fishery could be established to provide increased angling opportunities. Stocked fish do not grow substantially in the Arizona Canal, so a put-grow-take program is not supported at present.
