# Review of the Fossil History of North American Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #paleoichthyology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This review, by Ted M. Cavender, synthesizes the fossil record relating to the distributional history of North American freshwater fishes from the Triassic through the Pleistocene. Emphasis falls on the Cenozoic, particularly the Paleogene, because the Neogene record has been summarized more fully elsewhere, notably by [[G. R. Smith]] (1981a). The author argues that the fossil record is strongly biased by sparse geographic coverage, large stratigraphic gaps and fragmentary fossils, so it can inform but not fully resolve questions of zoogeography and fish origins.

## Background & Description

The review covers Mesozoic faunas briefly and concentrates on the Cenozoic. It builds on [[Cope]] (1884), whose *Tertiary Vertebrata* was the first review of the Cenozoic record, and on the work of [[Uyeno and Miller]] (1963) and Smith (1981a). The geologic time scale follows Dott and Batten (1981). The Paleogene is treated as the Paleocene, Eocene and Oligocene, with an upper boundary near 25 mya. The author notes that a proposed Eocene/Oligocene boundary, supported by extinction and floral and faunal succession, would reorganise the Tertiary into two periods of 30–35 my each, and that this boundary has merit for freshwater fishes because many taxa disappeared near it.

Most Tertiary fish localities lie between 30° and 53°N, between the Missouri River and the Pacific Coast. The richest deposits are in Oregon, northern Nevada, the western Dakotas, Montana and Wyoming. Fishes come mainly from lacustrine sediments in western mountain basins, fluvial deposits in the Plains and low-gradient flood plain or lake settings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The review frames faunal change against climate and tectonics:

- **Late Eocene cooling** and a marked global drop in sea-water temperature led to the loss of warm-adapted taxa such as Gonorynchidae, Osteoglossidae, Asineopidae, Ellimmichthyidae and percoid genera (Percichthyidae) at middle and northern latitudes.
- **Oligocene** tectonism (Basin and Range formation, [[Columbia River Basalt]] eruptions from about 16 to 8 mya) and drying of midcontinent habitats reshaped drainages and fish distributions.
- **Early Miocene** warm, moist conditions allowed widespread dispersal of fishes such as [[Centrarchids]] and *Fundulus* to middle and northern latitudes.
- **Late Cenozoic** uplift, rejuvenated streams and cooling caused further extinctions in the west, while eastern changes were mainly latitudinal shifts.
- The [[Continental Divide]] (the Laramide-age Rocky Mountain ridge) acted as a barrier to dispersal, with breaks such as those in the Miocene allowing crossings.

## Key Findings

**Family-level fossil records (summarised, not exhaustive):**

- *Petromyzontidae*: no confident North American freshwater fossils; *Mayomyzon pieckoensis* (Pennsylvanian, Illinois) and a Mississippian lamprey from Montana are the oldest records.
- *Acipenseridae* and *Polyodontidae*: Upper Cretaceous sturgeons (Old Man and Hell Creek formations); no fossil record of *Polyodon spathula* itself.
- *Lepisosteidae* (gars): extensive Early Cretaceous to Cenozoic record, nearly all freshwater, with the family persisting through the Cretaceous/Tertiary boundary.
- *Amiidae* (bowfins): records from the Early Cretaceous to Recent, with *Amia calva* first recorded in the Pliocene.
- *Hiodontidae* (mooneyes): earliest members from the Paleocene Paskapoo Formation, Alberta; likely sister group of Asian Lycopteridae.
- *Salmonidae*: the oldest undoubted salmonid, *Eosalmo driftwoodensis*, comes from Eocene freshwater sediments of British Columbia; *Smilodonichthys rastrosus* is a large extinct Pliocene salmonid.
- *Cyprinidae*: the oldest North American cyprinids are Oligocene (three sites in the northwest); the family became dominant by the end of the Miocene, possibly after immigration from Eurasia.
- *Catostomidae*: records back to the Paleocene (Paskapoo Formation), with many Paleogene forms formerly assigned to *Amyzon*.
- *Centrarchidae*: earliest forms are Eocene from northwestern Montana; by Late Miocene they were widespread in the west.
- *Percidae*: earliest North American *Perca* records are Pleistocene; percids appeared as a first North American record in the Pliocene.
- *Cottidae* (sculpins): oldest from Late Miocene Fossil Lake Idaho deposits, with three genera (*Cottus*, *Kerocottus*, *Myoxocephalus*).
- *Goodeidae*: fossils known mainly from Mexico, the oldest being the Miocene *Tapatia occidentalis*.

**Key interpretations:**

- Late Triassic freshwater faunas are relatively well known in both west and east, but none of the family-level elements survived to the Late Cretaceous in North America.
- Late Cretaceous freshwater faunas (Hell Creek, Lance) are dominated by "holosteans" and have very few teleosts, and they lack the Mesozoic origins of many teleost groups argued by Myers (1967), which Patterson (1981a) does not support.
- Some families, including Acipenseridae, Lepisosteidae and Amiidae, which survived the Cretaceous/Tertiary boundary, persisted in North America.
- Origins of the North American fauna are mixed: Acipenseridae, Polyodontidae, Lepisosteidae and Amiidae derive from the resident Cretaceous fauna; Hiodontidae trace to Asian Mesozoic freshwater; Cyprinidae, Percidae, Cottidae, Gasterosteidae, Gadidae and Salmonidae are mainly of Eurasian origin; and Atherinidae, Goodeidae, Fundulidae and Cyprinodontidae originated or dispersed at lower latitudes.
- The Pliocene–Pleistocene record includes a Pleistocene pike (*Esox*) from the Yukon and fossil lake whitefish, grayling and smelt records from northern Canada.

**Stated limitations:** The author concludes that large gaps exist in both stratigraphic and geographic coverage. The west is best represented, the east and Canadian Shield very poorly, and the Middle and Late Jurassic and Early and Middle Cretaceous are largely unknown. The weaknesses of the record have limited the ability of fossils to answer major zoogeographic questions.
