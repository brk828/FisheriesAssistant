# Population Manipulation Studies on West Virginia Smallmouth Bass Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Project F-1-R-(1-5), July 1, 1956) from the [[Conservation Commission Of West Virginia]], Division of Fish Management, covers federally aided surveys from November 15, 1951 to June 30, 1956 under the [[Federal Aid In Fish Restoration Act]]. The project tested whether removing competing fish from streams would improve growth of [[Smallmouth Bass]] in eastern West Virginia. Experiments on [[Mill Creek]] (sucker and centrarchid removal), [[Lunice Creek]] (removal of all undesirable species, with ground fish returned as fertilizer), and [[Lost River]] (natural fluctuations and gigging) were combined with a 16-stream habitat survey. The report concludes that removing undesirable fish by electrofishing is not a practical management tool. Stream habitat and cover, not competition, appear to control smallmouth abundance.

## Background & Description

The project was conceived by E. A. Seaman, then Chief of the Fish Division. Carl R. Sullivan served as Project Leader, with Harvey Beall, Ralph A. Bitely, George Holton, Jules Marron and others serving as Leaders or Assistant Leaders. Harry Van Meter was Chief of the Division.

The work was organized into seven jobs:

- **Job A:** Improvement of [[Electric Fishing Gear]] and its application.
- **Job B:** Pilot removal of [[White Sucker]] and [[Hog Sucker]] in Mill Creek.
- **Job C:** Pilot removal of centrarchids other than smallmouth bass in Mill Creek.
- **Job D:** Removal of all undesirable species in Lunice Creek.
- **Job E:** Natural population fluctuations in Lost River.
- **Job F:** Studies of other smallmouth bass streams.
- **Job G:** Literature review and report preparation.

Smallmouth bass are the principal game fish. The minimum legal length in West Virginia is 9 inches. Field methods relied on [[Electrofishing]] with a 230-volt D.C. shocker, [[Mark And Recapture]] population estimates, and [[Length-Frequency Method]] age and growth determination. Methods are described in a paper in the *Progressive Fish-Culturist* (January 1954).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mill Creek (Hampshire County)** is a small South Branch Potomac tributary. Its lower two miles run through a steep canyon with boulders and limestone springs. Upstream, quieter water has tree roots, undercut banks and boulders. Pools average 35 feet wide and reach 5 feet deep. Floods in 1954 and August 1955 repeatedly reshaped the pools.

**Lunice Creek** flows into the South Branch of the Potomac at Petersburg. The study section is about 60 percent pool and 66 percent riffle in some descriptions (the text gives pool-riffle figures of 34 and 66 percent). Water is hard (pH 8.5, specific conductance 301 x 10^-6 mhos/cm). Bulldozing for flood control had drained many pools.

**Lost River** has soft water (hardness about 38 as CaCO3; pH about 7.1). Water temperatures ranged from 95°F in late July to 32°F in winter (1952). Heavy floods once or twice a year reshaped the channel. The 1.13-mile section is about 75 percent riffle.

Across 16 eastern West Virginia streams, water temperatures ranged 32°F to 90°F, and pH ranged from 6.7 to 8.7. Conductance ranged from 93 to 417 x 10^-6 mhos/cm. Altitudes ranged from 400 to 1,550 feet, and pool widths from 32 to 99 feet.

## Key Findings

### Job A: Electrofishing Gear
- Project shortening to 9 months left very little work done.
- A November meeting with electrical engineers from the Carbide Company (Charleston, WV) planned a voltage regulator and variable pulsator, but these did not materialize.
- Gear improvements came mainly from trial and error. The report recommends further exploration of electrical methods.

### Job B: White and Hog Sucker Removal (Mill Creek, Grant County)
- In a 2.2-mile study area, suckers made up 90 percent of the weight of important species in 1952. About 90 percent of their weight was removed in December 1952, 1953 and 1954.
- Mill Creek had the largest sucker population per unit area of the 18 streams studied.
- Smallmouth bass showed no significant increase in growth after sucker removal began in 1952.
- Bass populations fell to under 10 percent of pre-manipulation levels, mostly due to flood habitat destruction. Bullheads were the only species not showing sharp drops.
- Mill Creek was judged unsatisfactory for testing sucker removal because its physical instability overwhelmed experimental effects.
- Job B concluded that sucker removal alone is unlikely to benefit smallmouth bass.

### Job C: Centrarchid Removal (Mill Creek, Hampshire County)
- Mill Creek held twice as many pounds of undesirable centrarchids per acre as any of 18 other local streams. Rock bass were the dominant undesirable species.
- **Table 1 removals:** February 1954: 4,891 fish, 382.8 lb; November 1954: 3,488 fish, 300.9 lb; April and July 1955: 1,832 fish, 137.9 lb. Combined total: 10,211 fish, about 822 lb.
- Rock bass made up 84 percent of numbers and 92 percent of weight removed.
- Removal was estimated at about 90 percent effective by weight. Reinvasion was rapid, especially by [[Rock Bass]] moving upstream during spring high water.
- Over the study, smallmouth growth increments were compared with three control streams (Patterson Creek, North River, Lost River).
- Pre-manipulation average growth increments were nearly identical: Mill Creek 2.64 in, Patterson Creek 2.69 in, North River 2.64 in, Lost River 2.66 in.
- After suppression, the 1955 average length of the 0+ age class was 3.44 in, the 1+ age class 6.24 in, and the 2+ age class 8.89 in.
- ANOVA gave F = 22.8, versus a 1 percent critical value of about 7.9. The authors concluded that faster growth was unlikely to be due to chance.
- Two years of suppression yielded an estimated 2+ bass 1.78 in longer and 0.18 lb heavier than without suppression. The text gives the percentage increase as 24 percent in the body and 23 percent in the summary.
- The authors judged centrarchid removal responsible for the growth increase, because it was the only known introduced variable.
- Discussion noted that the effect on 2+ fish was much stronger than on 0+ fish. Competition was thought to increase as bass shifted to larger food items, possibly crayfish.
- **Recommendation:** Electrofishing removal of centrarchids is not a practical management technique, given the value of these fish as game and pan fish. A selective poison might be worth exploring.

### Job D: All Undesirable Species Removal (Lunice Creek)
- Undesirable species were all fish except centrarchids and bullheads.
- Lunice Creek had 11 study pools covering 5.7 acres in 1953, expanded to 12 pools in later years.
- Removal totals were 93,000 fish weighing 1,861 lb (the summary gives 94,000 fish). Stonerollers were the most abundant species. Hog suckers ranked second by weight, and white suckers ranked third by weight.
- Shocking removed an estimated 50 percent (minimum) of undesirable weight per trip, but populations recovered within weeks, especially minnows.
- Fish were ground in a portable power chopper and returned to the stream as fertilizer. No detrimental effects were noted.
- Desirable fish in the 11 pools declined from 483.7 lb (1953) to 429.2 lb (1954) and 394.4 lb (1955). Smallmouth bass fell from 217.8 lb to 167.5 lb. The authors attributed this decline to floods and increased angling.
- Smallmouth growth increased. The 1955 0+ average was 4.09 in, and the average growth increment was 2.90 in before treatment, compared with 2.66 in on controls.
- The F value was 22.8, significant at the 1 percent level. Expected growth gains after three years were about 0.9 in (11 percent) longer and 0.095 lb (39 percent) heavier.
- The authors concluded that benefits were extremely small. The fertilizer effect of returned fish may have caused part of the increase.
- Conclusion: removing rough fish to boost bass growth through reduced competition will meet with little success. Ground-fish fertilization may help in streams with very heavy rough fish populations.

### Job E: Natural Fluctuations (Lost River)
- Gigging of non-game fish was legalized in spring 1953. Initial harvest reached about 65 lb of suckers per mile. It fell to about 20 lb per mile in the second year.
- White sucker numbers fell sharply by 1955. Causes included winter ice snaring and a summer flood that filled preferred habitat.
- Smallmouth bass and rock bass changed annually, but without a clear trend.
- Yellowbelly sunfish appear to be declining, possibly from competition with rock bass. The report notes that rock bass were introduced to the region later than smallmouth bass.
- Smallmouth bass were introduced into the Potomac from the Ohio River drainage in 1854.
- Growth differed by year. In 1955, growth was above average, and the length of the growing season was judged important. In 1955 the spring spawn split into two periods, with a three-week cold interval.
- Mortality was roughly 95 percent in the first year. Subsequent mortality was reported as about 78 percent (0+ to 1+), 48 percent (1+ to 2+), and 71 percent (2+ to older). Cumulative four-year mortality was 99.8 percent.
- Of 1,000 fry, an estimated six legal fish could be harvested, reduced to about three by natural mortality.
- Legal-size fish first appeared in the fourth summer. Stocking was judged costly: fingerlings at 3 cents each would yield legal fish costing about $2.00 each.
- Gigging of rough fish is described as a new sport that harvests a renewable crop, with no noticeable effect on game fish.

### Job F: Other Smallmouth Bass Streams (16 Streams)
- Seven streams were newly studied in 1955. Nine more came from earlier jobs. The study streams are tributaries of the Potomac River except the Tygart River, which is part of the Ohio River system.
- **Average smallmouth growth (at close of season):** 0+ 3.2 in, 1+ 6.1 in, 2+ 8.4 in.
- **Average pool biomass:** 147 lb per acre, comprising 53 lb desirable and 94 lb undesirable species.
- **Rank correlations with high smallmouth production:** hog sucker production (rs = 0.84), high altitude (rs = 0.83) and high pH (rs = 0.53) were significant. No negative correlations were found with other species, suggesting competition is of little importance.
- Higher-altitude streams had more boulder and rubble habitat, and more stable channels. The authors concluded that amount of good smallmouth habitat, namely cover, is paramount.
- Total fish production correlated with high pH and conductivity. Narrow, hard-water, alkaline streams produced more desirable fish per acre.
- Average catch composition by weight: 36 lb game and pan species and 64 lb rough species per 100 lb. Smallmouth bass made up about 11 percent of the total.
- Limitations: small samples, few pools in small streams, and sites not necessarily representative of whole streams.

### Overall Conclusions
- Removing undesirable fish, whether suckers, centrarchids or all rough species, produces little or no benefit to smallmouth bass in these streams.
- Growth gains observed in Mill Creek and Lunice Creek were statistically significant but small, and fertilization by returned fish may have contributed.
- Stream habitat, cover and flood regime appear to govern smallmouth abundance more than competition.
- Survival of young-of-year smallmouth through their first growing season is the critical population bottleneck.
- Electrofishing-based manipulation is not recommended as a practical management tool.
