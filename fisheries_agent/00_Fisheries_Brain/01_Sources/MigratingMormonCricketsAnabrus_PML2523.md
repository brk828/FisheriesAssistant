# Migrating Mormon Crickets as Food for Stream Fishes in Dinosaur National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #salmonidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Tyus and Minckley]] documents how mass migrations of [[Mormon Cricket]] (*[[Anabrus Simplex]]*) into the [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (DNM) in 1986 and 1987 supplied a large, seasonal food pulse to stream fishes. Swimming crickets were swept downstream and eaten by four endemic and seven introduced fish species, including two endangered fishes, the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]]. The authors argue that a periodic, massive insect input may be ecologically significant, and that aerial pesticide control of crickets could threaten these fishes, the aquatic food web, and people who consume sport fish.

## Background & Description

The Mormon cricket is a flightless, long-horned grasshopper (Orthoptera: Tettigoniidae) that occurs only in western North America. It is usually solitary at low density but becomes gregarious and migratory at high density, moving from mountain breeding areas to croplands. It is generally regarded as an agricultural pest. Records of outbreaks date to Mormon pioneers in the Salt Lake Valley in 1847 and the cricket plague of 1848.

Predation on crickets is recorded from about 50 terrestrial bird, mammal, and reptile species, but few references involve aquatic animals. Mormon crickets swim readily, and their movements into Utah streams and lakes date to 1848. The authors found no published record of fish predation on this species before this study.

The study had three objectives: (1) evaluate fish predation on Mormon crickets in DNM, (2) discuss the significance of a periodic, massive, seasonal food supply to fishes, and (3) assess possible impacts of cricket control on fishes, aquatic communities, and people. Methods included visual observation, use of crickets as bait in angling (bottom and surface), and stomach inspection of fish taken by angling, trammel nets, and electrofishing. Weekly river trips ran from May through July in 1986 and 1987.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Cricket input to the river.** Early-season cricket movements (May) occurred from roads, and large bands of late instars and adults entered river canyons from early June through July. Every cricket that reached a stream eventually entered the water. Crickets in river currents ranged from a few per hour to over 50 individuals per m² of water surface. Downstream of shorelines where bands entered, densities averaged 10 to 20 individuals/m². At a tributary inflow, 50 individuals/m² was a conservative estimate. Eddies accumulated live, moribund, and dead crickets for hours, and at times hundreds climbed onto the authors' rafts.

**Cricket size and biomass.** A total of 430 crickets from 12 collections in 1987 averaged 3.03 g each. A subsample of 112 males averaged 2.84 g (range 2.30 to 3.02 g) and 141 females averaged 3.12 g (range 3.07 to 3.27 g). The authors could not reliably estimate biomass, but at 10 to 20 individuals/m², bands of 1 km² or more entering the Green River could weigh an estimated 30 to 60 metric tons.

**Timing and river conditions.** The cricket pulse coincides with a period when spring flooding, scouring, and insect emergence reduce other food, and when high turbidity may limit visual feeding by predatory fishes. It also overlaps prereproductive periods for several native species.

**Hydrological change.** Dams have reduced high flows and seasonal flooding in the mainstream Green River. Historically, sustained high flows often exceeded mean discharge (181 m³/s, based on an 86-year record at Jensen, Utah) by more than an order of magnitude during snowmelt, inundating floodplains. Floodplain habitat is now less available, and the authors suggest that terrestrial inputs such as crickets may matter more today than historically.

**Nutritional value.** Defoliart et al. (1982) reported that adult Mormon crickets contain a mean crude protein content of 58% and fat content of 16.5%. Dried crickets contain 2,800 kcal/kg.

## Key Findings

**1. Fish predation was widespread.** Eleven fish species in five families (Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae, Salmonidae, Cottidae) fed on Mormon crickets between 18 May and 1 August 1987. Of these, four are endemic Colorado River fishes: [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*). The other seven are introduced species, including [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], and [[Mottled Sculpin]].

**2. Fish captures.** A total of 587 fish were captured by angling with crickets as bait in 1986 and 1987, or had crickets in their digestive systems. Roundtail and humpback chub were the most voracious feeders. In 1987, these two species made up 53% (N = 210) of the 399 fish captured on cricket-baited hooks. Three Colorado squawfish were caught on crickets, and a fourth attempted to eat one but was not landed. The authors note that bait records for 1986 were incomplete.

**3. Feeding behavior.** When crickets were present, every fish species large enough to eat them did so. Small channel catfish and black bullheads dismembered and consumed even the largest crickets. Some digestive tracts were distended, and crickets filled buccal cavities and protruded from mouths. Channel catfish and carp fed at the surface in eddies whenever crickets were present.

**4. Localized feeding hotspot.** On 28 May 1987, at the confluence of the Green River and a small tributary, 19 of 21 brown trout, 1 of 3 rainbow trout, and 1 cutthroat trout taken just below the inflow were gorged with crickets. No crickets were seen upstream of the confluence (13-km reach), and none of 25 fish of the same three species collected there had eaten crickets. This shows that tributary inflow concentrates crickets and that fish respond quickly.

**5. Significance for endangered fishes.** Much of the Green River basin's only known spawning site for humpback chub, and one of two confirmed spawning sites for Colorado squawfish, is in DNM. Crickets therefore provide a food supply at a critical time for threatened and endangered fishes. The authors do not claim that native fishes time spawning to cricket availability, but they suggest that long-lived native species (e.g., [[Bonytail Chub]] over 40 years, [[Razorback Sucker]] 30 to 40 years, and Colorado squawfish that reached 1.8 m) could use periodic outbreaks for life-history benefits.

**6. Pesticide risks.** Current government control uses pesticides of relatively low vertebrate toxicity, such as [[Carbaryl]] and [[Malathion]]. These can still harm birds and aquatic invertebrates. The authors identify several risks:
- Direct poisoning from aerial spraying, drift, or runoff into streams.
- Contamination of fish through consumption of pesticide-laden crickets, which may be eaten in large numbers.
- Reduced food supply for fishes if nontarget insects are killed.
- Possible effects on gamete production, gametes, or young if outbreaks, spraying, and spawning coincide.

A high incidence of lordosis (spinal curvature) in roundtail chub in DNM has been tentatively linked to past pesticide applications (Haynes and Muth 1985).

**7. Human exposure.** DNM streams are popular for sport fishing, and trout and channel catfish are harvested heavily. Northern pike (*Esox lucius*) are also caught and eaten. If pesticides bioaccumulate in sport fish, people could be exposed through their diet.

**8. Economic and ecological value of crickets.** Defoliart et al. (1982) valued dried cricket powder at $300 per metric ton. The authors estimate that a 1 km² band (10 to 20 crickets/m²) would be worth about $3,000 to $7,000. Mormon crickets also eat aphids and scavenge feces and carrion, which makes them beneficial. Their damage to range grasses is considered slight.

**9. Management recommendation.** The authors call for careful evaluation of aerial pesticide control because of its potential effects on endangered fishes, aquatic food webs, and people. They recommend enforcing regulations that limit direct pesticide impacts, and they note that the Park Service policy of using natural controls where possible was under review at the time. The paper concludes that the ecological role of periodic cricket outbreaks deserves more study, particularly the link between nutrition and reproductive success in native fishes.
