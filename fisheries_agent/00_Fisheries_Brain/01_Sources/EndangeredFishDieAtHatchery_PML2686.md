# Endangered Fish Die at Dexter Hatchery: Losses, Causes and Official Responses (1992)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

In March 1992, a series of accidents at the federal [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in Dexter, New Mexico, killed most of the endangered fish that had been captured in Colorado for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]. The losses involved [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]]. The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) described the setback as serious but not a threat of extinction. Press coverage, a Denver conservation leader's criticism and internal FWS memoranda then produced a dispute over the facts, the causes and the public messaging.

## Background & Description

The document is a set of news clippings and internal FWS memoranda (March 31 to April 13, 1992). It covers the loss, the media response and the agency's rebuttal.

- **Species and status:**
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]]: listed as endangered since 1967; can grow to six feet and 80 pounds; known to early settlers as "Colorado salmon."
  - [[Humpback Chub]]: listed as endangered since 1964; can grow to 20 inches; has a distinctive hump behind the head; a small population lives in the Colorado River on the Colorado-Utah border.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]]: listed as endangered in Colorado since 1979; can grow to three feet and 13 pounds; a small population remains in the northern Colorado River.
- **Recovery context:** Massive dams and reservoirs along the [[Colorado River]] nearly eliminated many fish species. Under the [[Endangered Species Act]], the FWS must prevent extinctions and revive small remaining populations.
- **Captive program:** Since 1988, biologists gathered 16 razorback suckers, 10 Colorado squawfish and 20 humpback chubs (46 fish in total) from Colorado waterways for [[Broodstock]] to restock rivers. Federal and state programs costing about $2 million a year funded the collection. The fish were shipped to Dexter, which holds 200 to 400 of each species. The fish were collected to preserve [[Genetic Diversity]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of loss (as reported):**

- An electrician repairing an incubator mistakenly turned on the backup water valve about two weeks before the die-off. The backup supply comes from the town's municipal water, which is periodically treated with [[Chlorine]], a chemical deadly to most fish.
- The die-off on the night of March 23-24 was attributed to "a combination of disease, problems with an aeration system and chlorine in the water."
- The hatchery's primary supply is natural groundwater pumped into 48 ponds and tanks through its own system. The fish involved were held in the holding house, which the FWS memo says was the site of the loss.
- Hatchery Director [[Buddy Jensen]] argued that the facility lacks a dedicated standby generator and backup water system for the holding house and incubators, a weakness he said he had requested for ten years.

**Conservation concerns:**

- [[Clee Sealing]], a senior fisheries biologist with the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], said the loss "cuts to the core of safety" and stressed the risk of "not having all your eggs in one basket." He warned that more wild fish might now have to be removed, asking how many could be taken "before you jeopardize the wild population."
- The fish from the upper basin were especially important because they came from a different population of razorback suckers and squawfish than those being raised at Dexter.
- Dexter is described as the only hatchery in the world with the sole purpose of propagating endangered or threatened species. It holds 16 endangered species collected across the United States and Mexico.

## Key Findings

**Losses and status of the fish**

- Thirty-four fish survived the transfer to Dexter, according to an FWS memo.
- Ten humpback chubs were eaten the previous year, by squawfish or by migratory birds, raccoons or other animals.
- The March die-off killed most of the razorback suckers, seven Colorado squawfish and a number of humpback chubs. The scanned memo's numerals for some losses are partly illegible.
- Only three razorback suckers and three Colorado squawfish remained alive afterward.
- On April 1, 1992, FWS reported the loss of one more razorback sucker from the four that had survived the disaster. The fish, identified by PIT tag, was found in the freezer after being picked up by a weekend employee.
- On April 8, 1992, a female Colorado squawfish was found dead in the pond, as recorded in a memo listing its PIT tag.

**Official positions**

- [[Tom Smylie]], assistant regional director for the FWS in New Mexico, said: "It's a freak thing... like a comedy of errors and things just multiplied." He added that it was "a step backward" but "not the end of a species."
- [[Galen Buterbaugh]], FWS director in Denver, said the losses "didn't move the species toward extinction, but it certainly didn't push us toward recovery" and that "we don't know how much" the program was set back.
- [[Kelly Drake]], executive director of the [[Colorado Wildlife Federation]], called the deaths a "major catastrophe," said it was a "stunning loss," and criticized the "casual treatment" of the fish, comparing it to the care given [[Black-footed Ferret]] recovery.

**Disputed claims (FWS rebuttal, April 8, 1992)**

Jensen's memo disputed several reported points:

- There was no "comedy of errors"; "nothing comical" occurred.
- The fish cost far less than $2 million, since the fish involved were only a small part of the recovery program.
- The fish losses were adequately documented in time frame and causes.
- The fish were not in ponds but in the holding house.
- The fish were not treated casually, and not all arrived in good or excellent condition.
- The memo also stated that Region 6 personnel had long known that different species were held in a single pond, and that Region 6 staff chose to bring additional fish to Dexter.

**Media and political response**

- The Rocky Mountain News article and the Associated Press wire described the incident in ways the FWS felt were erroneous, including the claim that "THE FEDERAL FISH HATCHERY AT DEXTER... IS UNDER INVESTIGATION."
- Jensen was inundated with calls from ABC, NBC, CBS and the Roswell Daily Record, and fielded calls on the story from a conservation group in Oregon and from a former supervisor. He reported that many outlets believed all fish at Dexter had been lost.
- The Colorado Division of Wildlife (Public Affairs Officers Fred Quartarone and Connie Young) prepared its own news release to correct the reports. It planned to include success stories about Dexter's work.
- Offices of Senators Skeen, Domenici and Bingaman were briefed. Staff from two of these offices were scheduled to visit the hatchery in April.
