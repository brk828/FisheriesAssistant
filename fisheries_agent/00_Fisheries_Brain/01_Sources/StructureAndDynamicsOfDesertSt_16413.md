# Structure and Dynamics of Desert Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Stuart G. Fisher]] (Arizona State University) argues that [[Desert Streams]] are substantially different from [[Mesic Streams]] and therefore serve as a critical test of ecosystem paradigms built largely on humid-region research. Desert streams are typically flashy, intermittent, and sand-bedded, with high insolation, high algal standing crops, very rapid invertebrate turnover, and metabolism driven largely by in situ primary production and flood-delivered fine particles. The author concludes that desert nutrient dynamics are nitrogen-limited, that the hyporheic zone must be included in metabolic budgets, that succession is an important but neglected concept in running waters, and that the [[River Continuum Concept]] should be tested across non-forested systems.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The desert stream is described as a composite system, generalized from Southwestern streams, and compared with the mesic stream paradigm drawn mainly from northern and eastern North America, the Rockies, Sierra Nevada, and Pacific Northwest.
- **Other desert waters:** Playas, rock pools (tinajas), cattle tanks, small natural lakes, and large reservoirs also occur, but none is as extensive or ancient as desert streams. Most native desert Southwest fishes are spring and stream fishes.
- **Physical template:** Most desert channels flow only minutes to days after rain. Only large watersheds are perennial. The example is [[Sycamore Creek]], near Phoenix, Arizona, draining 505 km², with elevations of 427 to 2164 m, precipitation of 30 to 75 cm/yr, and modal summer flow of 0.03 m/s. Potential evaporation exceeds annual precipitation roughly tenfold at lower elevations.
- **Hydrology:** Streams are flashy, with summer "wall of water" flash floods up to 50 cubic-metre-scale events as reported, and base flow sustained by slow release from storage.
- **Stream order:** Perennial flow often first appears in high-order channels (about 6 to 10), so desert headwaters are "underfit." Low-order channels (1 to 4), typical of mesic streams, are dry most of the year.
- **Morphology:** Wide, shallow, braided channels with sparse riparian vegetation on the flood-channel periphery. Spatial intermittency creates discrete source-sink segments of a few to several hundred metres. Figure 6.1 illustrates this generalized morphology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mesic baseline.** Mesic streams are shaded by riparian trees, so in situ photosynthesis is low and food webs depend on allochthonous detritus. Shredders, collector-gatherers, and microbial conditioning transfer energy. Low-quality (high C:N) detritus and cool temperatures produce mostly univoltine insects, with annual production:biomass ratios of about 2 to 5 (Waters 1969). Phosphorus often limits primary production in mesic systems. Mesic streams show gradually rising hydrographs, and their biota is relatively flood-tolerant.

**Desert contrasts.**
- *Litter:* Desert litter input has never been measured, but is presumed low. Riparian cover is discontinuous, leaves are readily exported in floods, and snags and large woody debris that retain leaves are scarce. Shredders are essentially absent, and leaf packs lack shredder use.
- *Fine particles:* Terrestrial fines arrive in convective summer storm runoff and are incorporated into sandy sediments. They are the main food of invertebrates after floods.
- *Light and algae:* Clear water, lack of silt, and high insolation support considerable photosynthetic potential. [[Cladophora Glomerata]] can exceed 700 mg chl a/m² locally.
- *Temperature and turnover:* Warm temperatures speed life cycles. Chironomids and mayflies complete egg-to-adult development in 7 to 14 days.

**Hyporheic zone.** Deep sediments (over 10 cm) show high oxygen demand. In a 30 m reach where sands extend 42 cm deep, hyporheic respiration was reported as equivalent to surface respiration. Interstitial water flows at 0.05 to 0.1 cm/s, with direction controlled by bed convexity.

**Hydrologic and chemical budgets.** Most water and particulate flux occurs in brief, catastrophic floods. A September flash flood raised suspended particulates from 0.3 g/L to 55 g/L. Small arid watersheds produce runoff that recharges aquifers, which later feed permanent streams and springs.

## Key Findings

**1. Primary production and standing crop**
- Mean midsummer chlorophyll a in Sycamore Creek was 191 mg/m²; total biomass was 268 g/m² (mean) and 427 g/m² (maximum).
- [[Tecopa Bore]], a spring-fed Mojave stream that never floods, reached a maximum standing crop of 260 g/m².
- Reported gross primary production (Pg) includes 3.0 g O₂/m²·d at Rattlesnake Springs, 5.3 g O₂/m²·d in Pinto Creek, 8.5 g O₂/m²·d in Sycamore Creek, and 8.7 g O₂/m²·d (annual mean) in Tecopa Bore.
- Flood recovery of preflood chlorophyll took two to three weeks after summer floods.

**2. Fishes and food webs**
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) reached 50 g/m² or more in Sycamore Creek and was perhaps the most common fish in Sonoran desert streams.
- [[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) were collectively an order of magnitude less abundant.
- Gut analyses show that Agosia and Pantosteus consume large amounts of algae, so these common fishes are largely herbivorous.
- Of about 100 macroinvertebrate species in Sycamore Creek, only a handful consume living Cladophora.

**3. Invertebrate energetics**
- Sediment-feeding chironomids and mayflies made up 85% of the invertebrate fauna.
- Collectors reached densities up to 300,000/m².
- Annual secondary production exceeded standing stock 60 to 70 times, and production of 135 g/m²·yr was among the highest reported for stream invertebrates.
- Assimilation efficiency was about 10%, so invertebrates ingested about four times their body weight per day. Organic matter turned over every three days.

**4. Autotrophy versus heterotrophy**
- The P/R ratio for Sycamore Creek was 1.9 without the deep sediment component and 0.9 with it (August 1982). Desert streams may therefore be less autotrophic than two-layer models suggest.
- The author proposes three-layer (water, surface sediment, hyporheic) models.

**5. Nutrient dynamics**
- Across 196 stream and spring sites in Arizona, nitrate-N was significantly higher in springs and floodwaters than in free-flowing streams. Phosphate-P did not differ significantly among system types.
- Spring and floodwater N:P ratios (atomic) were about 18.7 to 29.4, versus about 3.9 in summer low-flow streams. Downstream N:P declines are attributed to nitrate uptake by algae.
- Phosphorus concentration averages about 0.041 mg/L and may be controlled by calcium phosphate solubility.
- The author concludes that southwestern desert streams are most probably nitrogen-limited, not phosphorus-limited, which differs from many other regions.
- Runoff from a 0.65 ha watershed near [[Apache Junction]], Arizona, had nitrogen concentrations two to twenty-six times higher than precipitation. Less than 25% of precipitation appeared as runoff.

**6. Succession and disturbance**
- Sycamore Creek experienced two to nine flash floods per year. Floods removed more than 95% of the biota, except fishes.
- Diatoms colonized first, then filamentous greens and bluegreens. Algal standing crop reached 100 g/m² in two weeks.
- Invertebrate biomass returned to preflood levels within two to four weeks. Gross primary production exceeded respiration five days after recession.
- Desert succession departs from [[Odum Succession Model]] predictions: P and R do not converge, and communities stay dominated by small, fast-growing taxa.

**7. River Continuum Concept and research agenda**
- The author argues that desert streams could extend the [[River Continuum Concept]] to non-forested systems. Isolated reaches of Sycamore Creek and concrete canals offer natural experiments.
- Deserts cover about 25% of the global land surface. The author argues that desert research is valuable because it sharpens general ecosystem principles, and that treating deserts as exceptions makes the rule "parochial."
