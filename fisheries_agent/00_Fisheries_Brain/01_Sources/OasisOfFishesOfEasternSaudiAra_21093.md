# Oasis Fishes of Eastern Saudi Arabia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by W. Ross (*Fauna of Saudi Arabia* 7, 1985) inventories the fishes of seven oases in the Eastern Province of [[Saudi Arabia]], three coastal and four inland. The fauna comprises one secondary freshwater species, ten marine species occurring in drainage systems, and seven introduced species. The material was gathered by the author from September 1977 to May 1981 (the source reads "May 19811" in OCR, read here as May 1981) and supplemented by notes kept from 1979 onward. Specimens were held in brackish aquaria, photographed, preserved in formalin, and deposited at the [[British Museum Natural History]]. The paper reports that marine fishes were largely collected before 1981, and that land reclamation, altered drainage and oil pollution may have reduced many of these populations.

## Background & Description

**Scope and method.** The author describes fishes under three headings: secondary freshwater fish, marine fish found in the oases, and introduced species. No primary freshwater fishes are known from eastern Saudi Arabia (Krupp 1983). Marine specimens were collected alive from water up to 5 ppt salinity and kept at 1.2 ppt. Most were released when they outgrew the author's facilities. Meristic counts follow standard notation: anal (A), dorsal (D), gill rakers (GR), and lateral-line scales (LL), with Roman numerals for unbranched rays and Arabic numerals for branched rays.

**Study area.** The oases range from [[Al-Aba]] in the north to [[Hofuf Al-Hassa Oasis]] in the south. All supporting wells are artesian.

- **Coastal oases:** [[Al-Qatif]] (the largest, heavily populated, degraded by sewage), [[Anik]] (sewage-affected drainage ditches), and [[Al-Azizyah]] (once the most productive collecting site, but its well has collapsed and the sea connections have dried up).
- **Inland oases:** [[Al-Aba]] (uninhabited, reed beds, no introduced fish), [[Umm Al-Sahik]] (populated, *A. dispar* throughout the irrigation system), [[Al-Aujam]] (old-style wells, planned agricultural expansion), and Hofuf (the most inland, with a seasonal outlet to the [[Arabian Gulf]]).

**Hofuf irrigation network.** Al-Hassa has many villages and extensive farmland. Its springs are cemented and fitted with sluice gates. Gellner (1975) reports 1,624 km of concrete irrigation channels and 1,520 km of earth drainage ditches, all managed by the Al-Hassa Irrigation and Drainage Authority.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat connectivity:** The coastal oases have drainage ditches that connect to the sea. Hofuf drains to the Gulf in the cool season and fragments into landlocked pools in summer. Al-Azizyah's sea connections were lost when its well failed.
- **Pollution and land use:** Sewage disposal has degraded Al-Qatif and Anik. Land reclamation in Taroot Bay has redirected drainage ditches. The author links these changes, together with oil pollution in the Gulf, to declines in marine fish.
- **Conservation proposal:** The author suggests that [[Al-Aba]], a small island oasis surrounded by sabkha, could become a "mini national park" because of its abundant flora and fauna.
- **Introduction pathways:** Aquarium hobbyists who release unwanted livebearers, fish farm escapes, and deliberate stocking for mosquito or weed control have all introduced species. Introduced fishes are more often found in human-inhabited oases.

## Key Findings

**Faunal inventory**
- **Secondary freshwater:** One species, *Aphanius dispar*, from the family Cyprinodontidae. It is the only secondary freshwater genus so far described from Arabia.
- **Marine fishes in drainage systems:** Ten species, comprising nine identified species and one unidentified bream (family Sparidae). Their families are Chanidae, Hemirhamphidae, Theraponidae, Lutjanidae, Sparidae, Mugilidae, Gobiidae and Soleidae.
- **Introduced fishes:** Seven species. Four are poeciliids (*Gambusia affinis holbrooki*, *Poecilia latipinna*, *P. reticulata*, *Xiphophorus maculatus*) and three are cichlid tilapiines (*Oreochromis aureus*, *O. mossambicus*, *O. niloticus*).

**Selected species accounts**
- ***Aphanius dispar*** (Rüppell, 1828): Widely distributed from NE Africa to NW India, in fresh, brackish or marine water. Counts: D 8–11, A 9–11, LL 24–28, GR 11–17. Sexual dimorphism in fin length and colour pattern. It is abundant at Al-Aba and occurs in most oases with open water. It was introduced to Al-Kharg in 1948 to control anopheline mosquito larvae (Dacey 1959). Many specimens carry metacercarial larvae (Ross 1979).
- ***Lutjanus argentimaculatus***: One of the few snappers that lives in fresh water. At Al-Qatif it had penetrated oasis pools, where *A. dispar* was less numerous. The author suggests predation as a possible cause.
- ***Mugil cephalus***: Widespread and abundant in the main Al-Hassa drainage ditch. It feeds on diatoms, algae and benthic animal matter. The author is concerned that tilapiine introductions may compete with it for food.
- ***Chanos chanos***: Recorded in North Al-Qatif drainage ditches in 1979/80. Max length approximately 180 cm, usual catch about 100 cm.
- ***Bathygobius fuscus***: Collected in a tidal drainage ditch at the south end of Al-Qatif. Solitary and territorial.
- ***Synaptura orientalis***: A single specimen from soft sand in the lower reaches of a south Al-Qatif ditch.

**Introduced species: distribution and status**
- ***Gambusia affinis holbrooki***: Established throughout the Al-Hassa oases. The author found no evidence that it was introduced there for malaria control, despite Dacey's (1959) record of imports from Egypt before 1950 to Al-Qatif and Yabrin. It was on sale to aquarists in 1977.
- ***Poecilia latipinna***: Found in two Anik and Al-Qatif ditches in 1979, with three colour forms recorded at Anik in 1983 (wild, speckled, and black). The author hypothesises that the black variety originated from aquarium stock and reverted toward wild colours after breeding in the wild.
- ***Poecilia reticulata***: Large population in the Anik drainage ditches. Domesticated fish are reverting to original colours and fin shapes.
- ***Xiphophorus maculatus***: A small colony south of Al-Qatif, harder to find than the other introduced species.
- **Tilapiines (Cichlidae):** Three *Oreochromis* species are established in the Al-Hassa system. *O. aureus* is a single specimen from the main drainage ditch. *O. mossambicus* is less numerous than *O. niloticus*. *O. niloticus* is common and has an algae-eating habit that led to its introduction.

**Hybridisation and mixed origins**
- In 1981, Dr. Mohammed Shahjahan Howlader introduced tilapiines to Al-Hassa's wells and concrete channels at the request of the Irrigation and Drainage Authority, which needed weed and algae control. The stock was thought to be *O. niloticus* from Taiwan, but later proved to be a mixed population, possibly including *O. mossambicus*.
- Two bream-free fish farms on the Hofuf drainage ditch culture tilapiine hybrids (*O. niloticus* × *O. aureus*). Some red cherry tilapia (*O. mossambicus* × *O. urolepis hornorum*) have been observed among their stock. Escapees have entered the drainage ditch, though none were seen free in the wild.
- Morphological counts on two *O. niloticus* from the main irrigation channel showed 16 dorsal spines (modal 17). One drainage-ditch specimen had 17 dorsal spines but only 11 soft rays. The author proposes two populations: *O. niloticus* in the pre-irrigation water and a hybrid-like form in the drainage water.

**Other introductions under consideration**
- Dr. Shahjahan experimented with *Cyprinus carpio* to control algae and weeds, and it may be released in future. A small accidental release of *Labeo robita* into the irrigation system may also lead to establishment. A possible tilapiine introduction from the Philippines is unconfirmed.

**Discussion points and cumulative counts**
- The paper adds five freshwater species to the six previously recorded for Arabia (Krupp 1983). It also adds six marine families to the two previously reported as entering freshwater.
- Most marine collecting ended before 1981, so many species may now be rare or absent.
- The author expects further introductions as fish farming (at least one at Al-Qatif) and the aquarium trade expand.
