# A Conservation Plan for Native Fishes of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #conservation_biology #endangered_species_management

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: The lower Colorado River historically supported four federally endangered "big-river" fishes: [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). These species are long-lived with generation times of 4–8 years or more. Razorback suckers produce approximately 1700 ova per cm standard length (average 50 cm SL = 85,000 ova per female). Males reach maturity in 2–3 years, females in 3–4 years. In lower-basin waters at moderate densities, young razorback suckers reach >30 cm total length (TL) in the first year; bonytail exceed 25 cm TL in year one. Growth continues substantially with age; razorback suckers in Lake Mohave showed consistent growth across cohorts, with fish reaching 45 cm TL by 2–3 years post-repatriation. Adult Colorado squawfish historically occurred at densities of 1.4–80 fish per km (averaging ~17) in the Green River. The original fish fauna included 13 species (10 freshwater); historical species-level endemism for the Colorado River basin was approximately 75%.

**Habitat Associations**: Big-river fishes originally occupied the entire lower Colorado River basin including main stem channels, backwaters, floodplain habitats, and tributaries. Humpback chub currently persist in the [[Little Colorado River]]–[[Grand Canyon]] complex, with one viable population hovering near 10,000 adults in the early 1990s (subsequently declined). Approximately 10% occupy the adjacent Colorado River main stem. Razorback sucker populations in [[Lake Mohave]] declined from an estimated 73,500 wild adults (1980–1993) to 9,086 by 1999. Bonytail persist only in Lake Mohave and possibly [[Lake Havasu]] as remnant wild fish augmented by hatchery reintroductions. Colorado squawfish were extirpated from the lower basin; the last wild individual was caught in 1975. Cold water from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] precludes humpback chub reproduction downstream in the Colorado River main stem. The lower Colorado River is defined as the reach downstream from Glen Canyon Dam.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Adult razorback suckers show extensive seasonal movement, making them vulnerable to capture via movable traps or fixed weirs. Some young humpback chub move from the Little Colorado River into the Colorado River main stem, mature, and reenter the tributary to spawn, demonstrating tributary-main stem connectivity. Adult Colorado squawfish historically moved upstream to spawn in numbers sufficient for harvest as human and livestock food. The plan envisions panmictic adult populations residing in main channel and connected waters, exchanging reproductive adults and repatriated subadults with isolated off-channel breeding populations.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: The fundamental problem limiting native fish persistence is abundant nonnative predatory species that preclude recruitment of natives. Nonnative predators prey heavily on larvae and juveniles of native species. All native species tested reproduce successfully in predator-free habitats but few succeed in waters shared with nonnatives. The nonnative fish fauna includes ecological generalists that are widespread, competitive, predatory or omnivorous: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], [[Red Shiner]], and others. Flathead catfish now occupy the entire former range of Colorado squawfish in the lower river, at densities of 156–259 fish per km (Colorado River near Yuma) and ~70 fish per km (Gila River)—4 to 15 times more abundant than Colorado squawfish in the Green River. Size-dependent predation is the limiting factor: step-like relationships exist between body size of repatriates at release and their survival. Fish ≥30 cm TL are essentially immune to existing predators.

**Environmental Drivers**: Water development—damming rivers, creating impoundments and cold tailwaters, degrading habitats, and desiccating long reaches—has adversely affected native fishes. The Colorado River is one of the most controlled rivers on Earth. Glen Canyon Dam creates cold water that precludes reproduction downstream. River regulation per se is not the principal threat; rather, the establishment of nonnative predators in modified habitats drives recruitment failure. Natural recruitment can be 0.01% or less in the presence of nonnatives. For razorback suckers in communities with predatory nonnatives, recruitment failed for approximately 40–50 years because of near-total loss of juveniles.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: All four big-river species are critically imperiled and federally listed as endangered. Colorado squawfish: extirpated from lower basin (last caught 1975). Bonytail: persist only as remnant wild fish in Lake Mohave and possibly Lake Havasu, augmented by hatchery reintroductions; approximately 200,000 fish stocked 1981–1990 essentially disappeared (survival <0.001%). Humpback chub: one viable population in Little Colorado River–Grand Canyon complex, hovering near 10,000 adults into early 1990s, recently thought to have declined substantially. Razorback sucker: Lake Mohave population declined from 73,500 wild adults (1980–1993) to 40,093 (1992), 23,118 (1993), and 9,086 (1999)—an 88% decline over 7 years. By 1999, wild fish population was ~9,000 individuals plus an estimated 3,000 surviving adult repatriates (total ~12,000 adults). Repatriates first joined breeding aggregations in 1993 and by 1999 accounted for 12% of the population. The population consists mostly of large, old adults with no evidence of natural recruitment despite annual spawning.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Primary threats include nonnative predatory species (precluding recruitment), water development and river regulation, habitat degradation, and inadequate recovery planning. Existing recovery implementation plans are deemed inadequate for these critically imperiled species. USFWS proposed recovery goals of Nc = 700–5,800 wild fish per species or river reach are considered grossly inadequate and cannot be supported by contemporary science. Such low numbers are especially concerning given that far larger numbers and genetic variability can be readily maintained. Hatchery culture has severely restricted genetic variability of stocks: brood stocks developed from as few as 5 and up to 25 paired matings using 5 males and 5 females are "utterly inadequate." Genetic variation will decay quickly if population sizes remain small and populations are founded or maintained by small numbers of brood fish. Additional threats include potential contamination of off-channel habitats by nonnatives through vandalism or inadvertent introduction, requiring renovation and reestablishment protocols.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: The authors propose a science-based recovery strategy involving: (1) **Off-channel breeding habitats**: Excavate isolated, predator-free habitats (1–2 hectares each, elongate and narrow) where native species breed and progeny grow to subadult size. Exclude nonnatives by passing water through size-graded gravel at inlets/outlets. Position habitats to exploit gravity flow and high water tables. Recommend 10 complexes with multiple habitats per complex. (2) **Panmictic main-channel populations**: Maintain large, wide-ranging, genetically diverse adult populations in main channel, backwaters, and reservoirs. (3) **Repatriation program**: Rear larvae to subadults (≥30–35 cm TL) in off-channel habitats, then transfer en masse to channel plus connectives. Target annual or biannual harvesting. Model example: 100 ha with 50 females/ha yielding 10 young/female provides 50,000 subadults/year; at 5% first-year survival and 80% subsequent survival, Nc stabilizes at ~60,000 adults in ~20 years. (4) **Brood stock turnover**: Regularly transfer sexually mature repatriates (10% annually) back into off-channel habitats to maintain high turnover in parentage and complete turnover each decade. (5) **Genetic management**: Maintain effective population size (Ne) of at least 500–1,000 (requiring census population Nc of ~5,000–10,000 adults, assuming Ne/Nc ratio of 0.10). Authors advocate for far higher targets to preserve evolutionary potential. (6) **Harvest protocols**: Design habitats for mass collection via seining (large, smooth-bottomed, shallow areas), traps, weirs, electrofishing, or nets. Concentrate harvesting in cooler seasons to minimize mortality. (7) **Nonnative control**: May require limiting nonnative fish numbers through increased angling pressure or direct control, especially as future water diversions reduce habitat quantity/quality. (8) **Monitoring**: Incorporate parasite, pathogen, and contaminant monitoring into harvest-transfer protocols. (9) **Renovation**: Regularly destroy and reconstruct habitats to eliminate nonnatives that inevitably become established, mimicking natural channel realignment processes.

**Biological Reference Points**: **Effective population size (Ne)**: Franklin (1980) suggested Ne = 500 to balance mutation and genetic drift for neutral variants; Lande (1995) suggested Ne = 5,000 may be required for potentially adaptive variation. With Ne/Nc ratio of ~0.10, maintaining Ne = 500 requires Nc ≈ 5,000 adults; Ne = 1,000 requires Nc ≈ 10,000 adults. **Long-term female Ne estimates** from mtDNA: humpback chub 97,500, bonytail 89,500, razorback sucker 669,000 (assuming constant population size over evolutionary time). **Size-dependent survival thresholds**: First-year survival of repatriates increases from ~5% at 30 cm TL to ~80% in subsequent years as fish grow to 45 cm TL. Fish ≥30 cm TL are essentially immune to predation. **Growth rates**: Razorback suckers reach >30 cm TL in year 1, 45 cm by year 2; bonytail reach >25 cm in year 1, 30+ cm by year 2. **Fecundity**: Razorback sucker females produce ~1700 ova per cm SL (average 50 cm = 85,000 ova/female). **Survival to replace population**: 0.005% survival (2 young/female) of offspring from ~36,750 females (half the 1980–1993 Nc) would replace entire Lake Mohave population while preserving genetic variation. **Historical densities**: Colorado squawfish 1.4–80/km (avg ~17) in Green River; flathead catfish 70–259/km in lower basin streams. **Management goal**: Level III (stabilization) of USFWS conceptual plan—maintain genetic variability that existed a century ago, defined not by numbers but by evolutionary potential.
