# Winter-Run Chinook Salmon in the Sacramento River, California: Notes on Water Temperature Requirements at Spawning

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 Special Scientific Report by Daniel W. Slater (Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, [[Fish and Wildlife Service]]) documents the [[Winter-Run Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*), a race restricted to the [[Sacramento River]] system of California. The race holds in fresh water from December to April, spawns from April into July, and is distinguished from the late [[Fall-Run Chinook Salmon]] mainly by the green condition of its gonads. The author argues that the run is poorly understood rather than rare, that it has recovered strongly since the 1943-46 salvage operations at [[Shasta Dam]], and that water temperature in May through August is the main factor limiting its natural range.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon and status:** Winter-run chinook are described as the least-known of the [[Central Valley Chinook Salmon]] runs (Fry, 1961). Slater argues they are not necessarily the least abundant.
- **Why the race was overlooked:** (1) the adult run overlapped the late fall run and its fishery; (2) adults were present in winter and spring, when observation is difficult; (3) spawners were isolated in inaccessible sections of the [[McCloud River]] before Shasta Dam; and (4) runs were numerically small until recently.
- **Historical records:** Spawning was first recorded in 1902 (two fish, April 24, McCloud River opposite [[Baird Hatchery]]), reported by Rutter (1904). Hanson, Smith and Needham (1940) recorded 25 salmon over nests on May 26, 1939, in the upper McCloud River. Needham, Hanson and Parker (1943) first formally named the "winter run."
- **Survey method:** About 20 salmon agencies and researchers were queried by mail; all 18 responses reported the race was unknown outside the Sacramento River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and temperature**
- The historic home stream was the spring-fed McCloud River, with stable temperatures of about 46 °F at [[Big Springs]].
- Suitable hatching temperatures were given as 42.5–57.5 °F (Combs and Burrows, 1957; Brett, 1959). Survival is very low above about 58 °F (Seymour, 1956; Hinze et al., 1956).
- Spawning in [[Mill Creek]], [[Battle Creek]] and [[Deer Creek]] is normally unsuccessful, since July water temperatures exceed 70 °F and midsixties are lethal to eggs.

**Impacts of Shasta Dam**
- Dam construction blocked upstream migration from 1942-43 onward. Salvage trucks carried fish to Deer Creek, where spawned-out fish were found and eggs probably did not survive.
- Temperatures of 62–73 °F (1943) and 52–61 °F (1944) at Redding were unfavourable. In 1945 and 1946, June-July temperatures of 46–50 °F allowed successful reproduction in the main river.
- Reservoir releases carried oxygen deficiency and heavy-metal pollution.

**Migration and timing**
- Up-migration: adults reach the [[Feather River]] mouth (about 225 miles downstream of Keswick Dam) in early November. Arrival near Redding is delayed until about November 15 by a diversion-dam fishway closure.
- Down-migration: studied only incidentally. Balls Ferry netting (1951-52) suggests young of about 58–90 mm may be winter-run fish, but sampling before November was lacking.

## Key Findings

**1. Population recovery after salvage (1943–46)**
- The winter run fell to near extinction during the salvage period. Keswick trapping took about 200 fish in 1945 and only 20 in 1946.
- Recovery was rapid. A sizable run appeared in January-February 1949 (Smith, 1950). By 1961-62, anglers caught an estimated 11,000 winter-run fish between Hamilton City and Keswick Dam (Hallock, personal communication).
- Counts on May 22, 1962, in Battle Creek gave 457 fish observed and an estimated total of 2,687 fish between Coleman Hatchery and the county bridge.
- Slater concludes that the run is now much more abundant than the spring run in the mainstem and approaches the fall run in abundance.

**2. Spawning success in tributaries**
- In 1958, 420 winter-run fish were hauled from Keswick to [[Coleman National Fish Hatchery]], and 309 were trapped from Battle Creek. From 236 females, 381,065 eggs were collected (April 30–June 13, 1958).
- Mortality in the 1958–59 hatchery cycle was heavy. Accumulated mortality reached 97.6% by July, and only 3,036 fish were liberated on January 29, 1959. Battle Creek is therefore unsuitable for maintaining the run.

**3. Successful experimental hatching at 50 °F**
- In 1963, 52 ripe females yielded 235,700 eggs, with about 5% mortality through the eyed stage, when incubated in 50 °F Sacramento River water at Keswick. Fifty thousand eyed eggs were shipped to Victoria, Australia.

**4. Timing of the run**
- Adults arrive in the Redding area from late November through February, spawn from late April nearly through July (peak in May and June), and eggs incubate at least through August.
- The spawning period does not overlap that of any other run, which may explain the race's growing numbers. Spring-run numbers were held down by competition with early fall-run spawners and probable hybridization.

**5. Fishery value**
- Adults are described as "sleek, fat, silvery, and full of fight," with pale pink flesh. The principal fishing season is mid-December through March, with a peak in January–February.
- Anglers take a proportionally higher share of this race than of the fall or spring runs in holding areas below Keswick Dam.

**6. Key conclusions**
- Water temperature (May–August, about 42.5–57.5 °F) is the main factor limiting natural range extension. The run's reproductive cycle runs counter to all other chinook runs, since eggs mature under increasing daylight and temperature.
- Slater considers the race a promising introduction stock for areas with suitable May–August temperatures. Mine-waste pollution and predation of downstream migrants are noted as possible risks. Historic populations were probably confined to predominantly spring-fed streams.
