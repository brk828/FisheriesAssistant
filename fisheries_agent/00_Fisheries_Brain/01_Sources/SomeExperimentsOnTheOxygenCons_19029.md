# Oxygen Consumption of Goldfish in Relation to Swimming Speed

#ichthyology #marine-biology #carassius-auratus

## Executive Summary

This 1965 laboratory study by H. Smit examines how the [[Oxygen Consumption]] of [[Goldfish]] (*[[Carassius Auratus]]*) changes with [[Swimming Speed]], and whether oxygen uptake can reliably estimate swimming power. Using a rotatable annular chamber ([[Fry's Annular Respirometer]]) and [[Blazka's Apparatus]], the author found that oxygen use rises with speed, that a shift to a more regular swimming style allows about four times the speed at the same oxygen uptake, and that [[Excitement]] and [[Oxygen Debt]] can inflate or distort metabolic estimates. The study concludes that only non-excited rates should be used to calculate swimming energy, and that paired [[Carbon Dioxide Production]] measurements are needed to assess [[Anaerobic Metabolism]].

## Background & Description

Oxygen uptake of fish depends on activity. [[Fry's Scope For Activity]] (1947) is defined as the difference between active and standard oxygen consumption, and Fry showed a linear relation between goldfish swimming speed and the square root of scope. The paper addresses a methodological problem: at routine speeds, oxygen debt lowers estimates of power, while excitement raises them. Both also complicate estimation of [[Standard Metabolism]], which is commonly derived by extrapolation to zero activity (Spoor 1946; Beamish and Mookherjii 1964) or as the lowest value over 24 hours (Job 1955).

**Experimental subjects and conditions**

- Goldfish weighing 60 to 103 g, acclimated and tested at 20 °C.
- Ambient oxygen maintained at 6 to 8 p.p.m.
- Fish starved for at least 3 days and placed in the chamber at least 1 day before testing, with some training to acclimate them to the apparatus.
- Experiments limited to 15 minutes in the Fry apparatus and 30 minutes in the Blazka apparatus, so oxygen never fell below 6 p.p.m.

**Apparatus**

- *Fry's annular chamber*: rotatable, so fish can be spontaneously active or forced to hold position. Three barrier [[Photocell]]s at 120° intervals count swimming when the chamber is stationary. When rotating, passes past a fixed point are counted as forward (positive) or backward (negative) and used to correct the chamber speed.
- *Blazka's apparatus*: two horizontal concentric cylinders in which a propeller drives water through the inner cylinder, forcing the fish to swim against a current at a speed set by propeller speed.

Full data are held in five unpublished tables (Tables DI to DV) in the National Science Library Depository of Unpublished Data, National Research Council, Ottawa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study is a controlled laboratory investigation and does not address field populations, habitat use or natural water conditions. Its ecological relevance lies in energetics of routine swimming and the handling of respiratory data.

- **Routine versus excited swimming:** At low speeds the goldfish swam irregularly, braking with the pectoral fins and restarting, which increased energy cost without much distance covered. Individual fish differed in this behavior, which the author described as "nervous" versus "quiet" animals.
- **Swimming mode:** Above about 0.5 body lengths per second, goldfish shift to a more regular, tail-driven style that greatly reduces oxygen cost per unit distance.
- **Anaerobic metabolism in other species:** The paper cites Blazka (1958), who reported that trout repay oxygen debt while the crucian carp does not, and that crucian carp showed complete anaerobiosis over winter. Beamish (1964) questioned whether standard metabolism can be measured without an anaerobic component at low oxygen.
- **Oxygen conditions:** Measurements were kept well above the critical oxygen level, which the author noted limits interference with standard metabolism estimates.

## Key Findings

**1. Oxygen consumption rises with swimming speed, but the relation depends on individual behavior.**
- In the non-rotating annular chamber (four fish), semilogarithmic relations were found, but slopes differed. Extrapolated standard metabolism for fish 96 exceeded 100 mg O₂/kg·h, while fish 60 gave only about 50 mg O₂/kg·h.
- The author attributed these differences to excitement and irregular swimming behavior rather than to physiology alone.

**2. Regular swimming allows about fourfold speed at the same oxygen uptake.**
- For an 87 g goldfish, a clear drop in oxygen consumption occurred between about 8 and 10 cm/s.
- Above 10 cm/s, swimming became regular, with dorsal and pectoral fins laid flat and tail-only propulsion. The fish could quadruple its speed at the same oxygen uptake.
- The paper states that the goldfish passes to another swimming type above about 0.5 body lengths per second.

**3. Standard metabolism and scope for activity for the 87 g fish.**
- Standard metabolism estimated at 35 mg O₂/kg·h.
- Oxygen consumption minus 35 mg O₂/kg·h was used as a measure of work done.

**4. Energy calculations.**
- Caloric value of oxygen taken as 4.8 Cal per litre (1 mg O₂ ≈ 143 × 10² g·cm).
- Thermodynamic efficiency of propulsion muscles assumed to be 28%.
- Propulsion efficiency of the tail assumed to be 80%, following Dickmann (1953).
- Drag from [[Prandtl's Formula]] (F = ½ρAV²C) was modified for form drag by multiplying C by 1.2 (Bainbridge 1961).
- Surface area estimated as A = 0.4L², with L the body length in centimetres. The 87 g fish measured 16 cm.
- Drag coefficient taken as an intermediate value, C = 0.74R⁻⁰·¹⁵ ([[Reynolds Number]] R = LV/ν, with ν = 0.01 cm²/s, density 1.0).

**5. Theoretical versus measured oxygen consumption (87 g fish; Table I, Fig. 5).**
- Lowest oxygen uptake in short-term rotating-chamber experiments (20 minutes per speed) averaged about 3.6 times the theoretical values.
- Lowest values in long-term Blazka experiments (7 hours per speed) were about 1.5 times the theoretical rates.
- The author proposed that this excess may result from excitement.

**6. Excitement raises oxygen consumption.**
- Goldfish swimming at constant speed in Blazka's apparatus showed declining oxygen uptake over time, likely reflecting subsiding excitement (Fig. 6; four fish, 87 to 103 g, at 22, 29 and 41 cm/s).
- After about 3½ hours at 22 cm/s, an increase to 35 cm/s (Fig. 7; 82 g fish) produced a sharp rise in oxygen consumption that lasted several hours before falling.

**7. Postexercise oxygen consumption.**
- For a 60 g goldfish, extra oxygen consumption persisted for many hours after exercise (Fig. 8; 30 minutes at 25 cm/s).
- For an 87 g goldfish, 20 minutes at 10 cm/s and 20 minutes at 32 cm/s produced nearly identical mean extra oxygen consumption: 68 and 69 mg O₂/kg·h, respectively.
- The author suggests the extra uptake reflects postexercise excitement more than repayment of [[Oxygen Debt]], though both may contribute. Simultaneous [[Carbon Dioxide Production]] measurements were recommended to resolve this.

**8. Methodological conclusions.**
- Only rates measured when the fish is not excited should be used to calculate swimming energy.
- Precautions to limit excitement include prolonged habituation, constant illumination, shielding of the chamber, and avoidance of shocks and vibration.
- Long-term experiments, which gave lower oxygen values, and more experiments in general, were recommended for a more reliable energy estimate.
- The mechanism by which excitement increases oxygen uptake was left unexplained.
