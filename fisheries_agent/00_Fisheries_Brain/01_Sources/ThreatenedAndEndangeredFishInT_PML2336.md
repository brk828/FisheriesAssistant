# Endangered and Threatened Fish of the Yampa and Green Rivers, Dinosaur National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper summarizes the status of endangered and threatened fish in the Yampa and Green rivers of [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. It draws on a literature review and on research by the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]]. The four endemic fish of the [[Upper Colorado River]] system discussed are the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Sucker]]. Their decline is attributed mainly to dam construction, altered flow and temperature regimes, and competition and predation from introduced fish. The authors argue that the Yampa River is critical to the survival of these species and that further water development would add stress to an already over-allocated resource.

## Background & Description

The upper Green and Yampa rivers begin as cold, clear streams in the Rocky and Uinta mountains and flow through desert canyons carved in soft sandstone. Historically, the main stems were warm, turbid, and subject to sudden changes in volume and velocity.

The [[Colorado River]] is described as one of the most utilized and contested rivers in the world. It carries less water per square mile of drainage than any other major U.S. river, yet supplies about 15 million people. The region also holds major coal, oil, oil shale, and uranium deposits, and water is expected to be central to their development.

[[Flaming Gorge Dam]], completed in 1963 near the Utah-Wyoming border, changed the upper Green River. Flows that were once high in spring and low in winter became more stabilized seasonally, though they may fluctuate rapidly on a daily basis.

The study area contains three distinct habitat types and one transitional section:

- **Green River above Echo Park:** a cold-water environment under the artificial control of Flaming Gorge Dam.
- **Yampa River:** a relatively free-flowing, warm-water river.
- **Green River below the Monument:** a warm-water river that has largely recovered from dam effects. It extends about 550 km to the confluence with the Colorado River in [[Canyonlands National Park]].
- **Green River between Echo Park and the lower Monument boundary:** a gradient zone from cold to warm water, about 42 km long, from the Yampa-Green confluence to the Monument's southwest boundary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dam effects.** The 1963-1966 study, requested by Secretary of the Interior [[Stewart L. Udall]], found that the Green River changed significantly between the dam and the mouth of the Yampa. Below the confluence, the Yampa moderated the altered river enough to sustain all fish species present before impoundment. Large native fish disappeared from the 105 km reach below the dam because of cold discharge and were replaced by introduced [[Rainbow Trout]].

**Species distribution.** Downstream of the Monument, the Yampa and Green rivers hold common fish. The [[Red Shiner]] is rare in the Yampa but abundant in the lower Green. Juvenile Colorado squawfish were collected at Jensen, Utah, in October 1975, and the Squawfish Recovery Team captured more fish there in May 1976. Conditions therefore appear suitable for at least limited reproduction downstream of the Monument, but not upstream.

**Threat framework.** The authors outline likely causes of decline:

1. **Stream alteration:**
   - *Dams:* physical barriers to migration; more stable annual but less stable daily flows, which reduce scouring peak flows and spawning-triggering flows; cooler summers and warmer winters; reduced turbidity; reservoirs that are unsuitable for large-river fish reproduction and favor other species.
   - *Irrigation:* increased soil leaching and salinity through return flows, and water quality changes from fertilizers and pesticides.
   - *Dewatering:* reduced flow volume and velocity, and concentrated dissolved solids from transmountain diversion, evaporation, and water use.
   - *Channelization:* loss of slow backwaters and eddies, unstable substrate that harms invertebrate food, and unstable banks from grazing, roads, and other land use.
2. **Competition and predation** from introduced non-native fish.
3. **Pollution and eutrophication** from toxic industrial, agricultural, and municipal wastes, and rising salinity and sedimentation.
4. **Other factors:** parasites from exotic fish, shifts in food organism composition, and heavy fishing pressure on large adult squawfish and on bonytail and humpback chubs, which are very vulnerable to sport fishing.

## Key Findings

**Colorado Squawfish**
- Once abundant across the Colorado River system, from Wyoming to the Gulf of California, the species is now rare and restricted to parts of the upper basin.
- Adults were collected in the lower Yampa in 1968-71 and in 1974-75 during July and August, after water temperatures reached 20-21 °C. Most captured fish were males. Several suspected spent females and one ripe female (August 1975) were also reported, the latter from deeper water.
- Summer movement into the Yampa is assumed to be a spawning migration triggered by seasonal cues such as temperature, turbidity, flow, photoperiod, or water chemistry. Spawning has not been observed in turbid river water. At [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], Arizona, captive fish spawned after hormone injection, with eggs found on gravel in a raceway.
- The Yampa's high early-summer water provides habitat and food. Fish apparently return to the Green as the Yampa recedes. A few may overwinter in deep Yampa pools.
- Squawfish are piscivorous top carnivores. Fish as short as 50 mm total length had eaten fish, and fish make up nearly the entire diet of individuals longer than 200 mm. They also eat frogs and mice.
- Reproduction has declined since the 1960s. Vanicek (1957) found strong year classes in 1959, 1961, 1963, 1964, and possibly 1966, and weak ones in 1962 and 1965. Young-of-year counts were 275 (1964), 42 (1965), and 560 (1966). Holden (1973) recorded juveniles in Desolation Canyon and the Green at Canyonlands in 1970-71, abundant juveniles at Echo Park in 1968, very few in 1969, and none in 1970. Young (about 75 mm TL) were collected at Jensen in 1975.
- Sexual maturity occurs at a minimum total length of 420 mm, at about age VII. Squawfish once reached much larger sizes, possibly from greater longevity or better past growth conditions. Lower fecundity today combined with high juvenile mortality may explain fewer offspring.

**Humpback Sucker**
- Its range has shrunk drastically. It has been extirpated from the [[Gila River]] system, where a commercial fisherman caught more than six tons in one spawning season in 1949. No specimens were found when Sahuaro Lake was drained in 1966.
- It is still found in Lakes Mojave and Mead, but it is declining and appears to be approaching extinction below Lake Mojave.
- In the upper basin it was considered rare by earlier investigators. During 1974-76 it was relatively common, though not numerous, at the Yampa mouth and in a flooded gravel pit (Walter Walker Wildlife Area) near Grand Junction, Colorado.
- Spring 1975 collections included ripe males and females over a Yampa gravel bar about 400 m upstream of the mouth. One tagged female traveled 21 km upstream to Echo Park in three weeks. A second spawning bar lies about 2.5 km upstream at Box Elder Park, the farthest upstream spawning site recorded.
- Successful reproduction has not been documented recently, and no juveniles were captured despite many attempts.

**Humpback Chub**
- Found in the Green River and in the Colorado near the Utah-Colorado border. A 1973 Colorado Division of Wildlife survey documented a population in the Colorado River near that border.
- Recent collections include fish from eddies near the Yampa and Green in the Monument, and near Castle Park. The authors suggest that hybridization with other [[Gila]] species may occur in the Colorado system.

**Bonytail Chub**
- Vanicek (1967) found that 1-year-old bonytail were more numerous than the related [[Roundtail Chub]] in the Green, yet the bonytail has nearly vanished, while the roundtail remains common and not in apparent danger of extinction.
- Only a few bonytail have been recorded in the Monument, and the species appears close to extinction.

**Conservation Issues**
- In December 1975, the Colorado Squawfish Recovery Team held hearings on defining "critical habitat," a central problem for the squawfish recovery plan.
- Under the amended [[Endangered Species Act]] (P.L. 93-205, 1973), critical habitat must be defined so that effects of future alterations can be identified and alternatives that minimize impacts considered.
- The authors conclude that the Yampa River may be essential for many endemic fish that are now threatened or endangered. Its free-flowing character and its contribution to the Green are key to the recovery of the Monument's fish. They urge that adequate protection of the Yampa be built into water and energy development plans, and that the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and state agencies be involved in such planning.
