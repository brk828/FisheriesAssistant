# Age and Rate of Growth of Common Carp in Reelfoot Lake, Tennessee (1941 and 1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This short paper by Robert J. Schoffman examines the age, growth in length and weight, and size-age structure of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) in [[Reelfoot Lake]], Tennessee. Two samples were compared: 81 fish collected in 1941 and 204 fish collected in 1956. Ages were determined by the scale-reading method of Schoffman (1939). The study was motivated by the 1955 Tennessee Legislature ban on commercial fishing of game fish, which was expected to shift the balance of fish life in the lake. The author concludes that carp grow rapidly and steadily, live about thirteen years, and are being successfully harvested by commercial trammel-net fishing without evident decline in size or numbers.

## Background & Description

- **Study context:** Investigations of age and growth of game and rough fish in Reelfoot Lake began in 1937 (see [[Baker 1937 Reelfoot Lake Survey]]). Commercial fishing of game fish was legal until May 1955, when the [[Tennessee Legislature]] abolished it. One exception allowed the yellow bass or striped jack (*Morone interrupta*) to be taken commercially.
- **Expected effects:** Thousands of game fish would be left in the lake annually, and fewer commercial fishermen would lead to more rough fish. Growth studies were continued to test whether this change in fish community balance affected carp growth.
- **Species:** *Cyprinus carpio*, the common carp, also called the "German Carp."
- **Collection methods:** The 1941 sample came from commercial catches using [[Barrel Net]] and [[Trammel Net]] gear. The 1956 sample came from trammel nets only.
- **Ageing:** Fish were arranged into age groups, where a fish in age group six showed six annuli and was in its seventh year of life.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Age structure:** In 1941, age groups 4 and 7 were most abundant (21% for age group 7). In 1956, age groups 6 and 7 were most abundant (18.1% and 23%), indicating that more older fish were caught in 1956.
- **Longevity:** The oldest fish were in age group 12, and the life history spans about thirteen years. Growth slowed after the fourth year of life.
- **Harvest pressure:** The author argues that long-term intensive commercial fishing has not reduced carp size or numbers, and few fish die of old age or natural causes.
- **Gear shift:** Set net or barrel net fishing was no longer used to harvest carp by 1956. Carp remained important mainly to fishermen using trammel nets.

## Key Findings

- **Sample composition:** 285 carp total (81 in 1941; 204 in 1956). Age group distributions are given in percentages in the text; the largest groups in 1941 were age groups 4 and 7, and in 1956 age groups 6 and 7.
- **Length growth:** Relative to age group 12 (27.9 inches in 1941; 28.6 inches in 1956 taken as 100%), about 53% of length growth was completed by age group 4 in 1941 and 54% in 1956. Approximately 95% was reached by age group 11 in 1941 and 91% by age group 11 in 1956.
- **Weight growth:** Relative to age group 12 in 1941 (170 ounces = 100%), age group 4 had acquired 17% of total weight in 1941 and 19% in 1956. Weight increments increased progressively, which the author takes as evidence of a fast-growing fish with large yearly gains in older age groups.
- **Comparison of years:** In 1956, length was greater except in age groups 7, 8, and 9. Weight in all age groups was higher in 1956 than in 1941. The author suggests the weight difference may reflect the larger 1956 sample and the change in gear.
- **Maximum size:** No specimen exceeded 29 inches or 200 ounces (12.5 pounds).
- **Size-age relationship (Table 2, 204 fish, 1956):** Lengths of 14 to 15 inches, 27 to 28 inches, 28 to 29 inches, and 29 to 30 inches each occurred in only one age group. Fish of 15 to 19 inches spanned three age groups, and 19 to 27 inches spanned two. Most fish belonged to age groups 6 and 7.
- **Table 1 summary:** Average total lengths and weights were listed for age groups 4 to 12 in both years (for example, 1956 age group 12: 28.6 inches, 198.6 ounces, n = 7).

**Conclusions:**
- Carp in Reelfoot Lake grow rapidly and steadily and are being harvested throughout their life.
- Commercial trammel-net fishing is successfully harvesting the carp population, leaving few fish to die of old age.

**Limitations noted by the text:** The 1941 and 1956 samples differ in size (81 vs. 204) and in gear type (barrel plus trammel nets vs. trammel nets only), which the author acknowledges may explain part of the weight differences.
