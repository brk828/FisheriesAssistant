# Commercial Fishery and Biology of the Fresh-Water Shrimp, Macrobrachium, in the Lower St. Paul River, Liberia, 1952-53

#ichthyology #marine-biology #palaemonidae

## Executive Summary

This NMFS Special Scientific Report (Fisheries No. 626, February 1971) by [[George C. Miller]] documents a small, seasonal trap fishery for the large freshwater shrimp [[Macrobrachium vollenhovenii]] in the lower [[St. Paul River]] near Monrovia, Liberia, during 1952-53. The smaller [[Macrobrachium macrobrachion]] was culled from the catch and used by fishermen. The fishery ran from May to January, coinciding with low salinity during the rainy season, and yielded more than $7,500 to fishermen. The report also covers population structure, growth, weight-length relationships, fecundity, spawning, early development and a shrimp disease.

## Background & Description

The study was made while the author served on loan from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] as Technical Advisor in Marine Fisheries to the Liberian Government, so it was a secondary project limited by time. Macrobrachium (family Palaemonidae) are tropical freshwater and brackish shrimp with commercial fisheries and aquaculture interest because of their fast growth (Holthuis and Rosa, 1965; Ling, 1967).

**M. vollenhovenii** (Herklots, 1857) grows to more than 150 mm total length. Body is gray or brown with a blue lateral line. The rostrum is short, deep and slightly upturned, bearing 11-15 dorsal and 3-8 ventral spines. The carpus of the major chela is shorter than the palm, and the fingers are not pubescent. External eggs are orange to red early and brown before hatching. Its range extends from Senegal south to the Kunene River in Angola, plus offshore islands including Cape Verde, Fernando Po and Sao Thome.

**M. macrobrachion** (Herklots) is usually under 75 mm. Its body is light blue-gray mottled with dark spots. It has a long, slender rostrum with 8-10 dorsal and 4-6 ventral spines. The carpus of the major chela is as long as or longer than the palm, and the fingers are pubescent. Early eggs are green and later dark brown.

**Fishery gear and methods.** Traps were woven from 6-13 mm palm-frond strips into fusiform shapes, 101-152 mm wide and 457 mm long, with an involuted entrance and internal fyke. Bait was cassava root core. Traps were set in the intertidal zone and partly covered by rocks. Samples were taken at least monthly. Total length was measured from the eyestalk base to the telson tip, and shrimp were weighed after removal of the major chelipeds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Salinity and season.** The fishery tracked the rainy season (May-November) and a runoff lag to January. Estuarine water was turbid and fresh during the rains. In the dry season (February-May) it was clear and highly saline, and the fishery did not operate.
- **Rivers compared.** The adjoining [[Mesurado River]] is a small mangrove-type estuary with no runoff lag. It became saline months earlier and lacks rock-bottom habitat, so few freshwater shrimp were caught there. Salinities in November-December 1953 ranged from 0.0 to 1.8 ppt in the St. Paul River, versus 9.2 to 84.4 ppt in the Mesurado River.
- **Monrovia Freeport.** Salinity there was affected by runoff from both rivers. Monthly averages rose from 17.1 ppt in August 1953 to 31.5 ppt in January 1954.
- **Habitat.** Rock-gravel shoreline with large outcrops, in the tidal zone.
- **Disease.** A black-spot microbial infection, found around body and leg scratches, caused substantial mortality at times. In a sample of 150 shrimp on May 30, 1953, 137 were infected.

## Key Findings

**Fishery and economics**
- The fishery was seasonal (May to January) and tied to low salinity. Live shrimp retailed at 25 cents per dozen. Shucked tail meat sold for $2.63-$3.01 per pound in June.
- In July 1952, 4 fishermen caught 481 dozen shrimp worth $120.25. Extrapolating to 28 fishermen gave 8,367 dozen worth $841.75. The 9-month fishery was estimated to exceed $7,500.
- Tail (abdomen) weight was 51-53% of body weight, and cooked tail-meat recovery was 21%.
- Peak catch was up to 120 dozen large shrimp per fisherman per week.

**Population structure and growth**
- Monthly length distributions suggested the fishery was supported by a single age group (age group one), replaced late in the season by age group zero.
- Age group zero reached a modal length of 75-80 mm in 9 months. Adults grew slowly: 75-80 mm in January, 85-90 mm in May, and 100-105 mm in November.
- Two alternative explanations were rejected, one for multiple age groups and one for salinity-driven entry and exit of large adults.

**Weight-length relations** (W in g, L in mm; Log W = Log a + b Log L)
- Ovigerous females (N = 181): Log W = -4.656603 + 3.011392 Log L.
- Males and nonovigerous females (N = 911): Log W = -4.829560 + 3.092213 Log L.
- Ovigerous females were slightly heavier than the other group.

**Fecundity and spawning**
- Fecundity was estimated at 12,000 to 45,000 eggs per female, using egg counts displaced from a known volume of water.
- Ovigerous females were present from May to January. Peak spawning was in August and December in 1952, and in September and December in 1953.
- Estimated embryonic development was under one month. Early development to settlement (4.5-5.0 mm juveniles) was estimated at 50-65 days, based on the related [[Macrobrachium rosenbergii]].

**Juveniles and trap selectivity**
- Traps caught few juveniles below the minimum size of maturity (45-50 mm). Women used burlap push-nets to take the zero age group soon after settlement, with the catch smoked and eaten.

**Rostral spine comparison**
- Counts from 218 St. Paul River specimens were compared with Man (1904) data from the Catumbella River, Angola. The modal dorsal count was 13 in Liberia and 12 in Angola. Ventral counts were modally 4 in both. The author attributed the difference to size, not to separate populations.

**Culls and sustainability**
- Of 221 culled shrimp (42-77 mm) examined on August 4, 1952, 88% were M. macrobrachion. Only 12% were M. vollenhovenii.
- The trap fishery harvested adults of both species without apparent harm to either.

**Shrimp disease**
- Black-spot infection was widespread and ate through the carapace, abdomen and walking legs. The palatability of infected shrimp did not appear affected.
