# Group Calls Snake the Most Endangered River in U.S.

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary
This 1999 Associated Press news article reports on American Rivers' annual list of endangered U.S. rivers, naming the [[Snake River]] as the most endangered river in the nation due to four hydroelectric dams that have driven [[Pacific Salmon]] runs to near extinction. The article also highlights the [[Cedar River]] (Washington) at No. 6, where dredging operations damaged salmon spawning habitat, and briefly surveys other endangered rivers nationally, many linked to urban sprawl and water management pressures on aquatic ecosystems.

## Background & Description
- The **Snake River** in southeastern Washington was named the #1 most endangered river in the U.S. by the environmental advocacy group **American Rivers**.
- This marks the second consecutive year a Washington State river topped the list; the **Hanford Reach** of the [[Columbia River]] held the top spot the previous year.
- Four dams on the Snake River are implicated: **Ice Harbor**, **Lower Monumental**, **Little Goose**, and **Lower Granite Dams** — all completed in the early 1970s.
- The **Cedar River**, near Seattle, ranked No. 6 on the list, threatened by urban sprawl impacting fish runs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Dam Impacts on Salmon Populations**: Since the completion of the four Snake River dams in the early 1970s, fish populations have declined by nearly **90 percent**, attributed to blocked and degraded migration corridors for anadromous salmon.
- **Habitat Destruction via Dredging**: The Army Corps of Engineers conducted dredging in the lower Cedar River in summer 1998, intended for flood control and salmon protection. This operation instead:
  - Caused an estimated **$300,000 in damage** to salmon habitat.
  - Destroyed approximately **8 percent of fish egg nests (redds)** for the fall **sockeye salmon** run.
- **Urban Sprawl as a Systemic Threat**: Many rivers on the endangered list face degradation linked to rapid urban and population growth, including:
  - **Coosa-Tallapoosa-Alabama River system** (No. 3) — threatened by dam construction/modification near Atlanta, endangering aquatic life.
  - **San Pedro River** (No. 4) — aquifer depletion from growth in Sierra Vista, Arizona.
  - **Carmel River** (No. 8) — water diversion driven by growth in Monterey County, California.
  - **Bear River** (No. 10) — wasteful water practices in Salt Lake City reducing flows and damaging an associated bird refuge.
- Other listed rivers included the **Missouri** (No. 2), **Yellowstone** (No. 5), **Fox River** in Illinois/Wisconsin (No. 7), and **Coal River** in West Virginia (No. 9), though specific fish/ecological data for these were not detailed in this source.

## Key Findings
- **~90% decline** in Snake River fish populations since early-1970s dam completion, directly attributed by environmentalists to the four dams blocking or degrading salmon migration.
- **8% loss** of fall-run sockeye salmon egg nests in the Cedar River caused by a single dredging operation, despite the project's stated goal of flood control and salmon protection.
- American Rivers president **Rebecca Wodder** criticized costly, incremental mitigation efforts as ineffective, noting a continuing year-over-year decline in returning spawning fish.
- The report frames dam infrastructure and urban water demand as the two dominant, recurring threats to [[Anadromous Fish]] populations and river ecosystem health across multiple U.S. watersheds in this period.
- No direct water chemistry, population census, or taxonomic data beyond common names (salmon, sockeye salmon) were provided in the source text.
