# Fishes of the Wakarusa River in Kansas

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Centrarchidae #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 survey by Deacon and Metcalf documents the fish fauna of the [[Wakarusa River]], a 458-square-mile tributary drainage of the [[Kansas River]] in northeastern Kansas. The study synthesizes historical collections (1890s–1953) held at the University of Kansas Museum of Natural History and University of Michigan Museum of Zoology with new 1959 field collections to assess faunal change over roughly 60 years. A central finding is that a severe five-year drought (1952–1956), the driest period in 70 years of Kansas weather records, caused widespread stream intermittency and dramatically altered species distributions. The study identifies three faunal groups—lower-mainstream specialists, ubiquitous drought-tolerant generalists, and clear-water tributary specialists—and uses their post-drought recolonization patterns to infer relative dispersal ability and habitat dependency. The paper also compares the Wakarusa fauna to neighboring drainages ([[Big Blue River]], [[Cottonwood River]]) and discusses the influence of water turbidity, channel entrenchment, and impoundments (e.g., [[Lone Star Lake]]) on fish community composition.

## Background & Description

The paper is faunistic/distributional rather than morphometric, but includes several taxonomically diagnostic notes:

- **Carpiodes velifer** (highfin carpsucker): Identification confirmed via deeply compressed body and a discernible "pea-lip" trace on a damaged dorsal fin specimen (UMMZ 63182), distinguishing it from *C. cyprinus*.
- **Moxostoma aureolum** (northern redhorse): Wakarusa specimens regarded as morphological intergrades between *M. a. aureolum* and *M. a. pisolabrum* (per Minckley and Cross, 1960).
- **Etheostoma spectabile pulchellum** (orangethroat darter): Characterized as a riffle-dwelling, comparatively sedentary fish and a weak swimmer — a trait directly linked to its slow post-drought redispersal.
- **Anomalous specimen**: A *Micropterus salmoides* individual lacking a right pelvic fin was recorded from Lone Star Lake.
- Species identifications were based on standard external counts and body proportions typical of contemporary (1959–1961) regional taxonomic keys; no detailed meristic/morphometric tables are presented in the excerpted text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat structure of the drainage:**
- **Mainstream** ([[Wakarusa River]]): Entrenched channel in its own alluvium, high muddy banks, mud/sand bottom, turbidity consistently >100 ppm.
- **Upper tributaries** (e.g., [[Rock Creek]], [[Washington Creek]], [[Deer Creek]], [[Cole Creek]]): Lower banks, gravel/rubble/bedrock bottoms, clearer water, considered more stable refugia during drought.

**Three faunal groups identified:**

1. **Lower-mainstream group** — species common in the [[Kansas River]] with limited upstream penetration: *Lepisosteus osseus*, *Carpiodes carpio carpio*, *Ictiobus cyprinella*, *Moxostoma aureolum*, *Cyprinus carpio*, *Hybopsis storeriana*, *Hybopsis aestivalis*, *Notropis percobromus*, *Hybognathus nuchalis*, *Pylodictis olivaris*.

2. **Ubiquitous/generalist group** — dominant across nearly all tributaries regardless of habitat suitability for other species: *Notropis lutrensis* (red shiner), *Pimephales promelas* (fathead minnow), *Ictalurus melas* (black bullhead), *Lepomis cyanellus* (green sunfish).

3. **Clear-water tributary specialists** — centered in [[Rock Creek]], [[Washington Creek]], and [[Deer Creek]]: *Catostomus commersonnii*, *Semotilus atromaculatus*, *Hybopsis biguttata*, *Notropis cornutus*, *Notropis topeka* ([[Topeka Shiner]]), *Notropis umbratilis*, *Phenacobius mirabilis*, *Pimephales notatus*, *Campostoma anomalum*, *Noturus exilis*, *Percina caprodes*, *Etheostoma nigrum*, *Etheostoma spectabile*.

**Drought as an ecological filter:** The 1952–1956 drought (Table 1 hydrological data) rendered the muddy, turbid mainstream unsuitable as a refugium for clear-water species. Refugia were concentrated in upper Rock Creek, Washington Creek, and possibly Deer Creek — streams with greater gravel-rubble substrate, clearer water, and deeper pools. [[Lone Star Lake]] enhanced flow stability in Washington Creek, likely aiding species persistence (e.g., *Noturus exilis*, the slender madtom, found only immediately below the dam).

**Reservoir-associated species:** *Perca flavescens* (yellow perch), *Chaenobryttus gulosus* (warmouth), *Pomoxis annularis* and *P. nigromaculatus* (crappies) occur in Lone Star Lake and farm ponds, representing lentic-adapted species likely to spread via future impoundments.

## Key Findings

- **Hydrological context**: Stream-flow records (1951–1959) at the gaging station 2.1 mi south of Lawrence document the severity of the drought — e.g., 1953 had 83 days of no flow and 191 days below 5 cfs; flows rebounded sharply by 1957–1958.
- **Differential recolonization rates**: Compared to Larimore et al.'s (1959) Illinois study (Smiths Branch), where 21 of 29 species reinvaded within two weeks of flow resumption, the Wakarusa fauna showed markedly slower redispersal, attributed to the ecological barrier posed by the turbid, mud-bottomed mainstream separating tributary refugia.
- **Fast dispersers/drought-tolerant species**: *Catostomus commersonnii*, *Notropis umbratilis*, *Pimephales notatus*, *Percina caprodes* — recolonized the uppermost basin by 1959.
- **Slow dispersers**: *Semotilus atromaculatus*, *Notropis topeka*, *Phenacobius mirabilis*, *Campostoma anomalum* — not found beyond [[Burys Creek]] in 1959.
- **No redispersal / restricted survivors**: *Noturus exilis* (only below Lone Star Lake dam), *Notropis cornutus*, *Hybopsis biguttata*, and *Etheostoma nigrum* (only in Rock Creek). *Hybopsis biguttata* (hornyhead chub) had not been recorded since 1924, suggesting local extirpation.
- **Faunal decline signals**: Species like *Carpiodes velifer* (highfin carpsucker) have nearly disappeared from Kansas altogether, likely due to increased turbidity from agricultural land-use change since the early 1900s.
- **Introduced/stocking-influenced species**: The apparent recent establishment of *Micropterus salmoides* (largemouth bass) and *Lepomis macrochirus* (bluegill) in streams, absent from early collections, is attributed to stocking in regional impoundments followed by escapement into the drainage.
- **Regional faunal comparison**: 40 species shared with the [[Big Blue River]] Basin (Minckley, 1959); 26 species shared with the [[Cottonwood River]] drainage (Cross, 1954), with 19 species unique to the Wakarusa and 15 unique to the Cottonwood.
- **Water quality**: No untreated domestic sewage or industrial waste was discharged into the Wakarusa system at time of study (per Kansas State Board of Health), meaning observed faunal shifts are attributable primarily to hydrological/drought and land-use factors rather than point-source pollution.
- **Management implication**: The proposed [[Wakarusa Watershed Association]] flow-stabilization project (headwater retention structures) is projected to benefit riffle-dependent, clear-water species such as *Etheostoma nigrum* and *Notropis cornutus* by reducing turbidity and stabilizing flow.
