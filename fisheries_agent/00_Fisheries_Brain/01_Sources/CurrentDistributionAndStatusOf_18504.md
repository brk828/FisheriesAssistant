# Current Distribution and Status of *Lepidomeda vittata* (Little Colorado Spinedace) in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by C. O. Minckley for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (December 1984), surveys the current distribution and status of the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], an endemic cyprinid of the [[Little Colorado River]] drainage. In 1983, 54 localities were surveyed and the species was found at 11 localities, 9 previously reported and 2 new (both in [[Nutrioso Creek]]). Population status was rated "good" at four or five sites, "fair" at two and "poor" at one. The species' range has contracted and become fragmented through dams, water diversion, groundwater pumping, exotic fishes and drought. The report recommends federal listing as Threatened with [[Critical Habitat]] designation, annual monitoring, and basin-wide resurveys. The accompanying 1985 agency correspondence adopts these recommendations.

## Background & Description

The species was described by [[Edward Drinker Cope]] in 1874 from the Little Colorado River and belongs to the Plagopterini, a distinctive tribe of spiny-rayed cyprinids (see [[Miller and Hubbs 1960]]). It is known only from Arizona, mainly from the Little Colorado River and its north-flowing tributaries, with a possible historical range extending into the Zuni River watershed of New Mexico that lacks supporting records.

Life colors described by [[Miller 1963]] include silvery sides with dark vertical lines, a white venter, olivaceous to lead-grey back, and cream-colored spots near the dorsal fin origin. Reproductive data from [[Minckley and Carufel 1967]] indicate an initial spawning surge in June, sporadic summer and autumn spawning, likely multiple spawning per year, fecundity of 650–5,600 eggs, and a smallest ripe male of 50 mm standard length. Courtship behavior has been described only once (nibbling at the vent region).

Associated species include [[Roundtail Chub]], speckled dace, flannelmouth and bluehead suckers, and trout in East Clear Creek, and carp, fathead minnow, golden shiner, bluegill, green sunfish, Rio Grande killifish and channel catfish in lower Chevelon Creek. Roundtail chub are now extirpated or very rare in East Clear Creek.

**Historical trajectory:** The species was reported as abundant historically, declined to near extermination by 1960, was rediscovered in East Clear Creek in 1961, re-invaded much of its range by 1963–1966, declined again (considered endangered in 1973), and was found in lower Chevelon Creek in 1977.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Fish were collected with 1.8 × 1.8 m and 0.9 × 0.9 m seines (3.2 mm mesh), with ten hauls per site. Each haul recorded substrate, depth, area, habitat type (pool, run, riffle) and current (slow, moderate, fast). Densities were expressed as fish per square metre. Population status was assigned as follows: "poor" (<10 fish), "fair" (10–20 fish taken relatively easily), and "good" (more than 20 fish, several year classes, or evidence of reproduction).

**Habitat preferences.** Across sites the species occurred over mud, sand, gravel, bedrock or combined substrates. It was most common in pools, in slow to moderate current, and avoided the deepest heavily shaded pools and open shallow areas. Water clarity and temperature varied: sampling temperatures ranged from 12 °C (Nutrioso Creek, October) to 18 °C (Springerville), and turbidity was often noted.

**Threats.** The principal threats were:
- Dams and reservoirs (Chevelon Canyon Lake, Bear Canyon Lake, Blue Ridge Reservoir, Woodruff Reservoir, Lyman Lake, Nelson Reservoir) that dewater downstream reaches.
- Irrigation diversions and groundwater pumping for municipal supply.
- Introduced exotic fishes, whose effects remain uninvestigated.
- Chemical renovation (toxaphene in the 1950s–1960s; rotenone-type removal of *Notemigonus* in 1965 and 1975).
- Logging-related sedimentation in upper Nutrioso Creek.
- Protozoan parasite *Ichthyophthirius* sp., observed in July 1983 on fishes at East Clear Creek.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status (1983 survey)**

| Locality | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Little Colorado River, St. Johns (2 sites) | Good | Several age classes including young of year; population maintained partly by a diversion dam |
| Little Colorado River, Springerville | Good | At least two year classes; no reproduction evident; population known to fluctuate markedly |
| Nutrioso Creek below Nelson Reservoir | Good | Older year classes dominated; reproduction could not be documented (October collection) |
| Nutrioso Creek at Hunter Ranch | Fair | Smaller individuals only (mean TL 36 mm); newly documented locality |
| East Clear Creek, Jones Crossing | Fair | 1983 spawn present; second most common fish |
| East Clear Creek, junction FS Roads 95/96 | Poor | One fish in July, six in September |
| Leonard Canyon, Dines Tank | Good | Many 1983 spawn and older fish |
| Lower Chevelon Creek ("the steps" and Hugo Meadow) | Good | Hugo Meadow: several thousand young of year in one haul |

**Key quantitative results**
- Densities at Hugo Meadow, Chevelon Creek: 1.02 fish/m² total, of which *L. vittata* was 0.19/m².
- Chevelon Creek "the steps": 1.31/m² total, with 0.19/m² spinedace.
- Nutrioso Creek below Nelson Reservoir: 0.76/m² total, with 0.59/m² spinedace (highest spinedace density recorded).
- Springerville: 0.56/m² total, with 0.41/m² spinedace.
- Mean total lengths ranged from 23 mm (Leonard Canyon) to 82 mm (Nutrioso Creek below Nelson Reservoir); reproduction was evident at Jones Crossing, St. Johns (3.2 km site), Chevelon Creek sites and Leonard Canyon.
- Reproduction was not documented at Springerville, 8 km south of St. Johns, or Nutrioso Creek.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Nutrioso Creek is likely a major source population (refugium) for the Springerville reach of the Little Colorado River.
- Upper St. Johns reach, above the diversion dam, is critical for survival in that area during drought.
- Recommended actions: (1) federal listing as Threatened; (2) Critical Habitat for Chevelon, East Clear, Nutrioso and Leonard Canyon creeks and the Little Colorado River near St. Johns and Springerville; (3) annual monitoring in each 1983 population; (4) basin-wide resurveys every five to ten years.

**Agency correspondence (February 1985).** The Arizona Game and Fish Department director transmitted the final report to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, stating that the decline reflects dam construction, diversions, groundwater pumping, exotic introductions and drought. A departmental inter-office memo (Jim Brooks, February 12, 1985) concluded that *L. vittata* is threatened, with range reduced in East Clear Creek and eliminated from Silver Creek, and recommended listing with Critical Habitat for upper East Clear Creek, lower Chevelon Creek and Nutrioso Creek.
