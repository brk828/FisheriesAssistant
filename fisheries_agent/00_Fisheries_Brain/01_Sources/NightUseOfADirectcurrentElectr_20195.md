# Night Use of a Direct-Current Electric Shocker to Collect Trout in Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This methods paper by [[William C. Latta]] and [[Gerald F. Myers]] (Institute for Fisheries Research, Michigan Department of Conservation) describes a boat-based, night-time direct-current (DC) electric shocker used to collect [[Brook Trout]] in two small lakes near the Pigeon River Trout Research Station, Michigan. The gear proved far more effective than [[Wire Traps]] and was judged superior to [[Hoop Nets]] for these lakes. Night shocking captured as many trout per hour as roughly 30 to 36 trap-days of wire-trap effort.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** Earlier attempts to use DC shockers in lakes without pulsing the current had been relatively ineffective (citing [[Haskell Electric Trawl]], [[Loeb Electrical Surface Device]] studies).
- **Setting:** The [[Pigeon River Trout Research Station]] used a DC shocker in small trout lakes with considerable success.
- **Power and apparatus:** A Homelite DC generator (230 V, 9.3 A) powered both underwater lighting and the electrical field. Each of two 100 W, 230 V bulbs sat in swivel sockets on a thin-wall conduit frame beneath the boat. The ground was a 24 x 28 inch copper screen dragged behind the stern. Electrodes were 6 ft wooden dowels with the distal 1.5 ft sheathed in copper. A safety switch beside the oarsman controlled the electrode-ground circuit.
- **Wiring:** All circuits were wired in parallel. The measured generator voltage was 240 V against a 230 V rating. The bulb shell was connected to the positive terminal to avoid electrolytic destruction of the socket.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake characteristics:** Water was clear. Nearly all fish were found in water under 6 ft deep and were seen before capture.
- **Study lakes:**
  - Ford Lake: 10.6 acres, 29 ft maximum depth, about half less than 5 ft deep.
  - Hemlock Lake: 4.8 acres, 59 ft maximum depth, steep basin with little shoal area.
- **Population dynamics:** Both lakes contained only brook trout. No natural reproduction occurred, so populations were maintained by annual fall plantings of fingerlings.
- **Fish behavior:** Fish fled ahead of the boat to the edge of the light field until reaching cover, where they became vulnerable. Fish within about 1 ft of the electrode swam toward it and could be led into a scoop net. Spearing-style approaches frightened fish out of range, whereas a quiet approach with a quick thrust worked best.
- **Population estimation:** Mark-and-recapture estimates were made in spring and fall, when trout move throughout the lake and are accessible in shallow water.

## Key Findings

- **Electrical performance:** Ammeter readings were 3.2 A with both electrodes in the water, 0.2 A with lights alone, and 3.2 A with lights and electrodes together. Adding the lights therefore did not reduce the electrical output used to attract fish. Results were consistent across several days and two lakes.
- **Gear selectivity:** Hoop nets were discontinued because of size selectivity (Waters, 1960, showed many small fish escaped) and difficulty setting them on the steep Hemlock Lake slope. Wire traps (1/4-inch hardware-cloth, triangular cross-section, 33 x 33 x 36 inch, funnel-ended) were about as effective as hoop nets but much less effective than the shocker.
- **Catch comparison (Table 1):**

| Lake | Gear | Effort | Brook trout caught | Length range (in) | Estimated population |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hemlock, Apr 1958 | Wire traps | 120 trap-days | 217 | 5.0–10.8 | ~1,000 |
| Hemlock, Apr 1959 | Shocker | 8 hours | 514 | 5.4–12.4 | ~700 |
| Ford, Apr 1958 | Wire traps | 120 trap-days | 327 | 4.9–13.4 | ~2,000 |
| Ford, Apr 1959 | Shocker | 12 hours | 977 | 5.2–17.0 | ~1,400 |

- **Efficiency:** In Hemlock Lake, about 8 hours of night shocking in 1959 captured as many trout per hour as 36 trap-days in 1958, even though the trout population was smaller in 1959. In Ford Lake, night shocking yielded as many trout per hour as 30 trap-days.
- **Operational safety:** The generator was not started until lights were positioned, electrodes readied, and circuits connected. The oarsman could open the electrode circuit if an operator fell overboard.
- **Conclusion:** Night DC electrofishing from a boat is a markedly more efficient collection method than wire traps or hoop nets for small, clear, deep trout lakes.
