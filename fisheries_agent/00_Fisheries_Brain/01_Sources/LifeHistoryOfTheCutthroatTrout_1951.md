## Executive Summary

This 1951 study by George G. Fleener documents the life history of the [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*) in the Logan River, Utah, based on 306 specimens collected via electric shocking during 1948–1949. The population was concentrated in high-gradient headwater reaches, comprising 24–31.5% of the angler catch. Standing crop estimates yielded 43.6 lbs/acre and 565 fish/mile in the upper river. Growth was slow (smallest average annual increment: 53 mm) over a short ~200-day growing season (late March–early October). Spawning extended from early April to mid-August, peaking before mid-July. Diet was dominated by aquatic and terrestrial insects (Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Diptera, Coleoptera), with a near-total absence of forage fish contributing to the comparatively small body size of this population. The study is part of a broader Logan River drainage fishery investigation alongside work on [[Mountain Whitefish]] and general fish population dynamics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Steep, high-gradient headwater streams; strongly associated with gradients ranging 167–394 ft/mile (vs. ~35 ft/mile in lower river). Substrate dominated by rubble and large gravel; minimal pool habitat (average depth 0.74 ft, Brown 1935).
- **Distribution within drainage**: Concentrated above Right Hand Fork and near Temple Fork mouth; density often exceeded 150 fish per one-tenth mile above Section 23. Rarely found below Right Hand Fork.
- **Co-occurring species**: [[Brown Trout]] (dominant in lower river), [[Rainbow Trout]] (stocked throughout), [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (limited to upper reaches, minor competitor), [[Mountain Whitefish]] (deep pools, mid-river), [[Upper Snake Muddler]] (*Cottus sp.*, principal forage fish, absent above Station 24), [[Smallfin Redside Shiner]] (rare).
- **Movement/Home range**: Of 142 jaw-tagged fish, 15 recaptured (10.6%); none moved outside the one-tenth-mile release area — indicating strong site fidelity/limited movement.
- **Spawning habitat**: Preferred low-flow areas (≤4 second-feet total flow); suitable substrate present in Spawn Creek and lower pool margins of Beaver Creek (spawning riffles spaced 20–30 feet apart).
- **Trophic role**: Primarily insectivorous; occupies a niche largely unsupported by forage fish, contributing to stunted growth relative to populations with abundant forage fish.

## Key Findings

**Population & Abundance**
- Cutthroat trout made up 24% (1948) and 31.5% (1949) of the total angler catch in Logan River.
- Standing crop: 43.6 lbs/acre cutthroat trout (of 55.5 lbs/acre total fish biomass) and minimum 565 fish/mile, based on four sample stations (476 yards) extrapolated to 16.9 miles of channel.
- Comparable to (though on the lower end of) other trout streams (e.g., Minnesota North Shore streams: 2.85–101.2 lbs/acre; Hoover 1938, New Hampshire brook trout streams).

**Growth**
- Growing season ~200 days (late March–early October).
- Smallest average annual growth increment: 53 mm (third year).
- Scale annuli reliably formed by first winter in this population (contrasts with [[Golden Trout]] studies by Needham & Vestal 1938, where some fish delayed scale formation to second summer).

**Maturity**
- All fish age 2+ were mature.
- Males matured earlier/faster than females; smallest mature male at 120 mm SL, smallest mature female at 134 mm SL.
- Age group I: 34% mature; Age group II: 100% males, 92% females mature.
- Adult females generally exceeded the 7-inch (178 mm) legal limit; many adult males did not.

**Reproduction & Sex Ratio**
- Spawning period: early April to mid-August (most before mid-July) — an unusually long (~4 month) window compared to related studies (e.g., Smith 1941: May 4–15 for [[Heenan Lake]] cutthroat; Lahontan cutthroat spawning April 25–May 24 in Nevada).
- Adult females ranged ≤11 inches total length.
- Sex ratio: 111 females per 100 males (n=241, 35 collections).
- Fish could not be reliably sexed below 60 mm SL; confident sexing (external characters, spawning season) above 90 mm SL.

**Diet / Food Habits**
- Diet dominated by insects: [[Ephemeroptera]], [[Trichoptera]], and [[Diptera]] were principal aquatic orders; [[Coleoptera]] was the leading terrestrial order.
- Fish prey was rare (only 2 occurrences in entire study), both cases being cutthroat trout (cannibalism) — no significant predation on forage fish due to their scarcity.
- Small amounts of fish eggs consumed; no apparent negative effect on egg survival/recruitment.
- Plant material negligible in diet.
- Findings align with variability noted by Hazzard & Madsen (1933) across regions, but contrast with Muttkowski's (1925) Yellowstone study (90% stoneflies) and Calhoun's (1944) Sierra Nevada study (chironomid-dominated).

**Broader Implication**
- The near-total absence of forage fish (e.g., muddlers absent above Station 24) in upper Logan River is proposed as a key limiting factor for the small body size of cutthroat trout in this system, despite otherwise adequate standing biomass.
- Historical note: native [[Utah Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki utah*) believed extinct in this drainage, likely due to introgression from stocked [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki lewisi*) and hybridization with introduced rainbow trout.
