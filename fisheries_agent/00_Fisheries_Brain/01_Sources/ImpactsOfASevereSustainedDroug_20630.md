# Impacts of a Severe Sustained Drought on Colorado River Water Resources

#water-resources #hydrology #drought #colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This simulation study assesses how a severe, sustained drought would affect the [[Colorado River Basin]] water system. Using the [[Colorado River Model]], the authors compared a 38-year drought trace, derived from roughly 500 years of tree-ring-reconstructed streamflows, with a 38-year historical trace. Impacts were evaluated for streamflow, consumptive-use deliveries, reservoir storage, [[Hydropower Generation]], and [[Salinity]]. The system proved resilient in aggregate, but the burden fell heavily on the [[Upper Basin]]. Basin-wide hydropower fell sharply and salinity rose, while the [[Lower Basin]] experienced only slight shortages.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin drains about 243,000 square miles across seven U.S. states and parts of Mexico. It is divided at [[Lees Ferry]] into the Upper Basin (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming, and northern Arizona), which supplies most of the inflow, and the Lower Basin (Arizona, California, Nevada, and New Mexico). The average natural annual flow at Lees Ferry is 15.2 million acre-feet (maf), with recorded extremes above 23 maf and below 7 maf. More than 70 percent of natural flow occurs in May, June, and July.

The basin is already among the most fully developed river systems in the world. The 14 modeled reservoirs hold 61,375,000 acre-feet of active capacity, and [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Mead]] together provide more than 50 maf of storage. The authors note that the reservoir complex, with capacity about four times the river's average natural flow, mitigates frequent droughts. The system has never been tested by an infrequent severe sustained drought. Tree-ring evidence shows that droughts more severe than those in the historical record have occurred, and the authors cite possible [[Global Warming]] effects as a reason to quantify such events.

Water allocation is governed by the [[Law of the River]], a body of compacts, statutes, court decrees, and operating criteria. Key instruments include the [[Colorado River Compact]] (1922), the Boulder Canyon Project Act (1929), the Upper Colorado River Basin Compact (1948), the Colorado River Storage Project Act (1956), and the Supreme Court decree in *Arizona v. California* (1963), along with the Operating Criteria for reservoir operations.

**Model and simulations.** The [[Colorado River Model]] uses a network flow algorithm with a monthly time step. It includes 107 river reaches, 14 reservoirs, 29 inflow points, and 265 consumptive-use points. Both simulations used identical operating rules, demands, and initial conditions. Starting reservoir contents were set to storage reported on October 1, 1991. The drought inflow set was built from [[Severe Sustained Drought]] hydrology, which rearranged the most severe 22-year period (1579 to 1600) and appended reconstructed flows for 1601 to 1616. The historical set covers 1938 to 1975, chosen because its average flow equals the median of 41 38-year periods in the 1906 to 1983 record. Demand scenarios followed [[Bureau of Reclamation]] projections, with agricultural use held at 1992 levels.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses water resources and hydrology only. It does not report on fish, invertebrates, plankton, or aquatic habitat. The aquatic-environment content is limited to streamflow regimes, reservoir storage, and water quality.

**Streamflow.** The drought reduced average monthly flows at most of nine monitored locations, by 6 to 12 percent. The exception was the San Juan River above its Colorado confluence. Flows fell to zero at some reaches because no minimum streamflow requirements were assumed. At [[Lees Ferry]], the drought trace averaged 12.68 maf over 38 years, against 14.1 maf for the historical trace.

**Storage.** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] reservoir ([[Lake Powell]]) fell to dead storage by the end of year 18 and had zero active storage for eight years. [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] followed a similar pattern. Upper Basin storage reached dead-pool levels, whereas Lower Basin reservoirs kept active water. This was because equalization and minimum-release rules moved water downstream to [[Lake Mead]].

**Salinity.** Salinity increases downstream, so the greatest effect fell on the lowest users. Upper Basin shortfalls reduced return flows and salt loading during the worst years, which partly offset the drought's effect. Dead-storage dynamics also influenced results: when Lake Powell emptied, its salt inventory was removed from the model, which probably understated how long elevated salinity would persist in reality.

## Key Findings

- **Upper Basin impacts:** Upper Basin depletion shortfalls reached almost 59 percent in the worst drought year. Over half of Upper Basin consumptive-use requests went unmet in that year, when Lake Mead still held almost 7.5 maf.
- **Lower Basin impacts:** The worst Lower Basin shortfall was about 3 percent of basic entitlements, falling in Arizona and Nevada. [[California]] normal entitlement depletions were unaffected, but surplus deliveries were 69 percent lower on average. Deliveries to [[Mexico]] stayed at the 1.5 maf entitlement.
- **Shortage declarations:** Lake Mead dropped below the 10.762 maf shortage level in years 22 and 23. [[Central Arizona Project]] deliveries were cut to 450,000 acre-feet, and Nevada took a 4 percent share of the CAP curtailment.
- **Surplus deliveries:** Surplus was declared twice in the drought (years 6 and 7), compared with eight of 38 years in the historical scenario. [[Metropolitan Water District of Southern California]] surplus deliveries would be seriously curtailed.
- **Compact compliance:** Lees Ferry flow fell below the 8.23 maf objective release in four drought years. A [[Colorado River Compact]] call occurred in year 21, when the 10-year moving total dropped below 75 maf. Only 2.97 maf could be delivered that year, and the 75 maf requirement was not met again until year 26.
- **Lake Mead resilience:** The lowest Lake Mead active storage under drought was 7.50 maf (year 22). Equalization releases, the 8.23 maf minimum release, and the 75 maf compact delivery rule helped sustain it.
- **Hydropower:** Total annual system energy fell 84 percent in the worst drought year relative to the historical minimum. In the study's lowest energy year (year 22), about 73 percent of output came from [[Lake Havasu]] and [[Lake Mojave]] powerplants. Average annual generation was 9,716 gigawatt-hours (GWh) under historical flows and 7,104 GWh under drought.
- **Salinity:** Annual flow-weighted salinity below Lake Mead exceeded 1200 ppm for six years during the drought. It never exceeded 1100 ppm in the historical scenario. Below [[Hoover Dam]], the 723 ppm criterion was exceeded in 32 of 38 historical years and 30 of 38 drought years. Average salinity was 859 ppm (historical) and 908 ppm (drought).
- **Conclusion:** The authors judge the system remarkably resilient to an extreme drought, but the impacts are distributed unevenly. They recommend institutional coping mechanisms, which are evaluated in companion papers by [[Henderson and Lord]] (1995) and [[Sangoyomi and Harding]] (1995).
