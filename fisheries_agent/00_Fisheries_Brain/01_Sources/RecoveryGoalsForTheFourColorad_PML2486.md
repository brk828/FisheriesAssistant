# Colorado Pikeminnow Management Units and Draft Recovery Goals for Four Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines a critical review letter by [[Vernal CRFP]] biologists (Tim Modde, Bruce Haines and Chris Kitchen) with the Draft 2 recovery goals for four endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes, prepared for the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] (May 2000). The letter objects to the draft's treatment of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] in the [[Green River]]. It raises three concerns: merging two spawning populations into one management unit, using an imprecise standing-stock estimate, and consolidating threats across reaches. The four species covered are [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]], all listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Background & Description

The four species are members of a native Colorado Basin fish assemblage of 35 species with a high level of endemism (Miller 1959). The Service must review listed species every five years for possible reclassification. Both the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Humpback Chub]] were listed in 1967 and 1973, and recovery plans were approved between 1990 and 1991. Critical habitat was designated on March 21, 1994.

The recovery goals define recovery criteria using site-specific management actions and objective, measurable targets. The targets rest on population demographics, genetic guidelines and habitat carrying capacity. The document organizes the basin into three possible recovery areas: the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] and the [[San Juan River Basin]]. Five programs coordinate activities across these areas, including the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] and the [[San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]].

The Appendix A review applies the [[Distinct Population Segment]] framework (discreteness, significance and conservation status) to each species. Its results are:

- [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]: upper basin and San Juan populations are treated as separate segments.
- [[Humpback Chub]]: upper basin and [[Grand Canyon]] populations are separate segments.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]]: upper, lower and San Juan basins are treated as separate segments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regulation.** Peak spring discharge on the upper Colorado River has fallen by 29–38% since 1950 (Van Steeter and Pitlick 1998). Storage of spring flows has cut spring runoff in the [[Green River]] and reduced flows in the Colorado River during May and June by about 37%. A sudden decline in the Green River followed the completion of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1963. The draft proposes flow recommendation packages for the Green, Yampa, 15-Mile Reach, Gunnison and San Juan rivers. The letter adds that [[Taylor Draw Dam]] is run-of-river and has little effect on flow patterns.

**Nonnative fishes.** At least 67 nonnative species have been introduced into the basin. Tyus et al. (1982) recorded 42 established in the upper basin, and Minckley (1982) recorded 37 in the lower basin. Predation by [[Northern Pike]] is a concern in the Green River, and [[McAda]] data show these fish are much more abundant in the middle than the lower Green River.

**Water quality.** Selenium is a documented concern. Ashley Creek, a Green River tributary, carries 59–78 µg/L selenium. The USEPA criterion is 5 µg/L (four-day average), and the current water quality criterion is 35 µg/L. Razorback sucker eggs from the Green River contained 4.9–25 µg/g dry weight, and the draft requires further evaluation of selenium effects. Heavy metals are considered to have little evidence of significant impact. Total dissolved solids are tolerated by these species at higher levels than those observed in the basin.

**Hybridization.** Humpback chub, bonytail and roundtail chub show extensive introgressive hybridization in the Gila complex. Roundtail chub invasion of humpback chub habitat during low-flow years is linked to flow regulation. The letter notes that high spring flows are needed to maintain historic hybridization rates.

**Parasites and disease.** Colorado pikeminnow carry intestinal tapeworms, external copepods and protozoans. Bass tapeworms were found in 65% of fish longer than 200 mm in the Green River (Vanicek 1967).

## Key Findings

**1. Two spawning populations in the Green River should be separate management units.**
- Recovery data since the early 1980s show two major spawning aggregations in the Green River, near the [[Yampa River]] and at [[Three Fords Reach]]. Several smaller aggregations occur in the Colorado and lower Gunnison rivers in the [[Grand Valley]].
- Mean annual CPUE of age-0 fish (ISMP data, 1986–1998) showed no correlation between the Colorado River and either Green River reach.
- The middle and lower Green River correlation was r = 0.67, but this depended on a single year (1988). Without it, the value fell to 0.25.
- A [[Paired T-Test]] found a significant difference between middle and lower Green River age-0 numbers (P = 0.002).
- Bestgen et al. (1998) reported that larval production in 1994 was low in the Yampa spawning reach but average in the lower Green River. Haines et al. (1998) reported the same pattern for fall age-0 abundance.

**2. The draft's standing-stock estimate is unreliable.**
- The draft gives a carrying capacity of 4,560 and implies adults were at or near it. The letter argues this figure is inconsistent with the underlying estimates.
- The 2,400 Green River estimate was derived by subtracting 600 Colorado River fish from the basin total of "slightly over 3,000" (Crowl and Bouwes 1998). It has no confidence intervals and assumes a closed population, which the letter says was violated.
- Applying the draft's own density of 27.2 fish per kilometer to the middle Green and Yampa rivers would give 74,742 fish, not 4,560.
- The letter recommends larger numbers until the [[Closed-Population Capture Estimators]] study is complete. It also proposes an alternative figure from Tyus (1991) based partly on an opinion survey of fishery workers. The value is illegible in the scan.

**3. Threats differ by reach, so the Green River needs separate threat assessments.**
- Flow effects: [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] mainly affects the middle Green River, while tributaries have greater influence on the lower Green River.
- Nonnative fishes: [[Northern Pike]] are more abundant in the middle Green River.
- Diversions: listed diversions affect only the middle Green River.
- The letter concludes that different threats affect recruitment in each reach.

**4. Recommended revision.**
- Treat the Green River as two population/management units, not one.
- Base total population targets on a more accurate estimate of current carrying capacity, expected to be higher than the previous draft.

**5. Draft recovery targets (Appendix and Section 4 summaries).**
- [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]: about 3,000 wild adults across the Green and upper Colorado systems. The Green River subbasin total is 2,400 (1,000 at [[Three Fords Reach]] and 1,400 in the lower 32 km of the Yampa). The upper Colorado total is 597 (Osmundson and Burnham 1998). Downlisting requires a lower 95% confidence limit above 2,550 adults in the Green River subbasin and above 600 in the Colorado subbasin.
- [[Humpback Chub]]: about 14,000 wild adults across six populations. Downlisting requires five upper basin populations to be maintained, with the lower 95% confidence interval exceeding 2,100 adults in core populations.
- [[Razorback Sucker]]: the middle Green River population was estimated at 948 adults in 1989 and 524 in 1996 (Modde et al. 1996). Lake Mohave declined from about 60,000 (1991) to 9,300 (2000). Lake Mead holds about 400 adults.
- [[Bonytail]]: about 130,000 hatchery F1 and F2 fish were released into Lake Mohave between 1991 and 1997. Only 11 wild adults have been reported from the upper basin since 1977.

**6. Statistical approach.**
- A 5-year monitoring period is required for downlisting, and an 8- to 15-year period for delisting.
- The draft proposes [[Closed-Population Capture Estimators]] (Otis et al. 1978; Osmundson and Burnham 1998) and a 25% maximum lower confidence interval relative to the estimate.
- Trend tests include a linear regression slope significantly below zero (p < 0.10) and counts of years of decline.
