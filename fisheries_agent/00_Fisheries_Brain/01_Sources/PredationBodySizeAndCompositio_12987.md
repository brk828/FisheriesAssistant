# Predation, Body Size, and Composition of Plankton

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 study by [[John Langdon Brooks]] and [[Stanley I. Dodson]] examines how a planktivorous fish, the [[Alewife]] (*Alosa pseudoharengus*), reshapes lake zooplankton communities. Lakes in the eastern Connecticut coastal strip with self-sustaining, landlocked alewife populations lacked large crustacean zooplankters (Daphnia, Diaptomus, Mesocyclops, Epischura) and were dominated by small species such as [[Bosmina Longirostris]]. A resampling of [[Crystal Lake]] before and after the introduction of [[Glut Herring]] (*Alosa aestivalis*) documented the same shift within about 20 years. The authors attribute the pattern to size-dependent predation, and they combine it with a "size-efficiency hypothesis" that explains how competition and predation together determine the body size of dominant herbivorous zooplankton.

## Background & Description

- **Study organism:** The alewife is a herring-like clupeoid, originally anadromous, with marine populations along the western Atlantic from the Gulf of St. Lawrence to North Carolina. Landlocked populations are established in several freshwater bodies, including [[Lake Cayuga]] and the [[Great Lakes]]. Common names include "alewife" and "grayback"; the authors note that taxonomists disagree on the generic name (*Alosa* vs. *Pomolobus*).
- **Feeding biology:** Alewives feed chiefly on planktonic copepods and cladocerans. Their closely spaced gill rakers act as a plankton sieve (Fig. 1). Visual discrimination is still required for feeding.
- **Study system:** Seven Connecticut lakes with self-perpetuating alewife populations lie within about 40 km of the coast and drain into [[Long Island Sound]]. Sixteen lakes were sampled in total, with eight alewife-free comparison lakes.
- **Zooplankton groups:** Cladocera (water fleas) and copepods (Calanoida and Cyclopoida) are the focus, with the predaceous rotifer [[Asplanchna Priodonta]] as the only non-crustacean recorded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake morphometry:** Alewife lakes vary in area and depth. In Table 1, lake areas range from 9 to 440 hectares and maximum depths from 5 to 20 m. Large Daphnia generally dominate lakes deeper than about 5 m, while Bosmina dominates shallower water.
- **Trophic framework:** The authors model the open-water community as four levels: (1) microphytes (net phytoplankton and nannoplankton), (2) herbivorous zooplankton, (3) planktivores, and (4) piscivores. Particles passing a 50-micron sieve are called nannoseston.
- **Food particle size:** Planktonic herbivores (rotifers, cladocerans, calanoids) collect particles in the 1–15 micron range. Large cladocerans and calanoids can also take particles up to about 50 microns.
- **Spatial behavior:** Alewives avoid shores except during spawning and feed away from the bottom. Adult [[Diaptomus minutus]] are epilimnetic in summer, whereas adult [[Cyclops bicuspidatus thomasi]] concentrate in inshore and bottom waters.
- **Introduction history:** Passive dispersal of zooplankton means most continental species probably reach a given lake within 10 to 25 years.

## Key Findings

1. **Predator-induced community shift (Connecticut lakes):** Alewife lakes lacked all large zooplankters except one species of Cyclops. Dominants were small Bosmina longirostris, Cyclops bicuspidatus thomasi and Tropocyclops prasinus. Non-alewife lakes were dominated by Diaptomus spp. and Daphnia spp.
2. **Crystal Lake natural experiment:** In 1942 (before alewife-like fish were established), Crystal Lake was dominated by Daphnia, Diaptomus and Mesocyclops. In 1964, after *A. aestivalis* became abundant, the zooplankton resembled the natural alewife lakes. A total of 6,623 crustacean specimens from the 1942 and 1964 collections were identified. Resampling of other Connecticut lakes after the same interval showed no comparable change.
3. **Body size shift:** In Crystal Lake, no zooplankters over 1 mm were found in 1964, whereas in 1942 specimens reached 1.8 mm, and Leptodora kindtii (5–10 mm) was present. The modal size fell from 0.785 mm (without alewives) to 0.285 mm (with alewives). Most zooplankton were under about 0.6 mm when alewives were abundant.
4. **Size threshold for survival:** Species whose smallest mature females are too large to persist (Daphnia catawba, Mesocyclops edax, Epischura nordenskioldi, Leptodora kindtii) were eliminated. Diaptomus minutus (maturing at 0.6–1.0 mm) was eliminated, but Cyclops bicuspidatus thomasi was not, showing that factors beyond size (escape movements, spatial distribution) matter.
5. **Large lake comparison:** In [[Lake Cayuga]] (172 km², maximum depth 132 m), plankton was dominated by Bosmina longirostris, large cladocerans were rare, and Diaptomus was scarce. Large zooplankters persisted in the Laurentian Great Lakes, implying a weaker effect in very large systems.
6. **Size-efficiency hypothesis:** Planktonic herbivores compete for 1–15 micron particles. Under low predation, larger species win competitively. Under intense size-selective predation, small species dominate. Under moderate predation, larger competitors are held in check, allowing smaller ones to persist.
7. **Mechanistic basis:** Filtering area scales roughly with the square of body length. Filtration rates of four cladocerans (Bosmina, Diaphanosoma, Simocephalus, Daphnia magna) were approximately proportional to the square of body length (relative rates 1:10:18 versus squared lengths 1:2:9:16 after reduction to a standard condition). Larger species also show lower metabolic rate per unit mass, which favors egg production.
8. **Bohemian fish pond evidence:** In Poltruba Pond (1957, low fish stock), a large Daphnia made up 80% of zooplankton. Under high fish stock with Bosmina dominant, nannoseston standing crop was two to three times greater, and photosynthesis was about five times greater, supporting the hypothesis's prediction.
9. **Congeneric size partitioning:** Congeneric zooplankters are usually similar in size, consistent with the expectation of similar food-collecting efficiency. Wagler's review of 87 European *Daphnia cucullata* populations found that only the largest-bodied clones coexisted with *D. galeata*. Dwarf forms (carapace under 550 µm in midsummer) occurred only where Bosmina longirostris was present, implying dominance only under intense predation.
10. **Overall conclusion:** The authors propose that the antagonistic demands of competition (favoring large size) and size-dependent predation (favoring small size) together determine the body size of dominant herbivorous zooplankton.
