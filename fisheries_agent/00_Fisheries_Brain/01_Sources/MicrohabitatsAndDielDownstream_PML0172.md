# Microhabitats and Diel Downstream Migration of Young Grayling

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the microhabitat use and downstream movements of young-of-the-year [[European Grayling]] (*Thymallus thymallus*) during emergence and early growth in the [[River Suran]], a spawning tributary of the [[River Ain]] in France. Fry were sampled by [[Electrofishing]] and drift trapping between April and October 1988, with supplementary electrofishing in the Ain in June and July 1989. Fry initially occupied marginal, low-velocity areas and fed on drift near the surface, then shifted to benthic zones and moved toward the channel as they grew. Downstream migration, mostly nocturnal, removed nearly all young-of-the-year from the Suran by June. The authors note that the grayling's upper-water-column location is unusual among young salmonids.

## Background & Description

*Thymallus thymallus* is a rheophilic salmonid. Prior work on Thymallinae fry habitat and dispersion was limited. [[Scott (1985)]] described post-emergent grayling in an English river as favouring low-velocity surface waters before moving to benthic zones, and earlier studies of grayling movement focused mainly on northern populations. No field data on fry or juvenile dispersal under temperate climates were available, which motivated the study.

The aims were to (1) identify the microhabitats of young grayling and (2) describe their downstream movements under natural conditions.

Fish were assigned to six developmental types:
- **Type 1:** yolk sac still prominent (up to about 2 cm)
- **Type 2:** yolk sac not prominent, coloured abdominal spot still present
- **Type 3:** no abdominal spot, total length under 2.4 cm
- **Type 4:** 2.5–3.5 cm, still alevin-like
- **Type 5:** 3–3.5 cm to about 12 cm, with parr marks
- **Type 6:** juveniles

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The Suran (mean annual discharge 5 m³ s⁻¹) is cut by a small dam about 1 km upstream of its confluence with the Ain, which restricts spawning to a short, observable stretch. Sampling zones included Zone 2 (the main study reach), Zone 1 (upstream of the dam) and Zone 3 (upstream of the confluence).

**Methods.**
- *Microhabitat:* Electrofishing sampled points every 2–5 m along both banks and across the channel. Distance from bank, current velocity, depth, bank slope and co-occurring species were recorded.
- *Downstream movement:* Four drift traps (30 cm square frames, 1 mm mesh) were placed across the stream about 100 m upstream of the confluence, with catches pooled. The first trapping (8 April 1988) lasted 6 h around dusk. Three 24-h cycles followed (19 and 26 April, 5 May), with traps checked about every 3 h.

**Associated fauna.** Trout fry (*Salmo trutta*) and minnow (*Phoxinus phoxinus*) were captured at grayling points. [[Loach]] (*Noemacheilus barbatulus*) were common but were not counted, as they buried into the gravel when shocked. Adult trout, minnows and [[Blageon]] (*Leuciscus souffia*) co-occurred with grayling in summer and autumn.

**Discharge.** Electrofishing and trapping occurred under normal, low, small-flood and very low discharge conditions (0.62–7.65 m³ s⁻¹ across the sampling dates). Electrofishing efficiency depends on hydrology, so quantitative comparisons between dates may not be valid.

## Key Findings

**Growth and emergence**
- Emergence began in mid-April. Length increased almost threefold within two months. Fry measured about 6 cm by June, outgrowing young trout that emerged earlier at a larger size.
- Electrofishing in 1988 caught fry mainly in the Suran from early April to early May, after which they were nearly absent from the Suran and were found in the Ain.

**Microhabitat of early growth (Zone 2, 8 April – 5 May 1988)**
- Fry were caught only near the water's edge, mostly within 20 cm of the bank, especially at steep banks.
- Surface-water velocity was the main factor in distribution. Zero and low velocities were selected by types 1–4 (Fig. 2).
- Types 2 and 3 (77% of sampled fry) were mainly caught at 0–5 cm s⁻¹ (about 70%). Types 1 and 4 (23%) were caught at both low and higher velocities.
- The velocity difference between groups was significant (χ² = 11.06, df = 3, P = 0.05), but no clear correlation existed between fry type and velocity class.
- Fry were usually solitary or in groups of two to four (about 90% of groups, 50% of individuals). A single large group of about fifty was caught on 5 May, attributed to low water rather than shoaling.

**Later microhabitat (June–October)**
- From June to October, grayling were found more than 1 m from the banks, at depths of 20–80 cm, in fast-flowing channel zones often near small riffles.
- In the Ain (June 1989), 85% of young grayling were in water velocities of 30–60 cm s⁻¹ and depths of 30–40 cm. Few fish were near the banks.
- In July 1989, fish were in velocities of about 40–80 cm s⁻¹ at depths of 65–70 cm.
- Grayling rested 1–3 cm above the bottom but often fed in the upper water column.
- The shift from banks toward the channel appeared gradual and was accompanied by a change from surface drift feeding to benthic habitat.

**Diel downstream migration**
- Downstream migration began at the start of emergence (mid-April) and continued to the end of May. It was mainly nocturnal and affected all size classes.
- Nocturnal captures were 81% on the first trapping date, then 72% and 66% on subsequent dates.
- On the first two 24-h sessions, the pattern was bimodal, with peaks at the beginning and end of the night. On 5 May it was unimodal, peaking early in the night.
- Type 3 fry showed no end-of-night peak (Fig. 5). The proportion of diurnal migrants increased with fry age.
- Discharge changes and moonlight were considered as causes. Moonlight could explain the suppression of end-of-night migration on 5 May, when the moon was nearly full, but not the bimodality on earlier dates.

**Outcomes and interpretation**
- Downstream movement ended with the complete desertion of the Suran by young-of-the-year by June, indicating that the tributary served only as a spawning and early-growth area.
- Catches dropped by 80% within three weeks, suggesting a general depletion of the Suran stock. Some fry may have moved directly to the Ain, while others left during April and May before the habitat shift.
- Grayling's upper-column location is unusual among young salmonids, which typically occupy the bottom or banks. Proposed explanations include small body size restricting fry to low velocities, reduced competition from absent cyprinids in early spring, and a smaller mouth requiring smaller prey.
- Grayling spawn in spring and emerge late in the season, which may explain some unusual early behaviour.
