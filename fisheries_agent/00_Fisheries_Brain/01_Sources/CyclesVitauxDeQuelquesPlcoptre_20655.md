# Life Cycles of Some Laurentian Stoneflies (Plecoptera)

#entomology #plecoptera #stoneflies #freshwater-biology

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Peter Harper]] and [[Etienne Magnin]] documents the life cycles of stoneflies (Plecoptera) in a small stream on the grounds of the [[Station de Biologie de l'Université de Montréal]] at Saint-Hippolyte, Quebec. Twenty-four species were collected from five sampling stations. Adult emergence was recorded with cages and larval growth was tracked with monthly kick samples. The authors describe the larval growth of six common species and add field notes on the others. Most species complete their life cycle in one year, but *[[Paragnetina Media]]* appears to need at least three years as a larva. No autumn-emerging species were found.

## Background & Description

Stoneflies in Quebec were poorly known at the time. Earlier work had mainly addressed adult taxonomy, while larval and adult biology remained largely unexplored across North America. European studies were used as a guide, but the authors noted that few species are shared between Europe and North America. The study ran from February 1966 to June 1967.

The study stream drains into [[Lac Achigan]] and belongs to the [[L'Assomption River]] watershed. It rises in [[Lac Pilon]] and widens through a series of small lakes. The stream is torrential in spring and has lower flow in summer and winter. Water is acidic (pH 6.0), and the water temperature is strongly influenced by the lakes it passes through.

Temperature data for 1966 (maximum and minimum):
- June: 28°C and 14.5°C
- July: 27°C and 16.5°C
- August: 25°C and 15.5°C

Winter water temperatures stay close to 0°C (December to mid-April), and summer temperatures can reach 28°C. Because the stream has rapids and no pollution sources, dissolved oxygen is always high.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling stations.** Five stations were established:
- **Station 1:** at the outlet of [[Lac Long]], below an abandoned beaver dam, with coarse gravel and a steady current. Depth ranges from 10 to 40 cm, and the centre never freezes.
- **Station 2:** at the outlet of a peat-bog lake, with moss-covered stones, a torrent over boulders, and gravel. Only the gravel habitat is accessible in winter.
- **Station 3:** where the stream enters [[Lac Cromwell]], with four habitats (marsh, boulder field, gravel pool, torrent). It is inaccessible in winter due to ice and snow.
- **Station 4:** upper part of the Lac Cromwell outlet.
- **Station 5:** a small permanent stream linking Lac Triton and Lac Croche to Lac Cromwell, on a side branch. It fauna differed from the other stations. Stations 4 and 5 were visited only occasionally.

**Methods.**
- *Adults* were collected with [[Emergence Cages]] at Stations 2 and 3, operated from 15 May to 7 September 1966 and emptied at least once daily. Additional adults were collected by beating shrubs and turning stones along the stream.
- *Larvae* were sampled monthly during the last week of each month at Stations 1 and 2 using the [[Kick Sampling]] method of Hynes (1961). Two nets (50 and 8 meshes per cm) were combined. Larvae were separated from debris by flotation in a saturated calcium chloride solution. Hard-to-dislodge species such as *Paragnetina media* were hand-picked from stones.
- *Growth* was measured as head capsule width, with histograms plotted by month.

**Community composition.** Stations 1 to 4 had similar faunas. Station 5 was dominated by Filipalpia (phytophagous or detritivorous) species, and its only Setipalpia (usually carnivorous) were two Chloroperlidae species. Some species were abundant throughout, while others were dominant only at certain sites. *Hastaperla brevis* was dominant at Station 1, and *Isoperla orata* and *Paracapnia angulata* at Station 3.

**Seasonal succession.** Taeniopterygidae and Capniidae form the winter fauna and leave the water as the snow melts. The main emergence occurs from May onward. In August only *[[Leuctra Tenuis]]* was found in abundance, along with a single *Leuctra truncata* specimen collected on 8 August.

## Key Findings

**Diversity and timing**
- 24 species were collected from the five stations, spanning seven families.
- Capniidae and Taeniopterygidae emerge first, as soon as the snow melts. Other species emerge in May and June.
- *Leuctra tenuis* is the only species that emerges through the summer, from 7 June to 7 September.
- No true autumn species were found, which the authors describe as unexplained given the many autumn species in Europe.
- The Pteronarcidae were absent, and the Peltoperlidae are unknown at this latitude.

**Species life cycles**
- ***[[Taeniopteryx Burksi]]*** emerges in early spring. Larvae first appeared on 26 September, at about 1.5 mm total length. Growth was rapid through the winter, the sexes could be told apart in January, growth ended in February, and the species synchronized well. One egg mass held 1280 eggs. Oviposition extended from the second half of April to mid-May.
- ***[[Paracapnia Opis]]*** emerges at the same time as *T. burksi* or a few days earlier. Adults lay eggs from late April to early June. Small larvae were found from 28 June (about 1 mm), and larvae reached 2 mm by October. Growth was slow in summer and faster until February. Females reached 5.5 mm and males 6 mm (as written in the text).
- ***[[Isoperla Frisoni]]*** is a clearly summer species. Emergence was brief, from 1 to 23 June, with more than 95% of the population leaving the water in the first two weeks. Oviposition was observed on 7 and 9 July 1966. Larvae grew continuously in autumn, peaked in May, and were not synchronized.
- ***[[Isoperla Lata]]*** emerges in spring. Only 57 larvae were collected, so the cycle is only sketched. Eggs are probably laid mainly in June, with growth slowing before emergence.
- ***[[Hastaperla Brevis]]*** emerges at the same time as *I. frisoni* and shows a similar pattern, with larvae found from October to early June. Growth was continuous except for a slowdown in January, and it was not synchronized.
- ***[[Paragnetina Media]]*** has a very brief emergence, about one week in the third week of June 1966. Males (about 18 mm) emerge near the stream and females (about 25 mm) may travel several metres. Larval growth spans more than a year, and the larval stage is estimated at at least three years.
- ***Acroneuria abnormis*** and ***A. lycorias*** emerge in June. Larval data suggest a three-year larval life, but sample sizes were too small for certainty.

**Growth patterns and regulation**
- Most species complete their life cycle within one year. The exception is the Perlidae, which appear to need about three years.
- Adult emergence in all species except *Leuctra tenuis* occurs in a short, fixed window. The authors suggest this reflects [[Seasonal Regulation]] mechanisms, which were not studied directly in North America.
- Growth is more synchronized in detritivorous species (*Taeniopteryx burksi*, *Paracapnia opis*) than in partly or fully carnivorous species (*Isoperla frisoni*, *Hastaperla brevis*). The authors argue that asynchronous growth benefits predators by providing a wider range of prey.
- Diapause, a larval growth slowdown in summer, has been shown in *[[Capnia Bifrons]]* in England. The authors suspect it also occurs in *Paracapnia* and may be typical of the Capniinae.

**Related species and conclusions**
- *Paracapnia angulata* emerges about a month after *P. opis*, around early May. Its adults are brachypterous (short-winged), so flight is impossible or very limited. Its larval stages cannot be distinguished from those of *P. opis*.
- *Leuctra sibleyi* is spring-emerging (24 May to 5 June). *Leuctra tenuis* emerged from 7 June to 7 September and became dominant in July and August.
- The authors conclude that the stream's stonefly community is typical of Laurentian lake-linked streams, with representatives of most North American families.
