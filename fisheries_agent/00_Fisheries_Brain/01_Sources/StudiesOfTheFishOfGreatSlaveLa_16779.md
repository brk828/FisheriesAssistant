# Studies of the Fish of Great Slave Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #coregonidae #fish-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1951 report by D. S. Rawson (University of Saskatchewan) presents the results of a four-year study (1944–1947) of the fish of [[Great Slave Lake]], begun one year before commercial fishing opened on the lake. The aim was to estimate the lake's capacity for fish production and to describe the ecology of its unexploited fish population. Sampling relied mainly on standardized 300-yard gangs of [[Gill Net]], which took about 12,000 fish weighing roughly 20,000 pounds. The [[Lake Trout]], [[Common Whitefish]] and [[Ciscoes]] dominate the catch, and the first two support an extensive gill-net fishery. The [[Inconnu]] and [[Deepwater Sculpin]] are of special zoogeographic interest. The report also covers mesh selectivity, growth, diet, parasites and food-web structure.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** About 10,500 square miles, with a large open basin, a shallow north arm (toward Fort Rae) and a deep, island-broken east arm containing the Hearne and Hornby Channels and Christie and McLeod Bays.
- **Species inventory:** 21 species were identified, excluding the ciscoes (*Leucichthys* spp.), which could not be named to species level. Their identifications were verified by the Royal Ontario Museum and the University of Michigan. Preble (1908) listed 14 species, 13 of which were recollected; the fourteenth, *Moxostoma lesueurii*, was probably a misidentification.
- **Biogeography:** Most species are widespread across northwestern Canada and the Great Lakes drainage. Four genera (*Prosopium*, *Coregonus*, *Stenodus*, *Thymallus*) also occur in Asia. Only the inconnu and the lamprey are confined to the arctic drainage.
- **Sampling gear:** The standard gang had six nets of 1½ to 5½-inch stretched mesh, with specifications held constant across areas (Table I). Sets lasted normally 24 hours, and seines and small-mesh trawls captured young fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** Heavy catches occurred along the south and west shores (206 and 358 lb per standard set, against a whole-lake average of 136 lb). The east arm had the highest lake trout concentration, more than twice that of any other area. McLeod Bay had almost no common whitefish, which the author attributed to great depth and scarce bottom fauna.
- **Depth:** Catches were heavy down to 75 m and light from 75 to 300 m. Ciscoes peaked at about 20 m, common whitefish at about 15 m, and lake trout at about 25 m. Trout were taken at depths of 150, 210 and 225 m. Most secondary and restricted species were confined to the upper 35 m; burbot increased with depth.
- **Habitat associations:** Inshore species (pike, white sucker, goldeye, grayling, pikeperch, small fish) contrast with offshore species (ciscoes, whitefish, trout, longnose sucker, burbot, cottids). Lake trout approach shore in the east arm because surface waters rarely warm enough to exclude them, unlike in more southern lakes.
- **Food web:** The principal chains are plankton → cisco → lake trout, and plankton → detritus → bottom organisms (especially [[Pontoporeia]]) → common whitefish. [[Mysis]] and cottids are key links in deep water. Ciscoes ate mainly copepods and Mysis (88% of diet), whitefish ate amphipods and molluscs (90%), and trout ate fish (76%).
- **Parasites:** Twenty-one parasite species were recorded from ten fish species. The most significant is [[Triaenophorus Crassus]], whose larval cysts infect whitefish, trout, ciscoes, inconnu and grayling.

## Key Findings

1. **Community composition:** Ciscoes made up 40.6% of the 11,620 fish in 145 standard sets (7.3% by weight). Common whitefish made up 37.0% by number and 28.3% by weight. Lake trout made up 10.3% by number and 45.9% by weight.
2. **Mesh selectivity:** Ciscoes declined steadily from 1½ to 5½-inch mesh. Whitefish peaked in the 3-inch mesh, and lake trout in number at 3 inches and in weight at 5 inches. The 5-inch and 5½-inch meshes gave similar size ranges, so the larger mesh was kept only as the legal commercial size.
3. **Trout migration:** In the east arm, 75% of lake trout were under 60 cm. Elsewhere, 72% were over 60 cm. The author concluded that reproduction occurs mainly in the east arm, with a general westward migration over the fish's life, and that tagging by Kennedy showed movements of 150 to 200 miles.
4. **Growth:** Growth data were given for round whitefish, white sucker, longnose sucker, burbot and pikeperch. Most grew slightly slower than in southern lakes. A seven-year-old grayling measured 17.3 inches.
5. **Species status:**
   - *Round whitefish* were rare overall but concentrated in the east arm.
   - *Pikeperch* were rare in the main lake but occurred in the north arm and along the south shore. Growth was the slowest recorded for the species.
   - *Goldeye* occurred only on the south shore.
6. **Ciscoes:** At least three species appear to be present, but the naming awaits a revision of the genus by J. Keleher. Ciscoes are the principal plankton-eating forage fish for lake trout.
7. **Management relevance:** The author judged the lake's density comparable to the upper Great Lakes, supporting an annual commercial trout and whitefish production of about one pound per acre. Kennedy's ongoing catch sampling and growth work was described as part of the fishery management programme.
8. **Conclusion:** The lake's fish community has the characteristics of northern oligotrophic lakes, with a sharp inshore–offshore division and a dominant three-species core.
