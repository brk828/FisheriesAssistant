# Review of National Fish Hatchery Program and Recommendations for Future Direction

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #conservation-policy #hatchery-operations

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive review of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's National Fish Hatchery (NFH) program was conducted by a panel convened by the [[National Fish and Wildlife Foundation]] at the request of the Service Director. The review addresses fundamental questions about the role of Federal fish hatcheries in contemporary aquatic resource management, particularly in light of ecosystem management principles and the decline of native fish populations. The panel found that nearly 400 fish taxa require special protection, with 27 species and 13 subspecies having become extinct in [[North America]] since 1900. Major causes of decline include competition for water, habitat alteration, pollution, exotic species introductions, and commercial exploitation. Critically, the review concludes that NFHs have sometimes been part of the problem rather than the solution, through production of exotic species, saturation stocking that leads to overharvest of wild fish, and domestication processes that reduce fitness when hatchery fish interbreed with wild populations. The program currently operates 78 hatcheries producing 220 million fish of 69 species annually with a budget of $54.1 million, consuming approximately 80% of total Fishery Resources program funding.

## Background & Description

The Federal fish culture program began in 1871 at [[Bucksport]] (Maine) and 1872 at [[McCloud River]] (California), initially to extend ranges of commercial species and augment food supplies. The program expanded significantly following enactment of the White Act (1930), Coordination Act (1934), and Mitchell Act (1934), with construction and upgrading occurring between 1934-1960. The number of Federal hatcheries peaked at 96 facilities. Currently, the Fishery Resources program includes 78 hatcheries, 9 [[Fish Disease Control Centers]], and 6 [[Fish Technology Centers]]. Three facilities established between 1879-1889 ([[Neosho NFH]], [[Leadville NFH]], and [[Craigbrook NFH]]) remain operational. Of current facilities, 52 produce coldwater species, 13 produce warmwater species, and 13 produce both. Production in 1993 totaled 220 million fish of 69 kinds for mitigation, restoration, threatened or endangered species (TES) preservation, and recreational fishing, with $3.5 million received as reimbursable funds.

The review panel included representatives from four universities, two State game and fish agencies, five Federal agencies, a multistate commission, a private natural resources group, and commercial aquaculture. Information was gathered through oral and written contact with Service administrators, biologists, State Fish and Game Department directors, university researchers, members of professional societies ([[American Fisheries Society]], [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]], [[Desert Fishes Council]], [[Great Lakes Fishery Commission]]), and various stakeholder groups. The panel held three meetings in Bloomington, Minnesota during 1994 to formulate positions and develop recommendations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat degradation and loss have been identified as the major factors contributing to national declines in aquatic resources, not species or stock over-exploitation. No amount of stocking can correct problems of "inhospitable" environments. In [[Pacific Salmon]] populations, despite release of record numbers of hatchery-produced smolts over extended periods, runs continued to decline. Ecosystem-level changes have transformed rivers into series of near-slackwater reservoirs, reducing current, warming water, and favoring certain predators such as native [[Northern Pikeminnow]] and non-native [[Walleye]]. Additional upstream ecological damage from clear-cut logging, excessive road building, and livestock grazing has resulted in warmed spawning streams and erosion contributing to siltation of spawning beds and suffocation of fry and incubating eggs.

Problems associated with hatchery production include poor survival of stocked fish, naivete of hatchery fishes, introduction of parasites and diseases, genetic pollution, predation, and excessive competition by stocked fishes with native species. Domestication, or adaptation to culture conditions, is the natural outcome of living in a protected environment and reduces an organism's capacity for growth and survival in nature. When hatchery fish interbreed with wild fish, fitness of wild populations may be reduced ([[Reisenbichler 1994]]). The [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] has designated [[Evolutionarily Significant Units]] (ESUs) as groups contributing to diversity in populations that have evolutionary significance and should receive protected status under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Examples include [[Snake River Sockeye Salmon]] in [[Redfish Lake]] and Snake River fall and spring-summer [[Chinook Salmon]].

Thirty-eight of 78 hatcheries are involved in mitigation production, with some operating under statutory mitigation mandates and others under de facto mitigation arrangements. Many mitigation efforts have failed to achieve original goals. Natural runs of salmon are effectively blocked by hydropower and irrigation dams, with an estimated 80% or more of downstream migrants released from mitigation hatcheries killed in hydropower turbines. Altered habitat due to dams may have as much negative impact on fingerling/smolt survival as the turbines themselves.

## Key Findings

**Primary Recommendations (Priority Order):**

1. **Ecosystem Management Focus**: The primary mission of fish hatcheries should be to provide support for ecosystem management and managers, not numerical production targets. Artificial propagation should not substitute for aggressive habitat restoration programs or serve as an alternative to habitat protection.

2. **Habitat Restoration Priority**: Protection and restoration of habitat is the key to perpetuation of national fishery resources. Governmental agencies should work together to restore habitat of depleted anadromous stocks and threatened and endangered species rather than rely on hatchery fish to compensate for habitat losses.

3. **Regional Ecosystem Management Teams**: Immediate action should be taken to form an ecosystem management team (EMT) in each region including terrestrial and aquatic biologists, ecologists, population biologists, endangered species specialists, and economic effectiveness evaluators. First assignment should be careful examination of NFH operations in the region to assess negative ecosystem impacts and search for alternatives. Actions should be completed and recommendations delivered to the Director before end of fiscal year 1995.

4. **Facility Evaluation and Consolidation**: Not all 78 hatcheries will be required to meet ecosystem management needs. Each hatchery should be evaluated for production capabilities, efficiency, age, technological and physical condition, potential for contributing to programs, and impacts of closure on ecosystem management. Facilities found unalterably disruptive to ecosystem structure and function or economically infeasible should cease hatchery operations and not be transferred to State or private ownership to perpetuate problems.

5. **Threatened and Endangered Species Priority**: Before proceeding with divestiture/closure of any facility, consider its potential to protect aquatic or semi-aquatic threatened and endangered species. Hatcheries retained for such purposes should be modern with high-quality water supplies, provisions to prevent escape, and require no expensive renovations. Rigorous population viability analysis with predictive capabilities should be completed for target organisms before hatchery use is considered. If a species is brought into captivity for emergency protection, habitat should be restored within 3-5 generations or the species transferred to private/public facilities for protection as a living study population.

6. **Mitigation Program Reform**: All mitigation hatcheries should be reviewed critically to determine if original mitigation goals are being achieved. If suitable habitat no longer exists with no likelihood of restoration, the Service should negotiate with Congress and plaintiffs to change the nature of mitigation efforts and, if necessary, reduce or cease hatchery production. Efforts should be made to have developing agencies underwrite all operational costs, needed maintenance, and expenses to correct failed mitigation efforts.

7. **Stocking Protocols**: All stocking of fish in waters on Federal lands should be consistent with approved ecosystem resource management plans that preclude deleterious competitive and genetic effects on endemic species. Stocking should only occur in areas where suitable habitat, adequate food base, and appropriate spawning areas are available based on specific analysis and implementation plans. Fish from indigenous populations should be used as broodstock to maintain genetic diversity.

8. **Recreational Fishing Role**: The majority of the panel felt that provision of hatchery fish for enhancement of recreational fishing is not a Federal responsibility. Production to support recreational fishing should be phased out over time in coordination with State Fish and Game agencies. When waters created with Federal funds are stocked to establish fish communities, Federal obligation should end as soon as self-sustaining populations are achieved. Enhancement beyond natural levels (put-and-take fisheries) should be State responsibility.

9. **Genetic Management**: In each region where aquatic ecosystem assessment and management plans are implemented, a qualified population geneticist should be on staff or available to assist management teams. Competent geneticists should be consulted from the beginning and throughout propagation efforts to ensure maintenance of genetic variability in populations.

10. **Budget Restructuring**: FR should revise budget preparation to focus funding priorities on ecosystem management project needs. NFH operations should become better-defined parts of Regional budgets. The present proportion of approximately 80% of FR budget dedicated to hatchery operations is excessive and should be reduced to allow funding for new initiatives such as product evaluation and contributions to aquatic ecosystem management.

11. **Fish Health Services**: Temporarily postpone implementing the decision to discontinue fish-health services to non-federal clients until State or private capabilities are in place. Private aquaculturists should be given greater access to disease-free stocks maintained in the Federal broodstock registry. Generic responsibility for assisting private aquaculture should be transferred to [[USDA]] with establishment of an interagency group to ensure activities consider prevention of potential negative impacts on indigenous populations.

12. **Personnel Integration**: NFH personnel comprise the largest assemblage of trained fish culturists in the world and should be integral members of ecosystem management teams, informed of needs for specific species or stocks. They are professionally flexible and can be updated in ecosystem management and aquatic habitat assessment, restoration, and management.

13. **Public Education**: A new public outreach and education approach is needed emphasizing the role of Federal hatcheries as vital support to resource managers in aquatic ecosystem stewardship, not the "numbers game" concept. Visitors should be informed of resource problems in the region, Service programs to resolve those problems, and the role of each NFH in those efforts.

14. **Partnership Development**: Changes in Service policies should be preceded by discussions and negotiations with affected States and partners. Implementation should be time-phased to accommodate needs of State and Tribal partners. Consultation and communication should occur by Regional management to individual partners once Washington office decides on policy changes.

**Specific Production Priorities (in order):**
- Threatened and Endangered Species and other native biota
- Mandated mitigation/Tribal and treaty obligations  
- Sportfish restoration
- Recreational fishing (only if consistent with national goals, done in concert with State cooperators, and not in conflict with sound ecosystem management)

**Financial Considerations**: Appropriation of $270 million for needed maintenance and restoration of all 78 hatcheries is unrealistic. Combining programs on a region-to-region basis with possible divestiture of excess physical facilities is necessary. Any dollars saved through divestiture should be used for ecosystem work, operational costs, or to retrofit remaining facilities for uses such as TES or centers for aquatic habitat assessments, restoration, monitoring, and research.

**Minority Report Note**: The panel was unable to reach consensus on the proper Federal role in recreational fishing. A minority felt that if Federal lands are involved or if a project was constructed with Federal funds, NFHs should provide stocking in perpetuity, even if mitigation was not involved.
