## Executive Summary
This document, from the Arizona Riparian Council Newsletter (1997), is **not a fish-specific study** but a riparian ecosystem management report concerning the invasive shrub **saltcedar (Tamarix ramosissima)** and prospects for its **biological control** using host-specific insects. While fish are only briefly referenced as one of the taxa impacted by saltcedar invasion, the source provides critical context for understanding **habitat degradation mechanisms** that affect fish and other aquatic/riparian species in the southwestern United States. The paper outlines the ecological damage caused by saltcedar, the rationale for introducing biocontrol insects, and regulatory review processes involving the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Background & Description
No fish morphological data is presented in this source. The document instead describes the invasive plant and candidate biocontrol organisms:

- **Saltcedar (Tamarix ramosissima):** An exotic shrub, taxonomically isolated in the Western Hemisphere (no native Tamaricaceae species occur there), forming dense monotypic stands with small fruits/seeds and unpalatable foliage.
- **Athel (Tamarix aphylla):** A congeneric but ecologically distinct, "somewhat beneficial" species not targeted by control insects.
- **Candidate biocontrol insects** (Old World origin, host-specific to *Tamarix*):
  - Mealybug — *Trabutina mannipera* (Israel)
  - Leaf beetle — *Diorhabda elongata* (China to Turkey)
  - Weevils — *Coniatus* sp., *Cormalia* sp.
  - Gall midges — *Psectrosema* spp.
  - Foliage-feeding moths — *Ornativalva* sp., *Agdistis* sp.
  - Psyllids — *Crastina* sp., *Colposcenia* sp.
  - Leaf beetle — *Cryptocephalus* sp.
  - Scale insect — *Adiscodiaspis* sp.
- Of 26 genera and >200 species restricted to *Tamarix* in the Old World, only 21 species were preliminarily tested, with 10 in quarantine trials at [[Temple, Texas]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The paper frames saltcedar invasion as a driver of **riparian habitat degradation** with downstream consequences for aquatic and semi-aquatic species, including fish:

- **Hydrological impacts:** Saltcedar consumes large volumes of groundwater, lowering water tables, drying springs, and reducing streamflow — directly threatening fish habitat availability.
- **Channel morphology:** Dense saltcedar stands promote **sedimentation and channel narrowing**, increasing flood risk and altering stream geomorphology critical to fish spawning and rearing habitat.
- **Salinization:** Saltcedar increases soil and substrate salinity, degrading conditions for salt-intolerant native vegetation (e.g., [[Cottonwood-Willow Riparian Forest]]) and potentially affecting water quality parameters relevant to aquatic organisms.
- **Fire regime alteration:** High flammability of saltcedar increases fire frequency, further stressing native riparian plant communities that stabilize streambanks and provide shade/organic input to aquatic systems.
- **Displacement of native flora:** Loss of cottonwood/willow (*Populus* sp./*Salix* sp.) and seepwillow baccharis (*Baccharis salicifolia*) reduces habitat complexity and food-web inputs (e.g., insect prey) that support both riparian birds and aquatic/fish communities.
- **Endangered species linkage:** The text explicitly notes that "several endangered species, especially birds and fishes, are severely impacted" by saltcedar-driven habitat change, though no specific fish taxa are named. The [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*) is identified as the key indicator species for regulatory review.

## Key Findings
- Saltcedar invasion since the 1920s has caused **substantial habitat damage** in western U.S. riparian ecosystems, with cascading effects on water availability, channel stability, and native vegetation — all factors relevant to fish habitat quality.
- **Conventional control methods** (manual, mechanical, herbicidal, fire) are costly, potentially damaging to native vegetation, and require repeated application.
- **Biological control** is proposed as a higher-potential, lower-disturbance alternative, leveraging host-specific insects with minimal risk to nontarget plants due to the taxonomic isolation of *Tamarix* in the Western Hemisphere.
- Two agents — the mealybug *Trabutina mannipera* and leaf beetle *Diorhabda elongata* — were recommended by USDA APHIS's Technical Advisory Group ([[TAG]]) for field release pending environmental review.
- Historical biocontrol data (130 years, 729 projects, 51 countries, 94 weed species) show a **~33% rate of complete or substantial control** and rare, minor nontarget impacts, especially since modern safeguards were implemented ~30 years prior.
- A **Biological Assessment** was being prepared for the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] to evaluate impacts on endangered species (notably the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]) in saltcedar-infested southwestern riparian areas — a process that, if resulting in a "No Jeopardy" finding, would proceed to NEPA-required Environmental Assessment and potential APHIS release permits.
- Projected outcomes: biocontrol could reduce saltcedar abundance by **75–85%**, requiring multiple agents introduced over 5–10 years per site, with gradual recovery of native riparian vegetation expected to follow — a process with indirect but significant implications for restoring degraded fish habitat in southwestern watersheds.
