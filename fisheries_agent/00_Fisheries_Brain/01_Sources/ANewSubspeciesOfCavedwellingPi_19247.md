# A New Subspecies of Cave-Dwelling Pimelodid Catfish, *Rhamdia laticauda typhla*, from Belize, Central America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pimelodidae

## Executive Summary

Greenfield, Greenfield and Woods (1982, *Breviora* 19/20: 563–576) describe *Rhamdia laticauda typhla* as a new subspecies of cave-dwelling [[Pimelodidae|pimelodid]] catfish. The type series comes from a cave at Las Cuevas, near Millionario in the [[Mountain Pine Ridge]] of [[Belize]]. The fish show a range of [[Eye Reduction]], from small pits with a black spot to eyes covered by skin. The authors separate the subspecies from the surface-dwelling *[[Rhamdia laticauda laticauda]]* by pectoral-spine length, serration, head shape, snout shape and vertebral count. They argue that eye loss alone does not justify taxonomic status, and they suggest the cave population entered the subterranean system through capture of surface streams.

## Background & Description

**Prior work.** [[Carl Hubbs]] (1936, 1938) surveyed the variation of *[[Rhamdia guatemalensis]]* in southeastern Mexico, northeastern Guatemala and the Yucatán Peninsula. He collected pimelodids in caves and cenotes but found no blind individuals. He described two cave-modified subspecies, *R. g. decolor* (depigmented, slightly reduced eyes) and *R. g. stygaea* (depigmented, moderately reduced eyes). Hubbs predicted that eyeless populations would eventually be found.

**Discovery.** Blind catfish were first observed on 10 July 1972 in two underground-connected pools at Las Cuevas (16.45'N, 89.00'W). The larger pool had a surface diameter of 1.5 m, and the smaller one measured 1 × 2 m.

**Holotype and paratypes.**
- Holotype: FMNH 71605, 114.8 mm SL, collected 10 July 1972.
- Paratypes: sixteen specimens, 50.8–124.9 mm SL, in FMNH 80454, FMNH 80455, FMNH 80456 and UMMZ 192632.
- Comparative material: about 60 specimens of *R. laticauda laticauda* and *R. guatemalensis* from Belize and Guatemala, housed mainly in FMNH, with additional lots at NIU and USNM.

**Diagnostic characters (*R. l. typhla* vs. *R. l. laticauda*).**
- Very short pectoral-fin spine: 11.2–17.1 in SL, versus 8.1–11.1 in *R. l. laticauda* (Fig. 4).
- Weaker serrations on the posterior edge of the pectoral spine.
- Wider, shorter head and a less-rounded snout.
- Fewer post-Weberian vertebrae: 37–40 (mode 38), versus 38–40 (mode 39) in *R. l. laticauda* from Belize.
- Reduced eyes.

**Differences from *R. guatemalensis* in Belize.**
- Pectoral-spine serrations occur only on the posterior surface, not on both surfaces.
- Maxillary barbels are shorter and do not reach the anal-fin origin.
- The dark lateral stripe found in *R. guatemalensis* is absent.

**Meristics and morphometrics (holotype in brackets; paratype ranges with means in parentheses).**
- Dorsal-fin rays: i, 6.
- Anal-fin rays: 14–18, most often 15.
- Pectoral-fin rays: i, 7 or i, 8.
- Pelvic-fin rays: i, 5.
- Gill rakers: mostly 2+7 to 2+8.
- Body depth: 4.7–6.8 in SL (mean 5.4).
- Head length: 3.7–4.4 in SL (mean 4.1).
- Pectoral-fin spine length: 11.2–17.1 in SL (mean 13.1).

**Eyes.** Eye diameter in *R. l. typhla* is measured as pit diameter. Eyes range from 41.9 to 73.2 in SL. A few individuals have eyes about half the normal size of *R. l. laticauda* (23.0 in SL), covered with skin. The authors note that the term "blind" is questionable, since residual retinal pigment may still detect light.

**Coloration.** In alcohol, the body is brown dorsally and pale cream to yellow ventrally, with a very dark brown head. In life, the body is brown to orange-brown. Colour varies from nearly white to dark brown across the population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Cave pools with a water temperature of 23 °C on both collection dates in 1973. The only other animal observed in the same water was a pigmentless, eyeless shrimp, which the authors use to argue that eye loss was not caused by injury.
- **Collecting:** Minnow seines, hook and line, and a trap, with SCUBA photography in the larger pool. Specimens were collected on 10 July 1972 (five), 21 April 1973 (twelve) and 10 July 1973 (three). Three live fish were kept at the University of Michigan.
- **Population:** Twenty specimens have been collected to date, representing several year-classes. All of them have reduced eyes.
- **Geology:** The cave lies in Cretaceous and Eocene limestone, adjacent to Paleozoic shales, sandstones and granite. The area has been above sea level since at least the Pliocene.
- **Biogeographic hypothesis:** The authors reject the glacial-isolation model for troglobites ([[Barr]] 1968; [[Poulson and White]] 1969). They propose that tropical fishes entered caves through [[Subterranean Drainage Capture]] (Mitchell 1969), and suggest *R. l. typhla* entered the cave this way.
- **Comparative eye-loss cases:** Avise and Selander (1972) showed that cave characids of *[[Astyanax]]* are conspecific with the surface *[[Astyanax Mexicanus]]*. Mayhew (1957) reported blind *Ictalurus melas* in East Okoboji Lake, Iowa, and Weisel and McLaury (1964) reported blind *Ictalurus nebulosus* from Dog Lake, Oregon.

## Key Findings

1. **A new subspecies is described.** *Rhamdia laticauda typhla* is established from the holotype (FMNH 71605) and sixteen paratypes from the Las Cuevas cave in Belize. The paper reports twenty specimens collected in total.

2. **Eye reduction is variable and extreme.** Degrees of reduction range from small pits with a black spot, through skin-covered pits, to larger skin-covered eyes. The reduction exceeds that described by Hubbs for the Yucatán *R. guatemalensis* subspecies.

3. **Morphological diagnosis is clear.** The subspecies differs from *R. l. laticauda* in pectoral-spine length (11.2–17.1 vs. 8.1–11.1 in SL), pectoral serration, head width and shape, snout shape, and vertebral count (37–40, mode 38, vs. 38–40, mode 39).

4. **Eye loss alone is not taxonomically sufficient.** The authors cite Avise and Selander (1972) and other examples to argue that taxonomic status must rest on additional characters.

5. **Short pectoral spines are linked to ossification.** The spine's ossified portion is about half the ray length, versus nearly complete ossification in *R. l. laticauda*. Because some epigean species also have short spines, the authors judge that this character is not clearly a cave artefact.

6. **Likely origin by stream capture.** The cave's geological history rules out glacial isolation. The authors favour colonisation through subterranean drainage capture of surface streams.

7. **Research and collecting context.** The work was supported by a Council of Academic Deans grant from Northern Illinois University. Belize's government granted collecting permission, and transport was provided by the Associated Universities for International Education, whose 1972 and 1973 tropical ecology courses supported the fieldwork.
