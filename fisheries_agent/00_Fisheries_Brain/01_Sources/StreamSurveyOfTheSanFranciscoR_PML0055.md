# Survey of the San Francisco River: First Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This first report from the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] documents a 1977 fish and aquatic habitat survey of the [[San Francisco River]] and its tributaries along the New Mexico–Arizona border. The survey was prepared by Richard Anderson (graduate student) and Paul Turner (Assistant Professor of Fishery Science), [[New Mexico State University]]. Twelve main-stem stations plus several tributary reaches were sampled using [[Electrofishing]], seines, dip nets, and [[Surber Sampling]]. The Sonora sucker dominated the upper reaches, while the river as a whole showed a strong imbalance: non-native fishes were common, and several native species were no longer found near the [[Frisco Hot Springs]].

## Background & Description

The survey was designed to assess fish abundance, species diversity, aquatic invertebrates, water chemistry, and potential fish habitat across the river system. Physical parameters recorded at each station included percent pool, percent shallow and deep riffle, bottom substrate, and stream length and width, measured with a 100-m surveyor's tape. Pools were defined as more than 0.8 m deep and at least 2 m wide; shallow riffles were under 20 cm deep, with gravel or rubble bottoms; deep riffles exceeded 20 cm.

**Sampling methods:**
- **Fish:** Electrofishing of roughly 100 m sections, supplemented by 1/4-inch mesh bag seines blocked at the downstream end of each habitat type, a 15-ft minnow seine, a 50-ft bass seine, and dip nets. Estimated sampling efficiency was 50–75% of all fish within each station.
- **Invertebrates:** Three Surber samples per station; relative abundance by biomass was determined.
- **Water chemistry:** Turbidity, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, and pH measured in the field; alkalinity, hardness, sulfate, and nitrate determined with a [[Hach DR Photocolorimeter]]; total dissolved solids and total salts analyzed by the [[New Mexico State University]] Soil and Water Testing Laboratory.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The river is a warm, arid-region system with pools and riffles of varying current and substrate. Key habitat features included:

- **Frisco Hot Springs (Station 1A):** A warm spring run (46.6 m long, 12 cm deep at the source) that discharged into the river. Water temperature was reported as 30°F above air temperature. The spring pool supported mosquitofish, which were otherwise found only at this station.
- **Mule Creek (Station 4 area):** A cooler, rubble-bottomed tributary with dense aquatic macrophytes and riparian cover, but with low fish diversity.
- **Harden Cienega:** A canyon stream with cottonwood and sycamore overstory, with the Sonora sucker, desert sucker, and roundtail chub as the main fish; it held only native species.
- **Threats noted:** Cattle use of the river bottom had reduced bank stability and increased erosion, and cattle were observed to be degrading riparian understory. Heavy vehicle traffic, particularly on Memorial Day weekend (33 vehicles counted on 30 May), was suspected of disrupting riffles, which are used as river crossings. Periodic floods were thought to explain the absence of mosquitofish in some areas.

Wildlife observations included three black hawks in Mule Creek canyon, a nesting pair of [[Bald Eagle]]-like raptors (the species identity is not fully legible in the source), a nallard hen in Harden Cienega, and bighorn sheep sightings (22 ewes with 11 kids; five rams).

## Key Findings

**Fish community**
- **Station 1** (about 1 km below the Frisco Hot Springs, 140 m below a large boulder): Sonora sucker was dominant. Of 27 large fish netted, 22 were Sonora suckers. The only smallmouth bass recorded in the entire survey (269 mm TL) was collected here, along with a common carp (404 mm), a channel catfish (274 mm), and a desert sucker.
- In the second pool at Station 1 (20 × 25 m, 0.8 m deep), 63 suckers were collected, 75% of them desert suckers. Estimated total biomass of these 63 suckers was 7.5 kg. In 100 m of stream, the estimated total catostomid biomass was 14 kg.
- **Station 1A** (spring and pool): 29 desert suckers, two Sonora suckers, 38 mosquitofish, 5 fathead minnows, 3 longfin dace, and a young channel catfish. Mosquitofish were found only at this station, raising the possibility of reintroducing [[Gila Topminnow]] to the river.
- **Station 1B:** Highest species diversity of any station, with five native species (Sonora sucker, desert sucker, loach minnow, longfin dace, speckled dace) and five introduced species (carp, smallmouth bass, channel catfish, red shiner, and mosquitofish). Natives were clearly dominant.
- **Station 2A:** Five channel catfish (35–642 mm) in a pool; a flathead catfish (618 mm) at the base of the riffles; only one loach minnow in 60 m² of riffle habitat.
- **Station 3:** 26 small catostomids, 2 young-of-the-year loach minnows, and young carp.
- **Station 4:** Twelve channel catfish (392–512 mm) and one flathead catfish (431 mm) in a 30 × 2.5 m pool, the highest channel catfish density recorded in the survey.
- **Station 5** (5 June 1977, 3.2 km from the Mexico–Arizona border): Six carp, two flathead catfish, one channel catfish, two Sonora suckers, and one desert sucker in a 64-m stretch.
- **Station 6:** Largest flathead catfish captured in the survey (791 mm).
- **Station 7** (Citizen Canyon): Several Sonora suckers (116–184 mm), 3 red shiners, and a flathead catfish; loach minnow were found in the riffle.
- **Station 10:** 18 channel catfish (mean TL 344 mm) from three pools.
- **Station 11:** The only station where large suckers and catfish were found together.
- **Station 12** (Blue River mouth): Sonora suckers (381–439 mm) and carp in the pools; channel catfish and desert suckers in the riffles.
- **Blue River:** Only two channel catfish (non-native) were collected, suggesting limited impact of non-natives on native fish in this tributary.

**Species of concern and absences**
- No documented collections of [[Spikedace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], or [[Gila Topminnow]] have been made in the San Francisco River near Frisco Hot Springs since 1950–1955 (based on museum collection records reviewed by the survey team). Roundtail chub, however, were abundant in Harden Cienega (over 250 counted in one 300-m stretch).
- The [[Spiny Softshell Turtle]] (*Trionyx spiniferus*) was collected from a pool at Station 9.

**Macroinvertebrates**
- Aquatic insects were sampled by Surber sampling, with taxa grouped into Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Odonata, Hemiptera, Trichoptera, Megaloptera, Lepidoptera, Coleoptera, Diptera, and Gastropoda. Relative abundance was rated abundant (A), common (C), or rare (R); detailed counts are provided in the source's Table 7.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Non-native fishes (channel catfish, flathead catfish, carp, red shiner) have heavily influenced the San Francisco River mainstem near Frisco Hot Springs, whereas the Blue River and Harden Cienega remained largely native.
- Habitat degradation from cattle grazing and vehicle traffic was identified as an ongoing threat to native fish, particularly to riffle-dependent species such as loach minnow and speckled dace.
- The report recommended that the rarity of mosquitofish make reintroduction of Gila topminnow a viable option, provided the cause of their absence in other reaches is understood.
