# Quail Creek Dam Failure: Gypsum Bedrock and Seepage Problems, Southwestern Utah (1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrology

## Executive Summary

This *Salt Lake Tribune* news report (January 2, 1989) covers the failure of the [[Quail Creek Dike]] at [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] in Washington County, Utah. The dike burst early on Sunday, January 1, 1989, releasing floodwater into the [[Virgin River]] and forcing about 1,500 people to flee. The article attributes a likely cause to [[Gypsum Dissolution]] in fractured bedrock beneath the dam and dike, and it documents years of warnings, seepage, and repairs before the collapse.

The document does not address fish, invertebrates, plankton, or water chemistry. Its aquatic content is limited to reservoir storage, river flooding, and water supply.

## Background & Description

- **Project:** The [[Quail Creek Project]] was completed on September 20, 1985, at a cost of $23.5 million. The article calls it the largest state-supported water project ever built in Utah. The purpose was drinking water for the growing [[St. George Area]] communities.
- **Site selection:** Federal engineers, including the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], considered a dam at the site in the 1960s but rejected it because of gypsum soils and site size. [[Ellis Armstrong]], a former Bureau commissioner, described the site this way. State officials later concluded Quail Creek was the only feasible location in the region. Engineer [[Ralph Rollins]] said, "There were no illusions in our minds that this was a super site."
- **Geology:** Gypsum in the highly fractured bedrock dissolves when wet, leaving open cracks and tunnels that let water escape. Similar gypsum-related dam problems were reported in Texas, Oklahoma, New Mexico, and at the [[Frog Hollow Dam]] (1982), east of Hurricane, Washington County.
- **Design and warnings:** Preliminary engineering reports acknowledged bedrock lenses and recommended mitigation. In 1983, state officials advised that seepage would increase and that a [[Secondary Grouting Program]] might be needed. [[Bruce Kaliser]], then an engineering geologist with the [[Utah Geological and Mineral Survey]], warned in an April 11, 1983 memo that gypsum beds at least one-quarter inch thick occurred beneath the dike and could cause differential settlement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article reports on environmental and human impacts rather than biological ones:

- **Flood release:** A wall of floodwater was released into the Virgin River. The river destroyed the River Road bridge and battered the Man-O'-War Road bridge, the only route into [[Bloomington, Utah]].
- **Storage loss:** About 25,000 acre-feet of the reservoir's 40,325 acre-feet capacity drained out by 1 p.m. Sunday, according to [[National Weather Service]] hydrologist Randall Julander.
- **Agricultural and livestock impacts:** Drainage flows from the dam had been enough to irrigate 60 acres of farmland before the failure. Floodwaters were expected to ruin farmland. Dead horses and cattle were seen near Washington Fields and along the river. Coffmann stated that most dead animals were carried into Arizona.
- **Water supply:** Some culinary water supplies were disrupted, and residents were asked to restrict water use. Other public health facilities were damaged.
- **Property damage:** Bloomington homes were flooded. Resident Sheri Hansen estimated structural damage at $40,000.

## Key Findings

1. **Gypsum-related seepage was a known risk.** Engineers, state officials, and the Utah Geological and Mineral Survey all identified gypsum dissolution as a foreseeable hazard before construction.
2. **Seepage began soon after filling.** The dam began leaking after it was filled in 1985. A wet spot on the dam was noted on December 5, 1985, and drain flows were higher than expected.
3. **Dike wetness triggered emergency action.** Seepage through rock cracks from the 1,980-foot-long dike increased until the state ordered the [[Washington County Water Conservancy District]] to stop filling the reservoir on May 9 (1986 per context of the repair timeline). Robert L. Morgan, then-director of the [[Utah Division of Water Rights]], warned that saturation of the dam toe could cause instability.
4. **Repairs were costly.** The district spent $1.2 million on repairs, including a large downstream buttress, sinkhole fill, and bedrock crack sealing. Rollins said the work was meant to increase the safety factor.
5. **Collapse followed renewed seepage.** Seepage around the dike increased sharply on Saturday night, leading to the collapse on Sunday, January 1, 1989.
6. **Official response.** Standard procedure after failure is a Board of Inquiry, but the article notes it was too early to assign blame. Project engineers had assured the district that the structures were safe.
7. **Human impact.** About 1,500 people fled. The article reports the flood's effects on homes, farms, bridges, and water supplies in the Virgin River area.
