# Niche Separation Within a Population of Freshwater Fishes in an Irrigation Drain Near the Salton Sea, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1979 study by Allan A. Schoenherr (Division of Life Sciences, Fullerton College), published in the *Bulletin of the Southern California Academy of Sciences*, examines how habitat diversity shapes fish distribution in the [[King Street Canal]], a freshwater irrigation drain on the northwest shore of the [[Salton Sea]]. A [[Thermal Gradient]] created by hot artesian well water and cool irrigation runoff, combined with differences in flow and depth, sorted five introduced species into nearly pure populations. In 1977, [[Flooding Disturbance]] and canal reconstruction destroyed most of this habitat diversity, and the species sorting was no longer evident by 1978. The paper also documents the only native fish in the area, the [[Desert Pupfish]], whose range near the Salton Sea appears to have shifted toward freshwater drains. The study reports that the introduced [[Redbelly Tilapia]] had colonized the canal by July 1978.

## Background & Description

Freshwater fishes in the drains and canals flowing into the Salton Sea (Riverside and Imperial Counties) are a mixture of aquarium escapees, bait fishes, introduced game fishes, introduced "weed eaters," and one native form. Table 1 of the source lists 26 species across nine families, including the Dorosomidae, Cyprinidae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Centrarchidae, Ictaluridae, Sciaenidae, Cichlidae, and Gobiidae. Most of these canals carry permanent irrigation runoff. The native [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) is the only native fish in the area.

The five focal species are:

- [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), native
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), introduced
- [[Liberty Molly]] (*Poecilia sphenops*), identified by dentition following Hubbs (1961); coloration resembles the aquarium "liberty molly" variety of *P. mexicana*
- [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*), introduced
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), introduced

The study aims to document niche separation among introduced fishes in an "unnatural habitat," to observe how habitat alteration affects niche stability, and to describe habitat and threats to the desert pupfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and methods.** Observations and collections were made on 3 March 1977, 21 May 1977, 18 March 1978, and 17 July 1978. Depth varied only slightly (5 to 50 cm), so temperature was the main gradient, alongside turbulence and velocity. Flow was generally shallow and linear, with riffles alternating with laminar flow and undercut banks at meanders. Fish were collected with a 1 m nylon seine (about 3 mm mesh). Individual counts were not made to avoid harming living fish, and most fish were released after identification. Dissolved oxygen was measured with a Beckman Fieldlab oxygen analyzer, and other water-quality parameters with a Bausch and Lomb Minispec 20 spectrophotometer.

**Thermal and physical gradient.** A hot artesian well fed the canal. Source temperature is reported as 42°C in the abstract but 46°C in the Materials and Methods section; the two figures are inconsistent in the source. Water cooled to about 39°C within roughly 50 m downflow. Cold irrigation runoff entered at about 22°C through a submerged pipe. Water temperature ranged from 24 to 27°C in the slower reaches and was about 22 to 26°C in other areas. The southern half of the stream stayed noticeably cooler for about 100 m.

**Dissolved oxygen and water quality.** On 3 March 1977, dissolved oxygen ranged from 7.2 mg/L at 37°C to 8.5 mg/L at 22°C. Total dissolved solids were about 1.1‰ and varied little between stations. Because all canal water was flowing and near oxygen saturation, the authors concluded that temperature and physical factors, not oxygen, drove species sorting.

**Flooding and reconstruction.** Heavy rains in late summer 1977 and winter 1978 flooded the canal to 3 m deep and 15 m wide, removing nearly all rooted vegetation. The channel was rebuilt the following February with bulldozers, scraped bottoms, and rock-and-wire-mesh stabilization. A small earthen dam impounded the hot water to about 1 m deep, and local residents used it for bathing, washing clothes, and swimming.

**Salton Sea level and pupfish habitat.** The Salton Sea had risen about 60 cm since September 1975 (Skjold, pers. comm.). Former pupfish pools along the north shore and near the mouths of the King Street and Avenue 81 canals were occupied by sailfin mollies, not pupfish. The author suggests rapid inundation, organic substrate, or mineral-ion concentrations may be responsible.

**Tilapia invasion.** [[Redbelly Tilapia]] (*Tilapia zillii*) had been introduced to the Salton Sea area in 1971 for weed control (St. Amant, pers. comm.). In the Garfield Street canal, pupfish were collected as recently as 7 December 1975 in water 50 cm deep among cattail roots, but that site is now dominated by tilapia. Tilapia are aggressive and feed on detritus, vegetation, and possibly eggs.

## Key Findings

1. **Strong habitat sorting in 1977.** On 3 March 1977, each species occupied a distinct habitat with little overlap:
   - Desert pupfish: shallow wash, up to 10 cm deep, 39°C (5–10 cm in Table 2), abundant
   - Mosquitofish: flowing water 25 cm deep, 32°C
   - Liberty molly: cold-water outflow pool 18 cm deep, 22°C, nearly the only species present
   - Sailfin molly: slow water up to 50 cm deep under undercut banks, 26°C
   - Red shiner: riffles 8–15 cm deep, 26°C
   
   Table 2 dissolved oxygen values were 6.8 (pupfish), 7.0 (mosquitofish), 8.5 (liberty molly and sailfin molly), and 8.7 mg/L (red shiner).

2. **Limited overlap.** Species overlapped very little, although some liberty mollies occurred with red shiners in faster water in upper reaches at about 27°C.

3. **Downstream pupfish.** About 2 km downflow, where the stream widened into a floodplain no more than 10 cm deep and up to 5 m wide, pupfish were abundant among cattail (*Typha*) and rush (*Juncus*) roots.

4. **Post-flood changes.** By 21 May 1977, the distribution had changed little. Following the 1977 floods and reconstruction, the niche separation was largely obliterated. On 18 March 1978, the surviving assemblage had two parts: cool flowing water (red shiners, liberty mollies, a few pupfish) and impounded hot water (pupfish, sailfin mollies, and mosquitofish, with temperature of 36°C in the upper 3 cm and dissolved oxygen of 7.2 mg/L). Sailfin mollies also dominated the lower canal and the Salton Sea shoreline.

5. **Tilapia in the pond (July 1978).** The dam had partially failed, lowering the pond to 60 cm. Two adult tilapia were seen, and a shaded shallow (about 1 m²) held a swarm of pupfish, sailfin mollies, mosquitofish, and juvenile tilapia at 32°C, with surface water at 40°C near the center.

6. **Thermal tolerance.** The hottest water in the canal (about 40°C) was within or near the upper thermal limits reported for these species. The author cites the upper limits for *Notropis lutrensis* (39.5°C; Brues, 1928), *Gambusia affinis* (39.5°C; Hubbs, 1959), and *Cyprinodon macularius* (44.6°C; Barlow, 1958; Lowe and Heath, 1969). The upper limit for *Poecilia sphenops* appears in the source, but the value is garbled by OCR and cannot be reliably reported.

7. **Pupfish habitat shift.** Desert pupfish were historically abundant in marshes, sloughs, and backwaters of the Colorado, Gila, and Sonoyta Rivers and in shoreline pools of the Salton Sea. The author suggests that the species' range near the Salton Sea has shifted so that freshwater drains and watercourses now form its primary habitat.

8. **Flood effects on other species.** The author cites the 1970 flood on the Salt River, Arizona, which apparently extirpated the sailfin molly there (Minckley, 1973). Flooding in the King Street canal also reduced pupfish and mollies, but the species persisted.

9. **Unresolved questions.** The author notes that the ability of the pupfish population to tolerate pollutants from bathing and washing, and encroachment by tilapia, remains unknown. Long-term monitoring was planned to test whether tilapia replace *Cyprinodon*.

10. **Study limits.** No individual counts were made, so the results are presence, depth, temperature, and habitat observations rather than quantitative abundance estimates. Sample sizes are not reported.
