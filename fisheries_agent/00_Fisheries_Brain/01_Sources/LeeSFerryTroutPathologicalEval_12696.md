# Lee's Ferry trout pathological evaluation 1990-1992

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #fish-pathology #parasitology

## Executive Summary

This report by the Arizona Game and Fish Department documents a multi-year pathological investigation (1990–1993) of [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) in the [[Lee's Ferry]] tailwater fishery of the Colorado River, Arizona. Beginning in 1990, biologists observed widespread malnutrition and near-total absence of body fat in wild trout, subsequently linked to heavy infection by the nematode [[Bulbodacnitis Ampullastoma]] in the pyloric caeca. A comparative 1992 survey showed modest improvement in body condition alongside apparent reduction of the bacterial pathogen [[Yersinia Ruckeri]] (ERM-1). A follow-up 1993 feeding trial identified the freshwater amphipod [[Gammarus Lacustris]] as a probable intermediate host for the nematode, advancing understanding of the parasite's life cycle in this regulated river system.

## Background & Description

- **Host species:** *Oncorhynchus mykiss* (rainbow trout), sampled across a wide size range (Table 2: 132 mm–495 mm fork length; weights 28 g–1051 g).
- **Body fat scoring (ASSUM system, [[Goede 1988]]):**
  - Score 0 = no visible body fat
  - Score 1 = <50% coverage
  - Score 2 = >50% coverage
  - Score 3 = 100% coverage
- **1990 survey (n=60):** 100% of fish scored 0 (zero body fat) — total absence of fat reserves across the sample.
- **1992 survey (n=42):** Improved but still poor condition — 18 fish scored 0 (~43%), 14 scored 1 (~33%), 10 scored 2 (~24%), 0 scored 3.
- **Relative weight (Wr):** Calculated per fish (Table 2); smaller hatchery-stocked fish (90–110 mm) exhibited higher relative weight than larger wild-spawned fish. Values ranged from ~43 to ~129 across sampled length classes, generally declining with increasing length.
- **Parasite identification:** Nematode preliminarily identified as *Bulbodacnitis ampullastoma* ([[Maggenti 1971]]), confirmed by original author; type locality Hot Creek State Fish Hatchery, Modoc County, California. Found in pyloric caeca and intestinal lumen.
- **Co-occurring parasites (1990):** Light–moderate *Gyrodactylus sp.* and *Ambiphrya sp.* on skin/gills.
- **Co-occurring parasites (1992):** Yellow grub *Clinostomum minimum* found in light concentrations in a few fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** [[Lee's Ferry]] is a cold, clear tailwater reach of the Colorado River immediately below [[Glen Canyon Dam]], supporting a put-and-grow rainbow trout fishery reliant on both hatchery stocking and wild reproduction.
- **Trophic interactions:** Trout diet includes the amphipod *Gammarus lacustris*, abundant in the tailwater benthic/epiphytic habitat on aquatic plants; this amphipod is implicated as a likely intermediate host in the nematode's life cycle, linking parasite transmission to the trout's natural foraging ecology.
- **Host-parasite dynamics:** Nematode burden correlates inversely with body fat — nematode-free fish (recently stocked, ~3 months prior) showed the highest fat scores (70–90%), while fish stocked earlier in the year (parasitized) showed markedly reduced fat storage.
- **Population comparison:** Hatchery-stocked fish (90–110 mm) had higher relative weight than wild-spawned fish of similar size class in 1992, suggesting differential nutritional/parasitic pressure between stocked and naturally recruited cohorts.
- **Temporal persistence:** Retrospective necropsies (1984) and field surveys (1989–1990, Lee's Ferry to Lake Mead) confirmed the nematode has been endemic in this reach for at least a decade prior to the formal 1990 investigation; anecdotal reports place "skinny fish" observations as far back as 1980.

## Key Findings

1. **Chronic nematode infection drives malnutrition:** All 60 fish sampled in the 1990 survey were infected with *Bulbodacnitis ampullastoma* and had zero measurable body fat, establishing the parasite as the primary suspect in the fishery's malnutrition problem.
2. **Partial improvement by 1992:** Nematode prevalence remained high (88% of sampled fish, moderate–heavy concentrations), but overall body fat scores improved slightly compared to 1990, and *Yersinia ruckeri* (ERM-1) infections appeared to be declining.
3. **Stocking timing affects fat reserves:** Fish stocked in August (nematode-free, ~3 months pre-sampling) had the highest fat scores (70–90%), whereas fish stocked earlier in May were already parasitized and showed greatly reduced fat.
4. **Hatchery vs. wild condition disparity:** Hatchery-stocked fish (90–110 mm) exhibited higher relative weight (Wr) than wild-spawned fish of comparable size, indicating differing growth/health trajectories between stocking cohorts.
5. **Laboratory evidence of parasite impact ([[Hiscox & Brocksen 1973]]):** Nematode-infected trout showed reduced growth rates even under normal feeding; the parasite may mechanically interfere with fat absorption or trigger physiological fat mobilization. Under starvation, parasitized trout died 60% faster than non-parasitized controls.
6. **Amphipod implicated as intermediate host (1993 trial):** In a controlled feeding study, 64 nematode-free hatchery trout fed live *Gammarus lacustris* from Lee's Ferry developed nematode infections — 11 of 59 examined trout (≈19%) were positive after 198–230 days, with 1–4 worms per infected fish, supporting the amphipod's role as an intermediate host (though transmission from infected fish back to clean amphipods was not confirmed).
7. **Coded wire tag retention:** As a secondary methodological finding, 93% (55/59) of tagged trout retained coded wire tags after 15 months, validating this marking method for long-term studies in this system.
8. **Management implication:** Because Arizona hatcheries tested negative for the nematode (1991–1992 surveys), the parasite appears endemic to the wild Lee's Ferry environment rather than introduced via hatchery stock; eliminating it from the wild fishery is expected to remain difficult, meaning some proportion of thin, nutritionally stressed fish will likely persist in the population.
