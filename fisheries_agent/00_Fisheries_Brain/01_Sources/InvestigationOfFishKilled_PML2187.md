# Fish Kill Investigation: San Francisco River Below Chase Creek, Clifton, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This memorandum from an [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] field investigator documents a major fish kill in the [[San Francisco River]] below [[Chase Creek]] near [[Clifton Arizona]]. The kill followed the release of acidic-looking, red-orange, mud-laden leaching water from [[Leaching Ponds]] operated by the [[Phelps Dodge Corporation]] mine. The release began late on June 28 and lasted about 24 hours. The report estimates heavy mortality across fish, insects, and other aquatic organisms over about four river miles. It also tracks recovery over the following ten days and projects long-term repopulation. The year is not explicitly stated in the scanned text; the projection of recovery "by the fall of 1970" suggests the event occurred in 1969.

## Background & Description

- **Reporting officer:** Howard McDonald, who was assigned to investigate after a telephone request from the Regional Office in Pima.
- **Initial notice:** Wildlife Manager Terrence Starner (Clifton) reported that Chase Creek flowed heavily with a thick red-orange water source beginning late Saturday night, June 28, and continuing through Sunday, June 29, for roughly 24 hours.
- **Survey area:** Chase Creek mouth, its confluence with the San Francisco River, a picnic area about four miles downstream, and about eight miles of river surveyed on horseback by Don Wingfield.
- **Methods:** Visual counts of fish, frogs, and tadpoles over 700 feet of stream; two one-foot-square bottom samples taken with a [[Surber Sampler]]; one-gallon water samples; and measurements of D.O., pH, CO2, and temperature.
- **Source identification:** Workers from the Phelps Dodge mine reported that a leak from two leaching ponds on a hill above a cement-lined canal released water. Mud washed into the canal, plugged three pumps, and burned out their motors. The main leaching pond wall broke, and the leachate entered Chase Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mortality and extent**
- Estimated fish mortality was given in the report, but the percentage is illegible in the scan.
- Estimated insect mortality was reported at a partly legible high percentage. Other aquatic organism mortality was reported near 100%.
- Dead fish, insect larvae, and tadpoles were found throughout the picnic area. A red residue, probably iron oxide, covered the banks and was about 2.5 inches deep and four feet wide below the confluence. None was seen above it.
- Dead dragonfly larvae covered the river surface and moss on the bottom.

**Species affected**
- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*): the most abundant fish counted, in multiple size classes (1–3 inches up to 15–20 inches). The report notes these were the main commercial-size loss.
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): counted in several size classes (approx. 9–14 inches).
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*): found in isolated pools.
- [[Leopard Frog]]s and tadpoles: large numbers of tadpoles were counted; only three live frogs were seen across both surveyed areas.
- Invertebrates: dead beetle larvae, mayfly larvae, bloodworms, stonefly larvae, and corixids (water boatmen).
- [[Flathead Catfish]]: reported by the Regional Office as found in the lower three miles, lying in shallow water below pools 3–5 feet deep.

**Chemistry and water quality**
- Water quality readings (D.O., pH, CO2, temperature) and laboratory analyses were taken but the water quality inventory forms and lab results are largely illegible in the scan.
- The lab analysis was performed at the Arizona State University Hydrology Building on July 3, 1969 (per the text).

**Source and cause**
- Two leaching ponds, roughly 100 x 100 feet and 1 to 4 feet deep, held a minimum of 30,000 cubic feet each at 3 feet deep. A total minimum of 448,800 gallons was released (as stated in the document).
- The mine manager could not disclose the chemical composition of the effluent or the exact release volume.
- The field engineer had closed valves, but could not stop the flow in time.

## Key Findings

1. **Mass mortality occurred over roughly four miles of river** after a 24-hour release of mine leaching water into Chase Creek and then the San Francisco River.
2. **Fish counts at the initial survey** were limited. The only living fish found were 125 dead minnows and about twice as many live ones in an isolated pool approximately 20 yards long, 3 feet wide, and 10 inches deep. No live fish were captured or counted in that pool.
3. **Bottom samples produced no living organisms** in the initial survey, though several dead beetle larvae, mayfly larvae, bloodworms, and stonefly larvae were found.
4. **Recovery was rapid for minnows.** Ten days later, two schools of about 20 to 24 Longfin Dace each were seen in a two-foot-deep section. The bottom was free of residue and the moss was green again.
5. **Recovery of invertebrates and catfish was predicted to be slow.** The author expected aquatic insects to repopulate by downstream drift after one hatch season, and fish to depend on upstream populations and migration. Re-establishing a catchable catfish population was projected to take a number of years (partly illegible; a span beginning at 3 years is visible).
6. **Mine remediation was recommended** by the mine manager, including a settling pond with baffles to trap mud before water reached the well pond.
7. **Economic cause:** Mud blocked pumps, burned out motors, and forced pump rebuilds. This effectively caused the release, because the pumps had been used to recycle leaching water.

**Overall conclusion (author's opinion):** By July 9, the stream had returned to near pre-release conditions except for the lack of aquatic life. Minnows had already moved back into the main channel, and repopulation of other species was expected from upstream sources and drift.
