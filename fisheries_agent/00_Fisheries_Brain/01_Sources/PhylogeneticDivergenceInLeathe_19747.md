# Phylogenetic Divergence in Leatherside Chub (Snyderichthys copei)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Johnson and Jordan examines intraspecific phylogenetic relationships in [[Leatherside Chub]] (*Snyderichthys copei*), a desert fish endemic to the [[Bonneville Basin]] and upper [[Snake River]] drainages of western North America. Using the complete mitochondrial [[Cytochrome B]] gene (1140 bp) from 30 individuals at 10 populations, the authors found two deeply divergent mtDNA clades separated geographically into northern and southern drainage basins. The northern clade was genetically closer to the outgroup [[Lepidomeda Mollispinus Mollispinus]] than to the southern clade, suggesting that the species taxonomy may need revision and that the two clades should be managed separately. Combining [[Maximum Parsimony]] and [[Maximum Likelihood]] with [[Statistical Parsimony]] and [[Nested Clade Analysis]] revealed that geographic fragmentation structures genetic variation, with one long-distance colonization event between southern drainages.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Leatherside chub (*Snyderichthys copei*), a small desert cyprinid. Its distribution has declined sharply in recent decades, threatening long-term survival (Wilson and Belk 1999, cited in the manuscript).
- **Study objectives:** (1) identify phylogenetic and geographic relationships among haplotypes; (2) infer processes behind genetic differentiation; (3) provide an evolutionary framework for conservation decisions.
- **Sampling:** Fish were collected from 10 sites in four drainage basins (Snake, Bear, Provo, and Sevier). Three fish were examined per site, for a total of 30 individuals. Historically the species may also have occupied the Weber and Salt Lake basins, but extensive sampling found no extant populations there.
- **Molecular methods:** DNA was extracted from ethanol-preserved muscle tissue. The cytochrome b gene was amplified by PCR and sequenced in both directions using external and internal primers. A control tube showed no contamination.
- **Analytical framework:** The authors argue that traditional tree-building methods often fail to resolve closely related haplotypes. [[Statistical Parsimony]] (Templeton et al. 1992), which builds haplotype networks, was therefore used alongside [[Nested Clade Analysis]] (Templeton 1998) to examine geographic associations. Analyses were run in PAUP* 4.0b2, PARSPROB 1.0, and GEODIS 2.0. Model selection favored a [[General Time-Reversible Model]] (X² = 4.84, P = 0.09).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Drainage structure:** Northern clade haplotypes occur only in the Snake and Bear river drainages. Southern clade haplotypes occur only in the Utah Lake ([[Spanish Fork River]], [[Main Creek]], [[Thistle Creek]]) and [[Sevier River]] drainages (San Pitch River, Salina Creek, East Fork Sevier River).
- **Historical hydrology:** Genetic structure is attributed mainly to geographic barriers created by the late Pleistocene recession of [[Lake Bonneville]]. Late Pleistocene water connections were used to estimate dispersal routes, including a plausible connection between the Bear and Snake rivers (Jarrett and Malde 1987).
- **Biogeographic puzzle:** The outgroup *L. m. mollispinus* is currently restricted to the Virgin River, a Colorado River tributary in extreme southern Utah. The northern clade lies far to the north in southern Idaho and Wyoming, and the more distantly related southern clade lies geographically between them. The authors note this disjunct pattern requires a new biogeographic hypothesis.
- **Human influence:** Leatherside chub was formerly used as bait fish (Sigler and Miller 1963). The authors suggest that anthropogenic translocation cannot be excluded as a cause of one long-distance colonization event.

## Key Findings

**Genetic divergence and clade structure**
- The complete cytochrome b gene (1140 bp) showed 120 observed mutations, all point mutations. Nine were at first-codon positions, one at a second-codon position, and 110 at third-codon positions. Ninety-one were parsimony informative.
- Substitutions were synonymous in all but nine cases.
- Mean base frequencies were A = 0.25, C = 0.29, G = 0.17, T = 0.29, with no significant difference among taxa (X² = 1.81, P = 1.0).
- Divergence between northern (Snake and Bear) and southern (Utah Lake and Sevier) clades ranged from **7.7% to 8.1%**.
- Within-clade divergence was **0 to 0.3%** in the north and **0 to 0.7%** in the south.
- The northern clade was more similar to the outgroup *L. m. mollispinus* (3.7%) than to the southern clade (8.0%).
- Traditional analyses (maximum parsimony and maximum likelihood) consistently recovered two strongly supported clades. The southern clade was supported by 68 unambiguous characters and the northern clade by 19. Within-clade resolution was poor.
- The two clades differed by 87 diagnostic nucleotide characters.

**Haplotype networks (statistical parsimony)**
- Fourteen haplotypes were identified: four in the northern clade and 10 in the southern clade.
- Haplotypes differing by up to 14 mutational steps could be connected at the 95% confidence level.
- In the northern network, Bear River haplotype B3 occupied the central position and had the highest root probability (0.75).
- In the southern network, haplotypes S1 (0.30) and S3 (0.30) and U3 (0.23) were the most common and widespread. S7 (0.18) also had a high value. All other root probabilities were below 0.04.

**Nested clade analysis (geographic structure)**
- Northern clade: a significant allopatric fragmentation pattern was found between Bear River and Snake River haplotypes among one-step clades (P = 0.04).
- Southern clade: a significant long-distance colonization event was identified between the Spanish Fork River (Utah Lake drainage) and Salina Creek (Sevier River drainage) among one-step clades nested in clade 2-2 (P = 0.03).
- Two-step clades showed past fragmentation across the entire cladogram (P < 0.01).
- Of seven nested tests, the null hypothesis of no geographic association was not rejected in four. The authors attribute most of these to panmixia within widespread southern nested clades, except in northern clade 1-2 (P = 0.10), where small sample size may account for the result.

**Species boundaries and taxonomy**
- The authors judge the clades to meet Davis and Nixon's (1992) criterion for species candidacy and to be phylogenetically distinct and show no recent gene flow for mtDNA (Templeton 1994 criteria 1 and 2).
- Differences in reproductive timing have been noted between populations in the two clades, which may support Templeton's third criterion, but this needs further work.
- The authors conclude that the species "may be comprised of two non-sister species," and that a broader taxonomic review including other plagopterin species is needed.
- Mitochondrial introgression or lineage-sorting differences could make the mtDNA gene tree discordant with the species tree. The authors call for nuclear loci analyses.

**Conservation implications**
- Failing to recognize the two clades could lead to biodiversity loss through introgression from translocations, which would erode adaptive potential and distinct evolutionary trajectories.
- The authors recommend that northern and southern clades **be managed as separate entities**, drawing a parallel with [[Tuatara]] (*Sphenodon*), where taxonomic confusion contributed to extinction of one taxon and near-extinction of another.

**Evolutionary history**
- Root probabilities were used to infer relative haplotype age. B3 is probably the ancestral northern haplotype, with the Snake River haplotype likely derived from a Bear River haplotype.
- In the southern clade, U3 is ancestral in the Utah Lake drainage, and S1 and S3 are ancestral in the Sevier River drainage. The authors suggest genetic diversity radiated from the Sevier River system into adjacent Utah Lake drainages.

**Methodological takeaways**
- Traditional phylogenetic methods combined with statistical parsimony resolved more than either method alone.
- Phylogenies can test accepted taxonomy and identify unique lineages within rare species.
- Statistical parsimony with nested clade analysis can reveal historical processes, here showing that vicariant fragmentation largely partitioned genetic diversity among stream drainages.
