# Fish Population Status and Evaluation in the Cibola High Levee Pond

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report, submitted by [[Paul C. Marsh]] of Arizona State University to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (Agreement No. 99-FG-30-00051; final version dated 30 September 2000), assesses the population status of two imperiled big-river fishes, [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], in the [[Cibola High Levee Pond]]. The pond is a predator-free, off-channel oxbow on the lower [[Colorado River]]. Both species survive, grow, mature, reproduce and recruit there, which makes the site a rare model for self-sustaining native fish habitat. A mark-recapture survey in late 1999 estimated about 3,039 razorback suckers (95% CI 1,947–4,863). Too few bonytail were recaptured to estimate abundance.

## Background & Description

The Cibola High Levee Pond (HLP) is a cut-off oxbow created during a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] program to correct Colorado River channel deficiencies through dredging and levee construction (1964–1970). It is hydraulically connected to the river through a permeable low levee and lies on lands administered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] within the [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]].

Key physical features:
- Nominal surface area of 2.0 ha (4.9 ac) and volume of 25,500 m³ (20.7 ac-ft), both varying with river elevation.
- Maximum depth about 3 m (10 ft); substrate ranges from armored rock and boulders to silts and organic sediments.
- Emergent vegetation dominated by [[Cattail]] (*Typha domingensis*) in shallow northern margins; submergent plants include [[Sago Pondweed]], [[Coontail]] and spiny naiad.
- Shoreline cover is minimal.

The limnology resembles other lower Colorado River backwaters. Surface temperatures are about 5–12 °C in winter and 30–33 °C in summer. Dissolved oxygen is generally well oxygenated (≥5 mg/L) year-round but declines in deeper water during warm months. Conductivity is about 1,500–1,800 µS/cm and pH ranges from the mid-7s to mid-8s. Conditions are most severe in deep water in mid-to-late summer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report frames off-channel habitats free of [[Non-Native Fishes]] as a key management option. Adult native fish can survive in most waters, but they reproduce and recruit only where predatory exotics are absent.

Non-native species are present at low levels. Threadfin shad, [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*) and [[American Bullfrog]] were recorded in winter 1999. A 1993 [[Rotenone]] treatment removed non-native fishes, and few have been found since.

Stocking history (1993–1995) included 58,383 juvenile bonytail and 14,000 juvenile razorback suckers from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico. Records from the hatchery, the FWS Parker office and LaBarbara (1999) are not fully consistent.

Harvest for stocking into [[Lake Havasu]] removed large individuals on 15 occasions between December 1993 and September 1996. The report notes 735 bonytail and 864 razorback suckers were captured in the harvest records, with 99 bonytail and 558 razorback suckers PIT-tagged and stocked elsewhere. The harvest tables list 1,460 razorback suckers (1,082 in the 1995–96 series, plus 378 in 1999) and 838 bonytail in total for the monitoring period.

Other sites where these species have succeeded include [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] (razorback sucker recruitment), backwaters at Yuma Cove, [[Achii-Hanyo Native Fish Rearing Ponds]] and Dexter NFH hatchery ponds. The authors suggest that predator-free status is a prerequisite for recruitment.

## Key Findings

- **Recruitment confirmed:** Bonytail and razorback sucker both reproduced and recruited in situ. Bonytail cohorts were inferred for 1996, 1998 and 1999, based on fish near or below 100 mm TL. Bonytail were also observed spawning in June 1999 over an inundated gravel parking area, in less than one meter of water.
- **Razorback sucker population estimate:** 3,039 individuals (95% CI 1,947–4,863) at the end of 1999, derived from the [[Peterson Estimator]] using [[Capture-Recapture]] data from November and December 1999 (Table 2: November marked 340, captured 41, recaptured 24 in one sample series; December captured 333, recaptured 42 and 0 for bonytail).
- **Bonytail estimate not calculated:** Zero recaptures in December prevented an estimate. A minimum count of 66 fish was judged unrealistic.
- **Density:** The Cibola HLP holds about 1,500 razorback suckers per hectare (600 per acre) across its 2 ha.
- **Carrying capacity:** Unknown. The site may support about 5,000 fish per hectare while still allowing removal of the largest individuals. Stability requires a dynamic equilibrium, which may take many generations.
- **Suitability factors:** Predator-free status, year-round non-lethal temperature and dissolved oxygen, interconnection with the Colorado River, wind-driven mixing, adequate depth and volume, and isolation from colonization.
- **Management role:** The authors propose the Cibola HLP as a template for identifying additional habitats. It could help meet requirements of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion]] (FWS 1997) on lower Colorado River operations.
- **Caveat:** Bonytail abundance and density are assumed to be similar to razorback sucker values, but additional data are needed to confirm this assumption.
