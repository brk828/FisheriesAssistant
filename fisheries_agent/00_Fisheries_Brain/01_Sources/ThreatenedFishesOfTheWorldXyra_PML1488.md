## Background & Description

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*, Abbott 1860; Catostomidae)**
- Common names: Razorback Sucker, Humpback Sucker (English), *matalote jorobado* (Spanish).
- Conservation status: Endangered (U.S.A.).
- Morphology: elongate body with a sharp-edged keel behind the head that varies in development. Adults are larger, reaching about 5–6 kg.
- Coloration: dark olive or grey above and lighter (yellow) below. Breeding males are black or dark brown dorsally, with an orange-to-rosy lateral band, and yellow on the belly.
- Genus: monotypic and endemic to the Colorado River basin.

**Madtoms (*Noturus*), focus on *Noturus flavater***
- Madtoms are nocturnal and hide under rocks during the day, which reduces exposure to visually oriented predators.
- They have sharp pectoral fin spines with venom glands at their bases, which provides additional protection.
- Saddle patterns differ among species. *N. flavater* has a regular saddle pattern, whereas *N. miurus* and *N. flavipinnis* have more uneven saddles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and abundance (Razorback Sucker)**
- Historically found throughout the larger streams of the Colorado River basin, from Sonora and Baja California Norte (Mexico) northward.
- Wild populations are now concentrated in [[Lake Mohave]] and the [[Green River]]. Scattered individuals occur elsewhere in the Colorado River system, in major tributaries and in reservoirs.
- Restored populations exist in the [[Gila River Basin]] (Arizona).
- Historically common or abundant in Lake Mohave and the Green River. The current total is estimated at approximately 60,000 individuals (the figure is stated without a source or method in the text).

**Life history and ecology**
- Adults are omnivorous and mainly herbivorous, though adults also consume some benthic organisms.
- The species is long-lived, reaching more than 40 years.
- Juveniles are preyed upon by a suite of [[Non-Native Fishes]] (introduced species).
- Spawning occurs in shallow water near the bottom along shorelines. A single female is accompanied by one or more males. Spawning sites are depressions of roughly 0.5 m in diameter.
- Recruitment failure is a major constraint on natural reproduction and is a research priority.

**Threats**
- Hybridization, competition and predation by non-native fishes.
- Habitat modification and loss of natural flow regimes.
- Recruitment failure, which limits natural population replacement.

## Key Findings

1. **Status:** The Razorback Sucker is Endangered in the U.S.A. Remaining wild populations are small and scattered. Without intervention, they are likely to disappear, and the species would be reduced to hatchery stocks kept as zoological curiosities.
2. **Recovery priorities:** The text recommends a combined strategy that includes:
   - Reintroduction within historic range, guided by genetic characterization, life-history requirements and habitat enhancement.
   - Designation of critical habitat and habitat reclamation.
   - Control of non-native fishes through removal or manipulation.
   - Hatchery production, with attention to hybridization and genetic modification.
   - Population monitoring, a central information repository and continued research on recruitment failure, movements and genetic markers.
3. **Recovery plan:** The recovery effort is described in Minckley et al. (1991).
4. **Madtom color patterns:** Madtoms can be less cryptic than other saddled species because of their venomous pectoral spines and nocturnal behavior. Color patterns and their degree of pattern regularity in madtoms deserve further study.
