# On the dorsal light response in a mayfly nymph

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ephemeroptera #Baetidae #freshwater-invertebrates

> **Note:** This source concerns a freshwater aquatic insect ([[Baetis Harrisoni]], a mayfly nymph), not a fish species. The note is structured according to the requested format but reframed around invertebrate behavioral ecology, as the paper covers orientation behavior, drift, and photoreception rather than ichthyological data.

## Executive Summary

This study by Hughes (1966) investigates the **dorsal light response (DLR)** in nymphs of the baetid mayfly *[[Baetis Harrisoni]]*, a behavior that maintains dorso-ventral body orientation relative to light direction. The paper demonstrates experimentally that early, middle, and penultimate instar nymphs exhibit a strong, statistically significant dorsal light response, while the **ultimate (final) nymphal instar** shows a markedly reduced response. This ontogenetic shift is linked to observed opaqueness of the cuticle over the ocelli prior to sub-imago emergence, suggesting the ocelli initiate the response while compound eyes maintain it tropotactically. The findings offer a mechanistic explanation for the "somersaulting" swimming behavior previously observed in this species and provide insight into the phenomenon of **invertebrate drift** in running-water ecosystems, particularly its increased incidence at night.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** *Baetis harrisoni* Barnard (Order Ephemeroptera, Family [[Baetidae]])
- **Life stage examined:** Aquatic nymph, across multiple instars (approx. 4th–8th instar "early stages," 12th–16th instar "middle stages," penultimate instar, and ultimate instar)
- **Key sensory structures implicated:**
  - **Ocelli** — hypothesized to initiate the dorsal light response; cuticle covering ocelli becomes opaque/semi-transparent just prior to sub-imago emergence in the ultimate instar, correlating with loss of the behavioral response.
  - **Compound eyes** — hypothesized to maintain the response tropotactically (via balanced bilateral stimulation) once initiated.
  - **Absence of gills**: Unlike other mayflies, Baetidae lack gill movements to generate respiratory currents over the integument; this is proposed to explain the alternative upward-swimming behavior used to ventilate the body surface.
- **Behavioral motor pattern:** "Somersaulting" — a stereotyped tumbling behavior executed within a horizontal light beam (illustrated diagrammatically in the original paper), varying consistently in intensity between individuals but stable within an individual across trials.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Benthic nymphs inhabiting substrates in slow-flowing freshwater stream currents.
- **Behavioral ecology:**
  - In the absence of strong current, nymphs periodically release their hold on the substrate and swim upward toward the water surface, halting and sinking back down at variable heights — a behavior proposed to generate a respiratory current over the body surface in the absence of gill movement.
  - Nymphs may briefly invert (turn dorsal-side down) during this activity, sometimes failing to reorient efficiently before resettling on the substrate.
  - This imperfect reorientation, exacerbated by absence of overhead light or a reflective (white) substrate, is proposed as a proximate behavioral mechanism underlying **invertebrate drift** in lotic systems.
- **Substrate interactions:** Experiment 2 showed that substrate color strongly affects disorientation — nymphs over a **white substrate** flipped dorsal-side-up far more often (59%, 237/400 instances) than nymphs over a **black substrate** (11%, 45/400 instances), indicating light reflection from below can override/compete with the normal dorsal light stimulus from above.
- **Diel activity linkage:** Cross-referenced with [[Elliott 1965]] field/lab data showing far greater nymph drift at night (618 individuals) than during the day (73 individuals) for *Baetis* spp., consistent with known nocturnal activity increases in mayfly nymphs ([[Harker 1953]]) and the absence of orienting overhead light at night.

## Key Findings

1. **Ontogenetic decline in dorsal light response:** Statistical comparison (Student's t-test) of nymphs disequilibrated under alternating top vs. bottom lighting showed highly significant differences for early (t=18.8), middle (t=24.8), and penultimate (t=22.9) instars, but a much weaker (though still significant) effect in the ultimate instar (t=4.6, P<0.001).
2. **Somersaulting behavior ceases at final instar:** The "somersault" behavior observed in a light beam is frequent in penultimate and earlier instars but virtually absent in the ultimate nymphal instar, correlating with the loss of the strong dorsal light response.
3. **Substrate reflectivity modulates orientation:** A white substrate under uniform overhead lighting caused significantly more nymphs to invert (dorsal-surface-up) than a black substrate, demonstrating that reflected light can compete with/disrupt the dorsal light response even when a strong light source is present from above.
4. **Proposed sensory mechanism:** The dorsal light response is hypothesized to be **initiated by the ocelli** and **maintained tropotactically by the compound eyes**, analogous to mechanisms described in *[[Triops Cancriformis]]* (Seifert, 1930). Ocellar cuticle opacity just before sub-imago emergence is proposed to explain the abrupt loss of response in the ultimate instar, contrasting with the gradual, endocrine-mediated shift documented in hawk moth caterpillars ([[De Ruiter 1962]]).
5. **Ecological significance for drift:** The combination of disorientation from lack of overhead light and inefficient substrate "landing" after upward swims is proposed as a proximate mechanism contributing to increased nocturnal drift in running-water mayfly populations, corroborating field and laboratory findings of substantially higher night-time drift rates.
6. **Comparative context:** The dorsal light response in *Baetis harrisoni* is situated among a broader taxonomic survey of light-orientation behaviors in aquatic invertebrates, including [[Palaemon Xiphias]], [[Argulus Foliaceus]], [[Daphnia]], [[Triops]], [[Hirudo Medicinalis]], [[Acilius]], [[Dytiscus]], and [[Corixa]], as well as contrasting ventral light reflexes in [[Chirocephalus]], [[Artemia]], and [[Notonecta]].
