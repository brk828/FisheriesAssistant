# Imperial Native Fish Habitat Reconstruction: Design Workshop Final Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This design workshop report, prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Lower Colorado Region (July 11, 2005), presents a redesign of the DU2 Ponds at the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] (INWR). The original pond complex was built as a native fish sanctuary for [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) but failed because of [[Hypoxia]] (low dissolved oxygen), high water temperatures, and invasion by [[Non-Native Fish]], especially [[Warmouth]] (*Lepomis gulosus*). The proposed design expands the site from four ponds to six, adds roughly 40–50 acres of backwater habitat, and borrows features from the successful [[Cibola High Levee Pond]], including deep thermal refuge, [[Hummocks]], [[Gravel Spawning Beds]], and [[Fish Collection Kettles]]. The estimated construction cost is $3,106,000, excluding contingencies, design, contracting, project management, and permitting. The report explicitly states that this estimate should not be used for budgeting or construction.

## Background & Description

**Original site.** The "DU2 Ponds" were developed in 2000 as a partnership among [[Ducks Unlimited]], INWR, and Reclamation. They were meant to serve waterfowl resting and nesting, riparian values, and native fish sanctuary functions. The site covers 96 acres and originally held four cells with one pond each, totaling about 35 acres of water. Individual ponds ranged from 4 to 14 acres, with a maximum depth of 6 feet. Cell 2 is treated as a single pond in this report, giving four ponds in total.

**Management history.**
- Site construction was completed in autumn 2002. The site was treated with piscicide ([[Rotenone]]) between autumn 2002 and December 2002 (as summarized in the document's opening text).
- Razorback Sucker were first stocked in March 2003.
- Despite multiple rotenone treatments, Warmouth were found in small numbers and then increased.
- In spring and summer 2003 the stocked sucker population declined to an estimated level (the exact figure is garbled in the source scan). Non-native Warmouth increased to an estimated 2,000 individuals.
- The ponds were renovated again and Razorback Sucker were restocked in Pond 1 in 2004.
- A timer-controlled well ran water between midnight and 6 a.m. in summer. This kept fish alive in Pond 1 but had no effect on the other four ponds, where fish kills occurred.

**Riparian component.** The planned native riparian forest on about 50 acres was largely unsuccessful. Cottonwood, willow, and mesquite plantings in the terraces died, which the team attributed to [[Soil Salinity]]. The site is now dominated by [[Salt Cedar]], mesquite, and willows, with some areas bare.

**Pond physical setting.** Each pond has a single northern inflow and a single southern outflow, with gravity-driven outlets submerged about 3 feet and controlled by stop-log towers. Inlet towers have wire screens that exclude fish larger than 1 inch total length. Shorelines are dominated by cattails, common reed, and bulrush. The bottom is sandy-silt, and submerged macrophytes (*Najas*, *Myriophyllum*, and *Potamogeton*) dominate open water in spring and summer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality problems.** The report identifies two main causes of failure:
1. **Hypoxia and thermal stress.** Pond water becomes hot and low in oxygen. The report suggests the cause is site design, which allows only one inflow and one outflow per pond and cannot maintain the intended water surface elevation.
2. **Non-native fish invasion.** Current design does not isolate ponds, so invasions spread through the system.

**Hydrologic constraints.** The ponds cannot be held at their intended surface elevation because of a hydrologic connection with adjacent farm fields. Filling them as designed could kill trees and plants in the fields. Holding the ponds lower encourages emergent vegetation and reduces dissolved oxygen. The shallow ponds also expose fish to avian predators common in the area.

**Water source decision.** A semi-permeable rock connection to the river, as used at High Levee, was rejected. Groundwater or pumped river water were the only feasible options. [[Groundwater Delivery]] was chosen because it has the lowest risk of introducing non-native fish, eggs, and larvae. The alluvial substrate may also filter [[Selenium]] from Colorado River water. Passive subsurface flow was ruled out because there is not enough head gradient.

**Soil salinity.** Electrical conductivity (EC) in soil samples from salt-affected areas ranged from 29 to 40 mmhos/cm. Few cottonwoods or willows survive in soils above 3 mmhos/cm. Earlier rehabilitation, including leaching and sulfuric acid amendments, produced little improvement. A study by Anderson et al. (2004) on about 700 acres north of Yuma reached a similar conclusion.

**Geology and groundwater.** Groundwater did not intrude during 1997–98 construction, and the local hydrology is poorly understood. Whether dewatering will be needed is unknown. The report recommends drilling test wells to determine groundwater depth, clay lens presence, salt movement, and connection to the river.

## Key Findings

### Design Goals (prioritized)
- **Highest priority:** Reconstruct ponds as backwaters with the physical, chemical, and biological conditions needed for healthy native Lower Colorado River fishes.
- **Medium priority:** Expand surface area and/or marsh habitat within the 96-acre site. Design features that are easily maintained and have a target life of at least 50 years.
- **Lower priority:** Increase knowledge of managed habitat for Lower Colorado River fishes.

### Pond Configuration
The new design increases the total number of ponds from four to six, adding about 40–50 acres. Pond areas and layouts are summarized below.

| Pond | Area (acres) | Max depth | Key features |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8.9 | 12 ft | Inlet west/north, outlet east/south; hummock at south end |
| 2 | 13.1 | 12 ft | Inlet west/south, outlet east/north; hummock at south end |
| 3 | 14.3 | 12 ft | Two hummocks, one near inlet and one mid-pond |
| 4 | 10.2 | 18 ft | Based on High Levee concepts; two west-side hummocks |
| 5 | 17.2 | 12 ft | No hummocks; 70% of area at 5–10 ft |
| 6 | 9.2 | 14 ft | Experimental; hummocks as main shade structures, oriented East-West |

The six ponds total about 72.9 acres of water, computed from the individual pond areas given in the report. Pond sizes range from roughly 9 to 17 acres, as stated in the report.

Depth targets for most ponds are 20% under 5 ft, 60% between 5 and 10 ft, and 20% between 10 and 12 ft. Pond 4 differs (20% under 5 ft, 30% at 5–10 ft, 50% at 10–16+ ft, maximum 18 ft). Pond 5 differs (10% under 5 ft, 70% at 5–10 ft, 20% at 10–12 ft). Pond 6 differs (5% under 5 ft, 5% at 5–10 ft, 90% at 10–12 ft, maximum 14 ft). Ponds 1–3 have spawning beds covering 5% of pond area in water under 10 ft, with substrate of 1–3 inches.

### Water Delivery and Infrastructure
- **Groundwater supply:** An additional well will supply the larger open-water area. The existing wells run continuously at 75 HP for two wells to keep dissolved oxygen and temperature within limits.
- **Hydrologic isolation:** Each pond gets its own inflow and outflow, allowing separate water management and isolation if non-native fish appear.
- **Drainage:** An integrated drainage system separates pond and farm field management and prevents pond water from backing into fields.
- **Alternative supply:** An existing filtered pump could deliver larger volumes from the inlet canal, using fish screens to exclude non-native fish, eggs, and larvae. This is noted as an option to be assessed at final design.
- **Inflow placement:** Inflow pipes will discharge in water deeper than 10 ft. A pressurized aerated injection system will increase circulation and oxygen. Cooler, oxygenated groundwater will form a thermal refuge at depth.

### Habitat Features
- **[[Hummocks]]:** Planting beds for emergent vegetation (California bulrush, *Schoenoplectus californicus*). The design calls for 18 hummocks, each about 20 ft wide and 100 ft long, with tops within 12 inches of the water surface. They provide shade, cover, foraging habitat, and invertebrate production, and may benefit [[Western Least Bittern]] and [[Yuma Clapper Rail]].
- **[[Gravel Spawning Beds]]:** Lined with plastic and covered with 1–3 inch gravel, at depths of 2–6 ft. Spawning beds cover about 5% of each pond.
- **Steep slopes and riprap:** Steep banks limit emergent vegetation encroachment. Riprap stabilizes slopes and provides escape cover for native fish, as observed at High Levee.
- **Boat ramps and staging areas:** One per pond, near outflows, kettles, and drainage. Ramps are gravel-covered to inhibit vegetation.
- **[[Fish Collection Kettles]]:** Located in the deepest areas near outflow drain pipes to concentrate fish during draining, facilitating salvage and targeted chemical treatment of non-native fish.

### Monitoring Program
- **Water quality:** At minimum, dissolved oxygen, pH, specific conductivity, temperature, depth, and oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) should be measured with a multiprobe logger. Turbidity should be measured with a Secchi disc or nephelometric instrument. Measurements should be taken at three permanent sites per pond (inflow, mid-lake, outlet/deepest point), with vertical profiles at 0.5 or 1 m intervals. Sampling is quarterly in autumn, winter, and spring, and at least twice a month during the summer period when temperatures exceed 30°C.
- **Fish populations:** Larval fish collection at appropriate times, summer electrofishing or netting surveys of about one hour per pond, and at least one cool-season survey to assess health and population size. Surveys also document non-native species that could trigger renovation. Renovation uses draining and salvage, with [[Rotenone]] in kettles or drying out where needed.

### Excavated Material and Vegetation Management
- **Excavated soil:** Material is likely high in salinity. Rather than moving it off refuge (which would greatly increase costs) or spreading it on farm units (which would reduce their wildlife value), the team proposes using it for new roads, berms, road maintenance, and a fire break along the western edge. Some will be used to build hummocks and spawning beds.
- **Vegetation clearing:** Salt cedar must be removed. The team proposes root plowing, chipping with the Bureau of Land Management's chipper, and hauling material off-site, because burning is an option but not preferred, and spreading chips nearby is not desirable.
- **Materials sourcing:** Riprap and gravel will come from the Laguna East sources.

### Environmental Compliance
Compliance will be handled by INWR. It covers the National Environmental Policy Act, the Archaeological Resources Protection Act, Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act, and Sections 401 and 404 of the Clean Water Act. Reclamation will supply a project description and supporting materials.

### Cost Estimates and Operations
- **Construction:** The total estimate is $3,106,000, including mobilization. It excludes unlisted items, contingencies, design, contracting, project management, and permitting. The report notes that costs will be refined at final design.
- **Operations and maintenance (O&M):** The O&M table lists items such as pump power, pump and motor replacement (about $90,000 every 25 years), annual pump upkeep, roads and berm maintenance, spare parts (valves, pipe, sampling equipment), instrumentation (a Hydrolab at about $3,000), renovation (rotenone at $100/gal), and boat replacement (about $2,500 every 15 years). Staff estimates range from 0.5 to 1 person-year, depending on the period and monitoring intensity.
- **Contact:** Questions should go to Nathan Lenon, Biologist/Project Manager, [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Lower Colorado Region, Multi-Species Conservation Program Office.

### Overall Conclusion
The report concludes that the redesigned DU2 complex, built on High Levee principles but supplied by groundwater, can overcome the hypoxia, temperature, and non-native fish problems that caused earlier failures. Its success depends on hydrologic isolation of ponds, adequate oxygenation and thermal refuge, and sustained monitoring and renovation. Several design questions remain open until test-well data on groundwater and geology are available.
