# Ecological Factors Affecting Distribution and Abundance of the Centrarchids of the Recent Delta of the Mississippi River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 Louisiana State University M.S. thesis by [[Dudley Clay Carver]] examines eight centrarchid (sunfish family) species on [[Delta National Wildlife Refuge]], a Gulf of Mexico estuarine delta system in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana. Fieldwork ran from August 1963 to January 1965. The study combined multiple gear types, standing crop estimates, scale-based age and growth analysis, condition factors and limnological measurements. The author concludes that [[Salinity]] was the major factor limiting centrarchid distribution, with [[Pond Depth]] and [[Turbidity]] as secondary factors. Growth was fast in the first year, maximum sizes were smaller than in northern populations, and fish condition was excellent.

## Background & Description

The study was a portion of a larger investigation by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife and the [[Louisiana Cooperative Fishery Unit]], motivated by expected changes in flow, water quality and fish fauna in the delta. The eight species studied were:

- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*)
- [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*)
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- [[Redear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis microlophus*)
- [[Warmouth]] (*Chaenobryttus gulosus*)
- [[Spotted Sunfish]] (*Lepomis punctatus*)
- [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] (*Lepomis humilis*)

The stated objectives were to determine age and growth of five species (largemouth bass, bluegill, black crappie, warmouth and redear), to assess effects of salinity, turbidity and oxygen on distribution and abundance, and to determine condition factors.

**Study area and methods (summary):**
- The refuge covers 48,799.1 acres of deltaic marsh, shallow ponds, passes, bayous and canals. Sediments are Recent in age, and the area was shaped by [[Cubits Gap]], a crevasse through the Mississippi natural levee, which feeds the four major passes (Main, Octave, Brant Bayou, Raphael).
- Gear included rotenone, gill and trammel nets, bag and minnow seines, an electric shocker and an otter trawl. Electric shocking was limited to water under 0.20 ppt salinity.
- Standing crop was estimated with [[Block-Off Net]] and rotenone samples (two 1/4-acre and twenty-six 1/2-acre samples, July 1964 to January 1965). Earlier 1/4-acre wire-enclosure samples were abandoned due to bias and were excluded.
- Ages were estimated from scales using the direct proportion (Frazer) back-calculation, supplemented by length-frequency histograms.
- Water chemistry was measured for temperature, salinity (conductivity-based after 1963), dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, total alkalinity, pH (from summer 1964) and turbidity via Secchi disk.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and salinity.** The lower Mississippi is in flood for about eight months a year, so salinities are generally low. Dense saltwater enters only the deep navigable South and Southwest Passes at low river discharge, and the ponds are exposed to daily diurnal tides of roughly 14–16 inches at the Gulf end. Access canals cut through levees for oil operations are plugged by oil companies, which limits saltwater intrusion into ponds. [[Hurricane Hilda]] (October 1964) raised salinity north of Main Pass to 43 per cent sea water in some areas.

**Vegetation.** Roseau cane (*Phragmites communis*) dominates south of Main Pass, with black willow and marsh elder on pass banks. Submerged plants such as coontail and water milfoil clear the water in some ponds. Exotic [[Alligator Weed]] and [[Water Hyacinth]] were present, mainly at pond edges. North of Main Pass the vegetation is oyster grass (*Spartina alterniflora*), and black willow is absent.

**Climate.** The site is subtropical. In 1963 the Quarantine Station air temperature averaged 67.6°F and precipitation totaled 47.9 inches. In 1964 the average was 68.9°F with 62.12 inches of precipitation. Killing frosts seldom occur, averaging 350 frost-free days.

**Water chemistry.** Pond water temperatures ranged roughly from 11°C in winter to 34°C in summer. Dissolved oxygen was near saturation in most samples, with a low of 2.0 ppm in dense vegetation, where surrounding open water held 4.0–6.0 ppm. Free carbon dioxide ranged from 0.0 to 15.0 ppm. Total alkalinity was 111–191 ppm in the fresher Gondle Pond and 105–145 ppm in saline-influenced Sabot Pond. pH was 8.0–8.6 north of Main Pass and 7.2–8.4 in fresh ponds to the south.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and salinity**
- Salinity was the main factor limiting centrarchid distribution. Salinity north of Main Pass reached 43 per cent sea water in October 1964, and most centrarchids were excluded there except for spotted sunfish and some black crappie.
- Largemouth bass were concentrated in Gondle, Horseshoe and Horseduck ponds, the deepest on the refuge (about 3 to 5 feet). They occurred in salinities from 0.04 to 4.10 ppt.
- Spotted sunfish had the most diversified distribution of any centrarchid and showed no clear preference for salinity, depth or turbidity.
- Warmouth tolerated more turbid water than the other centrarchids.
- Orangespotted sunfish were rare (23 individuals total) and were never collected above 0.74 ppt salinity.
- White crappie were collected on six occasions (highest salinity 1.32 ppt) and were the only species not recorded in previous Louisiana estuarine surveys.

**Abundance (standing crop)**
- Average centrarchid standing crop was 31.13 lb/acre in the south area (ponds south of Octave Pass), 14.29 lb/acre between Octave and Main passes, and 2.12 lb/acre north of Main Pass.
- Gondle Pond recorded the highest value at 69.80 lb/acre.
- Redear sunfish, warmouth, spotted sunfish, black crappie and largemouth bass were the most abundant centrarchids, in that order.
- A one-way analysis of variance gave F = 4.64 (3 and 14 df), significant at the 0.05 level, for centrarchid standing crop among Gondle, Horseshoe, Horseduck and Sabot ponds.
- Centrarchids composed 45.05% (Gondle), 33.92% (Horseshoe) and 52.54% (Horseduck) of total fish poundage in those ponds, compared with 15.88% in comparison alluvial flood-plain lakes (Lambou and Geagan, 1961).

**Age and growth**
- Largemouth bass: scales showed three age groups; length-frequency data suggested four. Average calculated total lengths were 9.60, 13.24 and 15.80 inches at annuli 1–3. Growth was faster than in Oklahoma, Alabama and Louisiana oxbow comparisons, and the greatest growth occurred in the first year.
- Black crappie: scales showed six age groups (I–VI), with a mean calculated length of 5.77 inches at annulus 1 and 11.30 inches at annulus 6. Annuli formed from January to March.
- Bluegill: scale annuli were unreliable because of multiple growth cessations and spawning marks, so no age and growth could be determined. Length-frequency data suggested three age groups.
- Redear sunfish and warmouth: scales were not used for aging, following Lambou's recommendations. Length-frequency suggested at least three and four age groups, respectively.
- Spotted sunfish: length-frequency suggested only two age groups, indicating a short life span.

**Condition factor (K)**
- Largemouth bass K ranged from 2.81 to 3.06 by age group; black crappie averaged 3.68–3.95; bluegill averaged 4.60; redear 4.17; warmouth 4.17; spotted sunfish 4.98.
- These values exceeded comparison values for the same species in Louisiana oxbow lakes and in Carlander (1953), and were classed as excellent.

**Length-weight relationships**
- All species showed weight increasing faster than the cube of total length. Largemouth bass: Log W = -0.648 + 3.059 Log L (r² = 0.998). Black crappie: Log W = -1.45 + 3.98 Log L (r² = 0.946).

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Salinity controls the distribution of most centrarchids in the delta. Protecting pond levee plugs and freshwater inflow would therefore keep the fishery in place.
- Spotted sunfish appear well suited to saline coastal habitats.
- Young-of-the-year were abundant for most species, so reproduction appeared sufficient to sustain populations.
- Aging was limited for bluegill, redear and warmouth, and the author recommended further study on that point.
