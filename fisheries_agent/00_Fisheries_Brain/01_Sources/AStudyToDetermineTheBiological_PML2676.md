# Biological Feasibility of PIT Tags for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] (Grand Junction, Colorado, February 1990) evaluates whether [[PIT Tag]] (passive integrated transponder) tags are biologically compatible with endangered and candidate fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The study ran from 1987 to 1989 and combined laboratory, hatchery and pond trials. Pond recaptures were too few for statistical analysis, so several objectives were not met. Within the limits of the data, tags appeared well tolerated, with 100% retention in the sampled groups. The authors concluded that ponds were unsuitable for controlled tag evaluation, recommended continued hatchery-based work, and advised that PIT tags be used selectively because of their cost.

## Background & Description

**Tagging context.** Marking is fundamental to fisheries management. Earlier Colorado River endangered fish studies used [[Carlin Tag]]s, [[Floy Tag]]s, [[Coded Wire Tag]]s and fin clipping. Each carried risks: Carlin-dangler needles can cause secondary infection, and coded-wire tags require sacrificing fish for recovery. In 1986 the former Upper Colorado River Basin Biological Subcommittee recommended testing a new system.

**The PIT tag.** The tag is a passive, glass-encapsulated cylinder (about 11 mm × 2.1 mm, weighing under 0.05 g). It is energised by an external exciter and read by a hand-held scanner. Key characteristics reported:
- About 34 billion unique codes.
- Readable in vivo without anesthesia or sacrifice.
- No licence or special training required.
- Drawbacks: internal implantation, costly reader (about $1,750), and tag cost of $3.75–$6.00 per tag depending on lot size.

Earlier PIT tag work on [[Pacific Salmon]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] brood fish was described as promising. Salmon smolts (65 mm) showed about 0.5% mortality and 99% retention.

**Study objectives.** The study aimed to:
1. Identify the best anatomical placement and procedure.
2. Build tagging expertise among workers.
3. Determine tag retention.
4. Determine tissue response.
5. Assess effects on growth, survival and wound healing.
6. Test reader accuracy after injection.
7. Test durability of the detector/decoder for field use.

**Species and sizes tested.**
- [[Roundtail Chub]]: wild adults (about 260–435 mm TL), used partly as a surrogate for [[Humpback Chub]].
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: hatchery juveniles of 120–165 mm and 225–300 mm TL, plus brood fish.
- [[Razorback Sucker]]: hatchery juveniles of about 300–430 mm TL, young-of-year, and brood fish.
- [[Bonytail]] was named in the recommendations but was not tested.

**Study design.** Test groups received a body-cavity tag or a dorsal-musculature tag, with a no-tag control. Tags were implanted with a modified hypodermic syringe and 11-gauge needle under MS-222 anesthesia. Each fish received an external pelvic-fin mark to identify its group.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.**
- [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] (Colorado): the first culture site for roundtail chub and juveniles, with ground water at about 15 °C.
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico): principal endangered-fish hatchery and brood-stock source.
- Four ponds, all unsuitable for controlled work:
  - [[Gardner Pond]] (gravel pit)
  - [[13th-Hole Pond]] (Tiara Rado golf course)
  - [[West Pond]] (dense aquatic vegetation)
  - [[Humphrey Pond]]
- A field-station tank at Grand Junction, where water temperatures reached 27 °C.

**Problems encountered.**
- In 1987, two lots of roughly 100 adult roundtail chub died at Hotchkiss from uncontrolled [[Ichthyophthirius]] (external parasite) infestation, and that work was discontinued.
- Pond recapture rates were very low, so the cause of loss could not be separated from capture or handling stress.
- The Grand Junction tanks could not be kept cool, and all 40 juveniles died within about three weeks of 15 June 1989. That portion of the study was abandoned.

**Winter water quality.** Ice cover raised concern about [[Winter Dissolved Oxygen]] levels. January 1989 readings were adequate in all ponds. February levels fell in two ponds: Gardner Pond reached 3 mg/L on 9 February, and Humphrey Pond reached 2 mg/L on 9 and 24 February. West and 13th-Hole ponds stayed adequate. A previous winterkill at West Pond in 1985 killed about 25 Colorado squawfish. Hydrogen sulfide was present, but the authors concluded that heavy [[Asian Tapeworm]] infection was the main cause of death. No winterkill was found in 1989. Gardner Pond also held common carp, green sunfish, black bullhead and fathead minnow, which appeared to overwinter well. Ponds were fertilised with Triple Super Phosphate at 18.6 kg/ha to boost productivity.

## Key Findings

**Tag performance and retention**
- Tag retention and verification were 100% in all groups where tags could be recovered.
- The hand-held scanner usually read tags on the first pass. Extra passes were needed only where the insertion site was unknown.
- Hatchery Colorado squawfish of 120–165 mm (11–28 g) were successfully tagged. At day 70, 34 tagged fish remained, retention and verification were 100%, and 85% showed only a small scar that was visible on close inspection.

**Tissue response and wound healing**
- Healing had begun by day 2. By the end of the study, wounds on tagged fish had closed, and body-cavity wounds were less visible than dorsal ones (see [[Wound Healing Classification]]).
- Body-cavity tags were found near the pyloric caeca or in adjacent adipose tissue. Dorsal tags stayed within 5 mm of the insertion point.
- No swelling or edema occurred. The authors judged the tags biologically compatible with the fish tested, consistent with the [[Pacific Salmon]] findings.

**Survival and recapture**
- Pond recoveries were far too few for statistics. Growth and survival comparisons between control and test groups were not possible.
- Roundtail chub at 13th-Hole Pond: 2 controls, 7 body-cavity and 3 dorsal-tagged fish died after handling. By day 114, 14 fish were recaptured. None were captured on day 437.
- Razorback suckers at Gardner Pond: none of 10 surviving fish were recaptured across four sampling dates.
- West Pond: rotenone recovered 330 Colorado squawfish, but only 12 (20%) of the 60 originally released from control and test groups. A mark-release test on 47 squawfish returned only 12 (26%).
- Mortality from angling, handling and tagging was judged the most likely cause of the low recoveries, but this could not be confirmed.

**Brood-stock tagging**
- In September 1989, 164 adult Colorado squawfish and 527 juvenile and adult razorback suckers were PIT tagged at Dexter NFH. These fish are being monitored.

**Cost and practicality**
- PIT tagging is costly and may not suit batch marking of large stocking numbers. It is more practical for individual wild fish, brood fish, and genetic tracking, and tags can be recovered and reused from senescent brood fish.

**Recommendations**
1. Do not use ponds for future PIT tag performance studies. Controlled trials need a suitable warmwater hatchery or research facility, such as Dexter NFH.
2. Continue Colorado squawfish laboratory work at Hotchkiss NFH until July 1990.
3. Continue monitoring PIT-tagged adult fish at Dexter NFH.
4. Abandon the Grand Junction culture trials.
5. Ask the manufacturer to make the reader's power cord optional, replace the male connector with a metal one, and design a smaller hand-held scanner.
6. Continue [[Carlin Tag]] use, and evaluate its retention and attachment materials (polyethylene thread versus stainless-steel wire) in controlled settings.
7. Compare several marking systems, including [[Visible Implant Tag]]s, Carlin tags, acrylic dyes and Floy tags, on hatchery-reared Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker and bonytail at Dexter NFH.
