# Synopsis of the Taxonomy, Biology, and Fishery of Gulf of Mexico Mullets (Mugilidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mugilidae

## Executive Summary

This synopsis, by Luis R. Rivas of the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] Southeast Fisheries Center (Miami Laboratory, Contribution 80-37M, dated February 1980), reviews what was known about the mullet fishery of the Gulf of Mexico. It covers taxonomy, spawning, age and growth, migration, stock structure, production, processing, harvesting technology, management, and a list of research needs. The fishery is dominated by the [[Black Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*), which accounted for roughly 98% of the combined average annual catch from 1958 to 1978. The author concludes that the Gulf black and silver mullet should be managed as single stocks until stock structure is resolved, and that the fishery is not yet overexploited but needs preventive management. The note also draws on the author's own unpublished observations and a workshop question-and-answer session.

## Background & Description

- **Family and taxonomy:** Mugilidae is a worldwide family of tropical and temperate fishes. Six species may be involved in the Gulf fishery. The [[Black Mullet]] and [[Silver Mullet]] are the two commercially important species. The [[Fantail Mullet]] (*M. trichodon*) and [[Whirligig Mullet]] (*M. gyrans*) are also present. *M. liza* (lebrancho) and *M. gaimardianus* (redeye mullet) are mainly restricted to southern Florida and the Caribbean. The author notes that the scanned key assigns these two names unclearly.
- **Identification:** Species are grouped by coloration and scale patterns on the second dorsal and anal fins. The [[Black Mullet]] and lebrancho have dark longitudinal stripes and scaleless dorsal and anal fins. The other species have sheen-colored bodies and densely scaled fins. A simplified dichotomous key separates the six species using lateral scale counts, fin ray counts, and tooth visibility. The author stresses that correct identification is essential for stock assessment.
- **Historical confusion:** The [[Whirligig Mullet]] was first described from juveniles with two anal spines and was once placed in a separate genus (*Querimana*). Later it was shown that the spine count was a juvenile trait, and the species was returned to *Mugil*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Life cycle:** Most of the life cycle is spent in coastal bays and inlets. Young [[Black Mullet]] may enter lagoons with salinities above 75 ppt, such as the Upper Laguna Madre of Texas. In spring and summer, black mullet enter rivers, canals, and inland waterways. In fall and winter they return to salt water and migrate offshore to spawn.
- **Spawning (black mullet):** Spawning runs from late October to February, peaking in late November and early December. Spawning usually occurs 5 to 20 miles offshore and has been observed as far as 50 miles offshore. Size at maturity is 200 to 355 mm TL, and most fish mature at three years. Average fecundity is 0.5 to 1.0 million eggs.
- **Spawning (silver mullet):** Spawning runs from April to June, with a peak probably in May, well offshore over the outer Continental Shelf. Size at maturity is about 225 mm TL. The two species' spawning peaks are separated by about four months.
- **Migration and movement:** Tagging showed that 90% of tagged black mullet off Florida were recovered within 20 miles of release. One fish traveled 150 miles, from Cedar Key to Apalachicola. Black mullet in Lake Texoma, on the Texas-Oklahoma boundary, probably arrived by way of the Mississippi and Red Rivers, a journey of close to 1,000 miles.
- **Larval dispersal:** Eggs and larvae are planktonic and are carried by currents. Black mullet eggs and/or larvae have been found from 50 miles off the Mississippi coast to 70 miles off southwestern Texas. Larvae move ashore into nursery areas at about 1 to 1.5 inches.
- **Temperature and growth:** In black mullet, spring and summer growth is more than double fall and winter growth, which is probably linked to temperature.

## Key Findings

**Age and growth**

- Black mullet juveniles measure 24 to 25 mm in November and reach about 100 mm TL by June. At one year old they measure about 100 to 150 mm TL. First-year means in September were 110 mm at Pensacola, 115 mm at Apalachicola, and 148 mm at Cedar Key.
- Gulf black mullet live at least four years. Mean lengths were 150 mm FL at the end of year one, 250 mm at year two, 290 mm at year three, and 315 mm at year four. Mexican studies from Veracruz report a lifespan of at least six years and an asymptotic length (L∞) of 510 mm.
- Silver mullet growth is poorly known. Juveniles grow about 17 mm per month. The average Florida fishery fish is 250 mm, about two years old, and the maximum is 350 mm, at least three years old.

**Production and markets**

- U.S. production data began in 1895, when 10.5 million pounds were landed and valued at $120,533.
- From 1958 to 1978, the average annual catch was 29.2 million pounds of black mullet and 0.6 million pounds of silver mullet. Silver mullet were therefore only about 2% of the black mullet catch.
- The black mullet catch peaked at 36.3 million pounds in 1964 and was lowest at 18.5 million pounds in 1976. Its price rose from $0.05 per pound in 1963 to $0.19 in 1977 and $0.18 in 1978.
- Florida produced 561.2 million pounds of black mullet from 1958 to 1978, against 53.0 million pounds from the other Gulf states. Florida's share was 91.4%.
- In the Mexican Gulf (1968 to 1978), black mullet totaled 32.1 million pounds, and silver mullet totaled 35.7 million pounds. Silver mullet catches exceeded black mullet catches in 8 of 11 years.
- In 1980 the wholesale bait price was $0.40 per pound. Mullet also serve as bait for recreational billfish fishing.

**Stock structure**

- Tagging and racial studies (meristic and proportional characters) show that Gulf black mullet are separated from east Florida and northern stocks at Florida Bay. It is unknown whether a break exists between the Gulf and Caribbean around the Yucatan Peninsula.
- Three loosely knit Florida stocks were identified: Lemon Bay to Steinhatchee; St. Marks to Apalachicola; and a single Pensacola population. No boundaries have been defined.
- Because larvae disperse widely, the author recommends managing the Gulf black and silver mullet each as a single stock until the issue is resolved.

**Processing and utilization**

- Attempts at fish sticks (Miami, 1954), smoked mullet (Florida promotion, 1956), and canned mullet (early 1960s) all failed. Problems included strong flavor, dark meat streaks, rancidity, high fish prices, and a poor market image. The FDA approved the name "lisa," but canned product could only be sold in Florida.
- Canning offers the greatest potential. Canned mullet can be held at room temperature for up to two years without major quality loss.
- Mullet are classed as underutilized, with more available than are currently marketed.

**Harvesting technology**

- Gill nets are selective by size and are the least efficient method when used alone. Trammel nets catch a wider size range but cause similar concern.
- Night flambeau fishing causes mullet to scatter and become entangled but does not clear the area for long. It is dangerous to fishermen.
- Stop-nets and drag seines were criticized for bottom disturbance, but the study found this exaggerated. Stop-nets also catch many non-target fishes.
- Beach seines can catch up to 50,000 pounds of silver mullet in one set. Airboat gill netting, developed on Lake Okeechobee, sets a 220-yard net at about 40 mph.

**Management**

- Minimum legal fork length for black mullet ranges from 9 to 11 inches by county. Closed seasons were abolished in 1957.
- Black mullet landings fell after 1964, but effort declined at the same rate, so the decline does not by itself show overexploitation. A decline in catch per unit of effort or in average size would be warning signs. Average size has not yet fallen.
- The author proposes the "DAM" concept: Define, Assess, Manage, in that order. Cohort analysis is considered the most precise assessment method. Yield-per-recruit analysis is a useful complementary tool.

**Workshop discussion**

- The landings from Mexico were considered inaccurate. Unrecorded landings, such as Louisiana fish-meal (pogie) catches, may be substantial.
- The Texas participant raised concern that expanding the mullet fishery could affect [[Spotted Seatrout]] and [[Red Drum]], which depend on mullet as prey and are taken with the same gear. Purse seining was suggested as a selective alternative that allows trout and red drum to be released alive. A Mississippi observer reported good survival of released fish.

**Research needs**

- Yield per season and per area for mullet products.
- A solution to the rancidity problem.
- Consumer attitude surveys by region.
- Taste differences in mullet from different waters.
- Use of leftover parts for reduction or fertilizer.
- Use of minced mullet flesh.
