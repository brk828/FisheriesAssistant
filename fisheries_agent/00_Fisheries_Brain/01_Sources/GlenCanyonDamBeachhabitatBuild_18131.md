# Glen Canyon Dam 1996 Beach/Habitat-Building Flow: Abstracts and Executive Summaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This April 1997 volume collects abstracts and executive summaries from a symposium on the 1996 [[Beach Habitat-Building Flow]] (also called the experimental flood, test flow, or spike flow) released from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The flow was a seven-day release of about 1,274 m³/s (45,000 cfs), with 425 m³/s passing through the river outlet works. It was designed to redeposit sand, rebuild camping beaches and [[Backwater Habitat]], and reduce non-native fishes on the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]].

The flood was the culmination of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program. Its research design covered eight areas: water and sand movement, sediment deposition, aquatic ecology, native and non-native fish, terrestrial ecosystems, cultural resources, recreation, and economics. The volume is organized into four components: Physical System, Aquatic Biology (including backwaters and fish), Terrestrial System, and Cultural, Recreational and Economic. Its main conclusions are:

- Sand bar volume rose by an average of 48%, but bar area rose only 7%, so bars became higher rather than wider.
- Debris fans and rapids were partly reworked, and [[Lake Powell]] water quality changed.
- Benthic food base organisms were scoured and then recovered within weeks to months.
- Native fish were largely unaffected, while some non-native fish were displaced or reduced.
- Riparian vegetation, seed banks and endangered-species habitats suffered localized losses.
- Cultural resource studies found no adverse effect or a beneficial effect.

The editors also note that the flood was delayed a year, from spring 1995 to spring 1996, for NEPA and political reasons.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and history.** According to Patten, the flood was the third phase of research: GCES Phase I, GCES Phase II, and then the integrated experimental flood. Scientists discussed high-discharge flows from 1986 onward. A first flood plan was presented in 1993 and refined in 1994. The EIS objectives were to redeposit high-elevation sand, restore camping beaches, rejuvenate backwaters, and protect cultural and endangered-species resources.

**Hydrology and operations.** The 1996 release peaked near 1,270–1,275 m³/s, above powerplant capacity. Its pre-flood base was about 8,000 cfs and its post-flood base about 20,000 cfs. Several papers model the wave and channel geometry, including a one-dimensional unsteady flow model extended to 1,270 m³/s (Wiele and Griffin). Graf measured reach-averaged dye-tracer velocity of 1.8 m/s, compared with about 1.0 m/s at 425 m³/s in May 1991.

**Lake Powell.** Lake water quality was measured before, during, and after the flood. Releases from the river outlet works, drawing water 29 m below the penstock withdrawal zone (the first use in almost ten years), weakened the stratification. The effect lasted until February 1997.

**Monitored taxa and habitats.** The aquatic work covered benthic metabolism, periphyton, macroinvertebrates, rainbow trout, the aquatic food base, and backwaters. Terrestrial work covered vegetation, seed banks, the [[Kanab Ambersnail]], and the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]. Cultural and recreational work covered archaeological sites, ethnobotanical resources, campsites, and boating accidents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment supply and transport.** Webb and colleagues report that debris flows from 600 tributaries periodically build [[Debris Fan]]s, and that dam operations reduced the reworking of those fans. Topping describes the Grand Canyon reach as supply-limited. The post-dam sand supply is about 10% of the pre-dam supply, so sand concentrations fall over days rather than weeks. Schmidt used GIS to map about 150 eddy complexes in the study reaches. Eddy bars are the main sand-storage features.

**Sand budget.** Parnell and colleagues found that high dam releases before and after the test flow transported more sediment than the test flow itself. Sediment storage depends on tributary inputs, mainly from the [[Little Colorado River]] and the Paria River. The authors conclude that flood flows can redistribute stored sediment to higher channel-margin bars.

**Backwaters and nutrients.** Backwaters are low-velocity, shoreline habitats used as nursery areas by young fish. Under [[Interim Flows]], backwater area and number had declined. Groundwater chemistry in sandbars changed after burial of organic material (see the Key Findings section).

**Terrestrial and cultural settings.** Riparian vegetation, endangered snails, and nesting birds depend on the inundation zone. Many cultural sites are on recently formed terraces that contain buried remains. Ethnobotanical sites are significant to the Hualapai and Southern Paiute tribes.

## Key Findings

### Physical System

- **Sand bars (Parnell et al.):** Sand bar volume above 142 m³/s increased by an average of 48%, while area increased by 7%. The highest bars (above 426 m³/s) gained an average of 176%, which fell to 97% over the following months. Post-flood erosion reduced the gain, and eddies recovered close to two-thirds of the volume lost in scour.
- **Eddy complexes (Schmidt):** New deposits, often over 1 m thick, formed near eddy stagnation points. Sand bar area in the Little Colorado River reach was 271,000 m² (1984), 282,000 m² (1993) and about 240,000 m² after the 1996 flood.
- **Debris fans (Webb et al.):** Aggraded fan area decreased by 2 to 42%, and volumes fell on seven of nine fans by 3 to 34%. Stream power per unit width decreased in nine of ten rapids. Lava Falls velocities on the sides fell by about half.
- **Sediment textures (Rubin et al.):** Mean grain size coarsened upward in eddy deposits by about a factor of two (0.06–0.10 mm at the base to 0.10–0.19 mm at the top). Pool-stored sediment areal coverage was not significantly reduced.
- **Eddy erosion (Andrews et al.; Cluer):** Bars aggraded or degraded by as much as 4 m within 24 hours. One eddy bar lost 11,500 m³ of sand in a few hours.
- **Grand Canyon gage reach (Topping):** The bed aggraded by about 2 m during the rise and returned to its pre-flood level within 20 days. Suspended sand concentrations fell from 0.11% to 0.06% by volume as the bed coarsened.
- **Modeling (Wiele):** Sand concentrations were lower than during the Little Colorado River flood of January 1993, so the flood scoured main-channel deposits rather than building them.

### Aquatic Biology and Food Base

- **Benthic metabolism (Marzolf et al.):** Diel oxygen and pH amplitudes decreased by about 75% after the flood scoured biomass.
- **Periphyton and invertebrates (McKinney et al.):** Periphyton ash-free dry weight was unaffected, but chlorophyll *a* and diatom densities were reduced. [[Gammarus Lacustris]] densities fell 65–70% on cobble bars and depositional habitat. Recovery of amphipods on cobble bars occurred about four months after the discharge.
- **Rainbow trout (McKinney et al.):** Trout catch-per-unit-effort did not differ before and after the flood, and condition was unaffected. Mean length and weight increased, and about 10% fewer fish under 152 mm were caught, suggesting downstream displacement of small fish. Strong recruitment occurred eight months later.
- **Spike flow food base (Shannon et al.):** Benthic recovery was rapid, and most change occurred during the initial 24 hours of the hydrostatic wave. Management considerations suggest a steeper, shorter hydrograph (such as an 80,000 cfs peak for an hour or two) could be tested. The authors wrote that the hypothesis of a negative impact was rejected, and the flood had a significant positive effect on the food base.

### Backwaters and Nutrients

- **Backwater area and number (Parnell et al.):** The test flow increased backwater area by 0.58 ha and formed 8 new backwaters, a 2.1-fold areal increase and a 1.25-fold increase in number.
- **Mile 55.5R return current channel:** Sediment deposition reduced maximum backwater volume by at least 20%. Hydraulic conductivity fell 20–40% under as much as 1.8 m of new sand. Macrophyte biomass later rose to 34 g/m² in September.
- **Groundwater nutrients (Parnell et al.):** Groundwater non-purgeable organic carbon (NPOC) rose from 85% to 278%, with mean values of 4.95 mg/L before and 22.5 mg/L after. Ammonium rose by 79–617%. Dissolved oxygen fell significantly at miles -6.5R and 55.5R, consistent with microbial respiration.
- **Benthic invertebrates (Parnell et al.):** Biomass fell by about an order of magnitude after the flood and then doubled over the growing season, reaching 20% of pre-flood levels by September.
- **Backwater fish use:** The backwater served as a rearing habitat for young native fish, and young carp dominated the assemblage from July through October.
- **Invertebrate and plankton (Hoffnagle et al.):** Mean total invertebrate density fell from 11,426.9 to 3,581.8 individuals/m². Total zooplankton density rose from 2,733/m³ to 4,174/m³.
- **Fish response (Hoffnagle et al.; Valdez):** Native fish were largely unaffected. [[Fathead Minnow]] CPUE fell from 0.8 to 0.05 fish/24 hr near the [[Little Colorado River]]. Plains killifish seined in backwaters fell from 1.1 to 0 fish/100 m². Juvenile rainbow trout seined in backwaters rose from 0.04 to 0.3 fish/100 m². Backwater gains were temporary, as many new backwaters eroded at 8,000 cfs.
- **Humpback chub (Hoffnagle et al.; Valdez):** Adults were tracked by radio. Net movement during the flow (mean 0.40 km) did not differ significantly from the month before (mean 1.26 km). Gammarus occurred in 44% of fish before the flood, 94% during, and 89% after. The studies suggest the chub feeds opportunistically.
- **Flannelmouth sucker (Thieme and McIvor):** Adults sheltered in the inundated Paria mouth and then returned. Spawning continued normally, and 576 young-of-the-year were captured. A steady 20,000 cfs flow formed a slack-water rearing pool for young fish.

### Lake Powell and Water Quality

- **Vernieu and Hueftle:** The combined powerplant and river outlet releases weakened stratification and brought higher dissolved oxygen to the outlet level. Releases showed a transient temperature reduction and a slight salinity increase. Salinity fell and dissolved oxygen rose during the year after the flood. The authors propose varying withdrawal depth as a management tool.

### Terrestrial Biology

- **Vegetation and seed banks (Kearsley and Ayers):** Extant vegetation effects were minor and limited to patches near the river. Seed banks lost about 80% of individuals and 80% of species richness. The authors suggest higher releases would be needed to reduce near-shore vegetation.
- **Lower Grand Canyon riparian zone (Christensen):** Plant species per inundated polygon declined significantly at miles 241.5L and 246.0L. Changes were not significant at 249.0L and 254.0R.
- **Kanab Ambersnail (Stevens and Meretsky):** The population at [[Vaseys Paradise]] was estimated at 2,154 snails below the actual 45,000 cfs stage. Roughly 11% of primary habitat was lost. Of 1,242 snails marked and moved, 40.8% of the inundated-zone snails were estimated lost. The low-zone population rose to 10,646 on 20 July 1996 and had two reproductive peaks. The authors recommend continued bimonthly monitoring.
- **Southwestern Willow Flycatcher (Stevens et al.):** Marsh area declined by 1% to 75% at the four nest sites. Branch contacts in the 0–0.5 m layer declined in the inundation zone. The authors conclude there was little, if any, biologically important impact, and no long-term impacts are expected. Only one breeding pair was found at mile 50.5L.
- **Avifauna (Spence):** Mean individuals, species, ground gleaners and tree gleaners all declined significantly after the flood. The author suggests loss of river-edge habitat or reduced prey and seed may be responsible.

### Cultural, Recreational and Economic Studies

- **Archaeological sites (Balsom):** Enhanced monitoring covered 36 sites, and mitigation was carried out at eight locations. Findings indicate no effect, no adverse effect, or a beneficial effect.
- **Arroyo mouths (Yeatts):** Sediment was deposited at three of four study sites, filling arroyo mouths. The Lees Ferry site received no deposition and no erosion. A sand plug at mile 71.4 was breached by February 1997.
- **Ethnobotany (Jackson et al.; Phillips and Jackson):** About 80 plant species are culturally significant to the tribes. The Hualapai studied five sites with line-intercept transects. The ancient Goodding willow at Granite Park was stabilized with rock before the flood.
- **Other studies:** Papers in this section also cover campsite changes, a Grand Canyon River Guides beach adoption program, boating accidents, and a draft hydropower cost analysis that had not completed peer review.
