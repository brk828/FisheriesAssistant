# Utilization of Benthic-Feeding Fish by Inland Breeding Bald Eagles

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the diet and foraging habitat of the [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*) population breeding along the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] in central Arizona. The population is small and occupies the southern edge of the species' range in a desert riparian setting. Eleven nests were monitored, and prey was identified from both direct observation and prey remains. Fish made up 77% of the 481 prey items found in and around nests, and [[Channel Catfish]] was the most frequently taken prey. Catfish, carp and suckers together accounted for 74% of prey remains. The authors link eagle foraging success to [[River-bottom Profile]] features, especially deep pools bordered by riffles and sandbars. They also argue that nest placement reflects these aquatic habitat features. The study was designed to give agencies baseline data for judging the effects of proposed water-storage and flood-control dams on Arizona waterways.

## Background & Description

The Arizona population of [[Bald Eagle]] is described as unique because it breeds in a desert riparian environment at the southern extent of the species' range. Earlier work documented inland Bald Eagle prey use in general (Lincer et al. 1979), but little was published on this desert population. The authors had three objectives:

- Determine diet composition.
- Compare prey use with other regions.
- Examine foraging behavior to identify the aquatic habitat used for foraging.

The study area covered about 160 km upstream on each river from the Salt–Verde confluence. The Salt River drains the western White Mountains and eastern Mogollon Rim. The Verde River drains the western Mogollon Rim and central mountains. Several impoundments occur on these rivers, but no active nest is known at any of them. Surrounding vegetation falls within the Lower and Upper Sonoran Life Zones.

Methods included:

- **Observation:** Two to three observers at lookout points at each nest, with radio communication. Flight paths, perches and foraging sites were mapped on 7.5-minute USGS topographic maps.
- **Habitat measurement:** River-bottom profiles recorded substrate, bottom depth and water level at 1.5 m intervals, with substrate classified into nine categories (Trihey and Wegner 1981).
- **Prey identification:** Prey remains were collected from banding, adult foraging sites and nests after breeding, and identified from fur, bones and body parts.
- **Biomass estimation:** Standard lengths of [[Carp]], [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]] were derived from [[Opercle Regression]] equations. [[Channel Catfish]] lengths were derived from [[Pectoral Spine Regression]] equations. Whole-body weights were used.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Prey composition.** Fish dominated the diet. The study identifies a clear preference for [[Benthic-feeding Fish]], which the authors compare with other inland Bald Eagle studies (Table 5). These studies were in Maine, Chesapeake Bay, Florida, Louisiana and Minnesota. Wright (1953) found brown bullhead catfish preferred in New Brunswick. The authors state that the Arizona results strongly agree with these earlier findings.

**Mammalian prey and energy.** Mammals were relatively important given the desert setting. [[Black-tailed Jackrabbit]] was one of the largest prey items and accounted for nearly 15% of total biomass. Using [[Energy Content]] values from Stalmaster and Gessaman (1982), jackrabbit (0.9232 kcal/g) yielded more energy per gram than salmon (0.6751 kcal/g). The authors conclude that mammalian prey is essential to meet energy demands, while fish provide a steady, continued food source.

**Vulnerability.** Citing Swenson (1979) and Todd et al. (1982), the authors argue that benthic-feeding fish are more vulnerable to aerial predators. Their downward visual concentration, slower escape movements and proximity to the surface make them easier to catch.

**Habitat features.** Deep pools bounded by riffles or sandbars were common at all nest sites. Water depth in these pools stayed above 3 m at low flow or during flow cessation, providing refuge for prey fish. Shallows and riffles next to pools brought benthic fish closer to the surface.

**Nest placement.** Six of the seven nests with observed foraging were located immediately adjacent to foraging sites and were the most frequently used perches. The authors interpret this as evidence that nest placement is tied to the physical character of the river.

## Key Findings

**Sample and diet**

- Eleven active Arizona nests were monitored from 1976 through 1982. The abstract gives a "five-year period beginning in 1978."
- Of 697 observed deliveries, 73% were fish, 17% unidentifiable, 5% mammalian, 1% avian and 4% reptilian or amphibian.
- Prey remains (n = 481): 77% fish, 12% mammalian, 11% avian, 0.6% reptilian or amphibian.
- [[Channel Catfish]] was the most common prey taxon: 180 individuals, 37.4% of total individuals, 21.8% of biomass, and 49% of fish prey.
- Other major fish prey: [[Sonora Sucker]] (64 individuals), [[Carp]] (59), [[Flathead Catfish]] (22) and [[Desert Sucker]] (21).
- [[American Coot]] (27 individuals) was the most common bird. Cottontail rabbits and black-tailed jackrabbits together made up more than 6% of individuals.

**Biomass**

- Thirteen species made up about 96% of estimated biomass.
- Channel catfish, carp and black-tailed jackrabbit together made up about 54% of biomass.
- Carp and black-tailed jackrabbit were more important by biomass than by number because of their large body size.

**Fish size**

- Of 245 fish retrieved as prey remains with usable opercle or pectoral spine, 76% were 200–350 mm standard length.
- Mean predicted body weights ranged from 263 g to 636 g.
- Mean standard lengths from Table 2: channel catfish 253.6 mm (n = 141), carp 313.3 mm (n = 73), Sonora sucker 287.5 mm (n = 23), desert sucker 242.4 mm (n = 8).

**Foraging sites and success**

- Foraging was observed at 24 locations from seven nests.
- Total capture success was 78% (143 captures from 40 attempts in the table summary).
- Individual sites varied widely. Nest 4 pools exceeded 3 m and reached more than 6 m deep.
- Riffles, deep pools, shallow backwaters and sandbars were the main foraging habitats.

**Timing**

- 733 prey-related observations were recorded by hour after sunrise.
- Two peak foraging periods occurred: 1–4 h and 8–10 h after sunrise.
- For fish alone, no significant difference was found between hours. The authors therefore conclude that prey fish were accessible throughout the day.

**Dietary context**

- Catfish, carp and suckers made up 74% of all prey remains.
- The authors conclude that mammalian prey is essential for energy needs, while the large numbers of catfish, carp and suckers provide a continuous forage base.

**Conclusions and management implications**

- The authors argue that aquatic habitat is more strongly linked to Bald Eagle productivity than earlier studies suggested.
- Productive river bottoms are essential for benthic-feeding fish and therefore for nest establishment and productivity.
- The results are intended to help agencies evaluate proposed dams on the Salt and Verde rivers, which are tied to the [[Central Arizona Water Control Study]].

**Data discrepancies noted in the source**

- The abstract reports 22 foraging sites, while the methods report 23 and the results report 24.
- Desert sucker sample size is 36 in the methods and 37 in Table 3.
