# The Demise of Desert Springs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter, by [[Peter J. Unmack]] and [[W. L. Minckley]], reviews the causes of loss and decline of desert springs and their aquatic biota, with emphasis on experience in Australia and western North America. Springs are described as remnants of wetter climates and as natural laboratories for [[Speciation]] and ecological research. The authors argue that groundwater extraction is the dominant cause of spring loss, and that further damage arises from spring alteration, diversion of outflows, livestock grazing, [[Invasive Species]], and hybridization with introduced fishes. The chapter closes with a call for an international program to define, protect, and restore springs before further losses occur.

## Background & Description

- Springs are places where water rises at the intersection of groundwater and the land surface. They range from tiny seeps to outflows of underground rivers, and tiny seeps are far more common.
- Surface water is uncommon in deserts, so springs are often described as "aquatic archipelagos." Unlike oceanic islands, many spring biotas are remnants of wetter past climates rather than colonists from nearby mainlands.
- Because dry land is a strong barrier to obligate aquatic organisms, many species are restricted to one or a few springs. This isolation makes springs valuable for studying [[Speciation]] and for field studies of thermal and physicochemical tolerances along spring-run gradients.
- Organisms in springs are fully aquatic and "live in rather than of" deserts, except where life stages resist drying (for example, drought-resistant eggs and seeds).
- Fossil springs are marked by tufa or travertine deposits and peat beds, and they document extinct species and communities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Groundwater extraction.** Aquifers may hold water that originated in the distant past and is recharged slowly from distant zones. Natural aridification since the Cretaceous doomed many springs. Human extraction became dominant after the [[Rural Electrification Act of 1936]] enabled high-volume electric pumps. Population growth in [[Arizona]] (2.7 to 4.7 million, 1980–1997) and northern Mexico (5.5 to 8.3 million, 1980–1990) increased demand.

**Scale of losses.** In [[Texas]], 63 of 281 historically significant springs had gone dry by the 1970s, and many more lost volume. In northern Mexico, at least 92 springs failed in the two decades ending in 1993. At [[Elizabeth Springs]], Queensland, flow fell by approximately 95 percent since the 1880s.

**Large aquifers under stress.** The [[Edwards Aquifer]] of Texas is declining, with potentially catastrophic consequences for its springs. Similar declines are reported for the [[Ogallala Aquifer]] and for aquifers in Saudi Arabia. In Australia, a mining venture's extraction from the [[Great Artesian Basin]] rose from 1.5 x 10^4 to 3.3 x 10^4 cubic meters per day.

**Domino effects.** Flow reduction triggers linked biotic and physicochemical changes. Severity depends on (1) proportion of flow lost, (2) reduction in downstream extent, and (3) new connections between nearby outflows. Diversion near the spring head drains downstream marshes, which are also important resting and feeding habitat for waterbirds.

**Carbonate processes.** Groundwater carbon dioxide drives carbonate chemistry. When water emerges, CO2 degasses and calcium carbonate precipitates as travertine, which can form dams that isolate springs. Changes in flow or channel alteration shift where and how fast this occurs.

## Key Findings

- **Primary cause of loss:** Groundwater extraction, often exceeding natural recharge, is the principal driver of spring disappearance. Flow reductions correlate directly with quantities pumped, and even large aquifers are affected.
- **Lack of baseline data:** Recharge rates, porosity, aquifer volume, and aquifer extent are often unknown, and data are usually compiled only after surface effects appear.
- **Alteration of head-springs:** Excavation, explosives, pumping, capping, and piping destroy spring biota, though seepage may sustain small arthropods and snails. Filling Raycraft Spring in the 1950s eliminated the world's only population of [[Raycraft Poolfish]].
- **Diversion of outflows:** Diversions near the spring head cause the greatest damage. Beheading of Poza de la Becerra (Cuatro Ciénegas) reduced a marsh of about 10 km² to under 10–15 hectares in a few days, eliminating habitat for endemic turtles, fishes, and snails.
- **Recovery is possible:** Some systems recovered after disturbance. Jackrabbit Spring was restocked in 1977 after pumping in 1969–70, and Big Bend gambusia reestablished naturally by 1983 after its pond was drained and conditions re-created.
- **Protection by accident:** Recreational modification sometimes preserved biota. In Chihuahua, endemic species survived in four of five large springs converted to reservoirs, pools, or baths.
- **Livestock and vegetation:** Grazing damages springs, but its removal can allow cattails and reeds to choke habitat, as at Mexican Spring, Ash Meadows, a refuge for a tiny warm-springs pupfish population that dried in 1973. Shade removal raises temperature and evaporation.
- **Invasive organisms:** Invasion is described as the single most destructive force for desert-spring biota in western North America. Key threats include [[Largemouth Bass]], green sunfish, cichlids, [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia affinis, G. holbrooki), bullfrogs, Procambarus crayfish, the Thiara snail, water hyacinth, salt-cedar, and palms.
- **Hybridization:** Introduced fishes hybridize with natives, e.g., Owens tui chub, Leon Springs and Comanche pupfishes with sheepshead minnow, and Conchos and Tecopa pupfishes through artificial channels.
- **Parasites and disease:** Introduced parasites, including anchor-worm, Asian tapeworm (Bothriocephalus acheilognathi), and a nematode (Hysterothylacium sp.) found in endangered Gila topminnow, threaten native fishes. The authors state that fish disease and parasitology research is urgently needed.
- **Conservation success is limited but real:** Owens Valley's native-fish sanctuary (1967–68) and the Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge, established after a U.S. Supreme Court decision, show that protection can work. The authors note that conservation lagged behind recognition of the problem.
- **Call to action:** The authors recommend an aggressive international program to define springs, educate the public about their value, and set aside, restore, and perpetuate examples before they are lost.
