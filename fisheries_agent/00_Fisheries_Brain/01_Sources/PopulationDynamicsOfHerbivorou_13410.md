# Population Dynamics of Herbivorous Insects in Oil Refinery Effluent Holding-Ponds

#aquatic-ecology #entomology #Tendipedidae #Chironomidae #energy-transfer #pollution-biology

## Executive Summary

This 1963 doctoral dissertation by Richard Arnold Tubb examines the population dynamics of bottom-dwelling midge fly larvae (family [[Tendipedidae]], also known as [[Chironomidae]]) in a series of ten oil refinery effluent holding-ponds in [[Stillwater, Oklahoma]]. Monthly sampling from November 1960 to November 1961 documented three species: *[[Tendipes plumosus]]*, *[[Harnischia tenuicaudata]]*, and *[[Tanypus stellatus]]*. The study quantified standing crops, life histories, survival percentages, and energy transfer through emergence. Filter-feeding larvae significantly reduced effluent turbidity. Adult emergence removed approximately 62,458 kilocalories per year from the pond system. The research represents a pioneering attempt to measure energy flow through herbivorous populations in an organically enriched aquatic environment, demonstrating how biological treatment processes dissipate energy in industrial wastewater systems.

## Background & Description

The holding-pond series covered 634,556 square feet with ten ponds ranging 400-550 feet in length and 130-160 feet in width, averaging 5 feet deep. Effluent passed through the system in 6-8 days. The ponds were elevated above surrounding terrain with earthen dikes, placed in operation July 1960. Refinery operations included atmospheric and vacuum distillation, catalytic cracking, reforming, alkylation, and various treating processes. Effluent received pretreatment including oil removal, steam stripping for sulfides and phenol, and activated sludge treatment before entering holding-ponds.

Three tendipedid species dominated the benthic fauna:

- **[[Tendipes plumosus]]**: Average length 8.3 mm, dry weight 0.501 mg, calorific content 5843.75 cal/g. Life cycle 32 days in laboratory cultures. Eggs hatched in 1.5-2 days at 22.2-25.2°C. Mortality 82-94% between hatching and case-building, then 43% average to adult stage. First emergence 21-22 days after hatching, continuing 53 days. Male:female ratio 15:1 in laboratory (possibly skewed by small sample). Females averaged 804 eggs, oviposited once. Predominated in spring populations.

- **[[Harnischia tenuicaudata]]**: Larger, robust larvae averaging 13 mm length, dry weight 0.876 mg, calorific content 5344.48 cal/g. Estimated 30-day life cycle. Most abundant in summer in ponds 7 and 10. Latest spring emergence (May). Filter-feeding habits similar to *Tendipes*.

- **[[Tanypus stellatus]]**: Small larvae 6-8 mm, dry weight 0.131 mg, calorific content 5607.51 cal/g. Did not build cases. Omnivorous, feeding on decaying algae. Life cycle approximately 60 days in spring/summer, 60-80 days for overwintering populations. Most abundant in fall and winter. Most resistant to effluent toxicity.

Other invertebrates present in small numbers included [[Chaoborus]] (principal summer predator, maximum 206.64 larvae/ft² in July), [[Ceratopogonidae]], [[Hydrophilidae]] (*Hydrochara*), and [[Odonata]] (damselfly naiads as primary winter predators, maximum 92 naiads/ft² in December).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water temperatures ranged from 34°F (winter, pond 10) to 89°F (summer, pond 1), with greater temperature differences between ponds in winter than summer. Phenol concentration served as an index of effluent toxicity, typically below 1 mg/L in pond 1 after pretreatment, and uniformly low in pond 10 except during two toxic surges. pH values were higher in spring/summer (8.0-8.6 in pond 10) than winter (7.3-7.6), indicating algal photosynthesis utilizing dissolved CO₂.

Community metabolism studies by Copeland (1963) showed that during winter, atmospheric diffusion was the only oxygen source and anaerobic conditions prevailed. Summer conditions included nighttime anaerobiosis. The facultative anaerobic capabilities of tendipedid larvae were critical to their survival.

Population distribution patterns showed larvae concentrated near pond edges (especially E stations on east shore, possibly due to prevailing southwest winds) during summer. Winter populations shifted to deeper central areas, possibly due to ice formation and wind action. Overwintering *Tanypus* populations were larger in pond 7 (warmer but more toxic) than pond 10, suggesting greater resistance to toxicity than to lower temperature.

Effluent toxicity effects were evident: a surge of toxic effluent in August 1961 sharply reduced populations. *Tanypus* maintained residual populations during this event, confirming its resistance. *Tendipes* appeared more resistant than *Harnischia*, as it recolonized first after toxic surges. Spring dominance of *Tendipes* suggested its eggs were more tolerant of anaerobic conditions than other species.

## Key Findings

**Population Dynamics**: Larval populations generally increased in successive holding-ponds during summer. Winter populations were small and concentrated in ponds near the center of the system (pond 7). *Tendipes plumosus* predominated in spring, *Harnischia tenuicaudata* was most abundant in summer, and fall/winter populations contained large numbers of *Tanypus stellatus*. Pond 10 supported the largest summer larval populations; pond 7 had greatest overwintering populations. Pond 1 lacked macroscopic invertebrates.

**Standing Crops**: Numerical standing crops showed high variation typical of benthic communities. Summer populations in pond 10 reached maximum values. The August 1961 toxic surge dramatically reduced fall populations across all species.

**Survival and Emergence**: Laboratory survival for *Tendipes* was 57%. Winter field populations yielded survival estimates of 79% for *Harnischia* and 3.3% for *Tanypus* in pond 10, based on logarithmic population decrease. Pupae occurred June-November 1961, with *Tanypus* pupae most abundant in pond 4, while ponds 7 and 10 had largest combined *Harnischia* and *Tendipes* pupae. Pupal numbers decreased sharply during the August toxic event.

**Energy Transfer**: Adult emergence removed approximately **62,458 kilocalories/year** from the seven-pond system, equivalent to complete oxidation of 92.8 moles of glucose. Energy loss by pond: Pond 4 = 7,165 kcal, Pond 7 = 5,726 kcal, Pond 10 = 15,495 kcal. *Harnischia* transferred 44,759 kcal (71.7%), *Tendipes* 14,130 kcal (22.6%), *Tanypus* 3,569 kcal (5.7%). Despite large numerical populations, *Tanypus* transferred less energy due to small size and longer life cycle. More energy entered decomposer and predator trophic levels than was lost by emergence, as most females perished in ponds after oviposition. Only males (approximately 50% of emergent adults) represented actual energy loss from the pond system.

**Turbidity Reduction**: Laboratory cultures demonstrated that filter-feeding *Tendipes* larvae significantly reduced effluent turbidity (statistically significant at 95% confidence level across nine tests, calculated "t" values 2.267-3.785 vs. table "t" 2.010-2.032). High summer populations of *Harnischia*, also filter-feeders, likely contributed substantially to suspended particle removal including algae.

**Ecological Succession**: Species succession through seasons reflected differential tolerances: *Tendipes* eggs tolerated anaerobic spring conditions; *Harnischia* dominated aerobic summer conditions; *Tanypus* thrived in toxic fall/winter conditions. Competition between *Tendipes* and *Harnischia* was evident in sharp summer population shifts.

**Methodological Notes**: Stratified sampling with five stations per transect across three transects per pond (15 samples/pond) was designed to reduce variation but was only partially successful. Ekman dredge samples were sieved through #20 (0.840 mm) and #40 (0.420 mm) U.S. standard sieves. Standing crops were calculated by summing area-weighted averages. Caloric content determined by Parr bomb calorimetry on dried specimens. The study represents a first attempt to measure energy flow through herbivorous populations in organically enriched environments, with estimates considered of correct order of magnitude though provisional pending improved respiration data.
