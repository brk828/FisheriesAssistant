# Cibola High Levee Pond Annual Report 2003: Early Life Ecology of Bonytail and Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 2003 annual report describes the second year of a four-year field effort on the early life ecology of [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] (CHLP), an oxbow on the lower [[Colorado River]]. Bonytail produced swim-up larvae and recruited annually, while razorback sucker recruitment remained intermittent. Underwater videography, telemetry, larval light trapping, trammel netting, and predator/prey tank experiments were used to document spawning behavior, habitat use, and predation by nonnative species. The authors conclude that nonnative predators, including [[Bullfrog]] tadpoles and [[Red Swamp Crayfish]], pose a serious threat to early life stages.

The report is a collaborative product of the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], [[Arizona State University]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

## Background & Description

**Study species**
- **Bonytail** (*Gila elegans*), family Cyprinidae: adults in the study ranged from 358 to 514 mm. Telemetry fish were 404 to 514 mm total length.
- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*), family Catostomidae: a native sucker of the Colorado River basin, notable for a reflective sclera that produces a distinctive white flash when the fish rolls its eyes (observed in 59 videotaped individuals).

**Study site**
CHLP is an oxbow with a riprap-lined river levee and a high levee. In the 2002 community survey, the pond held approximately 1,100 razorbacks and 6,000 bonytail (>15 cm), with a standing crop of 635 kg/ha. Bathymetric mapping (July 2003) divided the pond into depth classes from 0.6 to 3.4 m.

**Objectives (2003)**
- Telemetry of adult bonytail during the suspected spawning period.
- Underwater videography to verify spawning sites, timing, and predators.
- Physical measurements, trammel netting, light trapping, water quality monitoring, mapping, and acoustic tests.
- Predator/prey tank tests at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and Achii Hanyo Fish Facility.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning substrate and timing**
- Razorback suckers spawned from late January to mid-March over gravel and large cobble at the levee toe (0.5 to 3 m depth). Spawning was already underway when fieldwork began on 18 February 2003.
- Bonytail spawned along the levee shoreline during April, in shallow water (<50 cm) on substrate previously disturbed by beaver activity. Water temperature during spawning ranged from 15.3 to 20.1 °C for razorbacks and from 16.6 to 21.6 °C for bonytail (per the April window in the text).
- Substrate scour or disturbance appears to be an important factor in spawning site selection.

**Water quality (Hydrolab, 2003)**
Temperatures ranged from 16 to 30 °C, dissolved oxygen 7.7 to 10.8 mg/L, conductance 1207 to 1234 µS/cm, and pH 7.1 to 7.6. The pond remained generally well mixed and within acceptable limits.

**Habitat and cover**
- Adult bonytail occupied interspaces of large riprap on the high levee during daylight. Historically, woody debris, snags, and root wads provided cover that has largely disappeared from the river, and the authors suggest cavity use may now increase predation risk from channel catfish and other nonnatives.
- Larval and juvenile fish concentrated in areas with dense overhead cover and near beaver den entrances where depths were about 1 m or more.

**Nonnative predators**
Bluegill and largemouth bass remained in CHLP despite removal in November 2002. Bullfrog tadpoles reached densities of up to 17.44 fish-sized organisms per m² at bonytail spawning sites on video, and crayfish were common among spawners.

## Key Findings

**Spawning and recruitment**
- Bonytail produced swim-up larvae again, with annual recruitment continuing. No measurable razorback recruitment was detected in the past two years (2002 and 2003 by this report's account).
- Razorback spawning density averaged 4.76 fish/m² (range 1.19 to 9.0; n = 256). The authors estimate nearly one third of the 2002 population was in the spawning area at one time.
- Bonytail video densities over spawning sites averaged 17.44 fish/m² (range 3.6 to 55.4; n = 222). This suggests that only about 5% of the community spawned at any given time.
- Two definite bonytail spawning acts were recorded, each lasting 1 to 2 seconds with 3 to 7 fish involved, followed by feeding frenzies of several dozen egg scavengers.

**Behavior**
- Adult bonytail were nocturnal and cover-oriented, remaining in riprap cavities during daylight and leaving no earlier than 30 minutes after sunset. They returned no later than 30 minutes before sunrise.
- Bonytail showed high site fidelity and were very sensitive to disturbance from outboard motors and rowing.
- All life stages schooled; younger year classes formed larger aggregations. Schools of fish under 10 cm numbered up to 50 individuals.
- Feeding frenzies followed gamete release; razorbacks were often seen feeding among spawning bonytail, while bonytail concentrated at the periphery to feed on emerging larvae.

**Diet and health**
- Gut contents of adult bonytail included algae, vegetative material, small fish, and crayfish.
- Tapeworm (species unknown) was found in 4 of 30 fish (13%). An earlier note cites 5 of 27 adults (18%).
- Recaptured telemetry fish showed chafing around the caudal peduncle from the cable-tie harness. The authors recommend a wider, toothless banding material such as electrician's tape.
- Five dead adults were found in the week of 22 July (1 bonytail, 4 razorbacks) with no obvious cause.

**Telemetry method**
- Ten bonytail were fitted with external sonic micro transmitters attached by a wire harness to the caudal peduncle, a process taking about 30 seconds. The authors believe this is the first use of this technique on freshwater fishes.
- All study fish moved during the study, and no mortalities were evident. Individuals showed fidelity to specific zones or cavities.

**Larval collections**
- Ten razorback larvae (8.5 to 9.6 mm, mean 9.0 mm) were collected by light trap in the week of 24 March 2003.
- Four bonytail-like larvae (7.8 to 15.8 mm, mean 11.1 mm) were taken in May; identification was pending.

**Trammel netting (CPUE, fish/hour/100 m²)**
- Bonytail: 12 fish (1.37) on 17 March; 28 fish (2.27) on 7 May; 3 fish (0.17) in July. Razorbacks: 6 (0.40) on 17 March, 1 (0.24) in May and 1 (0.40) in July. Catch was nearly twice as high in May as in the other efforts.

**Predator/prey tank experiments**
- Bullfrog tadpoles (n = 75) consumed all 300 razorback eggs within about 90 hours, a 100% predation rate.
- Crayfish (2 per tank) killed 96.7% of 16.2 mm razorback fry within 72 hours in the no-cover treatment, while deep cover reduced predation to 70%. The shallow-cover trial was inconclusive because the fry were a different size.
- Predation on 10 to 16 mm razorback larvae: red shiner 87.5%, bonytail 88.8%, rainbow trout (YC-1) 100%, yellow bullhead 98.75%, and bullfrog tadpole 7.0%.
- Predation on adult rainbow trout was 100% on 18 to 28 mm larvae. Bluegill, channel catfish, and largemouth bass predated 30 to 50 mm razorbacks at 18.3% to 66.7%.
- Mixed-prey trials found no preference between razorbacks and fathead minnows, and no size selectivity (p > 0.1).
- Control mortality was 1.03% (±0.46 SE).

**Conclusions and management implications**
- CHLP is described as the only oxbow community where early life stages of these two species can be studied.
- Removal of large predators may shift size structure toward smaller, more numerous predators that have easier access to shallow, vegetated nursery habitat. The authors consider nonnative predation an "overwhelming obstacle" to recovery that may never be fully resolved.

**Planned work for 2004**
- Telemetry of juvenile bonytail (15 to 20 cm) and continued sonic tracking of adults.
- Large hoop nets to track movement of multiple life stages, and additional gut-content sampling.
- At least four seasonal acoustic surveys.
- Further tank tests on tadpole egg and larval predation, behavioral differences between razorback and bonytail juveniles, and interactions among large and medium predators.
