## Executive Summary

This 1981 study documents a chemical renovation (piscicide) effort on Ord Creek, a high-elevation tributary stream in the White Mountains of east-central Arizona, undertaken to eliminate invasive [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) and [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) and restore the threatened native [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*). By the early 1970s, nonnative trout comprised over 90% of the fish assemblage, displacing the native species despite a 1964 fish barrier. Antimycin A (Fintrol), detoxified downstream with HTH (sodium hypochlorite), was applied in August 1977 following electrofishing removal and temporary holding of native trout. The first treatment failed — reintroduced Arizona trout disappeared and brook trout fry reappeared within a year, likely due to Antimycin A retention issues in cienega (marsh) hydrology and/or protected spawning redds. A second treatment in September 1978 successfully eradicated nonnative trout, and Arizona trout reintroduced in October 1980 persisted through at least mid-1981, though without confirmed reproduction. The study highlights the ecological, hydrological, and logistical complexities of piscicide-based native fish restoration in complex headwater habitats.

## Background & Description

- **Arizona Trout (*Salmo apache*)**: Adult body weights ranged 70–150 g; length-frequency sample (n=145 aged individuals) consisted entirely of age 3+ fish (up to age 5–6), with only 6 of ~200 fish (3%) measuring <120 mm — indicating an absence of young-of-year/juvenile recruitment for several years prior to treatment.
- **Brook Trout**: Dominated numerically (85%) and by biomass (78%) among nonnative adults recovered; average size was greatest in downstream reaches; large individuals sampled (225–270 mm) for stomach content analysis.
- **Brown Trout**: Distribution pattern inverse to brook trout — predominant downstream (~85% of nonnative totals in lower sections), average size greatest upstream.
- **Condition Factor (K_TL)**: Arizona trout = 1.10; brook trout = 0.90; brown trout = 0.94 — native trout in comparatively better body condition despite population decline, suggesting food availability was not the primary competitive bottleneck.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Ord Creek flows ~11 km north from Bull Cienega (2,650 m elevation) through Smith Cienega to a former fish barrier (2,230 m). Cienegas are open riparian marshes with deep organic sediment and dense sedge/grass sod; inter-cienega reaches are narrow, rocky, glacial moraine-derived channels canopied by mixed coniferous forest.
- **Hydrology**: Discharge ranged from 0.56 m³/min (Bull Cienega) to ~25 m³/min (old fish barrier). Current velocities averaged <10 m/min in cienegas, up to 22 m/min over moraine reaches. Gradient ranged from ~2% (cienegas) to ~6% (moraine crossings).
- **Water Chemistry**: pH 6.5–7.0; water temperature 5–8°C (night/day); total hardness ~30.0 ± 7.1 mg/L, dominated by magnesium hardness (71.3%) over calcium hardness (28.7%).
- **Species Interactions**:
  - Competition for reproductive space considered minimal since brook/brown trout are autumn spawners while Arizona trout spawn in spring.
  - Numerical dominance of nonnatives likely suppressed native spawning success indirectly.
  - Potential [[Predation]] and cannibalism inferred from low proportions of small fish (<95 mm) relative to adult nonnative populations, though stomach analysis of 50 large brook/brown trout showed no fish remains.
  - Brook trout showed strong upstream/headwater invasion capability; brown trout showed lower propensity for headwater colonization, concentrating downstream — implying differential habitat use and invasion dynamics relevant to [[Invasive Species Management]].
  - Aquatic macroinvertebrate populations were also significantly impacted by Antimycin A treatment (documented in companion study by Minckley and Mihalick 1981), raising concerns about future food-web recovery and potential loss of unique invertebrate taxa.

## Key Findings

- **Population Baseline (Aug 1977, pre-treatment)**: Total fish recovered across 4 stream sections (8,333 m² total): 4,951 brook trout (209.1 kg), 781 brown trout (48.9 kg), 102 Arizona trout fingerlings (9.25 kg), plus 3,700 unidentified nonnative fry — total 9,534 fish (270.47 kg).
- **Toxicant Protocol**: Antimycin A applied at ~10 µg/L (ppb) via calibrated drip stations (steel drums for 6-hr application, smaller cans for 3-hr/1-hr applications on tributaries/springs, backpack sprayers for marsh segments, impregnated sand for stagnant pools); detoxified downstream with HTH at 1 mg/L.
- **Treatment Efficacy**: All caged sentinel fish died within ~1 hr 40 min of toxicant arrival; no living fish detected in treated reaches within 3 hours of treatment onset — confirming rapid, effective toxicant action consistent with prior literature (Berger et al. 1969; Gilderhus et al. 1969).
- **First Renovation Failure (1977)**: Despite successful fish kill, reintroduced Arizona trout (91 individuals, Sept 1977) vanished by the following year's survey; only young-of-year brook trout (up to 60 individuals) were recovered, with no adult trout of any species found — implicating incomplete toxicant distribution/retention in cienega "tunneled channels," survival of protected redds via spring upwelling, and/or handling/stress mortality of reintroduced natives.
- **Second Renovation Success (1978)**: Retreatment with Antimycin A eradicated remaining brook trout population (fewer than 200 fry, no adults recovered), validating multi-treatment approach for complex habitats.
- **Reintroduction Outcome (1980–1981)**: 62 Arizona trout translocated from Paradise Creek in October 1980; July 1981 survey confirmed adult survival via angling, but no evidence of successful reproduction was documented.
- **Historical Reproduction Timeline**: Native trout in Ord Creek had not successfully reproduced for at least 3 years prior to treatment; last known successful spawn events occurred in 1973–1974, with an observed but non-recruiting spawning event in 1975.
- **Management Implications**: Findings support the need for early intervention (before complete nonnative invasion), comprehensive pretreatment surveys incorporating hydrological/habitat complexity, and consideration of trade-offs between endangered species recovery and non-target impacts on native invertebrate fauna during [[Piscicide Treatment]] programs.
