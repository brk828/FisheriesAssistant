# Check List of the Fresh-water and Anadromous Fishes of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #checklist

## Executive Summary

This 1950 paper by Leo Shapovalov and William A. Dill, of the Bureau of Fish Conservation, California Division of Fish and Game, presents a standardized checklist of the freshwater and anadromous fishes of [[California]]. It has two stated purposes: to provide a generally acceptable list of known species as a first step toward a comprehensive [[California Freshwater Fish Handbook]], and to give the [[California Division of Fish and Game]] official common names for fishes of State fresh waters. The main list includes 101 full species, comprising 64 native freshwater and anadromous species, 11 native species that occasionally penetrate fresh water, and 26 introduced species that have established breeding populations. A supplementary list of 22 unsuccessful or uncertain introductions documents species for which no evidence of established populations was found.

## Background & Description

The authors note that the first scientific description of a California freshwater fish was published by Dr. W. P. Gibbons in 1854. Accumulated literature has not made compilation easier, because growing knowledge of [[Systematic Relationships Among California Fishes]] revealed greater complexity. Earlier efforts were judged inadequate: [[Evermann and Clark 1921]] produced a list that was less inclusive, contained errors, and lacked official sanction. Marine common names had already been addressed by Walford (1931) and [[Roedel 1948]].

**Scope and inclusion criteria**
- Native forms were included if their taxonomic identity had not been disproved in the literature. When in doubt, the authors chose inclusiveness (e.g., a single 1890 collection of *Plagopterus* was retained).
- Exotic species were included in the main list only if breeding populations were known to have survived.
- Fishes recorded only outside California were excluded, though the [[Colorado River]] was included on the Arizona side, including records from the mouth of the [[Gila River]].
- Marine and brackish species that enter fresh water were listed. Strictly marine species introduced to the [[Salton Sea]] were omitted.
- Hybrids were omitted despite recorded interspecific and intergeneric hybrids.

**Common name selection rules**
The authors ranked five criteria, each to be applied "other considerations being equal":
1. Agree with names in actual common use, or with published literature; avoid "book names."
2. Agree with other authoritative lists, such as the [[American Fisheries Society]] (1948), the Outdoor Writers Association of America (1948), and Roedel (1948).
3. Indicate relationship without confusing it.
4. Be descriptive.
5. Prefer short, distinctive, catchy, or euphonious names.

A consistent naming scheme gives all species in a genus a shared base name with prefixes. For example, all *Siphateles* are "Chub," the *Siphateles bicolor* group are "Tui Chub," and *S. b. formosus* is the "Sacramento Tui Chub." The authors follow [[Alden H. Miller]] in believing that only full species deserve common names, but they assigned names to subspecies as well, so historically important names such as "Kokanee" and "Piute" are not lost. Some names are retained even after taxonomic revision.

**Acknowledgments:** Drafts were reviewed by [[Carl L. Hubbs]], [[Robert R. Miller]], [[George S. Myers]], and others. The authors note that not all disagreements were resolved.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The checklist covers primarily fluvial and anadromous fishes, which reflects the geographic and habitat diversity of the State's waters. Several taxa are tied to specific environments:

- **Lamprey and sturgeon** (e.g., [[Pacific Lamprey]], [[White Sturgeon]], [[Green Sturgeon]]) are anadromous or large-river species.
- **Salmonids** include anadromous forms (e.g., [[Steelhead Rainbow Trout]]) and landlocked or resident forms (e.g., [[Kokanee]], [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]).
- **Desert and saline waters** support endemic pupfishes of the genus *Cyprinodon* (e.g., [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Owens Valley Pupfish]]). The name "pupfish" replaced several earlier names including "desert minnow," "killifish," and "pygmy fish."
- **The Colorado River** contributes species such as the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Woundfin]].
- **Brackish and estuarine taxa** include the [[Tidewater Goby]], [[Longjaw Mudsucker]], and [[Arrow Goby]].
- **Introduced sport and forage fishes** such as [[Largemouth Black Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Striped Bass]], and [[Mosquitofish]] are widespread in the State.

The paper also notes that many native cyprinids are morphologically similar, which historically led laymen to refer to them only as "chub" or "shiner."

## Key Findings

**1. Main checklist (101 full species)**
- 64 native freshwater and anadromous species.
- 11 native species that occasionally penetrate fresh water (marked with "O" in the list), such as [[Pacific Herring]], [[Eulachon]], and [[Starry Flounder]].
- 26 introduced species that have established populations (marked with an asterisk), such as [[American Shad]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Eastern Brook Trout]], [[Carp]], and [[Channel Catfish]].
- Subspecies are listed under their parent species, for example:
  - *Salmo clarkii* subspecies: [[Coast Cutthroat Trout]], [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], [[Piute Cutthroat Trout]], [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]].
  - *Salmo gairdnerii* subspecies: [[Steelhead Rainbow Trout]], [[Shasta Rainbow Trout]], [[Kern River Rainbow Trout]], [[Eagle Lake Rainbow Trout]], and others.
  - *Salmo agua-bonita*: [[Golden Trout]] (South Fork of Kern and Little Kern forms).
- Families represented include Petromyzontidae, Acipenseridae, Elopidae, Clupeidae, Osmeridae, Salmonidae, Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Ictaluridae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Pleuronectidae, Serranidae, Percidae, Centrarchidae, Mugilidae, Embiotocidae, Cottidae, Gasterosteidae, and Gobiidae.

**2. Supplementary list (22 entries)**
These are exotic fishes that were unsuccessfully introduced, reported in error, or of uncertain occurrence. The authors state that they know of "no demonstrable evidence" that any are successfully established in California fresh waters today. Notable examples:
- [[Atlantic Salmon]] and [[Landlocked Atlantic Salmon]]: plantings occurred, but authentic recent records are lacking.
- [[American Arctic Grayling]]: a brief success in Yosemite National Park after plantings from 1929 to 1933, with the last authentic report in 1934.
- [[Muskellunge]] and [[Pike]]: introductions failed.
- [[Medaka]]: admitted on the basis of a 1935 statement and a 1943 conversation, not a verified specimen.
- [[Walleye]]: introduced in 1874 with no records of survival.
- [[Rock Bass]]: literature records of introductions are inexact; no verifiable record of occurrence was found.
- [[Blue-spotted Sunfish]] and the "Lepomis euryorus" record: the authors consider these misidentifications. The latter was later shown to be a hybrid of [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Pumpkinseed]].

**3. Taxonomic and nomenclatural notes**
- *Salmo clarkii pleuriticus* records may reflect misidentification, but the authors retained the form.
- *Plagopterus* (Woundfin) rests on a single 1890 collection.
- Several trout and sucker groupings were acknowledged as contentious, with a firm nomenclature still lacking for these "plastic groups."
- *Lepomis euryorus* was confirmed as a hybrid, illustrating the importance of verification in exotic records.

**4. Purpose and value**
The checklist is intended to serve as the foundation for a future comprehensive handbook and to establish official common names for use in all California Division of Fish and Game publications.
