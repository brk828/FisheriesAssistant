# The British Taeniopterygidae (Plecoptera)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Plecoptera #Taeniopterygidae #aquatic-entomology

> **Note on scope:** This source is an entomological taxonomic revision of the stonefly family [[Taeniopterygidae]] (Order [[Plecoptera]]) in Britain, not a fish-focused paper. Fish are mentioned only incidentally as part of habitat descriptions (river fauna context). The note below is structured to faithfully reflect the paper's actual taxon of focus — aquatic insect nymphs and imagines — while retaining the requested Obsidian format.

## Executive Summary

Hynes (1957) revises the taxonomy of the four British species of stonefly family [[Taeniopterygidae]]: **[[Taeniopteryx Nebulosa]]** (described here as a new subspecies, *T. nebulosa britannica*), **[[Rhabdiopteryx Anglica]]**, **[[Brachyptera Putata]]**, and **[[Brachyptera Risi]]**. Using both adult (imago) and nymphal morphology, the paper resolves long-standing identification problems in prior keys (Hynes 1940; Kimmins 1950), clarifies confused synonymies (particularly *B. putata* vs. *B. trifasciata* and *B. monilicornis*), and proposes new diagnostic keys for both adults and nymphs. The work also documents the restricted and disjunct distribution of *R. anglica* (four Yorkshire sites, one Radnorshire site) and *B. putata* (Scotland only), situating these species within specific lotic freshwater habitats, including one limestone river (River Rye) noted incidentally to support **trout, grayling, bullhead, and minnows** alongside a rich invertebrate assemblage.

## Background & Description

### Taeniopteryx nebulosa britannica ssp. n.
- Distinguished from continental *T. nebulosa* by: pronounced median humps on front and rear of nymphal pronotum (visible at 3 mm nymph length); tendency toward short wings in males (sometimes only reaching abdomen tip); male epiproct (supra-anal lobe) **narrower** than cerci (vs. wider in continental form); faint crescentic transverse bar across wings in both sexes (more visible in females).
- Coxal gills present at nymph length of 2 mm; abdominal spines appear between 2–3 mm (smaller thresholds than Swedish specimens per Brinck 1949).
- Adult diagnostic: coxa bears an inner posterior scar; fore wing lacks subcubital cross-veins; male often short-winged with 1-segmented cerci and a ventral vesicle on the ninth sternum; female subgenital plate broad, not drawn out.

### Rhabdiopteryx anglica Kimmins
- Closely allied to the Finnish *R. acuminata* Klapálek (known only from a single, possibly damaged, dried type specimen); differences (cerci segmentation, tergite shape) suspected artefacts of specimen preservation.
- Adult diagnostic: coxae without scars; fore wing usually with 1–2 subcubital cross-veins (variable); males fully winged, cerci multi-segmented, no ninth sternum vesicle; female subgenital plate long, drawn to a point.
- Nymph closely resembles *B. risi* nymph; distinguished by: equal dorsal/ventral darkness (vs. paler venter in Brachyptera); epicranial suture forms a distinct angular corner between ocelli (vs. rounded in Brachyptera); clothing hairs on abdominal terga lie flat, not upstanding bristles; cerci lack long dorsal hairs on basal segments.
- Coloration: pale yellow ventrally with brown/yellow dorsal pattern; legs pale except brown femur mark and tibial tip; full-grown nymphs 8–10 mm.

### Brachyptera putata (Newman)
- Long history of confused synonymy with *B. trifasciata*; Morton (1911) established distinctness; author finds strongest affinity instead with **[[Brachyptera Monilicornis]]** — shares moniliform antennae, three dark wing bands, darkened wing tips, and closely similar male genitalia (differing mainly in cercal basal segment: elongate/lightly chitinised in *monilicornis* vs. bladder-like in *putata*).
- Male wings shortened to abdomen tip (unlike the disproportionate fore/hind wing shortening seen in *B. trifasciata*).
- Nymph: brown/yellow, paler ventrally; complex meso-/metanotal pattern with three characteristic side stripes; pale tarsi with clearly defined dark tibial area; transverse rows of dark spots across abdominal terga; median dark spot on tenth tergum; male paraprocts less laterally twisted than in *B. risi*.

### Brachyptera risi Morton
- Widespread across Britain and Europe; no distinguishable difference found between British, Danish, and Swiss specimens.
- Adult diagnostic: antennae filiform (basal segments longer than wide, vs. moniliform in *putata*); fore wing apex not darkened; male fully winged; female fore wing typically with 3 (occasionally 4) branches to vein Cu₁.
- Nymph: dull brownish, only slightly paler ventrally; simple meso-/metanotal pattern; darkened tarsi; tibiae lack clearly defined dark area; terga uniformly brown (no transverse dark spot rows); tenth tergum with two small pale anterior marks; male paraprocts drawn into heavily chitinised, laterally/forward-projecting hooks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **River Rye (Yorkshire)** — habitat of *R. anglica*: ~15 m wide, riffles ~30 cm deep interspersed with pools; limestone bed of rough stones/gravel with *Fontinalis* and *Hypnum* moss; intermittent drying in some years. Supports **trout, grayling, bullhead, and minnows**, plus 14 other Plecoptera species and 5 Ephemeroptera species — a typical limestone river invertebrate assemblage. The species' restricted distribution (4 Yorkshire sites + 1 Radnorshire site) is not readily explained by habitat ecology alone, since suitable-seeming water bodies elsewhere lack it.
- **Strath Oykell, Sutherland** — habitat of *B. putata*: wide, lake-like river with muddy bed partly covered by *Littorella*, stony margins in places, *Carex* fringing elsewhere; contrasts sharply with the small, swift, stony stream habitat typical of *B. risi* ([[Brachyptera Risi]]). Co-occurring stonefly nymphs included *T. nebulosa*, *Nemoura cinerea*, *Nemurella picteti*, with occasional *N. avicularis* and *Leuctra hippopus*. Mayfly nymphs *Leptophlebia marginata*, *Centroptilum luteolum*, and *Procloeon rufulum* were common, alongside oligochaetes and chironomid larvae in the mud substrate.
- *B. putata* and *B. monilicornis* are both characterized as **large river species** (per Aubert 1946; Illies 1955), reinforcing the taxonomic affinity proposed between them.
- *B. risi* occupies **small, swift, stony streams** across Britain and Europe — an ecologically distinct niche from *B. putata*.
- *T. nebulosa britannica* occurs throughout mainland Britain; notably **absent from Ireland**.

## Key Findings

1. **New subspecies erected**: *Taeniopteryx nebulosa britannica* ssp. n., distinguished from continental *T. nebulosa* by nymphal pronotal humps, male wing shortening tendency, narrower male epiproct relative to cerci, and a faint wing bar — differences considered consistent enough across all British males to warrant subspecific status (extending Aubert's 1950 partial observations).
2. **Taxonomic relationship clarified**: *Rhabdiopteryx anglica* is likely very closely related to, or possibly conspecific/subspecific with, the poorly known Finnish *R. acuminata*; differences in the type specimen are attributed to preservation artefacts (shrinkage, possible cercal breakage) rather than genuine morphological distinction.
3. **Synonymy corrected**: *Brachyptera putata* is confirmed as a valid species distinct from *B. trifasciata* (resolving persistent confusion in Claassen 1940 and Aubert 1946), and shown to be most closely allied to *B. monilicornis* based on wing pattern, antennal form, and male/female genitalia — with the *trifasciata*/*risi* species-group distinction proposed to rest solely on antennal shape (moniliform vs. filiform) going forward.
4. **Restricted, disjunct distribution documented**: *B. putata* is confirmed as exclusively Scottish (contra earlier claims it replaces *trifasciata* in England), while *R. anglica* is known from only five total localities (four in Yorkshire, one in Radnorshire), with apparent local extinction at one historic site (Knaresborough).
5. **Improved identification keys produced**: New adult and nymphal keys are presented using more reliable characters (antennal segment shape, coxal scars/gills, abdominal bristle orientation, tibial/tarsal coloration, tergite spotting patterns) replacing previously unreliable characters (e.g., subcubital cross-vein counts, which vary intraspecifically).
6. **Two novel nymphal diagnostic characters** identified for separating [[Rhabdiopteryx]] from [[Brachyptera]] genera: (a) flat clothing hairs vs. upstanding bristles on abdominal terga, and (b) absence vs. presence of long dorsal hairs on basal cercal segments — validated across British and four continental comparative species.
