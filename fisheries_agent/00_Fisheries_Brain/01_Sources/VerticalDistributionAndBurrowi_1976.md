# Vertical Distribution and Burrowing Behavior of the Fingernail Clam (*Sphaerium transversum*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #malacology #Sphaeriidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study by Gale (1976) investigates the vertical distribution and burrowing behavior of the fingernail clam, **[[Sphaerium transversum]]**, a small bivalve (family [[Sphaeriidae]]) that plays a critical role in freshwater benthic food webs. Field sampling in Pool 19 of the [[Mississippi River]] revealed that clams are unevenly distributed with depth, with smaller/newborn clams burrowing significantly deeper (up to 16 cm) than larger adults (confined to the upper 2.6 cm). Complementary laboratory experiments at Lake Texoma, Oklahoma, demonstrated that newborn clams actively burrow within seconds of birth, reaching depths up to 24 cm depending on substrate composition. The paper proposes that deep burrowing represents a "resting-state" adaptive strategy that may buffer clam populations against [[Predation]], disease, and adverse water conditions, with direct implications for the food supply available to [[Diving Ducks]] and benthivorous fish such as those studied by Jude (1968) and Ranthum (1969).

## Background & Description

- **Size at birth**: 2.0–2.4 mm in length.
- **Adult size**: Often exceeds 13 mm in length.
- **Calyculate clams**: Individuals with "capped beaks," believed to be born in autumn and to overwinter buried in substrate.
- **Shell coloration change**: Clams held deep in substrate for extended periods developed notably darkened shells, likely due to anaerobic conditions.
- **Physiological state during burrowing**: Evidence of a "resting-state" — empty guts (non-feeding), and drastically reduced heart rate (a few beats/minute) under anaerobic, sealed conditions; clams survived at least 2 weeks in this state.
- No significant relationship found between newborn clam **body size** and **maximum burrowing depth** (104 deepest-burrowing clams averaged 1.88 mm vs. 84 clams in upper 2.5 cm, same average size).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Benthic freshwater substrates ranging from coarse sand to soft clayey silt; found in river pools (Mississippi River Pool 19), sluggish creek mud (Pennington Creek), and sandy, current-swept creek beds (Briar Creek).
- **Substrate preference for burrowing depth**:
  - Deepest penetration in **soft clayey silt** and **sandy-mud (85% sand)** substrates (lab: up to 24 cm).
  - Shallowest penetration in **coarse sand** and pure **mud** (lab: up to 11 cm in mud).
  - Substrate resistance appears to be the key driver — clams burrow deepest where **resistance is lowest**.
- **Trophic role**: *S. transversum* is a key prey item for:
  - **[[Lesser Scaup]]** (*Aythya affinis*) — Pool 19 supports "hundreds of thousands" of scaup feeding almost entirely on this clam (Thompson, 1973).
  - **Freshwater fish** (Jude, 1968; Ranthum, 1969) — cited as an important dietary component in Pool 19 fish communities, linking this bivalve directly to [[Benthic Food Web]] dynamics relevant to fisheries ecology.
  - **Leeches** (Gale, 1973).
- **Parasitism**: Adult clams are frequently parasitized by rediae (trematode larvae), which can render them non-gravid and disrupt reproduction — corroborated by Wenke (1965) and Heard (1965) in related Sphaeriidae/Pisidium species.
- **Comparative ecology**: Related species (*S. simile*, *S. occidentale*, *Pisidium* spp.) show similar or shallower burrowing behavior in other lake systems (Selenter Lake, Douglas Lake, Lake Simcoe), suggesting burrowing depth is substrate- and species-specific.
- **Evolutionary hypothesis**: Burrowing behavior may be a vestigial trait originating from ancestral populations in **temporary/vernal ponds**, where burial in bottom sediments was essential for surviving seasonal desiccation (cf. Kenk 1949 on *S. occidentale*; Thomas 1963 on *S. partumeium*).

## Key Findings

1. **Field vertical distribution (Pool 19)**: Clams occurred to a maximum depth of 16 cm; clams >3.0 mm were restricted to the upper 2.6 cm, while 59% of the total clam population occurred deeper than 2.6 cm and averaged only 2.1 mm in length — indicating newborns are the primary deep-burrowing cohort.
2. **Substrate-dependent penetration**: Laboratory Experiment B showed **statistically significant (p<0.01)** differences in burrowing depth across substrate types, with mean penetration of 6.2 cm in 85% sandy-mud vs. 3.7 cm in mud.
3. **Burrowing timeline**: Newborns burrow out of sight within seconds; 73% burrowed >2.5 cm within 12 hours; burrowing continued incrementally for 24–48 hours, with no statistically significant change after that (Experiment A, F-test, p>0.05).
4. **Reburrowing capability**: Clams displaced to the surface after burrowing readily **reburrowed** (Experiment C), sometimes to greater depths than initially, indicating retained behavioral tendency and available energy reserves for at least 3 days.
5. **No density effect**: Clam density (14,148–70,741 clams/m²) had no significant effect on burrowing depth (Experiment D); observed differences were instead attributed to **parental stock origin** (Pennington Creek vs. Briar Creek), suggesting a genetic/physiological or prenatal-environment influence on offspring burrowing behavior.
6. **Birth environment effect**: Clams born in a pan of lake water burrowed deeper (mean 1.5 cm) than clams born directly inside substrate tubes (mean 0.8 cm) (Experiment E), implicating handling/postnatal environment as a behavioral modifier.
7. **No upward migration**: In a stacked-substrate experiment (26 cm sand + 16 cm sand overlay), no clams moved into the upper 16 cm after 10 days, indicating burrowing is a one-way, non-feeding "resting" behavior rather than active foraging movement.
8. **Survival/ecological significance**: Deep burrowing is hypothesized to serve as a **population self-regulation mechanism** and a refuge from turbulence, predation ([[Predation|fish, duck, and leech predation]]), disease, parasites, and transient poor water quality — supported by a field observation where a surface-dwelling captive clam population was decimated while deep-buried individuals survived unharmed.
9. **Seasonal reproduction pattern**: Calyculate (capped-beak) clams appear to be born in autumn and overwinter buried; the presence of many deep-buried clams in July samples suggests either spring-born cohorts or a bimodal strategy where some summer-born clams grow immediately while others burrow and remain dormant.
