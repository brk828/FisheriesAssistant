# An Evaluation of Massachusetts' Trout Stream Fishery

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary
This paper (Mullan, 1955) synthesizes multi-year harvest and population studies conducted across seven major Massachusetts river drainages ([[Quaboag River]], [[Deerfield River]], [[Westfield River]], [[Millers River]], [[Squannacook River]], [[Ipswich River]], [[Merrimack River]]) to evaluate the efficacy and biology of the state's put-and-take [[Trout Stocking]] program. Using [[Rotenone]], [[Cresol]], and electric shocking for population sampling, plus tagged/fin-clipped hatchery trout for harvest studies, the work demonstrates that Massachusetts trout streams are overwhelmingly dependent on hatchery stocking (80–96%+ of catch), have low intrinsic productivity, and are strongly constrained by high summer water temperatures. The report concludes with sweeping regulatory recommendations — several of which (season dates, size limits) were adopted by the Division of Fisheries and Game in 1954–1955.

## Background & Description
- **Hatchery trout stocked**: Legal size, 6–20 inches, averaging 7–9 inches; recommendation made to raise minimum hatchery size objective to 8 inches.
- **[[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*)**:
  - First spawn as yearlings at 3.5–5.9 inches.
  - Average legal size small: 6–8 inches, with slight scattering up to 14 inches.
  - Growth reported as *below* literature averages for the species.
- **[[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*)**:
  - First spawn as two-year-olds at 7.0–9.0 inches.
  - Average legal wild size larger: 8–10 inches, with good scattering up to 22+ inches.
  - Growth reported as *average* compared to literature.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*)**:
  - No native/indigenous populations detected in any study — entirely absent as wild reproducers; occurs only via stocking.
- Associated non-salmonid species identified in headwater populations: [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonnii*), [[Blacknosed Dace]] (*Rhinichthys atratulus*), [[Longnosed Dace]] (*Rhinichthys cataractae*), [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*), [[Sculpin]] (*Cottus cognatus*), [[Common Shiner]] (*Notropis cornutus*).
- Main-stem "trash"/warmwater species: [[Fallfish]] (*Leucosomus corporalis*), [[Redfin Pickerel]] (*Esox americanus*), [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus nebulosus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), [[Chain Pickerel]] (*Esox niger*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Physiography**: Massachusetts topography grades from mountainous Berkshires (west, 1,500–2,000 ft) to flat eastern lowlands, with rolling uplands centrally. Rocks dominantly Paleozoic metamorphic/igneous; soils stony till with alluvial cover.
- **Climate/Hydrology**: Short growing season (160–180 days); precipitation 2–6 in., evenly distributed as rain/snow. Streamflow almost entirely surface-runoff dependent — peak flow in March, minimum flow August–September, with extreme (hundred- to thousand-percent) seasonal flow variability.
- **Thermal regime**: Larger main-stem streams closely track atmospheric temperature, often reaching mid-80s°F in summer — exceeding trout tolerance. Smaller, shaded tributaries stay cooler (rarely >75°F, often <70°F), providing critical refugia for [[Wild Trout]] populations.
- **Water chemistry**: pH range 5.7–7.3; color ranges dark brown to clear/white; small tributaries generally pollution-free, but larger streams affected by industrial/domestic sewage.
- **Habitat structure**: Western (Berkshire) streams = fast whitewater over rubble/boulder/bedrock; eastern lowland streams = sluggish, silt-bottomed, often impounded; central streams = mixed character.
- **Population zonation**:
  - Headwater tributaries: indigenous brook/brown trout populations coexisting with small benthic/forage species; standing crop few lbs/acre up to ~50 lbs/acre, with trout making up 50–100% of biomass.
  - Main-stem rivers: dominated by "trash" and warmwater panfish/game fish, few trout; standing crops 15–300 lbs/acre, dominated numerically/by weight by white sucker.
  - Mid-sized streams: mixed populations; brown trout typically larger and often more abundant than brook trout.
- **Competition**: Food/growth studies indicate severe intra- and interspecific competition; trout diet found largely insectivorous despite available forage fish stocks.
- **Carryover/Mortality**: Survival of stocked hatchery trout from spring stocking to mid-summer estimated at <15% in Westfield, Millers, and Squannacook drainages; ~50% of non-angled hatchery trout lost to non-harvest mortality between stocking and summer sampling. Carryover of uncaught hatchery trout to the following season deemed inconsequential.
- Wild trout populations described as being in ecological balance regarding reproduction, growth, recruitment, and mortality, though brook trout production is limited by overpopulation and the (then-existing) 6-inch minimum size limit.

## Key Findings
1. **Hatchery dependency**: 90–95%+ of the trout catch on major main-stem streams is composed of current-year [[Hatchery Trout]]; tributary streams show lower hatchery contribution (≤75%), supporting more wild trout fishing.
2. **Species-specific return rates**: Average creel return of stocked trout ranges 30–50% (Table 1), varying 2–3% to 80%+ between lots. [[Brook Trout]] give best average returns, [[Brown Trout]] the poorest, [[Rainbow Trout]] intermediate — though differences are not statistically significant.
3. **Stocking timing effects**: 60–80% of trout are stocked in March during peak flow/flooding, which substantially reduces returns (about half) compared to stocking near opening day or in-season under stable/low water. Severe 1954 flooding in the Merrimack/Ipswich drainages reinforced flooding as a major loss factor.
4. **Size-selective returns**: Tag return data (78,668 tagged trout, 3 years) show larger stocked trout (8 in.+) yield better returns than smaller trout (6–8 in.), especially in brown and rainbow trout — mechanism unclear (angler selectivity vs. swimming/survival ability).
5. **Temperature as the primary limiting factor**: Excessively warm summer temperatures on main-stem streams correlate with the poorest catch-per-hour indices, lowest wild trout contribution, and shortest effective angling season — identified as the principal limiting factor for late-season success and wild trout production.
6. **Low system productivity**: Massachusetts streams show markedly low productivity (10–40 lbs/acre standing crop) relative to other U.S. regions.
7. **Economic framing of put-and-take fishery**: Creeled trout cost $2.00–$4.00/lb (75¢–$1.33 per 9-inch fish); described as a "sociological marketing and production problem" more dependent on economics than biology. Only 25% of anglers account for 75% of the total trout harvest.
8. **Policy impact**: Findings directly informed regulatory changes — the trout season was shifted (3rd Saturday in April to 3rd Saturday in October) and all trout size limits were abolished by the Division of Fisheries and Game in 1954–1955.
9. **Management recommendations**: Included maintaining current hatchery production levels, raising stocked trout size to 8 in., abolishing outdated regulations (size limits, night-fishing restrictions), reduced early-season bag limits (12→6 fish/day for first 4 weeks), legalized spring sucker spearing as rough-fish control, and prioritizing stream-bank vegetation over structural improvements (V-dams, deflectors) to address thermal limitations.
10. **Research gap flagged**: Potential of [[Stream Rehabilitation]] via chemical treatment (analogous to lake rehabilitation) to manipulate species competition and improve trout carryover/growth was noted as an unexplored but promising avenue.
