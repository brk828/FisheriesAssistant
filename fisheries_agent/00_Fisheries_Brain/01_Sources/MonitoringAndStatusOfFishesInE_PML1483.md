# Monitoring and Status of Fishes in Eagle Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 summary report to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (Phoenix Area Office), prepared by Paul C. Marsh of [[Arizona State University]] under federal grant 5-FG-32-00450, documents fish community monitoring in Eagle Creek, a tributary of the [[Gila River]] in eastern Arizona. The stream supports a native fish assemblage that exceeds that of [[Aravaipa Creek]] in species richness, but it also hosts established non-native fishes that threaten native species. Fishes were sampled in July 1995 and June and July 1996. The report concludes that the community should be monitored regularly and that the federally threatened [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] populations warrant priority protection.

## Background & Description

Eagle Creek drains the southern edge of the [[White Mountains]], with high-elevation headwaters and lower reaches flowing through hot, arid desert. Nine native fish species are known, along with the reintroduced [[Razorback Sucker]]; a native salmonid is extirpated. Spikedace and loach minnow are both federally listed as threatened and are monotypic genera endemic to middle-elevation lotic habitats of the Gila River basin.

Multiple non-native species are established, including [[Red Shiner]] and [[Flathead Catfish]]. The report characterizes the native community as a potential barometer of regional stream health.

Sampling used electrofishing (riffles and runs), entanglement netting (pools), and seining (general collecting). Specimens were identified, counted, health-checked, and released, with vouchers accessioned into the ASU Museum. Collections were conducted under state and federal scientific permits. Access to sites on [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]] lands was not authorized, limiting coverage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** Stream flow constrained sampling timing; the planned spring and autumn sampling was replaced by three summer surveys.
- **Canyon reaches:** Gorge sections between Sheep Wash and the [[Phelps Dodge Diversion Dam]] are difficult to access, so sampling there is incomplete.
- **Diversion dam:** A roughly 5.0 m vertical-fall dam acts as a fish barrier except during major floods. Native fishes persist upstream, while non-natives are swept downstream and cannot return.
- **Flooding:** Floods removed fishes from the system in 1978 and 1983, and red shiners and channel catfish recolonized within a few years.
- **Non-native pathways:** Stocking records are incomplete. Possible sources include stocking, bait-bucket transfers, contamination of stocked fish, and movement from the Black River via an inter-basin aqueduct and [[Willow Creek]].
- **Water quality:** Lower-reach fish were stunted and some were unhealthy, which the field observer attributed to possible non-point-source pollution.

## Key Findings

**Community composition (1995–1996):**
- A total of 15 fish species was captured across the July 1995 and June–July 1996 surveys: 7 native and 8 exotic.
- Spikedace and razorback sucker were not captured in these surveys. Spikedace was last recorded in October 1989.
- In July 1987, native species made up 98.2% of specimens above the diversion dam but only 2.2% below it (the lower site was dominated by non-natives).

**Loach minnow:**
- Re-discovered in Eagle Creek in 1994 after more than four decades absent (last collected in 1950 by [[Robert R. Miller]]).
- The population appears localized. Genetic (mtDNA) analysis showed it is unique among sampled populations, and it had no mtDNA variation, suggesting small numbers and a probable past bottleneck.
- In 1996, loach minnow was absent from the first road crossing below Honeymoon Campground, possibly because cobble barriers altered habitat there.

**Razorback sucker:**
- Stocked between 1991 and 1993 by Arizona Game and Fish Department and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service personnel. The last recorded stocking was 6,727 fish averaging 10.1 cm TL on 2 June 1989 near the Willow Creek confluence.
- Only sporadic recaptures were recorded, including two sub-adults in the diversion impoundment in August 1996 and 10 individuals downstream of Sheep Wash in June 1987.

**Non-native fishes:**
- Non-natives are common in suitable habitat, especially below the diversion dam, where they comprised more than 75% of collections in 1987 and 1989.
- [[Red Shiner]] is considered a serious threat to native fishes and is implicated in declines of other species elsewhere in the Gila basin.
- [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] are predators on native suckers and minnows.
- [[Mosquitofish]] is poorly adapted to the natural flow regime and does poorly in Eagle Creek.
- Rainbow trout are routinely stocked for a put-and-take fishery at Honeymoon and occur downstream, and a cutthroat trout was captured in a pool above the diversion dam in July 1994.

**Recommendations:**
- Continue regular monitoring of native and non-native fishes, targeting spikedace and loach minnow populations.
- Seek [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]] cooperation to allow access to Tribal lands and adjacent [[U.S. Forest Service]] lands.
- Compile stocking records and examine fishes in the Black River, Willow Creek, and the Phelps Dodge inter-basin transfer channel.
- Coordinate among the [[Phelps Dodge Corporation]], Tribe, Forest Service, Arizona Game and Fish Department, Bureau of Reclamation, and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to conserve the stream and its watershed.

**Appended material:** The report includes a 1985–1996 collection record list, a 1991 reprint of the ASU/Eagle Creek fish survey (Marsh et al. 1990, which documented 9 native and 11 non-native species), 1989 and 1993 field memoranda, 1994–1995 trip reports with catch tables, a 1996 survey summary, and a book review that is unrelated to fish.
