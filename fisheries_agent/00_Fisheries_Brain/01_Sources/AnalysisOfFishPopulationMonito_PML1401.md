# Fish Population Monitoring in the Gila River Basin, Arizona (2000–2004)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This report analyzes five years (2000–2004) of standardized fish monitoring data from the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) canal system, interconnected regional canals, and selected reaches of the Gila, Salt, and San Pedro rivers. Prepared for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] by Paul C. Marsh and Brian R. Kesner, it documents species distributions and assemblage structure using graphical rather than statistical trend analysis, because earlier work found formal tests unreliable for individual species. The program recorded 29 new species records over 1995–2004 and documented apparent local losses of three native fishes from the Gila River. The authors judge the monitoring program successful in detecting new species but recommend more consistent station sampling and greater use of auxiliary gears.

## Background & Description

The CAP aqueduct delivers Colorado River water from [[Lake Havasu]] into the Gila River basin. A [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] biological opinion (1994) found the project would jeopardize four federally listed native fishes: [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Gila Topminnow]]. It also found adverse impacts to critical habitat for the first three. The resulting reasonable and prudent alternative required a long-term monitoring program, with two goals: detecting new species and detecting long-term trends in community distribution and structure.

Standardized monitoring began in 1995 under the [[Long-Term Monitoring Plan]] (Clarkson 1996), which defines six watercourses: the CAP aqueduct, [[Salt River Project]] canals, the [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]], the Salt River, the Gila River, and perennial reaches of the San Pedro River. Earlier evaluations (Wilson 1996; Abarca and Allison 2000; Allison 2000) concluded that only large-scale community changes would be detectable, because many species are rare, catch data are highly variable, and the program's geographic scope is broad. Allison (2000) found that trends could be adequately described for only two of 17 species, [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Red Shiner]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Fixed reaches (one to four per system) were stratified by geomorphology and hydrology on natural streams, and by [[Electric Fish Barrier]] locations and geopolitical divisions on canals. The CAP Canal has no electric barrier. Stations were sampled as upper, middle, and lower sites on streams, and at pumping-plant forebays on the CAP.

**Gear.** [[Backpack Electrofishing]] was the standard stream method, supplemented by seining. Boat electrofishing, trammel and hoop nets, minnow traps, and trot lines were used on canals, with drawdown sampling in shallow canal water. Only quantitative electrofishing data were analyzed for streams.

**Data handling.** Records were double-entered in Key Entry III software, merged in Microsoft Access, and combined across age classes. Stream data comprised 79 station samples, leaving 34 of 40 reach-year combinations for analysis. Six reach-year combinations had no station samples. Relative abundance was shown in stacked bar graphs, with species under 3% of catch grouped as "Other."

**Drought and drying.** Drought and stream drying affected sampling. In 2004 only four of 11 possible Gila stations were sampled because no water was present, and the lower Gila reach was generally dry after 2002.

## Key Findings

**New species records.** Twenty-nine previously unreported species records were documented: 22 in 1995–1999 and seven in 2000–2004. By system:
- San Pedro River: 2 (e.g., [[Bluegill]], Channel Catfish)
- Gila River: 5 (e.g., [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Flathead Catfish]])
- Salt River: 8 (e.g., [[Blue Tilapia]], [[Redbelly Tilapia]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Red Shiner]])
- CAP Canal: 4 (e.g., [[Grass Carp]], [[White Bass]])
- SRP Arizona (North) Canal: 3 (e.g., [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], [[Yellow Perch]])
- SRP South Canal: 1 ([[Striped Bass]])
- FCG Canal: 6 (e.g., [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Largemouth Bass]])

**Native species declines.** [[Longfin Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], and Sonora Sucker were captured in every year from 1995 to 1999 and at least once in all reaches sampled. In 2002 only Sonora Sucker was recorded, and none of the three was collected in 2003 or 2004. The authors call this potentially alarming and attribute the likely cause to non-native fishes, since habitat loss is not evident where other species persist. The cause remains unconfirmed.

**Catch-effort relationship.** Correlation was weak for the San Pedro (r = 0.23) and Gila (r = 0.44) rivers under backpack electrofishing, but strong for the Salt River (r = 0.94) under boat electrofishing. The authors attribute the difference to stream characteristics and gear.

**Stream assemblages.** Twenty fish taxa were recorded from the three natural streams: 10 from the San Pedro, 13 from the Gila, and 15 from the Salt. Only four are native to the basin: Longfin dace, Roundtail chub, Sonora sucker, and Desert sucker. Non-native Red shiner was the most abundant species overall, followed by Mosquitofish and native Longfin dace.
- San Pedro: composition changed little over five years. Longfin dace appeared to decline and Desert sucker to increase. The downstream reach was overwhelmingly Mosquitofish in 2001.
- Gila: Red shiner and Mosquitofish alternated in dominance, with Red shiner dominant in three of five years.
- Salt: dominated by Largemouth bass and Sonora sucker; Sonora sucker predominated in only one year.

**Canal assemblages.**
- CAP Canal (15 species; 3 reaches): dominated by sunfishes until 2004, when Red shiner also became abundant. The middle reach yielded low catch (average 28 fish per year), with a different dominant species each year.
- SRP South Canal (24 species): Undetermined Tilapia dominated above the barrier in 2000 and 2003, and Red shiner dominated below the barrier in 2004.
- SRP Arizona (North) Canal (23 species): Channel catfish and Sonora sucker were dominant overall.
- FCG Canal (12 species): Mosquitofish dominated in 2000 and 2002; Channel catfish dominated below the barrier in 2001; Red shiner dominated in 2004.

**Recommendations.**
1. Sample all stations every year, since missed stations reduce sample size and weaken presence assessments.
2. Use auxiliary gears (seines or others) to sample habitat beyond the fixed station, including deep pools that backpack shocking may miss. The authors note a correlation between auxiliary gear use and species detection.

**Limitations.** The authors acknowledge that the program cannot detect all species, that rare species may escape detection, and that the monitoring design has limited statistical power for trends.
