# Genetic Monitoring of Lake Mohave Razorback Sucker Repatriation Program (2004-2007)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from [[Arizona State University]] (School of Life Sciences), submitted to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Office, extends mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) monitoring of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]]. The study examines whether genetic variation is transmitted from wild adults through larvae into the [[Repatriation Program]] stocked population. Larval, repatriate and wild adult samples were analysed using [[Single-Stranded Conformation Polymorphism]] (SSCP) and sequencing. Pooled comparisons found no significant differences among larvae, repatriates and wild adults, supporting the earlier conclusion that the Lake Mohave sampling regime adequately represents genetic variation. Samples from backwaters and the lower Colorado River showed atypical variation, underscoring the importance of genetic management.

## Background & Description

- **Conservation context:** Genetic variation is tied to population size. Small populations face [[Inbreeding Depression]], [[Genetic Drift]] and reduced adaptive potential, which raise extinction risk.
- **Species status:** Razorback sucker is a critically endangered fish endemic to the modified Colorado River system. Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining population, but natural recruitment has been eliminated. Estimated numbers declined from more than 60,000 to fewer than 2,000 in the past decade, although genetic variation remains as high as in any vertebrate examined, suggesting remnants of a once very large population.
- **Repatriation program:** Begun in 1991 under the multi-agency [[Native Fish Work Group]], the program captures wild larvae from Lake Mohave, rears them in protected sites and stocks large juveniles back into the lake. Through 2007, more than 500,000 larvae were harvested and nearly 128,000 young fish were stocked.
- **Methods overview:**
  - Larvae were collected by night dip-netting at four Lake Mohave areas (Nine Mile, Tequila, Yuma, Wrong), with a target of 400 larvae per year.
  - Repatriates were sampled from fin clips, identified by a 10-digit [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tag, and wild adults were captured by trammel net.
  - mtDNA cytochrome *b* was screened by SSCP (radioactive, then fluorescent on a LI-COR 4300 analyser) and sequenced; haplotypes were analysed with PAUP* and diversity statistics computed in Arlequin, with significance tested by bootstrap re-sampling and [[Analysis of Molecular Variance]] (AMOVA).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Spawning timing and location vary by year, region and season, so the number of contributing spawners differs across areas. Wrong Cove produced the fewest larvae because few spawning adults were present; Yuma Cove produced the most.
- Fish movement among spawning areas has been documented ([[Mueller et al. 2000]]), and the study interprets temporal, not geographic, structure as the main source of allele-frequency differences.
- Lower Colorado River individuals were produced in hatcheries and were not intended for the Lake Mohave program.
- Repatriates now make up the major component of the adult population (about 67% of an estimated 1,579 adults in 2007), yet genetic variation has not declined.

## Key Findings

- **Sample base:** From 2004-2007, 1,912 additional larvae were characterised, bringing the total to 4,331 Lake Mohave larvae collected since 1997. A further 442 Lake Mohave fin clips were analysed, and 853 adults were characterised in total (342 wild, 511 repatriates).
- **Haplotypes:** Samples identified 33 haplotypes differing by 1-2 mutations. Phylogenetic analysis recovered 54 most parsimonious trees of 34 steps.
- **Larval diversity:** Gene diversity ranged from 0.00 to 0.81 (mean 0.58) and mean corrected haplotype number was 5.0 (range 1.0-8.0). These values closely match 1997-2003 estimates (0.55 and 4.6), indicating little change. ANOVAs detected no significant differences among years.
- **Significant deviations:** 37 of 152 bootstrap comparisons showed significantly lower diversity and/or fewer haplotypes than expected, compared with 25 of 186 in 1997-2003. Tequila Cove was the only area producing such deviations in several years, and deviations were more frequent early (Jan-Feb) and late (April-May) in the season.
- **Temporal and spatial structure:** Overall fixation indices were significant in all eleven years (range 0.020 to 0.147), while among-area F_ST was never significant, indicating no consistent use of areas by female groups with similar haplotypes. Pooled annual collections were essentially the same among years (F_ST = 0.0005, P = 0.326).
- **Repatriate cohorts:** Samples from 511 repatriates across 16 cohorts (1992-2007) carried 19 haplotypes. Most variation lay within cohorts (F_ST = 0.003, P = 0.254). Of 26 estimates, 23 fell within random expectation, with fewer haplotypes than expected in the 1995 cohort and reduced diversity in the 1997 and 2000 cohorts.
- **Repatriate-to-wild comparison:** AMOVA across adults, larvae and repatriates showed a statistically significant but biologically negligible difference (F_ST = 0.003, P < 0.001). Among life-history groups the variance was not significant (F_CT = -0.001, P = 0.923).
- **Atypical samples:**
  - The Yuma Cove backwater larval sample (March 2006) had fewer haplotypes than expected, but gene diversity was within expected range. It showed 12 significant pairwise comparisons, within the typical range for 2006 (mean 10.5, SD 6.3).
  - The A-10 backwater sample (51 larvae) had an extremely rare haplotype in 20 individuals and significantly different allele frequencies.
  - The Needles larval sample (28 of 30 larvae with the common E haplotype) showed much lower diversity than expected, suggesting few females contributed.
  - Cibola High Levee Pond fin clips contained a rare haplotype (BB) in 10 of 30 individuals; this sample differed significantly from all other adult samples.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Harvest for repatriation should be restricted to larvae taken directly from Lake Mohave.
  - Backwater volunteer spawn should be treated with extreme caution and not transferred into the wider population until a protocol assessing genetic integrity exists.
  - Lower river individuals should not be incorporated into the Lake Mohave program.
  - The backwater management plan (Mincklely et al. 2003) may maintain genetic variation if implemented properly.
- **Ongoing work:** The team, with T. Turner, is estimating effective female breeder numbers for 2004-2007, extending the 1997-2003 estimates of [[Turner et al. 2007]].
