# Use of Fish for the Olfactory Assay of Pollutants (Phenols) in Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1949–1950 study, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, tests whether [[Bluntnose Minnow]] (*Hybognathus notatus*) can be trained to detect [[Phenol]] and [[P-Chlorophenol]] at concentrations below the human odor threshold. Fish were conditioned with food rewards and electric shock punishment to associate each odor with a positive or negative outcome. After training, the minnows discriminated the two compounds at concentrations of at most 0.0005 p.p.m., below the human threshold of 0.01 p.p.m. The authors frame the method as a rapid detection tool for chlorophenol-related taste and odor problems in municipal water supplies, though not a quantitative one.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Phenol in municipal water reacts with chlorine to form odorous [[Chlorophenol]] compounds. These are often detected by consumers before chlorine treatment can remove them. Human odor testing (threshold odor test, A.P.H.A. 1946) is variable between individuals and over time, and cannot reliably detect phenol below 0.01 p.p.m.
- **Pollution sources:** Phenolic wastes come from industries including phenol, dye, medicinal, plastics, coke, charcoal, cresol, tar and oil refineries, and gas liquor (ammonia recovery) plants.
- **Rationale:** Earlier work by [[Walker and Hasler]] (1949) showed fish detect aquatic plant odors. Fish also use natural scents for feeding, cover and migration orientation, so they may respond to unnatural pollutant odors.
- **Apparatus:** Seven-gallon aquaria with siphon-airlift circulation creating two convection cells per tank. Activated charcoal filters cleared odors between trials to prevent olfactory fatigue. Electrodes at each end delivered 1.5 V at 11.5 mA for punishment; food was offered on perforated celluloid strips as reward.
- **Controls:** Fish were blinded by chemical cautery of the eye to remove cueing. Aquaria were mounted on sponge rubber, odor delivery did not alter water flow, and the observer whispered scores to the recorder to avoid auditory cues.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Odor concentrations:** Stock solutions contained 0.002 p.p.m. of phenolic compounds, diluted to about 0.0005 p.p.m. at the jets. If mixed fully with tank water, the concentration would be about 0.00002 p.p.m.
- **Behavioral basis:** Fish showed a natural aversion to both odors. A two-week preliminary period of positive-only tests was needed before fish responded to the positive odor without apparent fear.
- **Olfactory confirmation:** Ablation of the nasal capsule by heat cautery abolished responses, indicating the detection was olfactory.
- **Comparative sensitivity:** Neurath (1949) reported that a minnow (*Phoxinus laevis*) was 250 times more sensitive than humans to phenylethyl alcohol.
- **Relevance to plant operations:** Detection below human threshold could give water treatment operators early warning before chlorophenols reach consumers.

## Key Findings

- **Discrimination achieved:** After training, minnows reliably discriminated phenol from p-chlorophenol at concentrations of at most 0.0005 p.p.m., about 20 times below the human threshold.
- **Symmetric learning:** Fish learned equally well whether phenol or p-chlorophenol was the positive (food-associated) odor. Scores were recorded across six tests per period at 1, 2 and 4 months of training (tanks 4 and 5; tanks 9 and 10 for the reverse pairing).
- **Discrimination timing:** Discrimination was evident at least 2 months after training began. Higher test concentrations were suggested to speed learning, but this was not tested in the main experiment.
- **Generalization test:** [[O-Chlorophenol]] was not clearly associated with either training odor. Fish scored it as more negative than positive, possibly because shock is a stronger motivator than hunger and both odors were naturally offensive.
- **Scoring trend:** Pretest (random movement) scores fell over the study as fish became accustomed to the conditions.
- **Method limitations:** The assay is primarily qualitative (detection), not quantitative. Fish health must be maintained, and one operator should conduct observations for consistency.
- **Recommended protocol:** Train fish negatively to the pollutant in one set of aquaria and positively in another. A reaction in the negative group signals the pollutant, and reverse training controls for non-odor attractants. Reverse training is described as a prerequisite for valid testing.
