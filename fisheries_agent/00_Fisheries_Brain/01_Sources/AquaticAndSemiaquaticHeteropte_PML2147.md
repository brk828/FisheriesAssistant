# Aquatic and Semiaquatic Heteroptera of the Grand Canyon

#hemiptera #aquatic-insects #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1976 *Great Basin Naturalist* paper by John T. Polhemus and Martin S. Polhemus reports the aquatic and semiaquatic Heteroptera (true bugs) collected from 13 localities in [[Marble Canyon]] and the [[Grand Canyon]] in late May and June 1972. The authors list 14 species, compare the fauna with the water-bug assemblage of the southwestern United States and western Mexico, and describe one new species, [[Ochterus Rotundus]], which was previously misidentified in collections. The study is primarily a faunal survey and biogeographic analysis, not a fish study. The document contains no ichthyological data.

## Background & Description

The aquatic and semiaquatic Heteroptera of the Grand Canyon were poorly documented. An undescribed ochterid species had been found in both the Grand Canyon and the mountains of western Mexico, which prompted a targeted sampling expedition. Collecting permits were granted by the U.S. National Park Service, and the expedition received logistic support from Dr. Aron Ross of Ogden, Utah, who provided raft transport and advice on sampling sites.

The authors treat Marble Canyon and the Grand Canyon as one unit. The route ran from Lee's Ferry to a pull-out north of Peach Springs, Arizona. A separate sample from a spring in Davis Gulch on the [[Escalante River]] (tributary to [[Lake Powell]]) was added for comparison. Specimens are deposited in the University of Colorado Museum (CU) and the United States National Museum of Natural History (USNM).

The paper includes a site-by-site description of the 13 collection localities, a species list with collection numbers and sex/morph data, a distribution table, a taxonomic description of *Ochterus rotundus*, and a discussion of faunal affinities. The document also contains an unrelated fragment at the end on late Pleistocene packrat midden and pinyon records, which is not part of the Heteroptera study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The 13 localities span a range of habitat types:

- **Seeps and springs:** Vasey's Paradise (CL545), Buckfarm Canyon (CL546), Thunder River (CL552), and seep areas at several sites. Seeps on damp soil and rock faces were the richest sampling sites for many taxa.
- **Permanent streams:** Clear Creek (CL547), Shinumo Creek (CL549), Stone Creek (CL551), Deer Creek (CL553), and Havasu Creek (CL555). Havasu Creek has milky-blue water indicating mineralization, with travertine deposits and pools.
- **Large river and confluence:** The confluence of the [[Colorado River]] and [[Little Colorado River]] (CL548). The Little Colorado is milky blue because its flow originates in large springs upstream, and its rocks are coated with travertine, giving a depauperate fauna.
- **Shaded and cool sites:** Elves Chasm (CL550), a narrow canyon with limited sunlight and cool, crystal-clear water, which had a rich fauna of grottos, falls, and seeps.
- **Marsh and riparian sites:** Lava Falls (CL560), with saw-grass marsh fed by spring water and travertine-covered banks, and The Ledges (CL554), a spring-fed pool area.

Water chemistry noted by the authors includes travertine deposition, mineralization producing milky-blue water, and a generally clear, cool character at spring-fed sites. Vegetation was abundant at many seeps and was kept moist by the springs.

## Key Findings

**Species composition**
- The survey recorded 14 species of aquatic and semiaquatic Heteroptera from the Grand Canyon sites.
- The families represented are Gerridae, Macroveliidae, Veliidae, Hebridae, Saldidae, Ochteridae, Gelastocoridae, Corixidae, and Notonectidae.
- The most widespread species include [[Microvelia Torquata]], which occurred at many localities across both winged (apterous and alate) morphs, and [[Notonecta Lobata]], which is also widespread in the United States.
- Other recorded species include *Gerris remigis*, *Macrovelia hornii*, *Microvelia beameri*, *Rhagovelia distincta*, *Hebrus hubbardi*, *Hebrus obscurus*, *Saldula pezata*, *Saldula pallipes*, *Ochterus barberi*, [[Gelastocoris Oculatus]], and *Graptocorixa serrulata*.

**New species: Ochterus rotundus**
- The authors describe *Ochterus rotundus* n. sp. from the Grand Canyon and the mountains of western Mexico.
- The species is large, ovate, and greenish-brown, and is distinct from congeners because of its size, shape, and color.
- A Grand Canyon specimen at USNM had earlier been identified by Schell (1943) as *O. viridifrons*. The authors state that *rotundus* has little in common with that species.
- Material was collected from seeping rock faces at several localities.

**Faunal affinities and biogeography**
- The authors expected to find more Mexican species extending northward into the Grand Canyon. Many such species were not found.
- Of the 14 Grand Canyon species, most are widespread across the western and southwestern United States. *Ochterus rotundus* is the main exception, with a range centered in western Mexico and a single northward record.
- The authors suggest that this northward distribution may be relatively recent. Species of the western Mexican Sierra Madre (including the Mexican *Abedus* and *Belostoma*) were expected but not found in the Grand Canyon.
- Species not found but expected included members of *Notonecta*, *Abedus* (Belostomatidae), and *Gelastocoris* (Gelastocoridae). The authors suggest that species such as *Abedus* might occur in suitable side streams of the canyon.
- The Grand Canyon fauna is considered depauperate compared with other regions, and the authors note that the canyon is an isolated habitat relative to surrounding areas.

**Scope and sampling**
- The study was limited to a single expedition in late May and June 1972, plus a later sampling at Davis Gulch.
- Collection localities were described in detail so that ecologists could use the data.
- All material is held at the University of Colorado Museum and USNM.
