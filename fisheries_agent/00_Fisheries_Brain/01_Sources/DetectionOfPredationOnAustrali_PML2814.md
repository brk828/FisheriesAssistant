# Detection of Predation on Australian Native Fishes by Gambusia holbrooki

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This short communication describes simple laboratory techniques for detecting predation by the introduced [[Gambusia Holbrooki]] (eastern mosquitofish) on juvenile native fishes in eastern Australia. Clearing and staining of Gambusia guts, a [[Feeding Trial]] in aquaria, and examination of [[Regurgitation]] in fixative solutions allowed identification of ingested juvenile fishes, including [[Melanotaeniidae]] larvae and cannibalized Gambusia. The authors show that rapidly digested prey can be detected for longer than conventional [[Gut Content Analysis]] allows, extending the identifiable post-feeding window from roughly 2 h to about 6 h in cleared and stained specimens. The study tests a long-standing speculation that Gambusia preys on native fish larvae, but it cannot establish the wider ecological significance of this predation.

## Background & Description

Gambusia spp. (order Cyprinodontiformes, family Poeciliidae) have been introduced to many countries, mainly for [[Mosquito Control]]. Twenty-one Australian populations were identified as this species, usually referred to as G. holbrooki, and the paper uses "Gambusia" for G. holbrooki and the closely related G. affinis.

Gambusia is reputed to reduce endemic fish populations through aggressive interaction and predation. Aquarium and field-enclosure experiments have shown that it can extirpate [[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]] and [[Heterandria Formosa]]. Following extirpation, recovery of H. formosa depended on eradication of Gambusia. In Australia, Gambusia was speculated to prey on melanotaeniid larvae, but no earlier study had identified fishes other than cannibalized juveniles in the guts of wild Gambusia.

The study area was a 10 km stretch of the upper [[Orara River]] in New South Wales. Gambusia was sympatric with juvenile native fishes there from October 1997 to January 1998. Juveniles of six fish species were distinguished by pigmentation, mouth size, body length, flexion status, vertebral counts, and hypural number after clearing and staining. The species were Gambusia, [[Retropinna Semoni]], [[Melanotaenia Duboulayi]], Rhadinocentrus ornatus, [[Pseudomugil Signifer]] and [[Hypseleotris Galii]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Invasive predator impact:** Gambusia is linked to declines and replacement of native and endemic fishes, a pattern discussed under [[Biological Invasion]] and [[Predation]].
- **Cannibalism:** Gambusia readily eats conspecific juveniles, and cannibalism was the most frequently identified prey type in this study.
- **Digestion limits:** The predator's acidic gut dissolved ingested skeletal material, and rapidly digested prey such as small fish are underrepresented in routine gut analysis, unlike lignified plant debris and arthropod parts.
- **Scope limits:** The authors state that the ecological significance of predation on native fishes cannot be determined from this isolated study. Newly hatched fishes may be an important diet component for other species, and gut contents of endemic fishes were not analysed.

## Key Findings

**Methods and identification**
- Clearing and staining Gambusia, with alizarin red for skeletal material, enabled identification of ingested fish skeletal characters.
- Fixative solutions contained regurgitated items that are normally missed in gut studies. This method had not been reported in earlier studies of piscivory.
- Differentiation of ingested juveniles was reliable for larger fishes but not for small, well-masticated ones.

**Feeding trial (74 post-juvenile Gambusia, 4 L aquaria, fed melanotaeniid larvae)**
- Three weeks after capture, fish were acclimated for three days to once-daily feeding on commercial flake food and live wild-harvested larvae.
- Eight Gambusia were sampled at intervals of 0.5 to 12 h after feeding. Four fish per sample were examined in cleared and stained specimens.
- On the final day of the trial (5 November 1997), 70 adult Gambusia remained, with four unexplained disappearances. Juveniles were present in three aquaria.
- In non-cleared specimens, melanotaeniid larvae under 5 mm TL were found up to 2 h after feeding, and larger larvae were identifiable up to 3 h.
- In cleared and stained specimens, larger larvae were readily discernible up to 6 h. At 9 h, otoliths were discernible and the vertebral column was disarticulated. At 12 h, a female had a rectal faecal pellet containing a few centra.
- Larvae could not be identified at 6 h in non-cleared and stained specimens.
- At 3 h and 6 h, a male (19 mm SL) and a female (26 mm SL) each contained an ingested Gambusia of 6 mm and 8 mm SL respectively.

**Wild-caught Gambusia (631 fish examined via fixative solutions)**
- Regurgitated items included ten melanotaeniids, one Gambusia and one Hypseleotris galii.
- Twenty-one Gambusia contained an ingested fish: 18 had an ingested melanotaeniid or pseudomugilid larva, and three had a cannibalized fish.
- Regurgitated larvae showing wounds consistent with masticATION were distinguished from undamaged juvenile Gambusia.

**Conclusions**
- The proportion of rapidly digested prey, including ingested fishes, may be underestimated in routine gut content analysis.
- The techniques (differentiation of sympatric juveniles, examination of regurgitate, and clearing and staining) extend the identifiable post-feeding interval to about 6 h.
- The authors confirm that Gambusia preys on native fish larvae in the study area, but the wider impact on native fish populations remains untested.
