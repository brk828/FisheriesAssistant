# Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge Master Plan (1986 Draft)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #odontophoridae

## Executive Summary

This draft master plan, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 2, Albuquerque, November 1986), sets out management for the [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] in the Altar Valley of southeastern Arizona. The refuge, established August 11, 1985, covers about 112,000 acres and was acquired primarily to recover the endangered [[Masked Bobwhite]], which was extirpated from Arizona by about 1900. The plan's main thrust is masked bobwhite management, with diversity, public use and restoration of [[Sonoran Savanna Grassland]] as supporting programs. The plan is explicitly experimental, because basic data on carrying capacity and the original grassland community are lacking. Aquatic content is minor: the refuge historically had no native fishes, and the plan rules out a sport fishery.

## Background & Description

**Acquisition and status.** The refuge was bought for $9,000,000 ($5,000,000 approved by Congress in 1983 and $4,000,000 in 1984) after the 1984 revised [[Masked Bobwhite Recovery Plan]] recommended purchase of the [[Buenos Aires Ranch]]. The transaction closed on August 11, 1985. Of the roughly 112,000 acres, about 21,300 acres are fee title, about 90,200 acres are leased from the [[Arizona State Land Department]], and the remainder belongs to other owners. Four private inholdings total about 1,300 acres.

**Setting.** Elevations range from 3,000 to 4,800 feet. The climate is semi-arid, with about 12 inches of rain in the central valley and up to 20 inches on higher slopes, two rainy seasons, and temperatures from 12°F to 105°F. The refuge is designated a Class II area under the [[Clean Air Act]]. The headquarters lies about 56 miles southwest of [[Tucson]].

**Biological resources.**
- **Birds:** over 175 species documented, including four quail species ([[Masked Bobwhite]], [[Gambel's Quail]], [[Scaled Quail]], [[Montezuma Quail]]), with a casual birding day typically yielding 70–80 species.
- **Mammals:** mule deer, whitetailed deer, javelina, pronghorn (extirpated), and two rare bats; over 25 rodent species.
- **Reptiles and amphibians:** 15 reptile species, including the [[Gila Monster]] and [[Desert Tortoise]]; several arid-land amphibians.
- **Fishes:** none native. Five non-native species were collected in a 1985 survey.

**Human history.** Pre-contact Pima groups and later Apache raiders occupied the area. The [[Buenos Aires Ranch]] was founded by Don Pedro Aguirre and operated as a cattle ranch under successive owners, including a quarterhorse breeding center during Gill ownership.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation change.** The valley was historically Sonoran savanna grassland. Overgrazing, arroyo cutting (Brawley Arroyo, now about 20 feet deep and in places over 1,400 feet wide), mesquite expansion, and introduced grasses have converted it to semidesert grassland. [[Lehmann's Lovegrass]] is now the dominant perennial grass on over 60,000 acres of the Brawley Wash watershed, and [[Snakeweed]] and [[Burroweed]] have thickened under heavy grazing.

**Water.** Runoff is captured in stock ponds, but most stored water is lost to evaporation. Surface flow is sporadic: Altar Wash flowed an estimated 40,000 acre-feet in 36 hours in September 1962. Aguirre Lake, Mormon Lake and Grebe Pond are the main impoundments. The subsurface aquifer may be about 1,000 feet deep, and a 1975 feasibility study estimated deep-well yields of 250–1,000 gallons per minute.

**Aquatic habitat and fishes.** A 1985 survey of stock tanks found five non-native species: [[Fathead Minnow]], black bullhead, yellow bullhead, [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Mosquitofish]]. The refuge's perennial Arivaca Creek was fishless. The plan notes that native [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]] could replace mosquitofish's pest-control role in small tanks, and that larger tanks suit rearing of [[Razorback Sucker]], to be stocked in the upper Santa Cruz drainage for recovery purposes.

**Land use and access constraints.** A Military Operation Area (Davis-Monthan Air Force Base) covers the area south of Arivaca Road. The International Boundary and Water Commission holds a reserve zone along about 7 miles of the Mexican border. Pima County requires approval for well drilling under its water management area.

## Key Findings

**Management priorities (in order):**
1. **Masked bobwhite** is the highest-priority output. The plan aims for a self-sustaining population defined by the recovery plan as an annual average of 200 calling males, with a floor of 100 calling males. The 10-year objective is 273,750 user days (750 average birds, peak 1,250). Habitat factors include 15–30% shrub overstory cover, at least 18–20 grass and forb species, and minimum standing grass and forb crops.
2. **Habitat classification:** suitable/high-potential habitat is about 25% of the refuge, medium potential about 65%, low potential about 5%, and not suitable about 5%.
3. **Grassland restoration** through experimental fire, grazing, check dams, reseeding, riparian planting and mesquite thinning. Results are to be tested before wider use.

**Species of special emphasis:**
- **Pronghorn antelope:** extirpated after the 1930s; a 1945 reintroduction failed. A study team will decide between *mexicana* and *sonoriensis* subspecies. Objective: 150 average animals, peak 250.
- **Aplomado falcon:** possible reintroduction in Phase 2. No numerical objective is set.
- **Peregrine falcon and bald eagle:** protected through habitat and prey management. No nesting habitat exists on the refuge.
- **Waterfowl:** the refuge is a minor migration stopover. Dabbling duck objective is 2,750 average birds. Diving duck objective is 550.
- **Three listed plants** (Sheer's strong-spined cory cactus, Santa Cruz striped agave, needle-spine pineapple cactus) are to be surveyed, mapped and protected from collection.

**Public use:**
- Demand is estimated at 39,000 non-hunting visitors per year within 10 years, about 42,000 total with hunters, based on about 50% of [[Bosque Del Apache National Wildlife Refuge]] visitation (77,291 in 1984).
- Projected interpretive demand includes about 31,200 visitor contact center visits and 12,080 tour route cars per year.
- A 7-mile Phase 1 tour loop and a 15-mile Phase 2 loop are planned, with visitor center conversion of the Aguirre house.
- About 10% of the refuge (roughly 20,000 acres, around headquarters and Aguirre Lake) will be closed to hunting. Quail hunting is closed refuge-wide from fall 1987.
- Fishing is excluded. The plan states that a sport fishery would require a put-and-take program, which is against [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] policy.

**Hunting use:** total refuge hunter and trapper use was about 2,650 days in 1985/86 (about 1,500 mule deer, 400 javelina, 500 whitetail, and smaller counts). The plan predicts about 3,000 days in 10 years.

**Cost and phasing:** the Aguirre Lake–Grebe Pond rehabilitation is estimated at $20,000–$30,000, with check dams for Brawley Wash the other major expense. Two 5-year phases are planned, so impacts can be evaluated before major construction.

**Legal framework:** the plan rests on the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), the [[Refuge Recreation Act]], the [[Fish and Wildlife Act]], the [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]], and the [[National Historic Preservation Act]]. A "compatibility test" governs all public uses.

**Outstanding gaps:** the plan acknowledges that carrying capacity data, the original grassland community, and the subspecies of pronghorn are unknown. Archeological surveys have never been carried out on the refuge, and several historic structures (including the Garcia House and Pozo Nuevo School House) may qualify for the National Register of Historic Places.
