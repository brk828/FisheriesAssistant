# Artificial Propagation of Loach Minnow, *Rhinichthys cobitis*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report documents a 1996–1998 study at the [[Alchesay-Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]] (Alchesay Unit, Whiteriver, Arizona) to develop a repeatable artificial propagation protocol for the threatened [[Loach Minnow]]. Sixty-four wild fish were collected on 06/11/96 from two sites on the Ft. Apache Indian Reservation and held in captivity. Captive culture was refined through heavy losses to parasitic disease, a closed recirculation system, seasonal temperature manipulation, and tank-based spawning, producing eggs, fry, and sub-adults. Fungal infection of eggs was the main unresolved problem. The work was conducted under an interagency agreement between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], with cooperation from the [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]].

## Background & Description

The loach minnow is a small, elongate, dorsoventrally flattened cyprinid that seldom exceeds 60 mm total length. It is mottled greenish-brown dorsally, off-white ventrally, and is distinguished by two diffuse white spots at the base of the caudal fin. Breeding males show intense red coloration on the lips, ventral surfaces and paired-fin bases; nuptial females show yellow to yellowish-orange tones instead of red.

Historically the species occurred in the Gila River Basin of Arizona and New Mexico and the San Pedro River Basin of Arizona and Sonora. It is now restricted to scattered tributary populations in Arizona and New Mexico. It is listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]], as a threatened species by New Mexico, and as a species of special concern by Arizona. The [[Loach Minnow Recovery Plan]] (Marsh 1991) identified captive holding and propagation as a contingency measure.

The wild population on the Ft. Apache Indian Reservation occupies approximately 14 miles of stream. Survival depends on natural stream flows, riffle habitat integrity, aquatic vegetation control, and exclusion of non-native competitors and predators. Drought, low runoff and fire-related watershed conditions in spring and summer 1996 prompted removal of a small portion of the population for genetic conservation and propagation research.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Collection:** Fish were taken with a 1.83 m × 2.44 m, 4.5 mm delta-mesh seine. Site A yielded 20 fish from a 50 m reach; Site B yielded 44 fish from a 25 m reach. Physical separation of the sites suggested possible genetic differentiation, so the two groups were kept apart throughout.
- **Source water:** Hatchery production water came from the North Fork of the White River, which carried native and introduced fish and the parasite [[Ichthyophthirius multifiliis]].
- **Wild diet:** Adults feed mainly on chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs (1–5 mm), which the captive diet had to approximate.
- **Habitat:** Adults are strongly cover-oriented. Spawning occurred on the undersides of clay potsherds, where eggs adhered to shard surfaces and gravel.
- **Associated fauna:** Wild-collected and reared fish included *Oncorhynchus apache* and *O. gilae* (native trout), which provided the basis for the transport protocol.

## Key Findings

**Collection and transport**
- No mortality occurred during collection or transport of the 64 fish.
- Transport used 38 L ice chests with 0.5% sodium chloride, Furacin and tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222), with temperature reduced from 25 °C to 18 °C.

**Holding and disease**
- A total of 27 fish died in hatchery production water between 06/11/96 and 08/05/96. All of these deaths were in the Site B group.
- Formalin at 167 mg/L for one hour, repeated over six treatments, did not reduce mortality.
- After transfer to the recirculation system on 08/05/96, parasite cysts were cleared by 08/16/96 and no further parasitic deaths were recorded.
- The authors concluded that the species is highly susceptible to parasites in captivity.

**Closed recirculation system**
- Six 75 L aquariums were used with a sump, a 16 µm mechanical filter, a UV sterilizer rated at over 45,000 µW·s/cm², and biofiltration.
- Temperature control, mechanical filtration and UV treatment were all considered effective in limiting parasites.

**Feeding of adults**
- Brine shrimp nauplii were unsuitable for adults. Frozen diets of bloodworms, mosquito larvae, brine shrimp and krill were accepted.
- Preference order was bloodworms, mosquito larvae, brine shrimp, then krill.
- Feeding of about 0.40–0.60 g per fish per day gave a slight excess at 15 °C, with intake adjusted above and below that temperature.
- Dry diets were largely rejected.

**Spawning**
- Aquarium temperatures were lowered from 15 °C to 7.2 °C by mid-November, held through winter, then raised to 10 °C on 04/11/97.
- The first spawn was observed on 04/27/97 at 12.2 °C. The final spawn was on 06/19/97.
- Breeding used two 19 L aquariums. Both 2-male:1-female and 2-female:1-male pairings produced successful spawns.
- Egg masses were about 5 mm in diameter. Males guarded eggs and showed aggressive posturing when eggs were disturbed.

**Incubation and hatching**
- Egg diameter ranged from 1.3 to 1.8 mm.
- Mean days to hatch were 16.3 at 13.9 °C, 16.5 at 14.4 °C, 16.0 at 15.0 °C, 14.2 at 15.6 °C and 12.1 at 17.2 °C (174 eggs observed).
- Fungus (*Saprolegnia* sp.) was the major obstacle. Malachite green at 8.3 mg/L for one hour was most effective but appeared to induce premature hatching in later stages. Formalin and malachite green at standard salmonid levels were not successful.

**Fry development and feeding**
- Sack fry measured 6.5–7.6 mm. Yolk sacs were fully absorbed by about day 18 post-hatch.
- Initial diet was *Nannochloropsis oculata* algae with the rotifer *Brachionus calyciflorus*. Artemia nauplii were introduced around 30 days post-hatch, and frozen diets around 60 days.
- Fry stocking densities in nursery tanks ranged from 14 to 54 fry per tank.

**Growth**
- Growth data for grower groups G-1 to G-4 were reported. G-1 reached 25 mm at about 75 days post-hatch, and G-4 grew faster, which the authors attributed to higher rearing temperatures and better husbandry experience.

**Recommendations**
- The report recommends reliable temperature control, backup power, natural light cycles and clean, well-filtered recirculating systems.
- It recommends frozen food, with feeding regimes accelerated at warmer temperatures and slowed at cooler ones.
- It recommends that the 12-point protocol include a power-siphon cleaning system, which the authors described as invaluable for water quality and fish health.
- Fungal control on eggs remains unsolved; enzymatic removal of the gelatinous egg coat and prophylactic treatments are suggested. Malachite green is noted as potentially carcinogenic.
