# Simulated Changes in Lake Trout Yield, Trophies, and Forage Consumption under Various Slot Limits

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 study in the *North American Journal of Fisheries Management* uses population and bioenergetics simulations to evaluate how slot-length harvest limits affect the [[Lake Trout]] fishery in [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], Utah-Wyoming. Local managers had observed a 15% decline in the mean biomass of harvested lake trout between 1980 and 1988. They proposed two competing explanations: overexploitation, or reduced growth caused by declining [[Forage Fish]] availability. The two explanations call for opposite management responses.

The authors coupled a population model ([[GIFSIM]]) with a [[Bioenergetics Model]] to compare six proposed [[Slot Limit]] regulations with the previous harvest rule. Across the scenarios, more restrictive slots increased trophy catches, reduced yield, and increased forage consumption. The authors concluded that a 28-36-in slot offered the best balance, giving moderate trophy gains while limiting yield losses and added predation.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** Lake trout (*Salvelinus namaycush*) are piscivorous salmonids. In this study, fish smaller than 400 mm fed mainly on invertebrates, fish of 400-600 mm ate both invertebrates and fish, and fish larger than 600 mm ate only fish.

**Study system.** [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] was formed by impoundment of the Green River in 1962. Its fishery history includes:
- A [[Rainbow Trout]] yield fishery in the 1960s, with catch rates falling from 1.3 fish/h (1965) to 0.2 fish/h (1983).
- A [[Brown Trout]] trophy fishery in the mid-1970s.
- Naturally reproducing [[Kokanee]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] populations that became important in the 1980s.
- An "excellent" lake trout fishery in 1988-1989, with mean harvested biomass above 2 kg and catch rates above 0.04 fish/h.

**Management problem.** Managers wanted more large, trophy-sized fish without losing overall catch rates. The study also aimed to limit predation on forage fish, which the authors describe as historically under-considered in fisheries management.

**Regulations tested.** The previous rule allowed two fish per day, with one over 20 in. Six proposed slot limits were modeled (Table 1):
- 20-30 in (508-762 mm)
- 22-32 in (559-813 mm)
- 24-34 in (610-864 mm)
- 24-36 in (610-914 mm)
- 26-36 in (660-914 mm)
- 28-36 in (660-914 mm)

Each proposed rule allowed two fish per day, with one kept above the slot and one below it, and none kept within it.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Data sources.**
- Lake trout were captured in sinking monofilament gill nets (66 m long, 1.8 m deep, bar mesh 5.18-10.2 cm) during April and May.
- Almost 1,900 fish were tagged between 1981 and 1988, and 485 were returned. Anglers returned tags at about 85% during creel surveys.
- Greater than 95% of tagged fish were over 350 mm (age 3 or older).
- Aging from otoliths or scales was difficult, so growth was estimated from recaptured fish. Yule (1992) reported growth rates of 1.13 g/d (400-600 mm TL), 2.15 g/d (600-800 mm), and 1.83 g/d (>800 mm).

**Mortality.**
- Annual instantaneous fishing mortality ranged from 0.154 to 0.191 (mean 0.167).
- Mean natural mortality was 0.273, and mean survival was 0.560.
- Hooking mortality was assumed to be 15% and was included in natural mortality.

**Population and energetics inputs.**
- Recruitment was set at 30,000 age-3 fish per year. The resulting modeled abundance was 98,600, compared with an estimate of 120,000 ± 36,000 from multiple recaptures.
- Lake trout over 600 mm were assumed to lose 6.8% of body mass annually to spawning.
- Fish were assumed to select 10°C where possible, using 1990 temperature profiles.
- Conversion efficiency averaged 23% in the energetics model.

**Forage base.** The authors noted that Utah chub (*Gila atraria*) purse-seine catches showed a non-significant decline. Lake trout growth and condition had remained high through the 1980s, so the forage status was described as equivocal. A five-year hydroacoustic study was underway.

## Key Findings

**1. Population response.** All six proposed slots increased the modeled lake trout population relative to the previous rule. Most of the gain came from larger size classes.
- The 20-30 in slot produced the largest population, up 18%.
- The 24-36 in slot more than tripled the number of fish over 800 mm.

**2. Yield.** Every proposed regulation reduced yield.
- The 24-36 in slot cut yield by about 50%.
- The 20-30 and 28-36 in slots reduced yield by about 30%.

**3. Trophy harvest.** Trophy fish were defined as over 10 kg.
- The 24-36 in slot produced the greatest trophy harvest, a 3.5-fold increase.
- Trophy catch and yield moved in opposite directions across the scenarios.

**4. Forage consumption.**
- The 24-36 in slot had the highest forage demand.
- Most slots raised forage consumption by roughly 30% per year.
- The 28-36 in slot raised it by only 14% over the previous rule.

**5. Sensitivity analysis.** The 24-36 in scenario was varied by ±5% in growth, natural mortality, and fishing mortality (Table 4).
- Growth had the largest effect. A 5% decrease in growth reduced trophy harvest by 52.0%, and a 5% increase reduced it by 19.7%. Consumption changed by −32.2% and +22.7%, respectively.
- Natural mortality strongly affected trophy harvest but had smaller effects on yield and consumption.
- Fishing mortality had little effect on any response variable.
- Because growth was so influential, the authors warned that a growth response to reduced forage could largely cancel the benefits of restrictive rules.

**6. Tradeoff evaluation.** The authors scored each regulation as relative benefits (trophy gain) against detriments (yield loss), with and without the added consumption penalty (Figure 3).
- Without the consumption penalty, the 26-36 in slot scored highest. The 24-36 and 28-36 in slots both exceeded 1.8 times the previous rule.
- With the consumption penalty, the 28-36 in slot scored highest.

**7. Recommendation.** The authors concluded that a 28-36 in slot would give moderate increases in trophy catch while minimizing yield reductions and increases in forage consumption. They advised monitoring growth, forage abundance, and consumption.

**8. Methodological contribution.** The study shows how [[Population Model]] outputs can be combined with bioenergetics estimates to quantify predation on forage fish alongside traditional harvest metrics. The authors cite [[Lake Michigan]] as a precedent: stocking reductions for [[Alewife]] predators were guided by simulation models.
