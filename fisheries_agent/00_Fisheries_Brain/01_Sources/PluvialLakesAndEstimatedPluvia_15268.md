# Pluvial Lakes and Estimated Pluvial Climates of Nevada

#paleohydrology #quaternary-geology #pluvial-lakes

## Executive Summary

This Nevada Bureau of Mines and Geology bulletin (1979) maps late Quaternary pluvial lakes across more than 81 closed basins and uses their size to estimate the climate that sustained them. The authors recognize 53 pluvial lakes of [[Lahontan Age]] (Wisconsinan) and probable shoreline evidence for three pre-Lahontan lakes. They conclude that full pluvial climates were about 5°F cooler than modern climate, with about 68 percent more mean annual precipitation and about 10 percent less mean annual lake evaporation. The study frames these results as a test of how sensitive closed-basin hydrology is to small climate shifts, a question relevant to regional water transfers and to long-term [[Radioactive Waste Disposal]] planning in arid zones.

## Background & Description

The bulletin, prepared with the [[Desert Research Institute]] and the [[Mackay School of Mines]], is a revision of earlier pluvial lake maps. It draws on [[Aerial Photo Interpretation]], topographic base maps, field reconnaissance, and radiocarbon-based age control from the [[Lake Lahontan]], [[Lake Bonneville]], and [[Las Vegas Valley]] areas.

Key methodological points:

- **Mapping criteria:** Only lakes with preserved shoreline features were accepted. Playa deposits and fine-grained, ground-water-discharge sediments were excluded. Shoreline features were judged to be recognizable only where lakes were roughly 20 to 30 feet or more in maximum depth.
- **Core model:** A continuity equation (Equation 4) relates a "[[Pluvial Hydrologic Index]]" (Z) to basin area, lake area, precipitation, evapotranspiration, and lake evaporation. Modern [[Climatic Divisions]] (1931–1960 Weather Bureau data) provide the baseline.
- **Regression:** Of 33 nonoverflowing pluvial lakes tested, lake altitude and latitude explained 75 percent of the variance in Z. Latitude change of one degree had about the same effect on Z as 500 feet of altitude change.
- **Historical context:** Earlier work by [[Israel Russell]], [[G. K. Gilbert]], [[Hubbs and Miller]] (1948), and [[Snyder and Langbein]] (1962) is reviewed. Many earlier lake maps are judged to include errors of interpretation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The bulletin describes closed basins as hydrologic sinks. Under pluvial conditions, water entering surface or ground-water systems left only by lake evaporation or [[Evapotranspiration]] from phreatophytes. Modern basins retain only playas, small marshes, and a few perennial waters such as [[Pyramid Lake]], [[Walker Lake]], and [[Ruby Marsh]].

Shoreline preservation depends on the modern environment. Cool, moist northeastern basins preserve about 80 percent of Lahontan shorelines because vegetation stabilizes terrain. Drier northwestern and south-central basins preserve only 10 to 20 percent.

Fish distribution is part of the context. The bulletin notes that [[Hubbs and Miller]] (1948) used fish distributions to reconstruct drainage history, and that Lahontan overflow was more limited than those authors assumed. Pyramid Lake's indigenous fish, adapted to present water temperatures, are cited as evidence against large temperature drops. The authors also note that [[Summit Lake Basin]] may have allowed transfer of fish between the Alvord and Lahontan basins.

## Key Findings

**Lake distribution and age**

- 53 pluvial lakes of Lahontan (Wisconsinan) age were recognized in more than 81 basins.
- Three pre-Lahontan lakes in three basins have probable shoreline evidence. [[Diamond Valley]] shows pre-Lahontan shorelines between 6,000 and 6,080 ft MSL. Rye Patch (Illinoian) age is possible.
- Many previously mapped lakes were rejected, including large lakes in [[Steptoe Valley]], [[Pahrump Valley]], [[Hot Creek Valley]], and [[Las Vegas Valley]]. Their "lacustrine" deposits are attributed to paludal or playa processes linked to ground-water discharge.
- Maximum lake extents are considered essentially contemporaneous.

**Overflow**

- Lahontan-age overflow occurred in [[Buffalo Valley]], [[Diamond Valley]], [[Bawling Calf Basin]], [[Hawksy Walksy Valley]], [[Summit Valley]], [[New Years Valley]], and [[Washoe Valley]]. [[Lake Tahoe]] also overflowed but was ice-dammed at times.
- Evidence suggests a pre-Lahontan overflow from the Lahontan Basin southward toward [[Clayton Valley]], linked to regional northward downwarping.

**Estimated full pluvial climate (central result)**

- Mean annual temperature about 5°F lower than modern.
- Mean annual precipitation averaging 68 percent above modern values (range 52 to 80 percent by region).
- Mean annual lake evaporation about 10 percent lower.
- Tributary precipitation increases were about 25 percent greater in absolute terms than lake-basin values.
- Average lake-precipitation increase of about 6.5 inches. The derived increases range from 3.3 to 9.5 inches.

**Regional indices**

| Region | Mean measured Z | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme Northwestern Nevada (ENWN) | 0.68 | Highest index; coolest, moistest modern climate |
| Northwestern Nevada (NWN) | 0.084 | Based on 7 basins |
| Northeastern Nevada (NEN) | 0.295 | Based on 19 basins |
| Southcentral Nevada (SCN) | 0.031 | Nine of 20 basins with no recognizable pluvial lake |
| Southern Nevada (SN) | 0 | Mean of 14 basins; no perennial pluvial lakes inferred |

- The smallest confidently measured index was 0.03.
- Southern Nevada would have needed about a 10°F drop to approach the smallest measured index. The authors conclude that southern Nevada likely held no perennial Lahontan-age lakes.

**Lake Lahontan**

- Using a 5°F temperature drop, the derived runoff is consistent with the value needed to generate the lake (runoff ~6.75 inches, index 0.26 for the basin's modern-climate analog at the minimum curve).
- Sierra Nevada nourishment areas are considered moist enough to offset drier basin parts.

**Modern versus pluvial comparison**

- Modern index for Ruby Valley (approx. 0.05, or 0.026 for Ruby Marsh alone) is close to the estimated pluvial climate of south-central and northwestern Nevada. The measured pluvial index for Ruby Valley (Lake Franklin) is 0.61.
- Mono Lake's modern index (0.17) is comparable to several pluvial indices, supporting the relationship between modern and pluvial climates.

**Evidence against large temperature drops**

The authors cite the following as evidence against major cooling:

1. Weak weathering profiles between minor lake drops.
2. Lack of ice-marginal features at high shore levels.
3. Tufa precipitated by algae up to the highest Lahontan levels.
4. Indigenous fish adapted to present water temperatures in Pyramid Lake and northern Great Basin thermal springs.
5. Palynological data showing similar flora.

**Comparison with other estimates**

- [[Antevs]] (1952) proposed about a 5°F drop.
- [[Broecker and Orr]] (1958) proposed a 5°C (9°F) drop with 8 inches more precipitation.
- [[Snyder and Langbein]] (1962) proposed about an 8 to 9°F drop for Spring Lake.
- [[Galloway]] (1970) proposed up to about 20°F, which the authors reject.
- [[Morrison]] (1965a) proposed 8°F to 15°F.
- [[Weide]] (1974) proposed 8 to 9°F and about 4 to 6 inches more precipitation.

**Conclusions**

- Full pluvial climates were not greatly different from modern climates, but small changes produced large hydrologic effects.
- Indirect evidence suggests more winter frontal storms, greater runoff, and extended mountain snowpack, with lower soil-moisture-limited vegetation zones shifting downslope.
- Modern hydrologic indices remain very small in most closed basins, so basins are sensitive indicators of climate change.
- The authors state that pluvial lake evidence supports a regional approach to paleoclimate estimation using closed basins.
