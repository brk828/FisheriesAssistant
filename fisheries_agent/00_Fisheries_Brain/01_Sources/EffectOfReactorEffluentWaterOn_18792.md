# Effects of Hanford Reactor Area Effluent on Silver Salmon in the Columbia River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonid

## Executive Summary

This scanned report, dated to the period around the [[Fall of 1954]], tests whether [[Hanford Reactor Effluent]] discharged into the [[Columbia River]] harms [[Silver Salmon]]. Young fish and incubating eggs were held in several dilutions of reactor-area effluent. Mortality, growth and radioactivity were measured. Survival and growth fell at the higher concentrations, and the fish accumulated radioactivity. The authors judged that the radiation dose was too low to explain the damage. They attributed the observed effects mainly to temperature and chemical toxicity. They concluded that the effluent had no immediate effect on young silver salmon in the river, but they left open possible latent effects that would only show when adults returned in the fall.

## Background & Description

- **Subject:** Silver salmon (the common name used in the document; the scientific name is not given in the legible text).
- **Exposure system:** Eggs and young fish were held in mixtures of [[Hanford Reactor Effluent]] and river water. The tested concentrations included 2, 3, 5 and 10 per cent effluent, plus a control group.
- **Life stages studied:** Incubating eggs, hatchery fry and early-feeding juveniles, followed through the year from November to October.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Effluent source:** Discharge from the [[Hanford Site]] reactors, which entered the Columbia River.
- **Dilution and exposure:** Effluent concentrations in the river were said to fall well below 2 per cent, except in small, well-defined zones immediately below the outfalls. The authors judged that young salmon were unlikely to stay long in these high-concentration zones.
- **Temperature:** River water temperatures were near 5°C during the fry and early-feeding stages. They rose to about 10°C between April and May. Temperature is treated as a key confounding factor.
- **Radionuclide behavior:**
  - Most of the radioactivity in the fish (about 85 per cent) came from sodium-24 (Na-24) in the effluent.
  - About 10 per cent came from another radionuclide that is partly illegible in the scan.
  - Smaller amounts came from rare earths and from isotopes of the iron, zinc, cobalt and phosphorus groups.
- **Transfer to eggs:** Radioactivity density in the eggs rose as the embryos developed. It dropped noticeably at hatching, partly because the egg shell and its adsorbed radioactive material were discarded.
- **Ocean phase:** Salmon were expected to spend time in the ocean after leaving the river. Any latent effect of exposure there could only be assessed when adults returned in the fall of 1954.

## Key Findings

1. **Survival:** Mortality of silver salmon rose with effluent concentration. Figure 1 marks the difference as "highly significant." Survival at the 2 and 3 per cent concentrations was reduced compared with the control. (Exact values are not legible.)
2. **Growth:** By October, fish in the 5 and 10 per cent effluent were significantly smaller than controls. Growth was reduced at the higher concentrations, though the text is partly illegible.
3. **Radioactivity accumulation:** Silver salmon accumulated gross beta activity, with a gradual rise during the fry stage and a rapid increase between April and May as temperatures reached about 10°C. Later levels fluctuated at around 10 pc/g (as read from the scan).
4. **Radiation dose:** The authors reported that the radiation received was "comparatively low," so the observed adverse effects were probably caused by other factors.
5. **Causal factors:** The authors said the results closely resembled earlier studies in which temperature and chemical toxicity were clearly shown to harm fish. They attribute the observed effects to these factors.
6. **Immediate impact:** The authors concluded that the Hanford reactor effluent had no immediate effect on young silver salmon in the Columbia River during the study period.
7. **Open question:** Latent effects from exposure to 5 per cent effluent, which might appear while the salmon are at sea, could only be judged after adult fish returned in the fall of 1954.
