# Flora Representativa de Chipinque, 2a Parte: Flores y Helechos

#botany #flora #angiosperms #pteridophytes #polypodiaceae

## Executive Summary

This is the second part of the illustrated field guide *Flora Representativa de Chipinque*, a Spanish-language publication (April 1996) covering the flowers and ferns of the [[Chipinque Ecological Park]] in Nuevo León, Mexico. It was produced by a consortium led by the [[Consejo Consultivo Estatal para la Preservación y Fomento de la Flora y Fauna Silvestre de Nuevo León]] and the [[Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León]] (UANL), with funding from the company VITRO. The guide presents 44 vascular plant species, each with a botanical description, flowering season, fruit, distribution, and observations. It also includes an introduction to the park's physiography and vegetation, an illustrated botanical glossary, a technical glossary, and a bibliography. The document is a conservation-oriented botanical reference, not a fish or aquatic study.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and authorship.** The book pairs the first volume, *Árboles y Arbustos* (1995), with this volume on herbaceous and floral diversity. Scientific responsibility rests with Glafiro J. Alanis Flores, Marcela González Álvarez, Marco Antonio Guzmán Lucio, Gerónimo Cano Cano, and Magdalena Rovalo Merino. Specimens were identified at the UANL Herbarium of the [[Facultad de Ciencias Biológicas]].

**Rationale.** The introduction argues that "lo que no se conoce no se puede conservar" (what is not known cannot be conserved). It frames habitat protection, such as the managed Chipinque park, as one of the most effective strategies for conserving native flora.

**Species accounts.** Each entry follows a standard format: growth form and size, leaves, flowers, flowering period, fruit, geographic range, and observations. Ranges are mostly within Texas, Coahuila, Nuevo León, and Tamaulipas. Selected examples:

- [[Duraznillo]] (*Cercis canadensis*, Leguminosae): flowers appear before the leaves, colouring the park pink-lilac; 2–4 m tall.
- [[Corona de San Pedro]] (*Cornus florida*, Cornaceae): ornamental, with four white to pink bracts; reaches 12 m.
- [[Madroño]] (*Arbutus xalapensis*, Ericaceae): evergreen tree up to 10 m; edible fruit, but intoxicating in large amounts.
- [[Helecho Chino]] (*Phanerophlebia umbonata*, Polypodiaceae): fern up to 1 m tall, found on humid cliffs and along streams.
- [[Lantana]] (*Lantana camara*, Verbenaceae): shrub with orange-red flowers; its fruit is reported as toxic.
- [[Colorín]] (*Sophora secundiflora*, Leguminosae): flowers and seeds are toxic; seeds used in traditional medicine for earache.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Setting.** Chipinque covers 1,624 hectares in the municipality of San Pedro Garza García, on the northern exposure of the [[Sierra Madre Oriental]]. Elevation ranges from 650 to 1,800 m above sea level. Slopes range from 30 to 60% under vegetation and exceed 70% where bare rock is exposed.

**Climate and soils.** The climate is semi-warm subhumid, with summer rainfall, influenced by moist air from the southeast. The dominant soils are lithosols (stony, shallow soils). Fertile calcareous chernozem and phaeozem soils occur in some canyons and protected sites.

**Vegetation types described in the introduction:**

- **[[Pine Forest]]**: open community from about 800 m upward, 10–20 m tall, dominated by *Pinus teocote* and *P. pseudostrobus*, usually mixed with oaks and madroños.
- **[[Oak Forest]]**: the most abundant temperate community in the park, 15–20 m tall. Dominant species include *Quercus rysophylla*, *Q. polymorpha*, *Q. laceyi*, *Q. virginiana* var. *fusiformis*, *Q. canbyi*, and *Q. laeta*.
- **[[Submontane Scrub]]**: a thorny shrub and subtree community on lower slopes. Its vigour depends on water availability, soil depth, fertility, exposure, and human disturbance. Sheltered sites support tall, closed cover, while exposed or disturbed sites have shorter, spinier vegetation. Mesquite and huizache exceed 6 m where soils are deeper than 40 cm.

**Regional diversity.** The introduction cites about 2,400 vascular plant species recorded for Nuevo León (Alanis, 1993), and notes that native flora contains useful germplasm for forestry, food, forage, industry, pharmacology, and wildlife food and shelter.

**Conservation.** The text states that human activity has altered floristic diversity in some regions and accelerated species extinctions. Protected areas such as Chipinque, managed by a Patronato, are presented as a way to maintain regional biodiversity.

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** The guide covers 44 species in 27 families, mainly Compositae (8 species), Leguminosae (5), Polypodiaceae (6 ferns, including *Cheilanthes alabamensis*, *Thelypteris puberula*, *Llavea cordifolia*, and *Polypodium* spp.), and Ranunculaceae, Rosaceae, and Verbenaceae (2–3 species each). Full names and families appear in the "Lista Florística" (pp. 34).
- **Woody and herbaceous forms:** Species include annual and perennial herbs, shrubs, trees (e.g., *Prunus mexicana* up to 12 m), epiphytes (*Polypodium polypodioides*), and parasites (*Conopholis mexicana* on oak, juniper, and pine roots).
- **Flowering calendar:** Flowering spans the whole year. Many species bloom from February to May, including *Oxalis dillenii*, *Taraxacum officinale* (March–September), and *Sonchus oleraceus* (year-round). Some species, such as *Lantana camara* (May–December) and *Bouvardia ternifolia* (May–November), flower later in the year.
- **Toxicity:** Several species are reported toxic, including *Sophora secundiflora*, *Ungnadia speciosa* (seeds), *Lantana camara* (fruit), *Lupinus texensis*, and *Senecio sanguisorbae*. The last is poisonous but can be used as forage when treated.
- **Use value:** Species are noted as edible (*Arbutus xalapensis*, *Rubus trivialis*, *Selenicereus spinulosus* fruit), medicinal (*Helietta*-associated remedies are not described; instead *Acacia roemeriana*, *Bouvardia ternifolia*, *Rubus trivialis* leaf infusions), ornamental (*Cornus florida*, *Gaillardia mexicana*, *Lupinus texensis*), or important to wildlife (*Ipomoea lindheimeri*, *Vitis cinerea*, *Acacia roemeriana* seeds).
- **Invasive or weedy taxa:** *Oxalis dillenii*, *Oenothera speciosa*, *Taraxacum officinale*, *Sonchus oleraceus*, *Asphodelus fistulosus*, and *Anagallis arvensis* are listed as common weeds of disturbed sites, roadsides, and gardens.
- **Endemic-like regional restriction:** Some species are recorded only from northeastern Mexico and adjacent Texas, including *Heuchera mexicana*, *Cheilanthes alabamensis* (wide US range, Nuevo León included), and *Penstemon lanceolatus* (Coahuila, Nuevo León, Tamaulipas in Mexico).
- **Ferns:** Six fern species are described, including *Phanerophlebia umbonata* (sporulation March–November) and *Llavea cordifolia* (year-round sporulation). Ferns occur on humid cliffs, streams, shaded canyons, and as epiphytes on oak trunks.
- **Aids included:** Illustrated glossaries of plant structures, leaf shapes, inflorescences, and fruit types, plus a technical glossary of 100+ terms. The bibliography lists about 20 references, including Correll & Johnston (1979), Loughmiller (1992), and Alanis (1993).
- **Conservation message:** Habitat protection within a managed park is presented as an effective way to preserve native flora and the birds and mammals that depend on it.
