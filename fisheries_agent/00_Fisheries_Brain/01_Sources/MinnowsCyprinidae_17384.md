# Minnows (Cyprinidae) of Michigan: Culture, Biology, and Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This technical report from the Michigan Department of Conservation Institute for Fisheries Research provides comprehensive guidance on the biology, ecology, and commercial culture of minnows (family [[Cyprinidae]]) in Michigan waters. The [[Cyprinidae]] is one of the largest fish families globally, containing approximately 200 genera and over 2,000 species, with about 250 species found in [[North America]]. Michigan hosts no fewer than 38 minnow species. The report emphasizes the ecological importance of minnows as forage for game fish and other predators, describes the natural history of key bait species, and provides detailed protocols for pond culture, transportation, disease management, and handling. The Conservation Department encourages commercial minnow propagation to reduce pressure on natural populations while maintaining adequate forage for wild game fish populations.

## Background & Description

The family [[Cyprinidae]] includes shiners, dace, chubs, carp, and goldfish. True minnows are distinguished by four diagnostic characters: (1) no teeth on jaws, (2) no scales on head, (3) no spiny fin rays, and (4) single dorsal fin with fewer than ten rays. Most minnows are small, with many species never exceeding three to eight inches in length. Michigan's largest native minnow, the [[Horned Dace]] or [[Creek Chub]] (Semotilus atromaculatus), seldom exceeds 10 inches. The [[Carp]] and goldfish are introduced species from the Old World, not native to North America.

Each minnow species exhibits habitat preferences and specific spawning requirements. For example, [[Horned Dace]] and [[Common Shiner]] require stream conditions for spawning and cannot complete their life cycle in ponds. The [[Lake Emerald Shiner]] (Notropis atherinoides) is incredibly abundant in the [[Great Lakes]], forming an important dietary component for [[Lake Trout]] and other predators, but efforts to establish this species in small inland lakes have been uniformly unsuccessful.

Minnows are generally carnivorous, feeding on insects and crustaceans (water fleas), though some species are omnivorous, consuming vegetation (especially algae), organic slime, insects, and crustacea. None are strictly herbivorous. The report documents six primary bait species with their distributions, habitats, spawning requirements, and maximum lengths.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Minnows occupy critical positions in aquatic food webs, converting organic matter and microscopic plant and animal life into biomass available to larger predators. The abundance and well-being of larger food and game fishes in many waters depends upon permanent minnow stocks. Fish-eating birds, mammals, turtles, and various other animals depend on fish for food; abundant minnow populations reduce predation pressure on more valuable game and food fishes.

While some minnow species may prey on game fish eggs, they also consume eggs of noxious and less desirable species, thereby providing biological control. The [[Carp]] is the only minnow of direct importance as human food in America, though all minnows are edible. Their primary value lies in their role as intermediate links in the human food chain.

Natural minnow supplies in some Michigan lakes and streams were in serious depletion at the time of this report. Fishermen and dealers waste thousands of minnows daily through careless handling and improper transportation and holding facilities. The commercialization of inland water minnow resources conflicts with conservation programs, and some states had already moved to prohibit use of natural minnow supplies for bait purposes.

Pond productivity depends on maintaining balanced environmental factors favoring propagation and growth of fish and their food organisms. Unlike terrestrial livestock that consume forage directly, fish typically feed on smaller organisms that themselves consume plants or each other, making fish the final link in aquatic food chains. Fertilization increases natural food production (insects, water fleas, etc.). Recommended fertilization rates include one ton of horse manure per acre, or one quart of superphosphate of lime and six quarts of sheep manure per 100 cubic feet of water, or 11 pounds of soy bean meal and 5 ounces of superphosphate per 500 cubic feet of water.

## Key Findings

**Species-Specific Biology and Culture:**

**[[Blunt-nosed Minnow]]** (Hyborhynchus notatus): Wide distribution throughout Michigan in lakes and streams with sand or gravel-bottomed shoals. Maximum length 4 inches, maximum age 4 years. Thick-bodied with rounded snout protruding beyond small mouth. Prominent black spot at tail fin base. Breeding males display three rows of large calcareous tubercles across snout. Spawning extends May to late August at water temperatures around 70°F or higher. Eggs laid preferentially on undersurfaces of boards, stones, and other objects. Males guard nests. Feeds on animal plankton, aquatic insects, and algae. Easily cultured in ponds; production of 100,000 per acre possible.

**[[Golden Shiner]]** (Notemigonus crysoleucas): Found throughout Michigan in weedy lakes and sluggish streams. Maximum length 10 inches. Body deep and compressed with small head and upturned mouth. Lateral line strongly decurved. Fins yellowish; brilliant golden color in some localities, especially during breeding. Long spawning season from May to late August. Eggs adhesive, sticking to aquatic plants, roots, and filamentous algae. Reaches 3 inches by end of first year, though protracted spawning produces fish of all sizes. Maturity at one to two years. Feeds mostly on animal plankton (water fleas), occasionally insects and algae. Easy to raise in any small weedy pond; production of at least 100,000 per acre possible first summer.

**[[Horned Dace]]** or [[Creek Chub]] (Semotilus atromaculatus): Found in most active streams statewide. One of Michigan's largest minnows, males commonly reaching 8 inches. Robust body with large, broad head and large mouth reaching eye level. Conspicuous black spot at dorsal fin base and dark shoulder bar. Breeding males have two rows of large tubercles along sides of head. Spawns in clear streams on coarse gravel, usually at rapids heads, from late April to July. Male guards and builds nest. Diet largely carnivorous: insects, crayfish, earthworms, snails, small fish, some aquatic plants. Extremely hardy and active swimmer; excellent pike bait. Will not spawn in still pond water; requires stream current over gravel. Artificial propagation successful using egg stripping methods similar to trout culture. Eggs hatch in two to three weeks depending on water temperature. Attains 2-4 inches by end of first year.

**[[Common Shiner]]** (Notropis cornutus): Found throughout Michigan, most abundant in streams but occasionally in lakes. Large males attain 8 inches. Short, robust, deep body flattened side-to-side with large head. Large scales with exposed portions three times deeper than wide. Dark stripes between scale rows on back. Olive green to gray above, silver on sides, white below. Breeding males have large head tubercles; body and fins brilliantly colored in orange and pink. Spawns only on stream riffles over gravel bottom, possibly on gravel shoals in lakes, from late May to June at temperatures above 65°F. Reaches 2-3 inches by end of first year; maturity at 2-3 years. Feeds equally on plant and animal material from stream bottom to surface. Will not spawn in ponds; requires stream access or artificial current conditions.

**[[River Chub]]** (Nocomis biguttatus): Found in larger creeks and smaller rivers; seldom in stagnant waters. Robust form resembling creek chub but lacking black dorsal fin spot. Adult breeding males have entire upper head covered with large tubercles and large vermillion spots behind eyes on each side. Large, almost terminal mouth not reaching eye level. Large scales. Bluish gray with coppery reflections. Spawns over gravel riffles in one to two feet of water at 65°F or more. Male builds and guards nest. Males exceed females in size, attaining 10 inches. Maturity requires two or more years. Diet includes insect larvae and nymphs, smaller crustaceans, earthworms, terrestrial insects, algae, occasionally small crayfish and fish. Fairly hardy in storage; lively, excellent bait. Will not spawn in ponds; requires running water over gravel. Artificial stripping probably successful using horned dace methods.

**[[Northern Red-bellied Dace]]** (Chrosomus eos): Found in bog ponds (usually abundant), sluggish streams, and lakes. Maximum length 3 inches. Olive to dusky above, white below with two broad black bands along body sides. Breeding males have brilliant red abdomen and highly colored red and yellow fins. Small terminal mouth. Spawns late May into August; eggs deposited in filamentous algae masses. Maturity reached by end of first year for some individuals, second year for late-hatched fish. Mainly herbivorous. Fine bait for perch, crappie, and smallmouth bass. Extremely hardy and long-lived in crowded minnow pails. Production of well over 100,000 per acre attained in small weedy ponds. Fertilization advisable to ensure luxuriant algal growth.

**Holding and Transportation Protocols:**

Retaining tanks or screens necessary for holding minnows. Live boxes should be large (minimum 3'×3'×6') with water currents passing through. Tanks should have continuous flow of well-aerated water or continuous fine spray of compressed air near bottom. Natural water flow preferred over mechanical aeration due to equipment costs and breakdown risk. Tanks should be under cover or in shade to maintain low water temperature; cold water holds more dissolved oxygen. Avoid city water due to chlorination. Avoid water flow from one trough to another to prevent warming and disease spread. Three-quarter-inch pipe drilled with 1/8-inch holes 4-6 inches apart laid down trough center ensures constant aeration.

Avoid overcrowding at all times. Carry no more fish than can be disposed of in a few days. Maximum numbers vary with minnow size, tank size, water character, temperature, and other factors; best determined by experience. Greater numbers can be carried in cold weather than hot weather. Tanks should be drained, dried, and painted with asphaltum paint or tarred at least annually. At weekly or biweekly intervals, tanks and dip nets should be sterilized with concentrated salt solution or commercial copper sulfate (blue vitriol). Stock solution: several inches of copper sulfate in bottom of 2-quart fruit jar filled with water; approximately one pint sterilizes a 2'×2'×10' tank. Screens should be cleared daily or more often.

Ideal tank construction: wooden or smooth concrete with exposed surfaces painted with coal tar thinned with turpentine or asphaltum paint. Large metal stock tanks suitable. Rectangular outline preferred over square, with inlet at one end and outlet screen at other. Concrete tanks most satisfactory and economical at 12'×3'×2' deep. All tanks should slope slightly toward outlet to facilitate cleaning.

Transportation requires careful handling to avoid injury. When using seines, avoid drawing bag too tightly. Remove fish with small, shallow fine-mesh scoop or dip net, returning young game fish and protected species. Avoid excessive crowding, especially in hot weather. Keep water temperature below 70°F by adding ice or small quantities of cold water. Aeration increased by water splashing during transit. In large containers, hand-dipping may be necessary at short intervals. Insufficient oxygen recognized by fish at water surface, many on sides. Small oil drums or milk cans that are clean and sanitary are most efficient containers. Old syrup bucket or tomato can punched with holes makes hand aerator. Large tank fitted with water pump or aerator most satisfactory. Sterilize cans and tanks before use.

Avoid sudden temperature changes. Use pocket thermometer to compare water temperature in cans with tanks or ponds before placing fish. Get temperatures approximately equal by adding bucket of water from pond/tank to can, or placing can in tank/pond. Allow 10 minutes for each 2-3 degree change. When difference exceeds 10 degrees, allow at least 20 minutes for each 2-3 degree change. Maintain aeration during temperature adjustment. Sudden temperature changes will kill every fish.

**Feeding and Disease Management:**

Fish held in tanks for more than a few days should be fed, as undernourishment reduces vitality. Underfeeding less harmful than overfeeding, which makes fish sluggish. Give no more food than can be eaten at single feeding; surplus food becomes sour, decays, uses oxygen, pollutes water, and invites disease epidemics. Remove all uneaten food shortly after feeding. Most species can be kept for limited time on bread and cracker crumbs. Finely ground oatmeal or other cereal usually suffices. For long-term feeding, use balanced diet of vegetable, meat, and mineral materials. Cooked oatmeal and finely ground meat scraps good. Dog rations ("Balto" or "Rowena Dog Biscuits") sometimes used, mixed with raw beef liver or other ingredients. Mixture of clam or fish meals with oatmeal or other cereal also good. Occasional feeding of earthworms, Daphnia (water fleas), or other aquatic organisms valuable.

Most common diseases: fungus and tail rot. White fungus results from lack of oxygen, sudden temperature changes, decaying food, unsanitary conditions, or rough handling removing scales or protective mucous coating. Excrement from healthy fish is dark; white or yellow indicates overfeeding. Illness shown by bloodshot fins, drooping dorsal fin, fish resting on bottom, or lack of appetite. Single affected fish indicates overfeeding or constipation; many affected fish indicates problems with food, water, oxygen supply, or temperature.

Remove infected fish immediately; destroy or place in separate tank. Dip diseased fish in strong salt, potassium permanganate, or copper sulphate solution until fish shows acute distress, then remove to separate tank with fresh water. Repeat dippings daily. Twelve ounces salt per gallon water, or approximately one teaspoonful copper sulphate per gallon sufficient for dipping. If no improvement after several treatments, destroy fish. Transferring to fresh running water often sufficient. Weekly salt baths of about 3 ounces salt per gallon for healthy fish beneficial for correcting constipation and improving appetite. Leave fish in bath for half hour or until distress noted, aerating constantly. Small amounts of epsom salts sometimes used in bath or food.

**Commercial Culture Considerations:**

Starting commercial minnow culture requires plenty of pond space and small stream section with gravelly bottom. Hold certain number of adults over each year as breeders for following year. Allot certain number of fish annually to pond for bass and pike minnow supply. Most dealers find sufficient small minnows but not enough larger ones. Three to four years may be required before ample supply of larger minnows available.

Excellent first-year minnow yields possible. Small percentage lost to predation and natural causes. Numbers decrease appreciably each successive year fish are kept. Blunt-nosed minnow and fathead minnow live only two to three years, frequently dying after first spawning. Species like horned dace may eat smaller fish. If poor natural food conditions prevail, cost of feeding prohibitive, but fertilization would probably increase natural food amounts. Disease epidemics may kill all fish in pond, as occasionally occurs even in best-regulated hatcheries. Ponds must be sterilized at frequent intervals.

Until experienced in cultural work, ponds require constant attention. Fish culture does not consist merely of placing adult breeders in pond and reaping crop months later. Disease, undernourishment, overfeeding and consequences must be spotted immediately and remedy prescribed. After several years of experience, signs of trouble readily observed.

In removing fish from lakes, ponds, and streams, use traps rather than seines. Do not handle fish more than absolutely necessary. Excess handling knocks scales off and removes mucous layer, opening spots for infection.

**Preservation Methods:**

1. Formalin method: Take one part lime water (obtainable at any drugstore) and add 20 parts water. Place minnows in container that can be sealed tightly, add enough solution to cover minnows, and place in dark place (to retain natural colors and silvery hues) until ready to use. When ready to use, soak fish in water overnight to remove formalin and add a few drops of rhodium to disguise pungent formalin odor.

2. Freezing method: Place dead minnows in small containers (ice cream cartons inexpensive and easy to obtain). Place in freezer compartment of ice box. Before using, thaw minnows by placing in water.
