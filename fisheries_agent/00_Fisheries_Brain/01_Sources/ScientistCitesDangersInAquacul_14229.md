# Ecological Dangers in Aquaculture: Chemical Controls, Exotic Introductions and Population Pressure

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This 1970 opinion article by [[Clark Hubbs]], Professor of Zoology at the University of Texas at Austin, argues that fish farming ([[Aquaculture]]) offers major benefits in increasing world protein supply but carries ecological risks if practiced carelessly. The author identifies three main hazards: persistent chemical control agents, eutrophication from nutrient additions, and accidental or deliberate releases of exotic organisms. He warns that these effects can be irreversible and spread far beyond the site of application. The article closes by arguing that aquaculture should not be used to sustain continued growth of human population density.

## Background & Description

- **Publication:** Article in *The American Fish Farmer*, reprinted in the May 1970 issue (Vol. 1, No. 6) of *The American Fish Farmer & World Aquaculture News*, a monthly magazine priced at 75 cents.
- **Author:** [[Clark Hubbs]] (born 1921, Ann Arbor, Michigan). He received an A.B. from the University of Michigan in 1942 and a Ph.D. from [[Stanford University]] in 1951, after service in the U.S. Army infantry during World War II.
- **Author's research focus:** Why animals and fishes can live in their environments and how they became adapted to succeed in them.
- **Professional service:** Memberships in national societies in the U.S., South America and Japan; former editorial board member of *The American Fisheries Society* and *Texas Journal of Science*, serving as associate editor and then editor of the latter from 1955 to 1961.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Chemical pollution.** Chemical control of aquatic growth is identified as the most obvious risk. Pollution is most acute in flow-through stations, but any drained pond can affect the natural system, and chemicals can move through underground water supplies.

**Eutrophication.** Even beneficial additives such as fertilizers can be harmful where eutrophication already exists in the watershed. The risk is greatest in densely populated regions, where human waste disposal is a serious national problem.

**Persistent toxins.** Chlorinated hydrocarbons such as [[DDT]] are singled out for persistent toxicity and strong affinity for biological tissue. The author notes they concentrate in the food web at levels far exceeding initial application.

**Unpredictable chemical behaviour.** A chemical that degrades quickly under one set of conditions may persist or follow a different pathway under small environmental changes, producing more toxic by-products. Because physical, chemical and biological conditions vary widely, impacts are hard to predict.

**Exotic organisms.** Aquaculture relies on living organisms, so introductions must be carefully considered. Most aquatic organisms are harder to contain than chemicals, and accidental releases can become permanent. Successful introductions may also spread widely from the release point.

**Plant control and niches.** Dense growths of higher aquatic plants and filamentous algae in lakes and streams interfere with boating, swimming, sport fishing and water flow, and increase insect pests. Few native fishes can convert these plants into animal protein, so an exotic fish is likely to be needed for biological control.

**Niche limitation.** The author assumes a finite number of niches or adaptive peaks available to North American freshwater fishes. Each successful introduction reduces the chance for later introductions, or may drive out an earlier species. The eastern United States has a more speciose fish fauna with more finely divided niches. Introductions are therefore more likely to succeed in the West, where they may replace well-adapted native fishes.

## Key Findings

1. **Chemical controls carry major, hard-to-predict risks.** Chemical additives can move beyond the treatment site, persist, concentrate in food webs, and form toxic by-products.
2. **Persistence is an economic trade-off.** Long half-life agents may be cheap to use, but their long-term costs may exceed short-term gains.
3. **Nutrient inputs can worsen eutrophication.** Fertilizers and similar additions are most dangerous in watersheds that already suffer from nutrient overload, especially in densely populated areas.
4. **Accidental releases are inevitable and irreversible.** The author states that accidental fish releases from aquaculture are certain to occur, and a successful harmful introduction cannot easily be undone.
5. **Exotic fish selection is critical.** Any exotic chosen for aquatic plant control should be selected to minimise side effects. A mistake is more serious in natural waters than in culture facilities.
6. **Introduction dynamics favour gradual establishment.** Introduced fish typically persist in small numbers for perhaps 10 generations before rapid increase in density and range. Early introductions can reduce the success of later, better-suited species.
7. **Population growth is the root concern.** The article concludes that aquaculture should not enable continued growth in human population density, since greater food supply will eventually be followed by higher mortality, with larger populations suffering more deaths.
