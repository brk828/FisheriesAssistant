# Growth of the Walleye in the Des Moines River, Boone County, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines age, growth, harvest, and condition of [[Walleye]] in a marginal river reach of the [[Des Moines River]] in Boone County, Iowa, using samples collected from 1956 to 1958. Despite poor habitat, the walleye supports a considerable sport fishery. Tagging suggested an annual harvest rate of about 10 percent. Population size appears limited by [[Fish Kill]] events caused by summer high temperatures and winter oxygen depletion. Growth varied considerably within age groups and between year classes. The author proposed several explanations, including sexual differences, habitat vacancies from fish kills, low water levels, and hatchery stocking. Overall growth and condition were about average compared with other regions. The document also contains a truncated, unrelated paper on pheasant flushing bars in Iowa hayfields.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*), a major sport fish. Most prior age and growth work concerned lake populations, so stream and river populations were poorly documented.
- **Study area:** The section of the Des Moines River between a low-head dam at Fraser and the waterworks dam at Boone, about 7 miles long. Summer depths average 3 feet or less, with sand, gravel, and bedrock substrates and a gradient of 1.5 feet per mile. The area is described in Starrett (1950).
- **Fishery context:** Walleyes made up 16.4 percent of the catch in fall 1957 but less than 4 percent in spring and summer. They ranked third in total catch behind [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Carp]], yet attracted many anglers.
- **Sampling:** A total of 209 walleyes was collected in 1956–1958, mainly by anglers and [[Electrofishing]]. Young-of-the-year fish in 1958 were taken with a 15-foot minnow seine.

### Methods

- **Measurements:** Total length (tip of snout to tip of compressed caudal fin) and standard length (to the posterior edge of the hypural plate) were recorded. Weights were recorded to the nearest ounce. Separate conversion equations were used for fish under and over 10 inches (T.L.).
- **Ageing:** Scales were taken from below the lateral line, impressed onto plastic with a roller press, and read on a scale projector at about 42x. Each scale was read at least twice, with a third reading for disagreements.
- **Back-calculation:** A [[Body-Scale Relationship]] was derived from 54 walleyes. It was approximately linear and expressed as L = -0.26 + 0.528S, where L is total length (inches) and S is the anterior scale radius (inches) × 42. Growth was back-calculated with a [[Fish Nomograph]] (Carlander and Smith, 1944).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish kills:** A July 1955 high-temperature kill apparently decimated walleyes in parts of the river. Winter die-offs also occur when low water and heavy ice deplete oxygen.
- **Natural reproduction:** It is questionable whether walleyes reproduce naturally in the study area. The [[Iowa State Conservation Commission]] stocked large numbers of fry, fingerlings, and yearlings. Stocked fish were unmarked, so hatchery and wild fish could not be separated.
- **Stocking totals (1956–1958, from Table 1):**
  - Boone County: about 1,500,000 fry, 28,000 fingerlings, and 11,000 yearlings.
  - Webster County: about 3,000,000 fry, 28,750 fingerlings, and 10,000 yearlings.
- **Water levels:** Very low levels in September 1958 impeded adult movement over shallow riffles. The spring of 1958 was exceptionally dry with stable flows, which may have favored egg and fry survival.
- **Maturity:** Walleyes in the river may mature at 12–14 inches, as in [[Clear Lake, Iowa]] (Cleary, 1949).

## Key Findings

### Harvest and Population
- A tagging study released 44 adult walleyes in 1958; at least 4 were recaptured by anglers that year.
- The minimum annual harvest rate was about 10 percent, described as "rather high."
- Population size is limited by periodic [[Fish Kill]] events.

### Age and Growth
- Mean back-calculated total lengths (inches) at the end of each year of life were 8.4, 11.5, 14.4, 16.6, 18.7, 20.3, 21.7, 22.5, and 22.6 for successive ages (Table 3). Grand average n = 112.
- Growth was fastest in the first year and decreased steadily with age.
- Young-of-the-year grew steadily through the season. Older fish grew mainly in late spring and early fall, with little growth in midsummer.
- Annulus formation probably occurs in May or early June, based on fish taken in late May 1958 at different stages of completion.
- Individual variation within age groups was substantial. The standard deviation for the grand sample was 1.1 inches, and the paper credits the variation partly to fish from different stocking sources.
- Growth differed between the 1956 and 1957 year classes and the 1958 year class. Young-of-the-year in 1958 grew more slowly, which the author linked to low water, high population density, and competition for food.
- The 1956 year class grew somewhat faster after the 1955 kill, which the author attributed to habitat vacancies.
- Females are known to grow faster than males after about the third or fourth year, but sexes were pooled because sex was hard to determine in summer and fall.

### Comparative Growth
- First-year growth exceeded that of most comparison waters. Growth in later years was below average.
- Comparisons included [[Cedar River]] (Mayhew, 1956), several Iowa lakes (Carlander, 1948), [[Clear Lake, Iowa]], [[Spirit Lake]], Minnesota and Wisconsin lakes, and [[Norris Reservoir]], Tennessee.
- The only other river study (Cedar River) suggested faster growth in rivers than in lakes.

### Length-Weight and Condition
- Length-weight relationship: log W = -2.422 + 3.140 (log L), where W is in ounces and L is total length in inches.
- Mean ponderal index values were K = 1.58 and C = 33.4.
- Longer fish showed slightly higher C values, meaning they were slightly heavier for their length.
- Condition compared favorably with walleyes from other waters.

### Conclusions
- The Des Moines River is marginal walleye habitat, but it supports a considerable fishery.
- Growth and general well-being are about average compared with other parts of the country.
- Suggested explanations for growth variation are sexual differences, habitat vacancies after fish kills, low water, and frequent stocking of fry, fingerlings, and yearlings.

### Unrelated Paper in the Same Document
- The document also includes an abstract and introduction (truncated) for a separate study, "Evaluation of Effectiveness of Pheasant Flushing Bars in Iowa Hayfields," on the [[Ring-Necked Pheasant]].
- Over three years on 394 acres, flushing bars reduced hen mortality by an overall 38 percent (significant at p = .01). Only 1954 showed a significant reduction, at 54 percent (p = .05).
- Effectiveness declined as hay density and height increased. The author suggests a "security threshold" related to cover density.
- The study did not show a measurable increase in fall pheasant populations, and [[Carrying Capacity]] may offset any gain.
