# The Pleasant River: Fish Management and Restoration

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 report, issued by the [[Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Game]] and the [[Atlantic Salmon Commission]], condenses biological surveys of the [[Pleasant River]] drainage in eastern Maine. It evaluates the lakes and streams for sport and commercial fisheries, catalogues obstructions to fish migration, and identifies management and restoration actions. The main priorities are restoring the [[Atlantic Salmon]] run, restoring [[Alewife]] runs, maintaining the [[Brook Trout]] fishery, and removing or modifying barriers such as [[Hathaway Dam]], [[Saco Falls]], and the [[Pleasant River Lake Dam]]. The authors state that full fishery development depends on carrying out their recommendations.

## Background & Description

- **Drainage:** The Pleasant River rises in western Washington County and flows southeasterly about 36 miles to the Atlantic Ocean at Addison. Its drainage area above [[Columbia Falls]] covers 85 square miles of timber and blueberry lands.
- **Character:** The system is mainly a network of cold streams, many fed by overflow from large boiling springs. Lakes are relatively few. The main river is deep and slow-moving for much of its length, with an average gradient below [[Pleasant River Lake]] of less than 9 feet per mile.
- **Fish community:** The drainage supports salmon, brook trout, white perch, smelt, hornpout, eel, alewife, white sucker, fallfish (chub), creek chub, golden shiner, finescale dace, redbelly dace, lake northern chub, blacknose shiner, minnows, threespine stickleback, pumpkinseed sunfish, yellowbelly sunfish, and lamprey eel.
- **Study scope:** Surveys covered both lake and stream areas. Stream work was conducted jointly by the Fishery Research and Management Division and Atlantic Salmon Commission personnel. The report is a condensation of the original studies, intended for industries holding property in the drainage and for local sportsmen's groups.
- **Lakes of major importance:** [[Pleasant River Lake]] (Beddington, 949 acres) and [[Montegail Pond]]. Full lake reports appear in *Maine Lakes: A Sportsman's Inventory* (Everhart et al., 1953), available from the Department in Augusta.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lakes**

- **[[Montegail Pond]]:** A marginal trout pond with two unusual conditions: numerous spring holes across the bottom, and dense submerged aquatic vegetation that prevents complete mixing of warm surface and cold bottom water. A pond this shallow in eastern Maine would normally be warm throughout and unsuitable for trout. Introduction of competing species could eliminate the trout fishery, so live-bait fishing must be controlled. Periodic stocking is expected to help maintain a satisfactory fishery.
- **[[Pleasant River Lake]]:** Provides suitable coldwater habitat. Its large [[White Perch]] population will curtail salmon and trout production. The lake has moderate potential for alewives, and its outlet below the dam is among the best landlocked salmon spawning areas in the lake.

**Streams and Migration Barriers**

- **[[Hathaway Dam]] (Columbia Falls):** Built for sawmill water storage and an obstruction to upstream migration. A bypass canal, recently built, should allow fish passage if operated properly.
- **[[North Branch Dam]]:** Located about one-third mile above the North Branch junction and associated with a natural ledge falls. The remains are considered a complete barrier to upstream migration.
- **[[Saco Falls]] (Columbia):** A partial obstruction, mainly due to turbulence rather than jump height. A bypass was blasted around the lower section in 1955; the upper section needs similar work.
- **[[Saco Dam]]:** A low concrete structure above Saco Falls built to store water. It no longer blocks migration.
- **[[Colonel Brook Dam]]:** Formerly used for water storage and now non-functional. It is open enough on one side to allow both upstream and downstream migration.
- **[[Pleasant River Lake Dam]]:** In poor repair but probably passable around its right side at highest water levels.
- **[[Beaver Dams]]:** Numerous at the time of the most recent survey. Some block migration, and silt or impounded water may prevent spawn in the bottom rubble from developing.

**Pollution:** Present in such minor quantities that little or no harm to aquatic organisms results.

## Key Findings

**Atlantic Salmon**

- The salmon fishery has been kept to a minimum throughout the river's recent history. Past legal capture by nets and weirs, plus a probably small escapement above Saco Falls, likely caused the current small adult run.
- Spawning and nursery areas between tidal water and Saco Falls have apparently supported remnants of the original run.
- Stocking consists of about 10,000 hatchery-reared salmon annually. Stock was planted from brood years 1949, 1953, and 1954 in 1950, 1953, and 1955 (the source text is partly garbled here). Fish planted before October 1955 were unmarked; the 1955 plant was marked by removing the anal and left ventral fins.
- Stocking records list 9,999 fish in the 1955 marked plant (majority returns expected in 1959), about 10,005 fish with no mark (returns 1958), and about 10,004 fish with no mark (returns 1955 per source). Values are partly illegible in the scan.
- Surveys using seining and electrofishing showed a high population per unit area above Saco Falls.
- The report stresses that increasing the salmon population depends mainly on natural reproduction rather than hatchery introductions. It recommends efforts to increase spawning escapement to the upper drainage.
- Recommendation: manage the run for sport fishing only. Taking salmon by means other than angling in estuarine and freshwater sections will curtail sport fishery development.

**Brook Trout**

- The drainage offers excellent brook trout habitat, and the population is probably maintained mainly by natural reproduction.
- Stocking returns of legal-sized trout (percent):

|---|---|---|
| North Carolina | 6.4 | 44.5 |
| Michigan | 4.1 | 25.4 |

- Studies of five states in the 1940s found wild trout made up 61 to 98 percent of total catch. Fingerling stockings (3.0 to 6.0 inches) returned 0.4 to 2.6 percent.
- Because the comparison areas likely face heavier fishing pressure than the Pleasant River, stocked trout probably contribute little to the local catch. If stocking continues, spring stocking with large fish gives the best returns.

**Alewives**

- The drainage has moderate alewife potential. Based on other alewife-producing drainages, it could produce annual commercial landings approaching 300,000 pounds.
- Obstructions have held the population at a low level.
- Adult stocking restoration began in 1954 by the Department of Sea and Shore Fisheries, the Atlantic Salmon Commission, and the Pleasant River Conservation Club. Completing obstruction modifications would speed full recovery.
- Young alewives are valuable forage for both lake and stream fishes.

**Recommendations**

1. Manage the Pleasant River Atlantic salmon run for sport fishing only.
2. Keep a full head on the Hathaway Dam when possible. The bypass canal works efficiently only with sufficient flow. Removing facing boards would attract fish to the impassable dam face and reduce canal flow.
3. Fish passage at the North Branch Dam and falls is unnecessary because salmon spawning and nursery habitat there is limited.
4. Study further the reduction of the Saco Falls barrier by dynamiting and flow diversion, with changes made as needed and funds allow.
5. Modify the Pleasant River Lake Outlet Dam to allow unobstructed migration of game fishes and alewives and to regulate flows over upper-river salmon habitat. Stream nursery production is closely linked to flow volume.
6. Remove beaver dams that flood salmon spawning areas or block migration, after examination by Fish and Game Department personnel.
7. Prompt compliance with these recommendations will give the fastest development of salmon and alewife populations and fisheries.
