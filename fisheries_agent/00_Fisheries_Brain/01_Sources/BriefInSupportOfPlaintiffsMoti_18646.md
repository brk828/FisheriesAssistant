# Legal Brief: Colorado Wildlife Federation v. Turner - Razorback Sucker Critical Habitat Designation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #endangered-species-act #conservation-law

## Executive Summary

This legal brief, filed August 18, 1992, supports a motion for summary judgment compelling the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) to designate critical habitat for the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). The [[Colorado Wildlife Federation]] and co-plaintiffs argue that FWS violated its statutory duty by failing to designate critical habitat within the mandatory two-year deadline following the species' proposed listing. The razorback sucker, once abundant throughout 3,500 miles of the [[Colorado River Basin]], has declined to less than one-quarter of its former range due primarily to habitat destruction from water diversion, dam construction, and introduction of non-native predatory fishes.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a fish species native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Historical records document its former abundance: a significant commercial fishery existed in southern Arizona in the early 1900s, and residents near Clifton, Colorado reported several thousand razorbacks during annual spring runoff in the 1930s and early 1940s (56 Fed. Reg. at 54,958).

By 1991, the species had experienced catastrophic decline. Most individuals collected in the wild were older fish, indicating recruitment failure—young razorbacks were not surviving to adulthood (56 Fed. Reg. at 54,959). This demographic pattern signaled imminent extinction risk as the aging population died off naturally.

The FWS identified habitat destruction as the primary cause of decline, specifically: destruction, adverse modification, and curtailment of habitat through water diversion and depletion, and dam construction and operation (56 Fed. Reg. at 54,962-63). Introduction of predatory non-native fishes contributed secondarily to the decline.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The razorback sucker historically occupied approximately 3,500 miles of riverine habitat throughout the [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries. The species' range has contracted to less than 25% of its historical distribution.

**Habitat Requirements**: As emphasized in the brief, [[Congress]] recognized habitat destruction as "the major cause for the extinction of species worldwide" when enacting the ESA. The Act defines critical habitat as specific areas "essential to the conservation of the species" requiring "special management considerations or protection" (16 U.S.C. § 1532(5)).

**Threats**: Federal water-development projects proceeding in razorback habitat posed ongoing risks. The brief specifically mentions the [[Animas-La Plata Project]] and Blocks 7 and 8 of the [[Navajo Indian Irrigation Project]] as projects already found to affect the razorback.

**Population Status**: The demographic structure showing predominantly older individuals with poor recruitment indicated a population in terminal decline without intervention.

## Key Findings

**Legal Timeline and Violations**:
- March 1989: Plaintiffs petitioned FWS to list razorback as endangered
- May 22, 1990: FWS published proposed listing rule
- October 23, 1991: FWS listed razorback as endangered but declared critical habitat "not determinable"
- May 22, 1992: Statutory deadline for critical habitat designation passed without action
- August 18, 1992: This brief filed

**Statutory Requirements**: Under ESA § 4, FWS must designate critical habitat "concurrently" with listing "to the maximum extent prudent and determinable" (16 U.S.C. § 1533(a)(3)(A)). When not determinable at listing, designation must occur within two years of the proposed listing notice (16 U.S.C. § 1533(b)(6)(C)(ii)).

**FWS Proposed Timeline** (deemed inadequate by plaintiffs):
- Initial proposal: Draft rulemaking package by December 1992 (no firm commitment)
- Revised timeline (June 1992): Extended to September 1993 for proposed rule after deciding to include three additional endangered fish species ([[Colorado Squawfish]] *Ptychocheilus lucius*, [[Bonytail Chub]] *Gila elegans*, [[Humpback Chub]] *Gila cypha*)
- Estimated final rule: September 1994 at earliest—28 months past statutory deadline

**Comparative Case**: In [[Northern Spotted Owl v. Lujan]] (758 F. Supp. 621, W.D. Wash. 1991), FWS completed critical habitat designation in less than 11 months under court order:
- Court order: February 26, 1991
- Proposed rule: May 6, 1991 (2.5 months)
- Final rule: January 15, 1992 (10.5 months)

**Requested Remedy**: Plaintiffs requested the court order FWS to designate critical habitat within 90 days, arguing this timeframe was reasonable given the spotted owl precedent and that any delay beyond the expired statutory deadline contravened Congressional intent.

**Harm from Delay**: Without designated critical habitat, ESA § 7(a)(2) protections requiring federal agencies to "insure" actions do not destroy or adversely modify critical habitat could not be applied. Ongoing federal water projects in razorback habitat proceeded without this protective mechanism, posing continued risk to the species.

**Standing**: Plaintiffs established standing through members' aesthetic, conservational, and recreational interests. Wright McEwen had fished for razorbacks historically; Dr. Will Durant rafted portions of the [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] where razorback populations existed, with the species' presence contributing to his enjoyment.

**Legal Standard**: The brief emphasized that under *Tennessee Valley Authority v. Hill* (437 U.S. 153, 1978), the ESA requires endangered species receive "the highest of priorities," and courts must issue injunctions to remedy ESA violations with limited equitable discretion.
