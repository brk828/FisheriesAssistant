# Effects of Food Availability on Survival and Growth of Larval Razorback Suckers in Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Diana Papoulias]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] tested whether food limitation contributes to recruitment failure of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. Larvae were reared in twelve earthen ponds at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico, under high, medium and low fertilization regimes that generated different levels of zooplankton. Survival did not differ among treatments (grand mean 78.1 ± ‑ %), but growth and biomass were significantly greater in high and medium treatments. The authors conclude that nutritional deficiency at the lowest reservoir zooplankton densities may plausibly explain year-class failure in Lake Mohave, while higher densities are unlikely to cause starvation.

## Background & Description

- **Species of concern:** [[Razorback Sucker]], an endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Lake Mohave holds the largest known population, but adults are old (average >30 years) and no recruitment has been detected, although larvae are produced annually and disappear at about 12 mm total length (TL).
- **Hypothesized causes:** Egg and larval predation by non-native fishes has been proposed; the authors instead test [[Nutritional Mediation]] of larval mortality, a mechanism established for marine fish larvae and for [[Gizzard Shad]] / [[Threadfin Shad]] year-class failure in Lake Texoma and Lake Mead.
- **Study design:** Twelve ponds (0.04–0.10 ha; mean 0.06 ha) were assigned to high (341 kg/ha alfalfa pellets plus 57 kg/ha PO₄-P), medium (170 and 28 kg/ha) and low (no fertilization) treatments in a stratified block design. Ponds were stocked on 25 February 1985 with 9.4–10.7 mm TL larvae at 250,000/ha, with no supplemental feeding, and drained 18–19 April 1985 (about 50 days).
- **Methods:** Weekly sampling of chlorophyll *a*, invertebrates (by habitat: shallows, bottom, open water), surface temperature and larval fish; gut content analysis; prey selectivity assessed with [[Strauss Selectivity Index]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Surface temperatures rose from 12°C (February) to 17°C (April). Chlorophyll *a* peaked within two weeks of filling, earliest in high-fertilization ponds.
- High-treatment ponds averaged 43.3 organisms/L and 2455 mm³/m³ of invertebrates; medium and low treatments averaged 23.7 and 12.5 organisms/L, respectively. Cladocerans, copepods and ova were numerically greatest in high treatments.
- Invertebrate assemblages were dominated by small taxa, especially early on. Lake Mohave zooplankton is typically low (<5 organisms/L in many samples; 1.5 organisms/L in 1985 at larval sites), while [[Lake Mead]] and the lower Colorado reservoirs are generally oligo- or mesotrophic.
- [[Tiger Salamander]] larvae colonized three of four ponds per treatment; differences in their biomass among treatments were not significant, and their presence did not alter sucker survival or growth.
- Historically, [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae developed in backwaters of turbid, fluctuating desert rivers, with spring flows and snowmelt flooding in May–June.

## Key Findings

**Survival**
- No significant differences among low, medium and high treatments: 77.0 ± 8.1%, 89.8 ± 6.8% and 67.4 ± 15.7% (grand mean 78.1 ± %).
- Survival at low prey densities (~13 organisms/L) was higher than in [[White Sucker]] (55% at ~550 organisms/L), suggesting razorback suckers may be efficient at surviving on low food densities.

**Growth and biomass**
- Final biomass in high and medium treatments (29.6 ± 5.9 and 29.0 ± 2.7 kg/ha) was about twice that of low treatments (15.6 ± 3.5 kg/ha).
- Individual weights were 149.6 mg (high), 132.3 mg (medium) and 95.7 mg (low).
- Growth rate (log–log regression) differed significantly between high and low, and medium and low, but not between high and medium.

**Feeding and prey selection**
- Initial feeding involved diatoms, phytoplankton and detritus; by day 7 invertebrates dominated diets. Chironomids and rotifers were dominant early, with cladocerans becoming important later.
- Larvae <12 mm TL selected prey ~0.1 mm wide (rotifers and small chironomids); copepods (~0.2 mm) were taken at intermediate sizes; cladocerans (≥0.3 mm) dominated diets of fish above ~18 mm TL.
- Gut volume increased with larval size, with about 100 food items per gut maintained; larger prey were eaten as fish grew.
- Estimated search volume per 100 food items: 2.3 L (high), 4.2 L (medium), 8.0 L (low), offered as a possible energetic explanation for growth differences.

**Comparison with wild larvae**
- In Lake Mohave, 76% of 98 larvae (mean 10.8 mm TL) had empty guts, versus 20% of comparable larvae at Dexter ponds.
- Wild larvae averaged 10.6 mm TL in 1985 with no growth recorded, whereas Dexter larvae reached 16.0 mm in 49 days in unfertilized ponds.
- Laboratory work (Papoulias and Minckley 1989) identified a critical period at about 8–19 days post-hatching when larvae shift from yolk to exogenous food; cooler pond and reservoir temperatures may extend it.

**Conclusions**
- Nutritional deficiency at the lowest recorded reservoir zooplankton densities may account for year-class failure in Lake Mohave, though the mechanism is not proven.
- Starvation is considered unlikely at higher zooplankton densities unless few prey are of an edible size.
