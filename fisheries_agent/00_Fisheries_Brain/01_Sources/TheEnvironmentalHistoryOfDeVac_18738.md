# The Environmental History of De Vaca's Wondrous Journey

## Executive Summary
This source is a work of **historical/environmental ecology**, not ichthyology — it reconstructs pre-Contact and early-Contact ecological conditions of the Gulf Coast and Southern Plains through the 1527–1536 Narváez/Cabeza de Vaca expedition's survivor narrative (the *Relación*). No fish taxa are discussed; the dominant faunal and ecological subject is the **American Bison (Bison bison)**, supplemented by incidental natural history (opossum, deer, bear, "lion") and Indigenous land-management practices (fire ecology). The note below extracts all scientifically relevant ecological, faunal, and paleoclimatic data faithfully to the text's actual content.

## Background & Description
Faunal descriptions embedded in the *Relación*, transmitted via the Spaniard **Figueroa** and Cabeza de Vaca himself:

- **[[American Bison]] (Bison bison)** — described as encountered along the lower Colorado River (Texas) as early as the 1530s:
  - Body size comparable to cattle of Spain
  - Small horns, likened to "cows of Morocco"
  - Long, "flocky" hair compared to merino sheep wool
  - Coloration: tawny to black
  - Flesh judged "finer and fatter" than Spanish cattle
- **Opossum reference** — an unnamed "animal with a pocket in its belly, in which it carries its young," the earliest documented European description of *Didelphis virginianus*, cited as evidence of the *Relación*'s authenticity.
- Gulf forest fauna catalogued in passing: nine species of "immense trees," eleven bird species, deer of three types, bears, and "lions" (likely cougar) — described near [[Apalachen]] in present-day Florida.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
### Gulf Coast Forest / Bottomlands
- De Vaca's account of fallen, "astonishingly high" trees obstructing passage suggests **bottomland hardwood forest** rather than fire-maintained uplands.
- References to "fine pastures for herds" near Apalachen indicate an **active fire ecology** maintained by Indigenous burning practices, contradicting a purely "pristine wilderness" model of pre-Contact North America.

### Southern Plains Bison Economy
- **[[Figueroa's Bison Account]]** documents bison range extending to the lower Colorado River and possibly toward Florida, supporting evidence of a **regional bison economy** across the Southern Plains persisting with fluctuations for ~9,000 years.
- **Paleoclimate correlation**: Studies along the **Pedernales River** indicate the Texas Hill Country, upper Colorado, and upper Brazos underwent a drying (**xeric**) trend from **1000–1200 AD**, peaking **1300–1400 AD**.
- This aridification shortened grasses and opened Hill Country habitat, permitting bison range expansion **eastward and southward** — a "push/pull" dynamic: overcrowding on the High Plains (push) combined with expanding favorable habitat (pull).
- Indigenous fire management (documented via Figueroa) served multiple ecological functions:
  - Encircling and driving deer and lizards for hunting
  - **Manipulating bison movement/distribution**
  - Reducing mosquito populations (described as severe enough to cause afflictions of great suffering)
- A regional **hide-distribution trade network** is described: peoples living near bison range redistributed "a vast many hides into the interior country," indicating bison products as a currency/trade good far beyond the animal's immediate range — relevant to [[Trade Networks of the Southern Plains]].

### Human-Environment Interactions
- Diet stress among Gulf Coast and South Texas Indigenous groups (prickly pear reliance for ~3 months yearly) reflects marginal carrying capacity in that ecoregion.
- Disease dynamics intersect with faunal/human population data: "thinly peopled" descriptions of the Gulf Coast may reflect pre-existing epidemic depopulation rather than natural sparse settlement.
- Notably, afflicted Indigenous peoples attributed disease causation to the Europeans directly, rather than to broken cosmological relationships with animal spirits — a data point relevant to debates over Indigenous interpretations of disease causation.

## Key Findings
- **No fish or aquatic taxa are discussed** in this source; it is a terrestrial environmental history centered on human-faunal-climate interactions during the De Vaca expedition (1528–1536).
- The **earliest European written description of bison** in the Southern Plains region (via Figueroa) provides morphological and distributional data predating later, more famous bison accounts (e.g., Coronado expedition).
- Paleoclimatic drying (1000–1400 AD) is linked causally to bison range expansion into the Texas Hill Country and possibly beyond, an early example of **climate-driven megafauna range shift** documented via combined historical-ecological and sedimentary proxy evidence.
- Indigenous anthropogenic fire regimes are identified as a key driver of **both plant community structure and bison distribution**, challenging "pristine wilderness" models of pre-Contact North America.
- The opossum reference is flagged as a **provenance/authenticity marker** for the *Relación* as a natural history document.
- Disease ecology data suggest the Gulf Coast may have already experienced epidemic disease from Caribbean/Mesoamerican contact prior to the Narváez expedition's arrival, complicating population-density interpretations from the text.
