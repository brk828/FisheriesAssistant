# Strategy for Fisheries Management and Development

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-policy

## Executive Summary

This document is the FAO *Strategy for Fisheries Management and Development* (Rome, 1986), endorsed by consensus at the FAO World Conference on Fisheries Management and Development (27 June–6 July 1984). It is a non-binding set of principles and guidelines for governments and international organizations, supported by five associated Programmes of Action. It responds to the new ocean regime codified in the 1982 [[United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea]], under which coastal States (commonly up to 200 miles) gained management authority over most of the world's fish resources. The Strategy frames fisheries as a tool for food security, poverty alleviation for artisanal fishers, and sustainable resource use, and it stresses national self-reliance, training, data collection, stock assessment, and international cooperation.

## Background & Description

The document was prepared through a multi-stage process:

- **1979:** The FAO Conference (Twentieth Session, Resolution 4/79) endorsed a comprehensive assistance programme for developing coastal States managing fisheries in their [[Exclusive Economic Zone]]s.
- **October 1983:** The Committee on Fisheries (Fifteenth Session) considered the proposed Strategy and Programmes of Action, suggesting programme areas including planning, small-scale fisheries, aquaculture, international fish trade, and undernutrition.
- **19–23 March 1984:** An Ad Hoc Consultation of Government Experts reviewed drafts, incorporating comments from over 40 countries and several international organizations.
- **27 June–6 July 1984:** The [[FAO World Conference on Fisheries Management and Development]] was attended by 62 ministers responsible for fisheries and delegates from 147 countries, three Liberation Movements, 14 UN representatives, observers from 24 intergovernmental and 29 international non-governmental organizations.

The Director-General, [[Edouard Saouma]], described the Strategy as a "World Charter for Fisheries," a distillation of consensus experience rather than a binding treaty. The Strategy is structured in eight parts (Sections I–VIII), each with numbered principles and guidelines, and it explicitly does not reopen issues settled at the Third UN Conference on the Law of the Sea and respects national sovereignty.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Strategy treats fish stocks as renewable but vulnerable resources. Key ecological points include:

- Fishery resources are "subject to overexploitation, depletion and to the influence of environmental factors," so management must rest on knowledge of stock magnitude, distribution, annual recruitment variation, and species interactions (Principle 14(i)).
- The focus of management should shift "toward entire ecosystems," building on single-stock experience (14(ii)).
- Pollution and habitat degradation, including discharge from [[Aquaculture]] installations, must be controlled, with protection of critical coastal ecosystems such as [[Mangrove Ecosystems]] (8(xvii), 14(x)).
- Straddling stocks, highly migratory species, and inland water species that fall under more than one jurisdiction require harmonized regional management (3, 14(xi)–(xii)).
- Management systems should also reckon with [[Open Access Fisheries]], where lack of individual incentive to conserve leads to depletion and over-capitalization (14(ix)).

## Key Findings

**Section-by-section principles (summary)**

1. **Contribution to national goals (Sec. I, paras 6–8):** Fisheries policy is sovereign and multi-objective (food, foreign exchange, income, employment, conservation). Principles include integrating fishery plans into national economic and food security plans, explicit objective setting, periodic evaluation, and attention to [[Small-Scale Fisheries]], sport fishing, and low-cost products.
2. **National self-reliance (Sec. II, paras 9–10):** Substantial, sustained investment in training at all levels (administrators, mid-level staff, fishers), postgraduate courses, regional permanent training centres (with FAO, especially for stock assessment), and inclusion of women in training.
3. **Rational management (Sec. III, paras 11–14):** Management is "an essential tool for the sound, sustained development of fisheries," not a constraint. Governments should lead management, involve fishers in decisions, establish clear fishing rights, prevent catches exceeding productivity, and establish compliance controls (observer programmes, regional registers of foreign vessels) at the least possible cost.
4. **Small-scale fisheries (Sec. IV, paras 15–16):** Small-scale fisheries supply about half of world fish for human consumption, are devoted almost entirely to domestic consumption, and employ large numbers of poor fishers. Priorities include integrated rural development, credit access, post-harvest loss reduction, vessel safety, participation of fishing communities, and aquaculture as supplementary income.
5. **International trade (Sec. V, paras 17–18):** Developing countries face barriers including quality standards, tariffs and non-tariff barriers, and export concentration in raw materials. Recommendations include diversifying exports toward final products, intraregional trade, adherence to [[Codex Alimentarius]] standards, and coordinated market information.
6. **Investment (Sec. VI, paras 19–20):** Investment financing for fisheries had "lost momentum," and past projects often faced implementation problems. Recommendations emphasize more funding for small-scale fisheries and aquaculture, "global loan" mechanisms, better agency coordination, and conditions that attract private investment.
7. **Economic and technical cooperation (Sec. VII, paras 21–22):** Cooperation among developing countries and with developed countries, through joint ventures, regional fishery bodies, data sharing, pooled surveillance, and reinforced negotiating capacity, especially for small island States.
8. **International cooperation (Sec. VIII, paras 23–26):** The extension of national jurisdiction covers 95 percent of the world's marine fish resources currently under exploitation (para 23). [[FAO Regional Fishery Bodies]] are identified as the key mechanism for data exchange, allocation solutions, joint research, and technical assistance. Recommendations include central data collection from all fleets fishing a common resource, greater funding for regional bodies, coordination with UN agencies (UNESCO/IOC, UNEP, WMO, IMO), and expanded use of fish in food aid. Shared [[Research Vessels]] under the UNDP/FAO Fisheries Vessels Pool Agreement are also recommended.

**Overall conclusions**

- The Strategy is non-mandatory but represents global consensus on management and development principles.
- Its central message is that extended jurisdiction creates opportunity, but authority alone is "a necessary but not a sufficient condition" for sustainable management (para 3).
- Success depends on research capacity, legal and institutional frameworks, stock assessment capability, training, and sustained, coordinated international support.
