# Water and Environmental Issues of the Colorado River Border Region: Roundtable Workshop Proceedings

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ecosystem-management #binational-conservation #delta-restoration #wetlands #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

On April 30, 1998, the [[Pacific Institute]] and [[Defenders of Wildlife]] hosted a binational workshop in San Luis Rio Colorado, Sonora, Mexico, attended by approximately 80 stakeholders from both the United States and Mexico. The workshop addressed critical environmental and water management issues affecting the [[Colorado River Delta]], [[Salton Sea]], and upper [[Gulf of California]]. Participants included public officials, water users, scientists, and non-governmental organizations from both countries. The event focused on two major themes: the binational components of the [[Salton Sea]] ecological crisis and institutional developments in [[Colorado River]] management, including proposed changes to surplus flow criteria, off-stream storage, and the possibility of adding an environmental minute to the 1944 US-Mexico water treaty.

The workshop documented severe ecological degradation throughout the lower Colorado River basin, including massive disease outbreaks killing tens of thousands of birds at the Salton Sea, loss of native fish species, degradation of riparian habitat, and reduction of the Colorado River Delta wetlands from approximately one million acres historically to remnant patches. Despite this degradation, significant habitat remains in the delta region, including approximately 10,000 hectares of quality riparian and wetland habitat created incidentally by flood releases since 1979.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Delta]] historically supported one million acres of wetlands comprising one of the richest biodiversity regions in southwestern North America. By 1998, most of this area had been reduced to barren salt flats due to upstream dams and water diversions. However, six critical wetland areas remained within and around the [[Colorado River Delta-Upper Gulf of California Biosphere Reserve]]: [[El Doctor]], [[Rio Hardy]], [[Cienega de Santa Clara]], [[Laguna Salada]], the riparian corridor of the mainstem, and the inter-tidal zone.

The [[Salton Sea]], created accidentally in 1905-1907, had become an important stopover on the [[Pacific Flyway]], supporting several thousand geese and ducks in winter and several million shorebirds. By the 1990s, the Sea was experiencing severe ecological problems including increasing salinity (approaching 45 parts per thousand), agricultural drainage contamination, massive algae blooms, anoxic conditions, and widespread fish kills. The dominant fish species was [[Tilapia]], an introduced African species with wide environmental tolerance.

The 1944 US-Mexico water treaty allocated 1.5 million acre-feet annually to Mexico, with an additional 200,000 acre-feet in surplus conditions. The treaty created the [[International Boundary and Water Commission]] (IBWC) to manage binational water issues. By 1998, all Colorado River water had been allocated among the seven basin states and Mexico, though not all was being used. California was using 600,000-800,000 acre-feet beyond its 4.4 million acre-foot apportionment by utilizing unused portions of Arizona and Nevada allocations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The workshop documented catastrophic disease outbreaks at the [[Salton Sea]]. In 1992, more than 150,000 [[Eared Grebe]]s died within three months of unknown causes. In 1996, over 20,000 birds representing 64 species died of [[Avian Botulism]], including 1,229 endangered [[Brown Pelican]]s and 8,000 [[White Pelican]]s (representing 15% of the western North American population). The botulism outbreak was linked to [[Vibrio]] infections in tilapia, which weakened the fish and allowed botulism spores to penetrate their tissues. In 1997, [[Newcastle's Disease]] killed essentially the entire colony of [[Double-crested Cormorant]]s on Mullet Island. By April 1998, over 17,000 birds representing 70 species had died.

Research presented at the workshop demonstrated that flood releases since 1979 had recreated significant riparian habitat along the main Colorado River channel in Mexico, including 14-year-old [[Cottonwood]] stands. In 1997, approximately 800,000 acre-feet flowed through the delta. Vegetation zones were documented totaling over 12,000 hectares of quality habitat, including 160 hectares of dense [[Willow]] corridor, several hundred hectares of cottonwoods and willows, 6,000-7,000 hectares of mixed cottonwood-willow-mesquite, and 6,000 hectares of emergent wetlands supported by agricultural drainage.

Water quality monitoring of the [[New River]], which originates in Mexico and flows into the Salton Sea, revealed significant contamination from municipal wastewater and agricultural drainage. San Luis Rio Colorado was discharging approximately 300 liters/second of untreated wastewater into the Colorado River delta. Since 1972, some 55 million cubic meters of wastewater had been discharged. Only 38% of the city's 160,000 inhabitants had sanitation service, with half of that infrastructure over 30 years old.

Research demonstrated a strong positive correlation (with one-year lag) between Colorado River flows and shrimp catch in the upper Gulf of California, extending as far south as Guaymas. Freshwater flows during 200 cubic meters/second dominated almost to the end of the inter-tidal zone, lowering salinity even at the tip of Montague Island.

The workshop documented numerous endangered and threatened species in the region listed under the US [[Endangered Species Act]], including: [[Vaquita]] (harbor porpoise), [[Totoaba]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Yellow-billed Cuckoo]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. The [[Oax Pa]] (Cocopah) people, indigenous to the Rio Hardy area, had experienced drastic population decline and cultural disruption due to loss of traditional fishing and harvesting of [[Palmer's Grass]] (Distichlis palmeri).

## Key Findings

**Salton Sea Crisis and Binational Implications:**
The workshop identified 13 main problems relating to the Salton Sea through a 1997 Bureau of Reclamation workshop attended by over 60 participants including 50 top scientists. Disease outbreaks were identified as outcomes rather than causes, stemming from combinations of pathogens and susceptible hosts in degraded environmental conditions. Several proposed Salton Sea restoration alternatives would directly or indirectly affect Mexico, including plans to pump hyper-saline water into [[Laguna Salada]] in Mexico. To date, Mexico had not been formally consulted regarding these proposals, though the Salton Sea Authority and Bureau of Reclamation had recently indicated interest in briefing Mexico.

**Ecological Value of Flood Releases:**
Research demonstrated that incidental flood releases since 1979 had created approximately 10 times more quality habitat in the Mexican delta than existed on the US side of the border, at zero cost compared to the $312 million proposed for Salton Sea restoration. The habitat supported all age classes of cottonwoods from seedlings to 14-year-old trees. Flood flows were shown to be a logarithmic function, meaning moderate flows provided nearly the same benefit as huge flows, with approximately 20% of surplus flows providing 90% of the ecological benefit.

**Proposed Water Management Changes:**
The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] proposed an off-stream storage rule allowing interstate storage and transfer of Colorado River water, particularly enabling Nevada to store unused apportionment in Arizona through direct aquifer recharge or indirect storage (using Colorado River water for irrigation in lieu of groundwater pumping). Critics argued this would reduce Lake Mead storage, decrease flood releases to the delta, promote unsustainable growth in Las Vegas, and eventually threaten water supplies from the [[Virgin River]]. The proposal lacked adequate NEPA analysis, Endangered Species Act compliance, and objective definition of "surplus conditions."

**Water Conservation and Transfers:**
The [[Imperial Irrigation District]] (IID), serving 500,000 irrigable acres and receiving 70% of California's Colorado River allocation, had implemented water transfer agreements with [[Metropolitan Water District]] (up to 106,000 acre-feet/year focusing on conveyance efficiency) and [[San Diego County Water Authority]] (up to 200,000 acre-feet/year focusing on on-farm efficiency). Despite conservation agreements, IID's consumptive use increased due to more double-cropping, below-average rainfall, and market effects, though use would have been higher without conservation measures.

**Legal Framework and Environmental Minute Proposal:**
The workshop explored adding an environmental minute to the 1944 water treaty to address ecological restoration. Proponents argued that: (1) international law requires avoiding material damage to other countries (Trail Smelter principle); (2) Article 3 of the treaty includes "fishing and hunting" and "other beneficial uses"; (3) Article 17 requires avoiding material damage; (4) US environmental laws (Endangered Species Act, National Environmental Policy Act, Clean Water Act) mandate federal agencies to protect listed species and their habitat, including in Mexico; and (5) the IBWC has authority to expand functions related to the treaty without formal amendment.

Obstacles identified included: the need for consensus and strong political support before the IBWC could adopt new functions; recognition that minutes are binding executive agreements with treaty force; concerns about public participation in IBWC procedures; and the established, complex process for treaty modification. Mexican CILA representatives emphasized the need to follow established processes for change and to develop integrated views incorporating agricultural, urban, and environmental considerations.

**Institutional Constraints:**
The Bureau of Reclamation stated it does not control water use or flow in Mexico and believes it is not obligated under the Endangered Species Act to formally consider or protect endangered species in Mexico. However, the Bureau expressed openness to dialogue regarding win-win approaches addressing endangered species needs in Mexico. The 1964 Supreme Court Decree was cited as enjoining the Secretary of Interior from delivering water outside the entitlement system established by the Boulder Canyon Act.

**Sanitation and Water Quality:**
San Luis Rio Colorado faced urgent sanitation crisis with 81% of the population lacking adequate service, the highest deficit in Mexico for cities over 100,000 inhabitants. The city was growing at 5% annually, complicating infrastructure planning. Fecal coliform bacteria from San Luis Rio Colorado had been detected in the Gulf of California. The city was seeking funding for adequate wastewater treatment plant. The [[Mexicali Comprehensive Sanitation Project]] had been recently certified by the Border Environment Cooperation Commission, creating opportunity for Mexico to demonstrate ecological commitment in allocating newly available water.

**Research Needs:**
Workshop participants identified critical research needs including: determining minimum water quantities needed to maintain delta ecosystems; understanding disease resistance of bacteria to increasing salinity; mapping and monitoring existing habitat; understanding current ecological dynamics; determining frequency and volume of flows needed to stimulate riparian regeneration versus maintaining existing vegetation versus stimulating marine productivity; and developing integrated binational solutions recognizing the Colorado River, delta, and upper gulf as one ecosystem.

**Consensus Themes:**
Major themes throughout the workshop included: recognition of significant habitat and rich biological diversity remaining in the delta region despite massive degradation; the critical importance of addressing ecosystem restoration within a binational context rather than stopping at the political border; the need for cooperation between countries, states, agencies, and stakeholders; and the urgency of action before further species extinctions and ecosystem collapse occur. Multiple speakers stressed that the border is only a legal artifact that should not limit ecological management approaches.
