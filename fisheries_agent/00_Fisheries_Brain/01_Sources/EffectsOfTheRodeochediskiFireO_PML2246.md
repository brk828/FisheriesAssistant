# Effects of the Rodeo-Chediski Fire on the Fish Assemblage and Water Chemistry of the Salt River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department final report, funded by the [[State Wildlife Grants]] program and dated August 2004, assesses how the 2002 [[Rodeo-Chediski Fire]] affected fish populations and water chemistry in the [[Salt River]]. The fire was the largest recorded wildfire in Arizona history, burning about 186,000 ha, with roughly two-thirds of the burn area in the Salt River watershed. The authors combined spring electrofishing surveys from 1988 to 2004 with [[Water Chemistry]] and discharge data to test whether ash- and sediment-laden runoff reduced [[Nonnative Fish]] populations, particularly [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Common Carp]]. Catch rates of these species were lowest in the first post-fire year, but the differences were largely not statistically significant. Post-fire floods produced suspended sediment, nutrient, and dissolved oxygen extremes, and the authors attribute the observed fish mortality most likely to asphyxiation.

## Background & Description

- **Fire:** The [[Rodeo-Chediski Fire]] burned during June–July 2002 and was contained on July 7, 2002. The reported burn area was about 186,000 ha (the source text reads "186,000 ha" after OCR correction; the OCR shows "is6,:00 ha," so the exact figure should be verified against the original).
- **Study area:** The Salt River is a fifth-order stream draining the White Mountains and Mogollon Rim in east-central Arizona. Surveys covered the reach from Gleason Flat (upstream of Highway 288) to Roosevelt Lake and the Horseshoe Bend reach (about 34.6 km from Roosevelt Lake) to Highway 288.
- **Historical fish community:** Before dam construction ([[Theodore Roosevelt Dam]], [[Apache Lake]], [[Canyon Lake]], [[Saguaro Lake]]), the river supported native species including [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]. Several of these have declined sharply in abundance or are federally listed.
- **Nonnative species:** Flathead catfish were stocked above Roosevelt Lake for sport fishing. Common carp, channel catfish, and red shiners likely entered from Roosevelt Lake. The dam diversion just upstream of the lake acts as a fish barrier at low flows.
- **Rationale:** The authors proposed that if the fire killed nonnative fishes, it could create an opportunity to reintroduce native species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fire effects on streams:** Literature cited in the report indicates that wildfires can reduce periphyton and macroinvertebrate densities in the short term, increase nutrient concentrations (nitrogen and phosphorus), raise dissolved organic carbon, and cause fish kills through sediment loading. Effects on stream communities are not always consistent across studies.
- **Sediment and oxygen:** Bozek and Young (1994) reported that high suspended sediment combined with low dissolved oxygen can contribute to fish mortality. Suspended sediment can also clog gills.
- **Sampling constraints:** Pre-fire data (1988–2002) were compared with post-fire data (2003–2004). Sampling was done by boat electrofishing, with limited seining, trotlines, gill nets, and trammel nets. Gear use varied among years, which limited comparisons.
- **Water chemistry:** Pre-fire dissolved oxygen ranged from 5.8 to 15.9 mg/L over 26 years of monitoring (1976–2001). Water chemistry data came from [[USGS]] gage 09498500 near Roosevelt.

## Key Findings

- **Fire scale:** The Rodeo-Chediski Fire was the largest recorded wildfire in Arizona, with about two-thirds of its burn in the Salt River basin.
- **Flow and sediment response:** Summer thunderstorms after the fire caused several small flood events. Peak suspended sediment reached 60,600 mg/L during the August 5–7, 2002 spate, a record level.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Dissolved oxygen fell to 4.15 mg/L (57% saturation) during the July 16–18 spate and to near zero (0.1 mg/L, 2% saturation) during the August 5–7 spate. Levels returned near normal (6.8 mg/L) by the September 10–13 spate.
- **Nutrients and carbon:** Phosphorus, nitrogen, and organic carbon increased to record levels during post-fire spates. Water chemistry remained elevated above pre-fire levels into 2003, with dissolved oxygen again low during the early June 2003 sampling.
- **Fish catch:** Native fish were rarely captured after 1997. Only three species, all nonnative, were captured in 2003, compared with four species in 2002 (one native).
- **Electrofishing CPUE:**
  - Flathead catfish CPUE differed among years (F = 5.18, df = 10, 168, P < 0.001). The 2003 (first post-fire year) value was significantly lower than 1997–2002 but not different from 2004 or 1994–1996.
  - Common carp CPUE differed among years (F = 3.27, df = 10, 168, P = 0.001). The 2003 and 2004 values were significantly lower only than 1997.
  - Channel catfish CPUE differed among years (F = 3.28, df = 10, 168, P = 0.001). The 2003 value was significantly lower only than 1997.
  - Catch rates did not differ significantly between reaches for any of the three species (P > 0.05).
- **Length structure:** Flathead catfish length frequencies were skewed toward smaller fish, especially in the second year after the fire. A similar pattern followed the 1993 flood. No channel catfish were captured in 2003.
- **Pre-fire flood:** The largest recorded flood (4,407 m³/s peak, January 8, 1993) produced suspended sediment of 11,700 mg/L, the highest pre-fire level.
- **Mortality:** The authors concluded that asphyxiation was the most probable cause of observed fish kills, based on high sediment loads and very low dissolved oxygen. Direct observations of dead fish supported runoff-induced mortality.
- **Recovery:** The authors expect fish populations to recover within about two to four years through recolonization from upstream and tributary refugia, though native species may be slower to recover.
- **Management implication:** The fire-induced fish kill did not clearly eliminate nonnative species, and the study recommended continued monitoring to determine whether fire effects alter nonnative control and native restoration opportunities.
