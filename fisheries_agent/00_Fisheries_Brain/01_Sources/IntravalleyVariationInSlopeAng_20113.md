# Intravalley Variation in Slope Angles Related to Microclimate and Erosional Environment

#geomorphology #valley-asymmetry #slope-erosion

## Executive Summary

This study examines whether the orientation of a valley side (north- versus south-facing) produces measurable differences in the steepest, straight slope angle of mature erosional slopes through microclimate effects. Field data from the [[Laramie Mountains]] of Wyoming, [[Southern Arizona]], and earlier work by Strahler in California are analyzed with statistical methods. The author concludes that vegetation differences and resulting slope erosion alone do not produce asymmetric valleys. Valley asymmetry arises mainly from [[Asymmetric Basal Corrasion]] by the stream, and the [[Erosional Environment]] of each slope controls its angle more strongly than microclimate does. [[Frost Action]] is rejected as the main cause of asymmetry in the study areas.

## Background & Description

The question of whether slope orientation affects erosion enough to create valley asymmetry has been debated for at least 50 years. Two earlier quantitative studies, by [[Emery Asymmetry Study|Emery (1947)]] and [[Strahler (1950)]], reached conflicting results. Three hypotheses appear in the literature:

1. North-facing slopes are steeper in drier parts of the North Temperate Zone. Three explanations are offered: [[Gilbert's Law of Divides]] (less runoff and erosion on north-facing slopes), stream shifting caused by debris from the south-facing slope, and longer snow retention on north-facing slopes where the January mean temperature is 32°F or less.
2. North-facing slopes are less steep, as suggested for tundra and fossil tundra areas, where frost action is considered the cause.
3. Slopes on both sides have equal mean angles, as Strahler's California data appeared to show.

The author argues the first explanation is most plausible for arid to subhumid regions, with the stream's basal corrasion as the key mechanism. Because mean slope differences due to microclimate are typically small compared with mean slope angle, the work relies on large sample sets and statistical design.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Laramie Mountains, Wyoming.** Three east-trending valleys near the southern part of the Raggedtop Mountain quadrangle, at 7,500–8,000 ft, were sampled. Bedrock is deeply weathered granite. The valleys have channel gradients of about 0.5°–1.5° (maximum 3.5°) and carry intermittent or ephemeral streams. Alluvial fans at tributary mouths are still growing. Mean bare-soil area was 26 per cent on north-facing slopes and 41 per cent on south-facing slopes.

**Southern Arizona.** Eight areas in the Lower Sonoran, Upper Sonoran, and Canadian life zones were sampled. The chosen valleys had steep channel gradients (6°–23°), thin bed loads, and no alluvial fans at tributary mouths. Bare-soil differences across valleys were about 25 per cent for most valleys.

**Verdugo and San Rafael Hills, California.** These V-shaped ravines with narrow, steep channels were studied by Strahler, using 105 slope measurements.

**Frost activity.** Visher's (1954) climatic data indicate much lower frost activity in southern Arizona than in southeastern Wyoming. Days continuously below freezing number fewer than 1 in Arizona versus about 45 in Wyoming, and frost nights number under 30 in Arizona versus about 180 in Wyoming. Frost penetration is under 3 inches in Arizona and over 20 inches in Wyoming. Soils in both areas are unlikely to undergo much frost heaving.

## Key Findings

- **Laramie valleys:** North-facing slopes average 4.42° steeper than south-facing slopes (two-way ANOVA column effect, F significant at p = .001). The interaction between orientation and erosional environment was not significant, so the effects were treated as additive.
- **Erosional environment matters:** Slopes away from alluvial fans (F₀) have angles similar to the average of slopes facing or above fans. Slopes facing an alluvial fan are steepened, and slope angle is linked to channel proximity and activity. Table 1 gives a grand mean slope of 16.82°, with north-facing mean 19.03° and south-facing mean 14.61°.
- **Frost action rejected:** The strong association of slope angle with erosional environment, the opposite direction of asymmetry to tundra regions, and similar asymmetry in widely separated areas with different frost activity all count against frost action as the primary cause.
- **Strahler's null result:** Strahler's sample (105 measurements) had a 99 per cent confidence interval for the orientation difference that included zero. Power analysis shows it could detect a 3.0° difference well but might miss differences of 0.5° or 1.5°. Debris-protected slopes averaged 6.6° less steep than recently corraded slopes.
- **Channel gradient controls symmetry:** Valleys with channel gradients above about 6° show symmetry even with large vegetation contrasts. Asymmetry increases gradually as gradient decreases downstream (Campo Bonito valley, Arizona), with the difference approaching zero for gradients above 6°.
- **Southern Arizona test:** Forty-seven paired slope differences had a mean of −0.0255° and a standard deviation of 1.97°. The paired t test gave t = 0.089 (df = 46), and the 95 per cent confidence interval was −0.61° to +0.55°. The true mean difference is likely no more than about 1.0°, compared with 4° or more expected from Laramie-type valleys.
- **Climate zones:** Mean differences by life zone were −0.37° (Lower Sonoran, N = 15), 0.09° (Upper Sonoran, N = 26), and 0.33° (Canadian, N = 6). The one-way ANOVA (F = 0.36, df 2, 44) was not significant. Power against a 1° departure was 0.65, so gross climate effects are judged negligible. Field observations still show north-facing slopes at least 4°–5° steeper in low-gradient Lower Sonoran valleys with asymmetric fill.
- **Unifying mechanism:** Valley asymmetry from a range of causes is attributed to a single mechanism, asymmetric basal corrasion. Steep channel gradients prevent the buildup of asymmetric valley fill that would shift the stream against one slope. Debris is carried out of steep reaches, sometimes as mudflows.
- **Profile shape:** Slope angle and the shape of downslope profiles depend on erosional environment. Isolated hills with concave profiles reflect lithologic inhomogeneity (for example, a resistant summit layer), not debris accumulation.
- **Overall conclusions (Summary):** In arid to subhumid temperate climates, low channel gradients favor valley asymmetry, steep gradients favor symmetry, most asymmetry comes from asymmetric basal corrasion, and the effects of erosional environment components on slope angle are additive. Conclusions apply to subhumid to arid northern mid-latitude regions. The humid and extremely dry ends of the climate range need further study, as do Arctic and more humid areas.
