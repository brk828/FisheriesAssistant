# Green Mountain Reservoir Studies (1949–1950)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 Colorado Department of Game and Fish report by [[Wesley C. Nelson]] summarizes fisheries, limnological and bottom-fauna studies of [[Green Mountain Reservoir]], a long, narrow, deep [[Blue River]] impoundment in Summit County, Colorado, carried out from April 1949 to December 1950. The reservoir fluctuates about 70 feet annually, and the study examined whether such "half-lake" reservoirs can support productive fisheries. Trout productivity was low (catch per man hour 0.14–0.35), with [[Rainbow Trout]] sustained by annual stocking and [[Brown Trout]] maintained mainly by natural reproduction. [[Tendipedidae]] larvae dominated the bottom fauna, while [[Daphnia pulex]] was the key zooplankter. Suckers were abundant and competed with trout for food.

## Background & Description

- **Study scope:** Creel census, fish collection, stomach and scale analysis, marked-trout tagging, limnology (temperature, transparency, oxygen, pH, plankton), and bottom sampling.
- **Reservoir setting:** Built on the Blue River at about 7,950 ft elevation, between the Gore Range and the Williams River Mountains, and operated by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] for storage and hydroelectric power. The dam is 309 ft high; maximum depth is 247 ft; surface area at full pool is 2,098 acres (152,050 acre-feet). At minimum level (7,877 ft) the area is about 853–912 acres.
- **Context:** The author notes that such reservoirs had little prior biological data and are often described as "half-lakes" ([[Ellis]], 1941). Their deep, narrow basins and bottom outlets differ strongly from natural mountain lakes.
- **Fish species recorded:** Brown trout, rainbow trout, cutthroat trout, eastern brook trout, western white sucker, plains mountain sucker, longnose dace, northern fathead minnow and sculpin. Kokanee salmon were also planted in 1951.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrography and thermal regime**
- Ordinary thermal stratification was not found; density currents from spring inflow stratified the reservoir in June and persisted through summer.
- Heat budget: 18,858 calories (March 3–September 4, 1950) and 19,727 calories (1949, Station II), which the author compares with about 22,400 calories for [[Lake Mendota]].
- Inflow peaked mid-June (7,690 acre-feet/24 h in 1949; 6,809 acre-feet in 1950). Overturn was complete by mid-November.

**Water chemistry**
- Dissolved oxygen values were uniformly low and considered unreliable, though they indicated bottom depletion at Stations I–IV.
- pH ranged 6.3–7.8 in 1949 and 7.1–8.3 in 1950, with stratification at several stations.
- Transparency ranged from 0.85 ft (May 1950) to 17.5 ft (April 1950); low values in late May reflected turbid inflow, and [[Asterionella]] bloomed in October.

**Plankton**
- Zooplankton was sparse. Standing crops (July 3–September 19, 1950) averaged 3.1 Cladocera, 5.0 Copepoda plus nauplii per liter, and 17 Rotatoria per liter.
- Daphnia pulex peaked in July–early August at most stations; the highest single value (30.4 per liter) occurred at Station V in September.

**Bottom fauna and vegetation**
- Tendipedidae larvae were the dominant bottom organism, followed by oligochaetes. Mean standing crop in 1950 was 23 larvae per square foot (11 lb/acre) over the 0–80 ft area.
- Aquatic plants were limited by the water-level fluctuation; [[Elodea]] was the main taxon, with [[Potamogeton]] and [[Chara]] also recorded.

## Key Findings

**Fisheries and yield**
- 94,896 legal-size trout (91% rainbow) and 64,586 smaller trout were planted from 1943 to 1951.
- Catch per man hour fell from 0.34 (1944) to 0.14 (1947) and rose to 0.35 by 1951.
- Rainbow trout made up 35–60% of the catch and brown trout 20–45%.
- Estimated fishing use was about 4,600 anglers in 1949 and 4,500 in 1950.
- Estimated trout yield was 1.8 lb/acre (1949) and 1.1 lb/acre (1950).
- Recommendation: annual plants of about 5,000 rainbow trout appear adequate; larger plants did not materially improve catch.

**Tag returns and growth**
- Brown trout returned 16% (three-year average); rainbow trout returned 26%.
- Rainbow returns were highest in the first two months after planting.
- Finclipped 5–6 inch trout returned only 0.4–1.6%, which the author considers an ineffective stocking size.
- Growth was about 4 inches in the first year and about 2 more in the second, comparable to other Colorado reservoirs.

**Age and population structure**
- Annulus formation was complete by mid-June, with most fish completing it between May 15 and June 15.
- Age group III and the 14.0–15.9 inch size class dominated brown trout samples.
- Brown trout reproduction in the Blue River was documented by electrofishing.

**Diet**
- Brown trout relied mainly on aquatic insects (Tendipedidae larvae), with fish eaten by larger individuals.
- Rainbow trout relied mainly on Daphnia.
- White suckers consumed Tendipedidae larvae heavily; plains mountain suckers fed almost entirely on algae, so they competed little with trout.
- Brown trout took oligochaetes heavily in shallow water at the Blue River inlet during low water.

**Abundance versus utilization**
- Tendipedidae larval abundance and utilization were both high in May (emergence), then low in June.
- Daphnia utilization by trout rose in July and stayed high, with stomachs holding up to 8,000 individuals.

**Management implications**
- The author suggests confining the trout community to a single species is biologically preferable, and warns against overloading the reservoir with competitors that could exceed carrying capacity.
- Stocking should be limited in the upper Blue River, where brown trout reproduce naturally.
