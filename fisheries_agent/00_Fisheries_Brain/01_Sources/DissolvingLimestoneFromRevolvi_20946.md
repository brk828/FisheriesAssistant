# Dissolving Limestone from Revolving Drums in Flowing Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salvelinus-fontinalis

## Executive Summary

This short paper, reprinted from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 92, No. 2, April 1962), describes a low-cost method for neutralizing acidic stream water. Raw [[Limestone]] is tumbled in a steel drum that is rotated by the stream's own flow, which prevents coating and keeps the stone dissolving. The trial was run on [[Condon Run]], a tributary of [[Otter Creek]] in the [[Monongahela National Forest]], [[West Virginia]]. The treatment raised pH enough to support [[Brook Trout]] survival, and the author estimated that the method costs far less than chemical lime.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Acid drainage from soft, sulphur- and iron-rich [[Carbonaceous Shale]] made most of the Otter Creek system unsuitable for fish. Past limestone use suffered from coating with calcium sulfate and iron hydroxides, and from slow dissolution of coarse aggregate.
- **Prior approach:** [[Gehm Up-Flow Neutralization]] (1944) pushed acid water up through fine limestone grit, with agitation used to prevent coating.
- **Study area:** Otter Creek flows about 4 cubic feet per second (c.f.s.) in its upper section and about 50 c.f.s. at its mouth. Most of the drainage is below pH 5.5. About 12 miles of main stream and tributaries were mostly unsuitable for fish. Electrofishing and rotenone sampling found no fish from Condon Run downstream to Moores Run. A native brook trout population existed above Condon Run. Bottom fauna were nearly absent in the fishless reaches.
- **Applicator drum:** Constructed of 14-gauge steel with ⅜-inch steel ends. Inside dimensions were 34 inches in diameter and 44 inches long, with an overall diameter of 50 inches including 18 waterwheel-cup fins. The drum has a rubber lining held by angle steel, and 2-inch-centre washing holes. Self-aligning bearings support it. A removable end allows lining replacement. The drum weighs under 700 pounds and cost $550.00.
- **Sluice design:** Two drums sit in series under a common sluice. At low flow, all water drives the first drum. Excess water spills over a baffle to start the second drum as flow rises. Use rate can be tuned by changing slot size, baffle, drum count, or limestone type.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stone capacity and flow:** Each drum holds 1,800 pounds of No. 3 crushed limestone (average 1.5 inches maximum dimension). A minimum flow of 0.5 c.f.s. is needed to run one drum. Agitation occurs mainly at the surface of the stone load.
- **Limestone quality:** High-grade limestone of 98.8 percent calcium carbonate gave the most efficient treatment. Lower grades also worked. Two high-grade stones were compared on the [[Los Angeles Abrasion Test]], which rated the soft stone at 33 and the hard stone at 15. The hard stone's use rate was about half that of the soft stone.
- **Blast furnace slag:** Slag performed comparably to limestone and adds other elements (magnesium, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, molybdenum, cobalt). However, slag in acid water released [[Hydrogen Sulfide]] at 0.25 p.p.m.
- **Stream response:** The water turned milky below the station but cleared within the first 400 feet. At the mouth of Condon Run, 850 feet downstream, it was only slightly more turbid than upstream. Finely ground limestone deposited visibly on the stream bed. Sieve analysis of treated water showed 99.6 percent passing 40 mesh, 99.0 percent passing 100 mesh, and 98.2 percent passing 200 mesh.
- **Recovery after treatment stopped:** After two weeks of treatment, Condon Run stayed at pH 6.0 for two days after treatment ended, while the upstream pH was 5.1. The stream did not return to former conditions abruptly.

## Key Findings

1. **Treatment effectiveness (December 1961):** One drum, using 10 pounds of high-grade soft limestone per hour, operated at 2 c.f.s. and 34° F. Total [[Water Alkalinity]] rose from 27 p.p.m. after reaction with the drum limestone to 65 p.p.m. after passing through a [[Stability Indicator]] (Enslow, 1939). This showed the treated water was not saturated with calcium carbonate.
2. **Fish response (spring 1961):** Two wild brook trout were seen in water where they had not previously been recorded. One was directly below the station, and one was 1,000 feet below Condon Run in Otter Creek. They likely moved in from the head of Otter Creek.
3. **Caged trout trial:** Four live boxes, each with five hatchery brook trout, were placed in Condon Run. Two were above the treatment site (pH 4.9) and two were in treated water (pH 7.9). After 3 days, all trout in untreated water had died, while only one trout in treated water had died. The survivors lived another 11 days with no further mortality.
4. **Cost of neutralization:** Otter Creek's estimated annual acid load is 73 tons. Neutralizing it requires 85 tons of high-calcium limestone, costing $170.00 at local prices. Chemical-grade calcium hydrate would need 59 tons at $853.00, so raw limestone gives an 80 percent saving.
5. **Annual operating cost:** Including the station installation cost amortized over 10 years, plus labour, maintenance, supervision, and limestone, treating Otter Creek would cost less than $1,200.00 per year.
6. **Design flexibility:** The station's capacity can be changed through drum or sluice design, drum number, or limestone type.
