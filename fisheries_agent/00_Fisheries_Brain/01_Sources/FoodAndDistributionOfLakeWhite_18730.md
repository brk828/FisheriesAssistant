# Food and Distribution of Lake Whitefish in Lac la Ronge, Saskatchewan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coregonus

## Executive Summary

This study examines the diet, vertical distribution, temperature preferences, oxygen tolerance, and daily activity of [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*) in [[Lac La Ronge]], Saskatchewan. Stomach contents from gill-net catches were analyzed from 1948 to 1954. Diet differed between the main lake, where [[Chironomid Larvae]] dominated, and [[Hunter Bay]], where [[Amphipods]] were the chief food. Whitefish moved into deeper water as surface waters warmed and thermal stratification developed, concentrating between roughly 15 and 35 m. Dissolved oxygen did not appear to limit movement, and catches were higher at night than by day. The author attributes the observed patterns mainly to food abundance and temperature preference.

## Background & Description

Lac la Ronge lies about 180 miles north of Prince Albert, near the Churchill River in central Saskatchewan. It consists of two water bodies joined by a narrows about 150 yards wide: the main lake and Hunter Bay. The main lake covers an area reported as 453 (the unit is garbled in the scan; square miles is implied), and Hunter Bay covers 50 square miles. Only 15% of the main lake's water volume lies below 15 m, whereas about 40% of Hunter Bay's volume lies below that depth. Maximum and mean depths are 38 m and 12.7 m for the main lake, and 42 m and 20.7 m for Hunter Bay. Depth-zone and volume data were previously published by [[Rawson And Atton]] (1953).

Whitefish were sampled mainly during summers 1948, 1949, and 1954, with supplementary collections in October 1953 and February 1954. Fish were taken with standard gill-net gangs and ranged from 8 to 18 inches in fork length. Several surveys were run by the [[Saskatchewan Department Of Natural Resources]]. The author analyzed the data through 1954 as part of a [[Master Of Science Thesis]] at the University of Saskatchewan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diet.** Stomach contents were analyzed as percentage by volume. In the main lake, [[Chironomid Larvae]] made up 39 to 64% of summer diet, followed by [[Sphaeriids]], [[Caddisfly Larvae]], and [[Mayfly Nymphs]]. Plankton contributed 15% in 1949, and small amounts of [[Amphipods]] appeared in almost every sample. In Hunter Bay, amphipods (mainly [[Pontoporeia]] and a few young [[Gammarus]]) composed 44 to 90% of the diet, with chironomid larvae and sphaeriids sharing second place. The author concluded that food selection was largely opportunistic: whitefish fed on bottom organisms roughly in proportion to their abundance.

**Feeding during spawning.** In October 1953, four of five near-spawning whitefish had empty stomachs. This suggests that feeding declines as spawning approaches.

**Vertical distribution.** In early summer, whitefish were widely scattered. As the surface warmed, they moved into deeper water. In the main lake, catches were highest between 15 and 27 m. In Hunter Bay, fish were abundant from 15 to 35 m during midsummer.

**Temperature.** Thermal stratification was usually established in the last week of June or early July. Before that, the water was nearly uniform and fish were caught at all depths. Afterward, whitefish concentrated in water of about 11 to 14 °C in the main lake and 8 to 11 °C in Hunter Bay. The author suggests the main-lake fish would have moved deeper toward colder water had it been available, and that their preferred temperature was nearer to 11 °C than to 13 or 14 °C. In [[Lake Opeongo]], [[Kennedy]] (1943) also reported a preference for water warmer than 7 °C.

**Dissolved oxygen.** [[Dissolved Oxygen]] levels did not appear to force movement, because oxygen never reached lethal levels. A minimum bottom value of 2.9 cc/L was recorded at station 3 on 10 August 1949 and was described as unusual. Hunter Bay had a consistently plentiful oxygen supply.

**Diel activity.** Combined data from 1954 and 1955 showed 267 whitefish caught in six nights compared with 34 in six days. Similar night-biased catches were reported by [[Hart]] (1931) for [[Lake Nipigon]]. The author concludes that whitefish were more active at night or avoided gill nets during the day.

## Key Findings

- **Diet varies by basin.** Chironomid larvae dominated the main lake (39–64% by volume), whereas amphipods dominated Hunter Bay (44–90%). Sample results for 1948, 1949, and 1954 are summarized in [[Table 1]].
- **Food-dependent feeding.** Diet composition suggested little selectivity, with fish feeding in proportion to prey abundance.
- **Depth shifts with season.** Fish were dispersed in early summer and concentrated deeper as stratification developed. Main-lake catches peaked at 15–27 m. Hunter Bay catches peaked at 30–35 m, with 93.0 fish per set in that interval.
- **Temperature-linked distribution.** Whitefish were concentrated at 11–14 °C in the main lake and 8–11 °C in Hunter Bay during summer. The preferred temperature was judged to be near 11 °C.
- **Oxygen not limiting.** Oxygen levels never reached lethal levels, so they did not appear to cause movement.
- **Nocturnal activity.** Catches were far higher at night (267 in six nights) than by day (34 in six days), indicating greater nocturnal activity or avoidance of nets by day.
- **Reduced feeding before spawning.** Most near-spawning fish sampled in October 1953 had empty stomachs. In February 1954, 46 of 51 commercially caught fish from Hunter Bay had empty stomachs.
- **Data discrepancy.** The abstract reports 1,606 main-lake and 607 Hunter Bay stomachs, while the main text reports 1,601 and 556 respectively. The main-text figures are reported here as given in the body, and the discrepancy is noted for the reader.
