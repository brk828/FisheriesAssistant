# Spatiotemporal Variability in the Condition of Endangered Humpback Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines length-weight relationships (a measure of body condition) of adult [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), an endangered cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The largest population occupies the lower [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and the main-stem [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], where fish were sampled during 1990–1995. Condition varied seasonally and between habitats: main-stem fish in Grand Canyon were heavier at length than LCR fish, while condition at the LCR–Colorado River confluence showed an apparent long-term decline. The authors conclude that condition alone is a weak basis for judging population health and recommend more thorough monitoring of both population size and condition, with October–November identified as a stable monitoring window.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is one of four endangered big-river fish of the Colorado River Basin, alongside the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]]. It was first described by Miller (1946) and listed as endangered in 1967. Six extant populations persist: five in the upper basin (including the Yampa, Green, Westwater, Cataract, and Black Rocks Canyon areas) and one in the lower basin at the LCR–Colorado River confluence in Grand Canyon. The Grand Canyon population is the largest.

Diet is primarily aquatic macroinvertebrates, supplemented by terrestrial insects, plant material, and occasionally small fish and reptiles. The species appears to be a generalist opportunist. Historically, floods and insect migrations (e.g., [[Mormon Crickets]]) may have provided pulses of food.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dam effects:** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] eliminated most native macroinvertebrate species in the main stem and removed high turbulent spring flows. It also maintained cold water year-round, which precluded main-stem spawning. Instream production now occurs mainly in the first 26 km below the dam.
- **Food base:** The non-native amphipod [[Gammarus Lacustris]] is an important prey item near the LCR confluence; it is absent from the LCR and the upper basin. Chironomids and simuliids dominated diets in an earlier comparison.
- **Temperature:** The LCR is warm through summer and supports spawning, while the main stem stays cold. Historically, fish experienced 16–22 °C from April to October.
- **Parasites and hybridization:** The [[Asian Fish Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) was discovered in the LCR in 1990. Hybridization with [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) contributes to morphological variation among upper-basin populations.
- **Hydrology:** Historical annual maximum flows ranged from 700 to 56,600 m³/s over the period of record.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Fish were captured with multiple gear types, including hoop nets and seines, during monthly trips from July 1989 to December 1995 in the LCR and October 1990 to November 1993 in the main stem. Total length (mm) and weight were recorded, and sex was determined by breeding tubercles.
- Analyses used analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and multivariate methods, with [[Length-Weight Relationship]] modeling as the core approach.

**Seasonal and Habitat Patterns**
- Adult fish in the LCR and in main-stem aggregations with LCR access peaked in condition in January–March, just before spawning (late March).
- Main-stem fish without LCR access peaked slightly later, around May, before spawning in the upper basin.
- Spawning-season condition declined rapidly, averaging 4–8% per month.
- Post-spawning recovery was slower in the warm LCR than in the cold main stem.
- Main-stem fish showed a consistent 10–20 g weight-at-length advantage over LCR fish.

**Population Comparisons**
- Length-weight relationships differed significantly among the six known populations (ANCOVA, F = 67.54, df = 7, 6,865, P = 0.0001; interaction F = 60.98, P = 0.0001), precluding a simple test of differences among rivers.

**Decline at the Confluence**
- Condition of adult fish at the LCR–Colorado River confluence showed an apparent long-term decline during the 1980s and 1990s, preceding a population decline from 1991 to 1995.
- Possible explanations include shifts in spawning timing, the tapeworm, observer differences, and natural variation; evidence for each was described as sparse, and the authors could not explain the trend.

**Conservation Recommendations**
- Fish condition is a subtle indicator and should be used cautiously; population size is the most direct measure of decline.
- Recommended: mark-recapture to estimate population size and residency, and monitoring in October–November, after monsoon rains and after fish recover from spawning.
- Standardized monitoring should be extended to upper- and lower-basin sites, where most data are currently collected in the post-spawning summer.
