# Productivity Relations in Seven Fishing Impoundments in Eastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 study, published in the [[Journal of the Arizona Academy of Science]], examines what controls productivity in seven small fishing impoundments in eastern Arizona. The authors (Kemmerer, Glucksman, Stewart, and McConnell) of the [[Arizona Cooperative Fishery Unit]] measured gross primary productivity from diel oxygen changes and analyzed the chemistry of inflowing tributary water, rather than impounded water. They found that inflow [[Total Alkalinity]] and [[Total Phosphate]] were closely related to [[Phytoplankton]] photosynthesis, and that [[Nitrate]] appeared to affect the forage value of that plankton for [[Zooplankton]]. Morphometry (mean depth, area, shoreline development) was not an important predictor in these small lakes. The study also reports that [[Largemouth Bass]] harvest was supported largely by littoral invertebrates associated with [[Rooted Vegetation]].

## Background & Description

The authors review earlier work linking fish production in lakes to morphometry and water chemistry, including [[Rawson 1955]], [[Hayes and Anthony 1964]], [[Ryder 1965]], [[Moyle 1956]], [[Deevy 1940]], [[Northcote and Larkin 1956]], and [[Koshinsky 1965]]. They conclude that none of these relations was consistent enough to predict production in individual lakes with confidence.

The present study differs in three ways:
- It analyzes **inflow chemistry**, because impounded water chemistry rarely represents nutrient import. Inflow was largely intermittent.
- It uses **direct measurements of gross plankton and benthic photosynthesis** as productivity parameters.
- The lakes are **more uniform** in size, origin, climate, and location.

Inflow relations were also intended to help predict productivity of planned, unbuilt impoundments.

The study was supported by the [[University of Arizona]], the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and the [[U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods overview**

- **Gross photosynthesis** was calculated from diel oxygen changes in littoral (shallow cove) and limnetic (open lake) zones. [[Benthic Photosynthesis]] was the littoral rate minus the limnetic rate.
- **Limnetic oxygen** was measured mainly in vertical water columns enclosed in polyethylene tubes (16 in. diameter). Pena Blanca was measured in unconfined water on calm days only.
- **Water samples** were collected with a custom 500 ml self-filling syringe. Oxygen was analyzed by a [[Winkler Titration]] using phenylarsene oxide (PAO) as titrant for most samples.
- **Photic depth** was defined as the depth receiving 1% of surface light.
- **Zooplankton biomass** was sampled with a Clarke-Bumpus net towed at least 200 m, then oven-dried at 60°C for 24 hours.
- **Inflow chemistry** was based on interflood flow. Flood samples were excluded because of high variability. Analyses included ortho and total phosphate (ammonium molybdate-stannous chloride method), nitrate (brucine sulfate and phenol-disulfonic acid methods), and total alkalinity (titrated with 0.02N sulfuric acid, expressed as mg/l CaCO3). Salinity (TDS) was calculated from electrical conductivity.
- **Ice cover** was a confounding factor. The dimictic lakes (Woods Canyon, Fool Hollow, Becker, Hawley, and Rose Canyon) were not sampled under ice. Ice cover ranged from 10 days at Rose Canyon to 108 days at Hawley. Measurements were therefore restricted to the ice-free period.

## Key Findings

1. **Inflow chemistry predicts plankton productivity better than morphometry.** Inflow alkalinity and total phosphate were the main correlates of plankton photosynthesis. Mean depth, surface area, and shoreline development were judged "not as important as inflow chemistry in small lakes."

2. **An empirical index was developed.** Plankton photosynthesis was proportional to [(alkalinity)(10⁻²)] + total phosphate. Adding inflow nitrate (10 × NO3) gave a combined "APN index." Both relations were derived by trial and error and lack a known theoretical basis.

3. **Alkalinity and phosphate act differently across ranges.** A roughly linear photosynthetic response to alkalinity appeared between 0 and 100 mg/l CaCO3. Above that, total phosphate became more important.

4. **Nitrate affects forage quality, not photosynthesis.** Inflow nitrate was not significantly correlated with plankton photosynthesis. However, it appeared to explain zooplankton biomass differences that photosynthesis did not. The authors suggest nitrate influences protein content of phytoplankton, citing [[Wassink 1954]] on *Chlorella*. Nitrogen loss to the hypolimnion in stratified lakes may make it limiting.

5. **Fool Hollow Lake strongly influences correlations.** Its high phosphate values (polluted) drove the significant ortho-phosphate correlation. Correlations were weaker when it was omitted.

6. **Volumetric expressions are more meaningful than areal.** Volumetric plankton photosynthesis varied about 5-fold, while zooplankton biomass varied about 28-fold. Areal correlations were poor, because the reciprocal relation between photic depth and phytoplankton density tends to equalize areal production.

7. **Trophic efficiency may increase with primary productivity.** Moderate increases at low productivity produced much larger increases in zooplankton production. This was previously suggested for herbivorous fish ([[McConnell 1963]]) and for cladocerans ([[Goldman 1966]]).

8. **Rooted vegetation supports fish food webs more than zooplankton.** Dense weed beds did not significantly raise zooplankton production, but they harbored much denser invertebrate populations. At Pena Blanca, young largemouth bass (85–195 mm) consumed littoral insects in volume about double that of zooplankton.

9. **Largemouth bass harvest was substantial despite low zooplankton.** Mean annual harvest at Pena Blanca for 1959–1961 was 14.7 g/m² (about 130 lb/acre), even with mediocre zooplankton biomass. This suggests littoral invertebrates were trophically important there.

10. **Fishery comparisons were avoided.** Most lakes are stocked with catchable and fingerling trout, and stocking intensity varies yearly. Stocking and angling pressure can confound harvest comparisons, so the authors excluded them.

11. **Practical implication.** Inflow chemistry may help predict productivity of planned impoundments and diagnose problems in existing fisheries. Volumetric productivity measures are recommended over areal ones for analysis.
