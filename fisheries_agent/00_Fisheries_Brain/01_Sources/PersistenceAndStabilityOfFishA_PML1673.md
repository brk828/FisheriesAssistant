# Persistence and Stability of Fish and Benthic Invertebrate Assemblages in a Disturbed Sonoran Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Meffe]], [[Minckley]] and colleagues (American Midland Naturalist, 1987, 117(1)) tests whether fish and benthic invertebrate assemblages in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a flash-flood-prone [[Sonoran Desert]] stream, show persistence (presence or absence of taxa) and stability (constancy of rankings or densities) despite frequent natural disturbance. Fish assemblages proved persistent and stable, and all seven native species survived the most severe flood on record. Invertebrate assemblages were persistent and rank-stable over most of the record but lost persistence during a prolonged flood period from July 1977 through 1978. The authors caution that coarse taxonomic resolution limits the invertebrate conclusions.

## Background & Description

- **Conceptual framework:** Persistence is a qualitative measure of taxa presence through time, while stability is the constancy of relative abundances or ranks under disturbance. Both are considered when assessing community pattern and process, drawing on [[Connell And Sousa]] (1979), [[Orians]] (1974), and [[Whittaker]] (1974).
- **Study site:** Aravaipa Creek is an unregulated, perennial desert stream with a canyon reach (10–100 m wide, about 17 km) and a steep upper valley. Its riparian zone includes mature gallery forests of cottonwood and willow. The stream has been studied biologically since the 1940s.
- **Fauna:** The native fish fauna comprises five minnows (Cyprinidae) and two suckers (Catostomidae), a total of seven species. Only occasional transient non-native records exist.
- **Hydrology:** Summer flash floods are driven by intense convective storms. Between 1966 and 1979 the record includes ten summer floods and seven winter floods exceeding 100 m³/s. Maximum instantaneous discharge since 1920 is reported as 566 m³/s (flood record).
- **Study design:** Samples were collected at three fish reaches (upper, canyon, lower) and invertebrate collections were made across 26 samples from January 1975 to April 1978.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Disturbance regime:** The stream is subject to frequent, rapid-onset, short-duration flash floods that are highly erosive.
- **October 1983 flood:** This was the most intense flood on record, with instantaneous discharge peaking at 1,982.2 m³/s and water levels over 15 m above modal flow in the canyon. Essentially all substrate other than large boulders was mobilized, and more than 50% of the riparian gallery forest was destroyed.
- **Invertebrate impacts:** Single floods reduced invertebrate numbers by as much as 99% (per [[Minckley]], 1981). Recolonization was rapid, with some taxa re-established within days, and taxon rankings generally returned to pre-flood configurations.
- **Comparative systems:** Similar patterns were reported for a Sonoran Desert stream (Gray and Fisher, 1981) where invertebrate densities fell by an average of 86% and recovered within about a year.

## Key Findings

**Fish assemblage (Aravaipa Creek)**
- No native species became extinct or colonized since observations began in the 1940s, so the assemblage is long-term persistent.
- Persistence indices for fishes were high, ranging from about .865 to .919 across the upper, canyon and lower reaches (Table 4).
- Kendall's W for species rankings was high and significant in all three reaches (W = .634, .705, .831; P < .001; n = 7 samples, 14 species-year rankings), indicating stable rankings.
- Long-finned dace, desert mountain sucker and Sonoran sucker were consistently the most abundant species; the other four were relatively rare.
- After the October 1983 flood, all seven species persisted in roughly the same proportions as before the flood.

**Invertebrate assemblage**
- Of the taxa tracked, only Chironomidae was present in all 26 samples; all others underwent local extirpations and colonizations.
- Overall persistence was high, but it dropped after July 1977, when a period of greater discharge and frequent flooding began and invertebrate numbers fell sharply.
- Separate analysis gave high persistence before July 1977 and markedly reduced persistence afterwards (Table 4).
- Rankings were significantly concordant over the entire sample period and within seasonal collections (Kendall's W; null hypothesis rejected), indicating stable relative ranks at the taxonomic level used.
- Absolute population sizes were unstable, fluctuating through several orders of magnitude.

**Comparisons with other studies**
- Moyle and Vondracek (1985) found similar concordance of rankings in a California stream.
- Ross et al. (1985) reported high persistence in Oklahoma and Arkansas streams, with lower stability in the harsher Oklahoma stream.
- Matthews (1986) documented fish recovery to near pre-flood condition within 8 months after a catastrophic flood.
- Grossman et al. (1982) reported non-equilibrium trophic structure in an Indiana stream; the authors calculated persistence values of .837 (spring), .947 (summer) and .937 (autumn) for those data, showing the assemblages were nonetheless highly persistent.

**Conclusions and caveats**
- Fish assemblages in Aravaipa Creek are resistant to flood-driven change in composition and rank, supporting a non-equilibrium view of community structure.
- Invertebrate conclusions are provisional because many taxa were identified only to family or genus.
- Persistence and stability are distinct properties and should be assessed together when interpreting community dynamics under natural disturbance.
