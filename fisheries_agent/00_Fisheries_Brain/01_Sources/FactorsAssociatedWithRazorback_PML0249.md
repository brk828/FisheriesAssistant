# Factors Associated with Razorback Sucker Recruitment in a Small Southern Arizona Pond

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

Razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) survived and reproduced for several years in [[Rock Tank]], a pond of about 1 ha on the [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] in southeastern Arizona. Reproduction and recruitment of this endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] species in atypical habitats are rare. The study compared Rock Tank with two nearby stocked tanks, [[Carrizo Dam Tank]] and [[Chango Tank]], where fish did not reproduce or recruit. The authors attribute Rock Tank's success to three conditions: (1) absence of competing or predatory nonnative fishes, (2) a gravel and cobble bottom suitable for spawning, and (3) enough water depth and dissolved oxygen to carry juveniles through the hot Arizona summer. The authors suggest that replicating these conditions could allow recruitment in other desert ponds.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is an endangered fish native to the [[Colorado River System]]. It has been extirpated from much of the lower basin or survives only in relict populations. Declines are attributed to habitat change and predation on fry and juveniles by [[Nonnative Fishes]]. Reproduction occurs in some reservoirs, but eggs and fry there show virtually no survival.

Hatchery-raised juveniles have been stocked in Arizona ponds to grow to a size suitable for removal and restocking. In the late 1980s, juveniles were stocked into stock tanks on the Buenos Aires refuge with the intent of later restocking historical habitat. Most recapture attempts failed, and the fish were presumed dead. In summer 1997, refuge staff found razorback suckers at the surface of Rock Tank in obvious oxygen distress. The fish had apparently survived in the tank since stocking in the late 1980s. Age structure showed four size classes rather than one, and the fish had reproduced successfully in three different years.

The study had two objectives: (1) identify factors that allowed razorback suckers to survive and recruit in Rock Tank, and (2) discuss how this information could be used to promote reproduction and recruitment in other desert ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.** All three tanks (about 0.5–1.0 ha each) lie within the refuge at 1,000–1,200 m elevation, within 25 km of the Mexican border. They are surrounded by desert savannah grassland and are remote. All were rain-fed.

**Stocking history.** Rock Tank received 30,000 10-mm TL fry in March 1986. When oxygen stress was observed in May 1997, refuge staff seined 1,175 fish for [[Carrizo Dam Tank]], 1,294 for [[Chango Tank]], and over 400 for a headquarters tank. Sampled fish ranged from 145 to 400 mm TL and represented four age classes.

**Sampling.** Carrizo Dam and Chango tanks were sampled in September 2000, June 2001, and July 2001, using trap nets, seines, and gill nets. Chango Tank was drained in June 2001 to collect fish. Stomach contents were examined from a random sample of nonnative fish. Substrate was classified with the modified [[Wentworth Classification]] along 20 equally spaced transects, each extending 10 m into the littoral zone, with measurements every 1.0 m. Water quality was measured every two weeks at midday or afternoon, with temperature and dissolved oxygen profiles and [[Secchi Depth]] readings.

**Factors considered.** The authors tested four factors that commonly limit pond recruitment: low dissolved oxygen, unsuitable temperature, predation by introduced organisms, and availability of spawning and rearing substrate. They also checked food availability.

**Biota.** No fish other than razorback suckers were found in Rock or Carrizo Dam tanks from 1986 to 2001. [[Black Bullhead]] were present in Chango Tank, and green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*) was an introduced species in the wider literature discussed. Large nonnative [[American Bullfrog]] and their tadpoles were present in all three tanks, and tiger salamanders were present in Rock and Carrizo Dam tanks.

## Key Findings

**1. Substrate was the clearest difference between tanks.**
- Rock Tank's dominant substrate was gravel and pebble (mean 2.7 ± 0.1, 95% CI; particle size 2–63 mm).
- Carrizo Dam Tank (0.3 ± 0.1) and Chango Tank (0.4 ± 0.1) were dominated by silt and clay (particle size < 0.059 mm).
- Razorback suckers typically spawn over sand, gravel, and cobble, so the lack of suitable substrate probably limited reproduction in Chango and Carrizo Dam tanks.

**2. Adult survival and recruitment were confined to Rock Tank.**
- Four year-classes were captured from Rock Tank in 1997.
- Adults were present in Chango Tank in 2001, but no evidence of reproduction or recruitment was found following their 1997 translocation there.
- No fish were captured in Carrizo Dam Tank despite repeated, intense sampling, so adults were judged not to have survived translocation there.

**3. Nonnative fishes were absent from the tanks that succeeded.**
- Rock and Carrizo Dam tanks contained no fish besides razorback suckers from 1986 to 2001.
- Black bullhead were found in Chango Tank in 2000 and 2001. Their stomachs (32 examined, June 6, 2001) held insects, snails, bullfrog tadpoles, and detritus, a diet broad enough that they could also have eaten juvenile suckers.
- In Lake Mohave backwaters, groups of four larvae were found in 40% of green sunfish stomachs, supporting the link between introduced fishes and larval mortality (Marsh and Langhorst 1988).

**4. Bullfrogs were unlikely to be the limiting factor.**
- Bullfrogs were probably present in Rock Tank during reproduction.
- Fish are only an occasional part of the bullfrog diet, and bullfrogs seldom capture fish effectively in Arizona.
- Tiger salamanders did not prevent razorback sucker juveniles from surviving in Arizona golf course ponds (Mueller and Wick 1998).

**5. Temperature was not limiting.**
- Spawning and hatching occur at 12–20°C in Lake Mohave, and 20°C was reported as optimal in laboratory work.
- Littoral temperatures in the spring spawning season were suitable in all three tanks, so temperature is unlikely to explain the lack of recruitment.

**6. Dissolved oxygen and depth separated the tanks.**
- Many desert fishes need dissolved oxygen above 1–2 ppm to survive.
- Chango Tank was turbid (from suspended clay, not phytoplankton) and shallow, but wind mixing kept oxygen high until it was drained in June 2001.
- Rock Tank's epilimnetic oxygen stayed above 5 ppm year-round. Secchi depth exceeded 1 m except in late July and August. Even so, razorback suckers were observed gasping at the surface in 1997.
- Carrizo Dam Tank was shallow, sheltered, and highly productive. Phytoplankton turbidity kept Secchi depth below 0.5 m and never above 1.0 m. Maximum depth fell from 2 m to 1 m over the year, and summer oxygen was depleted 0.5 m below the surface. Boyd's (1990) tables suggest oxygen may have dropped below 2 ppm several times. The authors concluded that drying combined with oxygen depletion likely killed adults in this tank.

**7. Food was not limiting.**
- Laboratory work suggested food might limit larval recruitment in Lake Mohave, but later pond work showed larval mortality from food shortage would occur only at very low plankton densities.
- Tanks had similar low Secchi depths, suggesting high productivity, and large aquatic insects were abundant in the littoral zone.
- Growth of razorback suckers in refuge tanks matched or exceeded growth in Lake Mohave and many other sites (Marsh 1990).

**8. Management recommendations for the refuge.**
- Razorback suckers can spawn and recruit in ponds where the right conditions occur. The key factors in Rock Tank were absence of other fishes, adequate gravel, sand, or cobble substrate, and enough oxygen and depth to last the summer.
- Nonnative fishes must be removed from other refuge tanks. Earlier toxicant treatments removed fathead minnow and mosquitofish, but black bullhead proved much more resistant (Marsh 1987). Draining, drying, and disking pond soil with slaked lime kills most competing fishes and tadpoles (Huner and Dupree 1984).
- Surface water inflows can be screened with Saran cloth to block fish eggs and larvae. If ponds are not used for production, restricting public access can reduce illegal stocking by anglers.
- Consistent water sources, nutrient management, and sufficient depth should prevent oxygen depletion and drying. Dusk and dawn oxygen measurements can predict depletion, and paddlewheel aerators or lift pumps can provide emergency oxygen.
- Digging tanks into gravel, sand, or cobble, or lining pond bottoms with these materials, may improve spawning. Settling ponds or liners may be needed to keep sediment off the substrate.
- Stocking razorback suckers into existing or newly built ponds that match these characteristics would provide further evidence of the conditions needed for pond recruitment.
