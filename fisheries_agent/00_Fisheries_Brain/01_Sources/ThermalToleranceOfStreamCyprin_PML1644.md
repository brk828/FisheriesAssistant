# Thermal Tolerance of Stream Cyprinid Minnows

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study measures the upper thermal limits of stream [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]] minnows, mainly the [[Yellowfin Shiner]] and [[Dusky Shiner]], with a small comparison group of [[Creek Chub]]. The authors used the [[Critical Thermal Maximum]] (CTM), defined as the temperature at which fish lose equilibrium, and the cessation of opercular movement (COM) as endpoints. Fish were heated at several rates in the laboratory. The main conclusions are that a slow heating rate lowers the measured lethal temperature, that [[Thermal Acclimation]] to a constant sublethal temperature raises the lethal limit, and that slow gradual warming mainly reduces mortality at low temperatures rather than raising the upper limit. The fish in this study lived in a cold, seasonally variable stream environment at the [[Savannah River Plant]] (Energy Research and Development Administration site).

## Background & Description

The paper is a short research article (pp. 141–144 of the original publication) by R. W. McFarlane, Moore and Williams, from the [[Savannah River Ecology Laboratory]] and the [[Birmingham-Southern College]], with support from the [[Energy Research and Development Administration]] and the [[National Science Foundation]] (Grant GY-11167, University of Georgia).

The study species are small, schooling minnows of the family Cyprinidae that occupy shallow streams in the southeastern United States. The authors aimed to determine how far these fish can tolerate heat, and how that tolerance changes with heating rate and with prior exposure to temperature. The work was framed as a contribution to thermal ecology and to predicting how fish populations respond to warm effluent or temperature change in streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime:** Winter stream temperatures range from about 18°C down to near 0°C. The authors use this to argue that the 34°C upper limit observed in winter is ecologically relevant for these species.
- **Acclimation:** Minnows can tolerate higher temperatures after extended exposure to a constant sublethal temperature. Their behavioural and physiological responses to temperature change are therefore strongly dependent on recent thermal history.
- **Heating rate:** Body temperature may lag behind water temperature when heating is rapid. Slow heating allows the body to keep pace, which affects the measured lethal limit.
- **Mortality at low temperatures:** Slow, steady warming appears to reduce mortality at cold temperatures, rather than raising the heat limit.
- **Comparative context:** The reference list cites related work on [[Bluegill]], the [[Duskystripe Shiner]], and [[Grass Shrimp]] (*Palaemonetes*), showing that the same type of thermal-limit and salinity-temperature testing was applied to other aquatic taxa.

## Key Findings

**1. Heating rate changes the measured lethal temperature.**
Yellowfin and dusky shiners had consistently higher CTM and COM values at the 1.0°C/hr heating rate than at 0.1°C/hr. The differences were all significant (P < 0.01). Table 1 reports the following means (± standard deviation, with ranges in parentheses) for the two slowest rates:

| Species | Rate (°C/hr) | CTM (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Yellowfin Shiner | 0.1 | 29 ± 0.7 (28–30) |
| Yellowfin Shiner | 1.0 | 30 ± 1.2 (27–32) |
| Dusky Shiner | 0.1 | 32 ± 1.0 (30–33) |
| Dusky Shiner | 1.0 | 32 ± 1.3 (30–34) |

**2. Lethal limit of about 34°C in winter conditions.**
Only 2 of 405 fish survived temperatures above 34°C, and both were exposed to the fastest heating rate. The authors conclude that 34°C is the upper lethal limit for these species under winter conditions.

**3. Acclimation raises the lethal limit.**
Yellowfin shiners collected in 13°C water had a mean CTM of 29°C and COM of 32°C when tested on the day of collection (23 fish). After acclimation at 23°C, the CTM rose to 33°C and COM to 34°C (14 fish tested, all at 6°C/hr). The authors suggest that further acclimation at higher temperatures would extend the lethal limit beyond 40°C.

**4. Slow gradual acclimation does not raise the limit.**
Eight days of slow, gradual warming (0.125°C/hr) did not extend the lethal limit beyond the typical 34°C for non-acclimated fish. The principal effect of slow steady warming was reduced mortality at low temperatures.

**6. Interpretation.**
Thermal limits in these minnows reflect heating rate, the lag of body temperature behind water temperature, and prior thermal history. Laboratory CTM values therefore need to be interpreted alongside the acclimation state and seasonal conditions of the source population.
