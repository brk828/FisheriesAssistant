# Spatial Distribution and Taxonomic Composition of the Hyporheos of Several Sonoran Desert Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hyporheos

## Executive Summary

This study by [[A. J. Boulton]], [[H. M. Valett]] and [[S. G. Fisher]] (Arizona State University) is the first description of the hyporheos of desert streams, a habitat previously thought unlikely to support an obligate subsurface fauna. Sampling of streams in central and north-western Arizona yielded at least 56 taxa, numerically dominated by crustaceans (cyclopoid and harpacticoid copepods, amphipods, bathynellaceans, isopods, ostracods). Water mites were diverse, while insects were species-poor relative to surface benthos. The authors recognise four subsurface biotopes (shallow hyporheic, phreatic, parafluvial, dry channel hyporheic) that differ in dissolved oxygen, nutrients and physical stability, and propose them as functional subunits of desert stream ecosystems. The authors suggest that the hyporheos may serve as a biomonitoring tool for groundwater pollution in arid zones.

No fish species are reported in the document; the study addresses invertebrate fauna of the subsurface aquatic environment.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** Hyporheic and groundwater invertebrate studies had been conducted for European and North American rivers (mountain, sandy-bottomed, hard-water and braided streams), but none in desert streams. The authors argue that flash floods and desiccation would seem to exclude an obligate subsurface assemblage, yet a hyporheic refuge could be important.
- **Study sites:** Most samples came from [[Sycamore Creek]], a spatially intermittent stream draining 505 km² of mountainous terrain (425–2000 m elevation) in central Arizona. Modal flow was 0.01–0.05 m³/s in summer and 0.1–0.5 m³/s in winter. Additional samples came from the Big Sandy River, Hassayampa River (upstream and downstream), Santa Maria River and Bridle Creek, sampled in March 1990.
- **Methods:** Hyporheic water was drawn from PVC wells up to 2 m deep using a hand-driven bilge pump similar to the [[Bou-Rouch Pump]]. Samples were sieved through 50 µm mesh, live-sorted and preserved in 70% ethanol. Water chemistry included [[Dissolved Oxygen]] (Winkler method), nitrate-nitrogen, ammonium-nitrogen, soluble reactive phosphorus, dissolved organic carbon and conductivity. Multivariate analyses included [[HMDS Ordination]] and [[TWINSPAN]], with Spearman rank correlations and Kruskal-Wallis tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biotopes:** Four subsurface biotopes were defined by well depth and position: shallow hyporheic (mean 28 cm), phreatic (mean 93 cm, deeper than about 50 cm), parafluvial (mean 47 cm, beyond the wetted margins) and dry channel hyporheic (mean 55 cm, downstream of receding surface water).
- **Oxygen and nutrients:** The shallow hyporheic zone was well oxygenated and low in nutrients. The phreatic zone had low oxygen with higher nutrients and high hydraulic conductivity. The parafluvial zone was hypoxic with variable nutrients, and the dry channel zone had low nitrate and phosphorus levels.
- **Drying:** When surface water disappears, a subset of the shallow hyporheic fauna persists, dominated by water mites (*Meramecia*, *Neomamersa*), ostracods, ceratopogonid larvae and nematodes.
- **Riparian links:** The parafluvial zone may interact with riparian vegetation through root uptake and detritus inputs, and its chemistry may reflect riparian plant composition.
- **Comparison with mesic streams:** Desert hyporheos are dominated by crustaceans, whereas mesic streams in Colorado, Virginia and Canada were dominated by insects, rotifers or oligochaetes and copepods.

## Key Findings

- **Richness:** At least 56 taxa were recorded, all but one (a second bathynellacean species) from Sycamore Creek. Several groups were identified only to phylum or class.
- **Crustaceans:** The amphipod *Stygobromus* nr. *arizonensis* (3–5 mm) was collected mainly from phreatic wells. A new *Hyalella* species, the first known stygobiont in the genus, was recorded. Isopods of the genus *Microcharon* (family Microparasellidae) were reported as the first aquatic isopods from Arizona. Parabathynellid bathynellaceans reached up to 65 animals per litre in parafluvial wells. Four ostracod groups and at least four harpacticoid species were found, and three cyclopoid genera (*Acanthocyclops*, *Eucyclops*, *Mesocyclops*) occurred mainly in hyporheic habitats.
- **Water mites:** Nine families were represented. *Meramecia* and *Neomamersa* reached high densities after surface water disappeared. *Wandesia* was the only mite consistently found in phreatic habitats.
- **Insects:** Insects were mostly surface-dwelling taxa in early instars. *Corethrella* and *Krenosmittia* were never found in surface samples and appear to be obligate shallow hyporheic members. A single 20 mm megalopteran larva (*Protochauliodes*) from a phreatic well was the largest subsurface animal recorded and had never been collected from the stream benthos.
- **Overlap:** Little overlap existed between hyporheic and benthic taxa collected with 50 µm mesh, apart from some insects, cyclopoids and oligochaetes.
- **Spatial patterns:** The main TWINSPAN split matched geographic differences but correlated strongly with dissolved oxygen. Variation among sites within Sycamore Creek was as great as among streams. Differences among biotopes exceeded geographic differences.
- **Water chemistry:** Species richness, total abundance and many taxon densities correlated positively with dissolved oxygen or nitrate-nitrogen. Dissolved oxygen differed significantly among biotopes (H = 29.68, P = 0.0001). Ammonium-nitrogen did not differ among biotopes.
- **Tolerances:** Field tolerances of 23 common taxa were based on about 700 samples. Most taxa were found at mean temperatures of roughly 19–22 °C, dissolved oxygen of 3–4 mg/l and conductivity of 480–500 µS/cm. Many taxa tolerated wide ranges of temperature and oxygen.
- **Flood effect:** After a spate at Sycamore Creek sites IV and V, dissolved oxygen declined and assemblages shifted toward *Microcharon* and ameirid harpacticoids.
- **Conclusions:** The authors consider the four biotopes to be functional subunits of desert stream ecosystems. They call for energy and nutrient budgets for these subsystems and suggest that long-lived phreatic taxa could be used to monitor groundwater quality in arid zones.
