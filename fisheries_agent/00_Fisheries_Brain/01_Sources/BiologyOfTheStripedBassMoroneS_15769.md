# Biology of the Striped Bass, Morone saxatilis (Walbaum), in the Lower Colorado River (Arizona-California-Nevada)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percichthyidae #introduced-species #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This Master's thesis by Gary Bruce Edwards (Arizona State University, May 1974) documents the biology of [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) introduced to the [[Colorado River]] between [[Davis Dam]] and [[Parker Dam]] from 1962–1969. A total of 93,000 fingerlings and yearlings were stocked during this period. The study, conducted primarily 1966–1972, examined reproduction, food habits, growth rates, and angling success of this newly established population. Key findings include evidence of successful natural reproduction beginning around 1970, accelerated growth rates (20% greater length by age-6 compared to other populations), dominance of [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) in diet, and early sexual maturity (many females spawning at age-3). The introduction appears successful, with striped bass becoming the major trophy fish in this river reach.

## Background & Description

The [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) is a member of the temperate bass family [[Percichthyidae]] (order Perciformes). Native to the Atlantic coast of North America from the St. Lawrence River to northeastern Florida and Gulf of Mexico tributaries, the species exhibits anadromous behavior, spawning in fresh water while typically residing in coastal marine environments. The species is highly euryhaline but requires extensive freshwater spawning grounds where eggs drift with current until hatching.

Historical introductions to the Pacific coast occurred in 1879 and 1881 when fish from New Jersey were planted in the [[Sacramento River]], California, subsequently dispersing from San Diego to the Columbia River. Landlocked populations were successfully established in [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]] (South Carolina) and [[Kerr Reservoir]] (North Carolina-Virginia) in 1954, generating widespread interest in reservoir introductions throughout the United States.

The [[Colorado River]] study area extends 81.0 km from Davis Dam (completed 1953) downstream to the headwaters of [[Lake Havasu]] (impounded by Parker Dam, completed 1938). The river flows through alternating alluvial valleys and mountain canyons in the Lower Sonoran Life Zone, characterized by hot, dry climate (annual precipitation 7.5–12.6 cm, maximum air temperatures 46–52°C). Davis Dam regulates stream flow on daily, weekly, and annual cycles with maximum flow approximately 64,516 m³/min and minimum daily flow 2,547 m³/min.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lower [[Colorado River]] basin lies in the Lower Sonoran Life Zone with sparse vegetation on low mountains (palo verde, ironwood, smoke tree in washes; mesquite, cottonwoods, willows, saltcedar, arrowweed near water; cattail, bulrush in shallow waters). Water temperatures average 18°C annually with minimum 9°C in January, supporting year-round fish growth.

The river's regulated flow regime, warm water temperatures, and abundant forage base (particularly [[Threadfin Shad]] drawn through Davis Dam penstocks) created favorable conditions for the introduced striped bass population. The study area represents a novel landlocked environment for a species typically exhibiting anadromous life history, requiring adaptation to entirely freshwater conditions without access to marine habitats.

Native and introduced fish fauna in the study reach included [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) maintained by stocking below Davis Dam, centrarchids including [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[European Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), and the abundant [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) which became the primary forage base.

## Key Findings

**Stocking History**: Between August 1962 and August 1964, cooperative efforts by Arizona and California Fish and Game Departments introduced 82,935 fingerlings (average fork length 51 mm) and 896 yearlings (average fork length 158 mm) collected from Tracey Fish Screens, California. An additional 9,175 fish (9,000 at 102 mm; 175 at 178 mm) from Minck's Corner State Fish Hatchery (South Carolina) were stocked in October 1969, bringing total introductions to 93,000 individuals.

**Reproduction**: Evidence of limited reproduction occurred 1966–1969, but the first spawn producing a "dominant year class" occurred in 1970. Spawning takes place from Davis Dam approximately 16 km downstream during April through mid-June, with peak activity when water temperatures reach 15.6–18.4°C. Sexual maturity occurs early: 50% of age-class-III females (3 years) had mature, greenish ovaries with type-3 ova (mean diameter 1.04 mm) and were classified as ready to spawn; almost all age-class-IV females (4 years) were mature. This contrasts sharply with other populations where only 18.2–67.9% of age-IV females mature.

Fecundity was high: mature ova ranged from 149,142 to 308,086 per kg body weight (mean 231,643 per kg), considerably greater than other areas (Oregon: 220,000/kg; North Carolina: 176,000/kg). The ovary-weight to body-weight ratio was 1:7.6, compared to 1:12 in Chesapeake Bay. Ova development followed three types as described by Lewis (1962): type-1 (translucent, 0.03–0.23 mm, no yolk); type-2 (translucent, 0.16–0.30 mm, yolk forming); type-3 (opaque, 0.33–1.35 mm, mature). Pre-spawn females (March-April) had cream-colored ovaries with type-3 ova averaging 0.77 mm diameter. Ready-to-spawn females (May–mid-June) had green ovaries with type-3 ova up to 1.35 mm (mean 1.02 mm).

Sex ratio at spawning grounds was approximately 1:1 (53% females among 84% of bass sampled in the Davis Dam to 16 km reach), unusual compared to other populations where males greatly outnumber females (ratios of 10–50 males per female reported elsewhere). Post-spawn females (after June 15) had flaccid ovaries with thickened walls and residual unspawned type-3 ova.

**Food Habits**: Analysis of 100 stomachs (February–November 1969) revealed striped bass as total carnivores with generalized prey selection based on availability. Forty-five percent of stomachs were empty. Comparison by collection method showed 71.4% of spear-gun-collected bass had full stomachs versus 50.6% of angled fish, suggesting anglers catch fish beginning to feed. Regurgitation upon capture was rare (only 1 of 175+ observed angled fish).

[[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) dominated the diet, occurring in 37 of 55 stomachs with food (67.3% frequency), comprising 1,439 of 1,476 total identifiable food items (97.5% by number), and 2.46 kg of 4.98 kg total reconstructed food weight (49.4% by volume). Maximum shad count in a single stomach was 257 (from a 7.6-kg female). Shad abundance was attributed to individuals drawn through Davis Dam penstocks and expelled into the tailrace in moribund or dead condition.

[[Rainbow Trout]] ranked second in importance: 21 trout in 15 stomachs (27.3% frequency), comprising 1.37 kg (27.6% of total volume). Four of 21 trout were identified by tags from recent stockings. A focused study following trout stocking found 18 of 24 trout in 20 bass stomachs were tagged, suggesting high but temporally-limited predation on newly-stocked trout during their adjustment period. Despite this, overall trout predation was not considered significant enough to adversely affect the catchable trout fishery maintained below Davis Dam.

Other prey included [[Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarki*): 10 individuals in 4 stomachs (7.3% frequency), 0.32 kg (6.5% volume); centrarchids: 4 fish in 2 stomachs (3.6% frequency), 0.64 kg (12.9% volume, inflated by one 0.58-kg [[Largemouth Bass]] from a 10.8-kg female striped bass); one [[European Carp]] (1.8% frequency, 0.06 kg); two [[Tiger Salamanders]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) attributed to bait use; and 6 unidentified items.

Feeding trends indicated by time of capture (for angled fish): 57.5% caught 1800–2400 hours (evening peak), only 8.0% caught 1200–1800 hours (midday low), suggesting crepuscular feeding behavior with peak activity after dark and before dawn.

**Growth and Age**: Scale analysis of 200 specimens (1966–1972) provided age and growth data. Scales were mounted on cellulose acetate slides and examined at 15X magnification, circumventing problems of scale thickening in large/old fish. Body length-scale length relationship was linear (Y = -5.05 + 0.24X, N=85). Scale variability within individuals was low; anterior field measurements were used for consistency.

Growth rates were exceptional, exceeding all other reported striped bass populations. Introduced fish (year-classes 1961–1965 from California stock) grew mean 152 mm in first year. River-spawned individuals (year-classes 1966, 1968, 1970, 1971) grew mean 207 mm in first year, a 36% increase. The 1969 hatchery-reared introduction from South Carolina broodstock grew 237 mm in first year, exceeding river-spawned fish, possibly due to hatchery diet or freshwater-origin broodstock genetics versus saline-adjusted California breeders.

Calculated mean fork lengths at each annulus for the 1963 year-class (sexes combined): Age-I: 177 mm; Age-II: 483 mm; Age-III: 710 mm; Age-IV: 751 mm; Age-V: 788 mm; Age-VI: 832 mm. By age-6, Colorado River striped bass reached 832 mm fork length, 17% longer than any other region and 20% greater than combined mean of multiple other populations. This growth equaled that of 9–11 year-old bass from other areas.

Males and females grew at similar rates through age-3. Females grew faster beginning in year-4, maintaining consistently greater size. For the 1963 year-class at age-6: females averaged 78 mm longer than males. Greatest annual growth increment occurred in year-2 for both sexes: males 271 mm, females 262 mm. Annual increments decreased thereafter.

The largest specimen collected was approximately 1.02 m fork length at age-7. The largest verified catch (summer 1972) was a 1.2-m female weighing 21.3 kg; if from the 1962 introduction, this fish was only 11 years old maximum, compared to estimates of 20–25 years for 1.3-m fish in native Atlantic populations. Year-round growth was attributed to warm water temperatures (minimum 9°C) versus seasonal growth cessation in other regions (growth limited to 7 months in California, ceasing in winter elsewhere).

Accelerated growth was attributed to: (1) abundant, underutilized food supply (particularly threadfin shad); (2) year-round growing conditions due to warm water; (3) low population density reducing intraspecific competition; (4) high fertility and early maturation characteristic of newly-established populations.

**Angling Success**: Beginning 1966, anglers increasingly targeted striped bass. Data from the Annual Bullhead City Striped Bass Derby (1968–1972) indicated growing fishing effort and success, though data were limited to the contest period (late May–early September) and primarily reflected the Davis Dam to Needles reach.

Derby entries increased annually: 1968: 18 fish (mean 689 mm, 5.89 kg); 1969: 50 fish (mean 665 mm, 6.40 kg); 1970: 78 fish (mean 598 mm, 4.55 kg); 1971: 225 fish (mean 441 mm, 2.58 kg); 1972: 148 fish (mean 509 mm, 3.70 kg). The 1971 decrease in mean size (225 mm reduction) resulted from 66% of catch being age-II fish from the successful 1969 spawn and/or 1969 introduction. The 1972 catch included 29% age-II fish from the successful 1970 spawn.

Fishing methods evolved from primarily artificial lures requiring boats and heavy tackle (limiting participation) to cut anchovies for bait fishing (1972), dramatically increasing accessibility and success for average anglers including retired local residents. One observed angler caught and released 15 bass (mostly age-II) in 1 hour 19 minutes in Davis Dam tailrace, though this was not typical. Many successful anglers caught their legal limit (three bass, minimum 16 inches) in fewer than six hours.

Economic importance increased with three annual striped bass derbies established, offering prizes up to $1,000 for largest fish. The Arizona state record was broken annually beginning 1968. Striped bass became the major trophy fish sought in the Davis-to-Parker Dam reach, with increasing numbers of anglers traveling specifically to target this species.

**Population Status**: At the time of study completion (1974), the introduction appeared successful. However, three critical questions remained unanswered: (1) How long can the threadfin shad population support an increasing striped bass population? (2) To what extent will natural reproduction maintain the population without additional stocking? (3) What is the carrying capacity before overpopulation affects food supply and population health?

The study noted that as a newly-established population, the striped bass were likely experiencing a surge in growth rate, fertility, and other biological parameters due to low density and abundant resources. Comparison with Santee-Cooper Reservoir (where the population expanded rapidly until ~1960 then declined) suggested monitoring would be essential to detect population changes and potential density-dependent effects.

Limited data were available on young-of-the-year food habits, population dynamics in downstream reaches and Lake Havasu, and long-term reproductive success. The author recommended continued study of reproduction, juvenile ecology, and threadfin shad availability to assess long-term sustainability of the introduction.
