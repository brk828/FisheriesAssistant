# Effect of Sorting Brown Trout on Sex Ratio and Growth

#ichthyology #salmonidae #brown-trout #hatchery-management

## Executive Summary

This study examined the effects of size-sorting on sex ratios and growth rates in [[Brown Trout]] (Salmo trutta) fingerlings at the [[Randolph Hatchery]] in New York. Researchers individually tagged and tracked four sorted size groups over one year (September 1956 to September 1957). Key findings indicate that unsorted samples maintained an approximately 50:50 sex ratio, growth rates were nearly identical between males and females and among sorted groups, and initial size had little predictive value for subsequent growth. The study was significantly impacted by tag loss due to jaw erosion, reducing final sample sizes to approximately half the original numbers.

## Background & Description

The study followed previous work by Haskell and Griffiths (1956) on [[Brook Trout]] sex-based growth comparisons. Brown trout fingerlings were collected from [[Randolph Hatchery]] on September 20, 1956, and separated into four size groups using bar sorters. Each fish received an individual jaw tag, and weight and length measurements were recorded. The four groups were then combined and held under hatchery conditions until September 25, 1957.

**Sample Composition (Table 1):**
- Group I (largest): 34 fish (17 male, 17 female)
- Group II: 34 fish (17 male, 17 female)
- Group III: 25 fish (11 male, 14 female)
- Group IV: 31 fish (12 male, 19 female)
- Total: 124 fish (57 male, 67 female)

Individual weights and lengths were measured at intervals throughout the experiment. Sex determination occurred either at time of death or at experiment conclusion. Random selections from survivors ensured proportional sex representation matching original groups.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was conducted entirely under [[Hatchery]] conditions at the New York State Conservation Department facility. This controlled environment allowed for standardized rearing conditions across all groups, eliminating environmental variables that might affect growth comparisons. The research was supported by Federal Aid in Fish and Wildlife Restoration Project F-11-R.

The experimental design was significantly compromised by tag retention issues. Jaw tags eroded through bone tissue, causing substantial tag loss and reducing the dataset to approximately 50% of original numbers. This technical limitation affected the statistical power of conclusions but did not invalidate the observed patterns.

## Key Findings

**Sex Ratio:**
- Unsorted samples of brown trout fingerlings exhibited an approximately 50:50 sex ratio

**Growth Measured by Length:**
- Mean length of four sorted groups remained nearly parallel throughout the experiment (Figure 1)
- Mean length differences between sexes were consistently small (Figure 2)
- This contrasted with [[Brook Trout]], where males started slightly larger and gradually increased their lead

**Growth Measured by Weight:**
- Sorted groups showed diverging weights, suggesting larger fish grew more rapidly by this measure (Figure 3)
- Males were slightly heavier initially and gradually increased their lead (Figure 4)

**Growth Measured by Cube Root of Mean Weight:**
- Results closely paralleled length data (Figures 5 and 6)
- Larger fish did not demonstrate faster growth rates than smaller fish
- Growth rates were nearly equal across size classes

**Individual Fish Growth Patterns:**
- Individual length increases showed no correlation with initial length (Figure 7)
- Largest fish at a given time did not grow most rapidly subsequently
- Individual growth rates appeared entirely random, following laws of chance
- Uniform growth rates only emerged when fish were considered as groups

**Hatchery Management Implications:**
- Sorting effects on size uniformity were only temporary
- Selection of largest fish by length or weight had little value in predicting subsequent growth rate
- "Runts" selected for future rearing would grow at the same average rate as the entire lot when at comparable size
- Hatcheryman gains and production should not suffer from selecting smaller individuals

**Comparison with [[Brook Trout]]:**
- Brown trout showed more similar growth between sexes compared to brook trout
- Brook trout males showed progressive size advantage; brown trout males showed minimal length advantage
- Both species demonstrated that individual growth rate is largely random and unpredictable from initial size
