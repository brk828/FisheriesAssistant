# Hydrology of the Bill Williams River Basin: Modeling Study of the Riparian System

#hydrology #riparian-ecology #groundwater-modeling

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) brief, authored by hydrologist Celina Harshman of the USFWS Division of Water Management, summarizes a groundwater modeling study of the Bill Williams River basin. The study was run with the University of Arizona to examine how the riparian ecosystem of the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]] depends on ground water and surface water. The model shows that Refuge base flows depend on water stored in the [[Planet Ranch]] alluvial aquifer, and that agricultural pumping there reduces those flows. Stakeholders concluded that 18 cfs base flow is needed to sustain riparian resources. The USFWS proposes a five-year plan with [[City Of Scottsdale]] to cut pumping at 7 of the 13 production wells, a reduction of 8,346 ac-ft per year.

## Background & Description

The Refuge, near Parker, Arizona, contains the last extensive native riparian habitat along the lower [[Colorado River]]. Its primary resource is a dense [[Cottonwood-Willow Riparian Forest]] that supports neotropical migratory birds. Before river channelization and the spread of [[Salt Cedar]] (*Tamarix*), cottonwood-willow forests dominated riparian landscapes along the Colorado River. The document frames preserving this forest as critical to the Refuge and to surrounding regional ecosystems.

The study was initiated by the USFWS, performed with the University of Arizona, and used the following modeling framework:

- **Model:** A modular ground water flow model, MODXX (Jones, 1993), was applied. It is transparent to [[MODFLOW]] (McDonald and Harbaugh, 1988) but uses a finite element solution technique, allowing grids to conform to river contours. MODFLOW is limited to an orthogonal grid.
- **Study reach:** Alamo Dam to Lake Havasu, 40 miles long, with the lower 12 miles inside the Refuge.
- **Boundary conditions:** A prescribed flux from Alamo Reservoir releases at the upstream end. The Colorado River at the terminus may act as a constant head boundary. The alluvial system is bounded by mountains and assumed to rest on impermeable crystalline bedrock.
- **Stream package:** The [[Streamflow Routing Package]] (Prudic, 1989) lets streams go dry and resurface, which matters because surface flow is suspended over a six-mile reach at Planet Ranch. River stage is calculated from channel geometry and streambed hydraulic conductivity, using the [[Manning Equation]].
- **Mountain front recharge:** Simulated for the Castaneda, Centennial, and Mineral Washes. It was estimated in the field (Rivers West, 1990) and with the [[Hearne and Dewey Regression Model]] (1988), which applies to elongate southwest alluvial basins receiving less than 7.4 inches of winter precipitation.
- **Evapotranspiration:** Estimated from temperature, humidity, acreage, and vegetation type, using surveys above and below Planet Ranch and consumptive use rates from the Bureau of Reclamation's [[Vegetation Management Study]] (1990).
- **Pumpage:** Irrigation at Planet Ranch is the largest human consumptive use on the river. Its operations use thirteen main production wells and alfalfa cultivation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Refuge's riparian ecosystem is closely tied to ground water and surface water. Flow seeps into the aquifer at the east end of the basin and is stored in sand and gravel sediments 200 to 300 feet deep. Under historic, unaltered conditions, ground water emerged at the downstream end of Planet Ranch, where the gently sloping water table met the steeper river bed. The water table flattens there because aquifer materials are truncated by bedrock.

Evapotranspiration is a major water loss in riparian basins. The surveys found:

- **Above Planet Ranch:** 1,499 acres of riparian vegetation.
- **Below Planet Ranch:** 1,841 acres, with a much higher density of exotic plants.

Three evapotranspiration zones were simulated:

1. East of Planet Ranch to Alamo Dam: 5.5 ft/yr.
2. Five miles downstream of Planet Ranch: 6 ft/yr.
3. Final four miles of the Refuge: 9 ft/yr.

The document notes that the higher rate in the last zone resulted from calibration and reflects denser exotics. Reported rates from the Vegetation Management Study were 8 ft/yr for *Tamarix* and 9 ft/yr for Russian olive.

Agricultural pumping at Planet Ranch is the dominant human influence. The reported total of 15,500 ac-ft was split equally among thirteen wells. Because alfalfa evapotranspiration in the Parker valley would consume all pumped water, and sprinkler irrigation loses more water to evaporation than ditch irrigation, the author judged return flow to the aquifer unlikely.

## Key Findings

**Model reliability**

The model was validated through sensitivity analysis of aquifer transmissivity, specific yield, riverbed conductance, and evapotranspiration. It successfully simulated four system features:

1. Suspension of surface flow over the six-mile Planet Ranch reach, modeled consistently across a variety of conditions.
2. The buffering capacity of the Planet Ranch basin under non-extreme conditions. Severe storms of significant duration produce Refuge flows that correlate directly with reservoir releases.
3. The empirical reduction of Refuge baseflows that coincides with agricultural development.
4. Seasonal variation in Refuge flows, controlled by a pumping schedule that is lightest in winter and heaviest in summer.

Extreme event data from spring 1994 were also simulated successfully.

**Model results**

- Refuge base flows directly depend on stored water in the Planet Ranch alluvial aquifer, which buffers upstream flows and supplies consistent base flow across a wide range of system inflows.
- Agricultural pumping directly impedes this supply. A transient analysis of cumulative pumping effects, supported by empirical data, confirms significant impacts on Refuge base flows.
- The Refuge historically received 18 to 20 cfs of base flow. Pumping withdraws an annual average of close to 20 cfs. The basin cannot recover historic flows from reservoir releases, aquifer storage, or evapotranspiration.
- Four transient scenarios were run, with 13, 10, 6, and 0 wells operating (Figures 6 to 9). Any pumping reduces Refuge base flow, and reducing pumping produces a direct net gain in base flow.

**Recommendations and proposal**

- Refuge scientists (hydrologists, ecologists, and biologists, including the Rivers West Riparian Subcommittee) concluded that 18 cfs at the eastern Refuge boundary is necessary to sustain riparian resources. Only terminating Planet Ranch operations would guarantee this flow.
- Because ending operations may be unacceptable to the City of Scottsdale, the USFWS proposes a five-year agreement that protects riparian habitat and Scottsdale's water rights. Scottsdale's right to the Planet Ranch allotment depends on using the full allotment at least once every five years.
- The USFWS requests a reduction of 7 of the 13 production wells. This equals 8,346 ac-ft per year, or 54% of current pumping. The reduction would temporarily provide minimal Refuge flows and could also support all 2,400 acres of current alfalfa, or prevailing irrigation on about half of those fields.
- The USFWS anticipates that a federal agency may acquire Planet Ranch within about five years.
- **Key risk:** Drought during the five-year period would prevent adequate base flow even with a pumping reduction. Renegotiation of the pumping schedule may then be necessary.

**Data gaps noted by the author**

- The mountain front recharge estimate of 1,576,800 ft/yr is a low, year-round approximation. It does not reflect seasonality and lacks extreme-event estimates needed for future simulations.
- Streambed conductivity was initially set at 1/20 of aquifer conductivity, then calibrated, and was highest near Planet Ranch.
- Manning's roughness coefficient was set at 0.02 for a clean, natural channel.
