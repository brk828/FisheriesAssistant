# Net Loss: Fish, Jobs, and the Marine Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #marine-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This Worldwatch Paper (No. 120, July 1994, by [[Peter Weber]]) argues that world marine fisheries have reached or passed their biological and economic limits. Global marine catch peaked in 1989 (about 82 million tons) and has since stagnated. Overfishing is identified as the primary cause of declining fish populations, with pollution and habitat destruction as secondary pressures. The paper links this decline to excess fishing capacity, sustained by government subsidies (about $54 billion per year) and open-access management. It warns that millions of fishers' jobs are at risk, concentrated among 14 to 21 million small-scale fishers, and that the outcome depends on whether management shifts toward community-based control within a supportive government framework.

## Background & Description

The document is a policy-oriented research report rather than a species study. It addresses the "fishery" as a system of people, equipment, species and regions. Its organization covers:

- **Introduction:** Gunboat-style disputes (the April 1994 seizure of the Portuguese-crewed trawler [[Kristina Logos]] outside Canada's zone), job losses, and the economic importance of fishing.
- **Limits of the Sea:** Historical fishing, food-chain productivity, catch trends and collapse of the [[Peruvian Anchovy]] fishery.
- **Lost Bounty:** Causes of decline, bycatch, habitat loss, pollution and toxins.
- **Who Will Go Fishing?:** Fishing capacity, subsidies, open access, and consolidation policies such as [[Individual Transferable Quotas]].
- **Fruits of the Sea: Food and Fairness:** Distribution of fish between rich and poor consumers, export flows and aquaculture.
- **Fencing the Oceans:** Jurisdiction, the [[Exclusive Economic Zone]] system, high seas conflicts and bluefin tuna.
- **Fishing Within the Limits:** Management options, community-based systems and policy recommendations.

Source data comes largely from the [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] (FAO) fisheries database. Numerous tables (regional catch change, species declines, top ten species, fisher scale comparison, EEZs, international conflicts) support the argument.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Productivity and food chain.** More than 90 percent of the catch comes from the 10 percent of oceans closest to land. Coastal ecosystems (estuaries, wetlands, reefs) are highly productive. Primary production is estimated at about 190 billion tons per year, and biomass falls roughly tenfold per trophic level, so harvested species yield only about 1/1,000 to 1/10,000 of primary production.

**Sustainable yield estimates.** Estimates have declined over time: 80 million to 2 billion tons (1968 conference, with 200 million as an upper realistic limit), about 100 million tons (FAO 1971 and 1984). Scientists use 100 million tons per year as a rough potential for commercially viable marine species, against an actual 1993 marine catch about 20 million tons lower.

**Overfishing types.** The paper distinguishes growth, recruitment, ecosystem and serial overfishing. Slow-growing, low-fertility species are especially vulnerable. [[Orange Roughy]] takes 32 years to reach sexual maturity, and one-third of Tasmanian stocks are reported depleted.

**Habitat and pollution.** Development has destroyed an estimated 50 percent of coastal wetlands and half of mangrove forests; 5 to 10 percent of coral reefs have been destroyed. Dams cut nutrient flow, as with the [[Aswan High Dam]], after which Nile Delta phytoplankton fell 90 percent. Organochlorines such as dioxin bioaccumulate in fish, and [[Non-Native Species]] such as the ctenophore *Mnemiopsis leidyi* in the Black Sea have devastated fisheries.

**Wildlife bycatch.** Driftnetters ensnared about 42 million seabirds, marine mammals and other non-target animals before the ban. Purse-seine fishers killed more than 400,000 dolphins per year at the 1970s peak. The [[Steller Sea Lion]] population fell from 300,000 (1960) to 66,000 (1990).

**Climate.** Greenhouse-driven changes could alter ocean currents and displace fisheries. The paper treats this as a future risk, while overfishing is the present dominant threat.

## Key Findings

- **Stagnating catch:** World marine catch peaked at about 82 million tons in 1989 and has stagnated since. In 1991 the marine catch was 85% fish, 5% crustaceans and 10% mollusks. The freshwater catch was only 6.4 million tons in 1989.
- **Regional decline:** Of 15 major marine regions, catch fell in all but 2; in four (northwest, west-central and southeast Atlantic, and east-central Pacific) it fell more than 30 percent. Combined decline from peak years is 13 million tons.
- **Species declines:** Eighteen fisheries dropped by more than 100,000 tons each, a combined fall of about 29.7 million tons (58%) from peak years. The Peruvian anchovy fell from 13.1 million tons (1970) to under 2 million tons (1974).
- **Overcapacity:** The world fleet doubled between 1970 and 1990 (585,000 to 1.2 million large boats; 13.5 to 25.5 million gross registered tons). Fishers possess about twice the capacity needed. Norway estimates 60% overcapacity; the EU about 40%. The US East Coast surf clam fishery had 10 times the boats needed.
- **Jobs:** More than 100,000 fishers have lost income in recent years. Cutting the small-scale sector by half would eliminate 7 to 10 million jobs; continuing the industrialization trend could cost 14 to 20 million.
- **Subsidies:** Governments provide about $54 billion annually in subsidies to catch about $70 billion worth of fish. The EU subsidizes its fleets by over $500 million a year; US diesel tax exemption is roughly $250 million a year.
- **Potential gain:** Rebuilding depleted stocks could raise the catch by about 20 million tons (about 25% of the current catch), worth roughly $16 billion at today's prices.
- **Food security and equity:** Fish supplies 16% of animal protein worldwide. Average per-capita consumption in industrial countries is nearly three times that of developing countries. About one-third of the marine catch goes to animal feed and fish meal. Jack mackerel domestic consumption in Chile fell by half as output shifted to export feed.
- **Aquaculture:** Farmed fish is now the fastest-growing portion of supply, but it has contributed minimally to coastal poor. Marine shrimp farming drives mangrove loss and conflict. Freshwater integrated systems (e.g. Ghana, Mekong Delta, Bangladesh duckweed) are presented as more promising.
- **Management models:** Community-based systems such as Japan's Fishing Cooperative Associations, Maine's lobster territories, Indonesia's *sasi* and the Philippine 25-year community contracts succeed when fishers hold a strong sense of ownership. Consolidation programs (for example Taiwan, Malaysia, Canada's Nova Scotia quota) can concentrate benefits and cut crew employment.
- **Jurisdiction:** The 200-nautical-mile EEZ system transferred most prime fishing grounds to coastal states. Of 122 claimed EEZs, the top 15 hold 59% of the total area. High seas fishing remains weakly governed, and flags of convenience undermine regional agreements. Up to 80% of Atlantic bluefin catch may be taken by non-ICCAT vessels, reportedly around 500 flagged vessels.
- **Policy recommendations:** Eliminate subsidies, charge royalties for fishing rights, cap capacity, protect small-scale access, expand management toward full utilization and reduced bycatch, and extend shared governance over the high seas. The author concludes that without combined government and community management, marine fisheries will continue to decline.
