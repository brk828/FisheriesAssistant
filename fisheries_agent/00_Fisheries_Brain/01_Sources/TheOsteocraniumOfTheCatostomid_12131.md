# The Osteocranium of the Catostomid Fish, *Catostomus macrocheilus*: A Study in Adaptation and Natural Relationship

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study by [[George F. Weisel]] (Zoology Department, University of Montana) provides the first complete description of the skull (osteocranium) of a sucker, *Catostomus macrocheilus*. The author describes the neurocranium, branchiocranium, oromandibular region, pectoral girdle and jaw musculature, and interprets these structures as adaptations for suctorial, bottom-dwelling feeding. The skull is compared with that of *Carpiodes carpio* (another catostomid) and the [[Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus oregonense*, a cyprinid). The paper also evaluates [[Sagemehl]]'s 1891 criteria for regarding catostomids as primitive among the eventognathous fishes and concludes that they are not more primitive or ancestral to the cyprinids.

## Background & Description

Suckers are among the most common North American freshwater fishes, yet their osteology had received little focused study. Earlier work included Sagemehl (1891) on cyprinoid crania, [[Edwards]] (1926) on the protractile mouth apparatus, Gregory (1933) on fish skulls, and comparative studies of ethmoid bones, otoliths, Weberian ossicles and opercular series by Starks, Adams, Krumholz and Nelson. The author states that no complete study of the sucker skull had previously been made.

**Materials and methods:**
- Articulated and disarticulated skulls of mature *C. macrocheilus* and *C. catostomus*; *C. macrocheilus* serves as the basic type for description.
- Comparative skeletal material of *Carpiodes carpio* (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology specimens) and *Ptychocheilus oregonense*.
- X-rays of adults for orientation of larger bones; alizarin transparencies of a few juveniles; cleared skeletons of adults as the main basis.
- Fresh and formalin-preserved specimens for jaw musculature.
- Histology of lips, pharyngeal pad and cephalic lateral line, stained with hematoxylin-eosin and Heidenhain's Mallory-azan (the latter gave the best differentiation).
- Nomenclature follows [[Harrington]] (1955), based on the cyprinid *Notropis bifrenatus*, with muscle homology from Allis (1897), Takahasi (1925) and Edwards (1926).

**Key anatomical description (summary):**
- **Nasal region:** Single supraethmoid and ethmoid; paired pre-ethmoids and fused prefrontal-lateral ethmoids; a single prevomer with a ventral flexure at the rostral end. Ethmoid bones are cellular and spongy.
- **Orbital region:** Thick orbitosphenoid and flat pterosphenoids; no adult supraorbitals (present only in juveniles of 35–40 mm total length); the lacrymal and two small suborbitals.
- **Lateral line:** Sensory canals largely lie in the integument rather than within the bones, unlike cyprinids.
- **Otic and occipital regions:** Autosphenotic, supratemporal-autopterotic, prootic, epiotic and a large lateral post-temporal fossa; single supraoccipital, paired exoccipitals, and a single basioccipital with a delicate pharyngeal process.
- **Branchiocranium:** Hyoid apparatus with urohyal, basihyal, supplementary basihyal, and hypohyals; four respiratory arches with curved epibranchials bearing perforated plates; fifth arch of pharyngeal bones with 45–55 teeth on each arch.
- **Oromandibular region:** Prepalatine and second pre-ethmoid cartilages provide upper-jaw mobility; small premaxillaries; a kinethmoid; a dentary with a large flattened descending limb; five bones (retroarticular, articular, coronomeckelian, mentomeckelian, angular) derived largely from Meckel's cartilage.
- **Pectoral girdle:** Sickle-shaped supracleithrum; greatly expanded cleithra and coracoids for sternohyoid attachment; postcleithra with an expanded anterior end (unlike the simple spines of squawfish).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Suckers remain on or close to the substrate as adults. Aquarium observations showed resting on the bottom with the anal fin folded against the peduncle, the ventral surface flattened, and the anal fin base indented to keep the fin off the substrate. Eyes are small and high on a domed head, so vision is probably of minor importance for food location; taste and touch are thought to be primary. The author cites [[Stewart]] (1926), who estimated about 10,000 taste buds on the lips of *C. commersonnii*.

Suckers lack a true stomach, and the intestine is long and coiled. Gut contents consist mainly of insect larvae and nymphs, molluscs, protozoa, rotifers, and some algae and diatoms, usually mixed with sand and mud. Juveniles observed in an aquarium searched the bottom, inhaled sand and detritus, filtered water through the pharynx, and ejected sand and mud through the mouth. Similar habits were reported by Reighard (1913) for *C. commersonnii* in natural habitat.

The **[[Pharyngeal Pad]]** is a key feeding structure: a large, spongy, muscular and sensory pad, about 1 cm thick in mid-section in a 34 cm standard-length *Catostomus*. Gill rakers and curved epibranchials mold around it to form a strainer, which allows suctorial feeding on small bottom organisms but prevents swallowing large prey.

## Key Findings

- **Complete osteology:** This is the first complete account of the sucker skull, providing detailed descriptions of bones, cartilages, foramina and lateral line canals in *C. macrocheilus*.
- **Suctorial feeding mechanism:** Sternohyoideus contraction pulls the hyoid backward, rotating the hyoid bars and expanding the pharyngo-oral cavity. Levator arcus palatini assists, and the upper jaw is protracted through the prepalatine, second pre-ethmoid, premaxillary and kinethmoid linkages. The maxillaris dorsalis is the most powerful retractor, inserting on a tendinous ring above the lips.
- **Mouth position:** The mouth shifts from terminal in larvae to inferior in juveniles, partly through anterior downward arching of the neurocranium, especially the supraethmoid and prevomer.
- **Pharyngeal filter:** The pharyngeal pad and comb-like teeth on the fifth arch act as a filter, which the author considers highly specialized.
- **Lateral line:** Canals are detached from the bones and run within the thick subcutaneous layer, which the author links to the specialization of the skull.
- **Phylogenetic conclusion:** Based on the author's analysis, catostomids are not more primitive or ancestral to cyprinids. Most of Sagemehl's (1891) criteria for catostomid primitiveness are judged invalid; the author argues that the skull reflects specialization for suctorial bottom feeding and that the two families share a close basic osteological resemblance.
- **Diagnostic characters:** The author lists 21 characters distinguishing *Catostomus* from most American cyprinids. Examples include a single row of numerous pharyngeal teeth, a large pharyngeal pad, curved epibranchials, a large lateral post-temporal fossa, a rod-shaped kinethmoid, reduced premaxillaries, and postcleithra with an expanded anterior end.
- **Ongoing questions:** The author notes that the true relationship of the Eventognathi cannot be settled without a thorough comparative study of the group or further fossil evidence. The paper also calls for standardizing skull abbreviations and for a future comparative osteology of catostomids as a group.
