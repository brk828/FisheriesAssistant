# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Multi-Level Intake Structure Workshop Follow-up

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #dam-impacts #thermal-ecology

## Executive Summary

This memorandum and associated documents from the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) summarize a December 1992 workshop on the proposed [[Multi-Level Intake Structure]] (MLIS) for [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The primary objective is to evaluate whether selective withdrawal of warmer water from different reservoir depths could establish a second spawning population of endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the mainstem [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. Current hypolimnetic releases (8-11°C) prevent mainstem spawning by native fishes, which require 16-23°C for successful reproduction. The workshop identified critical research needs regarding thermal augmentation effects on native fish reproduction, non-native species invasion, aquatic food webs, reservoir water quality, and power production. Documents include technical analyses of physical/chemical considerations, proposed research priorities, biological concerns, and temperature requirements for the entire fish community.

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon Dam]], completed in 1963, fundamentally altered the [[Colorado River]] ecosystem in [[Grand Canyon]]. Cold hypolimnetic releases (approximately 8-11°C year-round) replaced the pre-dam thermal regime that ranged from 2°C in winter to 23°C in summer at [[Phantom Ranch]]. This thermal modification, combined with flow regulation, sediment trapping, and non-native species introductions, contributed to the decline of native fishes. Of eight original mainstem species, only five remain: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*). [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) have been extirpated from the region.

The [[Humpback Chub]] is federally endangered, and [[Grand Canyon]] supports one of only six remaining populations. A 1978 biological opinion established seven conservation measures, including Conservation Measure 7: "Establish a second spawning population of humpback chub in the Grand Canyon." Currently, all documented spawning occurs in the warm [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) tributary during March-May when temperatures reach 16-22°C. The proposed MLIS would modify dam penstocks to withdraw warmer epilimnetic or metalimnetic water, potentially enabling mainstem spawning.

The fish community now includes 22 species, with non-natives dominating many reaches. [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) are most common above the LCR, supporting a valuable tailwater fishery. [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) are increasingly abundant downstream. Other non-natives include [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), and various centrarchids from [[Lake Mead]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature Requirements for Native Species:**
Laboratory studies by Hamman (1982, 1985) and Marsh and Pisano (1985) established that [[Humpback Chub]] require 16-22°C for spawning and egg incubation, with optimum at 19-20°C. Hatching success below 16°C is less than 15%, and no hatching occurs below 12°C. Temperatures of 15°C and 25°C result in significantly higher incidence of physical anomalies. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Speckled Dace]] require similar ranges (17-23°C), while [[Razorback Sucker]] can spawn successfully at 10-22°C.

**Current Thermal Regime:**
In 1991, releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] ranged from 8-10°C, warming longitudinally at approximately 1°C per 35 miles. At the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence (77 miles downstream), maximum temperature reached only 12°C. At [[Diamond Creek]] (241 miles downstream), maximum temperature reached 16°C only in July-August. This regime is insufficient for mainstem spawning by native fishes throughout most of the canyon.

**Pre-Dam Conditions:**
Historical data from [[Phantom Ranch]] (103 miles below dam) show pre-dam temperatures ranged from 2°C in winter to 23°C in summer. Suitable spawning temperatures (16-22°C) occurred from late May through early July, suggesting native fishes historically spawned 1-2 months later than the current March-May spawning in the LCR. The Kolb brothers reported a "bonytail" (humpback chub) aggregation at the LCR mouth in May 1911, and Suttkus and Clemmer (1977) believed spawning occurred in June-July based on ripe fish and post-larvae.

**Aquatic Food Web:**
The tailwater below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] supports a unique cold-water community dominated by [[Cladophora glomerata]] (filamentous green algae) and associated diatoms. The macroinvertebrate community is depauperate, dominated by [[Gammarus lacustris]] (amphipod) and dipterans. Blinn's outline identifies several factors limiting macroinvertebrate diversity: reduced coarse particulate organic matter (CPOM), constant cold water eliminating thermal cues for development and reproduction, high discharges, tributary sediment inputs, and heavy Cladophora growth modifying substrates. Downstream of the [[Paria River]], *Oscillatoria* (cyanobacteria) becomes important.

**Lake Powell Water Quality:**
Vernieu's analysis describes [[Lake Powell]]'s thermal stratification and water quality. The reservoir exhibits typical temperate lake dynamics with seasonal epilimnetic warming. Hypolimnetic waters contain approximately five times higher nitrate levels than surface waters. Metalimnetic oxygen deficits occur from decaying organic matter at the thermocline. Current low lake levels (1992) increase deltaic resuspension in inflow areas, magnifying nutrient effects. Salinity is lower in upper strata, with overall lake salinity budget probably conservative except for evaporation/precipitation effects.

**Parasites and Diseases:**
The [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) was recently detected in [[Grand Canyon]], with 80% of larval humpback chub (13-35 mm) examined in 1990 infected. Egg maturation occurs at 25-30°C, suggesting maturation would not occur in the mainstem even under thermal augmentation. The parasitic copepod *Lernaea cyprinacea* (anchor worm) is common on native and non-native fishes in the upper basin. Naupliar development fails below 20°C, and egg production requires temperatures above 24°C.

## Key Findings

**Proposed Thermal Augmentation Scenario:**
Dr. Richard Valdez proposes releasing 11°C water in May and 15°C in June-September. This would minimize disruption of March-April spawning in the LCR while warming downstream reaches (between LCR and [[Diamond Creek]]) to 16-22°C during June-July, providing optimal conditions for mainstem spawning. The strategy targets either late LCR spawners or downstream aggregations of chubs found at [[Elves Chasm]] to [[Havasu Creek]] (131-172 miles below dam). Releases of 15°C at the dam would provide optimal growth temperatures for the [[Lee's Ferry]] tailwater trout fishery while discouraging downstream trout populations with above-optimum temperatures.

**Non-Native Species Concerns:**
Thermal augmentation poses significant risks from non-native species invasion. [[Channel Catfish]] (spawning at 21-29°C) and [[Carp]] (20-26°C) could increase reproduction in lower canyon reaches. [[Striped Bass]] already ascend to spawn (nine captured in 1991 between May-July), and warmer temperatures may increase their numbers, though stream velocity and lack of deep lentic habitat likely limit upstream distribution. [[Red Shiner]], currently common to [[Bridge Canyon]] (250 miles below dam), could expand upstream; this species was common in [[Grand Canyon]] pre-dam, suggesting temperature alone may have excluded it. [[Fathead Minnow]] populations could increase but are controlled by flow management due to intolerance of current. [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] would experience above-optimal temperatures (above 16°C), potentially reducing their abundance in areas where they currently prey on young native fishes.

**Research Priorities (Lechleitner):**
1. Develop operational criteria for MLIS to maximize mainstem spawning while minimizing impacts on LCR spawning
2. Establish long-term monitoring of [[Lake Powell]] water quality and biological productivity (begin October 1993)
3. Model heat, salinity, and nutrient budgets of [[Lake Powell]]
4. Determine and model longitudinal warming through [[Grand Canyon]] into [[Lake Mead]]
5. Design MLIS and model temperature output effects (mathematical models by October 1993, physical models and cost estimates by March 1995)
6. Determine effects of epilimnetic withdrawal on aquatic ecosystem through experimental streams at [[Glen Canyon Dam]]

**Specific Biological Studies Needed:**
- Thermal tolerance of [[Gammarus lacustris]] from tailwater (acute and chronic temperature changes, effects on fecundity and production)
- Thermal tolerance of significant macroinvertebrate species (primarily dipterans)
- Viability and egg maturation of [[Asian Tapeworm]] below 20°C
- Degree of tapeworm infestation in native and non-native fishes and cyclopoid copepods
- Effects of increased temperature on [[Cladophora glomerata]] (laboratory and artificial stream studies)
- Effects of temperature on *Lernaea cyprinacea* life stages using native and non-native host fish

**Management Alternatives for Current Population:**
Two experimental approaches proposed to increase survival of larval chubs from LCR:
1. **Backwater raising**: Create 10 isolated backwaters around LCR confluence using flow manipulation (20,000 cfs to form return channels, then reduce to 16,000 cfs and close channels). Transport larval chubs from LCR drift nets to backwaters, monitor growth, release fish to river in fall when water cools to 8°C.
2. **Non-native removal**: Intensive netting and electrofishing to remove [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Carp]], and [[Channel Catfish]] from 20-km reach around LCR confluence where larval chubs concentrate. Justified under [[Grand Canyon National Park]] mandate to manage for native species. Could extend to other tributaries ([[Shinumo Creek]], [[Kanab Creek]], [[Havasu Creek]]) that may be potential spawning sites.

**Lake Powell Impacts:**
Warm water withdrawals would lower lake heat content with largely unknown effects. Could affect [[Threadfin Shad]] fishery by approaching lower lethal temperature levels (minimum January-February temperatures already at shad thresholds). Could result in increased turnover frequency. Effects magnified at current low lake volumes (1992). Epilimnetic withdrawals would deliver lower nutrient levels downstream, potentially affecting primary productivity. Hypolimnetic withdrawals could deliver hypoxic water, though metalimnetic oxygen deficits from thermocline decay are probably short-lived and disappear with continued winter mixing.

**Reaeration and Water Quality:**
Reaeration potential in [[Lee's Ferry]] reach not well known, probably not very high, dependent on discharge level. Significant aeration occurs from turbine condensation. Some effects from photosynthesis and atmospheric conditions, but difficult to separate variables. Diurnal photosynthetic flux difficult to discern. Dissolved oxygen could be reduced if hypolimnetic withdrawals are used.

**Critical Questions Identified:**
Workshop participants identified 66 specific questions grouped into categories: downstream native fisheries (12 questions), non-native species (11 questions), other downstream components (13 questions), lake fisheries (4 questions), lake water quality (9 questions), economic/power production (6 questions), technical issues (6 questions), and decision-making criteria (5 questions). The overarching questions are: (1) Will temperature modification alone provide favorable spawning habitat for a second mainstem population? (2) Will probable benefits to non-native species offset native species benefits through increased predation and competition? (3) What are the criteria for success and how will they be measured?

**Cost and Alternatives:**
Estimated cost of MLIS is $60 million. Questions raised whether funds would be better spent improving chub populations elsewhere, given that LCR population is relatively stable. Alternative of using waste heat from [[Navajo Generating Station]] to warm river water proposed but may have problems with chlorine and heavy metal additions. Planning, construction, and operation of MLIS would require 5-10 years to implement.

**Three Hypotheses for Spawning Timing Shift:**
1. Humpback chub did not spawn in mainstem prior to [[Glen Canyon Dam]]
2. Cold releases forced mainstem spawners to switch to earlier spawning and ascend LCR to coincide with tributary temperatures
3. Two population components existed—one spawned in mainstem (late May-July), one in LCR (March-May). The mainstem component experienced reproductive failure post-dam and few individuals remain. This hypothesis is supported by downstream aggregations currently found at [[Bright Angel Creek]], [[Shinumo Creek]], [[Tapeats Creek]], [[Havasu Creek]], RM 120, and RM 213 ([[Pumpkin Springs]]).
