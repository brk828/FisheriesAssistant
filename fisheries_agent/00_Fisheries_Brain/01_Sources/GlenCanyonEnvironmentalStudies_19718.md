# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies - Multiple Level Intake Structure Workshop Follow-up

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #dam-management

## Executive Summary

This memorandum from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] summarizes the November 18, 1992 workshop on the proposed [[Multiple Level Intake Structure]] (MLIS) for [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The primary objective is to support maintenance and recovery of threatened and endangered fish species, particularly [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), by modifying water temperatures in the [[Colorado River]] through selective releases. The workshop identified critical technical concerns regarding impacts on [[Lake Powell]], [[Lake Mead]], and the Colorado River ecosystem, and emphasized the need for comprehensive evaluation before implementation.

## Background & Description

The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) convened a workshop to evaluate a proposed multi-level intake structure at [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The structure aims to modify water temperatures in the [[Colorado River]] by allowing selective withdrawal from different reservoir depths. The primary endangered species of concern is the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in [[Grand Canyon]]. The desired effect is to establish a second spawning population of humpback chub in the mainstem river. Case histories from other facilities demonstrate that properly designed structures can achieve temperature changes, though additional unpredicted effects often occur. The workshop included presentations and was moderated by Bruce Hawkinson, with participation from multiple agencies including [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[National Park Service]], and [[Bureau of Reclamation]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The workshop identified three primary areas of technical concern: effects on [[Lake Powell]], effects on [[Lake Mead]], and effects on the [[Colorado River]] ecosystem. The proposed MLIS would be part of an [[Integrated Systems Resource Management Plan]] (ISRMP) for [[Grand Canyon]]. Key ecological considerations include: temperature requirements for [[Humpback Chub]] spawning, interactions with non-native species ([[Threadfin Shad]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Common Carp]]), food base dynamics (periphyton and invertebrates), predator-competitor relationships, and riparian vegetation responses. The workshop noted that humpback chubs are not currently using the interface area between [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Powell]], raising questions about temperature as the primary limiting factor. The interaction between temperature and flow regimes was identified as critical to understanding species responses.

## Key Findings

**Primary Objective**: The MLIS objective is "to support the maintenance and recovery of the threatened and endangered fish species and establish a community balance for the Colorado River ecosystem by modifying temperatures in the Colorado River by selective releases." The first desired effect is establishing a second spawning population of [[Humpback Chub]] in the mainstem.

**Technical Concerns Identified**: Workshop participants raised multiple technical questions requiring research: (A) Effects of epilimnion withdrawal on [[Threadfin Shad]] population in [[Lake Powell]], including winterkill and entrainment of young downstream; (B) Impacts on hydropower production; (C) Effects on non-native fish ([[Common Carp]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]]) from Glen Canyon Dam to [[Lake Mead]]; (D) Potential to induce [[Humpback Chub]] spawning in mainstem later than [[Little Colorado River]] spawn; (E) Environmental impacts of warmer releases on [[Lake Mead]]; (F) Whether warm releases would encourage [[Striped Bass]] upstream movement; (G) Effects on riparian vegetation in [[Grand Canyon]]; (H) Direction of periphyton changes in tailrace; (I) Invertebrate (macro and micro) responses to periphyton changes.

**Management Framework Requirements**: Development of the ISRMP requires: (a) identifying affected resources; (b) including system components vital to resource health (temperature, food base, predators/competitors, pathogens); (c) defining limits, optimums, and allowable variances of system components. These steps require information from research, monitoring, and analysis.

**Recommended Actions**: The [[Reservoir Work Group]] should develop a "white paper" on issues and timing associated with MLIS, describing current knowledge and identifying information gaps and research needs. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] should reemphasize concerns and requirements to satisfy the Biological Opinion. The EIS team should continue MLIS evaluation. Bruce Hawkinson recommended two challenges for follow-up committees: (1) Define what the fish community should look like in 20 years with optimum water management; (2) Create an optimum water management plan (temperature, discharge, variability) to maintain an ecologically sustainable community. He emphasized the need for five-year review intervals and successive approximations, factoring in long time schemes (10-50 years) and life histories up to 40 years long.

**Critical Questions**: Participants questioned evaluation criteria, including: Will objective criteria be developed a priori? What will the evaluation period be? Will operations return to status quo if unsuccessful? What are management targets? Who decides management objectives and what is the [[National Park Service]] role? How can success be predicted? What is the time frame before "balance" is achieved? What constitutes stability? What role does monitoring play in long-term [[Adaptive Management]]? How long will design, funding, and installation take?
