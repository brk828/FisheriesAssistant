# Toxicity of MS-222 to Selected Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #toxicology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #percidae #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This document is Resource Publication 18 (January 1967) in the [[Investigations in Fish Control]] series, issued by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] and written by chemist [[Leif L. Marking]] at the [[Fish Control Laboratories]] in La Crosse, Wisconsin. It measures the toxicity of the fish anesthetic [[MS-222]] (tricaine methanesulfonate) to eight freshwater game fish species across several exposure durations, temperatures and water hardnesses. The work was undertaken to supply data that the [[Food and Drug Administration]] required before MS-222 could remain in use in fish culture. The 24-hour [[Lethal Concentration 50]] (LC50) values ranged from 33.8 to 63.0 p.p.m. Exposures beyond 24 hours added little toxicity, smaller fish were more sensitive, and the [[Safety Index]] results favored brief exposures.

## Background & Description

**Regulatory context.** The Foreword explains that the FDA required the Bureau to show that fish treated with certain culture and management chemicals pose no residue hazard to people who eat them. MS-222 was named because it is widely used to anesthetize fish for marking or tagging, spawning by stripping, and transport. Clearance required data on toxicity, anesthetic efficacy, and residues, with emphasis on muscle, the tissue people eat. FDA also required testing across fish sizes, at three or more temperatures, and in waters of different quality.

**The compound.** MS-222 is the methanesulfonate salt of meta-aminobenzoic acid ethyl ester. It was synthesized by Maurice Sandoz roughly 45 years before publication while searching for a cocaine substitute. It was used as a local anesthetic in humans, and its general anesthetic action in cold-blooded animals was recognized early. The report describes it as a fine, white crystalline powder (molecular weight 261.3; melting point 145–150 °C) that is soluble in water up to 11 percent (22.5 percent in the author's laboratory). Solutions are clear and acid, and they darken on light exposure without losing activity. The Foreword lists many synonyms, including Metacaine, Tricaine, and TS-222, and the authors standardized on three of them.

**Series context.** The Foreword and series list place this report within [[Investigations in Fish Control]], a set of 17 numbered reports from the La Crosse and Warm Springs, Georgia, laboratories. Topics include screening methods, [[Toxaphene]] and [[Antimycin A]] piscicide studies, and [[Sea Lamprey]] control. Reports 12 through 17 deal with MS-222, covering toxicity, efficacy on four salmonids, residue methods, residues after anesthesia, an annotated bibliography, and efficacy and residues in [[Channel Catfish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Test species and sources.** Fish were obtained from national and state hatcheries and quarantined for 10 days before acclimation. Eight species were tested: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Walleye]]. Size groups were 1–3 in, 3–5 in, and 6–9 in fingerlings and juveniles.

**Experimental conditions.**
- **Temperatures:** 7, 12, 17, and 22 °C, using water baths.
- **Water hardness:** 10, 42, and 176 p.p.m., made by adding reconstituting salts to deionized water. Alkalinity and pH were also recorded (pH about 6.7 to 7.7).
- **Exposure durations:** 15, 30, and 60 minutes, and 24, 48, and 96 hours, in [[Static Bioassay]] systems.
- **Vessels:** 5-gallon glass jars for 2-inch fish (10 concentrations, 10 fish each, 20 controls) and aerated polyethylene tanks holding 45 L for larger fish (5 concentrations per test).

**Water quality and behavior.** Drug responses were recorded for several hours and then daily. Dead fish were removed at once, and anesthetized fish were moved to fresh water to recover. Mortality data were analyzed with the [[Litchfield-Wilcoxon Method]]. The report notes that the larger brook trout lot was infected with furunculosis shortly before testing, which may have lowered their resistance, and that the northern pike were hungry and cannibalistic, which likely contributed to reduced resistance at 96 hours.

## Key Findings

1. **Consistent toxicity across eight species.** The 24-hour LC50 values ranged from 33.8 p.p.m. (lake trout, the most sensitive) to 63.0 p.p.m. (largemouth bass, the most tolerant). The author concludes the species values were not greatly different.

2. **Duration matters little after 24 hours.** For 2-inch rainbow trout, LC50 values were 64.6 p.p.m. at 15 minutes, 55.5 p.p.m. at 30 minutes, 39.0 p.p.m. at 24 hours, and 38.4 p.p.m. at 96 hours. Several species showed identical 24- and 96-hour LC50 values. Only 2-inch brown trout showed a notable drop, about 20 percent, between 24 and 96 hours.

3. **Size effects.** Smaller fish were generally more sensitive. The 24-hour LC50 for rainbow trout rose from 39 p.p.m. (2-inch) to 52 p.p.m. (9-inch). The 7-inch largemouth bass LC50 was described as 50 percent greater than for 1-inch bass.

4. **Species ranking among salmonids.** Lake trout were the most sensitive and brook trout the least. Brown and rainbow trout were equal and intermediate. The author links brook trout's higher resistance partly to lower drug residues found by [[Walker and Schoettger]] (1966) after anesthesia.

5. **Temperature effects.** Rainbow trout were slightly more resistant at 7 °C than at 12 °C and 17 °C, with little difference between 24- and 96-hour values. Bluegills were more sensitive at 12 °C and 22 °C than at 17 °C, though the author notes the 22 °C fish were smaller, which may explain their greater sensitivity.

6. **Water hardness.** Hardness had little effect on toxicity in rainbow trout, but results at 10 p.p.m. were erratic, with partial mortality at 33 and 34 p.p.m. and none at 36 p.p.m. The author concludes the drug acts unpredictably in very soft water.

7. **Recovery.** Rainbow trout exposed to 34–50 p.p.m. for 96 hours recovered within 1–5 minutes in fresh water. Walleye exposed to 40–50 p.p.m. needed 60–180 minutes. Of 356 fish that recovered in fresh water, only 8 later died. Surviving 2-inch rainbow trout showed no mortality over a 7-day observation and fed and behaved like controls.

8. **Safety indexes.** The Safety Index (LC50/EC50) and Maximum Safety Index (LC50/EC99) were calculated for 2-inch rainbow trout at 12 °C in 15-, 30-, and 60-minute exposures. Shorter exposures were judged safer. The drug was less safe in soft water (10 and 42 p.p.m.), and the maximum index more clearly showed this hazard. Sandoz's reported therapeutic index of 1.57 was not considered comparable because its exposure durations differed.

9. **Conclusions for practice.** The author recommends computing both the Safety Index and the Maximum Safety Index, since either may reveal a hazard the other misses. MS-222 was judged safer for rainbow trout in hard water than in soft.
