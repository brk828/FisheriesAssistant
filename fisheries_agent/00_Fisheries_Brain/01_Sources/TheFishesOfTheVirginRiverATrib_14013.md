# The Fishes of the Virgin River, a Tributary of the Lower Colorado River

## Executive Summary

This study by Deacon, LaBounty, and Bradley (Univ. of Nevada, Las Vegas) documents the distribution and relative abundance of fish species within the [[Virgin River]] system — a largely undeveloped tributary of the lower [[Colorado River]] entering [[Lake Mead]], Nevada. Based on field surveys conducted primarily 1965–66 (with supplementary museum records dating back to the 1920s–1968), the authors identify **14 fish species** occupying the system, including **6 native Colorado River Basin species** and several introduced/invasive taxa. The paper establishes baseline distributional data intended to evaluate the ecological impact of proposed water development projects, notably the **Bureau of Reclamation's Dixie Project (1961)**, which threatened to alter the hydrology of this then-largely pristine desert river. Key native species of concern include the endemic **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and the **[[Virgin River Spinedace]]** (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*), both restricted to and highly adapted to the Virgin River's distinctive turbid, swift-flowing conditions.

## Background & Description

The [[Virgin River]] system drains mountain ranges of southwestern Utah, flowing ~250 miles from ~9000 ft elevation down to 1163 ft at Lake Mead, passing through Utah, Arizona, and Nevada. Historically (pre-Lake Mead impoundment), the Virgin River flowed directly into the Colorado River, and the [[Moapa River]] was a tributary of the Virgin River, hosting two endemic species absent elsewhere in the system.

**Physical gradient:**
- **Headwaters** (above ~4000 ft, Zion National Park): clear, cool, trout-dominated streams flowing through farmland and oak-pine forest.
- **Middle/lower reaches**: increasing turbidity, torrential flows, frequent flooding, and marked seasonal variation in flow and temperature.
- **Lower river**: especially turbid and hydrologically variable.

**Methodology:** 30 sampling stations were established sequentially upstream from the river mouth and up tributaries. Sampling combined a 7mm mesh seine with a 110V AC backpack shocker (tennis racket electrodes), with each 100 ft. stream section sampled up to 5 times per period. Water chemistry (alkalinity, CO2, NO3, NO4, PO4, dissolved oxygen, pH, turbidity, temperature) was measured at select lower-river stations (see [[Table 1 - Virgin River Water Chemistry]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water chemistry data (Table 1) show that **alkalinity, CO2, and turbidity generally increase downstream**. An anomalously high PO4 reading at Gunlock (Station 6) was attributed to agricultural runoff from fertilized farmland via irrigation return flow.

The fish fauna segregates into three broad ecological zones:
1. **Downstream mainstream fauna** — dominated by *[[Woundfin]]* (Plagopterus argentissimus) and *[[Bonytail]]* (Gila robusta), adapted to swift, turbid, sandy-bottom conditions.
2. **Lower tributary fauna** — dominated by *[[Speckled Dace]]* (Rhinichthys osculus), *[[Bluehead Sucker]]* (Pantosteus clarki/delphinus), and *[[Virgin River Spinedace]]* (Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis), favoring clear, gravel-rubble substrates.
3. **Headwater trout fauna** — cold, clear streams occupied exclusively by *[[Brown Trout]]* (Salmo trutta) and *[[Rainbow Trout]]* (Salmo gairdneri), with occasional co-occurrence of speckled dace and bluehead sucker.

The authors emphasize that the Virgin River, unlike the Gila River (where *Plagopterus* had already disappeared due to habitat modification per Miller & Hubbs 1960), remained largely undisturbed at the time of study and thus maintained robust native populations — but flagged the **proposed Dixie Project dam** as a significant future threat to endemic species survival, particularly the woundfin.

### Endemism & Conservation Significance
- **6 native species** are endemic to the Colorado River System; **2** (Woundfin and Virgin River Spinedace) are presently restricted to the Virgin River drainage.
- The **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) is highlighted as the most ecologically significant and vulnerable native species — historically also recorded in the Santa Clara River (Gunlock) and Ash Creek (Toquerville, 1929) but now apparently extirpated from those tributaries, likely due to dam and irrigation development.
- The **[[Virgin River Spinedace]]** rarely co-occurs with trout, suggesting habitat partitioning along the thermal/turbidity gradient.
- Comparison to the **Gila River** case (Miller & Hubbs 1960) — where *Plagopterus* went extinct following early habitat modification — frames the Virgin River as a rare intact refugium, with the proposed **Bureau of Reclamation Dixie Project dam** identified as a direct extinction risk to the species.

### Morphological Variation in Pantosteus clarki (Table 2)
Meristic comparisons across three stations (2, 18, 26) revealed:
- **Predorsal and lateral-line scale counts increase upstream.**
- **Relative caudal peduncle width increases upstream** — attributed (following Smith 1966) to reduced current velocity in upstream/headwater reaches vs. swifter downstream flow.
- **Dorsal fin ray counts and gill raker counts (internal & external) tend to decrease upstream.**
- The Birch Creek population's unusually high predorsal scale count (>46, per Smith 1966) is confirmed to extend to the broader population above Hurricane, Utah.

### Water Chemistry Trends (Table 1)
- Alkalinity, CO2, and turbidity increase progressively downstream.
- Elevated PO4 at Gunlock (Station 6) linked to agricultural irrigation runoff.
- Dissolved oxygen and pH values remained relatively stable across sampled stations despite turbidity gradients.
