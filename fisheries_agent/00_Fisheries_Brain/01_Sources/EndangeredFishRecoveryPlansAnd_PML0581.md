# Endangered Fish Recovery Plans and Habitat Management in the Desert Fishes Council Proceedings

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This proceedings-style document, from a 1974 meeting of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], covers the operation of [[Recovery Plan]]s for endangered western fishes and the habitat-maintenance programs of federal and state agencies. Its core message is that listing a species does not ensure its recovery. Recovery requires a sequence of steps from listing through restoration, a flexible plan maintained by a recovery team, and a long-term management body. The document also flags a broad neglect of nongame species by states and a chronic shortage of fishery biologists in federal agencies. It reviews work on the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Woundfin]], [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], and an unnamed species in the Lower Colorado River Basin and the Great Basin region.

## Background & Description

**Recovery plan concept.** C. E. Ruhr of the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] (BSFW) described the recovery plan as a series of steps from listing to restoration, ending when a species is no longer endangered. A completed plan includes a discussion of concepts, a species status history, a statement of intent, and flow diagrams summarizing implementation. The plan must be flexible and dynamic. Recovery teams maintain it, coordinate local activities, and prepare annual reports. BSFW is working to motivate states to manage their entire fish and wildlife resources, not only species with economic value. New laws and public education may be needed.

**Focal species.**
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Included in operating recovery plans in Arizona, New Mexico, Oklahoma and Texas.
- **[[Colorado River Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): Its recovery plan is in draft, and a small broodstock was acquired.
- **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): Restricted to the main stream of the Virgin River and its tributaries; its recovery team was newly formed.
- **[[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Salmo clarkii pleuriticus*): Status undetermined; the Bureau of Land Management (BLM) hopes it will be recognized as threatened.
- An unnamed species, referred to by the epithet *pectoralis*, had a recovery plan developed by the BLM Nevada State Office, which was praised by Del Rasmussen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat evaluation (U.S. Forest Service).** Charles Kennedy described fish in Arizona and New Mexico as a challenging problem, with administrative and resource issues of equal importance. The Forest Service uses a numerical method to rate stream habitat, scoring gravel, pools, flow, riparian vegetation condition and temperature. The scores are summed to rank habitats. The stated basic flaw of the system is cut off in the source.

**Private lands (Soil Conservation Service).** John Yordin reported that the [[Soil Conservation Service]] (SCS) gives advisory technical help to private landowners on conservation plans. It has no regulatory power and cannot manage species. It conducts no wildlife research. SCS admitted past harm to fish and wildlife, and it now has a standing policy to consult Arizona Game and Fish before advising on projects that might affect wildlife or perennial water. SCS also advises against introducing exotic fishes into private waters without clearance.

**Oil shale and riparian work (BLM Colorado).** Possible effects of oil shale development on the Colorado River cutthroat trout could be significant. BLM is establishing riparian vegetation along East Fork, Parachute and Trapper creeks, and it requested information on similar work on Rock Creek, Montana, and by the Wisconsin Conservation Department. BLM reported that a shortage of fishery biologists limits its work.

**Salinity of the Virgin River system.** Measurements in 1973 at Laverkin Springs, the primary source for woundfin, showed flow of about 11 to 12 cfs. The springs contribute roughly 100,000 tons of salt per year to the Colorado River. A Bureau of Reclamation plan under the Colorado River Water Quality Improvement Program would divert spring flow to a desalting plant, return the water to the Virgin River, and send brine to an evaporation pond. The effect of salt removal on woundfin survival is an open question.

## Key Findings

- **Recovery requires long-term commitment.** Listing and attention alone do not restore a species. Recovery plans need flexibility, team coordination and handover to ongoing management.
- **States neglect nongame species.** BSFW is working to change state management priorities and may need new laws and public education campaigns.
- **Operating plans and status.** Recovery plans for the Razorback Sucker and Colorado River Squawfish were drafted. Azevedo reported operating plans in Arizona, New Mexico, Oklahoma and Texas. He warned that recovery teams should not grow too large.
- **[[Lower Colorado River Task Force]].** Formed in March 1973 as an interagency recovery team to protect the remaining populations of the Lower Colorado River fishes. Early efforts focused on the Razorback Sucker and Squawfish. Kobetich reported that a small Colorado squawfish broodstock was acquired, and that razorback broodstock collection was planned for March or April 1974. Work on the Gila complex was to begin later.
- **Woundfin recovery.** The team was in its infancy, with an acting chairman. BSFW and the Bureau of Reclamation jointly provided $14,000 to Dr. Deacon for a study of how Laverkin Springs flows affect woundfin survival. A graduate student, Jeff Cross, began a research project on woundfin biology and history.
- **Habitat assessment.** Forest Service stream rating allows ranking of habitats but has a basic flaw not fully stated in the source.
- **Private-land limits.** SCS can only influence private land use when landowners request federal help. It has a policy to consult state agencies and has pledged to protect rare fishes and avoid exotic introductions.
- **Cutthroat trout uncertainty.** Status is undetermined. Oil shale development is a potential threat that needs monitoring.
- **Agency capacity gap.** BLM and other agencies lack fishery biologists and depend on help from other agencies.
