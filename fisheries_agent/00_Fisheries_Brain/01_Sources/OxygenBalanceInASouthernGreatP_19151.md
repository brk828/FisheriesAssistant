# Oxygen Balance in a Southern Great Plains Stream in Southeastern Oklahoma

#aquatic-ecology #stream-metabolism #primary-productivity #benthic-communities

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation by William R. Duffer (Oklahoma State University, 1965) examines community metabolism and oxygen dynamics in the relatively undisturbed [[Blue River]] in southeastern Oklahoma from June 1963 to August 1964. The study investigated how different geological substrates (limestone, granite, sand) influenced primary productivity, respiration, and overall oxygen balance in stream reaches. Key measurements included water temperature, dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll a, organic matter, and photosynthetic efficiency. The research demonstrated that substrate type profoundly affected benthic producer communities: granite outcrops supported dense populations of bryophytes and algae with exceptionally high productivity (up to 48.0 g O₂/m²/day in summer), while limestone and sand reaches exhibited heterotrophic metabolism where respiration exceeded photosynthesis. The study provides important baseline data on unpolluted stream metabolism and demonstrates that attachment surfaces are critical environmental factors controlling primary production in flowing waters.

## Background & Description

[[Blue River]] is a fifth-order tributary of the [[Red River]] located in Pontotoc, Johnston, and Bryan counties in southeastern Oklahoma. The drainage basin covers approximately 2,000 km² with a channel length approaching 225 km. The stream gradient averages 1.1 m/km, ranging from 9.5 m/km in headwaters to 0.4 m/km near the mouth. Average annual precipitation is 96.7 cm.

The study area encompassed 48 km of stream beginning approximately 88 km from the source, characterized by continuous flow without municipal sewage inputs. Thirteen sampling stations were established across four distinct geological formations:

- **[[Arbuckle Group]]** (Cambrian-Ordovician): Limestones and dolomites up to 1,980 m thick; stream bottom contains eroded limestone boulders; numerous springs
- **[[Simpson Group]]** (Ordovician): Limestones, sandstones, and shales averaging 460 m thick; limestone boulders in channel
- **[[Tishomingo Granite]]** (Precambrian): Coarse-grained pinkish granite; channel features exposed granite scarps, riffles, and low falls separated by deeper pools
- **[[Paluxy Sand]]** (Cretaceous Trinity Group): 120-215 m of conglomerate, fine sand, and clay; shifting sand substrate

Water temperature ranged from 6.5°C (March) to 34°C (July). Total solar radiation varied from 160 to 654 cal/cm²/day. The stream exhibited low turbidity (5-23 ppm), alkaline pH (8.0-8.5), chloride concentration of 4 ppm, and only trace sulfates.

Principal primary producers were bryophytes (dominant genus [[Fontinalis]]), algae ([[Rhizoclonium]], [[Cladophora]], [[Spirogyra]], [[Schizothrix]]), and diatoms ([[Diatoma]], [[Melosira]], [[Synedra]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses fundamental questions about energy flow and community metabolism in unpolluted streams, contrasting with most contemporary research focused on polluted systems. The research demonstrates that high primary productivity is not limited to nutrient-enriched waters but can occur in unpolluted streams when favorable attachment surfaces exist.

**Substrate Effects on Community Structure:**

Benthic chlorophyll a concentration exceeded suspended concentration at all reaches except where shifting sand dominated. Summer benthic chlorophyll a was highest on granite (0.39 g/m²) compared to limestone (0.26 g/m²) and sand (0.02 g/m²). Suspended chlorophyll a remained constant at 0.004 g/m² across all reaches. Large boulders supported greater algal biomass than smaller substrates, consistent with findings from [[Logan River]], Utah, where rocks >12 cm yielded 0.30 g/m² chlorophyll a versus 0.15-0.25 g/m² for smaller rocks.

**Hydrochemical Patterns:**

Specific conductance and total alkalinity decreased downstream (543 to 472 micromhos/cm; 308 to 245 ppm total alkalinity) while carbonate alkalinity increased (8 to 21 ppm). This pattern resulted from photosynthetic removal of CO₂ from bicarbonates, causing precipitation of calcium carbonate. The downstream increase in carbonate alkalinity was attributed to excess calcium carbonate in metastable colloidal form.

**Oxygen Exchange:**

Oxygen exchange rates varied from 1.3 to 2.9 g/m²/hr at 100% saturation deficit, with higher rates in areas of greater velocity and shallow water (Reach 12-13 in sand formation).

**Comparison with Other Systems:**

The study compared [[Blue River]] metabolism with other aquatic communities including [[Silver Springs]] (Florida), [[Ivel River]] (England), [[Itchen River]] (England), [[Logan River]] (Utah), [[White River]] (Indiana), and [[Mission River]] (Texas). Unpolluted streams generally showed lower productivity than polluted recovery zones or thermostatic artesian springs, but [[Blue River]]'s granite reach exhibited productivity comparable to enriched systems.

## Key Findings

**1. Community Metabolism Patterns by Substrate Type:**

Three distinct metabolic regimes corresponded to geological substrates:

**Reach 5-6 (Limestone - Heterotrophic):**
- Photosynthetic productivity: 2.4 g O₂/m²/day (winter) to 12.0 g O₂/m²/day (summer)
- Community respiration: 6.1 to 16.4 g O₂/m²/day
- Annual primary production: ~2.5 kg O₂/m²/year
- Annual respiration: ~4.0 kg O₂/m²/year
- P/R ratio: 0.30 to 0.93 (always <1)
- Respiration consistently exceeded photosynthesis

**Reach 6-7 (Granite - Autotrophic):**
- Photosynthetic productivity: 10.1 g O₂/m²/day (winter) to 48.0 g O₂/m²/day (summer)
- Community respiration: 9.0 to 19.9 g O₂/m²/day (lowest in fall/early winter)
- Annual primary production: ~7.8 kg O₂/m²/year
- Annual respiration: ~4.6 kg O₂/m²/year
- P/R ratio: 1.01 to 3.92 (always >1)
- Photosynthesis always exceeded respiration
- Only 59% of production was consumed locally; remainder exported downstream

**Reach 12-13 (Sand - Heterotrophic):**
- Photosynthetic productivity: 1.5 to 5.1 g O₂/m²/day (highest in summer/fall)
- Community respiration: 6.1 to 11.0 g O₂/m²/day
- Annual primary production: ~1.1 kg O₂/m²/year
- Annual respiration: ~2.8 kg O₂/m²/year
- P/R ratio: 0.24 to 0.50 (always <1)
- Community produced only 39% of what it consumed

**2. Substrate as Primary Control on Productivity:**

The granite outcrop supported exceptionally high productivity despite having the same light intensity, temperature, and nutrient levels as adjacent reaches. High productivity resulted from:
- Dense populations of benthic bryophytes and algae
- Stable attachment surfaces on granite scarps
- Extensive aufwuchs communities on bryophyte streamers
- Continuous water renewal over fixed substrates

In contrast, shifting sand substrates at Reach 12-13 prevented establishment of substantial benthic producer communities, resulting in low productivity despite relatively high respiration from imported organic matter.

**3. Seasonal Patterns:**

Spring bloom period (March-May) exhibited:
- Greatest difference between productivity and respiration on granite
- Low respiration despite high productivity
- High photosynthetic efficiency of algal populations
- Minimal respiratory demand

Summer metabolism showed:
- Maximum productivity on granite (48.0 g O₂/m²/day)
- Highest benthic chlorophyll a concentrations
- Increased respiration rates with temperature

**4. Photosynthetic Efficiency:**

Daily photosynthetic efficiency ranged from 0.1% to 2.7% of total solar radiation:
- Highest efficiency: 2.7% (May, Reach 8-9)
- Lowest efficiency: 0.1% (March, Reach 12-13)
- Efficiency inversely related to light intensity

Hourly analysis revealed:
- Efficiency declined from ~3.7% at low intensity to 1.0% at highest intensity (72 cal/cm²/hr)
- Solar radiation partially limiting at all observed intensities
- Rate of productivity increase per unit radiation declined above 20 cal/cm²/hr
- Efficiency decreased most rapidly up to 30 cal/cm²/hr

This pattern supports the optimum efficiency-maximum power hypothesis: natural systems achieve maximum power output at optimum (not maximum) efficiency.

**5. Longitudinal Stream Succession:**

The study documented downstream metabolic succession:
- Autotrophic granite reach exported organic matter
- Heterotrophic downstream reaches imported organic matter
- Sand reach had not achieved steady-state metabolism (P/R = 1) due to high particulate organic matter input and limited benthic producers
- Tendency toward steady-state conditions downstream unless external organic matter supplied

**6. Comparative Context:**

[[Blue River]] granite reach productivity (7.8 kg O₂/m²/year) exceeded:
- [[Ivel River]], England: 3.5 kg/m²/year (P/R = 1.13)
- [[Itchen River]], England: 2.0 kg/m²/year (P/R = 0.54)
- [[Logan River]] canyon section: ~1.3 kg/m²/year

But remained below:
- [[Silver Springs]] headwater: 6.8 kg/m²/year (P/R = 1.06)

The granite reach productivity approached values typical of polluted stream recovery zones and thermostatic springs, but achieved through different mechanisms (attachment surface availability rather than nutrient enrichment or thermal stability).

**7. Methodological Contributions:**

The study employed the upstream-downstream oxygen change method with diffusion corrections, measuring:
- Diurnal dissolved oxygen curves at paired stations
- Time-lag adjustments for water travel time
- Atmospheric oxygen exchange corrections using clear plastic dome and Scholander device
- Benthic and suspended chlorophyll a via acetone extraction and spectrophotometry

This research demonstrates that attachment surfaces represent a critical but often overlooked environmental variable controlling primary production in streams, potentially as important as light, temperature, and nutrients in determining community metabolism.
