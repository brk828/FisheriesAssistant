# Long-Term Monitoring Plan for Fish in Selected Waters of the Gila River Basin, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #non-native-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Revision No. 2 plan (October 1996), prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], sets out a long-term program to monitor the presence, distribution, and abundance of non-native fishes, and changes in species composition, in target reaches of the Gila River Basin. The monitoring is required under the Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) of a 1994 Biological Opinion on the transport and delivery of [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water. That opinion found that translocation of non-native fishes and aquatic organisms through the CAP was the main jeopardy concern for several listed fishes. The plan is expected to run for the 100-year life of the CAP, or until the ESA status of the listed fishes changes. It defines parameters, sampling methods, a standardized database, statistical approaches, quality control, and a management action plan, and it is explicitly a living document to be revised as field experience accumulates.

## Background & Description

- **Legal basis:** The CAP is a series of aqueducts and pipelines moving Colorado River water from Lake Havasu to central and southern Arizona. A 1994 USFWS Biological Opinion (BO) concluded that CAP transport could jeopardize the Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), spikedace (*Meda fulgida*), loach minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*), and razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and could adversely modify designated critical habitat of the latter three.
- **Monitoring types:** The plan distinguishes baseline monitoring (establishing a database for future comparison) from trend monitoring (repeated measurements at regular intervals to detect long-term change). It is described as surveillance rather than impact assessment, because it does not, by itself, attribute impacts to particular causes.
- **Objectives:** Five are listed: (1) determine parameters to monitor; (2) identify statistical techniques and effect-size criteria; (3) determine sampling methods for richness, distribution, and assemblage structure; (4) develop a standardized statistical database; and (5) develop a management action plan.
- **Reach scope:** Six reach groups are monitored, including the CAP aqueduct, Salt River Project (SRP) canals, Florence-Casa Grande (FCG) canal, Salt River between Stewart Mountain and Granite Reef dams, the Gila River between Coolidge and Ashurst-Hayden dams, and perennial reaches of the San Pedro River downstream from the U.S.-Mexico border.
- **Acknowledgements:** The plan credits contributors from the USFWS, Arizona Game and Fish Department, Bureau of Land Management, New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, Salt River Project, Reclamation, and Arizona State University.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Non-native fish threat:** The primary concern is accidental or intentional spread of non-native fishes from the Colorado River into Gila Basin drainages. Table 4 lists recent (since 1970) occurrences of about 30 species across the monitored systems, including common carp, red shiner, sailfin molly, mosquitofish, Mossambique tilapia, largemouth bass, green sunfish, channel catfish, and walleye.
- **Native species of concern:** Gila topminnow, spikedace, loach minnow, and razorback sucker are the listed fishes. Native species such as the Sonora sucker, desert sucker, longfin dace, and humpback chub are also noted in the species list.
- **Habitat and hydrology:** Flows in most target reaches (except the San Pedro) are artificially controlled by dam releases or canal dry-ups. Annual dry-ups on the SRP canals prevent fixed stations there. Aravaipa Creek is described as a relatively pristine system with a native fish community that can serve as a reference for attainable conditions.
- **Habitat classification:** Macrohabitats are classed as pools, riffles, and runs. Substrate is classed from clay through bedrock, following Lane (1947).

## Key Findings

- **Reach and station design:** Reaches include San Pedro River (Hereford to Fairbank, Cascabel to Redington, Aravaipa Creek to Gila River), Gila River reaches (Coolidge Dam to Needles Eye, Little Ash Creek to Hayden, Hayden to Mineral Creek, Mineral Creek to Ashurst-Hayden Dam), Salt River (Stewart Mountain Dam to Granite Reef Dam), CAP aqueducts, FCG canal, and SRP canals. Fixed stations are placed in upper, middle, and lower positions within reaches, and pumping plant forebays are used as CAP stations.
- **Quantitative sampling:** Each fixed station is a 200 m stream reach. This length meets the Lyons (1992) criterion of at least 35 stream widths, provided mean width is 5.7 m or less. Random stations supplement the fixed sites to detect new or rare species.
- **Primary gear:** Electrofishing is the primary descriptor of assemblage structure, run as single-pass sampling in an upstream direction in pools and downstream into blocking nets in swift habitats. Pulse rates of 40 to 120 pulses per second are considered effective for native minnows, but rates below 30 pulses per second with smooth pulse shape are recommended if fish injury is a concern.
- **Sampling frequency:** Fixed stations are sampled annually, preferably in autumn, to avoid high flows. San Pedro sampling follows a randomized schedule from 1997 to 2006, with one additional random event per reach per year.
- **Statistical power (Wilson 1995 analysis):** For a 20% change in relative abundance to be detected at power >0.8, at least 30 individuals of a species must be collected per sample. With 50 individuals, a 10% change is detectable at power >0.9. Changes of 30% or less in rare species (<25 individuals per sample) are unlikely to be detected. A repeated measures nested ANOVA is the recommended analytic design, with a significance level of 0.05.
- **Voucher specimens:** At least three specimens of each permitted species are to be preserved, lodged in the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes, and any new species report must be communicated to USFWS and AGFD within five days.
- **Quality control:** Keypunched data are double-checked, with an initial acceptable error rate of 1 error in 100 lines of data, to be tightened over time. Data must be submitted within 90 days of field sampling completion.
- **Management action plan:** If a new non-native species is found or assemblage criteria are exceeded, biologists are to convene to assess the situation and recommend actions. Options include localized removal, habitat modification, barriers, and controlled flow management. Compliance with the National Environmental Policy Act must be completed before major actions.
