# Tempo of Evolutionary Processes in Mexican Cave Characins

#ichthyology #marine-biology #characidae

## Executive Summary

This German-language essay by [[Curt Kosswig]] (received 26 May 1967) asks how quickly evolutionary change can produce troglobiont (cave-adapted) forms. It uses the Mexican cave characin, [[Astyanax Mexicanus]] (cave form historically named [[Anoptichthys Jordani]]), as the test case. Kosswig argues that the cave forms are geologically young because their surface ancestor reached North America only recently, yet they show strong degenerative traits and some adaptive gains. Degeneration is highly polygenic, and cave populations remain isolated in separate pools. The author also cites evidence from Myers (1966) and mammal paleontology on the timing of the Central American faunal connection.

## Background & Description

- **Troglobionts** are defined as blind, depigmented cave animals derived from surface relatives. Their uniform degenerative traits are considered a better diagnostic than any compensatory structures. Kosswig cites [[Vandel 1965]] on the rarity of new constructive traits in true cave animals.
- **Debate on timing:** Some authors (including Kosswig, 1945) propose rapid transformation of surface forms into cavernicoles. Others, such as Vandel, view troglobionts as ancient relict lineages. Kosswig notes that many cave beetles of southern Europe are old, with specializations dating to the early Tertiary, but leaves open how fast degeneration proceeded.
- **Eye development:** In cave fishes, the eye forms normally in the embryo and degenerates later. Degeneration starts early in cavernicolous fishes and continues through life, so the endpoint varies among species. In [[Typhlotriton]] and [[Proteus]], larvae still have well-developed eyes, and regression begins only at metamorphosis.
- **Discovery:** [[Hubbs & Innes 1936]] described the first known blind characid, *Anoptichthys jordani*, from [[Cueva Chica]] in San Luis Potosí. They noted its close relationship to *Astyanax mexicanus*, which occurs near the cave in the Rio Tampaón. Later, [[Breder Jr]] and colleagues found similar forms in other caves that likely belonged to one former underground river system and are now isolated.
- **Nomenclature:** Kosswig's footnote notes that *A. mexicanus* appears synonymous with the widespread *A. fasciatus*, which ranges from Argentina to the Rio Grande. The cave populations are a strongly differentiated subspecies. Alvarez's splitting of the populations into separate species in *Anoptichthys* is criticised as unjustified, since the populations interbreed and produce fertile offspring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Cave populations:** Three sites were compared:
  - [[Cueva Chica]]: highly variable, with pigmented-blind and depigmented-eyed individuals and hybrid recombinants. Kosswig interprets this as a hybrid (F2-like) population resulting from occasional invasion by surface fish.
  - [[Cueva De Los Sabinos]] and [[Cueva Pachón]]: relatively uniform and showing typical troglobiont traits, namely blindness and loss of melanin.
- **Pigmentation:** The lack of melanin is environmentally stable and persists over generations in illuminated aquaria. Only guanine increases under light, and Sabinos fish turn silvery in captivity.
- **Behaviour:** Surface fish show positive light-avoidance (negative phototaxis), and the Pachón cave fish reverses this. The fish spawn preferentially in darkness, and cave-raised fish show no growth anomalies under permanent darkness, contrary to claims by Breder Jr.
- **Hydrology and drift:** Cave fish live in small pools or sinter basins. Slight changes in water supply could dry pools, eliminating subpopulations and shifting allele frequencies randomly. This may preserve some cave mutations by chance, as also proposed for [[Asellus Aquaticus]].
- **Dispersal:** Myers (1966) argues that primary freshwater ostariophysans entered Central America only in the late Tertiary or Pleistocene. Patterson & Pascual (1963), cited via Myers, place the Panama land bridge in the late Pliocene (possibly earliest Pleistocene). Kosswig estimates that *A. fasciatus* crossed roughly 3,000 km (air distance) in about 1 million years, moving by freshwater routes through the "filter bridge" of Central America.
- **Cave age:** Cave ages are unknown. Kosswig assumes they are Pleistocene, formed during wetter periods, with the underground river system spanning about 100 km before increasing aridity fragmented it.

## Key Findings

1. **Geologically young cave forms:** The cave characins of northern Mexico are young, because their surface ancestor *Astyanax fasciatus* arrived from South America only in the late Pliocene or early Pleistocene (~1 million years ago), according to Myers (1966) and related mammal evidence.
2. **Polygenic basis of adaptation:** Every difference between surface and cave fish is probably polygenic. Kosswig estimates that each of the 24 chromosomes of the haploid set carries at least one mutated locus separating cave from surface fish. Cave traits compared include eye size, retina structure, optic nerve, eye muscles, sclera, melanin, albinism, lack of alarm-substance response, taste bud number and body posture during feeding, and phototaxis reversal.
3. **Backcross evidence:** In the backcross of F2 hybrids to the cave fish from Pachón, none of several thousand offspring regained the F2 (sighted) condition. All showed the disoriented, blind behaviour of the cave ancestor. Backcrossing to the surface fish gave sighted, schooling offspring.
4. **Degenerative and constructive traits:** The cave fish show mainly degenerative traits. Kosswig reports an apparent gain in taste buds and a behavioural change in feeding posture, which he links to a separate gene system. He found no regression of the olfactory organ or the inner ear (Schemmel, unpublished).
5. **Chance and isolation:** Random drift in isolated pools, combined with a possible earlier hybridisation event in the Chica population, may explain the observed variation. Kosswig argues that the uniform blindness in Sabinos and Pachón reflects long isolation in separate pools.
6. **Rapid speciation in related fishes:** Comparisons with killifish (*Aphanius anatoliae*) suggest that reproductive isolation, including male sterility, can evolve quickly in postglacially isolated populations.
7. **Conclusion:** Kosswig's central answer is that the cave characins are young in geological terms, yet they evolved strongly degenerate traits alongside some adaptive ones. The speed of evolution he proposes is therefore fast, even though many genes are involved.
