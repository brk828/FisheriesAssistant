# Preliminary Observations on Populations of Brown Trout in Northern English Becks

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary survey by [[E. D. Le Cren]] describes populations of young and resident [[Salmo Trutta]] ([[Brown Trout]]) in small becks of northern England. The study focuses on [[Fry]] and [[Yearling]] trout, a life-history stage poorly documented in the literature at the time. Sampling used [[Electrofishing]] with mark-and-release methods across multiple becks over the year. Trout densities were roughly 1 to 4 fish per metre of beck, fry were concentrated in the smallest streams, and trout were largely sedentary outside spawning. Some becks functioned as [[Sea Trout]] nurseries with very few resident brown trout. The author concludes that small becks support dense, slow-growing resident populations or serve as nurseries for migratory trout. The work is explicitly preliminary, with survival, mortality and production analyses still planned.

## Background & Description

The biology of brown trout is well studied for adults, including growth, feeding, spawning and artificial hatching. Natural populations of young trout, however, were poorly documented. The aim of this programme was to quantify the population dynamics of fry and yearling trout in becks, including their numbers, age structure, growth, movements and spawning. The planned next steps were to estimate survival rates at successive ages, identify main mortality causes, and collect data for production calculations.

**Sampling method.** Sampling was done almost entirely with an electrofishing machine modified by [[Moore (1954)]] so that four operators could carry it on rucksack frames. Reaches of about 40 m could be sampled quickly, capturing all sizes of trout. Capture efficiency depended on water conductivity and flow. Under good conditions, up to 90% of yearling and older fish in a reach could be caught, but probably only about 50% of fry. The limiting factor was the ability to see and catch stunned fish, not the stunning itself. Most fish were marked by removing a fin (usually the adipose or a pelvic fin), measured by "pricking" their lengths onto waxed paper, and released alive. Repeated recaptures up to several months later suggested most fish survived handling unharmed. A few accidental deaths were used for stomach analysis.

**Study sites.** Most becks were 80 cm to 2 m wide, had stony bottoms, and flowed over hard rocks with low total ion content (<1 m equiv/l). [[Kingswell Beck]] in North Westmorland rises from springs in [[Carboniferous Limestone]]. Although similar in size, it had a total ion concentration of 3.5 m equiv/l and was included for comparison. Recorded water temperatures ranged from 4.0°C to 23.9°C, with colder conditions presumed in winter. All becks are subject to violent [[Flash Floods]]. During the exceptionally dry summer of 1955, some planned sites dried partly, but all sampled sites retained flow throughout the summer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population density.** Densities varied among becks, ranging from approximately 1 to 4 trout per metre of beck length (0.6 to 4 trout per square metre). The smallest becks (about 50 cm wide and 5 cm deep) held dense fry populations but few older fish. In larger becks, fry density was below 0.5 per square metre and fry were found mainly along the edges or in riffles less than 5 cm deep. Fish older than one year averaged roughly one per metre of beck across all becks. The largest fish were nearly always in the deepest, largest pools. Small rivers of 6 to 10 m width had sparse trout populations, possibly due to violent floods and unstable bottoms.

**Associated fauna.** The only other fish species recorded were [[European Eel]] (*Anguilla anguilla*) and [[European Bullhead]] (*Cottus gobio*). Both occurred in only some becks and at lower numbers than trout.

**Movement.** In contiguous 30 m reaches marked with distinct marks, trout largely stayed in their section of beck for several months. Occasional straying occurred, but apart from [[Spawning Migration]], there was no evidence of extensive movement.

**Spawning and migration (autumn 1955).** In [[Black Brows Beck]], a small tributary of [[Dale Park Beck]], only one spawning fish of probable local origin was caught. However, the beck was a spawning ground for about fifty [[Sea Trout]] up to 50 cm long, suggesting it is a nursery for sea trout with very few resident brown trout. In two other becks above falls impassable to upstream migrants, there was little or no spawning migration. Adults spawned where they lived or moved only a few metres into a small runnel. Very few fish over 20 cm were caught in these becks, and spawning occurred at a length of 12 cm and a weight of 20 g.

**Growth.** Scales had not yet been read, so growth was estimated from [[Length-Frequency Histograms]] for Black Brows Beck across six dates (6 August 1955 to 6 July 1956). The 0-group fish were distinct and grew to about 5.5 cm, with most growth before October. Length at the end of the second year was perhaps about 9.5 cm, but later growth could not be resolved from length data alone. Individual growth varied considerably, and small but significant differences existed among becks. In Kingswell Beck, length at the end of the first year was about 7 cm, and subsequent growth was probably faster than in the other becks.

## Key Findings

- **Density:** Trout numbered approximately 1 to 4 per metre of beck (0.6 to 4 per m²). Fry density was under 0.5/m² in larger becks.
- **Distribution by size:** Dense fry populations occurred in the smallest becks, while large fish were restricted to the deepest pools of larger becks.
- **Site stability:** Most trout remained within their marked reach for several months, with movement mainly tied to spawning.
- **Sea trout nurseries:** Black Brows Beck supported about fifty sea trout spawners and very few resident brown trout, consistent with use as a sea trout nursery.
- **Spawning size:** Fish spawned at about 12 cm and 20 g in becks without spawning migration. Few fish over 20 cm were caught.
- **Growth:** 0-group trout reached about 5.5 cm by autumn, about 9.5 cm by the end of year two (estimated), and about 7 cm at the end of year one in the limestone Kingswell Beck, where growth was probably faster.
- **Water chemistry:** Low-ion becks and the high-ion limestone Kingswell Beck differed in growth, though the study did not separate ion effects from other factors.
- **Sampling efficiency:** Electrofishing captured up to 90% of yearling and older trout but only about 50% of fry per pass.
- **Associated species:** European eel and European bullhead were present in some becks, in smaller numbers than trout.

**Discussion highlights (from participants):**
- [[Huet]] (Belgian Ardennes streams) reported similar findings: about one trout per square metre, rare fish above 20 cm, and sedentary fish outside spawning.
- [[Vivier]] (acid Vosges stream, five years of marking) found a very constant density of about 1 trout per 1.30 m of stream (OCR: "1 m, 30"), stunted growth, and sedentary populations. Recapture was a fraction printed as "*/s" in the scan.
- [[Larsen]] (Denmark) recorded up to 4 small trout per square metre in similar small waters, usually in rich agricultural districts.
- [[Frost]] suggested competition between resident brown trout and sea trout may explain the absence of adult residents in sea trout becks.
- [[Fasricius]] proposed transferring fry between resident-only and migratory-only becks. Le Cren had not yet done such experiments but considered them worthwhile.
- Le Cren noted no evidence yet that sexes differ in growth in the first two years, but cautioned against assuming equal growth after maturity.
- On electrofishing, Le Cren stated that stunning depends on the voltage gradient along the fish, and pulsed current allows higher voltage from a small power supply.

**Conclusion:** The author concludes that these small becks mostly support dense populations of slow-growing brown trout or serve as nurseries for migratory trout. Further study is needed on population dynamics and ecology.
