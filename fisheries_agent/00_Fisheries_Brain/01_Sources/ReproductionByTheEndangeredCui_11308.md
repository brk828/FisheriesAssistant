# Reproduction by the Endangered Cui-ui in the Lower Truckee River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by [[G. Gary Scoppettone]], [[Gary A. Wedemeyer]], [[Mark Coleman]], and [[Howard Burge]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) documents the spawning behavior, habitat use, and larval emigration of the endangered [[Cui-Ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), a catostomid endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. Adults were held in a controlled side channel of the lower [[Truckee River]] in spring 1981, allowing continuous observation of the reproductive cycle from spawning through larval emigration. The authors conclude that the river phase of the life cycle is brief, that spawning is largely nocturnal over gravel substrate, and that larvae move downstream to Pyramid Lake shortly after swim-up.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** The cui-ui is listed as endangered and is thought to be the sole surviving genetically pure member of its genus (Miller and Smith 1981).
- **Historical run:** Cui-uis once spawned in the lower 75 km of the Truckee River, which flows 190 km from [[Lake Tahoe]] to Pyramid Lake. The run began in April and peaked about May 1, with greatest spawning activity in riffles at night.
- **Habitat decline:** Water-project diversions since the turn of the century reduced river flows and lowered Pyramid Lake by approximately 25 m, creating a large shallow delta at the river mouth. Since the 1920s, spawners have reached the river only in infrequent high-flow years.
- **Lake reproduction:** Hatching success in Pyramid Lake water was poor because of high total dissolved solids (Chatto 1979), so lake reproduction likely occurs only near freshwater springs.
- **Recovery measures:** A fishway built in 1976 about 3 km east of the river mouth serves as a delta bypass for cui-ui and [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]; adults are collected there and released to spawn above the delta. Stored water from [[Stampede Reservoir]] is also used to supplement flows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study channel.** The site was a natural braid about 25 km upstream of Pyramid Lake, 213 m long and 8 m mean width, with depths of 0.03–1.5 m. The pool-to-riffle ratio was 1:0.5, and about 20% of the channel was lined with one-year-old [[Fremont Cottonwood]]. Aquatic vegetation was abundant in slow-water areas. The dominant fish was the [[Lahontan Redside]] (*Richardsonius egregius*); other species present included green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*), mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*), mountain sucker (*Catostomus platyrhynchus*), and Tahoe sucker (*Catostomus tahoensis*).

**Flow control.** A sandbag dam and a series of six flumes regulated flow at 0.4 m³/s from stocking until the last eggs hatched (May 10–June 5, 1981). Flow was reduced to 0.01 m³/s before peak larval emigration to avoid impingement in downstream plankton nets.

**Spawning habitat conditions.**
- Temperature: 12–17 °C
- Depth: 9–43 cm
- Velocity: 23–87 cm/s
- Mean substrate composition across three spawning areas: 13.7% rubble and cobble, 64.5% gravel, 14.2% sand, 7.6% silt

Gravel was the preferred spawning substrate, similar to preferences reported for [[Longnose Sucker]] and [[Spotted Sucker]].

**Predation.** Lahontan redsides aggressively preyed on eggs during diurnal spawning acts but not during nocturnal spawning. Eggs were buried to about 10 cm in the substrate.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking and sample:** Of 46 adults released, 8 were radio-tagged with external transmitters (6 g). Group I comprised 3 males and 4 females (captured May 13–14); Group II comprised 15 males and 24 females (captured May 25–26). Tags did not appear to affect spawning behavior.
- **Spawning clusters:** Clusters contained two to seven fish, most often one female flanked by two males. Males positioned their caudal fins posterior to the female's, and each spawning act lasted 3–6 seconds, with the group vibrating vigorously. The authors suggest this creates an eddy current that mixes gametes and limits downstream loss.
- **Spawning frequency:** Among Group II, 21 of 25 females and 13 of 15 males were active, producing at least 1,258 spawning acts. Peak activity was 60 acts in one hour at 2300 hours on May 28.
- **Individual activity:** The most active tagged male spawned 294 times and the most active tagged female 114 times. Males spawned over 4–5 days and females over 2.5–4 days.
- **Timing:** Spawning was predominantly nocturnal (2000–0600 hours), though some diurnal spawning occurred in Group II. Fish sought cover by day in a 1.7 m deep log-jam pool.
- **Egg deposition:** Females moved between broadcast areas, distributing eggs over an area of up to 50 m². No aggression among spawners was observed, and females spawned with different males.
- **Sex ratio:** The male-to-female ratio in the channel was 1:1.6. Several clusters contained more than one female, contrary to earlier reports of one female per cluster (Snyder 1918; Koch 1973).
- **Hatching:** The first hatch was observed June 2, seven days after spawning began. Sampling yielded 208 eggs and two larvae at three stations, followed by 26 and 14 yolk-sac larvae on June 7 and 10.
- **Emigration:** Emergence was first noted 13 days after the first spawning act. Peak emigration occurred June 11, 14 days after peak spawning, and the last larvae left June 16. Larvae moved almost exclusively at night, and about 20,000 progeny were estimated to have emigrated downstream.
- **Summary conclusion:** The river phase of the life history is extremely brief, and external radio-tags did not affect spawning behavior. Larvae apparently emigrate to Pyramid Lake immediately after swim-up.
