# Comparative Study of Two Fish-Sampling Methods in a Small Kentucky Impoundment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 fisheries study by John F. Hall, conducted for the Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources, compared two fish-sampling approaches in [[Old Kingfisher Lake]], a 14-acre, shallow, clear impoundment in western Kentucky. The methods were multi-panel netting (gill, trammel, wing and hoop nets) and [[Spot Poisoning]] of a one-acre cove with [[Rotenone]]. A subsequent [[Complete Eradication]] of the whole lake served as the benchmark. Netting suggested a population dominated by [[Golden Shiner]] and [[Black Bullhead]], spot poisoning pointed to stunted [[Bluegill]], and complete eradication confirmed that bluegill dominated the lake. The author concluded that spot poisoning gave a more accurate picture than netting, but that it is selective and should be supplemented by other evidence.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** In December 1952 the Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources bought two small impoundments as part of a lake improvement and management program. This study addressed one of them.
- **Study lake:** Old Kingfisher Lake was built around 1900. Maximum depth was 10 feet, average depth 5.8 feet, and surface area 14 acres. Bottom sediments were sand, mud and gravel. The drainage area of about 50 acres included second-growth timber, meadowland and cultivated fields.
- **Water and vegetation:** The water was very clear. Until June 1953 the lake supported a luxuriant pondweed flora, mainly [[Coontail]] (*Ceratophyllum demersum*).
- **Stocking history:** The lake had been stocked sporadically with fish seined from the nearby [[Ohio River]]. Fishing had declined steadily, with only a few [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]] and small bluegill caught each year.
- **Objectives:** Compare the reliability of netting and spot poisoning, using total eradication as a check on both.
- **Timeline:** Investigation ran from March 1 to July 15, 1953. Netting began March 11 and nets were moved every four days.
- **Publication:** Transactions of the Kentucky Academy of Science, 17 (3-4): 140-147 (1956). The work was based on a Master of Science thesis from the University of Louisville.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Netting gear:** Seven nets of four types were fished for 181 net days. Gear included a 2⅞-inch [[Gill Net]] (210 ft), a 100 ft [[Trammel Net]], a 30 ft small trammel, a [[Wing Net]] with two throats and 30 ft wings, two small [[Hoop Net]]s, and a 1¼-inch gill net. Summary text gives 131 net days, which conflicts with the 181 in Table I and the total-net-days line.
- **Spot poisoning:** On May 20 a one-acre cove was treated with powdered derris root (5% rotenone) at 1–2 ppm. Fish were collected for two days.
- **Complete eradication:** The whole lake was treated on June 4 with derris root at 1.8 ppm and fish were collected for five days. Weeds were then killed with about 300 gallons of liquid [[Sodium Arsenite]] (June 19–20). Arsenic trioxide in the water fell from 5.5 ppm at day 3 to 0.8 ppm at day 20. A second rotenone treatment followed once the weeds had disappeared.
- **Factors limiting efficiency:** Dense coontail prevented a stop net across the poisoned cove, so fish could escape. Water clarity and weed cover made netting unproductive. Turtles were present daily in the hoop nets and likely consumed some catch, and they also scavenged sinking fish during the final kill.
- **Rotenone limits:** Rotenone is effective only in warm water, so species in cooler water at the time of application may survive.

## Key Findings

**Netting results (181 net days)**
- Total catch: 247 fish weighing 72.8 lb.
- [[Golden Shiner]] and [[Black Bullhead]] made up 82.6% of the number and 66.8% of the weight.
- Netting was very unproductive because of clear water and weed growth. Hoop nets baited with soybean meal failed to catch [[Common Carp]] or buffalo.

**Spot poisoning results (one-acre cove)**
- Total catch: 4,296 fish weighing 156.4 lb (Table IV lists 4,204 fish, a discrepancy).
- Three- and four-inch [[Bluegill]] made up 74.4% of the number and 65.5% of the weight, so the author judged the population to be chiefly stunted bluegill.
- Other species included [[Warmouth]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]] and Kentucky Bass.
- A large [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] was gigged but escaped into deep water. It was later recovered during the full eradication.

**Complete eradication results (both poisonings)**
- Total catch: 74,739 fish weighing 4,208.8 lb (Table IV lists 74,730; discrepancy noted).
- [[Bluegill]] made up 90.2% of the number and 51.4% of the weight. Nearly all were 3 to 5 inches long.
- Buffalo and carp totalled 1,179.5 lb, or 28.0% of the weight.
- Other species recovered included [[Warmouth]] (5,792 fish), [[Black Crappie]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Chubsucker]], [[Northern Pike]] and [[Mud Pickerel]].
- Dead [[Common Carp]] were seen two days after the first treatment.

**Recovery estimate**
- Earlier mark-recovery studies gave: Ball (1948) recovered 58.9% of marked bluegill and 44.0% of marked trout; Krumholz (1950) recovered 91.0% of green sunfish and 87.1% of largemouth bass; Fredin (1950) recovered 86–90% in Iowa ponds; Jenkins (1951) estimated about 80% in an Oklahoma lake.
- Given dense weeds and scavenging turtles, recovery in Old Kingfisher Lake was estimated at about 65%.

**Method comparison**
- Spot poisoning missed golden shiners, chubsuckers, largemouth buffalo, pickerel, carp, smallmouth buffalo and northern pike. These may have been in deeper water or moved out of the cove without a stop net.
- Netting caught more golden shiners and pickerel than the complete kill. The author attributed this to netting injuries causing deaths before the final poisoning, and to gill nets being selective for these species.
- Netting suggested a population of golden shiners and chubsuckers. Spot poisoning and complete eradication both showed stunted bluegill were dominant.
- Carter (1954) found spot poisoning selective toward shad in [[Kentucky Lake]], under-representing white crappie compared with netting.

**Conclusions**
- Spot poisoning gave a more nearly accurate picture of the fish present than netting, judged against complete eradication.
- The author considered spot poisoning probably the best sampling method available, but not reliable alone. Annual spot poisoning is useful for tracking year-to-year fluctuations because it indexes young-of-the-year fish in shallow water.
- Neither method detected all species present. Adding a stop net would likely have improved spot poisoning results.
