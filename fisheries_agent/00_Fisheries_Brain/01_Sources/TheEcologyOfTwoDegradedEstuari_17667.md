# Estuarine Food Webs and Degraded Estuaries on the South African Coast

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mugilidae

## Executive Summary

The document combines two 1984 papers from the *South African Journal of Zoology* (vol. 19, no. 3). The first fragment (p. 223) discusses prey selection and energy pathways in eastern Cape estuaries, drawing on stomach content data. The second, and larger, paper by Blaber, Hay, Cyrus and Martin studies the biota of two degraded Natal estuaries, [[Tongati Estuary]] and [[Mdloti Estuary]], over one year (December 1980 to November 1981). It compares them with the relatively natural [[Mhlanga Estuary]].

The central finding is that both degraded estuaries have impoverished zooplankton and zoobenthos food chains. Their fish faunas are dominated by iliophagous Mugilidae and [[Sarotherodon Mossambicus]]. The authors conclude that the [[Benthic Floc]] to [[Iliophagous Feeding]] chain remains viable under the stresses present.

## Background & Description

**Eastern Cape food webs (p. 223).** The study examined the diets of estuarine predators, including [[Argyrosomus Hololepidotus]] (kob), [[Lichia Amia]] (leervis), *Pomatomus saltatrix*, and non-piscivorous carnivores such as *Tachysurus feliceps*, *Monodactylus falciformis* and *Pomadasys commersonnii*. Kob consumed a total of 33 identifiable prey species, and prey composition varied strongly between estuaries. Mullet and [[Gilchristella Aestuarius]] (mean mass 0.8 to 0.9 g) formed key prey. Larger mullet were found in the upper, muddy, more freshwater reaches of the Gamtoos and Sundays estuaries.

**Natal degraded estuaries.** The authors note that many small Natal estuaries have been affected by human activity (Begg 1978). The 1980 study was funded by the World Wildlife Fund through the South African Nature Foundation, as part of its "Seas Must Live" campaign. Begg (1978) rated the environmental condition of Mhlanga as good, Mdloti as fair, and Tongati as poor.

- **Tongati:** receives treated sewage works effluent and industrial effluent from sugar and textile mills. It is rarely closed to the sea, and its mouth is often opened artificially.
- **Mdloti:** occasionally affected by upper-catchment industrial pollution. The surrounding land is mainly intensive agriculture. The mouth is also artificially opened to prevent cane flooding, and the estuary was closed from late May to late August 1981.
- **Mhlanga:** minimal human interference, closed for most of the year, and used as the baseline.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions.** Tongati had low but fluctuating salinities and low oxygen. Bottom waters in the upper reaches had oxygen saturations below 25% for four months, and values below 5% were recorded in July and August 1981. Mdloti was well oxygenated, with surface oxygen rarely below 50% saturation, and showed typical estuarine salinity patterns. Water temperatures ranged from 13 °C in winter to 27 °C in summer. Turbidities in both estuaries were above 10 NTU even at low flow, compared with usually less than 2 NTU in the sea.

**Nutrients.** Tongati was rich in nitrogen, mostly as ammonia, probably from treated sewage, with adequate [[Soluble Reactive Phosphorus]] (SRP). This supported rapid growth of [[Eichornia Crassipes]] (water hyacinth), which in turn raised biological oxygen demand. Mdloti had low SRP, which probably limited primary production, and high nitrates, probably from agricultural fertiliser.

**Substrata.** Tongati's lower reaches were medium/coarse sand, and its middle and upper reaches were medium/fine and fine sand. Mdloti was predominantly medium sand. Both estuaries lacked the silt found in the upper reaches of Mhlanga, which the authors attribute to flushing from frequent mouth openings.

**Pollution.** No heavy metal levels in Mugilidae from Mdloti were of concern. Dieldrin was present in fish at a mean of 49.2 µg/kg, with a maximum of 187 µg/kg. Mugilidae were judged suitable indicators because of their high fat content and detritus-based diet. Coliform tests were negative for *E. coli*.

**Zooplankton and zoobenthos.** Both estuaries had impoverished zooplankton, dominated by larval forms of benthic animals such as chironomid larvae and [[Prionospio]]. Calanoid copepod [[Pseudodiaptomus Hessei]] was important, particularly in Mdloti during closed phases. Mdloti had higher benthic diversity but a very low standing crop. Tongati's benthos peaks were almost entirely due to *Prionospio* sp.

**Fauna of water hyacinth.** Roots of *Eichornia* in Tongati hosted a diverse insect and crustacean fauna. The crab *Varuna litterata* contributed over 50% of the energy in this fauna from March to September. Standing crops of this fauna were as high as or higher than the zoobenthos in most months. Phragmites reed swamps in both Tongati and Mdloti lacked the epifauna found at Mhlanga.

**Fish and birds.** Tongati had 32 recorded fish species: six freshwater, one truly estuarine, and the rest euryhaline marine. Mdloti had 29 species. Mugilidae (*Mugil cephalus*, *Myxus capensis*, *Valamugil cunnesius*) dominated both systems. Few piscivores were present. Bird numbers were low overall, with herbivorous ducks associated with the water hyacinth at Tongati.

## Key Findings

**Food web structure (eastern Cape).**
- Prey selection by predators varied with abundance, type and size of prey, and between estuaries.
- Energy flow occurs through a small number of pathways, each carrying high energy, with a large input from detritus (after Barnes 1974; Whitfield 1980).
- In the Eastern Cape system, group A species (most piscivores) draw energy via detritus and phytoplankton to mullet and via detritus and Copepoda to *G. aestuarius*. Group B species (mostly benthivores) draw energy via detritus and *U. africana*.

**Degraded Natal estuaries.**
- Benthic floc energy in Tongati was more than 10 times greater than in Mdloti, except in the lower reaches. Tongati upper reach mean was 1832 J m⁻² (S.E. 588), and Mdloti upper reach mean was 66 J m⁻² (S.E. 27).
- Zooplankton standing crops were low in both estuaries compared with Mhlanga. Mdloti's bottom and benthic zooplankton (mean 2897 J m⁻²) exceeded Tongati's (mean 693 J m⁻²), but Tongati's surface values (mean 190 J m⁻²) exceeded Mdloti's (mean 77 J m⁻²).
- Zoobenthos was impoverished in Tongati, with peaks due to *Prionospio* sp. In Mdloti, the standing crop was very low despite higher diversity. The authors suggest the cause may be low energy input or pesticide pollution (dieldrin).
- Water hyacinth in Tongati supported high standing crops of associated fauna, which provided an extra energy input to the food web.
- Fish faunas of both estuaries were impoverished and dominated by iliophagous species ([[Mugilidae]] and *S. mossambicus*). Piscivores and benthic-feeding species were few. Fish diets relied on insect larvae (Tongati) and planktonic and pelagic prey (Mdloti), not on *Prionospio*.
- Unlike Mhlanga, where benthic floc to iliophagous fish accounted for about 90% of energy flow, the zooplankton and zoobenthos chains in Tongati and Mdloti were impoverished. The floc-to-iliophagous chain remained intact.

**Conclusions.** The authors argue that, because zooplankton and zoobenthos are more sensitive to environmental disturbance than benthic floc and micro-organisms, these small degraded estuaries remain viable through the iliophagous food chain under a range of stresses. They describe the zoobenthic peaks as signs of instability in an artificially simplified community.
