# Malacca Tilapia Hybrids: Interspecific Crossing for All-Male Broods

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

C. F. Hickling, of the [[Fish Culture Research Station]] in [[Malacca]], reports a method for producing almost all-male [[Tilapia Mossambica]] fingerlings by crossing the acclimatised [[Malayan Tilapia]] with an unnamed African Tilapia from [[Zanzibar]]. The cross was made to avoid the waste of females in [[Mono-Sex Culture]] and to avoid the stunting caused by uncontrolled breeding in ponds. When an African male was mated with a Malayan female, the offspring were all males or nearly so. The reciprocal cross gave roughly a 3:1 male-to-female ratio. The author argues that the all-male result comes from a genetic sex-determination mechanism, not a lethal factor, and compares it with the [[Platyfish]] system described by [[Gordon]]. The hybrids also grew fast enough to reach about one pound in six months under minimal management.

## Background & Description

*Tilapia mossambica* Peters is a cichlid native to eastward-flowing rivers of East Africa, from Somaliland to Natal, and widely distributed in the lower [[Zambezi River]]. The closely allied *T. shirana* occurs in [[Lake Nyasa]]. The species is now treated as a super-species or group of closely related forms, three of which were distinguished by [[Lowe-McConnell]] (1955). An unnamed member of the Mossambica group is cultivated experimentally in brackish-water ponds on Zanzibar Island.

The species reached the Far East by an unexplained route. [[Schuster]] (1952) reports five specimens found in East Java in July 1939. The fish spread through Java and reached Malaya during the Japanese occupation. The Malacca stock descends from these five specimens, with no later recorded introductions.

Hickling describes the Tilapias as excellent table fish, hardy, disease-resistant, and easy to breed. They feed mainly on plankton and epiphytes but can use a wide range of food, including [[Chironomid Larvae]], [[Rotifers]], and [[Copepods]]. Pure-bred *T. mossambica* tolerates pH from about 3.7 to 10.5 (as printed, "3-7 to 10-5") and salinities up to about 4% (per Schuster). It survives long periods of poor oxygen supply. Sex can be determined at small size by examining the [[Genital Papilla]] (Vaas and Hofstede, 1952). Some females without a patent oviduct are misclassed as males, but the error rate is described as low.

The main problem with these fish in ponds is their fecundity and early maturity. A stocked pond fills with offspring that compete for food, so a 4 oz (100 g) fish is about the largest usually obtained. Mono-sex stocking and predator-controlled stocking have been tried. Both have limits: sexing is tedious and not fully reliable, and single-sex stocking wastes females. The author therefore attempted to produce sterile "mules" by crossing the Malayan and African stocks.

Malayan fish are mottled grey with dark vertical bars and yellow chin and throat. Breeding males are black with a livid yellow chin and crimson fin edges. African fish are more olive-green, with a longitudinal dark stripe. Their nests have a less raised rim and lack the "finger-marks" typical of Malayan nests. The hybrids resemble the African parent in male breeding dress and nest structure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Crosses between the two stocks were as easy as pure-bred matings. The first four successful broods (I to IV) were made in cement tanks. Later crosses used fishponds of two sizes: H ponds (1/10 acre, about 4 ares) and J ponds (1/100 acre, about 0.4 are). Breeding ponds were G ponds (1/4 acre, about 0.1 hectare), stocked with five males and five females, and some H ponds stocked with two males and two females.

Brood size depended on pond size. The author suggests the 1/10 acre ponds gave enough room for full broods, whereas the 1/100 acre ponds did not. Brood size also rose with female length, though the largest females sometimes produced fewer young. Broods from the Malayan male × African female cross were smaller than those from the African male × Malayan female cross.

Two earlier reports of hybridization are cited. [[Lowe-McConnell]] (1958) suspected natural hybrids of Tilapia in [[Uganda]]'s Lakes Bunyoni and Nkugute, both caused by introductions of new species. [[Gordon]] (1957) quotes a New York pond where three [[Lepomis]] sunfish species produced about 11% hybrids under crowding and competition for nest sites.

## Key Findings

**Sex ratio in hybrid broods (Table 1, single couples):**
- African male × Malayan female: Broods II and III, and several ponds, gave 100% males. Brood I was the exception with a normal ratio, which the author could not explain.
- Malayan male × African female: about 75% males in most broods, except pond H 9, whose two broods were near 50:50.
- Chi-square analysis: χ² = 66.10 for all broods from the cross. Excluding the two H 9 broods, χ² = 7.14, a good fit to the expected 75% males. The two H 9 broods fit 50% males with χ² = 0.452.

**Breeding ponds (Table 2):** African male × Malayan female gave all-male or near-all-male offspring, with the lowest male percentage at 96% (for example, pond H 18: 165 males, 0 females; pond G 17 bis: 612 young, 98.4% male). The reverse cross gave 70–80% males (for example, pond G 6: 173 males, 27 females, 86.5%).

**Pure-bred controls (Table 3):** Sex ratios were generally near 1:1. Ponds H 20 and J 44 showed significant male excesses, with deviations from the 75% expectation reported as χ² = 3.85 and 7.58.

**Fecundity:** A Malayan female in pond H 28 produced three broods in three months: 1,182 young (about 6 cm, all male), 1,025 young (about 3 cm, too small to sex), and a third brood too small to count. Fecundity did not seem lower in the all-male cross, so the author rejects a lethal-factor explanation.

**Back-crosses:** Sex ratios returned to near normal when F₁ hybrids were crossed back to the parent stocks (Table 5). Ratios were around 46–61% male across the pairings listed.

**Genetic interpretation:** Following Hubbs (1955) and [[Stebbins]] (1958), hybrid males are often sterile, but the author notes that the all-male broods showed no sign of sterility or intersexuality. The author suggests a mechanism like that of the Platyfish, where the X/Y and W/Y systems give a male-determining Y chromosome. In the Tilapia case, the Malayan males may be heterogametic while the African females may be heterogametic. Hubbs also describes the all-female [[Amazon Molly]], a case of matroclinous inheritance.

**Practical outcomes:**
- All-male fingerlings can be stocked directly into rearing ponds without sexing, and no female fingerlings are discarded.
- Hybrid all-male broods grew to about one pound in eight months. In a six-month trial, fish reached marketable weight at about 750 lb per acre per year (850 kg/ha). This was achieved with only about 20 lb per acre of P₂O₅ applied as [[Triple Superphosphate]] to limed ponds. The author states that pure-bred fish breeding freely would not have produced this result.

**Conclusions (Summary):** The African male × Malayan female cross produces nearly all-male offspring. The reverse cross gives approximately Mendelian ratios. The all-male broods are not caused by a lethal factor but by a genetic sex-determination mechanism, probably similar to that of the Platyfish.
