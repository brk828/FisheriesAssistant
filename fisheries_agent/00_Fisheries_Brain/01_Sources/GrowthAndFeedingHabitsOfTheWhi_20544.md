# Growth and Feeding Habits of the Whiting or Silver Hake in the New York Bight

#ichthyology #marine-biology #merluccius

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Richard H. Schaefer]] of the [[New York State Conservation Department]], published in the [[New York Fish and Game Journal]] (1960, Vol. 7, No. 2), examines the growth, length-weight relationships, and feeding habits of the [[Silver Hake]] (*[[Merluccius Bilinearis]]*) in the [[New York Bight]]. Derived from a Master's thesis at [[Rutgers University]], the study sampled 1,931 whiting from December 1957 through May 1958 at [[Long Branch Pier]] (inshore, sport fishery) and the [[Mud Hole]] area (offshore, trawl fishery), plus commercial landings at Belford and Point Pleasant, New Jersey.

Growth differed significantly between sexes, but not between locations or seasons. Length-weight relationships were highly significant for every equation. Principal prey were amphipods, [[Sand Shrimp]] (*[[Crago Septemspinosus]]*), [[Glut Herring]], and [[Silversides]]. Offshore whiting also preyed heavily on smaller whiting, a feeding behavior the author suggests could affect local population size.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The whiting or silver hake (*Merluccius bilinearis*) occurs in the New York Bight from November through May and supports important commercial and sport fisheries.
- **Fishery importance:** The commercial catch of whiting for the Middle Atlantic states (New York, New Jersey, Delaware) exceeded 13 million pounds in a single year. [[Table 1]] reports catches from 1897 to 1957, ranging from 13,000 pounds (earliest listed year) to 13,813,000 pounds.
- **Knowledge gap:** The author notes that little was known about the species' growth rate, feeding habits, movements, and migrations.
- **Prior work cited:**
  - [[Hickling]] (1927, 1933): seasonal distribution, feeding, growth, and age determination in the European hake (*Merluccius merluccius*) in the British Isles.
  - [[Bigelow and Schroeder]] (1953): breeding and feeding in the Gulf of Maine; suggested sexual dimorphism in growth.
  - [[Ginsburg]] (1954): morphometric studies of whitings on the American coasts.
  - [[Clark]] (1954): preliminary study of whiting escapement through trawl meshes.
- **Aims:** To study growth and feeding habits of whiting in the New York Bight.

### Sampling Methods

- **Inshore (Long Branch Pier):** Night collecting trips at about one-week intervals, December 1957 and January 1958. A random portion of each preselected fisherman's or group's catch was sampled.
- **Offshore (Mud Hole area):** Samples bought directly from trawlers at Belford and Point Pleasant, New Jersey, February 1 to May 31, 1958. Otoliths were preserved from offshore fish in February and March.
- **Measurements:** Total length to the nearest half-centimeter; sex determined from gonads (immature fish assigned sex at random); weights taken on an [[Ohaus Scale]] (1,100-gram capacity) where possible.
- **Otoliths:** Stored in 3 per cent [[Tri-Sodium Phosphate]] with the bactericide "Slimodyn" (a colloidal silver salt). Age was read as translucent rings using a 3X hand lens or 6X binocular microscope, following a modification of [[Hickling]] (1933).
- **Stomachs:** Preserved in 10 per cent [[Formalin]]; contents identified and volumes measured by water displacement in graduated cylinders. Frequency of occurrence and total volume were calculated for each food item.
- **Statistics:** Growth curves fitted with [[Gompertz Curve]] regression (after [[Mills]], 1956); length-weight relationships fitted with a power-function regression; growth differences tested by [[Analysis of Variance]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Seasonal movement:** During winter and early spring, a commercial trawl fishery operates offshore between Scotland Lightship and the Mud Hole. In spring, whiting move inshore and are taken by pound nets along Long Island and New Jersey. Sport fishing occurs offshore by "open" or "head" boats in winter daylight, and inshore at Long Branch Pier at night.
- **Prey base:** Inshore diet is dominated by amphipods, sand shrimp, glut herring, and silversides. Offshore diet is dominated by sand shrimp, opossum shrimp (Mysidacea), squid (*[[Loligo Pealii]]*), and whiting themselves.
- **Predator-prey notes:** Post-larval [[Summer Flounder]] (*[[Paralichthys Dentatus]]*) was found in a whiting stomach, suggesting this size class was present inshore in December, which was not previously expected.
- **Unexplained shad occurrence:** Seven whiting stomachs from the Mud Hole area (March 14 to May 2, 1958) contained [[Shad]] (*[[Alosa Sapidissima]]*), apparently young-of-year or yearling fish. The author notes their whereabouts in late winter and spring are not established.
- **Parasitism:** Every whiting examined had plerocercoid (larval) tapeworms in the viscera, identified by [[Ross F. Nigrelli]] of the New York Aquarium as belonging to Tetrarhyncha or Trypanorhyncha, similar to *[[Lacistorhynchus Tenuis]]*, a larval stage found widely in North Atlantic teleosts. Effects on the population are unknown.

## Key Findings

### Sample Composition
- Total whiting measured: **1,931** (772 males, 1,159 females).
- Weights recorded for **455** fish.
- Otoliths examined from **287** specimens; stomachs from **338** (137 from Long Branch Pier, 201 from the Mud Hole area).

### Growth
- Growth rates differed highly significantly between sexes.
- No evidence that growth of either sex varied with time or location.
- Females from the Mud Hole area and Long Branch Pier did not differ significantly in growth. The author attributes the divergence of the offshore curves to a few large fish from older age classes.
- Gompertz regression yielded separate equations for males and females in each area. Coefficients for the inshore and February–March offshore equations are partly garbled in the source scan, so they are not reproduced here.
- Correlation coefficients (r) for the length-weight regressions ranged from **0.97749 to 0.99355**. Each regression coefficient was significant at the **1 per cent** level.

### Length-Weight Relationships
- The power-function relationship was fitted separately by sex, area, and period. Reported equations (log form, X1 = weight, X2 = length):
  - Inshore (Long Branch Pier), Dec 1957–Jan 1958: males log X1 = −7.6032 + 3.1275 log X2; females log X1 = −8.2799 + 3.2830 log X2.
  - Offshore (Mud Hole), Apr–May 1958: males log X1 = −7.4491 + 3.0855 log X2; females log X1 = −6.9329 + 2.9772 log X2.
- Differences between regression lines were judged too small (less than one standard error of any regression coefficient) to require formal testing.

### Feeding: Long Branch Pier (inshore, n = 137)
- 43 stomachs (**31.39 per cent**) were empty.
- Invertebrates: 60 stomachs (**43.80 per cent** frequency).
- Amphipoda: 40 stomachs (**29.20 per cent**).
- Sand shrimp (*Crago septemspinosus*): 29 stomachs (**21.17 per cent**).
- Glut herring (*Pomolobus aestivalis*): 20 stomachs (**14.60 per cent**).
- Silversides (*Menidia menidia*): 12 stomachs (**8.76 per cent**).
- Mysidacea: 12 stomachs (**8.76 per cent**).

### Feeding: Mud Hole Area (offshore, n = 201)
- 39 stomachs (**19.40 per cent**) were empty.
- Sand shrimp present in 77 stomachs (**38.31 per cent**); the table reports a volume of 491.25 mL.
- Mysidacea present in 22 stomachs according to the text (the table gives a different figure).
- Squid (*Loligo pealii*): 8 stomachs (**3.98 per cent**), 298.30 mL.
- Whiting (*Merluccius bilinearis*): found in **48** stomachs (**23.88 per cent**), displacing an aggregate volume of over **1,000 milliliters**.
- Other fish: glut herring (13 stomachs), [[Red Hake]] (*Phycis chuss*, 13), striped anchovy (9), shad (7), [[Harvestfish]] (*Peprilus triacanthus*, 6), sand lance (5), and others.

### Cannibalism
- A female whiting 113 half-centimeters long had consumed a whiting 53 half-centimeters long, nearly half its own length.
- Small whiting found in stomachs contained opossum shrimp "in rather large numbers."
- The author suggests such predation could influence population size in a given year, especially if other food is scarce.

### Notable Records
- Post-larval summer flounder (*Paralichthys dentatus*) found in a male whiting taken at Long Branch Pier on December 30, 1957.
- Shad (*Alosa sapidissima*), apparently zero-age or yearling, in seven Mud Hole stomachs, March 14 to May 2, 1958.

### Parasitism
- All examined whiting carried larval tapeworms (plerocercoids) in the viscera.
- Identification by [[Ross F. Nigrelli]]: Tetrarhyncha/Trypanorhyncha, similar to *Lacistorhynchus tenuis*.

### Conclusions
- Whiting are sexually dimorphic in growth.
- Growth does not vary significantly with time or location (apart from the offshore size divergence noted above).
- Whiting are opportunistic feeders on crustaceans and small fishes, and they also prey on conspecifics offshore.
- The author recommends further study of population-level effects of cannibalism and parasitism; these remain unresolved.
