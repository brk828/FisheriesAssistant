# Notes on Diseases, Parasites, Food and Condition of Green River Fishes, 1959

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is Appendix B of the University of Utah Anthropological Papers No. 48 (November 1960, Upper Colorado Series No. 3), *Ecological Studies of the Flora and Fauna of [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] Basin, Utah and Wyoming*. Written by fisheries biologist Phil Dotson of the [[Utah State Fish and Game Department]], it reports on fish diseases, parasites, stomach contents and condition factors for fish collected from the [[Green River]] in 1959. Suckers showed the most disease, and fish near the town of Green River, Wyoming, were more often affected than those further downstream.

## Background & Description

The appendix is one part of a multi-author ecological study of the reservoir basin, which is part of the [[Upper Colorado River]] series. Collections were made in mid-summer 1959, with Carter Creek sampled in August and the Green River in July and August. The fish examined included:

- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus delphinus*)
- [[Bonytail Chub]]
- [[Humpback Sucker]]
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]
- [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and whitefish (Carter Creek)
- Carp

The author notes that disease and parasites are common but often overlooked. Many parasites are very small and need a microscope to see. Weakened fish are easy prey for other animals, and others sink to the stream bottom and decompose unseen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Disease and water quality.** Suckers were more susceptible to disease and parasites than other species. The author suggests this could reflect density-dependent factors, or domestic and industrial pollution entering the river near Green River, Wyoming. Diseased fish were more prevalent about seven miles below the town than farther downstream. External parasites were widely spread throughout the river and tributaries.

**Feeding.** Stomach analysis showed that the [[Bonytail Chub]] is an opportunistic feeder that will eat almost any food. Most of the stomach contents were aquatic and terrestrial insects, with ants the most numerous terrestrial items. Seeds, woody plant fragments and organic debris were also found. Some larger stomachs held one small minnow each, which the author says suggests a possible fish component in the diet but does not prove the species is carnivorous.

**Condition.** Fish condition was assessed with a [[Condition Factor]] (K) calculated from weight and standard length.

## Key Findings

### Diseases
- **Bacterial ulcer:** Open, reddened lesions were common on [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]]. Some fish had dark spots that proved to be decomposed flesh, and some had both spots and open lesions. Lesions started at the surface and rarely reached the muscle. Fins, especially the caudal fin, were infected and partly destroyed. The disease was not found in other species.
- **Prevalence:** Diseased fish were more numerous near Green River, Wyoming than downstream.

### Parasites
- **Leeches (Hirudinea):** Found only on the caudal and dorsal fins of trout and other fish.
- **Tapeweem larva:** A large post-plerocercoid larva protruded from the anus of a bluehead mountain sucker after preservation. Meyer (1954) notes that infected fish often have distended bellies, sometimes packed with worms. Plehn (1924) reports that the parasite's weight may equal that of its host.

### Food Habits
- **Sample:** Stomachs from 17 bonytail chubs from the Green River, 1959.
- **Empty stomachs:** Three of the 17 stomachs were empty; the larger stomachs each held one small minnow.
- **Diet:** Ants were the most numerous terrestrial insects. Other insect orders included Hymenoptera, Diptera, Ephemeroptera and Coleoptera. Seeds, woody plant fragments and organic material were also present.
- **Stomach table:** Table 4 gives percentage composition by item for these fish. The OCR text is too degraded to reproduce exact values reliably.

### Condition Factor
- **Method:** Condition factor K was calculated from weight and standard length using a standard formula with a constant. The exact scaling constant is unclear in the scanned text.
- **Results:** For most species, K stayed relatively constant across growth stages. The exception was [[Cutthroat Trout]], where growth was variable. Most cutthroat trout were subnormal in condition, but two were above normal.
- **Sample sizes (from Table 5):** 
  - Brown trout: 2 fish from Carter Creek (August 1959)
  - Whitefish: 5 fish from Carter Creek (August 1959)
  - Cutthroat trout: 10 fish from Carter Creek (August 1959)
  - Rainbow trout: 10 fish from Carter Creek (August 1959)
  - Humpback sucker: 1 fish from the Green River (July 1959)
  - Carp: 2 fish from the Green River (July 1959)
  - Colorado squawfish: 1 fish from the Green River (July 1959)
  - Flannelmouth sucker and bonytail chub: combined samples from the Green River (July and August 1959)
- **Range:** Individual K values for cutthroat trout ranged roughly from 0.8 to 2.8 in the scanned table.

### Context
The appendix uses the 1959 Green River survey to test whether disease and parasites affect fish condition and food. It gives baseline data on the condition of the [[Green River]] fish community before the reservoir changed the river system. Its references include Beeching (1953), Davis (1953), Meyer (1954), Plehn (1924), Slyter (1953) and Simon (1946).
