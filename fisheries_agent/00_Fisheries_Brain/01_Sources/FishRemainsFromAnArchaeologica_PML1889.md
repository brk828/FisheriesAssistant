# Fish Remains from an Archaeological Site along the Verde River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document reports identification of fish remains recovered from Perkins Pueblo Site N-4-2 (ASU site N-4-2), an archaeological site on the Verde River near Perkinsville, Yavapai County, Arizona. Authors W. L. [[Minckley]] and Robert T. Ashby (named in the source as "Nonvarv T. Ar,cnn"; the OCR reading is uncertain) analysed bone remains, identifying five fish taxes from the aboriginal occupation, dated tentatively to about 1300–1400 A.D. Three species still occur abundantly in the Verde River; two, [[Humpback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], no longer occur there.

## Background & Description

The site was studied by field parties from the Department of Anthropology at Arizona State University. At least 43 rooms and the 129 bones and bone fragments were recovered; fish remains were identified by comparison with skeletal material in the Department of Zoology collections at Arizona State University, with help from [[Robert R. Miller]] (University of Michigan). Previously reported fish from Arizona archaeological sites, including [[Catostomus]] and [[Gila Chub]] specimens, were also reviewed. Funding came from the University Research Committee of Arizona State University and the Sport Fishing Institute.

Five species are described:

- **[[Gila Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*)**: 53 pieces (42% of identified material), largest group of remains. Fish used at the site ranged 100–250 mm standard length.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)**: 47 pieces (37.7% of identified material). Fish probably 150–350 mm; largest known specimen exceeds 500 mm.
- **[[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**: a single diagnostic bone (tripus of the Weberian apparatus), ASU 2493(S).
- **[[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta robusta*)**: remains of fish mostly 150–200 mm; one pharyngeal arch suggests a fish near 375 mm.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)**: six thoracic vertebrae, tentatively identified, from a fish about 300 mm long, found in a burial with three infants (Room 3).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Gila suckers are widespread in flowing streams of the Gila River basin; smaller fish live in shallow, swift riffles, larger ones in deep channels. Sonora suckers occupy similar ranges but remain in the deepest available water and spawn on riffles. Humpback suckers inhabit large, strongly flowing rivers; their presence in the Verde headwaters in the past implies substantially different habitat conditions then. Roundtail chubs live in deep pools and channels. Colorado squawfish were reported in the Verde region by Camp Verde residents in the early 1900s (as "salmon").

Historical accounts from Mrs. Nick Perkins describe the Verde River near Perkinsville in the late 1800s and early 1900s as a slow, shallow stream with extensive marshes on its floodplain, a large island near Perkinsville, few cottonwoods, and desert willow on terraces. Late 1800s–early 1900s channel entrenchment, marsh drainage, and mesquite and cottonwood invasion were associated with a regional cycle of change. Authors suggest the fish may have been harvested by blocking and drying channels (such as irrigation canals), since no fish hooks or specialized spear points were found. The fish were probably dried, a practice recorded among Paiute groups at Pyramid Lake, Nevada, before cooking.

## Key Findings

- Five fish taxa were identified from Site N-4-2; three (Gila sucker, Sonora sucker, roundtail chub) still occur abundantly in the Verde River.
- Humpback sucker and squawfish are no longer collected from the Verde system; their presence in the past implies changed river habitat.
- The 53 Gila sucker and 47 Sonora sucker pieces together make up about 80% of identified material.
- The site is tentatively dated 1300–1400 A.D. from pottery and architecture, pending confirmation by radiocarbon dating (not yet available).
- Marine molluscs at the site indicate trade contact; the small fish species were considered unlikely trade items, and the fish were probably used as food.
- Authors conclude that the fish assemblage reflects a riverine habitat substantially different from the modern Verde River.
