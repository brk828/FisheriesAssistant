# Primary Productivity and Fish Harvest in a Small Desert Impoundment

#ichthyology #limnology #Centrarchidae #freshwater-ecology #primary-productivity

## Executive Summary

This study by William J. McConnell (1963) directly compared **gross primary photosynthesis** to **fish harvest** in Pena Blanca Lake, a small (49-acre) desert impoundment in Arizona, over a 3-year period (1959–1961). Average annual gross photosynthesis (benthic + planktonic) was **14,100 lbs/acre (1.6 kg/m²) of oxygen equivalent**. The harvest of [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]] (combined as "centrarchids") averaged **138 lbs/acre live weight**, representing **0.98% of gross photosynthesis** on a live-weight basis (0.15% on a dry-weight basis). This centrarchid harvest was judged to approximate the **maximum potential yield to the creel**, given intensive angling pressure and habitat saturation. In contrast, [[Channel Catfish]] harvest was judged to be substantially below its potential due to delayed angler exploitation techniques. The paper is a foundational early attempt to link basic aquatic productivity (gross photosynthesis) to realized fish yield in a controlled, closed system, building on and refining the approach of [[H.T. Odum]] (1957) at Silver Springs, Florida.

## Background & Description

**Study Rationale:** Historically, aquatic productivity was assessed via standing crop of forage organisms, a method with weak theoretical ties to actual fish production. The paper adopts the **gross primary productivity** framework (per Ryther 1956; Odum 1959) as a more direct and measurable index, and uses **direct creel-census harvest data** rather than calculated standing-crop-based productivity (as Odum 1957 had done), arguing this is a methodological improvement when harvest approximates true potential productivity.

**Study Site — Pena Blanca Lake:**
- Constructed by Arizona Game and Fish Department (Federal Aid to Fisheries Project FW-5-D)
- Location: Pajarito Mountains, ~14 airline miles WNW of Nogales, Arizona (border with Mexico); 33°30′ N, 3,800 ft elevation
- Surface area: 49 acres at spillway level; average depth 20 ft; max depth 57 ft; shoreline development factor 2.7
- Classified as a **warm monomictic lake** (Hutchinson 1957), stratified March–November, turnover in November (~60°F)
- Surface temperature extremes: 42°F to 85°F over first 3 years
- Total alkalinity: ~80 mg/L CaCO₃ equivalent; pH 6.8 (below thermocline) to 8.3 (protected coves)
- Impounded spring 1958; drainage is intermittent, so **no native fish species present** prior to stocking

**Initial Fish Stocking (1958):**
- 1,000 [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) brood stock
- 30,000 [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) fingerlings
- 38 adult [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*)
- Unknown number of [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) — a forage fish

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Photosynthesis Partitioning:** Approximately **50% of total lake photosynthesis derived from benthic (littoral) plants**, with the remainder from plankton (pelagial + littoral). This is notable because in larger, less intricately-shored lakes, planktonic photosynthesis would dominate — suggesting that lake morphometry (shoreline development) strongly influences the benthic/planktonic productivity ratio and potentially fish-yield efficiency.

**Organic Import:** Substantial allochthonous organic input occurred via cloudburst-driven summer runoff — an estimated **385 tons** of leaves, grass, bark, and cattle manure entered the lake in summer 1959 alone, roughly equal to that year's total gross photosynthesis. This import likely enhanced fish production, though much settled into the hypolimnion and was largely inaccessible to the food web due to sparse hypolimnetic invertebrate fauna.

**Trophic Structure:** Unpublished food-habit data showed **[[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]] have nearly identical diets** (mainly cladocerans and insects), justifying their treatment as **trophic equivalents** in this analysis. [[Channel Catfish]] were assumed to have a broader/different diet, limiting potential dietary competition with centrarchids.

**Population Cycles:** A **2-year cycle of recruitment** was identified in the [[Largemouth Bass]] population — dense yearling cohorts in one year suppressed survival of the subsequent year's spawn (via competition/predation), leading to alternating strong/weak year classes. [[Black Crappie]] recruitment appeared to track this same cycle.

**Diffusion Error & Methods Note:** Due to variable wind/turbulence typical of canyon-situated desert lakes, standard diffusion-correction techniques (Odum & Hoskins 1958) could not be reliably applied; measurements were restricted to calm periods, and littoral gross photosynthesis was often estimated from night community respiration alone (validated by the close 1:1 relationship between littoral respiration and gross photosynthesis, Figure 1).

## Key Findings

### Productivity Metrics
- **Average annual gross photosynthesis (all sources):** 14,100 lbs O₂/acre/year (1.6 kg/m²/year); ranged 12,600–16,900 lbs/acre across 1959–1961
- **Littoral (benthic) photosynthesis** consistently exceeded pelagial (plankton) photosynthesis (Table 3)
- **Oxygen/chlorophyll ratio:** 2.04 g O₂/g chlorophyll/hr (mean of 15 measurements, relative SE 10.7%)
- Total gross production: ~4.0 g O₂/m²/day (combined littoral + pelagial)

### Fish Harvest Metrics
- **Average annual angler pressure:** >400 angler-days/acre; ranged 15,200–35,300 total angler-days/year (1959–1961)
- **Average combined centrarchid harvest (1960–61):** 138 lbs/acre live weight
- **Centrarchid harvest / gross photosynthesis ratio:**
  - Live weight basis: **0.98%** (0.0098)
  - Dry weight basis: **0.15%** (0.00142)
  - Interpreted as: ~1 lb of largemouth bass (or trophic equivalent) harvested per 100 lbs gross photosynthesis/year
- **Channel catfish harvest/photosynthesis ratio:** 0.45% (0.0045) — judged 3–4× lower than true potential due to delayed angler effectiveness (catfish didn't become significant in the fishery until 1961, three years after stocking, despite reaching catchable size in year one)
- Average channel catfish harvest: **64 lbs/acre/year**

### Population Dynamics
- **Largemouth bass fishing success** dropped 80% from 1959 (1.29 fish/angler-day) to 1960 (0.27 fish/angler-day), interpreted as reflecting a proportional 80% decline in standing population — evidence of intense, effective exploitation
- Electrofishing length-frequency data (Figure 3) showed **few largemouth bass survived to their third year of life**, indicating harvest substantially truncates natural mortality/age structure
- **2-year recruitment cycle** observed for both bass and crappie
- Harvest of largemouth bass judged to be **near maximum potential yield to the creel**

### Comparative Context
- Comparison to [[H.T. Odum]]'s Silver Springs, Florida study (1957): combined predaceous fish (including centrarchids, top minnows, gars) production = **0.29% of gross photosynthesis** (dry weight) at Silver Springs vs. **0.14%** at Pena Blanca Lake — difference attributed to Odum's use of calculated biomass increase vs. actual harvest, and differing dominant primary producers (benthic phanerogams/Aufwuchs at Silver Springs)
- Precision of both ratios considered too low to treat the difference as statistically significant

### Methodological Conclusions
- Chlorophyll-based plankton photosynthesis estimation carries high natural variability; halving the standard error would require **60 rather than 15** oxygen/chlorophyll ratio determinations
- Direct oxygen production measurement may be preferable to chlorophyll-based estimation where feasible
- Harvest-to-photosynthesis ratios are best computed using **multi-year averages** (matched to population cycle length) to account for "**use lag**" — the deferred entry of primary production into fish biomass and deferred harvest of that biomass
- The **combined harvest ratio** (centrarchids + catfish vs. photosynthesis) was judged **invalid for predictive purposes** because catfish harvest did not reflect true production potential (due to angler inefficiency), unlike the centrarchid ratio which was considered a reliable near-maximal yield estimate
