## Executive Summary

This note synthesizes field data from a 1993 fish sampling and repatriation monitoring program conducted at **Davis Cove**, a protected backwater nursery habitat on **Lake Mohave** (lower [[Colorado River]]), targeting two federally endangered fishes: the **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and the **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*). The records span four sampling events (January 12–13, January 28, February 17–18, and April 28–29, 1993) and document individual fish capture data, morphometrics (total length, weight), PIT tag identification, fin-clip marking (used for cohort/origin tracking), recapture events, and mortalities. The dataset reflects a **head-start/repatriation strategy** in which hatchery-reared juveniles (many noted as originating from the **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]**) were reared in a predator-limited backwater (Davis Cove) before being released into the open waters of Lake Mohave once they reached a threshold size, consistent with management strategies for the [[Native Fish Working Group]] and lower Colorado River endangered fish recovery programs.

## Background & Description

### Razorback Sucker (RZB)
- **Total Length (TL) range:** 105–360 mm across all sampling dates
- **Weight range:** 12–563 g
- Larger individuals (TL > 270 mm, WT > 250 g) were generally selected for release to **Lake Mohave**, indicating a size-based release threshold likely tied to reduced predation vulnerability.
- Smaller juveniles (TL 105–190 mm) were retained in Davis Cove for continued rearing.
- Several fish displayed **regenerated right pectoral fin clips (RP1)**, indicating prior marking and recapture, useful for tracking cohort growth over time.
- One ripe male noted with regenerated RP1 — indicative of reproductive maturity (ripeness) under captive/backwater rearing conditions.

### Bonytail (BT)
- **Total Length (TL) range:** 120–259 mm
- Weight recorded only sporadically (e.g., 18 g at TL 140–146 mm in February)
- Consistently smaller-bodied than RZB cohorts sampled concurrently, reflecting a distinct growth trajectory or younger cohort.
- Marked with **RP1 (right pectoral) fin clips**; several returned to Davis Cove after measurement rather than released to open water, suggesting they had not yet reached the release-size threshold.

### Marking & Tagging Methods
- **PIT tags** (Passive Integrated Transponder) were applied to larger Razorback Sucker individuals for individual-level long-term tracking (unique alphanumeric codes recorded, e.g., `7F7D18405C`).
- **Fin clipping** (RP1 = right pectoral fin clip 1; LP1 = left pectoral fin clip 1) was used as a lower-cost batch/cohort marking method, particularly for smaller Bonytail not yet large enough for PIT tagging.
- Recaptured individuals were cross-referenced by PIT tag number (e.g., tag `7F7D182A02` recaptured between January 12–13 and January 28, 1993, showing growth from 283 mm/258 g to 280 mm/250 g — within measurement error, suggesting limited short-term growth or measurement variability).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Davis Cove** functioned as a **predator-limited nursery backwater**, isolated from the main body of Lake Mohave, allowing juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] to grow to a size less vulnerable to predation (notably from non-native piscivores such as largemouth bass and sunfish) before release.
- **Lake Mohave** serves as the receiving open-water habitat, part of the historic range and current refugial population center for both species in the lower [[Colorado River]] system.
- Both species are benthic/pelagic omnivore-invertivores native to the warm, turbid mainstem habitats of the Colorado River Basin; captive-reared cohorts sampled here likely originated from the **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]**, a key propagation center for Colorado River Basin endangered fishes.
- Predation and physical damage were significant mortality factors even within the protected cove:
  - **Crayfish damage** was recorded as a direct cause of mortality for at least one Razorback Sucker (TL 190 mm) and noted as damage on a Bonytail (TL 120 mm), indicating **crayfish predation/harassment** as a notable ecological pressure on rearing juveniles.
  - Physical injuries such as **lacerations** and **fin loss** were also documented, suggesting intraspecific aggression, handling stress, or additional predation pressure.

## Key Findings

- **Repatriation pipeline confirmed:** Fish were reared in Davis Cove and released to Lake Mohave once exceeding an approximate size threshold (~240–270+ mm TL for Razorback Sucker), consistent with a staged head-start conservation strategy.
- **Recapture data validate tag retention:** PIT tags remained functional and legible across multiple sampling events (Jan–Apr 1993), demonstrating the viability of PIT telemetry for long-term individual monitoring of these long-lived species.
- **Regenerated fin clips** observed in April sampling indicate that fin-clip marks, while useful, are not permanent (regenerate over time), reinforcing the value of PIT tags as the more durable identification method for large juveniles/subadults.
- **Reproductive readiness detected in captivity/backwater rearing:** A ripe male Razorback Sucker was documented in a spawning area in April, suggesting sexual maturity onset within a nursery cove environment, relevant to on-site spawning potential in refugial ponds.
- **Mortality causes identified:** Crayfish-related damage, lacerations, and fin injuries were the primary documented sources of juvenile mortality, highlighting **invertebrate predation/interference** as a management concern for backwater nursery habitats used in [[Endangered Fish Recovery Program|razorback sucker and bonytail recovery programs]].
- **Batch release events:** A notable pulse of 13 juvenile and 1 adult Razorback Sucker was released to Lake Mohave on April 28–29, 1993, representing a discrete cohort transition from nursery to open-water habitat.
- **Growth monitoring limitations:** Comparison of recaptured individuals (e.g., tag `7F7D182A02`) shows minimal apparent growth or even slight length/weight decreases between sampling events, which may reflect measurement error, seasonal growth stagnation (winter months), or stress-related condition loss rather than true negative growth.
