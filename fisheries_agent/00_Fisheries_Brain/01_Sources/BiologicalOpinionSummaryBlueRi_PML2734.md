# Biological Opinion: Blue Box Low-Water Crossing, Forest Road 281, Blue River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This [[Biological Opinion]] issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Arizona Ecological Services Field Office) on December 2, 1998 concludes formal [[Section 7 Consultation]] on reconstruction of a hardened low-water crossing on [[Forest Road 281]] at the Blue Box, upper Blue River, Greenlee County, Arizona. The [[U.S. Forest Service]] is lead agency, with the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] in a support role, and the project is to be implemented by Greenlee County. The Service finds the project **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). Informal concurrences were given for four other species.

## Background & Description

**Consultation history.** The Forest Service initiated formal consultation on August 4-5, 1998. Plans were missing from the initial package and were forwarded on September 16, 1998; revised plans followed on October 7, 1998. A draft project description was sent November 24 and 30, and Greenlee County replied December 1. The Forest Service concurred with the final project description by telephone on December 2, 1998. Earlier, a removable-bridge option had been judged infeasible, and the project was changed to a hardened low-water crossing. The consultation is tiered to a June 16, 1997 [[Programmatic Biological Opinion]] on normal maintenance and repair of the Blue River road, with amendments of September 11, 1997 and June 25, 1998, which applies [[Best Management Practices]].

**Proposed action.**
- Concrete pad 343 feet long and 18 feet wide, 6 inches thick, cast in place on an aggregate base, spanning two baseflow channels.
- Two 24-inch pipes in the western channel, set in concrete to prevent piping and ponding.
- Riprap (3 inches to 3 feet diameter) on the upstream side and along the downstream face, with a debris guard and grouted riprap at the pipe ends.
- Total width of the installed crossing 40 to 100 feet; total disturbance slightly less than 0.33 acres.
- Machinery is excluded from the stream channel beyond the disturbance area; later maintenance is done from the pad.
- Staging on the west approach; concrete washout rinse water to be held in a basin and kept out of the river; water for compaction drawn from the river in small amounts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species status.** Loach minnow was listed as threatened on October 28, 1986. It is a small, slender, elongate fish with upwardly directed eyes that lives on the bottom of shallow, swift water over gravel, cobble and rubble. It feeds exclusively on aquatic insects, has a life span of about 2 years, and spawns March through May (occasionally in autumn). Eggs are attached beneath rocks, and males may guard the nest. Its range has declined by about 85 percent. Critical habitat designated in 1994 was later revoked by the Service in 1998 after court action, so no critical-habitat finding was required. A petition to reclassify it as endangered was found warranted, but action is precluded by higher-priority listings. Genetic work (Tibbets, 1993) recommends managing genetically distinct units separately.

**Environmental baseline.** The Blue River aquatic and riparian system is described as badly damaged. Nine fish species, about 65 percent of the native fauna, have been extirpated in the past century. Floodplain soils and riparian cover are largely lost, base flows are low, and roads on the floodplain are a significant contributing factor to degradation. Surveys found loach minnow in Campbell Blue and Dry Blue creeks and the Blue River, where it is the least abundant of six native species, usually less than 5 percent of the fish population. The other native species are speckled dace, longfin dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker and razorback sucker. Nonnative species include rainbow trout, brown trout, fathead minnow, western mosquitofish and red shiner; channel catfish are locally abundant in the lower river.

**Effects.** Roads cause sediment input, bank erosion, loss of riparian vegetation and channel constriction. The Blue Box is a bedrock constriction that acts as a geohydrologic control point, and repeated washouts at the crossing have contributed to downstream erosion. Expected effects include:
- Loss of a small riffle area under the pad and riprap, judged insignificant because riffles are predominant in the river.
- Short-term sediment input during construction, judged localized, with a long-term reduction expected once the crossing is stable.
- Short-term toxic-spill risk from petroleum products, cement, and concrete washout.
- Minor riparian vegetation loss, with a net benefit expected from greater stability.
- Temporary water removal, estimated at 11 cubic feet per second or less 50 percent of the time, so removal is significant relative to flow and is considered short-term.
- Possible indirect effects from increased road use.

**Cumulative effects.** The primary cumulative effects come from private valley-bottom activities, including livestock grazing, residential development, and an aquaculture operation that adds predatory nonnative fish and diverts water. The Service concludes the crossing only partially ameliorates damage and that cumulative impacts must be addressed for recovery.

## Key Findings

- **Opinion:** Not likely to jeopardize loach minnow, with informal concurrences ("not likely to adversely affect") for razorback sucker, southwestern willow flycatcher, bald eagle and Mexican spotted owl, the latter conditional on completion before March 1. Peregrine falcon, jaguar, Mexican gray wolf and Chiricahua leopard frog were determined not to require Service concurrence.
- **Best option:** Removal of the crossing is the best outcome for the river, followed by a bridge. Neither is feasible, so a low-water crossing with minimal channel alteration is the next-best choice and is the one proposed.
- **Incidental take:** Take is anticipated through direct mortality (crushing, smothering, toxic introduction) and indirect habitat loss. Because loach minnow take cannot be quantified, take is indexed to the fish community. Take is exceeded if (1) more than 20 dead fish of any species are found within the project area or 500 yards downstream; (2) machinery operates outside a 50-foot zone on each side of the crossing centerline; or (3) any toxic spill occurs in the river or floodplain.
- **Reasonable and prudent measures:** Minimize direct mortality and habitat loss, monitor the fish community, and keep complete records.
- **Terms and conditions (nondiscretionary):**
  - Minimize work in the wetted channel.
  - No vehicle or equipment entry into surface water during spawning seasons (March 1 to June 1 and September 1 to October 31).
  - Limit water removal to 50 gallons per minute or 500 gallons per 24 hours.
  - Prevent pollutants from entering surface water, and have concrete-washout location and disposal reviewed by the Forest Service and Service.
  - Apply Best Management Practices and replant any cut willows.
  - Limit rock and material removal to the 100-foot construction strip.
  - Monitor for dead or dying fish within 500 yards downstream and notify the Service by phone if more than 20 are found.
  - Submit a written report within 60 days of completion, with before-and-after photographs and monitoring results.
- **Conservation recommendations:** None given.
- **Reinitiation:** Required if take is exceeded, new information emerges, the action is modified, or a new species is listed or critical habitat designated.
