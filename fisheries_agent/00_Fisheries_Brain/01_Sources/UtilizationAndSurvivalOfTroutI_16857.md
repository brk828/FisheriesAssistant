# Utilization and Survival of Trout in Indiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary
Ricker, Mosbaugh and Lung report on tagging experiments in 1943 and 1944 with hatchery-reared, catchable-size [[Rainbow Trout]] and a few [[Brown Trout]] planted in northern Indiana streams. Voluntary tag returns from rainbows ranged from 0 to 42 percent in the year of planting. Second-year recoveries in the [[Little Elkhart River]] were about 18 percent of first-year recoveries, which gave a rough estimate of annual mortality. Most recaptures came from within about a mile of the release point. Brown trout returns were poor (3 tags from 74 fish).

## Background & Description
- No native trout are known in Indiana apart from [[Lake Trout]] in [[Lake Michigan]]. Large-scale stocking began after about 1920, using [[Brook Trout]] (Salvelinus fontinalis), rainbow trout (Salmo gairdneri) and brown trout (Salmo trutta). Brook trout became established in only a few small streams. Rainbows and browns now occur in many places across the northern tier of counties.
- A 1931–32 survey by M. J. Murray rated 17 miles of stream as good trout water and 34 miles as doubtful. His temperature-based criteria proved too severe, because several hundred miles of northern Indiana streams now give fair to good trout fishing. Examples are the [[Little Elkhart River]], [[Little Kankakee River]] and [[Solomon's Creek]], some of which Murray rejected.
- Good trout streams seem to have always lacked large predatory fishes such as [[Northern Pike]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Rock Bass]]. They hold mainly [[Creek Chub]] and [[White Sucker]]. Streams where trout stocking failed (Fawn, Pigeon, Elkhart Rivers) support these predators.

**Methods**
- Before 1943 most stocking used fingerlings. In 1943 about 1000 yearling-age rainbows (progeny of the 1942 spawning) from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service hatchery at Neosho, Missouri, were jaw-tagged (No. 3 tag, [[Shetter Tagging Method]]) on April 14. They were planted the next day at single bridge sites ("spot" method).
- 1943 rainbows: mean fork length 9.9 in (252 mm), range 6.7–14.8 in (170–375 mm). The Pigeon River lot averaged 9.3 in (235 mm).
- 1944: rainbows averaged 9.5 in (241 mm), range 7.3–12.2 in, planted in about 20 streams in small lots (20–101 fish each). Three lots of adult brown trout from Northville, Michigan, averaged 12.4 in (314 mm), range 10.0–15.2 in. Recaptures were reported voluntarily by anglers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Streams studied are tributaries of the St. Joseph River (flowing to [[Lake Michigan]]), the [[Kankakee River]] (Illinois–Mississippi system), or Lake Michigan directly. Counties include St. Joseph, Elkhart, LaGrange, LaPorte, Porter and Steuben.
- Sites include [[Judy Creek]], Little Elkhart River (several sites), Little Kankakee River (upper and lower), [[Pigeon River]], [[Trail Creek]], Dutch Creek, Emma Creek, Crooked Creek, Willow Creek and others.
- The authors caution that returns are minimums, since some tags were taken but not turned in. Unreturned tags may equal from a quarter up to as many as those returned. Wartime restrictions on fishing in 1943–44 made exploitation estimates unrepresentative.

## Key Findings
- **Recovery classes (rainbow trout):**
  - Under 5 percent: Bloody Run, Cobus Creek, Coffee Creek, Fly Creek, lower Pigeon River, Potato Creek and Snake Creek. Planting adult rainbows there was judged not worthwhile.
  - 6–15 percent in at least one year: upper Little Kankakee, lower Little Elkhart, Buck Creek, Crooked Creek, upper Pigeon River, Solomon's Creek, Turkey Creek and Willow Creek.
  - 16 percent or more: Dutch Creek, Emma Creek, Judy Creek, Little Elkhart above Middlebury, West Branch Little Elkhart, lower Little Kankakee and Trail Creek. These mostly yielded 20–25 percent, with a maximum of 42 percent (Little Elkhart No. 1, 1943: 99 returns from 243 planted).
  - Other 1943 results: Judy Creek 32 percent, upper Little Kankakee 9 percent, lower Little Kankakee 20 percent, Pigeon River 0 of 35.
  - 1944 examples: West Branch Little Elkhart 26 percent, Emma Creek 22 percent, Trail Creek 20 percent, Dutch Creek 19 percent.
- **Comparison:** [[Shetter and Hazzard]] (Michigan creel census) found 10–62 percent recapture of rainbows (average 34 percent over 6 plants), so the best Indiana streams are comparable as survival habitat for hatchery fish.
- **Brown trout:** only 3 tags returned from 74 fish. Two came from the 25 put in the lower Little Kankakee (8 percent). Shetter and Hazzard reported 7–19 percent (average 14 percent) for browns, less than half their rainbow returns.
- **Survival and mortality:** Second-year tag recoveries came only from the Little Elkhart. In May 1944, 10 tagged fish plus 2 that had lost tags were taken from the No. 1 plant, against 99 in 1943 (12/99, about 12 percent). Plants No. 3 and No. 4 gave 2/17, also about 12 percent. Raising survival by half to allow for lower fishing effort and tag loss gives about 18 percent survival, or about 82 percent total annual mortality. Against a minimum 42 percent exploitation, about 40 percent disappeared from other causes, mostly natural mortality including [[Predation]]. In Michigan's Pine River, 8 tags were returned in 1939 from 1500 marked in 1938, against 633 in 1938, a survival of about 1.3 percent.
- **Wandering:** Most recaptures were near the planting site. Of 99 recaptures from the Little Elkhart No. 1 plant in 1943, 3 were upstream within half a mile and nearly all the rest were downstream, usually about half a mile away. Only 3 trout moved more than 2–3 map miles. One went to Elkhart City via the [[St. Joseph River]], and one West Branch fish traveled 19 miles.
- **Native trout:** Questionnaire returns from 4 anglers (reporting for 10 fishermen) on the Little Elkhart showed 230 rainbows caught in 1943, of which 88 were tagged. Untagged fish were more numerous but smaller. Fourteen browns were reported. Catches fell in 1944 to 43 rainbows and 6 browns.
