# Recognition and Return of Trout Tags by California Anglers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 report by [[Robert L. Butler]] of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (Inland Fisheries Branch) examines why voluntary angler tag returns often fail to represent the true catch of planted fish. Drawing on 76 tagging studies of catchable-sized [[Rainbow Trout]] in 17 California lakes and streams, the author tested tag color, fin-clipping, name-versus-number tags, and reward or award programs. The central conclusion is that tag return is "primarily a problem in human behavior": no single technique reliably guarantees complete returns, and biologists must measure and hold constant the level of [[Angler Nonresponse]] for catch and mortality estimates to be valid.

## Background & Description

Harvest rates, mortality and total catch are often calculated from tags returned voluntarily by anglers. The author argues that fishery workers frequently assume anglers are equally observant and responsive, and fail to distinguish among areas, seasons and fisheries.

**Study species and tag.** The only species used for large-scale catchable stocking in California, and the sole species in these studies, was [[Rainbow Trout]]. Catchable fish average 7.5 inches, or four to six per pound. Tags were [[Vinyl Plastic Subcutaneous Tag]]s, inserted beneath the belly skin just anterior to the ventral fins. The tag is visible for three to four weeks, after which a heavy pigmented area marks its location. Tagged fish formed part of regularly scheduled plants and were tagged one to two days before planting.

**Promotion methods.** Posters (11 x 14 inches, red and black on white fiberboard) and demonstration boards with preserved tagged specimens were placed at resorts, boat docks, stores and campgrounds. Some boards carried [[Franked Envelope]]s for return. Local newspapers covered the programs at [[Big Bear Lake]] and the [[Kern River]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fishing pressure varied widely among waters, and this strongly shaped tag returns:

- **Low pressure:** [[Lake Pillsbury]] recorded 0.2 angler hours per acre per day. Its instantaneous fishing mortality was one-thirtieth of the lowest rate recorded for the Kern River catchable fishery, and the fishery removed only a small share of planted trout. Most of its catch consisted of wild trout.
- **High pressure:** The Kern River fishery is intense, and virtually the complete planted population is removed within a short period.
- **Angler conditioning:** Anglers at waters previously stocked with marked fish, such as Big Bear Lake and the West Fork of the San Gabriel River, were more responsive to marks.
- **Cultural factors:** At [[Red Lake]], Minnesota, Red Lake Indians kept metal tags as beads and ornaments. Tags were returned only after trust in the fishery workers developed.

Non-trout comparisons were also cited. Tags on [[Walleye]] in Red Lake passed unnoticed through commercial handling, and some were recovered from consumers in Chicago. In the [[Striped Bass]] fishery of San Francisco Bay, nonreward tags returned only 60 percent as well as $5 reward tags (Chadwick, 1960).

## Key Findings

**1. Tag returns are a function of angler behavior.** Returns did not consistently track the number of tagged fish caught. In Table 1, estimated nonresponse ranged from 46.4 percent at [[Lake Gregory]] (6/28/57) to 90.6 percent at [[Lake Lagunitas]] (9/16/57). Nonresponse was computed by comparing voluntary return percentages against [[DeLury Estimate]]s, partial censuses or complete [[Creel Census]]es.

**2. Tag color did not matter.** A trial of 600 yellow and 600 green tags across three waters showed no significant difference (chi-square = 2.18, 1 df). Yellow tags blend with white belly skin, but high returns in some waters suggested this is not a serious drawback when anglers are well informed.

**3. Fin-clipping helps only where anglers are conditioned.** In the first test (Table 2), ventral-fin removal did not significantly change returns (chi-square = 0.78). A larger test (Table 3) found marked trout returned at 24.0 percent (276 of 1,148) versus 20.3 percent (233 of 1,149) for unmarked trout, a significant difference. The author attributes this to prior exposure to marked fish at most of the waters tested, since no difference was expected where anglers had not been conditioned.

**4. Names did not outperform numbers.** At [[Strawberry Lake]], 200 fish carrying first-name tags were compared with 200 carrying serial numbers. Chi-square analysis found no significant difference.

**5. Reward tags helped only in some fisheries.**
- **Kern River (1956-57):** 175 $5-reward tags were planted with 3,048 numbered tags across eight plants. Returns were 53.7 percent for reward tags and 33.1 percent for nonreward tags (1,009 of 3,048), a significant difference. If reward returns reflect true catch, nonresponse for nonreward tags was 38.4 percent. Reward tags beat nonreward tags in every individual plant.
- **Lake Pillsbury (1957):** 100 reward tags and 3,700 nonreward tags produced returns of 20.0 percent and 21.2 percent (786 of 3,700), with no significant difference.
- **Big Bear Lake (1956):** Reward tags (68 percent) and tag-drawing award tags (66.9 percent, 1,824 tags) performed almost identically. The drawing-award prizes included a boat, trailer, outboard motors, tackle, lodging and meals, and were heavily publicized. Only numbered tags were used in the award program.

**6. Awards can substitute for cash rewards.** When funds are unavailable, impressive prizes and intensive advertising may replace reward tags. Winners at Big Bear Lake learned of the study mainly through newspapers and posters, and secondarily through other anglers. Department personnel had little direct effect on winners.

**7. Sustained effort is required.** Returns fell when publicity and recovery effort declined (Table 8):
- June Lake: 52.5 percent (1956) fell to 27.8 percent (1957).
- Big Bear Lake: 66.9 percent fell to 34.8 percent.
- Lake Lagunitas: 26.3 percent (7/23/57) fell to 7.0 percent (9/16/57).

The author attributes this to reduced effort and "loss of novelty," not to poorer survival of planted fish.

**8. Tag return boxes and talks may help.** Earlier work by Smith and Smith (1945) and Mullan (1959) indicated return boxes along streams improve returns and give better indices of catch. Community talks were also judged useful for overcoming indifference.

**9. Recommendations.**
- Biologists should maintain a constant level of angler response throughout a study, since any change in effort may upset catch calculations (citing [[Ricker]], 1948).
- The level of nonrecognition and nonresponse should be measured and controlled wherever percentage return or mortality is derived from tag returns.
- No single technique guarantees consistent returns.

**Overall conclusion:** The number of tagged fish caught and the number returned are seldom the same, and their relationship varies widely. Tag return depends largely on the amount and type of recovery effort and on local interest.
