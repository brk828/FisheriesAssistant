# Age and Growth of Walleyes in Clear Lake, Iowa, 1935–1957

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stizostedion

## Executive Summary

This paper reconstructs the age and growth history of the [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) in [[Clear Lake]], Iowa, using [[Scale Analysis]] of fish collected across 1941–1958 (year classes 1935–1957). Scales were read by three biologists in successive periods, which introduced interpretive differences that the authors evaluate and judge to leave the data "basically reliable." The study finds that growth of a year class, measured as mean length at the third annulus, is negatively correlated with that class's abundance (r = −0.78), suggesting intraspecific competition. Calendar-year growth tracks summer temperature, with the [[Hile Growth Index]] correlating at r = 0.565 with mean May–September temperatures. Females grew faster than males in the sampled mature fish. The authors conclude that growth rates changed little over the study period, though growth has been slower since 1946.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Walleye, the most important game fish in Clear Lake according to the authors.
- **Prior work:** [[Cleary]] (1949) analyzed scales from 1941–1943 and 1947. [[Whitney]] read scales from 1948–1953 and [[Carlander]] from 1954–1958. Correlating data between the last two readers caused problems addressed in the paper.
- **Sampling:** Scales were taken mainly June–August from fish caught in experimental [[Gill Netting]] (equal lengths of 1.5-, 2-, 2.5-, 3-, and 4-inch stretch mesh). Lengths are total lengths in inches.
- **Subsampling:** [[Stratified Subsampling]] (after Ketchen, 1950) gave samples of about 200 fish per year for 1949–1952 collections, with extra fish drawn from strata with larger fish, which were harder to read.
- **Scale reading methods:** Scales through 1953 were read from wet mounts; scales from 1954 onward were read from plastic impressions.
- **Annuli criteria:** Crowding of [[Circuli]], wide spacing between circuli series at the anterolateral angle, and anastomosis of circuli on the lateral fields. Anastomosis was judged most significant, but all criteria involved subjectivity. Scales were read without knowledge of fish size at capture.
- **Funding:** Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit, Iowa State University, with sponsorship from the Iowa State Conservation Commission, cooperation from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and a grant from the Sport Fishing Institute.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Year-class strength and stocking:** [[Carlander]] and colleagues (1960) found that year-class abundance was related to [[Fry Stocking]]. Less abundant year classes came from years with no fry stocked. The abundance index ranged from 36 (1943 year class) to 154 (1952), with 40 (1955) and 58 (1956) also low.
- **Population density and growth:** The authors note that higher population densities often reduce individual growth. They do not believe total walleye density varied greatly, but they interpret the growth–abundance relationship as evidence of competition within particular year classes.
- **Temperature:** Growth in warm summers was generally better. Including April and October temperatures lowered the correlation with the Hile index from 0.565 to 0.387.
- **Regional comparison:** Clear Lake growth is nearly the same as in [[Spirit Lake]], Iowa; faster than in Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota, and Ohio; and slower than in North Carolina, Tennessee, and Virginia.
- **Young-of-year growth:** First-annulus lengths varied with mean September 1 lengths of young-of-year fish seined along shore (data from [[Ridenhour]], 1960), r = 0.57 (6 d.f.), not significant at the 95% level.

## Key Findings

### Body–Scale Relationship
- Regression from 1,209 walleyes collected 1948–1953: **total length (inches) = 3.26 + 1.31 anterior scale radius (×50)**.
- The intercept of 3.26 inches was used for back-calculation via a nomograph (Carlander and Smith, 1944).
- No significant difference was found among year classes (F = 0.85, 2 and 3 d.f.) or years of capture (F = 6.69, 2 and 3 d.f.), so a single regression was used. Interaction was non-significant (F = 1.54, 4 and 80 d.f.).
- The smallest walleye was 6 inches, and only 58 were under 9 inches, so curvilinearity near the origin was not apparent. Slope change above 22 inches was judged minor, since few fish were in that range.

### Reader Differences
- Mean lengths at the first annulus differed by 0.6 inch among the three readers. Differences at consecutive annuli were 0.6, 0.2, 0.8, 1.1, and 1.7 inches.
- Carlander interpreted 14.7% of scales as having six or more annuli, compared with 2.2% (Whitney) and 3.8% (Cleary).
- Discrepancies were greatest in older age groups, which the authors attribute mainly to annuli-counting differences rather than true growth changes.

### Age Composition and Sample Size
- Age-group I through XII samples (Table 2 and Table 4) declined from 3,079 fish in age I to 2 fish in age XII, with 2,713 in age II and 1,511 in age III.
- Age classes VIII through XII contained very few fish (72 to 2), which limits inference for older ages.

### Growth Trends by Year Class
- Mean calculated length at the third annulus was negatively correlated with year-class abundance (**r = −0.78, 11 d.f.**).
- The more abundant year classes (1952, 1950, 1946) showed slower-than-average growth.
- Growth rate showed no major change over the study period.
- Within a single 1953 sample of 28 age-IV fish, first-annulus lengths ranged from 5.9 to 8.3 inches (SD 0.51). Second-annulus lengths ranged from 8.9 to 12.2 inches; third from 12.6 to 15.4 inches; fourth from 14.5 to 18.6 inches.

### Walford Growth Transformations
- Combined data: **maximum length 31.1 inches; k = 0.836** (Walford line a). The fit was not entirely satisfactory, and the model underestimated sizes in later years. Several fish exceeded the theoretical maximum.
- Fit to ages 2–7 only: L(n+1) = 26.3(0.220) + 0.780 Ln (line b).
- Best line for predicting next year's size from the same fish: L(n+1) = 28.8(0.172) + 0.828 Ln (line c).
- Fit to ages II–VII: L(n+1) = 24.4(0.243) + 0.757 Ln (line d).
- Excluding fish beyond age VII (25 fish, 0.8% of the sample): L(n+1) = 25.0(0.236) + 0.764 Ln (line e). This was judged more homogeneous.
- Fit to fish completing each annual increment: L(n+1) = 23.9(0.25) + 0.75 Ln (line f).

### Instantaneous Growth Rates
- Instantaneous growth rate (*i*) decreases rapidly with age and length. On a logarithmic scale, the decrease is approximately linear.
- The second year of life did not fit the trend, and values for years 11 and 12 may reflect scale-reading error.

### Calendar-Year Growth Indexes
- Five index types were tested: [[Hile Growth Index]] (Hile, 1941), three *i*-based adjustments (Types 2–4), and the [[Parker-Larkin Growth Index]] (L*).
- Correlations with mean May–September temperatures: Hile index **r = 0.56** (reported as 0.565; significant at the 1% level, 21 d.f.); Type 2 r = 0.17; Type 3 r = 0.01; Type 4 r = 0.22; L* r = 0.13.
- The authors chose the Hile index as the most direct and easily interpreted measure.
- Slower growth has occurred since 1946 than in most previous years.

### Sex-Specific Growth
- Sex was determined for only one collection, from the 1953 spawning season, where all fish were mature.
- Youngest fish of each sex were age III; the oldest were age IX (one female), age X (one female), and age VII (males).
- Considering ages III through VII, 88% of males and 59% of females were ages III–V.
- **Females grew faster than males from the third through the sixth year.** Most studies cited also report faster growth and longer life in females.
- Comparisons may not be fully valid because age at maturity differs between sexes.

### Methodological Caveats
- Lee's phenomenon of apparent growth change was not evident. Older fish appeared to grow faster in early years, which the authors attribute to missed annuli on older scales or to a tendency to record the last annulus closer to the margin.
- Fish that live longer may have a different growth pattern, so the absolute growth curve may not describe the growth of fish of a particular size and age well. Annual increments were judged more useful for that purpose.
