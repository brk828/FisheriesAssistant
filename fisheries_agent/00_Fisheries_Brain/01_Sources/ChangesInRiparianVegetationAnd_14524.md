# Changes in Riparian Vegetation and Avifauna in Cattle-Excluded Bonita Creek

#riparian-ecology #avifauna #habitat-restoration #southwestern-ecology #grazing-impacts

## Executive Summary

This 1987 baseline report documents vegetation and breeding bird communities along a 3 km cattle exclosure established in 1984 on [[Lower Bonita Creek]], [[Graham County]], [[Arizona]]. The study was conducted by [[William C. Hunter]] from [[Arizona State University]] for the [[U.S. Bureau of Land Management]], [[Safford Resource District]]. [[Bonita Creek]] supports among the highest breeding bird species richness found in southwestern riparian systems (62 species recorded), though individual densities remain relatively low. Severe floods in 1978-1979 and 1983 removed much of the mature canopy vegetation, and subsequent regeneration was limited by cattle grazing (270 head licensed). The exclosure was designed to assess whether excluding cattle would allow successful regeneration of broadleaf riparian trees, stabilize stream banks, maintain water quality, and improve wildlife habitat. Baseline data from 1985-1986 show substantial variation in vegetation structure among seven transects, with honey mesquite dominating the midstory and mixed broadleaf species (cottonwood, willow, sycamore) forming the canopy where present.

## Background & Description

[[Bonita Creek]] is located 37 km northeast of Safford, Arizona, flowing from 975 to 1128 m elevation before joining the [[Gila River]]. The riparian vegetation belongs to the Mixed Broadleaf Series of the [[Southwestern Riparian Deciduous Forest Type]]. The system has been degraded over the last century, with particularly severe impacts from flooding events in 1978-1979 and 1983 that removed mature canopy trees.

**Vegetation Composition:**
- **Midstory canopy:** [[Honey Mesquite]] (*Prosopis glandulosa*) - numerically dominant throughout
- **Canopy layer broadleaf species:** [[Fremont Cottonwood]] (*Populus fremontii*), [[Goodding Willow]] (*Salix gooddingii*), [[Arizona Sycamore]] (*Platanus wrightii*), [[Bonpland Willow]] (*Salix bonplandiana*), [[Netleaf Hackberry]] (*Celtis reticulata*), [[Arizona Walnut]] (*Juglans major*), [[Velvet Ash]] (*Fraxinus velutina*)
- **Understory:** [[Seepwillow]] (*Baccharis salicifolia*), burro brush (*Hymenoclea monogyra*), regenerating broadleaf seedlings
- **Groundcover:** [[Bermuda Grass]] (*Cynodon dactylon*), herbaceous perennials and annuals

Between 1979 and 1983, natural regeneration occurred each spring but seedlings were trampled or clipped by cattle, preventing any from reaching pole size before the 1983 flood. The cattle exclosure extended from approximately 1.5 km to 5 km from the [[Gila River]] confluence. In 1985, approximately 120 head of cattle grazed outside the exclosure; this was reduced to about 60 head in 1986, with cattle largely absent from the creek bottom until late July.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area represents a critical riparian corridor in the arid Southwest, where such systems support disproportionately high biodiversity relative to their limited extent. [[Riparian Systems]] in this region have been heavily impacted by multiple stressors including livestock grazing, altered hydrology, and periodic catastrophic flooding.

**Historical Context:**
Grazing has occurred on [[Bonita Creek]] for over 100 years. Grazing pressure intensified after 1972 when allotments changed hands, corresponding with a downward trend in successful reproduction of all riparian broadleaf plants. Previous studies by [[Minckley and Clark]] (1981), [[Clark]] (1984), and [[Rucks]] (1984) documented pre-flood conditions and initial post-flood responses. Of five creek and river systems studied by Rucks (1984), only [[Aravaipa Creek]] (excluded from cattle since 1973) showed extensive successful regeneration after the 1978-1979 flood.

**Vegetation Structure:**
Seven transects (each 762 m length, 183 m width, covering ~14 ha) were established consecutively along lower [[Bonita Creek]]. Foliage density measurements using the [[MacArthur Board Technique]] revealed substantial variation among transects. Foliage Height Diversity (FHD) ranged from 1.104 to 1.705. The midstory layer (0.6-4.6 m) was least developed and least variable among transects. Transects outside the exclosure showed high foliage density either in canopy (BC-3) or understory (BC-8, 9). Inside the exclosure, each transect exhibited different structural characteristics.

**Elevational Gradient Effects:**
Tree species composition changed along the elevational gradient. On the three lowermost transects (BC-3, 4, 5), [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Goodding Willow]], and [[Arizona Sycamore]] comprised 65-93% of broadleaf species. On the four uppermost transects (BC-6, 7, 8, 9), these three species comprised only 37-48%, with [[Arizona Walnut]], [[Netleaf Hackberry]], [[Velvet Ash]], and [[Bonpland Willow]] becoming more prominent.

## Key Findings

**Avifaunal Diversity:**
- **Total species recorded:** 62 species during 1985-1986, compared to 46 species in Clark's (1984) earlier study
- **Species with measurable densities:** 38 species, most considered breeding
- **Species richness range among transects:** 35-46 species (years combined)
- **Total of 64 summering species** when combining Clark (1984) and present study
- [[Bonita Creek]] supports among the highest breeding bird species numbers found anywhere in the United States

**Bird Densities:**
- **Average density:** 452 individuals per 40 ha (compared to 244 per 40 ha in Clark 1984)
- **Highest densities:** Consistently on uppermost-inside transects (BC-6, 7)
- **Individual numbers relatively low** compared to other mature mixed broadleaf and cottonwood-willow habitats with similar species richness, attributed to large barren patches

**Bird-Habitat Relationships by Foraging Guild:**
- **Bark foragers:** 2 species; average 30 individuals per 40 ha (relatively consistent among transects)
- **Ground foragers:** 5-6 species; 15-34 individuals per 40 ha
- **Understory foragers:** 5-6 species; 67-86 individuals per 40 ha (slightly higher inside exclosure)
- **Midstory foragers:** 6-7 species; 66-88 individuals per 40 ha (least variable among transects, reflecting consistent midstory structure)
- **Canopy foragers:** 10-11 species; 71-161 individuals per 40 ha (substantially greater on lowermost-outside BC-3 and uppermost-inside BC-6, 7 categories, corresponding to well-developed canopy)
- **Granivores:** 2-6 species; 60-134 individuals per 40 ha (substantially greater on uppermost-inside BC-6, 7, partly due to mature fig trees)
- **Aerial foragers:** 3-4 species; 7-19 individuals per 40 ha

**Threatened and Sensitive Species:**
Five state-listed or federally proposed threatened species occur in [[Bonita Creek]]:
- [[Zone-tailed Hawk]] (*Buteo albonotatus*) - state threatened
- [[Common Black-Hawk]] (*Buteogallus anthracinus*) - state threatened
- [[Northern Beardless Tyrannulet]] (*Camptostoma imberbe*) - state threatened
- [[Yellow-billed Cuckoo]] (*Coccyzus americanus*) - proposed federal threatened
- [[Bell's Vireo]] (*Vireo bellii*) - proposed federal threatened

All five species are obligate riparian species that have suffered dramatic declines elsewhere in the Southwest.

**Vegetation Regeneration Patterns:**
- Natural regeneration of cottonwood, willows, sycamore, and seepwillow evident throughout lower [[Bonita Creek]] each spring, most prevalent within 8 km of confluence
- Regeneration concentrated at uppermost end outside exclosure (BC-8, 9) and lowermost end inside exclosure (BC-5)
- Regeneration inside exclosure has at least one-year lead on that outside
- Some regeneration outside exclosure exceeded seedling size in 1986 with reduced cattle numbers, but by August almost all broadleaf seedlings were browsed when remaining cattle concentrated in creek bottom
- [[Honey Mesquite]] relatively equally distributed among all transects; recruitment after flood was fast and extensive

**Management Implications:**
- Grazing pressure, especially since 1972, was severe enough to affect regeneration of even least palatable ash and walnut seedlings
- Continued reduction in cattle numbers outside exclosure may cloud interpretations of regeneration differences between inside and outside
- If tree regeneration is more successful inside exclosure, ground, understory, and midstory foragers should increase substantially more inside than outside
- Differences may be most pronounced with midstory foragers, as baseline data showed little difference in vegetation or birds in this group
- Bird numbers should increase appropriately as barren patches develop with regenerating vegetation
- If past management practices with high cattle numbers are reinstituted, individual bird numbers will likely drop with eventual loss of some uncommon species

**Predicted Future Changes:**
[[Bonita Creek]] should improve tremendously in terms of wildlife use, erosion control, and water quality given extensive successful regeneration of broadleaf tree species and overall stabilization of the watershed. The baseline data provided will allow assessment of whether cattle exclusion successfully promotes riparian regeneration independent of other factors affecting these systems.
