# Predator Recognition and Avoidance by Larval Razorback Sucker and Northern Hog Sucker

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study compares how larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], and [[Northern Hog Sucker]] (*Hypentelium nigricans*), a species from the predator-rich [[Mississippi River Basin]], recognize and avoid a predator. Both species reacted to attacks by a [[Largemouth Bass]] and avoided them by fleeing or hiding. Single northern hog sucker larvae showed a significantly higher initial avoidance rate, and grouped larvae of both species improved avoidance more slowly with age than single larvae. The authors conclude that the difference reflects an inherent predator wariness shaped by evolutionary history rather than a differential rate of improvement with age. They argue that [[Reintroduction Programs]] for razorback suckers will be limited by [[Nonnative Fishes]] that prey on early life stages.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was once abundant and commercially harvested in the Colorado River basin. It declined to the point of listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and was federally listed as endangered in 1991. Declines are commonly attributed to habitat alteration and competition with more than 120 nonnative fish species. Predation by nonnative piscivores, especially [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]], has been correlated with razorback sucker declines. [[Minckley]] (1983) concluded that predation by introduced fishes on early life stages is "the most important single factor in their extirpation."

From 1981 to 1990, more than 12 million larval and juvenile razorback suckers were stocked into historic habitats in Arizona and California. Preliminary findings indicated that few stocked fish survived to adulthood, likely because of predation by nonnative fishes.

The native fish assemblage of the Colorado River drainage includes about 32 species, most of them confined to small, isolated waters. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is described as the only obligatory piscivore in the system. In contrast, the Mississippi River drainage contains more than 260 native fish species that evolved under high predator contact. The authors hypothesized that the razorback sucker, endemic to predator-poor waters, is inherently less predator wary than ecologically equivalent Mississippi species, a difference that could explain failed reintroductions. The study examines this idea through [[Predator Avoidance]] tests on larvae of both species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study origin and rearing**

- Razorback sucker larvae came from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, where the species is held and spawned for the reintroduction effort. Larvae were obtained about 4 days after hatching and reared in indoor, aerated plastic wading pools.
- Testing began between 8 and 12 days after hatching, when larvae showed neutral buoyancy and swimming ability.
- Northern hog suckers were collected from [[Clear Creek]], Washington County, Arkansas, on March 27, 1991, and spawned in the field. Eggs were incubated at 17°C and most hatched between 72 and 126 hours. Larvae were fed commercial dry diets of crushed starter channel catfish granules and flaked food after day 8.
- All larvae were reared and tested in the same room. Temperature in rearing and test aquaria ranged from 15 to 18°C, with a 12 h light / 12 h dark photoperiod.

**Test apparatus and predator**

- The test chamber was a 75-L aquarium divided into three equal 25 × 30 × 30 cm compartments by clear plastic dividers. The two outer compartments held the predator, and the middle compartment was the larval test chamber.
- The aquarium was covered with black plastic (except top and front) to reduce visual interference. No intentional cover was provided.
- The predator was one of two largemouth bass (10 and 14 cm total length), conditioned to the aquarium and ready to attack visible larvae.

**Test design**

- **Test 1 (single larva):** One larva and one predator were tested. After about 5 min of acclimation, opaque dividers were removed, the predator usually attacked immediately, and the time the larva spent on the "away" side was recorded after about 1 min of mutual visibility.
- **Test 2 (group):** Groups of five larvae were tested in the same way, with the number of larvae on the away side counted at 15 s intervals for 5 min.
- After each test, the predator was allowed to consume the test larvae, which avoided reuse of individuals that might have learned avoidance behavior. Because only about 200 northern hog sucker larvae were available, some individuals were retested, which may have biased their avoidance.
- A Wilcoxon scores test found no significant side effect (P > 0.36), so the two 5-min tests were combined into daily 10-min evaluations.

## Key Findings

**Sample sizes**

- Razorback sucker: 40 trials in 1990 and 40 trials in 1991 (as reported). Testing began in 1990 on March 24, when larvae were 19 days old (mean 13 mm total length), and continued to day 63 (mean 19 mm). In 1991 testing ran from day 10 (March 14) to day 65 (May 8), with a final mean length of 18 mm.
- Northern hog sucker: 26 trials, beginning at 7 days old (8 mm) on April 14, 1991, and ending at 35 days old on May 12, 1991, when larvae had doubled in length.

**Responses to predator attack**

- All larvae of both species in the near side of the chamber reacted to attacks. The typical first response was a startle, followed by fleeing or hiding.
- Hiding involved seeking refuge between the gravel substrate and the chamber side. Young suckers hid for up to 1 min, while older fish sometimes hid for the full 5-min period.
- Northern hog suckers hid at a younger age and more frequently than razorback suckers.
- Larvae in the away side of the chamber also responded to attacks, usually with a less violent redirection of swimming.

**Single-larva avoidance**

- Razorback suckers averaged about 60% of their time away from the predator by day 30 and more than 80% by the end of testing.
- Single northern hog sucker larvae showed positive avoidance from their first test day (7 days old). By day 32 they spent all their time away from the predator.
- Slopes of the avoidance-versus-age regressions did not differ significantly (P = 0.93 for both comparisons). The intercepts for 1991 razorback suckers and 1991 northern hog suckers did differ (P = 0.004), indicating that northern hog suckers were initially better at avoiding the predator.

**Group-larva avoidance**

- Larvae in groups still improved with age, but more slowly than single larvae, especially in northern hog suckers.
- For razorback suckers, group and single tests did not differ significantly in 1990 (intercepts P = 0.45; slopes P = 0.37) or 1991 (intercepts P = 0.32; slopes P = 0.16).
- For northern hog suckers, intercepts (P = 0.0001) and slopes (P = 0.04) differed between single and group tests.
- Group razorback suckers and group northern hog suckers also differed in slope (P = 0.008) and intercept (P = 0.004) in 1991.
- Grouped larvae of both species fled but never hid. Fleeing larvae often oriented toward non-fleeing larvae before reaching the away side, which reduced the avoidance response. Two or three of five larvae often oriented to each other, but there was no evidence of obligate schooling behavior.

**Interpretation**

- The youngest razorback suckers approximated the 50% level expected with no avoidance, whereas northern hog suckers began avoiding at 7 days after hatching.
- Both species improved at about the same rate with age. By day 32, northern hog suckers were completely avoiding the predator, while razorback suckers still spent nearly 40% of their time near it.
- The authors interpret the results as an inherent species difference between predator-rich and predator-poor communities, not a differential improvement with age.
- Older larvae (over 30 days) of both species were sometimes caught by the predator while wedged between the chamber wall and the gravel or dividers.
- Larger razorback suckers (over 25 mm) were more likely to hide, but hiding often occurred on either side of the chamber, so the link between size and avoidance remains untested.

**Extrapolations and management implications**

- Extending the single-larva regression lines suggests individual razorback suckers would reach 100% avoidance between 70 and 90 days after hatching.
- Group regression lines suggest that schools of young razorback suckers could take 250 to 280 days to demonstrate total avoidance.
- The authors recommend stocking razorback suckers into the wild at least 9 months after hatching to reduce predation.
- They note that no large streams in the historic range lack nonnative fishes, so reintroduction streams may need to be selected or prepared through [[Chemical Renovation]]. They suggest the upper Salt River in Arizona as a candidate.
- Their central conclusion is that larval razorback suckers are unlikely to survive in habitats supporting high numbers of nonnative fishes, because fish that do not avoid predators are more likely to be eaten.
