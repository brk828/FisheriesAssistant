# Biogeographic History of Stoneflies (Plecoptera) in the Southern Hemisphere

#entomology #plecoptera #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented by [[Joachim Illies]] at the XI International Congress of Entomology (Vienna, 1960), reconstructs the distribution history of the insect order [[Plecoptera]] (stoneflies). Illies argues that the order originated in the Antarctic region of the Southern Hemisphere during the Permian, crossed the equator into the Northern Hemisphere through a land bridge in the Americas, and later returned south in two separate waves. The argument rests on the present distribution of recent families, a revised phylogeny, and the sparse fossil record.

## Background & Description

Stoneflies are larval aquatic insects that live almost exclusively in fast-flowing, cold, oxygen-rich waters. Adults are usually weak fliers that stay close to the water's edge. Because they are ecological specialists with poor dispersal ability, strict requirements for stable environmental conditions, and a great geological age, they are well suited to biogeographic analysis. Earlier European work by Illies (1953, 1955) had already shown this potential. Morphological revisions of the order (Ricker 1950; Illies 1960a, 1961) made a worldwide analysis of its distribution history possible.

The order contains 16 recent families in three suborders:

- **[[Archiperlaria]]**: the most primitive group. The [[Eustheniidae]] are considered morphologically closest to the ancestral form of all stoneflies. This suborder includes the Diamphipnoidae.
- **[[Setipalpia]]**: five families. Larvae are predatory, adults have reduced mouthparts and generally do not feed, and body size is often considerable.
- **[[Filipalpia]]**: nine families. Species are small, and larvae, and to a limited extent adults, feed on detritus and plant material.

Illies treats the Setipalpia and Filipalpia as phylogenetically linked to the Archiperlaria. He describes the phylogenetic sequence of the Filipalpia as beginning in the Southern Hemisphere, passing to the Northern Hemisphere, and returning south again. Illies had already covered the Setipalpia sequence in a separate publication, so this paper focuses mainly on the Filipalpia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most stonefly families have sharply limited ranges outside the tropics. Illies attributes this to their cold stenothermal physiology, which keeps them out of the tropical belt. The more unusual pattern is the near-absence of bipolar distributions, which are common in other insect families with similar ecological requirements. Instead, almost all families occupy only one hemisphere.

Several groups show exceptions to this rule:

- **Perlidae** (Setipalpia) are centred on the temperate Old World but extend into tropical parts of the Oriental region. One genus and species, [[Neoperla Spio]], occurs across all of Africa. The subfamily Acroneuriinae occurs throughout the tropical and temperate Neotropis.
- **Capniidae** (Filipalpia) are restricted to the northern temperate Holarctic in the subfamily Capniinae. The subfamily Notonemourinae, however, occurs only in southern temperate latitudes: in Australia and nearby islands, in South America, in southern Africa, and in Madagascar.

Illies notes the ecological logic of the group's northern expansion. Stoneflies entering the Holarctic found "empty niches" in its flowing waters and could diversify without competition. As a result, the number of families, genera, and species that evolved there is considerably larger than in the Southern Hemisphere.

Fossil evidence contributes to the timeline. The Southern Hemisphere fossil [[Stenoperlidium Permianum]], a eustheniid described by Tillyard (1935) from the Australian Permian, is the only Paleozoic stonefly known. Taeniopterygid wing impressions from the Lower Lias of Shurab, Persia (Martynov 1937), show that the group was already present in the Northern Hemisphere by the Jurassic. Upper Permian deposits in North America and Russia contain many Blattoidea but no true stoneflies.

## Key Findings

**1. Origin in the Antarctic region.** The sequence of recent distributions and phylogenetic relationships indicates that the whole order Plecoptera originated in the Antarctic region of the Southern Hemisphere. Illies relies on Hennig's (1960) reasoning about [[Austral Group Distribution]] (AS-groups). The Eustheniidae, distributed only in Australia and South America, consist of closely related sister groups (Eustheniinae and Stenoperlinae) that are themselves AS-groups. This pattern suggests that their development took place on the [[Antarctic Continent]].

**2. Primary transgression across the equator (Permian).** Ancestors of the Filipalpia crossed the equator from the south during the Permian, most likely during the [[Permian Glaciation]]. They reached the Northern Hemisphere through a land bridge whose route is inferred to follow the [[Cordillera]] of the Americas. The Cordillera is still a continuous mountain chain, interrupted only in Central America, and similar connections are assumed for earlier periods. Illies links this crossing to the Setipalpia, which he describes as a similar, Pteronarcid-like form that arrived together with the Filipalpia.

**3. Northern diversification.** After arriving in the Holarctic, the Filipalpia radiated into new families and genera, including the [[Taeniopterygidae]], [[Leuctridae]], and [[Nemouridae]]. Illies considers this radiation to have been unusually rapid and to have produced far more families, genera, and species in the north than in the south.

**4. Timing of the Jurassic record.** Taeniopterygid wings from the Lias establish that the group was present in the Northern Hemisphere by that time. The lack of true stoneflies in Upper Permian rocks of North America and Russia, combined with the presence of the first eustheniid in New Zealand in a similar period, fixes the northward migration to the Permian or Triassic to Lias.

**5. Secondary transgression, first wave (Upper Cretaceous).** The ancestors of the [[Notonemourinae]] returned southward in the Upper Cretaceous, probably around the Cretaceous to Eocene period. Their genera are now endemic to the southern tips of all southern continents and to offshore islands, including Madagascar, New Zealand, and Tasmania. Land connections between Madagascar and Africa, between Australia and Asia, and with New Zealand and Tasmania were still intact at that time. Illies dates this from mammal palaeontology (marsupials reaching Australia and edentates reaching South America).

**6. Secondary transgression, second wave (late Tertiary).** The [[Perlidae]] subfamily [[Acroneuriinae]] crossed the equator much later, during the late Tertiary. Illies links this to the "Felidenschicht" of South America, when felids spread from the Nearctic into the Neotropis. The Acroneuriinae therefore probably entered South America at that time. The species [[Neoperla Spio]] reached Africa from Central Asia at the same time or somewhat later, in the late Tertiary or Quaternary.

**Overall model.** Illies summarises the history as a three-stage cycle: origin in the Permian Antarctic, a northward crossing via South America during the Permian ice age, and two later southward waves that produced the present disjunct southern distributions. The paper proposes this as the most consistent explanation of the present distribution, though it rests largely on inference from fossils, phylogeny, and the geological history of land connections rather than on direct dating.
