# Colorado River Basin Native Fishes: Literature Compilation on Humpback Chub and Associated Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled bibliography and set of extracted notes (roughly 1950s to 1990s) on fishes of the upper and lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its central theme is the decline of endemic large-river fishes, especially the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and the roles of dam construction, non-native species, [[Rotenone Treatment]], and altered flow and temperature regimes. The material includes survey results from [[Glen Canyon]], [[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], [[Grand Canyon]], [[Little Colorado River]] and reservoirs such as [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Lake Powell]]. The text is largely annotated citations and field notes, with no single new study. Parts of the OCR text are garbled, so some numbers below are reported with the uncertainty noted.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River system holds a fish fauna with high endemism. Early surveys noted that of 17 species encountered in [[Glen Canyon]] in the 1959 survey, only 6 were native (Woodbury 1959). Most of the balance had been introduced, and the native fauna is described as "a unique group" with remarkable endemism (Kidd 1977).

The main taxa discussed include:

- **[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)**: A canyon-adapted species with a pronounced nuchal hump. It was described only in the 1940s (Miller 1946), and its taxonomic status has been debated. Holden (1958) examined 309 individuals (130 *G. robusta*, 99 *G. elegans*, 16 *G. cypha*, 55 *elegans x cypha*, 3 *robusta x elegans*) using 19 characters. Intergrades complicate classification.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)**: Considered a large-river species. In Lake Mohave, spawning was observed on gravel, and one 12-inch female was estimated to carry about 1?,000 eggs (figure garbled in OCR). Growth and feeding were compared with the roundtail (Hagen and Banks, Vanicek 1967).
- **[[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)**: Sometimes separated morphologically and ecologically from the bonytail. Samples from Glen Canyon yielded only a few immature specimens.
- **[[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**: Once common in the lower basin and reservoirs. Hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] have been known since Jordan (1891).
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)**: Formerly abundant; early settlers reportedly could pitchfork them from the river (USFWS 1981).
- Non-native species include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and red shiner, which spread through the basin during the 20th century.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Dam construction and flow regulation are the dominant threads. [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (closed 1962) and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (closed November 1962) changed temperature and flow patterns. Native fish were nearly eliminated in the first 7 miles below the dam, and reduced native fish numbers extended downstream to the mouth of the [[Yampa River]] at Echo Park (Vanicek et al. 1970). Cold tailwaters below Glen Canyon Dam were too cold for successful spawning of native species (Miller 1975; Minckley and Blinn 1976).

Interactions with non-natives were considered significant. Channel catfish, carp, and the roughly 12 exotic species recorded by Vanicek et al. (1970) were implicated in competition and possibly predation. Analysis of stomach contents from flannelmouth suckers and carp showed similar diets, suggesting competition (Woodbury 1959). Channel catfish were described as abundant in [[Lake Powell]] tailwaters and were noted as a possible factor in squawfish declines (Stone and Queenan 1967; Kaeding and Zimmerman).

Habitat associations: Humpback chub are associated with fast, deep, turbulent water and shoreline eddies (Karp and Tyus 1990; Miller 1980). Juveniles prefer backwaters and low velocities (Holden 1977). The Little Colorado River (LCR) was identified as critical habitat, and the Colorado River between the LCR and Shinumo Creek was also termed critical (Suttkus et al. 1976; Minckley 1977).

Water temperature is important for reproduction. Spawning was reported at 16–18 °C (Harnamm 1982; Kaeding and Zimmerman), and incubation success was highest near 20 °C (Marsh 1985). Embryos incubated at 12–13 °C died after 110 hours (Hamman note).

## Key Findings

**Population status and distribution**

- Humpback chub were common in Lake Powell soon after closure of Glen Canyon Dam in 1962 but declined steadily; few were collected later (Holden and Stalnaker 1975; Holden 1973).
- Humpback chub were eliminated from the Green River above the Yampa mouth after Flaming Gorge closure in 1962 (Vanicek et al. 1970).
- Minckley (1989) estimated the LCR humpback chub population at 578 individuals in 1987.
- Surveys of the [[Grand Canyon]] found 6 humpback chub in the main river in August 1968 (Miller and Smith 1972).
- Stone (1966) found humpback chub "RARE" and bonytail "COMMON" in Glen Canyon.
- Karp and Tyus (1990) recorded humpback chub at less than 1% of catch in Yampa whitewater reaches (n=130) and one in a whirlpool canyon.
- Adult humpback chub showed high site fidelity: 10 of 11 recaptures were in the same eddy as their initial capture (Karp et al. 1990).
- Kaeding and Zimmerman tagged 154 humpback chub at LCR mouth and 253 at LCR sites, with additional uncounted fish caught.

**Threats and management**

- Rotenone treatment in 1962 killed fish and aquatic insects in Dinosaur National Monument, apparently from downstream toxicant transport (Kramer 1967).
- The introduction of over 24 million salmonids and other fish into Flaming Gorge Reservoir in its first 8 years was documented (Varley et al. 1971).
- Warm-water modifications to Flaming Gorge outlet structures (1978) increased downstream temperatures, promoting warm-water species such as red shiner and sand shiner upstream and reducing cold-water species (Holden and Crist 1981).
- Humpback chub were federally listed as endangered on 11 March 1967 (USFWS 1967).

**Biology**

- Bonytail growth exceeded roundtail growth after the third year; mean annual increments were significantly smaller after impoundment (Vanicek 1967).
- Humpback chub stomach contents were mostly terrestrial insects and plant debris (Vanicek 1967); roundtail feeding was more sporadic.
- Holden (1958) found that roundtail and bonytail were well separated morphologically, while the relationship between *G. elegans* and *G. cypha* remained unclear due to intergrades.
- Muth (1990) and Valdez (1990) addressed early life stages and recovery prospects.

**Recommendations**

- Studies to clarify the taxonomy of the *Gila robusta* complex and *G. cypha* were recommended, along with ecological and genetic work to protect rarer forms (Smith 1969).
- Flow scenarios favoring humpback chub reproduction included higher spring flows and steady summer flows (USDI 1988).
- Backwater refuges for native fish should be sealed from non-native fish and managed separately (Valdez and Wick 1981).
