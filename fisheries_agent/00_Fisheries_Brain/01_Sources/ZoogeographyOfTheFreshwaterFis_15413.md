# Zoogeography of the Freshwater Fishes of Cascadia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #zoogeography #cyprinidae #salmonidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter reviews the zoogeography of the freshwater fishes of [[Cascadia]], the region west of the Continental Divide bounded by the [[Columbia River]] to the south and the [[Stikine River]] to the north. The region supports 61 native freshwater fish species, a small fauna attributed mainly to Pleistocene glaciation, which covered over three-quarters of the land area. Most of the present fauna derives from the unglaciated southern Columbia system, which served as the principal glacial refuge. Postglacial dispersal proceeded inland through shifting drainage connections and along the coast through the sea. The authors argue that salinity tolerance (euryhaline versus stenohaline) governs dispersal routes, and that the northward decline in species richness reflects the loss of stenohaline species.

## Background & Description

The authors define Cascadia as a mountain-dominated region with north-south ranges running from the coast to the Continental Divide. Its rivers mostly flow west to the Pacific, with the exception of the arid [[Snake River Plain]] and [[Columbia Plateau]]. The region contains hundreds of short coastal rivers and numerous interior lakes, ponds and swamps.

Of the 92 freshwater species recorded in Cascadia, 61 are native and 31 are introduced. Salinity tolerance is inferred from present distributions rather than measured directly: species that have crossed sea barriers are classed as euryhaline, and those that have not as stenohaline. Table 16.1 in the source gives the full list of species and their inferred tolerances across river systems.

The chapter also notes several species with multiple glacial origins, including [[Prosopium Coulteri]], [[Longnose Dace]] (Rhinichthys cataractae), [[Largescale Sucker]] (Catostomus catostomus) and [[Cottus Cognatus]]. These show a characteristic pattern of geographic variation in which one morphological form is continuous across a large area and an abrupt transition marks a barrier such as a waterfall or canyon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology.** Miocene basalt flows covered over 200,000 km² in central British Columbia, and the [[Columbia Basalts]] covered comparable areas in Washington, Oregon and Idaho. Cinder-cone volcanism in the mid-Pliocene to Recent deposited ash over large areas. Mountain building in the Miocene and Pliocene produced the Coast and Cascade ranges. Most major northwestern rivers have maintained their west-flowing courses since at least the Pliocene. The [[Snake River]] was an independent, southwest-flowing drainage until its connection to the Columbia in the Early Pleistocene.

**Glaciation.** The [[Cordilleran Ice Sheet]] covered much of the region during the last glaciation. Southern British Columbia was ice-free 25,000 to 20,000 years ago, and the Fraser glaciation peaked around 15,000 years ago. Ice dams produced catastrophic floods, including [[Glacial Lake Missoula]], which drained in less than two weeks and scoured the Columbia Plateau. Pluvial lakes in the Great Basin spilled into the upper Snake River, providing a dispersal route.

**Refugia.** The ice-free southern two-thirds of the Columbia system was the major refuge. A second refuge existed west of the Cascades in the [[Chehalis River]] system. A possible minor central-coast refuge is suggested by [[Queen Charlotte Islands]] plants and unusual black sticklebacks.

**Deglaciation and dispersal.** Retreat began around 14,000 years ago. Glacial lakes in the Okanagan, Thompson and Shuswap areas linked the Fraser and Columbia systems, while marine incursions severed coastal connections early. Euryhaline species dispersed along the coast, while stenohaline species used interior connections.

**Present faunas by river.** Table 16.4 summarizes native species counts and faunal similarity to the Columbia (excluding the upper Snake and Wood rivers):

|---|---|---|---|
| Columbia | 45 | 13 | 100 |
| Chehalis | 34 | 7 | 85.1 |
| Fraser | 39 | 8 | 83.7 |
| Skeena | 32 | 4 | 77.5 |
| Nass | 27 | 4 | 80.0 |
| Stikine | 27 | 0 | 71.1 |

**Preglacial fauna.** Fossils from the [[Glenns Ferry Formation]] and [[Deer Butte Formation]] record a diverse fauna in [[Lake Idaho]]. Of 22 genera, 11 still live in the region, four are extinct, and seven are extinct in Cascadia but extant elsewhere. The authors conclude the preglacial fauna was similar to, but somewhat more diverse than, today's.

**Upper Snake and Wood rivers.** [[Shoshone Falls]] is a total barrier to upstream fish movement. Eighteen species occur only below the falls, three only above, and 12 on both sides. The upper Snake and Wood rivers share only 35% and 40% average faunal resemblance with the Columbia, and both contain endemic taxa.

**Historic change.** Since the mid-19th century, agriculture, irrigation, urbanization, dams and reservoirs have altered inland waters. Streams that once supported salmon were turbid and salmon-free by 1900. Thirty-five exotic species have been introduced, and the presence of native [[Threespine Stickleback]] in lowland lakes appears linked to the introduction of [[Brown Bullhead]] (Ictalurus nebulosus), which can eliminate sticklebacks through nest predation.

## Key Findings

- Cascadia has 61 native freshwater fish species, against 31 introduced, out of 92 recorded.
- Glaciation is the principal explanation for the small fauna; the southern Columbia system was the main ice-free refuge and source of most present species.
- Dispersal ran inland through shifting drainage connections and along the coast through the sea. Salinity tolerance, inferred from distribution, determined which route each species used.
- Several widespread species, including Prosopium coulteri, Rhinichthys cataractae and Catostomus catostomus, entered Cascadia from more than one refuge, producing distinct morphological forms.
- Species richness declines northward mainly through the loss of stenohaline species, and faunal similarity to the Columbia drops from 85.1% (Chehalis) to 71.1% (Stikine).
- The Stikine is a transition zone between the Columbia fauna and a Bering fauna, with two Bering species (Osmerus mordax and Thymallus arcticus).
- Rapid divergence of sympatric lampreys ([[Lampetra macrostoma]]) and threespine sticklebacks in the Strait of Georgia area is attributed to post-glacial isolation and multiple marine invasions.
- Introduced species, especially Ictalurus nebulosus, may cause local extinctions, with the effect depending on habitat.
- The authors conclude that postglacial dispersal dominates distribution patterns, while physiological or ecological factors explain the absence of some mid-river species from the lower Columbia and lower Fraser.
