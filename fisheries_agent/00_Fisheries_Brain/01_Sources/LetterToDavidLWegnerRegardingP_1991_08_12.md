## Executive Summary
This document is a 1991 administrative letter from Dr. Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) to David L. Wegner (Glen Canyon Environmental Studies) concerning logistical challenges in a field research program studying [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the [[Little Colorado River]], Arizona. The letter does not present original morphological or ecological data but instead documents an unexpectedly high rate of fish capture and tagging activity during the initial field season, resulting in a shortage of [[PIT Tags]] (Passive Integrated Transponder tags) used for individual fish identification and tracking. The correspondence provides indirect but valuable evidence regarding population density, catch rates, and the scale of mark-recapture research on this endangered cyprinid.

## Background & Description
No direct morphological data (e.g., length, weight, meristics) are provided in this letter. The document is procedural/administrative in nature. However, the use of [[PIT Tags]] implies that captured individuals were of sufficient size to internally or externally accommodate tag implantation, consistent with standard practice for subadult and adult [[Humpback Chub]] in mark-recapture studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: The study population of [[Humpback Chub]] occupies the [[Little Colorado River]], a major tributary of the [[Colorado River]] in Arizona, historically recognized as critical habitat for this federally endangered species.
- **Study Context**: Research was conducted under a cooperative agreement between [[Arizona State University]] and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], reflecting the fish's status as a species of conservation concern under the broader [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program, which assesses downstream ecological effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations.
- **Population Dynamics Inference**: The unexpectedly high number of fish handled during the initial six-week field period (1 July–August 1991) suggests a higher-than-anticipated catchable population density or capture efficiency in the sampled reach of the Little Colorado River during summer months.
- **Seasonal Variation**: The author anticipates a decline in catch rates into autumn, suggesting a seasonal pattern in fish activity, movement, or aggregation (possibly linked to spawning behavior, water temperature, or flow conditions) that affects capture probability in the [[Little Colorado River]].

## Key Findings
- Field operations for [[Humpback Chub]] research began 1 July 1991 under Cooperative Agreement 1-FC-40-10490 between [[Arizona State University]] and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]].
- Within approximately six weeks, researchers nearly exhausted their allocated supply of [[PIT Tags]], despite receiving an additional delivery of ~500 tags.
- The volume of fish captured and tagged substantially exceeded pre-season predictions by both the research team and tag suppliers, indicating either an underestimated population size or higher-than-expected capture success in the [[Little Colorado River]].
- Researchers requested a minimum standing inventory of 1,000 PIT tags prior to each subsequent field trip (initiating 1 and 22 September 1991) to avoid interruption of tagging operations.
- The letter highlights logistical vulnerabilities in large-scale mark-recapture programs for endangered fish species, where supply chain constraints (tag availability) can directly threaten data continuity and research integrity.
- Anticipated seasonal decline in catch rates by autumn suggests temporal variability in [[Humpback Chub]] activity or accessibility, an ecologically relevant pattern for future sampling design.
- This correspondence serves as indirect historical evidence of the scale and intensity of early 1990s [[Humpback Chub]] population monitoring efforts tied to the broader [[Glen Canyon Dam]] adaptive management and environmental impact studies.
