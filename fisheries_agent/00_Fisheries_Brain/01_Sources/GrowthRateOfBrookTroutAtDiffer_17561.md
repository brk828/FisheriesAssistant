# Growth Rate of Brook Trout at Different Population Densities in a Small Infertile Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by [[Edwin L Cooper]], Joseph A. Boccardy and John K. Andersen examines why [[Brook Trout]] grow slowly in a small headwater stream, Mud Lick Run, a branch of Kinzua Creek in the [[Allegheny National Forest]] near Warren, Pennsylvania. The authors tested the common belief that competition for food and space limits trout growth by removing most of the trout population with [[Rotenone Sampling]] in 1958 and again in 1960. A severe population reduction produced only a slight increase in growth. The authors conclude that competition is at most a minor factor, that other limits such as low winter temperature and scarce food are more important, and that streams of this type should be managed for a moderate number of small trout rather than under a 6-inch minimum size limit.

## Background & Description

Brook trout are common in small Appalachian headwater streams, where populations are typically dominated by small fish, with most individuals needing three or more years to reach 6 inches. Earlier work in lakes and ponds showed faster brook trout growth when competition was reduced, but the authors argued that stream populations may respond differently.

Mud Lick Run rises at about 1,960 feet on the [[Allegheny Plateau]] and falls 660 feet over 3 miles. The watershed is fully forested, the bed is mainly stones and boulders, and the water is soft and infertile (total alkalinity under 20 ppm, pH near neutral). Aquatic plants are rare and the standing crop of fish is under 30 pounds per acre. The stream is divided into three sections:

- Lower section: 0.5 mi long, 15 ft wide, 0.91 acre, gradient 100 ft/mi
- Middle section: 1.0 mi long, 8 ft wide, 0.97 acre, gradient 180 ft/mi
- Upper section: 1.0 mi long, 4 ft wide, 0.73 acre, gradient 285 ft/mi (table 1 gives lengths, widths and areas as recorded)

The lower section held nine fish species, including [[Brown Trout]], brook trout, [[Creek Chub]], [[Blacknose Dace]] and [[Mottled Sculpin]], among others. The middle section was dominated by brook trout and mottled sculpins, and the upper section almost entirely by brook trout.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Methods centered on a 3,700-foot portion of the middle section. Rotenone treatments on August 8, 1958 and August 8, 1960 at low flow removed virtually the whole population. In 1958, 543 brook trout were collected and no fish were restocked. A re-poisoning of a 100-yard section on October 6, 1960 yielded only one fish. Ages were determined from scales (winter marks readable, brown trout scales especially clear). The brook trout population was mostly age-groups 0 and I, while the brown trout included older age-groups up to VIII.

Growth comparisons showed that males grew slightly faster than females in some age groups, but the differences were small. A possible sex bias in age-group II fish from fishing mortality was noted. Brook trout in the upper section grew more slowly than those in the lower section. Within a density range of about 15 to 30 pounds per acre, growth rate correlated with population density for young-of-the-year and yearling fish.

The authors noted that competition may slightly reduce growth, but that low winter temperature and general food scarcity appeared to have a marked effect. Feeding territory size may also be influenced by bottom topography, as reported by [[Kalleberg]] (1958) for brown trout.

## Key Findings

- Reducing the trout population by about 543 brook trout through rotenone sampling produced only a slight increase in growth for age-groups 0 and I, so competition is not the sole cause of slow growth.
- Age-group II growth declined after the reduction, which the authors attributed to downstream movement of fish treated in 1958.
- Only 1.7 percent of brook trout collected in 1958 (9 of 543) exceeded the 6-inch legal minimum; in 1960 only 2.8 percent (12 of 436) did.
- Brown trout remained in greater numbers and larger sizes after the season (34 of 92 in the lower section over 6 inches; largest 15.9 inches), reflecting older age structure rather than faster growth.
- Many brook trout were mature at 4.5 to 5.5 inches, and the population recovered rapidly after heavy losses.
- The authors recommend that a 6-inch minimum size limit is of questionable value for streams like Mud Lick Run and that such streams should be managed to produce a moderate number of small trout.
