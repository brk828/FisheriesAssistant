# Hydrologic Variability and Organic Matter Dynamics in a Desert Watershed-Reservoir Ecosystem

#ichthyology #limnology #reservoir-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This is a 36-month research proposal to the [[National Science Foundation]] under the NSF/EPA Partnership for Environmental Research (NSF 95-48), requesting $768,892 with a start date of 1 October 1995. The lead PI is Lawrence A. Baker (Civil Engineering, Arizona State University), with co-PIs James J. Elser (Zoology) and W.L. Minckley (Zoology, fisheries). The study examines how extreme hydrologic variability in a desert reservoir, [[San Carlos Reservoir]] on the [[Gila River]], controls the relative supply of externally derived (allochthonous) and internally produced (autochthonous) organic matter (OM) to the reservoir food web. The central hypothesis is that the allochthonous:autochthonous ratio shifts predictably with hydrologic regime. The approach combines a partial OM budget, [[Stable Isotope Analysis]] of food web compartments including fishes, and [[Paleolimnology]] of sediment cores spanning about 67 years.

## Background & Description

**Motivation.** The proposal states that surface water reservoirs supply much of the municipal water in arid regions. It reports that 68% of the municipal water supply for western U.S. cities with populations above 50,000 comes from lakes or reservoirs. It also cites projections that the global population experiencing chronic water stress will rise from 335 million in 1990 to 3.0 billion in 2025 (Engleman and LeRoy, 1993). Desert watershed-reservoir ecosystems are described as barely studied.

**Hydrologic extremes.** The defining feature is flashiness. For 1000–2000 mi² watersheds in central Arizona, the reported peak flow/mean flow ratio is 660:1, compared with under 70:1 in midwestern and eastern states. The ratio of maximum annual to average annual discharge is 6.2 for central Arizona and under 3.0 for the Midwest and East (Linsley et al., 1982). The Project Summary gives a related figure of a tenfold higher peak:average ratio for medium-sized watersheds. Desert reservoirs can flood in one year and become nearly dry in drought.

**Study site.** San Carlos Reservoir was formed by [[Coolidge Dam]], built in 1928. Key characteristics reported:
- The watershed drains about 30,000 km²; about 90% of inflow comes from the Gila River and 10% from the San Carlos River.
- The reservoir is about 40 km long, with maximum capacity given as 1.4 × 10^10 m³ (OCR-corrected from the source).
- Water level changes above 30 m are not uncommon, and the reservoir has nearly dried several times.
- Inflow TDS was about 2000 mg/L in 1989 (Ca-Mg type with NaCl signature from saline springs).
- Turbidity exceeds 10 g/L during floods. The reservoir is monomictic, with strong summer stratification and hypolimnetic oxygen depletion.
- Average runoff is about 1 cm/yr, giving a mean flow of 9 m³/s. The recorded peak flow is 4,225 m³/s.
- The Safford Valley reach is Arizona's first Nonpoint Source Management Area and has been called Arizona's "most threatened river."
- The reservoir is a recreational fishery and an important income source for the [[San Carlos Apache Nation]], which owns or controls much of the surrounding land.

**Team and collaborators.** The proposal names Baker (biogeochemistry), Elser (stoichiometric limnology), Minckley (fisheries), [[Larry Benson]] (isotope geochemistry and paleoecology, U.S. Geological Survey, subcontract) and [[Don Charles]] (diatoms and paleoecology, Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia, subcontract). Letters of intent from Benson and from the Academy are included. The San Carlos Apache Recreation and Wildlife Department supports the project, pending tribal council approval.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Organic matter sources.** In lakes and reservoirs, OM is either allochthonous (imported from the watershed) or autochthonous (produced in situ). The proposal argues that runoff alters both imports and in situ primary production (PPr). Turbidity during floods can shade phytoplankton, while high nutrient loading may offset this effect.

**Management relevance.** The authors link OM supply to:
- Taste and odor problems and trihalomethane (THM) precursors
- Hypolimnetic anoxia and associated reduction of Fe, Mn and As
- Carbon (BOD) and nutrient loading criteria
- Fisheries management and watershed management
- Evaluation of mitigation for grazing, logging and nonpoint pollution

**Land use.** Watershed land use includes irrigation agriculture in the Safford Valley, return flows that increase TDS, mining at higher elevations, and grazing throughout the basin. Basin population is about 20,000, mostly near Safford.

**Climate.** The region has a biseasonal precipitation pattern. Precipitation ranges from 32 cm at about 1000 m to 75 cm on the highest peaks. Summer convective monsoon rains fall in July–August.

**Fish community.** Non-indigenous fishes introduced for food, sport or forage dominate the reservoir. No indigenous species remain abundant enough to sample effectively. Ten common to abundant taxa are reported (Minckley 1973). The five named in the proposal are [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Common Carp]], [[Black Crappie]] and [[Bluegill]]. The proposal states that all ten can be sampled with seines, gillnets and electrofishing.

## Key Findings

**Central hypothesis.** Hydrologic variability alters the relative importance of allochthonous and autochthonous OM in reservoir metabolism. The proposal states five testable predictions:

1. **Lake-wide, inter-annual:** Imported OM dominates in wet years, and in situ PPr dominates in dry years. Nutrient loading may modify this pattern.
2. **Within-lake, intra-annual:** Following the "river-lake hybrid" concept (Thornton et al., 1990), the in situ PPr share will vary between "up-lake" and "down-lake" reaches, with up-lake OM resembling riverine and terrestrial signatures.
3. **Whole-lake, inter-annual (consumers):** Short-lived consumers (bacteria, zooplankton, young-of-year fish) will track riverine or phytoplankton signatures depending on inflow. This tests a shift between a "conventional" food web and a "microbial loop."
4. **Within-lake, intra-annual:** Short-lived consumers near river inputs will show more riverine signatures.
5. **Whole-lake, decadal:** Sediment cores will show a consistent relationship between carbon source (isotopes) and hydrologic regime (inferred from δ¹⁸O and other markers).

**Methods.**
- **Hydrologic budget:** A 70-year water budget from gauged inflows and outflows, with evaporation estimated from open-water rates and lake area. Errors will be checked with a chloride or TDS budget.
- **OM budget:** Perennial streams sampled 20–30 times per year with flow-stratified sampling. Regression and Beale's ratio methods will be tested. Dry washes will be sampled at storm events, with about a dozen storms targeted over three years. Atmospheric deposition will be collected with a wet/dry collector. Bulk precipitation TOC is reported as about 5–6 mg/L.
- **Reservoir sampling:** Four primary stations plus five auxiliary stations, sampled bi-weekly and weekly during floods. PPr will be measured by ¹⁴C incubations at seven light levels (0–60% incident irradiance).
- **Isotopes:** Analysis of δ¹³C, δ¹⁵N and δ³⁴S across watershed plants, DOM, phytoplankton, bacteria, zooplankton, benthos and fishes. Phytoplankton signatures will be assessed by three approaches: seston size-fractionation, in situ *Scenedesmus* culture, and chlorophyll isotopes.
- **Fish sampling:** Young-of-year fish sampled seasonally. Two- and three-year-old fish captured in autumn of years 2 and 3. In year 3, 8–10 large adults of four long-lived species will be analyzed. Ages will be estimated from otoliths.
- **Sediment coring:** Four cores using a 6-foot Kullenberg piston corer, guided by seismic survey. ²¹⁰Pb dating is not expected to work because total accumulation is likely to exceed 15 m. Chronology will rely on the known hydrologic record and on markers including δ¹⁸O, diatoms, particle size, Pb and DDT.
- **Diatoms:** Planktonic versus riverine diatom proportions are proposed as a direct index of the allochthonous:autochthonous balance.

**Expected outcomes.** The authors expect δ¹⁸O to rise during drought and fall during wet periods. They also expect the results to support predictions about climate change effects on arid ecosystems (Carpenter et al., 1992), nutrient and BOD loading criteria, evaluation of nonpoint source mitigation (the Gila is a designated Nonpoint Source Management Area), and new sediment-based ecological indicators for regional monitoring.

**Analytical capacity.** ASU's Zoology Department has a Europa Scientific isotope-ratio mass spectrometer. The proposal states a per-sample cost under $10, compared with $160 per plant sample at Woods Hole and charges above $100 elsewhere. About 1,500 samples are expected per year.

**Budget (3 years, 36 months).**
- Total direct costs: $611,044. Indirect costs at 26% of MTDC: $157,848. Total: $768,892.
- Annual totals: Year 1 $295,286; Year 2 $251,758; Year 3 $221,848.
- Major items: senior personnel salaries, one post-doc, two technicians, one Ph.D. student, about $20,300 in permanent equipment (including a flowmeter, a recording raingauge, a wet/dry collector and a LI-COR radiometer), $69,359 in materials and supplies, $84,600 in subcontracts and about $15,000 per year for mass spectrometry.
- Subcontracts: Benson (coring, δ¹⁸O analysis, seismic survey, trips) and Charles (diatom counts of about 120 samples at $200 each, $50,000 total). A further $7,500 per year supports on-reservation Apache guides and field assistants.
- Travel: about 40 trips per year to the reservoir (120 miles from campus), with $12,519 requested for in-state travel.

**Broader significance.** The proposal argues the study will be the first to link long-term hydrologic records with OM supply in an arid river-reservoir system, and the first to apply stable isotopes to OM sources in such a system. It also commits to involving Apache students through summer field employment and the university's undergraduate research programs.
