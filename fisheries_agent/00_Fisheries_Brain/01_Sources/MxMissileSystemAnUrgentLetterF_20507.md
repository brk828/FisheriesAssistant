# Urgent Letter from Desert Fishes Council Regarding MX Missile System Threat

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #desert-ecology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This December 1979 urgent letter from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] to NANFA members warns of catastrophic threats to desert aquatic ecosystems in [[Nevada]] and [[Utah]] from the proposed [[MX Missile System]]. The $33 billion Department of Defense project would construct 200 "racetrack loops" with 23 missile launch sites each, 5,000-8,000 miles of highways, railroads, and communities for 10,000-15,000 workers across 30-40 valleys in mid-Nevada and mid-western Utah. The project threatens numerous endemic and endangered fish species through groundwater depletion, habitat destruction, and increased human impacts. The letter calls for immediate congressional action to prevent exemption from environmental laws and reconsider this defense alternative. An accompanying article describes the [[Desert Fishes Council]]'s establishment in 1970, its expansion to cover all American southwestern deserts, and its Eleventh Annual Symposium held November 15-17, 1979 at [[Death Valley National Monument]].

## Background & Description

The [[Desert Fishes Council]] was established in 1970 in response to serious threats facing native fishes of the [[Death Valley]] region. Initial focus centered on pupfishes of genus [[Cyprinodon]], especially the endangered [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon diabolis]]). Annual symposia have been held in Southwestern states since 1969 to exchange information on status, protection, and management of desert fishes and their habitats.

By 1979, the Council's interests expanded beyond [[Death Valley]] to encompass all American deserts of the Southwest and Mexico, including the [[Colorado River]] basin and its former Pleistocene tributaries. The organization divided these arid regions into 12 areas, each with a responsible coordinator. Membership exceeded 300 persons comprising federal, state, and university scientists, resource specialists, conservation organization members, and concerned individuals.

The [[MX Missile System]] represents what has been called "the largest public works project ever attempted by mankind." Each racetrack loop would span 20-30 miles with missile launch sites spaced every 7,000 feet. Construction would require groundwater systems as primary water sources for construction and municipal use. The entire project, designed for a 20-year life expectancy, would be completely abandoned at termination.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The affected region encompasses 30-40 valleys through mid-Nevada and mid-western Utah, representing critical habitat for numerous endemic desert fish species. These groundwater-dependent aquatic systems support highly specialized and geographically restricted populations that evolved in isolated spring and valley systems throughout the [[Great Basin Desert]].

The document identifies fish species in three categories:

**Directly Influenced Species (Nevada):**
- [[Big Smoky Valley]]: [[Rhinichthys osculus lariversi]] (Big Smoky Valley speckled dace), two undescribed [[Gila bicolor]] subspecies
- [[Grass Valley]]: [[Rhinichthys osculus reliquus]] (Grass Valley speckled dace, possibly extinct)
- [[Diamond Valley]]: [[Gila bicolor obesus]] (Lahontan tui chub, aberrant form), [[Rhinichthys osculus robustus]] (Lahontan speckled dace, aberrant form)
- [[Monitor Valley]]: [[Rhinichthys osculus robustus]] (Monitor Valley speckled dace, aberrant form)
- [[Newark Valley]]: [[Gila bicolor newarkensis]] (Newark Valley tui chub)
- [[Fish Creek Springs]]: [[Gila bicolor euchila]] (Fish Creek Springs tui chub)
- [[Clover Valley]]: [[Rhinichthys osculus oligoporus]] (Clover Valley speckled dace)
- [[Independence Valley]]: [[Rhinichthys osculus lethoporus]] (Independence Valley speckled dace), [[Gila bicolor isolata]] (Independence Valley tui chub)
- [[Railroad Valley]]: [[Crenichthys nevadae]] subspecies (Locke's Ranch springfish, Duckwater springfish), eight undescribed [[Gila bicolor]] subspecies
- [[Steptoe Valley]], [[Goshute Valley]], [[Butte Valley]], [[Ruby Valley]]: [[Relictus solitarius]] (relict dace)
- [[White River Valley]]: [[Crenichthys baileyi]] subspecies (Preston springfish), [[Rhinichthys osculus velifer]] (White River speckled dace), [[Pantosteus intermedius]] (White River mountainsucker), [[Lepidomeda albivalis]] (White River spinedace)
- [[Pahranagat Valley]]: [[Gila robusta jordani]] (Pahranagat roundtail chub), [[Lepidomeda altivalis]] (Pahranagat spinedace, possibly extinct), [[Crenichthys baileyi]] subspecies (Southern springfish)
- [[Muddy Valley]]: [[Moapa coriacea]] (Moapa dace), [[Gila robusta]] subspecies
- [[Reese River Valley]]: [[Salmo clarki henshawi]] (Lahontan cutthroat trout)
- [[Meadow Valley Wash]]: [[Lepidomeda mollispinis pratensis]] (Big Spring spinedace)
- [[Spring Valley]]: [[Empetrichthys latos latos]] (Pahrump killifish, refuge population), [[Relictus solitarius]] (relict dace, refuge population)

**Directly Influenced Species (Utah):**
- Throughout mid-west portion: [[Iotichthys phlegethontis]] (least chub)

**Secondarily Influenced Species:**
- [[Death Valley System]]: [[Cyprinodon diabolis]] (Devils Hole pupfish), multiple [[Cyprinodon nevadensis]] subspecies (Warm Springs pupfish, Ash Meadows pupfish, Amargosa River pupfish, Saratoga Springs pupfish), [[Rhinichthys osculus nevadensis]] (Amargosa speckled dace)
- [[Black Rock Desert]]: [[Eremichthys acros]] (desert dace)
- [[Summit Lake Area]]: [[Salmo clarki henshawi]] (Lahontan cutthroat trout)

The list notably includes many of Nevada's endemic fish species. Additional threats include nuclear waste disposal sites, vast groundwater removal, and increased human population bringing ORV users and other recreationists who may additionally impact and abuse aquatic systems.

## Key Findings

**Immediate Threats:**
1. Construction scheduled to begin in early 1982 despite Congress not having completely authorized funding
2. Test facility construction at [[Vandenberg Air Force Base]] already authorized and proceeding
3. Air Force seriously considering requesting Congressional exemption from all environmental laws
4. Extremely short time frame provides insufficient time for appropriate biological data collection
5. Groundwater removal and construction pose direct threats to aquatic habitats
6. Abrupt human population increase projected to bring additional impacts from recreation and abuse

**Conservation Actions Requested:**
1. Write Congress and the President questioning how such an environmentally unsound defense alternative could have been chosen
2. Demand Congress ignore Air Force petition to exempt MX from environmental laws
3. Immediate action required due to Air Force's rapid timeline

**Desert Fishes Council Activities:**
The Eleventh Annual Symposium (November 15-17, 1979) at [[Death Valley National Monument]] attracted nearly 200 attendees representing 24 universities across Mexico and the United States, multiple federal agencies ([[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Water and Power Resources Service]], [[National Park Service]]), state fish and game agencies from five states, six environmental consulting firms, two California regional water quality control boards, and ten public utilities, museums, and conservation groups.

The symposium featured 28 technical and research papers, reports from 12 Great Basin area coordinators and recovery teams, status update on the [[Endangered Species Act]] by Dr. Jim Williams (Washington Office of Endangered Species), and field trips to desert fish habitats in Nevada's [[Ash Meadows]] and [[Railroad Valley]]. The Council passed 13 resolutions, with major concern expressed regarding the MX Missile Defense System's potential devastating effect on aquatic ecosystems throughout the [[Great Basin]].

Future proceedings will include Spanish abstracts due to increasing involvement of Mexican biologists. The 1980 symposium was scheduled for November at the [[University of Nuevo Leon]] in [[Monterrey]], Mexico.

The Council's work during its first three years is summarized in E.P. Pister's 1974 paper "Desert Fishes and Their Habitats" (Transactions of the American Fisheries Society 103: 531-40). The organization operates through area coordinators and recovery teams, determining which fishes are endangered or threatened and implementing restoration recommendations for desert fishes and their habitats.
