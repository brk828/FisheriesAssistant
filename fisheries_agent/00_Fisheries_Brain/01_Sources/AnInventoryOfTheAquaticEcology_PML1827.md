# Aquatic Ecology of the Colorado River: Yuma Valley and Palo Verde Division Surveys (1974–1975)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This pair of reports by W. L. Minckley, submitted to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], inventories the aquatic ecology of the Yuma Valley (Arizona) and the Palo Verde Division of the mainstream [[Colorado River]]. The first report (August–September 1974) covers the mainstream Limnotrophe Division, canals, drains and backwaters, and reports about 40 fish species were obtained or expected in the area. The second report (January–April 1975) covers the Palo Verde Division, where physico-chemical conditions were different, higher vascular plants were essentially absent, and fish sampling was largely unsuccessful. The Yuma Valley waters are described as highly productive, dominated by introduced and non-native biota, and the Palo Verde results are considered inconclusive.

## Background & Description

- **Yuma Valley study area:** Approximately 60,000 acres (the document also gives about 50,000 acres of irrigated land in the abstract) in Yuma County, Arizona, bounded by the Colorado River, the U.S.–Mexico boundary, and the terrace escarpment. At least 80% is under intensive agriculture. Surface waters make up roughly 0.53–0.58% of the total acreage.
- **Surface water habitats (Table 1):** Colorado River mainstream below [[Morelos Dam]] (about 31% of surface waters), major canals, major drains, lateral canals and minor drains, and backwaters (Gadsen Lake, Hunter's Hole, off-river connectives).
- **Palo Verde Division:** The reach runs from about river mile 568.8 at the Palo Verde Irrigation Diversion (PVID) to about RM 596.6. It is mostly riprapped, with few natural or modified backwaters. Water levels fluctuate strongly, up to six feet in under an hour during later phases.
- **Methods:** Electronic instruments (e.g., Hydrolab) measured dissolved oxygen, conductance, pH, temperature, phosphate and turbidity, with Hach spectrophotometry for some parameters. Fish were collected with trap nets, gill nets, seines, visual counts and a pesticide fish-kill; standing crop was estimated from quadrats and excavation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mainstream (Yuma):** Waters near the Wellton-Mohawk Drain inflow were high in inorganic phosphate, with diel cycling in dissolved oxygen and pH indicating substantial primary productivity. Bottoms were largely reducing sapropel sediments. Standing crop and gross drift were highest where bottoms stabilized, mainly by [[Thin-Leaf Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), coontail and charophytes, along with *Bacopa monnieri*, recorded for the first time in Arizona in this study. The introduced clam *Corbicula manilensis* was present in the channel. Sand aggradation and human activity (swimming, fishing, bathing) reduced vegetation downstream.
- **Canals:** Conductance was consistently lower than in the river, usually by almost 3,000 µmhos/cm in many instances. Dissolved oxygen was generally higher. Annual turnover rates for higher plants were substantially higher than in the river. Canal clearing operations removed large volumes of vegetation.
- **Drains:** Conditions were intermediate between canals and river. Dissolved oxygen decreased to levels lethal to some fishes in the Southwest and South Central drains (<2.0 ppm), and temperatures exceeded 85°F in several drains. Standing crops were very high, exceeding 104 pounds per acre in 4 of 7 sampling areas. Cattail and sedge root systems contributed up to about 75% of biomass.
- **Backwaters:** Gadsen Lake (shallow, relatively clear, slow flow-through) and Hunter's Hole (deeper, wind-exposed, with a narrow inlet from the river) differed in stratification and oxygen profiles. Gadsen Lake had higher standing crops of higher plants per unit area than the deeper, turbid Hunter's Hole.
- **Palo Verde (1975):** Dissolved oxygen was inversely related to temperature, suggesting little production or respiration. Conductance and turbidity were erratic, and phosphate showed no diel depletion pattern, indicating a general lack of productivity in the reach during winter and early spring.

## Key Findings

- **Fish diversity (Yuma, 1974):** Sixteen species were obtained in the mainstream, with about 40 species recorded or expected across the Limnotrophe Division. Six species dominated numerically and in biomass: [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), [[Mozambique Mouthbrooder]] (*Tilapia mossambica*), poeciliids ([[Mosquitofish]] *Gambusia* and *Poeciliopsis*), and the native [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*).
- **Native fish status:** Only two native species (mullet and machete/threadfin shad) were taken in 1974. The mullet and machete populations in the lower Colorado are described as unique because they are landlocked, since the river no longer reaches the sea.
- **Sampling bias:** Trap nets missed the threadfin shad; seines avoided mullet; small-mesh seines over-represented juvenile tilapia. Visual counts and gill netting gave different estimates. The channel catfish appeared comparatively rare in the mainstream but was abundant in canals and drains and in young-of-year catches in laterals.
- **Regression analysis (Table 16):** Multiple regressions of fish numbers/weights against flow speed, temperature, dissolved oxygen and conductance showed that largemouth bass avoided the highest temperatures, carp and tilapia showed habitat relations, and tilapia results were inconsistent, so their ecology was judged poorly understood.
- **Overall Yuma conclusion:** Aquatic habitats were considered highly productive, with abundant game and food fishes, adequate nutrients, and plant and animal foods. Channel catfish abundance was unexplained.
- **Palo Verde (January–April 1975):** Only eight fish species were collected or observed in 120 man-days of effort. Netting was largely ineffective (nets buried by sand), and seining yielded only red shiner (fewer than 300 specimens from one school). No young-of-1974 were found. Visual estimates were about 1,000 red shiners, about 10 carp, about 10 sunfish and 3 largemouth bass. The author recommends further work in summer to obtain comparable data.
- **Palo Verde plants:** No rooted submergent vegetation was found, attributed to winter dormancy and shifting sand bottoms. Only fragments of spiny naiad, pondweed and coontail were caught in drift nets.
- **Report status:** Addenda on plankton, periphyton, benthic invertebrates and riparian vegetation were pending at the time of writing.
