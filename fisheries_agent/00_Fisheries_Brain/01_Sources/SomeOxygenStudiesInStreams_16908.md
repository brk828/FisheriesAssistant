# Some Oxygen Studies in Streams

#water-chemistry #freshwater-ecology #dissolved-oxygen

## Executive Summary

This 1959 paper from the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] reports three field studies of [[Re-Aeration]], the absorption of atmospheric oxygen by flowing water, and the problems of measuring it in natural streams. The authors used a [[Radiochemical Tracer]] to test flow timing and mixing, then applied a [[Sodium Sulphite]] de-oxygenation method to a [[Lowland Stream]] and to the polluted [[Sincil Dike]]. The document contains no fish data, although the [[Maple Lodge Effluent Channel]] was used in a separate fish ecology survey that is only cited. The authors conclude that accurate re-aeration rates are hard to obtain in polluted streams, and that rates of oxidation, photosynthesis and mud oxygen demand must also be known before dissolved oxygen can be predicted.

## Background & Description

The paper follows an earlier study by Gameson, Truesdale and Downing (1955) on the effects of synthetic detergents on re-aeration. Its purpose is to show when the rate of oxygen absorption in a natural watercourse can be measured accurately enough to support oxygen-balance calculations. The key condition is that the discharge must be small enough that adding sodium sulphite does not disturb the oxygen equilibrium beyond practical limits.

The study uses several linked concepts:

- **[[Longitudinal Mixing]]**: the spread of a pulse of water or tracer along a channel. It matters for stormwater and accidental discharge studies.
- **[[Exchange Coefficient]] (f)**: the rate of re-aeration, expressed in cm per hour. The authors prefer this term to "exit coefficient".
- **[[Retention Period]]**: the time water takes to travel between sampling stations, estimated from the half-recovery point of the oxygen curve.
- **[[Salt-Dilution Method]]**: used to measure stream discharge with sodium chloride.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper concerns water quality in three settings:

1. **[[Maple Lodge Effluent Channel]]** (Colne Valley Sewerage Board, flowing to the [[River Colne]]): a 1954 study of mixing and dissolved oxygen in winter 1953–54. The channel was also used in a Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food fish ecology survey (Allan, Herbert and Alabaster, 1958). That survey is cited but its results are not given.
2. **[[Lowland Stream]]**: a small, unpolluted stream about 600 yd long that enters the River Lee. It had water-cress beds and muddy or ruffled reaches, and no diurnal flow variation.
3. **[[Sincil Dike]]** (Lincolnshire): a sluggish, polluted distributary of the Witham that receives sewage effluent from Canwick Sewage Works, which treats all sewage from the city of Lincoln. It rejoins the Witham about 7.5 miles below the outfall. Infiltration from the Witham, which stood about 10 ft higher, complicated the survey.

Comparison values in the paper come from other waters, including [[Thames Estuary]] and [[Lakeland Beck]] studies.

## Key Findings

**Longitudinal mixing (Maple Lodge channel, early 1954)**
- A rubidium chloride tracer (Rb-86, 33 mc) was added at the weir. Samples were taken at 660, 1,215 and 1,500 yd.
- Calculated percentage recoveries were 102%, 103% and 86%. The low third-station recovery was attributed to adsorption during ponding and to low activity in the curve tail.
- For the first 660 yd, mean travel time was 50 min with a standard deviation of 7 min, giving a coefficient of variation of 14%. The same coefficient applied to the second curve. The third gave 9%, but its value is not reliable because recovery was low.

**Lowland stream experiments (summer 1955)**
- Retention periods from the sulphite run: about 11 min from K to L (range roughly 9–15 min), and under 5 min from L to M.
- Exchange coefficients from the main run (Table I):
  - Reach KL: 5.6 cm/hr from the calculation shown.
  - Reach LM: 37.8 cm/hr from the calculation shown (flow 52.5 cu m/hr; surface area 62.8 sq m).
- Eight LM values from the July 1955 survey were 30, 30, 28, 25, 38, 27, 30 and 32 cm/hr. Mean 30 cm/hr, standard deviation 3.6 cm/hr, coefficient of variation 12%.
- The upper reach (KL) gave less certain values. Across seven runs the mean was 3.8 cm/hr, and one run gave a negative value because of mixing problems.

**Sincil Dike (early 1957)**
- The dosing ran for about 3.5 hr. Longitudinal mixing destroyed the level section of the oxygen curve after about half a mile. The authors therefore used a modified method based on the total oxygen deficit.
- Reach estimates ranged from −3.3 to 11.7 cm/hr. The weighted mean was 2.2 cm/hr.
- Simulated curves for f = 1, 2, 3 and 5 cm/hr suggested that the average exchange coefficient lay between 1 and 3 cm/hr. The data could not show whether it varied along the reach.

**Comparative exchange coefficients (Table II, selected)**
- Stagnant water: 0.4–0.6 cm/hr
- Clean water in a small channel: 0.5 cm/hr
- Polluted water in a dock and tidal basin: 1.3 cm/hr
- Sincil Dike (sluggish polluted river): 2 cm/hr
- Turbulent Lakeland beck: 30–200 cm/hr
- Water flowing down a 30° slope: 70–300 cm/hr

**Conclusions**
- Accurate re-aeration rates are hard to measure in polluted streams, and no single method works in all conditions.
- Predicting dissolved oxygen requires better knowledge of oxidation rates for dissolved and suspended matter and for mud deposits, plus the effects of photosynthesis and nitrogen oxidation.
