# Evaluation of Pre-Introduction Stress on the Survival of Bonytail Chub Stocked in the Green River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This final report from the [[Utah Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit]] (Utah State University) for the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] examines whether pre-introduction stress from [[Fish Transport]] and [[Radio Telemetry]] transmitter implantation reduced the survival of hatchery-produced adult [[Bonytail Chub]] stocked into the [[Green River]]. The question arose after nearly total mortality of stocked fish in the summer of 1988. Fish came from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], and the study ran in 1988 and 1989 with holding and "pen trials" in the river.

The authors found that direct mortality from transmitter implantation and transport was low. Most deaths occurred after release, in river pens, and were linked to entanglement, water-level drops, abrasion in fast water, and unexplained late-June and July losses. The design was seriously flawed (non-independent observations, small and overlapping groups), so the report is purely descriptive with no statistical analysis. The authors concluded that the high post-stocking mortality was more likely due to the failure of hatchery-conditioned fish to adapt to natural conditions than to an inability of the species to occupy Green River habitats.

## Background & Description

**Species and stock.** The study focused on the bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*, cited in the source as "cira ezegans"), a species central to the [[Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1990). The recovery plan depends on a captive broodstock that produces offspring for reintroduction. The fish used were F1 offspring (1981 cohort) of wild stock collected from Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada.

**Motivation.** The authors note that the genetic integrity of hatchery bonytail has received considerable attention, but little attention has been paid to whether hatchery progeny can behaviorally adapt to natural conditions. Wiley et al. (manuscript) reviewed literature suggesting culture reduces fish's ability to cope with natural activities such as [[Predator Avoidance]] and feeding. The authors argue that genetic diversity alone may not ensure that reintroduced populations establish.

**Scope.** Although pre-stocking survival was not an original objective of the research contract, the near-total mortality of bonytail stocked into the Green River in summer 1988 prompted an evaluation of contributing factors. The study was part of a larger effort by the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources to evaluate habitat use and movement of stocked bonytail.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Holding sites.** Fish were held in several locations:
- Backwater pens in the Green River (wire mesh pens, roughly 4 × 5.4 ft in the 1988 trial).
- A higher-flow run site, used for a second pen in October 1988.
- Tanks at [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] (NWR), near Vernal, Utah.

**Water conditions noted.**
- September 1988 pen: water temperature 15 °C.
- November 1988: temperatures 5–6 °C.
- May 12, 1988: Ouray NWR tank temperature 13 °C, compared with 16 °C at Dexter.
- In June 1989, a river-level reduction isolated the backwater pen housing bonytail.
- Falling river elevation was cited as the cause of early-June 1989 deaths.

**Stressors examined.**
- Surgical implantation of dummy or functional external radio transmitters.
- Transport from Dexter NFH to Vernal (about 17 hours in one May 1988 trip).
- Acclimation in river pens before release.
- Fast water, which caused fin and snout abrasion.
- Entanglement of external antennas on wire cage mesh.

## Key Findings

**1. Transmitter implantation and transport caused little direct mortality.**
- Of the 4 fish implanted at Dexter NFH on May 11, 1988, three survived beyond one month.
- Across three implantation trials, only two mortalities were recorded directly attributable to implantation (per the report's account).
- Only one fish died in transit (June 1989, Dexter to Vernal).

**2. The 1988 pen trial showed deaths tied to physical conditions rather than health.**
- The pen trial involved 13 fish (6 from May 13 and 7 from June 13) in four treatments: dummy transmitter (5), incision only (3), handled and anesthetized (3), and control (2).
- The first death, on September 17, 1988, was a dummy-transmitter fish whose antenna apparently caught in the wire mesh. Internal injuries and damage near the antenna exit point were noted.
- Fish moved to the higher-velocity run site (October 1988) developed rapid abrasions on snouts and fins. A transmitter-implanted fish died there on November 7, and the trial was terminated on November 18.
- No further deaths were recorded in the slow-water pen through November 18, 1988 (the report notes an incision fish died on November 14, but its location is not clearly stated).

**3. The 1988 implanted fish that died after release were mainly from the first group.**
- Four fish implanted and released on May 13, 1988 (per Appendix 1): two outlived their transmitters, and two died 7 and 38 days after release.
- The "1988 Results" log records four fish still alive at Ouray NWR as of July 28, 1988.

**4. 1989 river-pen holding was associated with high losses.**
- Fish were acclimated in river pens for about 19 days before stocking on May 30, 1989.
- Three fish implanted on June 27 died within two days. Between June 27 and July 2, the report states that ten of 22 fish died (5 implanted and 4 non-implanted). The listed components sum to 9, a discrepancy in the source.
- Fish transported on June 3, June 22, and July 20, 1989 did not survive well in pens. Fish transported in July, despite a new protocol (3 ppm [[Furacin Treatment]], injection with oxytetracycline and vitamin C, and fungicide dip after surgery), had about 70% mortality in pens within a short period (the exact day count is illegible in the scan).
- Control fish placed in a river pen on May 12, 1989 survived until June 12, 1989.

**5. Overall survival was very low.**
- Confirmed mortality for fish introduced into the Green River in 1988 and 1989 was 64 of 86 fish.
- An additional 16 fish most likely died but were unaccounted for.
- Only 6 of 85 fish introduced were known to survive an extended period. (Note the 86 and 85 totals differ in the source.)

**6. Literature supports a hatchery-conditioning explanation.**
- Vincent (1960) and Johnsson and Abrahams (1991) reported that hatchery-reared trout showed little fright response, making them susceptible to predation.
- Wiley et al. (manuscript) reported that hatchery-reared trout often do not occupy energetically advantageous stream positions and frequently do not switch to a natural diet.
- Nehring (1991) observed that rainbow trout stocked above 12 cm in length rarely survived, though smaller fish were more likely to establish populations.

**7. Conclusion of the authors.** Although the design was seriously flawed, mortality appeared to relate more to introduction into the Green River than to the general health of fish at release. The authors concluded that the high mortality reported by Chart (1990) was more likely due to the failure of individuals to adapt to natural conditions (preconditioning to the hatchery environment) than to an inability of bonytail chub to survive in a natural environment.

**8. Methodological limitations.**
- Observations were not independent, because limited fish were reused across treatments once an effect failed to appear.
- Possible cumulative effects of treatments could not be separated.
- Controls were not present for every treatment.
- No statistical analysis was possible, so the report is strictly descriptive.

**9. Implications for reintroduction.** The findings suggest that genetic diversity alone may not ensure reintroduction success. Pre-release conditioning or behavioral training of hatchery-reared [[Bonytail Chub]] may be needed, a point linked to the broader [[Hatchery Rearing]] literature cited by the authors.
