# The Influence of Size on Striped Bass Foraging

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae #predator-prey-dynamics

## Executive Summary

This laboratory and field study quantified how prey-to-predator size ratio (PPR) affects foraging success in [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*). Capture success declined linearly with increasing PPR (attack success = 0.861 - 1.82PPR), while handling time increased exponentially (h = 0.339e^(6.74PPR)). Peak prey profitability occurred at PPR = 0.12, matching the modal PPR (0.12, mean 0.14) observed in wild [[Chesapeake Bay]] striped bass stomachs (1990–1992, N = 788 prey items). Striped bass could not consume prey exceeding ~40% of their own length. Comparison with [[Bluefish]] (*Pomatomus saltatrix*) foraging data suggests that much smaller bluefish exploit larger absolute prey sizes than striped bass, indicating potential dietary overlap and competition for small prey in sympatric populations.

## Background & Description

[[Striped Bass]] is a top predator in estuaries and coastal systems along the US Atlantic coast and in California and inland waters where it has been stocked. Juveniles feed on aquatic invertebrates but become increasingly piscivorous with age. Despite their ecological and economic importance—including roles in controlling prey populations and potential competitive interactions with [[Bluefish]]—little was known about how relative prey size affects their foraging efficiency. This study used 300–400 mm TL age-0 striped bass (Hudson River origin) reared at the Great Lakes Center, Buffalo, New York, and fed live [[Emerald Shiner]] (*Notropis atherinoides*) and [[Striped Shiner]] (*N. chrysocephalus*) in controlled laboratory trials. These shiners are morphologically similar to natural prey such as [[Atlantic Silverside]] (*Menidia menidia*) and [[Bay Anchovy]] (*Anchoa mitchilli*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Striped bass are key piscivores in [[Chesapeake Bay]] and other Atlantic estuaries. Prey shortages have been hypothesized to drive coastal migrations of adults and correlate with disease incidence and poor body condition. Negative correlations between striped bass and bluefish abundances have raised concerns about competition or predator-prey interactions regulating coast-wide populations. Understanding size-dependent foraging is critical for assessing trophic roles, competitive dynamics, and population regulation. Field diet data from mid-Chesapeake Bay and tributaries (January 1990–March 1992) included [[Spot]] (*Leiostomus xanthurus*), [[Atlantic Croaker]] (*Micropogonius undulatus*), [[Atlantic Menhaden]] (*Brevoortia tyrannus*), bay anchovy, and Atlantic silversides.

## Key Findings

**Laboratory Foraging Experiments (N = 17 trials, 3 fish per trial, mean 340 mm TL):**
- **Capture Success:** Declined linearly with PPR (r² = 0.754, p < 0.01): CS = 0.861 - 1.82PPR. Capture success fell below 20% for prey >35% of predator length.
- **Maximum Prey Size:** Striped bass could not consume prey ≥56% of their length. In one trial with striped shiners at PPR = 0.559, no prey were consumed in 24 h despite attacks.
- **Handling Time:** Increased exponentially with PPR (N = 13, r² = 0.835, p < 0.01): h = 0.339e^(6.74PPR).
- **Prey Profitability:** Peak profitability (mass/time) occurred at PPR = 0.12. Using a 75% of maximum criterion, most profitable prey ranged from 7–18% of predator length.

**Field Diet Comparison (300–400 mm TL striped bass, Chesapeake Bay, 1990–1992, N = 788 prey):**
- Modal PPR = 0.12, mean PPR = 0.14 (SD = 0.092), matching laboratory-derived peak profitability.
- Both field diet and model profitability distributions were skewed toward larger PPR, though field diets slightly favored larger relative prey sizes than predicted.

**Comparison with Bluefish:**
- [[Bluefish]] (80–155 mm TL) consumed Atlantic silversides up to 63% of predator length (Scharf et al. 1998), versus ~40% for striped bass.
- Peak profitability for 340 mm TL striped bass = 41 mm TL prey (PPR 0.12); for 118 mm TL bluefish = 71 mm TL prey. Thus, striped bass optimal prey size overlaps with much smaller bluefish, suggesting competition for small prey and differential vulnerability to prey shortages.
- Bluefish's shearing dentition enables consumption of larger relative prey sizes; striped bass must swallow prey whole.

**Implications:**
- Striped bass foraging is highly size-dependent, with steep declines in profitability outside a narrow PPR range.
- Field diets closely match laboratory-predicted optimal prey sizes, validating foraging models despite use of non-native prey (shiners vs. natural prey).
- Shortages of small prey may disproportionately affect striped bass compared to sympatric bluefish.
