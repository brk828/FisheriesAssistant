# Re-evaluation of the native fishes of the River Yaqui in the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae #Catostomidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1974 re-evaluation by Randy M. McNatt (Arizona State University) reassesses the native fish fauna of the [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage within the United States, focused on two sub-systems in extreme southeastern Arizona: [[Whitewater Draw]] (with tributaries [[Rucker Canyon]] and [[Leslie Creek]]) and [[San Bernardino Creek]] (including [[Astin Draw]]/Black Wash and the [[San Bernardino Ranch]]). Although the Rio Yaqui drains only a small portion of Arizona, it historically contributed roughly one-quarter of the state's native ichthyofauna, and every species/subspecies present is endemic to this drainage within the U.S. Prior work (Minckley 1973) had listed several of these taxa as extinct or probably extinct in the United States. Fieldwork in spring 1974 overturned some of these presumptions, rediscovering previously "lost" populations of [[Gila purpurea]] (Yaqui chub) in an artesian bore and in Leslie Creek, and reconfirming persistence of [[Campostoma ornatum pricei]] (Mexican stoneroller) in Rucker Canyon. Conversely, the [[Yaqui sucker]] (Catostomus bernardini) and [[Yaqui shiner]] (Notropis formosus mearnsi) could not be located and are considered extinct or extirpated from U.S. waters, alongside the previously extirpated [[Yaqui catfish]] (Ictalurus pricei). The paper emphasizes habitat desiccation, arroyo cutting, groundwater pumping, artesian well capping, and livestock trampling as primary drivers of decline, and argues strongly for rigorous, voucher-based, repeated survey work as the only reliable basis for status assessments of desert fishes.

## Background & Description

- **[[Gila purpurea]] (Yaqui chub)**: Pre-breeding males exhibit a steely-blue sheen under sunlight or bright artificial light; females show a straw-yellow cast. No definitive breeding coloration was documented; surface coloration alone is an unreliable sexing character even after months in captivity.
- **[[Agosia chrysogaster]] (longfin dace, Rio Yaqui form)**: Differs morphologically from other regional populations of longfin dace in coloration, head size/shape, and possession of longer barbels. Also shows physiological distinctness — notably difficult to maintain during transport or in laboratory aquaria, unlike the highly hardy Gila River populations.
- **[[Campostoma ornatum pricei]] (Mexican stoneroller)**: Multiple age classes (up to three, possibly four) documented in pools lacking trout predation; in pools co-inhabited by rainbow trout, only large adults persisted, with smaller individuals apparently consumed (found in trout stomachs). Evidence of cleared-gravel spawning substrate was observed, though no specimens were in full breeding color and no females carried ripe ova.
- **[[Poeciliopsis sonoriensis]] (Yaqui topminnow)**: A small, live-bearing poeciliid. Taxonomic status debated — treated as a full species (Miller & Lowe 1967), a subspecies of [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] (Gila topminnow) (Minckley 1969, 1973), or synonymous with the Gila topminnow (Moore et al. 1970). The author retains it as a distinct species based on morphological data and markedly divergent breeding strategy relative to Gila River topminnow populations, plus pronounced female aggressiveness and cannibalism of offspring (a trait purportedly unique within the genus Poeciliopsis).
- **[[Notropis formosus mearnsi]] (Yaqui shiner)**: Part of the broader Notropis formosus species complex, with taxonomic overlap noted with the red shiner (Notropis lutrensis); this synonymy debate is acknowledged but not resolved in the paper.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Drainage structure**: The upper Rio Yaqui in the U.S. comprises two disjunct sub-systems — Whitewater Draw (fed by the Mule Mountains, southern Sulphur Springs Valley, and Chiricahua Mountains) and San Bernardino Creek (entering from New Mexico, becoming Rio de San Bernardino in Sonora). Habitats include intermittent stream reaches, artesian wells/bores, natural springs, and artificial stock/fishing ponds.
- **[[Gila purpurea]]**: Found in deep pools and detritus-laden, heavily overgrown cut-bank habitat in Leslie Creek; also persisted in a single small artesian bore on the San Bernardino Ranch (hand-dug pit with iron casing, rock-filled), where population size is limited to a few tens of individuals. Extremely secretive and difficult to collect despite relative abundance.
- **[[Agosia chrysogaster]]**: Historically wide-ranging across Whitewater Draw, San Bernardino Creek mainstem, and San Bernardino Ranch artesian wells/ditches; now restricted to Rucker Canyon and Leslie Creek. Considered otherwise one of the most adaptable, "toughest" cyprinids of Sonoran Desert streams (per Minckley & Barber 1970), yet the Yaqui population shows habitat and physiological specialization.
- **[[Campostoma ornatum pricei]]**: Persists at its type locality (Rucker Canyon) and in Leslie Creek. Co-occurs with introduced rainbow trout (Salmo gairdneri) in some pools, with an apparent size-refuge dynamic. Persistence linked to human-modified hydrology — a leaking fishing-lake dam above the main Rucker Canyon population sustains base flow during drought, and a silted-in old dam on Leslie Creek may perform a similar buffering role. This parallels findings from [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Barber & Minckley 1966) where anthropogenic modification incidentally supported native fish persistence.
- **[[Catostomus bernardini]] (Yaqui sucker)**: Formerly inhabited San Bernardino Creek and Astin (Black) Wash; extirpated from Arizona concurrent with the temporary loss of Gila purpurea and Poeciliopsis sonoriensis from the same locality, due to spring failure (1969) and cattle trampling.
- **[[Ictalurus pricei]] (Yaqui catfish)**: Historically entered Arizona via San Bernardino Creek; only confirmed U.S. records are from an introduced (now extirpated) population in the Santa Cruz River basin.
- **[[Poeciliopsis sonoriensis]]**: Formerly widespread paralleling Agosia's distribution (Whitewater Draw, San Bernardino Creek, San Bernardino Ranch artesian waters). Now confined to one natural spring and three small artesian bores. Exhibits strong seasonal reproductive cycling, with minimal winter reproduction (Schoenherr 1973). Successfully transplanted from Astin Wash to the cooler, higher-elevation Leslie Creek, where it now thrives alongside three other native species.
- **[[Notropis formosus mearnsi]]**: Historically co-occurred with introduced largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) and bluegill (Lepomis macrochirus) in San Bernardino Ranch ponds. Likely declined due to capping of artesian sources feeding these ponds, eliminating refugia and exposing the shiner to predation by centrarchids and bullfrogs (Rana catesbeiana).

## Key Findings

- **Rediscovery of Gila purpurea**: Thought extinct in the U.S. after failed collections in Leslie Creek (1969–1973), the species was found in 1974 in (1) a small reproducing population within a historic artesian bore on the San Bernardino Ranch, and (2) a large, multi-age-class population in Leslie Creek — the latter apparently overlooked by prior surveys due to the species' secretive habits.
- **Successful emergency translocation**: ~200 Gila purpurea individuals were moved from failing Astin Draw springs to Leslie Creek just before habitat collapse in 1969; Poeciliopsis sonoriensis was similarly transplanted and unexpectedly thrived despite predicted unsuitability (higher elevation, cooler climate).
- **Confirmed extinctions/extirpations from U.S. waters**: Catostomus bernardini (Yaqui sucker) and Notropis formosus mearnsi (Yaqui shiner) not found in the 1974 survey and considered extinct/extirpated in Arizona; Ictalurus pricei (Yaqui catfish) previously extirpated via loss of an introduced Santa Cruz River population.
- **Mexican stoneroller persistence contradicts prior report**: Minckley (1973) had reported Campostoma ornatum pricei extinct in Arizona based on 1971 collections; the 1974 survey found it abundant in Rucker Canyon, underscoring the risk of false-negative extinction claims from limited sampling.
- **Anthropogenic modifications as unintended refugia**: Leaking dams and silted reservoirs that maintain minimal baseflow during drought appear critical to sustaining several native populations (Campostoma, Agosia, and others) in intermittent, flash-flood-prone streams — echoing similar dynamics described for [[Aravaipa Creek]].
- **Primary threats identified**: Arroyo cutting (since the late 1800s), historical mining activity, groundwater pumping in both the U.S. and Mexico, capping of artesian wells feeding natural cienegas, livestock trampling of spring habitats, and predation by non-native centrarchids/bullfrogs following habitat modification.
- **Methodological argument**: The paper's central thesis is that accurate status assessment of desert/semi-desert fishes requires precise knowledge of locality, timing, and collection technique, plus voucher specimens deposited in permanent museum collections; erroneous "extinction" or "extirpation" claims (as occurred with both Gila purpurea and Campostoma ornatum pricei) arise from insufficient or non-repeated survey effort.
- **Overall conservation status (1974)**: Of the seven native ichthyofaunal taxa, three are extinct or extirpated from U.S. waters (Catostomus bernardini, Notropis formosus mearnsi, Ictalurus pricei), while four persist in greatly fragmented populations: Gila purpurea and Poeciliopsis sonoriensis are confined to single-digit natural localities; Campostoma ornatum pricei and Agosia chrysogaster persist in two or three refugia each. All remaining populations face imminent threats from continued groundwater development, further well capping, and entrenchment of arroyo channels.
