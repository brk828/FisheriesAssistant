# A Nonlethal Technique to Recover Gut Contents of Roundtail Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This study evaluates a nonlethal stomach-pumping technique designed to recover gut contents from adult [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), serving as a surrogate species for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). Forty adult chubs were captured via electrofishing from the [[Colorado River]]; 20 were subjected to the pumping technique and 20 served as unpumped controls. Unlike other fish species where gut contents are flushed out the buccal cavity, the unique simple S-shaped gut and absence of a pyloric sphincter in *Gila* species caused contents to be flushed out the anal vent. Necropsy confirmed the technique achieved 100% recovery efficiency with no observable damage to the esophagus, intestinal lining, or [[Pharyngeal Teeth]]. The authors conclude this method is safe and effective, and recommend its application to endangered *Gila* species to study food habits without lethal sampling.

## Background & Description
- **Gut Morphology**: *Gila* species possess a simple, S-shaped intestine.
- **Pyloric Sphincter**: Absent in the genus *Gila*, which precludes backpressure buildup and allows free passage of intestinal material out the anal vent rather than backflushing through the buccal cavity.
- **Pharyngeal Mill**: Roundtail chub possess a pharyngeal arch with teeth used for processing food; tooth structure and counts are nearly identical among *G. robusta*, *G. cypha*, and *G. elegans* (Holden 1968).
- **Tooth Replacement (Ankylosis)**: Pharyngeal teeth are continuously shed and replaced. Loose teeth observed in both test and control fish showed tooth buds/emerging teeth adjacent to existing teeth, indicating natural ankylosis (new tooth growth constricts blood vessels to the existing tooth's pulp, causing it to atrophy and shed) rather than pump-induced damage.
- **Size Range Sampled**:
  - Test group: mean 279 mm total length (TL); range 220–404 mm.
  - Control group: mean 260 mm TL; range 219–347 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Mainstem Colorado River near the Colorado–Utah state line.
- **Diet Composition**: Gut content analysis revealed the [[Roundtail Chub]] primarily consumes **insects**, often mixed with **algae** and unidentifiable organic debris.
  - Of 20 pumped fish: 8 yielded measurable food (mostly insects/algae), 8 yielded only trace algae/debris, and 4 contained no food.
- **Feeding Ecology Implications**: Because *Gila* species primarily consume small, easily-dislodged items (insects, organic debris) rather than large hard-bodied prey, the flushing technique is well suited to their diet without risking intestinal blockage under normal conditions.
- **Conservation Context**: Understanding food habits is critical to identifying limiting factors for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]], both endemic to the [[Colorado River]] Basin (Valdez and Clemmer 1982).

## Key Findings
1. **Technique Efficacy**: The stomach-pumping technique recovered **100% of gut contents** as confirmed by post-pumping necropsy of residual gut material.
2. **Novel Evacuation Route**: Contents were flushed from the **anal vent**, not the buccal cavity as reported in prior stomach-pump studies on salmonids, centrarchids, ictalurids, percids, and esocids — a direct consequence of *Gila*'s simple gut and lack of pyloric sphincter.
3. **No Structural Damage**: No broken or damaged pharyngeal teeth were found in test fish. Loose teeth (7 control fish, 9 test fish) were attributed to natural shedding (ankylosis), not the procedure.
4. **Physical Stress Test**: A qualitative knife-blade test confirmed pharyngeal teeth require substantial force to break/loosen — far more force than a flexible plastic tube could exert.
5. **No Anesthesia Required**: Roundtail chub were docile during handling, and MS-222 was used only for post-test euthanasia, not sedation — suggesting anesthesia may be unnecessary for related chub species.
6. **Safety Recommendations**: Use only light bulb pressure; halt after two or three gentle flushes if no food is evacuated, to avoid risk of intestinal blockage or injury from a bolus of algae or large food items.
7. **Applicability to Endangered Species**: The technique is recommended as a viable nonlethal method for studying gut contents of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] due to their nearly identical gut configuration and pharyngeal mill structure to *G. robusta*.
