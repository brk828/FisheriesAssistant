# Circle Size Discrimination and Transposition Behaviour in Cichlid Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 study by R. F. Mark and Alan Maxwell tested whether the cichlid fish [[Oscar Fish]] (*Astronotus ocellatus*) could develop a concept of relative size. Eight fish were trained with automated [[Visual Discrimination Apparatus]] to choose the larger of two light circles. Only six of eight fish learned the initial discrimination, and only one of those showed partial [[Transposition Behaviour]] to a new pair of circles. When the absolute size of the circles varied randomly across trials, that fish performed at chance level. The authors concluded there was no evidence that these fish developed a concept of relative size.

## Background & Description

- **Subject species:** [[Astronotus Ocellatus]], a cichlid obtained from a local breeder; eight fish were used.
- **Housing:** Fish were kept in pairs in six-gallon tanks separated by a perforated Plexiglas partition, which the authors noted made them easier to tame.
- **Conditions:** Water temperature was held at 25°C under an electronically controlled 50% light/dark cycle. All training and testing took place in the home tank.
- **Apparatus:** Following the design of [[Haralson and Bitterman Apparatus]], stimuli were back-projected onto screens outside the tank. Fish responded by nosing transparent Plexiglas panels (2.5 cm square) on levers inside the tank. Correct choices were rewarded with fish food delivered to a feeding ring, and errors triggered a 30-second projector shutdown.
- **Mastery criterion:** 85% correct over three successive days; training stopped after 1000 trials without reaching criterion.
- **Literature context:** The authors note a rough correspondence between phylogenetic position and intelligence, and that fishes have been studied less than birds and mammals on complex visual tasks. Prior work included [[Matthews Shape Discrimination Study]] on blue acaras (*Acquidens latifrons*), which suggested concept formation, and transposition studies by [[Herter Fish Training Experiments]] and [[Zunini Fish Learning Studies]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was conducted entirely under laboratory conditions, so no field habitat, ocean basin, or natural population data are reported. Relevant context is limited to the controlled environment (temperature, photoperiod, tank size, and feeding regime). Fish not in training received a small lump of beef liver daily, and no other food was given during training. The authors note that olfactory or other non-visual cues could not be ruled out in earlier experiments by other workers, which bears on how learned responses should be interpreted.

## Key Findings

- **Pre-training:** All fish learned a light-dark discrimination within a few days. Red versus white discrimination took longer. Three fish were not tested on colour.
- **Circle size B/D (larger rewarded):** Six fish learned it in 134 to 884 trials (fish 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 values reported as 134, 884, +550, +250, 576, 429). Two fish (4 and 8) failed after 1000 trials.
- **Transposition to circles A/C:** Scores fell to chance for five of six successful fish. One fish (fish 3) solved it in 354 trials, a slight saving over its original learning.
- **Transposition to circles C/E:** That same fish reached criterion in 64 trials, with very few errors even when the intermediate circle was rewarded against the smallest and punished against the largest.
- **Combined problem (A/C/E):** Fish 3 reached criterion in 32 trials.
- **Random presentation of all pairs (A/B/C/D/E):** The remaining fish failed to show consistent preference for the larger circle after a further 1000 trials.
- **Conclusion:** The authors found no evidence that these fish developed a concept of relative size. The single apparent transposition was not maintained when absolute sizes varied.

**Methodological differences from earlier fish studies** (the authors' explanation for discrepant results with [[Herter Fish Training Experiments]] and [[Zunini Fish Learning Studies]]):
1. The original discrimination was harder than colour or brightness tasks, as measured by trial numbers.
2. Earlier reward delivery was placed directly in front of stimuli, so olfactory cues were not excluded.
3. Different species were used.
4. The transfer pair in this study overlapped in size with the original pair, unlike Herter's design.

**Broader comparisons:** The authors reviewed transposition research in birds, rats, monkeys, and children, and discussed the absolute versus relative theories of transposition (see [[Spence Absolute Relative Theory]]). They suggested that failure to transpose may indicate that the original discrimination relied on features of each stimulus separately rather than on the relationship between them.
