# Fertility, Sex Ratio, and Exposure to Predation in Populations of the Mosquitofish *Gambusia manni* at Bimini, Bahamas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Louis A. Krumholz (University of Louisville) compares three populations of the viviparous [[Bimini Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia manni*) over roughly sixteen months (April 1955 to July 1956). Two populations came from opposite ends of [[Daniels' Pond]], a man-made, low-salinity pond with no predatory fish; the third came from [[Caloo Mangrove]], a tidal pool open to the bay and exposed to many piscivorous species. Comparing growth, fertility, senility and sex ratios, the author concludes that the absence of predation in Daniels' Pond produced a population with extended longevity, many senile females, a lower birth rate and male-biased sex ratios at times. Caloo Mangrove fish matured smaller but were more fertile.

## Background & Description

*Gambusia manni* was first described by [[Hubbs]] (1927) from New Providence, and the Bimini fish was retained under this name for convenience, although its taxonomic rank may be subspecific. Mature males are identified by a bright orange band along the proximal edge of the dorsal fin and orange coloration posterior to the gonopodium, which signals maturity. Males fertilize females by direct copulation using a modified anal fin (gonopodium); a single fertilization suffices for several broods. Young are born fully formed at about 9 mm, with no free-swimming larval stage.

The fish are omnivorous, feeding on plankton and, in captivity, on fish gonads and flesh. They are among the most common fishes in the Bimini mangrove swamps, where aggregations of thousands, possibly up to 50,000, have been estimated. The study was conducted by the author while Resident Biologist at the [[Lerner Marine Laboratory]], American Museum of Natural History. Methods were adapted from the author's earlier work on [[Gambusia affinis]] in Illinois.

Key new records reported in the study include the medusa *Vallentinia gabriellae*, the fish *Rivulus marmoratus*, and the nematode *Contracaecum*, each recorded from Bimini for the first time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Daniels' Pond:** A U-shaped, man-made pool about 275 feet long with a maximum depth of about four feet. It had no surface connection to the sea, a salinity of about 28 ppt, a persistent phytoplankton bloom, and shading by Australian pines. The only other fish present was the killifish *Cyprinodon baconi*, and a single unidentified blenny was taken once. Gambusia were therefore exposed only to cannibalism.

**Caloo Mangrove:** A tidal pool in a mangrove cay about two miles from the laboratory, with salinity of 36 to 38 ppt (possibly above 40 ppt in summer). It was connected to the bay by channels and could be isolated at low tide. Fish taken included [[Eucinostomus gula]], [[Sphyraena barracuda]], [[Mugil curema]], and *Cyprinodon baconi*, and the author regarded it as the normal habitat, with heavy predation.

**Temperature extremes:** Cold weather in January 1956 killed many fish in both habitats, with an especially severe impact on the West End of Daniels' Pond. Water temperatures reached about 95 °F in July 1956.

**Sampling:** Collections used a 15-foot minnow seine, later fitted with mosquito bar mesh (about 2.5 × 1.5 mm openings) from May 1955. There were 42 collections in total: 16 from each end of Daniels' Pond and 10 from Caloo Mangrove. A total of 36,622 gambusia were measured and sexed (15,211 males and 21,411 females), and 12,617 gravid females were examined, with 76,777 eyed embryos counted.

## Key Findings

**Age, growth and size**
- Males stop growing, or grow very little, after gonopodial maturity. Females continue to grow until death.
- Males at Caloo Mangrove were about 5 mm shorter on average than mature males from Daniels' Pond, a statistically significant difference. The largest mature male was 44 mm long.
- The largest female was a 67 mm gravid female from the East End of Daniels' Pond (5 August 1955). The largest from Caloo Mangrove was 45 mm.
- Female longevity was about 10 to 11 months at most for females born in late summer and about six months for those born early in the reproductive season. Male lifespans were shorter, typically two to seven months in Daniels' Pond and two to five months at Caloo Mangrove.
- Gestation was 22 to 28 days (mean 24.5 days, from seven observed periods). Females could produce about four or five broods per season.

**Fertility**
- The reproductive season lasted nine to ten months, from late December or early January to about September or October, peaking in March, April and May.
- Mean brood size was 6.4 embryos at Caloo Mangrove, 6.2 at the East End and 5.6 at the West End of Daniels' Pond. On matched dates, Caloo Mangrove averaged 6.4 versus 5.3 (East End) and 4.9 (West End).
- The largest brood was 101 embryos from a 67 mm female in Daniels' Pond. The largest brood at Caloo Mangrove was 24 embryos.
- Caloo Mangrove females had higher fertility for their length at every size class, although they were smaller. Brood size correlated well with female length at Caloo Mangrove but poorly in Daniels' Pond.
- The author estimated the realized reproductive rate at Caloo Mangrove as about 20 per cent higher overall, and about 70 per cent higher over three broods in January to March 1956.

**Senility**
- A total of 716 senile females were recorded in Daniels' Pond and none at Caloo Mangrove. Senile females made up 34.1 per cent of mature non-gravid females overall (28.4 per cent East End, 44.8 per cent West End).
- Senile males were also suspected in Daniels' Pond, with the author noting long-lived, gaunt males.
- The author attributes senility to the absence of predation: senile fish that would be eaten at Caloo Mangrove survive indefinitely in Daniels' Pond.

**Sex ratios**
- Sex ratios were generally female-biased in both habitats, but Daniels' Pond had more males (about 45 per cent) than Caloo Mangrove (about 37 per cent).
- Males outnumbered females in 15 of 32 collections from Daniels' Pond and in one collection the ratio was 50:50. At Caloo Mangrove males never outnumbered females.
- The author links male-biased ratios in Daniels' Pond to the accumulation of long-lived senile fish of both sexes.

**Parasites**
- The only parasite recorded was a larval nematode, *Contracaecum*, found in 89 specimens (36 East End, 41 West End, 12 Caloo Mangrove). None occurred in males or in immature females.

**Discussion and conclusions**
- The author concludes that predation shapes population dynamics: predator-free populations show longer lifespan, senility, depressed birth rate and skewed sex ratios, while predated populations maintain higher reproductive output.
- The discussion draws parallels with studies of fish, birds, mammals and insects on density-dependent fecundity and compensatory reproduction, including work by [[Errington]], [[Nicholson]], [[Cole]] and [[Ricker]].
- The author suggests that Daniels' Pond illustrates an unusual case: this is presented as the first reported instance with good supporting evidence that lack of predation directly increased fish lifespan.
