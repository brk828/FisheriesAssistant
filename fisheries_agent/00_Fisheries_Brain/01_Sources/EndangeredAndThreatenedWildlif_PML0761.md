# Critical Habitat Designation for the Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final rule, published in the [[Federal Register]] on March 8, 1994 and effective April 7, 1994, designates [[Critical Habitat]] for the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), a small, slender, endangered freshwater fish of the [[Gila River]] basin in [[Arizona]] and [[New Mexico]]. The rule was issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and follows a 1985 proposal that was revised after public comment and hearings. The designated areas comprise river reaches of the Gila, San Francisco, Tularosa, Blue, and Aravaipa Creek systems, and the rule defines the [[Primary Constituent Elements]] (physical and biological features) needed by the species. The Service concluded that economic impacts are minor, with identifiable added costs of about $406,500 over the designated reaches, and that no areas needed to be excluded.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The loach minnow is a small, slender, elongated cyprinid. The document describes it as reaching roughly 3 inches in length, though the sourced text is partly garbled on this point.
- **Listing status:** The species is listed as endangered. Critical habitat was proposed in 1985, with the comment period reopened later. The final designation was deferred in part to allow completion of economic analysis.
- **Distribution:** Historically widespread in the Gila River basin. Remaining populations are widely separated and occur in the Gila, San Francisco, Tularosa, White, and Blue rivers and in [[Aravaipa Creek]].
- **Genetics:** Allozyme studies (Tibbets 1992) indicate that the populations are genetically distinct. Maintaining them as independent units buffers the species against threats to any single population.
- **Population status:** The Aravaipa Creek population is the only remnant of the south-central portion of the historic range and is under the most protective land management. The Gila River population is the largest and may represent the species' "core" population. The San Francisco and Tularosa populations are highly fragmented.
- **Process:** The rule was developed through public comment, three public hearings, and responses to about ten grouped issues. The [[Economic Analysis]] (Souder 1992) was prepared after the proposed rule was published, a procedure the document justifies on the grounds that concurrent listing and critical habitat proposals should not be delayed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements (Primary Constituent Elements):**
- Permanent, flowing, unpolluted water
- Adult habitat with moderate to swift flow (15–100 cm/s) in shallow water (5–40 cm deep) over gravel, cobble, and rubble
- Juvenile habitat with moderate to swift flow in shallow water over sand, gravel, cobble, and rubble
- Larval habitat with slow to moderate flow (0–30 cm/s) in shallow water (5–30 cm deep) with abundant instream cover
- Spawning habitat with slow to swift flow (3–85 cm/s) over uncemented cobble and rubble
- Low fine sediment and low substrate embeddedness
- Riffle, run, and backwater components
- Low to moderate stream gradient (generally 0.5–1.5%)
- Water temperatures of about 4–30 °C (40–85 °F) with natural variation
- Abundant aquatic invertebrate food base
- Periodic flooding and a natural, unregulated hydrograph
- Few or no predatory or competitive non-native species
- Healthy, native riparian community and moderate to high bank stability

**Threats and activities of concern:**
- **Hydrologic alteration:** groundwater pumping, impoundment, and water diversions that reduce minimum flows or change natural flow regimes.
- **Watershed disturbance:** vegetation manipulation, timber harvest, road construction, prescribed burning, livestock grazing, mining, and urban development.
- **Channel and habitat change:** channelization, impoundment, loss of substrate supply, riparian alteration, and excessive sedimentation.
- **Water quality:** chemical or biological pollutant releases, from point or non-point sources.
- **Non-native fish:** stocking of game fish, use of live bait fish, biological control stocking, aquaculture, and aquarium dumping. These species act as predators and competitors.

**Other relevant context:**
- Other federally listed species in the area include the [[Spikedace]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]]. The [[Bald Eagle]] also occurs near loach minnow habitat.
- The [[Central Arizona Project]] Upper Gila Water Supply Study may affect the basin. An informal conference (USFWS 1986) and an uncompleted formal consultation have been held for this project.
- The [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] does not currently maintain loach minnow stock. Captive propagation techniques are not yet developed, so captive stock cannot be used as an immediate alternative.

## Key Findings

1. **Critical habitat is designated** along reaches of the Gila (East, West, and Middle forks and the main stem), San Francisco, Tularosa, Blue (including Campbell and Dry Blue creeks), and Aravaipa Creek systems. Specific river segments and boundaries are given in the rule's legal description.

2. **Designated areas exclude some previously proposed sites.** Several areas were removed or deferred, and the Service noted that some unoccupied areas important for recovery may be considered for future revision.

3. **Economic impacts are limited.** The economic analysis identified added costs of about $406,500, of which about $150,000 is attributable to 84 km (52 mi) of river designated for both the loach minnow and the spikedace. Impacts are expected only for three federal actions: FEMA-cost-shared irrigation diversion repairs after major floods, fencing and alternative water developments to keep cattle out of riparian zones on National Forest land, and limited preventive measures at developed recreation sites.

4. **No areas were excluded** from designation on the basis of economic effects. The Secretary found that the benefits of exclusion did not outweigh the benefits of designation.

5. **Section 7 consultation is expected** for federal activities such as grazing, mining, timber harvest, recreation, and water development. The Service expects mainly administrative costs and some mitigation requirements.

6. **Environmental and regulatory review:**
   - The Service determined that an Environmental Impact Statement is not required under NEPA. This relies on Pacific Legal Foundation v. Andrus and on the Service's experience with about 120 Environmental Assessments, none of which resulted in a significant impact finding.
   - A Regulatory Impact Analysis was not required because Executive Order 12291 was rescinded on September 30, 1993.
   - The Department of the Interior determined that the rule will not have a significant economic effect on a substantial number of small entities under the Regulatory Flexibility Act.

7. **Non-native fish are a major threat.** The Service stated that threats from non-native species do not relieve it of its duty to designate habitat. It is working with state agencies to control the introduction and spread of non-native fish.

8. **Public comment shaped the rule.** Comments were grouped into about ten issues, including the deferral of economic analysis, the adequacy of NEPA review, cumulative economic effects, the role of captive propagation and reintroduction, and the value of designation given the threat from non-native fish. The Service responded to each.
