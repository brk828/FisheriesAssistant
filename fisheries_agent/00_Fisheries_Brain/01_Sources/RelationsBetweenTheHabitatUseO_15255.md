# Habitat Use of Age-0 Colorado Squawfish Relative to Sympatric Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Colorado River Fishery Project, Grand Junction Field Station (McAda and Kaeding, August 28, 1989), compares the physical habitat used by age-0 [[Colorado Squawfish]] with that used by six sympatric fishes in the [[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]]. It reanalyzes seine data from the [[Standardized Sampling Program]] (1979-1980, postrunoff period). Using [[Discriminant Analysis]] on depth, velocity and substrate, the authors found little habitat separation between age-0 Colorado squawfish and most of the species examined. They concluded that flow-based habitat management aimed at favoring squawfish while suppressing common introduced fishes may not be feasible.

## Background & Description

Three fishes native to the Upper Colorado River Basin were listed as endangered at the time: Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Bonytail]] (*G. elegans*). The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was described as extremely rare. The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (USFWS 1987) identified physical-habitat management, supported by operation of upper-basin dams and reservoirs, as one recovery option.

Physical-habitat simulation models relate river discharge to habitat quantity, but their outputs had not yet been correlated with population changes in the endangered fishes. The study asked whether flow management could maximize habitat for important life stages of the endangered fishes while minimizing habitat for undesirable sympatric species. The comparison targeted age-0 Colorado squawfish, generally 25-60 mm TL in the July-September period.

The six sympatric species compared with Colorado squawfish were:
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*)
- [[Sand Shiner]] (*N. stramineus*)
- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)
- *Gila* spp.

Small (<100 mm) [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*C. latipinnis*) were also profiled.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** The Green and Colorado rivers were divided into relatively homogeneous sections based on river morphometry. Two randomly selected 0.5-mi reaches per section were sampled over 24 hours. Each section was sampled in prerunoff, runoff and postrunoff periods. Only postrunoff seine samples (July to September) from 1979 and 1980 were analyzed, because that is when age-0 squawfish are available. The postrunoff period was not sampled in 1981.

**Sampling methods.** Seines with 0.3- or 0.6-cm mesh were used in habitats categorized as backwater, eddy, pool, shoreline, run or riffle. Seining was usually done where average depth was <1 m, roughly the maximum depth at which seining is efficient. Fish were identified, grouped by size, counted and released alive. Per haul, water depth and water velocity (at 0.6 total depth) were averaged from two points along a transect, and dominant substrate was estimated visually and by feel. Substrate codes were converted to particle-size midpoints after [[Hynes]] (1970): silt 0.07 mm, sand 1.1 mm, gravel 9 mm, rubble 16 mm.

**Analysis.** Discriminant variables were mean depth, mean column velocity and dominant substrate size, weighted by catch per effort (C/E, fish per m² seined). [[Wilks Lambda]] measures group separation: 0 indicates complete discrimination and 1 indicates no discrimination. The authors treated values above 0.6 as biologically meaningful similarity. Pearson correlations between squawfish C/E and each sympatric species' C/E were also calculated, using SYSTAT (Wilkinson 1988).

**Environmental setting.** The authors described the Green and Colorado rivers as dynamic, discharge-influenced systems dominated by silt and sand bottoms. High turbidity prevented collection of water-column position data in the Colorado River system.

## Key Findings

**1. Little habitat discrimination overall.** Analyses "showed little discrimination" between age-0 Colorado squawfish and most sympatric species. Lambda values for red shiner ranged up to 0.996 (essentially complete overlap, Colorado River 1980, RM 0-70). Lambda exceeded 0.9 in every case for red shiner and in all but one case for sand shiner.

**2. Variable results by species.**
- *Fathead minnow*: lambda ranged from 0.497 to 0.995, and exceeded 0.9 in 60% of cases.
- *Channel catfish*: lambda ranged from about 0.4 to 0.995. Three cases exceeded 0.9 and three fell between 0.8 and 0.9.
- *Gila* spp.: lambda ranged from 0.587 to 0.938. Only one value exceeded 0.9, and most fell between 0.8 and 0.9.
- *Speckled dace*: showed the most discrimination, with lambda from 0.2 to 0.770. Two cases exceeded 0.7, two fell between 0.6 and 0.7, and four were below 0.6.

The lowest value, 0.219 for speckled dace, is attributed to the Green River in the text, but Table 1 lists it under the Colorado River (RM 0-185, 1979).

**3. Discriminating variables.** Depth was the main discriminator between *Gila* spp. and squawfish habitat. Velocity and substrate were the main discriminators between squawfish and channel catfish or speckled dace. Because no separation existed for red shiner, sand shiner or fathead minnow, their variable contributions were considered meaningless.

**4. Correlation of abundance.** In the Green River, fathead minnow C/E correlated significantly with squawfish C/E in backwaters in five of six cases, but correlation coefficients were low (r = 0.36-0.82). Red shiner C/E correlated with squawfish in all habitats and in backwaters in three of four Colorado River cases (r = 0.4-0.7), but in only one Green River instance. Sand shiner correlations were stronger in the Colorado River, which the authors linked to higher sand shiner abundance there. Other species were rarely correlated with squawfish.

**5. Interpretation of weak correlations.** The authors suggested that weak correlations may partly reflect the much greater abundance of red shiner and fathead minnow compared with the rare Colorado squawfish. Overlap with the two native species (*Gila* spp. and speckled dace) was moderate and below the overlap with introduced species.

**6. Possible causes of similarity.** Matthews and Hill (1980) found less partitioning in shallow, sand-bottom streams, which the authors related to the dynamic, silt/sand habitats of these rivers. The authors also noted that their measurements were coarse: only two depth, velocity and substrate readings per seine haul, with some hauls covering about 250 m². Water-column position, a key partitioning variable elsewhere, was not measured.

**7. Management conclusion.** The authors concluded that water management to increase squawfish habitat while reducing common introduced species "may not be feasible," because habitat use is too similar. Subtle differences would likely have little practical value because flows cannot be closely regulated and flow effects vary among river locations.

**8. Recommended next steps.** Ongoing detailed Green River studies (later published as [[McAda and Tyus]] 1984) were expected to clarify subtle differences. The authors also said that identifying which sympatric species actually threaten squawfish may require rigorously controlled laboratory studies.
