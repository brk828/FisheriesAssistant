# Endemic Mesa Central Fishes Threatened by Largemouth Bass Introductions: 1969 Conservation Correspondence

#ichthyology #marine-biology #goodeidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of three letters from 1969 (September 5, September 10, and October 3) about the conservation of endemic freshwater fishes on the Mexican Plateau. [[Clyde D. Barbour]] and [[Robert R. Miller]] argue that the relict fish fauna of the [[Mesa Central]] is scientifically valuable and is being eliminated by the indiscriminate introduction of [[Largemouth Bass]] and other exotic game fish. [[John S. Gottschalk]], Director of the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, reports on his meeting with Mexican fisheries officials. Those officials acknowledged the problem and planned a survey program and a new laboratory, but Gottschalk expects that commercial and sport-fishing interests will continue to drive stocking decisions.

## Background & Description

The Mesa Central is a plateau region drained mainly by the [[Lerma-Santiago River]] system, with small coastal rivers (Balsas, Panuco, Armeria, Ameca) whose headwaters have cut back into the plateau. Aquatic habitats include lakes, ponds, springs, and streams, many of them in interior basins. The Pleistocene lakes that once covered the region were larger than today, so the present-day water bodies are relicts of a much more extensive system.

The fish fauna shows a high degree of [[Endemism]]:

- **[[Goodeidae]]:** A wholly endemic viviparous family of about 35 species, related to the cyprinodontids and almost entirely restricted to the Mesa Central.
- **[[Chirostoma]] (Atherinidae):** A genus of 21 species, also largely restricted to the region.
- **Other groups:** Unique genera and species of Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, and Ictaluridae.

According to the letters, nearly every pond or swamp holds a unique assemblage of species, and local populations often differ slightly from one another. Barbour describes the area as a natural laboratory for studying evolution.

Miller has worked on Mexican plateau fishes for nearly 20 years. His laboratory held live stocks of about 40 percent of the known goodeid species, and his group was studying their reproductive biology, behavior, biochemistry, cytogenetics, and interrelationships.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The letters identify [[Introduced Species]] as the main threat:

- Bass have been introduced into the main channel of the Lerma-Santiago River and into small interior basins such as the [[Laguna de Santa Maria]], a volcanic caldera lake about 27 miles southeast of Tepic, Nayarit.
- Many goodeid populations are going extinct before they can be adequately studied, and some [[Chirostoma]] populations have been reduced to very low levels.
- Miller visited a lake that held localized goodeid stocks in 1959. In 1968 he found that introduced bass and sunfish had apparently eliminated all native fishes. The exotic fish were not used by Mexicans but were drawn by U.S. anglers seeking "fabulous bass fishing."
- In small water bodies, bass have little ecological space and cannot support a significant fishery, so Barbour considers their introduction of questionable value.

Barbour also reports [[Guppy]] introductions into the warm overflow of a resort swimming pool near [[Balneario Cointzio]]. The guppies cannot survive in the colder lake itself.

The 1969 letters also treat policy and diplomacy. Barbour proposed that the problem be raised under [[Article 14]] of the US–Mexico treaty on traditional fishing in exclusive fishery zones, since preserving the fauna serves Mexico's interest. He presented the issue as a scientific concern shared with Mexico and not as outside interference in Mexican affairs.

## Key Findings

**Barbour's five proposed points (letter of September 5, 1969, for the September 17 Mexico City meeting):**

1. The Mesa Central supports a unique relict fish fauna worth preserving for its intrinsic and scientific value.
2. A similar relict fauna in the western United States has already suffered heavily from thoughtless introductions and habitat modification.
3. Introducing bass and carp into large water bodies as a protein source is understandable, but stocking small, ecologically limited waters is of doubtful value.
4. The indiscriminate introduction of largemouth bass should be halted where possible, and conservation should be adopted for small lakes and interior basins that have not yet been contaminated.
5. Three priority sites should be included in a preservation program, with the list open to expansion:
   - **[[La Alberca]]:** A Tertiary volcanic caldera west of Valle de Santiago, Guanajuato. It is the type locality and total known range of [[Chirostoma Bartoni]], and the range of a possibly new goodeid species.
   - **[[Zacapu]] lake:** A spring-fed lake beside a Celanese plant in Michoacán. The outlet is heavily polluted, but the lake holds one cyprinid, one poeciliid, six goodeids, and one atherinid.
   - **[[Balneario Cointzio]] lake:** A lake about five miles southwest of Morelia, Michoacán, holding two cyprinids, one poeciliid, six goodeids, and one atherinid.

**Miller's follow-up (September 10, 1969):** He supplied background on the goodeid research and the 1959–1968 losses. He recommended contacting [[Juan Luis Cifuentes]], a trained Mexican biologist at the Dirección General de Pesca in Mexico City, who would be receptive to conservation discussions. He also noted that [[Enrique Beltran]] would likely support the issue if present at the meeting.

**Gottschalk's report (October 3, 1969):**

- He met with Cifuentes and [[Amin Zarur Menes]], Director of the National Fisheries Research Institute, during a trip mainly about international fisheries and the billfish tagging program run by [[Gerald Talbot]].
- Zarur said his staff knew of the problems caused by bass stocking and was starting a survey program to guide stocking more responsibly.
- Immediate plans included a laboratory at [[Cuatrocienegas Laboratory]] (near Monclova), staffed by four professionals for survey work.
- Because research and fish culture both fall under Zarur, he is expected to direct the work and eventually issue regulations to prevent unwise bass stocking.
- Gottschalk expects some indiscriminate stocking to continue. Mexican fisheries are driven by commercial returns, so there is little support for protectionism, and even small preserves may be exploited for sport fishing if they benefit the local economy.
- He recommends that Barbour meet Mexican officials directly and present photographs and population samples. He notes a widespread Mexican reluctance to accept domination by American scientists, and a preference for delaying work until Mexico can manage its own affairs. U.S. agencies have encountered more delays each year.

**Overall conclusion:** The letters agree that the endemic Mesa Central fauna is scientifically irreplaceable and endangered by largemouth bass stocking. They also agree that official Mexican action is possible but uncertain. Barbour's approach was to frame the issue as a shared conservation concern, and Gottschalk's outcome shows that any progress depends on Mexican leadership and commercial considerations.
