# Social Control of Male Maturation and Seasonal Body Size in Gambusia affinis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

Hughes (1985, *Environmental Biology of Fishes* 14(4): 251–258) tests whether late-summer male [[Gambusia affinis]] ([[Western Mosquitofish]]) delay maturation and reach larger body size because of the presence of already mature conspecific males, a process termed [[Social Inhibition of Maturation]]. The study combines seasonal field collections from [[Hovey Lake]] in 1982 with a 1983 [[Field Experiment]] that removed males from experimental ponds. The results support the view that delayed maturation at large size, rather than post-maturational growth, drives the seasonal rise in male body size, consistent with the [[Hubbs Hypothesis]]. The author also finds that male removal shifted the sex ratio and male size distributions, and suggests that [[Temperature Effects]] and genetic variation may contribute to the pattern.

## Background & Description

**Species and group.** [[Gambusia affinis]] is a livebearing poeciliid fish. The study uses the western subspecies, *G. affinis affinis*, which is distinguished from other taxa by its small size and by sexually dimorphic anal fin development (the [[Gonopodium]]). Males can be sexed by the method of [[Turner Gonopodium Method]], based on the segmentation and development of the anal fin rays. Females are identified in the field by the presence of an anal spot.

**Prior work.** The paper reviews several lines of evidence:

- [[Borowsky Xiphophorus Studies]] (1973, 1978) showed that in *Xiphophorus variatus*, the larger of two males matured first and suppressed maturation in the smaller male.
- [[Sohn 1977]] repeated such experiments with *G. manni* and argued that social control should have demographic consequences.
- [[Krumholz 1948]] found that adult male *G. manni* were larger on average in a population not exposed to fish predators than in one exposed to them. He proposed that intense intraspecific competition, with predation reducing competitor density, might allow males to grow to larger sizes.
- Turner (1941) asserted that male mosquitofish grow little after maturation. Later studies (e.g., [[Snelson 1982]]) found post-maturational growth in some poeciliids.

**Hypotheses tested.**

1. The [[Hubbs Hypothesis]]: males maturing early in the breeding season do so rapidly at small body size, while males maturing later delay maturation until they reach larger size.
2. The social control hypothesis: late-summer males delay maturation and reach larger size in response to the presence of mature conspecific males.
3. The [[Krumholz-Sohn Competition-Predation Hypothesis]], tested through the removal experiment.

**Measurement.** Total length (TL) was used as the length measure, as in Krumholz (1948), because it is easier to measure on small individuals. In a sample of 40 randomly selected adult males from Hovey Lake (1983), TL was related to standard length (SL) by a regression with R² = 0.988 (mean TL 24.0 mm, range 18–31 mm; mean SL 20.3 mm, range 15–26 mm).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Hovey Lake is in Posey County, in the extreme southwestern corner of Indiana, near the confluence of the Wabash and Ohio rivers. The population studied is a natural, free-living population of *G. affinis*.

**Experimental ponds.** The field experiment used two experimental ponds and five control ponds, each about 340 m² with a maximum depth of about 0.5 m. These were located at the [[Grassy Fork Hatchery]] in Martinsville, Morgan County, Indiana, a commercial goldfish-breeding facility. Ponds were spring-fed and contained substantial populations of *G. affinis* at apparently equal density. Ponds were not stocked with predators that might have confounded the results.

**Life-history context.** The breeding season in the study population runs from late spring through summer. Overwintering females produce near-synchronous broods early in the season, and young-of-the-year females contribute increasingly to production later. Mean male body size changes across the season as a result of differences in timing of maturation.

**Temperature.** Average temperatures at Martinsville from mid-August to mid-September 1983 were markedly lower than those from mid-July to mid-August. Laboratory work by the author (unpublished) indicated that warm temperature hastens male maturation in the absence of social inhibition.

## Key Findings

**1. Sampling and sex ratio (1982, Hovey Lake).**
- Collections were made on approximately nine dates from late March to late September 1982.
- The overall sex ratio of individuals above 14 mm TL was 0.998 (males:females), not significantly different from 1:1.
- Overwintering adult males (March–April) were mainly large (>26 mm TL).
- In early June, when the first young-of-the-year adult males appeared, mean male TL was much lower; most were ≤21 mm TL.
- Mean TL of adult males increased slightly from early June to late July and rose markedly in late August and September.

**2. Maturing males and delayed maturation.**
- In August, maturing males were abundant relative to adult males, suggesting that many males were delaying maturation.
- Maturing young-of-the-year males showed a pattern of increasing mean TL from May to September, closely paralleling the increase in adult male TL.
- Even in August and September, some males as small as 14 mm TL were found.

**3. Oscillations in early season.** Immature male mean TL oscillated in late May through early July. The author attributes this to near-synchronous brood production by overwintering females, with broods growing and maturing together, "like separate year classes."

**4. Post-maturational growth.** Laboratory studies (Hughes 1984, cited) showed that adult male *G. affinis* grow slightly after maturation. Over 90 days, the increase was about 1.3 mm TL and about 1.1 mm SL. The author concludes these rates are too slight to account for the observed seasonal changes in field populations.

**5. Field experiment design and collections.**
- Males were removed repeatedly from experimental ponds on dates in July, August and September 1983; control ponds were left intact.
- Total collections were: experimental ponds, 14 adult, 354 maturing, 203 immature; control ponds, 35 adult, 241 maturing, 160 immature.
- Maturing males are defined as having elongated gonopodia with incomplete differentiation of the terminal holdfasts (hooks), and adults as having fully developed gonopodia.

**6. Interaction effects.**
- Two-way ANOVA of TL for adult, maturing and immature males showed a highly significant treatment × time interaction in every case (P < 0.0001; Table 3).
- Adult males: mean TL in experimental ponds rose only slightly from July to September, whereas mean TL in control ponds rose much more.
- Maturing males: mean TL increased in both treatments, but more dramatically in controls.
- Immature males: both treatments increased from July to August, but the August increase was greater in experimental ponds; both dropped in September.

**7. Sex ratio shifts after removal.**
- In September, the sex ratio in control ponds was 436:412 (1.06).
- In experimental ponds, it was 572:645 (0.89).
- These differ significantly (P < 0.05). The control ratio did not differ from 1:1, which the author takes to confirm that male removal was effective.

**8. Interpretation.**
- The results agree with Hubbs's (1971) data showing an increase in mean TL of adult males over the breeding season.
- The author concludes that the seasonal change in male size is due to delayed maturation at large size by late-summer and fall males, rather than growth by already mature males.
- The findings support [[Sohn 1977]]'s interpretation of [[Krumholz 1948]]'s data on *G. manni*, suggesting a similar competition-predation mechanism.
- Temperature and incomplete male removal in experimental ponds could partly explain the weaker maturing-male response; the author does not rule out other factors.

**9. Bias check.** The author considered whether size-selective removal could explain the results. Two arguments weigh against bias: male TL variance in July and August was low, and because swimming speed scales with TL (Beamish 1978), any net bias would be expected to favor smaller males. Such a bias would have produced the opposite of the observed pattern.

**10. Genetic variation.** Stearns (1983) found that *G. affinis* populations introduced to Hawaii differ genetically in male developmental rate and adult size, and the author notes a similar genetic component may exist in Indiana. The author also reports variance in TL among early- and late-summer males in natural populations, which may have a genetic basis.

**11. Conclusions.** The author concludes that:
- Late-summer male mosquitofish delay maturation and attain larger body sizes, consistent with the Hubbs hypothesis.
- Social inhibition of maturation is a plausible mechanism, although other factors (temperature, genotype, incomplete removal) are not excluded.
- Predation and competition may jointly affect male demography in mosquitofish.
