# Distribution of Spikedace, Loach Minnow and Chub Species in the Gila River Basin, New Mexico, 1908–2007

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This technical report, submitted to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, compiles 1908–2007 collection records for five rare native cyprinids in the [[Gila River Drainage]] of New Mexico: [[Gila Chub]], [[Headwater Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. The authors, Yvette M. Paroz and David L. Propst of the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] (NMDGF), used 1,739 collection records to produce geo-referenced distribution maps for three periods (pre-1980, 1980–1999, 2000–2007). The central finding is a sustained contraction in range and abundance. Chubs have disappeared from the [[San Francisco River]] sub-drainage and the lower mainstem Gila River, spikedace has been reduced to a short reach, and roundtail chub is probably extirpated from New Mexico. Loach minnow persists but has declined locally.

## Background & Description

The Gila River drainage in New Mexico historically supported 13 native fish species, five of them endemic. The rare species treated here are the three chubs of the Gila River basin, which were only recently recognized as distinct (Minckley and DeMarais 2000; Nelson et al. 2004), and two small minnows, spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) and loach minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*).

Protection status differs among the species:

- **Gila chub:** federally endangered (USFWS 2005) and endangered in New Mexico.
- **Headwater chub:** federal candidate for listing (USFWS 2006) and endangered in New Mexico.
- **Roundtail chub:** federal protection found warranted but precluded (USFWS 2006) and endangered in New Mexico.
- **Spikedace:** federally threatened (USFWS 1986) and up-listed to endangered by New Mexico in 2006.
- **Loach minnow:** federally threatened (USFWS 1986) and threatened in New Mexico.

Two other species fall outside the report's scope. [[Gila Topminnow]] was extirpated from New Mexico by the early 1960s and is the subject of restoration efforts. [[Gila Trout]] is listed federally and by the state but was not analyzed.

Table 1 lists native fishes of the drainage, with conservation status codes. Among these, longfin dace, speckled dace, desert sucker and Sonora sucker are rated common or uncommon and widespread. Colorado pikeminnow and razorback sucker were never documented in New Mexico, though both occur downstream in Arizona and likely entered the state at least seasonally.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report is a compilation of existing records, not a systematic survey. It draws on four main sources:

- NMDGF Scientific Collecting Permit annual reports
- NMDGF field notes
- agency reports
- museum accession records from nine institutions, including the University of New Mexico Museum of Southwest Biology and the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History

Most sampling occurred at permanent annually sampled sites. This gives reliable trend information for those locations and is taken as indicative of nearby reaches. Coldwater reaches were excluded.

Sampling history reflects the people involved. [[W.J. Koster]] and students collected from the late 1940s through the 1960s and produced the first general accounting of drainage fishes (Koster 1957). P.R. Turner and students worked in the 1970s. D.L. Propst, K.R. Bestgen and C.W. Painter led the 1980s work, and NMDGF, the [[U.S. Forest Service]] and Western New Mexico University carried most sampling after the early 1990s. Sampling gaps persisted in several reaches, including the upper warmwater reaches of the West and Middle forks, the [[Gila Wilderness]] reach of the mainstem, and the upper San Francisco River.

The chub taxonomy is unresolved. The American Fisheries Society did not recognize Gila chub as separate from roundtail chub until 1991, so earlier records may be misidentified. Location was not used to reclassify specimens collected before 2000. Consequently, the chubs were grouped as a "roundtail chub complex" for mapping.

## Key Findings

**Overall data and methods**
- 1,739 collection records were used, spanning 1908 to spring 2007.
- Records represented 2,509 chub (Gila sp.) specimens, 34,709 spikedace and 18,755 loach minnow.
- Thirty-four New Mexico records were excluded because their locations could not be placed within 15 km. Nineteen Arizona records fell outside the map scope or lacked UTM references.
- Circle size on the maps indicates the number of individuals per sampling occasion, which gives only a rough measure of abundance.

**Chubs (roundtail chub complex)**
- No chub has been collected in the San Francisco River in New Mexico since 1948. The authors describe the Koster and Rafferty specimens as roundtail chub.
- Chub was last collected in the lower mainstem Gila River almost 15 years ago. No chub has been found downstream of the forks area in recent sampling.
- Roundtail chub is probably extirpated, with no confirmed specimen in at least 15 years.
- Gila chub, if it persists in New Mexico, occurs as a single population in about 2 km of [[Turkey Creek]]. Genetic work (Schwemm 2006) found Turkey Creek chubs quite similar to headwater chub, so the taxonomic status remains unresolved.
- Headwater chub is limited mainly to the Gila River forks, in less than 60 km of habitat. It has declined in abundance over the past 15 to 20 years, especially in the East and Middle forks.
- The largest recent chub collections were over 200 individuals from Turkey Creek in 2004 and 88 juveniles from the West Fork Gila River in 2006.

**Spikedace**
- Spikedace was last collected in the San Francisco River sub-drainage in 1950 (Koster). Some sources describe it as eliminated by the late 1950s.
- It was not collected in the San Francisco sub-drainage during 1980–1999, apart from one collection in Mangas Creek.
- No spikedace has been captured in the East or Middle forks since the mid-1990s, apart from sporadic records. The species persists in the lower West Fork.
- Currently the Cliff–Redrock reach is the only area where spikedace is regularly collected. It is moderately common in about 8 km near the [[Gila Bird Area]].
- Collections of several hundred fish were common before 1980. In the most recent period, numbers elsewhere in the sub-drainage rarely exceeded 50.

**Loach minnow**
- Loach minnow persists in much of its historical range in both sub-drainages, though local declines have occurred.
- It was found at a large proportion of sampled warmwater sites before 1980. Its broader distribution in 1980–1999 reflected more thorough sampling, not range expansion.
- Loach minnow was not found in the Gila Wilderness reach or in the San Francisco River between San Francisco Plaza and Reserve.
- Recent collections in the Gila Forks are very small. The species has not been collected in the upper East Fork since 2001, and was last collected in the Tularosa River in 2002.
- Large annual collections (over 100 individuals) still occur in the Gila Bird Area. Elsewhere, recent catches are usually under 25 individuals. The permanent site on the San Francisco River near Glenwood yielded more than 75 in some years during 1980–1999, but normally fewer than 25 now.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The authors state that the range and abundance of spikedace, loach minnow, headwater chub and Gila chub have declined considerably over the past 100 years, and that roundtail chub is likely extirpated. Further loss "will further compromise their conservation and increase the probability that each will be extirpated."
- Stream reaches needing survey include Mule, Pueblo and Negrito creeks, the upper San Francisco Box, and the Gila Wilderness and Middle Box reaches.
- Intensive, systematic surveys of the West, Middle and East forks are recommended. NMDGF and the Gila National Forest were conducting such inventories at the time.
- The report notes that these maps are not an absolute assessment of abundance trends and do not replace quantified population analysis.
