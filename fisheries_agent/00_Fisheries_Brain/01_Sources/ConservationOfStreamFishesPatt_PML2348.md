# Conservation of Stream Fishes in North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1988 essay in *Conservation Biology* (Vol. 2, No. 2) by Paul C. Marsh and Andrew L. Sheldon argues that North American stream fishes need biogeographic, system-level conservation rather than only habitat preservation and single-species management. The authors note that the continent's freshwater fish fauna is rich, that many stream fishes have limited ranges and are locally rare, and that declines are documented. Between-drainage diversity is large but threatened by [[Habitat Fragmentation]], [[Interbasin Transfers]] and [[Species Introductions]], which homogenize faunas. The authors recommend a biogeographic perspective, focus on mid-sized streams (order 4) and the upstream portions of each drainage, and protection of rare or threatened species.

## Background & Description

- **Diversity:** Approximately 700 species regularly spend all or part of their life histories in fresh waters of the United States and Canada (Lee et al. 1980). The authors state this exceeds the bird diversity of the same region. The Mexican fauna adds many more species.
- **Ancient lineages:** Families include bowfins (Amiidae), gars ([[Gar Family]]), sturgeons, paddlefish and mooneyes; some families are shared with Eurasia, such as Cyprinidae, while others such as Centrarchidae and Ictaluridae are largely North American.
- **Endemic families:** Suckers (Catostomidae) are nearly restricted to North America, as are pirate perches, trout-perches and cavefishes.
- **Darters:** [[Darters]] (Etheostomatini, Percidae) have radiated spectacularly and account for about 20% of the species in the total fish fauna north of Mexico.
- **Dominant groups:** Minnows (Cyprinidae) dominate the fauna; Percidae (mostly darters) rank a close second. Killifishes and livebearers are characteristic of southwestern remnant waters. Salmonids and sculpins become more common northward.
- **Rarity and listing:** More than half of freshwater fishes in the US and Canada receive some legal protection in part of their range. A total of 75 species and subspecies are federally listed as endangered or threatened (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1987). Endangered taxa are concentrated in the arid West; threatened taxa are predominantly western but include more southeastern species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** Few stream fish species are widely distributed (McAllister et al. 1986). Local abundances approximate lognormal distributions, so most species are geographically restricted and numerically rare where they occur. Patchy distributions are common.
- **Regional patterns:** Rivers west of the Continental Divide contain relatively few but distinctive, highly endemic species. East of the divide, the [[Mississippi River System]] is far richer than smaller Gulf and Atlantic coastal rivers, which in turn exceed Pacific drainages. Diversity is greatest in the southeastern United States and decreases northward.
- **Species-area relationships:** Fish richness increases with drainage area (Eadie et al. 1986; Larsen et al. 1986; Swift et al. 1986) and discharge (Livingstone et al. 1982; Sheldon 1987). Drainages are treated as islands, and isolated systems support fewer native species than interconnected systems of comparable area. Speciation may be higher in larger drainages.
- **Riverine archipelago:** Watershed divides, large lowland rivers, and saltwater barriers, some breached by low Pleistocene sea levels and stream capture, create an archipelagic structure. Interdrainage differentiation was quantified with Preston's (1962) resemblance index, where 0 means complete identity and 1.0 means no shared species. Preston proposed that an index of about 0.27 implies "isolates in equilibrium." Gulf Coast drainages are more strongly differentiated than Atlantic coastal drainages.
- **Introductions:** Introduced species erode regional uniqueness through stocking, aquarium releases and bait-bucket introductions. The Clark Fork in western Montana has been trebled in fish species by introductions (Brown 1971), harming native [[Cutthroat Trout]]. The New River has 43% nonnative fishes (Hocutt et al. 1986). Green sunfish reduced native fishes in North Carolina streams (Lemly 1985).
- **Waterway connections:** The Tennessee-Tombigbee Waterway connects two of the richest and most distinctive faunas (Mobile and Tennessee). The Great Lakes fauna has been catastrophically altered by introductions, exploitation and pollution, with three species and one subspecies extinct (Williams & Nowak 1986).

## Key Findings

- **Historical declines:** The rosyface shiner remains widespread but disappeared from much of its Ohio range by midcentury (Trautman 1957). The harelip sucker vanished from rivers of nine southeastern and midwestern states before 1900 (Ono et al. 1983).
- **Extinction from impoundment:** Impoundment of virtually the entire 1000 km length of the Tennessee River caused the loss of the silvery minnow (*Hybognathus nuchalis*) from the whole system, including unaffected tributaries (Etnier et al. 1979). Other losses may have occurred in the 50 years since construction began. Records are probably inadequate to document all extinctions.
- **Faunal management:** Because species-rich faunas contain mostly rare species, reductions in species number from stress or fragmentation are more predictable than the identities of lost species. The authors therefore call for faunal management rather than single-species focus.
- **Directional systems:** Protecting any stream segment requires control over the entire upstream network and surrounding landscape. Little protection is likely for many order-4 or higher streams, yet these support the greatest fish diversity.
- **Recommended actions:**
  - Prohibit interbasin transfers of water and strengthen controls on introductions.
  - Apply riparian control and prevent industrial and agricultural pollution, channelization and impoundment where feasible.
  - Focus conservation on the largest reasonably natural drainages in as many major river systems as possible.
  - Pursue a biogeographic overview as a necessary component of any comprehensive plan, while noting that single-species management remains compatible with the broader approach.
- **Public perception:** Small non-game fishes are commonly seen as "minnows," making public support for their conservation difficult, and they have few advocates in water-resource conflicts.
- **Conclusion:** The authors state that a comprehensive conservation plan for North American fishes remains a goal for the future. Fish conservation also aligns with protecting molluscs, crayfishes, riparian vegetation and water quality.
