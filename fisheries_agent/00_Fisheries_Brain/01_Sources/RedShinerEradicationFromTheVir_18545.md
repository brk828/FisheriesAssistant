## Executive Summary

This 1988 report by W. L. Minckley documents the background, execution, and unintended consequences of a rotenone (ichthyocide) application intended to eradicate the invasive [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) from a targeted ~34-km reach of the Virgin River, Arizona-Nevada-Utah, in order to protect the Endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and Candidate species [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*). Due to unexpectedly rapid downstream transport of rotenone and delayed detoxification, the treatment escaped its intended containment barriers, killing fish for at least 70 km downstream into Nevada, far beyond the planned zone. The document assesses actual and potential ecological, taxonomic, and genetic impacts of this accidental over-application, and proposes a long-term monitoring and genetic-sampling program to convert the incident into a scientific opportunity for studying repopulation and recolonization dynamics in desert stream fish communities.

## Background & Description

The report emphasizes cryptic morphological and putative taxonomic diversity among several native [[Virgin River]] fishes, distinguishing **channel-adapted "forms"** from **tributary/spring-adapted "forms"**:

- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): At least three subspecific/form-level units recognized — *R. o. yarrowi* (mainstream/large tributaries) and an undescribed *R. osculus* subspecies restricted to springs and spring-fed tributaries.
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** (*Catostomus latipinnis*): Exhibits an elongate, thin-bodied mainstream "form" versus a shorter, thicker-bodied tributary "form" (the latter described elsewhere as the undescribed Little Colorado River sucker, *Catostomus* sp.).
- **[[Desert Sucker]]** (*Pantosteus "clarki"*): Two co-occurring forms in the Virgin River basin — a swift-water form with large fins and thin caudal peduncle (mainstream) and a thicker-bodied, shorter-finned tributary form. Evidence of intergradation/hybridization occurs near the mouth of Beaver Dam Creek, Arizona. A distinct, geographically isolated taxon (formerly *Pantosteus intermedius* Tanner 1942) persisted in the Pluvial White River remnants.
- **[[Virgin River Chub]]** and **[[Virgin Spinedace]]** (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*) are similarly hypothesized to include distinct genetic/ecological units segregated by channel versus tributary/spring habitat, though not yet taxonomically resolved.

No new morphometric data are presented; rather, the report synthesizes historical taxonomic uncertainty (citing Hubbs, Miller, Smith 1966, and Minckley 1973) to argue for genetic (allozyme and mitochondrial DNA) analysis to resolve form/species status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System description**: The Virgin River is a hydrologically ancient, intermittent desert stream draining the southwestern Colorado Plateau, flowing from Utah through Arizona into [[Lake Mead]], Nevada. Flow is heavily influenced by spring inputs (e.g., Pah Tempe Spring) and tributaries (Beaver Dam Wash, Meadow Valley Wash, Moapa River).
- **Native fish community** (six native taxa, excluding Moapa/pluvial White River endemics) partitions habitat between **mainstream channel** and **tributary/spring** environments, a recurring pattern across multiple species.
- **Invasion history**: [[Red Shiner]] entered the lower Virgin River from Lake Mead following 1950s introduction into the lower Colorado River basin, coexisting with [[Woundfin]] for ≥25 years at low densities upstream of Littlefield, Arizona. Construction of an irrigation reservoir on Quail Creek in the early 1980s stabilized flows, enabling red shiner to expand upstream to the Washington Fields Diversion (WFD) barrier, displacing woundfin and vectoring an exotic Asian tapeworm into native fish populations.
- **Trophic/community dynamics**: Red shiner expansion is linked to historical declines of the related [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) elsewhere in Arizona, suggesting a broader pattern of competitive displacement of native cyprinids by this invasive species in Southwestern desert streams.
- **Post-treatment recolonization pathways** proposed: (a) in situ reproduction of accidental-kill survivors, (b) downstream dispersal from above WFD, (c) dispersal from unaffected tributary/irrigation-diversion refugia, or (d) combinations thereof — each with distinct genetic and community-ecology implications.

## Key Findings

1. **Eradication design vs. outcome**: The intended treatment zone was a ~34-km reach between two constructed barriers below WFD; rotenone escaped detoxification controls and caused fish mortality at least 70 km downstream to Mesquite, Nevada, and possibly farther.
2. **Selective mortality**: Rotenone-sensitive, channel-dwelling **native** species were disproportionately killed, while rotenone-resistant non-natives (red shiner, bullhead catfish *Ameiurus* spp.) survived — potentially favoring exactly the invasive species the program aimed to eliminate.
3. **Scale of potential loss**: Up to ~80% of the known range of the Endangered [[Woundfin]] and Candidate [[Virgin River Chub]] may have been affected, alongside likely losses of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Speckled Dace]] mainstream populations.
4. **Biogeographic significance**: Flannelmouth sucker populations in the Virgin River and Grand Canyon represent the last extant mainstream populations in the lower Colorado River basin (extirpated elsewhere below Lake Mead), heightening the conservation stakes of the incident.
5. **Genetic bottleneck risk**: Surviving channel populations may be reduced to sizes small enough to cause genetic bottlenecking, and tributary "forms" may recolonize the channel, altering community and genetic composition relative to the historic population.
6. **Salvage efforts**: Prior to treatment, woundfin and Virgin River chub were partially salvaged to Dexter National Fish Hatchery (New Mexico) and a holding pond near Quail Creek Dam; no salvage was attempted for other native species.
7. **Research opportunity framing**: Despite (or because of) the accident, the Virgin River's exceptional long-term monitoring dataset (dating to federal woundfin listing) provides an unprecedented baseline to empirically test hypotheses about repopulation vs. recolonization dynamics, gene flow between channel and tributary forms, and genetic differentiation of hatchery/refugium stocks vs. wild populations.
8. **Recommendations**: The report calls for immediate post-eradication sampling of survivors and tributary populations, multi-generational genetic monitoring, and a formal long-term collaborative study (ASU/UNLV/USFWS) to responsibly document impacts and inform future management of desert stream fish conservation and ichthyocide use.
