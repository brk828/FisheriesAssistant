# The Age and Growth of the Silverjaw Minnow (*Ericymba buccata*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #age-and-growth

## Executive Summary

This study examines the age and growth dynamics of the [[Silverjaw Minnow]] (*[[Ericymba buccata]]*) in a small tributary stream system (North Branch of Otter Creek, Indiana) over a full annual cycle (Oct 1964–Oct 1965). Using scale annuli analysis on 2,465 collected specimens, the author validated the annulus as a reliable year-marker and reconstructed growth histories back to 1961. Key findings include sexually dimorphic growth (males eventually larger), evidence of compensatory growth in slower-starting individuals, extended and size-dependent timing of annulus formation, and a documented poor growth year (1963–64) correlated with below-average spring/summer air temperatures. Maximum lifespan is estimated at approximately 4 years.

## Background & Description

- **Scale morphology**: Annulus identified by "cutting over" of circuli in lateral fields and abrupt transition from closely-spaced to widely-spaced circuli, most visible in the posterior field.
- **Measurement standards**: Standard length (S.L.) measured snout tip to posterior edge of hypural plate; scale radius measured from focus to posterior scale edge (magnification 112X for growth calculations).
- **Body-scale growth relationship** (linear/rectilinear model, r = 0.98 for both sexes):
  - Males: L = 5.653 mm + 0.250R
  - Females: L = 5.271 mm + 0.257R
  - (L = standard length in mm; R = scale radius × 112)
- **Sexual size dimorphism**: Males attain significantly larger size than females starting in their 3rd year of life, particularly evident in "large adults" (41+ mm) across spring, summer, and autumn seasons (not significant in winter, likely due to differential winter mortality of faster-growing males).
- **Maximum lifespan**: ~4 years (fourth-summer individuals represented only 2.3% of the large March sample).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: North Branch of Otter Creek, a tributary of the [[Wabash River]], Vigo Co., Indiana. Stream flows through Illinoian drift substrate (sand, fine gravel, silt, some clay); gradient ~6.7 ft/mile; typical depths 6 inches–3 ft.
- **Relative abundance**: *Ericymba buccata* was the **most abundant fish species** in the study reach, underscoring its ecological importance in small warmwater stream assemblages.
- **Reproductive/growth ecology**: Long breeding season causes substantial size overlap between late-spawned young of one year and early-spawned young of the subsequent year, making length a poor proxy for age beyond the first summer of life.
- **Seasonal growth patterns**:
  - Young-of-the-year (YOY) continue growing later into autumn than older fish.
  - YOY resume growth earlier in spring than older individuals.
  - Older fish show **no growth** from mid-autumn through winter (Oct–March).
  - Growth for 2nd/3rd-summer fish is concentrated between May and July, tapering off by late summer.
- **Comparative context**: Referenced growth-compensation patterns are consistent with other freshwater species: striped bass ([[Roccus saxatilis]]), lake herring ([[Leucichthys artedi]]), rock bass ([[Ambloplites rupestris]]), and warmouth ([[Lepomis gulosus]]).
- **Temperature-growth linkage**: Growth rate correlates with mean spring/summer air temperature, paralleling findings in rock bass (Hile 1941) and spottail shiner *[[Notropis hudsonius]]* (McCann 1959).

## Key Findings

### Annulus Validity & Timing
- Annulus validated as reliable year-marker via four independent lines of evidence: (1) agreement between calculated and observed lengths, (2) annuli count increasing consistently with length, (3) consistent calculated increments across year classes within a collection, (4) cross-year-class agreement on relative growth quality in a given period.
- **Annulus completion is size/age-dependent**:
  - Smaller (mostly yearling) fish (22–37 mm S.L.): median completion ~3rd week of April.
  - Larger fish (39–47.5 mm S.L., 3rd/4th summer): median completion ~1st week of June (~6 weeks later than yearlings).
  - Overall completion window spans ~2.5–3 months across the population; ~4 weeks within each size group.
- Late-hatched YOY show less-defined first annuli due to reduced pre-winter growth, consistent with prior findings in bridled shiner ([[Notropis bifrenatus]]) (Harrington 1948).

### Sex-Specific Growth
- Growth comparable between sexes in years 1–2.
- Year 3: males exceed females by 2.7 mm (not statistically significant in this specific comparison, but broader seasonal length data confirm males significantly larger in spring, summer, and autumn for large adults).
- Weighted male lengths by year (1–4): 24.6, 34.6, 42.1, 49.1 mm.
- Weighted female lengths by year (1–4): 24.5, 35.8, 40.6, 45.1 mm.

### Compensatory Growth
- Analysis of 59 fish (1962 year class) split by initial size (small 18–24mm, moderate 25–28mm, large 29–35mm at 1st annulus):
  - Small-initial-size fish showed largest 2nd-year increment (15.9 mm) vs. moderate (11.9 mm) and large (8.4 mm) groups.
  - Growth differences largely equalized by the following year (increments 2.6, 2.7, 2.2 mm respectively).
  - Supports growth compensation phenomenon also documented in [[Striped Bass]], [[Lake Herring]], [[Rock Bass]], and [[Warmouth]].

### Seasonal/Environmental Effects
- **1963–64 growth period identified as anomalously poor**:
  - YOY growth increment: 21.9 mm vs. 26.6 mm (prior period) and 27.9 mm (following period).
  - 2nd-year growth: 11.9 mm vs. 13.9 mm (1964–65).
  - Correlated with lower-than-normal mean air temperatures in both early-summer (May–June: −2.1°F deviation) and late-summer (July–August: −5.8°F deviation) periods, per meteorological station records.

### Age Structure (28 March 1965 Collection, n=301)
- 26.1% completed 1st summer
- 37.5% completed 2nd summer
- 34.1% completed 3rd summer
- 2.3% completed 4th summer

### Methodology Notes
- Scale sampling: minimum 4 scales per fish, taken from scale row above lateral line, anterior to dorsal origin.
- Statistical testing: one-tailed t-test (Steel and Torrie 1960) used for mean length comparisons across groups/sexes/seasons.
- Growth calculation followed Hile (1941) scale-based back-calculation methodology.
