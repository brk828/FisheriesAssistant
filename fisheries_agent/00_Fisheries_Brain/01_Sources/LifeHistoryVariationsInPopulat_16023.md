# Life history variations in populations of American shad, Alosa sapidissima (Wilson), spawning in tributaries of the St John River, New Brunswick

## Executive Summary

This 1975 study by Carscadden & Leggett examined **intraspecific life-history variation** in [[American Shad]] (*Alosa sapidissima*) spawning at four sites within the [[St John River]] system, New Brunswick, Canada: St John Harbour, Kennebecasis River, Washademoak Lake, and Mactaquac Dam. Sampling occurred over three consecutive spawning seasons (1971–1973). The researchers found that shad populations spawning in different tributaries of the *same* river system exhibited reproductive strategy differences (fecundity, age at maturity, repeat spawning incidence) analogous to those observed across the *entire* Atlantic coast latitudinal range of the species. This supports a hypothesis of **fine-scale homing to natal tributaries** and localized adaptive divergence in reproductive traits, rather than these traits being fixed solely at a broad geographic/latitudinal scale.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: American shad, *Alosa sapidissima* (Wilson), family [[Clupeidae]] — an anadromous, semelparous-to-iteroparous herring relative.
- **Study system**: The [[St John River]] originates in Maine/Quebec, flows south through New Brunswick, and empties into the [[Bay of Fundy]]. In its lower ~160 km, four major tributaries provide spawning habitat.
- **Historical range**: Shad historically migrated as far as Grand Falls; by the study period, only small numbers reached [[Beechwood Dam]].
- **Sampling sites** (with approximate distance from river mouth):
  - **St John Harbour** (river mouth) — commercial gill nets (1971); experimental multi-panel drift gill net (1972–1973), panels of 9.5, 11.4, 13.0, and 14.0 cm stretched mesh.
  - **Kennebecasis River** (~48 km) — 11.4, 12.7, and 14.0 cm stretched mesh gill nets.
  - **Washademoak Lake** (~88 km) — gaspereau trap nets (incidental catch of shad).
  - **Mactaquac Dam** (~140 km) — mechanical fish lift, operational since 1967.
- **Methods**: Fork length, weight, sex, scale-based ageing (Cating 1953 method, validated by Judy 1961), and fecundity via gravimetric/subsample egg-counting (Leggett 1969 method, sieved into four egg-size classes). Multiple regression of fecundity on length, weight, and age was used per population/year (Table I) to derive standardized fecundity estimates for five hypothetical "standard shad" (Table II).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Shad are **anadromous** and do not feed during the freshwater spawning migration (Walburg & Nichols, 1967), so energy allocation during migration is a zero-sum trade-off between **fecundity, growth, and post-spawning survival**.
- The [[Mactaquac Dam]], completed in 1967, introduced a mechanical fish lift that altered upstream recruitment; adult mortality during lift operations was estimated at a minimum of 25% in early years, confounding reproductive trait estimates for this population.
- Migration distance to spawning grounds (Kennebecasis ~48 km vs. Washademoak Lake ~88 km) and associated **water temperature exposure and starvation duration** are proposed as key environmental drivers of variation in repeat-spawning (iteroparity) rates — consistent with hypotheses from [[Chittenden 1969]] and [[Leggett 1969, 1972]].
- The commercial gillnet fishery at St John Harbour was found to have **minimal impact** on upstream spawning stocks, since it collapsed early each season due to falling shad prices; larger mesh sizes disproportionately caught older, larger, repeat-spawning fish.
- Broader comparative context: similar intraspecific plasticity in reproductive life history has been documented in [[Pacific Sardine]], [[Steelhead Trout]], [[Kokanee]], [[Lake Herring]], [[Atlantic Herring]], [[Pacific Herring]], [[Atlantic Cod]], [[Greenland Cod]], [[Pacific Cod]], and [[Plaice]], generally interpreted as latitudinal clines (Nikolsky, 1970).

## Key Findings

### Age at Maturity
- Males matured significantly younger than females in **all** populations and years (P<0.05).
- Weighted mean age at maturity (years):

| Population | Males | Females |
|---|---|---|
| St John Harbour | 4.1 | 4.5 |
| Kennebecasis River | 4.1 | 4.4 |
| Washademoak Lake | 3.8 | 4.2 |
| Mactaquac Dam | 3.9 | 4.4 |

- **Washademoak Lake** shad of both sexes matured at the youngest age; differences between Kennebecasis River and Washademoak Lake were significant (P<0.05, and P<0.01 for weighted means).

### Growth
- Males were consistently shorter than females at equivalent ages (Fig. 2).
- Inter-population differences in mean fork length were generally small (<1 cm) and inconsistent year to year.
- Virgin (first-time spawning) males and females from **Washademoak Lake** were the smallest at maturity across most age classes (Table V).

### Repeat Spawning (Iteroparity)
Percent repeat spawners (sexes combined, all years):

| Population | % Repeat Spawners |
|---|---|
| St John Harbour | 78% |
| Kennebecasis River | 84% |
| Washademoak Lake | 40% |
| Mactaquac Dam | 35% |

- **Kennebecasis River** (closest spawning site to the river mouth) had the highest repeat-spawning proportion.
- **Washademoak Lake** and **Mactaquac Dam** (farther upstream) had markedly lower proportions, consistent with the hypothesis that migration distance and associated temperature/starvation stress reduce post-spawning survival.
- Gillnet mesh-size selectivity at St John Harbour showed larger mesh caught progressively older, larger, and higher proportions of repeat spawners (Table VII).
- Mactaquac Dam repeat-spawner proportions declined sharply over the study period (56%→27%→22% for males; 41%→39%→24% for females), likely reflecting disruption from dam/fish-lift operations since 1967.

### Fecundity
- Mean virgin, mean population, and mean lifetime fecundity were calculated per population (Table VIII).
- **Kennebecasis River** exhibited the highest mean lifetime fecundity (423,941 eggs, all years combined) versus Washademoak Lake (233,569) and Mactaquac Dam (201,980).
- **Relative fecundity** (standardized "standard shad," Table IX) was highest in **Washademoak Lake**, and increased more steeply with shad size/age there than in other populations — consistent with a strategy trading higher per-spawning fecundity against lower repeat-spawning incidence.
- Overall pattern: **lower relative fecundity was associated with higher proportions of repeat spawners** — mirroring the trade-off pattern Leggett (1969) reported across the broader Atlantic coast latitudinal range (St John River, FL; York River, VA; Connecticut River, CT), where fecundity and age at maturity decreased and repeat spawning increased with increasing latitude.

### Mactaquac Dam Anomaly
- The Mactaquac population did **not** fit the predicted combination of traits (expected: highest relative fecundity, lowest repeat-spawner proportion, lowest age at maturity based on its upstream position). Only the low repeat-spawner proportion matched predictions.
- Numbers of shad passing the fish lift declined sharply post-construction (Table X): 38,843 in 1968 → 1,497 in 1972, with partial rebound to 8,000 in 1973 — attributed to differential mortality and disrupted recruitment from the dam's fish-lift operations.

### Overarching Conclusion
- The same **combination of reproductive life-history traits** (fecundity, age at maturity, repeat-spawning rate) that differentiates shad populations at the macro-geographic (coastwide, latitudinal) scale also differentiates populations at the **micro-geographic scale** within a single river system.
- This pattern is best explained by **homing behavior to specific natal tributaries**, producing reproductive isolation sufficient to allow localized adaptive divergence — corroborated by companion meristic data (Carscadden & Leggett, 1975) on [[Homing Behavior in Shad]].
