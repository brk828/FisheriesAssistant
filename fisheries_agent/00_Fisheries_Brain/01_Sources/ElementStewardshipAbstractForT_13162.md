# Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis) Stewardship Abstract

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Element Stewardship Abstract, prepared for The Nature Conservancy's Arizona Field Office and compiled by Stephen P. Vives of Georgia Southern University, summarizes management-relevant information on the [[Loach Minnow]], a small, bottom-dwelling cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona, New Mexico and Sonora, Mexico. The species was listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1986, but critical habitat had not been designated at the time of the update (dated 90-10-26). The abstract concludes that recovery potential is good only if remaining riffle habitat is vigorously protected and that non-native fishes, particularly the [[Red Shiner]], are a major threat.

## Background & Description

- **Size and form:** Small (<70 mm standard length), elongate, subfusiform and slightly flattened ventrally, giving a "darter-like" appearance.
- **Meristics:** Eight dorsal and seven anal fin rays; 61–65 lateral line scales; pharyngeal teeth in two rows, 1,4–4,1.
- **Diagnostic features:** Very small, terminal, highly oblique mouth; no barbels; upper lip attached to the snout by a broad frenum; restricted gill openings; olivaceous body with dark blotches and off-white spots near the dorsal fin base and caudal peduncle.
- **Breeding coloration:** Males show vivid red to red-orange markings on fins, mouth, head and abdomen; females may show yellowing of fins and ventral body.
- **Similar species:** Most similar to the [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), which has a single barbel per side of the upper lip, a larger and less oblique mouth, and lacks the off-white spots. Loach minnow have more dorsally oriented eyes.
- **Taxonomy:** No other hypothesized relationships beyond a "Rhinichthys-like" stock.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use**
- Occupies shallow, turbulent, rocky riffles up to about 2200 m elevation.
- Restricted almost exclusively to the bottom because of a reduced gas bladder; lives among rock interstices and over gravel-pebble substrate.
- Rinne (1989) found shallow water (<20 cm) at 30–40 cm/s over 16–256 mm substrate in Aravaipa Creek and the upper Gila basin. Larvae used shallower (<10 cm) and slower (<20 cm/s) stream margins.
- Turner and Tafanelli (1983) reported highest use at 46–73 cm/s in Aravaipa Creek and 6–37 cm/s in the Gila River. The authors attribute the difference to methodology (velocity measured at 5 cm above substrate versus at 0.6 depth).
- Some sites have dense filamentous green algae; in others this association was not observed.

**Associated fauna:** Spikedace, speckled dace, longfin dace, roundtail chub, Sonora sucker and desert sucker. Woundfin, bonytail, Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker were historically associated but are extirpated from the Gila basin.

**Life history**
- Maturity at about 12 months; longevity typically 15–24 months, maximum perhaps 36 months.
- Spawns on riffles at average temperatures of 21°C. Adhesive eggs are attached to the ceiling or sides of cavities under rocks; males guard and fan the eggs.
- Breeding colors noted December–June and in September; nests found April–June and September; peak breeding reported in April.
- Nest egg counts ranged 4–250 (means 52 and 63 per nest). Mature ova ranged 144–1200 and increased with female size.
- Larvae about 5 mm at hatching, reaching 30–40 mm SL by midsummer. Mean SL is 55 mm at age I and 60 mm at age II.

**Diet:** Benthic insectivore feeding on riffle-dwelling mayflies, black flies and midges. Larval and juvenile fish rely heavily on chironomid larvae. Adult diet is more diverse, though still narrow relative to other cyprinids.

**Parasites:** Described by Mpoame (1982) and Mpoame and Rinne (1983).

## Key Findings

**Population quality criteria**
- A quality occurrence must span time: all year classes present for at least 3 years, with males in breeding color and nests present during spawning.
- A shorter-term assessment uses healthy fish of all age classes with reproductive activity, habitat quality (bank stability, riparian vegetation, riffle substrate, adequate flow, low non-native abundance) and threat assessment (water control structures, grazing, logging, erosion).

**Threats**
- Watershed changes reduce riffle habitat through impoundment, channel downcutting, sedimentation, water diversion and groundwater pumping.
- Non-native predators include channel catfish, flathead catfish, mosquitofish, green sunfish, largemouth bass and smallmouth bass. Competitors include the [[Red Shiner]].
- Red shiner and loach minnow do not co-occur, except in the Blue River, Arizona. This suggests red shiner may be a causal factor in the decline, though no direct evidence of competition exists.
- Natural flooding is important for maintaining suitable substrate. Increased sedimentation may reduce breeding habitat.

**Legal status**
- Federally listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (FWS 1986).
- Arizona: Group 3 threatened species. New Mexico: Group 2 endangered species.

**Land protection and critical habitat**
- Riffle habitat must be protected. Hardy et al. (1990) recommend instream flows 10–20 cfs above current BLM permitted flows for Aravaipa Creek.
- Critical habitat was proposed in 1985 but remained undesignated after the June 1987 deadline, so the existing proposal provides only limited protection.
- Proposed critical habitat covers seven reaches in Aravaipa Creek, the Gila River, the San Francisco River, the Tularosa River, the Blue River, Campbell Blue Creek and Dry Blue Creek.
- Much occupied habitat is on federal, state, conservation or tribal lands, but full protection requires critical habitat designation and [[Endangered Species Act]] compliance.

**Recovery potential**
- Recovery potential is good only if suitable habitat is vigorously protected. Reestablishment into historic range is problematic until causes of prior decline are corrected.
- Recommendations follow the draft recovery plan by Marsh (1988).

**Monitoring**
- All populations must be identified, including potential sites in Sonora, Mexico and on U.S. Forest Service and Apache tribal lands.
- Known populations should be monitored twice yearly, in spring (breeding) and late autumn (recruitment), using standardized methods.
- Five electroshocking passes in a net-blocked area were needed to capture 99% of loach minnow (Minckley 1981). Sampling should cover natural units (riffles, pools, runs, channels).
- No species-specific monitoring program is known. The Arizona October Fish Count and annual collections in Aravaipa Creek are the closest efforts.

**Research needs**
- Seven priority questions: natural abundance variation; interactions with non-native fishes; food competition with red shiner; effects of habitat modification; movement patterns; damage from diversion and barriers; and systematics of remaining populations.
- Only one active research program was identified: reproductive behavior studies by S.P. Vives. Loach minnow have bred successfully in aquaria.

**Management recommendations (Marsh 1988)**
- Protect existing populations, especially riffle habitat, and discourage detrimental water and land use.
- Maintain perennial flows with natural variation.
- Curtail non-native fish introductions, stockings and transport, and build barriers to invasion.
- Reintroduce populations to suitable historic streams using local stocks.
- Riparian management is essential. Cattle exclusion at Aravaipa Creek since 1973 is associated with successful reproduction and dominance of broadleaf riparian vegetation.
- If fire is used, controlled burns during winter (non-spawning season) are preferred, since crown fires can fill riffles with sediment.

**Management programs**
- No management programs beyond state and federal law protection are underway. BLM plans to hire a fisheries biologist in 1990 for Arizona public lands.

**Summary of stewardship needs**
- Monitor and protect existing populations, eliminate detrimental land and water use and exposure to non-native fishes, and reclaim degraded habitat. An ecosystem approach that addresses all native Gila basin fishes is recommended.
