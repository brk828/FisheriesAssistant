# Food Availability, Food Habits, and Growth of Tahoe Sucker (Catostomus tahoensis) in a Reservoir and a Natural Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the diet, benthic food supply, and growth of [[Tahoe Sucker]] (*[[Catostomus Tahoensis]]*) in a natural lake ([[Webber Lake]]) and a downstream reservoir ([[Stampede Reservoir]]) in the northern Sierra Nevada of California. Both waterbodies lie in the [[Truckee River Drainage]]. Reservoir suckers had a narrower diet dominated by [[Chironomid Larvae]], while lake suckers fed mainly on [[Mollusks]] and [[Gammarus]] amphipods. Despite this restricted diet and reduced benthic food diversity, reservoir suckers grew faster and reached greater length at older ages. The authors conclude that reservoir fish are probably not limited by food quantity, and that drawdown-driven simplification of the benthos may favor more energetically favorable feeding conditions.

## Background & Description

The Tahoe sucker is a bottom-feeding catostomid native to the Lake Tahoe and Truckee River basins. The species is common in both the lake and the reservoir, where it feeds in the benthic zone. The authors describe it as a generalist feeder whose diet reflects the relative abundance of benthic prey when many prey types are available.

The study addresses how reservoir impoundment changes the food base of a native fish. Reservoirs differ from natural lakes in several ways that matter to benthic consumers:

- Reservoirs are often not basin-shaped, and their discharge is frequently drawn from the hypolimnion, which alters nutrient retention and heat storage (Martin and Arneson 1971).
- Nutrient cycling and sedimentation in impoundments tend to favor phytoplankton, which can form the base of the food chain because benthic invertebrates are reduced (Ostrom 1971).
- Reservoir drawdown limits benthic fauna to especially tolerant organisms and removes rooted aquatic vegetation from the upper littoral zone (Lindstrom 1973).

The Tahoe sucker has been studied in several sites, including [[Sagehen Creek]], the [[Little Truckee River]], [[Lake Tahoe]], and [[Burton Creek]], providing comparative data on age, growth, and feeding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites**

- **Stampede Reservoir** (elevation 1794 m) lies about 25 km downstream of Webber Lake. It has undergone wide volume fluctuations since initial filling, particularly after drought conditions from 1975 onward.
- **Webber Lake** (elevation 2073 m) is a natural lake in the northern Sierra Nevada. It is cooler than the reservoir and has more diverse submerged vegetation.

**Habitat differences**

- Stampede Reservoir is less diverse than Webber Lake in both submerged vegetation structure and benthic invertebrate composition.
- Vegetation in Webber Lake included weedy beds of *Anacharis* (*Elodea*) and *Ceratophyllum*, along with filamentous green algae (*Rhizoclonium*).
- *C. tahoensis* was absent from benthic and plankton samples from the reservoir but was present in Webber Lake samples, mainly in weedy areas.

**Benthic invertebrate communities**

- Similar macroinvertebrate groups occurred in both systems, but densities differed.
- Oligochaetes were significantly different between the reservoir and the lake.
- Mollusks were more abundant in Webber Lake (mean 1,761/m²) than in Stampede Reservoir.
- Chironomid larvae did not differ significantly in density between the two sites.
- Caddisfly and other aquatic insect pupae were rarely recovered from the reservoir.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**

- Fish were captured biweekly from July to October, using fyke nets and traps set both day and night at Webber Lake and at four stations in Stampede Reservoir.
- Stomach contents were analyzed and food items were compared between lake and reservoir fish using square-root transformation and t-tests.
- Benthic macroinvertebrates were sampled with cores from three depth zones at both sites. Densities were compared using the Mann-Whitney U-test.
- Substrate and vegetation cover were recorded along 200 m transects by snorkel and SCUBA diving surveys.
- Age was determined from sectioned structures and compared using back-calculated lengths and t-tests.

**Diet**

- Gut contents differed significantly between lake and reservoir suckers.
- Lake suckers ate a varied diet dominated by mollusks, amphipods (*Gammarus*), and chironomid larvae. Mollusks and amphipods together made up the largest share of lake-sucker food, at roughly 29% and 21% of the diet, respectively.
- Reservoir suckers relied mainly on chironomid larvae, which were almost the only major invertebrate food item.
- Oligochaetes were abundant in the reservoir but made up a small portion of reservoir sucker diets, and they were not found in lake suckers.
- Detritus and sediment made up about 42% of material in the guts of reservoir fish. These values were excluded from the volumetric food calculations because they were thought to be incidental ingestion.
- Reservoir suckers consumed more plant material than lake suckers. The authors suggest this may reflect a more omnivorous diet or incidental ingestion while foraging.

**Size and growth**

- Lake suckers ranged from about 55 to 270 mm, while reservoir suckers ranged from about 100 mm upward.
- Length differences between lake and reservoir suckers were statistically significant.
- Age-length regressions for seven populations are summarized in a comparison table. The authors note that the reservoir relationship is curvilinear, so linear slopes do not fully represent growth differences.
- Reservoir suckers were significantly longer than lake suckers at older ages (approximately 7 to 11 years, as recorded in the source).
- Growth in the reservoir was higher than in Webber Lake, and reservoir suckers were longer on average than the Sagehen Creek and Little Truckee River populations. Lake Tahoe and Burton Creek populations were smaller at comparable ages.

**Factors influencing growth**

- Webber Lake is higher in elevation and cooler than Stampede Reservoir, which may affect growth rates (Martin 1980).
- Nutritional value of detritus may differ between the lake and reservoir, even though the volume of detritus consumed was similar.
- After impoundment, sucker populations changed in two ways. Compared with the pre-impoundment population in Sagehen Creek, reservoir suckers were larger on average, and the reservoir population contained fewer young-of-year and yearling fish.

**Intraspecific partitioning**

- Diet composition was similar among size classes of suckers in Stampede Reservoir, whereas diet differed among size classes in Webber Lake.
- Such overlap could lead to lower growth in the reservoir if fish were competing for limited food, but the growth data show the opposite.

**Conclusions**

- Reservoir suckers are probably not limited by the quantity of available food, despite a restricted diet and overlap among size classes.
- Simplified benthic food webs in the reservoir may produce a more energetically favorable situation for Tahoe suckers than more stable natural systems.
- The key implication is that reservoir suckers can maintain high growth rates by feeding on the abundant chironomid larvae, even with fewer food types than lake suckers use.
