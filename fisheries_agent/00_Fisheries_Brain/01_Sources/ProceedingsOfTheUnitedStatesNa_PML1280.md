# Notes on a Collection of Fishes from the Gila River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This note summarizes a short taxonomic paper by Philip H. Kirsch, published in the *Proceedings of the [[United States National Museum]]* (1888, pp. 555–558). The paper reports a collection of fishes made by Lieutenant W. L. Carpenter, U.S. Army, at Fort Thomas on the [[Gila River]], Arizona Territory, and sent to the museum of the University of Indiana on 4 June 1887. Kirsch treats seven species, describes one new species (*Catostomus gila*), and erects a new genus, *Xyrauchen*, for the sucker long known as *Catostomus cyphus*. The paper is largely morphological and comparative, using meristic and proportional characters.

The source is the front matter and table of contents of the 1888 *Proceedings* volume, which is largely a list of papers on many subjects (birds, fossil plants, mammals, insects, ethnology). Only one article, Kirsch's, concerns fishes. The summary below focuses on that article.

## Background & Description

Lieutenant Carpenter's field notes on the fishes included habits and local names. He held that only seven species occur in the Gila River at Fort Thomas, and that several species previously described from the stream are not distinct. He also noted that [[Gila Trout]] (*Gila emoryi*) and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) show varying characters at different growth stages, so these forms may have been mistaken for separate species.

The seven species treated are:

1. ***Catostomus latipinnis*** [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Baird & Girard). One specimen, 19 inches long. It differs from the Jordan & Gilbert description in having a longer preorbital bone, more than twice as long as deep.
2. ***Catostomus gila*** Kirsch, sp. nov. Three specimens, 12 to 14 inches long. Closely allied to *C. arcuatus* (Jordan & Gilbert), but with a more compressed body, a less elevated antedorsal region, a shorter and more compressed caudal peduncle, and a shorter, higher dorsal fin. The lower lip is divided by a narrow fissure into two fleshy lobes with about six rows of papillae each. Coloration is dark brown above, yellow below.
3. ***Catostomus insignis*** [[Sonora Sucker]] (Baird & Girard). One specimen, about 12 inches long, with moderate scales on the sides and very small scales on the belly.
4. ***Catostomus clarki*** [[Desert Sucker]] (Baird & Girard). One specimen, 12 inches long. Back scales are not quite as large as those of *C. insignis*. There are 65 scales along the lateral line.
5. ***Xyrauchen cypha*** (Lockington), the [[Bony-Tail Sucker]] or buffalo fish. Kirsch proposes the new genus *Xyrauchen* Eigenmann & Kirsch, gen. nov., separated from *Catostomus* by a large, sharp-edged dorsal hump. The hump is formed by an enlarged anterior interneural bone, which has a thick central pillar with anterior and posterior wings. The description is detailed, covering the fin rays, scales, lips, and the hump's relation to the skull and the first vertebrae.
6. ***Ptychocheilus lucius*** Girard (Gila salmon). One specimen, 15 inches long. It agrees generally with the Jordan & Gilbert description.
7. ***Gila emoryi*** (Baird & Girard) (Gila Trout; Bony Tail). Three specimens, 8 to 14 inches long. Kirsch lists differences from the published description, including a maxillary that does not reach the front of the eye, pectorals that do not quite reach the pubic bones, and a caudal peduncle about 4 times as long as deep. The pharyngeal teeth are falcate and compressed, with grinding surfaces that wear down with age.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fishes come from the [[Gila River]] at Fort Thomas, Arizona Territory, a desert river system. Carpenter's notes say that the fishes were valued for food and had local names, but the paper gives few habitat details. Carpenter reported that the Gila Trout is very tenacious of life. Specimens revived after being taken out of the water and frozen, and the paper notes that they were hardy in general. Seasonal observations include spring iris coloration and changes in the pharyngeal grinding surface during winter.

The sampling was a single collection, so the paper makes no claims about population structure, abundance, or distribution beyond the Fort Thomas reach.

## Key Findings

- Carpenter's collection at Fort Thomas contained **seven species**, which Carpenter regarded as the full local fauna. Kirsch reports them as they were identified in the collection.
- **One new species** is described: *Catostomus gila* Kirsch, sp. nov. It is distinguished from *C. arcuatus* by body shape, fin proportions, and lip structure.
- **One new genus** is erected: *Xyrauchen* Eigenmann & Kirsch, gen. nov., for *Catostomus cyphus* (*cypha*). The diagnostic character is the large, sharp-edged dorsal hump supported by enlarged interneural bones.
- Kirsch argues that some earlier species from the Gila River are not distinct, because growth-stage variation in *Gila emoryi* and *Ptychocheilus lucius* can produce apparently different forms.
- Specimen counts are small (1 to 3 per species), and most identifications rest on single individuals.
- Kirsch's comparative notes on the Gila River fishes are the main new contribution. They revise several earlier descriptions based on this regional material.

The rest of the 1888 volume, listed in the table of contents, covers unrelated fields. These include birds (such as Ridgway's descriptions of new subspecies of hawks and pigeons), fossil plants, mammals, Hymenoptera (including a large list of new Braconidae), mollusks, and ethnology. These papers are not summarized here.
