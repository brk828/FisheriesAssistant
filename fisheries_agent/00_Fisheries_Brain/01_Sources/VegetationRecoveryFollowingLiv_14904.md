# Vegetation Recovery Following Livestock Removal Near Quitobaquito Spring

#botany #plant-ecology #desert-springs

## Executive Summary

This technical report, by Peter L. Warren and L. Susan Anderson (January 1987, [[Quitobaquito Science Series]] No. 3), documents vegetation change around the spring complex at [[Quitobaquito Spring]], [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], after domestic livestock were removed from the monument in 1978–79. It focuses on [[Williams Spring]], [[Burro Spring]] and [[Aguajita Spring]], which the authors regard as more natural than the heavily modified Quitobaquito pond. Methods combined permanent plots, resampled in 1984 and compared with 1975 baselines, with repeat photography from 1974–76 and 1984. The marshy and riparian vegetation recovered substantially, mainly through the growth of previously browsed plants and some new germination. Desertscrub recovered far less. The authors also suggest that natural spring flow at Quitobaquito would have been reduced by dense vegetation, which bears on the historical presence of the [[Desert Pupfish]].

## Background & Description

Quitobaquito and its associated seeps are among the few spring-fed freshwater sources in hundreds of square miles of arid North America. The area has been used by people for thousands of years. Occupation by [[Papago Indians]] and related Sand Papagos ended with the last family's departure in the 1950s. Earlier, people of the Amargosa culture may have arrived about 5,000 years ago. The first Europeans are thought to have visited in 1540, and Father Kino described irrigated pastures in 1698. The spring was used for irrigation and livestock watering for over 250 years, until the National Park Service bought the rights from Jim Orosco, the last Papago resident.

The spring series lies along a southeast–northwest fault on the south side of the Quitobaquito Hills. Major springs, from the southeast, are Aguajita, Quitobaquito, Muddy, Burro and Williams, with smaller unnamed seeps between them. Quitobaquito has the greatest flow and the most human disturbance: its source was excavated, ditched, dammed and plowed. Williams has a cement trough but no agricultural disturbance. The other springs were mainly affected by livestock grazing.

The springs support plants and animals found nowhere else within many miles. The marsh and mesquite woodland host breeding birds such as [[Yellow-Breasted Chat]], [[Hooded Oriole]], [[Vermilion Flycatcher]] and [[Western Kingbird]]. The pond supports Desert Pupfish and Sonoran mud turtles, both with restricted local distributions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Springs in the [[Sonoran Desert]] contrast sharply with the sparse surrounding scrub. Their position within the watershed governs how they respond to disturbance. Most springs sit high in their watersheds, within a few hundred meters of the divide, so they are rarely exposed to flooding. Aguajita is different: it lies in the bottom of a major wash draining many square miles and floods almost yearly. Its source is scoured clear of dense herbaceous growth and feeds a free-flowing rivulet several meters long. Water chemistry also suggests Aguajita has a different source from the other springs (P. Bennett, pers. com.).

Drought and rainfall shaped the observed changes. Several cattle died at Williams Spring in the 1974–75 drought. High rainfall in 1982–83 probably drove the increase in [[Brittlebush]] on hillsides near Burro Spring, rather than recovery from grazing, because brittlebush is unpalatable to livestock.

Livestock congregated at springs for water and shade. This trampled the ground, hedged shrubs, and removed herbaceous cover, especially under mesquite trees.

## Key Findings

**Study design**
- One-tenth hectare permanent plots (20 m × 50 m) were established in 1975 as baselines (Steenbergh and Warren 1977). Perennial cover was measured by the [[Line Intercept Method]] along 11 parallel 20-m transects per plot.
- Density was counted in ten 20 m × 5 m subplots.
- Three plots were resampled in 1984: plot 200 (40 m north of Aguajita Spring), plot 2E1 (0.8 km east) and plot 2E2 (2.4 km east). They were set along a gradient from heavy livestock use near the spring to lighter use farther away.
- Comparisons used the [[Student T-Test]]: cover N = 11 transects, density N = 10 subplots.
- Repeat photographs were taken with a 35-mm camera and 50-mm lens. Photo points were located near springs where livestock use was heaviest, so they are not representative of the wider desertscrub.
- The authors note inconsistent timing: the study period is described as 8–9 years after the original sampling, while the Conclusions refer to recovery "in the 5 years since livestock was removed."

**Williams Spring**
- The spring source, on a terrace with [[Mesquite]] and [[Seepweed]], was severely cropped and trampled in 1974–76. Native marsh species were absent.
- By 1984, [[Bulrush]] and [[Cattail]] dominated the source. Woody species such as alkali goldenbush, seepweed and mesquite were increasing at the marsh margin. The moist swale, previously grazed [[Bermuda Grass]], was dominated by native shrubs.
- On the terrace, seepweed canopy grew and annual herbs increased. Mesquite seedlings grew to about 4 m in eight years.
- On the saline flat, [[Saltgrass]] cover rose from 20–30% to roughly 70–80% (Figure 9b), and alkali sacaton immigrated. A single yerba mansa patch appeared to be shrinking, possibly from competition with saltgrass.

**Burro Spring**
- The area influenced by the spring is about 5–10 m wide and 20–30 m long. The spring source was an open mud hole in 1976.
- By 1984 the source was choked with [[Phragmites Australis|Reed]], bulrush and arrowweed. The understory of the arrowweed/mesquite thicket was filled with marshy growth.
- Adjacent hillside desertscrub changed little, with most large perennials recognizable in paired photographs.

**Aguajita Spring**
- No pre-1976 photographs of the source exist, so the evidence is limited to the three plots.
- Total perennial cover increased in all plots. Plot 200 increased about six-fold (Table 1 totals 3.43% to 18.85%); plot 2E1 about two-fold; and plot 2E2 about 1.5 times, per the authors' text.
- Gains were concentrated in palatable species: seepweed, two saltbush species (Atriplex polycarpa and A. linearis), white ratany and bush muhly.
- Density did not rise comparably. Seepweed density fell on plot 200 because adjacent canopies merged and were counted as single individuals (Table 2).
- The authors caution that clumped plants give high variance, so statistically non-significant differences may still be biologically important. Browsing of flowers and fruit can cut saltbush reproduction even when cover changes little.

**Conclusions**
- Recovery took two forms. (1) Severely browsed plants grew larger and produced more seed. Reproductive structures are at plant tops, which livestock browse first. Seed supports desert granivores such as pocket mice, kangaroo rats, ants and birds. (2) New plants germinated and survived where grazing and trampling had previously prevented them. Most were existing species, though a few herbs and grasses were new to the area.
- Cover, especially of palatable species, recovered fastest because established plants regrew from hedged condition. Density recovered more slowly, possibly because seedling establishment takes longer and dense cover may suppress seedlings.
- Desertscrub recovered less than the mesic vegetation near springs, where cattle had congregated.
- Burro and Williams, high in the watershed, became choked with vegetation and their flow was reduced after livestock removal. Aguajita stayed open because periodic floods scour it.
- By analogy, the Quitobaquito pond, which is not flood-scoured, would probably also choke with vegetation under natural conditions. Reduced standing water could mean [[Desert Pupfish]] were absent from Quitobaquito before the man-made pond was built. The authors present this as a possibility, not a confirmed finding.
