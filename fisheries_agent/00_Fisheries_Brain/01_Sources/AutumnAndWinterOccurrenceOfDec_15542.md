# Autumn and Winter Occurrence of Decapod Crustaceans in Chesapeake Bight, U.S.A.

#decapoda #crustacea #marine-biology #benthic-ecology #zoogeography

## Executive Summary

This study by [[John A. Musick]] and [[John D. McEachran]] (Virginia Institute of Marine Science) documents the distribution, abundance, and molt condition of 21 decapod crustacean species collected by bottom trawling across the [[Chesapeake Bight]] continental shelf during autumn 1967 and winter 1968. Using a [[Demersal Trawl Survey]] design across six depth strata, the authors tested species co-occurrence with coefficients of association and interpreted the patterns in terms of [[Zoogeographic Zones]]. They conclude that the bight can be divided into three zones: a [[Gulf Stream Thermal Front]] zone with tropical and southern fauna, a stable outer shelf and upper slope fauna, and a seasonally variable inner shelf dominated by Virginian species.

## Background & Description

The [[Chesapeake Bight]] lies between [[Cape Henlopen]] and [[Cape Hatteras]]. Its strong seasonal temperature swings and the faunal barrier at Cape Hatteras make it zoogeographically significant (Parr, 1933). Earlier decapod work in the region includes Williams (1965), Williams, McCloskey & Gray (1968), and Cerame-Vivas & Gray (1966), the last of which assessed Cape Hatteras as a barrier and tested the Carolinian, Virginian, and Caribbean provinces of Johnson (1934).

The survey was part of a study of the seasonal distribution of demersal fishes, supported by the U.S. Commercial Fisheries Research and Development Act (Project No. 3-5-D), with Dr. Jackson Davis as principal investigator. Taxonomic identifications were provided by Dr. Raymond B. Manning (*Nephropsis aculeata*) and Dr. Marvin Wass (pagurids).

Twenty-one species were recorded, in ten families:
- Crangonidae: *Crangon septemspinosa*
- Nephropidae: *Homarus americanus*, *Nephropsis aculeata*
- Galatheidae: *Munida iris*
- Porcellanidae: *Porcellana sigsbeiana*
- Paguridae: *Dardanus insignis*, *Pagurus acadianus*, *Pagurus annulipes*
- Calappidae: *Calappa sulcata*
- Portunidae: *Ovalipes ocellatus*, *O. guadulpensis*, *Portunus gibbesii*, *P. spinimanus*, *P. spinicarpus*, *Callinectes sapidus*, *Bathynectes superba*
- Cancridae: *Cancer irroratus*, *C. borealis*
- Majidae: *Libinia emarginata*, *Rochinia crassa*
- Goneplacidae: *Geryon quinquedens*

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Two cruises aboard the trawler *Sea Breeze* were timed to seasonal hydrography. The autumn survey occupied 64 stations (14 November–18 December 1967), and the winter survey occupied 76 stations (18 January–28 February 1968). The area was gridded (1° latitude × 12.5' longitude) and sampled across six depth intervals: 0–18, 19–37, 38–73, 74–110, 111–165, and 166–274 m. Each station used a bathythermograph cast and a one-hour tow with a [[Western Otter Trawl]] (50-mm stretched mesh in the cod end). The net mainly retained animals larger than 50 mm carapace diameter, so smaller individuals were under-represented.

**Distribution and statistics.** [[Cancer Borealis]], [[Cancer Irroratus]], and [[Homarus Americanus]] were abundant enough for statistical analysis, and all three were contagiously distributed. Data were natural-log transformed as y = log(x + 1) to normalize the counts. Abundance indices were calculated by depth stratum, separately north and south of 37°N, and by one-degree temperature intervals.

**Species association.** Cole's coefficient of association (C_J), with a second coefficient (C_S) that gave very similar results, tested whether species pairs co-occurred more or less often than expected by chance.

**Bottom substrate and temperature.** The shelf is mostly sand with patches of gravel. The slope is mostly silt and clay. Rocky bottoms are few and occur only near submarine canyons. Bottom temperature patterns follow the [[Gulf Stream]] front at the shelf edge between Cape Hatteras and Oregon Inlet. The authors note that the [[Gulf Stream]] thermal front was clearly visible in both seasons.

## Key Findings

**Species-level results**

- *Cancer irroratus*: 12 autumn stations (241 individuals, 8–13 °C) and 48 winter stations (472 individuals, 2–11 °C; 43 ovigerous and 35 showing ecdysis). Ovigerous animals were taken only at depths under 30 m. Recent ecdysis was recorded only under 17 m. It is widespread across the shelf and most abundant inshore.
- *Cancer borealis*: 6 autumn stations (56 individuals) and 20 winter stations (227 individuals, 3–11 °C; no ovigerous or molting specimens recorded). It is concentrated along the shelf edge.
- *Homarus americanus*: 18 autumn stations (187 individuals) and 20 winter stations (196 individuals), with 74, 44, and 83 individuals in additional records from March–April, May–June, and August–September 1967. Its depth distribution is similar to *C. borealis*.
- *Munida iris*: 1 autumn station and 6 winter stations (64 individuals, 121–274 m). It was never caught shallower than 121 m.
- *Ovalipes ocellatus* was taken at 8–12 °C and 11–22 m. *O. guadulpensis* was taken at 11–19 °C and 18–49 m. Both were caught only in autumn. The authors suggest they may burrow in winter and become unavailable to trawls.
- *Portunus gibbesii* was caught both in the Gulf Stream and inshore. Its lowest capture temperature was 6 °C, near the northwest front of the Gulf Stream.
- *Geryon quinquedens* was caught once but was later found to be abundant at 400–1000 m (Musick & McEachran, unpublished data).

**Association results**

- *C. irroratus* and *C. borealis*: C_J = −0.064, not significant. The two species are distributed randomly relative to each other in the bight. This contrasts with Narragansett Bay, where they rarely co-occur (Jeffries, 1966).
- *H. americanus* and *C. borealis*: C_J = 0.571, significant at P < 0.001 (t = 4.830). The two species are closely associated.
- *M. iris* and *H. americanus*: C_J = 0.771, significant at P < 0.005 (t = 3.29).
- *M. iris* and *C. borealis*: C_J = 0.778, significant at P < 0.001 (t = 3.44).

**Zoogeographic conclusions**

- The authors propose three zones: (1) the Gulf Stream, with tropical and southern species (*Porcellana sigsbeiana*, *Dardanus insignis*, *Calappa sulcata*, *Portunus spinicarpus*, and *P. spinimanus*, all captured only at the single Gulf Stream station each season); (2) the stable outer shelf and upper slope, where *C. irroratus*, *H. americanus*, and *M. iris* are abundant; and (3) the seasonally variable inner shelf, dominated by Virginian species such as *C. irroratus* and *O. ocellatus*.
- *Ovalipes guadulpensis* had previously been recorded from North Carolina to Brazil. This study extends its known range north to the Virginia coast within the bight.
- In winter, the inner shelf held boreal species (*Pagurus acadianus*) and ubiquitous species (*P. annulipes* and *Libinia emarginata*), all taken at temperatures of 5 °C or lower.
- *C. borealis* may be more stenothermal than *C. irroratus*, but the authors note this could be an artifact of sampling. Its abundance is highest along the shelf edge, where slope water is more thermally stable.
- The similar depth distributions of *H. americanus* and *C. borealis* may indicate separate inshore and offshore populations, as Saila & Flowers (1968, 1969) found for lobsters. The wide distribution of *C. irroratus* suggests a continuous population from southern New England to the bight.
- Bottom type seems less important here than Jeffries (1966) suggested. *C. irroratus* is more likely to be caught over sand than over silt-clay or coarse canyon sediments, while *C. borealis* and *H. americanus* show the reverse pattern.

**Summary:** The bight can be divided into three zoogeographic zones. Each is characterized by a distinct decapod assemblage and by different temperature regimes.
