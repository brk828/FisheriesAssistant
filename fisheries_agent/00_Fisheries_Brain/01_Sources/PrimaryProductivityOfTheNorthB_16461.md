# Primary Productivity of the North Branch of the Raritan River, New Jersey

#freshwater-biology #phycology #limnology #diatoms

## Executive Summary

This study by David A. Flemer (University of Maryland, Chesapeake Biological Laboratories) measured [[Primary Productivity]] in a shallow, weakly buffered stream, the [[Raritan River]] (North Branch), New Jersey, from October 1961 to November 1962. Gross photosynthesis was estimated from diurnal dissolved-oxygen curves at three riffle stations, and the standing crop of algae was assessed through [[Periphyton]] and [[Phytoplankton]] counts and [[Chlorophyll A]] measurements. The study aimed to (1) assess the influence of nutrients on production and producer abundance, and (2) document the seasonal pattern of production and standing crop.

Production was highest downstream of a sewage input. Mean gross production was 4.7 g O₂ m⁻² day⁻¹ at Station I (upstream, May–September 1962), 9.9 g O₂ m⁻² day⁻¹ at Station II, and 11.6 g O₂ m⁻² day⁻¹ at Station III. Daily maximum pH correlated strongly with production, and the author suggests pH may serve as a simple productivity estimate.

## Background & Description

The paper's introduction notes that stream primary productivity is less well studied than lacustrine and marine productivity. Earlier work relied on oxygen changes, chlorophyll, and periphyton biomass, and the methodological difficulties were reviewed by [[Wetzel]] (1965).

**Study setting.** The North Branch of the Raritan lies in north central New Jersey, on Precambrian Highlands and Triassic Piedmont substrata. Three stations were sampled:

| Station | Length (m) | Width (m) | Depth (m) | Velocity (m s⁻¹) | Discharge (m³ s⁻¹) | Elevation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | 172 | 15 | 0.11 | 0.34 | 0.4 | 42 m (highest) |
| II | 152 | 19 | 0.22 | 0.27 | 1.0 | N/A |
| III | 104 | 14 | 0.19 | 0.31 | 0.8 | 30 m (lowest) |

All stations were shallow riffles dominated by benthic algae. Station I was shaded by hardwood trees and had mainly large rubble. Stations II and III were less shaded, and Station II had more shale and fine sand. Domestic sewage (from an [[Activated Sludge]] plant) entered above Stations II and III, but not above Station I. Water temperature averaged about 15 °C (range 0.0–28 °C), and the river was ice-covered in winter.

**Methods overview.**
- *Physical:* depth by transect, velocity by wooden floats, discharge by Embody's formula, solar radiation from a pyrheliometer about 18 air miles away.
- *Chemical:* nitrate, nitrite, alkalinity, inorganic and total phosphorus, pH in situ, and dissolved oxygen by the Winkler method.
- *Biological:* chlorophyll a by spectrophotometry and Millipore filters; plankton by filtering 25–300 L through a No. 25 net; periphyton on glass slides and cement blocks exposed about two weeks; diatoms identified from slides.
- *Production:* the [[Upstream-Downstream Method]] and the [[Single Curve Method]] (Odum 1956). Reaeration coefficients were 1.3 g O₂ m⁻² hr⁻¹ (Stations II and III, from Elk Creek data) and 1.5 at Station I. Light available for photosynthesis was assumed to be 50% of incident radiation, and 1 g O₂ was taken as 3.4 kcal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physicochemical conditions.**
- *Nitrate-N* averaged 0.66 mg L⁻¹ at Station I and 0.96 mg L⁻¹ at Station II. Highest values occurred in winter, with a marked decrease in early spring. Nitrite-N was usually below 5% of nitrate-N and seldom exceeded 0.03 mg L⁻¹.
- *Phosphate:* inorganic PO₄-P was usually below 0.01 mg L⁻¹ at Station I, but ranged from trace to 0.21 mg L⁻¹ at Station II. Lowest values occurred in spring.
- *Alkalinity* was low, averaging 30, 46 and 48 mg L⁻¹ at Stations I, II and III.
- *pH* differed little among stations, but diurnal ranges at Stations II and III sometimes approached 2 units (greatest: 7.30 to 9.75 at Station II, 23 April 1962).

**Biota.**
- Diatoms were the most common algae, with 18 genera. The most abundant were *Achnanthes*, *Cymbella*, *Diatoma*, *Fragilaria*, *Gomphonema*, [[Navicula]], [[Nitzschia]] and *Synedra*.
- Filamentous greens ([[Ulothrix]], *Cladophora*) and blue-greens (*Oscillatoria*, *Phormidium*) were also common. *Ulothrix* bloomed in spring.
- The planktonic blue-greens *Anabaena* and *Gomphosphaeria* made up about 80% of stream phytoplankton in late summer and fall. The author attributes them to [[Ravine Lake]], an impoundment upstream of Station I.

**Seasonal and spatial patterns.** Production and standing crop were greater at the downstream stations. The author notes that the downstream stations had higher nutrient concentrations, and that unmeasured effluent factors may also have contributed. Station I's periphyton showed no clear seasonal trend.

## Key Findings

**1. Production rates**
- Mean gross production (Upstream-Downstream Method):
  - Station I: 4.7 g O₂ m⁻² day⁻¹ (May–September 1962, n = 8)
  - Station II: 9.9 g O₂ m⁻² day⁻¹ (n = 15), range 2.5–16.7
  - Station III: 11.6 g O₂ m⁻² day⁻¹ (n = 11), range 2.5–25.1; peak on 20 May
- Single curve estimates averaged about half of the upstream-downstream values at Stations II and III, and about two-thirds at Station I.
- Production efficiency was 1.0–7.2% at Stations II and III and 1.0–4.2% at Station I. Efficiency was highest on overcast days with little direct sun (4.6% on 16 July and 6.5% on 21 July at Station II).

**2. Nutrients and productivity**
- Stations II and III had higher nitrate and phosphate than Station I, and higher production and standing crop. The author attributes this to nutrient enrichment, which is consistent with eutrophication.

**3. Standing crop**
- Periphyton chlorophyll a (mean, mg m⁻²): glass slides 6.7 (I), 32.7 (II), 21.6 (III); cement blocks 12.6 (I), 41.5 (II), 34.3 (III).
- Net plankton chlorophyll a (mg L⁻¹ ×10⁻³): 99 (I), 121 (II), 78 (III).
- Chlorophyll a from glass slides and cement blocks were statistically equivalent, so both methods are considered valid for tracking seasonal change.

**4. Correlations**
- Plankton chlorophyll a vs. counts: r = 0.90 (Station II, n = 21); r = 0.62 (Station III, n = 21).
- Periphyton counts vs. chlorophyll a (glass slides): r = 0.67 (I, n = 13), 0.76 (II, n = 13), 0.85 (III, n = 12).
- Cement block vs. glass slide chlorophyll a: r = 0.84 (II, n = 11); r = 0.87 (III, n = 11).
- Daily pH maximum vs. gross production: r = 0.88 (II, df 10); r = 0.84 (III, df 8). Correlations with 24-hour respiration and net daytime production were also high, though weaker at Station III for respiration (r = 0.55, not significant).
- Standing crop estimates were not significantly correlated with production from oxygen curves.

**5. Limiting factors**
- Light showed little evidence of limiting production; the relationship with gross production was weak.
- The author proposes that CO₂ limitation may explain the pH–production correlation, though this is inferred from comparisons with the [[Madison River]] (Wright & Mills 1967) rather than measured directly.

**6. Methodological notes**
- Single curve and upstream-downstream results diverged, as expected in a heterogeneous reach. Station III (104 m) was long enough to yield measurable differences.
- Artificial substrates serve best as relative measures of periphyton growth, since their exposure durations and positions differ among studies.

**7. Conclusion**
- The river is productive, though below the maxima reported for other freshwater streams. The author describes it as a *Nitzschia–Navicula–Diatoma–Ulothrix* community, and considers daily pH maximum a potentially useful, simple productivity predictor pending further testing.
