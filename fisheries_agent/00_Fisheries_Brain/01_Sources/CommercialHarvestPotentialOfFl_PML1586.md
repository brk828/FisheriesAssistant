# Flathead Catfish Population Dynamics, Commercial Harvest Potential and Contaminant Survey in Arizona Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report, prepared by Paul C. Marsh, Mark L. Young and Lewis J. Hinckley for the Arizona State University Center for Environmental Studies and the Arizona Game and Fish Department, assesses the [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) in Arizona. It combines a survey of commercial harvest across the United States, pesticide residue sampling of fish tissue, age and growth analysis in four river systems, a population estimate for a 35.5 km reach of the [[Lower Colorado River]], and a yield-per-recruit assessment of potential harvest.

The authors conclude that a commercial fishery for flathead catfish in the Lower Colorado River could yield about 5,276 kg annually. Angler harvest is estimated at roughly 3,077 kg, leaving an unexploited surplus of about 2,200 kg available for commercial harvest. They caution that harvest at this level would reduce the population of larger fish and lower average weight, and that sustained harvest is not recommended under present conditions. Estimated value to commercial fishermen is about $3,700 to $11,000 annually, with gross values of $3,700 to $13,000 possible.

## Background & Description

The flathead catfish is a large, piscivorous ictalurid native to the Mississippi, Missouri and Ohio basins, Gulf Coastal plain and Rio Grande drainages. It is a prized sport and commercial species across much of its range and has been introduced to several western states, including Arizona, where it was stocked in the [[Salt River]] system and later in the [[Verde River]] and Lower Colorado River.

Morphologically, the species has a large, flattened head, a projecting lower jaw, a rounded, truncate or slightly emarginate caudal fin, and a fleshy adipose fin. Coloration is highly variable, typically yellow-brown above and whitish to yellow below, with dark mottling. The authors note that the species is poorly studied in the southwestern United States, which motivated the project.

Life history includes spawning in cavities such as hollow logs, undercut banks and rock crevices, with males guarding the nest. Age at maturity is about 4 to 5 years for males and 5 to 7 years for females in reservoirs and rivers, with reported ages of up to 15 years. Juveniles feed mainly on insect larvae, crayfish and fish, while adults become almost exclusively piscivorous at lengths above about 150 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Flathead catfish are established in several Arizona systems, with the largest populations in the Lower Colorado River below Parker Dam, and smaller populations in the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]]. Study sites were chosen for accessibility to both recreational and commercial anglers and for the presence of target populations.

Sampling took place in the Lower Colorado River (Yuma Division), Gila River, Verde River, and Salt River. Fish were collected by electrofishing, trammel and gill nets, hoop nets and setlines. Non-target species captured included channel catfish, common carp, largemouth bass, bluegill and green sunfish, indicating a mixed assemblage in which native fishes were generally scarce.

Contaminant testing of flathead catfish tissue for organochlorine pesticides (aldrin, dieldrin, DDD, DDE, DDT, endrin, heptachlor, heptachlor epoxide, toxaphene, lindane and delta-BHC) was performed by [[Western Technologies Inc.]] under contract to the project. Residues were compared against FDA tolerance levels.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution:** Flathead catfish occur in the Lower Colorado River from Parker Dam to Imperial Dam, the Gila River, the Verde River, and the Salt River below Roosevelt Lake; they have been reported from the San Francisco River and the Ghost Ranch area.
- **Commercial harvest in the US:** Harvest was reported from 21 states. The largest annual total was in Arkansas, with about 13,500 kg. Harvest was historically high in the lower Mississippi River but has declined since the early part of the century.
- **Age and growth:** In the Lower Colorado River, fish reached about 1.5 years to 450 mm total length. Growth was rapid in reservoirs compared with rivers, and fish in the Gila River grew faster than in the Salt and Verde rivers.
- **Population estimate:** The Lower Colorado River population within the 35.5 km Yuma reach was estimated at 1987 by mark-recapture; annual maximum potential yield was estimated at 5,276 kg, with 3,077 kg harvested by anglers and about 2,200 kg potentially available for commercial harvest.
- **Yield-per-recruit:** Potential yield was maximized at a fishing mortality (F) of about 0.5 to 1.0, with the estimated instantaneous rate of total mortality (Z) about 1.0 and natural mortality (M) about 0.25 to 0.50.
- **Economic value:** Potential commercial value was estimated at $3,700 to $13,000 per year, and recreational angler value at $5,000 to $30,000 or more, depending on assumptions.
- **Contaminants:** Most flathead catfish samples were below FDA tolerance levels. Two Colorado River samples exceeded the FDA heptachlor tolerance (0.32 ppm and 0.49 ppm). Fish from the Lower Colorado River had higher DDT-group and heptachlor residues than fish from other rivers.
- **Management concern:** Population decline in the Lower Colorado River after 1987 was associated with reduced river flow, habitat loss and intense angler harvest. The authors recommend a comprehensive survey and monitoring program before any commercial fishery is opened, and suggest restricting commercial harvest to the Lower Colorado River reach.
