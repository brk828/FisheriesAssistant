# Odour Perception and Orientation in Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #olfaction #chemoreception #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 review by [[Arthur D. Hasler]] (Department of Zoology, University of Wisconsin), published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, synthesises research on how fishes perceive odours and use them in food-finding, social behaviour, predator avoidance and homing. It covers olfactory anatomy across fish groups, the nerve pathways from olfactory tissue to brain, the boundary between taste and the [[Common Chemical Sense]], and the experimental methods used to train fish to odours. Its central applied thesis is that salmon return to their natal streams by recognising a stream-specific odour acquired as young fish, and that this could be exploited for decoying salmon into rehabilitated streams.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Odour perception is defined as chemically induced sensations relayed from the olfactory sac to the central nervous system. Taste and the common chemical sense are treated separately, as sensations perceived by gustatory nerve endings in the mouth and over the body.
- **Human and general chemistry:** Odorous substances must be volatile at ordinary temperature and soluble in fat solvents. Man detects methyl mercaptan at roughly 9 × 10⁻¹⁸ M, about 10,000 times more dilute than the minimum concentration of the taste stimulant strychnine hydrochloride.
- **Theory:** [[Kistiakowsky Enzyme Theory of Odour]] (1950) proposes that odorants act by interfering with enzyme-catalysed reactions in receptors, which explains high sensitivity and rapid adaptation.
- **Anatomy:** Olfactory organs vary widely. In sharks and rays, paired pits lie on the ventral snout. In lungfishes, posterior openings correspond to the choanae of higher vertebrates. In bony fishes, pits lie dorso-anteriorly. [[Burne 1909]] described olfactory organs in 51 genera from 32 families.
- **Classification of water-flow mechanisms:** Burne grouped fish by habitat (bottom fishes, sluggish shallow-water fishes, free-swimming open-water fishes). [[Pipping 1927]] proposed four general types of olfactory mechanism, based on how water is deflected, drawn or circulated through the nasal capsule.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Odour as a distance cue:** Odour acts as a distance receptor along a concentration gradient, unlike taste, and responds to many mainly organic substances. Most of nearly half a million listed synthetic organic compounds have sufficient vapour pressure to be smelled.
- **Schooling and aggregation:** [[Wrede 1932]] showed that Elritze (*Phoxinus*) are attracted to conspecific mucus, which aggregates them and reduces restlessness.
- **Alarm substance (Schreckstoff):** [[Von Frisch 1941]] identified an alarm substance in the skin of injured minnows, which is odoriferous, non-detectable in intestine or liver, and possibly purine- or pterin-like (Hüttel 1941). Skin extracts from 41 freshwater species were compared. Eighteen were non-cyprinids and contained no appreciable alarm substance. Only two of 28 cyprinid species approached Elritze skin activity.
- **Aquatic plant odours:** [[Walker and Hasler 1949]] showed that blunt-nose minnows discriminated between odours of several aquatic plant pairs, including *Myriophyllum exalbescens* and *Ceratophyllum demersum*. They proposed that plant odours guide fish to feeding grounds in turbid water and keep juveniles in littoral cover.
- **Pollutant detection:** Phenol, which forms odorous chlorophenols after chlorination, can be detected by trained minnows at concentrations below the human threshold of 0.01 p.p.m. ([[Hasler and Wisby 1950]]).
- **Homing and imprinting:** Salmon return to natal streams, and this review argues the mechanism is odour-based imprinting.

## Key Findings

1. **Olfactory mechanisms differ widely.** Pipping's four types are: (1) water deflected through a capsule during forward motion (e.g. *Esox lucius*); (2) breathing compresses a pouch to draw water in and out of one opening (e.g. *Zoarces viviparus*); (3) water enters one opening and leaves another via breathing or cilia (e.g. *Pleuronectes flesus*); (4) water circulates from anterior to posterior nostril (e.g. *Perca fluviatilis*, *Gobius niger*).
2. **Eel nasal structure is highly elaborate.** The olfactory rosette of *Anguilla* has many lateral folds, increasing the number of sensory cells activated.
3. **Stickleback olfaction is poor.** *Gasterosteus aculeatus* has a simple nasal sac with only the small upper two folds bearing receptors, and it does not use smell in food-getting.
4. **Bullhead olfactory bulb responses.** In the bullhead, [[Adrian and Ludwig 1938]] recorded burst discharges after a latency of 0.5 to 5 seconds, with stronger responses to particulate material than to filtered fluid.
5. **Olfaction versus taste in food detection.** [[Parker 1910]] found that five bullheads detected worm-baited wads 11 times in an hour, while sight and taste ruled out the controls. Bullheads with severed olfactory tracts never seized worm wads, while fish with removed barbels (taste) still seized them 34 times, showing that olfaction works at a distance beyond taste.
6. **Taste thresholds.** [[Strieck 1924]] measured thresholds in the minnow: 10–11% for grape sugar, 6% for NaCl, 0.15% for acetic acid and 0.02% for quinine. [[Krinner 1935]] reported minnow sugar thresholds 512 times and salt thresholds 184 times lower than human thresholds, with olfactory removal not altering them.
7. **Discrimination learning.** [[Géz 1941]] showed Elritze could discriminate 15 fish species from eight families by odour, and that individuals in a school have recognisable individual body odours.
8. **Alarm response specificity.** Alarm reactions in *Squalius cephalus*, *Rodeus amarus* and *Scardinius erythrophthalmus* were strongest for their own skin extracts, indicating species specificity. Perch did not react to their own skin extracts.
9. **Repellents.** [[Brett and MacKinnon 1952]] found human hand rinses repellent to ascending coho salmon, while water, urine and tomato juice were not. [[Hiatt et al. 1953]] reported that some halogenated compounds dispersed schooling fish at concentrations as low as 0.05 p.p.m.
10. **Homing odour hypothesis.** [[Hasler and Wisby 1951]] proposed that young salmon are conditioned to a stream-specific odour. Trained minnows discriminated two Wisconsin creeks, and the difference lay in the total organic nitrogen fraction, specifically a volatile aromatic substance. Trained salmon fry also discriminated these creeks.
11. **Candidate decoy odour.** Of many organic odours tested on unconditioned salmon, none attracted them. Two were suitable for further testing. [[Morpholine]] was soluble, stable under stream conditions, and detectable at low concentrations, with neither attractant nor repellent effects on unconditioned salmon. Field tests were planned to decoy conditioned fish to other streams.
12. **Nasal plugging and homing.** [[Wisby and Hasler 1953]] found that most normal coho salmon returned to their stream of first choice, while salmon with plugged nasal sacs returned nearly at random. This is evidence that a functional olfactory system is important for orientation.
13. **Open questions.** The review identifies unresolved issues, including whether fish can discriminate water masses, learn multiple stream odours, and combine odour cues with sight and hearing.
