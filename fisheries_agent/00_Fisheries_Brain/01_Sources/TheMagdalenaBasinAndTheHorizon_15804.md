# The Magdalena Basin and the Horizontal and Vertical Distribution of Its Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #characidae

## Executive Summary

This classic zoogeographic study, a contribution from the Zoological Laboratory of Indiana University (No. 177) by Carl H. Eigenmann, analyses the origin, composition and distribution of the fishes of the [[Magdalena Basin]] in western Colombia. The author reports roughly 150 species in about 70 genera of fresh- and brackish-water fishes. He argues that the basin's fauna derives in small part from the ocean and in larger part from Central America, and that it is most closely related to the [[Orinoco Basin]] to the east. He proposes that the [[Cordillera De Bogota]] was an effective barrier that segregated the Magdalena fauna before most present species evolved. The analysis is based on collections of uneven quality, and the author stresses that large parts of the basin remain poorly known.

## Background & Description

The Magdalena basin lies between the [[Cordillera Central]] and the Cordillera Occidental on the west and the Cordillera de Bogota (Oriental) on the east, draining almost all of Colombia into the Caribbean Sea. The river system is divided into five sections:

1. Andean torrents flowing from the heights to the valleys
2. The Upper [[Cauca River]]
3. The Upper Magdalena
4. The Lower Magdalena
5. The Cesar (a southward-flowing tributary that enters the Magdalena at El Banco)

The Cauca and Magdalena rise near 2° N and join near 9° 30′ N before entering the Caribbean near 11° N. The author describes the [[Plain of Popayan]] as the divide between Atlantic and Pacific drainages, where no marked mountain crest exists, and notes the Cali plain (about 3,000–3,500 ft) as a relatively flat, heavily settled area.

Historical collecting was patchy. Collections came from the Cienega near the Magdalena mouth, Girardot, Cali and its tributaries, Honda to Bogota, Ibague, Popayan and Antioquia, and from parties including the British Museum, the University of Michigan and the American Museum of Natural History. The Cesar had never been sampled, and the upper Magdalena and Cauca rapids were poorly known.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Origin of the fauna.** The author assigns the basin's fishes to several sources:

- **Marine:** The [[Common Eel]] (*Anguilla chrysypa*) and the [[Tarpon]] (*Tarpon atlanticus*) reached the system via the Caribbean. A single small eel specimen, collected by the University of Michigan expedition, is treated as a stray.
- **Central American:** Genera including *Gambusia*, *Mollienisia*, *Agonostomus* and four Eleotridine genera (possibly also *Rivulus*) are attributed to Central America.
- **Andean:** Genera such as [[Astroblepus]] (family Astroblepidae, reaching the highest altitudes, up to 10,700 ft for *A. grixalvii*), *Lasiancistrus*, *Pseudancistrus* and *Chetostomus* (Loricariidae), and [[Pygidium]] (a swift-water genus ranging from southern Panama to Patagonia) may be autochthonous or may have come from the south.

**Endemic and widespread genera.** About 76 per cent of the genera in the basin also occur east of the Andes. Endemic or near-endemic genera (e.g., *Xyliphius*, *Eremophilus*, *Grundulus*, *Genycharax*, *Gilbertolus*) are regarded as ordinary tropical South American lineages that could have evolved anywhere east of the Andes.

**Relation to the Orinoco.** The Magdalena fauna resembles that of the [[Orinoco Basin]] far more than that of the Guayas (Ecuador) does. The author reports a resemblance five times greater, measured by identical species. About 20 per cent of Magdalena species also occur east of the Andes. Examples include *Pseudoplatystoma fasciatum*, *Sorubim lima*, *Hoplias malabaricus*, *Plagioscion surinamensis* and *Synbranchus marmoratus*. Most of these show wide distributions, and the author notes that parallel forms have evolved in many genera on the two sides.

**Absent taxa.** Many widespread eastern genera have not been found in the Magdalena, including *Electrophorus* (electric eel), *Arapaima*, *Osteoglossum*, *Cichla* and *Pachyurus*. Several eastern genera (e.g., *Chasmocranes*, *Erythrinus*, *Crenicichla*) occur at the base of the Andes east of Bogota but not in the Magdalena itself.

**Cauca compared with Potaro.** The upper Cauca and the Potaro river of Guiana share only three genera (*Pygidium*, *Astyanax*, *Rivulus*), all of very wide distribution. Their species differ, and the two faunas are otherwise very dissimilar.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** About 150 species in roughly 70 genera of fresh- and brackish-water fishes occur in the Magdalena basin.
- **Regional tallies:** The author's distribution table totals 78, 54, 29 and 35 species for its four regional columns, about 50, 35, 18.7 and 22.6 per cent of the total. The 54 Upper Magdalena species include 32 also found in the Lower Magdalena. Of the upper Cauca species, 11 occur in the Upper Magdalena and 5 in the Lower Magdalena.
- **Highest altitudes:** The table records maximum altitudes for many Andean species, such as *Astroblepus grixalvii* (10,700 ft), *Astroblepus chote* (10,000 ft), *Pygidium venulosum* (10,000 ft) and *Pygidium bogotense* (9,000 ft).
- **Shared species across the Cordillera de Bogota:** The author lists about 30 species found on both sides of the barrier. He notes that these are generally distinct between sides, indicating segregation before most species arose.
- **Primary conclusion:** The fauna was derived in small part from the ocean and in larger part from Central America. It shares most of its origin with the Orinoco basin, and segregation resulted from the uplift of the Cordillera de Bogota, which predates most extant species.
- **Persistence of barbel stripes:** The author argues that the striped pattern of the large catfish *Pseudoplatystoma fasciatum* (Bagre tigre) has persisted since the Cordillera de Bogota became an effective barrier.
- **Future work:** The author recommends collections in the [[Maracaibo Basin]] and eastward through the Pass of Hato (about 800 ft) into the Orinoco basin to test the origin of the Magdalena fauna. Collections in the Cesar, the upper Magdalena, the Cauca rapids and the torrential streams of the Western and Central Cordilleras are also needed.
- **Barrier alternatives:** The author considers two possibilities for the connection: a route through [[Lake Maracaibo]], or a lowland link that was cut off when the Cordillera de Bogota rose. He judges transport over the present barrier to be out of the question.
