## Executive Summary

This document is a 1993 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 2) memorandum recommending three southwestern sucker species — the [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Little Colorado Sucker]] — for addition to the federal Animal Candidate Review List under 50 CFR Part 17, each assigned to **Category 2** (candidates with insufficient data to support an immediate proposed rule, but with credible evidence of vulnerability). The request was compiled by Sally Stefferud based on the collective expertise of the **Desert Fishes Recovery Team**, led by Dr. W.O. Minckley of Arizona State University. All three species are members of the family **Catostomidae** (suckers) and are native to arid-land river systems of Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, Utah, and Sonora, Mexico. The threats identified are broadly consistent across all three taxa: habitat destruction from water development, watershed degradation, and displacement by nonnative fish species.

## Background & Description

The source document is a regulatory/administrative listing form and does not provide morphological descriptions (body form, meristics, coloration, or size data) for any of the three species. It classifies them taxonomically as follows:

Note: The Little Colorado sucker is listed only at the genus level (*Catostomus sp.*), suggesting that at the time of writing it was not yet formally described as a distinct species — likely reflecting taxonomic uncertainty or a then-undescribed form within the [[Catostomus]] genus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** All three species inhabit desert and montane river/stream systems in the arid Southwest, environments characterized by naturally variable flow regimes, high erosion potential, and vulnerability to water withdrawal.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]:** Historic and current range spans Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico, within the [[Gila River]] and [[Rio Yaqui]] drainages.
- **[[Desert Sucker]]:** Broader range including Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, Utah, and Sonora, Mexico. Two subspecies occurring in portions of the Nevada range were already listed under Category 2 prior to this submission, indicating a taxonomically fragmented conservation status.
- **[[Little Colorado Sucker]]:** A narrow endemic restricted to the upper reaches of the [[Little Colorado River]] basin in Arizona — the most geographically restricted of the three, and thus the most vulnerable to localized habitat disturbance.
- **Community interactions:** All three species face competitive exclusion and predation pressure from introduced nonnative fishes, as well as exposure to exotic diseases and parasites co-introduced with those nonnative species — a recurring theme in [[Desert Fish Conservation]] literature.

## Key Findings

- **Uniform threat profile:** All three sucker species face the same primary drivers of decline — dewatering, water diversion, groundwater pumping, dam construction, and channel/watershed erosion — reflecting systemic hydrological alteration across southwestern watersheds rather than species-specific pressures.
- **Nonnative species as a major threat:** Historic and ongoing introduction of nonnative fish is identified as a serious threat for all three species through both direct predation/competition and introduction of exotic diseases/parasites.
- **Regulatory inadequacy:** The memorandum explicitly states that existing regulatory mechanisms were insufficient to halt continuing declines for all three species, supporting elevation to candidate status.
- **No known overutilization:** None of the three species were reported to be significantly impacted by commercial, recreational, scientific, or educational harvest.
- **Endemism as a compounding risk factor:** The [[Little Colorado Sucker]] was flagged as particularly vulnerable due to its narrow endemic range, limited to the upper Little Colorado River basin, in contrast to the more widely distributed [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]].
- **Category assignment rationale:** All three species were placed in Category 2 based on the professional judgment and unpublished field data of the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]], rather than on a single published status report—highlighting reliance on expert consensus in candidate species review processes.
- **Institutional coordination:** The listing process involved coordination across multiple field stations, including the Arizona and New Mexico Ecological Services Offices, Dexter National Fish Hatchery, Arizona Fisheries Resources Office, and (for the Desert Sucker) regional offices in Salt Lake City (R6) and Reno (R1), reflecting the multi-state distribution of these taxa.
