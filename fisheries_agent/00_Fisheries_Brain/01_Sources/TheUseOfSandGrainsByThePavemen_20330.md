# The Use of Sand Grains by the Pavement Ant Tetramorium caespitum While Attacking Halictine Bees

#entomology #behavioral-ecology #formicidae #halictidae #tool-use

## Executive Summary

This observational study documents an unusual predatory behavior in [[Tetramorium caespitum]] (pavement ant) attacking nests of the primitively social bee [[Lasioglossum zephyrum]]. Ants employed sand grains as both weapons and construction materials, dropping small grains onto defending bees while using larger grains to plug nest entrances. A specialized "guard ant" maintained position at the burrow entrance, preventing the bee from escaping or clearing debris. Observations were conducted at the [[Parade Grounds]] in Brooklyn, New York during summer 1957 and spring 1958. The coordinated attack strategy involved multiple behavioral components including grain bombardment, entrance plugging, and guard duty, though the observed attempts were ultimately unsuccessful.

## Background & Description

[[Tetramorium caespitum]] is an omnivorous ant species measuring approximately 4 mm in length that lives mainly on a diet of insects (Morley, 1953). [[Lasioglossum zephyrum]] is a primitively social halictine bee measuring about 7.5 mm in length. The bee nests were located on a sandy footpath in a sandlot ballfield. Nest burrows opened via neat circular holes approximately 2 millimeters in diameter at ground surface. Nest entrances were frequently guarded by a bee within the burrow that plugged the entrance with its head; when disturbed, the bee would turn around and plug the hole tightly with its abdomen.

The study is based primarily on detailed observations from two occasions: July 21, 1957 (7:10-10:45 A.M.) and June 2, 1958 (9:30 A.M. onward, approximately 90 minutes of continuous observation). On the following day after the 1958 observation, the nest was partially excavated and two adult bees were recovered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The observations occurred in the [[Parade Grounds]], a sandlot ballfield environment in Brooklyn, New York, providing sandy substrate suitable for both bee nesting and ant foraging activities. The sandy footpath habitat facilitated the ants' use of readily available sand grains as tools. According to Wheeler (1926), nearly all ants throw pellets of earth or debris on substances they cannot remove, such as strong-smelling liquids, sometimes burying them completely. However, the present situation appeared distinctly aggressive rather than a simple covering response.

The pavement ant's omnivorous diet and reliance on insects as primary food sources (Morley, 1953) provides ecological context for the predatory behavior. Supporting evidence for bees as prey items includes an observation on July 10, 1957 (7:00-7:50 A.M.) when an ant was holding a dead [[Lasioglossum zephyrum]] on top of an almost completely plugged nest entrance that had been clear with a guarding bee the previous day.

## Key Findings

**Attack Strategy Components:**

1. **Grain Bombardment**: Ants walked to the burrow rim carrying sand grains in their mandibles, extended their heads over the rim, and dropped grains onto the bee's head or abdomen. Grains were sourced generally 25 millimeters or more from the nest. Grain size varied considerably, from considerably larger to considerably smaller than an ant's head. Dropping rate during active phases was approximately one grain every few seconds.

2. **Entrance Plugging**: Larger grains, smaller grains, and debris were used to block the nest entrance. Large grains tended to be deposited around the burrow rim and placed in the entrance after barrages of small grains. Ants demonstrated precise placement behavior, positioning grains in specific openings between the burrow rim and plugging materials. Groups of 2-3 ants jointly carried larger grains and debris, leaving them next to the nest entrance.

3. **Guard Ant Behavior**: One specialized ant maintained a stationary position with its head protruding slightly over the burrow rim throughout most of the observation period. This ant was initially coated with fine light sand and remained motionless, appearing dead until disturbed. The guard ant moved "aggressively" toward the bee whenever it attempted to emerge or remove grains, causing the bee to withdraw. When the guard ant left its post (moving about 10 millimeters from the entrance), ant activity dramatically decreased and the bee began successfully removing sand grains.

**Behavioral Dynamics:**

The bee defended by blocking the burrow just below the surface with its head or abdomen. When the bee thrust its head upward, it could dislodge the entire mass of grains, causing them to fall into the burrow. The bee attempted to remove grains from within the nest and place them outside, but was repeatedly forced to withdraw by approaching ants.

**Activity Patterns After Guard Departure:**

Following the guard ant's departure, ant activity decreased in five measurable ways:
1. Fewer ants participating
2. Decreased speed of ant movement
3. More haphazard approach paths to the nest (circling, wrong directions rather than direct approaches)
4. Large reduction in grain-dropping rate (intervals between drops varied from approximately 4 to 73 seconds with a mean of 30-32 seconds)
5. Apparent decrease in grain size

The bee's "boldness" increased correspondingly, with the bee eventually emerging and flying away after a two-minute period without ant intervention.

**Cessation of Activity:**

During an estimated 15-minute period when the bee was away from the nest, no grains were dropped, though one or two ants came to the entrance and stuck their heads over the rim. This was the longest period grain-dropping ceased. About two minutes after a bee returned, ants resumed activity. One ant crawled down the vertical burrow wall until almost completely inside, appeared to slip and fall within, and a struggle ensued in the dark before the ant emerged speedily.

**Environmental Factors:**

A piece of leaf blown across the nest entrance by wind triggered renewed ant activity. An ant that approached and touched the leaf particle left with great rapidity, suggesting sensitivity to novel stimuli.

**Outcome:**

The ants appeared unsuccessful in their operation over the approximately 90-minute observation period. Grain-dropping eventually ceased with no further activity during nearly 30 minutes of continued observation. There was no evidence that more than one bee was associated with the nest during observation, though two adult bees were recovered when the nest was partially excavated the following day.

**Functional Interpretation:**

The grains appeared to function as weapons, hitting the bee and causing it to withdraw deeper into the burrow. The guard ant appeared to be the key figure with a two-fold function: (1) keeping the bee within the nest, and (2) maintaining efficient and continued activity of other ants through her presence. It appeared that ants were attempting to trap the bee within the nest rather than simply plug the nest entrance. An alternative hypothesis suggests ants were merely closing a hole in response to its form or odor, with aggressive components arising independently when a bee was perceived nearby.
