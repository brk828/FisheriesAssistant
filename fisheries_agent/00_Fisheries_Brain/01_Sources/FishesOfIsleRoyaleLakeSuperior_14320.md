# Fishes of Isle Royale, Lake Superior, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #salmonidae #coregonidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Hubbs and Lagler (1949, *Papers of the Michigan Academy*, vol. XXXI, for 1947) present a systematic and ecological survey of the fish fauna of [[Isle Royale]], an island in northern [[Lake Superior]]. The fauna comprises 49 kinds of fish representing 41 species, 31 genera and 14 families. Field work included 1945 collections at stations L45:797 to L45:851 using seines, gill-nets and angling, supplemented by earlier surveys (Ruthven 1906, 1909; Koelz 1929) and commercial fishery records. The authors analyse zoogeographic origins, reinvasion routes after [[Glaciation]], ecological associations by habitat type, speciation in isolated inland lakes, and conservation policy for the national park.

## Background & Description

The study serves four aims: contributing to the ichthyological survey of Michigan, adding to the analysis of the Great Lakes fish fauna, addressing postglacial reinvasion, dispersal, faunal origin, isolation and speciation, and documenting a major segment of a national park's fauna.

Field work drew on several sources:
- **1904–1905 state surveys** (Ruthven), limited to bays, coves and a few inland lakes.
- **1920**, Captain R. E. Ellsworth's fish-patrol collections (McCargoe Cove, Washington River).
- **1929**, [[Walter Koelz]]'s intensive survey of inland lakes, unpublished in full but used extensively.
- **1930**, George Stanley's geological work, including lake-level elevations.
- **1945**, Karl F. Lagler's expedition, with 55 numbered collecting stations (L45:797–852), mostly seine hauls plus several gill-net sets.

Lake morphology was summarised in Table I (about 30 inland lakes, ranked by depth, with elevations from under 100 ft to about 400 ft above Lake Superior). Lily Lake is the highest, with Lake Desor at 235 ft and Siskiwit Lake, the largest inland lake, at 57 ft.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeography.** The authors attribute the depauperate fauna to the island's northern latitude, its isolation in a large cold lake, and the sparse fauna of the adjacent Lake Superior shore. Thirteen southern families are absent, including [[Polyodontidae]], [[Lepisosteidae]], [[Clupeidae]], [[Ictaluridae]] and [[Centrarchidae]] (only a few representatives present). Compared with the Great Lakes fauna, the island holds about one half of families, one third of genera, one quarter of species and one fifth of total kinds.

**Reinvasion routes.** Postglacial immigrants arrived through several routes: northern types (salmonids, coregonids, esocids, gadids, cottids and *Pungitius pungitius*) probably came from the northwest or from meltwater; many others entered from the Mississippi basin via glacial outlets. Four hypotheses for crossing the roughly 25-mile, 600-ft-deep channel to the mainland were considered: chance transport (improbable), a land bridge (rejected, as no evidence of postglacial connection exists), dispersal across cold deep water (favoured, supported by the [[Lake Michigan]] creek-fish analogy), and a warm postglacial period when the barrier was weaker (also considered plausible).

**Habitat associations.** Seven habitat types were recognised:
1. Lake Superior (down to about eight fathoms and the shore zone), about 20 typical species.
2. Exposed rocky shore, about 20 species.
3. Stream mouths, base-level streams and sheltered coves, about 31 species.
4. Boggy streams, beaver ponds and dystrophic lakes, nine species characteristic of these waters, including *Pfrille neogaea*, *Chrosomus eos* and *Pimephales promelas*.
5. Rapid streams, with *Catostomus commersonnii*, *Rhinichthys cataractae* and *Couesius plumbeus*.
6. Eutrophic lakes without coregonids (two to eight species).
7. Oligotrophic coregonid lakes, with the highest richness (Siskiwit 17, Richie 11, Desor 11, Sargent 9).

**Elevation and richness.** Species richness declines with elevation: about 40, 20, 15, 11 and 3 species across five elevation classes. Historical arrival sequence and limited access by fish to higher lakes through small outlets are presumed causes.

**Speciation.** Lakes have been isolated for roughly 12,000–25,000 years since the last ice recession. Notable differentiation includes three endemic minnow subspecies in [[Harvey Lake]] (about 15,000 years of isolation estimated), the endemic [[Siskiwit Lake Cisco]] (*Leucichthys bartletti*, roughly 5,000 years, described by Koelz in 1931), and the distinct [[Lake Desor Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis dustini*).

**Conservation.** The authors urge a permanent ban on introducing exotic fishes or exotic stocks of native game species into Isle Royale, maintenance of indigenous trout races, and regulation of angling and commercial fishing to prevent depletion of native stocks.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna composition:** 49 kinds, 41 species, 31 genera, 14 families. Three species are exotic or introduced to the island (sea lamprey, rainbow trout, smelt); brown trout was stocked but did not establish.
- **Sea lamprey:** A single 9.5-inch specimen taken off Rock Harbor on 15 August 1946 marked its first Isle Royale record.
- **Rainbow trout:** Well established through stocking, particularly in Washington Creek and Harbor. Brown trout did not persist.
- **Lake trout and siscowet:** Widespread in nearshore and deep waters; the siscowet subspecies' validity remains unresolved.
- **Cisco (*Leucichthys*):** Several local forms recognised. *L. bartletti* is judged specifically distinct from Siskiwit Lake, with gill-raker counts and snout proportions used to argue that young "artedi" are juveniles of *bartletti*.
- **Whitefish:** Siskiwit Lake whitefish show significantly fewer gill-rakers than Lake Superior fish (mean 25.64 vs 27.87), supporting subspecific differentiation.
- **Harvey Lake endemics:** *Margariscus margarita koelzi* (new), *Notropis heterolepis regalis* (new), and *Pimephales promelas harveyensis* (new). The *N. h. regalis* holotype measures 81 mm standard length, far above the usual maximum of about 60 mm for typical *N. h. heterolepis*.
- **Smelt:** Spreading through the Great Lakes; young abundant at the mouth of the Big Siskiwit River in July 1945, suggesting a possibly established spawning run.
- **Dispersal:** Several [[Ecological Analysis]] findings suggest that cold deep water is a weaker barrier than generally assumed for northern fishes.
- **Management recommendations:** Ban exotic introductions, protect indigenous trout races, and regulate commercial and angling take to prevent depletion of native stocks.
