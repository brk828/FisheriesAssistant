# Creel Census of the Colorado River Below Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department report, part of the Glen Canyon Unit tailwater and reservoir fisheries investigations under the [[Colorado River Storage Project]], documents a creel census at [[Lee's Ferry]] on the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The census ran from November 1, 1963 to July 1, 1964 and covered 128 days of roadblock and riverbank checks. Angling success was excellent, at an estimated 1.05 fish per man-hour, and 81.9 percent of anglers caught fish. Fishing pressure was light. Return of stocked, marked [[Rainbow Trout]] to the creel was estimated at 11.7 percent. The authors conclude that stocking is currently the only way to sustain a trout fishery at Lee's Ferry, because natural reproduction is hampered by unstable dam-controlled water levels. An appendix summarizes a shorter 1963 census that ended early after a flood-related sediment event.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** Lee's Ferry lies about 12 river miles below Glen Canyon Dam and offers the only public vehicular access to the Colorado River between the dam and [[Lake Mead]]. The fishable reach is about 13 miles. Roughly 3 miles are accessible from the bank and 6 miles by boat at low water. At high water, about 12 miles can be fished from boats. This census is the first segment of a planned six-year study. The second segment was to extend the work to [[Lake Powell]]. Only the last seven months of the first segment were completed because of an initial shortage of personnel.

**Design.** The census was planned for two days per week (one weekday and one weekend day). Manpower allowed it to expand to about four days per week, for 128 census days in total. Because only one road reaches Lee's Ferry, a roadblock check was used when the number of anglers allowed. A riverbank check supplemented it, mainly for overnight campers. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] standard creel form recorded party size, hours fished, number and species of fish, fish length, method and bait, number of successful anglers, and whether the angler fished from a boat or the shore.

**Estimation methods.**
- *Pressure* was estimated by direct expansion of daily counts, with weighting for differences between weekday and weekend pressure.
- *Return of planted fish* used tagged and fin-clipped fish. Five plants of marked fish totaling 7,500 were made during the census. Return was estimated by direct expansion of marked fish in the creel. Tag boxes were placed at convenient fishing locations to improve tag returns.
- *Measurements.* All fish checked were measured. About 50 trout per month were weighed to the nearest 1/100 pound and measured to the nearest 1/10 inch. The rest were measured to the nearest inch.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water regime.** Water levels and flows in the tailwater below Glen Canyon Dam fluctuate widely. The authors state that this drastic change in levels greatly hampers natural trout reproduction. High water in March through May, together with flow fluctuations, is assumed to have reduced fishing success during those months. Time since the last fish plant may also have contributed.

**Stocking history.** Between May 1963 and July 1964, catchable and fingerling rainbow trout were planted in the tailrace of Glen Canyon Dam and at Lee's Ferry. The abstract gives 50,000 catchable trout planted during the study period. The body text gives different figures for the same interval (about 11,600 catchable and 10,200 fingerlings, as partly obscured in the scan). These figures are inconsistent in the source and should be checked against the original records.

**Access and use.** Pressure was light, which the authors attribute to the remoteness of Lee's Ferry and its limited reputation as a trout fishery. Use was almost entirely by Arizona residents. Non-resident anglers made up only about 5 percent of pressure.

**Gear and technique.**
- *Boat versus shore:* Nearly all fishing (about 96 percent) was from the bank. Reasons given were the lack of rental boats, a safe boating range of under five miles (over half of it reachable from the bank), and the good bank catch rates.
- *Bait:* Most anglers (about three-quarters, as partly obscured in the scan) used prepared bait. About 6 percent used only artificial lures or flies. The remainder used a combination of both.

## Key Findings

**1. Angling success was excellent.**
- Main census (November 1963 to June 1964): 1,461 angler trips checked, which produced 6,297 rainbow trout and 61 channel catfish in 5,912 hours.
- Estimated catch rate: 1.05 fish per man-hour, with 81.9 percent of anglers successful.
- Monthly catch rates in Table I ranged from about 2.8 fish per hour in December to about 0.6 in May. Success ranged from 99 percent of anglers in December to 72 percent in May.
- Success declined in March through May, which the authors attribute to high water, flow fluctuations, and time since the last plant.

**2. Fishing pressure was light.**
- Estimated total: 2,477 angler trips, 9,765.9 angler hours, 10,654 rainbow trout, and 98 channel catfish.
- The abstract reports 10,554 rainbow trout, which conflicts with the 10,654 given in the body text and Table II.

**3. Stocked trout returned at a modest rate.**
- Overall return of marked fish to the creel: estimated at 11.7 percent of planted fish.
- Tag returns (from tag boxes and creel checks) varied by plant. The March 5, 1964 plant returned about 18 percent. The March 12, 1964 plant, made directly below the dam where pressure was low, returned about 1.9 percent.
- Excluding the May 12, 1964 plant, the estimated return was 13.3 percent.
- The authors describe the return data as of limited value for management because of irregular flows and unfulfilled pressure potential.

**4. Trout size and condition.**
- 460 rainbow trout were weighed and measured. They averaged about 0.31 pounds and 9.7 inches, giving a mean condition factor (K) of about 1.34.
- The most common size class was the 10-inch group.

**5. Channel catfish condition declined.**
- The appendix (1963 census) found higher catfish success in summer, which the authors attribute to warmer water.
- The main census recorded a marked reduction in channel catfish condition factor, with the exact percentage partly obscured in the scan.

**6. Recommendations.**
- Continue the creel census at Lee's Ferry to evaluate the fishery and its potential.
- Stocking of catchable and fingerling trout is currently the only reliable way to maintain the fishery, because natural reproduction is limited by water-level changes.
- Make at least one plant of marked fingerling rainbow trout to determine their growth and contribution to the catch.
- Continue marked plants of catchable trout once water levels stabilize or follow a predictable pattern.

**7. Appendix: 1963 Lee's Ferry creel census (Jack Sinn, Arizona Game and Fish Department).**
- Period: June 3 to August 31, 1963, with 13 sample days (weekend road checks and weekday interviews at the fishing area).
- Totals: 204 anglers, 1,074 hours, 364 channel catfish, and 784 rainbow trout.
- Catch rates: 0.34 channel catfish per hour and 0.73 rainbow trout per hour. Average fisherman day: 5.27 hours. Average per trip: 1.78 catfish and 3.84 trout.
- Stocking of catchable rainbow trout began May 2, 1963, with later plants in May, July, September, and October. Catchable trout were the only stocking method the report considered effective.
- A heavy local rain on August 30, 1963 deposited large amounts of sediment in the river, disrupting fishing and causing an unknown fish loss. The census was discontinued as a result.
