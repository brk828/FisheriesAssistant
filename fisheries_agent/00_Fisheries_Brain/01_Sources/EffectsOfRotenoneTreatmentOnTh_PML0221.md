# Effects of Rotenone Treatment on the Fauna of the Green River, Wyoming

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Wyoming Game and Fish Commission Fisheries Research Bulletin (Number 1, 1967) by Niles Allen Binns reports the effects of a September 1962 [[Rotenone]] treatment (five percent emulsified product, applied at roughly 1.5 to 10 ppm) on the fish and invertebrate fauna of the [[Green River]] in southwestern Wyoming. Sampling ran from July 1962 to October 1964. The treatment was carried out to remove rough (non-game) fish ahead of the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Fontenelle Dam]] impoundments, and to create a sport fishery.

The treatment largely exterminated the fish and drastically reduced invertebrates in the mainstream. Invertebrate populations began to recover within months, and by late summer 1963 they were equal to or greater than pretreatment numbers, though composition changed. Recolonization occurred mainly through downstream and upstream migration from untreated tributaries, sloughs and backwaters, with survival of eggs and some surviving populations in poorly treated off-channel areas. Several species (notably [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Black Bullhead]]) did not return. Rainbow trout stocking established a successful fishery.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Green River, southwest Wyoming, on the west slope of the Continental Divide. It is divided into four sections: the New Fork River (I), Upper Green River (II), Middle Green River (III) and Lower Green River (IV). The drainage includes parts of three national forests and about 10,000 square miles above Green River City. Elevation falls from high mountain headwaters to about 5,600 feet at the Flaming Gorge site.
- **Water chemistry:** Alkalinity ranged from 64 to 158 ppm CaCO3 at station A (New Fork River) and pH from 7.25 to 8.8. Upper Green River alkalinity was 74 to 200 ppm and pH 7.5 to 8.8. Lower river alkalinity was 89 to 228 ppm and pH 7.5 to 8.75. The minimum recorded water temperature was 31°F.
- **Rotenone background:** The authors cite a commonly used minimum of 0.5 ppm for lakes, up to 2.0 ppm for resistant bullheads. Streams require higher concentrations; [[Berry and Larkin]] (1954) recommended more than 5.6 ppm for rehabilitation where carp and suckers are present.
- **Study design:** Nine permanent invertebrate sampling stations (A to I) plus temporary lower-river stations, using [[Surber Sampler]] bottom samples and drift nets. Fish were sampled with gill nets, dynamite, seines, traps and rotenone. Emergence traps were also used.
- **Treatment mechanics:** Rotenone was introduced from stations about every ten miles, with mainstream discharge lasting seven hours and tributary discharge six hours. Concentrations ranged from 9.4 ppm to 2.5 ppm at station 22 after a sudden flow drop.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish responses:** Fish fled downstream ahead of the toxicant or were trapped at downstream introduction stations. Intoxicated whitefish thrashed at the surface, while carp became sluggish and lost equilibrium after about three hours. Adult carp in the river near station 9 first showed distress 105 minutes after exposure.
- **Invertebrate mortality:** Adult beetles, leeches, snails, stonefly nymphs and many others died. Dead animals collected in quiet eddies. Survivors were mainly dragonfly (Gomphidae) and snipe fly (Rhagionidae) larvae.
- **Persistence of toxicant:** Accumulations downstream prolonged contamination. Rotenone was present at Green River City for at least 131 hours, and about 0.1 ppm was still found there after 11.5 hours on September 11.
- **Detoxification:** Warm, alkaline, turbid water and aquatic vegetation seem to speed decomposition. Dehydrorotenone, a decomposition product, may remain insecticidal.
- **Recolonization drivers:** The authors cite migration from untreated areas as the most important factor, along with passive drift, survival of eggs and resistant life stages. Snails were passively transported; mobile flying adults such as Baetis could recolonize quickly.

## Key Findings

- **Fish:** Pretreatment populations in the New Fork and Upper Green were typical, dominated by mountain whitefish, then flannelmouth suckers. Brown trout were probably the most common game fish. Redside shiners and fathead minnows were recently introduced exotics that had become very common.
- **Fish recovery:** Whitefish re-entered rapidly, moving about 17.5 miles downstream from untreated waters. Brown trout needed nearly two years to regain their pretreatment distribution. Carp were found in scattered areas (including 1962 year class fish that had survived in poorly treated sloughs). Flannelmouth suckers spread to most sections by 1964, though the sucker population sizes and distributions were uneven.
- **Species not recovered:** Squawfish, humpback sucker, channel catfish and black bullhead did not reappear, apparently because the only recruitment source was below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. Bonytail chub reappeared in the second summer from upstream populations.
- **Stocking:** Rainbow trout were planted six months after treatment (fry, plus larger fish in upper reaches) and supported a successful fishery.
- **Invertebrates:** The Green River fauna was drastically reduced. The upper 30 miles began recovering within a month. The lower treated area was nearly barren until spring 1963. By September 1963 numbers equaled or exceeded pretreatment levels across the treated area, though composition differed. Sufficient fish food for a fishable trout population was present by late summer 1963.
- **Succession:** Tendipedidae (midges) became dominant in most areas. Two rare mayfly genera, Pentagenia and Heptagenia, did not reappear. Five other mayfly genera not previously found (including Leptophlebia, Traverella and Isonychia) appeared during recovery.
- **Stations:** At station D (below the introduction station), recovery was fastest, due partly to proximity to untreated water and good habitat in moss, algae and Ranunculus beds. Station E recovered numerically but had abnormal composition for eight months. Station H showed long-term changes, with Tubificidae dominant after treatment.
- **Rotenone determination:** The colorimetric Post method gave erratic results. Field and laboratory bioassays were judged the most reliable.
- **Evaluation:** The authors concluded the Green River Rehabilitation Project was successful in meeting its basic fishery management objectives. Non-game populations in the lower treated area could become a problem again.
- **Ecological notes:** Water clarity increased after the 1961 test treatment, probably because bottom fish were removed. Plant growth increased with the loss of grazing fish.
