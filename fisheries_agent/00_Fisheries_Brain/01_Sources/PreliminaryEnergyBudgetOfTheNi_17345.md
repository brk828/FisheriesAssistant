# Preliminary Energy Budget of the Ninespine Stickleback (Pungitius pungitius) in an Arctic Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gasterosteidae

## Executive Summary

This paper presents a first-order energy budget for a population of [[Ninespine Stickleback]] (*Pungitius pungitius*) inhabiting shallow [[Tundra Lake]] Ikroavik, near Barrow in northern Alaska. The study was part of the [[U.S. Tundra Biome]] program of the [[International Biological Programme]] and was designed to supply input for a whole-lake modelling effort. Metabolic rate, growth, feeding, and population density were measured over two summers (1971 and 1972). The authors combined these into a balanced energy equation (pR = M + dW/dt) and produced individual and population-level estimates. The authors describe the result as a preliminary approximation, given the variability of the environment and the methods used.

The French summary (Résumé) is a translation of the English abstract and carries the same data.

## Background & Description

- **Study context:** Arctic fish metabolism and energetics had been studied comparatively little. Most earlier work involved temperate species and was rarely built into a full population energy budget (Mann 1969). Alaskan arctic work was limited to [[Wohlschlag]] studies on whitefish and cisco near Barrow, and to [[Scholander et al. 1953]] on sticklebacks and other poikilotherms. European work on this species came from Hynes (1950) on feeding and Jones and Hynes (1950) on age and growth.
- **Approach:** Metabolism was measured with respect to size, temperature, time of day, and sex. Feeding was studied in the laboratory and the field. Growth was measured from length-frequency modes. Natural history was also recorded.
- **Energy equation:** pR = M + dW/dt, where R is ration, p is utilization coefficient (set at 0.8), M is total metabolic expenditure, and dW/dt is growth rate. Any two parameters could be measured to calculate the third, so the measured and calculated values checked each other.
- **Lake setting:** Ikroavik Lake has an ice-free period of about 2 months. The fish moved from the lake basin into flooded marginal marsh by late June or early July and stayed there while water remained. The lake basin proper had no sticklebacks found in mid-summer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Marginal marshes with 1–15 cm of water were highly variable in temperature. A movement of about a meter could cause a large temperature change. Basin temperatures reached up to 15 °C by mid-August in some years.
- **Diurnal temperature change:** The fish experienced diurnal temperature changes of up to 20 °C in early summer when they moved between basin and margin.
- **Seasonal shifts:** Marshes shrank through evaporation and runoff. Water levels and ice push at breakup could strand fish. The authors suggest that dead sticklebacks reported by Wohlschlag (1957) at breakup may have resulted from such entrapment.
- **Food web:** Plankton and benthic fauna were both more abundant in the marginal marsh than in the lake basin, according to the cited studies (Stross 1971; Bierle 1971; Livingstone et al. 1958).
- **Spawning:** Spawning seems to occur early in the season, based on the presence of ripe 2+ females only at that time and the later appearance of 0+ fish.

## Key Findings

**Metabolism**
- Oxygen consumption of 87 fish was summarized by the regression Log Y = −2.795 + 0.823 log W + 0.094 T, where Y is µl O₂ min⁻¹, W is log weight (mg), and T is temperature (°C). The multiple correlation was R = 0.811 and the F-value was 81.81.
- The weight coefficient (0.82) is close to typical values for fish. The temperature coefficient was higher than expected for a eurythermal species. No significant differences were found for time of day or sex, and no significant departure from linearity in the T–log metabolism relation was detected.
- Metabolic rates were relatively high compared with temperate fishes. The authors doubt this reflects cold adaptation and suggest short acclimation, irritability, and uncontrolled disturbance instead.

**Growth**
- Fish reached about 21 mm at the end of the first year, 42 mm in the second, and 65 mm in the third. No fish older than 2+ were found, based on otolith examination.
- The length–weight relation was Log Wt = −2.579 + 2.745 Log L (R = 0.99), with weight in mg and length in mm.
- Summer growth was about 1.1%/day for 0+ and 1+ classes and 0.5%/day for 2+ fish.

**Diet**
- Analysis of 226 stomachs showed that chironomid larvae and zooplankton were the main foods. Zooplankton consisted of cyclopoid copepods, *Daphnia*, and *Eurycercus* sp. Only 17 stomachs were empty.
- Chironomid larvae were taken size-selectively. Smaller larvae made up a significantly higher share of the diet in 0+ fish (P < 0.05).

**Ration Estimates (30-mm, 300-mg fish)**
- Energy budget at 5 °C, 10 °C, and 15 °C: growth 8.2 cal/day; metabolism 4.20, 10.5, and 31.1 cal/day; ration 14.7, 23.4, and 49.1 cal/day; growth efficiency K = 56%, 23.5%, and 17% respectively.
- Feeding-experiment ration at 10 °C was 24.0 cal/day, and the gastric clearing ration at 15 °C was 12.2 cal/day. The 5 °C gastric clearing estimate was 4.55 cal/day, but the authors judged those 5 °C experiments less satisfactory.
- The mean gastric clearing rate was 5.0%/hr in the first treatment group and 13.4%/hr in the second. The authors note that gastric clearing rates probably underestimate true feeding rates.

**Population Density and Biomass**
- Density was uneven, but fish were more abundant in early summer (late June and July) than later. Larger fish preferred *Arctophila* stands, while late-summer catches in *Carex* areas were almost entirely 0+ fish.
- The highest density recorded was 74 g/m² in a marginal *Arctophila* area on 1 August 1972.
- The population sample of 30 June 1972 gave 16.5 fish/m², a biomass of 3.97 g/m², population ration of 96.6 cal·day⁻¹·population⁻¹, and population growth of 13.4 g and 338 cal·day⁻¹ with a mean population growth efficiency of 0.247.

**Limitations and Conclusions**
- Mark–recapture failed because of clumped distribution. A drop-net frame of 0.7 m² was used instead.
- The authors call the budget a first-order approximation valid only under the conditions described. They identify sources of error in every method and note that laboratory and field results will differ from nature.
- The paper concludes that Arctic lake systems are less simple than often assumed and that high spatial and temporal variability complicate complete population analyses.
