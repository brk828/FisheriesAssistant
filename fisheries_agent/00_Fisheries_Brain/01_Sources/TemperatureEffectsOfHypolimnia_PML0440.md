# Temperature Effects of Hypolimnial-Release Dams on Early Life Stages of Colorado River Basin Big-River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This experimental study by Clarkson and Childs (2000, *Copeia* 2:402–412) tests how cold water released from [[Hypolimnial-Release Dams]] affects early life stages of four imperiled [[Colorado River Basin]] big-river fishes: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Larvae and early juveniles were reared at 10 °C, 14 °C and 20 °C. Growth, specific growth rate, and time to transformation from larva to juvenile were all significantly lower at the colder temperatures, and fish moved abruptly from 20 °C to 10 °C lost equilibrium ("cold coma"). The authors conclude that thermal modification of dam discharges is needed for recovery, and that this may favour [[Nonnative Fishes]], so broader control measures would also be required.

## Background & Description

The mainstem and major tributaries of the Colorado River have been altered by more than a dozen large dams. These dams blocked migration routes, changed temperature, discharge and sediment transport, created lentic habitats, and facilitated the establishment of nonindigenous species. Together these changes contributed to declines and endangerment of native "big-river" fishes that historically ranged through the larger streams of the system.

Hypolimnial-release dams are especially damaging because they depress spring-summer tailwater temperatures. Water temperatures below 15 °C inhibit gonadal maturation, spawning and embryonic development. Cold tailwaters also reduce growth, swimming performance and survival of young fish, and can lower fecundity and overwinter survival.

Documented case histories include:
- **[[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (Green River, Utah, closed 1962):** spring-summer tailwater temperatures fell to near 6 °C (previously 12–21 °C). Several native species disappeared from the upper 104 km tailwater reach, and no native reproduction was observed in the lower reach during high-flow, cold years. Reproduction resumed after temperature controls warmed the water to highs near 13 °C in 1978.
- **[[Glen Canyon Dam]] (Grand Canyon, Arizona, completed 1963):** tailwater temperatures fell to near 10 °C. [[Colorado Squawfish]], Roundtail Chub and Bonytail were extirpated from the mainstem, Razorback Sucker was functionally extirpated, and Flannelmouth Sucker mainstem reproduction was reduced or eliminated. Most successful reproduction now occurs in tributaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study setting.** Experiments were conducted at Bubbling Ponds State Hatchery near Cornville, Arizona, using pathogen-free artesian well water (18.6 °C, pH 7.6, 366 µS/cm specific conductance, 4.3–7.3 mg/L dissolved oxygen).

**Source populations.**
- [[Gila Cypha]] (Humpback Chub) eggs were collected in April 1993 from the lower [[Little Colorado River]], Grand Canyon, and fertilized using pituitary-injected, manually stripped fish.
- [[Ptychocheilus Lucius]] (Colorado Squawfish) came from hatchery broodstock spawned 20 May 1993.
- [[Xyrauchen Texanus]] (Razorback Sucker) embryos came from broodstock at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico, hatched March 1994.
- [[Catostomus Latipinnis]] (Flannelmouth Sucker) gametes were taken from wild fish near Lee's Ferry, Arizona, on 14 May 1995 (river temperature 12 °C) and hatched 21–22 May.

**Experimental temperatures.** 10 °C mimics the year-round temperature below unmodified dams; 20 °C approximates spring-summer temperatures in unregulated reaches; 14 °C represents mixing of tributary and mainstem flows or warm backwater habitats in tailwaters. Three age groups (about 7, 14 and 41 days posthatch) were used to represent different times of entry into cold water from warm tributaries.

**Cold-shock trials.** Young fish were moved from 20 °C to 10, 12 or 14 °C after 24 h acclimation to examine short-term behavioural responses, using [[Thermal Shock]] as the main stressor.

## Key Findings

**Growth and survival**
- Mean specific growth rate (SGR) was significantly lower at both 10 °C and 14 °C than at 20 °C control temperatures on all sampling dates and in all age comparisons (Kruskal-Wallis tests, Holm-adjusted).
- For Razorback Sucker and Flannelmouth Sucker, length and weight differed significantly among temperatures for most age groups and sample dates. Exceptions included some older-group comparisons (for example, 14-day Razorback Sucker at days 2, 10 and 24, and 41-day Flannelmouth Sucker at day 35).
- For Humpback Chub and Colorado Squawfish, all sampling dates were significant on replicate-mean tests, except the day 24 length and weight comparisons for Colorado Squawfish. After multiple-test correction, all probabilities for these two species became nonsignificant, probably because only replicate means were analysed (small n).
- The authors concluded that cold water had the same effect on both catostomids and cyprinids.

**Development and transformation**
- Transformation was delayed at low temperature. For the 41-day Razorback Sucker group, the first juveniles appeared at 20 °C (day 63, 65% of sample) and at 14 °C (day 128, 30%), but none had transformed by day 128 at 10 °C.
- For 7-day Flannelmouth Sucker, juveniles first appeared on day 59 at 20 °C (70%) and day 91 at 14 °C (40%). No fish reared at 10 °C had transformed by that time.
- For 41-day Flannelmouth Sucker, all fish had transformed by day 64 at 20 °C only.
- Transformation was also delayed for Humpback Chub and Colorado Squawfish, with the 10 °C groups remaining larvae after the 20 °C groups had transformed.
- Transformation lengths in the laboratory were about 27–30 mm TL for Razorback Sucker and 28–29 mm for Flannelmouth Sucker. Finfold loss occurred at about 27 mm (Humpback Chub) and 25 mm (Colorado Squawfish).

**Thermal shock**
- Transfer from 20 °C to 10 °C caused 5–90 min of cold coma in 5–7-day Humpback Chub and in Colorado Squawfish larvae. Fish could not maintain equilibrium or position in the water column and settled to the bottom; recovery followed over about 60 min.
- Some 11–14-day Humpback Chub also entered cold coma at 10 °C but recovered within 15 min.
- Razorback Sucker and Flannelmouth Sucker showed brief cold coma only at 10 °C (30–60 s and 5–10 s, respectively).
- Transfers to 12 °C or 14 °C caused few or no short-term effects, though some lethargy was seen.
- No mortality attributed to temperature shock was recorded in Razorback Sucker or Flannelmouth Sucker trials, and no Humpback Chub died during the 4 h observation periods.

**Implications and recommendations**
- Cold-induced delays in transformation extend exposure to existing mortality sources such as food scarcity, hydrological disturbance, predation and parasitism.
- The authors argue that, short of dam removal, more flexible operations, especially warming of discharges, are needed to conserve these fishes. Tailwater reaches should not be ignored in recovery plans.
- Warming tailwaters may also benefit [[Nonnative Fishes]]. Recovery may therefore require basin-wide measures, such as high-magnitude floods, taxon-specific piscicides, segregation of native and sport fisheries, and public education.
- Hatchery production and repatriation may slow declines, but are not sufficient alone.
