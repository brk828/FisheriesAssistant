# Delaware Lake and Pond Survey (1955)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This Delaware Board of Game and Fish Commissioners publication (Fisheries Publication No. 4, 1955), written by fisheries biologist Jay L. Harmic under [[Federal Aid Projects]] F-1-R-3 and F-1-R-4, is the final booklet in a series of preliminary biological surveys of the state's freshwater fishing waters. It covers 31 fishing ponds and two streams open to public fishing, plus six private fishing areas. The surveys combined field sampling, [[Creel Census]] interviews, [[Aerial Census]] counts, seining, and limited water chemistry. The authors conclude that overfishing is not a problem on any surveyed pond, that much good fishing goes unused, and that the main limits on fishing are aquatic vegetation, water-control structures, restricted access, and pollution. The note is written for anglers and pond owners, and it defers detailed procedures and statistics to the commission's files.

## Background & Description

The survey was conducted by the fisheries division of the Delaware Game and Fish Commission over roughly four years, with field work in 1953 and 1954. The text names the crew and the state officials who supervised the work, and it acknowledges support for field work, drafting, and typing. Two earlier booklets in the series had already been distributed to sportsmen, pond owners, and scientists.

The ponds are grouped by watershed and county: the [[Nanticoke Watershed]] (Hearns, Concord, Records, Chipmans, Trap, Raccoon, and Portsville ponds), [[Broad Creek]] and nearby waters, the [[Other Sussex County Ponds]] (Diamond, Wagamons, Betts, Millsboro, Morris, McGinnis, and Moores Lake), [[Silver Lake]] at Dover and Middletown, the [[Hockessin Quarry Pits]] in New Castle County, and several private areas. Each pond entry gives a surface area, watershed drainage, water-control and dike condition, vegetation, facilities, and a creel summary. Most entries end with a list of "needs."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation.** Aquatic plants were the most frequent limit on fishing. Floating heart, spatterdock, [[Elodea]], coontail, sago pondweed, floating pond lilies, and spike rush appear across the ponds. [[Hydrodictyon]], a net alga, covered Millsboro and Morris ponds, and [[Blue-Green Algae]] bloomed at Wagamons and [[Moores Lake]]. The survey links the weedy condition of some ponds to high forage-fish numbers and suggests that vegetation both shelters forage fish and favors pike and bass.

**Water quality and chemistry.** Analyses covered dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, alkalinity, and pH. These were carried out over only a three-day period. None were judged unsatisfactory for fish survival or reproduction, but the authors note that conditions may change through the year. [[Hockessin Quarry Pits]] were the exception: some deep pits had no dissolved oxygen in their lower layers. Domestic sewage from Wyoming and Georgetown, the [[Middletown Sewage Disposal Plant]] effluent, and canneries on Broad Creek were identified as pollution sources. [[Silver Lake]] at Middletown had heavy plankton blooms attributed to fertility from the sewage effluent.

**Fish community and forage.** Seining and creel work listed the principal species. Bass, pickerel, yellow perch, bluegill, and common sunfish dominated the sport catch. Golden shiners, killifish, sunfish, and mud shad made up much of the forage base. The overabundance of [[Golden Shiner]] at Wagamons Pond was judged more harmful than useful. [[Carp]] removals were conducted at several waters, and suckers and bullheads were also present.

**Bottom life.** Chipmans Pond had considerably fewer bottom food organisms than any other pond surveyed, but no link to other biological features was found. The quarry pits had a sterile silt layer about 1.5 feet thick with almost no life.

**Shad and herring.** The tumblehole below Concord Pond and the Nanticoke River support spring runs of shad and herring. The river has a long-term decline in sport and commercial catch, with [[Drift Net Fishing]] for shad as the main commercial method. Causes are not fully known.

**Management tools.** Management tools included [[Rotenone]] and [[Copper Sulfate]] treatments, the algicide [[Delrad]] (from Hercules Powder Company), manually operated gate-type and permanent spillway structures, drawdowns, and stocking of [[Largemouth Bass]] and bluegill.

## Key Findings

- **Survey scope:** 31 fishing ponds, two open streams, and six private fishing areas were considered. Thirteen Sussex County ponds were included in the creel census.
- **No overfishing:** Census results "prove conclusively" that no surveyed pond faces overfishing. Most ponds were under-fished, and the authors describe the situation as "good fishing going to waste."
- **Most-used pond:** Hearns Pond (65 acres) was fished more heavily than any other pond in the unit. 1,149 fishermen caught 2,206 fish, 988 of them bass.
- **Hearns Pond condition:** Good facilities and good catches. No needs identified.
- **Concord Pond (68 acres):** Floating weeds reduced the fishing area. 313 fishermen caught 615 fish, only 75 of them bass. Needs were weed control, gate repair, a boat livery, and a launching ramp.
- **Records Pond (90 acres):** 1,179 fishermen caught 1,167 fish, including 365 bass and 318 pickerel. High water was needed to hold back vegetation.
- **Chipmans Pond (55 acres):** Owner Carlton Lowe ran rental boats and cabins. 468 fishermen caught 824 fish, including 384 bass. The dike had inadequate freeboard and a professional examination was recommended. Sunday fishing was prohibited by the owner.
- **Trap Pond (95 acres):** Ranked sixth of thirteen for fishing pressure. 876 fishermen caught 2,063 fish, including 841 bass and 1,205 pickerel. The pond had the highest bass and pike per man-hour in the survey. Rapid vegetation growth was expected to require complete weed control.
- **Raccoon Pond (8.5 acres):** Only 23 fishermen caught 11 fish after a heavy fish kill. Restocking with bass was recommended.
- **Diamond Pond (38 acres):** 587 fishermen caught 2,407 fish, but only 141 were bass. Dense Elodea made casting nearly impossible. Needs were a boat livery and weed control.
- **Wagamons Pond:** 535 fishermen caught 1,685 fish, only 86 of them bass. Yellow perch, bluegill, and pickerel dominated. Golden shiners were overabundant. Weed control and reclamation were recommended.
- **Betts Pond (58 acres):** Fish per man-hour was second highest in the survey, which the authors attributed to a recent drawdown. Needs were a boat livery, a launching ramp, and an end to planting aquatic vegetation.
- **Millsboro Pond (146 acres):** The second-most heavily fished pond, with 1,150 fishermen catching 2,033 fish, 889 of them bass. The control structure needed replacement because a flood could wash it away.
- **Moores Lake:** Repeated copper sulfate treatments had little effect on algae. Delrad gave "fairly good control" when towed in a burlap bag, and a 1954 box-diffusion method gave good control. Only temporary control is possible until domestic sewage is removed. About several tons of carp were removed in late 1954.
- **Silver Lake (Dover, 168 acres):** Rotenone treatment removed over 50 tons of carp. Recovered by weight: about 500 pounds of game fish and 106,000 pounds of rough fish. The 24-inch drain was inadequate for drawdown. Partial restocking with bass and bluegill was underway.
- **Silver Lake (Middletown):** Good bass and bluegill fishing, with a swimming beach and a boat livery. The upper end was filling with weeds.
- **Hockessin Quarry Pits:** Eight small, deep, cold pits, some with no dissolved oxygen at depth. Visibility was about six feet, dropping to about one foot after rain. Owners were private, so access by agreement, lease, or acquisition was the first priority. Some pits might support put-and-take trout fishing.
- **Private areas:** [[Smawleys Pond]], [[Sunset Lake]], and [[Shallcross Lake]] are private. Weed control was recommended at Sunset and Shallcross, and carp removal at Smawleys, which supplies city water.
- **Hoopes Reservoir:** The largest impounded water in Delaware and a Wilmington water supply, closed to fishing. The authors argued that controlled public fishing could succeed financially and recreationally, as in other cities.
- **Nanticoke River:** Sport fishing for bass, pickerel, shad, and occasional striped bass. Sport fishing declined over 20 years for uncertain reasons.
- **Williams Pond:** Rebuilt by the State Highway Department and due to open for public fishing in 1955. Initial bass stocking was recommended.
- **Policy conclusion:** Most improvements depend on private owners, since the commission cannot spend money on private property. State General Assembly appropriations were a possible maintenance route.
