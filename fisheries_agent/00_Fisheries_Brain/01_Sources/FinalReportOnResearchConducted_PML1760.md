# Humpback Chub Population Monitoring in the Little Colorado River, Spring 1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This progress report, prepared for the Arizona Game and Fish Department by C. O. Minckley of the [[Biology Department]] at Northern Arizona University, documents the fourth year of monitoring the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the lower [[Little Colorado River]], covering the period from 15 April to 16 May 1990. The study aimed to document reproduction and to conduct mark-recapture work for population estimates and movement. Fish were collected with [[Hoop Nets]] and [[Trammel Nets]] at two camps, one at the [[Salt Trail Canyon]] and one at the [[Confluence]] with the Colorado River. Beyond the chub, the report records non-native fish, a rare [[Razorback Sucker]] capture, the first local record of juvenile [[Striped Bass]], and evidence that the species is spreading in the Grand Canyon.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is an endangered cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River basin. The 1990 work was the fourth year of monitoring this population, building on earlier studies (Minckley 1980, 1981, 1989). The team included personnel from the Arizona Game and Fish Department, Northern Arizona University, Arizona State University, the Navajo Natural Heritage Program, and the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies group, with logistical support from Canyoneers, Inc.

Sampling used hoop nets (1 m diameter, 2.5 m long, some with 6 m wings) and trammel nets (91.5 m x 2.5 m, 7.6 cm outer and 2.5 cm inner mesh), plus seines. Fish were measured to the nearest mm and weighed to the nearest gram. Sex was determined by expression of gametes where possible. Tagging used both carlin (dark blue finger-ling) tags and [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT), the latter with reported 99% retention and survival in earlier studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Confluence:** Water temperatures in the Little Colorado River (16–21°C) were generally cooler than the mainstream Colorado River during spring, and the confluence is subject to tidal fluctuation from mainstream flows.
- **Spawning:** Larval fish and young humpback chub (under 20 mm) were present at the start of research, indicating spawning was underway in April. The spawning trigger is probably water temperature, along with increasing day length and food availability.
- **Competition and predation:** Common carp were abundant in the confluence area and were considered a threat to larval chub and razorback sucker eggs and larvae. Stomach analysis showed channel catfish and striped bass were feeding on native fish.
- **Groundwater:** The Little Colorado River is fed largely by warm blue springs, which keeps flows and temperatures relatively constant.

## Key Findings

**Collections and sex ratio**
- Confluence nets (N = 1,414 fish, 12 species): humpback chub made up 65.7% (N = 931), and native fish 95.5%. Overall, males were about 50% of sexed fish.
- Salt Trail nets (N = 1,212 fish, 7 species): humpback chub made up 49.7%. Of sexed chub, 67% were male.
- Total catch: 2,626 fish across 12 species. Non-native fish were under 4% of the catch.

**Reproduction**
- Ripe males averaged 360 mm and ripe females 340 mm in total length at the confluence, with the smallest ripe male at 139 mm.
- Spent females were present, and the proportion of females was higher at the Salt Trail site.

**Growth and population estimate**
- Recaptured fish showed observed growth of up to 49 mm, with the greatest growth in fish at large for the longest periods.
- The population estimate from the upstream nets was 1,788 humpback chub, and from the confluence nets 8,493 chub (N = 1,539 total).

**Mark-recapture and movement**
- Of 1,020 fish tagged at the confluence, 5% were PIT tagged and 95% carlin tagged.
- Movement was very limited. Most recaptured fish were captured within 500 m of the initial capture site; 52% of recaptured fish did not move, 36% moved downstream, and 12% moved upstream.

**Rare and non-native species**
- Three razorback suckers were captured from the parallel trammel net between 26 and 28 April, and two of them were recaptured within 24 hours.
- The first juvenile striped bass (351 mm, 434 g) was recorded from the mouth of the Little Colorado River. The author predicts that striped bass will establish a population in the Grand Canyon, threatening trout and humpback chub.

**Recommendations**
1. Continue annual monitoring of the species and its habitat in this region.
2. Analyse carp impacts on native fish early life stages.
3. Initiate studies on the life history stages of native fish.
4. Conduct intensive studies on spawning ecology and early life stages of the humpback chub.
