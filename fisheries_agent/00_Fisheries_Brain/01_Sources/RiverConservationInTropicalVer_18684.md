# Tropical versus Temperate River Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by C.M. Pringle argues that river conservation in tropical regions cannot simply transfer strategies developed on temperate streams. Tropical rivers are more biodiverse and more migratory, and they respond to human disturbance differently. They are also often under weaker regulation and greater socio-economic pressure. The chapter examines these contrasts, then reviews critical habitats such as [[Floodplain Forests]] and [[Estuarine Areas]], development pressures including agrochemicals, [[Hydroelectric Projects]] and [[Interbasin Water Transfers]], and closes with priority research and management needs.

## Background & Description

The chapter defines the tropics as the equatorial area roughly 30° north and south of the Equator, not strictly bounded by the Tropics of Capricorn and Cancer. Tropical rivers have experienced long periods of climatic stability, which, together with Quaternary climate fluctuations and sea-level changes that aided dispersal (notably in South America), has supported high biodiversity and endemism.

The chapter stresses that tropical rivers are characterized by higher water temperatures with little seasonality, heavy seasonal precipitation, high discharge variation, floods, and high diversity of migratory fauna. The author notes that much of the basic ecology of tropical rivers remains poorly known, including which nutrients limit primary production.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Human pressure and population.** Humans already appropriate over 50% of accessible surface water, projected to reach 70% by 2025 (Postel et al., 1996). The tropics are expected to produce 80% of the world's population increase over the following two decades (Pereira, 1989). Many tropical countries have annual population growth above 3%; the chapter cites 3.4% for South Africa, which implies a doubling in about 23 years.

**Water quality and health.** Water quality in many tropical countries is driven mainly by human health concerns rather than ecological conservation. About 80% of tropical diseases are water-related and linked to poor sewage treatment or lack of sanitation. Examples include the 1991 cholera epidemics in Latin America, less than 10% treatment of domestic wastewater in the Caribbean, and raw sewage discharged in West Africa.

**Temperate comparison.** Changes in temperate rivers unfolded over centuries, whereas tropical rivers are changing within years or decades. In the United States, over half of wetlands present 200 years ago have been lost, more than 85% of inland surface waters are artificially controlled, and there are more than 5500 dams over 15 m high. Of 800 fish species, 34% are rare, endangered, critically endangered or extinct. Of native unionid mussels and crayfish, 73% and 65% respectively fall into these categories. The [[Clean Water Act]] of 1972 reduced discharges of toxic chemicals and raw sewage, but has been less effective against non-point-source pollution. Over 30% of US streams and rivers fail chemical water quality standards.

**Globalization.** Tropical countries with developing or unstable economies are vulnerable because foreign investment often operates with fewer environmental restrictions than in temperate nations. Toxic waste trade has shifted from Africa to Latin America, the Caribbean, Eastern Europe and, more recently, Southern Asia as regulations tightened in those regions.

**Hydrology and climate.** High solar input drives rapid evaporation. The [[Intertropical Convergence Zone]] produces erratic, high-intensity rainfall, making floods and droughts inescapable. Deforested slopes in Costa Rica lose 20 to 200 t of soil per hectare per year (Hartshorn et al., 1982). Low flows reduce dilution capacity in urban rivers, and high temperatures further slow waste degradation.

## Key Findings

1. **Temperate models do not transfer directly.** Contaminant fate, hydropower design, and ecotoxicology based on temperate studies may be ineffective or harmful in the tropics.

2. **Tropical floodplains are productivity engines.** Welcomme and Hagborg (1977) found that flooded area was the most significant factor influencing annual fish growth, with fish achieving 75% of annual growth during the prime season. Flooded forests also serve as gene pools and feeding grounds, and over half of the 200 mammal species in the Amazonian lowlands use floodplains at some point in the year (Goulding et al., 1996).

3. **Logging threatens floodplain fisheries.** Logging of floodplain habitat is considered a major cause of Amazonian fishery decline. Few floodplain reserves exist; there are none in the lower 2500 km of the Amazon. The [[Mamiraua Ecological Reserve]] (11 000 km², established 1990) is the first upstream reserve, with at least one-third flooded forest.

4. **Estuaries link the whole river network.** The fresh/salt interface moves over 200 km in the Amazon seasonally. Many freshwater shrimp use estuarine habitats as larval nurseries. The lower Amazon estuary is the largest and most productive fishery in the basin.

5. **Catfish harvests require management.** [[Barthem and Goulding Catfish Model]] shows that dourada (*Brachyplatystoma flavicans*) and piramutaba (*B. vaillantii*) depend on estuarine nursery habitat and are vulnerable to overharvesting. The authors recommend fewer industrial boats and larger net mesh sizes.

6. **Mangroves are critical but under-protected.** About 240 × 10³ km² of mangroves occur in tropical deltas and estuaries. The [[Matang Mangrove Forest Reserve]] in Malaysia uses a 30-year cutting cycle with 3 m buffer strips and is cited as a model of sustained-yield management.

7. **Agrochemicals pose high risk.** Tropical aquatic systems show higher biological uptake, oxygen depletion and nutrient effects, and lower toxicity thresholds. Banana plantation drainage often feeds directly into rivers. Organochlorine pesticides banned in Costa Rica since 1990 were still imported, with 32 000 kg recorded in 1993. Gold mining adds mercury pollution.

8. **Hydropower risks are underestimated.** Only 2% of hydropower potential in humid African and Asian countries and 7% in Latin America had been developed, per a 1984 World Bank study. Seventy-nine dams were planned or existing in the Brazilian Amazon.
   - At [[Brokopondo Reservoir]] in Suriname, 1500 km² of rainforest was flooded. Decomposition caused severe deoxygenation and hydrogen sulphide emissions, and oxygen levels recovered only about 110 km downstream.
   - At the [[Tucuruí Dam]] and [[Balbina Dam]], Eletronorte cleared less than one-fifth of a 2250 km² area and 2% of 3150 km² respectively, despite legal requirements.
   - Decomposition of vegetation in the [[Yacyretá Dam]] reservoir is believed to have killed more than 120 000 fish after turbine testing in 1994.

9. **Fish passes are often mismatched.** The Yacyretá fish elevators (over US$30 million) were modeled on Columbia River salmon passage, yet many Parana River species are potamodromous and migrate repeatedly. Temperate fish-pass designs are often impassable for native tropical fish.

10. **Invasive plants follow impoundment.** Over half of the Brokopondo Reservoir was covered by [[Water Hyacinth]] two years after filling. Control with 2,4-D aerial spraying also poisoned other plants and animals.

11. **Interbasin transfers carry biological risks.** Transfers could spread competition, predation and parasitic diseases among previously isolated faunas. Amazon-Orinoco-Plata linkages have been considered for two centuries.

12. **Irrigation is expanding and dewatering streams.** Irrigated rice area increased in Indonesia from about 4 × 10⁶ ha (1968) to nearly 5.5 × 10⁶ ha (1980), in the Philippines from 740 000 ha to 1.2 × 10⁶ ha, and in Thailand from 1.8 to 2.6 × 10⁶ ha over the same period.

13. **Priority research gaps remain large.** The chapter calls for basic biology of tropical aquatic organisms, pre-disturbance baselines, understanding of nutrient limitation, and studies of low-level pesticide mixtures. It also recommends headwater-to-mouth river surveys before dam construction, and notes that the baseline for tropical streams may still be collected in some systems.

14. **Governance frameworks offer guidance.** [[Agenda 21]] included a chapter on integrated freshwater management, and the [[Code Of Conduct For Responsible Fisheries]] addresses inland fisheries and aquaculture (FAO, 1995). The chapter argues that protecting and enforcing legislation is foundational in temperate countries as well, given the United States spends about 2.4% of GNP ($150 billion/year) on environmental protection.
