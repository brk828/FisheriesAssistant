# Growth and Mortality of Sockeye Salmon During Their Last Two Years at Sea

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

W. E. Ricker (Fisheries Research Board of Canada, Nanaimo Station) compares the ocean growth and natural mortality of [[Sockeye Salmon]] during their final two years at sea. Growth rates are back-calculated from scale annuli for stocks at [[Cultus Lake]] and [[Karluk Lake]]. Instantaneous growth rates of 0.081 to 0.156 per month exceed a maximum estimate of natural mortality (0.038 per month) for the same period. The paper concludes that [[High-Seas Fishing]] before maturity forfeits most of the potential yield. Capture the year before maturity retains only about 31% to 50% of the potential catch, while capture in the final year still causes losses of roughly 6% to 38%.

## Background & Description

Sockeye are anadromous and are classified by the [[Gilbert System]], which records freshwater growing seasons (subscript) and ocean growing seasons. A "3-ocean" fish spends three growing seasons at sea before returning to spawn. The paper defines three ocean growth-years, separated by scale annuli (the "winter ring" of closely spaced circuli, see [[Scale Annuli]]):

- **First ocean growth-year:** from smolt departure to the first ocean annulus, usually slightly under 12 months.
- **Penultimate growth-year:** from the first to the second ocean annulus, about 12 months.
- **Final growth-year:** from the last annulus to estuarine entry, often only 1 to 5 months.

Growth was calculated from scales collected by [[Foerster Cultus Study|Foerster]] (Cultus Lake, 1925–27 maturing years) and [[Barnaby Karluk Study|Barnaby]] (Karluk Lake, 1931). Weights were computed from lengths using a length–weight relationship derived from Cultus sockeye. Dominant types were Cultus 4₂ (3-ocean), Karluk 5₃ and Karluk 6₃ (3-ocean). Minor types, including [[Jack Sockeye]] (2-ocean), Cultus 5₂, and 4-ocean fish, were also considered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mortality estimates.** Ocean natural mortality was compiled for six stocks: Karluk, Cultus, [[Babine Lake]], [[Chilko Lake]], [[Bare Lake]], and [[Lake Dalnee]]. The author treats the Karluk 1926–33 value as a maximum estimate for the last two growth-years, since it is likely inflated by early-ocean mortality. Total ocean losses ranged from about 64% to 95% of smolts, or 3.2% to 9.6% per month. Mortality declined as smolt length increased.

Key comparisons:

| Stock / smolt | Length (mm) | Total ocean mortality | Instantaneous monthly rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Karluk 3-freshwater | 143 | 65.8% | 0.0382 |
| Karluk 2-freshwater | 132 | 72.6% | 0.0407 |
| Cultus (1930–31) | 99 | 92.7% | 0.0935 |
| Babine (1955–58) | 82 | 95.1% | 0.1007 |
| Chilko (1951–55) | 76 | 91.0% | 0.0893 |

**Marking mortality.** Karluk and Cultus returns used fin-clipped smolts. Cultus experiments gave a marked-to-unmarked instantaneous mortality ratio of 1.27, and the Babine comparison gave 1.33. The Karluk mortality rates were adjusted by dividing by 1.27.

**Sensitivity.** Varying the marking adjustment (no adjustment to a ratio of 1.33) or the annulus date (March 1 vs. April 1) changed high-seas loss estimates only modestly. Parker's (1962) estimate of 0.0267 per month would have increased the potential yield lost to fishing, but not proportionally.

## Key Findings

### Growth during the last two ocean years

- Penultimate-year instantaneous growth rates in weight for 3-ocean fish: 0.0812 to 0.1199 per month, equal to 8.4% to 12.8% monthly weight increase (Table I).
- Final-year rates: 0.279 to 0.507 per month for Cultus and Karluk types. Karluk 5₃ reached 0.359 per month, and Karluk 6₃ reached 0.279 per month (Table II).
- Final-year monthly weight increases were 10.5% to 16.9%, reflecting only months of rapid growth.

### Growth minus natural mortality

- Using a maximum natural mortality rate of 0.0382 per month, minimum net increase in bulk over the penultimate year was 67% to 138% per year (4.4% to 7.5% per month) (Table V).
- Minimum net increase in the final year was 6.6% to 12.6% per month (Table VI).
- Growth exceeded the maximum mortality estimate in every case, so each growth type increased in bulk during its last two years at sea.

### Yield lost to high-seas fishing

**Capture on April 1 of the year before maturity** (Cultus 4₂, Karluk 5₃, Karluk 6₃):
- Maximum harvest: 31%, 28%, and 43% of potential yield, respectively.
- Wasted: 69%, 72%, and 57%.

**Capture on July 1 of the year before maturity:**
- Maximum harvest: 40% (Cultus 4₂), 35% (Karluk 5₃), and 50% (Karluk 6₄).
- Waste: 60%, 65%, and 50%.

**Capture during the final growth-year:**
- One month before reaching shore: 6.2% to 11.2% of the possible catch lost.
- Four months before reaching shore: 22.6% to 37.6% lost (Table VII).

**[[Jack Sockeye]] (2-ocean):** the final-year loss was 15% for capture 1 month early and 48% for 4 months early (Cultus). Karluk jacks lost 13% to 14% (1 month) and 42% to 47% (4 months).

**4-ocean fish:** Karluk 6₄ fish showed almost no growth in the final year, so early capture could increase yield. Cultus 6₄ fish were about even between growth and mortality. Losses from capture in the year before maturity were substantial but smaller than for 3-ocean fish.

### Distribution of mortality

- Mortality is concentrated early in ocean life. Within Cultus, 63 mm smolts had 1.2% survival, versus 14.7% for 107 mm smolts (Table IV).
- Babine smolts (82 mm) vs. Karluk smolts (143 mm) differ by an instantaneous rate of about 1.95, roughly 86% actual mortality.

### Conclusions

- High-seas fishing that takes any substantial fraction of non-maturing sockeye "must markedly decrease the total yield" from the affected stocks.
- Changes in natural mortality near maturity (for example, predation by seals and sea lions) would shift loss estimates between maturing and non-maturing fish but cannot be ignored.
- The author recommends targeted studies on the timing of annulus formation, the end of final-year growth, and stock-specific mortality distributions.

### Notable case: Babine rock slide (1951)

A rock slide on the Babine River passed only about one-third of the sockeye. Marked (fin-clipped) fish passed at 33% of the rate of unmarked fish, implying a continuing handicap of missing fins in difficult passage conditions. This supports the interpretation that marking reduces survival throughout life, not just immediately after marking.
