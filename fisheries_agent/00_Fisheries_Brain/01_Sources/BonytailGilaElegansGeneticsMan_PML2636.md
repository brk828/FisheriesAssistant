# Bonytail (Gila elegans) Genetics Management and Captive Propagation Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service plan, prepared at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center, sets out how the endangered [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) broodstock should be maintained and propagated. The species is nearly extinct in the wild. Its [[Recovery Goals]] (2002) call for captive propagation to prevent extinction and to establish self-sustaining populations. The plan's central aim is to preserve the genetic diversity of the captive founder population, which descends from only ten wild-caught adults, and to prevent genetic risks from [[Hatchery Domestication]], inbreeding and outbreeding. It proposes microsatellite-based monitoring, pedigree tracking through PIT tagging, and breeding rules that exclude related pairs.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Gila elegans*, a cyprinid of the Colorado River system. The document notes that few specimens have been collected in recent decades, so historical densities and genetic structure cannot be predicted.
- **Founders:** Ten wild adults captured in the late 1970s and 1980 were pair-mated in 1981 ([[Hamman 1982]]; [[Hamman 1985]]). Their progeny form the 1981 broodstock, described as the entire available genetic reserve of the species. Wild fish found between 1976 and 1980 were the only founders available.
- **Early rearing:** In 1981, wild bonytail were artificially spawned, and progeny were reared to fingerling size at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] on the Colorado River below Hoover Dam.
- **Broodstock history:** Dexter began building a new broodstock in 1999 because the 1981 stock was aging and attrition had been high over the previous five years. Fry were reared in separate family lots, PIT tagged once they exceeded 100 mm, and randomly sampled (90 individuals) once the smallest member reached at least 90 mm, to avoid artificial selection.
- **Genetic assessment:** A 2002 assessment reconstructed the five original family lots. Some lineage sorting had occurred, but representatives of all ten founders remained, although some family lines had few survivors.
- **Management framework:** The plan is guided by the [[Bonytail Recovery Plan]] (1990), the Bonytail Recovery Goals (2002), the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] genetics guidelines (1994) and the [[Multi-Species Conservation Program]] conservation plan (2001).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Recovery units:** The Bonytail Recovery Goals divide Colorado River basin management into an upper and a lower basin. The lower basin unit, centered on [[Lake Mohave]], is the focus of this plan. Mainstem Colorado River dam construction in the twentieth century dramatically altered the system.
- **Historical data:** Five individuals collected in the 1980s from the Colorado River below the Green River confluence are the only records noted (Valdez and Williams 1993). Whether any population was genetically isolated from the mainstem is unknown.
- **Captive habitat:** Dexter's broodstock has been held in outdoor ponds for more than twenty years. These ponds approximate the backwater habitat considered most likely preferred by bonytail (Valdez and Williams 1993). Natural pond features include aquatic insects, diurnal temperature and oxygen fluctuation, and aquatic vegetation. Terrestrial and avian predators are present. Piscivores are the only missing component, and Dexter is developing a plan to imprint juveniles on a piscivorous surrogate.
- **Hybridization threat:** Hybridization with closely related [[Gila]] species, especially [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]], is a concern. Cataract Canyon and the upper basin are identified as areas where putative hybrids may occur.
- **Stocking history:** Between 1981 and 2000, Dexter propagated and stocked about 326,365 fish total into Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu, mostly as fingerlings (Table 3). The largest single stocking listed is 85,232 fish in 2000 (Lake Mohave).

## Key Findings

1. **Genetic reserve:** The 1981 founder population derives from ten wild adults. Remaining F1 progeny represent the only available genetic reserve, so every management step must protect allelic diversity.
2. **Recovery criteria:** Recovery is achieved when four self-perpetuating populations are established and stable (two in the upper basin, two in the lower basin), and a genetic refuge is maintained in the lower basin.
3. **Broodstock target:** Establish a new captive broodstock of at least 3,000 adults with equal representation of the five original family lots.
4. **Marking and pedigree:** All broodfish are PIT tagged. A pedigree database with microsatellite genotypes is the baseline for all facilities. All future broodstock are PIT tagged, and only non-sib matings are used for production.
5. **Breeding rules:** Relatedness matrices are used to prohibit pairs with high genetic similarity. Spawning uses two-female by two-male designs in separate bowls to limit family contributions. Stocking constraints include a maximum of 5,000 fish from any one parent pair for a single stocking location and 20 pairs minimum when more than 100,000 fry are needed.
6. **Monitoring:** Microsatellite markers developed from the bonytail genome (Keeler-Foster et al. 2004) are used for annual inventory and genetic monitoring. Production fish are sampled at 5% per year to compare observed with expected allele frequencies. Marker development includes 30 initial loci, of which 11 were selected to screen broodfish. Three loci were diagnostic between humpback and bonytail in an initial screen of ten humpback chubs.
7. **Hybrid research:** Hybrid humpback × bonytail family lots were created in 2003 to verify markers and produce a field morphometric sheet. Roundtail chub marker development was planned for July 2006.
8. **Fecundity data:** Fecundity values from 1981 to 2003 paired matings show considerable variation in egg counts across years (for example, 1,065 to 17,484 eggs per female in the table excerpt), indicating variable reproductive output among broodfish.
9. **Inbreeding and outbreeding:** Inbreeding is only a problem if it causes inbreeding depression. Outbreeding depression is also a concern, particularly in hybrid breakdown. Because the 1981 parents were likely from different populations and historic divergence in Lake Mohave has been lost, outbreeding risk is considered a lower concern than inbreeding.
10. **Long-term objectives:** Seven genetic management objectives include conserving diversity (Objective 1), a genetic database (Objective 6), long-term genetic integrity monitoring (Objective 7), and targets from 2003 through 2017, with monitoring of allele frequencies and a goal of retaining 90% of genetic variation as a short-term target (Lynch 1996).
