# Growth of Brook Trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) and Brown Trout (*Salmo trutta*) in the Pigeon River, Otsego County, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 study by [[Edwin L. Cooper]], published by the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]], estimates the growth and age structure of wild [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] in the [[Pigeon River]] research area. Growth was calculated from scale measurements using the Monastyrsky method, based on 4,439 brook trout and 1,429 brown trout older than one year. The author concludes that angling selectively removes fast-growing brook trout, that brook trout populations are short-lived and heavily exploited, and that current minimum-size regulations may be driving wild stocks toward slow growth.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The [[Pigeon River Trout Research Area]] was established in April 1949. It covers 4.8 miles of trout stream divided into four experimental sections, plus seven small lakes. Fishing is by daily permit, which allows a complete creel census by trained fisheries workers.
- **Sampling period:** Collections ran from April 20, 1949, to November 30, 1951.
- **Sampling methods:** Fish were taken by [[Hook and Line Angling]] and by [[Electric Shocking]]. Electric shocking captures a higher proportion of large fish, and angling is limited by a 7-inch minimum-size limit.
- **Sample sizes:** 4,439 brook trout and 1,429 brown trout older than one year.
- **Age determination:** Age was read from scales ([[Scale Aging]]). For brook trout, earlier work (Cooper, 1951) validated the method against fish of known age, and agreement was reported in all cases. For brown trout, annuli are distinct and the method was validated through the first two annuli. In this river, feeding, growth, and annulus formation resume in late April or early May, depending on water temperature.
- **Growth calculation:** The [[Monastyrsky Method]] was used, with nomographs patterned after Hile (1950). Scales begin forming at about 40 mm total length, and the method fits well from the first year onward.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Population structure:** Very few old fish occur in either species. Few trout reach age IV, and over 95 per cent of the angler catch is less than three years old.
- **Brook trout exploitation:** Three brook trout are caught by anglers for every one of legal size left at the end of the season. The population is short-lived compared with populations in Maine and Manitoba, although the cause is unknown because Michigan lacks age data from unfished populations.
- **Brown trout persistence:** Brown trout are less vulnerable to exploitation and contain more old, large fish. Their reported maximum life span is 12 to 15 years in Norway (Dahl, 1918), versus about 10 years for brook trout.
- **Comparative growth:** Body-scale relationships for brook trout were best described by Monastyrsky formulas across five Michigan populations, with minor differences among them. Direct-proportion methods could produce growth errors of up to 78 per cent.
- **Comparative fisheries:** Selective angling of grayling in Lake Storsjö, Sweden, was effective up to the third year of life (Gustafson, 1949), with fast-growing young fish captured first.

## Key Findings

1. **Scale-length relationships:**
   - Brook trout (582 fish, 2.5 to 8.0 inches): log ASR = 0.99217 + 0.8723 log L.
   - Brown trout (1,291 fish, 2.5 to 23.7 inches): log ASR = 1.253877 + 0.8968 log L.
2. **Age structure:** Brook trout (1,829 by hook and line; 2,610 by shocker) and brown trout (633 and 796) show very few fish older than age group II or III. Brook trout older than age IV were nearly absent.
3. **Selective angling:** Hook-and-line fishing selects faster-growing brook trout within each age group, as shown in the [[Gangle Lake]] data (Montmorency County, Michigan). Brown trout show no consistent selectivity in the Pigeon River, and their calculated lengths from the two methods differ only slightly, probably not significantly.
4. **[[Lee's Phenomenon]]:** Calculated early-year lengths decline as the age of the sample increases. This appears clearly in brook trout, both across age groups and across monthly samples from the same year class (1948 year class, sampled July 1949 to September 1950). Brown trout show it to a much smaller extent, which the author attributes to lower exploitation.
5. **[[Growth Compensation]]:** Slow-growing yearlings grow faster in later years than fast-growing yearlings, in both species. However, compensation does not offset the initial difference, so large yearlings remain larger through age III. The age structure prevents analysis beyond three years.
6. **Interspecies comparison:** Brook and brown trout appear to grow at similar rates at annulus 1 of the first individuals caught. By age 2, brown trout grow much faster. The author attributes most of this difference to selective harvest of fast-growing brook trout rather than to a true species difference.
7. **Bias limits:** Once brook trout reach a size vulnerable to angling, no unbiased growth-rate estimate can be obtained from exploited populations. Michigan has not yet produced growth data from wild brook trout populations closed to angling. Calculations to the first annulus are also biased by the 7-inch minimum-size limit.
8. **Management implications:** A low minimum-size limit removes the best-growing fish while they are small and favors slow-growing runts as spawning stock. Under current laws, the author argues, wild stocks are being selected for slow growth through excessive removal at too small a size.
