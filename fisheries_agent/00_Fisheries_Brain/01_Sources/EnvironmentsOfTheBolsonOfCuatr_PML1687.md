# Environments of the Bolsón of Cuatro Ciénegas, Coahuila, Mexico: With Special Reference to the Aquatic Biota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 monograph (Texas Western Press Science Series No. 2, by W. L. Minckley) provides a comprehensive synthesis of the physiography, climate, vegetation, aquatic habitats, geology, drainage history and aquatic fauna of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in northern [[Coahuila]], Mexico. The basin is an arid desert valley (floor about 740 m a.s.l.) whose aquatic systems, sustained by thermal and cool springs, support a remarkably diverse and highly endemic biota, including [[Endemic Snails]], [[Endemic Isopods]], and several endemic fishes. The author argues that the basin is an urgent priority for study, salvage and protection because its isolated springs are disappearing under human development.

## Background & Description

The study is based on field work from 1958 onward, with intensive studies in 1960 and additional work through summer 1968. Minckley notes that the author's own data are distinguished from those of other workers, and he explicitly invites corrections. The document includes a gazetteer keyed to a 2.5 km grid (Fig. 27), a sketch map of Coahuila (Fig. 1), and a long index of illustrations.

The basin measures about 40 km east to west and 30 km north to south, with an extension (Cañón de las Peñas) reaching roughly 75 km south-southeast. It is bounded by the [[Sierra de San Marcos]], [[Sierra de la Fragua]], [[Sierra de Anteojo]] (Sierra de la Madera), and other ranges. Mountain peaks rise above 3,000 m, while surrounding ranges reach 1,000 to 1,900 m. Water and sediments are shaped by [[Bajada]] fans, [[Barrial]] (basin floor) and extensive gypsum dunes.

The climate is arid. Mean annual precipitation is roughly 200 mm, with a mean of about 20 mm in the driest month and rainfall bursts exceeding 30 mm in four hours. Winter ice occurs in desert pools far from springs, and summer air temperatures reach 33 °C in the shade.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic habitats.** Surface waters range from ephemeral playa flooding to permanent lakes, rivers, marshes and springs:

- **Rivers and creeks:** The [[Rio Mesquites]] (originally Rio San Marcos) rises from a thermal rheocrene near Tierra Blanca (31 °C maximum, 16.3 °C minimum recorded), is stable (less than 10 cm water level fluctuation in more than 30 visits), and carries an estimated 1.3 to 1.5 m³/s. The [[Rio Puente Chiquito]] is highly unstable, at times dry. The [[Rio Cañón]] flows from Cañón del Agua and is the town water supply, with carbonate-rich water.
- **Lagunas, posos and marshes:** A successional series runs from newly foundered pits (posos) to spring-fed lagunas and finally to marshes. Some posos are sinkholes linked by subterranean channels. Plankton was noted in only three of over a hundred posos visited.
- **Barrial lakes and playas:** [[Laguna Grande]] (4 to 5 g/L total dissolved solids, depth under 1 m) is one of the few such lakes retaining fauna other than brine shrimp (Artemia). Cut-off shore pools may exceed 400 g/L and are azoic.
- **Canals:** Canals have greatly increased aquatic habitat, and the [[New Canal]] (built 1958–1962) is the largest, 8 to 10 m wide at the top.

**Hydrogeology and climate interactions.** Waters rise along fracture zones associated with mountain structures. Desert evaporation drives salt and travertine deposition, which can progressively roof over posos and channels.

**Vegetation.** Dominant plants include mesquite (Prosopis), creosote bush, tarbush, ocotillo, chopped *Allenrolfea* and *Distichlis* on saline soils, and marsh sedges (*Eleocharis*, *Scirpus*) with *Typha* in deeper water. Riparian trees include ash, willow, pecan and sycamore.

**Geology.** The basin lies on Mesozoic strata over marine Permian sediments. The region was affected by the Laramide Revolution (late Cretaceous to early Tertiary) and Tertiary uplift. Multiple connections to the [[Rio Grande]] system are inferred from past pluvial lakes and drainage captures.

**Drainage.** The author recognises seven major epigean drainages, including the isolated Laguna Churince–Laguna Grande system, the Rio Mesquites system (the largest integrated system), the Rio Puente Chiquito, and the Rio Salado de los Nadadores (Rio Grande).

**Aquatic fauna summary.** Only some groups have been studied. Seven crustacean taxa are known from permanent waters, with four endemic isopods. Aquatic molluscs include about 18 forms, with 12 new endemic species in five new genera (Table 2 lists the species). Fishes are the most intensively studied group.

## Key Findings

- **Fish fauna:** Nine families, 14 genera and 20 species of fishes (plus one unconfirmed possible introduction) occur in or adjacent to the valley. Nine of the 18 species present inside the basin are endemic. For comparison, the Bonneville system has 5 families, 11 genera and 21 species, and Death Valley has 3, 5 and 10. Endemism in those systems is 62%, 69% and 90% respectively.
- **Mexican tetra (*Astyanax fasciatus mexicanus*):** Abundant mid-water fish in deep lagunas (schools reaching 10 m depth) and in rivers and canals.
- **Mosquitofishes:** [[Gambusia marshi]] is perhaps the most abundant fish, with dark and spotted colour phases; mate selection by males was documented (Arnold, unpublished). [[Gambusia longispinis]] is an endemic, secretive species living in dense vegetation, largely allopatric with *G. marshi*.
- **Platyfish:** *Xiphophorus gordoni* occurs in only three adjacent lagunas and a few posos in the southeast lobe.
- **Pupfishes:** [[Cyprinodon atrorus]] lives in variable habitats, while [[Cyprinodon bifasciatus]] is restricted to limnocrenes north of Sierra de San Marcos and is thermally sensitive. Canalization allowed hybridization, with hybrid swarms sporadically occurring. The canal segment in which hybrid populations were studied was dried in 1967.
- **Endemic killifish:** [[Lucania interioris]] is a small, secretive fish that buries itself in the substrate.
- **Cichlids:** Three undescribed endemic *Cichlasoma* species show trophic specialisation (snail-crushing, detritus-feeding, piscivorous forms) paralleling African Great Lakes cichlids. They co-occur with the Rio Grande cichlid (*C. cyanoguttatum*).
- **Other fishes:** Introduced carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) and centrarchids were noted; the largemouth bass is the top carnivore in larger open waters. A single darter (*Etheostoma* sp.) was found in the Rio Mesquites system. Gray redhorse (*Moxostoma congestum*) occurs immediately east of the basin.
- **Crustaceans:** Four endemic isopods, including *Speocirolana thermydronis*, and a new genus of blood-red asellid (Stenasellinae, an African–Eurasian group new to the Western Hemisphere). The amphipod *Hyalella azteca* and the shrimp *Palaemonetes suttkusi* are widespread.
- **Molluscs:** Taylor (1966) described highly endemic snails, including *Mexipyrgus* species, that may have diverged over 2 to 9 million years.
- **Conservation conclusion:** The loss of isolated spring systems is more serious than the drying of tributaries, because it can eliminate a species' entire gene pool. Rapid study, salvage and protection are urged.
- **Basin history:** Isolation was intermittent, with past pluvial lakes and drainage connections to the Rio Grande. Ancient faunal elements are attributed to geologic stability, while more recent faunal additions are linked to human activity.
