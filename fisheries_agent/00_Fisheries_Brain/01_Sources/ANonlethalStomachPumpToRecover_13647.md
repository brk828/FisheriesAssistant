# Non-Lethal Stomach Pump Feasibility Study for Humpback Chub

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #methodology

## Executive Summary

This feasibility study proposal evaluates non-lethal stomach pumping techniques for assessing food habits of [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the [[Grand Canyon]]. The study addresses concerns about potential damage to pharyngeal teeth during stomach content recovery. Using surrogate species including hatchery-reared [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and wild [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), the research will determine: (1) whether damage occurs to pharyngeal teeth of processed chubs, and (2) efficiency of stomach flushing. The study involves laboratory experiments at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] with 90 bonytail chubs divided into three test groups, plus field studies on wild roundtail chub and rainbow trout. A pilot study in January 1991 with eight wild humpback chub showed stomach and intestinal contents flushed through the anal vent with no adverse effects.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is an endangered cyprinid requiring non-lethal sampling methods for diet studies in the [[Grand Canyon]]. Previous research has demonstrated stomach pumping effectiveness on various fish families including salmonids, centrarchids, ictalurids, percids, and esocids without injury. Meehan and Miller (1978) found no significant survival difference between treated and control juvenile salmonids after 30 days. Gengerke et al. (1973) reported pumping 864 stomachs of nine species with no mortality. However, the technique has rarely been used on cyprinids, raising concerns about potential damage to the cyprinid pharyngeal mill during tube insertion.

The custom stomach pump consists of flexible clear plastic tubing and a hand-held rubber bulb pump. The tube is inserted into the esophagus and water is pumped into the stomach. Fish are positioned head-down to allow contents to flush from the mouth into a container. Fish are mildly anesthetized with MS-222 before processing.

The pilot study revealed that the S-shaped intestinal system and lack of distinct pyloric sphincter in [[Colorado River]] *Gila* species may cause contents to flush through the anal vent rather than the buccal cavity, requiring evaluation of this alternative flushing route.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses critical needs for [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] to assess food habits of endangered [[Humpback Chub]] populations in the [[Grand Canyon]] without lethal sampling. The research involves multiple habitats and species:

**Laboratory Setting**: [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico maintains [[Bonytail Chub]] in outdoor earthen ponds and 12-foot diameter circular holding tanks with constant flow at 17.6°C. Test fish weigh 2-2.5 pounds and measure 300-500 mm total length.

**Field Settings**: Wild [[Roundtail Chub]] will be sampled from the [[Dolores River]] in Colorado and Utah using trammel nets and electrofishing. Rainbow trout and carp will be sampled from the [[Colorado River]] in the [[Grand Canyon]].

**Diet Components**: Test diets include commercial trout pellets and *Cladophora* (filamentous algae), representing natural food items found in these river systems.

## Key Findings

**Study Design and Timeline**:
- Laboratory experiments scheduled for last week of March and first week of April 1991 at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]
- Field studies in [[Dolores River]] April 4-12, 1991
- Field studies in [[Grand Canyon]] during 1991
- 14-day post-treatment monitoring period for behavior and survival assessment

**Test Groups**:
- **Group I (Regular-Feed)**: 30 bonytail chubs fed trout pellets to satiation after 1-day starvation, pumped 1-2 hours post-feeding. Ten fish euthanized immediately to measure unflushed contents and examine pharyngeal teeth. Twenty fish held 14 days for survival monitoring.
- **Group II (Cladophora Force-Feed)**: 30 bonytail chubs force-fed standard volume of *Cladophora* after 24-hour starvation. Ten fish pumped at each of three time intervals (1 h, 4 h, 8 h) to assess flushing efficiency. All fish held 14 days post-treatment.
- **Group III (Control)**: 30 bonytail chubs anesthetized and handled identically but not pumped. Five fish euthanized for pharyngeal mill comparison. Twenty-five fish held 14 days.
- **Group IV (Wild Roundtail)**: Up to 30 wild roundtail chubs from [[Dolores River]] captured by trammel net or electrofishing, pumped, euthanized, and examined. Up to 10 control fish euthanized immediately after capture.
- **Group V (Rainbow Trout)**: 20-30 rainbow trout and carp from [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]] pumped and euthanized for stomach content comparison.

**Analytical Methods**:
- Flushing efficiency calculated as percentage of contents recovered by volume using water displacement in graduated cylinders
- Chi-square test for stomach evacuation efficiency against expected 100% recovery
- One-way ANOVA to compare *Cladophora* flushing efficiency across three time treatments
- Visual examination and photography of pharyngeal teeth and gill arches for damage assessment
- All euthanized specimens labeled, frozen, and transferred to [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]

**Preliminary Results**: January 1991 pilot study with eight wild [[Humpback Chub]] demonstrated majority of stomach and intestinal contents flushed through anal vent with no adverse effects observed in test fish.
