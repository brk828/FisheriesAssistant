# Genetic Structure of Three Species of Endangered Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final project report, prepared by Anthony A. Echelle, Alice F. Echelle, and D. R. Edds (Oklahoma State University, Dept. of Zoology), compares the genetic structure of captive populations held at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH) with natural populations of three endangered species: the [[Leon Springs Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon bovinus*), the [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon elegans*), and the [[Pecos Gambusia]] (*Gambusia nobilis*). Using horizontal starch gel [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]], the study found that DNFH stocks show statistically significant but minor divergence from their progenitor populations, mainly in allelic frequencies, with no loss of heterozygosity. Part B extends the work with a [[Morphological Differentiation]] and electrophoretic analysis of *G. nobilis* populations, identifying the [[Toyah Creek]] populations as the most divergent and genetically valuable. The report concludes with management recommendations for mixed, periodically replenished hatchery stocks drawn from multiple habitats.

## Background & Description

**Study species**

- **Leon Springs Pupfish (*Cyprinodon bovinus*)**: Discovered in 1851 by personnel of the U.S.-Mexico Boundary Survey. The type locality at Leon Springs was extirpated after flow declined to zero in the 1940s. The species was rediscovered in Leon Creek and now occurs only in Diamond-Y Spring and the lower portion of Leon Creek, 16 km north of Fort Stockton, Pecos County, Texas.
- **Comanche Springs Pupfish (*Cyprinodon elegans*)**: Also discovered in 1851. The type locality at Comanche Springs, Fort Stockton, has gone dry. The species now occurs in interconnected springs near Balmorhea (Reeves and Jeff Davis counties, Texas) and in nearby Toyah Creek. Two morphs are recognized: a streamlined form (Phantom Lake Spring) and a deep-bodied form (Toyah Creek), with Giffin Spring intermediate.
- **Pecos Gambusia (*Gambusia nobilis*)**: A live-bearing poeciliid endemic to the Pecos River drainage, federally listed as endangered. It occurs in four areas: Leon Creek near Fort Stockton, the spring-fed irrigation system near Balmorhea, Blue Spring (Eddy County, New Mexico), and [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]] (Chaves County, New Mexico). The species once occurred in three additional Pecos County springs that have since gone dry.

**Hatchery context**

DNFH maintains populations of these fishes for reintroduction and serves as a gene bank. Present practice is to take one sample of fewer than 50 live fish from a single site and transplant them into 0.1- to 0.2-hectare hatchery ponds.

**Study methods**

Samples were collected by seine 12-18 August 1982. Tissues (eye-brain, liver, epaxial muscle) were analyzed by horizontal starch gel electrophoresis across 24-28 presumptive loci. Analysis used BIOSYS-1, chi-square tests for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium and heterogeneity, and Rogers' (1972) genetic distance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Factors driving genetic change in captivity**

The report identifies four interacting factors: the [[Founder Effect]], [[Inbreeding]], [[Genetic Drift]], and altered selection pressures. Founder populations at DNFH are started from small numbers (fewer than 50 individuals), which can create genetic bottlenecks. Effective population size is likely smaller than the number stocked because of unequal sex ratios, territoriality, overlapping generations, and differential fecundity. The hatchery ponds are ecologically uniform and differ from the spring pools, spring runs, and marshes of the native habitats, which may shift selection pressures.

**Threats**

1. Habitat destruction
2. Competition with introduced species
3. Degradation of genetic integrity through hybridization with introduced congeners (notably *Cyprinodon variegatus* in Leon Creek, first recorded in August 1974)
4. Rarity and restricted natural ranges

**Habitat and water chemistry**

- Leon Creek springs typically have high levels of chlorides, silica, and sulphates.
- Phantom Lake Spring populations of *G. nobilis* live in freshwater springs (less than 3 ppt total dissolved solids). Diamond-Y Spring and sinkhole 20 at Bitter Lake are more saline (10-15 ppt).
- Irrigation canals, sluice gates, and well pumpage have fragmented populations and reduced flow, particularly at Phantom Lake Spring.

**Genetic theory invoked**

The report cites [[Heterozygosity]] as linked to adaptive capacity, thermal tolerance, reproductive rate, and survivorship, and notes that microgeographic subdivision is common in fish populations.

## Key Findings

**Part A: Captive versus natural populations**

- **Cyprinodon bovinus**: 28 loci were examined, and 5 were polymorphic at the 0.95 criterion. Mean heterozygosity (direct count) was 0.041 in the DNFH sample (n = 51) and 0.041-0.055 across natural samples. The DNFH sample lacked allele Phi-A-a (natural frequency 0.000-0.078), but the chance of missing it at a frequency of 0.018 in a sample of 51 was 0.16. No locus deviated significantly from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. Genetic distance (Rogers' D) ranged from 0.003 to 0.015 among natural samples and from 0.008 to 0.016 between natural samples and DNFH.
- **Cyprinodon elegans**: 6 of 28 loci were polymorphic. The DNFH sample (n = 46 for most loci) lacked allele Ck-C-a, found at frequency 0.042 in the progenitor GIFL sample. The chance of missing it in a sample of 30 was 0.08. Significant heterozygote deficiency occurred at Adh in the DNFH sample. Genetic distance ranged from 0.014 to 0.050 among natural samples and from 0.013 to 0.055 between DNFH and natural samples. The DNFH sample was most similar to TOYA and GIFL (D = 0.013) and least similar to the Phantom Lake Spring populations (0.044-0.055).
- **Gambusia nobilis**: 24 loci were resolved in 211 samples, with 6 polymorphic. Percentage of polymorphic loci ranged from 0 to 16.7. Three alleles present in Leon Creek were absent from DNFH (Fum-a, Idh-A-c, Phi-B-b). The probability of missing all three by chance was 0.029, though omitting LC4 gave P = 0.56. Genetic distance between DNFH and Leon Creek samples ranged from 0.002 to 0.013.
- **Comparative divergence**: DNFH *C. bovinus* diverged from natural populations more than DNFH *G. nobilis* did (D = 0.008-0.016 versus 0.002-0.013; Mann-Whitney U = 27, P < 0.05).
- **Overall conclusion (Part A)**: Captive populations retain levels of genetic variability comparable to progenitor populations. Electrophoresis "almost certainly underestimates" the change that has occurred.

**Part B: Morphological and genetic differentiation in *Gambusia nobilis***

- 293 specimens from 12 localities (8 extant, 4 extinct) were analyzed by principal components analysis.
- Three morphological subsets were identified: (1) Blue Spring, with high gonopodial counts and short dorsal- and anal-fin bases; (2) Comanche Springs and the Balmorhea-area springs (Phantom Lake, Giffin, East Sandia), with high fin-ray counts and long fin bases; (3) Bitter Lake/Roswell and Leon Creek populations, intermediate between subsets 1 and 2.
- The Toyah Creek populations (Phantom Lake, Giffin, East Sandia) share three alleles absent from other extant populations (GPI-A-D, IDH-A-C, PGM-B-C). ADH-B is shared among all Texas populations.
- Phantom Lake Spring is the most divergent extant population (genetic distance to Giffin Spring D = 0.073 and to East Sandia Spring D = 0.095). Variability there is higher than in any other extant *G. nobilis* population, and this is attributed to historical isolation and irrigation barriers, not drift.
- Genetic distances between Phantom Lake Spring samples (D = 0.011-0.027) are an order of magnitude higher than other intra-spring comparisons.
- The authors conclude subspecific designations are unwarranted, given lack of geographic cohesiveness and evidence of ecotypic or ecophenotypic variation.

**Summary and management recommendations (Summary and Conclusions)**

1. Recommendations aim to maximize allelic diversity and genetic variability rather than match allele frequencies.
2. DNFH stocks differ significantly from progenitors, mainly through allelic frequency shifts, with possible loss of rare alleles (sample sizes 30-51).
3. Spatial heterogeneity is highest between *G. nobilis* and *C. elegans* populations in the Balmorhea complex. Lower heterogeneity occurs between partially isolated subpopulations.
4. A sample from a single location poorly represents species-wide genetic structure at the macrogeographic scale.
5. DNFH stocks should be mixed from several habitats and include fish from each major spring system or watercourse.
6. Effective population sizes at DNFH seem large enough to minimize inbreeding. The *C. elegans* Adh heterozygote deficiency is not yet explained.
7. Bottlenecks were likely brief and associated with founding events.
8. Present hatchery populations contain "several hundred" adults. This is above the "safe" level of 100 cited by Allendorf and Phelps (1980) and near the level of more than 500 recommended by Franklin (1980), but Soulé (1980) argues long-term potential may require much larger sizes.
9. Periodic infusions of wild individuals are recommended, with frequency and size set by logistical considerations, to maintain qualitative similarity with progenitor populations (following Kimura and Ohta 1971).
