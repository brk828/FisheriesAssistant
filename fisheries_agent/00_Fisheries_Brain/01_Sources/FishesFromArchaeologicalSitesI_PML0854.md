# Fishes From Archaeological Sites in Northern New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #lepisosteidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two 1961 papers from *The Southwestern Naturalist*. The first, by [[Frederick R. Gehlbach]] and [[Robert Rush Miller]], reports three fish taxa recovered from Pueblo Indian ruins in northern New Mexico: [[Longnose Gar]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Blue Sucker]]. The authors argue that these remains show the fishes were used as food and that the blue sucker and gar once lived in a river system that is now absent or reduced, implying a larger, clearer, and more stable Rio Grande than exists today. The second, by [[Clark Hubbs]] and [[Mantua V. Johnson]], compares egg complements of the [[Dusky Darter]] from Austin and San Marcos, Texas, and frames the differences within theories of [[Egg Number Versus Competition]]. The excerpt ends before the darter's numerical results are presented, so no egg counts or statistics are reported here.

## Background & Description

### Archaeological fish remains (Gehlbach and Miller)

Fish remains from abandoned pueblos can reveal the diet of past peoples and help reconstruct past environments and [[Zoogeography]]. The authors cite the use of the [[Colorado Squawfish]] by the Sobaipuri people of southern Arizona about 200 years ago as evidence that the [[San Pedro River]] was then larger and more stable. The material came from three localities:

- [[Bandelier National Monument]] (Rio Grande drainage, Sandoval County)
- [[Chaco Canyon]] and [[Aztec Ruins National Monument]] (San Juan River drainage, Colorado River system, San Juan County)

**Longnose gar (*Lepisosteus osseus*).** A partial left mandible (46 mm total length) from the general fill at Kin Kletso, Chaco Canyon, probably came from an individual more than two feet long. Twenty-five scales from Room 16 Fill at Kin Kletso are identified only to genus, although they may be *L. osseus*. Tree-ring dates for Kin Kletso range from 1076 to 1178 A.D. Gar are not part of the Colorado River fauna.

**Bonytail (*Gila robusta elegans*).** Two detached caudal vertebrae from P-15 Fill, Aztec Ruins, are associated with [[Mesa Verde Pottery]] dated to after 1225 to about 1275 A.D. The bonytail is distinguished from other large *Gila* by its neural and haemal spines, which run nearly parallel to the vertebral axis along the caudal peduncle (about vertebrae 34 to 46). The archaeological individual was estimated at nearly 14 inches standard length. The second vertebra may belong to the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. r. robusta*), but this is uncertain.

**Blue sucker (*Cycleptus elongatus*).** A complete [[Urohyal Bone]] and an incomplete [[Weberian Apparatus]] were recovered from Kiva One, Rainbow House (LA 217), Bandelier, associated with Kidder's Glaze IV pottery dated to about 1550 to 1600 A.D. The urohyal is unlike that of any other North American sucker, which the authors take as conclusive for *Cycleptus*.

### Egg complement study (Hubbs and Johnson)

The authors summarize theory that egg number and egg size trade off against competition (Svardson 1949; Lack 1954). Earlier work by Hubbs and Delco (1960) suggested that a closely related species can reduce intraspecific competition and raise egg complement. The Austin and San Marcos comparison tests whether egg number rises with lower intraspecific and higher interspecific competition. Samples came from the vicinity of Austin (Colorado River) and San Marcos; other Texas localities showed variation but lacked adequate samples.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Past stream conditions.** The Rainbow House blue sucker implies that the upper Rio Grande once offered a favorable niche for a species now absent from northern New Mexico. The authors infer that the river was then clearer, larger, and more stable than it has been in the past century ([[Miller 1961]]).
- **Habitat loss.** The authors propose that extensive siltation and reduced flow from river modification may have eliminated the blue sucker, as it appears to have done for the longnose gar and [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus platorynchus*), which was taken near Albuquerque in the early 1870s.
- **Chaco Canyon hydrology.** Bryan (1954) concluded that no permanent stream existed in Chaco Canyon during Pueblo occupation, so the gar were likely obtained from the Rio Grande basin.
- **Range limits.** The blue sucker does not occur upstream of the vicinity of [[Big Bend National Park]] on the Rio Grande. Its southern limits are the Rio Salado, Nuevo León, and Rio Conchos basins in Mexico. In New Mexico it was recorded only from the lower [[Pecos River]], where it is rare. A 1960 survey in White Rock Canyon found no evidence of it in the Bandelier region.
- **Barrier to migration.** Blue suckers ascend streams to spawn. The lower falls of the Rito de los Frijoles (about 40 feet high) likely blocked upstream movement from the Rio Grande.
- **Food value.** Suckers were a readily available and important food source. The bonytail and blue sucker were probably eaten, and the authors suggest the bonytail's use was limited because larger, less bony [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) were also available.

## Key Findings

**Archaeological fishes (Gehlbach and Miller, 1961)**

- Three taxa were identified from Pueblo ruins: longnose gar, bonytail, and blue sucker. The authors believe all three were eaten, but the gar scales do not show how they were used.
- The longnose gar and blue sucker are not currently known from northern New Mexico. The gar's presence suggests it may have inhabited the upper Rio Grande, and gar depictions on Mimbres pottery support this possibility.
- The blue sucker was estimated at slightly over 25 inches (urohyal) and about 33 inches standard length (Weberian apparatus), indicating very large individuals.
- The bonytail vertebrae are diagnostic and suggest a fish near 14 inches standard length. Intergrades between *elegans* and *robusta* are the only forms known from the region today.
- The blue sucker's presence at Rainbow House indicates a past river that was clearer, larger, and more stable than the modern Rio Grande.

**Egg complement (Hubbs and Johnson, 1961)**

- Dusky darter eggs from near Austin are reported as smaller and more numerous than those from near San Marcos.
- The abstract links larger egg diameter with more intense interspecific competition and inversely with intraspecific competition.
- The excerpt does not include the egg counts, sample sizes, or statistical tests needed to evaluate this claim.
