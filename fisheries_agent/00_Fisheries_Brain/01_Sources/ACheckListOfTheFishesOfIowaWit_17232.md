# Check-List of the Fishes of Iowa, with Keys for Identification

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #percidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1951 paper by Reeve M. Bailey, first issued in *Iowa Fish and Fishing*, provides a revised check-list of the native and established fishes of [[Iowa]] together with dichotomous keys for identification. The check-list comprises 25 families, 61 genera, 133 species and 137 total kinds (including subspecies) of native fishes, plus four established exotic species (brown trout, rainbow trout, carp and goldfish). The author removed 10 forms from Aitken's (1941) list of 135 and added 16 others, and he lists an additional set of species that may occur in the state but have not been verified. The paper also argues that the fish fauna changed markedly during half a century of intensive agriculture.

## Background & Description

The paper traces Iowa ichthyology through two main survey periods: 1884–1891, dominated by [[Seth Eugene Meek]] for the U.S. Fish Commission, and 1932 onward, when collections were made under [[Carl L. Hubbs]] for the Iowa 25-Year Conservation Plan. Later work by the author, by [[Kenneth D. Carlander]] at Iowa State College, and by the Upper Mississippi River Conservation Committee (1944–1948) added distributional data and new state records.

The keys are dichotomous, built on external characters where possible, and are a complete revision of mimeographed keys prepared for student use in 1940. The text stresses that minnow identification generally requires examination of pharyngeal teeth, and that small fish need a hand lens or dissecting microscope. Key terminology (for example, [[Standard Length]], [[Pharyngeal Teeth]], [[Lateral Line]], [[Gill Rakers]]) is defined in a glossary section.

Specimens are housed in the Iowa State College collection (I.S.C.), the [[Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan]] (U.M.M.Z.), and the [[Chicago Natural History Museum]] (C.N.H.M.).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper notes that comparison of Meek's late-nineteenth-century collections with recent collections shows that many species once common are now rare, if they survive in Iowa, and that the ranges of others have contracted. The author attributes this to "half a century of intensive agriculture," though he states that range extensions probably also occurred and that the data are too limited to verify them.

Several habitat and distribution notes are given:
- Brown and rainbow trout are stocked in cool, spring-fed streams of northeastern Iowa, with limited natural reproduction.
- Carp are found in most rivers and large lakes as well as many small lakes and ponds; goldfish occur in municipal and farm ponds.
- The plains carpsucker was found in overflow pools of the Cedar River.
- Several species are tied to large rivers, such as the Mississippi (for example, the pallid shiner and the sturgeons) and the Missouri (for example, the sturgeon chub and plains shiner).
- The Iowa darter, bluntnose darter and other small darters are associated with specific lake or pool habitats.
- Removed species were removed because records lacked verifiable specimens or fell outside the known range, not because they were confirmed absent.

## Key Findings

**Faunal revisions (Species Removed from the Iowa Faunal List)**
- Ten forms were deleted from Aitken's (1941) list, including *Couesius plumbeus* (probable hybrid), *Notropis boops*, *Notropis anogenus* (moved to hypothetical), *Percina caprodes caprodes* (intergrades), *Etheostoma blennioides blennioides*, *Xenotis megalotis megalotis* (misidentification of *Lepomis megalotis peltastes*), *Eupomotis microlophus* (redear sunfish), and *Cottus bairdii bairdii* (moved to hypothetical).
- Sixteen forms were added. Nomenclature changes followed the 1948 International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature meeting in Paris, including reinstating *Anguilla rostrata* and emending several names (for example, *Salmo gairdneri*).

**Check-list summary**
- 25 families, 61 genera, 133 species, 137 total kinds of native fishes; four established exotics.
- Notable species include [[Paddlefish]] (*Polyodon spathula*), [[Lake Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser fulvescens*), [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*), [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides salmoides*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus lacustris lacustris*) and [[Pallid Shiner]] (*Notropis amnis*, described by Hubbs in 1951).
- The genus *Hybopsis* is used in a broad sense, grouping *Couesius*, *Erimystax*, *Nocomis* and several other genera.

**Hypothetical and possible species**
- A set of species likely to occur but not yet verified is listed, for example [[Alligator Gar]], [[Spotted Gar]], [[Northern Brook Lamprey]], [[Greater Redhorse]], [[Freckled Madtom]] and [[Northern Sculpin]]. The author notes that hypothetical lists have a poor record of predictive accuracy but may guide collecting.

**Taxonomic clarifications**
- The ghost shiner (*Notropis buchanani*) was accorded full species status after William Ralph Taylor found that its infraorbital canal is undeveloped.
- Sturgeon chub and sicklefin chub records from the Missouri River were re-identified; the Sioux City record was referred to *H. meeki*.
- The pallid sturgeon was retained on the basis of a single 1930 Keokuk report, pending confirmation.

**Keys**
- A family-level key (with a 4-choice item 7) leads to 24 family keys and species keys for lampreys, sturgeons, gars, trouts, herrings, mooneyes, pikes, suckers, minnows, shiners, catfishes, killifishes, basses, sunfishes and perches/darters. Keys rely on counts of fin rays, scales, teeth and other structures, with explicit definitions of measurements and terms.
