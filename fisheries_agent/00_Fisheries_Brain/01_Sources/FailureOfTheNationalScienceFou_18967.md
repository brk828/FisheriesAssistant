# NSF Mt. Graham Telescope Project: Biodiversity and Cultural Dispute (1994)

#mammalogy #conservation-biology #mt-graham-red-squirrel

## Executive Summary

This 1994 correspondence is a dispute between the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]] (SWCBD), led by [[Robin D. Silver]], and the [[National Science Foundation]] (NSF) over NSF funding for astronomical facilities on [[Mt. Graham]], Arizona. SWCBD argues that the project destroys habitat essential to the [[Mt. Graham Red Squirrel]] and interferes with the religious practices of the [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]]. It also contends that NSF evaded [[Peer Review]] and "best scientific advice." The letter of May 18, 1994, addressed to NSF Director [[Neal Lane]], corrects point by point an NSF response of April 9, 1994, signed by [[Hugh M. Van Horn]], and sets out five demands. The document is advocacy correspondence, and its factual claims are SWCBD's assertions, not independently verified results.

## Background & Description

- **Mt. Graham:** Described by SWCBD as an "inland Galapagos" with more life zones than any other isolated mountain in the U.S. It rises from a 2,900 ft. desert floor of lower desertscrub to a 10,700 ft. boreal or Hudsonian summit. The summit [[Spruce-Fir Forest]] is described as the southernmost spruce-alpine fir forest in North America.
- **Biodiversity claims:** SWCBD states that Mt. Graham has 14 perennial streams, three unique high-altitude cienegas containing freshwater clams, and 18 plants and animals found nowhere else in the world.
- **Cultural significance:** SWCBD states that the mountain is of central sacred importance to the traditional San Carlos Apache, who have occupied the area for hundreds of years.
- **Telescope project:** NSF provided $600,000 to the [[University of Arizona]] in partial support of the joint University of Arizona-[[Max Planck Institute]] Submillimeter Telescope Project.
- **Legal framework:** The [[Arizona-Idaho Conservation Act]] (AICA) deemed the provisions of the [[Endangered Species Act]] satisfied for the first three telescopes on Mt. Graham. Its terms include [[Reasonable and Prudent Alternative Three]], which originates in the July 14, 1988, [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat bottleneck:** SWCBD argues that past habitat loss, especially at the highest elevations, has left the Mt. Graham Red Squirrel facing a "historic bottleneck of essential habitat."
- **Critical habitat type:** The [[Engelmann Spruce-Corkbark Fir Forest]] at the highest altitudes is described as rare and essential for the squirrel's long-term survival.
- **Population dynamics:** SWCBD states that squirrel populations fluctuate dramatically, depending primarily on the cone crop. Population risk is greatest when cone crops are sparse and the only food source is in the spruce-fir association. SWCBD notes that this situation has not occurred in approximately five years.
- **Cumulative impact:** SWCBD argues that additional construction would destroy more habitat, cumulatively jeopardizing squirrel survival.
- **Cultural and ecological overlap:** SWCBD argues that habitat destruction on the summit and landscape alteration impair traditional Apache religious practice.

## Key Findings

**NSF statements disputed by SWCBD (April 9, 1994 letter):**

1. **Monitoring adequacy:** NSF stated that squirrel monitoring shows earlier environmental analyses were adequate. SWCBD disputes this, arguing the project destroys essential habitat.
2. **Population increase:** NSF cited a population increase. SWCBD responds that short-term increases do not justify destroying rare spruce-fir habitat.
3. **Expanded habitat:** NSF cited a larger-than-expected suitable habitat area. SWCBD argues that high-elevation spruce-fir remains essential because it sustains the population during cone crop failures in lower forest.
4. **Conflict reconciliation:** NSF claimed AICA reconciled environmental protection with astronomical facility needs. SWCBD argues the Biological Opinion behind this provision was fraudulent, citing a [[U.S. General Accounting Office]] investigation, a congressional hearing, and sworn testimony. The courts ruled that Congress codifying the provision made the fraud finding irrelevant.
5. **Cultural claims resolved:** NSF, relaying University information, said cultural legal claims were resolved in the project's favor. SWCBD counters that Native Americans lack legal protection for religious practices and that the University argued sacred sites on federal lands need no protection.
6. **Tribal relations:** NSF cited ongoing University work with the San Carlos Apache Tribe. SWCBD calls this statement "ethnocentric and racist" and notes that it cites a [[Booz-Allen & Hamilton]] consultant report (October 23, 1991). The University initiated its program on January 6, 1993, aimed at satisfying "the vast majority" of San Carlos Apache.
7. **Scientific mission:** NSF stated its commitment to advancing science. SWCBD argues that NSF participation does not advance biology, earth science, or ethnography.
8. **Peer review:** NSF claimed disagreements are resolved through the best scientific advice and peer review. SWCBD requests evidence of this process and a list of biologists and ethnographers consulted before NSF became involved.

**Legal references cited by SWCBD:** 18 U.S.C. 1505 (obstruction of proceedings), 18 U.S.C. 371 (conspiracy to defraud the United States), and 18 U.S.C. 1001 (false statements in a government matter).

**Demands made of NSF (SWCBD's five suggested actions):**

1. Demand the return of all NSF Mt. Graham funds for redirection to projects that survive peer review.
2. Investigate how the scandal developed.
3. Relieve of duties NSF officials involved in evading peer review and scientific advice.
4. Refer officials who may have violated the felony statutes for criminal investigation.
5. Issue a directive that NSF will no longer become involved in telescope development on Mt. Graham.

**Status:** The document records no NSF response to the May 18, 1994 letter. The letter was copied to [[Peter Raven]], [[President Bill Clinton]], [[Vice President Al Gore]], several Senate and House committee chairs, and NSF officials [[William C. Harris]] and Hugh M. Van Horn.
