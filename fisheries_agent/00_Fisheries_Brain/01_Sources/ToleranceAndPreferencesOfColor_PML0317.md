# Tolerance and Preferences of Colorado River Endangered Fishes to Selected Habitat Parameters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final completion report from the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] (Utah State University, Logan, December 1981) by Bulkley, Berry, Pimentel and Black quantifies habitat tolerances of four endangered or threatened Colorado River fishes: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]], plus hybrid humpback-bonytail chubs. Work covered temperature preference, egg temperature tolerance, total dissolved solids (TDS) preference, baseline hematology, and swimming stamina. The stated aim was to give water development agencies data to minimize project effects on protected species.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River drainage supports several endangered and threatened fishes, but habitat requirements were poorly documented. Factors most likely altered by development include temperature, water velocity, and TDS concentration. The objectives were to determine temperature and TDS preferences of juvenile and adult fish, measure egg temperature tolerance, assess swimming performance at varying velocities and temperatures, and measure the effects of swimming and transport stress on blood parameters.

Fish were sourced from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and related facilities, then held at the [[Utah Water Research Laboratory]] in Logan, Utah, under a 14L:10D photoperiod. Acclimation temperatures were typically 8, 14, 20 and 26 ± 1 °C. Razorback suckers were derived from a single female spawned in 1974. Sample sizes varied by experiment and life stage, with large juvenile groups for preference tests and smaller adult groups (n = 25–35 for many tests).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature:** Final preferenda were estimated by shuttlebox (razorback sucker) and horizontal thermal gradient (other species). Estimates were compared with historical summer temperatures in the Colorado River drainage.
- **Water quality:** Flow-through systems held dissolved oxygen at 90–100% saturation, ammonia below 0.2 ppm, and pH near 8.0. The hatchery and laboratory water sources differed markedly in hardness and TDS (Table 2, summarized here: hatchery TDS 730 mg/l versus laboratory 164 mg/l).
- **Salinity sources:** The TDS experiments modeled saline input from oil shale processing and evaporative concentration in reservoirs, using ions characteristic of Colorado River water at Moab, Utah.
- **Fish passage and flow:** Swimming data were framed for culvert design, fish impingement at water intakes, dam fishways, and downstream flow effects.
- **Reservoir effects:** The authors linked cold tailwater avoidance to the disappearance of native fishes from the Green River below [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]].

## Key Findings

**Temperature preference**
- Razorback sucker final preferendum was estimated at 23–24 °C; the geometric mean of individual modes across all acclimation temperatures was 24.9 °C.
- Juvenile [[Colorado Squawfish]] final preferendum was 24.6 °C (graphical method) or 25.5 °C (linear regression). Adult estimates were 25.4 °C and 25.3 °C respectively.
- Humpback chub final preferendum was 24 °C; hybrid chub acute preferendum at 20 °C was 23.8 °C.
- Adult and juvenile squawfish acute preferenda did not differ significantly, suggesting factors other than temperature may influence adult distribution in the wild.
- Preferred temperatures for native species (21–29 °C, individual modes) were judged to agree with historical summer river temperatures.

**Egg temperature tolerance**
- Squawfish eggs: one lot hatched only at 20 °C (50% normal hatch, 5-day incubation); the other lots failed to develop.
- Bonytail eggs: no hatch at 5, 10, 31 or 37 °C; hatch at 14, 20 and 26 °C, with the highest hatch rates at 20–26 °C.
- Humpback chub eggs: no hatch at 5 °C; 30% at 10 °C; 50% at 14 °C; 100% in two replicates at 20 °C; 90–100% in two replicates at 26 °C.

**Total dissolved solids preference**
- Colorado squawfish preferred the lowest TDS (1.0–1.4 mmhos), with avoidance at about 5.0 mmhos. 97% of 90 fish tested avoided TDS above 3.6 mmhos.
- Bonytail chub preferred 3.4–3.8 mmhos, with avoidance near 5.1 mmhos.
- Humpback chub showed no clear TDS preference; avoidance was above 8.5 mmhos. Further testing with larger sample sizes was recommended.
- The authors predicted squawfish would be most affected by TDS increases from reservoir evaporation and oil shale salt release, with bonytail also at risk.

**Hematology**
- Juvenile razorback suckers (n = 56 across sexes): hematocrit 33.5%, hemoglobin 7.5 g/100 ml, erythrocytes 1.32 × 10⁶/mm³, leucocytes 27.1 × 10³/mm³, glucose 52.7 mg/100 ml, chloride 108 mEq/l. No sex or length differences were found.
- Juvenile squawfish (n = 31) and humpback chub (n = 30) had distinct leucocyte profiles. Humpback thrombocytes were the most abundant cell type (62.5%).
- Transport stress (about 700 fish hauled 15 hours) raised glucose from 54 to 144 mg/100 ml. Chloride fell from 108 to 99 mEq/l, a non-significant change. No hauling mortalities occurred. The authors suggested salt in hauling water may help reduce osmoregulatory stress.

**Swimming ability**
- Juvenile razorback suckers could cruise about 2 hours at 0.15–0.3 m/s but fatigued in about one minute at 0.6 m/s.
- Squawfish swam for one hour or more at about 0.39 m/s and fatigued within a minute at 0.57 m/s. Performance at 20 and 26 °C was significantly better than at 12 °C.
- Humpback chub swam for about 2 hours at 0.32 m/s but only a few minutes at 0.78 m/s. Performance was significantly related to temperature and fish length.
- Sustained speeds for juvenile [[Colorado River Fishes]] (2.2 and 1.6 body lengths/s range in the reported comparison) were broadly comparable to other species tested.
- Worked culvert example: at 20 °C and 0.45 m/s, razorback suckers could travel 466 m, enough to pass a 100 m culvert; at 0.6 m/s they could travel only 63 m.
- Impingement guidance: at a 0.3 m/s approach velocity all tested species could avoid impingement, while at 0.6 m/s most would be impinged.
