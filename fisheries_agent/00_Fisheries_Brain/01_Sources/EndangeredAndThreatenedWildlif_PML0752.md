# Rio Grande Silvery Minnow Endangered Species Proposal (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1993 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) package on the proposed listing of the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus amarus*) as an endangered species with [[Critical Habitat]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It contains a cover letter to Dr. Paul Marsh of Arizona State University, the Federal Register proposed rule (58 FR 11821–11828, March 1, 1993, 50 CFR Part 17), a supporting letter from Dr. Marsh, and a proposed critical habitat map and regulatory text. The species survives in only about 5 percent of its known historic range, and the Service concluded that habitat loss from water management, water quality decline, nonnative predators, and disease threaten it.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Hybognathus amarus* is one of seven species in the genus *Hybognathus*. It was first described by Girard (1856) from specimens collected near Fort Brown, Cameron County, Texas.
- **Morphology:** The species is a stout, silvery minnow. The dorsal fin is distinctly pointed, with its origin closer to the snout tip than to the tail base. The body is silvery-white and the fins are plain. Barbels are absent.
- **Historic status:** The species was once the most abundant and widespread fish in the Rio Grande basin, occurring from New Mexico to the Gulf of Mexico (Bestgen and Platania 1991). It was also found in the Pecos River.
- **Introduced population:** The Pecos River population is believed to derive from a plains minnow introduction. Minnows collected in the Arkansas River drainage were released into the Pecos drainage during a flood, a mistake that occurred within about a decade of the event.
- **Public process:** The Service sent notification letters to governmental and private parties (the count is partly illegible in the scan). Public hearings were scheduled in [[Albuquerque]] (April 27, 1993) and [[Socorro]] (April 28, 1993), with written comments due April 30, 1993, to the Albuquerque Field Office.
- **Supporting comment:** Dr. Paul C. Marsh (Research Professor, [[Center for Environmental Studies]], Arizona State University) wrote on April 6, 1993, that he had not worked directly with the species. He supported listing, saying that available data confirm "the perilous and declining status" of the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regulation and habitat loss.** Since [[Elephant Butte Dam]] was completed in 1916, four additional main-stem dams have been built on the Rio Grande and two on its tributary, the Rio Chama. Reservoirs capture spring runoff and summer inflows and release water over prolonged winter periods, replacing natural flood pulses. From Elephant Butte Dam to the confluence with the Rio Conchos (about 322 km / 200 mi), the river is fully controlled by reservoir releases and irrigation return flows. Meanders, oxbows, and other historic riverine habitat have been eliminated. Water flow manipulation may be one of the primary reasons the species has been extirpated from much of its range, though the mechanisms are not well documented.

**Water quality.** Agricultural and urban growth in the [[Middle Rio Grande]] Valley has degraded water quality. During low-flow periods, municipal and agricultural discharge makes up a large share of river flow, which reduces dilution. Poor water quality near Albuquerque, combined with low numbers of the minnow and a reduced overall fish community there, has been documented (Bestgen and Platania 1991).

**Predation and competition.** Confining fish to pools during low flow increases vulnerability to predators. The document lists nonnative predators that threaten the species, including [[Northern Pike]], [[Walleye]], [[White Crappie]], [[White Bass]], black and brown bullheads, [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]. Native predators, including the Rio Grande chub and bluegill, may also prey on subadults. Avian and mammalian predation probably increases when fish are confined to small clear-water pools.

**Disease and parasites.** Stress from confinement can trigger outbreaks of the protozoan *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* (ich). External copepods of the genus *Lernaea* are more common in confined fish. No studies have assessed the impact of disease or parasites on this species, so their significance is unknown.

**Nonnative species.** Game fish were introduced mainly by state and federal agencies to build reservoir fisheries. These species have spread into the river both upstream and downstream of the dams. They compete with the minnow for space and food, and some prey on it, especially during low flow, when fish are crowded into limited habitat.

**Extinctions.** Two native mainstem Rio Grande fish, the phantom shiner (*Notropis orca*) and the bluntnose shiner (*Notropis simus*), have already gone extinct. The document states that the silvery minnow may also face extinction through habitat modification and destruction in the last remnant of its range.

**Regulatory status.** The New Mexico Department of Game and Fish lists the species as endangered (Group 2, New Mexico State Game Commission 1985). The document says the state listing provides inadequate protection.

**Interstate water management.** The [[Rio Grande Compact]] among Colorado, New Mexico, and Texas, administered by the Rio Grande Compact Commission, affects flow timing and volume. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] plays a major role in dam operations and water delivery in the Middle Rio Grande.

**Overutilization.** None is known. Because the population has declined, the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish is expected to issue fewer and more restrictive collecting permits for scientific purposes.

## Key Findings

1. **Listing proposal:** The Service proposed endangered status with critical habitat under the Endangered Species Act (Section 4(a)(1)). The five listing factors were assessed as follows:
   - **Factor A (habitat destruction, modification, or curtailment):** The main threat. Dams, flow regulation, and dewatering have altered the river.
   - **Factor B (overutilization):** None known.
   - **Factor C (disease or predation):** Nonnative predators and confinement-related disease are threats. The effect of disease is unstudied.
   - **Factor D (inadequate regulatory mechanisms):** The state listing is considered insufficient.
   - **Factor E (other natural or manmade factors):** Nonnative species and the cumulative effects of drought and water diversion.
2. **Range reduction:** The species occupies about 5 percent of its historic range. Extirpation has occurred across most of its former distribution.
3. **Proposed critical habitat:** The area covers portions of Socorro, Valencia, and Sandoval counties, New Mexico. It begins just downstream of the [[Cochiti Dam]] and extends south about 262 km (163 mi) to the point where the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe Railroad crosses the river near San Marcial, Socorro County. Landowners include six Pueblos, the Bureau of Reclamation, the Service, the Bureau of Land Management, and state agencies.
4. **Conservation tools:** Section 7 consultation, Section 10 permits (50 CFR 17.22 and 17.23), and cooperation with states and private groups are available. Federal actions that reduce minimum flows or alter the river channel could adversely affect critical habitat. Such actions would require consultation.
5. **Economic and policy considerations:** Under Section 4(b)(2), the Service must consider the economic impacts of critical habitat designation. The Service held a March 1992 meeting in Albuquerque to explore flexible water delivery schedules to prevent dewatering of the river. No new distribution, abundance, or threat information was provided at that meeting.
6. **Supporting evidence:** Dr. Marsh supported the proposal, stating that the available data "clearly justify federal listing as endangered with critical habitat."
