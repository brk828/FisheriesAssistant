# The Colorado River: A Natural Menace Becomes a National Resource (Bureau of Reclamation Project Planning Report No. 34-8-2, 1946)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This March 1946 Bureau of Reclamation report, issued under Secretary J. A. Krug and the regional directors E. A. Moritz (Region III) and E. O. Larson (Region IV), presents a basin-wide plan for developing the water resources of the [[Colorado River Basin]] for irrigation, power production and other beneficial uses. It describes the basin's resources, needs and problems, inventories 134 potential within-basin projects with estimated costs and benefits, and recommends a next construction stage plus further investigations. The report is a planning inventory, not a fish or ecosystem study. Aquatic content is limited to the river's historic flow regime, sediment load, reservoir fisheries and stocking, and trout-related recreation. The central conclusion is that there is not enough water in the river system to build all potential projects or to meet all demands, so the states must settle their rights to the river's flow under the [[Colorado River Compact]].

## Background & Description

- **Scope**: Covers the basin's resources, needs, problems, and present and potential development. Export projects to adjacent basins are discussed without cost or benefit estimates.
- **Authority**: Made under the Reclamation Act of 1902 and the [[Boulder Canyon Project Act]] (1928) and its 1940 adjustment act.
- **Contributors**: Federal agencies (Geological Survey, National Park Service, [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], Grazing Service, Bureau of Mines, Office of Indian Affairs, General Land Office, Federal Power Commission, Forest Service), the Corps of Engineers, and the basin states through the Committees of Fourteen and Sixteen.
- **Physical setting**: The river rises in the Rocky Mountains of Colorado and Wyoming and flows about 1,400 miles to the Gulf of California. The drainage area is about 242,000 square miles, roughly one-twelfth of the continental United States' area, with a further 2,800 square miles in the Salton Sea basin. The river passes through the [[Grand Canyon]], and the basin is divided into seven hydrologic divisions: Green, Grand, San Juan, Little Colorado, Virgin, Boulder and Gila.
- **Climate**: Extremes range from year-round snow above 10,000 feet to desert conditions, with a 90-day growing season in the high mountain valleys and year-round cropping in the Yuma-Phoenix area. Annual precipitation averages about 2.6 inches along the Mexican border and about 40 inches in the high mountains.
- **Population**: In 1940 the basin had fewer than one million people, averaging 5.6 persons per square mile (against a national average of 44.2). The Lower Basin grew about fivefold from 1900 to 1940, while the Upper Basin roughly doubled.
- **Economy**: Livestock (cattle and sheep on range lands), irrigated hay, fruit and vegetables, coal, oil shale, uranium/vanadium, copper and other metals, and tourism and recreation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report treats aquatic and terrestrial biology only briefly.

- **Native life**: The mountains support pine, fir and aspen; the desert supports Joshua trees, yucca and saguaro cacti; the basin is native habitat for bighorn sheep, ptarmigan and wild turkey.
- **Fish**: Cold mountain streams hold rainbow, eastern brook, native rainbow, and Lake trout, and warm reservoirs such as [[Lake Mead]] are described as fisheries holding bass, crappie and bluegill. Lake Mead is called "a fisherman's paradise," and reservoirs are kept stocked from hatcheries.
- **Sediment**: The river carries an enormous volume of silt, about 100,000 acre-feet deposited annually in the delta region, and the report notes that silt in Lake Mead will shorten its useful life. Tributaries such as the San Juan, Little Colorado and Virgin rivers are highly erosive.
- **Salinity**: Returning irrigation flows make the lower river increasingly saline; the Gunnison and Green river tributaries carry high salt loads that must be studied during planning.
- **Stream depletion**: Development would deplete the river by about 7,000,000 acre-feet annually on top of present depletions; the total would exceed 10 million acre-feet, or about 20 percent more than the estimated water supply.
- **Fish and wildlife recommendations**: The Fish and Wildlife Service recommended stream-flow protection and a new combined trout-bass hatchery near Page Springs, Arizona. Its findings were accepted in principle by the Bureau of Reclamation.

## Key Findings

- **Water supply**: Virgin flow at Lee Ferry is estimated at about 16,400,000 acre-feet per year (average). Average annual flow across the International Boundary is about 17,000,000 acre-feet before diversions. Mexico is guaranteed 1,500,000 acre-feet under the 1944 treaty, with present U.S. uses depleting the virgin supply by about 7,000,000 acre-feet.
- **Water division**: The 1922 [[Colorado River Compact]] apportioned 7,500,000 acre-feet each to the Upper and Lower Basins annually and requires the Upper Basin not to deplete flow at Lee Ferry below 75,000,000 acre-feet over any ten consecutive years. The states had not agreed on allocations among themselves, and the report does not interpret the compact.
- **Potential projects**: 134 potential projects (about 1,000+ potential irrigation acres or equivalent units, 42 power plants in the Upper Basin and the Lower Basin, and export diversions) were inventoried. Total estimated construction cost is about $1,444,000,000 on January 1940 prices.
- **Irrigation**: Full development would add about 2,656,000 acres of irrigable land (about 1,555,000 new land and 1,122,000 supplemental), and would create roughly 500,000 acre-feet of annual pasture irrigation depletion.
- **Power**: The Upper Basin plants would total about 1,500,000 kilowatts and about 9.2 billion kilowatt-hours per year; the Lower Basin plants about 1,945,000 kilowatts and 10.2 billion kilowatt-hours.
- **Economics**: Estimated annual measurable benefits are about $65,000,000 for irrigation, $22,000,000 for power, $1,000,000 for flood control and $500,000 for municipal use; annual costs are about $85,000,000 (benefit-to-cost ratio about 1.1 to 1, per the report's tables).
- **Recommendations**: The states should select a group of projects for the next construction stage; the states should determine their rights to deplete the river consistent with the Compact; and additional investigations should be approved and funded through the Department of the Interior.
- **Conclusion**: Full development would benefit the national and local economies and should be carried out under a basin-wide plan, with a construction group of projects forming a logical next stage.

*Note: This document is a 1946 planning report; the figures are historical estimates rather than current data.*
