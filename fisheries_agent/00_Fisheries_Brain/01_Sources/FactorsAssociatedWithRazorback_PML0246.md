# Factors Associated with Razorback Sucker Recruitment in a Small Southern Arizona Pond

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit report (Fisheries Report 01-02, January 2002) by [[Scott A. Bonar]], [[Jon Flinders]], [[O. Eugene Maughan]] and [[William J. Matter]] examines why [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered species native to the [[Colorado River]] system, successfully survived and reproduced for several years in Rock Tank, a roughly 1 ha pond on the [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]]. Rock Tank was compared with two nearby tanks, Carrizo Dam Tank and Chango Tank, where stocked fish did not recruit. Rock Tank differed by lacking competing fishes, having a gravel bottom, and maintaining sufficient depth and dissolved oxygen through the hot Arizona summer. The authors suggest replicating these conditions could allow recruitment in other desert ponds.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker has been extirpated from much of the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] or survives only in relict populations. Reproduction occurs in some reservoirs, but egg and fry survival is virtually nil, largely due to habitat change and predation by [[Nonnative Fishes]].

In the late 1980s, hatchery-raised juveniles were stocked into stock tanks on the refuge to grow to a size that would avoid predation before restocking into historical habitat. Most recapture efforts failed and fish were presumed dead. In summer 1997, refuge staff observed many fish at the surface of Rock Tank in apparent oxygen distress. Captured fish were identified as razorback suckers that had survived since stocking. Age structure showed four size classes rather than one, indicating reproduction in three separate years (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 2001).

Study objectives were to (1) identify factors that allowed survival and recruitment in Rock Tank and (2) assess how this knowledge could guide reproduction in other desert ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.** Rock Tank, Carrizo Dam Tank and Chango Tank (each about 0.5–1.0 ha) lie within the refuge in southeastern Arizona at 1,000–1,200 m elevation. They are surrounded by desert savannah grassland, are remote, lie within 25 km of the Mexican border, and are rain-fed.

**Stocking history.** Rock Tank received 30,000 ~10 mm TL fry in March 1986. In May 1997, after oxygen stress, fish were seined and moved to Carrizo Dam Tank (175 fish), Chango Tank (1,294 fish) and Headquarters Tank (over 400 fish). Sampled fish ranged 145–400 mm TL across four age classes.

**Substrate.** Using the modified Wentworth classification (silt/clay = 0, sand = 1, gravel = 2, pebble = 3, cobble = 4, boulder = 5), Rock Tank was dominated by gravel and pebble (mean 2.7 ± 0.1), whereas Carrizo Dam (0.3 ± 0.1) and Chango (0.4 ± 0.1) were dominated by silt and clay. Razorback suckers typically spawn over sand, gravel and cobble.

**Predators and competitors.** No fish other than razorback suckers were recorded in Rock or Carrizo Dam tanks from 1986 to 2001. [[Black Bullhead]] were found in Chango Tank in 2000 and 2001; their stomachs (n = 32 in June 2001) contained insects, snails, bullfrog tadpoles and detritus. [[American Bullfrog]] and [[Tiger Salamander]] were present in the tanks but were judged unlikely to have limited recruitment.

**Temperature.** Successful spawning occurs between 12–20 °C in [[Lake Mohave]], with 20 °C optimal in laboratory work. Littoral temperatures fell in this range between February and April in all three tanks, so temperature was not considered limiting.

**Dissolved oxygen and clarity.** Many desert fishes need dissolved oxygen above 1–2 ppm. Rock Tank had daytime epilimnetic oxygen above 5 ppm year-round and Secchi depths above 1 m except late July–August. Chango Tank was wind-exposed, turbid from suspended clay, and shallow, with oxygen remaining high until it was drained in June 2001. Carrizo Dam Tank was shallow, wind-sheltered and phytoplankton-rich, with Secchi depths often below 0.5 m; its maximum depth fell from 2 to 1 m over the year, and oxygen may have dropped below 2 ppm several times in summer and fall.

## Key Findings

- **Survival and reproduction:** Rock Tank supported razorback sucker survival and reproduction over several years. Four year-classes were captured in 1997. Adults were found in Chango Tank in 2001, but no evidence of reproduction followed the 1997 translocation there.
- **Carrizo Dam Tank failure:** No fish were captured there despite repeated intense sampling. The authors concluded adults did not survive translocation, likely due to drying combined with oxygen depletion.
- **Substrate:** Gravel-pebble substrate in Rock Tank contrasted with silt-clay in the other two tanks. Lack of suitable spawning substrate may have limited reproduction in Chango and Carrizo Dam tanks.
- **Predation and competition:** The absence of other fishes was a principal factor in Rock Tank. Black bullheads in Chango Tank could have preyed on juveniles. Bullfrogs were probably present during Rock Tank spawning but were judged unlikely to limit recruitment.
- **Temperature:** Not limiting in any tank.
- **Food:** Ample food was inferred from numerous littoral aquatic insects, low Secchi depths indicating productivity, and growth matching or exceeding Lake Mohave. Food was judged unlikely to limit juvenile survival.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Remove nonnative fishes from other refuge tanks. Toxicants removed fathead minnows and mosquitofish but not black bullheads.
  - Drain, dry and lime ponds before stocking, and screen surface inflows with Saran cloth to exclude fish eggs and larvae.
  - Restrict access to non-sport ponds to limit illegal fish introductions.
  - Maintain consistent water sources, nutrient management and sufficient depth.
  - Use dusk and dawn oxygen measurements to predict depletions, with paddlewheel aerators or lift pumps for emergencies.
  - Dig ponds into gravel, sand or cobble, or line pond bottoms with these substrates. Settling ponds or liners may be needed to prevent sediment accumulation.
  - Stock razorback suckers into existing or constructed ponds that replicate these conditions to test recruitment further.
