# A Review of Running Water Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This review, presented by T. T. Macan at the Verhandlungen Internationale Vereinigung für Theoretische und Angewandte Limnologie (Stuttgart, 1961), surveys post-war research on running-water ecosystems. Macan argues that classification of streams remains premature: the species should be the basic ecological unit, the body of comparable data is thin, and existing schemes based on temperature, substratum, biological associations, and current are each partial. He concludes that "more facts" are needed and warns against rigid frameworks that confine ecological thinking.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Macan restricts attention to post-war work, since earlier studies (e.g. on [[Baetis]] nymph taxonomy and Plecoptera identification) were limited by taxonomic knowledge and regional coverage.
- **Major study sites cited:** the [[Susaa River]] (Denmark), the Welsh [[River Rheidol]], the [[Fulda River]], the [[Malle Stream]], and the [[Schwechat]] and other Austrian streams.
- **Taxonomic groups discussed:** Ephemeroptera (mayflies), Plecoptera (stoneflies), Trichoptera (caddisflies, larvae not yet reliably identifiable), Simuliidae (blackflies), Acari (water mites), Chironomidae (midges), Planaria (triclads), and Gammarus.
- **Fish:** Fish are discussed only as comparators, notably [[Speckled Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) and [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) in oxygen and temperature physiology, plus a brief mention of [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) spawning and carp temperature selection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Classification approaches**
- *Biological approach:* collect at random, group stations by shared species, then seek common factors.
- *Physical approach:* divide the area into arbitrary biotopes and sample typical stretches.
- Schemes reviewed include [[Thermal Zonation]] (Quellgebiet, Oberlauf, Mittellauf, Unterlauf by annual temperature range, after Illies and Dittmar), a temperature-difference scheme (Schultz), a species-count graph by [[Illies]] (1953a) on the Fulda, a [[Biocoenosis]] approach by Schmitz, and Marcier's "synusies" concept.

**Substratum**
- Unstable round-stone bottoms near the head, fixed stones lower down, and slate-like stones in the lower valley.
- [[Ancylus]] is almost confined to fixed stones; [[Rhithrogena]] is absent from fixed stones; Glossosoma prefers medium stones and other Trichoptera prefer large stones.

**Current and drift**
- Current governs main substratum types and determines which species can persist (Dorier and Vaillant measured withstood, ascent, and washing-away speeds).
- Stream animals are continually washed downstream, a major food source for trout (Müller; Kalleberg).

**Temperature**
- Planaria alpina behaves differently above 12 °C and tolerates short exposure to higher temperatures.
- Competition can restrict range: Planaria montenegrina stops at 13–14 °C when P. gonocephala is present, versus 16–17 °C alone.
- Cold-water species generally breed in winter; temperature shapes life histories and therefore productivity.

**Oxygen**
- Resting Salvelinus requires more oxygen than resting Carassius; active Salvelinus requires much more.
- Oxygen consumption of stream invertebrates is significantly greater than that of still-water relatives (Fox and Simpson).

**Other factors:** water regime (glacier-fed versus rain-fed streams), drought, turbidity, food supply, calcium and magnesium content, and pollution.

## Key Findings

1. **Classification is premature.** Macan judges that current data cannot support a robust classification scheme; he submits that biological and physical approaches are both valuable.
2. **Substratum matters.** Pencival and Whitehead (1929) recorded density differences per square decimetre among Baetis, Rhithrogena, Ephemerella, Hydropsyche, Agapetus, Chironomidae, and others across fixed stones, loose stones, gravel, and Cladophora (for example, Chironomidae reached 1453 per dm² on Cladophora).
3. **Mosaic communities.** Marcier's "synusies" may be small and abrupt; Macan doubts that zoocoenoses can be defined as neatly as plant communities, because animal species may each have independent ranges along environmental gradients.
4. **Ephemeroptera zonation.** Table 2 compiles records from across Europe; river systems can be divided by mayfly faunas, with species numbers increasing as streams grow larger.
5. **Current tolerance.** Dorier and Vaillant's measurements show species-specific current limits (e.g., Simulium ornatum at 114 cm/s; Agrion sp. at 10 cm/s), though interpretation is uncertain.
6. **Behaviour as adaptation.** Gammarus succeeds in streams through escape behaviour rather than morphology, and most stream animals live among stones where current is weak.
7. **Oxygen limits.** Carassius tolerates lower oxygen than Salvelinus; for Salvelinus, activity becomes limited near saturation at 20 °C but only at about 95 mm Hg at 5 °C. Incipient limiting levels for Carassius are 15 mm Hg at 5 °C and 40 mm Hg at 25 °C.
8. **Thermal tolerance.** Lethal temperature is 25.8 °C for Salvelinus fontinalis and 41 °C for Carassius auratus. Acclimation raised Carassius's lethal temperature from 27 to 41 °C (acclimation from 0 to 36.5 °C).
9. **Temperature-driven distribution.** Ephemeroptera distribution correlates with temperature: Rhithrogena alpestris occupies the coldest water, while R. hybrida and R. semicolorata occur at higher temperatures.
10. **Conclusion.** Macan calls for more facts and "freedom of thought," cautioning that rigid frameworks "make a cage" and disserve science.
