# Delaware Marsh Survey: Waterfowl, Muskrats and Marsh Management (1951)

#ornithology #wetland-ecology #waterfowl

## Executive Summary

This 1951 final report from the Delaware Board of Game and Fish Commissioners, prepared under [[Pittman-Robertson Project]] 7-R by Everett B. Chamberlain (leader) and Alexander R. Bumsted (assistant leader), surveys the state's tidal and freshwater marshes. It addresses waterfowl, [[Muskrat]] populations and marsh vegetation. Its central argument is that drainage, filling, pollution and salinity change have degraded wetland habitat, reducing both waterfowl production and the muskrat trapping industry. The report recommends water-level management of marshes, with a goal of about 10,000 acres of developed marsh within twenty years. Fish are not a subject of the document.

## Background & Description

The project was activated on May 15, 1949, under the supervision of Norman G. Wilder, Director of Conservation and Federal Aid Coordinator. Field work used county maps at about one inch to the mile, and aerial mosaics and photographs at roughly one inch to 660 feet. Observations covered plant species, water pH, temperature and salinity, and signs of waterfowl and mammal use. Data came from trappers, landowners and hunters, [[Hunter Bag-Check]] records, and a [[Aerial Waterfowl Census]].

The foreword notes that tidal marshes cover about eight percent of the state's area. Historically, marsh use moved from fur trade and seafood harvesting to drainage. In 1762 the St. Georges Creek act created a marsh company covering about 3,000 acres. Mosquito-control ditching in the 1930s was undertaken on a large scale. Report authors argued that this ditching, though sometimes effective against mosquitoes, lowered water tables and damaged wildlife habitat.

Muskrat economics are summarized in the report. Revenue was estimated at $50,000 to $500,000 annually. Pelt prices were about 10 cents in the late 1800s, peaked at $4.50 in 1920, and averaged about $2.10 in the 1950-51 season, with meat at $2.50 per dozen. Other furbearers (mink, otter, beaver) were considered commercially unimportant because their habitat had been largely eliminated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salinity as the controlling factor.** The authors concluded that salinity is the single greatest influence on marsh plant and animal distribution. Tide range, pH, bottom type, water depth and turbidity were secondary. Going north through the state, salinity drops, and plant diversity and use by muskrats and waterfowl rise. Most plants, waterfowl and muskrats occur where salinity is 10 parts per thousand (ppt) or less. Salinity readings varied strongly with tide stage, rainfall and evaporation; isolated potholes sometimes exceeded seawater salinity during dry spells.

**Vegetation.** Table 1 lists plants valued by waterfowl and muskrats, and undesirable "weed" species, with water depth and salinity tolerances. Valued waterfowl foods include [[Wild Celery]], [[Widgeon Grass]], pondweeds, [[Wild Rice]] and three-square bulrush. Cattail, bur-reed and cordgrass are valued for muskrats. Common weeds include giant reed, groundsel bush, high tide bush and marsh mallow. Salt-tolerant [[Cordgrass]] and [[Salt Hay]] dominate saline portions of most units. The survey covered 358 stations, and a frequency table of 55 genera and species was compiled across salinity zones at 10 ppt intervals.

**Waterfowl use and migration.** Up to 27 waterfowl species use Delaware during spring and fall migration, but only about five remain in nesting season. The [[Black Duck]] is the most important species: it is the only one known to produce significant numbers of young in Delaware (apart from possibly the wood duck), and it dominates hunter bags across the [[Atlantic Flyway]]. The [[Bombay Hook National Wildlife Refuge]] is a key habitat, with large fall counts of [[Snow Goose]] and [[Canada Goose]].

**Muskrat dynamics.** Ditching for mosquito control lowered water tables. In one experimental plot, mean water level fell more than five inches within three years. Downstream, the Mispillion-to-Broadkill area declined from an estimated 25,000 hides per year (1940 bulletin) to about 1.1 rats per acre on average, and only 0.9 rats per acre on the best 4,000 acres checked in 1950-51.

## Key Findings

- **Hunter harvest composition:** Across 1949 to 1951, 3,372 waterfowl were examined in bag checks, and 1,452 (43.00%) were black ducks. Black duck, green-winged teal, pintail and mallard were the most common species in Delaware bags.
- **Age ratio:** The adult-to-juvenile ratio improved from 1.0:1.20 in 1950 to 1.0:2.26 in 1951, which the authors judged too few years of data to define a normal value.
- **Cripple loss:** Delaware losses were 11.8%, 11.97% and 11.6% (1949-1951; three-year average 11.8%), compared with 21.9%, 19.4% and 19.2% for the Northeast Region.
- **Duck stamp sales:** Rose from 1,259 (1935-36) to 5,122 (1951).
- **Migration peak:** Monthly aerial counts over 11 census units (from September 1950) show that peak migration over most of the state occurs in December. The report recommends that hunting seasons start about mid-November.
- **Refuge contribution:** Bombay Hook produces many times the black duck production of the same acreage before development, plus growing numbers of blue-winged teal and shovelers. Aerial counts show no sudden population shift from adjacent areas after hunting opens.
- **Refuge counts:** Fall 1949 approximately 14,000 waterfowl plus 2,500-3,000 snow geese; 1950 about 11,000; spring 1951 averaged about 5,000, including 400-500 Canada geese.
- **Aerial methods:** Aerial counts were considered reliable indices when done by the same pilot and observers each time. Muskrat house counts from the air averaged about 85% accuracy against ground checks, with air counts always lower.
- **Recommended development goal:** About 10,000 acres of developed marsh within twenty years (excluding Bombay Hook), which would roughly double present migration-feeding capacity.
- **Development cost:** The Primehook Marsh (about 4,000 acres) could be diked and water-controlled at about $10 per acre. Developed impounded muskrat marshes could gross up to $40 per acre annually.
- **Site requirements:** Reliable fresh water source, adequate control structure, and location within 50-100 miles of a known waterfowl concentration. Small tracts of 100 acres or less need protection from cattle and people during breeding season.
- **Management tools:** Water-level control and impoundment for stable levels; periodic draw-downs to reduce vegetation and mosquito breeding; blind ditches (open trenches that do not drain marsh) to create open water; and lateral ditches with killifish ([[Fundulus]] and [[Gambusia]]) for mosquito control.
- **Regional priorities:** Preserve remaining feeding grounds in northern Delaware (Pennsylvania line to the Chesapeake and Delaware Canal), maintain central Delaware marshes, and create fresh feeding grounds in the south.
- **Ownership:** Most marshes are privately owned, so the report emphasizes private development, with state, county or federal assistance where needed. Earlier assistance from highway funds had developed the St. Georges and Primehook marshes.
- **Work unit summaries:** Twenty-one work units were described. Units 1 and 2 (Delaware River, urban) were judged unlikely to support management because of development pressure. Units 7 and 9 adjoin Bombay Hook and have high hunter success. Unit 13 (Primehook) was singled out as the most promising site for water-level control.
