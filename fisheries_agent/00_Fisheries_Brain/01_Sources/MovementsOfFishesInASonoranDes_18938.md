# Movements of Fishes in a Sonoran Desert Stream, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines seasonal movements and standing crops of native fishes in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a desert stream in southeastern Arizona. Standing crops varied strongly through the year. Fishes were more abundant, and more biomass was present, within the central [[Aravaipa Gorge]] in summer than outside it. The pattern reversed in winter. Roughly 2,100 individuals of three large species were tagged with anchor tags between January 1978 and November 1979. Fish movement radiated seasonally into and out of the gorge: dispersal was from the gorge in winter and toward it in summer, presumably in response to high water temperatures. Floods had little influence on movement, and home ranges or territories were not evident for most tagged individuals.

## Background & Description

Earlier fish movement research in streams focused mainly on sport and commercial species. Observations on native Southwestern fishes were largely anecdotal. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, Cyprinidae) was reported to have run upstream in large numbers in now-dry rivers in spring, earning the name "white salmon of the Colorado" (Miller 1961). Such large fishes were once common and are now rare.

Aravaipa Creek supports a nearly intact native fish fauna (Barber and Minckley 1966). Of its seven native species, three reach at least 10 cm total length and could be marked with anchor tags:

- ***Gila robusta*** ([[Roundtail Chub]]), a minnow (Cyprinidae)
- ***Catostomus insignis*** ([[Sonora Sucker]]), a sucker (Catostomidae)
- ***Pantosteus clarki*** ([[Desert Sucker]]), a sucker (Catostomidae)

The study was motivated by the observation that larger species were seasonally common, then essentially absent, from some reaches of the stream. The authors were Darrell J. Siebert and W. L. Minckley of the Department of Zoology, Arizona State University.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime.** Perennial flow arises from gravels about 6.5 km northwest of Klondyke, Graham County, and maintains surface discharge for 40 stream-km before receding into the substrate 7.6 km upstream from the confluence with the [[San Pedro River]] (Pinal County). The upper 10 km lies in a broad, open valley. The central 17 km pass through [[Aravaipa Gorge]], which is 10 to 100 m wide and bounded by sheer walls more than 200 m high. The final 14 km flow through a steep-sided valley. Surface water reaches the normally dry San Pedro River only during unusually high, sustained flow.

Base winter discharge is 0.32 (units as given in source, m/s). Summer shows a bimodal pattern of 0.14 and 0.28, with the lower value reflecting irrigation withdrawal and [[Evapotranspiration]]. Winter floods arise from regional precipitation or snowmelt and are long-term with broad hydrographs. Summer [[Flash Flooding]] comes from local thunderstorms and is short-term and abrupt. Flood probability is highest in February–April and July–September, and low in May–June. Few floods exceed 2.0 (m³/s as stated in the source). Maximum instantaneous flow was 566, and minimum recorded discharge was 0.008.

**Water temperature.** Two thermal seasons are recognized:

| Period | Mean water temperature | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Winter (November–March) | Given in source (approx. 13 °C, OCR-garbled) | 4.0–20.5 °C |
| Summer | 23.9 °C | 17.0–33.2 °C |
| Spring and autumn transition | 16.6 °C | 12.4–22.5 °C |

Heating is rapid in open reaches, and daily maxima occur between 1200 and 1600 h. Water is coolest at sunrise. Reaches above the gorge are slightly warmer in winter and cooler in summer, owing to aquifer inflow and underflow through coarse alluvium.

**Gorge thermal regime.** Canyon walls shade much of the gorge, and evaporation into low-humidity air produces substantial cooling. Mid-day summer temperatures in the gorge were 3.1 to 8.8 °C cooler than at other stations. At 1300 h in winter, water was 15.0 °C in the upper gorge (upstream mean 16.4 ± 1.5 °C) and 11.0 °C near the lower gorge (downstream mean 14.1 ± 3.3 °C).

**Channel and habitat.** Substrate, discharge, and channel configuration prevent development of typical pool–riffle habitat. The stream is a braided channel over coarse gravel and sand. Runs are separated by short, turbulent riffles less than 15 cm deep, with coarse gravel, rubble, and small boulders. Pools are infrequent, form where canyon walls, boulders, or other obstructions cause scour, and reach 0.75–1.5 m deep. Their bottoms are unstable because of continuous sand transport. Only pools maintained by the most permanent features persist; others are transient.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**

- **Standing crop sampling (1976–78):** Reaches were blocked with 6.3-mm netting and repeatedly [[Electrofishing]] until no more fish were captured. Fish were preserved in 10% formalin, then counted and weighed. Weights were not corrected for preservative change.
- **Tagging (January 1978–November 1979):** Backpack electrofishing collected fish for marking with colored, individually numbered anchor tags (Floy Tag and Manufacturing Co.). Tags were inserted through the dorsal musculature and secured among the medial pterygiophores. Potassium permanganate was applied to wounds. Tag loss and marking mortality were not directly assessed but were considered low. Mortality was 0.5% or less within 24 hours. Recovered fish matched the tagged size frequency.
- **Tagging effort:** About 2,100 fish were tagged. Six sub-reaches were designated, from the upper stream above the gorge (Segment I) to the last 7.0 km near the San Pedro River (Segment VI). Tagging was not conducted June–September to avoid combined heat and electrofishing mortality.
- **Recapture:** Sampling ran bi-weekly to monthly from September through May and every six weeks from June through August. Minimum distance moved was calculated as the difference between capture and recapture locations, with upstream movement positive and downstream negative. Localities were recorded as meters upstream from the San Pedro junction, with precision within 10 m.
- **Statistics:** [[Analysis of Variance]] and [[Linear Regression]] (BMDP package) tested effects of sex, species, tagging segment, and season, and relations between distance moved and total length or tagging locality. Movement direction was treated as a [[Bernoulli Trial Analysis]] with p = 0.5 and compared against the binomial distribution.

**Standing crops of large fishes (g/m², mean ± SE)**

| Season | Above gorge | Within gorge | Below gorge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring | 10.70 ± 3.82 | 3.32 ± 1.98 | 5.80 ± 1.46 |
| Summer | 0.40 ± 0.23 | 25.60 ± 28.27 | 8.07 ± 1.79 |
| Autumn | 2.79 ± 1.11 | 5.77 ± 2.41 | 13.54 ± 7.22 |
| Winter | 65.05 ± 40.97 | 16.46 ± 3.69 | 19.58 ± 5.57 |

- The three large species made up more than 75% of total fish biomass on average.
- In winter, biomass concentrated outside the gorge, especially at the upstream end. In summer, fish almost vacated the upper reach and concentrated in the gorge.
- In spring, biomass was highest outside the gorge and upstream. In autumn, it was highest below the gorge, concentrated downstream.
- Numbers per m² were far more uniform than biomass, because they include small species and young fish. Percentage composition still showed large species concentrated in the gorge in summer and dispersing from it in other seasons.

**Directional movement (Bernoulli trial analysis)**

- **All species, late winter 1978, upper reaches:** 13 moved up and 39 down (n = 52, p* = 0.0001). Downstream movement dominated.
- **All species, late winter 1978, lower reaches:** 45 up and 26 down (n = 71, p* = 0.007).
- **All species, late summer 1978, upper reaches:** 31 up and 14 down (n = 45, p* = 0.005). Upstream movement dominated.
- ***Catostomus insignis*, late winter 1978, lower reaches:** 28 up and 10 down (n = 38, p* = 0.002).
- ***Gila robusta*, late winter 1978, upper reaches:** 4 up and 13 down (n = 17, p* = 0.018).
- ***Pantosteus clarki*, late winter 1978, upper reaches:** 4 up and 16 down (n = 20, p* = 0.005).

**Main conclusions**

1. Standing crops show a clear seasonal shift of large fish between the gorge and the outer reaches.
2. Seasonal dispersal was from the gorge in winter and toward it in summer. The summer pattern is presumably a response to high water temperature.
3. Floods had little influence on movement patterns.
4. Home ranges or territories were not evident for most individuals studied.
5. Recapture totals and individual movement distances are not given in the source text, and the reported statistics are summarized here as the authors presented them.
