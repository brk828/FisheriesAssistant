# Endangered Lost River and Shortnose Suckers of the Klamath Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1992 federal biological opinion (USFWS) and supporting review covering two endemic catostomid fishes, the [[Lost River Sucker]] and the [[Shortnose Sucker]], both federally listed as endangered on July 18, 1988. It traces the historical abundance of the suckers in the [[Klamath Basin]], their decline linked to dams, wetland drainage, diversions, overharvest, non-native fish and [[Hypereutrophication]] of [[Upper Klamath Lake]], and it sets out lake-level and water-quality terms intended to protect spawning, larval rearing and refuge habitat across the lake, [[Clear Lake]], [[Tule Lake]], [[Gerber Reservoir]] and the Lost River system. The opinion also addresses [[Bald Eagle]] prey availability under water-management scenarios.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Both species were described by Cope (1879) from Upper Klamath Lake specimens. Both are endemic to the Klamath Basin. The Lost River sucker is the only species in the genus *Deltistes*, so the genus is also listed as endangered.
- **Size and age:** Lost River suckers reach up to 1 m total length and were aged to 43 years in Upper Klamath Lake. Shortnose suckers were aged to 33 years in [[Copco Reservoir]].
- **Maturity:** Lost River suckers in Upper Klamath Lake mature at 6 to 14 years, most at age 9. Shortnose suckers mature at 5 to 8 years, most at 6 or 7.
- **Historical use:** Spring sucker runs were described by Gilbert (1898) as occurring "in incredible numbers." They supported Klamath and Modoc Indian subsistence, local food and livestock feed, a cannery on the Lost River, and commercial oil and dried-fish processing. Sport fisheries on the [[Williamson River]] and [[Sprague River]] persisted into the 1960s and 1970s.
- **Reproduction:** Both species are lake suckers that generally spawn in rivers or streams and return to the lake. Some populations spawn near springs in Upper Klamath Lake, notably at [[Sucker Springs]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat loss and water management.** More than 100,000 of the 240,000 [[Klamath Project]] irrigated acres were marshland before project actions. More than 30,000 acres of marsh around Upper Klamath Lake were lost during the 20th century. Project and non-project irrigation reduced lake and river inflows and increased nutrient loading from return flows. Annual project-induced lake fluctuations average about three feet, versus a pre-project maximum of 2.3 feet, and can exceed five feet.

**Water quality.** Upper Klamath Lake has become hypereutrophic, with blooms dominated by *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*. Summer and autumn conditions can push pH above 10.0 and drop dissolved oxygen to 0.3 mg/l. Fish kills occurred in 1971 and 1986. Juvenile shortnose suckers tolerated a maximum pH of 9.55, and the minimum critical dissolved oxygen concentration was 0.7 mg/l (Castleberry and Cech 1990). Refugial areas near stream and spring inflows maintain better conditions.

**Tule Lake.** Tule Lake was reduced from about 96,000 surface acres to 13,000 acres. Its inflow is largely reused irrigation return water, with dissolved oxygen as low as 0.41 mg/l and total ammonia as high as 0.88 mg/l recorded in June 1992. In situ survival of fathead minnow fry was very low, with no more than two of 20 surviving in any of four tests.

**Entrainment.** Larval suckers are lost to the [[A Canal]] headworks. Estimated entrainment peaked at 43,887 suckers per day in early June 1991 and 21,773 per day in early July. The cumulative estimate for 13 May to 15 July 1991 was 759,150 larvae. This was extrapolated from a catch of only 51 larvae, 35 of which were identified as Lost River suckers.

**Predation and competition.** Suckers spawning on riprap-type substrate at Sucker Springs are vulnerable to predation. A rainbow trout had stomach contents matching sucker eggs. Both species likely compete with the many non-native species introduced to Clear Lake.

## Key Findings

- **Decline and listing:** Surveys in 1984 to 1986 showed a major decline (Bienz and Ziller 1987). The fishery was closed in 1987, and both species were listed as endangered on July 18, 1988. Harriman and Barkley Springs stocks may already be lost.
- **Overharvest:** Angler exploitation of Lost River suckers was below 6% in 1984 and 1985, but a decline in average harvested size from 1966 to 1974 suggests the fishery impacted the population.
- **Recruitment:** Recruitment in Upper Klamath Lake is inconsistent. The last known strong year classes are from 1977 and 1978. A 1991 juvenile year class may represent future recruitment, but its survival is unknown.
- **Lake-level targets (Upper Klamath Lake):** The opinion sets a minimum of 4137.0 ft through 30 September in low-water years and 4139.0 ft in normal years. Spawning at Sucker Springs is possible from 4141.0 ft during March and April. A lake level of 4142.1 ft was suggested as the lower spawning limit in the August 14, 1992 opinion. Stability matters more than level, with a maximum rise of one foot during spawning and incubation.
- **Deviation limits:** Upper Klamath Lake allows no more than two consecutive low-water years of deviation. Clear Lake allows up to four, because its recruitment is more consistent.
- **Clear Lake minimums:** The opinion sets 4524.0 ft during the spawning season (for Willow Creek access), 4523.0 ft for the rest of the year, and 4521.0 ft for the west lobe during droughts, with a dike between the lobes required by the May 1, 1992 opinion.
- **Gerber Reservoir:** In May 1992, over 200 shortnose suckers (78 to 461 mm FL) were collected, and no Lost River suckers. Successful reproduction occurred in recent low-water years, but stressed fish showed low body weight and poor gonadal development. The reservoir was predicted to fall to 4796.9 ft in October 1992 (226 surface acres, 937 acre-feet). The opinion requires no releases below 4799.6 ft.
- **Spawning migration:** Spawning runs into the Williamson and Sprague rivers begin in late March or early April and continue into May. Over 90% of Lost River larvae emigrated between 5 May and 15 June in 1987 and 1988. Emigration occurs mainly from 2000 to 0700 hours.
- **Sprague River habitat:** Potential spawning habitat covers 50 km, of which 48 km lies upstream of [[Chiloquin Dam]]. Few suckers have been observed passing its fish ladder. Dam removal or alteration could expand spawning habitat, and this may be more effective than any hatchery program (Scoppettone and Vinyard 1991).
- **Hybridization:** Genetic research has found that hybridization "has not occurred at an appreciable rate" (Moyle and Berg 1991). Morphological variation in shortnose populations remains unexplained.
- **Bald eagles:** Consecutive dry years may adversely affect breeding territories at Gerber Reservoir and winter foraging across the Basin. Reservoir storage has stayed below 70% of capacity since the recent drought years and fallen below 10% each year.
- **Stranded and diverted fish:** Fish, including endangered suckers, have been observed stranded in isolated pools below Clear Lake Dam. Irrigation diversions from Tule Lake may kill larval suckers directly through pumps, through desiccation, or in poor canal water quality.
