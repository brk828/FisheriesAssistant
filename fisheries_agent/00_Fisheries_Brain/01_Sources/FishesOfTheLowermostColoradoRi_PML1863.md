# Fishes of the Lowermost Colorado River, Its Delta, and Estuary: A Commentary on Biotic Change

#ichthyology #marine-biology #multiple-families

## Executive Summary

This commentary by [[W. L. Minckley]] compiles the fish fauna of the [[Colorado River]] downstream of [[Davis Dam]], its [[Colorado River Delta]], and the uppermost [[Sea Of Cortez]]. The list records 126 taxa: 45 freshwater species (8 native, 37 non-native) and 81 marine species, all native. Every native freshwater fish is exceedingly rare or extirpated from the study area, and six are listed under the U.S. [[Endangered Species Act]]. The author attributes this decline to upstream water diversion, reservoir construction and nutrient interception, which together dried the delta and altered estuarine salinity. He argues that the marine list is incomplete and that the low number of euryhaline species may reflect sampling gaps as much as biogeography. The paper provides a baseline for future assessments and for integrated resource management.

## Background & Description

The paper is a compilation and commentary, not a field study. The author combined records from several sources, including [[Castro-Aguirre et al. (2000)]], an unpublished manuscript by [[Robert Rush Miller]], the author's own forthcoming treatment of [[Fishes of the American Southwest]], and a thesis by Guevara-Escamilla (1974) on trawl samples from the upper Sea of Cortez. The compiled list is presented in Table 1, which gives nativity (N/I), habitat (F/M/E), and conservation status for each taxon. Taxonomy follows Castro-Aguirre et al. (2000).

The scope covers all freshwater taxa but only marine fishes known to enter fresh or brackish water. The marine coverage is acknowledged to be far too short. The author notes that Thomson et al. (1979) and Castro-Aguirre et al. (1999) document many more marine species from the upper sea.

The paper is dedicated to Salvador Contreras-Balderas on his retirement from the Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical river and delta.** Less than a century ago the lower Colorado was a sinuous, braided, sand-bottomed river that flooded unpredictably. It ran through mesquite, cottonwood, willow and marsh, and ended in a large wetland delta covering about 8000 km² between Yuma, Arizona, and the sea (Sykes, 1937). The author describes the delta as the most stable component of an otherwise unpredictable riverine system, a dynamic refuge where biota could persist through floods and droughts.

**Present conditions.** Flow is now stored, diverted and controlled. Channels are constrained by rock-armored banks and levees and lined with non-native saltcedar. In some recent years no surface flow reached the sea. Silt and sand no longer move, salts accumulate, and the delta has become desiccated, hypersaline, mud-cracked flats with halophytic vegetation.

**Marine and estuarine effects.** Reduced freshwater inflow, combined with evapotranspiration exceeding 2000 mm per year at the northern end of the sea, raised estuarine salinity (Thomson et al., 1979). More than 30 major upstream reservoirs, with total storage more than 400% of the average annual watershed outflow (as reported in the source; OCR-affected figure), intercept nutrients that once reached the estuary. Fishes that depend on salinity gradients now meet the reverse near the river mouth, and formerly brackish areas are hypersaline. The author concludes the system has been altered at its base and, if not already collapsed, has changed greatly.

**Human dependence.** Almost 30 million people depend on the river for irrigated agriculture, hydroelectric power and domestic use. Sport and commercial fisheries, recreation, and legally mandated species protection create competing demands.

**Conservation framing.** The author argues that fishes are often treated as "non-resources" because they lack obvious human use and are poorly known. Native fishes sit near the top of the aquatic food chain and respond to ecosystem damage through changes in abundance, distribution, or local loss, so they serve as indicators of ecosystem health.

## Key Findings

**Richness and composition**
- 50 families of fishes are recorded from fresh or brackish water in the lower Colorado, its delta and the estuarine area. Nine families are alien to the region, and three more have both native and introduced representatives.
- 126 taxa are recorded in total: 45 freshwater species (8 native, 37 non-native, nearly all freshwater non-natives) across 13 families, and 81 marine species across 37 families, all native. The results section gives 125 species with 87 native and 38 non-native; this differs from the abstract's 126 and 89/37, probably through a typographic or scan error.
- Only 11 of the 45 freshwater species (2 of the 8 native taxa) are demonstrably euryhaline somewhere in their range.
- Ten non-native freshwater taxa were found only once or twice after introduction or stocked and disappeared. They are listed for completeness but considered extirpated or hypothetical.
- Excluded from Table 1 are several introduced salmonids, American shad (*Alosa sapidissima*), and a non-native buffalofish (*Ictiobus* sp.), none of which are expected to be established under present conditions.

**Native freshwater fishes**
- All but one of the 8 native freshwater taxa are exceedingly rare or extirpated from the study area. Three (*Gila elegans*, *Xyrauchen texanus*, *Cyprinodon macularius*) are being reintroduced.
- None of the 8 is extinct. Six are listed as endangered by the USFWS under the U.S. Endangered Species Act (1973, as amended).
- The Mexican government's SEDESOL (1994) list includes 8 freshwater and 2 marine taxa as rare, threatened or endangered. Within the region, two of these are represented by introduced, pestiferous populations.

**Marine and euryhaline fishes**
- Castro-Aguirre et al. (2000) document more than 550 euryhaline species for Mexico, about half from the Sea of Cortez. Only 80 (about 32%) are recorded from the study area.
- The author suggests the low proportion may reflect exclusion of tropical fishes from the northern sea by winter cold, the unpredictable river, inadequate sampling, or neglected publication. Several sources point to sampling and publication gaps.
- Guevara-Escamilla (1974) recorded more than 70 taxa in trawl samples from two brief (3-day) summer sampling periods, illustrating how much remains undocumented.

**Notable taxa and historical records**
- Totoaba (*Totoaba macdonaldi*), listed by SEDESOL, formerly entered delta brackish waters in large numbers to breed. Its present status is unknown.
- Fishermen reported striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*) and cichlids (probably *Oreochromis aurea*) from the west side of the delta during flooding in 1983–84. The author considers these identifications reliable.
- Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*) is endangered and is among the species being reintroduced.

**Sampling and data limitations**
- Few modern delta surveys have been published. Most older work is by Gilbert & Scofield (1898), Snyder (1913), Follett (1967) and Walker (1961). Recent work has focused on special-concern taxa or specific localities such as Ciénega de Santa Clara.
- An apparent absence of non-native euryhaline fishes may partly reflect insufficient sampling.
- Faunal reconstruction from widely separated samples may be the only way to estimate the original community. Relevant collections are held at the Natural History Museum (Los Angeles), Scripps Institution of Oceanography, Universidad Autónoma de México, the University of Arizona, UCLA, and Arizona State University, where most freshwater specimens are deposited.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Ecosystem health of the lower Colorado, its delta and the upper Sea of Cortez has deteriorated substantially.
- The compiled list is intended as a baseline for future comparison and for integrated resource management, which the author considers urgently needed.
- Additional studies of fishes and other taxa are needed to support conservation of aquatic biodiversity and to inform management conflicts among agencies and users.
