# A qualitative and quantitative study of trout food in Castle Lake, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #freshwater-ecology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This study (Swift, 1970) examined the diets of [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) in **Castle Lake, California**, using stomach content analysis (1963–1968) combined with microbomb calorimetry to assess the energetic contribution of prey items. The two sympatric trout species exhibit distinct dietary niches: Brook Trout rely primarily on **benthic organisms**, while Rainbow Trout rely primarily on **terrestrial insects**. Both species consume [[Zooplankton]] (notably *Daphnia rosea*) in roughly equal proportions. Energetically, across both trout populations combined, **~48% of consumed calories were terrestrial in origin, ~36% benthic, and ~21% pelagic**, though this partitioning differs substantially by species. The study also revealed a large discrepancy between the standing energy available in the benthos and the fraction actually consumed by trout, attributable to microhabitat refugia (e.g., chironomid larvae living below the mud-water interface).

## Morphological / Physiological Characteristics

- No morphological data on the fish themselves are reported; the paper instead focuses on **energetic/caloric characteristics of prey items**.
- Prey caloric content (per gram ash-free dry weight) was determined via nonadiabatic Phillipson microbomb calorimetry, calibrated with benzoic acid. Representative values:
  - **Damselfly nymphs**: 6374 ± 125 cal/g (highest measured)
  - **Sialis larvae**: 5928 ± 225 cal/g
  - **Dragonfly nymphs**: 5514 ± 285 cal/g
  - **Hymenoptera (ants)**: 5898 ± 319 cal/g
  - **Coleoptera**: 5738 ± 596 cal/g
  - **Caddisfly larvae**: 4409 ± 871 cal/g (relatively low)
  - **Cladocera (*Daphnia*)**: 5272 ± 149 cal/g
  - **Snails**: 3484 ± 229 cal/g (lowest, likely due to shell mass)
- Trout were sampled by age/size class: Rainbow Trout by year class (I–IV, fin-clip coded); Brook Trout by arbitrary length classes (<6, 6–8, 8–10, >10 inches).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: [[Castle Lake]] is a cirque lake at 1,707 m elevation, 20.1 ha, with a shallow northern basin (~3 m) and a deep southern basin (37 m). Strong summer thermal stratification occurs, with the thermocline at ~9 m and surface temperatures reaching 22°C in August. Ice-covered December–April/May.
- **Niche partitioning (interactive segregation)**: Following [[Nilsson (1963, 1965, 1966)]]'s framework on sympatric salmonid segregation, the presence of Rainbow Trout combined with Brook Trout's avoidance of warm summer surface waters (22°C) displaces Brook Trout to lower, colder strata. This spatial segregation allows Rainbow Trout to dominate exploitation of surface waters and terrestrial food inputs.
  - Historical comparison: when Brook Trout existed **alone** in Castle Lake (pre-Rainbow Trout introduction), terrestrial food comprised ~46% of their diet by volume ([[Wales and German 1950]]). During this later study (with Rainbow Trout present), terrestrial food exceeded 25% of Brook Trout diet volume only in September 1963 — evidence of competitive displacement.
- **Population maintenance**: Brook Trout are self-sustaining (natural reproduction in-lake); Rainbow Trout do not reproduce in the lake and are maintained via annual stocking (~10,000 fingerlings each August).
- **Prey categories** (as defined by the study):
  - **Terrestrial**: food items originating outside the lake (ants, beetles, Homoptera, Hymenoptera, etc.)
  - **Pelagic**: zooplankton (*Daphnia*, *Holopedium*, *Diaptomus*) and fish
  - **Benthic**: permanent bottom dwellers (snails, clams, oligochaetes) plus transient juvenile aquatic insects (chironomid larvae, caddisfly larvae, dragonfly/damselfly nymphs, Heleidae)
  - **Debris**: unidentifiable/non-food material (cigarette filters, rocks, insect wings, pine needles)
- **Seasonal dynamics**: Pelagic food peaked in late summer coinciding with the *Daphnia* population peak. Under winter ice cover, benthos comprised virtually 100% of the diet for both species; terrestrial feeding resumed upon spring thaw.
- **Ontogenetic diet shift**: Fingerling Rainbow Trout consumed small terrestrial insects and cladocerans post-stocking; overwintering small Rainbow Trout consumed copepods (probably *Diaptomus novamexicanus*). Benthic food importance in Rainbow Trout decreases with age ([[Wales 1946]]).

## Key Findings

1. **Dietary divergence**: Rainbow Trout ate ~3× more terrestrial food than Brook Trout; Brook Trout ate ~3× more benthic food than Rainbow Trout. Pelagic food was split roughly evenly between species. Debris comprised a fairly constant 40–60% of stomach volume across both species.
2. **Zooplankton**: *Daphnia rosea* was the dominant zooplankton prey for both trout species.
3. **Caloric contribution by species**:
   - **Brook Trout** — dominant caloric sources: [[Dragonfly Nymphs]] (61.6%), ants (11.3%), *Daphnia* (10.0%), chironomid larvae (8.2%) → 91.1% of total calories consumed.
     - Category breakdown: **75.9% benthic, 14.1% terrestrial, 10.0% pelagic**.
   - **Rainbow Trout** — dominant caloric sources: ants (42.8%), *Daphnia* (25.6%), beetles (11.1%), dragonfly nymphs (8.1%) → 87.6% of total calories consumed.
     - Category breakdown: **55.9% terrestrial, 25.6% pelagic, 18.5% benthic**.
   - **Combined (both species)**: 48.2% terrestrial, 36.3% benthic, 20.7% pelagic energy contribution.
4. **Benthic energy utilization mismatch**: Chironomid larvae held the largest share of total benthic standing energy (83.8%) but only 5.8% of that potential energy was actually consumed by trout — likely because they live below the flocculent mud-water interface, protected from predation.
   - Conversely, more exposed benthic taxa were consumed at much higher rates relative to their standing energy: **dragonfly nymphs (59.8% utilized)**, **caddisfly larvae (37.1%)**, **damselfly nymphs (33.5%)** — consistent with their exposed positions on vegetation/rocks.
5. **Management implication**: Because much of the benthic energy pool (especially chironomids) is functionally unavailable to trout due to microhabitat refugia, increasing fish yield may require deliberate food-web manipulation (e.g., micronutrient fertilization, introduction of new prey items) rather than simply increasing benthic productivity — though such manipulation carries risk of unintended energy loss without full systems understanding.
6. **Comparative context**: Findings align with [[Lord (1938)]]'s Vermont stream study showing Brook Trout aquatic food intake ranging from 38% (summer) to 98% (winter) by volume — broadly consistent with the strong winter benthic dependence observed in Castle Lake.
