# Zoogeographic Patterns of North American Subterranean Amphipod Crustaceans

#carcinology #marine-biology #Amphipoda

## Executive Summary

This review analyzes the zoogeography of the subterranean amphipod fauna of North America, which includes Mexico, Central America and the West Indies. The author reports 169 species in 28 genera and 10 families or family groups, most of them [[Stygobiont]]s (obligate aquatic subterranean species). Three zoogeographic patterns are proposed: (1) ancient freshwater distribution, (2) marine relict distribution, and (3) marine-brackish water distribution. Each pattern is linked to a principal taxon: [[Stygobromus]] for pattern 1, the [[Weckeliid Group]] of [[Hadziidae]] for pattern 2, and [[Metaniphargus]] for pattern 3. The analysis relies on current distributions, cladistic hypotheses, and vicariance and dispersal events tied to plate tectonics, marine regressions, Pleistocene glaciation and karst development.

## Background & Description

Subterranean amphipods are defined as species occurring in five subsurface settings: cave-stream and pool groundwater; phreatic waters in wells and fissures; interstitial media beneath or beside streams (hyporheic and parafluvial) and in littoral and sublittoral sediments; outflow of seeps through fine sediments; and [[Anchialine Habitat]] pools with subterranean sea connections.

Two ecological categories are used. Stygobionts (also called phreatobites, or troglobites when cave-bound) show loss of eyes and pigment and often have elongated bodies or appendages. Stygophiles occur in both surface and subsurface habitats and show little morphological modification. Stygobionts make up about 81% of the species in the broader inventory, which the author attributes to their strong ability to colonize subterranean habitats.

The classification follows [[Bousfield Classification]] (1978, 1982, 1983), although the author notes it remains controversial. Table 1 lists 28 genera with subterranean species. Excluded are two coastal hadziid genera, *Dulzura* and *Protohadzia*, which were collected from open water and not from subterranean habitats.

The paper focuses on [[Crangonyctidae]] and the weckeliid group of Hadziidae, which the author has studied for many years. Earlier views on *Stygobromus* zoogeography were revised as more species were described and as [[Vicariance Biogeography]] and plate tectonics became accepted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Crangonyctids and their allies are cold-stenothermal, photonegative amphipods. They are known almost exclusively from small, secluded groundwater biotopes. About 126 of 154 described extant crangonyctid species (82%) are stygobionts.

Species richness is strongly tied to geomorphology and climate. *Stygobromus* has 97 described North American species, most in the eastern half of the continent, where [[Karst Terrain]] with many caves and extensive aquifers occurs in the Appalachians, Interior Low Plateaus, Ozarks and [[Edwards Aquifer]] region. West of the Great Plains, only 23 described species are known. The author links this disparity to younger tectonic activity in the Cordillera, lower cave density, and a drier climate that limits aquifer recharge.

Pleistocene glaciation altered distributions. Most pre-Pleistocene groundwater fauna were likely extirpated, but some *Stygobromus* species may have survived in [[Subglacial Refugia]], supported by *S. canadensis* in a Canadian Rockies cave that is still partly covered by an active glacier. In contrast, *Bactrurus mucronatus* appears to have colonized glaciated areas after the Pleistocene, based on its occurrence in glacial drift.

Marine regressions and transgressions shaped the weckeliid and bogidielloid distributions. Pattern 3 taxa are found in habitats from fully marine to marginally fresh water, roughly 400–1000 mg/l dissolved salts, on and around West Indian islands.

## Key Findings

**Pattern 1: Ancient freshwater distribution**
- Taxa are restricted to Holarctic continental freshwater and have no known marine relatives. Examples include *Allocrangonyx* (2 species), *Bactrurus* (3 species, endemic to eastern North America), *Stygobromus*, *Crangonyx* and *Synurella*.
- Crangonyctidae sensu stricto and its satellite genera (*Allocrangonyx*, *Pseudocrangonyx*, *Procrangonyx*) are argued to be independent clades that split from the crangonyctid lineage long ago, meriting full family-level recognition (Holsinger, in prep.).
- The fossil genus [[Palaeogammarus]] (Eocene to Oligocene, Baltic amber) suggests the "crangonyctid facies" had evolved in freshwater by the early Tertiary.
- *Stygobromus* has 97 described North American species and two Eurasian species. Subgeneric group A is the largest and most widespread, with 79 described species plus about 62 undescribed species. Groups B, C and D have 4, 14 and 2 described species respectively.
- Cladistic analysis of *Stygobromus* subgroups matches their geographic distributions. The oldest, most "primitive" group (A) is the most widespread.
- *Crangonyx* has 19 to 22 or 23 described species, with fewer than half stygobiont. Five North American species are stygobionts, and about 10 stygobionts are expected once the undescribed packardi complex is described. Florida karst supports *Crangonyx* as the only subterranean amphipod, which the author attributes to young Cenozoic limestones and to ancestors on the Coastal Plain.
- *Synurella* has four North American species, only one of which (*S. dentata*) is recorded subterranean, as a marginal stygophile. The author rejects Barnard & Barnard's (1983) synonymy of *Synurella* with *Stygobromus*.
- The author proposes two adaptation levels within pattern 1. Level 1 (*Allocrangonyx*, *Bactrurus*, *Stygobromus*) consists of highly specialized, exclusively subterranean genera with no epigean congeners, likely subterranean since the early Tertiary or earlier. Level 2 (*Crangonyx*, and *Synurella* in eastern Europe) comprises less specialized, mostly epigean-related species that are probably no older than the late Tertiary.
- Stygobromus species are estimated to have invaded western North America after a vicariant split across the Great Plains in late Tertiary times, possibly driven by Miocene aridity or Pleistocene glaciation. A Wisconsin member of the hubbsi group lies about 1400 km from its nearest relative in Colorado, supporting a previously continuous distribution.

**Pattern 2: Marine relict distribution**
- Taxa are freshwater but have marine or brackish relatives. They are hypothesized to have been stranded during Late Cretaceous and Tertiary marine transgressions, as in the [[Regression Model]] of Stock (1977a).
- The [[Weckeliid Group]] of Hadziidae comprises nine mostly monotypic genera, which the author considers the most interesting example. Genera are recorded from south-central Texas, northern Mexico, the Yucatan Peninsula, Cuba and Puerto Rico. Each cladistically derived group is geographically discrete.
- Stranding of ancestral weckeliids in northern Mexico and south-central Texas probably occurred between the Late Cretaceous and the Eocene. Puerto Rican weckeliids probably originated during emergence of the island in the middle or late Miocene, and western Cuban forms in the late Oligocene. *Mayaweckelia* of Yucatan is the most recent colonist, since the area was not fully freed of marine water until the late Tertiary.
- Bogidielloids (Artesiidae and Bogidiellidae) are more freshwater-oriented and better represented in the Southern Hemisphere. *Artesia* and *Parabogidiella* occur in south-central Texas, and *Bogidiella* in southern Mexico and Guatemala. These are interpreted as stranded marine forms. Stock (1981) proposed either marine origin with later freshwater invasion, or Pangaean freshwater origin with later brackish invasion.
- The sebid *Seborgia relicta*, known only from the [[Edwards Aquifer]], is considered a relict of a marine embayment in the Late Cretaceous or early Tertiary. Its congener *S. minima* lives in an oligohaline-brackish lake on Rennell Island in the Indo-West Pacific. Sebids (2 genera, 14 described species) are proposed as good candidates for interstitial colonization during marine regressions.
- *Paraweckelia*, a monotypic genus from a single central Cuban cave, was moved to Melitidae because of its affinity with marine ceradocids.

**Pattern 3: Marine-brackish water distribution**
- Taxa live mainly in marine to brackish littoral, sublittoral and anchialine habitats, and sometimes in marginally fresh wells and caves near the coast. They are endemic to West Indian islands.
- The genus [[Metaniphargus]] (11 species) is the principal example. Most of its species are in this pattern, and Stock (1977a) explained their origin through stranding during middle Tertiary emergence of Antillean islands.
- Pattern 3 is described as a transitional stage between marine and freshwater ecology. The author notes that pattern 3 taxa are morphologically close to forms now fully isolated in freshwater, especially among bogidielloids and hadziids.

**Other points**
- Among 10 or so strictly freshwater *Gammarus* species from North America, only four are recorded subterranean. None is considered a true stygobiont. Subterranean populations probably invaded cave streams since the Pleistocene by migrating upstream from springs.
- The occurrence of Hadziidae in the Hawaiian Islands is described as a zoogeographic enigma that remains unexplained.
- The author argues that subterranean amphipods are strong candidates for zoogeographic study. They have limited dispersal, comparatively old lineages, and inhabit old, stable habitats that are relatively protected from perturbation.
