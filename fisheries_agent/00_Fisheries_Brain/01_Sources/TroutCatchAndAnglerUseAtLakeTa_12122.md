# Trout Catch and Angler Use at Lake Tahoe in 1962

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report presents the 1962 results of a joint [[California Department of Fish and Game]] and [[Nevada Fish and Game Commission]] creel census on [[Lake Tahoe]]. The census estimated total angler use at 144,147 angler-hours (47,732 angler-days), equal to 1.2 angler-hours per surface acre. The estimated total catch was 20,018 fish, or 0.14 fish per angler-hour. About 60 percent of the catch was [[Lake Trout]]. Wild [[Rainbow Trout]] made up 21 percent and planted trout 17 percent. Estimated yield, excluding planted trout, was 0.34 pounds per acre. The census was designed to create a baseline for a larger study aimed at improving trout fishing, and it relied on two coordinated surveys: a check station at [[Cave Rock Boat Landing]] and a lakewide [[Boat Census]].

## Background & Description

The study was started on July 1, 1960, as a joint project under Federal Aid to Fish Restoration funds (California F-21-R, Nevada F-15-R). The census had four stated purposes:

- Provide a quantitative description of the sport fishery.
- Establish a baseline so future changes can be detected.
- Evaluate experimental trout plants.
- Collect biological data from angler-caught fish.

Lake Tahoe is a 123,300-acre [[Oligotrophic Lakes|oligotrophic]] lake in a down-faulted trough formed in the late Tertiary. It has steep sides, a flat bottom, and a small littoral zone. The average depth is 1,027 feet and the maximum is 1,645 feet. The lake is roughly 12 miles wide and 22 miles long, with a 71-mile perimeter. Annual water-level fluctuation is only about 2 to 4 feet, and the watershed covers about 506 square miles.

The lake once supported a sizeable commercial and sport fishery for [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]. Juday (1907) reported commercial catches approaching 75,000 pounds in good years. That species became extinct during the 1930s. Anglers have been generally dissatisfied since then, despite several exotic game introductions and other management efforts.

The present fishery depends on lake trout and wild and planted rainbow trout. Smaller numbers of [[Brown Trout]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Eastern Brook Trout]], and [[Kokanee Salmon]] are also caught.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions**
- Water temperatures are cold: low 40s °F in winter, and mid-60s °F at the surface in summer.
- Dissolved oxygen is near saturation at all depths.
- Transparency is high, with Secchi disk readings usually 75 to 100 feet.
- Dissolved solids are low (about 60 to 65 ppm), mostly bicarbonates.
- pH usually ranges from 7.5 to 8.0.

**Fish distribution and fishing zones**
Net sampling indicated that game fish occur mainly in relatively shallow water, over less than half of the lake bottom. Because of fish distribution, most fishing is restricted to a narrow zone around the perimeter. The exception is the Tahoe City shelf, where anglers fish as far as 2.4 miles from shore and were located with binoculars.

**Angler access**
[[Cave Rock Boat Landing]] on the east shore, beside U.S. Highway 50, was the only sizeable public launch facility available in 1962. It has no piers. Anglers were classified as pier, shore, topline (non-metallic line from boats), or deepline (metallic line from boats) anglers.

## Key Findings

**Census design and sampling**
- The [[Creel Census]] used two coordinated surveys: a check station at Cave Rock, where anglers were interviewed after fishing, and a boat census covering the entire lake perimeter, where most anglers were contacted before finishing.
- Sampling covered 13.8 percent of estimated total angler-hours. The boat census checked 8.9 percent of lakewide hours, and Cave Rock checked 37.2 percent of hours originating there.
- Each angler's home town was assigned to one of 11 origin areas.

**Total use**
- Total 1962 use was 144,147 angler-hours (47,732 angler-days), or 1.2 angler-hours per surface acre. The authors describe this as extremely low.
- Use peaked at 35,671 hours in June (12,097 angler-days) and was concentrated from April through September. December was also high, which the authors attributed to good weather and holidays.
- Use by method was: deepline 39,046 hours (8,404 angler-days), topline 42,931 hours (12,060 angler-days), shore 27,752 hours (12,170 angler-days), and pier 34,422 hours (15,098 angler-days).
- Average angler-day lengths at Cave Rock were 4.65 hours for deepliners, 3.56 hours for toplines, and 2.28 hours for shore anglers.

**Fraction of Cave Rock anglers**
- The fraction of lakewide anglers originating at Cave Rock ranged from about 0.09 to 0.15 from April through October and from about 0.19 to 0.25 in other months.
- The fraction is higher in winter because Cave Rock stays free of snow while other access areas close.
- The 1961 and 1962 monthly fractions were similar, which suggests the ratio may be fairly constant within a given month.
- Total lake use was estimated by dividing Cave Rock angler-hours by this fraction.

**Catch**
- Total estimated catch was 20,018 fish, or 0.14 fish per angler-hour.
- Lake trout accounted for 11,967 fish (about 60 percent).
- Wild rainbow trout accounted for 4,125 fish (about 21 percent).
- Planted trout accounted for 3,365 fish (about 17 percent). This group includes several strains and species, and its evaluation is deferred to a separate report.
- Brown trout, whitefish, and eastern brook trout together made up about 2 percent.
- Within the wild catch, about 72 percent was lake trout and 25 percent was rainbow trout.
- Unmarked rainbow trout were assumed to be wild. Some may have been unmarked planted fish, a source of error the authors considered small.

**Yield**
- Yield, excluding planted trout, was 0.34 pounds per acre. The report's component values (0.28 lb lake trout, 0.05 lb rainbow trout, 0.01 lb brown trout) sum to 0.34, so the "0.84" in the detailed results appears to be a transcription error.
- Lake trout made up about 82 percent of the yield by weight.
- The authors note that yield per acre based on the whole lake has limited meaning. Because fish use less than half the lake bottom, per-acre yield in occupied areas could be about twice as high.

**Angler origin**
- About one-fourth of angler-days were by local anglers from within 5 miles of Lake Tahoe.
- Other significant origins, in descending order, were the San Francisco Bay area (Region 3), central California (Region 2), Reno and Sparks, and the Los Angeles area (Region 5).
- Anglers from distant areas fished mostly in spring and summer. Local anglers were less affected by season.

**Biases and limitations**
- The authors judged the methods acceptable but found biases that would cause use and catch to be underestimated.
- Early-morning censuses in July 1962 showed that 5.2 percent of all angler effort was completed before 9:30 a.m. (2.9 percent of hours). Cave Rock censuses started later than that, so some early effort was missed.
- Evening pier fishing on the California side often lasts after dark. Anglers outside the census window were likely missed, though the authors believe this error is small.
- Estimates are point estimates with no confidence limits. The authors offer the bias discussion as a rough guide to reliability.
- Weather did not appear to influence the Cave Rock fraction within the range of conditions that allowed boat censuses. One-boat and two-boat days, and weekday and weekend days, showed no consistent differences.

**Conclusions and planned work**
- The census provides a baseline for the joint study. Later reports are planned on the remaining census years and on the success of trout stocking practices, including the planted trout returns.
- The authors' bottom line is that the methods were sound, and that any remaining bias leads to underestimates of use and catch.
