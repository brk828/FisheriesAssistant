# Biogeochemistry of Nitrogen in Sonoran Desert Streams

#limnology #stream-ecology #nitrogen-biogeochemistry #periphyton

## Executive Summary

This 1992 review by Nancy B. Grimm (Arizona State University) synthesizes a 12-year study of nitrogen dynamics in [[Sycamore Creek]], a northern [[Sonoran Desert]] stream in Maricopa County, Arizona, and places it in the wider context of dryland streams of western North America. The central argument is that [[Nitrogen Limitation]] is common in arid-region streams, a pattern long overlooked because of an eastern North American bias toward [[Phosphorus]] limitation. Inorganic nitrogen is delivered in pulses by [[Flash Floods]], is retained and transformed in surface algae and the [[Hyporheic Zone]], and is exported or lost through several pathways. The review concludes that short, intense hydrologic events are the main windows for nitrogen movement from atmosphere to terrestrial to aquatic systems.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** Sycamore Creek is a moderately hard-water stream with conductivity of 300 to 500 µS/cm and alkalinity near 250 mg/L as CaCO3. Calcium dominates cations and bicarbonate dominates anions. Silica (5 to 10 mg/L) is unlikely to limit diatoms, and [[Soluble Reactive Phosphorus]] (SRP) averages near 50 µg/L with little variation.

**Hydrologic disturbance.** [[Flash Floods]] are the dominant structuring force. They can strip in-stream and riparian biota and reset sediment distributions. Channels are therefore wide relative to the wetted stream, and riparian vegetation casts little shade. High insolation and temperature favor high standing crops of algae and macrophytes.

**Hyporheic zone.** The saturated sediments beneath the streambed exchange water and nutrients with the surface channel and interact with groundwater, which in arid regions is recharged through stream channels. Its depth is set by alluvium depth over bedrock. This subsystem is central to both [[Ecosystem Metabolism]] and nutrient dynamics.

**Nitrogen forms.** Most inorganic nitrogen in Sycamore Creek is [[Nitrate]]. [[Ammonium]] concentrations reach as high as 50 µg/L in surface water.

**Study scope.** The review covers spatial and temporal variability of nitrogen, its sources, intrasystem transformations, and sinks. It draws on [[Grimm and Fisher]] surveys across [[Arizona Streams]] and comparative data from the [[Tallgrass Prairie]], the Pacific Northwest, and lakes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nutrient limitation concepts.** A limiting factor is one whose change produces a response in a variable such as biomass. Bioassays, which add a suspected limiting nutrient and compare the response to a control, are the proper test. Using environmental ratios alone is misleading. The review distinguishes:
- **Potential N limitation:** environmental [[Nitrogen-Phosphorus Ratio]] (N:P) lower than the optimum N:P of the primary producers.
- **Actual limitation:** demonstrated by enrichment experiments.

The [[Redfield Ratio]] (N:P of 16) is discussed as a reference point, and the review notes that algal mean cellular N:P in this system is 17.1.

**Regional patterns.** Southwestern streams were potentially N-limited at low flow (mean N:P of 11.4). Many streams of the Pacific Northwest are secondarily N-limited, though light limits small, heavily shaded headwaters. Lakes also show frequent N limitation (see [[Eutrophication]] literature). The review proposes that regional N limitation reflects physicochemically controlled, abundant phosphorus rather than unusually low nitrogen supply.

**Biological linkages.** Nitrogen supply shapes algal assemblages, favoring [[Cyanobacteria]] in late succession. Cyanobacteria are also endosymbionts of certain diatoms that colonize early. Nitrogen limitation of microbial colonization of detritus is hypothesized to degrade the food quality of fine particulate matter for [[Macroinvertebrates]], linking nitrogen to heterotrophic production.

**Spatial and temporal variability.**
- Nitrate declines longitudinally below springs and sources. Mean NO3-N was 491 µg/L at sources versus 68 µg/L in streams below sources at low flow.
- Hyporheic nitrate generally exceeds surface-water nitrate.
- Across 157 southwestern streams, the coefficient of variation was 128% for conductivity, 132% for SRP, and 221% for nitrate. Nitrate is therefore the most variable of these solutes.
- Interflood periods averaged about 60 days in Sycamore Creek. Interflood [[Dissolved Inorganic Nitrogen]] (DIN) is strongly skewed toward limiting levels, while SRP is normally distributed and does not fall below 20 µg/L.
- Precipitation DIN ranges from about 200 to over 4,000 µg/L. Runoff often exceeds precipitation concentrations, which indicates mobilization of additional DIN from desert soils.

**Intersystem transfers.** During floods, nitrogen moves from the desert catchment toward the stream, and this may be the largest long-term nitrogen source. Between floods, transfer reverses toward the land and groundwater through riparian uptake, aquatic insect emergence, algal stranding, and hyporheic-to-groundwater loss. [[Nitrogen Fixation]] by cyanobacteria such as *Anabaena*, *Calothrix*, and *Nostoc* may be the sole nitrogen input during interflood periods.

**Denitrification.** Anoxic zones beneath the sediment surface, created by high productivity and intense decomposition, can support [[Denitrification]], a potentially important nitrogen sink.

**Water quality implications.** Because stream channels recharge groundwater, nitrogen dynamics in streams may affect groundwater quality. The review argues that [[Peterjohn and Schlesinger]] (1990) erred in assuming no net nitrogen loss from semiarid catchments to streamflow.

## Key Findings

1. **Nitrogen limits production in Sycamore Creek.** Four nutrient enrichment bioassays showed nitrogen limitation at ambient concentrations as high as 55 µg/L, while phosphorus was neither primarily nor secondarily limiting. Phosphorus was adequate for periphyton growth even when nitrogen was in excess.

2. **Limitation is common.** Inorganic nitrogen was below 55 µg/L about 53% of the time (n = 211, non-flood samples), especially in summer low flow. Across southwestern streams, 82% of 92 low-flow sites were N-limited at this threshold.

3. **Floods are the principal nitrogen pulse.** Storm precipitation had mean NO3-N of 596 µg/L and NH4-N of 834 µg/L (n = 26 events). Floodwaters and sources of surface flow have similar chemistry, which the review attributes to hyporheic discharge.

4. **Nitrogen supply links to macroinvertebrate production.** Densities of macroinvertebrates aged over 30 days correlated positively with nitrogen flux (concentration × discharge; r = 0.74, p < 0.001) across five post-flood successional sequences.

5. **Hyporheic sediments release nitrogen.** In sediment cores, net release was 6.55 µg N mg AFDM⁻¹ h⁻¹ for NO3-N (SE = 1.55, n = 12) and 0.84 µg N mg AFDM⁻¹ h⁻¹ for NH4-N (SE = 0.76, n = 12). Ammonium release was highly variable and was immobilized in two cases. Mineralization C:N was 7.3, a low but plausible value for benthic particulate matter.

6. **Sediment recycling matters at the ecosystem scale.** When scaled to a 1 m² × 50 cm sediment column, sediment-mediated nitrogen recycling represents a significant fraction of surface-stream inorganic nitrogen utilization. The review concludes that hyporheic inputs may alleviate surface nitrogen limitation where exchange is hydrologically active.

7. **Nitrification dominates aerobic sediments.** Inhibitor experiments with nitrapyrin indicated that nitrification was the dominant process affecting nitrate, with losses to denitrification and dissimilatory reduction judged insignificant under aerobic conditions. Potential nitrification rates were similar to net release rates.

8. **Algal uptake explains stream nitrogen loss.** Nitrate uptake by isolated algal assemblages ranged from −7.76 to 51.7 mg m⁻² h⁻¹ (negative values indicate release). This overlaps the whole-system range of −1.4 to 38 mg m⁻² h⁻¹ reported for desert streams. Enriched assemblages took up nitrate faster in 7 of 9 comparisons (t-test, p < 0.05), and in three cases the enrichment response was an order of magnitude higher.

9. **Uptake is controlled by concentration and standing crop.** Chlorophyll-specific uptake increased with initial nitrate (consistent with enzyme saturation) and decreased with chlorophyll a standing crop. The regression equations explained 55 to 66% of variance in uptake. The pooled model (equation 4) had a multiple r² of 0.58.

10. **Predictions match observed retention.** Algal uptake predictions for seven dates (1981 to 1983) brought observed retention within the predicted 95% confidence limits in nearly all cases. The review concludes that algal assimilation is the main process retaining nitrate in desert streams, though this relationship weakens as algal mats thicken and anaerobic zones expand.

11. **Oxygen indicates hyporheic flow.** Hyporheic oxygen depends on residence time, surface concentrations at recharge sites, and microbial consumption. Interstitial velocities of 1 to 50 mm/s in clean gravels replenish oxygen, so aerobic transformations dominate there.

12. **Management implication.** Brief, intense hydrologic events are the key periods for nitrogen movement from atmosphere to land to water. Riparian aquatic productivity in arid landscapes can be a significant nitrogen source to linked ecosystems during long interflood intervals.
