# The Ecology of a Spring Stream: Doe Run, Meade County, Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae #cottidae #freshwater-ecology #spring-stream

## Executive Summary

This Wildlife Monographs issue (September 1963, No. 11) is W. L. [[Minckley]]'s comprehensive study of Doe Run, a limestone spring stream in Meade County, Kentucky, that drains directly to the [[Ohio River]]. Sponsored jointly by the [[United States Atomic Energy Commission]] and the [[University of Louisville]], the monograph was conducted during the impoundment period of the stream, when the lower reach was being dammed for a proposed reservoir. The study integrates physical hydrology, water chemistry, benthic algae, macrophytes, invertebrates, food relations and fishes into a single ecological account, and it concludes with a theoretical argument about ecological equivalence and climate in flowing waters.

## Background & Description

The monograph is the report of research carried out for a doctoral dissertation at the Department of Biology, University of Louisville, and was prepared for the Wildlife Monographs series of [[The Wildlife Society]]. The series was created to publish longer manuscripts that exceed the length limits of the *Journal of Wildlife Management*, and it is financed by sponsoring agencies.

Doe Run originates at a spring about 575 feet above mean sea level and enters the Ohio River at 427 feet, a fall of about 150 feet over roughly 9.7 miles. Its average gradient is 20.7 feet per mile. The stream is about 30 feet wide, narrowing to about 6 feet at some riffles and widening to over 50 feet at large pools. Five permanent sampling stations (I–V) were established, and three mill dams (Dams 1–3) created impoundments in the upper reaches. Upstream reaches are bedrock-dominated with a 100–200-yard floodplain, while downstream from Mile 5.0 a broader alluvial floodplain developed. Lands along the valley were historically cleared and cultivated.

Methods included:
- Water chemistry (dissolved oxygen, pH, alkalinity, free CO2, turbidity, total iron, phosphate, nitrate and nitrite) using standard procedures, with duplicate analyses and 24-hour diurnal studies.
- Quantitative benthos sampling with a Surber sampler and a 6×6-inch dredge, sorted and weighed.
- Qualitative collections and gut-content analyses of invertebrates and fishes (gut contents from 10–20 individuals per species per period).
- Fishes collected by seines, electric shocker, hoop nets, experimental gill nets and rotenone.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Doe Run is a second-magnitude spring with a modal discharge of about 20–40 cfs; mean discharge at Station I (October 1959–October 1961) was about 65 cfs and about 140 cfs at Station III. Discharge was highly variable, with flood peaks of 300 cfs at Station I (June 23, 1960). Much of the flow is subterranean, and surface inflow from rainfall was pronounced only in some tributaries (e.g., [[Rushing River]]).

**Water chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen was usually above 70% saturation and often exceeded 95%, with photosynthetic supersaturation in summer above Dam 1. Water chemistry showed a sharp shift in the marl section: alkalinity and carbon dioxide reduction downstream from Mile 2.36 led to calcium carbonate precipitation as [[Marl]]. Total iron was 0.31–0.36 mg/l at Stations I and III, largely colloidal. Phosphate and nitrate declined downstream, and nitrate in the aquifer suggested fertilizer input. Water temperature at Station I was consistently 13.0–13.5 °C in modal discharge (13.3 °C typical).

**Benthic algae and plants.** Benthic algae dominated primary production. Key taxa included [[Cladophora glomerata]] (dominant in swift riffles), [[Batrachospermum]], [[Lemanea]], [[Nostoc]], [[Phormidium]] and [[Oscillatoria]]. Macrophytes included [[Fissidens]] moss beds (upstream, spring-dominated fauna), [[Nasturtium officinale]] (watercress), [[Myriophyllum]] (milfoil), [[Nitella]] and [[Mimosa]]-type emergents such as [[Sagittaria]]. Marl formation was linked to algal and plant activity, and Doe Run marl accumulated at rates of about 25 mm per year in one estimate.

**Invertebrates.** The macrobenthos comprised over 100 taxa, including turbellarians ([[Dugesia]], [[Phagocata]]), oligochaetes, isopods ([[Asellus]]), amphipods ([[Gammarus bousfieldi]] and [[Gammarus minus]]), crayfishes ([[Cambarus bartoni]], [[Orconectes rusticus]]), mayflies ([[Baetis]], [[Ephemerella]], [[Isonychia]], [[Hexagenia]]), stoneflies ([[Paragnetina media]], [[Isogenus]]), caddisflies (Hydropsyche, [[Agraylea]]), and many Diptera (chironomids, [[Tipula]], [[Atherix]]). Invertebrate density at Station II ranged to about 18,000 individuals per square meter in moss riffles. Food analyses placed Asellus and Gammarus as detritivores and Paragnetina as a predator.

**Fishes.** A total of 66 fish species was collected from Doe Run. Abundant and common species included [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*), [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*), [[Black Nose Dace]] (*Rhinichthys atratulus*), [[Banded Sculpin]] (*Cottus carolinae*), [[Central Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*), [[Emerald Shiner]] and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*). Gizzard shad ([[Dorosoma cepedianum]]) and carp were present downstream. Stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) persisted briefly with limited reproduction. Thirteen species were classed as rare, and 18 further species were found only occasionally or in backwaters associated with Ohio River influence.

**Anthropogenic influences.** Mill dams, road crossings, channel clearing, and the 1961 impoundment for [[Doe Valley Lake]] substantially altered the stream. Rotenone eradication in July 1961 and subsequent flooding removed much of the fish community, which recovered from upstream and downstream sources in ensuing months.

## Key Findings

- **Physical controls:** Discharge, gradient and the mill-dam sequence were primary determinants of temperature, dissolved oxygen, turbidity and substrate; the flow regime was strongly modulated by subterranean aquifer inputs.
- **Carbonate chemistry:** The marl section represents a zone where carbon dioxide loss and photosynthesis drove calcium carbonate precipitation, even in the presence of persistent supersaturation of bicarbonate.
- **Benthic assemblages:** Three assemblages were recognized: a spring (Fissidens-dominated) assemblage, a pool assemblage, and a riffle assemblage. Station II represents a transition zone.
- **Invertebrate dynamics:** Invertebrate standing crops were relatively stable in Fissidens and milfoil beds but fluctuated with flood disturbance; oligochaetes and dipterans increased during modal discharge and in the absence of watercress.
- **Fish distribution:** Fish distributions were controlled by gradient, temperature and barriers (falls and dams); white suckers exhibited limited home ranges with restricted movements, and riffles acted as barriers.
- **Food webs:** Detritus formed a major energy base, with autochthonous algae and allochthonous leaf litter important for many invertebrates and fishes; the author argued that detrital inputs increase downstream.
- **Theoretical conclusions:** Stream "climates" and ecologically equivalent communities, rather than taxonomic groupings, provide a more useful framework for stream ecology; succession and climax concepts require modification for flowing waters.
- **Management implications:** Impoundment and dam construction cause large changes in fish and invertebrate communities; rotenone treatment and flood events provide natural experiments on recovery.
