# Size-Selective Predation on Daphnia by Rainbow Trout and Yellow Perch

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by Merle G. Galbraith, Jr. (Institute for Fisheries Research, Michigan Department of Conservation) tested whether [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) and [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) select Daphnia by size, and whether fish predation changes the size structure of daphnid populations. Fish stomachs were compared with net plankton samples from two [[Michigan Lakes]]: Stager Lake, a eutrophic "combination" lake managed for trout and warmwater game fish, and Sporley Lake, a lake reclaimed with toxaphene and managed solely for trout.

Both fish species were strongly size selective. They ate mostly [[Daphnia]] larger than 1.3 mm and ignored smaller, often more abundant, zooplankton. In Sporley Lake, trout and smelt predation coincided with the loss of the large species [[Daphnia Pulex]], a drop in mean daphnid size, and a shift toward smaller zooplankton. The author argues that abundance measured by number or volume can mislead when assessing food available to planktivorous fish, and that the abundance of Daphnia of the right size matters more.

## Background & Description

Galbraith's work was part of a broader investigation of how food affects the survival of rainbow trout planted in Michigan lakes. Stomach examinations had shown that Daphnia was the principal plankton eaten by stocked rainbows. Earlier studies had mostly used density or biomass as indices of availability to fish, and paid little attention to Daphnia size. The paper cites prior work on size-specific predation: [[Hall 1964]] on ciscoes and black crappies, Gerking (1962) on bluegills, and [[Brooks and Dodson 1965]], who linked alewife predation to loss of large Daphnia in Connecticut lakes.

Study design:
- **Sporley Lake:** 76 acres, maximum depth 36 ft, sparse rooted plants, sand bottom, total alkalinity 11–32 ppm CaCO3. Toxaphene treatment in fall 1955 eradicated fish; the lake stayed toxic until 1959. Rainbows were planted from fall 1959 at 40 fingerlings and 13 legal trout (7–9 in) per acre. [[Fathead Minnow]] appeared in 1960; [[Rainbow Smelt]] was illegally introduced in 1962.
- **Stager Lake:** more eutrophic, abundant vegetation, maximum depth 51 ft, total alkalinity 60–137 ppm CaCO3. Resident species included yellow perch, bluegills, largemouth and smallmouth bass, common suckers, and pumpkinseeds. Rainbows were introduced in fall 1958 at 27 fish per acre.

Sampling covered 1957–1965, mainly July–September. Twenty-five random open-water stations were set in each lake. Plankton was collected with a Clarke-Bumpus sampler (No. 10 mesh) hauled vertically through the epilimnion (to 28 ft at Sporley, 25 ft at Stager). Daphnia were identified to species by Dr. J. L. Brooks (Yale University), measured from the top of the head to the base of the spine, and grouped into 0.28 mm size classes. Size at maturity was defined as the length of the smallest female carrying eggs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Size-specific predation.** Fish stomachs held 6,252 Daphnia eaten by 35 rainbow trout and 5,768 eaten by 24 yellow perch, collected over several years and seasons. In the summer subset with paired plankton data (24 trout, 17 perch), up to 46% of the Daphnia population in Sporley Lake and 58% in Stager Lake were small (below 1.3 mm), yet 96% of Daphnia eaten by trout and 82% eaten by perch exceeded 1.3 mm.

**Possible mechanisms.** The author considered two explanations: fish either pick out large individuals one at a time, or ingest all sizes and let the gill rakers sieve them. Very few small copepods and cladocerans appeared in stomachs, and any that did were at least as large as the smallest Daphnia eaten. A gill-raker test on 26 rainbow trout (4.2–18.2 in) and 10 yellow perch (1.5–13.2 in) showed many openings below 1.1 mm, and about 20% of spacings between the 4th and 12th rakers were under 1.4 mm. Filtration alone should therefore have retained smaller plankters, so some form of active discrimination is implied.

**Fish length.** Trout measured 7.9–17.2 in and perch 2.8–9.8 in. Regression of prey size on fish length was significant only for rainbow trout in Stager Lake (slope b = 0.051, P < 0.01). The other two regressions were not significant at the 5% level, so the author judged a general length–prey size relationship difficult to establish.

**Food-web buffering.** Stager Lake's more diverse fauna showed no detectable change in Daphnia after trout introduction. Its wider prey base was thought to buffer predation effects. Sporley Lake, with fewer species after toxicant treatment, was more vulnerable.

**Maturity and food level.** Size at first reproduction indicates food level. Before 1960, [[Daphnia Pulex]] in both lakes matured at about 1.8 mm. In 1960, some Sporley Lake individuals reproduced at 1.2 mm. In Sporley Lake in 1964, [[Daphnia Galeata Mendota]] and [[Daphnia Retrocurva]] matured at 0.8 mm, compared with 1.4 mm in Stager Lake from 1958 to 1960.

## Key Findings

1. **Strong size selectivity.** Trout and perch ate Daphnia over 1.3 mm and largely ignored smaller, often more numerous, zooplankton. Daphnids were the only zooplankton eaten by trout in both lakes, and by perch in the combination lake.

2. **No consistent length–prey relationship.** Only the Stager Lake trout regression was significant (b = 0.051, P < 0.01). The author treated generalization as difficult.

3. **Sporley Lake community change.** After trout, [[Rainbow Smelt]], and [[Fathead Minnow]] became established, D. pulex disappeared between 1960 and 1964, replaced by D. retrocurva and D. galeata mendota. Mean daphnid length fell from 1.4 mm (1958–60) to 0.8 mm (1964–65). The percentage of Daphnia larger than 1.3 mm dropped from an average of 53.8% to 4.7%. In some 1964–65 monthly collections, no individuals exceeded 1.3 mm.

4. **Volume fell but density did not.** Analysis of variance showed a significant year effect (P < 0.01) for both volume and percentage composition. Daphnia volume and share of net plankton declined, while numbers stayed roughly stable and fluctuated sharply. The author read this as a less stable population.

5. **Replacement by smaller zooplankton.** [[Bosmina]], [[Chydorus]], and to a lesser extent copepods increased as large Daphnia declined. This matches the size-efficiency hypothesis of Brooks and Dodson, though the Sporley Lake Daphnia did not disappear entirely.

6. **Age-specific effects.** Because trout and perch selected a size class, they were also selecting certain ages. Removal that is not proportional to the population's size or age structure may lengthen population turnover time and increase vulnerability to predation.

7. **Implications for trout management.** The author proposed that the eventual failure of trout plantings in poor, warmwater-containing lakes may result from predators removing the larger zooplankton. Trout would then fail to compete for the remaining food. This hypothesis needs further testing, especially on whether Daphnia supply is critical to survival and at what times of year.

8. **Abundance metrics can mislead.** Total numbers or volume may suggest adequate Daphnia when few are of the size usable by rainbows. In September 1964 and 1965, trout ate almost no Daphnia at Sporley Lake, although large numbers had been found in their stomachs in other years. The author concludes that the abundance of size-appropriate Daphnia should be the focus of management studies.

9. **Surface feeding on large Daphnia.** On 11 November 1965 at Johnson Lake, Marquette County, the author observed trout feeding at the surface on large Daphnia that concentrated in surface films. This suggests visual, concentrated feeding can increase foraging efficiency.
