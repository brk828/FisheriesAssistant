# Development of Electric Fish Shocker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #electrofishing #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1957 New Hampshire Fish and Game report documents a multi-year (1953–1956) engineering investigation into [[Electrofishing]] methodology, prompted by the failure of conventional electric fish shockers to perform reliably in New Hampshire streams. Four shocker designs were tested: a 220V AC alternator-type unit, a commercial German-made "Salmo Super" shocker, and two experimental low-current pulse-type devices built under the project. While the experimental pulse-type shockers performed well in controlled laboratory tank tests, all four designs failed to consistently capture fish under real stream conditions. The investigation identified two primary causes of failure: (1) high electrical conductivity of New Hampshire stream substrates (bottom losses), which shunt current away from the water column, and (2) distortion of the electric field caused by rocks and boulders, creating zones of both dangerously concentrated voltage and inadequate voltage for effective fish capture. The report concludes that existing and experimental shockers have value only in low-obstruction, low-conductivity-bottom streams, and recommends against use in rocky, obstruction-heavy stream systems typical of northern New Hampshire.

## Background & Description

- **Objective**: Develop a lightweight, low-cost electric shocker suitable for [[Fish Population Sampling]] in New Hampshire streams with naturally high water electrical resistance, particularly in the northern part of the state.
- **Study Period**: 1953–1956.
- **Initial Trigger**: A 220V AC generator modeled after equipment used by Haskell (1954) in New York failed to capture any fish in New Hampshire streams during spring 1953 trials, using both grounded and ungrounded two-electrode systems.
- **Core Hypothesis**: A high-voltage, low-current pulsed signal could momentarily overcome high water resistance, allowing use of lightweight, low-power equipment.

### Equipment Tested

1. **Pulse-Type Shocker (Model 1)** — 12-watt draw; damped wave pulsed at 30–1,000 pulses/second exciting tuned circuits (3,000,000–28,000,000 cps range tested); discharged into water via two electrodes.
2. **Pulse-Type Shocker (Model 2)** — Incorporated a secondary earth circuit carrying current equal and opposite in polarity to the main catching circuit, intended to offset stream-bed losses.
3. **"Salmo Super" (German-made shocker)** — Commercial unit with built-in 12V battery charger (operable from 110/220V AC supply), selenium rectifiers, vibrator-driven transformer producing a pulsed waveform with a ~600–700V leading-edge "spike." Output regulated in four steps from 200–350V via secondary taps.
4. **220V AC Alternator** — Borrowed from Massachusetts Fish and Game Department; ~7x the power output of the experimental models; modeled on equipment used by Haskell (1954) in New York.

### Testing Methodology

- Laboratory testing in wooden tanks (6" × 8" × 48") using stream water samples from multiple locations.
- Field testing at [[Stirrup Iron Brook]] (Boscawen, NH), [[Stirrup Iron Pond]], [[Deer Meadow Brook]] (Boscawen, NH), the [[Baboosic Watershed]] (Milford, NH), and [[Swift River]] and its tributary [[Olivarian Brook]] (northern, rock-strewn NH stream).
- Water resistance measured in ohms/cm³; substrate resistance sampled from the top half-inch of stream bed, following methods described by Haskell (1954).
- Voltage-distortion experiments used a 3.5-inch polystyrene "fish probe" (modeled on Haskell's design) with copper disk electrodes connected to a vacuum tube voltmeter, tested in a tank with artificial glass obstructions simulating stream rocks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water Chemistry**: New Hampshire streams, especially in the north, exhibit unusually high electrical resistance (low conductivity) due to low mineral content — a major barrier to conventional [[Electrofishing]] techniques calibrated for higher-conductivity waters (e.g., New York streams per Haskell 1954).
- **Seasonal Conductivity Change**: Water conductivity increases somewhat as temperatures rise above 60°F (~15.5°C), likely due to organic matter decomposition. However, stream-bed (soil) conductivity increases at a nearly proportional rate, negating any net gain in shocker efficiency from seasonal timing.
- **Substrate Composition**: A critical variable — comparatively conductive stream beds (mud-sand, decaying organic matter) caused major current losses ("bottom losses"), diverting current away from the water column and into the earth.
- **Physical Habitat Structure**: Granite boulders and rocks (common in northern NH streams like [[Swift River]]) are less conductive than surrounding water, causing current to divert around them. This produces:
  - Up to a 50% voltage reduction ("shading") in zones behind/around obstructions — allowing fish to shelter safely near rocks.
  - Dangerous voltage concentration (measured up to ~3x baseline) in narrow unobstructed flow paths between obstructions.
- **Site-Specific Results**:
  - Southern, low-gradient, mud-sand streams with few obstructions ([[Deer Meadow Brook]], [[Baboosic Watershed]]) yielded moderate-to-good shocker performance.
  - Northern, boulder-strewn, high-current streams ([[Swift River]], [[Olivarian Brook]]) yielded consistently poor results regardless of shocker type or power.

## Key Findings

- **Pulse-Type Shocker (Model 1)**: Successfully induced narcosis over a 14-foot path in laboratory wood-tank tests using very high peak voltage but extremely low current (0.001–0.003 mA). No fish migration toward the positive electrode was observed at the 1,000 cps pulse rate. However, it failed completely in real streams due to current shunting through conductive stream-bed soil rather than through the water.
- **Pulse-Type Shocker (Model 2, opposing earth circuit)**: Showed more field promise — 8 fish captured — but effective catching area was confined to a narrow straight line between ground probes, insufficient for practical field sampling.
- **Salmo Super**:
  - At [[Stirrup Iron Brook]] (rock bottom, 2,800 ohms/cm³, 52°F): only 15 of 100 stocked fish recovered; probe had to be within 3 feet of fish; captured fish escaped immediately if current was cut.
  - At [[Stirrup Iron Pond]]: shocker successfully dispersed fish schooling near shore within 5 minutes, though this did not translate into efficient capture.
  - At [[Deer Meadow Brook]] (mud-sand bottom, 2,400 ohms/cm³, 64°F, few obstructions): best performance recorded — 60% catching success, with 4–8 trout collectible simultaneously on the probe.
  - At [[Baboosic Watershed]] (1,600 ohms/cm³, 80°F): zero fish captured despite confirmed fish presence (verified via later rotenone sampling); mechanical/electrical failures compounded poor results.
  - At [[Olivarian Brook]]/[[Swift River]] (rocky bottom, boulders 0.5–6+ cubic feet, 3,100 ohms/cm³, 50°F): only 1 seven-inch trout captured; fish were observed to be stunned only transiently before drifting into unaffected zones of the field.
- **220V AC Alternator**: Produced good-to-excellent results in the low-obstruction [[Baboosic Watershed]] under low-flow conditions (73°F), but performance declined in the same location during higher flow (66°F) thirty days later, and failed almost entirely at [[Swift River]].
- **Field Distortion Experiments (Tank Studies)**:
  - No obstruction: uniform electric field, weakest at tank center, strongest near electrodes.
  - Single obstruction: ~3:1 voltage decrease in the "corner" behind the obstruction; ~50% voltage attenuation across roughly one-third of the catching area; voltage concentration in the unobstructed 1"×5" free path exactly matching the un-obstructed centerline reading (10V).
  - Two obstructions: consistent ~50% voltage decrease in the "shade" of obstructions, with voltage rising to a maximum in the narrow free path between them.
  - Results were consistent (voltage ratios stable) across water samples from four different streams, indicating the distortion effect is structural (obstruction-driven) rather than water-chemistry-driven.
- **Root Causes of Failure Identified**:
  1. High stream-bed conductivity relative to water conductivity → excessive current loss to substrate ("bottom losses").
  2. Physical field distortion from rocks/boulders → uneven voltage distribution, sheltering fish near obstructions while creating hazardous over-concentration in open channels.
- **Safety/Reliability Implication**: Increasing shocker power to compensate for bottom losses and field distortion is technically feasible but was assessed to produce unreliable population data, increase fish mortality, and pose a hazard to field personnel.
- **Final Recommendation**: Electric shockers should be restricted to censusing efforts in comparatively rock-free, low-obstruction streams, where moderate success has been demonstrated at existing power outputs; they are not recommended for rock-strewn, high-conductivity-bottom streams typical of northern New Hampshire.
