# Recovery of Endangered Fishes of Upper Colorado River Basin - Conservation Plan Proposal

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #river-ecology

## Executive Summary

This June 1983 document from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Region 6 presents a preliminary conservation plan for three endangered native fishes of the upper Colorado River basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). Part I describes the current precarious status of these species, which have experienced dramatic population declines (60-70% for adult squawfish, 94% for young-of-year over 20 years) and range contractions due to water development projects, habitat alteration, and non-native fish introductions. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] now occupies only 25% of its historical 3,540-mile range; [[Humpback Chub]] populations persist primarily in [[Black Rocks Canyon]] and [[Westwater Canyon]]; and [[Bonytail Chub]] is functionally extinct in the upper basin with no authenticated collections since 1971. Part II proposes a comprehensive 15-year conservation program with six management goals, estimated to cost $25 million, funded through FWS budgets and Section 7 consultation assessments based on water depletion volumes. The plan establishes minimum flow and temperature criteria for spawning and rearing, identifies essential habitats, and prescribes research, monitoring, habitat enhancement, fish passage, and hatchery propagation programs to prevent extinction and achieve recovery.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] system, the fifth largest in the United States, traverses 1,700 miles with over 2,800 miles of major tributaries across five western states. Due to long geologic isolation, the basin contains 74% endemic fishes (Miller 1958). Originally 35 native species inhabited the drainage, with 23 belonging to Cyprinidae and Catostomidae families. The upper basin now supports 55 fish species: 42 non-native and only 13 native (Tyus et al. 1981).

**Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): The largest minnow in North America, historically occupied approximately 3,540 river miles throughout the basin. Now restricted to about 880 miles (25%) in the upper basin only, extirpated from 75% of former range. Population trends show 60-70% decline in juveniles/adults and 94% decline in young-of-year over 20 years (1960-1980). Highly mobile, utilizing widely dispersed habitats to complete life cycle. Adults captured more frequently in [[Green River]] than [[Colorado River]] during 1979-1981 studies.

**Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): Historically limited to deep canyon areas of Colorado and Green rivers, approximately 1,200 miles. Presently, sizeable populations exist only in [[Westwater Canyon]] and [[Black Rocks Canyon]] (Ruby Canyon) in the upper basin, plus [[Grand Canyon]] near [[Little Colorado River]] mouth in lower basin. Tag-recapture data demonstrate relatively sedentary, localized populations with all life stages present within short river reaches. No distinct self-sustaining population confirmed in [[Green River]] system; captures very dispersed suggesting preferred habitats may no longer exist.

**Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*): Formerly abundant and widely distributed in mainstem Colorado and Green rivers plus larger tributaries. Distribution reduced to about 2% of original range. Only a few senile individuals remain in [[Lake Mohave]] (lower basin). No authenticated specimens collected in upper basin since 1971 (Holden and Stalnaker 1975). Probably the rarest of the three species. Vanicek (1967) collected 69 adult bonytails versus 49 roundtails in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] during 1964-1966, but populations collapsed rapidly after dam construction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Basin Development History**: The [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922 divided the basin at [[Lee Ferry, Arizona]] into upper and lower basins, each allocated 7.5 million acre-feet (maf) annually. Lower basin development began with [[Laguna Dam]] (1909), followed by [[Hoover Dam]] and eight additional dams by 1966 on 410 miles of lower mainstem. The [[Gila River]] system now has eight major dams with minimal discharge to Colorado River. Upper basin development accelerated with [[Colorado River Storage Project]] (CRSP) Act (1956), authorizing four storage units: [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (Green River), [[Navajo Dam]] (San Juan River), [[Wayne N. Aspinall Unit]] (Gunnison River), and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (Colorado River). Nine major dams now line upper mainstem and tributaries.

**Hydrologic Alterations**: Post-development spring runoff flows reduced 32% in Green and Colorado rivers from historic conditions; average non-runoff flows increased 23%. Daily flow fluctuations below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] increased nearly 4-fold due to hydroelectric operations, affecting over 100 river miles with oscillations up to 30% of mean daily flows during base periods. Of 1,850 river miles historically occupied by endangered fishes in upper basin: 240 miles isolated by dams/diversions, 350 miles impounded, 160 miles adversely affected by temperature changes. Future plans would impound another 125 miles and obstruct 139 miles of tributary streams.

**Temperature Regime Changes**: Water temperatures substantially depressed in 220-mile reach between [[Lee Ferry]] and [[Lake Mead]] headwaters, unsuitable for endemic fish reproduction despite being essentially unmodified riparian habitat. Cold-water releases from dams have altered thermal regimes critical for spawning and early life stages.

**Non-Native Fish Impacts**: Of 42 non-native species in upper basin, only 5 restricted to impoundments; 16 species occupying riverine reaches are predatory (Tyus et al. 1982). Species such as [[Northern Pike]], [[Walleye]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] have expanded from reservoirs into adjacent riverine habitats. Lower basin now sustains 37 non-native versus 6 native species (Minckley 1979). Competition and predation from non-natives pose direct threats to young native fishes.

**Historical Precedents**: Lower basin provides cautionary model: in [[Salt River]] system (1890-1967), 12 of 14 native fishes disappeared while 20 non-natives established, coincident with flow modifications (Minckley and Deacon 1968). Between [[Davis Dam]] and International Boundary, nine native fishes extirpated or exceedingly rare. In [[Grand Canyon]], only three native fishes remain in significant numbers; four are extinct (Carothers and Minckley 1981).

**Habitat Requirements - Colorado Squawfish**: Spawning occurs June 16-July 31 in specific reaches requiring minimum flows of 2,400-4,000 cfs and temperatures of 20-22°C. Young-of-year (YOY) rearing (August 1-October 15) requires 500-2,000 cfs and 20-28°C. Essential spawning reaches identified: Colorado River at Salt Wash-Westwater (RM 75-112) and Black Rocks-Gunnison confluence (RM 137-170); Green River at Gray Canyon (RM 146-156); Yampa River at Green confluence-Harding Hole (RM 0-20). Small squawfish drift downstream after hatching; upstream migration of juveniles and adults occurs to occupy otherwise uninhabited reaches. Extensive pre- and post-spawning adult movements complicate distribution patterns.

**Habitat Requirements - Humpback Chub**: Spawning May 1-June 15 requires minimum 10,000 cfs and 16-18°C in [[Westwater Canyon]] (RM 116-124) and [[Black Rocks]] (RM 135-137). Populations confined to narrow, deep canyon areas that change little with moderate discharge variations, suggesting less sensitivity to flow changes than squawfish. Valdez and Clemmer (1982) hypothesized that reduced flows and accelerated temperature increases cause overlapping spawning periods with [[Roundtail Chub]], potentially leading to hybridization. Maintenance of high spring flows critical for genetic isolation.

**Flow Criteria Development**: Minimum instantaneous flows for November-May based on 90% exceedence flow prior to major development (Table 3). Preliminary spring runoff peak requirements set at twice mean annual flow (May-June) to maintain channel morphology and natural hydrogeomorphic processes (Table 4): 17,000 cfs for Colorado River (Gunnison-Green confluence), 13,000 cfs for Green River (Colorado-White confluence), with specific criteria for other reaches. Natural flows should be maintained during drought when criteria cannot be met.

## Key Findings

**Current Population Status and Trends**:
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] populations declined 60-70% for juveniles/adults and 94% for young-of-year over 20 years (1960-1980) in Green and Yampa rivers
- Catch per effort data (1979-1981) show variable distribution by river reach and life stage (Table 1)
- [[Humpback Chub]] populations at [[Black Rocks]] and [[Westwater Canyon]] show similar densities (approximately 5.0 and 7.5 fish captures per river mile respectively for 1979-1981)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] population trend shows precipitous decline from 0.50 captures/river mile (1960) to functional extirpation by 1980
- No authenticated bonytail collections in upper basin since 1971

**Section 7 Consultation Framework**:
- Two basic assumptions: (1) three species in precarious state bordering extinction; (2) certain essential environmental components cannot be altered without negative impacts
- Streamflow and temperature criteria established for various life stages in primary occurrence reaches
- Conservation measure funding based on water depletion volumes as common denominator
- Bureau of Reclamation (1982) estimates 1.675 million acre-feet remains available in Green/upper Colorado system (excluding 231,000 acre-feet for Arizona/New Mexico)
- Project depletions assessed against this total to calculate contributions toward conservation measures
- Current approach addresses only grosser aspects of flow/water quality; subtle influences not yet identified pose risk to fishes

**Six Management Goals Proposed** (15-year timeframe):

**Goal I - Maintain Current Colorado Squawfish Status**: Preserve 1979-1981 population levels and distribution. Tasks include: continue habitat preservation recommendations; locate/define spawning areas and migration routes (1983-1985, $279,000); annual YOY abundance inventory (1983-1996, $1,306,000); refine flow/habitat modeling (1983-1986, $339,000); geomorphologic study (1984-1987, $232,000); test fish passage devices (1985-1988, $163,000); develop flow-temperature-habitat model (1983-1984, $31,000); turbidity importance experiments (1984-1987, $144,000); spawning trigger research (1985-1988, $144,000); environmental contaminant monitoring (1983-1985, $49,000); competition studies (1985-1989, $434,000).

**Goal II - Maintain Humpback Chub at Black Rocks/Westwater**: Preserve 1979-1981 population levels. Tasks include: locate/describe spawning areas (1983-1985, $279,000); annual abundance inventory (1983-1996, $651,000); flow/habitat modeling (1984-1986, $88,000); geomorphologic study (1984-1987, $232,000); hybridization research (1984-1985, $32,000); competition studies (1985-1989, $434,000); develop flow-temperature-habitat model (1983-1984, $31,000); salinity standards refinement (1983-1984, $31,000); contaminant monitoring (1983-1985, $48,000).

**Goal III - Reestablish Bonytail Chub Population**: Establish 500 individuals in upper basin if none exist and hybridization can be prevented. Tasks include: exhaustive field search (1983, $23,000); taxonomic evaluation (1984, $17,000); habitat requirements analysis (1984-1987, $51,000); hybridization research (1984-1985, $33,000); field identification methods (1983, $17,000); warmwater hatchery planning/design/construction/operation (1983-1996, 1/4 allocation = $2,452,000); site selection for reintroduction (1989, $39,000); stocking/monitoring program (1990-1994, $957,000); develop protective criteria (1993, $33,000); contaminant bioassays (1983-1985, $16,000).

**Goal IV - Increase Colorado Squawfish Population/Range**: Achieve 500% increase in YOY (to 7.2 fish/10m² Green River, 0.4 fish/10m² Colorado River) and 200% increase in adults/juveniles (to 0.56 fish/hour Green River, 0.42 fish/hour Colorado River) by September sampling. Tasks include: backwater characteristics experiments (1983-1986, $584,000); habitat/flow manipulation experiments (1983-1992, $3,938,000); habitat enhancement handbook (1990, $17,000); warmwater hatchery (1983-1996, 50% allocation = $6,308,000); semi-annual adult/juvenile monitoring (1983-1995, $372,000); examine former habitats for reestablishment feasibility (1985, $34,000).

**Goal V - Establish Humpback Chub in Green River System**: Secure or establish one self-sustaining population. Tasks include: exhaustive field search (1983, $23,000); taxonomic evaluation (1984, $17,000); inventory reintroduction sites and prepare plan (1984, $33,000); warmwater hatchery (1983-1996, 1/4 allocation = $3,154,000); stocking/monitoring program (1988-1992, $829,000); develop protective criteria (1990, $33,000).

**Goal VI - Develop Public Value**: Increase public awareness and develop squawfish as game fish after downlisting. Tasks include: compile agency directory (1983, $5,000); active media program (1983-1996, $107,000); produce recreational brochure (1986-1987, $101,000); study ESA changes to permit managed taking (1983-1990); cooperate on sport fishery development (1990-1996, $545,000).

**Total Estimated Costs**: Approximately $25 million over 15 years (Table 6), with annual costs ranging from $1,116,000 (1983) to $2,649,000 (1996). Funding sources: FWS budget requests and Section 7 consultation assessments to project sponsors based on water depletion volumes.

**Coordination Requirements**: Establish coordinator position to: track basin activities; facilitate early FWS involvement; act as information repository/disseminator; organize interagency meetings; promote bi-annual council of wildlife agency representatives; facilitate interactive planning; prioritize objectives; develop proposals; select investigators; monitor performance; integrate scientific information into biological opinions. Plan requires major reassessment every five years to incorporate new information and changing conditions.

**Critical Research Needs Identified**: Spawning area locations and characteristics; migration routes; flow-habitat relationships; geomorphologic flow requirements; fish passage capabilities; turbidity effects on feeding efficiency and competition; spawning triggers and homing mechanisms; environmental contaminants; competition with non-natives; temperature/salinity tolerances; hybridization mechanisms and prevention; taxonomic differentiation of young Gila species; habitat enhancement effectiveness; artificial propagation techniques.

**Infrastructure Proposed**: Warmwater hatchery and research facility for propagating endangered Colorado River fishes, with capacity allocated: 50% [[Colorado Squawfish]], 25% [[Humpback Chub]], 25% [[Bonytail Chub]]. Planning/design 1983-1985 ($1,721,000), construction 1986-1987 ($6,850,000), operation/maintenance 1988-1996 ($4,045,000). Facility serves dual purpose: provide stocks for reintroduction/research and maintain refugia populations against total extinction.

**Legal/Policy Framework**: Plan extends Recovery Plans issued by Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team. Integrates with Section 7 consultation to provide coherent policy between case-by-case project reviews and long-term recovery needs. Without coordinated approach, conservation measures lose efficiency and effectiveness; too much time may elapse before critical factors discovered. Plan recognizes 1.906 million acre-feet remains available under Colorado River Compact provisions, with project sponsors assessed proportionally for conservation funding.
