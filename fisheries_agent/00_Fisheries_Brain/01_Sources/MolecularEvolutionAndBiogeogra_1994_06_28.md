## Executive Summary

This document is a 1994 grant proposal (National Geographic Society, Committee for Research and Exploration) submitted by Clyde D. Barbour (Wright State University), with co-investigators Dan E. Krane and Salvador Contreras-Balderas, proposing a molecular phylogenetic and biogeographic study of the highly endemic freshwater fish fauna of the [[Mesa Central]] (Mexican Plateau), Mexico. The fauna comprises ~80 species, ~88% of which belong to three major endemic lineages: the [[Goodeidae]] (splitfins, 40 species), the cyprinid chubs [[Algansea]] and [[Yuriria]] (9 species combined), and the atherinid silversides [[Chirostoma]] and [[Poblana]] (21 species combined). The proposal frames the region as a freshwater analog to the Galápagos, driven by volcanic and tectonic fragmentation of the ancestral [[Rio Lerma]] drainage. Field collections (Jan–Feb 1996) were intended to support mitochondrial (rRNA gene) and nuclear (GAPDH gene) DNA sequencing to reconstruct phylogenies independent of morphology, test hypotheses about common geologic drivers of diversification, recency of speciation, and the role of sexual selection in goodeid evolution.

## Background & Description

- **Goodeidae (splitfins):** Small, colorful, endemic livebearers; 40 recognized species. Noted for bright coloration and complex reproductive behaviors, suggested to be linked to sexually selected trait evolution. First taxonomically reviewed by Hubbs & Turner (1939); subsequent work largely limited to species descriptions and generic revisions. A fossil goodeid from the Miocene of the Lerma basin indicates long-term regional persistence of the family.
- **Cyprinidae (chubs):** Genus [[Algansea]] (7–9 species cited variably in text) and genus [[Yuriria]] (2 species) — minnow-like fishes ("chubs, minnows and carps" family). *Algansea tincella* (spottail chub) highlighted as a wide-ranging species targeted for dense population sampling. Genus revised by Barbour & Miller (1978); *Algansea monticola* diversification described by Barbour & Miller (1994).
- **Atherinidae (silversides):** [[Chirostoma]] (19–21 species) and [[Poblana]] (2 species), forming a freshwater "species flock" derived from a predominantly marine family. *Chirostoma jordani* (mesa silverside) cited as a wide-ranging species used for population-level sampling. Prior morphological/allozyme studies (Barbour 1973; Echelle & Echelle 1984) failed to fully resolve species relationships via parsimony cladograms of shared derived electromorphs.
- Voucher preparation protocol described: tissue sampled from right side of specimen or whole fish sliced longitudinally, with the left side preserved as a voucher for morphological confirmation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** The [[Mesa Central]] constitutes the southern third of the Mexican Plateau, lying between the Sierra Madre Oriental and Sierra Madre Occidental, extending from ~22°N south to the Transverse Volcanic Axis (~19°N). This tropical highland region contains flat-floored, often endorheic valleys with lacustrine sediments, remnant lakes, and volcanic explosion craters (some containing small lakes).
- **Drainage system:** Predominantly drained by the [[Lerma-Santiago River]] system. The Rio Lerma originates near Toluca and flows into Lake Chapala; the outflow continues as the Rio Grande de Santiago, which exits the plateau via a barranca through the Sierra Madre Occidental to the Gulf of California near San Blas, Nayarit.
- **Biogeographic history:** Headward erosion by neighboring drainages (Ameca, Armeria, Balsas, Coahuayana on the Pacific slope; Panuco on the Gulf slope) has captured portions of the ancestral Lerma basin, creating opportunities for vicariant speciation. Evidence suggests the ancestral Lerma once drained areas now isolated as interior basins, including the Valley of Mexico and Lakes [[Cuitzeo]], [[Zacapu]], [[Patzcuaro]], and [[Zirahuén]] in Michoacán. Disjunct populations occur in a desert spring in San Luis Potosí and near Durango (Rio Mezquital headwaters), indicating former connections between the Mesa del Norte and Mesa Central.
- **Geologic drivers:** Basin formation attributed to erosional deposition, volcaniclastic accumulation, and local uplift beginning in the early Tertiary, intensifying through the Miocene–Pleistocene; lava flows blocked rivers to form lakes subsequently infilled by ash, alluvium, and mudflows. Ongoing volcanism and sedimentation continue to shape the region.
- **Population dynamics:** Vicariance via tectonic/volcanic compartmentalization is proposed as the principal mechanism reducing gene flow and enabling diversification and speciation across the fish fauna.

## Key Findings

- The Mesa Central fish fauna (~80 species) is analogized to the Galápagos as a natural laboratory for studying gene–geography interactions in speciation.
- Three testable hypotheses were proposed: (1) shared geologic events underlie diversification across [[Goodeidae]], [[Algansea]]/[[Yuriria]], and [[Chirostoma]]/[[Poblana]]; (2) most species are of recent evolutionary origin; (3) sexual selection is a primary driver of diversification in the [[Goodeidae]].
- A Miocene goodeid fossil from the Lerma basin challenges a purely "recent origin" narrative for at least some lineages, while the marine ancestry of Atherinidae leaves open the possibility of independent, older invasion timing for [[Chirostoma]].
- Proposed molecular methods: PCR amplification and sequencing (≥1000 bp per locus) of a mitochondrial rRNA gene (fast-evolving, useful over ~50,000-year timescales) and the nuclear GAPDH gene (slow-evolving, useful over timescales up to ~100 million years), enabling multi-scale phylogenetic resolution via PAUP and MacClade analyses.
- Molecular phylogenies were intended to be independent of morphological/allozyme datasets (e.g., Grudzien et al. 1992; Echelle & Echelle 1984) to permit direct testing of the sexual-selection hypothesis in goodeid evolution, in collaboration with concurrent doctoral research by Shane Webb (University of Michigan).
- Specimen and tissue vouchers were planned for deposition at Wright State University, the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, and the Universidad de Nuevo León ichthyological collection, ensuring future accessibility for the broader research community.
- The work was explicitly designed to complement parallel studies on western North American fishes by T. Dowling and W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University), highlighting a broader comparative biogeographic framework for [[Freshwater Fish Speciation]] in Mexico and the American Southwest.
