# Assemblage Structure of Small, Cryptic Coral-Reef Fishes: An Atlantic-Pacific Comparison

#ichthyology #marine-biology #apogonidae #blennioidei #gobiidae

## Executive Summary

This study by David W. Greenfield (Department of Zoology and [[Hawaii Institute of Marine Biology]], University of Hawaii) examines how assemblages of small, cryptic, sedentary [[Coral Reef Fish Assemblages]] are structured across habitats. Over 400 [[Rotenone Collection]] samples were taken in [[Belize]] and [[Honduras]] between 1970 and 1984. Three taxonomic groups were analysed with [[Detrended Correspondence Analysis]]: [[Blennioid Fishes]], [[Apogonid Fishes]] (cardinalfishes), and [[Gobies]]. The groups differed sharply in how strongly they tracked habitat, and the author argues that conclusions about reef fish community structure must be drawn for specific taxonomic groups rather than the whole fauna. Preliminary apogonid data from the [[Pacific Ocean]] suggest assemblage patterns there differ from the Atlantic.

## Background & Description

Small, often cryptic, sedentary species account for much of the high species diversity typical of coral-reef fish communities. The Gulf of Honduras offers a wide range of habitats within a short distance, making it a suitable western Atlantic site for studying assemblage structure. Sampling used rotenone collections, a method that captures small, cryptic fishes missed by conventional gear. The study covers three families or groups that were available for analysis at the time of writing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study frames its results within a debate on mechanisms controlling reef fish community structure. An [[Equilibrium Hypothesis]] view holds that competition and other deterministic processes structure communities, whereas a [[Non-Equilibrial Community Structure]] view holds that stochastic processes and disturbance play the larger role. The author argues that the blennioid data alone could support an equilibrial interpretation, while the apogonid data clearly support a non-equilibrial one. Gobies fall between these two extremes. The results also show a gradient in [[Habitat Specificity]], with blennies showing the strongest association with particular habitats and cardinalfishes showing none in the Atlantic.

Habitats named in the study include [[Mangrove Habitat]], [[Seagrass Habitat]], rock, rubble/algae, tidepool/strand, shallow and deep forereef, patch reef, spur and groove, dropoff, and offshore rock.

## Key Findings

**Blennioid fishes (Atlantic):**
- Data came from 194 reliable rotenone collections containing more than two blennioid species.
- 60 species and 7,732 individuals were included.
- The analysis recognised 11 distinct habitat-associated assemblages: dropoff; spur and groove; shallow forereef; patch reef; rubble/algae; tidepool/strand; mangrove; mainland rock; shallow seagrass; deep seagrass; offshore rock.
- Blennies showed the greatest level of habitat specificity of the three groups.

**Apogonid fishes (Atlantic):**
- Data came from 122 reliable collections with two or more apogonid species, covering 17 species and 3,680 individuals.
- Detrended correspondence analysis did not recognise any assemblages corresponding to specific habitats, so cardinalfishes showed no habitat specificity.

**Gobies (Atlantic):**
- Data came from 265 rotenone collections, covering 50 species and 6,185 individuals.
- Assemblages from mainland rock and mainland mangrove were clearly separable from other goby assemblages.
- Offshore mangrove, offshore rock, and rubble/algae assemblages were also distinct from other reef habitats.
- Within coral-reef habitats, only the dropoff and spur-and-groove assemblages clustered together.
- Gobies sit intermediate between blennies and cardinalfishes in habitat specificity.

**Methodological conclusion:**
- Taxonomic group matters. Blennioid data alone could support an equilibrial interpretation, whereas apogonid data support a non-equilibrial view, and gobies are intermediate.

**Pacific apogonid preliminary data:**
- **Kaneohe Bay, Hawaii:** 31 collections with two or more apogonid species; six species present. Six separate habitat-associated assemblages were recognised.
- **Saipan:** Seven collections with two or more apogonid species, covering 17 species. Distinct assemblages were found for reef crest, terrace, shallow forereef, and patch reef habitats.
- **American Samoa:** 13 species from 17 sites with two or more apogonid species. Distinct assemblages were recognised for shallow and deep spur-and-groove habitats, isolated deep coral heads, and dropoff habitat.
- These data suggest major differences between Pacific and Atlantic assemblage structure. Broad conclusions about coral-reef fish assemblages, or even about a single family, cannot be drawn across ocean basins.

**Future work:**
- Further Pacific sampling is needed to produce a data set comparable to the Atlantic one so these preliminary hypotheses can be tested.
