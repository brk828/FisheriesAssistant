# Chemical Treatment of Ord Creek to Re-establish Arizona Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports a [[Chemical Piscicide Treatment]] of Ord Creek in the [[White Mountains]] of east-central Arizona, carried out to remove introduced [[Brown Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] and restore the threatened [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*). The toxicant [[Antimycin A]] was used in August 1977 and detoxified with [[Sodium Hypochlorite]]. The first renovation failed: native trout reintroduced in September 1977 died, and brook trout young-of-the-year appeared in treated reaches. A second treatment in September 1978 removed the remaining nonnative trout, and 62 Arizona trout were stocked in October 1980. Adults were present in July 1981, but no reproduction was observed.

## Background & Description

Arizona trout were historically the only fish in Ord Creek. The species was listed as endangered by the U.S. Department of Interior, then relegated to threatened status the same year to support management by state and local agencies. (The year in the scanned text is partly garbled; the cited reference is 1980.) A barrier was built on lower Ord Creek in 1964 to block upstream movement of nonnative trout from the North Fork of the White River. It failed to stop them. By the early 1970s, brook and brown trout made up more than 90% of the fish population, and the native population was declining.

The authors were John N. Rinne, W.L. Minckley, and James N. Hanson (Rinne et al. 1981). The treatment also allowed evaluation of whether chemical renovation could restore native fishes, and it collected population data for future management. Effects on [[Aquatic Macroinvertebrates]] were reported separately (Minckley and Mihalick 1981).

**Study area:** Ord Creek flows north for about 11 km from Bull Cienega (2,650 m) through [[Smith Cienega]] to the old fish barrier (2,230 m). Both cienegas are open riparian marshes with deep organic sediments and thick sedge and grass sods. The reaches between them are narrow and rocky, canopied by mixed coniferous forest, and lie on former glacial moraines.

**Physical and chemical conditions (August 1977):**
- Discharge ranged from 0.66 m³/min in Bull Cienega to 17 m³/min at the lower end of Smith Cienega, and approached 25 m³/min at the old barrier.
- Gradient ranged from about 2% in the cienegas to almost 6% over moraines.
- Current in cienegas averaged under 1.0 m/min. Over moraines it reached 6.8–22.0 m/min.
- Mean speed of flow was 13.2 m/min (about 0.8 km/h).
- pH ranged from 6.5 to 7.0.
- Total hardness averaged 30.0 ± 7.1 mg/l (n = 5 samples), of which 71.3% was magnesium hardness and 28.7% calcium hardness. (The reported range is garbled in the scan.)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Ord Creek is a high-elevation southwestern stream with complex habitat, which the authors identified as a key factor in the failed first treatment. The cienegas and moraine reaches differ sharply in flow, and flow exchange in Smith Cienega's tunneled channels was thought to retain water and toxicant.

Nonnative trout displaced the native fish largely through numbers. Brook trout were far more abundant in the uppermost reaches, showing that they could penetrate to the headwaters of an alpine stream. This makes effective barriers essential. Brown trout dominated the downstream reaches, about 85% of total numbers and biomass there, suggesting less tendency to invade the headwaters. Competition for spawning space was judged minimal, because the nonnatives spawn in autumn and Arizona trout spawn in spring. Predation by the nonnatives on small native trout was considered a possible factor. Stomach examination of 50 large (226–270 mm) brook and brown trout found no fish remains, but low numbers of small fish relative to adults suggested that predation and cannibalism were occurring. Food competition was considered likely, since the nonnative trout feed efficiently and were far more abundant.

Antimycin A also reduced aquatic invertebrates, which could affect the food supply for restored fish and risk the loss of unique taxa.

## Key Findings

### Methods
- **Pre-treatment:** Live Arizona trout were collected by battery-powered d.c. [[Electrofishing]] in two passes per area and held in an instream tank. Nonnative fish were preserved in 10% formalin. Flow, depth, velocity, and travel time were measured, and salinometry tracked NaCl passage to calibrate toxicant doses.
- **Pilot application:** A 0.3-km tributary to upper Smith Cienega was treated to test the procedure and remove nonnative trout that would supply untreated water to the holding tank. Caged brook trout were placed 10, 150, and 300 m below the detoxification station.
- **Toxicant:** Antimycin A (Fintrol) was selected for its documented effectiveness on salmonids in cold, low-pH water (Berger et al. 1969; Gilderhus et al. 1969). It was applied to reach about 10 µg/l (ppb).
- **Application:** 208 l steel drums gave 6 hours of flow. 18.9 l cans gave 3 hours on smaller tributaries, and 3.8 l cans gave 1-hour treatments on springs under 0.1 m³/min. Backpack sprayers treated marshy reaches, and Antimycin A–impregnated sand was used in stagnant areas.
- **Detoxification:** Sodium hypochlorite (HTH) was applied at 1 mg/l at drip stations below treated areas.
- **Timeline:** Final electrofishing occurred on August 30, 1977, and treatment began August 31, 1977. Dead fish were collected August 31 to September 1.

### Toxicant and Fish Response
- Fish in pilot cages below the detoxification station all survived.
- In the treated reach, the first stressed fish appeared after 46 minutes. After 4 hours, 101 dead brook trout had been recovered.
- The first caged fish lost equilibrium 43 minutes after salinometry showed the toxicant had arrived. Four of six caged fish were dead in just over 1 hour, and all were dead within 1 hour 40 minutes.
- Response matched earlier literature (Berger et al. 1969; Gilderhus et al. 1969).
- No living fish were found in any treated reach 3 hours after toxicant arrival.

### Results of Renovation
- **Biomass and abundance (Table 1):** Electrofishing and treatment recovered brook and brown trout, Arizona trout fingerlings, and unidentified fry across four sections totalling 8,333 m² (1,000; 2,353; 1,460; and 3,520 m²).
- **Adult composition:** Brook trout made up 85% of numbers and 78% of biomass of adult fishes.
- **Native trout:** Adult Arizona trout were evenly distributed and weighed 70–150 g each.

### Failure and Re-treatment
- Arizona trout were reintroduced September 5, 1977. Planned 1-week delay was cancelled because restrained fish began dying. About 10% of fish returned to Smith Cienega showed signs of distress.
- In September 1978, electrofishing recovered one brook trout under 90 mm and several fry. The next day's survey produced 60 young-of-the-year brook trout and no adult Arizona trout.
- Lower-reach treatment was cancelled. The upper reach was retreated in September 1978, yielding fewer than 200 brook trout fry and no adults of any species.

### Possible Causes of Failure
The authors considered several explanations for the first failure:
- Antimycin A may have reduced aquatic insects, so the food supply had not recovered before winter.
- Handling, days of holding, and electrofishing may have caused stress mortality, though not total mortality.
- Toxicant may have been retained in marshy areas of upper Bull Cienega. The authors considered this the only mechanism alone capable of causing total mortality.
- Tributary discharge (about 5.8 m³/min from first- and second-order tributaries, plus 2.2 m³/min from each of the next two) totalled 120% of Smith Cienega's middle-section discharge (10.2 vs. 8.5 m³/min). Discharge at the barrier (17 m³/min) was about 167% of that at the upper tributaries and 200% of that within the cienega, indicating water retention within the cienega.
- Young brook trout were probably not from surviving adults. No adults were collected in autumn 1978. Young may have passed the incompletely sealed new barrier, but catch rates (1, 11, 22, and 26 fish from sections 4 to 1) argued against this. Eggs in spring-fed redds may have survived under upwelling and hatched, with fry then moving downstream, as is common in salmonids.

### Native Population Status
- A sample of 145 native trout was aged 3 or older (up to age 5 or 6). Only 6 (3%) of about 200 collected were under 120 mm long (the comparison symbol is garbled in the scan). Absence of young fish indicates no successful reproduction for at least 3 years.
- The last successful spawning was probably in 1973 and 1974. Spawning was noted in 1975 but did not lead to recruitment.
- Estimates from [[Big Bonita Creek]] (Harper 1978) suggest that several thousand adult Arizona trout should have inhabited Ord Creek.

### Condition Factors
- Mean condition (K_TL) was 1.10 for Arizona trout, 0.90 for brook trout, and 0.94 for brown trout. Food was therefore not considered critical for the natives, but the nonnatives' feeding efficiency and abundance gave them a competitive edge.

### Second Stocking
- On October 20, 1980, 62 Arizona trout from [[Paradise Creek]] were introduced into Ord Creek.
- The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] surveyed in July 1981. Adults were captured by angling, but no signs of successful reproduction were noted.

### Conclusions and Recommendations
- Antimycin A was effective for removing unwanted fish from high-elevation southwestern streams, but multiple treatments may be necessary.
- Timing and habitat complexity likely contributed to the first failure.
- The authors recommend that pretreatment surveys consider biotic, temporal, and physical-chemical factors (remoteness, stream length, habitat complexity).
- Renovation in the early 1970s, before complete invasion, would likely have been more successful.
- Impacts on invertebrates need consideration, especially where they are a food source for restored fish or host unique taxa.
- Restoring habitat for native fish is increasingly popular (Johnson and Rinne 1982), but sacrificing other native fauna may be questioned under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- Nonnative brook and brown trout were introduced for sport fishing at the expense of Arizona trout. With proper management, Arizona trout could provide the same sport-fishing role.
