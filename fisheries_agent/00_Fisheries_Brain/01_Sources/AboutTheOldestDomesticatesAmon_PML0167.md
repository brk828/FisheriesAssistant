# About the Oldest Domesticates Among Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[E. K. Balon]], the thirteenth J.W. Jones Lecture published in the *Journal of Fish Biology* (2004, Supplement A), examines when and how fishes became true domesticates. Balon argues that fish domestication began much later than that of mammals, that the first true fish domesticate was the [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) in Central Europe, and that the [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) was domesticated in China. He challenges the widespread claim that common carp were domesticated in China more than 2000 years ago, calling it a myth. Most cultured fishes, he concludes, remain "exploited captives" rather than domesticates, with only a few aquarium species, notably the [[Guppy]] and [[Neon Tetra]], clearly qualifying.

## Background & Description

**Definition of domestication.** Following [[Clutton-Brock]] (1999), a domestic animal is one bred in captivity for economic profit under human control of breeding, territory and food. Balon adapts five criteria for true domestication: (a) the individual is valued for a specific purpose; (b) breeding is under human control; (c) behaviour differs from the wild ancestor; (d) morphology and physiology show variation never seen in the wild; and (e) some individuals could not survive without human protection. "Exploited captives" meet only the early criteria and remain little changed from wild stock.

**Timing.** Fish domestication is dated to about 10,500 years ago (Diamond, 2002), much later than mammals and plants. Only the common carp (acquired c. 2000 years ago by the Romans in south-central Europe) and the goldfish (selected and kept in "ponds of mercy" c. 1000 years ago in China) are considered true domesticates.

**Common carp.** Wild carp from the Danube inland delta were torpedo-shaped and golden-yellow, with two pairs of barbels. Domesticated forms developed deeper, laterally compressed bodies, irregular scalation, and scaleless variants. The Chinese "carp" origin claim is discussed as a taxonomic confusion with grass, silver, bighead and black carp.

**Goldfish.** Derived from the wild silver-grey 'chi' (*Carassius auratus*). Xanthic (golden) aberrations were kept in Chinese ponds, and later in aquaria, producing many fancy forms (e.g., veiltail, telescope, lionhead).

**Koi.** Colour aberrations of common carp in Niigata Prefecture, Japan, which became commercially selected as *nishikigoi* mainly from the 1950s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Feral forms and conservation:** Domesticated animals released into the wild usually perform poorly. The Danube wild common carp has been degraded by the Gabčíkovo Dam; Balon argues the wild ancestral form may be close to extinction, replaced by feral domesticated progeny.
- **Goldfish tolerance:** The goldfish is one of the most resistant fishes to environmental perturbation, tolerating low oxygen (as low as 0.6 mg L⁻¹ O₂), pH 4.5 to 10.5, high turbidity and heavy metals. Its establishment outside its native range (wild range: East and Central Asia) is widespread due to escapes from aquaria and garden pools.
- **Guppy ecology:** Colour patterns reflect a balance between predator-driven crypsis and sexual selection for conspicuousness.
- **Pond culture:** Medieval carp ponds were sequentially managed (spawning, nursery and growing ponds), dried, limed and fertilized, a system contrasted with rice-paddy integrated culture in East Asia.

## Key Findings

1. **Common carp domestication in Europe, not China:** Balon concludes that wild carp from the Danube were exploited by the Romans (Pannonia frontier, with bones of wild common carp dominating fish remains), and that true domestication occurred in the 12th century in medieval ponds.
2. **Chinese claim rejected:** The claim that common carp were domesticated in China over 2000 years ago is called a myth. Chinese carp are considered semi-domesticated or feral European stock.
3. **Morphological and physiological divergence:** Domesticated carp have a much smaller wild mouth gape (wild 4.46–5.57 vs domesticated 8.12 in the mouth-gape index, per Steffens, 1964), intestines 15–25% longer, more altricial eggs, and a larger anterior swim-bladder chamber. Wild carp have 18–19% more erythrocytes and haemoglobin and better muscle vascularisation.
3. **Koi origin:** Koi from the Niigata area of Japan were commercially selected mainly from the 1950s. Only about 1 in 10,000 or more of fry were retained for breeding.
4. **Goldfish history:** Domesticated goldfish were first kept by Chinese monks in "ponds of mercy" in the Sung Dynasty (968–975 A.D. onwards). Aquarium keeping after 1548 led to an explosion of "monstrosities" by selective culling. Goldfish reached Europe only in the late 1600s–1700s.
5. **Crucian versus goldfish:** Goldfish differ from crucian carp (*C. carassius*) by gill raker counts (37–53 vs 23–33), a black vs light peritoneum, and the lack of a caudal spot.
6. **Which fishes count as domesticates today:** Balon concludes that only the guppy and neon tetra in aquarium culture clearly qualify. Zebrafish may become one. Most other cultured species, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], Chinese carps, tilapias and sturgeons, remain exploited captives.
