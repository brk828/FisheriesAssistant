# Status of the Speckled Dace in Canada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This status assessment examines the Canadian population of the [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), a small cyprinid whose Canadian range is restricted to the [[Kettle River]] and [[Granby River]] in southern [[British Columbia]]. The authors, working at the [[British Columbia Provincial Museum]], report that the Canadian population appears to have adequate reproductive capacity and is not endangered, provided its habitat in the Kettle River is not adversely altered. The population is considered stable based on surveys in 1978, 1979 and 1980. Because the range is so limited, the species is vulnerable to a catastrophic event in a single drainage. The authors recommend a status of "rare," which was approved by [[COSEWIC]] on 6 April 1983.

## Background & Description

The Speckled Dace is a small, elongate cyprinid of western North America. Canadian specimens are about 50–70 mm in length, with a robust anterior body that is laterally compressed behind the dorsal fin. The fish are grey or grey-brown, with darker flecks above the midline, a faint lateral band ending in a diffuse spot at the base of the caudal fin, and creamy-white to yellow underparts. In Canada the species has little economic value because of its limited distribution, although elsewhere it is used as forage and bait.

The species is widely distributed between the [[Colorado River]] drainage in Arizona and the [[Columbia River]] system, and it is divided into many morphologically differentiated, isolated populations. The Kettle River is the northern limit of its recorded range. In Canada, fish have been collected along a 112 km stretch of the East and West Kettle Rivers, from [[Carmi]] to the international border at [[Cascade]], and along a 27 km section of the Granby River above [[Grand Forks]]. Dace have not been found in other Canadian tributaries of the Kettle system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use.** Adults are associated with rock and boulder habitat in moderate-to-strong current, where siltation and scouring are not excessive. Juveniles dominate the samples and are widely distributed. Most of the Granby River juveniles occur over a clean sand bottom with little algae. In the Kettle River, juveniles were found among smooth stones with a slow to moderate flow and only a thin algal scum at the water's edge. The river's flood regime strongly shapes this habitat: spring floods scour the bed and remove accumulated algae and detritus each year.

**Water conditions.** Water temperature increases downstream. Average summer temperatures reached 14 °C at Carmi (elevation about 884 m) and almost 18 °C at Laurier (about 457 m). Kettle River water was very clear, with average Jackson Turbidity Units between 1.0 and 4.0 for 1965–1978 (spring runoff is more turbid). Alkalinity rose from 60 to 77.5 mg/L and pH fell from 8.7 to 7.7 as water flowed downstream. The Granby River had lower dissolved solids, with an average alkalinity of 37.3 mg/L.

**Biotic interactions.** Speckled Dace are bottom-dwelling and have few competitors, apart from sculpins (Cottidae) and juvenile suckers (Catostomidae). They co-occur with schooling mid-water species such as the [[Red Shiner]] and [[Northern Squawfish]]. The strongest potential competitor is a [[Umatilla Dace]]-like *Rhinichthys* that replaces Speckled Dace below Cascade. Predation is not expected to be severe, as the dace habitually hide under rocks.

**Dispersal barriers.** Waterfalls at Cascade and ecological exclusion by the Umatilla-like dace are believed to prevent upstream dispersal from Washington State. Floods can sweep fish over the falls. Because of this isolation, the Canadian population would be unlikely to recover from immigration if it were decimated.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- The Canadian range is about 112 km of the Kettle River and 27 km of the Granby River. The species is absent from other Kettle tributaries.
- Surveys drifting downstream in 1980 found no adult dace below Cascade. Juveniles were replaced by a different dace species, probably *R. umatilla*.
- Stable population in 1978, 1979 and 1980 surveys. Insufficient data exist to detect cyclical fluctuations or long-term trends.
- Adults were rare in the samples and found mainly below the Cascade falls, at the Granby River mouth in Grand Forks, and in a boulder pile at Carmi. The authors believe more adult habitat exists in inaccessible reaches.

**Life history**
- Fish under 40 mm standard length are estimated to be no more than about 1¼ years old.
- Maturity occurs at 40 mm, possibly 50 mm, standard length.
- Spawning probably occurs in summer (July?). Juveniles hatched in summer probably breed at two years.
- Ovary counts of large eggs ranged from about 450 in small fish to about 2000 in large fish. The authors consider reproductive potential high.
- Males were much less common than females in samples. The authors suggest males may prefer swifter water, which was not sampled effectively.
- Juvenile mortality is thought to be high during spring floods. Only fish finding suitable habitat survive to adulthood.

**Threats and limiting factors**
- No formal legal protection exists. Maintaining water quality and preventing siltation are the main requirements for survival.
- Possible threats include sewage from Grand Forks (the authors noted an absence of dace just below the town), logging, road construction, agriculture, inactive coal mines, and dam construction, which would eliminate riffle habitat.
- No baseline data exist to document habitat loss. Conditions in the Washington section of the river are unknown.

**Taxonomic significance**
- Five races of the polymorphic species are considered endangered or threatened by the [[American Fisheries Society]]: *R. o. lethoporus*, *R. o. nevadensis*, *R. o. oligoporus*, *R. o. thermalis* and *R. o. moapae*.
- Kettle River Speckled Dace may represent a unique Canadian taxon. Their sympatry with the Umatilla-like dace at Cascade suggests two distinct species. Its relationship to *R. falcatus* in Canada is unresolved.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The Canadian population is not endangered if its habitat and water quality are maintained, but its restricted range makes it vulnerable to catastrophe in a single drainage.
- Monitoring population size after unusual flood events would help predict future status.
- Recommended status: "rare."
