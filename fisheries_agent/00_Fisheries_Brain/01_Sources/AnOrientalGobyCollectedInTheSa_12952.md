# Oriental Goby (Acanthogobius flavimanus) — First California Record

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Gobiidae

## Executive Summary
This note documents the first scientific record of the [[Oriental Goby]] (*Acanthogobius flavimanus*), a species native to Japan, China, and Korea, in Californian waters. Two specimens were collected in early 1963 by California Department of Fish and Game personnel in the [[San Joaquin River Delta]] near Stockton, California — one at Prisoners Point (Venice Island) and one in the Stockton Deepwater Channel near the Calaveras River confluence. The find raises the possibility that a local breeding population of this introduced species is becoming established in the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta, drawing parallels to a contemporaneous introduction of an Oriental shrimp, *Palaemon macrodactylus*, into San Francisco Bay.

## Background & Description
- **Standard Length (SL):** Specimen 1 = 123 mm SL (155 mm total length); Specimen 2 = 69 mm SL (85 mm total length)
- **Dorsal fin:** 8 spines, 14 soft rays
- **Lateral scales:** 46–56 scales in midlateral series, beginning at the upper limit of the opercular opening
- **Body markings:** ~8 diffuse dusky spots along each side, each larger than the eye diameter, arranged in a nearly evenly-spaced series
  - First 3 spots concealed beneath the [[Pectoral Fin]]
  - Last spot forms a prominent mark at the base of the [[Caudal Fin]]
- **Coloration:** Sides and back indistinctly mottled
- **Caudal fin pattern:** Upper two-thirds bears ~10 narrow, vertical, dusky zigzag bands; lower third is plain dusky
- **Maximum size:** Reaches approximately 8 inches, comparable to the largest native Californian gobies — the [[Mudsucker]] (*Gillichthys mirabilis*) and the [[Longtail Goby]] (*Gobionellus longicaudus*)
- Diagnostic combination of fin ray counts, scale counts, and spot/banding pattern reliably distinguishes this species from all native Californian [[Gobiidae|gobies]]

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native distribution:** Widely distributed in estuarine environments across Japan, China, and Korea; ascends short distances into streams and lakes near sea level
- **Habitat preference:** Adults live mostly in brackish water around river mouths; occasionally enter lakes, swamps along seacoasts, and rivers
- **Salinity tolerance (California collection sites):**
  - Prisoners Point (Venice Island): <30 ppm chlorides (very low salinity)
  - Stockton Deepwater Channel: ~70 ppm chlorides (slightly higher, ~12 miles upstream difference notwithstanding)
- Demonstrates strong [[Euryhaline]] and [[Eurythermal]] tolerance typical of the family [[Gobiidae]]
- **Diet:** Adults feed on small fishes and crustaceans; larvae/young feed primarily on planktonic copepods
- **Reproductive biology** (per Okada 1961; Dotu & Mito 1955):
  - Matures after approximately one year
  - Breeding season: January–March (peaks February–March in Kyushu)
  - Constructs a flat, Y-shaped breeding chamber with two entrances, built vertically in tidal flat sediment
  - Eggs are club-shaped (5.5 mm × 0.95 mm), with adhesive filaments, attached to the burrow wall
  - Incubation period: ~28 days at 13°C water temperature
  - Newly hatched larvae: ~4.6 mm total length
  - Larvae (4.9–12.0 mm) are pelagic, living in the bay; juveniles (15–20 mm) move to estuary bottoms
  - Young aggregate near river mouths; descend to the sea as autumn temperatures drop
- **Comparable introduced species:** The Oriental shrimp *[[Palaemon Macrodactylus]]* was similarly introduced into San Francisco Bay around 1954, likely via ship ballast/seawater systems fouled with tubeworms and barnacles — proposed as an analogous introduction mechanism for *A. flavimanus*

## Key Findings
1. **First confirmed record** of *Acanthogobius flavimanus* in California waters, based on two specimens collected in the San Joaquin River Delta in 1963.
2. Specimens closely matched reference material from Stanford University's collection (Tsuruga and Nagasaki, Japan) and classical taxonomic descriptions (Temminck & Schlegel 1845; Jordan & Snyder 1901; Okada 1961).
3. The small size of both specimens suggests the possibility of a **locally reproducing, established population** rather than isolated stray individuals.
4. The [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] and [[San Francisco Bay]] region closely resemble Oriental estuarine habitats in salinity and structure, suggesting suitable conditions for range expansion.
5. Introduction pathway is unknown but likely **accidental**, potentially via ship transport, paralleling the documented case of the introduced shrimp *Palaemon macrodactylus*.
6. Potential for economic use as a **bait fish**, similar to the native mudsucker in southern California.
7. Specimens were deposited at Sacramento State College (SSC nos. 35-1 and 36-1) for long-term reference and taxonomic verification.
