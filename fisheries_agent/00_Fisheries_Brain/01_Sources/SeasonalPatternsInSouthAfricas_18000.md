# Seasonal Patterns in South Africa's Western Cape Purse-Seine Fishery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #small-pelagic-fishes #purse-seine-fishery

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[R. J. M. Crawford]] (Department of Industries, Sea Fisheries Branch, 1980, *Journal of Fish Biology*) summarizes well-defined seasonal trends in the availability and distribution of six species that make up the [[Western Cape Purse-Seine Fishery]]: [[Pilchard]], [[Anchovy]], [[Horse Mackerel]], [[Mackerel]], [[Round Herring]] and [[Lantern-Fish]]. The patterns are largely age-specific, track spawning behaviour, and are driven by the regional [[Upwelling]] and [[Agulhas Current]] system. They shape where and when vessels fish, and they give legislators options for long-term management through regulating the open season or closing areas. The author argues that multi-species complexity, differential mortality and variable product values require modelling before management strategies can be compared quantitatively.

## Background & Description

The fishery is multi-species and multi-age. Each of the six contributors has its own spawning season, recruitment timing, and migration pattern. Predators that depend on the same fish are noted as subjects for future work, including the [[Cape Fur Seal]], [[Cape Gannet]], [[Jackass Penguin]], [[Cape Cormorant]], [[Snoek]], [[Tuna]] and [[Yellowtail]].

Each species is described briefly below.

- **Pilchard (*Sardinops ocellata*):** Spawns mainly September to February. Nought-year-olds recruit along the entire west coast from May, peaking in September. Two-year-olds are about 50% reproductively active. Older fish are linked to the summer offshore spawning region near St. Helena Bay.
- **Anchovy (*Engraulis capensis*):** Spawns mainly October to January, east of Cape Point. Recruitment peaks in June, about six months after peak egg production. Many fish mature at the end of their first year.
- **Horse Mackerel (*Trachurus trachurus*):** Two separate South African stocks exist, but only the Western Cape stock has contributed to purse-seine landings. Spawning season and area are not fully defined. Egg production is thought to occur between Saldanha Bay and Cape Hangklip in spring and early summer. Sexual maturity typically begins around age two, with most fish reproductively active from age three.
- **Mackerel (*Scomber japonicus*):** Spawning season is June to September. It is the only winter spawner in the group. Adults migrate inshore to spawn near Cape Columbine and are mature before their fourth year.
- **Round Herring (*Etrumeus teres*):** A distinct spring/summer spawning season is suggested, based on a plankton survey by [[P. A. Shelton]]. Spawning area appears extensive and offshore. Nought-year-olds recruit from April to September.
- **Lantern-Fish (*Lampanyctodes hectoris*):** Landings are sporadic and mostly comprise immature females. Best catches occur from midsummer to early autumn between the Olifants River and Dassen Island, especially near [[Cape Columbine]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Oceanography.** Summer southerly to easterly winds drive strong [[Upwelling]] west of Cape Point. Cool, low-salinity plumes extend up to 100 km offshore. These are separated from oceanic water by sharp temperature and salinity gradients, and are associated with equatorward jet currents. East of Cape Point, the [[Agulhas Current]] warms the water. In winter, northerly to westerly winds bring warm, clear oceanic water inshore, while storm mixing maintains a narrow strip of cooler, plankton-rich water along the mainland. The shallow area north of Cape Columbine shows less variability in plankton standing stock. The [[Cape Canyon]] lies off Cape Columbine and influences the vertical position of lantern-fish.

**Fishing grounds.** All processing factories lie between Lambert's Bay and Gans Bay. The fishery is divided into six grounds: Namaqua, St. Helena Bay, Saldanha Bay, Hout Bay, Gans Bay and Agulhas. These follow major environmental regions.

**Temperature and spawning.** Laboratory work indicates that the lower lethal temperature limits for pilchard and anchovy eggs are 13°C and 14°C respectively, and development rates rise exponentially with temperature. Surface temperatures in the St. Helena Bay spawning area never fell below 13°C during the spawning season from 1964 to 1967. Horse mackerel eggs tolerate at least 12°C, and round herring eggs tolerate lower temperatures still. Winter mackerel spawning occurs in water of 14–15°C, brought inshore by north-westerly winds.

**Food and feeding.** Nought-year-old pilchard and anchovy feed mainly on zooplankton. The mature pilchard and anchovy that remain east of Cape Point develop a fine filtering mechanism at about one year old, which lets them feed on phytoplankton as well as zooplankton. Mackerel shift from zooplankton to lantern-fish as they age, which may drive their movement into deeper water in winter.

**Rivers.** Many juveniles congregate near the mouths of the Olifants and Berg rivers. These rivers have their greatest discharge in winter. The author suggests that river input may influence biological production, but the mechanism is complex and needs further study.

**Predators and prey.** Pilchard and anchovy are heavily preyed on by [[Hake]] (*Merluccius* spp.) east of Cape Agulhas in May and June. They are rarely found in hake diets in the Benguela Current region. This suggests that they occur below the surface east of Cape Agulhas.

## Key Findings

- **Seasonal trends are clear and mostly age-specific** across all six species, and they explain the deployment of fishing effort.
- **Spawning separates the species spatially.** Pilchard and adult anchovy spawn east of Cape Point, horse mackerel and round herring spawn offshore, and mackerel spawn inshore near Cape Columbine in winter.
- **Juveniles move south in spring.** Nought-year-olds of the four spring/summer spawners recruit on the northern grounds and then shift south into newly upwelled water. This indicates that much of the ichthyoplankton originates in the south and is carried north by the [[Good Hope Jet]]. [[Shelton & Hutchings]] (1979) demonstrated this for anchovy eggs and larvae.
- **Temperature sets the spawning limits.** Egg lethal limits and spawning temperatures help explain the rarity of anchovy eggs in the cool west coast water.
- **Fishing effort follows availability.** Effort on the Namaqua and St. Helena Bay grounds peaks from mid-autumn to late winter. Purse-seining east of Cape Point is mainly a summer and early autumn activity targeting two- to four-year-old pilchard and adult anchovy.
- **Effort has shifted over time.** After severe depletion of older pilchard, horse mackerel and mackerel, effort rose in the southern regions in 1974. Shoals of adult pilchard and anchovy east of Cape Point are now under intensive exploitation.
- **Management options are presented.** One option is to prohibit late autumn and winter fishing, at least west of Cape Point, to reduce pressure on younger age classes and raise product value. The author notes that this would only pay off if growth gains outweigh natural mortality losses, especially for anchovy, and that modelling is needed. The alternative is to protect spawning biomass by reducing summer fishing for pilchard and anchovy and winter fishing for older mackerel.
- **A small-mesh net in the mid-1960s lowered the average age of fish caught**, and most raw material is now converted to fish oil and meal rather than canned products.
- **Abnormal years complicate management.** In March 1967 there was an unusually large catch of one-year-old mackerel. In 1974, high anchovy availability produced record anchovy catches, while pilchard and round herring catches were very poor. Such events, along with recruitment fluctuations and shifts in species composition, will make management goals harder to achieve.
- **Data limitations.** Catch rates from 1964 to 1976 were used for monthly availability indices. Data from the later part of the year were too sparse because of closed seasons, so those values are interpolated.
