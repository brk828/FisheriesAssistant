# Biological Survey of Streams and Lakes of Tonto National Forest, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonid-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1930s-era survey by the [[Bureau Of Fisheries]] assessed the physical, chemical and biological conditions of streams and reservoirs in the [[Tonto National Forest]] (Arizona) to guide fish stocking. The field party, led by M. J. Madsen, worked the forest from June 28 to July 22, 1954, according to the text. The cover page reads 1935, so the publication date should be verified. The survey gathered data on water chemistry, stream structure, aquatic food organisms and the existing fish community. It then recommended which species to plant, and in what numbers, in streams and lakes. The report stresses that it is a condition survey and not a complete study of any water body.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Four stated aims: (1) describe biological, chemical and physical conditions; (2) use these data to choose the species, size and number of fish best suited to each water; (3) show where habitat alterations could raise fish production; (4) flag problems needing further study.
- **Study area:** The forest lies directly south of the [[Mogollon Rim]] in the Tonto Basin. It covers about 2,500 square miles, with elevations of roughly 4,000 to 6,500 feet (the OCR reads "41000"), and a major portion averaging about 3,000 feet. Its namesake, [[Tonto Creek]], is the largest and most important stream in the forest.
- **Major drainages:** [[Tonto Creek]], [[Cherry Creek]], [[East Verde River]] and the [[Salt River]]. Most streams dry up in summer because of irrigation diversions and high evaporation. Only the headwater streams hold permanent water and offer fishing.
- **Methods:** Stream stations were 200 yards long. Lake stations were areas workable from a rowboat. Fish were sampled with gill nets, seines, dip traps, angling and visual observation. Bottom food was taken with a square-foot sampling net in streams and an Ekman dredge in lakes. Plankton was collected with a standard net. Chemistry used field kits for dissolved oxygen (Winkler method), pH, carbon dioxide, alkalinity and hydrogen sulphide. Pools and riffles were graded for size, type and frequency. See [[Stream Survey Methodology]] for the full protocol.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream physical features**
- Gradient: at most about 700 feet per mile, averaging about 200 feet per mile. Streams are rapid in their upper reaches and sluggish lower down.
- Temperature: high-elevation streams reached a maximum of 76°F. Low-elevation streams reached 96°F.
- Two stream types were identified: small, rapid headwater streams, and larger, slow meandering lowland rivers. Headwater pools and riffles were about equal (50 percent each). In the larger rivers, pools were very large, usually no more than 4 feet deep, and riffles were long and wide, with low food production.

**Stream chemistry and vegetation**
- Chemical analyses (summarized in the report's Table 2) indicated that all streams were generally suitable for fish.
- Aquatic plants were sparse. Most streams had only a thin film of slime algae in riffles. East Verde River held considerable filamentous algae, and Haigler Creek had watercress near its springs.

**Food organisms**
- In order of abundance, the main food groups were mayfly nymphs, caddisfly larvae, dipteran larvae, beetles, mollusks, dobsonflies, mites, stoneflies, dragonflies, roundworms and freshwater worms.
- Benthic food was graded I (over 200 animals per square foot), II (100 to 200) or III (under 100).
- Maximum count: 460 organisms per square foot, at both Haigler and Horton creeks. The station average was 256. Mayflies and caddisflies averaged 93 individuals per station.
- Horton and Haigler creeks had the richest food supply. Lower Tonto Creek and lower East Verde Creek were markedly deficient.
- Minnows and sucker fry were moderately common and served as food for larger fish. Terrestrial insects falling into the water were also an important food source, though they were not quantified.

**Fish community**
- Recorded fishes, in order of abundance: rainbow trout, European brown trout and eastern brook trout, then catfish and cutthroat trout.
- No centrarchid (sunfish) species were captured, though the authors believed they might be present.
- Suckers and bonytails were found in all streams and were most abundant in the warmer lower reaches.
- Natural predators included the great blue heron, green heron and kingfisher. None appeared to cause significant damage to game fish.
- No disease or parasites were found on the game fish examined.

**Reservoirs (lakes)**
- There are no natural lakes of importance within the forest. The four reservoirs formed by damming the Salt River cover about 43 square miles at high water. They are [[Roosevelt Lake]], [[Horse Mesa Lake]], [[Canyon Lake]] and [[Stewart Mountain Lake]]. The report states that most of the state's bass fishing is supplied by these reservoirs.
- Depths: the report gives both 250 feet and 275 feet for Horse Mesa Lake, and 40 feet for Roosevelt Lake at survey time. Water levels fluctuate greatly with irrigation demand.
- Turbidity was usually low to moderate, about 12 to 25 feet of visibility.
- Surface temperatures ranged from Roosevelt Lake to 84°F on Stewart Mountain Lake. Temperature differences from surface to bottom were about 15°F in deeper lakes.
- Chemistry was unusual: all lakes showed oxygen depletion in deep water, high methyl orange alkalinity and high carbon dioxide. A strong hydrogen sulphide odor was present near each outlet.
- No rooted vegetation was found. Plankton and benthic food were scarce, and the report notes that the reservoirs were classed as "irrigation lakes, not fish lakes."

## Key Findings

1. **Two stream types require different management.** Small headwater streams are well suited to improvement. Large meandering lowland rivers are not practical to improve and should be excluded from improvement plans.
2. **Habitat balance is the main management message.** Past work in the forest focused too heavily on building dams and ignored cover and food supply. The report argues that a properly improved stream needs a correct balance of pools, cover and riffles. A pool without riffles or cover is not ideal.
3. **Stocking recommendations were species-specific.**
   - [[Rainbow Trout]] for cooler headwater streams. Natural reproduction was not expected because heavy fishing removed a planting in about two weeks.
   - [[Brown Trout]] for streams with adequate food and spawning gravel but average temperatures too high for cooler-water species.
   - Only warm-water species for the reservoirs, given their conditions. See [[Lake Stocking]] and [[Warm Water Fish Stocking]].
4. **Stocking assumptions.** Warm-water fish were assumed to sustain themselves for at least five years through natural reproduction, provided anglers protected spawners. Stocking numbers were based on a table by Dr. G. C. Embody, with input from Fred J. Foster, Regional Director of the Division of Fish Culture.
5. **Food and fish numbers.** Stocking decisions depend on food abundance, water temperature, width and depth, previous stocking, natural spawning success and existing species. Accurate data on harvest, yield and growth rates were said to be needed before a maximum-yield stocking plan could be set.
6. **Survey limitations and gaps.** The survey was a condition assessment, not a full study. Some terrestrial food was not quantified, and centrarchid species were not confirmed. The report flags further study as needed on special problems and notes that the Arizona Game and Fish Commission had not yet established spawning dates for largemouth bass along the Salt River.
