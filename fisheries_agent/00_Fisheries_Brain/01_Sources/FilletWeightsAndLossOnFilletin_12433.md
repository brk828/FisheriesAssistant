# Fillet Weights and Loss on Filleting of Yellow Pikeperch from Saginaw Bay

## Executive Summary
This 1945 Michigan Department of Conservation study, authored by Louis A. Krumholz, was commissioned to solve a fisheries law enforcement problem: commercial fishermen were filleting undersized [[Yellow Pikeperch]] to evade minimum total-length regulations, since filleted fish could not be measured for length compliance. The study measured round weight and fillet weight relationships across 932 [[Yellow Pikeperch]] specimens (13–17 inches total length) collected from [[Saginaw Bay]] over four sampling periods (fall 1942, spring 1943, spring 1944, spring 1945). The research directly informed a legislative amendment (P.A. 84, 1929, amended 1945) establishing a **9.0-ounce minimum legal fillet weight**, corresponding to the existing 15¼-inch minimum total length regulation. The study also quantified filleting weight loss at approximately **50–55%** of round weight, refuting higher anecdotal industry estimates (58–60%).

## Background & Description
- **Species studied:** [[Yellow Pikeperch]] (*Stizostedion v. vitreum*), synonymous with walleye/walleyed pike/yellow pickerel.
- **Length range sampled:** 13–17 inches total length, measured to nearest quarter-inch, straddling the legal minimum of 15¼ inches.
- **Round weight range:** ~9.1 oz (13 in.) to ~25.7 oz (17 in.) average; individual maxima up to 31.5 oz.
- **Fillet weight range:** ~5.6 oz (13 in.) to ~12.8 oz (17 in.) average; individual maxima up to 14.3 oz.
- **Fillet weight variability:** Within a single quarter-inch length class, fillet weight range varied from 2.2 oz (at 13 in.) to 4.4 oz (at 15¾ in.), indicating substantial individual variation not strictly tied to length — the heaviest fillets did not always come from the heaviest fish.
- **Filleting weight loss:** Consistently **50–55%** of round weight lost during filleting across all collections, compared with unsubstantiated industry claims of 58–60%.
- **Seasonal morphometric differences:** Fall-caught fish (~20.3 oz round, minimum legal length) yielded heavier average fillets (10.3 oz) than spring-caught fish of the same length (19.7 oz round, 9.3 oz fillet), suggesting body condition differences by season, though this may relate to gonadal development in the spring spawning run.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** [[Saginaw Bay]], an embayment of Lake Huron in the [[Great Lakes]] system, serving as a key freshwater commercial fishery ground.
- **Migratory/spawning behavior:** Yellow pikeperch exhibit two distinct annual runs into Saginaw Bay:
  - **Spring run** (April–June, peak in April): accounts for 85.3% of annual catch (11-year average, 1933–1943) and includes most of the spawning season.
  - **Fall run** (September–November): accounts for 10.9% of annual catch, relatively minor.
- **Sexual maturity:** Ripe females were noted to have the greatest filleting weight loss, but yellow pikeperch females are seldom mature at the 17-inch-or-less lengths used in this study, limiting confounding effects from gonadal weight in the sampled size range.
- **Fishery context:** Total annual production in Saginaw Bay averaged 1,473,908 lbs/year (1933–1943), making it the principal yellow pikeperch fishery in Michigan and a critical case study for the broader [[Great Lakes Commercial Fisheries]] regulatory framework.

## Key Findings
1. **Minimum legal fillet weight established:** A 9.0-ounce minimum fillet weight was recommended and subsequently adopted into Michigan law (amendment to P.A. 84 of 1929), corresponding to the 15¼-inch total length minimum already in place for [[Yellow Pikeperch]] harvested from Great Lakes waters (excluding Lake Erie).
2. **Precedent for regulation type:** This approach mirrored a successful 1939 law (Public Act 339) establishing minimum fillet weights for [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), which had effectively eliminated a similar undersized-filleting enforcement problem.
3. **Seasonal weight discrepancy:** Fall-run fish produced measurably heavier fillets than spring-run fish of equivalent length; a weighted average (accounting for 85.3%/10.9% seasonal catch proportions) of 9.4 oz was calculated, later refined downward to 9.0 oz for practical/enforcement reasons.
4. **Overlap zone in legal/illegal fish:** Because of natural variability, some undersized fish (14¾–15¼ in.) produced legal-sized fillets, and some legal-sized fish (15¾–16¼ in.) produced undersized fillets — a necessary and accepted tradeoff of any weight-based regulation, quantified in detail in Table 4.
5. **Filleting yield rate quantified:** Loss on filleting averaged 50–55% of round weight, varying slightly by season (higher loss in spring vs. fall) and by legal status (higher loss in undersized vs. legal-sized fish), providing an empirical benchmark that contradicted higher informal industry estimates of 58–60% loss.
6. **Historical comparison:** Fish sampled in 1943–1945 were noted to be heavier at comparable lengths than fish sampled by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1929–1930, suggesting temporal shifts in body condition within the [[Saginaw Bay]] population, a relevant consideration for long-term regulatory calibration.
7. **Practical regulatory design:** A whole-number ounce limit (9.0 oz rather than 9.25 oz) was chosen for enforcement practicality and to provide a buffer against post-filleting weight loss during freezing/storage processing.
