# Hybrids and the Endangered Species Act: Policy Draft, Solicitor Opinions and Supporting Science

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hybridization #endangered-species-act

## Executive Summary

This document is an internal, pre-decisional package from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) on how the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) should treat hybrids. It contains a draft National Policy Issuance, a draft Federal Register notice, a November 1990 working group report, and a series of [[Solicitor's Opinions]] from 1977 to 1984. It also includes supporting scientific material and related correspondence. The working group's recommendation is that listed taxa be treated as inclusive of all individuals sharing the taxon's characteristic morphological and genetic traits, even where some genetic introgression is present. Individuals that are "evident hybrids" at the species level would be presumed covered by the "similarity of appearance" provisions. The document also records a long legal history in which the Solicitor's Office moved from protecting hybrids (1977, first opinion) to excluding them (1977 onward), and it documents scientific disagreement over mitochondrial DNA evidence of [[Wolf-Coyote Hybridization]] and the status of the [[Florida Panther]].

## Background & Description

The document is a mix of memoranda, draft notices, a meeting agenda and participant list, legal opinions, research bulletins, and published papers. The core question is whether the ESA protects hybrid offspring of listed taxa. The Act protects species, subspecies, and, for vertebrates only, distinct populations, so the boundary between "species" and "hybrid" is unclear.

Working definitions used in the record:
- **Hybrid:** offspring of two animals or plants from different species, where at least one parent is listed (Solicitor, 1977; 1983).
- **Cross:** genetic flow between subspecies or populations, which is not hybridization under the proposed policy.
- **Evident hybrid:** an individual that the best biological information shows to be a hybrid at the species level.

Legal history (Solicitor's Office opinions, as summarized in the record):
- **May 18, 1977:** hybrids covered, based on statutory language ("fish or wildlife" includes any offspring).
- **August 2, 1977:** reversed. Coverage would impede conservation and dilute gene pools. Congress was concerned with species, not hybrid offspring.
- **September 16, 1977:** no authority to issue Section 10 permits for hybrids.
- **May 6, 1981:** reaffirmed exclusion.
- **September 21, 1983:** offspring of two listed subspecies of the same species not protected; hybrids of two listed species also not covered.
- **August 24, 1984:** offspring of two populations of the same subspecies (one listed) not a hybrid; covered by the ESA. Augmentation of the [[Southern Selkirk Mountain Caribou]] herd with unlisted caribou was deemed lawful, with [[Experimental Population]] status (Section 10(j)) suggested as the safest route for transplants.

The Solicitor's Office also said in 1980 that it would not write a further opinion on hybrid subspecies until the Service adopted a consensus position.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Genetic and taxonomic background.** Scientists noted that hybridization is common in plants, with examples including [[Tragopogon]] amphidiploid species and [[Agave arizonica]]. Among fish, the record cites rainbow trout by inland cutthroat hybrids, pallid by shovelnose sturgeon hybrids, and chum by pink salmon crosses. Striped bass hybridization in the Southeast was reported to have swamped the native race in the wild.

**Wolves and coyotes.** A [[Lehman et al. 1991 Study]] of mitochondrial DNA reported coyote mtDNA in more than 50% of 72 (later 86) Minnesota gray wolves. In the Service's review, the study showed past, infrequent hybridization in the Great Lakes region and none in Rocky Mountain wolves. Coyote nuclear DNA and coyote phenotypic traits were not documented in U.S. wolves. The Service noted that mtDNA is maternally inherited and that its frequency says nothing about nuclear introgression.

**Red wolf.** The [[Red Wolf]] was lost to hybridization with coyotes in the wild. Captive-bred animals were reintroduced to Alligator River National Wildlife Refuge from 1987. The Great Smoky Mountains reintroduction (first phase starting March 1990) was designed to test whether released wolves could displace resident coyotes.

**Florida panther.** [[O'Brien et al. 1990]] reported two genetic stocks: a Big Cypress stock (historic *F. c. coryi*) and an Everglades stock carrying South American mtDNA and an APRT-B allele. The likely source was the captive Piper stock and releases in the 1950s to 1960s. Big Cypress panthers showed high inbreeding signs, including more than 90% defective sperm and 52% cryptorchidism. The Service proposed listing non-identifiable free-ranging *Felis concolor* in Florida as threatened under similarity of appearance (Federal Register, August 27, 1990).

**Other cases.** A [[Gray Wolf Delisting Petition]] was filed by Farm Bureau federations in Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho, and was denied with a 90-day finding. A [[Hawaiian Duck]] study (Griffin and Browne, 1990) concluded that koloa are seriously threatened by introgressive hybridization with feral [[Mallard]]s. [[Spotted Owl]] allozyme work (Barrowclough and Gutierrez 1990) found no variation in six coastal populations and an F_ST of 0.55 between New Mexico and coastal samples. [[Pacific Walrus]] mtDNA work found four haplotypes in six specimens. The [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*F. p. anatum*) population under the Barry Bridge in 1990 was addressed in a memo concluding that the species is listed only under similarity of appearance.

**Policy and conservation context.** The [[Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species]] (CITES) 1979 Resolution (Conf. 2) decides that hybrids are subject to the Convention if a parent taxon is listed, and that the stricter appendix applies to hybrids of taxa in different appendices. The Florida Panther and CITES issue is discussed as an example.

## Key Findings

- **Draft policy (National Policy Issuance #91-, 1991):** all listed taxa are treated as inclusive of individuals sharing the parent taxon's morphological and genetic traits, even with some introgression. Evident hybrids at the species level are presumed covered by similarity of appearance. Captive and feral exotic hybrids abroad are excluded.
- **Generic rule proposed for 50 CFR Part 17** to cover hybrids at the specific level unless a special rule states otherwise at listing.
- **Listing procedures (50 CFR Part 424)** to be amended so that taxa of naturally occurring, self-sustaining hybrid origin may be listed.
- **Augmentation policy (proposed):** when pure stocks are depressed to the point of extinction, introducing genetic material from a closely related stock at the minimum level needed is consistent with the Act. The group flagged the risk of creating surrogate species, as with eastern anatum peregrines and southeastern bald eagles.
- **Three options reviewed:** (1) do not protect hybrids; (2) protect hybrids; (3) protect hybrids case by case. The working group's recommendation combines the flexibility of option 3 with the ease of option 2.
- **Working group recommendations (November 1990):** rethink the species definition away from reproductive isolation, develop guidelines for "species" under the ESA, formulate factors for treating hybrids, prepare a generic rulemaking, and coordinate with outside experts.
- **Scientific caution:** the group noted that a few dozen loci may not show genome-wide differences; mtDNA results are not nuclear evidence; and preliminary results had reached the public without peer review.
- **Gray wolf finding:** the Service found the delisting petition did not present substantial information. It stated that the probability of successful recovery is not a permissible factor in a listing or delisting decision.
- **Florida panther proposal:** threatened status under similarity of appearance for free-living *Felis concolor* in Florida, with comments due October 26, 1990.
- **Internal status:** the policy, notice, and working group report are pre-decisional and should not be released publicly while the Service is in litigation.
