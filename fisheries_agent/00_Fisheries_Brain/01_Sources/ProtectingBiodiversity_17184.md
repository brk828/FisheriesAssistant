# Protecting Biodiversity Through Planning Rather Than Confrontation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This document combines a 1994 *Nature Conservancy* magazine essay by Interior Secretary [[Bruce Babbitt]] with a sidebar by Conservancy staff on managing biodiversity at the ecosystem scale. Babbitt argues that conflicts over endangered species, such as the [[Snail Darter]] and [[Northern Spotted Owl]], arise from late, crisis-driven responses. He proposes ecosystem-scale planning, use of existing laws such as the [[Endangered Species Act]], stronger science through a new [[National Biological Survey]], and market pricing of natural resources. The companion piece by Deborah B. Jensen argues that land acquisition alone cannot protect biodiversity, using the [[Loach Minnow]] of the San Pedro River as the central example. The sidebar on Joan Bird's article "Managing Biodiversity" is captioned only in the source and has no body text.

## Background & Description

The essay is written by Bruce Babbitt, who had served as Attorney General and Governor of Arizona and as president of the League of Conservation Voters. It is opinion and policy commentary rather than research, and it reports no original sample sizes or measurements.

Babbitt uses the [[Snail Darter]] case to illustrate the costs of confrontation:

- In 1973 opponents of the [[Tellico Dam]] on the Little Tennessee River used the 3-inch fish as a cudgel against the project.
- In 1978 the United States Supreme Court ordered work stopped on the nearly complete, $137-million project.
- Tennessee legislators persuaded Congress to exempt the dam from the Endangered Species Act two years later, and thousands of acres were flooded.
- The snail darter was taken off the endangered-species list in 1984.

Other cases in the essay are the Pacific Northwest timber and [[Northern Spotted Owl]] dispute, the [[California Gnatcatcher]] planning effort in southern California, and the [[Red-Cockaded Woodpecker]] agreement with [[Georgia-Pacific Corporation]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pacific Northwest old growth.** The essay describes a dispute over timber production and the northern spotted owl that Babbitt frames as a broader crisis in forest management and ecological balance. Environmentalists urged preservation of 3 million acres of old-growth trees not yet cut. Timber representatives testified that logging restrictions could cost tens of thousands of jobs.

**Southern California coastal sage scrub.** About 250,000 acres of coastal sage scrub support the California gnatcatcher, a small blue-gray songbird that rarely moves more than a few acres from its nest. The bird stays in its habitat even when it is bulldozed. The cactus wren shares this habitat.

**Southeastern pine forests.** Georgia-Pacific, described as the nation's largest forest products company, owns 4.2 million acres of pine forest in the South. About 56,000 acres hold 300 to 400 red-cockaded woodpeckers, which nest in cavities in living old pine trees.

**San Pedro River basin, Arizona and Mexico.** Thirteen native fish species once inhabited the river and its tributaries, and five have already disappeared. Groundwater pumping, surface-water diversion and vegetation change have left many dry stretches. Non-native [[Red Shiner]] and [[Yellow Bullhead]] compete with native fish in the altered habitat.

**Coral reefs and migratory birds.** Jensen notes that reef-building colonies depend on larvae carried by ocean currents and on water quality from upstream. Migratory birds use many places across their annual range, so no single preserve can protect all the processes they need.

Other ecosystems mentioned include Louisiana coastal wetlands, Pacific Northwest streams and riparian habitat, and areas affected by offshore oil drilling.

## Key Findings

**Babbitt's main arguments and proposals:**

- **Avoid crisis.** Waiting until a species is near extinction leads to conflict. The Pacific Northwest plan "should have been hammered out 20 years ago."
- **Use existing law.** Babbitt argues that no new laws are needed and that the authority of the Endangered Species Act is underused. He describes it as "probably the most revolutionary environmental law of this century" and cites successes with the [[Bald Eagle]], [[Peregrine Falcon]] and American alligator.
- **Plan at the ecosystem scale.** Protection should consider whole ecosystems rather than small parcels.
- **Use state planning with federal backstop.** California's [[Natural Communities Conservation Planning Program]], begun in 1991, lets developers build on part of the land while preserves are set aside. The gnatcatcher was listed as threatened rather than endangered, and the federal government will take over if the planning groups fail to reach sound agreements or keep to timetables.
- **Cooperate with industry.** Georgia-Pacific agreed to halt logging on woodpecker colonies, create buffer zones and avoid road building near colonies, in return for freedom from certain Endangered Species Act restrictions.
- **Build a science base.** Babbitt announced a [[National Biological Survey]] at the Interior Department, modeled on the U.S. Geological Survey. Its tasks would include basic research, reporting on the status and trends of U.S. wildlife habitats, and addressing wetland loss, offshore drilling, species decline and invasive species.
- **Reform resource pricing.** Grazing, mineral extraction, reservoir water use and timber cutting should pay fair market prices, which Babbitt says encourages conservation.
- **Balance with nature.** Babbitt describes three eras of American conservation: Theodore Roosevelt's resource ethic, the 1960s–1970s response to pollution (prompted in part by [[Silent Spring]]), and a present era that requires "peace with the natural environment."

**Jensen's main conclusions (Conservancy sidebar):**

- Protecting biodiversity is more complex than acquiring land.
- Key ecological processes cross preserve boundaries and change over time.
- Conservation plans should include ecosystem-scale threats and processes.
- Success requires a range of strategies, including land acquisition, habitat management and partnerships.
