# Ecological Survey of White Oak Creek, Roane County, Tennessee (1950–1953): Summary of Findings

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #freshwater-ecology #radioecology

## Executive Summary

This report summarizes a three-year (June 1950 – July 1, 1953) cooperative survey by the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] for the [[Atomic Energy Commission]] on the physical and biological effects of radioactive effluents from the [[Oak Ridge National Laboratory]] on [[White Oak Creek]] and [[White Oak Lake]]. The survey covered botany, limnology and fishery biology. Organisms throughout the system accumulated radionuclides far above water concentrations, with [[Radiophosphorus]] dominant in plankton and [[Radiostrontium]] dominant in fish skeletons. Fish populations showed slower growth, shorter lifespans and the disappearance of two species, which the author attributed partly to chronic radiation exposure, though causation was not proven. The author strongly recommended long-term continuation of the work.

## Background & Description

The survey was initiated to determine whether seepage from underground storage tanks and routine low-level discharges into White Oak Creek affected flora and fauna, and whether tolerance might permit savings in waste disposal. The Tennessee Valley Authority was chosen for its stream-sanitation expertise and proximity to Oak Ridge. Laboratory space was provided by the [[Health Physics Division]].

The watershed (3,850 acres) lies in the Ridge and Valley Province. White Oak Creek flows to the [[Clinch River]] at about mile 20.8. White Oak Lake is an impoundment formed behind a highway fill and concrete culvert, with full pool at about 750 ft and an area of roughly 1.5 miles upstream reach; the normal working pool of ~748 ft gave an area of 35.87 acres. The lake serves as a settling basin for Laboratory effluent. The Settling Basin outfall, averaging about 650,000 gallons per 24-hour period in 1953, divides the uncontaminated upper creek from the contaminated lower section.

Study scope included:
- **Botany:** 38 bryophyte species, 392 vascular plant species and radionuclide accumulation in plants.
- **Limnology:** physico-chemical monitoring, plankton pulses and invertebrate accumulation.
- **Fishery biology:** six semiannual netting estimates with rotenone verification, dissections, food-habit analysis, and tissue radioassay.
- **Vertebrates:** including waterfowl banding.

Over 25,000 radioassay samples were prepared, with results expressed as gross beta counts per minute per gram.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biota.** Of 523 plants and 681 animals listed (partial inventory, terrestrial insects excluded), the vertebrate fauna comprised 228 species: 23 fish, 11 amphibians, 17 reptiles, 158 birds and 16 mammals. The invertebrate fauna included 253 species in 12 phyla. Algae were represented by 93 genera.

**Climate and disturbance.** Average annual rainfall was about 52 inches. The survey coincided with unusual conditions: a complete ice cover in late 1950 (lowest temperatures in 80 years), and severe droughts in 1951 and 1952. Construction on the Laboratory site caused heavy silting, making the lake turbid for the first two years. Turbidity, from clay particles, limited rooted plants and attached algae but apparently did not affect planktonic forms.

**Water chemistry.** Nitrate nitrogen reached up to 10 ppm at times and was considered a likely cause of algal pulses. Total alkalinity ranged 35–160 ppm, total hardness 60–390 ppm, and pH 7.10–9.55. Free CO₂ was never at deleterious levels. Ammonia values (0.209–0.388 ppm on September 16, 1952) indicated no organic pollution problem. The primary-treated sewage effluent from the Laboratory was judged to make the lake unusually fertile.

**Plankton.** Plankton volume followed a bimodal curve peaking in spring and fall. Pulses of [[Volvox]], [[Euglena]], [[Pandorina]], [[Chlamydomonas]] and others were typically short-lived, lasting from two days to over a month. The largest Volvox pulse exceeded 700,000 colonies per liter in mid-May 1952.

**Radionuclide gradients.** Accumulation decreased from the Settling Basin downstream, and from the upper lake toward the dam. Autoradiograms of plants exposed for 24 hours below the basin, 72 hours from the lake, and no darkening below the dam after seven days.

## Key Findings

**Plants**
- Radionuclide concentration in plants was greatest in bark of woody plants and leaves of forbs and grasses. Concentration fell from the Settling Basin downstream.
- All living plants selectively concentrated specific radionuclides (strontium, cesium, ruthenium, rare earths, zirconium).
- One American elm (*Ulmus americana*) showed leaf-edge injury attributed to radioruthenium. Some variegated cat-tail (*Typha latifolia*) leaves reverted to normal when transplanted away from contamination.
- Dead plant material that fell into water accumulated large amounts of radioactivity by adsorption, and an organic fraction of lake bottom soil adsorbed more than the mineral fraction.

**Limnology and invertebrates**
- Radiophosphorus was the dominant concentrated element in plankton and benthos. Concentration factors were estimated at roughly 100,000 or more for phytoplankton, although decay before counting and the unmeasured water fraction make these estimates approximate.
- Spirogyra mats (estimated over one ton, with more than 100 millicuries) gave a phosphorus concentration factor of about 850,000.
- Midge larvae were the most abundant littoral organisms. The sublittoral fauna comprised Tubifex, Tendipes and Chaoborus. Creek benthos below the outfall was reduced relative to the upstream section, which had nearly twice as many genera.

**Fish population**
- Black crappies were more than twice as vulnerable to hoopnetting as bluegills. The last two netting periods were considered unreliable due to reduced netting efficiency.
- The black crappie estimate for spring 1953 was 1,810 fish, 29% smaller than the 2,33x fish recovered by rotenone a month later.
- [[White Crappie]] and [[Redhorse]] declined. By April 1953 no white crappies remained, and fewer than 50 redhorse persisted (17 were recovered by rotenone).
- Growth was slower than in nearby TVA impoundments, and life spans of black crappie and largemouth bass were up to 25% shorter.
- Black crappies fed mainly on Chaoborus larvae (43% of food volume) and midge larvae, with about 75% of their diet being small invertebrates. Bluegills were omnivorous and fed along the littoral zone.
- Skeletal accumulation was 40–50 times that in muscle. Black crappies concentrated more radioactivity in hard tissues (about 5:3 ratio), and bluegills more in soft tissues (about 3:2 ratio).
- Both species accumulated [[Radiostrontium]] at 20,000–30,000 times water concentrations.
- Seasonal peaks occurred by midsummer, with a sharp decrease in August suggesting aestivation. Winter levels settled at about one-third to one-half the summer peak.

**Other vertebrates**
- Migratory waterfowl accumulated at least 5 microcuries of activity per bird, mostly in edible tissues, emitted by phosphorus-32.
- One muskrat carried nearly 100 microcuries of strontium-90 in its skeleton and had metastatic osteogenic sarcoma.
- In September 1952 to the next season, 649 waterfowl were banded (390 mallards, 137 wood ducks, 96 black ducks), and an estimated 6,500+ birds used the lake that season.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Continuous low-level irradiation was judged a likely contributor to population decline and reduced fitness, though the author states the evidence is not unequivocal.
- The author estimated the external dose from surrounding water at 1.1 rep per week; fish and turtles may have received more than 50 roentgens of external irradiation annually.
- It is strongly recommended that studies of radioactive effluent effects on wild populations continue on a long-term basis.
