# Natural Resources of New Mexico

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #sport-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1964 booklet from the U.S. Department of the Interior is a state-wide survey of New Mexico's natural resources: land, water, fish and wildlife, minerals, forests, Indian resources, geology, and recreation. Its aquatic content centers on freshwater sport fisheries, the scarcity and management of water, and the federal agencies that run hatcheries, reservoirs, and water projects. The fish material is descriptive rather than research-based. It gives stream and reservoir inventories, hatchery production figures, and fishing use totals, and it does not report sampling designs or population studies.

## Background & Description

New Mexico, the "Land of Enchantment," covers 122,634 square miles and had a 1960 census population of 951,023. The state is semiarid, with a mean elevation above one mile. Its terrain runs from the Sangre de Cristo and San Juan Mountains in the north to the Basin and Range and High Plains provinces in the south and east. The highest point is Wheeler Peak at 13,160 feet, and the lowest is the Pecos River exit at Red Bluff, at 2,876 feet. Total relief exceeds 10,000 feet.

The booklet describes New Mexico's natural water surface area as only 155 square miles. Its major rivers are the [[Rio Grande]], Canadian, Cimarron, [[Pecos River]], [[San Juan River]], and Gila. The Rio Grande bisects the state north to south. The Pecos heads in the Sangre de Cristo Mountains, runs more than 500 river miles before leaving the state, and loses flow in its lower reaches through evapotranspiration, percolation, and salt-bed influence.

The Federal Government's role is framed around conservation and the "wise use" of resources. The document was prepared under Secretary Stewart L. Udall and covers programs from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[Corps of Engineers]], the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Forest Service]], the [[Bureau of Land Management]], the [[Geological Survey]], and the [[Office of Saline Water]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water scarcity.** Water is described as the factor that will control future economic growth. The Upper Rio Grande and Pecos basins are classed by the Senate Select Committee on Water Resources as the No. 1 water-short area in the nation. Surface waters of the Rio Grande basin are fully allocated under interstate compact and a treaty with Mexico. Most surface water outside the Colorado drainage has been appropriated.

**Aquatic habitat.** The booklet contrasts cold, clear mountain trout water in the north and southwestern mountains with warm-water reservoirs and streams in the south. Lowland watercourses are mostly intermittent and offer no fishing. Irrigation return flows and drain ditches are also part of the aquatic landscape. The Rio Grande, for example, carries stocked warm-water fish through 600 miles of drain ditches between Elephant Butte Lake and a point about 60 miles east of El Paso.

**Water quality.** Pecos River quality declines downstream. The document links this to natural salt beds beneath a porous limestone. The Office of Saline Water built a demonstration plant at Roswell to convert brackish well water. It produces one million gallons per day, and the cost of converted water is described as falling from about $4-$5 to $1-$1.25 per thousand gallons over 1952-61.

**Wildlife associations.** Waterfowl habitat is an important part of the aquatic picture. [[Bosque del Apache National Wildlife Refuge]] was established in 1939 for wintering migratory waterfowl and covers 57,191 acres. [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]] on the Pecos supports large numbers of ducks and sandhill cranes.

**Terrestrial context.** Altitude controls floral zones more than latitude does, ranging from subalpine vegetation and spruce-fir forests through ponderosa pine and piñon-juniper woodland to Chihuahuan desert cacti. The state's game includes [[Mule Deer]] (estimated herd of about 200,000), elk, antelope, black bear, and mountain lion.

## Key Findings

**Fishery resources and sport fishing**

- Cold-water streams in the north and southwest support trout. Species named are rainbow, brown, Loch Leven, cutthroat, brook, and west-slope cutthroat trout.
- The [[Rio Grande]] offers about 100 miles of fishing from Embudo to the Colorado state line. The rainbow and brown trout are described as the state's largest fishes, often weighing 15 pounds.
- Warm-water fishing comes mainly from four large reservoirs (surface acreage at spillway level):
  - [[Conchas Lake]]: 16,500 acres
  - [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]]: 40,000 acres
  - [[Caballo Reservoir]]: 11,500 acres
  - Alamogordo Lake: 4,500 acres
- About 175 smaller lakes and reservoirs total roughly 375,000 surface acres. Most provide trout or warm-water fishing.
- About 15 fishing streams wider than 20 feet have a combined length of about 1,300 miles. About 250 streams narrower than 20 feet total about 2,250 miles. Most are on public land and free to fish.
- Sport fishing on Indian reservation waters supported about 55,000 fishermen-days in a recent year, on 715 surface acres of reservoirs and 30 miles of streams.

**Hatchery production (recent-year figures as reported)**

- [[Eagle Nest National Fish Hatchery]]: a rainbow trout egg-collecting station. Usually 500,000 to 750,000 eggs are hatched per season, and the fry are stocked in Eagle Nest Lake.
- [[Santa Rosa National Fish Hatchery]]: 5,873 pounds of rainbow trout, 155,040 largemouth bass, 138,725 channel catfish, and 183,010 sunfish.
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]: 515,000 largemouth bass, 193,000 channel catfish, and 1,000 sunfish.
- [[Hot Springs National Fish Hatchery]]: 7,568 pounds of trout, 17,285 bass, and 28,625 sunfishes.

**Management and use**

- The state Game and Fish Department surveys populations and range condition, transplants game, acquires land, and improves access. It has provided access to about 25 fishing streams and lakes.
- Federal aid supports management surveys, aquatic weed control, rough fish control, stream improvement, and impoundment development.
- Fishing on Federal and Indian lands is being developed with tribal councils. The booklet notes this produces tribal revenue and jobs.
- Reclamation reservoirs had more than 1,500,000 visitor-days, with a total water surface of 39,262 acres.

**Water and power**

- New Mexico is described as the oldest irrigated area in the nation. Irrigation depends on reservoirs, pumped or artesian wells, and ground water from alluvium and limestone.
- The Pecos average annual water production is estimated at 1,095,000 acre-feet, 67 percent of it in New Mexico.
- The San Juan basin allocation under the Upper Colorado River compact is expected to average about 838,000 acre-feet annually.
- Elephant Butte Dam operates the state's only Federal hydroelectric plant, rated at 24,300 kilowatts.
- Elephant Butte Reservoir has a capacity of 2,206,800 acre-feet. The [[Rio Grande Project]] supplies irrigation to about 102,000 acres of New Mexico land.

**Planned or ongoing projects**

- The [[Navajo Reservoir]] on the San Juan River was under construction as part of the Colorado River storage project.
- Corps of Engineers projects under construction included Abiquiu Dam, Two Rivers Dam, and the Rio Grande Floodway.
- The San Juan-Chama and Navajo irrigation projects were recently authorized.

**Broader resource base**

- Land area is 122,634 square miles. Of the 77.8 million acres, 32.3 million are private, 27.5 million Federal, 11.5 million State, and 6.3 million Indian.
- Petroleum and natural gas make up about two-thirds of the more than $650 million annual mineral value.
- New Mexico leads all states in uranium and potash production.
- Forests cover about 21.3 million acres, of which roughly 6 million are commercial stands dominated by ponderosa pine.
- [[Carlsbad Caverns National Park]] is described as having the largest underground room in the world.
