# Introgressive Hybridization of Redeye Bass and Smallmouth Bass and Its Management Implications

#ichthyology #centrarchidae #hybridization #conservation_genetics

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Redeye Bass]] (*Micropterus coosae*) is the smallest of the black basses, endemic to the Black Warrior, Cahaba, Coosa, and Tallapoosa rivers and Alabama River tributaries above the Fall Line in Alabama, the Chattahoochee River above Columbus, Georgia, and headwaters of the Savannah River in Georgia, North Carolina, and South Carolina. In Tennessee, redeye bass are endemic only to Shedds Creek and Cohutta Creek of the Alabama River system. The species has slower growth rates than other *Micropterus* and was historically confined to small, cool, headwater streams. [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*) is endemic to a larger geographic area and has been widely introduced since the mid-nineteenth century. Age, maturity, fecundity, and specific growth parameters are not reported in this document.

**Habitat Associations**: Redeye bass historically inhabited headwater reaches of upland streams in middle Tennessee where other *Micropterus* species were absent. [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Spotted Bass]] (*Micropterus punctulatus*) were abundant in downstream reaches. Water quality parameters measured at seven sites on [[Roaring River]] showed no significant differences (p = 0.05) in temperature (range 11.8-14.4°C), dissolved oxygen (8.1-9.0 mg/L), total alkalinity (117-140 mg/L), pH (7.8-8.4), or conductivity (199-222 µmhos). Significant differences existed among sites for substrate composition (silt, sand, gravel, cobble, boulder, bedrock), canopy cover (27-96%), and maximum velocity (0.40-1.28 m/s). Following introduction in the 1950s, redeye bass maintained reproducing populations and extended their range into downstream reaches, resulting in multispecies communities of *Micropterus* with overlapping distributions in middle reaches.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Downstream movement of introduced redeye bass from headwater reaches into middle and lower reaches of [[Roaring River]] resulted in range expansion and overlap with endemic smallmouth bass populations. Earlier studies (Tatum 1965, Cathey 1973, Gwinner 1973) noted that redeye bass were confined to headwater reaches while smallmouth bass occupied middle and lower reaches. By 1987, Crumby reported overlap of redeye bass with smallmouth bass in middle reaches, indicating progressive downstream colonization over approximately 30 years.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Not reported.

**Environmental Drivers**: The introduction of nonendemic redeye bass into streams containing endemic smallmouth bass populations created conditions conducive to hybridization. Hubbs (1955) theorized that introductions and habitat alterations may encourage hybridization. Because redeye bass have not evolved sympatrically with smallmouth bass, natural reproductive isolating mechanisms may not exist to prohibit interspecific spawning. The document notes that redeye bass were introduced to supplement existing headwater sport fisheries, creating artificial sympatry between species that did not historically co-occur.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Introgressive hybridization between redeye bass and smallmouth bass has occurred throughout the observed *Micropterus* population in [[Roaring River]]. Biochemical genetic analysis revealed heterozygous individuals (B₂B₃ genotype) throughout the river system, indicating hybridization has occurred over several generations. Many introgressed individuals were not phenotypically distinguishable from parental species based on meristic characters alone. The presence of various genotypes suggests development of a genetic admixture or hybrid swarm population. Future generations will become more introgressed as genes pass through succeeding generations.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: The genetic integrity of redeye bass populations is threatened by continued introgression with smallmouth bass genes. Similar hybridization events have been documented between smallmouth bass and other *Micropterus* species (spotted bass, [[Guadalupe Bass]] *M. treculi*, [[Largemouth Bass]] *M. salmoides*) following introductions of congenerics outside their endemic ranges. These hybridization events resulted in loss of genetic integrity of endemic *Micropterus* populations. F₁ hybrids produced in laboratory studies have demonstrated ability to produce viable F₂ and F₃ generations. While some F₁ hybrids may exhibit positive heterosis, outbreeding depression may become dominant if introgression continues, potentially resulting in adaptively inferior fish with less growth potential than either parental species. The genetic integrity of less abundant species of *Micropterus* is especially threatened by continued introductions of congenerics.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Greater consideration should be given to the introduction of congenerics if genetic integrity is to be maintained. The document emphasizes the importance of genetic conservation by increasing awareness of dangers involved when nonendemic species are translocated. Continued introductions of congenerics should not be permitted to protect genetic integrity of endemic *Micropterus* populations. Management agencies should recognize that meristic and morphological analyses alone are insufficient to detect introgressed individuals; biochemical genetic analyses are necessary to identify the full extent of hybridization. The widespread nature of introgression documented in this study (heterozygous individuals found throughout the river system) indicates that prevention of initial introductions is critical, as remediation after establishment of hybrid populations is extremely difficult.
