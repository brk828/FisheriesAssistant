# Morphological and Physiological Aspects of Swimming Performance in Three Cyprinid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation (University of California, Los Angeles, 1994; author Ana Esther T. Escandon, advisor [[Malcolm S. Gordon]]) compares the morphology, hydromechanics, swimming endurance and metabolism of three North American cyprinids: the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] ([[Gila Elegans]]), the introduced [[Fathead Minnow]] ([[Pimephales Promelas]]), and the threatened native [[Arroyo Chub]] ([[Gila Orcutti]]). Because arroyo chubs could not be found in habitats where they were reported, the fathead minnow was used as a surrogate for the arroyo chub. The work is organized as three chapters: morphology and hydromechanics, [[Critical Swimming Speed]] (Ucrit), and [[Active Metabolic Rate]] (AMR) and [[Standard Metabolic Rate]] (SMR).

## Background & Description

- **Study species.** The bonytail chub is a large-river Colorado River endemic with a slender body, thin [[Caudal Peduncle]] and pronounced predorsal hump in adults. It is extirpated in California and listed as endangered. The fathead minnow is a deep-bodied, eastern and Great Plains species that has been introduced to California and tolerates a wide range of conditions. The arroyo chub is a native southern California minnow, closely related to the bonytail but morphologically similar to the fathead.
- **Taxonomic context.** Cyprinidae is the most species-rich freshwater fish family. California has thirteen native minnow species, with at least six introduced. Introduced species, habitat loss and urbanization have endangered or depleted several native minnows.
- **Biogeography.** Fathead minnows are widely distributed across the Hudson Bay, Mississippi, Great Lakes, Ohio, Rio Grande and Western Gulf Slope drainages. The bonytail belongs to the "big river fish" group of the Colorado River, a fauna with many endemic and morphologically specialized species. The arroyo chub occurs in the Los Angeles Plains, upper Santa Clara River, and San Luis Rey and Santa Margarita rivers. Its closest relatives are the Sonora chub and Yaqui chub in northwestern Mexico.
- **Study rationale.** The original aim was to test whether the two closely related but morphologically different species (arroyo chub and bonytail) differed in swimming performance and activity metabolism. Arroyo chubs were never found, and their habitats were occupied by fathead minnows. The author suggests the arroyo chub has largely been replaced by the fathead.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat degradation.** Dams, sedimentation changes, riparian loss and inputs of sewage and fertilizer have reduced Colorado River Delta habitat, which led to the bonytail's endangered listing. Coastal streams of southwestern California support largely endemic fishes because of isolation.
- **Competition and displacement.** Introduced fathead minnows are tolerant of unsuitable conditions and are aggressive and territorial during breeding. Replacement of arroyo chubs by fathead minnows may already be occurring. Hybridization between the related arroyo chub and the [[Mohave Tui Chub]] has also been documented.
- **Thermal regime.** The bonytail is reared at Dexter National Fish Hatchery in ponds where water temperatures range from 1 to 28 °C annually, with an optimum around 20 °C. Sespe Creek, where fathead minnows were collected, ranged from 9 to 33 °C in the author's measurements. Laboratory holding temperatures varied from 16 to 22 °C.
- **Swimming ecology.** Swimming performance relates to feeding, predator escape and reproduction. Streamlined bodies, narrow caudal peduncles and deeply forked tails are associated with fast waters and sustained swimming. Deep bodies are associated with slower waters.

## Key Findings

### Chapter 1: Morphology and Hydromechanics
- The arroyo chub (G. orcutti) and fathead minnow (P. promelas) are morphologically and hydromechanically similar.
- Both are significantly different from the bonytail chub (G. elegans).
- The arroyo chub was not collected in the habitats where it was reported, so P. promelas served as its surrogate.

### Chapter 2: Swimming Endurance (Ucrit)
- **Method.** Ucrit was measured in a [[Blazka-Type Respirometer]] at 15, 20 and 25 °C. Fathead minnows (44–64 mm SL, collected at Sespe Creek in March–April 1989) were tested in October–November 1990 and in a later spring 1991 set. Bonytail juveniles (57–77 mm SL, from Dexter National Fish Hatchery) were tested in May–June 1991. Velocity increased in 5 cm/sec steps every 45 min for fathead minnows and every 60 min for bonytails. Ucrit values were scaled to body lengths per second (BL/sec).
- **Seasonal differences.** Fall 1990 and spring 1991 fathead minnow data sets did not differ significantly, so they were combined.
- **Results.**
  - At 15 °C, G. elegans Ucrit (3.1 BL/sec) was significantly lower than P. promelas (4.2 BL/sec).
  - At 20 and 25 °C, G. elegans Ucrit (4.4 and 4.1 BL/sec) was not significantly different from P. promelas (4.2 and 4.2 BL/sec).
  - Bonytails swam longer before fatigue than fathead minnows, despite lower Ucrit values.
  - Among bonytails, Ucrit at 15 °C was significantly lower than at 20 and 25 °C. Fathead minnow Ucrit did not differ among temperatures.
- **Comparison to literature.** Ucrit values for both species (fathead 4.2–4.7 BL/sec; bonytail 3.1–4.4 BL/sec) fall within the range reported for other fishes (0.08–18.0 BL/sec, per Beamish 1978).

### Chapter 3: Active and Standard Metabolic Rates
- **Method.** AMRs were estimated by regressing log oxygen uptake against swimming speed. SMRs were estimated by extrapolating regressions to zero activity. Results were scaled to body mass (ml O₂/kg·hr) and to relative swimming speed. Bonytail tests used single fish; fathead tests used groups of two to three fish, because single fathead minnows behaved erratically or died.
- **Bonytail chub.** AMRs were lowest at 15 °C and highest at 20 °C, with all three temperatures significantly different. SMRs at 20 and 25 °C were similar and both were significantly lower than SMRs at 15 °C.
- **Fathead minnow.** AMRs at 15 °C were significantly higher than at 20 and 25 °C, which did not differ. The reported AMRs were 68, 224 and 214 ml O₂/kg·hr at 15, 20 and 25 °C. SMRs at 20 and 25 °C were identical and significantly higher than at 15 °C.
- **Interspecific differences.**
  - At 15 °C, neither AMRs nor SMRs differed between species.
  - At 20 and 25 °C, bonytail AMRs were significantly higher and SMRs significantly lower than fathead minnow values. The bonytail therefore showed greater oxygen-uptake efficiency and lower maintenance cost at warm temperatures.
- **Metabolic scope.** The author notes that swimming can raise metabolic rate by up to about 15-fold above standard values, which is the difference between AMR and SMR.

### Conclusions and Management Implications
- Morphologically and hydromechanically, G. elegans differs strongly from G. orcutti and P. promelas. The bonytail shows features of rapid swimming in swift water, whereas the arroyo chub and fathead minnow show features of slow-moving streams.
- The two species have similar endurance at 20 and 25 °C. The bonytail performs better energetically at warmer temperatures. Both its endurance and efficiency are lowest at 15 °C.
- The fathead minnow has better endurance and efficiency at 15 °C. This may help it exploit cold, fast late-winter and early-spring runoff in southern California streams.
- Because the arroyo chub resembles the fathead minnow morphologically, the author predicts similar critical swimming speeds and physiology. Replacement of arroyo chubs by fathead minnows is therefore likely.
- The author argues that physiological and ecological knowledge of native and endangered California minnows is needed to guide effective conservation.

### Study Limitations
- Bonytails came from a hatchery and may not represent wild performance.
- Acute temperature tests did not include acclimation.
- Interspecific comparisons were complicated by different group and single-fish protocols, and by differences in body size.
