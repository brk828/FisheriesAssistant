# Biotic Communities and Wetlands of the Southwestern United States and Northwestern Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is Part 5, "Wetlands," of a regional survey of [[Biotic Communities]] in the American Southwest and northwestern Mexico, written by [[Dale E. Brown]] with W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) and David E. Brown (Arizona Game and Fish Department). It describes the wetland and riparian communities of the region, their physical settings, their climatic zonation and the anthropogenic forces that have degraded them, and includes a distribution table (Table 3a) of native freshwater fishes across the region's drainage basins. The text is primarily ecological and descriptive rather than a single-species study. Its central message is that wetlands are small in area but disproportionately important biologically, and that most of them have been reduced or lost through dams, diversions, groundwater pumping and channelization.

## Background & Description

The chapter is part of a larger work originally published in *Desert Plants* (vol. 4, nos. 1–4, 1982) by the Boyce Thompson Southwestern Arboretum, and reissued by the University of Utah Press. A companion four-color map, "Biotic Communities of the Southwest," was distributed separately.

The authors define **wetlands** as landscapes that are periodically, seasonally or continuously submerged and that support species or life forms differing from adjacent biotas. Wetlands depend on conditions more moist than local precipitation alone provides. They occur in or near drainageways and floodplains ([[Riparian Zone]]s), on poorly drained land, along seacoasts, and around springs, ponds and lake margins. They may take the form of forest, woodland, scrubland, marshland, strand or almost entirely submergent vegetation.

Wetland communities are often dynamic. Floods, inundation, desiccation and grazing create successional stages, and the authors note that growth of aquatic and semiaquatic plants can reach at least a quasi-climax within weeks or months. Although formation classes can be complex, the number of dominant species is often surprisingly small. Monospecific stands (consociations) of aquatic plants recur remarkably often in time and space.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climatic zonation.** The chapter is organized by climatic zone, from Arctic-Boreal and Cold-Temperate through Warm-Temperate to Tropical-Subtropical. Montane riparian forests occur at roughly 2,100–2,300 m descending to about 1,700 m, and locally as low as 1,350 m. Subalpine riparian scrub is dominated by willows (*Salix* spp.), with Thin-leaf Alder (*Alnus tenuifolia*) and Red Elderberry (*Sambucus racemosa*). In warm-temperate zones, riparian forests can have canopies more than 30 m high.

**Rivers and river alteration.** Dams have altered most of the Southwest's major rivers, changing flow and temperature regimes and reducing once-perennial lower reaches. The [[Colorado River]], the region's largest river, has been reduced to a series of impoundments connected by canals, with much of its former nutrient base lost. Before about 1880, lowland alluvial plains were wetter and less well drained, with boggy margins and marshy sloughs. Channel cutting (arroyo cutting) began in the 1880s and, together with channelization, impoundment, diversion and gravel mining, has nearly eliminated the spring-fed cienega as a common feature.

**Lakes and playas.** Natural lakes are rare in the Southwest because of aridity, high evaporation, high siltation rates and steep topographic gradients. Most natural lakes are tectonic in origin, and most are marshy and seasonal. Large endorheic lakes are typically saline and support abundant invertebrates, which attract waterfowl and shorebirds, but offer little habitat for fishes. [[Playa]]s, normally dry closed basins, occur in all four North American deserts. The [[Salton Sea]] in California and [[Laguna Salada]] in Baja California del Norte have been, or are becoming, permanent water bodies through diversion of Colorado River water.

**Springs and rockpools.** Springs within bolsones have supplied aquatic habitat for a large share of the region's unique "desert" fishes. Each spring ranges from a few square meters to several hectares of surface water, and many act as permanent refugia for relict plants, invertebrates and vertebrates. Rockpools (*tinajas*) in arid mountain ranges served as oases for ancient peoples and later desert travelers. [[Laguna Prieta]] in Sonora is a permanent saline lake fed by springs, though photographs from 1979 showed it nearly dry, possibly from natural fluctuation and nearby well fields.

**Artificial wetlands.** Reservoirs, farm ponds and cattle tanks (*stockponds*) now far outnumber natural freshwater habitats. They have created lentic habitat in a region formerly dominated by streams. Canal systems link formerly isolated waters, allowing dispersal, but concrete lining has sharply reduced their seepage-supported riparian habitat.

**Coastal and marine systems.** The coastlines of Baja California, southern California and Sonora, with adjacent marine environments, contain the richest and most extensive wetland and aquatic habitats in the American Southwest. Tidal marshes once occupied most estuaries but are now greatly reduced. Offshore [[Kelp Forest]]s (*Macrocystis pyrifera*) and [[Eelgrass]] (*Zostera marina*) meadows support a flora and fauna still known to only a relatively few researchers. The Sea of Cortez has a tropical-subtropical fish fauna derived from the south, while the California Current keeps temperate conditions south to below the 27th parallel.

**Fish zonation and drainage patterns.** Fish species richness in a drainage basin depends more on geologic, climatic and evolutionary history, and on watershed size and complexity, than on elevation or terrestrial vegetation. Cold-water salmonids are restricted to cool, high-altitude streams. Cyprinodontids and poeciliids are restricted to lower, warmer places. Native fishes overall are dominated by the minnow (Cyprinidae) and sucker (Catostomidae) families.

**Threats.** The authors describe estuarine, riparian and other wetland communities as "continuing to be rapidly destroyed" by reduced streamflow and falling water tables. Other pressures include nutrient entrapment in Colorado River reservoirs, the near cessation of freshwater inflow to the upper Sea of Cortez, and sewage inputs off southern California. Water mining has been especially damaging to relict wetlands. For example, springs in massive limestone of western Texas stopped flowing entirely when pumped for agriculture (Brune, 1975). A cottonwood and mesquite bosque on the [[Santa Cruz River]] near San Xavier was virtually destroyed by groundwater pumping.

## Key Findings

**1. Wetlands are small in extent but disproportionately important.** The authors say aquatic, riparian and other wetland communities of the Southwest "have rarely been differentiated or shown on maps," tend to be small, and have importance and biological interest "totally disproportionate" to their limited geographic occurrence.

**2. Native fish distributions are documented by basin.** Table 3a lists native freshwater fishes across the Rio Grande, Colorado River, Gila, Yaqui, Guzman, Pecos, Death Valley and Pacific coastal drainages, along with Gulf of Mexico and interior closed basins. Footnotes flag occurrences outside the area of present coverage and identify taxa with uncertain status. Key groups include:
- **Minnows (Cyprinidae):** many chubs (*[[Gila]]* spp.), spinedaces (*Lepidomeda* spp.), shiners (*Notropis* spp.) and dace. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is a large-river Colorado basin species.
- **Suckers (Catostomidae):** numerous *Catostomus* and *Pantosteus* (mountain suckers).
- **Trouts (Salmonidae):** relict native trouts such as *Salmo apache*, *Salmo gilae* and Mexican Golden Trout (*S. chrysogaster*), now largely displaced or hybridized with introduced Rainbow Trout (*S. gairdneri*) and Brown Trout (*S. trutta*).
- **Pupfishes (Cyprinodontidae):** numerous *Cyprinodon* species, many restricted to single springs or small basins, including the [[Pecos Pupfish]] (*C. pecosensis*).
- **Livebearers (Poeciliidae):** *Gambusia* species and the [[Sonora Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*).
- **Other families:** sunfishes, darters, sculpins, gobies, cichlids and flounders occur in restricted ranges.

The distribution table indicates which species are native to each basin. Individual entries are not reproduced here.

**3. Colorado River tributaries show strong endemism.** Each major middle-Colorado tributary has its own spinedace species or subspecies, reflecting extreme isolation. Examples include *Lepidomeda vittata* in the Little Colorado River, *L. mollispinis mollispinis* in the Virgin River, and *L. m. pratensis* in Meadow Valley Wash. Thermal endemics of the Moapa River, *Crenichthys baileyi* and *Moapa coriacea*, occur at Moapa Springs.

**4. Introduced fishes threaten native wetland fauna.** Golden Shiner (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*) has created "substantial management problems" by overpopulating fishing lakes in Arizona. Green Sunfish and Goldfish are cited in marshland waters, and Northern Pike (*Esox lucius*) may exert predation pressure on young native fishes and waterfowl if established in streams.

**5. Riparian cottonwood and willow depend on spring floods.** Southwestern riparian forests are "vernally adapted." Cottonwood and willow seeds germinate in spring and remain viable for less than seven weeks, so reproduction fails without a suitable seedbed. Summer precipitation seldom sustains streamflow in seasonal channels. Introduced [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) produces seed from March through October and can colonize seedbeds that native trees need, and it often replaces cottonwood and willow below dams with stabilized flow.

**6. Dams and water storage are the dominant cause of riparian decline.** The authors attribute a "great reduction" of interior cottonwood-willow communities to water storage, regulated streamflow, reduced flood frequency and Saltcedar invasion, compounded by livestock grazing. Mixed broadleaf riparian forests, which include Arizona Sycamore (*Platanus wrightii*), Velvet Ash (*Fraxinus velutina*) and Arizona Walnut (*Juglans major*), have been reduced to scattered trees, and flash flooding has destroyed many miles of streamside forest.

**7. Cold-temperate and montane marshlands support waterfowl and specialized amphibians.** Sandhill Cranes, Mallards, Pintails, Redheads and Ruddy Ducks use these marshes, and Leopard Frogs, Tiger Salamanders and Cricket Frogs are found throughout the region. Fishes are rarely present in these habitats except as young minnows and suckers.

**8. Marine and coastal systems remain poorly known.** Offshore kelp forests and eelgrass meadows support a poorly known fauna. Gray Whale, Harbor Porpoise, dolphins and formerly the Southern Sea Otter are noted. Felger et al. (1976) recorded over-wintering of the Green Sea Turtle in the Sea of Cortez. The authors call the lack of knowledge surprising given the economic, scientific and recreational value of these resources.

**9. Future outlook is tenuous.** The authors conclude that increasing demands on Southwestern water resources make the future of remaining wetland communities "tenuous at best." Within a single generation, unique riparian and marshland systems, ranging from tiny spring-fed marshes to the once-extensive backwaters of the Colorado River delta, were lost.
