# Control of Prespawning Behaviour in Sunfish and the Endocrine Basis of Pigeon Breeding Behaviour

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two 1969 papers in *Animal Behaviour*. The first, by R. J. F. Smith, is the complete study and the focus of this note. It tests whether [[Gonadal Androgen]] controls prespawning behaviour in two freshwater sunfish, the [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]] (*Lepomis gibbosus*) and the [[Northern Longear Sunfish]] (*L. megalotis*). The main result is that [[Nest Digging]] and nest-site and shallow-water choice depend on gonadal androgen, while [[Aggression]] does not. The second paper, by Murton, Thearle and Lofts, is truncated after its introduction. It sets out to test how exogenous gonadal steroids and pituitary hormones affect the pre-incubation behaviour of intact male [[Feral Pigeon]] (*Columba livia*). It reports no results.

## Background & Description

**Sunfish study.** The study addresses a gap: the link between gonadal hormones and behaviour had not been investigated in centrarchid sunfishes beyond a preliminary study of [[Gonadectomy]] and aggression in the green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*) by Hale (1956). Both focal species overlap widely in range and share similar reproductive behaviour. Males establish territories in shallow water and dig nest pits. Longears nest in dense colonies, sometimes rim to rim in groups of fifty or more, while pumpkinseeds space nests about 2 to 3 m apart.

**Study animals and conditions.** Adult males were captured by seining and angling near London, Ontario, and held in 850-litre concrete tanks under natural photoperiod until use. Experiments ran at 25°C on a 16L:8D light cycle, which induces nest digging in intact fish. Two apparatus types were used:
- Twelve plywood boxes (about 1 m²), each divided into four compartments with one fish per compartment. Light on the tank bottom was 590 lux, with gravel 5 cm deep and water 25 cm deep.
- Two plastic wading pools (2.25 m diameter), each split into a shallow half (8 cm water) and a deep half (33 cm water), in which fish could contact each other and choose nest sites.

**Behavioural measures.** Three agonistic patterns were scored as indicators of aggression: [[Opercular Spreading]], fluttering (or "approach" in the pools), and [[Tail Beating]]. Nest digging was recorded as the extent of gravel disturbance. Fish in boxes were observed for 15 min per box and those in pools for 30 min per pool, every other day in random order.

**Methods.** Gonadectomy was performed through a single ventral incision under [[MS 222]] anaesthesia, with sham operations as controls. Fish recovered at least 2 weeks before testing. Experimental fish received 17α-[[Methyltestosterone]] dissolved in water and renewed every 7 days, with alcohol-only controls. The stated dose was one part testosterone per 1.5 × 10⁶ parts water (the scan reads "1-5 x 106"), chosen as effective in preliminary tests and lower than that used in [[Three-Spined Stickleback]] studies.

**Pigeon study.** The pigeon paper introduces the view that androgens restore male-type behaviour in castrates and that oestrogens feminise male birds, with recognised exceptions. It argues that male oestrogen production has been assumed to be incidental, although Van Tienhoven (1961) noted that its role in male reproduction had not been established. The authors propose correlating endocrine state, behaviour and gonad/pituitary cytology. The paper's stated experiments involve three gonadal steroids (androgen, oestrogen, progesterone) and three pituitary hormones (FSH, LH, prolactin), but the text ends before any results.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Seasonal timing:** Reproduction is restricted to a temperate breeding season. Sunfish gonadal maturity is regulated by temperature and photoperiod (Hoar 1965), and the study ties behaviour to gonadal secretion so that conspicuous, costly behaviour occurs only at the right time.
- **Nest-site ecology:** Male sunfish move into shallow water, where nest pits are dug in gravel. Longears may nest in water under 8 cm deep. Field observations place longear nests in water as deep as the pool's deep half, while the pool's shallow half approaches the minimum depth of field nests.
- **Vulnerability:** Shallow-water occupation is a relatively exposed position, which the author argues makes it adaptive for gonadal maturity, digging readiness and shallow-water entry to be closely matched.
- **Crowding:** Sunfish are more aggressive when crowded, regardless of sex or season (Erickson 1967; Huck 1965; author's observations). Female sunfish also become aggressive in confined conditions.
- **Comparative taxa:** Sex-role differences may explain control differences. In [[Tilapia Macrocephala]], nest digging continues without gonads, whereas in sticklebacks and sunfish only males build nests.

## Key Findings

**1. Nest digging requires gonadal androgen.**
- *Longears (box experiment):* Eight castrates versus eight sham-operated and eight unoperated fish. All control fish dug nests within 36 days; no castrates dug.
- *Pumpkinseeds (box experiment):* Eight castrates versus eight sham-operated fish, terminated at 28 days. Six of eight sham-operated fish dug nests; no castrates dug (P = 0.05, Fisher exact test).
- *Testosterone restoration:* Four testosterone-treated castrate longears dug nests within 6 days. None of the four alcohol-controls dug by day 18. Three of four treated pumpkinseed castrates dug within 30 days; the fourth died at day 35. No controls dug.

**2. Aggression is not gonad-dependent.**
- In longears, opercular spreading did not differ significantly among unoperated, sham and castrate groups. Fluttering differed (P = 0.05, Wilcoxon), but castrates still reached relatively high levels. Sham-operated fish were lower than unoperated fish, suggesting operative stress.
- In pumpkinseeds, no significant differences occurred in any of the three patterns between castrates and sham controls. Testosterone did not greatly change aggression in either species.
- Male breeding coloration was retained in all fish in the box experiments.

**3. Wading pool experiment: nest location, depth preference and aggression.**
- Sham-operated longears dug nests readily; castrates did not dig until testosterone was given at day 28. Nests of intact fish were close together, while testosterone-treated castrates' nests were scattered. The author suggests a dosage difference may explain this.
- Intact longears moved into shallow water in numbers and shifted depth preference on 18 May, coinciding with the first shallow-water nests. Castrates did not shift in response to testosterone.
- Opercular spreads, approaches and tail beats were significantly higher in sham-operated fish before testosterone (P = 0.01 for each). Testosterone did not clearly raise aggression in castrates.
- Pale coloration with yellow pelvic fins appeared in gonadectomised fish in the pools. Longears castrated and kept in glass aquaria, with separate compartments, retained full coloration and black pelvic fins. Testosterone darkened castrates in the pools but did not match sham-operated fish.

**4. Conclusions of the sunfish study.**
- Nest digging and nest-site location are controlled by gonadal androgens.
- Aggression and coloration are unaffected by hormones in crowded conditions but are increased by androgens in open conditions, possibly because intact fish crowd themselves by nesting in close groups.
- Crowding and confinement, rather than androgen alone, appear to drive aggression in longears.
