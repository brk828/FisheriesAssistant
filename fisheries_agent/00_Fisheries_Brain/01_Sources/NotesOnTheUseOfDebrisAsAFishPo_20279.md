# Notes on the Use of Derris as a Fish Poison

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1938 paper by Justin W. Leonard of the [[Michigan Institute For Fisheries Research]] reports laboratory experiments on [[Derris]] (powdered derris root, containing a warranted [[Rotenone]] content of 5 per cent) as a tool for eliminating unwanted freshwater fish populations. The experiments measured lethal concentrations for several species, how quickly toxicity dissipates in standing water, the effects of temperature and water acidity, the decay of stored derris, and the effects on invertebrates and fish eggs. The author concludes that derris can kill all species tested at about 0.5 ppm (one part in two million), that fish-food organisms are not harmed at that dose, and that the poison loses its effect within roughly 20 to 41 hours in a tank. The paper also records field advice given in a discussion session.

## Background & Description

Fisheries managers sometimes remove whole fish populations to replace stunted or coarse fish with more desirable species, to control disease, or to study how natural fish stocks relate to a water body's productive capacity. Nets could not remove an entire fauna, [[Dynamite]] was costly and unreliable, and [[Copper Sulphate]] killed food organisms and plants along with fish. Derris was considered efficient, affordable, and apparently harmless to plants and fish food.

Rotenone, the most toxic principle in the roots of various Leguminosae, was first crystallised by Nagai in 1902. Commercial derris is mostly prepared from *Derris elliptica*. Related compounds include deguelin, tephrosin, and toxicarol, none as virulent as rotenone. Historically, derris was used as a fish poison by indigenous peoples in Southeast Asia and was an ingredient in arrow poisons. By 1939, most commercial derris went into insecticide manufacture, and Roark had abstracted 456 publications on the genus between 1747 and 1931.

Human exposure was also discussed. Large doses can be fatal, and one suicide case is cited. Dust from the powder causes nose and throat irritation, and the author reports headaches after inhalation. Protecting the nose with a cloth prevented these symptoms.

The experimental setup used 12-litre glass aquaria, with 10 litres of water held at 60 ± 1°F by circulating tanks. Some trials were run at 74°F. Water came from a hatchery spring at Drayton Plains, tap water from the [[Ann Arbor]] supply, and naturally acid water from [[Spruce Lake]], a small bog lake. Stock suspensions were made by mixing 10 g of derris root with 1,000 cc of water. Concentrations were stated as parts of root by weight, not of rotenone.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species tested.** Fish species were [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Common Sunfish]] (*Lepomis gibbosus*), [[Common Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*), [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), [[Common Shiner]] (*Notropis cornutus*), [[Brook Stickleback]] (*Culaea inconstans*, given as "Eucalia" in the scan), [[Mud Minnow]] (*Umbra limi*), and [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*). Invertebrate food organisms included damselfly and stonefly nymphs, corixids and belostomatid bugs, caddisfly and cranefly larvae, crayfish, *Hyalella* amphipods, and *Physa* snails.

**Physiological mode of action.** Rotenone acts mainly on the respiratory system. Daneel (1933) reported that oxygen consumption in crucian carp fell to 3 per cent of normal within ten minutes in a 1:100,000 solution. Histology showed that the gill epithelium withered and broke down. Scheuring and Heuschmann (1935) confirmed this corrosive action on the gills and found rotenone a weak stomach poison.

**Behavioural sequence.** In most species, poisoning began with violent, erratic dashing and surfacing. This gave way to convulsions, paralysis, and eventually death. Mud minnows were the exception. They became gradually lethargic without the seizure.

**Limits on comparison.** The rotenone content of wild derris root varies from 0 to 5.5 per cent, and total ether extract from 5 to 23 per cent (Roark, 1931). Leonard noted that this made it hard to compare his results with those of Scheuring and Heuschmann, whose root was of unknown content and apparently more toxic.

## Key Findings

- **Relative sensitivity.** The common shiner was much more sensitive than the common sucker. At 1.00 ppm, common sunfish lost equilibrium in an average of 17 minutes. At 0.50 ppm, the average was 102 minutes. At 0.25 ppm, no effect was seen over 96 hours. The mud minnow was the most resistant species, dying at an average of 628 minutes at 0.50 ppm. Goldfish were also strongly resistant.

- **Least to most resistant (Summary 3).** The least resistant were common shiner, golden shiner, bluegill, common sunfish, and brook stickleback. The mud minnow and goldfish were the most resistant.

- **Persistence in standing water.** In a tank treated at 1.00 ppm, toxicity dropped below the lethal threshold for sunfish between 20 and 41 hours after addition. Sunfish added at 41 hours survived 72 hours. Removing fish to pure aerated water did not save them once equilibrium was lost.

- **Stock decay.** A 1:100 stock suspension lost 60 to 70 per cent of its toxicity after 34 days, even when stored tightly stoppered in darkness. Only a slight decline occurred over 10 days. Commercial powder exposed to air and subdued light for six months lost about 43 per cent of its strength, based on goldfish death times of 709 minutes (new stock) versus 11,245 minutes (old stock).

- **Temperature.** Raising the water from 60°F to 74°F cut the time to equilibrium loss and death to roughly half. At 60°F, goldfish lost equilibrium in 455 minutes and died at 1,042 minutes at 0.50 ppm. At 74°F, the figures were 261 and 565 minutes.

- **Acidity.** Goldfish in naturally acid bog water (pH 6.5) lost equilibrium in 183 minutes and died at 269 minutes. Goldfish in tap water (pH 8.2) had all lost equilibrium after about 985 minutes, and the trial was stopped at roughly 16 hours before any died. Acidity sped up toxic action most in resistant species such as goldfish, and had less effect on sensitive species such as golden shiners and brook sticklebacks.

- **Lethal concentrations.** A concentration of one part derris to two million parts water (0.50 ppm) killed all species tested. Leonard describes this as the lowest concentration certain to kill fish of all species. Trout and other susceptible species may be killed at one part in three or four million. Bullheads and carp call for about 0.50 ppm.

- **Invertebrates.** All invertebrates tested survived 96 hours at 1.00 ppm, including stonefly nymphs. Leonard concluded that a dose sufficient to eradicate fish would not affect the food supply. He cited Scheuring and Heuschmann, who found *Daphnia* killed in 1.5 to 4 hours only at 5 ppm.

- **Eggs and fry.** Eyed [[Brown Trout]] eggs survived 0.50 ppm. Fry hatching in the poisoned water could not free themselves from the shell and died, though the toxicity had already fallen below the lethal point for small bluegills.

- **Field recommendations (discussion).** Derris should be applied as a suspension, pumped or poured from portable tanks around a shoreline, then mixed with a motorboat if available. Dissolving in acetone was not needed. In a deep lake of about 70 feet and 10 acres, Leonard believed all fish were killed. Treating a single cove on a calm day was thought feasible. Toxicity was expected to disappear within a week or ten days, faster if the water body had an outlet. Test minnows can be used to confirm that the poison has cleared. Caution is advised where livestock drink from the water, because cold-blooded vertebrates are more sensitive. A colour test for rotenone exists, described in Gersdorff's publications.

- **Management implications.** Adam's comments suggested that poisoning fish on spawning grounds, or removing under-developed [[Yellow Perch]] to favour [[Lake Trout]], could shift the balance of wild stocks. Leonard's experiments supported derris as a practical tool for that purpose, with the caveats above.
