# Evaluating Recovery Criteria in U.S. Endangered Species Act Recovery Plans

#conservation-biology #endangered-species #recovery-planning

## Executive Summary

This study examines whether U.S. Endangered Species Act (ESA) recovery plans define the objective, measurable criteria needed to determine when a listed species can be delisted. The authors reviewed recovery criteria for 180 listed species, covering both plants and animals (the Key Findings section notes that later analyses cite 181 species in 135 recovery plans). The main result is that the 1988 ESA amendments, which required "objective, measurable" delisting criteria, were followed by an increase in the total number of criteria. The share of quantitative criteria did not rise, however, and many criteria remain weakly tied to biological information. The authors call for stronger use of biological evidence and quantitative metrics to monitor recovery.

## Background & Description

The ESA of 1973 was enacted to stop the loss of plant and animal species to human activity. The Act defines an *endangered* species as one in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of its range, and a *threatened* species as one likely to become endangered within the foreseeable future. Because the Act gives no clear criteria for these categories, the responsible agencies define recovery and delisting criteria for each species in recovery plans.

Recovery plans lack direct legal force, but they are the only documents that specify the criteria for removing a species from the List of Threatened and Endangered Wildlife. Earlier analyses found that many plans lack biologically defensible listing guidelines (Wilcove et al. 1993). In one study, 28% of threatened and endangered species with available population data had recovery goals set at or below their existing population size (Tear et al. 1993). The authors argue that the split between biological and political recovery goals has produced criteria that are either impractical or unsustainable (Scott et al. 1995).

The 1988 amendments required recovery plans to include objective, measurable delisting criteria. The study tested three hypotheses:

- Use of quantitative criteria increased after the 1988 amendments.
- Biological information is used more clearly to select criteria.
- Criteria use varies among taxa and plan types.

The authors also tested whether the inclusion of explicit criteria correlates with the population status of listed species.

**Data and methods**

- Source data: questionnaire responses from the [[National Center for Ecological Analysis and Synthesis]] recovery project, covering 180 species, with plans written between 1974 and 1998.
- Criteria were classed as population size, population trend, habitat, demography, legal/policy, or other.
- Each criterion was coded as quantitative or qualitative.
- Each criterion was rated for its link to biological information as unclear, somewhat clear, or very clear.
- Statistical tests were [[Mann-Whitney U Test]] and [[Kruskal-Wallis Test]].
- 1990 was used as the cut point, chosen to allow a predicted two-year lag after the amendments and to match the [[1990 Recovery Plan Guidelines]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most species in the sample were terrestrial or freshwater taxa. The animal group included mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, [[Fish]], insects, crustaceans, molluscs, and other invertebrates. [[Large Whales]] are cited as an example of aquatic species whose populations have recently increased, which has prompted efforts to develop quantitative recovery criteria. The paper's focus is policy and planning, not species biology, so it provides no habitat, water chemistry, or ecosystem-level measurements.

Key ecological framing:

- Recovery criteria are meant to show when a species has escaped extinction.
- Their reliability depends on the quality of the biological information behind them.
- Population size is the criterion category most often used, and it has the highest share of quantitative metrics.

## Key Findings

**Sample composition**

- 85 plant species (including lichens, nonvascular plants, ferns, gymnosperms, angiosperms, and fungi) and 96 animal species were analysed.
- Plans by type: 100 single-species, 69 multi-species, and 12 ecosystem plans.
- 18 species were classed as in "conflict" in the 1996 USFWS Recovery Report to Congress.

**Overall criteria use**

- The mean number of criteria per species plan was 2.9 for unclear links to biological information, 1.5 for somewhat clear links, and 1.1 for very clear links.
- 91% of species had at least one criterion. A majority (the scan garbles this figure; it is read here as about 81%) had at least one quantitative criterion.

**Temporal trends (before vs. after 1990)**

- The percentage of unclear criteria increased significantly (P = 0.0005).
- The total number of criteria rose significantly (Mann-Whitney U = 2925, P = 0.004).
- The number of qualitative criteria also rose significantly (P = 0.006).
- The number of quantitative criteria did not change significantly. The authors conclude that the relative share of quantitative criteria declined.
- Quantitative metrics rose significantly for population size (U = 2714, P = 0.004) and population trend (U = 2959, P = 0.02).
- Population size is the most frequently used category, with the highest share of quantitative metrics.
- Fewer than half of habitat, demographic, and legal/policy criteria have quantitative metrics.

**Taxonomic differences**

- Plants had more criteria per plan than animals (U = 3046, P = 0.003).
- The fraction of plant criteria with quantitative metrics increased significantly after 1990 (P = 0.015). The rise in total criteria for plants was driven by qualitative metrics.
- Plant criteria were more often clearly linked to biological information than animal criteria (U = 2119, P = 0.0002).
- The authors found no trend in quantitative criteria for animals.

**Plan type**

- Plan type did not affect the total number or quantitative share of criteria.
- Ecosystem plans had fewer very clear biology-based criteria (Kruskal-Wallis H = 6.6, P = 0.04). The small sample of 12 ecosystem plans limits this finding.

**Population status**

- Analysis was restricted to species with plans approved before 1990, which reduced the sample from 181 to 63.
- Species with improving status had more very clear biological links than declining species (H = 7.16, P = 0.03).
- Species with improving status had a non-significant trend toward more total and quantitative criteria (H = 3.66, P = 0.16).
- Among animals with known status, improving species had more quantitative criteria than declining species (P = 0.03).

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- Plants appear to benefit from the 1988 initiative. Animal and plant plans still need more biological justification and quantitative metrics.
- Recovery criteria tied to biological information and quantitative metrics help focus efforts on short- and long-term conservation goals.
- Future research should assess, case by case, how metrics and target values are chosen.
- Monitoring based on well-designed metrics would let managers revise delisting criteria adaptively.
