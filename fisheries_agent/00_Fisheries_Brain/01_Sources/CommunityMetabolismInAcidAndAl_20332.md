# Community Metabolism in Acid and Alkaline Strip-Mine Lakes

#limnology #community-metabolism #aquatic-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1970 study by Owen T. Lind and Robert S. Campbell (Department of Zoology, University of Missouri) measured [[Community Metabolism]] over 24 months in three Missouri [[Strip-Mine Lakes]]: one acid lake (As) and two formerly acid lakes (B and D). Metabolism was estimated with a modified [[Diel Oxygen Method]]. Water pH ranged from 3.2 to 8.1, and biotic diversity was inversely related to acidity. Despite lower diversity, the metabolic rates of the acid lake were broadly comparable to those of non-acid natural waters. The authors conclude that acid conditions do not seriously impair the density of organisms or their metabolic processes.

## Background & Description

Strip-mine lakes form unusual aquatic communities whose development is governed mainly by the quantity of sulfuric acid in the water. Earlier work recognized an acid successional stage followed by a later alkaline stage (Campbell and Lind, 1969). The three study lakes were:

| Lake | Area (m²) | Mean depth (m) | pH range | Dissolved solids (mg l⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| As | 2,160 | 2.6 | 3.2–4.1 | 230–840 |
| B | 6,060 | 2.6 | 6.3–8.2 | 320–600 |
| D | 3,020 | 2.5 | 6.3–7.6 | 110–360 |

Lake As was acidic. Lakes B and D were no longer acidic, and Lake D had been free of acid influence for at least 28 years. (The abstract gives the pH maximum as 8.1, whereas Table 1 gives 8.2.)

### Methods

- **Oxygen sampling:** Integrated water-column samples were taken through the photic zone (to 1% of surface light) with a 1-liter plastic syringe sampler. Dissolved oxygen was measured by the [[Winkler Method]]. Inflow mixing was checked with malachite green dye.
- **Sampling design:** Each diel series comprised three collections (late-afternoon maximum, pre-dawn minimum, and second afternoon maximum). Five locations were sampled per lake at each time.
- **Calculation:** Gross photosynthesis and community respiration followed the [[Mcconnell Procedure]], an adaptation of the Odum diel curve method suited to small, quiet waters.
- **Diffusion check:** Surface agitation was low and storm dates were avoided. Two 24-hour tests with enclosed water columns (25 cm diameter cylinders) showed no significant difference (p = 0.05), so no diffusion correction was applied.
- **Supporting biology:** Net plankton, nannoplankton (membrane filter technique), benthos (Petersen or Ekman dredge), and fish (electroseining) were also sampled.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Biotic Communities

Biotic diversity increased with lake age and recovery from acid pollution.

- **Lake As (acid):** Taxa were few but abundant. There were 17 phytoplankton taxa, dominated by [[Closterium]] sp., [[Chlamydomonas Polypyrenoideum]], and [[Aphanizomenon Flos-Aquae]]. Five zooplankton and 35 benthos taxa were found. No vascular macrophytes or vertebrates occurred, and waterfowl were never seen.
- **Lake B (near-alkaline):** This was the only lake with substantial macrophyte growth, including [[Potamogeton Diversifolius]] (replaced by [[Chara Sejuncta]] in August). Macrophytes covered about 40% of the lake area at maximum development. The lake held 30 phytoplankton, 31 zooplankton, and 58 benthos taxa, plus 11 fish species. Frogs (bullfrog and green frog) were common, and beaver had built bank houses before 1964.
- **Lake D (alkaline, turbid):** High turbidity limited macrophytes. There were 44 phytoplankton, 30 zooplankton, 60 benthos taxa, and 11 fish species.

### Physical Setting

Lake D was more thermally stable, which produced stronger horizontal zonation of phytoplankton. Its water was fertile because of nutrient-rich drainage from pasture land, but turbidity restricted the photic depth.

## Key Findings

### Gross Photosynthesis
- Maximum daily gross photosynthesis was similar across lakes: 7.9 g O₂ m⁻² day⁻¹ (B), 7.6 (As), and 6.5 (D). A mid-winter minimum below 0.1 g O₂ m⁻² day⁻¹ occurred in all lakes.
- The highest annual volumetric gross photosynthesis (586 and 682 g O₂ m⁻³ yr⁻¹, for 1963–64 and 1964–65) was found in the more nearly eutrophic alkaline lake.
- Annual areal values (g O₂ m⁻² yr⁻¹):

| Lake | 1963–64 | 1964–65 |
|---|---|---|
| As | 1,072 | 754 |
| B | 1,061 | 680 |
| D | 739 | 788 |

- Lake As and B showed higher areal production than Lake D, which was limited by turbidity. Production declined in 1964–65 in Lakes As and B, partly because of lower water levels.
- Two seasonal maxima occurred each year, in June and September–October. The autumnal peak was slightly higher.
- Assimilation quotients (V/O) were As 0.46 and 0.40, B 0.50 and 0.42, and D 0.81 and 0.75 (1963–64 and 1964–65). The larger values for Lake D indicate more fertile water.

### Community Respiration and P/R
- Maximum respiration was 9.10 g O₂ m⁻² day⁻¹ in Lake As (associated with algal blooms and decomposition), 6.25 in Lake B, and 6.40 in Lake D.
- Respiration was undetectable at least once in winter in Lakes As and B.
- Respiration rates were considerably lower than those reported for [[Oil Refinery Holding Ponds]], [[Oklahoma Farm Ponds]], and [[Baffin Bay]]. Respiration peaked in autumn, later in Lakes B and D.
- Respiration closely followed gross photosynthesis, so each lake's [[P/R Ratio]] fluctuated around unity. The 1963–64 mean P/R values were 0.94 (As) and 0.96 (B). The reported range was 0.56–1.51 for As, 0.44–1.75 for B, and 0.29–1.57 for D.

### Succession and Stability
- A P/R near one indicates a balanced community. Lake B, in an early successional stage, had synthesis slightly exceeding respiration and low volumetric production because its nutrient base was not yet established.
- Lake D was judged closest to maturity for small regional lakes. Its nutrient inflow supported high production, but turbidity offset some of the benefit.
- Lake As, the youngest ecologically, was expected to have the highest P/R apart from the effect of sulfuric acid. Its acid environment restricted multi-species plankton, and its large, erratic blooms deposited organic matter in deeper water, creating a constant respiratory demand.
- Greater species diversity provides [[Ecosystem Stability]] by buffering metabolic and biomass fluctuations.

### Conclusions
- Acid Lake As has lower species diversity than non-acid lakes, but its populations are large and its metabolic rates compare favorably with non-acid natural waters.
- Despite distinct limnological differences among the three lakes (Campbell et al., 1965a, 1965b; Campbell and Lind, 1969), their photosynthesis and respiration were surprisingly similar.
- The [[Diel Oxygen Method]] proved usable for small, quiet, acid and alkaline waters.
