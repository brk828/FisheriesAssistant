# Glen Canyon Dam Temperature Control Workshop Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #dam-operations #adaptive-management

## Executive Summary

This document summarizes a three-day workshop (November 8-10, 1999) at Saguaro Lake Ranch convened to evaluate a proposed temperature control device (TCD) for [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The workshop brought together scientists, resource managers, and conservationists to provide input on research and monitoring plans developed by the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center]] (GCMRC) and to inform a National Environmental Policy Act Environmental Assessment. The primary focus was assessing how selective withdrawal of warmer reservoir water might benefit the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and other native fishes while minimizing harm to the [[Lee's Ferry]] rainbow trout fishery and aquatic ecosystem. Participants examined lessons from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], which has operated a TCD since 1978, and developed recommendations for experimental design, monitoring protocols, and operational scenarios. The workshop revealed significant scientific debate about data sufficiency, experimental design complexity, and the balance between temperature modification and other limiting factors (hydrology, predation, food availability).

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon Dam]] releases perennially cold hypolimnetic water (approximately 8°C year-round) that has been identified as a constraint on native fish reproduction and recruitment since the first biological opinion in 1978. The 1995 Environmental Impact Statement biological opinion required the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] to investigate TCD feasibility as part of the reasonable and prudent alternative under the Endangered Species Act. The proposed fixed-elevation, single-inlet TCD at 3630 ft elevation would draw warmer epilimnetic water during summer months, potentially achieving 16-17°C releases in 50-80% of future years depending on reservoir elevation and Upper Basin water development scenarios.

Workshop presentations covered physical and chemical limnology, algal and invertebrate communities, and sport and native fish populations in reservoirs and rivers above and below both [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (Green River) and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (Colorado River). The [[Flaming Gorge]] TCD, operational since 1978, provided a comparative case study: it increased summer water temperatures from 6°C to 12.8°C, producing a seasonal pattern reminiscent of pre-dam conditions but achieving only approximately 40% of the original temperature range (4-13°C versus historical 0-22°C).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] ecosystem in [[Grand Canyon]] has undergone fundamental transformation following dam closure. Pre-dam conditions featured strong seasonal temperature variation, high sediment loads, variable discharge patterns, and an intact native fish assemblage. Post-dam conditions include stable cold temperatures, clear water, altered flow regimes, and a fish community dominated by nonnative species. At [[Lee's Ferry]], rainbow trout comprise >90% of fish; 20 fish species (6 native, 14 introduced) declined to 3 species (rainbow trout, flannelmouth sucker, common carp).

The primary carbon source shifted from allochthonous detritus to autochthonous production by *Cladophora glomerata* and epiphytic diatoms. Benthic invertebrate communities changed dramatically: mayfly dominance (pre-dam) shifted to dipterans and amphipods (*Gammarus lacustris*, later *Hyalella azteca*). At [[Flaming Gorge]], aquatic macroinvertebrate genera declined from >70 to <30 after dam closure, with densities increasing from 1,000 m⁻² to 10,000 m⁻². Despite thermal modification since 1978, insect richness did not appreciably increase, possibly due to remaining thermal regime differences, low colonization rates, low reproductive success, and competitive dominance by amphipods.

[[Humpback Chub]] populations face multiple constraints: the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) is believed to be the principal spawning area for the majority of the Grand Canyon population, with the mainstem acting primarily as a dispersal corridor. Cold mainstem temperatures reduce swimming ability of young chubs descending from the LCR, increasing predation susceptibility by brown trout, channel catfish, and rainbow trout. Mainstem aggregations at river mile 30 and Middle Granite Gorge contain small numbers with uncertain reproductive success.

## Key Findings

**Workshop Purpose and Process:**
The workshop served three primary functions: (1) forum for scientific input on GCMRC's research and monitoring plan, (2) opportunity for Bureau of Reclamation to seek input on NEPA Environmental Assessment, and (3) knowledge exchange between scientists with expertise on [[Flaming Gorge]] and [[Glen Canyon]] systems. Forty-six participants from universities, federal and state agencies, tribes, and conservation organizations attended.

**Temperature Control Device Design and Capabilities:**
The proposed TCD for Glen Canyon Dam would use a fixed-elevation inlet at 3630 ft, compared to current penstock depth of 230 ft and bypass tube depth of 326 ft (Lake Powell maximum depth: 570 ft). Achievable release temperatures are affected by reservoir elevation; the BETTER (Box Exchange Transport Temperature Ecology Reservoir) model estimates 16-17°C releases available in 50-80% of future years. The rate of downstream warming in the Colorado River below Glen Canyon Dam is considerably less than in the Green River below Flaming Gorge Dam, meaning the 16-22°C range produced approximately 30 river miles below Flaming Gorge could be duplicated over approximately 150 miles in Grand Canyon.

**Flaming Gorge Dam Case Study - Physical and Biological Responses:**
Following Flaming Gorge Dam closure in 1962, the Green River tailwater was stocked with rainbow trout (1963) and brown trout (1965). Initial good growth declined with colder temperatures when the reservoir filled. The 1978 TCD installation increased summer/autumn temperatures to 12.8°C through epilimnetic withdrawal, resulting in 3X increase in trout growth and annual yields of 47-99 lbs ac⁻¹. However, survival was highly variable, and harvest peaked in 1981 then declined. High winter mortality of juvenile trout occurred due to insufficient habitat. A 1992 biological opinion prompted flow regime adjustments to more closely mimic natural hydrograph, resulting in noticeably warmer summer temperatures and increases in brown trout, mountain whitefish, and northern pike.

For native fishes, thermal enhancement immediately restored reproduction by most resident species. The regulated reach supported 9 native fishes during 1994-1996, with native fishes numerically dominant in much of Lodore Canyon. However, nonnative cool and warmwater fishes also increased, particularly problematic piscivores: channel catfish, smallmouth bass, and northern pike. Interactions among geomorphology, hydrology, and water temperature proved important in determining fish distribution and abundance.

**Glen Canyon Dam Ecosystem Responses:**
The [[Lee's Ferry]] fishery chronology divides into three eras: (1) 1964-1984 young fishery, (2) 1984-1991 decline, (3) 1991-present recovery. Reduction of daily flow fluctuations reduced stranding of spawning trout and desiccation of redds, improving reproduction and recruitment. Recent population estimates indicate approximately 260,000 rainbow trout >305 mm total length in the Lee's Ferry reach, exceeding suggested carrying capacity of 150,000-170,000. Food may be limiting for larger trout. Dam operation is identified as a major factor influencing the fishery, tending to override other environmental variables.

**Humpback Chub Status and Temperature Effects:**
All mainstem recruitment of humpback chub is believed to originate from LCR reproduction; survival of young descending to the mainstem is limited by cold temperatures. Temperature effects include: (1) diminished reproduction and recruitment, (2) reduced growth rates, (3) reduced swimming ability, (4) reduced parasite load. Estimated growth rates are available from artificial propagation, laboratory studies, scale back-calculation, and mark-recapture measurements. Length-weight relationship slopes for Grand Canyon populations are generally higher than upper Colorado River basin populations, suggesting relatively good condition. Temperature augmentation could increase swimming ability and growth rates, allowing chubs to grow past the predator vulnerability window more quickly.

**Parasites and Disease Concerns:**
Eleven parasites have been discovered in 4 native and 7 nonnative fishes in Grand Canyon, collected from three tributaries (Little Colorado River, Kanab Creek, Havasu Creek) and the Colorado River. Humpback chub was infected by a flatworm, tapeworm (Asian tapeworm *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), roundworm (*Truttaedacnitis truttae*), and parasitic copepod (*Lernaea cyprinacea*). Two known parasites could benefit from increased water temperature. Temperature preferenda for most parasite species are unknown. Intermediate hosts include oligochaete worms, snails, copepods, and mayflies. Whirling disease (*Myxobolus cerebralis*) is less of a concern at Flaming Gorge because brown trout are resistant and 7-inch rainbows (older, less vulnerable life stage) are stocked; however, warmer temperatures could provide more conducive environment for disease establishment.

**Food Base Considerations:**
Elevated water temperatures from a TCD could negatively affect the food base through inhibition of *Cladophora* growth, reduction of diatom biomass, and changes in diatom species composition to forms less available to grazers. Benthic community composition and standing biomass is more variable in time and space below Lake Powell than above. Release fluctuations have significant negative impact on benthos, with slow recolonization, especially under fluctuating flows. More "niches" for aquatic and semiaquatic insects are created by wider temperature ranges above Lake Powell than in Grand Canyon. Conclusions emphasized that the post-dam trophic system is dependent on primary production, natural condition provides stable food source, and unpredictable environmental variation destabilizes lotic communities. Temperature modification needs management in a predictable manner on annual and hourly/daily scales to positively affect benthic communities.

**Recommended Operational Scenarios:**
Workshop participants evaluated four scenarios: (1) closely mimic pre-dam thermal warming (not achievable with proposed design), (2) promote spawning/recruitment at river mile 30 and downstream aggregations, (3) target Middle Granite Gorge and further downstream, (4) reduce thermal shock to young fish descending from tributaries, particularly LCR. Scenarios 2-4 are not mutually exclusive; all three can be achieved in some but not all years given proposed design and fluctuating reservoir elevations. The most acceptable scenario involved releasing 14-15°C water from the dam beginning as early as possible but no later than June 1, maintaining warm releases for 7 months until October-November, to achieve approximately 16°C at river mile 30. Projected downstream temperatures would reach 16.1°C at RM 30, 17.2°C at RM 62 (LCR), 19.2°C at RM 120 (Middle Granite Gorge), and 22.9°C at RM 225 (Diamond Creek) during June-July peak warming.

**Study Design and Monitoring Recommendations:**
Participants strongly recommended establishing reliable monitoring of native and exotic species BEFORE implementation, with sufficient baseline to quantify variability from monitoring and natural recruitment variation. If immediate action is legally required, begin with lower-risk restoration strategies (e.g., predator control) before implementing TCD. Success of monitoring depends on: (1) magnitude of true response, (2) natural variability, (3) experimental duration, (4) monitoring precision. Specific recommendations included: renew intensive survivorship sampling, PIT tag all native fish, establish stable consistent sampling sites and methods, take advantage of high precision-low cost opportunities (tributary sampling), test alternate methods (acoustic), and ensure data availability through centralized database with quality assurance/control.

**Interdisciplinary Data Needs:**
Physical/chemical monitoring should include reservoir integration of data and modeling, CE-QUAL-W2 model calibration, dissolved oxygen measurement through range of flows and elevations, and tailwater monitoring of dissolved oxygen, conductivity, pH, and temperature with emphasis on cross-sectional and longitudinal changes. Food base monitoring should measure plankton entrainment, algae and invertebrate biomass/composition/densities quarterly through drift and benthic sampling, conduct in situ pre-TCD temperature experiments at three locations with different baseline conditions, and use stable isotopes to determine energy source importance. Fish monitoring should evaluate reservoir fish entrainment potential, measure baseline humpback chub population size, survival, growth, recruitment, and reproductive success, evaluate parasite hosts including terrestrial and birds, and develop standardized sampling protocols ensuring comparability.

**Participant Survey Results (19 responses):**
100% agreed cold water temperature is one factor restricting humpback chub reproductive and recruitment success. Ranking of limiting factors (mean ranks, lower = greater importance): hydrology (1.5), water temperature (2.7), predation (2.7), food availability (3.7), parasitism (4.5). 69% agreed "do no harm" scenario is best approach; 93% agreed TCD operation should be approached as experiment. Regarding duration: 0% favored 1-2 years, 6.3% favored 3 years, 31.2% favored 5 years, 62.5% favored 10 years. 93% agreed annual evaluation process should determine whether to continue. 78% agreed primary success measure should be humpback chub reproductive and recruitment rates. Opinion was split on whether temperature effects can be tested independently of hydrology effects: 47% yes, 53% no.

**Major Scientific Controversies and Opposing Views:**
Significant debate emerged on data sufficiency: some participants argued insufficient scientific information exists to justify TCD construction/operation and more baseline data collection is warranted; others countered that 15 years of data collection has occurred and the issue is willingness to take calculated risk with appropriate monitoring and decision processes. On temperature-hydrology interactions, some emphasized these factors are linked and not independent (mistake repeated from GCD EIS), requiring treatments addressing both variables and their interactions; others questioned what has changed to make the traditional "one variable at a time" experimental approach inadvisable. Regarding food base, concerns were raised that reversion to cold water after years of warm releases could severely impact newly developed algal/invertebrate communities; proponents argued adaptive management with "no harm" approach and iterative feedback loops would minimize risks. On native fish status, debate centered on whether evidence demonstrates Grand Canyon humpback chub population decline (insufficient evidence to justify TCD risks versus need for action given small mainstem aggregations and threats to LCR population). Participants noted lack of consensus on validity of all mainstem chub aggregates, with some aggregates ill-defined while others supported by data.

**Lake Mead and Downstream Considerations:**
Changing temperature of water flowing into [[Lake Mead]] will affect water density and depth of reservoir entry. Timing of shifts from underflows to overflows will be modified, potentially affecting fishery and nutrient distribution. Altered temperature regime through Grand Canyon could affect productivity in upper Lake Mead, with increased productivity in inflow area likely having upstream effect through increased density, distribution and abundance of nonnative warmwater predators in lower Grand Canyon. Workshop participants noted ecological impacts for Lake Mead and resources further downstream were inadequately addressed.

**Implementation Concerns and Recommendations:**
Multiple participants emphasized the complexity of ecosystem responses and need for ecosystem-perspective evaluation rather than single-factor assessment. Temperature affects food base, food base affects fish, hydrology affects food base through velocity and depth changes, and all fish community species (natives and nonnatives) respond to environmental changes. Effects cannot be assessed separately. Concerns were raised about confounding treatments if TCD implementation occurs simultaneously with other management actions (new flow regime, nonnative fish control, second population establishment). Long-term monitoring (>20 years at Flaming Gorge) was necessary to detect changes, and even with this duration, temperature effects could not be distinguished from hydrology effects. The lesson emphasized: avoid confounding treatments and ensure sufficient flow-resource relationships are identified from existing data to remove hydrological effects in TCD experiment. Several participants advocated for nonnative fish control strategy in advance of TCD to pre-empt deleterious response by nonnatives, with aggressive mechanical control and flow management as part of strategy.
