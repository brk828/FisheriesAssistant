# Growth of Black Bullhead Catfish in Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This report, issued by the [[Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory]] and supported by the [[Oklahoma Wildlife Conservation Department]] and the [[University of Oklahoma Biological Survey]], summarizes the age, growth, and population characteristics of [[Black Bullhead Catfish]] (*[[Ictalurus Melas]]*) across Oklahoma waters. It compiles data collected from 1950 to 1961 by fishery biologists and students from 1,173 fish in 93 bodies of water, covering 41 of the state's 77 counties. Growth is reported statewide and by water type. The report also gives a [[Length-Weight Relationship]], notes on [[Annulus Formation]] timing, and a review of bullhead population density and management, including a trapping removal study.

## Background & Description

Growth-rate studies for Oklahoma game fish and other important species had been published earlier. This study was intended as a reference for fishery biologists who often manage fisheries from limited data. Black bullheads are found across the state and in nearly every aquatic habitat. Their tolerance of poor conditions gives them a wide, uniform distribution. They occur in reservoirs, lakes, and streams, but significant populations occur mainly in smaller waters.

Before the construction of large impoundments, sportfishing in Oklahoma was concentrated in streams and ponds. Bullheads were commonly used as sportfish in those waters. Today, impoundments provide most sportfishing, and bullheads are of interest mainly because of the management problems they create in [[Farm Pond Management]] and small lakes.

**Methods.** Fish were collected by rotenone, seines, gill nets, hoop nets, trap nets, electrofishing, angling, and draining, mostly in summer but in every month of the year. Waters were grouped as streams, ponds (0-10 acres), small lakes (11-150 acres), large lakes (151-500 acres), and reservoirs (over 500 acres). Ages were determined from thin cross-sections of [[Dorsal Spines]], following the [[Sneed Method]] for channel catfish. The author chose dorsal rather than pectoral spines because spine-radius to body-length relationships varied too much across localities and seasons for a statewide average. Lengths at each age were calculated by [[Direct Proportion]]. The statewide average is a simple average of the weighted locality averages. All lengths are total lengths in inches, and weights are in grams and pounds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Growth variation.** Growth varied widely among waters. The slowest growth occurred in streams and in some badly overpopulated ponds. The fastest growth occurred in new populations, particularly in reservoirs. Stream averages were considerably lower than those of other waters, but there was little difference between the impounded waters.

**Standing crop.** Bullheads maintain their highest standing crops in smaller waters. Ponds and small lakes support larger standing crops than reservoirs and large lakes.
- In a survey of 42 Oklahoma ponds by [[Robert M. Jenkins]] (1958), 19 ponds had bullhead populations. Their [[Standing Crop]] reached 831 pounds per acre and averaged 162 pounds per acre. The average for all species combined was 341 pounds per acre. [[Bluegill]] was the only other species with comparable standing crops. Jenkins found that bullheads tend to dominate and overcrowd pond populations, and that only 22 percent reached harvestable size (0.3 pounds and larger).
- Nineteen small-lake estimates by mark and recapture (rotenone and traps) averaged 18.3 pounds per acre. Six large-lake estimates averaged 44.5 pounds per acre. In 30 reservoir rotenone samples, the few specimens collected amounted to insignificant standing crops.

**Reservoir dynamics.** In newly impounded reservoirs, bullheads often build large populations that flourish briefly, then decline irreversibly until they become an incidental species. They appear unable to withstand competition in mature reservoir populations.

**Harvest value.** Because of overcrowding, bullheads rarely sustain good fishing in ponds, and they are even less productive in mature reservoirs. Creel census data from some small and large lakes show that the species can provide continued, substantial harvests of desirable-size fish. Population estimates and creel data suggest that large lakes offer the most suitable environment for producing desirable-size bullheads. Competition for food and space, along with predation, appears to operate within the most tolerable limits in these waters.

**Survival.** Survival decreases sharply beyond age four, and few fish live beyond five years. Some uncertainty exists in older age counts because annuli on older fish were partly obliterated by tissue deterioration around the spine lumen.

## Key Findings

**Statewide growth.** The average total lengths at the end of each of the first six years in Oklahoma waters are 3.7, 6.7, 9.0, 10.8, 12.3, and 13.8 inches. The corresponding calculated weights are 0.022, 0.148, 0.379, 0.676, 1.022, and 1.475 pounds. **Comparison with other studies.** Very little published information exists for black bullhead growth. [[William M. Lewis]] (1949) aged Iowa pond fish using vertebrae centra. As summarized by [[Carlander]] (1950), those fish reached 3.4, 5.1, 6.5, 6.9, 7.5, and 7.6 inches over the first six years, much slower than Oklahoma fish. Harvestable size was 0.3 pounds according to [[Swingle]] (1950), and 7 inches was reached in the third year according to [[Surber]] (1959).

**Length-weight relationship.** The equation was derived from 1,173 measurements of fish 1.8 to 15.4 inches long:

log W = -0.7994 + 3.1804 log L

where W is weight in grams and L is total length in inches. The source text prints the intercept as -7.994, but the value -0.7994 reproduces the tabulated weights (e.g., 240 g at 10.0 inches), so -0.7994 is used here. The report also states 3,703 fish in Table 3 and Figure 4, which conflicts with the 1,173 figure given in the text. Fish larger than 15.4 inches were excluded because of extreme weight variation.

**Annulus formation.** Annuli formed mainly during the second week of May. Nearly all fish formed annuli in May, with one exception each in late April and early June. All age groups formed annuli at approximately the same time.

**Management trial.** Houser and Grinstead (1960) removed black bullheads and bluegill by trapping from a 17-acre lake in southern Oklahoma over three years. Removals were 42 pounds per acre in year one, 7 pounds per acre in year two, and 89 pounds per acre in year three (as printed). Analysis showed no significant improvement in the remaining fish populations, and fishing was not materially improved. The authors concluded that removals alone would not reliably improve desirable species populations unless environmental conditions were also corrected. Trapping was also judged impractical for most pond and lake owners because of the effort required.

**Management recommendations.**
- Where overpopulation develops, the usual management response is eradication with [[Rotenone]]. This is considered the most desirable course in ponds.
- Restocking with more desirable species generally improves fishing.
- Without controlling bullhead access, overpopulation is likely to recur. Populations often start when people stock wild fish from nearby streams, but more often they enter through spillways during high runoff, which do not block fish moving upstream.

**Data sources.** The summary was compiled from appendices of growth data:
- Appendix A: 16 reservoirs (500+ acres), 458 fish.
- Appendix B: 8 large lakes (150-500 acres), with a combined average of 186 fish sampled.
- Appendix C: 25 ponds (0-10 acres).
- Appendix D: 25 small lakes (10-150 acres).
- Appendix E: 19 streams, 89 fish.

Each appendix lists growth by individual water body, with average calculated lengths at the end of each year of life.
