# Short-Term Methods for Estimating the Chronic Toxicity of Effluents and Receiving Waters to Freshwater Organisms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-toxicology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Environmental Protection Agency manual (EPA/600/S4-89/001, second edition, 1990) describes standardized short-term chronic toxicity tests for [[Effluent]] and [[Receiving Water]] samples using freshwater organisms. It supports [[NPDES Permit]] compliance and related water-quality decisions. The manual covers three freshwater test organisms, a [[Green Alga]] test, sampling and handling rules, quality assurance, dilution water choice, and the statistical analysis of endpoints. Its central argument is that a "safe" or "no-effect" concentration cannot be derived from statistics alone. It requires a biological judgment about what level of adverse effect is acceptable.

## Background & Description

The manual is a technical methods document prepared by EPA's [[Environmental Monitoring Systems Laboratory]] in Cincinnati, with contributors from EPA Region IV (Atlanta), the Environmental Research Laboratory in Duluth, and Computer Sciences Corporation. Its stated purpose is to estimate the chronic toxicity of effluents and receiving waters to freshwater organisms, with the NPDES permit program as the primary application.

The full report and a supplement are sold through the National Technical Information Service. A separate copy is available from the Superintendent of Documents. The document also notes that the methods are "well-tested and well-documented," and that they are chosen to be applicable across many test data sets and usable without a computer if necessary.

### Test Organisms and Methods

The manual summarizes four standard tests:

1. **[[Fathead Minnow Larval Survival and Growth Test]]** (*[[Pimephales Promelas]]*): larvae are exposed for seven days in a static-renewal system. Endpoints are survival and growth (increase in weight), compared with controls.
2. **[[Fathead Minnow Embryo-Larval Survival and Teratogenicity Test]]**: embryos and larvae are exposed for seven days in a static-renewal system, starting shortly after fertilization. Endpoints are total mortality plus gross morphological deformities (teratogenicity).
3. **[[Ceriodaphnia Survival and Reproduction Test]]** (*[[Ceriodaphnia Dubia]]*, a cladoceran): organisms are exposed in a static-renewal system until 60% of surviving control organisms have produced three broods. Endpoints are survival and reproduction. Under proper conditions, controls should produce three broods in seven days.
4. **[[Selenastrum Capricornutum Algal Toxicity Test]]** (*[[Selenastrum Capricornutum]]*, a freshwater green alga): cultures are exposed for 96 h in a static system. Responses are measured as cell density (cell counts per mL), biomass, chlorophyll content, or absorbance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Interpreting Toxicity Endpoints

The manual distinguishes between point estimates and hypothesis-test thresholds:

- **Point estimates** such as the [[Lethal Concentration 50]] (LC50), EC50, or IC25 come from mathematical models that assume a continuous dose-response relationship. Any such value estimates some amount of adverse effect, so a "safe" concentration requires a biological judgment about an acceptable effect level.
- **[[No-Observed-Effect Concentration]] (NOEC)** and **[[Lowest Observed Effect Concentration]] (LOEC)** rely on hypothesis testing. They assume either a continuous dose-response or a true threshold below which no adverse effect occurs. Results depend heavily on experimental design, including the statistical method, the alpha level, and the variability between replicates. The manual stresses reporting test sensitivity, either as the minimum significant difference or as the percent change from control that it represents.

The manual concludes that a statistical analysis alone supports a "no-effect" concentration only if a strict threshold model is accepted and the threshold effect is statistically detectable. Otherwise, the result is a "safe" concentration, meaning one whose effects are judged biologically insignificant.

### Dilution Water and Receiving Water

Dilution-water choice depends on the study objective:

1. To estimate the **inherent chronic toxicity** of an effluent (the primary objective of NPDES testing), a standard [[Moderately Hard Water]] is used.
2. To estimate effects in **uncontaminated receiving water**, a non-toxic grab sample collected upstream of the outfall is used, or water with similar pH, hardness, alkalinity, conductivity, and total suspended solids. Receiving-water properties must be measured before each use because seasonal variation can affect toxicity.
3. To assess the **additive effect** of a discharge on already-contaminated water, the dilution water is receiving water collected upstream of the outfall.

### Sampling, Handling, and Preservation

- Samples should be taken at the NPDES permit sampling point. Exceptions include sites with better access, samples taken before chlorination, or influent and separate industrial streams.
- Grab samples are preferred when effluent quality varies quickly, since they can reveal toxicity spikes. Twenty-four-hour composite samples average quality but may dilute spikes.
- Holding time from collection to test initiation or renewal should not exceed **36 h**. Composites are chilled and kept at **4°C**. Samples for on-site tests should be used within **24 h**.
- Samples may be shipped in 4-L cubitainers or plastic milk jugs. Containers are rinsed with source water and punctured after use to prevent reuse.

### Sample Preparation and Water Quality

- For the Ceriodaphnia and fathead minnow tests, effluents and surface waters are filtered through a **60-µm plankton net** to remove indigenous organisms.
- Algal test waters are filtered through a **0.45-µm** filter. A nylon sieve with **2 to 4 mm** holes may first remove debris. The manual cautions that filtration may remove toxicity.
- [[Dissolved Oxygen]] should be near saturation before use. After heating to 25°C, samples with DO above 100% or below 40% saturation are moderately aerated with a pipet tip until DO is within range.

## Key Findings

### Quality Assurance and Safety

- **Quality assurance** covers nine elements: effluent sampling and handling; source and condition of test organisms; equipment condition; test conditions; instrument calibration; replication; use of reference toxicants; record-keeping; and data evaluation.
- **Health and safety** guidance warns that effluent collection and testing can expose personnel to infectious agents, corrosive or toxic substances, and asphyxiation. Staff should confirm that safety equipment is available and in good condition before fieldwork and laboratory work.

### Statistical Analysis

- **Expertise:** analysts without statistical proficiency are strongly advised to consult a statistician. The recommended methods are not the only valid options. Alternatives such as [[Williams' Test]] and bootstrap methods are discussed.
- **Data handling:** data should be plotted both as a diagnostic step and for interpretation. Transformations (such as arcsine square root or log) are used when assumptions such as normality are not met. [[Statistical Independence]] is critical and is secured by rigorous randomization of organism placement and chamber location.
- **Replication:** test sensitivity generally rises with the number of replicates, but diminishing returns set in quickly. Enforcement cases or non-repeatable tests may need more replicates than the minimum.
- **Multiple endpoints:** the lowest NOEC across all endpoints (survival, growth, reproduction) is adopted as the test NOEC. Concentrations with significant effects on one endpoint are excluded from analysis of other endpoints.

### Specific Tests

- **[[Dunnett's Procedure]]:** an ANOVA-based comparison of each treatment mean with the control. It requires at least two replicates per treatment and equal replicate numbers. Its assumptions (independence, normality, homogeneity of variance) must be verified (Appendix B). It reports the NOEC and the minimum detectable difference as a percent of the control mean.
- **[[Steel's Many-One Rank Test]]:** a rank-based alternative for non-normal data, requiring at least four replicates per concentration. It is less sensitive than Dunnett's for normal data, and its sensitivity cannot be stated as a minimum detectable difference.
- **[[Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test]]:** a nonparametric treatment-versus-control test with [[Bonferroni's T-Test]]-style adjustment for multiple comparisons. It is less powerful than Steel's test when replicate numbers are equal.
- **[[Bonferroni's T-Test]]:** used when replicate numbers are unequal. It sets an upper bound on the overall error rate, making it less powerful than Dunnett's.
- **[[Probit Analysis]]:** used for percentage response data to estimate concentrations affecting a given percent of organisms, with confidence intervals. It requires at least two partial mortalities. Results should be used cautiously if the chi-square heterogeneity test is significant, if deviations from the model are systematic, or if data are sparse near the estimated point. Natural (control) mortality can be used to adjust results.
- **[[Fisher's Exact Test]]:** used to screen Ceriodaphnia mortality data. Concentrations with significant mortality are excluded before reproduction analysis.
- **Interpolation approach:** used for chronic data. Round-robin results show that endpoints estimated this way are much less variable than those from hypothesis testing.

### Central Conclusion

The manual's main conclusion is that a "safe" concentration cannot be established from statistical results alone. Its determination requires biological judgment about which adverse effects are acceptable. Reporting test sensitivity is essential because it shapes what the NOEC represents.
