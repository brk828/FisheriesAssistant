# Summer Home Range Movements and Habitat Use of Largemouth Bass in Mary Lake, Minnesota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 study in *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* uses [[Radio Telemetry]] to test whether [[Largemouth Bass]] wander randomly or keep to defined areas. Four bass were tracked in [[Mary Lake]] during summer 1970: three resident fish captured in the lake and one fish transplanted from [[Lake Itasca]]. The three resident bass kept to compact [[Home Range]]s, mostly in shallow water, with occasional excursions of up to 7 days. The transplanted bass also moved widely but did not establish a home range. The author concludes that the [[Home Range]] concept applies to these fish and that movement data must be tied to a defined time frame.

## Background & Description

**Context.** Home range is the area an animal normally travels, and a defended part of it is a [[Territory]]. The author notes that home range knowledge supports population census design and harvest decisions. Earlier work on stream fishes ([[Gerking 1953]]) and sunfish ([[Gunning and Shoop 1963]]) supported the idea of restricted fish home areas.

**Conflicting prior reports.** Largemouth bass movement studies disagreed:
- [[Ball 1947]] tagged 52 bass in a 4.0-hectare Michigan lake and concluded the fish roved across the whole lake.
- [[Dequine and Hall 1950]] tagged 1,616 bass in six connected Florida lakes. Of 308 angling returns, 39.6% were within 2.59 km of release and 84.1% within 8.0 km; the maximum distance was 20.1 km.
- [[Moody 1960]] described a resident population with restricted ranges in Lake George and a mobile population moving through the St. Johns River.
- [[Lewis and Flickinger 1967]] reported that 96% of recaptures in a 3.4-hectare Illinois farm pond were within 91 m of capture.

**Study design.** The author used radio tracking because it minimizes observer influence and yields many locations per fish. A second objective was to test whether a transplanted bass moved like resident fish.

**Study area.** Mary Lake lies within Itasca State Park. Key characteristics:
- Area: 22.6 ha, with a 7.5-ha littoral zone
- Elliptical basin: 852 m long, mean breadth 265 m, maximum depth 12.5 m
- Thermally stratified in summer
- Inlet and outlet did not permit fish migration
- Vegetation: sedge (*Carex* spp.) beds, emergent bulrush, cattail, reed, and arrowhead; water lilies; submergent pondweeds, coontail, milfoil, muskgrass, and spikerush to 4–5 m depth
- Fish community: mainly centrarchids and cyprinids

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Equipment.** Receivers were built around an automobile radio tuned to 53 MHz. Underwater transmitters were adapted from [[Cochran and Lord 1963]]. Each transmitter had the following specifications:
- Mass in water: 14–15 g (about 1% of fish mass)
- Expected life: 60–90 days
- Maximum range: 0.8–1.2 km
- Cost: $30 per unit

**Tagging.** Transmitters were attached beside the dorsal fin with teflon-coated wires and a plastic washer. Four bass (1.4–1.6 kg) were caught between 26 June and 14 July by angling or trap net. Bass #1–#3 were released at their capture sites, and bass #4 was transplanted from Lake Itasca.

**Location and mapping.** Positions were found by triangulation from shore markers placed at 6-m intervals, with stakes and floats at 30 m and 61 m. An average of seven fixes per day were taken at intervals of 15 minutes or more. Positions were converted to an X–Y lake grid for analysis with the [[Computer Analysis of Telemetry Data]].

**Home range methods.**
- *Maximum home range*: the smallest convex polygon enclosing all locations, following the [[Odum and Kuenzler 1955]] approach. Shorelines bounded polygon edges. Obvious wandering was excluded, including bass #3's lake-wide excursion.
- *Utilized or primary range*: a [[Grid-Square Method]] using 5-m squares, chosen because mean location error was under 5 m.
- *Stability check*: an [[Observation-Area Curve]] to test whether home range area had levelled off. The recommended endpoint was where further observations added less than 1% to area.
- *Activity centre and radii*: geometric centre of activity and distances from it ([[Activity Radii]]).
- *Shape*: an index of linearity (maximum length divided by width), where values above 2.0 indicate oblong ranges.

## Key Findings

**Home range size and shape**
- Maximum home ranges ranged from 0.3 to 1.4 ha according to the abstract. The primary ranges were given as "about 0.4 to 0.35 hectare," an internal inconsistency; [[Table 1]] lists utilized areas of 0.35, 0.04, and 0.26 ha for bass #1, #2, and #3, with none for bass #4.
- Maximum home range shapes were oblong, with indices of linearity above 2.0 (2.04, 2.34, and 3.33 in the table's reading).
- Approximately 94.2% of locations (about 409 of 434) were in water shallower than 3 m.
- The bass spent afternoons in very shallow water (about 0.6 m).
- Of the 434 total locations, 288 were taken between 0600 and 1759 h and 146 between 1800 and 0559 h (Central Standard Time).

**Tracking periods**
- Resident bass were tracked from late June or early July to 20 September 1970; monitoring spanned 14–87 days, with 19–56 days of actual tracking.
- Bass #2's transmitter failed after 25 days.

**Activity and mobility**
- Activity radii (363 fixes, three resident bass) had a mean of 32 m from the geometric centre. Cumulative proportions were 54% within 25 m, 87% within 50 m, 92% within 75 m, and 95% within 100 m.
- Bass #3 made a 7-day excursion around the lake, covering 1,600 m along the shoreline or 1,160 m in straight lines, at 166–229 m per day. It then returned to its primary range and stayed there for the rest of the summer.
- Bass #1 and #2 made shorter excursions of up to 110 m and 70 m.
- Bass #3 showed a similar movement 3 days after release, which the author suspects was an excursion or a handling effect.

**Transplanted bass (#4)**
- The bass from Lake Itasca covered much of the littoral zone of the northern half of Mary Lake without establishing a home range.
- Its longest stay in one place was 5 days in a 0.4-ha area that overlapped bass #3's primary range.
- Bass #4 moved around bass #3 from 3 to 8 August; the author did not determine whether this was an agonistic encounter.

**Late-season shifts**
- Bass #1 moved to deeper water around 15 August after its shallow area became overgrown and smelled of H₂S.
- Bass #3 was found 40–80 m from its primary range on 18–20 September, which may have been a fall shift or home range break-up.

**Transmitter effects**
- No mortality or fungal infection at the attachment site was recorded (one bass had fungus on one eye, possibly from handling).
- The author observed surface breaking that appeared to be feeding on four occasions. One bass was hooked but lost at the boat.
- Bass initially tipped toward the transmitter side, then compensated within 24 hours.

**Comparison with other studies**
- The author's 95% within 100 m result matches [[Lewis and Flickinger 1967]]'s 96% within 91 m.
- [[Warden and Lorio 1975]] found one home area rarely exceeding 30 m in a Mississippi impoundment, with multiple home areas at other times. The author suggests the term "home area" needs clearer definition.
- Differences from [[Ball 1947]] and [[Dequine and Hall 1950]] are attributed to the author's larger number of locations per fish and shorter monitoring period. Tracking over longer periods could show migration or home range change.

**Habitat use and temperature**
- Contrary to fishermen's common belief, Mary Lake bass did not retreat to deep water at midday. The author cites dense vegetation for cover and shallow water that seldom exceeded [[Coutant 1975]]'s preferred temperature near 27 °C.
- [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] and northern largemouth bass may differ in behaviour, but the author could not fully explain the differences.

**Management implications**
- Home range information can guide population census timing and sampling sites, and identify areas for non-harvest or harvest quotas.
- Movement findings are time-dependent and should be reported with explicit time frames.
