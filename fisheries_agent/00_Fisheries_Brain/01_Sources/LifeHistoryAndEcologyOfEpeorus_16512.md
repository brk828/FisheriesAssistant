# Life History and Ecology of Epeorus pleuralis (Ephemeroptera: Heptageniidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #heptageniidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the [[Life History]] and [[Ecology]] of the mayfly *Epeorus pleuralis* (Banks) in [[Morgan's Creek]], a small, spring-fed stony stream in Meade County, Kentucky. Observations ran over about 20 months, from 1 February 1963 to 26 September 1964. The species is univoltine. Hatching begins in late September or early October and peaks in January or February, and emergence runs from late February into June, peaking in late April or early May. Nymphs grow through winter, feed mainly on allochthonous leaf detritus, and are restricted to cool, fast-water riffles and cascades on loose rubble. Longitudinal distribution is shaped by temperature, substrate and current, while seasonal abundance is driven mainly by life-history events.

## Background & Description

The study is a reprint from *The American Midland Naturalist* (Vol. 78, No. 2, October 1967) by Judy N. Minshall, Department of Biology, University of Louisville, as part of a wider study of benthic community dynamics in the stream. The author notes that detailed life-history studies of North American Ephemeroptera were rare, and that this was the first detailed account of *E. pleuralis* life history.

*E. pleuralis* is the only abundant heptageniid mayfly in Morgan's Creek. It is the largest obligate herbivore in the stream and ranked eighth in overall benthic abundance and second among mayfly taxa. Seven other mayfly species occur in the mainstream, including *Baetis amplus*, *B. herodes*, *B. phoebus*, *Centroptilum rufostrigatum*, *Paraleptophiebia moerens*, *Pseudocloeon carolina* and *Stenonema interpunctatum*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Morgan's Creek rises in Morgan's Cave and flows about 0.8 mile (1335 m) to the [[Ohio River]]. Eight permanent stations were established, from Station I at the spring source to Station VIII at the mouth. About 73% of the stream area is rubble-riffle complex. Riparian vegetation is dense near the source and at the mouth, but cleared between Stations IV and VI.

**Physical conditions.** The stream is cool. Station I stays nearly constant in temperature through the year, and Station II tracks it closely (range about 10–14 °C). Downstream stations vary more, from near zero to between 19 °C (Station III) and 23 °C (Station VI). Modal discharge at Station IV was 0.007 m³/sec (range 0.005–0.35 m³/sec). Current velocities during modal flow ranged from 19 cm/sec at the source to 35 cm/sec at Station VII. Dissolved oxygen was about 10 mg/l at the cave outflow and at least 8.9 mg/l at Station V.

**Disturbance.** A spate in March 1963 coincided with a drop in nymph numbers. A larger flood in March 1964 scoured the source to bedrock. Floodwaters from the Ohio River covered Station VII from early March to mid-April 1963.

**Sampling.** Samples were taken with a standardized 5-minute net technique, with biweekly sampling from February 1963. Regular sampling at Stations II, V and VIII stopped after February 1964. Gut analyses, egg counts and rearing experiments were also carried out.

## Key Findings

**Distribution**
- Nymphs were restricted to the headwaters and upper middle sections. None were taken at Station VIII or in the pool at Station V.
- Nymphs were found mainly on the undersurfaces of smooth, flat rubble stones in fast water. They were rare on bedrock and in slow-water areas.
- Station II (cascade) held the largest number of nymphs over the whole study, and about 78% of 245 recruits were taken there.

**Life history**
- Recruitment begins in late September or early October. Recruits were first common in mid-January, and the peak occurred in January or February.
- Growth continues through winter. A few large nymphs (8.0–9.5 mm) were taken in mid-January, and mature nymphs were present by mid-February.
- Mature nymph length ranged from 5.0 to 13.0 mm. Of 170 mature nymphs, 85 were male (mean 7.8 mm) and 85 female (mean 9.7 mm). The largest male was 9.5 mm and the largest female 13.0 mm.
- Emergence occurs underwater. Subimago stage lasted 2 to 2½ days in the laboratory.
- Of 66 mature nymphs reared in the laboratory, 25 died as subimagoes and 17 failed to shed their skins completely. Only 24 (about 36%) became reproductive adults.

**Diet**
- Gut analyses of five nymphs per month (February–June 1963) showed mineral particles and detritus in all guts and diatoms in all but one. Detritus, mainly from allochthonous leaf material, was the main food.
- Diatoms peaked in April, when thick mats covered the rocks.
- Mineral particles dominated 18 of 25 gut samples. The author suggests these may be ingested for attached microorganisms or taken in incidentally.

**Reproduction**
- Egg counts of mature nymphs ranged from just under 2000 to just over 6000 per female. The mean was 4260 for 10 estimates and 4300 for five total counts.
- Mature eggs measure 85–90 × 135–155 µm. Hatching of artificially inseminated eggs began after about 6 weeks at 8–14 °C, so the egg stage can be short under some conditions.
- Oviposition was observed in March and April 1963, with females striking the water surface over rocky ledges.

**Environmental control**
- Longitudinal distribution is determined mainly by temperature, substrate and current, with Station II the most suitable location.
- Temperature partly controls emergence and hatching timing, especially at variable downstream stations. Near the source, where temperature is nearly constant, the author argues that another seasonally varying factor, such as photoperiod, must play a role.
