# Larval Razorback Sucker Predator Avoidance and Vulnerability: A Comparative Study Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Master of Science study proposal, submitted in partial fulfillment of degree requirements, examines why [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) fails to recruit in the [[Colorado River Basin]], particularly in [[Lake Mohave]]. The proposal argues that two factors may be involved: predation by [[Nonnative Fishes]] on early life stages, and the loss of historic turbidity caused by [[Habitat Modification]]. It proposes a series of laboratory experiments comparing the [[Predator Avoidance Behavior]] of larval razorback sucker with that of [[White Sucker]] (a Mississippi Basin species), measuring larval preference for [[Suspended Sediment]] concentrations, and testing how turbidity affects predation vulnerability. The proposal contains no experimental results. Its content consists of background, testable hypotheses with predictions, and a detailed experimental design.

## Background & Description

**Conservation context.** The document notes that alterations such as dam and levee construction, channelization, dewatering, pollution, and introductions of nonnative fishes have strongly affected North American fish fauna. Ono et al. (1983) is cited for the claim that a large share of the world's endangered and threatened fishes occur in North America (the OCR-garbled figure, read as 70%). Of 810 native North American freshwater fish species, 600 are exclusively east and 170 exclusively west of the continental divide. As of 1989, 219 eastern taxa (36% of the total) and 135 western taxa met [[Endangered Species]] criteria of the American Fisheries Society. Western fishes show high [[Endemism]], low diversity, and unique adaptations to harsh conditions.

**Species profile.** The razorback sucker is a monotypic, endemic catostomid of the Colorado River basin. It is characterized by large size (often about one meter), a flattened head, and a pronounced keel anterior to the dorsal fin. It was historically abundant and was commercially harvested as recently as the 1940s. It is listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1991). Females over 3 kg produce more than 100,000 ova.

**Historical change.** Cattle grazing from the late 1800s degraded riparian habitat. After the Reclamation Act of 1902, large water diversion projects were built, and the Colorado River was converted from a warm, silt-laden, turbulent stream into a series of 20 clear-water reservoirs. Sixty-seven nonnative fishes have been introduced into the basin. Declines of the "big river" fishes, including razorback sucker, began during this period of dam construction.

**Missouri River parallel.** The Missouri River was historically one of the most turbid rivers in North America. Impoundments have reduced its suspended sediment, which has elevated plankton and favored sight-feeding game fishes. Populations of turbidity-adapted native fishes have declined substantially.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Recruitment failure in Lake Mohave.** Lake Mohave was created by the closure of Davis Dam and filling in 1953-1954. Razorback sucker spawned there but recruitment did not occur. Remaining fish are estimated to be adults that hatched during the early 1950s filling. Four explanations for recruitment failure were considered:
1. Larvae are transported out of the system through Davis Dam (not confirmed despite sampling).
2. Insufficient nourishment causes starvation (not supported; zooplankton of appropriate size is available, and larvae grow in a backwater).
3. Predation on early life stages by introduced fishes.
4. Physical alteration of habitat reducing larval survivability.

**Nonnative predators.** Potential larvivores in Lake Mohave include [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), and [[Threadfin Shad]], along with juvenile [[Channel Catfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]. The document calls predation by nonnative fishes "the single most likely factor" for recruitment failure, while acknowledging that evidence is largely circumstantial. The strongest support comes from a 1986 pond experiment: after a berm isolated a cove and ichthyocide removed nonnative fishes, adults spawned by late March, larvae grew well, and by December the pond-spawned fish reached a mean total length of 189 ± 20 mm.

**Native predators.** The Colorado River basin historically held 36 fish species, of which 12 were known from the mainstream. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] was the only obligate piscivore there and becomes predominantly piscivorous at about 10 cm total length. The basin is characterized as predator-poor, so prey species may be predator-naive. By contrast, the [[Mississippi River Basin]] supports about 260 species, with individual streams often holding 30-60 species, and is characterized as predator-rich.

**Turbidity and predation.** The document reviews evidence that turbidity can benefit larval fishes by reducing predator visual acuity and reactive distance, and that many predators are sight-feeders whose foraging declines in turbid water. Examples include reduced larval herring feeding in high turbidity, reduced bluegill feeding rates, and the proposed "turbidity as cover hypothesis" (Gregory 1993). Larval Pacific herring foraged most successfully at moderate turbidity. The Colorado River now has lower suspended sediment and clearer water than before impoundment, which may favor sight-feeding nonnative predators.

**Behavioral background.** Johnson et al. (1993) compared larval razorback sucker and northern hog sucker. Hog sucker showed a significantly higher initial avoidance rate, and both species improved equally with development. The authors attributed this to an inherent difference, possibly due to the evolution of razorback sucker in a predator-poor environment. Work on guppies, minnows and sticklebacks shows that predator-avoidance behavior varies among populations with different predation histories, and that some of these differences appear to be heritable.

## Key Findings

The proposal's main points are its objectives, hypotheses, and methods.

**Objectives and predictions:**
1. **Predator avoidance:** Determine whether razorback and white sucker differ in predator avoidance. *Prediction:* razorback sucker will show reduced avoidance.
2. **Sediment preference:** Determine the suspended sediment preference of larval razorback sucker. *Prediction:* larvae will prefer low concentrations.
3. **Sediment and predation:** Determine whether suspended sediment affects predation on early life stages. *Predictions:* vulnerability will decline with increasing sediment; razorback sucker vulnerability will decrease with turbidity while white sucker vulnerability remains constant; razorback sucker will be more vulnerable to Colorado predators than to Mississippi predators as sediment increases.

**Methods overview:**
- **Source fish:** Razorback sucker eggs (eleutheroembryos) from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]; white sucker larvae from Leech Lake Hatchery, Minnesota. Fish are held about 90 days at 20-28 °C on a 12 L:12 D photoperiod, fed ground Tetramin.
- **Predators:** 1- or 2-year-old [[Green Sunfish]] (predator avoidance trials) and green sunfish and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (predation trials). Predators are fasted 24 hours before trials.
- **Avoidance trials:** Two larvae per trial in a three-chamber aquarium, with a predator on either side. Position is recorded with [[Focal Sampling]] at 5-second intervals (36 points per trial). An F − P index is calculated, where larger positive values indicate greater avoidance. Four replicates (eight trials) run daily, five days a week, for at least 60 days. An F-test checks for end effects. Each larva is tested only once.
- **Sediment preference:** A [[Gradient Chamber]] with three sections (0, 1.5, and 10 ppt) using [[Bentonite Clay]]. Forty-five larvae per trial, with 15 minutes of acclimation and 15 minutes of testing. [[Chi-Square Test]] for goodness of fit. Controls use no sediment to detect end effects.
- **Predation trials:** Eight larvae per enclosure, one hungry predator, one hour of acclimation, 12-minute trials, 20 trials weekly per predator per sediment level (ten razorback, ten white sucker). Analysis uses regression, covariance, and a two-factor ANOVA.
- **Mortality correction:** A = m + n − mn, where A is the total proportion killed, m the proportion dead in controls, n the experimental proportion killed, and mn the interaction term (Ricker 1975).

**Limitations noted by the author:** The proposal states that acclimation of larvae to different sediment levels will not be attempted because of time constraints. It also notes that the sediment preference experiment cannot definitively show habitat selection, although a preference for turbid water would be informative.

**Significance:** Results could clarify whether turbidity once protected razorback sucker larvae, inform whether predation or habitat change limits recruitment, and guide managers on the recovery potential of [[Endangered Species]] habitats in the remaining mainstream rivers.
