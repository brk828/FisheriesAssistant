# Age and Growth of Grand Canyon Suckers and Copepod Infestations of Native Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two studies of fishes in the [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona, both conducted by researchers at the Museum of Northern Arizona. The first is an age and growth study of the native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*). The second reports heavy infestations of the parasitic copepod *Lernaea cyprinacea* on native fishes, mainly in [[Kanab Creek]]. Both studies took place in a river system altered by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and by the establishment of exotic fishes. The text covers only these two topics; it provides no budget, management plan, or broader regulatory content.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River in Grand Canyon was historically a warm, sediment-laden river. Since the completion of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963, the river has been regulated, with cold, clear, and daily-fluctuating releases that have changed sediment transport, water temperature, and the native fish community. The document reports that native fishes have declined, and that some species are now restricted to the tributaries and reaches downstream of the dam. [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) are noted as an exotic species in the system, but none were collected in the sampled tributaries.

The flannelmouth and bluehead suckers are the native catostomids of the region. Before this study, their life histories in Grand Canyon had not been documented, although their occurrence was known for many years. The age and growth study had two aims: to determine age and growth patterns in these species, and to compare them with populations from Grand Canyon and from the upper Colorado River basin. Comparisons were also made with related populations in other river systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Age and growth study.** Fish were collected from the Colorado River and selected tributaries in Grand Canyon, and from tributaries in Coconino and Mohave counties, from October 1977 through October 1978. Age was estimated from the opercle and scales, and the document reports that opercle and scale ages agreed well. Back-calculated lengths were computed using the [[Dahl-Lea Method]] with a [[Back-Calculation]] procedure, and growth was compared using a t-test on two-way comparisons across collection periods.

**Parasitism study.** The copepod *Lernaea cyprinacea* is an introduced fish parasite originally described from European fishes. It is known to cause serious disease and mortality in fish, particularly under conditions of high temperatures and high population densities. In Grand Canyon, infestations were recorded on native suckers in tributaries but were largely absent from the mainstem. The text notes that copepod infestations in the Colorado River had been reported only once before, on *C. carpio* and other exotic species.

Kanab Creek and the Little Colorado River are described as key tributaries. Kanab Creek is a seasonal, spring-fed stream with warm water in summer and pools that support copepod habitat. The Little Colorado River provides spawning habitat for native fishes, but the authors noted that *L. cyprinacea* was present there in 1975 and absent in 1978.

## Key Findings

**Age and growth of the suckers**

- The age classes in the sample ranged from zero (young-of-the-year) to about ten years, with the oldest fish being a large female.
- *Catostomus latipinnis* grew rapidly in its first year, with an average first-year increment of 121.6 mm, followed by a sharp drop to 63.5 mm in the second year. Growth then declined steadily in later years.
- The first-year increment for age class I was reported as 77.2 mm, which was substantially lower than the all-age average.
- For *Pantosteus discobolus*, the first-year increment was 93.7 mm and the age-class I increment was 69.3 mm.
- Females of *C. latipinnis* were generally larger than males, and this difference increased with age. Among the oldest *P. discobolus*, several individuals of unknown age and sex were larger than the rest of the sample.
- The length-frequency distribution showed a uniform spread of individuals across most size classes, with young-of-the-year appearing in the population during the summer months.

**Parasitism by *Lernaea cyprinacea***

- Infestation was heavy on native suckers in Kanab Creek. About 20.7% of fish examined in Kanab Creek were parasitized.
- Parasitized fish were most common in the late summer and fall, when water temperatures in Kanab Creek and the Colorado River were within the optimal range for copepod development.
- Copepods were attached mainly near the base of the fins and on the body surface, and several copepods were found on single individuals.
- No infestation was found on the exotic *Cyprinus carpio* collected in the same survey.

**Management implications**

- The authors suggest that native fish that move from tributaries into the colder mainstem may be at risk, because the colder water may slow parasite development and stress the fish.
- The native suckers and their habitat in Grand Canyon remain at risk from both exotic fish and parasite infestations. The authors recommend continued monitoring of the tributaries and the mainstem.
