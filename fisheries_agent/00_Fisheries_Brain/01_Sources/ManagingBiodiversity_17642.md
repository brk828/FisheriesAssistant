# Stewardship of Land and Life: Bison, Invasive Species and Adaptive Management

#conservation-biology #land-stewardship #botany

## Executive Summary

This Nature Conservancy feature (January/February 1994) is a essay on land stewardship, not a fish study. It argues that stewardship is a young science built on humility about the limits of human knowledge. The authors cite past blunders, such as predator eradication and introduced biocontrol insects, and call for cautious experimentation, long-term active management, and monitoring. Examples come from [[Bison]] behavior on a Montana ranch, [[Prescribed Fire]] on a Nebraska island, invasive-species control in [[Hawaiian Rain Forest]], and the role of Indigenous peoples in shaping plant distributions and grasslands. The piece also addresses [[Climate Change]] and the long-range thinking found in Native American philosophy.

## Background & Description

The article opens with rancher Bud Griffith's observations of bison on the Flying D Ranch in Montana. Bison ran downhill and climbed back repeatedly, which appeared to damage the hillside but may have created bare soil that rare plants such as spurred gentian and Lemhi's penstemon need to germinate.

The text defines stewardship as "the ongoing preservation of land and life," based on the view that every living thing has intrinsic value. It describes this as a response to past management failures:

- Campaigns against [[Predators]] (wolves, bears, coyotes, cougars) were followed by deer populations that denuded landscapes.
- Introduced insects meant to control agricultural pests attacked beneficial species.
- Unmanaged native prairies were overtaken by [[Invasive Species]] such as leafy spurge and spotted knapweed.

The article traces the field's shift from [[Single-Species Conservation]] toward [[Ecosystem Approach]] thinking, which recognizes that biodiversity cannot be protected on small isolated preserves and must include lands with human use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Management tools and trade-offs**
- [[Pesticides]] applied to individual plants can protect rare species such as the prairie carnation in Montana, but they are costly, require repeated application, and may threaten groundwater or other species.
- Natural predators and parasites are alternatives but carry their own risks.
- Fire is a restoration tool. Burning Grand River Island in Nebraska's Platte River allowed the federally listed western prairie fringed orchid to germinate, though it had not been recorded there before fire was restored (per botanist Bonnie Heidel of the Montana Natural Heritage Program).
- Suppressing natural fires, floods and grazing can also threaten species, while these same disturbances can open ground for weeds.

**Indigenous influence on landscapes**
- Scientists hypothesize that the distribution of blue camas and bitterroot, important to the Salish and other tribes, may partly reflect deliberate propagation.
- The Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara used bison scapula hoes to till soil for corn, squash and beans.
- Pueblo peoples in the Southwest built rock dams and ditches for irrigation centuries before European arrival.
- Some scientists propose that Great Plains grasslands were shaped by people. Botanist Daniel Axelrod linked a shift from shrub-steppe to grassland 10,000 to 12,000 years ago with evidence of human arrival. Lewis and Clark reported Indigenous burning, and the article asks whether the [[Great Plains Biome]] is natural if most fires were human-caused.

**Climate and atmosphere**
- Rising carbon dioxide from fossil fuel combustion is expected to intensify the greenhouse effect. The article notes that slight climate differences strongly affect flora and fauna.
- Reserves at higher elevations may offer cooler, wetter refuges for species.
- The Hopi regard the Colorado Plateau as important to global weather patterns. The article notes that Colorado Plateau meteorological and geophysical phenomena include the highest land-based lightning activity of any place in the world during July and August.

**Hawaiian rain forest**
- Hawaii's forest evolved for 70 million years without land mammals other than a small bat. Non-native hoofed animals (escaped livestock and introduced game) open the canopy, allowing weeds, insects, birds and diseases to establish.
- Invasion can convert dense forest to barren mud and rock, or gradually dismember native systems.
- Prevention is cheaper than removal, and the article calls for cooperation with neighboring communities, farmers and agencies.

## Key Findings

1. **Stewardship requires active management.** Setting aside land without long-term management may lead to biological decline, as seen in Hawaii's rain forest.
2. **Human intervention is unavoidable and sometimes needed.** Burning, pesticide use and disturbance can restore rare species, but each intervention carries risk.
3. **Cautious experimentation is the working method.** Managers test fire, pesticides and other treatments on small sample plots before scaling up. New journals, [[Natural Areas Journal]] and [[Restoration Biology]], reflect growth in the field.
4. **Monitoring reveals ecological complexity.** Peter Lesica's seven-year study of Spaulding's catchfly showed that seedlings sprout and return to dormancy, a pattern also reported for some Cypripedium orchids that stay dormant for as long as 20 years. Environmental triggers remain poorly understood.
5. **Prevention of invasive species is more efficient than control.** Broad, cooperative strategies are recommended.
6. **Human history shapes present ecosystems.** Indigenous practices may have influenced plant distribution and grassland formation, which challenges the idea of "natural" landscapes.
7. **Climate change must be factored into reserve planning.** Conservation planning had assumed a constant climate; higher-elevation refuges are one response.
8. **Long-range, cooperative stewardship is the goal.** The article draws on the Great Law of the Iroquois Confederacy ("consider the impact of our decisions on the next seven generations") and on the Native American principle of "walking in balance." Its closing message is that stewardship means "husbanding" nature: caring, planning, protecting, and passing it on intact.
