# Game Fishes of Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1950s-era Arizona Game & Fish Department pamphlet, written by Ernest E. Mulch and William C. Gamble, is a popular angling guide to eight [[Game Fish]] species found in the state's streams and lakes. It combines species descriptions (fin counts, scale counts, spawning and growth information) with practical fishing advice for [[Trout Fishing]], [[Bass Fishing]], [[Catfish Angling]] and [[Pan Fish Fishing]]. The authors state explicitly that the selection is not a ranking of the "most common" or "principal" game fish. The document also records the history of stocking by the [[U.S. Bureau Of Fisheries]] and the decline of the [[Arizona Native Trout]].

## Background & Description

The pamphlet is the first full-color publication of the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], printed in 1954 and reprinted in 1956. It was aimed at licensed anglers, school children and teachers. The foreword notes that the pamphlet was paid for through fishing license revenue, and that a printing-cost charge might be introduced later depending on demand.

The eight featured game fish are:

- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Salmo gairdnerii*), not native to Arizona and stocked from hatcheries.
- **[[German Brown Trout]]** (*Salmo trutta fario*), an introduced, hatchery-reared species.
- **[[Largemouth Black Bass]]** (*Micropterus salmoides salmoides*), a member of the sunfish family Centrarchidae.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus lacustris*), which the document describes as a deeply forked-tail species.
- **[[Bluegill]]** (*Lepomis macrochirus macrochirus*) and **[[Crappie]]** (*Pomoxis nigro-maculatus*), the two main pan fish.
- **[[Arizona Native Trout]]** (*Salmo gila*), the only featured species native to the state.

The pamphlet also mentions *Salmo clarkii pleuriticus*, the [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], which once lived on the north slopes of Mount Baldy and is "probably extinct" at the time of writing. Cutthroat trout are discussed in the fishing section as lake fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes habitat preferences in general terms:

- **Elevation and temperature:** Trout were planted generally above 6,000 feet. Largemouth bass tolerate warmer water than trout and are therefore more widely distributed across the state, allowing year-round fishing. Pan fish are found mainly at lower elevations.
- **Stream structure:** Trout hold behind rocks and obstructions where they can ambush food. The document calls long, flat, languid stretches without cover "sterile trout water."
- **Lake structure:** Bass concentrate along breaks in the lake bottom (rocks, logs, brush piles, weed beds). Productive areas in bowl-shaped lakes lie in the [[Littoral Zone]]. Cold-water trout use shallow areas with weeds no deeper than 12–15 feet.
- **Water flow:** Channel catfish prefer swiftly moving river channels. Their feeding increases when flows fluctuate, and they move into flooded meadows during high water.
- **Water temperature (catfish):** Feeding should not occur below 40°F. Optimum temperature is 70°F or higher.
- **Water depth and diel patterns:** Catfish are usually in deep water during the day and range into shallows at evening and night. Trout move into shallow water at dawn, dusk and night.

### Stocking and Distribution

- The [[U.S. Bureau Of Fisheries]] stocked many of the state's lakes and streams using specially built railway cars shortly before and after the turn of the century. Carp, catfish, bass, crappie, bluegill and trout were released "indiscriminately" from railroad trestles.
- All featured game fish except the Arizona native trout were introduced.
- Rainbow trout have been planted in all trout waters of the state. Natural reproduction is spotty, but it is known in the [[Colorado River]] below [[Hoover Dam]] (where [[Lake Mead]] forms) and below Davis Dam (where [[Lake Mohave]] forms), and in lakes and streams of the White Mountain area and the Mogollon Rim.
- Largemouth bass occur in the [[Salt River]] lakes (Roosevelt, Apache, Canyon and Sahuaro), the Colorado River lakes ([[Lake Havasu]], Mohave and Mead), the Verde River lakes (except Sullivan), the [[Gila River]] (for about 20 miles below Coolidge Dam), and [[San Carlos Reservoir]].
- Channel catfish are found in the Colorado River main stream from the Mexican border north. The document states that any water supporting bluegill and largemouth bass will also support channel catfish.
- The [[Arizona Native Trout]] is now restricted in pure form to the upper headwaters of [[Eagle Creek]] in Greenlee County (as of 1954). It is probably also present as a hybrid in the Black and White Rivers and in streams of [[Mount Baldy]].

## Key Findings

**Species status and biology**

- Rainbow trout are not native to Arizona. Hatchery fish are typically over seven inches when planted. Colorado River rainbows have been taken over 18 pounds.
- Rainbow trout grew about two inches per month during peak summer in some high mountain lakes, which the document says far exceeds rearing-pond growth.
- Brown trout may weigh over seven pounds and can survive in waters too warm for other trout. The document notes that a 24-inch brown may eat five to eight 7-inch trout in 24 hours.
- The largemouth bass spawns from April through July on the Colorado River and in April to early May on Lakes Mead, Mohave and Roosevelt. Males guard nests for 5–15 days. Females lay about 2,000–6,000 eggs per pound.
- Channel catfish may exceed 35 pounds. They spawn from late May to the end of July when water reaches about 75°F. Females lay 3,000–20,000 eggs, and nests typically hold more than 5,000. Incubation lasts six to ten days.
- The Arizona native trout is small, typically five to six inches, with a maximum of 16 inches. It spawns in March and April, with females laying 200–600 eggs in a gravel "redd."

**Angling guidance**

- Trout: Small hooks (size 14–18) and light leaders are recommended. The authors say that upstream fishing with nymphs or wet flies is preferred, and that dry flies must be fished upstream to avoid unnatural drag. Good trout water is found in fast pools behind rocks and logs.
- Brown trout: The authors describe brown trout as among the most difficult trout to take. In summer they are most reliably taken on artificial insects (nymph, wet fly or dry fly). Worms work better during spring, fall and rainy periods.
- Bass: Anglers should retrieve surface lures slowly and let them rest about two minutes near logs, shoals or shorelines. The authors advise lighter line (eight to ten pound test) over heavy line, because heavy line causes more missed strikes.
- Catfish: Fish should be set after the catfish has moved away with the bait for several feet. If the bait is pulled free, leave it in place for several minutes so the fish may return. Once a food type proves effective, catfish tend to keep feeding on it, so the angler should stay with that bait.
- Pan fish: Crappie are usually schooling fish, so locating a school is the key step. Crappie have soft mouths and tear free under heavy pressure. Both species are suited to beginners and children.

**Editorial and historical points**

- The authors emphasize that no single "best" lure or method exists and that fishing advice is contested among anglers.
- The Arizona native trout was the only one of the eight featured species not introduced to the state. The authors describe its restricted range by 1954 as a conservation concern.
- The pamphlet was funded by license fees, and the authors note that printing costs could be charged to readers if demand grows.
