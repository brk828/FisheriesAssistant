# Habitat Use and Streamflow Needs of Rare and Endangered Fishes in the Yampa River, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #freshwater

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Report (No. 89(14), July 1989) by [[Harold M. Tyus]] and Catherine A. Karp evaluates the habitat use, potential limiting factors, and general streamflow needs of four federally rare fishes in the [[Yampa River]], Colorado: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. It is Step 1 of a two-step process requested by the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Coordinating Committee]]; the report does not quantify flow volumes, durations, or timing (Step 2).

The Yampa is the only large river in the upper [[Colorado River Basin]] whose flow patterns have not been substantially altered by water development. The authors conclude that its natural, fluctuating hydrograph sustains native fish populations and helps maintain usable habitat for rare fishes downstream in the [[Green River]]. The report recommends that the Yampa receive the highest priority for water rights acquisition.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** The Yampa River is the largest tributary of the Green River, flowing about 320 km from the Rocky Mountains (about 3,780 m elevation) through northwestern Colorado and into [[Dinosaur National Monument]] before entering the Green River at Echo Park (about 1,524 m elevation). Its mainstem alternates between high-gradient canyon reaches with boulder, cobble, and gravel substrates and low-gradient meandering reaches with finer substrates. Average annual discharge is about 60.5 m³/s, with the [[Little Snake River]] a major contributor.

**Hydrology.** Flows rise in late March with [[Spring Runoff]] and remain high through July. Mean spring-runoff flow is about 153.18 m³/s, and a maximum of 939.56 m³/s was recorded on 18 May 1984 at Deerlodge Park. Base flow of roughly 14–15 m³/s prevails from August through March. The authors term this recurring pattern of high spring flows followed by lower flows the [[Natural Flow Regime]].

**Fish community.** Native species include Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*), speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), razorback sucker, flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*), bluehead sucker (*C. discobolus*), mountain sucker (*C. plebeius*), Colorado River cutthroat trout (*Oncorhynchus clarkii pleuriticus*), Rocky Mountain whitefish (*Prosopium williamsoni*), and sculpins (*Cottus* sp.). At least 18 nonnative fishes have been introduced. Electrofishing and trammel netting found native fishes made up more than 70% of the catch, and Miller et al. (1982) reported 54% of all fishes captured, including small fishes, were native.

**Historical context.** The four rare species were once widespread and common in major rivers of the basin. All are now threatened by habitat loss, flow and temperature regulation, sediment changes, introduced competitors and predators, and other human disturbance. The Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, and bonytail chub are federally listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973). The razorback sucker is a federal candidate species protected by state statutes in several western states.

**Program context.** The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (1987) aims to recover and delist the three endangered species and to develop management options for the razorback sucker. Protecting streamflow needs is a core element, and the Yampa was assigned top priority for water rights acquisition. Conflicts among water developers, the Service, and environmental groups led to the formation of the coordinating committee in 1984.

**Study methods.** Studies began in 1980 after the Service adopted standardized methods (Archer et al. 1980). The lower 198.4 km (Echo Park to near Williams Fork) was divided into eight homogeneous river sections. The study area was extended to river kilometer (RK) 224 after angler returns of tagged Colorado squawfish from Craig, Colorado. Methods included radiotelemetry, electrofishing, trammel netting, angling, and seining.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow–life history linkages.** The report links each life stage to flow conditions:

- **Spring:** high flows and rising temperatures trigger spawning migrations, and flooded shorelines provide feeding and physiological benefit.
- **Early summer:** declining flows and decreasing sediment transport accompany spawning and downstream larval drift. Cleaned cobble surfaces are needed because squawfish eggs are adhesive.
- **Late summer–fall:** low flows maintain backwater nursery habitat for young fish. Abundance and growth of young Colorado squawfish in the Green River were negatively correlated with late-summer and fall flows.
- **Winter:** stable flows protect overwintering habitat and reduce ice scouring of shorelines.

**Spawning habitat.** Colorado squawfish spawn in the lower 51.2 km of the Yampa Canyon, with the spawning reach extending from Warm Springs Rapid (RK 6.4) to near Harding Hole (RK 32). Ripe fish concentrate near [[Mathers Hole]] (RK 28.3). Spawning occurs on cobble bars below deep pools and eddies. Groundwater and spring-fed inflows near spawning areas may serve as orientation cues.

**Nonnative species.** Introduced [[Channel Catfish]], [[Northern Pike]], green sunfish, red shiner, fathead minnow, and common carp compete with or prey on the rare fishes. Channel catfish have been found lodged in squawfish throats and in humpback chub spawning habitat. Flooding may suppress nonnative fishes while native species appear less affected, which supports the hypothesis that natives tolerate fluctuating flows better.

**Anthropogenic threats.** Dams and diversions block migration routes. Flaming Gorge Dam (closed in the 1960s) eliminated spring peaks and eliminated the four species from the Green River between the dam and the Yampa confluence. Abrupt water-level changes can strand larvae and small fish, exposing them to terrestrial predators such as herons, raccoons, and garter snakes.

## Key Findings

### Colorado Squawfish
- Adults overwinter in the upper Yampa (RK 81.6–198.4), using backwaters, runs, and eddies. Nonmigratory movements may indicate home-range behavior.
- Spring habitat use depended on flow: shoreline backwaters were used in a low-flow year (1981; 66% of N = 6 fish), while flooded bottomlands were used during the high-flow year (1983; 40%, N = 10).
- Of 43 radio-tracked fish that reached the Yampa Canyon spawning reach (1981–88), average one-way migration was 124.8 km. One White River fish traveled about 372.8 km.
- Spawning migrations started between 27 May and 3 July, earlier in low-water years and later in high-water years. Spawning occurred about 26 days after migration began and was associated with high spring flows and water temperatures above about 14°C.
- Discharge and temperature were highly correlated with spawning period (r = 0.84 and 0.88, respectively; P < 0.05), but the authors conclude that no single flow or temperature event triggers spawning.
- Fidelity to spawning sites was documented. Of four fish tracked for more than a year, two returned to the same location in consecutive years.
- Three hundred eight Colorado squawfish were collected at spawning sites (1981–88), including 208 ripe adults. Only 14 ripe or spent females were positively identified, compared with 194 ripe males. The authors suggest this female scarcity may reflect differential mortality.
- Juveniles (60–450 mm) made up only about 3% of Colorado squawfish greater than 60 mm collected in the Yampa (N = 198). Upstream recolonization probably occurs through late juvenile or early adult movement.
- Limiting factors include blockage of migration routes, angling mortality of large adults, competition and predation by nonnative fishes, altered sediment and nutrient loads, and winter flow fluctuations.

### Humpback Chub
- Adult humpbacks were captured in canyon-bound habitat in the lower 89.6 km of the Yampa. Seven adults were captured from 1981 to 1985. From 1986 to 1988, 88 captures (9 recaptures) were recorded between RK 6.4 and 73.6.
- Adults were most often found in shoreline eddies created by boulders and rapids (average depth about 2 m).
- Thirty-two mature humpbacks (3 recaptures) were captured in spawning condition in the Yampa Canyon (RK 19.2–64) in 1986–88. Spawning occurs shortly after peak spring flows.
- Thirteen juveniles (16–227 mm) were collected between RK 0.1 and 64. The Yampa is considered one of the few remaining self-sustaining populations of this species.
- Hybridization with roundtail chub and the presence of intermediate forms in altered systems highlight the importance of natural riverine environments for recovery.

### Bonytail Chub
- Bonytails were never reported as abundant in the Yampa, and no decline is indicated there.
- In Echo Park, the species has apparently declined, possibly due to flow and temperature changes after Flaming Gorge Dam closure.
- Holden and Stalnaker (1975) captured 36 bonytails in the lower Yampa and upper Green between 1968 and 1970. Only one bonytail was captured in 1979, and one suspected juvenile in 1987.
- Radio-tracking of adults stocked in the upper Green River (1988–89) is expected to clarify habitat use.

### Razorback Sucker
- More than 500 adults have been captured in flat-water sections of the upper Green (RK 282–552) and the lower 21 km of the Yampa.
- Adults were most common in shoreline runs and mid-channel sand bars (average depth under 2 m, velocity under 0.5 m/s). They overwinter in the Echo Park area of Dinosaur National Monument.
- Thirty-two ripe razorback suckers (6 females, 26 males, including 2 recaptures) were captured on cobble and gravel bars in the lower Yampa (1975, 1981, 1988, 1989), at an average depth of 0.61 m, velocity of 0.64 m/s, and temperature of about 15°C.
- Spawning occurs on the ascending limb of the spring hydrograph. A ripe male recaptured at RK 0.1 in 1988 had been captured at the same site in 1981, indicating site fidelity. In 1988 this fish had moved at least 52.8 km from the upper Green.
- Recruitment to the juvenile stage is scarce. Causes include lower water temperatures, predation by common carp and channel catfish, and loss of inundated shoreline habitat after dam closure.

### Conclusions and Recommendations
- The Yampa and Green rivers must be managed as a single ecosystem for rare-fish recovery.
- The Yampa should receive the highest priority for water rights acquisition in the upper Colorado River basin.
- Seasonal flow recommendations include: high spring flows for migration, spawning, and bar and eddy maintenance; declining summer flows for spawning, larval drift, and nursery formation; low, stable late-summer and fall flows for backwater nursery habitat; and stable winter flows to prevent ice damage.
- Coordinating Green River releases from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] with Yampa flows is needed.
- The authors note that protecting one species or life stage will not adequately protect all four, and that professional judgment is critical given limited life-history data.
