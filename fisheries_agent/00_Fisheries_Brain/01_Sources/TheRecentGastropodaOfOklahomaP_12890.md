# The Recent Gastropoda of Oklahoma, Part I: Historical Review, General Comments and Higher Taxonomic Categories

#malacology #gastropoda #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This 1959 paper by [[Branley A. Branson]] (Department of Zoology, Oklahoma State University) is the first part of a series on the recent land and freshwater snails of [[Oklahoma]]. It reviews the history of malacological work in the state, divides Oklahoma into biotic provinces and districts that structure the snail fauna, outlines the higher classification of Oklahoma gastropods, and provides a dichotomous key to the families. The paper is a regional taxonomic and biogeographic synthesis rather than a field study with new survey data. Fish and other animal groups are explicitly excluded, with the author noting that [[Blair and Hubbell Biotic Districts]] and [[Dice Biotic Provinces]] already cover them.

## Background & Description

**Historical review.** Compilation of Oklahoma molluscan species lists began with [[Simpson]] in 1888, who listed 34 forms that were mostly collected near railroad stops. Later contributions by [[Pilsbry]], [[Ferriss]] and [[Pilsbry and Ferriss]] added localities, species and subspecies. Pilsbry and Ferriss (1902) is described as the most intensive work, though limited to a small area. Between 1909 and 1924, a handful of additional papers appeared (Walker, Baker, Greger, Isely). [[Leonard and Franzen]] (1944, 1946) and [[Franzen and Leonard]] (1947) added fossil data, mostly from the Lower Pliocene, that inform the origin of the fauna. Many later papers were simple checklists or range extensions, including [[Wallen]], [[Webb]], [[Dundee]], and the author's own 1959 works.

**Higher taxonomy.** Oklahoma gastropods fall into two subclasses:
- **[[Prosobranchia]]**, considered the more primitive group, identified by a shell operculum. It includes the order Archaeogastropoda, represented by the terrestrial, lunged [[Oligyra Orbiculata Tropica]], and the order Mesogastropoda, which contains the aquatic operculate forms. Nine Mesogastropod species were known from Oklahoma at the time.
- **[[Pulmonata]]**, which lacks an operculum and includes the orders Stylommatophora (eyes at the tips of the posterior tentacles) and Basommatophora (eyes at the tentacle bases). Basommatophorans are aquatic apart from [[Carychium Exile]]. Their aquatic families in Oklahoma are Planorbidae, Lymnaeidae, Physidae and Ancylidae.

**Identification of Pseudosuccinea.** Table I gives modal shell measurements for [[Pseudosuccinea Columella]], [[Succinea Avara]] and [[Oxyloma]]. Ranges overlap broadly, so shell dimensions alone cannot separate the species. The author recommends eye position and the jaw structure (a sclerotized structure opposing the radula), extracted by boiling the head in KOH, for reliable identification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeographic framework.** Oklahoma lies within the Eastern Molluscan Division of [[Henderson Molluscan Division]] (1931). The division is distinguished from the western division by the absence of Campeloma, Viviparus, Pleurocera (sensu stricto) and Lioplax. The state includes parts of five biotic provinces, with boundaries described as subjective and gradational. Streams serve as migratory corridors, and forest "fingers" and islands extend eastern fauna westward.

**Provinces and districts:**

- **[[Navahonian Province]]** (Mesa de Maya district): Only in the northwestern Black Mesa. Average temperature 54.8°F, average precipitation 17.9 inches, maximum elevation just under 5000 feet, the highest point in the state. Perennial springs support luxuriant grasses and sedges. The genus [[Pupilla]] (*P. muscorum*, *P. blandi*) is considered a Pleistocene relict.
- **[[Kansan Province]]**: Covers over half the state. Subdivided into the mixed-grass plains district (average annual temperature 58.6–62.7°F, rainfall 26.8–27.6 inches, with an east-to-west moisture gradient), the short-grass panhandle district (eroding, in places nearly devoid of life), the [[Mesquite District]] (*Prosopis glandulosa*) and the [[Wichita Mountains]], which lack an endemic fauna. The province's fauna is general; *Physa anatina* is the only snail exclusively characterizing it.
- **[[Texan Province]]**: Includes the Osage district (a zone of intergradation, with the amnicolid incursions from the east) and the Cherokee prairie district. [[Striatura Meridionalis]] is considered the most distinctive species. The western limit of [[Bulimulus]] roughly parallels the Texan boundary.
- **[[Austroriparian Province]]** (Mississippian district): Southeastern McCurtain County, average rainfall slightly over 46 inches. Heavily shaded forests with pine, sweetgum and oaks, sandy and clay cretaceous soils lacking limestone (so snail diversity is lower than in comparable Carolinian areas), and sluggish streams that dry in summer. [[Campeloma Decisum]] is the only gastropod characteristic of the Oklahoma Austroriparian, restricted to mud-bottomed streams, plant micro-habitats around [[Zannichellia Palustris]], and oxbow lakes such as Grassy Lake.
- **[[Carolinian Province]]**: The richest snail fauna in the state, with several endemics.
  - The **[[Ozarkian District]]** has the sharpest demarcation in the state. Its streams are swift, clear, spring-fed, slightly alkaline, with riffles and pools, and its rainfall is about 43 inches. It has boone chert and limestone and rich leaf mold. Endemic taxa include [[Mudalia Plebia]] (found in almost every cool, clear stream in the district), [[Ferrissia Tarda]], [[Polygyra Jacksoni]] (with three nominal subspecies), and [[Philomycus Carolinianus]]. Its gastropods are thought to have originated by radiation from the Tertiary Appalachian Mountains.
  - The **[[Ouachita District]]** is an intergradation zone. Its only restricted species is [[Mesodon Kiowaensis]], the rarest North American mesodon, found around Pittsburg County.

**Hydrology and vegetation.** Stream descriptions include clear, swift Ozark waters with Cladophora and mosses on riffles, and Austroriparian waters that are sluggish and vegetated with Spirodela, Lemna and horned pondweed.

## Key Findings

- Oklahoma's gastropod fauna is structured by a complex mosaic of biotic provinces, with boundaries that are subjective and gradational rather than sharp.
- Major environmental gradients, including an east-to-west moisture cline, and forest corridors along streams, shape the distribution of many taxa.
- The Ozarkian district of the Carolinian Province has the richest and most endemic fauna, while the Kansan Province is comparatively homogeneous.
- The Austroriparian fauna is depauperate relative to comparable Carolinian forests, largely due to the lack of limestone in soils.
- Campeloma decisum is the single characteristic Austroriparian gastropod, and the Pleistocene appears to have had little effect on Carolinian snail faunas.
- The paper provides a family-level dichotomous key covering 21 couplets, covering aquatic and terrestrial families, and an identification protocol for the Pseudosuccinea–Succinea complex.
- The author plans further parts of the series, including range extensions and additional faunal records, as cited in the bibliography (Branson 1959a, 1959b; Branson and Wallen 1959).
