# Fishery Implications Associated with Prolonged Temperature and Oxygen Stratification

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

Charles D. Ziebell (Arizona Cooperative Fishery Unit, University of Arizona, Tucson) examines the fishery consequences of an unusually prolonged thermal and oxygen stratification regime in [[Pena Blanca Lake]], a small warm monomictic impoundment near Nogales, Arizona. Published in the *Journal of the Arizona Academy of Science* (Vol. 5, No. 4, October 1969), the paper links regional desert climate and basin morphology to severe hypolimnetic anoxia, hydrogen sulfide production, and reduced habitat for foraging and spawning fish. The study is a short descriptive limnological assessment based on 1967 monthly measurements and earlier unpublished data, with a comparison to [[Lake Providence]] in Louisiana.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Arizona lakes were essentially absent before 1958, since regional erosional landforms do not produce natural lake basins. Recent artificial impoundments are shaped by a climate that rarely supports small lakes: high solar radiation, cloud-free skies, rainfall of about 40 cm/yr, and rare inflow.
- **Pena Blanca Lake:** The first of several regional impoundments, described as a prime example of unusual [[Limnology]] driven by desert climate and basin morphology. Its origin and physical features were previously reported by McConnell (1963) and Kemmerer (1965).
- **Methods:** Temperature and dissolved oxygen were measured monthly at selected stations, January through September 1967, supplemented by unpublished McConnell data from 1959 and 1960.
  - Temperature: [[Bathythermograph]] (Wallace and Tiernan Model FA 190111, calibrated for 1150 m altitude).
  - Dissolved oxygen: [[Winkler Method]] (azide modification), with reagents added in the field and titration the next morning.
  - Total sulfide: colorimetric method (B) per Standard Methods (1965), analyzed in the field.
  - Sampling used a [[Kemmerer Sampler]].
- **Funding:** Jointly supported by the University of Arizona, the Arizona Game and Fish Department, and the U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife through the Arizona Cooperative Fishery Unit.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal stratification cycle at Pena Blanca Lake**

- **Complete mixing:** late December through January, when solar income is low.
- **Onset:** February–March, with partial thermocline formation as solar radiation increases.
- **Established stratification:** by April, with a thermocline at 4–7 m. Waters at 9 m and below were devoid of dissolved oxygen.
- **Persistence:** April to mid-November, most intense in July and August, when the epilimnion was only about 3 m deep. Water below 7 m (July) and 6 m (August) was anoxic.
- **Turnover:** cooling began in October, mixing increased through November, and the lake was fully circulating by late December.

**Climate comparison with Lake Providence**

- Both lakes lie near 32°N and have similar depth and basin morphology, but Pena Blanca sits at 1150 m versus 30 m for Lake Providence.
- Mean surface water temperature tracks mean air temperature, with a lag of roughly the preceding 7 days; this lag was the strongest correlate in the study.
- At Lake Providence, surface and air temperatures were nearly identical February through June under 50–66% of possible sunshine. In midsummer, sunshine rose to 76%, and surface water exceeded air temperature, with stratification from June to September.
- Pena Blanca surface temperatures were consistently above mean air temperatures throughout the year. Solar duration and intensity were always higher there for comparable months, which the author identifies as the chief driver of intense stratification.
- Pena Blanca stratified earlier and remained stratified longer than Lake Providence despite its higher elevation, lower air temperatures, and likely greater evaporative cooling.

**Hydrogen sulfide and anaerobiasis**

- Anaerobic conditions in the hypolimnion produce hydrogen sulfide. Several hypolimnetic samples exceeded 1.0 mg/l sulfide, a concentration generally lethal to fish (Doudoroff and Katz, 1950).

**Water level and evaporation**

- High evaporation lowers the lake approximately 1 m during spring drought, shrinking the epilimnion and shoreline habitat.

## Key Findings

- **Habitat loss from anoxia:** In April, about one month after stratification began, approximately **33% of the bottom area and 15% of the lake volume** were unsuitable for fish.
- **Worst-case conditions:** In August, **60% of the bottom area and 46% of the lake volume** were denied to foraging fish and their prey. Only the upper 4 m held oxygen adequate for fish and most food organisms.
- **Duration:** Severe oxygen depletion in the hypolimnion lasted about 7 months (March/April through November), with the stratified season running March through November.
- **Climatic driver:** Higher solar income at Pena Blanca than at Lake Providence is considered the main cause of its more intense and prolonged stratification.
- **Competition and overcrowding:** Concentrating fish in the remaining oxygenated water can intensify:
  - Inter- and intraspecific competition, with the most aggressive species likely to displace preferred game fish into marginal habitat.
  - Predation, which may be harmful or beneficial depending on whether the prey is a game species.
  - Competition among nest-building fish in shallow areas during spring drought, potentially reducing reproduction and recruitment.
  - Bottom-feeder stress from reduced benthic foraging space, with possible overcropping of forage organisms, slower growth, and population declines.
  - Angler vulnerability, though the magnitude of this effect is unknown.
- **Management conclusions:**
  - Artificial aeration to break stratification is possible but may not be feasible, since maintenance costs could outweigh the benefits.
  - The recommended approach is to study the ecological and social aspects of mixed fish populations under these prolonged stratification conditions in controlled studies, then develop management guidelines.
- **Broader significance:** The author describes the fishery problems at this southern Arizona lake as unparalleled in the United States.
