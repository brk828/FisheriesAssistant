# Regaining Fossil Creek

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 2003 article from the *Arizona Wildlife Federation* describes the planned return of roughly 14 miles of free-flowing stream to Fossil Creek, Arizona, in late 2004. The flow had been diverted since 1908 to power the [[Childs-Irving Hydroelectric Project]]. [[Arizona Public Service]] (APS) agreed to decommission the plant, and several federal and state agencies are assessing the removal of nonnative fishes, the construction of a fish barrier, and restoration of the native fish fauna. The article also explains the [[Travertine Formation]] process that is expected to rebuild dams and pools in the channel.

## Background & Description

Fossil Creek rises from springs a few miles north of Strawberry and flows about 14 miles before joining the [[Verde River]] south of Childs. The springs are the largest in Arizona outside the [[Grand Canyon]], producing more than 300 gallons per second of clear water at a constant 72°F. The creek lies within the [[Fossil Creek Wilderness]].

The creek supports a diverse native fish assemblage: roundtail chub, headwater chub, Sonora sucker, desert sucker, longfin dace, and speckled dace. Nonnative species include green sunfish, yellow bullhead, and smallmouth bass, which became established in the 1990s. Crayfish, also nonnative to the Colorado River basin, are abundant and prey on fish and frog eggs and larvae.

Water from the springs was diverted in 1908 into penstocks, flumes, siphons, tunnels, and pipes to run the Childs (about 3.8 megawatts) and Irving (about 1.6 megawatts) generators. The power supported mining at Jerome and growing Phoenix in the early 20th century. By the time of decommissioning, the facility accounted for less than 0.1% of APS's total generating capacity. APS applied to the [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] for a permit renewal in 1993, and after environmental and economic analyses, the company and partner agencies decided to remove the facilities and return full flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Impacts of diversion and nonnative species.** Diversion reduced the distribution and abundance of vegetation and animals, but many species persisted. Sycamores, willows, and other riparian plants survived through seepage and flood flows. Agency scientists documented the replacement of roundtail chub by smallmouth bass and "relentless predation" on other native species. Chiricahua leopard frog populations have also declined.

**Travertine dynamics.** Water rich in calcium carbonate and dissolved carbon dioxide emerges from limestone bedrock. As carbon dioxide degasses downstream, the water becomes supersaturated and calcium carbonate precipitates, especially at riffles, drops, constrictions, and around logs. Before diversion, more than 80 travertine dams existed upstream of Irving, some several tens of feet high. Floods and overgrazing broke many of them down, leaving the channel mostly scoured to bedrock. Maintenance releases have shown that dams re-form quickly once flow returns.

**Expected changes.**
- Upstream of Irving: deep turquoise pools alternating with travertine dams, cascades, and fast-running passages that let fish migrate upstream.
- Downstream of Irving: because most calcium carbonate is deposited upstream, changes will be less dramatic, but the channel will be wider and deeper, riffles will be wetted, and riparian vegetation can colonize the floodplain.

**Management risk.** Restored flow will also help nonnative fishes, which without intervention are likely to dominate and eliminate native fish and frogs. Agencies are therefore weighing the removal of nonnative fishes and construction of a barrier to block reinvasion. Crayfish control is also part of the concern.

## Key Findings

- **Restoration timeline:** APS and partner agencies agreed to return full flow to Fossil Creek on December 31, 2004, with flow beginning in January 2005.
- **Hydropower scale:** The Childs-Irving facility produced about 3.8 MW (Childs) and 1.6 MW (Irving) and contributed less than 0.1% of APS's total capacity.
- **Spring output:** Springs yield more than 300 gallons per second at 72°F, enough to fill a backyard swimming pool in under a minute.
- **Native fish decline:** Smallmouth bass are replacing roundtail chub, and predation threatens other native species; Chiricahua leopard frog populations have also declined.
- **Nonnative threats:** Green sunfish, yellow bullhead, smallmouth bass, and crayfish all pose risks; agencies are evaluating removal and a reinvasion barrier.
- **Travertine recovery:** Pre-diversion records show more than 80 travertine dams above Irving; research and maintenance releases indicate these will re-form rapidly once flow resumes.
- **Habitat outlook:** Upstream habitat will shift to pools, cascades, and migration routes; downstream habitat will be wider and deeper with expanded riparian vegetation.
- **Historical and recreational value:** Early accounts by [[Charles Lummis]] (1892) and biologist F. W. Chamberlain (1904) documented the creek's beauty and fauna; the area remains popular for day hiking, camping, and swimming.
