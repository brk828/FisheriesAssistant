# Lower Colorado River Native Fishes PIT Tag Data Base: Management Summary, October 1997 to May 1999

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This summary report, prepared by [[Carol A. Pacey]] and [[Paul C. Marsh]] of Arizona State University for the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Biological Resources Division, covers the 18-month period from October 1, 1997 through May 31, 1999. It documents the management and analysis of Passive Integrated Transponder (PIT) tagging data for endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. The database, begun in 1991, tracks wild adult fish, hatchery-reared juvenile "repatriates," and recaptures across the lower Colorado River system. The report draws on a comprehensive file of more than 45,000 records for Lake Mohave razorback sucker.

## Background & Description

Both species are fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and are federally listed as endangered because of non-native fishes and habitat alteration linked to water development. Historically common to abundant, they are now uncommon to rare except in Lake Mohave. Recovery plans (USFWS 1990, 1998) share priorities: preventing extinction, repatriating propagated young, and maintaining ecological and management information.

The [[Lake Mohave Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), formed in 1990, coordinates agencies including the Arizona Game and Fish Department, ASU, the Nevada Department of Wildlife, the Bureau of Reclamation, the Fish and Wildlife Service, the Biological Resources Division, and the National Park Service. Its immediate goal is to repatriate a reproductive stock of 50,000 adult razorback suckers. The group also runs a wild adult monitoring program begun in 1968 by W.L. Minckley and ASU. Sampling occurs in March (the "Razorback Round-up"), May, and November, using mainly [[Trammel Netting]] and [[Electrofishing]].

PIT tagging replaced finclips, floy tags and other marks, and has been used on most wild adult and repatriated fish since November 1991. Tag codes are 9 or 10 character alphanumeric sequences. ASU has served since 1988 as a central repository for native fish tagging data from the Glen Canyon Dam to the U.S.-Mexico border, managed in Microsoft Excel and Access.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mohave was divided into six areas for movement analysis: River, Uplake, Arizona Bay, Coves Nevada Side, Coves Arizona Side and Downlake. These areas were judged limnologically distinct and relevant to razorback sucker distribution. [[Arizona Bay]] and the Nevada-shore coves held the largest adult aggregations and the greatest sampling effort. [[Yuma Cove]] is identified as an important spawning area. The report notes that Downlake (Area F) was not sampled during the study period, though PIT-tagged fish have recently been reported there and below Davis Dam.

Growth measurements are limited by measurement error and the extreme ages of wild fish, which earlier studies also observed (Minckley 1983; Minckley et al. 1991).

## Key Findings

**Razorback sucker captures and population status**
- A total of 1,345 razorback suckers was handled, of which about 30% were repatriates (the report also gives 1,341 in its Results section). Sex composition was 598 females (44%), 710 males (53%), 6 juveniles and 23 unknown.
- 637 untagged wild adults (42% of sample) were captured and released untagged, and 708 PIT-tagged fish were recaptured (53%).
- The 1999 Schnabel estimate for wild adults was 9,087 fish, and wild numbers are declining steadily since 1991.
- The estimated repatriated population was about 3,104, implying roughly 30% post-stocking survival from nearly 26,000 fish released. The authors estimate about 12,000 razorback suckers in the lake in May 1999, roughly 75% wild adults and 25% repatriates.
- The report's population-size calculation is marked as not current and is updated as new data arrive.

**Growth and movement**
- Monthly growth in wild adults was ≤0.5 cm for most fish. Repatriate females grew an average of 25 cm and males 19 cm between release and recapture, with average monthly growth below 1 cm.
- Eight females and 13 males moved more than 50 km between areas. Females showed the largest average change in river km.
- Four females and one male were at large for almost six years, growing from 33 cm to 58 cm TL.

**Repatriation**
- From October 1997 to May 1999, 14,176 juvenile razorback suckers (mean 29 cm TL) were reared and released from lakeside backwaters and grow-out facilities. Since 1992, 25,914 repatriates have been released; backwaters contributed 48%.
- Less than 1% of released repatriates were recaptured. Recaptured fish were mostly males (91) and females (48), a male-to-female ratio of almost 2:1.
- Grow-out facilities included Boulder City Wetlands Park, Boulder City Golf Course Ponds and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].

**Bonytail**
- Only 9 bonytail were captured, all in May. About 2,864 juveniles were released during the study period and about 4,705 since 1993, with 93% of grow-out releases reared at Willow Beach NFH.
- Only one repatriated bonytail has been recaptured, and only five recaptures exist in the database. Too few data exist for population estimates.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
1. Keep Arizona Bay, including Yuma Cove, as a focal area for management.
2. Continue PIT tagging of wild adults to keep population estimates reliable and to assess repatriation success.
3. Develop a more intensive bonytail monitoring program and capture critical individual data (TL, weight, tag, sex, condition, health) promptly.
4. Rear juvenile razorback suckers to at least 30 cm before release, since larger fish survive significantly better and fewer, larger fish are more cost-effective.
5. Record fish health in field notes (eyes, fins, spine, body, fungus, infection, hemorrhage, ripeness) for both wild and repatriated fish.
