# Coronado River Basin Salinity Control Project: Final Environmental Statement Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This is a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Final Environmental Statement, prepared under Public Law 93-320, for the Coronado River Basin Salinity Control Project, Title I. The project addresses high-salinity drainage from the [[Wellton-Mohawk Division]] that is discharged into the [[Colorado River]]. Its main structures are a [[Desalting Plant]], a replacement of the Main Outlet Drain Extension, and a concrete-lined [[Bypass Drain]] that would carry Wellton-Mohawk drainage around the river to [[Santa Clara Slough]] in Mexico.

The document describes the existing biological, physical, and cultural environment of the Yuma area. It then assesses the impacts of construction and operation. The main consequences are the loss of sustained river flows below [[Morelos Dam]], the loss of riparian and aquatic habitat, and the loss of fish populations in river backwaters and drains. Several mitigation measures are proposed, including a fish rearing facility and a wildlife management area along the [[Gila River]].

## Background & Description

The project area lies in the southern Sonoran Desert of Yuma County, Arizona. Irrigated agriculture is the dominant land use. Crops include wheat, alfalfa, citrus, cotton, lettuce, melons, and grass seed. The document gives irrigated acreage for the Yuma area (1973) and for the Imperial and San Luis valleys.

Native vegetation is sparse desert scrub, with creosote bush and mesquite on the Yuma Mesa. Riparian stands of cottonwood, willow, saltcedar, and mesquite line the river. About 3,190 acres of riparian vegetation lie adjacent to the Colorado River below Morelos Dam.

Wildlife includes:
- **Big game:** mule deer in washes and desert bighorn sheep in the Chocolate Mountains, in small numbers.
- **Small carnivores:** coyote, gray fox, kit fox, raccoon, ringtail, and striped skunk.
- **Rodents and rabbits:** kangaroo rats, pocket mice, cottontails, and beaver and muskrat near waterways.
- **Reptiles and amphibians:** desert tortoise (considered depleted), soft-shelled turtle, bullfrog, several toads, and numerous lizards and snakes.

The [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] is an endangered species that occurs in the area. The [[Colorado River Squawfish]] is listed as endangered, but the recovery team reports it no longer exists in the river basin below Hoover Dam.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish fauna.** Roughly eleven fish species occur in the Yuma irrigation canals. Their composition is largely the same as the river, except that striped mullet are absent and channel catfish are more abundant. The main drains and laterals hold fewer species overall. Tilapia dominate biomass in the drains, while mosquito fish and mollies are numerically dominant. Backwaters and ponds have comparable diversity but lower numbers and weights of game and food fish.

**Hunter's Hole.** Five interconnected ponds, covering about 12 acres of open water, lie about 2.5 miles south of Gadsden, Arizona. Monitoring from October 1971 onward recorded total dissolved solids of about 1,850 to 5,300 ppm and dissolved oxygen of about 2 to 11 ppm. A 1974 survey used electrofishing and netting. Species recorded included carp, threadfin shad, largemouth bass, bluegill, redear sunfish, channel catfish, tilapia, and mosquito fish. The survey data were not considered fully comparable between the two methods.

**Water quality.** Most surface and groundwater in the Yuma area exceeds 1,000 ppm TDS. Wellton-Mohawk drainage runs about 1,400 to 1,600 ppm TDS. Water diverted at Imperial Dam ranges from 780 to 1,100 ppm, with a 1973 mean of about 850 ppm. Sodium and chloride are the principal ions in local groundwater.

**Ecological interrelationships.** Wildlife and riparian communities depend on the hydrology of the valley. Fluctuating ground-water levels directly affect vegetation. Small seepage ponds near Gadsden are mostly ground-water fed. The Hunter's Hole ponds are maintained by backwater from river flows. Flows below Morelos Dam were often interrupted after the dam was completed, and at times the river was essentially dry.

**Cultural and aesthetic setting.** No designated scenic sites exist. However, citrus groves, mesquite thickets, and cultivated fields create picturesque contrasts. The West Main Canal and Yuma Valley Levee mark the boundary between the valley and the riparian zone.

## Key Findings

**Desalting plant impacts**
- The plant would consume about 372,000,000 kWh per year of electricity, supplied on an interim basis by the [[Navajo Project]] power plant, which burns about 8,395,000 tons of coal per year.
- The plant would use about 60 to 64 acres of agricultural land. Loss of production is estimated at about $381,400 per year.
- Approximately 130,000 acre-feet of Wellton-Mohawk drainage would be salvaged annually. The Colorado River would retain an equal volume of water for use elsewhere in the system.
- Salinity of water delivered to Mexico would be no more than about 10 ppm above the annual average at Imperial Dam.
- Chlorine would be the only algaecide, and there would be no significant pesticide or herbicide residues.
- Residual sludge recalcination could require about 22,460 tons of coal or 157,000 barrels of fuel oil per year.

**Fish and aquatic impacts**
- The desalting plant's intake structure would block fish movement past the plant site.
- Fish species in the bypass drain would be limited to tilapia, mosquito fish, and perhaps some carp. A viable sustained fishery is not expected.
- The Colorado River below Morelos Dam would lose about 141 surface acres of water, which is roughly 41 percent of the surface water in Yuma Valley excluding the river above Morelos Dam. Affected fish include threadfin shad, carp, catfishes, sunfishes, largemouth bass, tilapia, and mollies.
- The Colorado River squawfish is not present in the project area. No threatened or endangered fish listed in the 1973 Fish and Wildlife Service list occur there.

**Wildlife and habitat impacts**
- The bypass drain would require about 260 acres of federally owned levee right-of-way and 70 acres of additional rights-of-way.
- Losses of riparian vegetation below Morelos Dam would total about 351 acres. Surface water losses would be about 120 acres, including backwater ponds.
- Losses along the Coachella Canal route would total about 2,847 acres of seep-supported vegetation. Mitigation would offset less than 50 percent of these losses, but would fully replace marsh habitat.
- The Yuma clapper rail is expected to be only temporarily displaced, with a potential increase in population after mitigation.

**Mitigation measures**
- A fish rearing facility would produce an estimated 200,000 to 300,000 fish per year to stock 200 to 300 surface acres of fishing water in the Yuma area.
- A roughly 900-acre wildlife management area along the Gila River would be acquired, with riparian and marsh habitat enhancement.
- Hunter's Hole management would continue under Arizona Game and Fish Department cooperation.

**Physical and cultural effects**
- The bypass drain would carry Wellton-Mohawk flows to Santa Clara Slough. It is about 37 miles long from the Southerly International Boundary.
- The project would convert about 2,979 acres of land to rights-of-way, including the desalting site, drain alignment, well fields, and canal.
- About 1,000 acres of irrigated land would be retired from cultivation, and 4,200 acres would be retired from agriculture with annual economic loss estimated at $2,100,000.
- Mitigation measures for Painted Rock Dam would affect about 6,600 acres of irrigated land under periodic flood pool conditions.

**Irreversible and irretrievable commitments**
- Energy commitments are estimated at 372,000,000 kWh per year for the desalting complex and 52,000,000 kWh per year for the well fields.
- The project commits about 2,979 acres of land and 22,100 acres of flood pool to long-term use.
- The water commitment is seen as a commitment to a type of use rather than a loss of resource, because Mexico's groundwater would be conserved.

**Archaeology**
- Archaeological clearance was granted by the National Park Service. Consultation with the State Historic Preservation Officer confirmed no known historic sites in the bypass alignment.
- Any discovery during construction would be professionally investigated under consultation with the State Historic Preservation Officer.
