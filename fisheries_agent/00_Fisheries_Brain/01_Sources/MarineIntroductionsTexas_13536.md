# Marine Introductions in the Pecos River System, Texas (1957-1971)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Sciaenidae #fisheries-management #experimental-stocking

## Executive Summary

This Federal Aid completion report documents a 14-year experimental program (1957-1971) introducing marine fish species from the [[Gulf of Mexico]] into the saline waters of the [[Pecos River System]] in western Texas. The program was initiated because high salinity levels (800-7,000 ppm chlorides) in [[Red Bluff Reservoir]], [[Imperial Reservoir]], and the Pecos River precluded establishment of traditional freshwater game fish populations. Six marine species were stocked: [[Red Drum]] (*Sciaenops ocellata*), [[Southern Flounder]] (*Paralichthys lethostigma*), [[Pinfish]] (*Lagodon rhomboides*), [[Golden Croaker]] (*Micropogon undulatus*), [[Spot]] (*Leiostomus xanthurus*), and [[Spotted Seatrout]] (*Cynoscion nebulosus*). Red drum proved most successful, with excellent survival (8% overall recovery), growth rates equal to or exceeding coastal populations, and exceptional recreational value. No natural reproduction occurred in any species. The study demonstrates the viability of marine species as sport fish in saline inland waters, particularly red drum which reached weights exceeding 25 pounds and provided high-quality angling and food value.

## Background & Description

### Study Area Characteristics

The [[Pecos River System]] in western Texas exhibited extreme salinity fluctuations documented through chloride measurements at [[Red Bluff Reservoir]]. Surface chloride concentrations ranged from 140 ppm (August 1966) to 6,990 ppm (1953), with bottom readings reaching 7,000 ppm (September 1971). Surveys conducted 1957-1958 revealed poor native game fish populations due to these saline conditions.

### Species Descriptions and Stocking History

**Red Drum** (*Sciaenops ocellata*, Family [[Sciaenidae]]): The primary focus species. Initial stocking occurred in 1955 with approximately 500 individuals near Imperial, Texas. From 1956-1958, Lewis stocked 1,382 redfish in [[Lake Kemp]]. Systematic stocking in the Pecos System began April 1960. Total stocking from 1960-1966 included 1,372 red drum in Imperial Reservoir and 1,022 in Red Bluff Reservoir. Fish were obtained by seining along the Texas coast near [[Rockport]] using 20-foot to 100-foot seines with ¼-inch to ½-inch mesh.

**Southern Flounder** (*Paralichthys lethostigma*): Total of 96 individuals stocked across both reservoirs. Proved second-most hardy species after red drum. Three of 28 flounder stocked were recovered.

**Atlantic Croaker** (*Micropogon undulatus*): 470 stocked in Red Bluff (April 1961), 12 additional in 1966. One individual recovered August 1967, demonstrating 13-month survival.

**Black Drum** (*Pogonias cromis*): 192 total stocked, primarily in 1966. One individual stocked in Red Bluff was recovered 100 miles downstream in Imperial Reservoir, confirming downstream passage capability.

**Spotted Seatrout** (*Cynoscion nebulosus*): 60 individuals stocked. Least hardy species; minimal survival documented.

**Pinfish** (*Lagodon rhomboides*, Family [[Sparidae]]): 608 stocked. Low survival; one dead specimen recovered July 1966.

### Methodology

Sampling employed multiple net types: (1) Standard survey nets: 150 feet long, 1-inch to 3.5-inch square mesh in 25-foot sections, 8-foot webbing tied to 6 feet; (2) "Catfish" nets: 150 feet long, 0.75-inch square mesh; (3) Trammel nets: 300 feet long, 8 feet deep (1966 only); (4) "Redfish" nets: 300 feet long, 2.5-inch square mesh, 4 feet deep, used for large red drum. Sampling concentrated October through March when 92.15% of recaptures occurred (47 of 51 fish). Most recaptures (88.23%) occurred October-December in shallow marginal waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Conditions

Water salinity created a unique ecological niche unsuitable for most freshwater species but potentially viable for euryhaline marine fishes. The system experienced dramatic water level fluctuations; Imperial Reservoir was completely dry by July 1966. Temperature extremes during winter northers caused mortality events—8 putrefied red drum (estimated 20-25 pounds each) were found March 1964, and 12-13 additional dead red drum were reported winter 1963 when Imperial was only 4.5 feet deep.

### Food Web Dynamics

Stomach content analysis (Table 9) revealed piscivorous feeding habits in red drum. Identified prey included:
- [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*): up to 4 inches, with one red drum containing 7 shad
- [[Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon* sp., Pecos River endemic)
- [[Common Carp]] (small individuals)
- Unidentified fish remains

One red drum (467 mm, 4.83 pounds) contained 7 shad. The availability of abundant freshwater forage species supported excellent growth rates. Red drum from gravel pit ponds near Imperial contained pupfish, indicating adaptation to available prey.

### Reproductive Biology

No natural reproduction occurred in any marine species throughout the 14-year study. Red drum reach sexual maturity at 28-29 inches length (Miles, 1950), typically in their fourth or fifth year. Fifteen mature red drum (over 17.97 pounds) were present in Imperial Reservoir during their fourth, fifth, and sixth years without producing offspring. One possible reproduction event was documented in 1966 when an undersized red drum (372 mm, 2.62 pounds) was captured in May from Red Bluff, but this individual could also have been from February 1965 stocking if growth was slightly retarded.

The extended spawning season of red drum (August through January in coastal waters, per Simmons 1969) was not replicated inland. Spawning requires specific environmental cues absent in the reservoir system.

## Key Findings

### Survival and Recovery Rates

- **Overall recovery**: 4% of all marine species stocked were recaptured by Department personnel
- **Red Drum in Imperial Reservoir**: 12.19% of 246 stocked fish recaptured (30 individuals), plus uncounted fishermen harvest
- **Red Drum 1964 cohort**: 21% recovery from Imperial, 8.31% from Red Bluff (Wilcox 1965)
- **Total red drum recovery**: 8% across entire study period
- **Species hardiness ranking**: Red drum and southern flounder most hardy; black drum and golden croaker second; pinfish and spotted seatrout least hardy
- **Seasonal capture efficiency**: 92.15% of recaptures occurred October-March; 88.23% in October-December specifically

### Growth Performance

Growth rates equaled or exceeded those in coastal Gulf waters. Age estimates assumed October 1 hatch date based on mid-point of August-January spawning season.

**Age Group II (370 days, ~1 year)** - Red Bluff sample (n=12):
- Mean length: 338.5 mm (13.32 inches)
- Mean weight: 864.75 grams (1 pound 14.5 ounces)
- Mean K factor: 2.28
- Length deviation: 66% within 1 inch of median

**Age Group IV (707 days, ~1 year 11 months)** - Imperial sample (n=19):
- Mean length: 550.52 mm (21.67 inches)
- Mean weight: 2,908 grams (6 pounds 6.57 ounces)
- Mean K factor: 1.75
- **Exceeded coastal growth by 1.17 inches** compared to life history studies

**Age Group II (387 days)** - Imperial October 1964 (n=21):
- Mean length: 362.95 mm (14.29 inches)
- Mean weight: 935.07 grams (2 pounds 1 ounce)
- Mean K factor: 1.95

**Age Group II (395 days)** - Red Bluff October 1964 (n=62):
- Mean length: 338.33 mm (13.30 inches)
- Mean weight: 813.5 grams (1 pound 8 ounces)
- Mean K factor: 2.21
- Closely paralleled coastal data from Pearson (1927)

**Maximum sizes documented**:
- Largest recaptured: 709 mm (27.91 inches), 11,368 grams (25 pounds 1 ounce) at 1,487 days (~4 years)
- Estimated dead specimens: 17-20+ pounds
- Fishermen reports: up to 27 inches (Campbell 1957), 24-30 inches/10 pounds (1965)

**Condition factors**: K values ranged 1.64-3.59, with most fish showing excellent condition (K > 1.7). "Tail-bitten" fish (caudal fins lost during transport) showed compensatory growth, averaging 4.58 pounds versus expected 6.65 pounds for intact fish of same age.

### Population Dynamics

In October 1964, red drum comprised 18.67% by number and 28.02% by weight of Red Bluff's fish population based on 19 standard survey nets. Maximum theoretical population was 1,150 red drum if no mortality occurred since stocking began, indicating the 8.31% return represented substantial survival given concurrent fishermen harvest.

### Tagging and Movement

Fifty-six adult red drum stocked in Imperial Hole (1959) received metallic jaw tags. Two tags were returned by fishermen from fish described as "about 3 pounds and over a foot in length." Detailed tagging data from October 1965 salvage operations (Table 5) documented 36 tagged fish transferred from Imperial to Red Bluff. One black drum stocked in Red Bluff (1966) was recovered in Imperial (September 1967), confirming downstream passage through spillway or irrigation bypass—a distance of approximately 100 miles.

### Fishery Value

Red drum provided exceptional recreational fishing. Concessionaire reports from Red Bluff documented approximately 200 red drum caught January-May 1965 at "about 10 per week." Majority measured 16-18 inches total length; smallest ~2 pounds. Authenticated catches included photographs of multiple-fish stringers. Fishermen universally reported red drum as "exceptional game fish and excellent eating"—flesh described as white, firm, and succulent. Southern flounder "created much excitement when caught 500 miles inland." Golden croaker received mild interest. Parasites were found in golden croaker livers but red drum examined were clean.

### Environmental Tolerance

Red drum tolerated chloride concentrations from 140 ppm to 7,000 ppm, demonstrating remarkable euryhalinity. However, extreme winter conditions caused mortality: shallow water (4.5 feet) combined with severe cold fronts killed mature spawning-age fish. The species showed preference for shallow marginal waters during fall/winter sampling periods.

### Stocking Methodology Insights

Transport mortality was significant for juvenile fish. Wilcox (1965) noted: "The use of plastic bags and air shipment seems to be the most successful method of transporting marine fry and fingerling seatrout." Three criteria identified: (1) do not hold fry in plastic bags over 5 hours; (2) keep water temperature under 75°F; (3) be prepared to replenish oxygen and/or water if fish become moribund. Small red drum exhibited cannibalistic behavior during transport, occasionally consuming caudal fins of tankmates, resulting in "stub-tailed" fish with reduced growth potential.

### Management Implications

The study concluded that stocking appears to be the only viable source of game fish populations in the Pecos River System due to salinity and water level fluctuations preventing reproduction of both marine and freshwater species (white bass excepted). Marine species, particularly red drum, represent "the most promising means of stocking these lakes" given: (1) excellent survival and growth on abundant freshwater forage, (2) exceptional fighting ability and food quality, (3) potential for trophy-sized fish (>25 pounds), and (4) tolerance of extreme salinity fluctuations. The report states: "The potential use of these fish in inland waters is relatively untested and extremely promising, particularly in the Pecos River System in Texas."
