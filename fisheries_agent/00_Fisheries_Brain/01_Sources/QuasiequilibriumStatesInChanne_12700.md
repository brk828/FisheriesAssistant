# Quasi-Equilibrium States in Channel Morphology

#geomorphology #fluvial-geomorphology #hydraulic-geometry

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Walter B. Langbein]] and [[Luna B. Leopold]] (U.S. Geological Survey) argues that the observed form of stream channels can be derived theoretically rather than only described empirically. The empirically measured mean form, known as [[Hydraulic Geometry]], is treated as a **quasi-equilibrium state**: the most probable condition lying between two opposing tendencies in the fluvial system. The first is a tendency toward [[Minimum Total Work]] in the whole river system. The second is a tendency toward [[Uniform Energy Distribution]] across the bed and channel. The authors apply this framework to the longitudinal profile, downstream channel geometry, shallow tidal estuaries, single cross sections, and recirculating sand-bed flumes. Theory and measurements are reported to agree satisfactorily. The paper is conceptual and omits most of the mathematics.

## Background & Description

The paper begins from the general observation that landscapes contain many examples of each landform (hills, rills, valleys) that differ slightly from one another. Two sources contribute to this variance:

- **Feedback and governing mechanisms.** Adjustment processes lag behind perturbations, so systems "seek" a modal value, much as a sluggish steam-engine governor oscillates about its set point.
- **Natural inhomogeneity.** Chance features such as a pebble, a coarser lens, a fallen tree, or a local storm produce deviations that cannot be fully separated from governor-driven variance.

The authors focus on the first source. They draw an analogy to [[Thermodynamic Entropy]], building on their earlier work ([[Leopold and Langbein 1962]]). They also note that [[Scheidegger]] (1964) confirmed the applicability of this analogy. The approach treats a geomorphic system as an open system in steady state. Its most probable energy distribution is a compromise between uniform energy expenditure and minimum total work. Mathematically, the most probable state is the one where the product of probabilities of deviations is maximal. Equivalently, it is the state where the total variance of the hydraulic components (width, depth, velocity, slope) approaches a minimum.

Key quantities:
- Power per unit length: γQs, where γ is specific weight of water, Q discharge, and s slope.
- Uniform energy per unit bed area: ws constant along the river.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a geomorphic and hydraulic study rather than an ecological one. It contains no biological data. Its environmental relevance lies in the physical controls it describes:

- **River profiles** in humid regions, where discharge increases downstream and relief is finite.
- **Bed configuration**, including ripples, dunes, antidunes, pools, riffles, bars, and bends, which act as the "governors" that resist initial disturbances.
- **Estuarine tidal prisms**, where filling and emptying of channels governs the work done.
- **Lithology, geologic structure, and history**, which can override the quasi-equilibrium tendency in particular reaches.
- **Landscape degradation and tectonic uplift**, which proceed far more slowly than channel adjustment.

The authors also note that intense floods and long droughts cause temporary departures from the most probable condition.

## Key Findings

1. **Quasi-equilibrium is an intermediate state.** The two opposing tendencies cannot be satisfied simultaneously. The stable state lies between them and is approached through scour, deposition, and internal adjustment. Field conditions match the intermediate state derived from theory, characterized by minimum variance among the components of stream power.

2. **Longitudinal profile.** Minimum total work would be reached as the slope-discharge exponent approaches −1.0, a very concave profile. Uniform work per unit width requires an exponent near −0.50, a straighter profile. Measured rivers fall between these limits, with an average near s ∝ Q^−0.75.

3. **Downstream channel geometry.** Width increases downstream, depth increases less rapidly, and the width-to-depth ratio grows. Mean velocity rises slightly, and hydraulic resistance decreases somewhat. The decline in grain roughness is nearly, but not fully, offset by form roughness from pools, riffles, bars, and sinuosity.

4. **Shallow tidal estuaries flare faster than rivers.** [[Myrick and Leopold 1963]] measured estuary width increasing downstream as approximately Q^0.7 (the source OCR reads "Q°-7"), compared with Q^0.5 in rivers. The estuary's more rapid width increase is offset by a smaller depth increase. Theory predicts this difference, which the authors state agrees with field data.

5. **Single cross sections.** Because the channel has one degree of freedom, an extra relation is needed. Adding a condition of equal rate of work gives the following exponents with discharge: velocity Q^0.35, depth Q^0.42, width Q^0.23. The authors state these compare favorably with [[Leopold and Maddock 1953]]. The width-depth relation (width ∝ depth^0.55) is a limiting condition for uniform bank and bed force distribution.

6. **Field validation of the depth exponent.** Across 170 river-gaging stations, the distribution of the depth exponent peaks near the theoretical value of 0.42.

7. **Recirculating sand-bed flumes.** With slope held constant, the friction factor (f) should vary inversely with the square root of discharge under the equal-action principle, which yields a friction factor decreasing roughly as Q^−0.5. Grain roughness alone would give only about Q^−0.2. Observed values fall between these limits:
   - 42 runs on medium sand (0.45 mm) from [[Simons and Richardson 1961]] give f ∝ Q^−0.48.
   - Other flume data summarized by Johnson (1943) give exponents from −0.35 to −0.43.

8. **Phase transitions in flumes.** Friction factor declines across the dune, transitional smooth, and antidune phases. In the dune phase, resistance falls sharply when dunes are "wiped out" at high energy. Antidune-phase resistance is generally lower than in the dune phase.

9. **Statistical, non-unique solutions.** Because the tendencies are statistical, there is no single unique solution. Probability peaks at a particular value, which explains why similar channels differ individually while their averages match.

10. **Rapid equilibrium relative to landscape evolution.** Adjustment toward quasi-equilibrium is much faster than uplift or relief reduction. Graded rivers therefore remain near equilibrium throughout landscape degradation. The condition of grade is independent of stage, so "youthful" and "mature" reaches show similar geometry where lithology and structure do not dominate.

11. **Limitations.** The theory assumes linear regressions. Very large deviations, such as steep cliffs or major knickpoints, can introduce nonlinear processes that the paper does not treat.
