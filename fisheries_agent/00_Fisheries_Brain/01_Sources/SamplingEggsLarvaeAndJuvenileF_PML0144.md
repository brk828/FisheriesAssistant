# Guidelines for Sampling Fish Eggs, Larvae and Juveniles in Inland Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae #Salmonidae #Esocidae

## Executive Summary

This FAO technical guideline, written by T.B. Bagenal (Freshwater Biological Association, Windermere) and W. Nellen (Institut für Meereskunde, Kiel), sets out methods for sampling the eggs, larvae and juveniles of freshwater fish. It notes that methods for adult fish were developed largely through commercial and sport interests, whereas early life stages have received little dedicated attention. Many freshwater techniques were therefore adapted directly from [[Marine Fish Larval Sampling]] work, which the authors argue often transfers poorly to inland waters. The guide defines life-stage terminology, classifies spawning behaviour, and reviews gear for each stage, including [[Townet]] designs, [[Miller High Speed Sampler]] tests, [[Buoyant Net]] stealth sampling, traps, seines and [[Electric Fishing]]. It closes with a discussion of sampling efficiency, avoidance and the statistical handling of patchy larval data.

## Background & Description

The guideline opens by noting that terminology for fish development is confused. The authors adopt four terms:

- **Egg:** the embryonic stage enclosed in an egg shell or case, from extrusion until hatching.
- **Larva:** from hatching until the fish is scaled (where applicable) and resembles the adult.
- **Juvenile:** from scaling until seaward migration (for [[Anadromous Fish]] species) or, otherwise, until one year old while still sexually immature.
- **Alevin:** the yolk-bearing larva of a salmonid, which transforms directly into a feeding juvenile.

Spawning behaviour is central to method choice. The authors draw on [[Balon's Reproductive Guilds]], itself based on Kryzhanovsky (1949), which groups freshwater fish as non-guarders (pelagic, semi-buoyant and adhesive eggs), guarders, and bearers (mouthbrooders and viviparous species). Sampling strategy for eggs is organized around these guilds, with separate sections for pelagic, semi-buoyant, adhesive, hidden, guarded and carried eggs.

The reference list is extensive, comprising roughly 100 entries spanning fisheries, limnology and marine plankton research, including the works of Breder and Rosen (1966), Balon (1975), Hass (1968), Noble (1970) and Forney (1975).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Egg habitat and spawning types**

- Truly pelagic freshwater eggs are rare. The Freshwater Drum (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) is the main example, with a buoyant oil droplet.
- Semi-buoyant eggs are free-floating near the bottom and drift into upper layers under currents. Examples include Burbot, Grass Carp, Shads (*Alosa* spp.), some whitefish and Goldeye. Twaite Shad eggs in the River Elbe were sampled by Hass (1968); 97% occurred 0 to 2.5 m above the bottom.
- Adhesive eggs are laid on rock, gravel, weed or sand. Quantitative sampling is difficult on coarse substrates, and weed-bed eggs are laborious to count.
- Hidden eggs in [[Salmonid Redds]] or nests are sampled by digging or drift nets. Bitterling deposits eggs inside freshwater mussels.
- Guarders, such as [[Three-Spined Stickleback]] and [[Ten-Spined Stickleback]], build nests. [[Smallmouth Bass]] nests were a mean of 84 cm deep (Neves, 1975), and male removal caused total egg mortality.
- Mouthbrooding cichlids carry eggs and larvae, so sampling risks egg loss before the adult is examined.

**Larval behaviour and distribution**

The guideline stresses that most freshwater larvae are not truly planktonic. Pike (*Esox lucius*) and many cyprinids attach to plants by head adhesive organs, and they escape when plants are disturbed. Later, larvae often remain in shallow bays, hollows and backwaters. Wind and current effects are documented: in the Eider, percid larvae were most abundant on the up-wind shore (Link, 1973), and walleye larvae drift into the main lake body in wind-generated currents (Houde and Forney, 1970). Larval fish of 8 mm or more can avoid plankton nets (Noble, 1970).

**Juvenile habitat**

Juveniles are generally easier to sample. Shore seines, purse seines and self-cleaning traps are used for juvenile [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Coho Salmon]] and [[Atlantic Salmon]] in rivers, creeks and estuaries.

## Key Findings

**Sampling principles**

- Gear choice should follow knowledge of spawning habit and habitat. Balon's guild system and Breder and Rosen (1966) are recommended starting points.
- Freshwater larvae are not reliably sampled with methods borrowed directly from marine pelagic studies, although well-tried marine methods may be used where appropriate (for example, for epipelagic coregonid larvae).
- A net's filtration efficiency is near 100% when the total area of straining apertures is at least 5 times the mouth area (5:1 ratio). Encased samplers with mouth-reducing cones behave differently and depend on case shape (Tranter and Smith, 1968).
- Volume filtered must be measured with a calibrated flowmeter in the mouth of the sampler and checked for clogging.

**Larval gear and avoidance**

- Simple [[Townet]] designs were used in horizontal tows. [[Gulf III Sampler]] and [[Bongo Net]] designs were developed for marine work and need research vessels with derrick and winch facilities.
- The [[Miller High Speed Sampler]] (mouth opening 10 cm in Noble's study) was evaluated for yellow perch and walleye fry. Key results:
  - Fry of yellow perch and walleye could avoid a standard Miller sampler towed at 12.9 km/h in daylight, with avoidance starting below about 8 mm length.
  - Speeds above 12.9 km/h increased catch per unit volume filtered.
  - Night catches and catches from translucent or clear samplers showed that avoidance is a visual response.
  - Avoidance was reduced by an electric shocker (two 61 cm grids, 23 cm apart, 220 V AC) placed in front of the sampler.
  - Avoidance was not eliminated at 17.7 km/h.
  - Avoidance at 0.5 m was consistently higher than at 1.5 m with the clear sampler, linked to light intensity 25% lower at depth.
- Marine evidence cited: avoidance is mainly a problem for older, more mobile larvae at low speeds (about 3.7 km/h), while at about 9.3 km/h it does not bias results.
- In Lake Opinicon, twin conical nets (61 cm diameter, 2.5 m long) towed at 2.3 km/h for 2 minutes sampled perciform larvae (Amundrud et al., 1974).
- A brackish-water study in the Schlei (40 km long) found good smelt and perch catches with a 1 m net towed at 3.7 km/h, but no cyprinid larvae in the open water.

**Buoyant nets and traps**

- Bagenal (1974) designed a [[Buoyant Net]] that sinks to the bottom on a weight, rises when a polo mint dissolves after about 2 hours, and caught all larvae in the water column above it quantitatively. Ten nets were set on two days per week to estimate mortality in a pond.
- Breder (1960) described a clear plastic fry trap, and Heard (1964) an emergent trap for salmonid alevins.
- [[Light Traps]] catch far more fish at night than unlighted traps. In one study (Nagiqé, 1975), mean nightly catches from four lighted traps reached 292 perch, 1,390 ruffe and 26 pike-perch on different occasions.
- Traps are generally not quantitative, and some species are not attracted to light.

**Purse seines and seines**

- Nellen and Schnack (1975) suggested a small-mesh [[Purse Seine]] as an alternative to townets in inland waters, since it needs little mechanical power.
- A plankton purse seine used for anchovy larvae (Murphy and Clutter, 1972) was 30 m long, 7 m deep, and took 15 to 20 minutes per haul. Comparisons showed a 1 m townet seriously underestimated larger larvae.
- Nagiqé (1975) used a shallower purse seine (27.5 m long, 3.5 m deep, 5 mm mesh) for pike-perch fry at night, with good results except on soft mud.

**Juvenile sampling**

- Juvenile chinook salmon were sampled by shore seines with variable mesh (Raymond and Collins, 1975).
- Floating self-cleaning traps worked in flows above 1.2 m/s, and "Merwin" traps in impoundments.
- Shearer (1975) used an angled fence trap on Atlantic salmon smolts, with Bailey's modification of Peterson's method for population estimates.
- In general, small fish are harder to catch by [[Electric Fishing]] than large ones. Young trout were caught below 2.6 cm, and young white bass were caught down to 48 mm using 230 V AC.

**Egg sampling methods**

- Semi-buoyant eggs can be sampled with a horizontally set plankton net from an anchored boat in current (Hass, 1968).
- The Nissinen salt-water sampler lifts eggs from the bottom into a collecting chamber on a salt-water flow, for estimating egg density in [[Vendace]] spawning grounds.
- Redd eggs can be sampled by digging and by drift nets, and eggs washed out of gravel were collected by drift nets (Elliott, 1976).
- Artificial substrates (tiles, rope, canvas strips) can give data on both egg production and mortality (Rothschild, 1961).

**Data handling**

- Larval precision improves with more sampling units of smaller size. Patchy distributions create a correlation between mean and variance, so a logarithmic or similar transformation is needed before analysis.
- No net samples all size classes of larvae in a fully non-selective way (Hempel, 1974).
