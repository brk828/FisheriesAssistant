# Tall Grass and Flowing Water: An Oral-Environmental History of Texas John Slaughter's San Bernardino Ranch

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Cochise County Parks Advisory Commission]] (December 1983), uses oral history to reconstruct environmental change at the [[San Bernardino Ranch]] in extreme southeastern Cochise County, Arizona. Five in-depth interviews with "old-timers" (September–November 1983) describe the ranch's "Heyday" (1900–1915) and its landscape, water resources, vegetation, animals and cultural features. The author, Richard V. Francaviglia, frames the evidence as "historical environmental perception" meant to supplement, not replace, scientific measurement. The main conclusions are that surface waters have been fairly stable, vegetation has changed most (loss of grassland and large cottonwoods, mesquite invasion), and the ranch's overall character has persisted. The report also records community debate over whether restoration should backdate the ranch or preserve its later character.

## Background & Description

The San Bernardino site was used as a Jesuit *visita*, a presidio used in the 1770s campaigns of [[Juan Bautista de Anza]] against the Apache, and a grant of 75,000 acres to Ignacio Perez in 1822. Perez was driven out in 1831. Following the [[Gadsden Purchase]] (1853) the ranch was split between American and Mexican portions. [[John Slaughter]] (Texas John Slaughter) bought the ranch in 1884, when most of its roughly 65,000 acres lay in Mexico. The present house was completed in 1892, and a U.S. Cavalry outpost was established there in 1915. Ownership later passed to the Williams family (1937), the Ramsowers (1968) and, in the late 1970s, to The Nature Conservancy, with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service acquiring most of the land.

The study covers the physical setting: a [[Volcanic Upland]] (the Mesa de la Avanzada, basaltic "malpai") and an [[Alluvial Lowland]] that slopes into Mexico, joined by [[Black Draw]], a major arroyo.

Interviewees included Ben Williams, Sr. (b. ca. 1902), Mary Quisenberry (b. ca. 1903), Ervin Bond (b. 1906), Helen Ramsower (b. 1925) and Eileen Taylor (b. 1920). Their remembrances mostly span 1913 to 1983, and the oldest interviewees were in their early 80s. Interviews averaged two and a half hours.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water resources.** The ranch depends on springs, [[Artesian Wells]], the intermittent Black Draw, man-made ponds and a [[Cienega]] (marsh). Williams described two groundwater types: warm artesian water from several hundred feet below the valley, and cool spring water issuing from the volcanic tableland. The artesian water in one well was encountered at about 360 feet, in a well drilled to 403 feet in "1942 or 1943." The main pond, the "Frog Pond," is fed by about seven springs and is roughly an acre in area, with depths of about ten feet.

**Vegetation.** Oral evidence describes three main changes: reduction of native grassland, invasion of [[Mesquite]] and other scrub, and decline of large cottonwoods. Interviewees also noted that the ranch's grasses depended strongly on rainfall.

**Animals.** The fauna was described as rich, with mountain lions, deer, rabbits, coyotes, skunks, quail, ducks (probably Mallards) and hawks mentioned. Beaver and muscrat were said never to occur on the ranch. Ringtailed cats and an opossum (reported from Cottonwood Canyon, c. 1915–1916) appear in the accounts, though the opossum record is unconfirmed in the scientific literature.

**Fish.** Interviewees called the small native fish "minnows," which proved to include rare native species, and larger introduced sport fish were stocked. Details are covered in Key Findings.

**Cultural landscape.** Chinese farmers, who lived on the ranch until about the 1920s, cultivated roughly 200 acres of fields, supplying vegetables and watercress to Douglas. Ranch structures include the adobe house, ice house, commissary, bath house (demolished 1945), a barn built in 1936–1937, and a small cemetery near well #10.

## Key Findings

- **Interview base:** Five in-depth interviews from an initial list of about fifteen names. Oral accounts were used to evaluate environmental change, with written sources (Lanning 1981, Bond 1982) cited as support.
- **Water stability:** Surface waters have been "fairly stable," though Black Draw is now less predictable and ephemeral, and recent flooding is affecting its bed. Williams reported that Black Draw "ran clear for six or nine months a year in the old days."
- **Well flows:** Williams stated that the artesian wells at the cienega have "never fluctuated significantly." Fluoride-low well #10 supplied the City of Douglas in the early 20th century.
- **Grassland loss:** All interviewees noted reduced productivity as grassland declined. Slaughter was first attracted by "tall grass and flowing water" in about 1884.
- **Mesquite invasion:** Stevens (reported c. 1969) commented on mesquite dominance; Quisenberry recalled mesquite and prickly pear in 1913–1916; Williams stated that mesquite "was always" present but less common in places.
- **Cottonwood decline:** Interviewees felt large cottonwoods were fewer than in the 1900s–1920s. Williams removed many dead cottonwoods in the 1930s.
- **Native fish:** The "minnows" were later identified (c. 1978) as rare native fishes, including [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Yaqui Chub]]. Lanning (1981) listed fish species on the ranch, including Largemouth Bass, Green Sunfish, Black Crappie, Gambusia, Carp, Goldfish and possibly [[Yaqui Catfish]].
- **Birds:** 158 bird species were recorded at the ranch in 1981 (Lanning 1981).
- **Mammals:** Interviewees reported a diverse mammal fauna, though beaver and muscrat were denied by all interviewees, and beaver was described as "probably occurred" by Lanning.
- **Preservation conflict:** Williams argued the ranch "shouldn't" change, and called efforts to return it to c. 1900 "useless." Taylor asked which date restoration should target. Removal of trees and vegetation around the porch and the draining of ponds to kill stocked fish were criticized.
- **Conclusion:** The report concludes that the ranch's character has persisted despite documentable change, and it recommends that preservation and restoration groups work together. It also recommends that the Parks Advisory Commission be kept informed of ongoing activities.
- **Approval:** The report was unanimously approved by the Cochise County Parks Advisory Commission on December 6, 1983.
