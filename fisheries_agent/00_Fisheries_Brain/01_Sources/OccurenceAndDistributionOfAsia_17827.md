# Occurrence and Distribution of Asiatic Clam, Corbicula leana, in Pearl River, Louisiana

#malacology #freshwater-biology #corbicula

## Executive Summary

This short paper by [[Gerald E. Gunning]] and [[Royal D. Suttkus]] of [[Tulane University]] reports the occurrence of the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] in the [[Pearl River]] system of Louisiana. Specimens first identified as [[Corbicula Fluminea]] were referred by a national museum specialist to *Corbicula leana*. Collections from two consecutive years (1963 and 1964) show that the clam was spreading into new reaches and increasing in numbers. Size-class analysis suggests the species had been established in the river since about 1959. The authors conclude that the clam is spreading rapidly through the Pearl River system.

## Background & Description

- **Introduction and range:** The Asiatic clam was introduced into the United States several years before this study. Earlier records covered rivers and sloughs in Washington, Oregon, and California, the [[Tennessee River]], and the [[Ohio River]] near Cincinnati.
- **Identification:** Using [[Pennak Freshwater Invertebrates]] (a key to pelecypod families and genera), 32 specimens from a Pearl River collection near Bogalusa were tentatively assigned to *C. fluminea*. Five were sent to Dr. J. P. E. Morrison of the [[United States National Museum]], who judged the determination correct under the existing literature but recommended the name *Corbicula leana* Prime, which he considered better supported (personal communication, March 26, 1964).
- **Study aim:** To document the rate of dispersal and increase in abundance of *Corbicula* over a one-year period.

**Methods**

- Small pelecypods were collected incidentally during fish sampling with a ten-foot, one-inch-mesh seine (see [[Seine Sampling]]).
- Additional samples came from hand-picking and sorting bottom material.
- Sampling was not strictly quantitative, but effort was kept similar among stations so that samples would be reasonably comparable.
- Valve measurements were recorded to the nearest 0.5 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Specimens were abundant in sand, gravel, silt, and mixtures of these substrates.
- **Study area:** Stations were distributed along the main Pearl River, the West Pearl River (including [[Wilson's Slough]]), and the East Pearl River. Station positions were given as river miles and landmarks.
- **Spread:** The authors interpret the pattern as dispersal into new areas of the river system, accompanied by rising numbers.
- **Population structure:** Size data indicate that the population was made up of several year-classes, showing that it was reproducing and persisting in the river rather than representing recent individual arrivals (see [[Year Class Analysis]]).

## Key Findings

1. **Dispersal and increase:** The clam was found at more stations in 1964 than in 1963. Large collections in 1964 came from stations 1, 2, 4, and 7, where none had been taken in 1963. Overall numbers rose between the two years, although the table totals are partly illegible in the source.
2. **Size range:** In the 1963 size series (Table 2), the smallest specimen measured 4.0 mm and the largest 28.0 mm total length. The largest specimen recorded anywhere in the river measured 37.0 mm and belonged to the 1959 year-class. Mean lengths by station ranged from roughly 9 to 16 mm, with standard deviations of 2 to 6 mm.
3. **Year-classes:** Four modal size groups (about 8, 14, 22, and 34 mm) were interpreted as four year-classes present in 1963, from 1959 through 1962. This suggests establishment in the Pearl River around 1959. The authors compare this with [[Keup et al. 1963]], who reported three year-classes in the Ohio River and an invasion there around 1960.
4. **Significance:** The authors describe the large numbers of the clam in the Pearl River as an unexpected find. They point to its rapid spread across the river system as shown by two successive years of collections.
5. **Future work:** Collections were continuing, with the aim of learning more about the species' ecology.

**Supporting references:** The paper cites six works: [[Pennak Freshwater Invertebrates]], [[Ingram 1959]] (California water supplies), [[Keup et al. 1963]] (Ohio River range extension), [[Sinclair and Ingram 1961]] (Tennessee River record), and two [[Sinclair and Isom]] reports on Tennessee (1961, 1963).
