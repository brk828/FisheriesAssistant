# Fisheries Habitat Dynamics in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 study by [[John G. Carter]], [[Richard A. Valdez]], [[Ronald J. Ryel]] and [[Vincent A. Lamarra]] (Ecosystems Research Institute, Logan, Utah) quantified how the surface area of fish habitat types in a two-mile reach of the [[Upper Colorado River]] near Parachute, Colorado changes with river discharge during 1983. The authors propose this habitat-flow approach as a tool for setting flow recommendations for [[Endangered Fishes]] in a basin where water development conflicts with fish needs.

## Background & Description

- **Basin context:** The [[Colorado River]] system drains about one-fifteenth of the United States and is largely arid to semi-arid, with rivers of drastically fluctuating flows, temperatures and turbidities. Its fishes show 73% endemism, the highest of any river basin in the United States (Miller 1961).
- **Listed species:** Three large river fishes are listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act of 1973: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*). The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a candidate species. Four of these are protected as endangered by the State of Colorado. Nine other native species occur in the upper basin, along with exotic species (Tyus et al. 1982).
- **Causes of decline:** Dams and habitat modification, water withdrawals, and introduction of non-native fishes are generally recognized as the main factors.
- **Conflict:** Resolving conflicts between endangered fishes and development is difficult given many interests (federal and state agencies, municipalities, industry, environmental groups) and uncertainty about the relative contribution of each activity.
- **Study approach:** The authors note that in the absence of definitive quantitative fisheries data, a multifaceted approach is needed. One element is linking river flows to habitats needed at critical life-history stages. The assumption is that habitat implies fish presence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** The reach was judged representative of the upper Colorado River between Rifle (river-mile 241) and De Beque (river-mile 210), based on consistent gradient, bed material and meander wavelength (Ruhe 1975). Colorado Squawfish are not found there, and Razorback Sucker occur in low numbers. The reach was considered representative of downstream areas that support endangered fishes.

**Methods:**
- Detailed maps at a scale of 1 inch = 200 feet with 2-foot contours were built from aerial photo interpretation.
- Habitats were mapped on 3 March 1983 and weekly from 6 June to 2 September 1983.
- Twelve habitat categories were used: [[Backwaters]], eddies, pools, isolated pools, embayments, concavities, riffles, runs, rapids, rubble flats, shorelines and [[Flooded Woodlands]].
- Dimensions were visually estimated and measured from maps. Habitat area was correlated with discharge from [[USGS Station 09093700]] near De Beque.

**Hydrology:** Flows ranged from 1,710 to 28,300 cfs during the six-month period. The peak occurred on 26 June and the minimum on 3 March.

## Key Findings

1. **Habitat responses to flow:** Seven of twelve habitats changed predictably with discharge: backwaters, flooded woodlands, rapids, riffles, runs, eddies and rubble flats. Embayments, concavities, pools, shorelines and isolated pools were not directly related to discharge.
2. **Backwaters:** This was one of the most dramatic relationships, with two thresholds. Below about 7,000 cfs, backwaters were small, discrete units in shoreline depressions. Around 10,000 cfs, a nearly continuous band of backwaters formed. Backwaters were fewer but larger at high flows, and were usually warmer than the main channel. Large backwaters may serve as resting, maturation and spawning areas during the descending limb of runoff, and as nursery habitat for young fish.
3. **Flooded woodlands:** Area increased sharply at about 10,000 cfs. This habitat appears important for small fish spawning and as shelter during runoff.
4. **Rapids, runs and riffles:** Rapids appeared only above 10,000 cfs. Riffle area decreased with discharge. Runs and riffles are important habitat for suckers and dace.
5. **Eddies:** Eddy area increased directly with discharge. Eddies provide food and habitat for chubs and suckers. Roundtail Chub are abundant along eddy fences.
6. **Shorelines and rubble flats:** Shoreline area stayed relatively constant, and shorelines likely support most riverine fish populations (Hynes 1970). Rubble flats occurred only between about 5,000 and 12,000 cfs and are important for speckled dace and young suckers.
7. **Isolated pools:** These formed at most flows as low-lying areas lost river contact. Valdez et al. (1982) found many non-native fishes but few native fishes stranded in isolated pools, suggesting native fishes leave backwaters before becoming isolated.
8. **Total habitat dynamics:** Total potential fish habitat peaked during high flow in late June and declined to a minimum from late July through early September. The authors argue that peak-flow habitat was largely deep midchannel runs and rapids that forced fish into marginal shoreline habitats, due to instability and high competition and predation. A marked transition from a turbulent, silt-laden river to a more confined, moderate-flow river occurs near Parachute at about 10,000 cfs.
9. **Spawning hypothesis:** Colorado Squawfish are believed to spawn in riffles at the upper end of resting pools, similar to Northern Squawfish in the Columbia River. Radiotelemetry studies support the use of this habitat for staging and resting during spawning (Archer and Tyus 1984).
10. **Management implications:** Similar patterns are likely in comparable reaches, but relationships may differ with local channel morphology and land use. The method could help managers predict habitat for endangered fishes and assess consequences of flow decisions. Its use depends on better knowledge of the habitat needs of the endangered fishes and of the essential river reaches for critical life stages.
