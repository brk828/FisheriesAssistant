# Examination of Native Fish Recruitment and Fish Communities of the San Juan and Colorado River Interface Zones of Lake Powell, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Open-File Report (multiagency: USGS, Bureau of Reclamation, Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, National Park Service) documents fish communities sampled in the reservoir/river interface zones of the [[Colorado River]] and [[San Juan River]] arms of [[Lake Powell]] during 1999 and 2000. The study had three objectives: (1) search for young [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and PIT-tag any found; (2) evaluate collection techniques for juvenile native fish; and (3) describe fish communities in these transient, migratory habitats.

Nearly 40,000 fish were captured, representing 23 species. Nonnative fishes made up about 99.4% of the sample, and natives only 0.6%. The authors conclude that nonnative predation has effectively eliminated native fish recruitment from the San Juan flood plain, so the persistence of native fishes depends on upstream stocking.

## Background & Description

The interface zone ("inflow area") advances and retreats with reservoir elevation. Spring runoff flooding can extend the reservoir upstream into tributary arms, inundating flood plain habitat. The authors note that these shallow, nutrient-rich deltas are highly productive and had been proposed as nurseries for native fishes. Historical sampling had yielded only a few wild adult [[Razorback Sucker]] from these areas.

Sampling used seven gear types: [[Trammel Net]]s (small- and large-mesh), [[Electrofishing]], [[Hoop Net]]s, [[Minnow Trap]]s, [[Crab Trap]]s, [[Light Trap]]s for larval fish, and [[Seining]]. Acoustic surveys used a [[Scientific Echosounder]] to estimate pelagic fish density. Sampling occurred at the San Juan inflow (RM 48–56), the Colorado River inflow (between Imperial Rapids and Farley Canyon), and a reservoir control site at [[Spencer's Camp]] (RM 38–39.5).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reservoir elevation:** Lake Powell fluctuated 5.3 m during the study. The low was about 1,120.8 m (April 1999) and the peak about 1,126.1 m. In 2000 the peak was only 1,122.7 m.
- **Flooding:** Over 1,000 ha of bottomland at Piute Farms were flooded and drained in 1999, creating about 30,000,000 m³ of new habitat. Flooding lasted nearly two months, roughly twice the volume-duration of historic extremes as reported by the authors.
- **Hydrology:** 1999 runoff was 89% of average on the Colorado River and 110% on the San Juan. 2000 runoff was well below normal (72% on the Colorado).
- **Sediment and habitat:** Delta building and tamarisk encroachment were isolating side canyons and backwaters. The authors expect these to fill in and cease functioning as nurseries. Colorado River inflow was confined by steep walls in Narrow Canyon, with little backwater habitat.
- **Control site:** Spencer's Camp was a steep, deep, clear reservoir basin with maximum depth of 30–40 m and little vegetation, indicating more stable conditions.

## Key Findings

**Sampling totals and native status**
- Nearly 40,000 fish were captured, representing 23 species (18 nonnative, 5 native).
- Nonnatives made up 99.4% of the passive-netting and electrofishing sample. Natives made up 0.6% of all fish collected.
- 13 adult [[Razorback Sucker]] were captured. All were believed to be hatchery-stocked. No juvenile razorback suckers were found.
- Three juvenile [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and one small [[Gila]] chub were collected.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] was the most common large native (41 fish, 0.4%).
- [[White Crappie]] and [[Gizzard Shad]] were recorded for the first time in Lake Powell, both in the San Juan inflow. The authors attribute these to probable unintentional stocking or escape.

**Community composition and habitat**
- The most common fishes were red shiner, threadfin shad, common carp, striped bass, and channel catfish.
- Inflow areas had higher relative abundance than the reservoir control. Common carp and bullheads were about twice as abundant in inflow habitats, while smallmouth bass and green sunfish were more abundant in the reservoir.
- Adult striped bass and walleye were more common in channel habitat. Broad flood plain habitat supported more black crappie and juvenile fish.
- The community was more indicative of the Mississippi River basin: 63% of the 23 species were from that basin.

**Sampling efficiency**
- No single gear was effective for any species. Success depended more on life stage.
- Most adult flannelmouth suckers (36, or 86%) and razorback suckers (11 of 13, or 92%) were taken by [[Trammel Net]]s.
- [[Light Trap]]s initially used 10 mm entrances. Predators entered, so they were reduced to 5 mm in 2000. Two larval razorback suckers were captured in 2000.
- The authors believe electrofishing underestimated numbers in turbid inflow waters.

**Pelagic production**
- Maximum post-spawning densities reached about 190,538 fish/ha (1999) and 71,485 fish/ha (2000) at the inflow. Most were believed to be threadfin shad.
- In 1999 densities over 100,000 fish/ha extended from RM 50 to about RM 44. In 2000 densities were about one-third of this and extended only about 3 km.
- Shoreline densities reportedly exceeded 220,000 fish/ha in the interface zone.

**Tagging and health**
- Only one Colorado pikeminnow and two razorback suckers were PIT-tagged. One stocked razorback had traveled about 562 km from the upper Green River (RM 319) to Sheep Canyon, where it died with a heavy [[Learnea]] infestation.
- Fish appeared generally healthy. Skin lesions on some fish were associated with spawning. Dead striped bass were common in summer.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- The authors conclude that nonnative predators, including red shiners, ate native larvae and early life stages, effectively eliminating native recruitment in this portion of the basin. Their evidence is the absence of subadult natives and the lack of juvenile razorback suckers.
- Continued persistence of razorback sucker and Colorado pikeminnow in the reservoir depends on upstream stocking.
- The authors state that natural razorback recruitment would likely require total removal of nonnative fishes. This conflicts with recreational fishery goals, so they argue that restoration efforts without such removal may waste time, funding, and labor.
- Interface zones are highly productive, supporting one of the most valuable recreational fisheries in the western United States.
- The authors also raise concern about the potential spread of gizzard shad through the Colorado River basin.
