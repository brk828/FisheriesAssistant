# Pools in Desert Streams: Limnology and Response to Disturbance

#periphyton #limnology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1992 study from the [[Arizona State University]] Department of Zoology examines the limnology of [[Pools]] in [[Sycamore Creek]], a low desert stream in Arizona, and compares how pools and [[Riffles]] resist and recover from a flood-deposited silt event. The work covers June through October 1989 at the [[Round Valley Study Site]]. Pools stratified thermally and chemically on a diel basis. Algal ([[Periphyton]]) responses to silting were similar in pools and riffles, but [[Macrophytes]], which were concentrated in riffles, were more resistant and resilient than algae. Overall, riffles were more stable than pools under a silting flood. The authors conclude that silting floods are prolonged disturbances because recovery is delayed, unlike [[Scouring Floods]].

No fish were studied. The subject is the physical, chemical and primary-producer response of a desert stream ecosystem.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** Sycamore Creek (elevation 707 m) is a low desert tributary of the [[Verde River]], located 32 km northeast of Phoenix. It drains a watershed of 505 km². Riparian trees are restricted to high-water margins, and [[Seepwillow]] (*Baccharis salicifolia*) rarely exceeds 3 m, so stream shading is limited. High evaporation and low precipitation make the stream spatially intermittent. Perennial flow at Round Valley results from impermeable bedrock that forces [[Groundwater]] to the surface.

**Reach types.** Riffles (shallow water over cobble) and runs (shallow water over sand) each make up about 45% of stream area. The remainder consists of slow-flowing pools over bedrock and sand. Relative reach proportions shift with large floods and as the stream dries, when pools expand in areal extent.

**Conceptual framework.** The authors use [[Stability Theory]] as their framework for disturbance response:
- **[[Resistance]]** is the minimal response to disturbance, measured as percent decrease in a state variable across the disturbance.
- **[[Resilience]]** is the rate of recovery to predisturbance levels, measured as the slope of recovery over time.
- **[[Disturbance]]** is defined as an event that kills organisms or removes them, changing the ratio of biota to resources (Sousa 1984; Pickett and White 1985).

Streams have generally been characterized as having low resistance and high resilience (Webster et al. 1983; Grimm and Fisher 1989). Pools were previously understudied relative to riffles and runs, which had been the focus of earlier work (Fisher et al. 1982; Grimm 1987; Fisher and Grimm 1988; Grimm and Fisher 1989).

**Methods (summary).**
- **Thermal and oxygen profiles:** Taken at three pools on four dates (11 June, 13 July, 2 August, 19 September 1989), plus a 24-hour profile in one pool on 28 October. Temperature was measured at 5 cm intervals. Oxygen was measured by [[Winkler Titration]] (azide-modified).
- **Light:** Vertical extinction was measured at 5 cm intervals with a [[Protomatic Underwater Photometer]].
- **Standing crop:** Algal chlorophyll a and [[Ash-Free Dry Mass]] (AFDM) were measured by patch type, using five 26 cm² cores per patch. Macrophyte dry weight was measured from five 80 cm² cores per patch. Values were patch-weighted to describe each reach.
- **Sampling schedule:** Preflood data came from pools on 44, 32 and 10 days before the flood, and from riffles on one date 10 days before. Postflood data came from both reach types on Days 5, 8, 14, 48 and 69.
- **Metrics:** Resistance was the percent change in chlorophyll a between preflood and postflood samples. Resilience was the slope of the regression of log-transformed chlorophyll a against days since flood.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal and chemical stratification.** Pool water stratified vertically. Average temperature declined 2.2°C with depth, and average oxygen declined 2 mg/L (abstract). Results section reports an average oxygen difference of 2.3 mg/L between epilimnetic and hypolimnetic water across all dates and locations. The largest differences were at deeper transects, with declines of 5.5°C and 6 mg/L within 1 m depth. Stratification persisted through the day despite water turnover times of 1.5 to 3.6 hours. It broke down at night.

**Oxygen dynamics.** Photosynthesis in the system is benthic, so daytime surface oxygen was attributed to upstream riffles. Bottom-water oxygen was lowest during the day, which the authors interpret as respiration exceeding photosynthesis in sediments even in the light.

**Flow model.** Water entering pools from riffles flows along the surface over a cooler, isolated [[Hypolimnion]], which differs from the bottom-flowing pattern described by [[Minckley 1963]] for [[Doe Run Kentucky]] pools. The authors attribute this to canopy differences: desert streams are open and pools are often canyon-shaded, while mesic streams are shaded by riparian canopy and pools open a gap that admits more sun.

**Silting flood (21 July 1989).** A small flood (maximum discharge < 1.0 m³/s) deposited an average of 9 cm of fine silt across the reach.
- Benthic periphyton in pools was still green on Day 8 but was indistinguishable by Day 14.
- Only remnants of the previously dominant alga [[Cladophora]] remained in riffles.
- Riffle macrophytes were coated in silt but otherwise intact.
- Turbidity cleared in riffles by Day 12, but pools stayed turbid for six weeks.
- Silt resuspended easily under turbulence, including disturbance by cattle.
- The silting event was confined to the Round Valley reach because of intermittency and did not affect downstream sites.

**Light penetration.** The vertical extinction coefficient at a silted pool was 5.61, compared with 0.54 at an unflooded downstream pool. These values correspond to 50% light removal at 11.5 cm and 150.0 cm, respectively. The authors argue that silt could limit benthic production.

**Morphological change.** Deposition was greater in pools than riffles, concentrated in former deepest areas. Within pools:
- Maximum depth decreased an average of 27%.
- Surface area decreased an average of 37%.
- Volume decreased an average of 8%.

The authors note that scouring floods may enlarge pools, whereas silting floods reduce their size.

**Flood regime.** Over three years, Sycamore Creek experienced 34 flash floods. Eleven were large floods with major geomorphic change, and four were silting floods with peak discharge < 1 m³/s. The authors consider silting events frequent enough to be a regular disturbance, not a rare one.

## Key Findings

1. **Pools stratify diel-thermally and chemically.** Surface-to-bottom temperature differed by an average of 2.2°C, and oxygen differed by 2 to 2.3 mg/L, depending on how the figure is reported. Stratification broke down at night.

2. **Pools and riffles did not differ in algal resistance.** Both averaged a 74% reduction in areal chlorophyll a after the silting flood. Algal recovery was delayed in both reach types, and algal growth was suppressed for at least 48 days. Because of this delay, a direct comparison of algal resilience could not be made.

3. **Macrophytes were more resistant and resilient than algae.** Macrophyte standing crop did not change significantly during the flood, and significant increases were observed by 48 days post-flood. Macrophytes occurred significantly only in riffles.

4. **Overall stability favors riffles.** When algae and macrophytes are considered together, riffles were more resistant and resilient than pools. The authors argue that differential distribution of components, not any single component, drives differences in stability between reach types.

5. **Benthic AFDM increased about an order of magnitude in pools after silting.** This reflected the organic-rich silt, and it stayed high throughout the study.

6. **Silting floods are prolonged disturbances.** Unlike [[Scouring Floods]] that remove biota and leave clear water, after which recovery starts immediately, silting floods delay recovery. Grimm and Fisher (1989) reported an average of 21 days to predisturbance algal chlorophyll after summer flash floods. The authors argue that the sediment a flood deposits may matter as much as its magnitude.

7. **Silting floods are a regular feature of this system.** Four of 34 flash floods over three years were silting events, so the authors do not regard them as rare.

8. **Study scope and funding.** The work was funded by NSF grant BSR88-18612. The study covered three pools and three riffles over June to October 1989.
