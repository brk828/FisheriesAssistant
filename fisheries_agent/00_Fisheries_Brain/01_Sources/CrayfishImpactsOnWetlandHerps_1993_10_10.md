## Executive Summary

This grant proposal (Fernandez & Rosen, 1993) documents the ecological impacts of introduced [[Crayfish]] (genus *Orconectes*) on Arizona's native wetland and riparian vertebrate communities. While the primary focus is herpetofauna—[[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] (*Rana chiricahuensis*), [[Northern Leopard Frog]] (*Rana pipiens*), [[Mexican Garter Snake]] (*Thamnophis eques*), [[Narrow-Headed Garter Snake]] (*Thamnophis rufipunctatus*), and [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon sonoriense*)—the authors explicitly identify native fish, including the threatened [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*), as additional species jeopardized by crayfish-driven habitat and food-web alteration. The paper frames crayfish as an underappreciated exotic predator and ecosystem engineer causing devegetation, invertebrate decline, bank erosion, and siltation across Arizona's aquatic habitats, with cascading effects on multiple native taxa including fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Introduced Crayfish (*Orconectes* spp.)** occupy a dual trophic role: initially described by prior literature (Pennak, 1989) as benign omnivores and scavengers feeding mainly on aquatic vegetation, but the authors argue this characterization is misleading. Observed and laboratory behavior indicates crayfish:
  - Consume aquatic vegetation used by invertebrates, anurans, and reptiles for cover and food (devegetation).
  - Drastically reduce macroinvertebrate (aquatic insect and mollusk) populations that serve as **primary food for native turtles and fish**.
  - Directly attack and consume tadpoles, newly metamorphosed frogs, and injure adult and larval herpetofauna.
  - Burrow into banks, causing erosion, increased turbidity, and stream-bed siltation — degrading habitat quality for species dependent on complex littoral zone structure, which would include lotic fish habitat.

- **Native fish** are positioned within the broader food web as competitors and co-victims of crayfish invasion: crayfish are noted to "displace aquatic insects and mollusks that are the primary food for native turtles **and fish**," implicating bottom-up food base disruption for fish populations, not just direct predation.

- **Habitat context**: Streams in the White Mountains that were previously "crystal clear" with diverse aquatic vegetation were observed to become "murky and barren of vegetation" over a decade coincident with crayfish spread — a habitat trajectory with direct relevance to salmonid (Apache trout) and other native fish persistence.

- Cross-reference to established literature: [[Minckley And Rinne 1991]] cited regarding introduced predator impacts on native Arizona fish; this proposal extends that concern to crayfish as an additional and underrecognized invasive stressor alongside non-native predatory fish and bullfrogs.

## Key Findings

- Crayfish (*Orconectes* spp.) are **non-native to Arizona** (Pennak, 1989) and have undergone explosive, human-assisted range expansion across the state's aquatic habitats (White Mountains, Coconino Plateau, Tonto Basin, Sycamore Creek).
- Prior ecological characterization of crayfish as harmless omnivorous scavengers or beneficial food-base additions for game fish is challenged by the authors' field and laboratory observations.
- Crayfish invasion correlates with:
  - Elimination and decline of northern leopard frog populations (White Mountains, last decade).
  - Failed recruitment of hatchling mud turtles.
  - Inverse abundance relationship with narrow-headed garter snakes (Eagle Creek; [[Rosen 1987]]).
- **Native fishes are explicitly flagged as impacted**, both through direct habitat degradation (turbidity, siltation, vegetation loss) and indirect food-web effects (loss of aquatic insect and mollusk prey base). [[Apache Trout]] is specifically named as a threatened species due to crayfish-driven habitat and food base alteration.
- Proposed methodology includes Surber stream-bottom sampling and standardized minnow trapping protocols to quantify relative abundance of crayfish, native invertebrates, vegetation, and anuran larvae — methods with direct applicability to fish community monitoring.
- The study proposes testing whether flash flooding provides natural regulation of crayfish populations in Arizona riparian corridors, with implications for long-term recovery potential of native fish and herpetofauna habitats.
- Broader significance: the proposal argues that crayfish-mediated disruption of aquatic food webs has been an underattended threat to native aquatic vertebrate biodiversity in Arizona, extending beyond the herpetofauna focus to encompass native fish conservation concerns.
