# Allocating Fresh Water to Aquatic Ecosystems: The Case of the Colorado River Delta

#aquatic-ecosystems #river-management #delta-restoration #water-allocation #conservation #endangered-species #wetlands

## Executive Summary

This article examines the ecological degradation of the [[Colorado River Delta]] and proposes strategies for reallocating water to restore and protect this critical aquatic ecosystem. The [[Colorado River]] supplies water to nearly 30 million people and irrigates over 1 million hectares of farmland across seven U.S. states and México. However, legal water entitlements exceed the river's average annual flow by approximately 10 percent, and virtually no water reaches the delta in an average year since completion of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in the early 1960s. The authors demonstrate that a combination of policy reforms, economic incentives, and efficiency improvements in irrigation could free up substantial water supplies for delta restoration while maintaining regional economic productivity. Three critical wetland areas are identified as high-priority conservation sites: the [[Ciénega de Santa Clara]] (20,250 hectares), the [[Rio Hardy]] wetlands, and the [[El Doctor]] wetlands.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] flows 2,300 kilometers from the Rocky Mountains to the [[Sea of Cortez]], controlled by over 30 dams. The [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922 and the United States-México Water Treaty of 1944 divided the river's flow without explicitly reserving water for ecological functions. Total legal entitlements amount to approximately 16.5 million acre-feet (maf), exceeding the river's long-term average annual flow at [[Lee Ferry]], Arizona by about 10 percent. Current annual consumptive water use totals approximately 14.4 maf, roughly 85 percent of the basin's 16.9 maf flow.

Prior to major dam construction, the delta supported 200-400 plant species and numerous birds, fish, and mammals. The river historically transported between 45 and 455 million metric tons of silt through the [[Grand Canyon]] annually, with 20-30 percent deposited in the floodplain and delta. Since the 1960s, the delta has been severely degraded, with approximately 250,000 hectares of delta land remaining at its southern end, now consisting primarily of mud flats, salt flats, and dry sand.

The [[Cocopa Indians]], traditional fishers and flood-recession farmers who numbered approximately 5,000 four centuries ago, have been devastated by the loss of freshwater flows. They last harvested their staple grain, nipa ([[Distichlis palmeri]]), in the early 1950s. Today, only 40-50 Cocopa families remain south of the border.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reduction in freshwater flow has severely impacted the [[Sea of Cortez]], one of the world's most productive marine ecosystems. Nutrient influx has decreased, reducing critical nursery habitat for fisheries. Shrimp fishery catches have dropped steeply, and other fisheries are in decline. Local fisherfolk report that the "golden days" occurred after the 1983 flood year, when massive snowmelt caused unprecedented flows, and again briefly after January 1993 flood flows.

Numerous species dependent on the lower Colorado-upper Sea of Cortez ecosystem are now threatened or endangered, including:
- [[Green Sea Turtle]]
- [[Yuma Clapper Rail]]
- [[Desert Pupfish]]
- [[Vaquita]] (world's smallest porpoise and most endangered sea mammal, population estimated at only a few hundred)
- [[Totoaba]] (steel-blue fish growing to 2 meters length and 135 kilograms, formerly supporting popular sports and commercial fishery, now near extinction)

Three critical wetland areas remain:

1. **[[Ciénega de Santa Clara]]**: Largest remaining wetland at approximately 20,250 hectares, located in the eastern delta. Major stopover point for migratory birds along the [[Pacific Flyway]]. Species documented include [[Virginia Rails]], [[Least Bitterns]], [[American Coots]], dowitchers, [[White-faced Ibis]], [[Green-backed Heron]], and [[Black-crowned Heron]]. May contain the largest remaining populations of endangered [[Yuma Clapper Rails]] and [[Desert Pupfish]]. Originally covered only 200 hectares in the early 1970s, fed by artesian springs and agricultural drainage. Expanded dramatically after 1977 when the M.O.D.E. (Main Outlet Drain Extension) drain was constructed, channeling drainage from the [[Wellton-Mohawk Irrigation District]] in Arizona. Currently receives approximately 100,000-130,000 af/year of drainage water, but this supply is tenuous.

2. **[[Rio Hardy]] wetlands**: Located on the western side of the delta where the [[Hardy River]] joins the Colorado. Historically described as a gallery forest of cottonwoods and willow. Area peaked at approximately 66,400 hectares after 1983 flooding. For 35 years prior to 1983, sustained by water backed up behind a natural dam, spanning approximately 18,200 hectares. After the 1983 floods destroyed the natural dam, area dropped as low as 1,200 hectares, expanding only during high flood periods.

3. **[[El Doctor]] wetlands**: Named for a small settlement on the northeastern delta. Supported by artesian springs ("pozos") with lower salinity, resulting in greater plant species diversity. Cover approximately 500-700 hectares and have remained fairly stable over two decades, though subject to overgrazing by local cattle.

## Key Findings

**Near-Term Restoration Priorities:**
- Secure water supply for [[Ciénega de Santa Clara]] through Bureau of Reclamation commitment to maintain agricultural drainage flows of sufficient quantity and quality, or provide alternative water source if Yuma desalination plant operates
- Reconstruct structure to recreate natural earthen dam effect for [[Rio Hardy]] wetland (estimated cost: $250,000)
- Redirect agricultural drainage and urban wastewater from [[New River]] (currently emptying into [[Salton Sea]]) toward delta
- Channel treated wastewater from planned Mexicali treatment plant into [[Rio Hardy]]

**Policy and Institutional Options for Water Reallocation:**

1. **Water Surcharges**: Institute surcharge on water used, transferred, and/or banked in Colorado River system. Example: $2 per acre-foot surcharge would generate over $21 million annually at current U.S. consumption levels. Alternative: 10 percent surcharge on voluntary transfers (100,000 af transferred would generate 10,000 af for ecosystem). Third option: surcharge on banked water (5 percent annual surcharge on Metropolitan Water District's 185,000 af in [[Lake Mead]] would generate 9,000 af).

2. **Amend Law of the River**: Allocate water to river system itself, with each basin state contributing proportionally to current entitlements. Example: if 3 percent (500,000 af) devoted to habitat preservation, upper basin states would contribute 225,000 af/year, California 135,000 af/year, Arizona 85,000 af/year, Nevada 9,000 af/year, and México 45,000 af/year.

3. **Redefine "Normal" and "Surplus" Flow Conditions**: Bureau of Reclamation currently releases only minimum required amount from [[Lake Powell]] (8.23 maf) in most years. Redefining surplus/shortage conditions could explicitly redirect water toward stressed aquatic ecosystems.

**Conservation and Efficiency Potential in Arizona Agriculture:**

- 90 percent of irrigated acreage watered by surface/gravity methods prone to substantial evaporation losses
- Only 1.3 percent under drip irrigation
- **Estimated water savings of 445,000 af/year** possible by: placing half of irrigated cotton and major vegetable/citrus crops under drip irrigation (reducing consumptive losses from 30 percent to 5 percent), and upgrading half of alfalfa, wheat, and barley to surge, LEPA, or other methods (reducing losses from 30 percent to 15 percent)
- **Additional savings of 362,000 af/year** possible by shifting one-quarter of cotton and alfalfa areas to higher-valued citrus and vegetable crops

**Water Productivity Analysis (Arizona, 1994):**
- Cotton: 1,604,900 af consumed, $192 per af economic value
- Alfalfa hay: 1,287,500 af consumed, $95 per af economic value
- Lettuce: 55,400 af consumed, $3,316 per af economic value
- Cantaloupe: 29,300 af consumed, $1,752 per af economic value
- Broccoli: 20,000 af consumed, $1,091 per af economic value
- Cauliflower: 11,400 af consumed, $1,820 per af economic value

Cotton and alfalfa together consumed approximately 2.9 maf in Arizona in 1994, with water productivity many times lower than vegetables and citrus.

**Economic Benefits of Restoration:**
- Ecotourism potential: [[Ciénega de Santa Clara]] already attracting birdwatchers and naturalists; local residents developing small enterprises
- Sport fishing, kayaking, and boating could generate income for local communities
- Revitalized [[Totoaba]] population could draw anglers from Los Angeles, Phoenix, San Diego, Tijuana, and Tucson
- Commercial shrimping and fishing industries could revive
- Expanded waterfowl and wildlife habitat could support ecotourism while protecting biodiversity

**Research Needs:**
- Bi-national team of scientists should design and study experimental freshwater releases through delta, similar to March 1996 experimental flood releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]
- Determine whether flood pulse every few years is sufficient or whether minimum annual/seasonal flow needed
- Assess beneficial impacts of particular quantities and schedules of increased freshwater flows on fisheries, birds, wildlife, endangered species, and regional economy

**Policy Tools for Implementation:**
- Properly designed pricing structures
- Low-interest loans for efficiency-enhancing capital improvements
- Groundwater depletion taxes
- Voluntary water transfers to higher-valued uses
- Efficiency standards for agricultural and industrial water uses (U.S. Congress established standards for residential toilets, faucets, and showerheads effective January 1994)

The authors conclude that creative mix of policy reforms, economic incentives, and efficiency investments could free up substantial water quantities for ecosystem restoration, representing an important test of society's ability to share water with the environment that supports it. Success requires cooperative, binational effort and formal agreement between United States and México.
