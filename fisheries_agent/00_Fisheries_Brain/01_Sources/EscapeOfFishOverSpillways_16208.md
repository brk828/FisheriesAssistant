# Escape of Fish over Spillways in Southern Illinois Impoundments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 study by [[Darrell Louder]] (Cooperative Fisheries Research Laboratory, Southern Illinois University) tested whether fish are significantly lost over the spillways of lakes. Wire-netting traps were rigged on the spillways of two artificial lakes in southern Illinois to strain the overflow and capture escaping fish. Over two observation periods, the traps caught 665 fish at [[Little Grassy Lake]] and 10,182 fish at [[Lake Murphysboro]]. Losses were dominated by [[Largemouth Bass]] and, at Little Grassy, [[Gizzard Shad]]. The author concluded that large numbers of fish, including game fish, are lost during flooding, and recommended screening spillways.

## Background & Description

The controversy addressed by the study concerned whether spillway losses are ecologically or managerially significant, and which sizes and species are affected. Louder designed the study to gather direct evidence by capturing fish that passed over the spillways.

**Study lakes**

- **[[Little Grassy Lake]]:** An impoundment about 10 miles southeast of Carbondale. At spillway elevation it covers about 1,200 surface acres, with a maximum depth of 60 feet.
- **[[Lake Murphysboro]]:** An impoundment about 2 miles northwest of Murphysboro. At spillway elevation it covers about 160 surface acres, with a maximum depth of 30 feet.

Both lakes receive water from surface runoff, have concrete spillways, and overflow only during rainy seasons.

**Fish communities (from earlier surveys)**

- **Little Grassy Lake:** 14 species collected by electrofishing, wire baskets and gill nets (earlier survey by Walker, 1951). Most abundant in probable order: [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Common Carp]], Longear Sunfish, [[Bluegill]], [[White Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]], Black Crappie, Green Sunfish, Spotted Sucker, Yellow Bullhead, Black Bullhead, Warmouth, [[Golden Shiner]], and Mud Pickerel.
- **Lake Murphysboro:** 12 species collected by electrofishing and wire baskets. Most abundant in probable order: Bluegill, Largemouth Bass, [[Redear Sunfish]], White Crappie, Warmouth, Green Sunfish, Yellow Bullhead, [[Channel Catfish]], Western Lake Chubsucker, Stoneroller, Bluntnose Minnow, and Golden Shiner.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling method.** Escaping fish were captured in [[Seine-Line Trap|seine-line traps]] made of wire netting, secured at the bottom by nails in the spillway expansion joints and held upright against the current by guy wires.

- **Little Grassy Lake:** 1-inch wire netting; net 40 feet long; strained 41 percent of the spillway flow. Catches were multiplied by 2.5 to estimate total losses. Because of the mesh size, small fish were not captured.
- **Lake Murphysboro:** 0.25-inch (1/4-inch) hardware cloth; net 24 feet long; strained 33 percent of the flow. Catches were multiplied by 3.0 to estimate total losses.

Nets stayed in place for the whole study and were cleaned after rain. Observers recorded overflow depth, duration of overflow, number of each species, and size of each fish.

**Environmental drivers.** Losses were recorded during overflow periods, which occur only in rainy seasons. The author found that loss was not closely tied to the depth of water over the spillway. Season, particularly spring, appeared more important.

## Key Findings

**Little Grassy Lake (5-month observation, Dec 1954 to Apr 1955)**

- Total of 665 fish passed over the spillway during 885 hours of overflow (Table 1, 41 percent strained).
- [[Gizzard Shad]] (adults) made up 46 percent of the catch.
- [[Common Carp]] (14 percent), [[Largemouth Bass]] (11 percent), Spotted Sucker (9 percent), and [[Bluegill]] (8 percent) were about equally represented among the remainder.
- The largest loss occurred in April 1955.

**Lake Murphysboro (23-month observation, May 1954 to Apr 1956)**

- Total of 10,182 fish passed over the spillway during 1,737 hours of overflow (Table 2, 33 percent strained).
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (6 to 12 inches) formed 53 percent of the total catch. Of 2,032 largemouth bass taken, 1,811 were 6 to 12 inches long.
- Adult [[Redear Sunfish]] (15 percent), young Bluegill (11 percent), and young Green Sunfish (7 percent) followed.
- The largest losses occurred in June 1954 and April 1955.
- Summary totals: first year, 546 largemouth bass (1 to 5 inches) and 840 largemouth bass (6 to 12 inches), plus 342 redear sunfish and bluegill (about one-third catchable size). Second year, 108 largemouth bass (1 to 5 inches) and 4,218 largemouth bass (6 to 12 inches), plus 42 redear sunfish and bluegill (half catchable size).

**Discussion points**

1. **Species affected:** In Lake Murphysboro, four of the five dominant species appeared in the escapement. [[White Crappie]], the fifth dominant species, did not occur in the catch. A large Ohio impoundment (Clark, 1942) showed high crappie losses, so the pattern is not consistent across systems.
2. **Significance:** About 10,000 fish, mostly adults, passed over a 160-acre lake in 23 months. The author considered this significant, although the loss might be partly beneficial.
3. **Sizes lost:** Adults predominated in the Lake Murphysboro catch. The Little Grassy net mesh excluded small fish, so size data there are limited.
4. **Conditions:** Losses were erratic and seasonal, with heavy losses in spring. Louder suggested this erratic pattern explains the long-running controversy.

**Management conclusions**

- Fish are lost in large numbers over spillways during flooding. All sizes and most species are affected.
- Louder recommended installing a wire screen, set in a foot or more of water, in front of spillways to prevent loss of game fish. He noted that game fish passing over spillways could sometimes enrich populations downstream.
- A spillway trap is a convenient source of brood-size fish for stocking, and many fish in this study were used that way (see [[Fish Stocking]]).
- Heavy losses occurred over short periods, and their timing was linked to season rather than to the magnitude of overflow.

**Method note:** Capture data were gathered with [[Seine-Line Trap|spillway traps]] and compared against lake populations surveyed earlier with [[Electrofishing]] and gill nets. Totals were scaled up using the strained-flow fraction (multipliers 2.5 and 3.0).
