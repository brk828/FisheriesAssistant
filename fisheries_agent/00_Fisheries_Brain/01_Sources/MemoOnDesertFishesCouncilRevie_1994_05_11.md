## Executive Summary

This document is a 1994 memo from Bob Wiley (Wyoming fisheries biologist) to Phil Pister, forwarding a 1993 letter Wiley wrote to Secretary of the Interior Bruce Babbitt. Rather than presenting primary morphological or survey data, the letter is a policy-oriented ecological argument concerning the decline of four endangered native "big-river" fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Sucker]]. Wiley, drawing on 31 years of fisheries management experience (including 21 years representing Wyoming on the [[Colorado River Fish and Wildlife Council]]), argues that large-scale water development and dam construction—not sport-fishery stocking practices alone—are the primary drivers of native fish decline, because they converted warm, turbid, free-flowing river habitat into cold, clear, regulated reservoir/tailwater habitat unsuitable for these species.

## Background & Description

The source contains no direct morphological measurements or descriptions. However, it characterizes the four endangered native species collectively as:
- **Large-bodied, warm-water fish** adapted to high-turbidity, high-velocity riverine conditions.
- Evolutionarily specialized for the "untrammeled habitat of the free-flowing [[Colorado River]]," implying life-history and physiological adaptations (e.g., tolerance of turbidity, warm temperatures, and variable/violent flow regimes) rather than the cool, clear, stable conditions found below dams.
- No fin ray counts, coloration, or body-shape data are reported in this correspondence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic habitat**: The pristine [[Colorado River]] system (comprising the Colorado, [[Green River]], [[Gunnison River]], and [[San Juan River]]) was a continuum from cold, clear, erosional Rocky Mountain headwaters to warm, turbid, depositional lower reaches — the latter being the core habitat of the endangered native fishes.
- **Habitat alteration**: Construction of [[Lake Powell]], [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], [[Navajo Reservoir]], [[Blue Mesa Reservoir]], and [[Fontenelle Reservoir]] now controls over 90% of the basin's runoff, converting warm/turbid river reaches into cool, clear, sediment-starved tailwaters — conditions favoring introduced coldwater [[Salmonidae|trout]] over native warm-water fish.
- **Niche mismatch post-impoundment**: The four endangered species are not lake-dwellers; they performed poorly or disappeared entirely from reservoirs (e.g., extirpation from the Fontenelle tailwater of the Green River following dam closure, which shifted the river from warm/turbid/depositional to cool/clear/erosional).
- **Refugia**: Currently, the [[Yampa River]] and [[White River]] — undammed, less-developed tributaries — serve as the primary refugia retaining habitat conditions suitable for native fish persistence.
- **Nonnative species introduction context**: [[Common Carp]] and [[Channel Catfish]] were established in the basin in the early 1900s via intentional introductions by federal, state, and territorial conservation agencies seeking to enhance food and sport fisheries — an early precedent for nonnative stocking practices in the basin.
- **Chemical treatment history**: In 1962, 425 miles of the Green River system (Wyoming and Utah) were chemically treated to suppress fish species then considered undesirable; none of the 20 native and introduced species present were permanently eliminated by this treatment or by subsequent trout stocking — later population changes were attributed to habitat alteration, not chemical or biological control efforts.

## Key Findings

- **Primary driver of decline**: Water development and dam-driven habitat conversion (not sport-fish stocking alone) is identified as the single largest cause of native fish decline in the Colorado River system.
- **Habitat mismatch, not sole species interaction**: The shift from warm/turbid/depositional to cool/clear/erosional river conditions is the principal mechanism excluding native species and favoring coldwater nonnatives such as trout.
- **Historical stocking precedent**: Carp and channel catfish were deliberately introduced by government agencies in the early 20th century for food/sport purposes, establishing a long history of nonnative fish management in the basin predating modern endangered species concerns.
- **Limited efficacy of past control efforts**: The 1962 chemical treatment of 425 miles of the Green River system did not eliminate any of the 20 fish species present, underscoring that habitat change—rather than direct biotic control—drove subsequent community shifts.
- **Policy implication**: The letter argues that meaningful recovery of the endangered [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Sucker]] requires restoring warm, turbid, riverine habitat (not merely altering reservoir release regimes or regulating nonnative stocking), and questions whether Endangered Species Act (ESA) administration will preserve state fisheries management authority.
- **Refugia-based conservation reality**: Given the scale of impoundment (>90% of runoff controlled), the author concludes native fish may only be sustainable in refugial reaches (e.g., [[Yampa River]], [[White River]]) absent major structural changes to the reservoir system.
