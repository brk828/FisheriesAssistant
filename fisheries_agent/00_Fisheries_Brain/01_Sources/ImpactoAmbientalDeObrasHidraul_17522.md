# Impacto Ambiental de Obras Hidráulicas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #cyprinidae #ecology-of-dams

## Executive Summary

This 1975 (second printing 1985) monograph by [[Salvador Contreras Balderas]], of the Laboratorio de Ictiología at the Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León, examines the environmental impact of hydraulic works (dams, weirs, canals, water wells and mines) with an emphasis on fishes. The author argues that impact assessment is a biological discipline and not merely an engineering one. The work reviews published impacts worldwide, mostly from temperate and extratropical regions, and presents detailed case studies from northern Mexico, where the ichthyofauna has been reduced and endangered by dams, canals, groundwater extraction, salinization and introduced species. It closes with recommendations for research, monitoring, prioritization of endangered species and mandatory environmental impact assessment for hydraulic projects.

## Background & Description

The document is a technical report and monograph, not a primary field study. Its objectives are:

- To summarize the main types of hydraulic works: [[Dams]] (Presas), diversion weirs (Presas Derivadoras or Azudes), [[Canals]], water wells (Pozos) and water mines (Minas de Agua).
- To review ecological impacts on physical, chemical and biological parameters, drawing on the literature.
- To characterize the [[Northern Mexican Ichthyofauna]] and identify rare, threatened and endangered species in the Chihuahuan, Tamaulipan and Sonoran arid zones.
- To propose preventive and remedial measures and research priorities.

The report draws on the author's collections, which include more than 800 collecting trips and about 900,000 specimens accumulated over more than 15 years in the region, as well as material consulted in museum collections (University of Michigan, Smithsonian, Tulane, Arizona State). Taxonomic and distributional data are summarized in numerous tables, which are not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir succession.** Following [[Lowe-McConnell]] (1973), impoundment releases nutrients from flooded vegetation, which first boosts bacteria and fish populations. Fish abundance then declines after 2-3 years, and a new equilibrium is reached after 25-30 years in temperate zones or 6-10 years in the tropics. The final ichthyofauna depends on the native fauna and on each species' response to changes in temperature, turbidity, flow, oxygen and spawning conditions.

**Physical and chemical changes.** Impoundments produce thermal and oxygen stratification, with hypolimnetic releases cooling downstream water and epilimnetic releases warming it. Sediment trapping can reach 99.3%. Evaporation can reach roughly 5% of inflow on average, and groundwater levels rise near reservoirs. Hypolimnetic discharge can produce gas supersaturation and fish kills. Salinization is a major concern in irrigation systems in arid zones.

**Biological and social effects.** The report documents eutrophication, aquatic weed proliferation (including disease vectors such as those for schistosomiasis), migratory fish blockage and turbine mortality, shifts in fisheries and effects on public health.

**Northern Mexican context.** The region is an arid to semiarid area in which water is the main limiting factor. The ichthyofauna is depleted, typically to one-half or one-quarter of the species found in better-watered rivers of similar size. Most northern species are endemic (139 of 176). Interior basins prevent recolonization, and the author notes that simple communities cannot resist introduced exotic species.

## Key Findings

**Overall fauna status (northern Mexico, 176 species and subspecies, 11 families):**

- 139 (78.9%) are endemic.
- 6 (3.4%) are extinct, and 4 (2.3%) are considered almost extinct.
- 40 (22.7%) are endangered (En Peligro) and 78 (44.3%) are threatened (Amenazadas).
- Combined, 124 (70.4%) show impacts; 52 (29.5%) show none yet.
- Of the 176 species, 115 are Nearctic and 61 Neotropical; 105 are primary (salinity-intolerant) and 71 secondary.

**Taxa most affected.** Primary Nearctic species show the highest impact rate. Cichlids, goodeids and cyprinodontids show high endemism. Losses of carnivores, bottom-dwelling families, surface insectivores and herbivores are reported.

**Case studies (examples):**

- **Río Conchos basin, Chihuahua:** Five operating dams. In Ciudad Chihuahua, 11 species have disappeared locally from an original 13 native species.
- **Río San Juan, Nuevo León:** No native species recorded between Santa Catarina and Guadalupe by 1972-1974, owing to pollution and barrier effects.
- **Río del Tunal, Durango:** Seven native species lost in a considerable stretch below Guadalupe Victoria dam, replaced by an impoverished exotic fauna.
- **Río Bravo lower basin:** Primary freshwater species retreated sharply. In the Matamoros-Brownsville area, primary species fell from 15 (1850-99) to 7 (1953) and 3 (1975), linked to increasing salinity from irrigation return flows and reduced flow.
- **Cuatro Ciénegas valley:** Canalization threatens an endemic fauna of up to 200 species, including at least 12 fishes, and has already caused extinction of a mollusc population.
- **Parras, Coahuila:** Four endemic species lost, attributed partly to overexploitation of aquifers by water mines.
- **Groundwater:** Of 19 aquifers assessed, 10 (52.6%) are exploited without known recharge rates, 4 (21.1%) are underexploited and 5 (26.3%) are overexploited. The report estimates overexploitation at 66.2% of recharge. El Paso aquifer is falling about 10 cm/year.

**Impact of dams on fisheries and migrants:**

- Turbine passage mortality of migratory fish averaged 11%, versus 2% for spillway passage (Schoeneman et al., 1961).
- In the Mekong, fish communities fell from 74 to 51 species.
- Lake Nasser sardine catch dropped from 18,000 to 10 tonnes per year.

**Measures recommended by the author:**

1. Biological, taxonomic, ecological, toxicological and ecosystem research on native fishes.
2. A monitoring and prioritization system for impacted species and critical habitats.
3. Integration of biological, social and economic criteria in decision making.
4. Mandatory environmental impact assessment for all hydraulic projects.
5. Regional pilot programs in arid zones (Monterrey recommended) and tropical zones.
6. Caution with exotic species introductions, and preference for mechanical, biological and chemical weed control in combination.

**Priority scoring.** The author proposes a priority list in which Cuatro Ciénegas, Parras, San Juan (Querétaro), Bustillos and La Media Luna rank highest, based on the percentage of impacted species. The author acknowledges the system needs refinement.

**Key caveat.** The author notes that the scarcity of biological knowledge about Mexican freshwater fishes means that threat categories are provisional and that the data are open to revision.
