# Experimental vegetation control by largemouth bass-tilapia combinations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by [[William F. Childers]] and [[George W. Bennett]] of the Illinois Natural History Survey tested whether the herbivorous [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*) could control nuisance aquatic vegetation in a small central Illinois pond, and how [[Largemouth Bass]] predation altered that outcome. Experiments ran from 1962 to 1965 in [[Arrowhead Pond]], a 2.6-acre pond at [[Allerton Park]]. Tilapia survived and reproduced during normal summers but died when water temperatures fell below about 55 °F. Tilapia populations were strongly suppressed by bass, and the authors concluded that tilapia can control vegetation in ponds where aesthetic values matter most, provided predator pressure is limited.

## Background & Description

Chemical control of aquatic plants is costly, often requires repeated applications, can leave residues in bottom sediments, and may cause oxygen depletion. Nutrient release from decomposing treated plants can also trigger algal blooms. These drawbacks motivated searches for biological control using herbivorous fishes. The authors place this work within a broader program, begun at the Auburn University Fisheries Laboratory in the late 1950s, that imported vegetation-feeding fishes from around the world.

*Tilapia mossambica* was chosen because it had long been cultured as a food fish in Africa and Asia. Its features include:

- Young fish begin spawning at 2–3 months old and spawn at 5- to 8-week intervals.
- It can produce large poundages of fish quickly and tolerate low oxygen.
- It belongs to [[Cichlidae]], a family of more than 600 species, mostly native to Africa and Central and South America.
- It is cold-sensitive: small fish begin dying near 45 °F (7.2 °C), and prolonged temperatures below 55 °F (12.8 °C) kill fish of all sizes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arrowhead Pond was built in 1948 in the State 4-H Club Camp at Allerton Park. It has a surface area of 2.6 acres, a maximum depth of 12 ft, and an average depth of 5 ft, and sits in [[Black Prairie Soils]]. Its drainage basin includes part of a fertilized cultivated field and an adjacent hog lot, separated from the pond by a fenced grassland strip 70–165 ft wide. A seep on the southwest shore augmented surface runoff, so the basin usually refilled within 6–10 weeks after draining.

Nuisance vegetation, including the rooted submersed plant *Potamogeton foliosus*, floating filamentous algae, and cattails, was abundant from 1954 to 1959. In 1959 the pond received one ton of inorganic fertilizer (12-10-35 N-P-K formulation). The fertilizer sustained dense algal and submersed plant growth through subsequent seasons. Its other ecological components were [[Bullfrog Tadpoles]] (*Rana catesbeiana*) and two crayfish species, mostly *Procambarus blandingi* with about 10 percent *Orconectes virilis*.

Sampling used a weir at the outlet flume, with fish, tadpoles and crayfish censused by draining the pond at the end of each season. At least half the crayfish stayed in the mud and could not be counted.

## Key Findings

- **Tilapia alone (1962):** With no bass present, tilapia rose from an original stocking of 58 fish to about 10,800 per acre within four months. Tadpoles were four times as numerous as tilapia and represented nearly twice their weight. Submersed vegetation and algae disappeared after June 25.
- **Bass suppression (1963):** Adult bass stocked at 15 per acre, plus 109 yearlings per acre, produced a large year class. The census found 826 bass per acre (mean 3.7 in) and only 11 tilapia per acre. Tadpoles and crayfish were reduced to 4 and 11 per acre. Nuisance vegetation persisted through the summer.
- **Fingerling stocking (1964):** Bass were stocked as 2-inch fingerlings at 126 per acre, with 12.9 percent mortality. The 1964 census found 164 tilapia per acre, about 1,000 tadpoles... with more than 5,000 tadpoles and 2,000 crayfish, suggesting bass preferred tilapia over these other prey. Vegetation was present but did not choke the shallows.
- **1965 season:** Bass were stocked as 3-inch fingerlings at 58 per acre; 37 survived. Tilapia numbered more than 7,500 per acre, weighing 449 lb per acre, which exceeded the 1962 weight despite fewer fish. Crayfish were numerous and tadpoles were relatively scarce.
- **Bass density analysis:** Authors classified bass populations as follows: 37 per acre (22 lb/acre, 1965) as underpopulated, 164 per acre (1964; 68.2 lb in the 1963 overpopulated case noted) as near optimal, and 826 per acre (1963) as overpopulated.
- **Threshold:** The authors estimated that controlling vegetation may require feeding by more than 1,000 tilapia per acre of assorted sizes, though this was not directly tested.
- **Recommended use:** Tilapia are suggested for estate ponds where aesthetics dominate and fishing interest is low, especially where septic overflow enriches the water. Fish would be stocked in early summer, overwintered indoors at 70–80 °F, and restocked the following spring.
