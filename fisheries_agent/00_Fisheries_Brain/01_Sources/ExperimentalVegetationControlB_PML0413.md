# Experimental Vegetation Control by Largemouth Bass-Tilapia Combinations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This study, published in the *Journal of Wildlife Management* (Vol. 31, No. 3, July 1967) by Childers and Senner of the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]], tests whether [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*) can control nuisance submersed aquatic vegetation in a farm pond when stocked alongside [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). The experiment ran at Arrowhead Pond near Monticello, Illinois, from 1959 to 1965. The authors conclude that tilapia are highly valuable for vegetation control in largemouth bass ponds, but that tilapia cannot overwinter in northern ponds and require restocking each spring.

## Background & Description

- **Study site:** Arrowhead Pond, near Monticello, central Illinois. Surface area is given as roughly 2.6 acres (OCR partly illegible), maximum depth 12 ft, and average depth 5 ft. The pond sits in [[Black Prairie Soils]].
- **Watershed:** Drainage comes from a fertilized cultivated field and an adjacent hog lot, separated from the pond by a 70–100 ft grass strip. Runoff entered through a tile inlet, and a drainpipe outlet controlled water level.
- **Fertilization:** Inorganic fertilizer was applied in 1959 at a rate of about 200 lb per acre, and the pond was fertilized again in later years (OCR partly illegible).
- **Tilapia:** Non-native species from [[Africa]] that is sensitive to cold. The text notes tilapia spawn beginning at about 2.5 months of age, with spawning intervals of 5 to 8 weeks. Tilapia are known to be capable of producing large numbers of young quickly.
- **Stock origin:** Tilapia were supplied from the Fisheries Laboratory at [[Auburn University]], Alabama, and stocked in spring 1959, 1960, 1961, and 1965 after the earlier fish were removed each October.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vegetation problem:** Submersed rooted plants, mainly [[Leafy Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton foliosus*), together with filamentous algae, had been present at increasing abundance since 1954, and especially after fertilization in 1959. Nuisance vegetation can be controlled with herbicides, but chemical programs may be costly and require repeated applications.
- **Biological control rationale:** Earlier work in other regions had shown that herbivorous fishes can reduce aquatic plant growth, and the authors describe the present study as aimed at finding and measuring the usefulness of exotic fishes that consume aquatic plants.
- **Other organisms:** Bullfrog tadpoles and crayfish were also sampled, and bass were known to prey on them. Tadpole numbers in the 1962 census exceeded 40,000 while crayfish numbers were very low. By 1965, crayfish exceeded 10,000 and tadpoles were fewer.
- **Temperature limits:** Tilapia stop feeding and become stressed once water temperatures fall below about 60 °F (15.5 °C). Fish held indoors at 70–50 °F (21.1–10 °C) survived and could be restocked the following summer.

## Key Findings

- **Tilapia control vegetation.** The authors argue tilapia are highly effective for controlling nuisance aquatic vegetation in ponds used for [[Sport Fishing]] and aesthetic purposes.
- **Bass-tilapia relationship.** Largemouth bass stocked at the start of each season kept tilapia numbers in check. When bass were present, tilapia populations multiplied less rapidly, and tilapia biomass was reduced by predation. Bass spawning was also recorded.
- **Stocking results.** Largemouth bass were stocked at 126 fingerlings per acre (2-inch) in 1961 and at a lower rate of 3-inch fingerlings in 1965. In 1961, 15 bass were recovered from the 1960 census, which shows reduced survival after heavy tilapia harvest by fishermen.
- **Population fluctuations.** Tilapia numbers varied across census years. Tilapia were the only fish in the early stocking years and multiplied to large numbers without bass.
- **Vegetation response.** Tilapia reduced submersed vegetation and filamentous algae, in contrast to earlier years when vegetation dominated. Plant control was strongest in years with high tilapia densities.
- **Winter mortality.** Tilapia cannot survive winter in northern ponds, so the population must be restocked each year, which the authors note as a practical limit.
- **Management recommendation.** Use tilapia for vegetation control in largemouth bass ponds, stock bass at the start of the season, and remove the tilapia by fall to avoid excessive spread.
