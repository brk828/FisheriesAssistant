# Restoration Strategies for the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This report by Duncan T. Patten synthesizes the ideas generated at a [[Workshop Symposium]] held on July 8-9, 1998, by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the Bureau of Reclamation. The document provides background on the heavily regulated [[Lower Colorado River]], discusses concepts of restoration, and offers guidance for restoring, rehabilitating or recovering its riparian and aquatic ecosystems. The authors conclude that dam removal is highly unlikely, so the most realistic strategy is to mimic natural hydrological events through managed flow releases, combined with floodplain reconfiguration, groundwater management, sediment augmentation and the protection of native fish from non-native predators. The synthesis is intended as guidance for future restoration planning, and it stresses careful stakeholder involvement, site selection and long-term adaptive management.

## Background & Description

**Historical change.** The river was once a free-flowing, sediment-laden system in an arid region. Over the last 150 years it became one of the most controlled rivers in North America. Flows are regulated by 12 dams, the largest being [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Mead]]. The river is more than 1,400 miles (ca. 2,250 km) long and drains more than 240,000 square miles (ca. 622,000 km²) across seven states and northern Mexico.

**Key regulatory milestones:**
- The [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922 divided river flows among the seven basin states, based on wet-year measurements from the early 20th century.
- The Reclamation Act of 1929 authorized [[Hoover Dam]], completed in 1935. Peak flows at [[Yuma]] fell below 20,000 cfs (566 cms), and the peak shifted from May/June to winter.
- [[Davis Dam]] and [[Parker Dam]] further regulated lower river flows, and irrigation and urban takeouts essentially eliminated flows to the delta and gulf.
- [[Glen Canyon Dam]], completed in 1963, regulated discharge through [[Grand Canyon]]. Except in wet years, only 8 million acre-feet (986,800 ha·m) moved downstream, with the remainder held in Lake Powell.

**Pre-dam conditions.** Before development, discharge near Yuma often peaked above 125,000 cfs (ca. 3,540 cms), with extremes above 200,000 cfs (5,660 cms). Extensive [[Fremont Cottonwood]], [[Goodding Willow]] and [[Honey Mesquite]] riparian forests lined the river, and steamboats relied on them for fuel.

**Sediment and temperature.** Before Glen Canyon Dam, more than 60 million tons of sediment passed the Grand Canyon gage near Phantom Ranch, and more than 200 million tons often left the canyon, of which 140 million tons reached Yuma. After dam closure, sediment load fell to about 10% of unregulated levels. Releases below Glen Canyon Dam are cold and clear, at 46-50°F (8-10°C), whereas the historic river ranged from about 80°F (27°C) in summer to near freezing in winter.

**Table 1 summary.** The report's Table 1 contrasts past (pre-dam), transitional (post-Hoover) and present (post-Glen Canyon) conditions. Present conditions include flows of <500 to 4,000 cfs, a deeper and more stable channel, a straightened and incised channel, diked floodplains, and non-natives dominating fish communities and habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish community.** The fish community has changed drastically. Two main explanations are given. First, the shift from a turbid, fluctuating, flood-driven river to a clear, cool stream discourages native spawning except in tributaries, while impoundments favor non-natives such as [[Striped Bass]], which prefer lentic conditions. Second, a high proportion of predatory non-native fishes keeps native numbers low. Blocked up- and downstream migration has also been cited (Minckley 1991).

**Riparian vegetation.** Most original cottonwood and willow stands and honey mesquite bosques along the lower river have been lost to channelization and floodplain development. Dense [[Tamarisk]] stands now provide wildlife habitat in some backwaters. Uncontrolled spills in 1983 and 1993 stimulated cottonwood and willow recruitment near the confluence of the Colorado and Gila Rivers, showing the value of normal hydrology.

**Birds.** Regulated flows and dense vegetation may have increased avian diversity in Grand Canyon. Along the lower river, reduced structural diversity has lowered avian diversity, although tamarisk stands can support large populations of a few species (Ohmart et al. 1988).

**Restoration concepts.** The authors treat restoration as the return of structure and function to a reference condition, which is probably unattainable in full. They distinguish three outcomes along a degraded-to-original gradient (see [[Restoration Concepts]]):
- **Restoration** in the purest sense, which is probably impossible here.
- **Rehabilitation**, returning most structure and function, which is the realistic target.
- **Replacement**, using surrogate or non-native components, which is acceptable where necessary but is not true restoration.

Rivers and floodplains should be managed as one ecosystem, and projects that account for natural disturbance cycles, hydrology and geomorphology show the greatest potential for sustainable improvement.

**Significant resources.** Eight resource categories are identified for habitat planning: fish, aquatic food base, terrestrial vegetation, wildlife (especially threatened and endangered species), recreation, cultural resources, water storage and power production.

## Key Findings

**Workshop charges and questions.** The workshop asked participants to generate conceptual approaches across spatial and temporal scales, consider legal, social, hydrological, geomorphic and ecologic constraints, and address 14 main questions. These covered required hydrologic levels, acceptability of surrogates, sediment sources below Parker Dam, channel incision, use of artificial oxbows for native fish, predation control, reach length and whether restoration is a multi-decade commitment. The workshop also addressed linkages to Grand Canyon and the upper basin. The guiding premise was that it is easier to change the "laws of man" (such as the law of the river) than the "laws of nature."

**Tradeoffs.** Riparian ecosystems and riverine habitat have been the most degraded functions, while water supply, flood control, hydropower, recreation and agriculture drive the alterations. Restoration therefore requires explicit tradeoffs with human benefits.

**Temporal phases.** The workshop proposed a staged timeline:
- Enhancement in the short term (0-5 years).
- Rehabilitation in the midterm (5-25 years).
- Restoration in the long term (more than 35 years).

**Dam-dependent variables.** Flow volume, timing of peaks and base flows, daily flow fluctuation, ramping rate, and water temperature and quality are directly controlled by dam operations. Below-dam variables include canyon and floodplain geomorphology, sediment input and dynamics, riparian vegetation dynamics, bank storage, nutrient dynamics and trophic dynamics. Anthropogenic variables include recreation and landscape modification.

**Riparian restoration targets.** Three community types are recommended for restoration: marsh or margin wetlands, cottonwood/willow communities and mesquite bosques. A fourth, phreatophytic shrubs such as seepwillow and arrowweed with young cottonwoods and willows, may also be considered. Candidate sites should retain natural fluvial geomorphology or have shallow groundwater. Named sites include the Laguna-Yuma area, Limitrophe Division and Mexico, and Cibola.

**Hydrology.** Simulating natural floods, with high peaks timed to historical peaks, is the central recommendation. The natural hydrograph also needs base flows low enough to avoid scouring seedlings. Downstream flows decline from Hoover Dam to the Mexican border, so creating floods of adequate magnitude is difficult. Reducing consumptive use in the lower basin may be necessary.

**Groundwater.** Most riparian vegetation is phreatophytic. Many projects have failed where groundwater was too deep or was lowered after planting. Groundwater pumping may need to be reduced in restoration reaches, and levee and berm removal or channel infilling may be needed to raise the thalweg.

**Sediment transport.** Small tributaries downstream of dams may supply enough sediment for seedbeds. If not, sediment augmentation should be considered.

**Biological activities.** Early successional species (cottonwood, willow, seepwillow) should be planted at channel margins that high flows wet, and later species (mesquite) on elevated terraces. Planting should use local genotypes from nurseries, and herbaceous understory should also be established. Non-natives should be discouraged and removed mechanically, by fire, or with selected herbicides where they dominate.

**Floodplain modification.** Levees should be removed or moved back, floodplains lowered and meanders reformed. Oxbows and backwaters can serve as riparian recovery sites and as rearing habitat for native fish. There are hundreds of backwaters and oxbows below Davis Dam. Salinity is the main soil-quality concern.

**Economic incentives and land use.** Many candidate sites are privately owned. Tools include conservation easements, recreation-based returns, water-rights purchases from states, tribes and private entities, and funding from the Land and Water Fund or Arizona's Heritage Fund. Land use planning can designate restoration areas and limit floodplain building.

**Aquatic restoration.** Restoring the aquatic ecosystem requires similar measures, with particular attention to water quality. Salinity has risen through impoundment evaporation and irrigation return flows, and reducing salinity and selenium is described as critical. Backwaters for young native fish are a priority, and the authors recommend that rearing ponds be kept hydraulically disconnected from the main channel and filled by subsurface flow to exclude non-native predators. Hatcheries using diverse local native fish gene pools are recommended, and genetic diversity should also be considered at lower food-web levels.

**Conclusions.**
1. Restoration requires careful planning that includes all interested parties, including the public.
2. The whole lower river is not suitable for restoration, so appropriate sites must be identified and characterized.
3. Activities should follow a temporal sequence: enhance existing riparian habitat, select long-term sites, improve water availability and delivery, and acquire lands for future rehabilitation.
4. Long-term planning, research, monitoring and [[Adaptive Management]] are essential.
