## Executive Summary

This document is a **conservation funding proposal** by Conservation International (CI) and American Rivers, submitted to the Tode Foundation, requesting $110,000 to conduct a bi-national "Conservation Priority Setting Workshop" for the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] and [[Gulf Of California]] ecosystem. The proposal is not a primary research paper but synthesizes existing scientific consensus to argue that the transboundary river-delta-gulf system — once "one of the world's great desert estuaries" — is at risk of ecological collapse due to decades of damming, water diversion, and agricultural development. The core scientific value of the document lies in its identification of critical endangered taxa and its call for integrated bi-national data-sharing on species distributions, water quality, and habitat change from the Davis Dam/Hoover Dam region to the Upper Gulf of California.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document frames the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] and its terminus in the [[Upper Gulf Of California]] as an integrated riverine-deltaic-marine ecosystem with distinct ecological zones:

- **Freshwater/riparian zone (U.S. side):** Historically supported native riparian species and "big-river" fish species, now designated critical habitat under U.S. multi-species habitat conservation plans (Lake Powell to Mexican border). Decline in freshwater flow has led to extirpation of native species and introduction of **freshwater exotic species** described as "biological pollutants."
- **Delta/estuarine zone (Mexico):** Once a vast network of "a hundred green lagoons" (Aldo Leopold, 1922) fed by river nutrients and silt; now severely diminished due to upstream damming, resulting in negligible freshwater and sediment flow reaching the delta.
- **Marine/Gulf zone (Upper Gulf of California):** Designated a UNESCO-recognized [[Biosphere Reserve]] by the Mexican government in June 1993. Characterized as one of Earth's most diverse and productive marine ecosystems, supporting:
  - **~800 species of fish**
  - Thousands of marine invertebrate species
  - Over 15 species of marine birds
  - Up to **60% of Mexico's total national fish catch**

The proposal emphasizes that declining freshwater inputs threaten the trophic linkage between river, delta, and marine ecosystems, jeopardizing spawning habitat critical to estuarine and marine productivity.

## Key Findings

- The [[Colorado River]] system from Davis Dam to the Upper Gulf retains restoration potential despite decades of degradation, per bi-national scientific consensus reached at a November 1994 CI/American Rivers workshop.
- A dam-driven **reduction in freshwater and sediment flow** has been identified as the primary driver of ecosystem decline, affecting both the U.S. riparian corridor and the Mexican delta/estuary.
- Species explicitly identified as **threatened** due to declining water flows: [[Vaquita]] (vaquita porpoise), [[Totoaba]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Green Sea Turtle]], and [[Elegant Tern]], along with unspecified spawning habitat in the delta estuaries.
- On the U.S. side, portions of the lower mainstem river have been designated **critical habitat for two "big-river" fish species** (unnamed in text, likely referencing species such as [[Bonytail Chub]] or [[Razorback Sucker]] typical of Lower Colorado conservation planning, though not explicitly stated in source).
- A major identified **data gap**: research has historically been confined within national boundaries, preventing a unified bi-national understanding of the ecosystem; no formal U.S.-Mexico data-sharing network currently exists.
- The proposed **Conservation Priority Setting Workshop** methodology (previously applied in the Amazon Basin, Papua New Guinea, and other regions) would involve two phases: (1) pre-workshop data gathering and GIS mapping of species distributions, water quality, and land-use change; (2) an integrative workshop combining thematic groups (hydrology, water quality, marine biology, system ecology, agriculture, anthropology) into consensus-based bi-national conservation priorities.
- Proposed budget totals **$110,000**, allocated across pre-workshop data collection ($70,000), the workshop itself ($20,000), and publication/dissemination ($10,000).
- The Mexican government's June 1993 declaration of the **Delta and Upper Gulf Biosphere Reserve** is cited as a first concrete step toward integrated ecosystem management.
