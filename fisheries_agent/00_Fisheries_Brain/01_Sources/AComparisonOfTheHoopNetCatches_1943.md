## Executive Summary

This 1943 study by Lawrence F. Miller (Tennessee Valley Authority) analyzed hoop-net catch data from [[Wheeler Reservoir]], a main-stream impoundment on the Tennessee River in Alabama, to assess the relative abundance of fish species across four distinct habitat zones. Over a six-month period in 1941 (April onward), 280 overnight hoop-net sets (~22 hours each) were deployed across the [[Guntersville Dam Tailwater]], Upper Wheeler Area, Middle Wheeler Area, and Lower Wheeler Area. The study found strong habitat partitioning among species: tailwater habitats favored longnose gar, mooneye, skipjack, mud catfish, black bullhead, white bass, sauger, and both crappie species; the upper reservoir (retaining riverine characteristics) favored [[Freshwater Drum]]; the middle reservoir (extensive shallow backwaters) favored rough/coarse fish species; and the lower reservoir (deep, low-turbidity water) favored [[Kentucky Bass]] and sunfishes. The paper emphasizes that hoop nets are selective gear, limiting cross-species abundance comparisons, but valid for within-species zonal comparisons.

## Background & Description

The paper does not describe individual morphological traits in detail, but distinguishes species by functional/taxonomic groupings relevant to habitat associations:

- **Rough/Coarse Fish**: Gars (*Lepisosteus productus*, *Lepisosteus osseus*), Dogfish/Bowfin (*Amia calva*), Mooneye (*Hiodon tergisus*), Skipjack (*Pomolobus chrysochloris*), Gizzard Shad (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), buffalofishes (*Megastomatobus cyprinella*, *Ictiobus niger*, *Ictiobus bubalus*), carpsuckers, suckers, and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*).
- **Food Fish (Catfishes & Drum)**: Channel catfish (*Ictalurus lacustris*), Blue catfish (*Ictalurus furcatus*), Mud/Flathead catfish (*Pilodictis olivaris*), Black/Brown/Yellow bullheads (*Ameiurus* spp.), and [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*).
- **Game Fish**: Chain pickerel (*Esox niger*), White bass (*Lepibema chrysops*), Yellow bass (*Morone interrupta*), Sauger (*Stizostedion canadense*), [[Kentucky Bass]] (*Micropterus punctulatus*), Largemouth bass (*Huro salmoides*), Warmouth bass (*Chaenobryttus gulosus*).
- **Pan Fish**: Green sunfish, Longeared sunfish, [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), Redear sunfish, Rock bass, Black crappie (*Pomoxis nigro-maculatus*), White crappie (*Pomoxis annularis*).

Taxonomic identifications follow contemporary (1943) nomenclature, some of which differs from modern classifications (e.g., *Ictalurus lacustris* for channel catfish, *Huro salmoides* for largemouth bass).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Wheeler Reservoir, part of the TVA main-stream impoundment system on the [[Tennessee River]], is divided into four ecologically distinct zones, each shaping species distribution:

1. **Guntersville Dam Tailwater**: Shallowest depth, most rapid flow, high turbidity variability. Yielded the highest overall catch rate per net. Favored by longnose gar, mooneye, skipjack, mud catfish, black bullhead, white bass, sauger, and crappie (especially black crappie).
2. **Upper Wheeler Area**: Confined to the original river channel, retaining natural riverine characteristics including molluscan fauna. Lowest overall catch rate per net. Strongly favored by [[Freshwater Drum]], likely due to reliance on mollusks as a primary food source.
3. **Middle Wheeler Area**: Water overflows into extensive shallow backwaters bordering the original channel; most susceptible to water-level fluctuations, which can expose large areas of bottom substrate. Favored most rough fish species (buffalo, suckers, carp) as well as channel catfish, blue catfish, and brown bullhead.
4. **Lower Wheeler Area**: Deepest zone, minimal flow, lowest turbidity due to sediment settling. Favored by [[Kentucky Bass]] and several sunfish species. [[Bluegill]] were relatively abundant across all four zones, showing weaker habitat specificity.

**Crappie Zonation**: Both black crappie (*Pomoxis nigro-maculatus*) and white crappie (*Pomoxis annularis*) were present in all four zones and collectively made up ≥66% of the catch in each. However, their relative ratios shifted markedly along the reservoir gradient (tailwater → lower third): 7.7:1, 1.5:1, 1:1.8, 1:2.3 (black:white), demonstrating a clear longitudinal replacement of black crappie by white crappie moving downstream.

**Seasonal Caveat**: The study explicitly notes it does not account for seasonal migratory dynamics — species like white bass, largemouth bass, sauger, and longnose gar are known to migrate into tailwater areas in large pulses at certain times of year (citing [[Eschmeyer 1943]] on Clinch River fish migration), meaning the snapshot distribution may not hold true year-round.

## Key Findings

- **280 total overnight hoop-net sets** were conducted: 40 in Guntersville tailwater, 24 in Upper Wheeler, 145 in Middle Wheeler, and 71 in Lower Wheeler, standardized to catch-per-100-net-sets for comparison.
- **Catch abundance per net** was highest in the tailwater and lowest in the upper third of the reservoir.
- **Tailwater specialists**: Longnose gar, mooneye, skipjack, mud catfish, black bullhead, white bass, sauger, and both crappie species were caught in greatest numbers here.
- **Drum specialization**: [[Freshwater Drum]] was disproportionately abundant in the Upper Wheeler Area relative to the other three zones, linked to its molluscivorous diet and the area's retained riverine mollusk fauna.
- **Middle zone rough-fish dominance**: The extensive shallow backwaters of the Middle Wheeler Area supported the highest catches of most rough (coarse) fish species, plus channel catfish, blue catfish, and brown bullhead.
- **Lower zone game/pan fish dominance**: [[Kentucky Bass]] and several sunfish species were best represented in the Lower Wheeler Area, associated with reduced turbidity.
- **Crappie ratio gradient**: A clear downstream shift from black crappie dominance (tailwater, 7.7:1) to white crappie dominance (lower third, 1:2.3) was documented — a notable finding on interspecific niche partitioning within a single genus along a reservoir's longitudinal gradient.
- **Methodological limitation**: Hoop nets are gear-selective for certain species, so while within-species zonal comparisons are valid, the data cannot support cross-species abundance comparisons or total population estimates.
- **Temporal limitation**: The six-month (April–onward, 1941) sampling window does not capture known seasonal migratory pulses (e.g., white bass, largemouth bass, sauger, longnose gar into tailwaters), meaning true relative abundance patterns may vary substantially by season.
- This work builds on gear methodology described in [[Smith & Miller 1943]] (*A comparison of the hoop-net catch on several waters in the Tennessee Valley before and after impoundment*).
