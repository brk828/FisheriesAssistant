# Environmental Impacts in Cuatro Ciénegas, Coahuila, Mexico: A Commentary

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #conservation

## Executive Summary

This commentary by [[Salvador Contreras-Balderas]] documents human-caused damage to the spring systems and endemic biota of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in Coahuila, Mexico, between 1963 and 1983. The author notes that numerous papers describe the valley's environments and biota, but no anthropogenic environmental impact studies had been carried out there. The damage described includes water diversion and temperature change at [[Poza La Becerra]], loss of habitat at an unnamed poza, extraction and destruction of [[White Dunes]] gypsum formations, and agricultural conversion at [[Ejido Tierra Blanca]]. The author warns that growing human needs threaten further losses of endemic species.

## Background & Description

The valley was lightly developed when the author first visited in 1969. It had a few dirt roads to ranches and spring-fed pozas, some of which were little more than wheel-track impressions. From about 1964, tourism (fishing, swimming, water exploration) and agricultural and cattle settlements began to expand. The commentary presents only a sample of the damage recorded.

The valley is home to a rich endemic biota. The text lists the following aquatic and semi-aquatic taxa:

- **Invertebrates:** the snails *Mexipyrgus churinceanus*, *Mexithauma quadripaludium*, and *Nymphophilus minckleyi*, plus a shrimp whose name is illegible in the scan.
- **Fishes:** *Astyanax mexicanus*, *Notropis xanthicara*, and *Cichlasoma minckleyi*.
- **Reptiles:** the endemic [[Coahuila Box Turtle]] (*Terrapene coahuila*), *Pseudemys scripta taylori*, and *Trionyx ater*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Poza La Becerra** is the most detailed case. A water-measuring channel, the first stages of a swimming resort, and a drainage canal to carry irrigation and industrial water out of the basin were built there in 1963–64 (Minckley 1969; Taylor 1966). The spring's marshes were drained, and the spring flowed fully only once (1982) afterward. Changes in flow and water level altered the angle of repose of soft sediments, and the banks slumped toward the center. The bottom, originally more than 8 m deep, became much shallower. Water temperature in the headspring was once 32–33°C throughout, but southern parts now often drop to 25–26°C, possibly because of altered flow patterns and changes in surface-to-volume ratio.

**The unnamed poza** was a small spring and marsh northwest of the northern tip of the Sierra de San Marcos. It dried up in about 1982. Its marshy outlet had sheltered a substantial population of *Terrapene coahuila* (Brown 1968, 1974). The species is now locally extirpated there.

**The White Dunes** are gypsum dunes that are unique in Mexico and one of only three such sites in North America (Cole 1968, Cole 1968 as cited). Exploitation began in 1968 at low levels and expanded to an industrial scale later. About five hectares have been affected, with excavation to a depth of 6 m. Machinery scars and consolidated dune heads are documented in the figures.

**Ejido Tierra Blanca** is a communal farm on the valley floor a few kilometers northwest of the Sierra de San Marcos. The site was converted for agricultural development, but production was essentially nil, and the ejido was abandoned in whole or in part because of lack of investment. Discarded trash and debris are visible in the surrounding area.

## Key Findings

- Human activities in the basin have caused documented losses of water, temperature stability, and species between 1963 and 1983, and the author says these changes are still ongoing.
- At Poza La Becerra, water diversion lowered the headspring by 0.2 m, altered sediment behavior, reduced depth substantially, and lowered water temperature in parts of the spring.
- The unnamed poza dried up, and the endemic turtle *Terrapene coahuila* was lost from that site.
- Gypsum mining at the White Dunes has affected about five hectares to a depth of 6 m, and the author notes that the dunes are rare in both Mexico and North America.
- Agricultural conversion at Ejido Tierra Blanca failed because of poor soils, rough terrain, and lack of investment, leaving the area with trash and abandoned land.
- The Conclusions state that the trend of attempted exploitation is continuing, with a lack of protective safeguards. The author warns that some species could be lost through irreversible damage.
