# Recent Climatic History in California and Adjacent Areas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #paleoclimatology

## Executive Summary

This informal talk by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] of the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]], given at the 1957 Conference on Recent Research in Climatology (La Jolla, March 25-26, 1957), presents fourteen working hypotheses on climatic change in California, Baja California and the arid Southwest from the Tertiary through the Pleistocene and Postglacial periods. The central thesis is a long-term trend toward drier, less uniform climate, accelerated in Postglacial time, with the Southwest now entering a "march of the deserts." Hubbs draws on marine and freshwater fish distributions, mollusk middens, [[Mima Mounds]], radiocarbon and oxygen-isotope dating, and historic weather records. He explicitly labels many points as speculative and invites criticism.

## Background & Description

Hubbs frames the talk as the view of "merely a naturalist, reporting on what I have seen, heard, and thought." He identifies a central hypothesis (trend toward aridity since the Tertiary) and thirteen related or corollary hypotheses. Several are closely interrelated, so that "they may sink or swim together." He notes that some points are well established, while others are young, bold or nebulous.

The talk draws on several lines of evidence:

- **Paleobotany:** Floral succession (Arcto-Tertiary, Madro-Tertiary and Neotropical-Tertiary Geofloras), credited to [[Cheney and Axelrod]] and Axelrod's Mio-Pliocene Nevada compendium.
- **Marine fauna:** Antitropical distributions, cool-water relicts and reef fish extinctions.
- **Fish distributions:** Relict freshwater populations in arid settings, including the trout or salmon relicts and the subantarctic genus [[Galaxias]] in New Caledonia.
- **Geomorphology and soils:** Mima mounds, desert pavement, and fan and bajada sediments.
- **Archaeology and middens:** Shell middens and charcoal dated by [[Radiocarbon Dating]] and [[Oxygen Isotope Paleothermometry]].
- **Historic records:** The 1853-1857 Pacific Railroad Survey faunal lists and weather station data from about 1850.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pleistocene ocean cooling.** Hubbs argues that Pleistocene refrigeration reached the tropics and affected sea temperatures. Evidence includes cool-water planktonic Foraminifera in low-latitude deposits, cool-temperature fish and freshwater relicts near the equator, and [[Antitropical Distribution]] patterns of organisms. Modern species such as the [[Pacific Sardine]] and [[Giant Kelp]] are thought to have crossed the equator during the Wisconsin. Hubbs estimates that the eastern Pacific was cooled by about 5 or 6°C, and he attributes the cooling of the Panamic region mainly to increased easterly winds.

**Wisconsin cold and megafauna extinction.** He proposes that the [[Wisconsin Glaciation]] was among the coldest glacial periods, and that its limited ice extent reflected aridity. Extreme cold followed by drought is offered as a plausible cause of the extinction of [[Pleistocene Megafauna Extinction|Pleistocene megafauna]] such as mammoths, giant ground sloths, camels and horses. Hubbs also cites [[Claude Hibbard]]'s work on late mammal persistence.

**Soil freezing near the Mexican border.** Mima mounds from Puget Sound to Baja California are interpreted as relict giant frost polygons rather than pocket gopher works. Hubbs considers gopher explanations inadequate because of the mounds' regular spacing, uniform structure, large cobbles, and occurrence on steep slopes. The frozen-earth model fits a southward decline in mound size, from about 7 feet high and 20 feet in diameter near Mima Prairie to smaller forms that "rapidly peter out" south of Tijuana.

**Upwelling and coastal zonation.** The coastal zonation pattern is thought to have persisted, with shifts toward cool or warm phases. Areas of [[Upwelling]] are regarded as long-standing controls on sea and air temperature.

**Aridification and physiographic change.** Hubbs argues that much of the desert landscape is a recent modification. Once mountains were largely vegetated and soil-covered. Erosion has since removed that soil, leaving fan and bajada deposits. He proposes three interacting causes: accelerated block faulting, increased aridity, and erosion from overgrazing by Pleistocene herbivores. On [[Santa Rosa Island]], bajada formation accompanied dwarf mammoths dated 16,000 to 27,000 years, and recent drought and introduced cattle, deer, elk and wild boar have accelerated denudation.

**Fish and water loss in the Southwest.** Fish provide key evidence of desiccation:

- [[Fish Creek]], a desert-slope stream near San Diego, supported a Native American fishery in which fish ran up from [[Lake LeConte]] to spawn. Some small fish species persisted until 1916, and the stream later ceased flowing after the 1916 flood.
- Stream fishes in the [[Salton Sink]] have become extinct through recent drying.
- Isolated relict fish populations occur in springs along former stream courses.
- An endemic mosquitofish in a spring beside the Rio Grande in the Big Bend region of Texas has gone dry, and the giant Comanche Spring has ceased to flow.

## Key Findings

The talk presents fourteen hypotheses. The main points are as follows.

1. **Long-term aridity trend (H1, H10).** A trend toward drier, less uniform climate has persisted from the Tertiary through the Pleistocene and Postglacial periods, with fluctuations. Postglacial time saw acceleration, and the paleobotanical record supports this over long timescales.

2. **Worldwide Pleistocene refrigeration (H2).** Cooling extended across the tropics and affected sea temperatures. Hubbs estimates a 5 to 6°C cooling of the eastern Pacific, derived from antitropical distribution, bottom cores and assumed deep-seated thermal tolerances. He notes that the estimate is an assumption, and that comparable isotope data remain to be obtained.

3. **Extreme Wisconsin cold (H3-H4).** The Wisconsin was probably the coldest glacial period, its lesser ice extent reflected aridity, and cold followed by drought contributed to the extinction of the great Pleistocene land fauna.

4. **Soil freezing near Mexico (H5).** Mima mounds are interpreted as relict frost-polygon features, indicating that soil freezing reached near the Mexican border in at least brief Wisconsin intervals. Hubbs recognizes that this view will be contested.

5. **Consistent zonation with latitudinal shifts (H6).** The climatic zonation has been stable in general pattern through glacial and Postglacial time, with shifts in position and intensity.

6. **Coherent regional fluctuations (H7).** Fluctuations have been broadly consistent along the coast, from the arid interior to the coast, and onto the islands. Hubbs admits this is largely an intuitive impression because dating is inadequate.

7. **Out-of-phase recent changes (H8).** Some recent fluctuations in the local region have not followed patterns elsewhere, and have sometimes been negatively correlated with changes farther north. Key data points:
   - Midden shells from north of La Jolla dated at about 7000 years B.P. suggest temperatures close to the present.
   - Pismo clam shells near Scripps dated at about 4000 years B.P. suggest temperatures cooler than the present.
   - A cold period indicated by [[Cryptochiton stelleri]] middens dated at 600 to 1100 years B.P. (about 850 to 1350 A.D.) conflicts with contemporary cold-period estimates elsewhere.
   - Small [[Pismo Clam]] middens dated at about 300 years B.P. indicate a warm-moist period that Hubbs links to the large Native American populations found by the Mission fathers about 200 years ago.
   - Historic faunal lists from 1853-1857 show warm-water species at Monterey, and weather records show air temperatures declining from about 1850 until at least 1910.
   - The apparent rise since 1910 is attributed to urbanization and a change in weather station location.

8. **Recent desiccation (H9-H10).** The desert is a recent modification of a less extreme regime. Evidence includes dried lakes, relict fish in isolated springs, woodchuck range retraction in Arizona, root casts on San Nicolas Island, and laterite soils.

9. **Population fluctuation with water supply (H11).** Human populations in the Southwest fluctuated greatly and correlated with water availability. Each time Lake LeConte filled, people gathered around its shores to exploit the fish.

10. **Most severe recent drought (H12).** Droughts over the last 25 years have been the most severe in the Southwest since the Pluvial period. Examples include the cessation of Fish Creek's flow after 1916, the extirpation of fish life in Nevada sustained since Pluvial times, and the disappearance of Sonoran Plateau lakes.

11. **Continued "march of the desert" (H13-H14).** Hubbs expects continued desertification in the Southwest for a long period and concludes that people in desert regions must "think and act boldly, or face disastrous consequences."

**Note on evidence quality.** Hubbs repeatedly states that the dating is insufficient for firm conclusions. Several radiocarbon results come from shell carbon, and the Pleistocene isotope estimates are still in progress. Hubbs expressly requests criticism and identifies the Cryptochiton and recent-temperature sequences as unresolved.
