# Environmental Assessment: Critical Habitat for the Woundfin and Virgin River Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This **draft** [[Environmental Assessment]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Utah Field Office, dated August 9, 1999) evaluates the formal designation of [[Critical Habitat]] for two endangered fishes, the [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and the [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*), on the mainstem [[Virgin River]] in Utah, Arizona and Nevada. The designation is required under Section 4 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] and responds to a federal court order.

The document compares three alternatives:

- **Alternative A (No Action):** maintains the status quo without designating critical habitat.
- **Alternative B (Preferred):** designates 87.5 miles of river and its 100-year floodplain, from La Verkin Creek, Utah, downstream to Halfway Wash, Nevada.
- **Alternative C:** designates 94.8 miles, extending to [[Lake Mead]].

The assessment concludes that designation would have small economic effects, mainly by changing the timing of water-development and agricultural-conversion decisions rather than the total amount of water reallocated. It also finds no cumulatively significant impacts and no unique effects on minority or low-income populations.

## Background & Description

**Species status and taxonomy**

- The woundfin was listed as endangered on October 13, 1970. Its critical habitat was first proposed in 1977, then withdrawn under the 1978 Act amendments.
- The Virgin River chub was listed as endangered on August 24, 1989, initially as the subspecies *G. robusta seminuda*. In 1992, DeMarais et al. elevated it to full species status as *Gila seminuda*, and the Service and the American Fisheries Society accepted that change.
- The Muddy River population of the chub, geographically isolated by Lake Mead, was excluded from this designation and is being considered separately.

**Regulatory history**

- The Virgin River Fishes Recovery Plan (1995) superseded earlier woundfin recovery plans and covers both fishes.
- A 1995 proposed rule (60 FR 17296) recommended designating 94.8 miles. A public hearing was held in St. George, Utah, on May 9, 1995. The Service received 14 written comment letters and testimony from 6 people.
- Listing Priority Guidance placed critical habitat designation last in priority. A December 1998 Tenth Circuit ruling on the Rio Grande silvery minnow then removed that justification, and a hearing on the Virgin River case was scheduled for August 10, 1999.

**Primary constituent elements**

The essential features identified for both species are:

- **Water:** sufficient quality (temperature, dissolved oxygen, contaminants, nutrients, turbidity) delivered under an appropriate hydrologic regime.
- **Physical habitat:** river channels, side and secondary channels, backwaters and springs used for spawning, nursery, feeding and rearing, plus connecting corridors.
- **Biological environment:** food supply, predation and competition. Predation and competition are out of balance because of nonnative fishes.

**Species life history (summary)**

- **Woundfin:** an omnivore that uses runs and quiet waters beside riffles over sand substrates. Adults are typically found at 0.5–1.4 ft depth and 0.8–1.6 ft/s velocity. Spawning occurs during declining spring runoff, and timing varies with snowmelt. Larvae use backwaters with filamentous algae.
- **Virgin River chub:** adults and juveniles use slow-to-moderate velocity deep runs and pools with boulders or cover and sand substrates, in velocities up to 2.5 ft/s. Young feed on debris and invertebrates, while adults feed almost exclusively on filamentous algae. Spawning has been recorded in April–June, but timing is not well documented.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Basin setting**

- The Virgin River is a tributary of the Colorado River, about 320 km (200 mi) long, and drains about 15,281 km² (5,900 mi²). About 59% of the basin is in Utah, 33% in Arizona and 8% in Nevada.
- It is a typical desert stream with highly fluctuating flows, high salinity, temperature and turbidity. La Verkin Springs contributes about 12–19 cfs at 35–39 °C with a salinity of 9,650 mg/L.
- Mean annual flow at Littlefield, Arizona (water years 1930–1992), is 170,600 acre-feet.

**Land ownership along the designated reaches**

- Lengths by state: 37.3 miles in Utah (Washington County), 31.6 miles in Arizona (Mohave County) and 18.6 miles in Nevada (Clark County).
- Shoreline ownership: federal lands 50.5 miles (57.7%), state lands 2.1 miles (2.4%) and private lands 34.9 miles (39.9%).

**Threats and nonnative species**

- Dam construction, water diversions, dewatering and altered hydrographs, nonnative species and habitat loss have caused declines in all six native fishes.
- The native fish fauna includes six species. Fourteen introduced fish species are recorded, and the [[Red Shiner]] is the most numerous and most problematic. It competes with and preys on eggs and early life stages. Black bullhead and channel catfish also prey on both listed fishes.
- Crayfish (Astacidae) may prey on larval and young-of-year native fishes.
- Invertebrates are sparse. Drifting taxa include chironomids, oligochaetes, blackfly larvae, caddisflies, mayflies, cladocerans, ostracods and copepods.

**Other wildlife of concern**

- The [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (federally endangered) and [[Bald Eagle]] (federally endangered in the Appendix A listing) use riparian habitat included in critical habitat.
- The [[Desert Tortoise]] is federally threatened but does not occupy the river or its floodplain.
- Appendix A lists about 200 native vertebrates and several invertebrates, with state and federal status codes. Only the most relevant entries are discussed here.

**Human context**

- **Washington County, Utah:** population rose from 48,560 in 1990 to a projected 380,592 by 2040. The services sector is the largest economic sector (31.2% of output). Irrigated agriculture is the largest per-capita water user, with 40% on-field efficiency.
- **Mojave County, Arizona:** sparsely populated, with little economic activity. The town of Littlefield is the main settlement.
- **Clark County, Nevada:** population was 741,459 in 1990 and is projected to reach 2,103,500 by 2040. Services account for 48.2% of output and recreation for 15.1%.

## Key Findings

**Alternatives and proposed action**

- **Alternative B (preferred)** designates 87.5 miles of the mainstem Virgin River and its 100-year floodplain. It would protect approximately 12.5% of the woundfin's historic range and 65.8% of the Virgin River chub's historic range.
- The lower boundary was moved to Halfway Wash after public comment showed that the lower river, affected by sand deposition from [[Lake Mead]] backwater, is unsuitable habitat. Very few woundfin or chub have been collected below Halfway Wash.
- **Alternative C** is 7.3 miles longer, all in Nevada. Its impacts are essentially the same as Alternative B's.
- Areas excluded from designation include the [[Gila River Basin]] (nonessential experimental population), the lower La Verkin Creek reach, the Quail Creek Diversion to Pah Tempe Springs reach, and the Muddy River chub population.

**Regulatory effects**

- Designation does not create a wilderness area or refuge and does not prohibit human access or use. It mainly adds a critical-habitat analysis to [[Section 7 Consultation]] for federal actions.
- Activities on private land, such as farming, grazing or home construction, are generally not affected.
- Developed lands excluded from critical habitat include paved roads, bridges, levees, railways, urban areas, canals outside the channel, active gravel pits and cultivated fields.

**Economic analysis (Brookshire et al. 1995, confirmed current in 1999)**

- Analysis covered 1995–2040 using three scenarios: a baseline "without fish" scenario (WOFBA), a "with fish" structural scenario (WFST) and a "with fish" conservation scenario (WFCO).
- Under WFST, the present-value output change is:
  - Washington County: −$47.5 million (0.0016% of baseline); annualized −$1.95 million.
  - Clark County: −$10.63 million; annualized −$0.43 million.
  - Region: −$59.8 million (0.0001% of baseline); annualized −$2.45 million.
- Under WFCO, Washington County output falls by −$13.7 million (0.00046%) and the region by −$20.9 million, which the assessment describes as an almost complete mitigation of effects. There are no conservation-scenario impacts for Clark County.
- Impacts are mainly timing effects: earlier agricultural conversion, earlier water delivery projects, and small reductions in hydroelectric revenue for the Washington County Water Conservancy District.

**Cumulative impacts and conclusions**

- The assessment finds no cumulatively significant impact. Population growth will require water delivery projects and agricultural conversion regardless of designation.
- Benefits of designation are considered limited because of existing efforts: the [[Virgin Spinedace Conservation Agreement]], the [[Virgin River Fishes Recovery Plan]], the [[Red Shiner]] eradication project and the proposed Virgin River Resource Management and Recovery Program (currently planned for Utah only).
- The assessment concludes that a finding of no significant impact (FONSI) would be appropriate, unless the Service decides the impacts are significant, in which case an environmental impact statement would be prepared.

**Comparison of alternatives**

- The No Action alternative would keep the existing Section 7 requirements but would not designate critical habitat, which the assessment says would ignore the legal requirement and the court order.
- Alternative B and Alternative C produce nearly identical effects, so the discussion focuses on Alternative B.
