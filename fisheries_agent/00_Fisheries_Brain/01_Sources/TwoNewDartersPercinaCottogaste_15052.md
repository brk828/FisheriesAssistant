# Two New Darters, *Percina (Cottogaster)*, from the Southeastern United States, with a Review of the Subgenus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Tulane University Museum of Natural History Occasional Paper (Number 4, 1994) by [[Royal D. Suttkus]], [[Bruce A. Thompson]] and [[Henry L. Bart Jr.]] describes two new darter species, *Percina aurora* (the [[Pearl Darter]]) and *Percina brevicauda* (the [[Coal Darter]]), and revises the subgenus [[Cottogaster]], which had long been regarded as monotypic and characterized mainly from the [[Channel Darter]] (*Percina copelandi*). The authors also review the complex nomenclatural history of the genus, reject a close relationship between Cottogaster and subgenus [[Imostoma]], and propose a closer affinity with a restricted subgenus [[Alvordius]]. Both new species are assessed as threatened by habitat loss and sedimentation, with *P. aurora* possibly extirpated from much of its former range.

## Background & Description

- **Subgenus history:** Cottogaster was erected by [[Putnam]] (1863) for a form Thompson (1853) had called *Boleosoma tessellatum*. The name *Rheocrypta copelandi* was proposed by [[Jordan]] (1877a) for the channel darter. Between 1863 and 1896 the group was moved repeatedly among genera (Cottogaster, Boleosoma, Etheostoma, Rheocrypta, Hadropterus, Imostoma). [[Collette and Knapp]] (1967) later synonymized several names with *P. copelandi*.
- **Prior diagnosis:** Earlier treatments relied mainly on the absence of a premaxillary frenum. [[Winn]] (1953) judged the rudimentary air bladder to be variable and of little systematic value.
- **Material examined:** Specimens came from about 20 institutional collections, with 2,017 *P. copelandi* examined in total. Counts and measurements followed [[Hubbs and Lagler]] (1958), with 16–17 body measurements analyzed statistically using Student-Newman-Keuls tests and principal components analysis (SAS).
- **Diagnostic features:**
  - *P. aurora*: largest of the three species (maximum 64 mm SL); nuptial males lack tubercular ridges and tubercles; cheek usually fully scaled; nape naked in a wide strip (about 10–12 scale rows); modified belly scales with high numbers of marginal spines (mean 8.5).
  - *P. brevicauda*: smallest species (maximum 50 mm SL); nuptial males heavily pigmented; cheek typically naked; longest snout and shortest pectoral and caudal fins; low number of marginal spines (mean 3.9); tubercular ridges well developed.
  - *P. copelandi*: maximum 52 mm SL; squamation variable by region; modified belly scales in males range from 2 to 16.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preferences:** *P. copelandi* is found in moderate to fast current over gravel, rubble or bedrock for spawning, and uses slower current over sand after spawning. The authors dispute [[Page]] (1983), who described it as a quiet-pool benthic species, and note that *P. copelandi* overwinters in quiet water. Historic reports mention Lake Erie beaches and shoals, and rapids on the White, Green and Obey rivers.
- **Pearl Darter habitat:** The only reproductive site found was the rapids of Strong River, a tributary of the Pearl River, where riverweed (*Podostemum ceratophyllum*) once covered the bedrock. The authors report siltation has since increased substantially, harming both riverweed and the darter.
- **Coal Darter habitat:** Found in the Cahaba River over rubble, gravel and bedrock riffles with water willow (*Justicia americana*) and riverweed. Spawning occurred from early May to early June at water temperatures of 18–23 °C.
- **Spawning:** *P. copelandi* spawns in June–July in the Great Lakes region and mid-March to mid-April in southwestern populations. *P. aurora* ripe males were recorded from February to May, at water temperatures of 8–21 °C.
- **Associated species:** The authors list about 60 species at the Pearl Darter type locality (including [[Ictalurus]] catfishes, [[Notropis]] shiners and [[Lepomis]] sunfishes), and about 40 species with the Coal Darter, including [[Cyprinella]] and [[Micropterus]] species.
- **Hybridization:** [[Hubbs]] (1955) reported hybrids between *Percina* species. [[Thompson and Hartel]] (1994) documented six hybrids between *P. copelandi* and *P. caprodes semifasciata*.
- **Conservation status:**
  - *P. copelandi* has been listed as rare, endangered, or of special concern in Ontario, Kentucky, Virginia, Tennessee and Michigan, and as "Vulnerable" in Canada. It has declined sharply in Lake Erie and disappeared from the Maumee River.
  - *P. aurora* was listed as "Threatened" in Louisiana and Mississippi by [[Deacon et al.]] (1979). No specimens were collected from Strong River after July 1971, from Pearl River (Pearl River County) after October 1972, or from Lawrence County after 1973, despite roughly 24–32 annual samples there from 1972 to 1994. The authors consider natural recovery "highly unlikely."
  - *P. brevicauda* is threatened by habitat destruction (listed "T" by [[Williams et al.]] 1989). The Cahaba population appears viable, while the Coosa and Black Warrior populations are small and isolated.

## Key Findings

- The subgenus *Cottogaster* now comprises three species: *P. copelandi*, *P. aurora* and *P. brevicauda*. The authors conclude the subgenus is not closely related to *Imostoma*, citing differences in breeding ornamentation and the lack of shared derived characters.
- *Cottogaster* shares three characters with a restricted *Alvordius* (six species): strongly developed ventral scales, an enlarged mid-breast scale in males, and quadrate to oblong lateral blotches.
- Meristic counts for *P. copelandi* overlap widely with the other two species. Lateral-line scales averaged 50.8 in northern populations and 58.2 in the Red River system. Vertebrae ranged from 36 to 41 (mean 38.4 in 1,022 specimens).
- A premaxillary frenum appears in about 20% of *P. copelandi* (36% in Great Lakes specimens), 23% of *P. aurora*, and only 3% of *P. brevicauda*.
- *P. copelandi* from the Ohio River drainage has longer pectoral fins and shorter upper jaws than other populations. *P. brevicauda* males have the shortest pectoral and dorsal spines.
- Principal components analysis showed that *P. aurora* separated from the other species on body size, but after size adjustment it clustered with them. *P. brevicauda* males separated most strongly, with smaller fins.
- The authors reject the earlier view of [[Page]] (1974, 1976) that the ventral modified scales are caducous, and they consider breeding tubercles weakly developed and not homologous with those of *Imostoma*.
- The authors suggest that the reproductive advantage of modified belly scales in these species may be replaced in *Imostoma* by enlarged tuberculate anal fins. This is a hypothesis they propose for future testing.
- Historic records suggest *P. brevicauda* was more continuously distributed across the Black Warrior and Coosa systems before impoundment.
- The authors recommend monitoring all remaining populations and taking immediate action in some places to halt habitat destruction, because the continued fragmentation of remnant populations by impoundments and stream degradation reduces the chance of recovery.
