# Roundtail Chub and Headwater Chub: 12-Month Petition Finding (2006)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a Federal Register package (May 3, 2006) containing three items. Two are FCC radio-channel allotment notices (Kentucky and Tennessee FM tables), which contain no biological content. The substantive item is a [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) 12-month finding on a petition to list fishes of the cyprinid genus *Gila*. The Service found that listing a [[Distinct Population Segment]] (DPS) of [[Roundtail Chub]] in the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] is *not warranted*, but that listing the [[Headwater Chub]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] is *warranted*. Immediate listing is precluded by higher-priority listing actions, so the headwater chub was added to the candidate species list. Its main threats are predation and competition by nonnative fishes, habitat destruction and fragmentation, and wildfire.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Mixed Federal Register issue. The FCC notice (MB Docket No. 00-22, adopted April 5, 2006) proposes FM channel changes in Kentucky and Tennessee. The USFWS notice (50 CFR Part 17, signed April 27, 2006, by Director Dale Hall) addresses the petitioned listings.
- **Taxa:** The roundtail chub and headwater chub are both cyprinid fishes of the Colorado and Gila basins. The headwater chub was earlier treated as a population within a broader taxon (*G. robusta* group) and was reviewed through a status and nomenclature history in the source.
- **Roundtail chub:** Historical range covered the upper and lower Colorado River basins in WY, UT, CO, NM, AZ, and likely NV, Baja California, and Sonora, Mexico. Upper and lower basin populations have been physically separated by [[Glen Canyon Dam]].
- **Headwater chub:** Described as a fish with dark gray to brown overall coloration. It is restricted to the Gila basin and adjacent drainages in AZ and NM, and it was historically a predatory fish in a community with low species diversity.
- **Petition history:** The petition was reviewed through a 90-day finding and subsequent status review, with public comment and consultation with recognized experts and state and federal agencies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Basin differences (roundtail chub):**
- Lower basin populations occur in the Tonto Transition and Painted Desert sections of the Colorado Plateau Semi-Desert Province and in the White Mountain–San Francisco Peaks–Mogollon Rim section. Upper basin populations occur in the Northern Canyonlands, Uinta Basin, Tavaputs Plateau, and Utah High Plateaus sections.
- Precipitation is given as 3–65 in/yr for the upper basin and 5–25 in/yr for the lower basin (values as printed in the source).
- Lower basin streams have a [[Monsoon Hydrograph]] (or mixed monsoon–snowmelt), with abrupt, highly variable flood peaks. Upper basin streams have a [[Snowmelt Hydrograph]] with low variability.
- Lower basin habitat has lower gradient, smaller substrate, higher water temperatures and salinity, smaller base flows, higher flood peaks, and greater erosion.

**Headwater chub habitat and status:**
- Habitat is lost once surface flow drops below 0.3 m³/s (10 cubic feet per second).
- Of 19 known populations (Voeltz 2002), 16 remain extant, occupying about 20% of the former range; the species has been extirpated from about 50% of its historical range (figures as printed in the source).
- Voeltz (2002) classed populations as stable-secure, stable-threatened, unstable-threatened, threatened, extirpated, or unknown. Six extant populations were considered stable, but the Service believes all six have a high likelihood of extirpation in the foreseeable future because nonnative species are present in them.
- Populations are fragmented and isolated in four separate drainages, including the Verde River system, the Tonto Creek system, and the upper Gila River.

## Key Findings

### 1. Roundtail chub (lower Colorado River DPS): not warranted
- The DPS status of lower basin populations was not conclusively resolved, because genetic data came from small samples (DeMarais 1992; Dowling and DeMarais 1993; Minckley and DeMarais 2000; Gerber et al. 2001).
- The Service found the lower basin populations **not significant** to the taxon. Ecological differences exist, but they do not produce significant life-history differences.
- Genetic evidence is sparse, but roundtail chubs from Chevelon Creek (Little Colorado River drainage) share an mtDNA haplotype with upper basin fish.
- Loss of the lower basin population would not remove a form of the species found in a unique setting. The southern lower basin is the southern edge of the range, and there are no records from Mexico.

### 2. Headwater chub: listing warranted, but precluded
- The headwater chub was determined to be a valid taxon. Threats are high in magnitude but not immediate.
- Immediate listing is precluded by higher-priority actions. Over 280 species are candidates, 95 of which share the headwater chub's listing priority. Almost the entire FY2006 listing budget was committed to court orders, settlements, and statutory deadlines.
- The species was added to the candidate list on publication. An emergency listing was not warranted because several populations remain and some appear stable, but the status will be monitored.

### 3. Principal threats (Section 4(a)(1) factors)
- **Factor A (habitat destruction/modification):** Groundwater pumping, surface diversions, impoundments, channelization, grazing, mining, roads, logging, and development. Water withdrawal threatens at least 6 of 16 extant populations. Channelization affects at least 50% of extant populations. Mining occurs in at least 6 of 16 extant populations. Logging is a land use in 13 of the 16 streams. Roads occur in every drainage with extant populations. Urban and suburban development affects about 25% of known populations.
- **Factor B (overutilization):** Not considered a threat in Arizona (angler catch is light). Discarding of chub by anglers in the upper Gila River (NM) may affect local populations.
- **Factor C (disease or predation):** Considered the most serious and persistent threat. Nonnative fishes occur in every known population, and they caused range-wide declines and local extirpations (for example, Christopher Creek, Rye Creek, Horton Creek).
- **Factor D (inadequate regulatory mechanisms):** No specific federal protections exist. Live bait use of nine nonnative species is legal in Arizona, and enforcement of fish-introduction rules is difficult. Goldfish were formerly allowed as bait in New Mexico and are no longer.
- **Factor E (other natural or manmade factors):** Wildfire and post-fire ash flows (for example, the 1990 Dude Fire, the 2002 Rodeo-Chediski Fire, and the 2004 Willow Fire), parasitism, and small isolated populations vulnerable to stochastic events.

### 4. Nonnative species and parasites
- Introduced fishes include channel and flathead catfish, green sunfish, largemouth and smallmouth bass, rainbow trout, carp, and goldfish, among others. Nonnative crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii* and *Orconectes virilis*) occur in at least four streams.
- Miller et al. (1989) concluded that introduced nonnatives were a causal factor in 68% of North American fish extinctions in the last 100 years.
- The Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) and anchor worm (*Lernaea cyprinacea*) are documented threats. Heavy Lernaea infections were reported in Gun Creek during low-flow conditions.

### 5. Conservation recommendations and actions
- Listing will proceed as priorities allow. Critical habitat determinations will be made during the proposed rule.
- The Service will continue accepting information on the species.
- The U.S. Forest Service manages much of the current range and is required under the [[National Forest Management Act]] and related acts to manage habitat for species viability.
- Continued restriction of live bait use is expected to reduce nonnative input, but it will not address unauthorized bait use or [[Bait-Bucket Transfer]] of aquarium fish.
