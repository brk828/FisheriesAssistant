# Growth of Brown Trout in Selected Pennsylvania Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the seasonal growth, length-weight relationship, and condition of wild [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) in three Pennsylvania streams during a single year-class study (1958–59). Growth in length and condition followed similar seasonal patterns in all three streams, with rapid growth in spring and little growth in winter. [[Spruce Creek]] trout had the highest average condition and the greatest mean length after two growing seasons. Growth in weight was analyzed in Spruce Creek using a [[Specific Growth Rate]] approach. The authors argue that fishery managers should rely more on data from natural populations rather than on put-and-take stocking.

## Background & Description

The authors note that growth studies in fishes are important both for ecologists studying productivity and for fishery managers aiming to maximize yield. Most earlier brown trout growth work reported annual length increments. Studies on hatchery-reared fish under laboratory conditions (Brown, 1946a–c, 1951) provided useful insight but could not be directly applied to self-sustaining wild populations. This study therefore examined seasonal growth of wild trout in three streams over one year, focusing on the two youngest age groups (age-groups 0 and I). Trout older than 2 years were scarce because of natural and angling mortality.

**Study areas**

- **[[Spruce Creek]]:** A headwater stream draining a limestone-rich valley in the Ridge and Valley Province of the Appalachians. Total alkalinity was 130–135 ppm as CaCO₃.
- **[[Kettle Creek]]:** Drains a hilly portion of the Allegheny Plateau. Total alkalinity was 10–15 ppm.
- **East Branch of the [[Tionesta Creek]]:** A tributary of the Allegheny River in the Mississippi drainage. Total alkalinity was 15–20 ppm.

All three streams are subject to severe flooding and appeared to have similar flow volumes during the study, though flow was not measured.

**Fish community.** Table 1 lists 23 species collected across the three sites by electrofishing. In Spruce Creek, brown trout and [[Slimy Sculpin]] (*Cottus cognatus*) made up most of the population. Kettle Creek and Tionesta Creek supported brown trout alongside many other species, including cyprinids, percids, and centrarchids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling.** Fish were collected by [[Electrofishing]] until adequate numbers of age-groups 0 and I were captured. Roughly 30 fish per sample was judged sufficient to detect 5% differences in mean length at the 95% probability level (following Carlander, 1956). Electrofishing is biased toward larger fish within a year class, which inflates mean lengths. The authors judged this bias uniform enough not to affect between-season or between-stream comparisons. Efficiency depended on water conductivity, turbidity, stream velocity, and operator skill. Low conductivity and high water limited sampling in Kettle Creek.

**Sampling schedule.** Sampling in Spruce Creek began March 7, 1958. Collections were made every 2 weeks from April 11 to August 2 and monthly from August 1958 to February 1959. Complete seasonal data were not available for Kettle Creek and Tionesta Creek because study areas were selected late and high water and icy roads prevented later collections.

**Seasonal growth environment.** The authors link the seasonal pattern to temperature, food availability, and behavior, citing laboratory work by Brown (1946c). They note that the cause of seasonal growth changes in wild populations is not yet fully explained.

## Key Findings

**1. Seasonal growth in length**
- The seasonal growth pattern was similar across all three populations.
- About half of annual length growth occurred from mid-April to mid-June.
- Growth was almost absent from December through March.
- Estimated mean total length after two complete growing seasons was 7.0, 7.8, and 8.5 inches for the three streams, respectively.
- Spruce Creek trout had a significantly greater mean length than the other two populations at the end of the 2-year period.
- Spruce Creek fish gained an early growth advantage in the first season. The authors partly attribute this to greater food abundance (citing Frost, 1945) or lower competition from other fishes.
- Yearling and young-of-the-year fish appeared to start spring growth at different times. The authors attribute the apparent difference to extended recruitment of swim-up fry, which emerged from about March 21 into early May.

**2. Length-weight relationship**
- Weight was computed from the formula w = a·lᵇ (following Ricker, 1958), where w is weight in grams and l is total length in inches.
- The exponent b ranged from 2.94 to 3.01 across the three populations, close to the cube law value of 3.0.
- Comparisons with [[Lake Windermere]] brown trout (b = 3.0, Brown, 1957) and wild trout in [[Crystal Creek]], New York (b = 3.003, Schuck, 1942) were similar.
- The relationship was computed from pooled data across all seasons, which the authors considered more representative than single-date samples.

**3. Seasonal condition**
- Condition was computed with the index of Cooper and Benson (1951), where weight (grams) is multiplied by 10 and divided by length cubed.
- Seasonal changes in condition were similar among streams.
- Condition was lowest in fall and winter, at about 1.50, and highest in June, at about 1.75.
- Spruce Creek trout had the highest condition for most of the year, and Tionesta Creek trout were generally in better condition than Kettle Creek trout.
- The period of most rapid length growth coincided with the rise in condition in spring. Growth slowed when condition declined in summer.

**4. Growth in weight (Spruce Creek only)**
- Specific growth rate was calculated as the percentage increase in weight per day, using a modified method from Brown (1946a).
- In the first year, the specific growth rate fell from about 5.5–5.6% of body weight per day in early June to negative values in November and December.
- The apparent rise in May may be an artifact of continued recruitment of small fish.
- In the second year, the specific growth rate rose to a high of 3.1% per day in April (abstract) or about 3.0% (main text), then declined to small negative values by November.
- Weights for young-of-the-year fish were estimated from the length-weight relationship and yearling condition data, because young fish were not weighed in the field.
- Growth may occur in interrupted rapid pulses rather than as a smooth function of time, as Brown (1946b) observed in laboratory fish.

**5. Comparison with other populations**
- Growth in length was near the middle of values reported for brown trout (Frost, 1945; Allen, 1951) and somewhat faster than trout from Crystal Creek, New York (Schuck, 1945).
- The seasonal specific growth pattern resembled that of the [[Horokiwi Stream]] trout in New Zealand (Allen, 1951). Spruce Creek fish, however, dropped to a much lower winter growth rate. The authors suggest both populations have similar maximum growth capacity, but Spruce Creek fish grow more slowly over a longer period.

**6. Alkalinity and productivity**
- The authors note that the relationship between alkalinity and trout production is poorly understood.
- Spruce Creek combined high alkalinity with low competition from other fishes, and it showed faster growth than the two low-alkalinity streams with more competing species.

**7. Year-to-year variability**
- Between-stream differences in growth were larger than between-year differences.
- First-year growth of the 1957 and 1958 year-classes in Spruce Creek was nearly identical. If confirmed, long-term multi-year sampling of a stream may not be needed to estimate growth rates for management.

**8. Fisheries management implications**
- Seasonal growth data can help predict when population biomass peaks and estimate recruitment to a sport fishery.
- Under the 6-inch [[Minimum Size Limit]] in effect in Pennsylvania at the time, most trout in these streams reached legal size by July of their second year.
- Only a few males and almost no females were mature after 2 years, so the effects of angling on spawning stocks should be investigated.
- The authors criticize the reliance on [[Put-And-Take Stocking]] programs, arguing that they neglect the natural populations that supply most trout to anglers. They recommend more research on the population dynamics of wild fish and greater use of such data in setting regulations (citing Ricker, 1945, and Allen, 1954).
