# Rhinichthys deaconi, A New Species of Dace (Pisces: Cyprinidae) from Southern Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 taxonomic paper in the *Occasional Papers of the Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan* (No. 707, by Robert Rush Miller) describes *Rhinichthys deaconi*, the [[Las Vegas Dace]], a now-extinct freshwater cyprinid from [[Las Vegas Creek]] in the [[Colorado River Basin]] of southern Nevada. The description is based on 777 specimens collected between 1891 and 1940. The species belongs to the [[Rhinichthys Oscuhts Complex]] (subgenus *Apocope*) and is diagnosed chiefly by its unusually shaped anal fin and tiny pectoral fins. The paper also reconstructs the species' historic and pre-historic habitat and discusses its relationships with other western dace populations.

## Background & Description

- **Genus context:** *Rhinichthys* (daces) are widely distributed in fresh waters of Canada, the United States and northern Mexico. Five species in three complexes are recognized: the *R. atratulus*, *R. cataractae* and *R. osculus* complexes. [[Speckled Dace]] (*R. osculus*) is the most ubiquitous and variable fish in western North America.
- **Diagnosis:** The new species differs from other members of the *R. osculus* complex in having:
  - a low-profile anal fin whose first ray is about equal in length to the last;
  - tiny pectoral fins (10–14 rays), reaching at most slightly more than half way to the pelvic fins in nuptial males;
  - a short, weakly forked caudal fin and a long mandible;
  - a narrow, oblique, terminal to subterminal mouth with a virtually absent maxillary barbel;
  - a premaxillary frenum usually absent (present in 8 of 40 specimens);
  - very large scales (40–52 along the incomplete lateral line), the largest in the complex.
- **Meristics:** Dorsal rays 6–8 (usually 7 or 8); anal rays 6–8 (usually 7); pelvic rays typically 7. The dental formula is typically 1,4-4,1.
- **Size and sexual dimorphism:** Adult females reach 62 mm standard length (SL); males only 42 mm. Males have larger pectoral fins and develop nuptial tubercles on pectoral rays 2–4, a feature absent in females.
- **Type material:** Holotype UMMZ 125007 (adult female, 58.3 mm SL), collected 30 August 1938 by C. L. Hubbs and family; allotype UMMZ 211169 (male, 42.0 mm SL); 734 paratopotypes (UMMZ 125008); additional paratypes from Las Vegas Creek and Lorenzi Ranch.
- **Coloration:** In preservative, adult females show a prominent dark lateral stripe and dorsal splotches; males have weaker mid-lateral stripes and a black blotch near the caudal-fin base.
- **Etymology:** Named for [[James E. Deacon]], whose conservation work and ecological studies on southwestern fishes are acknowledged.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic habitat:** Las Vegas Creek was fed by three springs ([[Little Spring]], Middle Spring and Big Spring). In 1903 the creek's discharge was about 4.74 ft³/s (per the source's reading). In 1938 it was roughly 1.6–1.8 m wide, with sand, gravel and mud bottoms, a water temperature of 25.5 °C, and dense cattails. Shoal currents varied from moderate to swift.
- **Decline:** Flow was progressively reduced by water withdrawals. By 1947 flow was restricted to winter months. The last known collection was in 1940; the fish likely survived until about 1955–1957, when artesian-basin withdrawal policy changed, and it was extinct before 1961.
- **Pluvial history:** [[Pluvial Las Vegas Lake]] existed from about 30,000 to 15,000 years before present (BP). Pollen records show a shift toward warmer, drier conditions, with sagebrush–shadscale vegetation by about 12,000 BP and a Mojave-type vegetation by about 7,000 BP. The Las Vegas River became a dry wash between 4,000 and 1,000 years ago.
- **Fossil record:** Fossils from the [[Tule Springs Archaeological Site]] contain no fish remains, which the author regards as surprising given the presumed continuous drainage.
- **Related populations:** Quiet-water spring dace in western Utah, Nevada and adjacent California are the closest relatives. Along [[Meadow Valley Wash]], dace from tributary springs show the same quiet-water traits (chubby bodies, small rounded fins), even though the wash itself is highly stressed and fluctuating.
- **Associated species:** The nearby [[Pahrump Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys latos*) survives in a sanctuary near the former Las Vegas Creek area.

## Key Findings

- *Rhinichthys deaconi* is a distinct, now-extinct species, diagnosed by its unique anal fin and tiny pectoral fins, and is the sole member of the Las Vegas lineage described here.
- Based on 777 specimens (1891–1940), the species probably disappeared around 1952 and was definitely gone before 1961.
- Quiet-water spring populations of the *R. osculus* complex share a set of traits that contrast sharply with the swift-water Colorado River forms (elongate bodies, large falcate fins, well-developed frenum and barbel).
- The species' extinction was linked to groundwater withdrawal from the artesian basin beneath Las Vegas, which ended spring-fed flow.
- The study suggests further unrecognized taxa exist within the widely distributed *R. osculus* complex, including an undescribed Utah dace.
- *Rhinichthys oscuhts velifer* is confined to the Pahranagat Valley, midway in the pluvial White River basin, and is the only current-adapted form in those areas.
- *Rhinichthys lariversi*, described by Lugaski (1972), is treated provisionally: the author reserves judgment on its validity because the original data do not clearly support subspecific recognition.
