# Use of Genetic Marks to Assess Stock Dynamics and Management Programs for Chum Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #population-genetics #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This study (Seeb, Seeb & Utter 1986) applied **genetic marking**—the intentional manipulation of allelic frequencies via selective breeding—to a hatchery-supplemented population of [[Chum Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus keta*) at Kennedy Creek, a tributary of Totten Inlet in southern Puget Sound, Washington. By selecting male spawners carrying the rare *Aat1,2(115)* and *Pgdh(90)* alleles, researchers shifted allele frequencies in hatchery progeny well above native baseline levels, creating a distinguishable genetic "tag" for the stocked cohort. This allowed (1) a Petersen mark-recapture census of chum salmon smolts in Totten Inlet, and (2) quantification of the contribution of hatchery-marked fish to subsequent adult spawning runs. The work validated genetic marking as a viable, non-invasive alternative to physical tagging for small-bodied, rapidly seaward-migrating species like chum and pink salmon.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: [[Chum Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus keta*), family Salmonidae; comparisons drawn to [[Coho Salmon]] and [[Chinook Salmon]], which are large enough for conventional tagging, unlike chum and [[Pink Salmon]], which migrate to seawater almost immediately after hatching.
- **Problem addressed**: Conventional physical tagging methods (microtagging, fin-clipping) require individual handling, which is impractical for small-bodied fry of chum and pink salmon.
- **Genetic marking concept**: Uses naturally occurring, Mendelian-inherited allelic (allozyme) variation as a "tag." When a hatchery stock lacks sufficient natural genetic distinctiveness, its allele frequencies can be deliberately shifted through selective breeding to create an identifiable signature distinguishable from wild conspecifics.
- **Precedents**: Genetic marking previously proposed/applied in salmonids (Utter et al. 1966, 1974; Reisenbichler & McIntyre 1977), marine larvae (Jamieson 1974), walleyes (Clayton et al. 1974), carp (Moav et al. 1976), and American lobsters (Hedgecock et al. 1976).
- **Marker loci selected**:
  - *Aat1,2* (aspartate aminotransferase, EC 2.6.1.1) — variant allele **115** (common allele 100); loci could not be resolved individually, so genotypes were scored across combined loci (five possible genotype classes).
  - *Pgdh* (6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase, EC 1.1.1.44) — variant allele **90** (common allele 100); three genotype classes.
  - Both loci expressed in muscle tissue and resolvable with the same electrophoretic buffer, facilitating sampling.
  - Additional loci screened but not used as marks: *Aat3* and *Mpi* (vitreous humor).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Location**: Kennedy Creek, a small tributary of Totten Inlet, southern [[Puget Sound]], Washington, USA (North Pacific drainage). See [[Totten Inlet]] and [[Tacoma Narrows]] as key geographic features controlling smolt emigration routes.
- **Pre-project wild run**: 8,000–10,000 wild adult chum salmon annually, with no hatchery fish present prior to the program.
- **Hatchery method**: Washington Department of Fisheries (WDF) implemented streamside gravel box incubators (per [[Bams 1972]]/[[Bams 1974]] methodology) beginning fall 1976, which reduce egg/larval mortality relative to natural, less-controlled conditions.
- **Migration pathway**: All emigrating smolts from southern Puget Sound tributaries must pass through the Tacoma Narrows, a natural bottleneck—identified as an ideal future sampling point for broader stock-mixing studies.
- **Smolt residency**: Juvenile chum salmon were present in Totten Inlet in shallow-water concentrations for approximately 6 weeks each spring.
- **Life history**: Chum salmon mature at 3, 4, or 5 years of age; marked adults began returning in 1979 (as 3-year-olds), with age composition across the run of 3% five-year-olds, 75% four-year-olds, and 22% three-year-olds.

## Key Findings

### Broodstock & Rearing Statistics (1976–1980)
- Broodstock numbers, effective number of spawners (*Ne*), and inbreeding coefficients (ΔF) were tracked annually (Table 1).
- Inbreeding coefficients (ΔF) ranged from **3.5 × 10⁻⁴ to 1.6 × 10⁻³** — substantially below the ~10⁻¹ threshold at which measurable inbreeding depression effects have been documented (cf. [[Kincaid 1976]]).
- Total juveniles released 1976–1980: approximately **11 × 10⁶** hatched fish released to Kennedy Creek.

### Allele Frequency Shifts
- Baseline (native) frequencies: *Aat1,2(115)* ≈ 0.12; *Pgdh(90)* ≈ 0.005–0.01.
- Marked progeny frequencies: *Aat1,2(115)* shifted to **0.214–0.321**; *Pgdh(90)* shifted to **0.040–0.121**, depending on brood year (Table 2).
- Of 15 possible combined *Aat1,2*/*Pgdh* genotypes, **7 occurred consistently** and showed marked frequency shifts between wild adults and marked progeny (Table 3).
- Projected equilibrium frequencies for the Kennedy Creek stock (post full life-cycle integration): ~0.20 for *Aat1,2(115)* and ~0.04 for *Pgdh(90)*.

### Smolt Population Estimates (Totten Inlet, 1978–1980)
- Marked fish comprised an estimated **15–51%** of sampled juvenile chum salmon per year (Table 4).
- Modified Petersen mark-recapture population estimates for the total (marked + unmarked) smolt population:
  - 1978: **2.2 × 10⁶ ± 0.59 × 10⁶**
  - 1979: **4.3 × 10⁶ ± 0.3 × 10⁶**
  - 1980: **3.4 × 10⁶ ± 0.3 × 10⁶**
- Weekly sampling (~200 fish/week) across the ~6-week smolt residency period was necessary and sufficient to reliably detect marked alleles and produce robust annual estimates; nonrandom mixing of marked/unmarked fish was observed in individual samples, underscoring the importance of repeated sampling over single large samples.

### Adult Return Contributions (Kennedy Creek Spawning Run)
- Marked adults first returned in 1979 (as 3-year-olds).
- Estimated contribution of marked fish to spawning runs (Table 5):
  - 1979: **~6–7%** (two independent calculation methods agreed closely)
  - 1980: **~20%**
  - 1981: **~29%**
- Overall range across 1979–1981: **6–29%** of returning adult spawners were traceable to marked hatchery broods.

### Methodological Contributions
- Validated a **FORTRAN maximum-likelihood mixture analysis** (provided by George Milner, NMFS), comparable to methods of [[Fournier Et Al. 1984]], for estimating proportions of genetically marked fish in mixed juvenile and adult samples.
- Demonstrated genetic marking overcomes key limitations of physical tagging methods:
  - No minimum size requirement (applicable to very small larvae).
  - No mark loss (unlike fin regeneration, tag loss, brand illegibility).
  - No handling-induced stress or differential mortality/behavioral bias.
  - Marks transmitted via simple Mendelian inheritance across generations.
- Limitation: genetic marking identifies **stock-level** origin probabilistically, not individual fish identity, unless both sexes are selectively bred or unique exogenous alleles are introduced.
- Neutrality assumption for *Aat1,2(115)* and *Pgdh(90)* supported by their broad geographic distribution across diverse environments ([[Okazaki 1981]]) and by population genetics theory indicating stochastic processes dominate over selection at these loci ([[Kimura 1982]]).

### Management Implications
- Authors estimate that releasing approximately **10 × 10⁶** genetically marked smolts would be needed to obtain adequate recoveries for a full mark-recapture population census encompassing all of southern Puget Sound (sampling at the Tacoma Narrows bottleneck).
- The differential allele frequency between marked and unmarked populations offers a tool for measuring **straying rates** of chum salmon between adjacent watersheds over multiple generations.
- Findings support genetic marking as broadly applicable in fisheries management wherever artificial propagation occurs, and are compatible with standard hatchery sex-ratio practices (e.g., 1:9 male:female ratio recommended by [[McNeil And Bailey 1975]]).
