# Disposal of Petroleum Wastes on Oil-Producing Properties

#ichthyology #marine-biology #petroleum-pollution

## Executive Summary

This 1930s Bureau of Mines report, prepared with the [[Kansas State Board of Health]], examines how [[Petroleum Wastes]] from oil-producing leases damage soils, vegetation, [[Aquatic Life]], livestock, and waterfowl, and how such wastes can be reduced and disposed of properly. The authors argue that petroleum wastes should be handled separately from [[Oil-Field Brine]], because each requires different methods. The document is not a fish study. Fish appear only in general terms, and its strongest conclusions concern lease "housekeeping," [[Emulsion Treatment]], and [[Oil-Water Separator]] design. A chapter by Ogden S. Jones adds a regional view of soils and water resources in the Kansas oil areas.

## Background & Description

The report opens by defining three categories of petroleum waste:

- Emulsions of petroleum and brine, or petroleum and sand, that cannot be economically broken down.
- Crude oil escaping from pipelines, fittings, and tanks through leaks, connection failures, or accidents.
- Oil-saturated materials that accumulate during normal operation of a lease.

The authors note that brine and petroleum wastes both damage fresh water, vegetation, and aquatic and animal life, but require different disposal methods. Operators were often found to combine them in one system, which the authors consider inefficient. The work was conducted by the [[Bureau of Mines]] (Petroleum Experiment Station, Bartlesville, Oklahoma) in cooperation with the Kansas State Board of Health, and was motivated by the acute problem in agricultural areas.

The Jones chapter, "Soil and Water Resources of the Kansas Oil Areas," describes soil as the portion of the earth's crust that supports plant growth. It estimates that 1 inch of soil takes 500 to 800 years to form. It also notes that Kansas has limited fresh water, and that the state's oil frontier moved from east to west.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Soils and vegetation.** Early experiments cited in the report found that soybean growth improved with 0.75 percent oil by volume, but that 4 percent killed the plants, largely because they could not draw enough water from the soil. In wheat experiments by Murphy, 500 gallons of crude oil per acre mixed into the surface 4 inches reduced the stand by 36 percent. At 2,500 gallons per acre the stand was reduced by 77 percent. Nitrate formation was also suppressed: about 0.4 percent surface oil cut nitrates roughly in half, and about 1 percent mixed into soil practically stopped nitrification. Baldwin's work indicated that these effects can be temporary, since crude oil in soil is gradually broken down. Plant injury by oil is described as both chemical (absorbed volatile products) and physical (blocked transpiration and gas exchange, chlorophyll loss, and premature leaf fall).

**Aquatic life.** Oil pollutes water in two ways: a surface film, and a "bottom pollution blanket" of oil-water emulsions that trap sediment and sink. Fish response depends on species and film thickness, so no general statement is possible. Ellis is cited as stating that 5 parts per million of [[Dissolved Oxygen]] is roughly the lower limit of favorable conditions for varied warm-water fish fauna in inland streams at 20°C or above, and that it is not the lethal point. Surface oil can block reaeration and so lower dissolved oxygen. Lane's coastal survey found that oil destroys the larval forms of shellfish and makes adult mollusks and finfish unfit for food. Galtsoff's studies of [[Oysters]] in Louisiana waters found that dissolved oil substances irritate the ciliated feeding mechanism, and that higher concentrations can stop the flow of water through the gills and slow diatom growth, the oyster's food supply.

**Birds and livestock.** Oil fouls [[Water Fowl]], causing matted feathers, impaired flight, skin irritation, and death from inability to navigate, feed, or regulate body temperature. Injury was reported on the Mississippi Wild-Life and Fish Refuge and the Ouachita River, and at a Potomac River refuge fed by salvage-yard drainage. Livestock data are limited to case records. Cattle that drink or wallow in oil-covered water can be blistered, and some consume crude oil deliberately, causing intoxication and death.

**Human and public use.** Oil films interfere with the gas exchange needed for sewage digestion, which can contribute to epidemics such as typhoid. Oily waste also disrupts aquatic recreation and creates odor and skin irritation. Fresh crude and volatile products on water present an acute fire hazard. Oil can also seep into sand used in plaster and later stain finished walls.

**Regional settings.** Jones describes soils and water resources across several oil areas:

- **Southeast Kansas:** Shallow, impervious soils derived from limestone, sandstone, and shale, especially in the [[Flint Hills]]. Fractured limestone can carry chloride waters for miles, and the region has a high run-off ratio with few beds suitable for ground-water storage.
- **El Dorado area:** Porous Florence Flint beds transport contaminated water, and the western fields sit on more permeable soils with shallow ground water.
- **[[Equus Beds]]:** An alluvial aquifer covering about 600 square miles, used for farm and municipal supply.
- **Northwest of Hutchinson:** Sandy, pervious soils over shallow water beds, where dead trees mark high water conductivity.

**Ground-water movement.** Ground water generally moves toward surface drainage at less than 10 feet per day, so contamination may take years to reach a farm well less than a mile from its source.

## Key Findings

1. **Separate disposal streams.** Petroleum wastes and oil-field brine require different methods and should be handled separately. Combined brine-and-oil ponds were found to be inefficient.

2. **Soil and crop damage.** Crude oil at 500 and 2,500 gallons per acre reduced wheat stands by 36 and 77 percent, respectively. Soil effects may be detrimental but are not necessarily permanent.

3. **Oxygen threshold.** Ellis recommends 5 ppm dissolved oxygen as approximately the lower limit of favorable conditions for warm-water fish in inland streams.

4. **Good housekeeping.** Most lease losses are preventable through maintenance. Key measures include repairing or replacing worn [[Stuffing Boxes]] and bleeder valves, avoiding wood-plug repairs, burying lead lines, and building drainage ditches and sumps. Operators reported that good practice reduces fire hazards, damage claims, worker injuries, and material losses.

5. **Emulsion control.** Emulsions form mainly through agitation. Recommended measures include [[Pressure Separators]], replacing worn pump parts, and shortening and levelling lead lines. Heat and chemicals are the main treatments, and Mississippi limestone production is harder to treat, often needing heat of up to 180°F.

6. **Pond placement and design.** Brine ponds should be sited away from surface run-off. The report recommends a [[Brine Pond]] and a separate preliminary pond for oil wastes, with a lower dividing dike so overflow is trapped.

7. **Separator standards.** The [[American Petroleum Institute]] recommends an average horizontal velocity of no more than 2 feet per minute, a partition velocity of no more than 4 feet per minute, and a displacement time of at least 1 hour. This is expected to yield effluent with no more than 30 ppm oil. Lane's recommendations are 1 to 1.5 feet per minute, a displacement time of 2.5 hours or more, a depth of 6 to 8 feet, and 4 to 10 compartments.

8. **Waste-water company economics.** The Santa Fe Springs cooperative plant (initial cost about $507,000) handled about 72,000 barrels of waste water per day in 1936. Reclaimed oil sales covered almost 60 percent of operating expenses over 1930 to 1935. Operating costs fell from $45,130 in 1930 to $30,663 in 1935, and reclaimed oil fell from 74,900 to 39,156 barrels.

9. **Discharge limits.** At Santa Fe Springs, the maximum oil content of discharged brine was 13 ppm, with a normal minimum of 5 ppm. This equals at most 39.3 gallons of oil per day, and 15.1 gallons under the most efficient operation.

10. **Film visibility.** The report tabulates oil-film thickness against visibility. Oil films of about 0.0000015 inch are barely visible, while 0.0000800 inch shows much darker colors. Stroop found that films up to 0.0000013 inch usually disappeared within 5 hours when the water was agitated, and films of 0.00004 inch usually disappeared within 24 hours.

11. **Fire hazard.** Oil of 175°F flash point on 54°F water could not be ignited by ordinary means at a film thickness of 0.064 inch, which corresponds to about 1,115,000 gallons spread over one square mile. Accumulated oily residues on wharves can still ignite when heated.

12. **Regional protection.** Jones concludes that the Kansas oil areas need strict control of brine disposal, because porous soils and fractured bedrock carry contamination to fresh-water aquifers, farm wells, and surface supplies. Earthen impoundments of brine in porous soils are not recommended.
