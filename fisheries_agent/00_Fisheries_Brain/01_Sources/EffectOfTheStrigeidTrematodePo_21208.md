# Effect of Posthodiplostomum minimum on Growth and Mortality of Bluegill

#ichthyology #marine-biology #lepomis

## Executive Summary

This 1966 experimental study, reprinted in the FAO *World Symposium on Warm-Water Pond Fish Culture* proceedings, tested how infection by the strigeid trematode [[Posthodiplostomum Minimum]] (the [[White Liver Grub]]) affects [[Bluegill]] ([[Lepomis Macrochirus]]) growth and survival. Infection at about 103 [[Metacercariae]] per fish did not measurably slow growth. Infection at about 353 and 547 metacercariae per fish significantly reduced growth. At about 353 metacercariae per fish, mortality was more than twice that of controls (47.4% versus 22.5%). The author concluded that parasitism at this level, which is not uncommon in nature, could impair the bluegill's ability to withstand other environmental stresses.

## Background & Description

- **Parasite:** *Posthodiplostomum minimum* is a digenetic trematode whose [[Metacercariae]] encyst in the visceral organs of 97 North American freshwater fish species in 18 families (Hoffman, 1958). Fish serve as the second intermediate host. [[Physa Snails]] serve as first intermediate hosts, and fish-eating birds such as the [[Great Blue Heron]] serve as definitive hosts.
- **Host:** The [[Bluegill]] is an important pond fish. Natural infections in bluegill viscera are often heavy, and the author notes that earlier workers speculated about effects on host function.
- **Objectives:** Begun at [[Auburn University]] in 1962, the study aimed to determine (1) the effect of experimental infection on bluegill growth and (2) comparative mortality of parasitized and parasite-free fish.
- **Authorship:** R. Oneal Smitherman, Agricultural Experiment Station, Auburn University. The note is a reprint with French and Spanish abstracts. The original pages are numbered 380–388.

### Methods (summary)

- **Fish sources:** Pool-reared fry (4–8 cm, about two months old, verified parasite-free in 100 fish) and about 1,000 hatchery bluegills (6–9 cm, about six months old) from the [[Eastaboga State Fish Hatchery]].
- **Infected snails:** Day-old chicks were infected with the parasite, and their droppings were used to infect laboratory-reared physid snails. Snails producing cercariae were selected after 60 days.
- **Exposure:** Pool-reared fish (groups of 10 in 40-litre aquaria) received about 4,500 [[Cercariae]] over four days. Hatchery fish (groups of 200 in troughs) received about 25,000 cercariae over three days, plus six infected snails for five more days. A second trough was re-exposed two months later with 24 snails.
- **Growth:** Fish were fed commercial pellets at 4.5% of body weight per day, and some were later reduced to 0.5%. They were weighed bi-weekly over 10 months.
- **Parasite counts:** At least 15 fish per group were digested in pepsin solution and counted under a stereoscopic microscope.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Behavioral response:** Exposed fish swam erratically, rubbed against surfaces, gaped, and trembled for 1–2 hours. They then rested listlessly with fins clamped. Hemorrhages were found in the musculature, and hyperemia appeared at fin bases.
- **Pond culture effects:** The author argues that pond practices intensify infection. Impounding water and increasing fertility favor snails. High bluegill stocking density increases cercarial penetration. Fish concentration also promotes predation by herons, which completes the life cycle.
- **Natural context:** Heavy infections are less likely to be critical in strictly natural settings, but the parasite load in cultured stock can be high.

## Key Findings

**Parasite distribution (average of 103.3 metacercariae, 14 months post-infection):** Pericardium 7.1, liver 39.9, kidney 44.6, other organs 11.7. Other frequently infected sites were the spleen, mesenteries, gonads, and lymphoid tissue.

**Infection-level results:**

| Mean metacercariae per fish | Duration | Growth result | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 103.3 | 6 weeks (pool-reared, 70 vs. 70 controls) | No discernible change | Infected fish were later maintained 14 months; many reached sexual maturity. Controls died after a water-supply failure. |
| 353.1 | 32 weeks (hatchery, 200 vs. 200 controls) | Significantly slower than controls | At 16 weeks: infected 12.0 g (n=170) vs. controls 13.3 g (n=171). At 32 weeks: controls 19.8 g (n=146) vs. infected about 18.4 g (n=121). |
| 546.8 | 16 weeks | Significantly slower than controls | Infected 11.9 g (n=190) vs. controls 13.1 g (n=190). Discontinued after accidental loss of most infected fish. |

- **Growth reduction:** At the 353.1 level, growth was reduced by about 10% over 32 weeks.
- **Mortality:** Over the 10-month (reported as 40-week) period, 47.4% of parasitized fish died versus 22.5% of controls. The control figure is described as comparable to farm-pond stocks. Mortality was attributed to reduced tolerance of handling and weighing stress. A mild chronic bacterial infection occurred in both groups.
- **Reference mortality:** [[Swingle 1950]] reported 15% first-year and 19% second-year mortality for fingerling bluegills in fertilized ponds.
- **Nutritional effect:** A lot of fish fed at 0.5% of body weight had fewer parasites (mean 264.2, n=6, versus 353.2 for the original group). The author suggests a low-nutrition host may limit parasite numbers, possibly reflecting a well-adjusted host-parasite relationship.
- **Re-exposure:** Fish re-exposed after two months averaged 546.9 parasites (heart/pericardium 59.1, liver 248.1, kidney 130.8, other organs 108.9).
- **Significance:** The author describes this as the first known experimental test of how [[Posthodiplostomum Minimum]] infection affects host growth. Heavy infections could produce significant growth decreases in bluegill.
- **Prior literature cited:** [[Hubbs 1927]] (retarded growth in a cyprinid from strigeid infection); Krull (1934) (arrested development in young sunfish from heavy *Neascus* infection); Cross (1934, 1935) (smaller perch in heavily parasitized age-classes); [[Hunter and Hunter 1938]] (weight loss in smallmouth bass infected with metacercariae); Petrushevskii (1957) (reduced growth in rainbow trout from *Diplostomum spathecum* in the eye); Lewis and Nickum (1964) (no effect on plumpness of naturally infected bluegill, up to 1,035 parasites per fish).

**Conclusion:** Infection at about 350 metacercariae per fish, a level the author describes as not uncommon in nature, could significantly impair bluegill growth and survival under stress. The author also notes the study was limited by the accidental loss of the 547-level group and the discrepancy in reported durations for the 353-level group (32 weeks for growth, 40 weeks for mortality).
