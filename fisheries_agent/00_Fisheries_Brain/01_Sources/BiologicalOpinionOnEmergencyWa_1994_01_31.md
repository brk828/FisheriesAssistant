# Biological Opinion: Emergency Watershed Protection Project at Fillman Ranch, Eagle Creek

## Executive Summary
This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion (Consultation No. 2-21-94-F-002) evaluates an Emergency Watershed Protection (EWP) project proposed by the Soil Conservation Service (SCS) to repair flood-damaged dikes along [[Eagle Creek]] at Fillman Ranch, Greenlee County, Arizona. The action concerns potential effects on the threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), a member of the endemic [[Gila River Basin]] fish fauna. The project site itself has never documented spikedace presence, and no critical habitat has been proposed on Eagle Creek. The Service concluded that the project is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of the spikedace, with anticipated effects limited to downstream sediment transport and localized channel destabilization rather than direct habitat loss. No incidental take was authorized because the creek was expected to be dry (non-flowing) at the work site during construction.

## Background & Description
The source document is a regulatory biological opinion rather than a taxonomic or morphological study, and provides no direct anatomical descriptions of *Meda fulgida*. However, ecologically relevant traits are noted:
- **Habitat preference**: Typically occupies intermediate-sized streams (as opposed to small headwater creeks or large rivers).
- **Spawning substrate**: Utilizes shallow, sand- and gravel-bottomed riffles (Sublette et al. 1990).
- **Feeding mode**: Described as a sight-feeding fish that forages in the water column, implying reliance on visual clarity to locate prey — a trait making it vulnerable to turbidity/sedimentation stress.
- **Diet**: Plankton, aquatic invertebrates, and terrestrial invertebrates (Propst et al. 1986).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Distribution**: Endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico (Minckley 1973; Propst et al. 1986). In Arizona, currently restricted to [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Eagle Creek]], and a limited portion of the [[Verde River]].
- **Population isolation**: The Eagle Creek population is likely isolated from other spikedace populations due to a downstream diversion dam and the absence of spikedace in the Gila River mainstem in eastern Arizona. This isolation prevents natural recolonization following local declines.
- **Habitat context at Fillman Ranch**: Eagle Creek at the project site is a braided, cobble/boulder-dominated channel with common aquatic macrophytes and the alga *Cladophora glomerata* (Marsh et al. 1990). The creek is **perennial** in this reach, though spikedace have never been recorded at this specific location — the nearest historical records are approximately 18.6 miles downstream near [[Sheep Wash]].
- **Population trend**: First recorded in Eagle Creek in 1985 (Bestgen 1985); found to be common below Sheep Wash in 1987 (Marsh et al. 1990); by July 1993, sampling found **no spikedace** in Eagle Creek (Marsh 1993), suggesting a population that "expands and contracts... perhaps in response to natural variation," or a serious decline.
- **Co-occurring non-native threats**: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) are present in Eagle Creek and are known to adversely affect native fish assemblages, compounding habitat degradation pressures.
- **Anthropogenic watershed history**: Despite being described as "comparatively undisturbed," Eagle Creek's watershed has a long history of open-pit copper mining, cattle grazing, and logging (Marsh et al. 1990), contributing to over a century of fine sediment loading, streambank destabilization, riparian vegetation loss, and altered hydrology (Heede 1980; Heede and Rinne 1990).

## Key Findings
- **No direct habitat overlap**: The dike repair project occurs on the floodplain (approximately 328 feet from the active channel) and does not involve in-water work, eliminating direct habitat destruction at the spikedace's known range.
- **Primary risk pathway is indirect**: Effects to spikedace would stem from (1) downstream sediment transport reducing water clarity and impairing visual foraging, and (2) long-term channel destabilization and geomorphic changes radiating upstream and downstream from the repair site.
- **Direct mortality risk deemed low**: Crushing of individual fish by machinery-moved cobble/gravel is possible only if water and fish are present at the work site; since the creek was expected to be non-flowing there during construction, **no incidental take was anticipated or authorized**.
- **Cumulative habitat concern**: The Opinion emphasizes that continued small-scale bank/dike stabilization projects, in combination with grazing and other watershed disturbances, risk perpetuating or worsening already-deteriorated channel conditions, undermining long-term recovery prospects for the species.
- **Regulatory conclusion**: The project was found **not likely to jeopardize** spikedace, but the Service issued conservation recommendations: (1) minimize downstream sediment transport during construction, (2) prevent discharge of pollutants (oils, gas, cement) into the stream, and (3) maintain records of any actions resulting in take.
- **Reinitiation triggers**: Consultation must be reinitiated if incidental take is exceeded, new information reveals unconsidered effects, the action is modified to cause new effects, or a new species/critical habitat designation applies.
- **Broader significance**: This case exemplifies the challenges of managing isolated, relict populations of threatened cyprinids in fragmented desert stream systems, where lack of connectivity prevents recolonization following local extirpation events, and where even indirect, downstream, non-in-water actions carry potential compounding risk under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
