# Eleven New Species of Vejovis (Scorpiones: Vejovidae) from the Baja California Peninsula, Mexico

#arachnology #scorpiones #vejovidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper by [[Stanley C. Williams]] of the [[California Academy of Sciences]] describes eleven new species of scorpions in the genus [[Vejovis]], all collected on the [[Baja California Peninsula]] during a two-month field expedition in summer 1968. The study is terrestrial. It contains no fish or other aquatic taxa, so the aquatic-ecology framing applies only through the peninsula's coastal and island settings. The new species raise the known number of Vejovis from Baja California to roughly double its previous total. Most appear endemic to the peninsula, though several have close relatives in California, Arizona and mainland Mexico.

## Background & Description

The expedition was run by two parties. Williams led the arachnid survey, focused on scorpions. A second party, led by Mont A. Cazier of Arizona State University, focused on insects, especially flies of the family Apioceridae. Collecting was mostly done with [[Ultraviolet Detection]]. Rock rolling and turning surface cover were largely unproductive, probably because of hot, dry summer conditions. The work was supported by National Science Foundation grant GB-7679.

The peninsula is about 800 miles long, bordered by the Pacific Ocean to the west and the [[Gulf of California]] to the east. Its habitats range from tropical in the south to Colorado desert in the northeast and chaparral in the northwest. Other habitats include coniferous mountains, volcanic ranges, sedimentary basins, foggy deserts and sand dunes.

Each description follows standard scorpion taxonomy: carapace, mesosoma, metasoma, telson, [[Pectine Teeth]], genital operculum, chelicerae and pedipalps. Sample series are reported as [[Holotype]], allotype, and [[Paratopotype]] or paratype counts. Measurements are given in millimeters in tables.

The eleven species and their key diagnostic traits are:

- **[[Vejovis Grandis]]**: one of the largest Vejovis; a member of the subgenus [[Subgenus Paruroctonus]]; male holotype total length 64.9 mm.
- **[[Vejovis Harbisoni]]**: a large species in the *wupatkiensis* group; longer pedipalp fingers; fewer pectine teeth than *V. wupatkiensis*.
- **[[Vejovis Minutis]]**: one of the smallest Vejovis; male holotype total length 14.7 mm.
- **[[Vejovis Vittatus]]**: yellow with two pairs of dorsal mesosomal stripes; a close relative of *V. punctatus*.
- **[[Vejovis Pumilis]]**: very small, with total length under 30 mm and a distinctive orange telson in males.
- **[[Vejovis Schwenkmeyeri]]**: medium-sized, pale yellow, lacking distinctive markings; related to *V. confusus*.
- **[[Vejovis Diazi]]**: medium-sized, with reddish telson and pedipalp fingers in adults; related to *V. eusthenura*.
- **[[Vejovis Hoffmanni]]**: medium-sized, pale golden yellow; related to *V. schwenkmeyeri*.
- **[[Vejovis Bruneus]]**: medium-sized, light brown; related to *V. punctipalpus* and *V. insularis*.
- **[[Vejovis Insularis]]**: bright yellow with a reduced vesicle and elongate aculeus; known only from Gulf islands.
- **[[Vejovis Gravicaudus]]**: medium to large; robust metasoma; related to *V. spinigerus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most species were found in rocky volcanic habitats with accumulated soil, or on fine-textured sedimentary soils. Several were described as burrowers that were not found under rocks during the day. Some species were nocturnal surface-active animals, and they were most often collected at night with ultraviolet light.

Habitat specificity varied. *V. minutis* was restricted to relatively dense vegetation on low, poorly drained terrain near La Paz. *V. pumilis* was concentrated on the Magdalena Plain, where it preferred sparse plant cover and fine sedimentary soils. *V. insularis* occurred only on Isla Partida and Isla Espiritu Santo, where rocky volcanic hillsides met open fine soils.

Several species formed local colour races that matched substrate. *V. grandis* was dark on dark soils and light on light soils. *V. hoffmanni* was lighter near the San Angel sand dunes and darker at San Ignacio. Populations on the two islands also differed slightly from mainland populations of *V. vittatus*.

Sampling showed recurring demographic patterns. Juveniles were scarce in many samples for several species, including *V. grandis*, *V. bruneus*, and *V. gravicaudus*. Sex ratios were skewed in some species: males predominated in *V. vittatus* and *V. pumilis*, while females predominated in *V. diazi*.

The two islands off La Paz were recently separated by a narrow channel. The scorpion populations there are probably biologically isolated, but the isolation is too recent for divergence to be detectable.

## Key Findings

- **Eleven new species** of *Vejovis* were described from Baja California, roughly doubling the known Vejovis fauna of the peninsula.
- Most of the new species are likely endemic to the peninsula. Several have close relatives in California, Arizona and mainland Mexico, but some have no identified close relatives.
- Sample sizes were large for most species. For example, *V. grandis* had 273 paratopotypes and 1622 additional paratypes, and *V. diazi* had 19 paratopotypes and 1394 additional specimens.
- Size ranged widely. *V. minutis* (holotype 14.7 mm total length) and *V. pumilis* (26.5 mm) are very small. *V. grandis* (64.9 mm) and *V. gravicaudus* (59.7 mm) are among the largest.
- Pectine tooth counts varied by species and sex and were used as a key diagnostic. For example, *V. grandis* had 22–26 teeth in females and 30–35 in males, while *V. pumilis* had 7–8 in females and 12–16 in males.
- *V. pumilis* appeared to inhabit only soils from a region of very recent geologic origin. The authors suggest that some scorpion species may be genetically plastic and ecologically adaptable.
- *V. insularis* is known only from Isla Partida and Isla Espiritu Santo, and the authors suggest it may occur on other Gulf islands but not on the peninsula itself.
- The author proposes that Vejovis is unusually diverse and abundant in Baja California.
