# Post-Stocking Dispersal, Habitat Use, and Behavioral Acclimation of Juvenile Razorback Suckers in Two Colorado River Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This USGS Open-File Report (98-301), prepared with [[Arizona State University]], the [[National Park Service]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], tracks the first post-stocking movements of juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] catostomid, in [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Mohave]]. Because surgically implanted transmitters were unsuitable for small fish, the authors externally attached [[Sonic Telemetry]] transmitters to the dorsal keel. Fish dispersed widely and mostly out of study areas, habitat use was concentrated in shallow, vegetated, flooded shorelines with dense cover, and the authors suggest that holding fish on site for three days may influence dispersal rates. The report recommends further testing, wider use of external attachment for short-term studies, and research into predation on early life stages.

## Background & Description

Information on juvenile razorback sucker movement and habitat is scarce; most prior telemetry work targeted adults. Earlier juvenile studies relied on pond-reared fish, stocking dispersal studies, and rare wild observations. Augmentation with juveniles has generally failed, with a ten-year Arizona program stocking nearly 12 million razorback suckers (mostly as fingerlings or fry) yielding no measurable results, as young fish moved en masse downstream and were eaten by [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]].

The study addressed two questions: whether juveniles could be tracked after external transmitter attachment, and whether behavioral acclimation (holding fish on site before release) influences short-term dispersal. Fish were sourced from the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] (Lake Powell group, "Green River" surplus stock, mean length 358 mm, 335–402 mm) and the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Lake Mohave group, mean length 241 mm, 201–290 mm, also PIT-tagged). Transmitters were Sonotronics MT-95-2 units (9 × 36 mm, 3 g in water, about 90-day battery life, nominal detection range 500 m). [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] was chosen because signals are detectable regardless of depth, which in Lake Powell can exceed 100 m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat in both reservoirs was broad, shallow, shoreline habitat. Fish were typically found within 10 m of shore and in depths under 2 m. Cover was central: it made up more than 50% of observations, with flooded [[Tamarisk]] (*Tamarix pentandra*) used most (about 80% in Lake Mohave), followed by submergent vegetation such as [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton* spp.) (about 40%) and rock ledges or rubble (12–14%). Juveniles favored flooded backwater complexes at Castle Creek Canyon and Mike's Canyon, which resemble habitats where juvenile razorbacks were found in the Green River and Old Charley Wash.

Reservoir elevation strongly shaped conditions. Rising water in spring and June flooded hundreds of hectares of tamarisk that proved impenetrable to boats and acoustically damaging, reducing signal range to under 40–50 m. Lake Powell and Lake Mead can fluctuate about 20 m annually, which the authors note could manipulate predator densities and nursery habitat. The authors also link habitat loss from channelization, loss of backwaters and oxbows, hydroelectric flow fluctuation, and the decline of large snags to reduced cover availability.

## Key Findings

**Methods and transmitter attachment**
- Surgical implantation caused high transmitter shedding and possible mortality in earlier work, and saddle-mounted and metal galvanic-release attachments performed poorly.
- A single-suture dorsal keel attachment with a backing plate was modified to a single suture with no plate, to allow shedding and faster healing. Lake Powell: 28 of 30 transmitters functioned properly (one weak signal, one no signal). Initial failure rate was 2.5% (2 of 80).
- Blocking nets held fish in coves for three days (held group) while others were released immediately. Five suckers were found entangled in nets after 72 hours.

**Lake Powell (San Juan Arm, Castle Creek Canyon, RK 72)**
- Contact was made with 23 of 28 released fish (Table 1). Most movement was upstream; 21 of 23 (92%) moved upstream, and two moved downstream (the farthest 2.7 km).
- Maximum detected distances were 4.6 km upstream and 2.7 km downstream from the release site.
- The average maximum distance traveled per day by 21 fish exceeded 2,080 m/d (range 400–5,000 m/d). Fish #438 traveled a total of 5 km/d.
- Fourteen fish remained in the study area more than one month; of these, 64% of the cove-held fish were still in the area versus half as many released fish.
- Contact was lost with about 50% of study fish, so true dispersal range could not be determined.

**Lake Mohave (106 km reach, Arizona-Nevada)**
- Of 27 initially released fish, 17 were detected, but only 7 remained in contact after the second week. Of the 17, 2 moved upstream and the rest moved downstream (Table 2).
- Fish #238 moved at more than 3 km/h, reaching 20.5 km/d. Its transmitter then traveled about 63 km through pelagic zones over four days, suggesting it may have been eaten by [[Striped Bass]].
- Nearly 80% of Lake Mohave transmitters disappeared, possibly from shedding, predation, or entry into impenetrable flooded habitat.

**Habitat use**
- Habitat use was strikingly similar in both reservoirs: fish were found within 10 m of shore 62% of the time in Lake Mohave and 65% in Lake Powell, and beyond 50 m for only 15–20% of sightings.
- Cover was used for concealment and varied with availability. In Lake Powell, flooded tamarisk and submergent vegetation dominated, while emergent vegetation was absent from observations.
- Juveniles appeared nocturnal and concealed in submergent vegetation during the day, consistent with earlier pond observations.

**Tank tests and retention**
- Five fish (x = 23 cm, 21–27 cm) with single sutures held transmitters for an average of 3.8 weeks (2 to more than 8 weeks). All recovered, and sutures healed within 2–4 weeks with no infections.
- Ten larger fish (x = 29 cm, 27–32 cm) with double sutures plus one control were tested. Five fish, including the control, died of unknown causes. The first transmitter was lost in week 5, and the remaining five kept tags through week 9.
- Lake Mohave fish (mean TL 247 mm) retained transmitters for about 4 weeks. The authors attribute shorter retention to snagging on aquatic macrophytes and to less developed dorsal keel ossification in younger fish.

**Acclimation and stocking conclusions**
- The authors recommend further testing of whether holding fish on site for three days influences dispersal rate.
- Roughly 50% of stocked suckers were estimated to emigrate from the 46 km Lake Powell study area during the first month.
- Juveniles were found in shorelines used by predators such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]]. The authors suggest this may help explain the near absence of young razorback suckers and recommend research on predation.
- Until more is known, the authors suggest stocking fish larger than 30 cm, delayed until winter when predators are dormant. They support a 40 cm stocking size for augmenting San Juan River stocks.
- External attachment to the dorsal keel is recommended for short-term studies of juveniles and wild fish. It is non-lethal, less intrusive than abdominal surgery, and allows shedding at all ages.
