# Population Differentiation and Introgressive Hybridization of the Flannelmouth Sucker and of Hatchery and Native Stocks of the Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This allozyme-based study examines [[Introgressive Hybridization]] between the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the [[Colorado River]] system. The authors estimate introgression at roughly 0–5% toward flannelmouth sucker and 0–3% toward razorback sucker. Genes presumed introgressed were no more common in the [[Dexter Fish Hatchery]] razorback stock than in the wild parental population at [[Lake Mohave]], so the hatchery stock was judged at least as genetically suitable as the natural source. The paper also warns against recycling hatchery-reared broodstock.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a member of the "big-river" fish assemblage of the Colorado River basin and is listed as "vulnerable" (Williams et al. 1985). Its decline has prompted large-scale artificial propagation based at the Dexter Fish Hatchery, New Mexico. Because hatchery broodstock is a small subsample of the wild population, [[Genetic Drift]] and sampling error can shift allele frequencies, and hybridization with flannelmouth sucker could further overrepresent introgressed genes in released fish.

The study used [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] (starch gel electrophoresis with histochemical staining). Allozymes were chosen because their expression is codominant and not influenced by environment. Tissues (brain, liver, heart, skeletal muscle) were analysed for 21 presumptive loci, covering enzymes such as acid phosphatase, creatine kinase, glucose-6-phosphate isomerase, malate dehydrogenase and superoxide dismutase, following standard [[Teleost Locus Nomenclature]].

**Samples analysed:**
- Flannelmouth sucker: four localities (Virgin River, N = 15; Paria River, N = 3; Little Colorado River/East Clear Creek, N = 16; upper Colorado River near Grand Junction, N = 39).
- Razorback sucker: Lake Mohave adults (N = 18, biased toward suspected hybrids), Lake Mohave juveniles (N = 100, random), Senator Wash Reservoir (N = 14, reared from eggs), and Dexter Fish Hatchery (N = 52).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Hybridization between the two species is documented in the wild, with flannelmouth sucker involved in more reports than Sonora sucker or Utah sucker. Hybridization is thought to be linked to habitat modification of the Colorado River, which brings historically separated forms into contact. The authors suggest that introgression may increase as the river system continues to be altered, although current levels are low enough that both species appear to maintain the integrity of their gene pools.

Both species are tetraploid-derived catostomids. The number of gene loci controlling the enzyme systems examined was identical in both species, so this trait could not be used to identify hybrids. Genetic variability in razorback sucker was below the catostomid average but well above the low end of the range, and was similar to that of flannelmouth sucker in the Colorado drainage.

## Key Findings

- **Flannelmouth sucker structure:** Nine of 21 loci were variable. Genotype arrays conformed to Hardy-Weinberg expectations within samples, but significant differences among the four samples were found at four to five loci (χ² = 126.157, df = 30, P = 0.00). The F_ST value of 0.231 indicates substantial differentiation, and the authors describe the pattern as restricted gene flow with no complete allelic fixation.
- **Razorback sucker structure:** Eleven of 21 loci were polymorphic. Only the Dexter Hatchery sample at S-Mdh-A² departed significantly from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P < 0.001), owing to heterozygote deficiency. Native samples showed little differentiation (F_ST = 0.011). Genetic identity was 1.000 (D = 0.000) between the hatchery stock and both native samples.
- **Interspecific differentiation:** Average Nei's genetic identity between the two species was 0.802 and genetic distance was 0.220. Four marker loci (Ck-A, G6pi-A², M-Icdh-A, S-Sod-A²) were species-specific or nearly so.
- **Introgression evidence:** Ck-A and M-Icdh-A show gene sharing in both directions, with M-Icdh-A favouring flannelmouth-to-razorback flow and Ck-A favouring razorback-to-flannelmouth flow. Introgression was found in the upper Colorado River and Virgin River flannelmouth samples. No F₁ hybrids were detected in any razorback sample.
- **Introgressed gene proportions (Table 5):** Razorback samples were 0.028 (Lake Mohave adults), 0.015 (Lake Mohave juveniles), 0.000 (Senator Wash Reservoir) and 0.010 (Dexter Hatchery). Flannelmouth samples were 0.950 (Virgin River), 1.000 (Paria River), 1.000 (Little Colorado River) and 0.993 (upper Colorado River) for flannelmouth-derived genes at these loci.
- **Hatchery stock assessment:** The hatchery stock carries no more introgressed genes than the Lake Mohave parental population, and the authors conclude it is "at least as good" as the natural population. The Lake Mohave adult sample was biased toward suspected hybrids, so the random juvenile sample (N = 100) provides the more representative estimate.
- **Management recommendations:** Broodstock should continue to be collected in adequate numbers from wild populations. Selecting future breeding stock from hatchery-reared fish risks "a subsample from a subsample" and loss of variation, so this practice is advised against. Random collection of parental stock at Lake Mohave may not require electrophoretic screening if introgression there is confirmed as rare.
- **Open questions:** The authors raise three questions: whether razorback morphology reliably indicates introgression, whether morphological and allozyme characters are decoupled by selection, and whether introgression at Lake Mohave is too low for selected individuals to be the only evidence. Some introgressed variants at G6pi-A² and S-Sod-A² may have gone undetected because of small sample sizes.
