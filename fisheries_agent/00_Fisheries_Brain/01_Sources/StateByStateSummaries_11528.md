# National Survey of Needs for Hatchery Fish: State-by-State Summaries (Part II)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sport-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This is Part II of a 1968 U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife report, *Resource Publication 63*, prepared by the Division of Fishery Services with the Divisions of Fish Hatcheries and Fishery Research. It is a cooperative state-by-state assessment of present (1965) and projected (1973, 1980, 2000) sport-fish habitat, angler numbers, stocking requirements, and [[Hatchery]] production capacity. Its purpose was to guide State and Federal managers in providing recreational fishing for a growing number of anglers. The survey was conducted in fall and winter 1966 and covered Federal and State agencies, plus private hatcheries.

The summaries consistently project large growth in fishable waters, mostly from reservoir construction, and in angler numbers. They repeatedly find that hatchery output will fall short of requirements unless facilities are expanded or the species mix changes. The document as supplied is cut off partway through the New York summary.

## Background & Description

- **Survey design:** Five data categories per state: sport-fish habitat (amount and type), habitat stocked, numbers and types of fishermen, stocking requirements, and hatchery production capability.
- **Structure:** Each state summary covers habitat (acres by cold-water, warm-water, Federal, public, farm pond, and private categories), angler data (fishermen and man-days), hatchery facilities, special conditions, projections, and conclusions. Each also includes a hatchery location map.
- **Hatchery types:** [[National Fish Hatchery]] facilities run by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], State hatcheries (salmonid, warm-water, or combination), and private stations.
- **Units:** Requirements and production are given in thousands of fish, with fry, fingerlings, catchables (catchable-size fish), and yearlings as stocking stages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat change:** Impoundment is the dominant trend. Many states expect stream acreage to decline while reservoir and farm-pond acreage rises. Examples: Arkansas expects a gain of about 52,000 acres by 1973 with the loss of many stream miles; Georgia expects a loss of warm-water stream habitat but substantial reservoir and pond gains; Mississippi estimates it will lose one acre of stream for every 20 acres of reservoir.
- **Cold-water "second story" habitat:** Cool layers beneath warm reservoir surfaces (see [[Second Story Fishery]]) create trout habitat in states such as Arkansas, Georgia, Kentucky, and Tennessee. These waters must be stocked and may greatly increase trout demand.
- **Tailwaters:** Cold water below dams (for example, below [[Table Rock Reservoir]] in Missouri and [[Lake Mojave]] in Arizona) supports trout fisheries.
- **Pollution and water quality:** Cited as limiting factors in several states. In the [[Potomac River]] in the District of Columbia, silt, organic pollution, and air pollution suppress fishing. Maine suspects pollution in its cold-water decline. Connecticut expects reduced pollution to permit runs of Atlantic salmon and shad.
- **Anadromous fish:** Restoration programs for [[Atlantic Salmon]] and shad are underway in the Connecticut River, and striped bass and shad runs occur in several states. Logging silt affects southeast Alaska.
- **Natural acidity:** The Pine Barrens of New Jersey have acidic conditions that reduce aquatic productivity.

Other notable points:
- Hawaii had only 2,268 inland acres in 1965 and about 4,000 inland anglers.
- The District of Columbia had about 5,100 acres of inland water and no hatcheries.
- Delaware had 4,890 inland acres, with 20 acres of cold water.

**Angler trends**
- California's inland anglers were projected to rise from 1.93 million in 1965 to about 4.5 million by 2000.
- Florida's freshwater anglers were projected to rise from 2.38 million to 6.75 million.
- Maryland expected 960,000 inland anglers by 2000.
- New Hampshire expected its angler numbers to rise about 680 percent by 2000 (296,000 to about 1.82 million by table value).
- Kentucky's trout anglers were projected to grow from 19,300 to over 144,000.

**Hatchery capacity and deficits**
- **Kansas:** Predicted deficits of 4.67 million (1973), 41.8 million (1980), and 57.6 million (2000). Most are fry-size northern pike, walleye, and striped bass.
- **Kentucky:** Deficit of 349,000 trout and 44.1 million warm-water fish in 1965, projected to reach 4.24 million trout and 65.6 million warm-water fish by 2000.
- **Colorado:** Production fell about 42 million short of 1965 needs. Walleye fry made up most of the gap (30 million in 1965).
- **Arkansas:** Projected deficit of nearly 2 million trout by 2000 and about 5 million warm-water fish, mostly species not yet propagated.
- **Nevada:** Projected 10.5 million-trout deficit by 2000.
- **Maryland:** 1965 shortfall of 38,000 trout and 211,000 warm-water fish.
- **Idaho:** Surplus production (about 27–30 million fish projected) driven by fry included beyond catalog requirements, plus a trout fingerling deficit.
- **Alaska:** Production of 2,710,000 fish in 1965 met requirements. By 2000 the State expected to need about 20 million fish.
- **California:** Production (about 88 million) nearly matched requirements (about 90 million) in 1965. Coleman National Fish Hatchery was built to offset salmon runs displaced by Shasta Dam.

**Species and stocking needs**
- Warm-water requirements repeatedly exceed production for species not yet propagated: [[Striped Bass]], [[Walleye]], [[Northern Pike]], adult [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Channel Catfish]]. Several states consider these experimental.
- Trout needs are the most acute in states expanding reservoir "second story" fisheries.
- [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] brood stock was a critical gap in Nevada, and the [[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]] program in Montana was underway at Creston.

**Principal conclusions and recommendations**
- Most states conclude that existing hatcheries can meet needs only if facilities are enlarged or species and size mixes change. Examples: Georgia, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, and Missouri.
- Several states recommend a "new hatchery concept" focused on new species and larger fish (Alabama, Georgia).
- Louisiana recommended a salt-water hatchery to supply marine species for its management program.
- Idaho recommended coordinating State and Federal production and evaluating stocking requirements for each water development project.
- Colorado and New Mexico recommended a reliable source of walleye eggs, plus new trout or channel catfish facilities by about 1973.
- Maine's Atlantic salmon restoration depends on the Green Lake rearing facility and access over dams.
- Connecticut and Massachusetts flagged added demand from the Connecticut River anadromous fish restoration program.
- Several states, including Florida and Missouri, argued that hatchery output should shift from forage species to piscivorous (predatory) fish to control shad and other forage populations.

**Limitations noted in the document**
- Alaska's habitat data are rough estimates because the State is not fully mapped.
- Some hatchery figures may be unrealistic. Illinois reported that bulk plants for new reservoirs were not captured in its requirements data.
- Nevada's projected 200 percent angler increase was described as "extremely optimistic" by its own managers.
- The document ends partway through the New York summary, so the remaining states (New York onward) are not covered.
