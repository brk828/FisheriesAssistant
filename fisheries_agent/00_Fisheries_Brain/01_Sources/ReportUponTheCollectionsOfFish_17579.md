# Collections of Fishes from the Western Territories (1871–1874)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic report, prepared by [[Edward Drinker Cope]] (fish descriptions) and [[Henry Yarrow]] (comparisons, synonymy, bibliography, and field lists), covers the [[Field Survey Collection]] of freshwater fishes gathered in Nevada, Utah, California, Colorado, New Mexico, and Arizona during 1871–1874. The 1871 collection was largely lost to fire, but the 1872 and 1873 material reached Washington in good condition. The report establishes three new genera and nineteen new species, plus several varieties, and documents range extensions, economically important fisheries, and early overfishing concerns in Utah. The authors state that the list of western fishes could grow substantially with further exploration.

## Background & Description

The collection is organized as a systematic report with full descriptions, meristic formulas (fin ray counts, scale counts, pharyngeal teeth), body measurements, color notes, and specimen tables giving locality, date, and collector. Most material was gathered by survey naturalists including Dr. H. C. Yarrow, H. W. Henshaw, C. E. Aiken, Dr. O. Loew, Dr. J. T. Rothrock, and Prof. E. D. Cope.

The report's major taxonomic results include:

- **Plagopterine group (Cyprinidae):** A new subgroup restricted to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and San Luis basin, comprising three genera: [[Plagopterus]], [[Lepidomeda]], and [[Meda]]. Members share a compound defensive spine formed by two fused dorsal fin rays, and modified ventral fin rays. The authors note that the only comparable ossification of the ventral rays is found in the extinct Saurodontidae of the Cretaceous, with the closest living analogue being the internal spine of *Amphacanthus*. Dentition and intestines indicate carnivorous habits.
- **Range extension of *Ceratichthys biguttatus*:** A common eastern species, previously known no farther west than the Smoky Hill River, was found by Yarrow at Harmony, Southern Utah, in November 1872. This gives it the greatest known range of any American cyprinid of the time.
- **Apocope and Rhinichthys:** Small cyprinids of New Mexico and Utah were revised with a synoptic key. Yarrow's *Apocope couesii* was named for Elliott Coues.
- **Catostomid revision:** Cope proposed the genus *Pantosteus* for suckers with a complete union of the parietal bones (closed fontanelle), separate from *Catostomus* proper.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fishes were collected across the [[Great Basin]], the Colorado River system, and the [[Rio Grande]] drainage. Several sites are notable for habitat:

- **Provo River and Utah Lake:** Provo River, a tributary of [[Utah Lake]], held abundant *Coregonus williamsonii*, *Gila montana*, *Gila tenia*, and *Salmo virginalis*. Some cyprinids were found in Lake Utah's inflow but seldom in the lake itself.
- **Gunnison ditch:** *Hybopsis timpanogensis* was found "fairly teeming" in a stagnant, alkaline ditch with no visible inlet or outlet, about one mile from the Sevier River.
- **Beaver River and Sevier Lake:** *Pantosteus platyrhynchus* and *Myloleucus parovanus* were found in Beaver River, which flows into Sevier Lake, a very alkaline body where no fish were found.
- **Snake Creek Valley, Nevada:** *Siboma atraria* var. *longiceps* was abundant there, while nearby Schell Creek Valley had no fishes; the authors could not explain the difference, as conditions appeared similar.
- **Arizona and New Mexico streams:** Deep pools of the White River, Ash Creek, and the Rio Grande held many *Gila* species, which were sluggish and readily taken on bait.

Economic and fisheries observations are a notable theme. The Utah fisheries relied on [[Salmo Virginalis]] (Utah Lake trout), *Coregonus williamsonii* ("Mountain Herring"), and *Siboma atraria* (called "Mullet" by settlers). The authors reported decreasing trout yields and warned of destructive net practices.

## Key Findings

- **Collection scope:** The report covers the 1872 and 1873 collections in full, with the 1871 specimens largely destroyed by fire. The 1872 alcohol leakage affected one can only.
- **New taxa:** Three new genera and nineteen new species, plus several varieties, were described. The authors explicitly anticipate more discoveries from future exploration.
- **Endemic Colorado Basin group:** The plagopterines (*Plagopterus*, *Lepidomeda*, *Meda*) occur only in the Colorado and San Luis basins among known fish groups.
- **Key species:**
  - *Plagopterus argentissimus* from the San Luis Valley, Colorado.
  - *Lepidomeda vittata* (the largest species of its group), from the Colorado Chiquito River, Arizona.
  - *Lepidomeda jarrovii*, dedicated to Dr. Yarrow.
  - *Meda fulgida*, from the Rio San Pedro, a tributary of the Gila.
- **Range extensions:**
  - *Scaphirhynchus platyrhynchus*: two specimens from the Rio Grande near Albuquerque (collected by Dr. Oscar Loew) extended the sturgeon's range farther west.
  - *Ceratichthys biguttatus*: see Background above.
- **Coregonus williamsonii:** Found "singularly abundant" in the Provo River in 1872. It had been known from Des Chutes River, Oregon, and was the first record for the Great Salt Lake valley. Many were captured annually with hooks baited with [[Gila Tenia]] and sold in the Salt Lake City market.
- **Salmo virginalis (Utah Lake Trout):**
  - Average length about 14 inches and average weight 1.5 pounds; the largest reported was three feet and about 15.5 pounds.
  - Yields declined by an estimated one-third. In 1864 one seine haul yielded 3,500–3,700 pounds, whereas 500 pounds was considered large in 1872.
  - Spawning occurs from mid-March to mid-May in mountain streams. Males build nests by rotating their tails in gravel.
  - The authors recommended protective legislation regarding net mesh sizes, which were being ignored.
  - Trout fed heavily on minnows, specifically *Gila tenia*.
- **Uranidea wheeleri:** A new cottid (Cottidae), named for Lieut. G. M. Wheeler, described as the only physoclistous (spiny-rayed) fish yet found in the Great Basin of Utah.
- **Parasite record:** The report cites Dr. Leidy's description of a tapeworm, *Dibothrium cordiceps*, from Yellowstone lake trout. Parasites reached five to six inches and could number 5 to 50 per fish. Utah trout were not known to be affected.
- **Fossil fish:** An appendix describes *Syllaemus*, a Mugiloid fish from Mesozoic strata. It is known from one specimen, the posterior of which is missing, found near a mountain peak and attributed to Cretaceous or Jurassic deposits.
