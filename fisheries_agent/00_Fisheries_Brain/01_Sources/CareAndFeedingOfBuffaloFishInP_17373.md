# Care and Feeding of Buffalo Fish in Ponds

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Catostomidae #fish-culture #pond-management

## Executive Summary

This 1922 Bureau of Fisheries circular provides comprehensive guidance on the artificial propagation and pond culture of three buffalo fish species: [[Ictiobus bubalus]] (razorback buffalo), [[Ictiobus cyprinella]] (bigmouth buffalo), and [[Ictiobus urus]] (black buffalo). The document details species identification, spawning protocols including artificial water level manipulation, feeding practices, and growth rates based on operations at the [[U.S. Biological Station]] in [[Fairport Iowa]]. Key innovations include controlled pond flooding to simulate natural spring rises that trigger spawning, and documentation of growth rates from fry to market size under various feeding regimes.

## Background & Description

Three species of buffalo fish occur in the United States, differentiated by morphological characters:

- **[[Ictiobus bubalus]]** (razorback, small-mouth, current buffalo): Compressed body, elevated back, pointed nose, turned-down mouth. Predominates in rivers.
- **[[Ictiobus cyprinella]]** (gourd-head, stub-nose, big-mouth buffalo): Elliptical robust body, blunt nose, turned-up mouth. Found almost exclusively in northern lakes; ranks second in rivers.
- **[[Ictiobus urus]]** (mongrel, sheep's-nose, blue-rooter buffalo): Elliptical robust body, turned-down mouth. Predominates in southern lakes; ranks third in rivers.

Sexual maturity indicators include raised spots or tubercles on head and body indicating ripeness. Females exhibit reddened and swollen vents several weeks before spawning; males do not show this characteristic. Sex ratios of three males to one female ensure maximum fertilization.

At [[Fairport Iowa]], both [[Ictiobus bubalus]] and [[Ictiobus cyprinella]] have been successfully reared artificially. Ponds as small as 3,845 square feet have produced buffalo fish successfully.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Distribution**: [[Ictiobus bubalus]] predominates in rivers, [[Ictiobus cyprinella]] in northern lakes, and [[Ictiobus urus]] in southern lakes. All three species thrive in rivers within the [[Mississippi Drainage]] system.

**Feeding Ecology**: Buffalo fish are primarily benthic feeders, ingesting aquatic invertebrates from bottom soil and ooze by thrusting noses into substrate. They consume:
- Aquatic vegetation and associated animal life
- Terrestrial and aquatic grains and seeds
- Surface-floating vegetation and attached algae
- [[Euglena]] (pond bloom)
- [[Buffalo Gnat]] larvae from submerged brush during spawning (southern waters)

Buffalo fish are not cannibalistic. Natural enemies include turtles, snakes, and predacious birds.

**Pond Requirements**: Graduated depths from 18 inches to 6 feet are superior to uniform depth. Ample aquatic vegetation maintains food supply for first year without artificial feeding.

## Key Findings

**Stocking Density**: 12 to 20 brood fish per acre of pond surface, varying with fish size and age.

**Spawning Protocol**:
- Pond filled to one-quarter depth when brood fish introduced, then to two-thirds maximum depth
- Artificial rise initiated at 56°F water temperature
- Rise controlled over 10-15 days until pond overflows, with temperature rising to 62-64°F
- Spawning begins at 56-58°F, accelerates at 60-62°F
- [[Ictiobus cyprinella]] can spawn without artificial rise; [[Ictiobus bubalus]] requires it
- Spawning occurs primarily in morning or late evening
- Eggs adhere to submerged grasses, brush, or débris
- Eggs "eye" in 3-4 days, hatch in 9-10 days
- Yolk sac absorbed in about 3 days

**Artificial Feeding**: When natural food insufficient, acceptable feeds include:
- Whole or ground corn, wheat, barley, oats, and other grains
- Ground meat and vegetable scraps
- Broken cotton seed, oil cake, damaged cheese
- **Excluded materials**: Coffee grounds, eggshells, citrus peels, decayed wastes

Daily feeding in multiple locations, with quantities consumed within 24 hours, proves most economical and healthful.

**Growth Rates**:
- Fry hatched in May: 1-8 ounces by October
- Age 1 year: 4 ounces to 1.5 pounds (depending on food availability)
- Age 5 years (poor food): less than 3 pounds
- Age 13-15 years ([[White River Arkansas]] specimens): over 40 pounds
- Estimated annual weight gain: 1-1.5 pounds per year after first year under favorable conditions (ages 1-15 years)
- Sexual maturity: 4 years old in [[Fairport]] ponds

**Management Recommendations**:
- Maintain pond free of turtles, snakes, predacious birds
- Monitor for overcrowding (indicated by slow growth, vegetation depletion)
- No separation of young from adults necessary (non-cannibalistic)
- Weathering of exposed pond area before rise cleanses bottom, favoring egg incubation and fry rearing
- Feeding season extends into cold weather
