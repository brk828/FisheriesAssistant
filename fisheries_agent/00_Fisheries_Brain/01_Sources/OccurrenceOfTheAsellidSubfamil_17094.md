# Mexistenasellus coahuila: First Western Hemisphere Stenasellinae (Crustacea, Isopoda)

#crustacea #isopoda #asellidae

## Executive Summary

Cole and Minckley describe [[Mexistenasellus]] coahuila, a new genus and species of [[Stenasellinae]] isopod, from thermal waters of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in [[Coahuila]], Mexico. It is the first member of this Eurasian-African subfamily recorded from the Western Hemisphere. The authors base the description on nine known specimens, compare its morphology with Old World stenasellines and with [[Synasellus]] and [[Parastenasellus]], and review the distribution of freshwater isopods in North and Middle America. They use the find to discuss ancient transatlantic faunal links and possible origins before the Tertiary.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic context.** The authors treat the widespread family [[Asellidae]] as two subfamilies. [[Asellinae]] includes [[Lirceus]], [[Synasellus]], and [[Asellus]]. The more primitive Stenasellinae, proposed by Vandel (1964) and diagnosed by Magniez (1966b), includes the North African monotypic genus [[Johannella]] and the former [[Stenasellus]] species, which Magniez split into Stenasellus s. str., [[Parastenasellus]], and [[Metastenasellus]]. Lanza (1966) later erected [[Magniezia]].

**Distribution of the subfamily before this study.** Stenasellus is widespread in subterranean waters of Europe, especially the peri-Mediterranean region. Twelve European forms were listed by Stragkraba (1967). One species occurs in Turkmenia, and at least six have been described from tropical Africa. Mexistenasellus is the first record from the Americas.

**Diagnosis.** The genus is bright red in life and eyeless. Key diagnostic traits include:
- Male pleopod 2 with a disto-lateral process armed with setae, a single-segmented exopod bearing one distal seta, and a two-segmented endopod enclosing a sharp spike.
- Pleopods 3 to 5 with non-bifid, non-respiratory exopods, and no plumose setation on any pleopod.
- Dactyls of pereopods 2 to 4 with one spine, and those of pereopods 5 to 7 with two.
- Uropods slightly shorter than the pleotelson.

**Type material.** Nine specimens are known, all from thermal springs of the Cuatro Ciénegas basin:
- Holotype: male, 8.2 mm.
- Allotype: non-ovigerous female, 7.4 mm.
- Paratypes: seven additional specimens, 4.4 to 7.2 mm, from [[Laguna Juan Santos]], [[Laguna Escobeda]], a small spring, and [[Pozo Barbado]].
- Two specimens are deposited in the Colección Nacional de México and the remainder in the U.S. National Museum.

**Morphology.** The male description covers the holotype and the three largest paratypes. The body is slender, about five times as long as wide. The first antenna has a peduncle of four segments and a flagellum of 10 to 14 articles. The second antenna is about 0.6 times body length, with a flagellum of 33 to 44 articles. The female resembles the male, but has fewer setae on the mandible and maxilla 2, which the authors attribute to smaller size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Juan Santos (1967).** Five specimens were extracted from a single travertine block pulled from the bank of [[Laguna Juan Santos]], about 25 cm below the surface. The animals lived in tiny crevices and tube-like channels in the porous stone and retreated rapidly when disturbed. The laguna is more than 100 m wide, has deep (5 m) depressions near its center, and is fed by thermal springs. Water temperature was a uniform 31 °C, and total residue was 2.1 g/liter. The laguna supports a large and diversified fish fauna. The only other crustacean recorded there is the amphipod [[Hyalella Azteca]].

**Other sites (1968).** Single specimens were collected from varied habitats:
- A male from [[Laguna Escobeda]] was found in copropelic sediment about 3 m deep, at 34.2 °C.
- A male from a travertine fragment in a detritus-choked spring inlet, 50 cm deep, at 33 °C.
- A male from soft detritus beneath a floating vegetation mat at a small limnocrene outlet, at 31 °C.
- A female from Pozo Barbado at 30.5 °C. Its habitat is unknown because it was collected with amphipods.

The authors state that defining the ecology will require more specimens. They refer readers to Taylor (1966) and Minckley (1968) for broader Cuatro Ciénegas water chemistry and biota.

**Regional isopod fauna.** The authors review other freshwater isopods of the region:
- **Sphaeromatids:** [[Exosphaeroma]] thermophilum (warm springs at Socorro, New Mexico) and E. dugesi (Aguascalientes, Mexico). Most North American freshwater sphaeromatids are secondarily derived from estuarine or marine stock.
- **Cirolanids:** Eight genera are known in the Western Hemisphere. These include [[Speocirolana Thermydronis]], which may occur only in Cuatro Ciénegas, and an undescribed genus restricted to the same area. The cirolanids form an arc around the Gulf of Mexico.
- **Asellids:** [[Asellus]] (including Caecidotea) and Lirceus are the most widespread and abundant North American freshwater isopods. A new Conasellus species was found in Puebla in 1966.
- **Thermosbaenaceans:** [[Monodella Texana]] from the Edwards Plateau of Texas links Western Hemisphere fauna to the Old World order Thermosbaenacea.

## Key Findings

- **First Western Hemisphere stenasellineé:** The description of Mexistenasellus coahuila extends the Eurasian-African subfamily Stenasellinae to North America, the first record of its kind in the Western Hemisphere.
- **Mixed morphological affinities:** The male pleopod 2 resembles [[Parastenasellus]], while the single distal exopod seta and oval pleopod 1 recall [[Synasellus]]. The authors describe [[Synasellus]] as intermediate between [[Asellus]] and primitive stenasellines, but caution that the significance of these similarities is not yet clear.
- **Limited material:** Only nine specimens are known, and no ovigerous females were found. This limits interpretation of relationships.
- **Restricted, extreme habitat:** The species occurs in warm (30–34 °C) thermal waters with high dissolved solids (2.1 g/liter at Juan Santos), in travertine crevices, detritus, and sediments.
- **Biogeographic implications:** The authors argue that the presence of stenasellines in North America, together with Monodella texana and Horatiini snails, supports direct biogeographic links between the continental masses for ancient subterranean fauna. They suggest the group's origin predates the Tertiary and may bear on [[Continental Drift]] research.
- **Regional context:** Freshwater isopods of North and Middle America are poorly studied, and the authors describe the field as roughly 50 years behind Eastern Hemisphere work. The review in this paper aims to close part of that gap.
