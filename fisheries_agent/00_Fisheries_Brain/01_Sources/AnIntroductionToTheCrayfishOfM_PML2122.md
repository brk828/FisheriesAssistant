# Crayfish of Missouri: An Introduction to Identification, Distribution and Use

#carcinology #freshwater-biology #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This popular-science article by [[William L. Pfleger]], a senior fisheries research biologist with the [[Missouri Department of Conservation]], introduces the crayfish fauna of Missouri. It explains why crayfish are ecologically important, outlines how non-specialists can identify the state's species using visible features, and describes the four [[Aquatic Regions of Missouri]] and the crayfish typical of each. The article also notes the commercial and aquaculture potential of crayfish and recommends a species for pond culture in Missouri. It is a regional identification guide and natural history overview, not a research study, and it reports no original sample sizes or statistics.

## Background & Description

Crayfish, also called crawfish or crawdads, are among the largest and most common invertebrates in Missouri's lakes and streams. They are an important food for many animals and a popular fish bait. The article notes growing interest in crayfish as food, given their nutritional qualities and rising popularity with health-conscious consumers, as well as among farmers seeking alternative crops during the farm crisis.

A main barrier for non-specialists is that published keys rely on subtle differences in the [[Gonopods]] (reproductive structures) of mature males. The author argues that Missouri species can be identified from readily observed features, and that the limited ranges of many species reduce the number of possible candidates in any given area. Key identification structures include:

- **Size:** measured from the tip of the [[Rostrum]] to the tip of the tail fan.
- **[[Areola]]:** an hourglass-shaped area on the carapace set off by grooves. Where the grooves touch, the areola is described as absent.
- **Gonopods:** paired male structures on the underside of the thorax, whose tip length and shape often separate similar species.
- **Color and markings:** such as spots, knobs, bands, and rings on the pincers, fingers, carapace, and abdomen.

The article contrasts two species to show these differences. The [[Longpincered Crayfish]] (*Orconectes longidigitus*) can reach six inches or more and is olive-tan with bright red trim and dark blue-green pincers studded with yellow knobs. The [[Neosho Midget Crayfish]] (*Orconectes macrus*) rarely exceeds two inches, is mottled brown and black, and has a stout body with short, heavy pincers.

The article provides brief descriptions of many species, including:

- **Prairie Crayfish** (*Procambarus gallus*): a burrower of grasslands that can be found far from surface water.
- **Devil Crayfish** (*Cambarus diogenes*): Missouri's most widely distributed crayfish, found in burrows in timbered floodplains, often marked by mud chimneys.
- **[[Hubbs' Crayfish]]** (*Cambarus hubbsi*): a species of the southern Ozark slope, found in clear, permanent streams.
- **Freckled Crayfish**: a yellowish-tan species with black spots, endemic to the Meramec River basin, which has no formal scientific name.
- **[[Ozark Crayfish]]** (*Orconectes ozarkae*), **[[Woodland Crayfish]]** (*Orconectes hylas*), and **Ringed Crayfish** (*Orconectes neglectus*): species distinguished by fine patterns of spots, rings, and bands.
- **Cave crayfish**: blind, white species of the Ozarks, including the bristly cave crayfish (*Cambarus setosus*) and the Salem cave crayfish (*Cambarus hubrichti*).
- **[[Red Swamp Crayfish]]** (*Procambarus clarkii*) and **[[White River Crayfish]]** (*Procambarus acutus*): the most common crayfish of lowland swamps and sloughs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Crayfish occupy many aquatic habitats, and the species diversity in Missouri reflects the variety of habitats in the state. The article groups crayfish into four broad habitat categories: (1) surface streams; (2) swamps, marshes, and pond and lake shallows; (3) burrows away from surface water; and (4) underground streams. The categories overlap. Some species live in seasonally flooded pools and burrow into the bottom as the pools dry in summer.

Crayfish behavior is linked to body form. The Neosho midget crayfish is bite-sized for predators such as goggle-eye and bass. It avoids them by burrowing into gravelly shoals with its powerful pincers. The longpincered crayfish, in contrast, is large enough to mount a strong defense and forages on the bottoms of bass-filled pools at night. It feeds on living and dead plant and animal material, including other crayfish. The two species occupy non-overlapping ranges in the southern Ozarks and do not occur together naturally.

The article divides Missouri into four [[Aquatic Regions of Missouri]], each with a characteristic crayfish assemblage:

- **[[Prairie Region]]** (north and west Missouri): four common species, including the prairie crayfish and devil crayfish, with the northern crayfish in most prairie streams and the paper shell and golden crayfish in specific habitats.
- **[[Ozark Region]]** (south-central Missouri): 18 species, the center of crayfish distribution in the state. Eight have never been found outside Missouri, and nine others have limited ranges in neighboring states. Species distributions follow river basins.
- **[[Lowland Region]]** (southeastern Missouri): ten species, mostly in swamps, sloughs, and seasonally flooded areas, where crayfish burrow during dry periods.
- **[[Big River Region]]** (Missouri and Mississippi rivers): crayfish occur only as small local populations or stray individuals. A four-inch shrimp (*Macrobrachium ohione*) was abundant in the Mississippi River but disappeared about 30 years before the article was written.

The article also describes the status of several restricted species. The [[Mammoth Spring Crayfish]] has a very localized distribution in Missouri and Arkansas. The [[Coldwater Crayfish]] is restricted to clear, cold rivers and springs of southern Missouri and Arkansas and is the most common crayfish in Spring Branch. The article does not provide population or conservation status data for these species beyond their distributions.

## Key Findings

- Missouri has at least 32 crayfish species, more than most neighboring states, and the state's habitat diversity supports this richness.
- Species can be identified from visible features such as size, color, markings, the areola, and the shape of the rostrum. Male gonopods are needed only for finer distinctions.
- Many species have restricted ranges. The article notes that this narrows identification to a few candidates in any given area.
- The Ozark Region has the greatest crayfish diversity in Missouri, with 18 species. Eight of these are endemic to the state.
- Crayfish harvest for food in the United States is concentrated in Louisiana and dominated by the red swamp crayfish and the White River crayfish, which are farmed in ponds that are drained and refilled on an annual cycle. The article gives the national harvest as "approaches" a figure that is illegible in the scanned text.
- In Missouri, winter temperatures are too low for the southern pond-culture method. The article recommends managing ponds without regular draining and using the northern crayfish (*Orconectes virilis*), which grows large in ponds and is adapted to Missouri conditions. It notes that the longpincered crayfish is the largest native species, but its suitability for pond culture is unknown.
- The paper shell crayfish is prolific in ponds and is an excellent bait species.
- The article notes that crayfish flourish in waters of 55 to 60 degrees (the scan does not show a temperature unit, probably Fahrenheit). It also observes that crayfish, which are often treated as bait or as a regional food, are nutritionally comparable to other seafoods.
