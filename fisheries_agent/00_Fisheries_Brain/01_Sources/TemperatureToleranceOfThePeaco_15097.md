# Temperature tolerance of the peacock bass and a pond test of its value as a Piscivorous species

## Executive Summary
This 1966 study by Hugh A. Swingle at the Auburn University Agricultural Experiment Station investigates the thermal tolerance limits of the introduced [[Peacock Bass]] (*Cichla* sp.) and evaluates its efficacy as a piscivorous game species compared to the native [[Largemouth Bass]]. Following the species' introduction into Florida in 1964, several southeastern U.S. states considered stocking it as a new sport predator. Laboratory trials demonstrated that fingerling peacock bass cease feeding around 63°F and die at temperatures at or below 61°F, severely restricting their viable range to tropical or artificially heated waters. A parallel pond study found that while peacock bass exhibited growth rates comparable to largemouth bass, they were less effective at controlling forage fish populations (specifically [[Tilapia Melanopleura]] and fathead minnows), resulting in an unbalanced, overcrowded pond population.

## Background & Description
- **Taxonomic Identity:** Initially identified as *Cichla ocellaris* (Bloch and Schneider) upon introduction to Florida in 1964 (Ogilvie, 1965). Subsequent observation of true *C. ocellaris* in Venezuela by Ogilvie (1966) revealed morphological differences, casting doubt on the Florida population's specific identity (referred to broadly as *Cichla* sp.).
- **Growth Metrics:**
  - Fry stocked in a concrete pool grew to approximately 3 inches by September 1965 (from an initial fry stage in June 1965).
  - In pond trials, average individual weight increased from 0.21 lbs (April) to 0.72 lbs (October) over a 6-month growing period.
  - Comparable [[Largemouth Bass]] weight gains under similar conditions ranged from 0.3 to 0.7 lbs (Auburn University Fisheries Annual Report, 1959–1961).
- **Feeding Behavior Indicators:** Field examination of 20 peacock bass stomach contents (Ogilvie, 1965) found only one specimen had consumed a [[Bluegill]], suggesting a strong prey preference for slender, minnow-like fish over deep-bodied sunfish—a preference pattern mirrored in [[Largemouth Bass]] foraging ecology (Snow, 1961; Lewis et al., 1961).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native Range:** Tropical waters of South America.
- **Introduced Range:** Southern Florida (1964); subsequently considered/introduced by other southeastern U.S. states as a potential piscivorous game fish.
- **Thermal Tolerance:**
  - Sterba (1963) reported a lethal threshold of 68°F for *C. ocellaris*.
  - Ogilvie (1966) recorded natural survival at temperatures as low as 50°F in January 1961 in Southern Florida with no observed mortality.
  - However, the winter of 1965 caused near-complete winter kills of peacock bass stocks in both Georgia and Florida, highlighting significant population vulnerability despite prior survival records.
- **Trophic Role:** Functions as a [[Piscivorous Fish]] within pond ecosystems, co-occurring with prey species [[Tilapia Melanopleura]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*).
- **Population Balance Dynamics:** Using the Swingle (1950) balance classification system, the test pond was deemed "unbalanced" with overcrowded small forage fish:
  - **Ag (harvestable yield percentage):** 18.5% (109.2 of 689.0 total lbs).
  - **Y/C ratio (forage-to-predator ratio):** 6.8 lbs of small forage fish per lb of peacock bass — more than double the desirable ratio, indicating insufficient predation pressure to control forage fish overpopulation.

## Key Findings
1. **Lethal Temperature Threshold:** Peacock bass fingerlings ceased normal feeding behavior at 65°F, fed only sporadically at 63°F, and stopped feeding entirely at 63°F. Mortality began at 61°F (4 of 8 fish died), with the remaining fish dying after 12 hours at 60°F.
2. **Distributional Limitation:** These thermal tolerance results indicate peacock bass fingerlings cannot survive average winters in any U.S. region where minimum water temperatures fall to 61°F or below, severely restricting natural overwintering range to the southernmost tropical/subtropical zones.
3. **Management Implication:** Viable introduction into cooler regions would require overwintering strategies such as warm springs, heated water systems, or indoor holding — analogous to existing practices for [[Tilapia Melanopleura]] management — followed by spring stocking.
4. **Predatory Efficacy:** When stocked at 108 peacock bass per acre alongside tilapia and fathead minnows, peacock bass were **less effective predators** than [[Largemouth Bass]] under comparable stocking conditions, based on the resulting unbalanced pond population structure.
5. **Growth Comparability:** Despite lower predatory efficiency, peacock bass growth rates (0.21 to 0.72 lbs over 6 months) were roughly equivalent to those documented for largemouth bass under similar pond conditions.
6. **Stocking Density Consideration:** The authors speculate that increasing peacock bass stocking density (e.g., to 200/acre, referencing a Georgia pond stocked at 212 yearlings/acre that eliminated most tilapia within 12 days) might improve predatory control and shift the pond toward better ecological balance.
7. **Overall Assessment:** The study concludes that further research is needed before a definitive verdict can be reached on the peacock bass's overall value as an introduced piscivorous game species in the southeastern United States.
