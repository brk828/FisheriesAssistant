# Benthic invertebrate standing crop and diversity of a small desert stream, Big Bend National Park, Texas

#entomology #benthic-macroinvertebrates #limnology

## Executive Summary

This study by Bane and Lind (1978, *The Southwestern Naturalist* 23(2): 215–226) quantitatively describes the benthic invertebrate community of [[Tornillo Creek]], a small, intermittent desert stream in [[Big Bend National Park]], Texas. The work was part of a limnological survey for the [[National Park Service]]. Numerical standing crop and biomass varied strongly among seasons, driven by physical scouring from summer floods and by insect emergence. Species diversity was moderately high, which the authors read as a non-impacted community. The benthos also correlated with algal mats, and the authors documented a [[Predator-Prey Interactions|predator-prey]] explanation for a fall-to-winter decline in diversity.

## Background & Description

Few quantitative studies exist for natural permanent aquatic ecosystems in the desert southwestern United States. Tornillo Creek is a tributary of the [[Rio Grande]] and is typical of the infrequent small streams in this region. Because of its size, fish populations are small (Hubbs and Wauer 1973), so most of the stream's life is associated with the substrate. Before this study, the benthos had not been described ecologically.

The stream lies about 4 km above the Rio Grande within the Christmas Mountains region. It has a continuous-flow reach that extends roughly 300 m above an intermittent section, with an alluvium bed and terrace deposits. Riparian vegetation includes willow (*Salix*), common reed (*Phragmites communis*), and tobacco (*Nicotiana*). The channel usually has three to four riffles, each 60 to 90 cm long, and a small pool (3 to 4 m across) near the source of flow. Substrate is coarse sand and gravel. Filamentous algal mats, chiefly [[Spirogyra]] and [[Cladophora]], occurred mostly at shallow ends and over the riffles.

**Methods (summary):** Samples came from riffles and one pool on five dates between August 1973 and June 1974. Nine 1 ft² [[Surber Sampling|Surber samples]] were taken on each date, supplemented by hand-picking of substrate. Organisms were sieved (10, 20, and 30 mesh), preserved in 70% alcohol, identified (with specialists consulted for each order), and weighed after air-drying for 24 hours. Diversity was calculated with the [[Shannon-Weaver Diversity Index]], once using numbers of individuals and once using biomass.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical setting:**
- Total alkalinity was moderately high (183–249 mg l⁻¹) and fairly uniform, except for an increase in June that the authors attributed to evaporation.
- pH ranged from about 6.7 upward, with values above 7 most of the year.
- Turbidity was 7 ITU in April and 2 ITU in a later sample.
- Dissolved oxygen showed a strong diel cycle, falling below 3.0 mg l⁻¹ at some predawn periods in the pool, while riffles stayed better oxygenated.
- Water temperature also varied diurnally.

**Disturbance:** Heavy rains in July and August [[Spate Scouring|scoured]] the channel and nearly eliminated the surface benthos. Only three beetles turned up in hand-picked samples, and few organisms appeared in the July and August Surber samples. The authors describe this rainy-season scouring as typical of Tornillo Creek and of many desert streams. Comparable late-summer spate eradication was reported for [[South Willow Creek]], Utah (Moffett 1936).

**Biotic interactions:** Several taxa showed winter declines or disappearances followed by a spring resurgence. The authors suggest this reflects overwintering in the egg stage or deep-substrate burrowing by immature or adult insects, but the causal factor remains unknown. The algae also supported the community. The abundance of several taxies correlated directly with algal mats, and [[Chironomidae]], [[Baetis]], [[Helicopsyche borealis]], and [[Ambrysus Buenoi]] were among those using algae as food or substrate.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic richness and occurrence**
- 115 invertebrate taxa were collected, 77 of them in quantitative Surber samples. Most were benthic and immature stages.
- The beetle *[[Hydroscapha Natans]]* represents a range extension, having previously been recorded only from California, Nevada, New Mexico, and Arizona.
- *Baetis* sp. was the most numerous taxon in November and December; Chironomidae were the most numerous in June.

**Numerical standing crop**
- Mean standing crop over all five dates was 2,944 m⁻². Excluding the August collection (after scouring), the mean for four dates beginning in November was 3,679 m⁻².
- Density ranged from almost 8,000 m⁻² down to very low values after the summer flash-flood washout.
- These means are similar to the Yoro River, Japan (Tanaka 1971) and Spring Creek, Pennsylvania (Cole 1973). The maximum was higher than the [[Guadalupe River]] of central Texas (Young et al. 1974).

**Biomass**
- Mean biomass over five dates was 698.2 mg dry weight m⁻²; after reestablishment it was 872.5 mg m⁻².
- The lowest post-recovery biomass was 73.8 mg m⁻² in June 1974, attributed to other mortality events affecting several populations.
- The spring maximum resulted mainly from the growth of relatively large individuals, not from more individuals. Dominant contributors included *[[Erpetogomphus]]* spp., *Corydalus cognatus*, and *Ambrysus buenoi*.
- Biomass was similar to other running waters but well below South Willow Creek, Utah (Moffett 1936).

**Species diversity**
- Mean species diversity was moderately high throughout most of the year, consistent with non-impacted communities (Wilhm 1970).
- Maximum diversity occurred in April, just before the main insect emergence period.
- Diversity fell between November and December as a result of the disproportionate abundance ("swamping") of *Baetis* sp. Kimmel (1969) reported similar swamping, and recalculating his values without the dominant species raised them from 1.20, 0.85, and 0.96 to 2.35, 1.80, and 1.71.

**Biomass diversity vs. numerical diversity**
- Mean diversity was 2.58 using individuals and 2.40 using biomass.
- Biomass-based diversity varied much less among seasons than numerical diversity. The fall-to-winter drop in biomass diversity was small, reflecting high evenness of biomass among many small organisms.
- Biomass-based values were considerably higher than those reported for White Oak Creek, Tennessee (Wilhm 1968), and lower than those for Spring Creek, Pennsylvania (Cole 1973).

**Body size and growth**
- *Baetis* sp. was among the smallest organisms all year, averaging 0.03 mg per individual. The smallest nymphs (1.5–2 mm) averaged 0.002 mg in November, and the largest subimago (4 mm) weighed 0.8 mg in April.
- After converting Tanaka's wet weights to dry weights, Japanese *Baetis* averaged 0.22 mg m⁻², compared with 0.03 mg m⁻² at Tornillo Creek. The authors considered the Tornillo weights unusually small.

**Predator-prey hypothesis for the December decline**
- The authors judged a [[Predator-Prey Interactions|predator-prey]] explanation most applicable. Larger taxa (*Ambrysus* spp., *Corydalus cognatus*, *Erpetogomphus* spp.) declined and may have been selectively harvested by fish.

**Emergence and seasonal patterns**
- Actual emergence was not measured. Density and mean-weight data indicated a major emergence of chironomids between April and June, along with some dragonflies and *Cheumatopsyche* sp., followed by a spring resurgence.
- *Helicopsyche borealis* appeared only in April samples, which the authors attributed to a roughly two-week pupation period.

**Algal association**
- Mats of *Spirogyra* and *Cladophora* were prolific in December, and several invertebrates (*Cheumatopsyche*, *Oxyethira*, *Baetis*, Collembola, *Parargyractis jaliscalis*) correlated with them, likely as food or substrate.
- Cole (1973) also found that flora affected benthic abundance.

**Conclusions**
- Tornillo Creek supports a moderately diverse, non-impacted benthic community whose abundance and biomass are strongly shaped by seasonal floods, emergence, and algal growth.
- The authors recommend that future studies explain the seasonal disappearances of several taxa and the mechanisms behind the observed biomass and diversity patterns.
