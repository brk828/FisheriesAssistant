# Analysis of Larval Razorback Sucker Collections from Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 summary report, prepared by Paul C. Marsh of [[Arizona State University]] for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] under order 1425-96-PG-30-08200, analyzes 153 larval [[Razorback Sucker]] collection records from [[Lake Mohave]] made between 22 January and 31 March 1996. The work supports the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) repatriation goal of up to 50,000 adult razorbacks to offset the declining natural population. The analysis tested whether lunar phase or collection effort explained larval catch. Neither did in any meaningful way: lunar phase showed no correlation, and effort explained only 11 to 33 percent of catch variation depending on the subset. The report recommends geographically focused, catch-guided collecting and a new standardized data sheet.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is an endangered endemic catostomid of the Colorado River system. Lake Mohave, a mainstream impoundment on the Arizona-Nevada border, holds the largest remaining stock of the species. The NFWG program uses the following sequence:

- Capture of larvae produced naturally by wild adults in the lake.
- Transfer to [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and/or [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] facilities for post-yolk-absorption feeding and growth.
- Stocking into adjacent backwaters for grow-out.
- Release into the lake at a nominal size of 30 cm total length.

Larvae are collected at night using underwater lights and fine-mesh aquarium nets. The method is labor-intensive, and collectors were asked to log date, collector, location, effort, larval count, start and end times, water depth and comments. Data were checked for completeness and consistency, transferred to a spreadsheet, and annotated with lunar phase from [[Lunar Phase]] tables (Appendix B).

Effort was computed two ways:
- **E1** = number of lights × collection time (hours).
- **E2** = number of collectors × collection time.

Analyses used ANOVA and simple linear regression on the full dataset and four geographic subsets: [[Upper Reservoir]], [[Arizona Bay]], [[Tequila Cove]] and [[Hog Farm]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Larval catch peaked at distinct times rather than cycling with the moon. The report identifies major peaks on 22 January, 12–13 February and 6–11 March 1996, and states that the causes are unknown. Candidate explanations include weather (not evident from the forms), spawning behavior that produces synchronized hatch or swim-up, and larval dispersal dynamics. Repositioning a collector by only a few meters could markedly raise or lower catch, which suggests strong microhabitat control of larval capture.

Lunar phase was examined as a potential environmental cue. Monthly lunar data for January–March 1996 (Las Vegas, NV reference) are provided in Appendix B.

## Key Findings

**Lunar phase:** No correlation was found between larval catch and lunar phase. The report recommends against scheduling collections by moon phase.

**Effort metrics:** Regression of larval number against time, E1 and E2 was statistically significant, but R² values were low:
- Full dataset (E1): R² = 0.150 (Table 2). Its intercept and slope were significant (p ≈ 7.7E-07 for the slope).
- Full dataset (E2): R² = 0.118 (Table 3).
- Interpretation: effort explains little of the variation in catch, so the number of people or lights used did not determine catch.

**Spatial subsets (E1 regressions):**

| Subset | Collections | R² (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| [[Upper Reservoir]] | 17 | 0.195 |
| [[Arizona Bay]] | 40 | 0.199 |
| [[Tequila Cove]] and vicinity | 68 | 0.166 |
| [[Hog Farm]] | 28 | 0.330 |

The Hog Farm subset had the highest R², but the author concludes that effort explaining about a third of catch has little predictive value.

**Recommendations:**
- Concentrate effort in each of four areas (Upper Reservoir, including the 15 km reach below Hoover Dam; Arizona Bay; Tequila; Hog Farm), guided by immediately prior success.
- Move to a new spot if a location is unproductive within 11 minutes (0.25 hours).
- Use total light-hours (time lights are in the water × number of lights) as the most promising effort measure. Total collection time is inappropriate. Number of collectors is a poor measure, since multiple collectors at one light do not catch more larvae.
- Adopt a new standard data sheet (Appendix C) with columns for date, time (24-hour clock), number of people, number of lights, collection time (ET), larval count, location and fishing conditions. Record a new entry for every location change.
- Stress that all fields must be completed. The 1996 analysis was weakened because many entries contained only date and count.

**Appendices:** Appendix A contains the scanned raw field logs (handwritten, not reproduced here). Appendix B gives lunar and sun/moon tables for January–March 1996. Appendix C provides the revised collection form (31 Dec 1996).
