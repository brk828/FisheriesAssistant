# Biological Criteria for Defining Species and Distinct Population Segments of Anadromous Salmonids under the U.S. Endangered Species Act

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1981 review by [[Fred Utter]] of the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NOAA Northwest and Alaska Fisheries Center) sets out biological criteria for defining species and distinct intraspecific population segments of anadromous salmonids under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 (ESA, amended 1978). The paper argues that homing alone cannot define population segments, that [[Protein Electrophoresis]] data are necessary but often insufficient, and that transplanted and hatchery fish pose genetic risks to native stocks. It proposes a three-phase, successive-approximation process for identifying population structure, allows provisional ESA decisions on incomplete data, and concludes that the capacity of artificially propagated populations to restore threatened stocks cannot be generalized.

## Background & Description

The ESA rationale is that every species, and isolated populations within species, represent irreplaceable groups with ecological, scientific, historical, recreational, aesthetic and educational value, warranting management and, where needed, protection. The paper was prepared as part of a [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] mandate to assess the status of certain U.S. anadromous stocks, with two objectives: (1) state reliable criteria for defining species and discrete populations, and (2) consider the confounding effects of transplantations and hatchery management.

Species are defined under the [[Biological Species Concept]] as groups of interbreeding natural populations reproductively isolated from other such groups. Divergence among the Pacific salmon and the trout taxa examined is believed to exceed 10^6 years (Neave 1958). The paper notes that [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]] are morphologically distinct but interfertile in the laboratory, and that they hybridize in nature in some areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sympatric populations.** Several coexisting, genetically distinct groups suggest incipient speciation:
- Sockeye salmon: innate migration tactics of fry differ between inlet and outlet populations, and crosses between them are maladaptive, indicating genetic isolation.
- Bonneville whitefish: two morphologically distinct groups coexist in Bear Lake, differing in growth and age/size at maturity.
- Brown trout in Lake Bunnersjoarna, Sweden: two demes differ in biochemical profiles, growth and spawning areas. One is thought to be a relict from a glacial refuge about 125,000 years ago. Table 1 shows significant allele-frequency differences at several loci (for example MDH-4 chi-square 51.08, P = 0.001).
- Lake Washington sockeye: a non-anadromous population in Issaquah Creek is genetically distinct from all other sampled populations in the drainage.

**Allopatric populations.** Inland cutthroat trout form geographically isolated groups with substantial divergence (mean genetic similarity about 0.85 among three interior subspecies, from about 40 loci). Coastal cutthroat trout show strong local population integrity.

**Temporal isolation.** [[Pink Salmon]] have a rigid two-year life cycle, producing odd- and even-year lines with distinct gene pools and different intrinsic growth rates. Genetic similarity is greater within a year-class than between year-classes, even in the same stream.

**Nominal species.** Rainbow trout, golden trout (*S. aguabonita*) and [[Redband Trout]] show a complicated taxonomy. Genetic similarity among these groups exceeds 0.925, which is higher than the cutthroat subspecies comparisons, illustrating the arbitrariness of morphology-based species classification.

**Geography and homing.** Genetic similarity generally increases with geographic proximity, but the stream-distance model fails where geological events created discontinuities. Populations east and west of the [[Cascade Crest]] are more similar to others on their side of the crest than to neighbours in the same drainage (Columbia and Fraser rivers). Coho and chinook also show such discontinuities.

## Key Findings

1. **Homing is insufficient alone.** The strong homing of anadromous salmonids makes a stream-hierarchy model a useful first approximation, but it ignores geological discontinuities, differing radiation capacity among species, and transplant effects.
2. **Protein data are necessary but incomplete.** Electrophoretically detected allozymes meet the ideal criteria (Mendelian, genetic-basis data) better than other methods. However, minimal allele differences do not prove homogeneity: Yellowstone cutthroat trout show little allelic variation despite large physiological and behavioural diversity.
3. **Temporal stability supports use as population markers.** Protein frequencies remained stable across life stages, generations and overlapping year-classes in Chambers Creek steelhead, supporting their use as markers of population rather than environmental differences.
4. **Steelhead population structure revised.** Additional protein systems (Milner 1977) separated the Dworshak Hatchery steelhead (Snake River) from upper Columbia populations that the initial data (Allendorf 1975) had not clearly distinguished.
5. **Three-phase process.** The paper outlines (I) background data collection, including transplant records, population records, habitat changes, allelic and meristic data, and geological history; (II) systematic sampling from range extremes, then successive finer-scale synthesis; and (III) determination of population structure and biological status. Provisional decisions based on phase I data are acceptable where urgent.
6. **Hatchery and transplant impacts are real but poorly measured.** [[Hybridization]] and introgression with introduced fish have replaced native interior cutthroat populations and reduced their growth capacity. Other studies of rainbow trout found no introgression despite long planting histories. Genetically marked hatchery fish (Kalama River steelhead) allow measurement of long-term genetic impact beyond the first generation.
7. **Domestication alters adaptive qualities.** Reisenbichler and McIntyre (1977) found genetic differences in growth and survival between hatchery and wild Deschutes River steelhead, with wild fish performing best in streams and hatchery fish in ponds.
8. **Artificial propagation of endangered stocks is unresolved.** Domesticated rainbow trout are widely established and retain high heterozygosity, but this capacity probably does not extend to threatened salmonids with depleted genetic variation. Each case requires separate evaluation.
9. **Recommendation.** Genetic impact measurement should be a requirement for new or extended transplantation and hatchery programs, since the techniques are technically and economically feasible.
