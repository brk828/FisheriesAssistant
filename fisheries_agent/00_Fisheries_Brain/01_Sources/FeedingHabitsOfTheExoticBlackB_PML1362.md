# Feeding Habits of the Exotic Black Bullhead *Ameiurus melas* in the Iberian Peninsula: First Evidence of Direct Predation on Native Fish Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study, published in the *Journal of Fish Biology* (2008, 73, 96–114) by Leunda and colleagues, is the first dietary analysis of the exotic [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*) in the Iberian Peninsula. Stomach contents of 255 fish from several [[Iberian Basins]] were examined to assess the species' threat to the native [[Iberian Ichthyofauna]]. Aquatic macroinvertebrates, mainly [[Chironomidae]] larvae, dominated the diet across all size classes and sites. The species behaves as a generalist or opportunist. Fish prey occurred in nearly 10% of specimens, and the authors report direct predation on native fishes, supporting concern that the species negatively affects native fauna through predation and competition.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** *Ameiurus melas* (Rafinesque), a demersal ictalurid catfish native to North America, from the Great Lakes to northern Mexico.
- **Ecology:** Active mainly at night. Young fish feed on immature insects, leeches and crustaceans; adults also take clams, snails, plant material and fishes.
- **Size:** Reported to exceed 600 mm total length and 3.5 kg, but Iberian individuals do not reach these sizes (Doadrio, 2001).
- **Introduction history:** Introduced to Spain around 1910–1913 (authorized and localized, e.g. Lake Banyoles). It reached Portugal much later, in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. Subsequent spread in Iberia was attributed to illegal, indiscriminate introductions for angling.
- **Invasion success traits:** High fecundity, parental care, voracious and versatile feeding, tolerance of temperatures up to 30 °C, high turbidity, wide pH range, hypoxia and pollution.
- **Context:** Native Iberian fauna is highly endemic (mainly small to medium cyprinids, cobitids and cyprinodontids) and naturally lacks obligate piscivores. As many as 25 exotic fish species have been recorded spreading in the region.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites and sampling**

- **Arga River** (Ebro tributary, 145 km, 2760 km² catchment, Navarra): 99 specimens (77–167 mm TL) by electrofishing. Native fishes included *Barbus graellsii*, *Parachondrostoma miegii*, *Gobio lozanoi*, *Phoxinus phoxinus* and *Barbatula barbatula*; exotic *Cyprinus carpio*.
- **Mainstem Ebro River** (Navarra): 51 specimens (39–204 mm TL), October 2005. Co-occurring fishes included *Salaria fluviatilis*, *Micropterus salmoides* and *Silurus glanis*, among others.
- **Guadarrama River** (right-bank Tagus tributary, 1700 km² catchment, 130 km): 74 specimens (31–265 mm TL), July–October 2006. Native fishes included *Barbus bocagei* and *Squalius pyrenaicus*; exotic species included *Gambusia holbrooki* and *Lepomis gibbosus*.
- **Ribeira de Santo Estevão reservoir** (Tagus basin, Portugal): 31 specimens (107–123 mm TL), August 2000, plus 32 specimens from Museu Bocage.
- **Guadiana River** (Portugal): one specimen (289 mm TL) by gillnet, February 2001, representing the first Portuguese records.

**Methods**

- Stomachs of all 255 specimens were examined. Fish were assigned to three length classes: <100, 100–150 and >150 mm.
- Prey were grouped into four categories: plant material, terrestrial invertebrates, aquatic invertebrates and fishes. This yielded 51 prey items overall.
- Analyses included detrended correspondence analysis ([[Detrended Correspondence Analysis]]), Horn's overlap index with UPGMA clustering, the Shannon index of trophic diversity, frequency of occurrence (%F), relative abundance (%A) and [[Costello Feeding Strategy Plots]] (modified by Amundsen et al., 1996).
- Fish prey size was back-calculated from bony remains using [[Miranda & Escala Identification Guide]] relationships.

**Diet interpretation**

- Empty stomachs (vacuity) accounted for about 26% (67 of 255).
- Diet variation among sites and size classes was linked to habitat type: riverine versus artificial lentic (reservoir).
- Plant material was common in occurrence but low in relative abundance, which the authors attribute to low energy content.

## Key Findings

**Diet composition**

- A total of 4276 animal prey items were identified from 188 non-empty stomachs.
- Plant material and algae appeared in 58.5% of stomachs (%F), usually in small quantities.
- Aquatic prey were the main food by both occurrence and abundance. Chironomidae larvae dominated in riverine sites (Arga, Guadarrama and mainstem Ebro). Microcrustaceans (Anomopoda, Copepoda, Ostracoda) dominated in the reservoir.
- Terrestrial prey occurred more often in the Guadarrama and Santo Estevão sites than in the Arga and Ebro, but their abundance was low. It was lowest in the reservoir, likely because of poorly developed riparian forest.

**Fish predation**

- Fish prey occurred in nearly 10% of specimens in both habitat types, but made up less than 2% of prey abundance and were lower in the reservoir.
- Consumed fishes included native *Barbus graellsii*, *Parachondrostoma miegii* and *Gobio lozanoi* (Arga), *P. miegii* (Ebro), and exotic *Gambusia holbrooki*, *Lepomis gibbosus* and *Cyprinus carpio*. Centrarchids and carp were the only fish prey in the reservoir.
- No positive predator–prey size relationship was found (r = 0.077, n = 16, P > 0.05). The authors interpret this as piscivory on dead or dying fishes alongside predation on smaller live fish.

**Between-site differences**

- Site-group scores differed significantly on both DCA axes (Kruskal-Wallis, P < 0.001 for each).
- Trophic diversity (H′) was lower in the reservoir than in natural rivers, and was significantly lower than in the Guadarrama and Arga rivers.

**Ontogenetic shift**

- Fish length correlated moderately and significantly with DCA axis 1 scores (rs = 0.40, P < 0.001), indicating an ontogenetic diet shift.
- Microcrustacean consumption declined with size. Mayflies (Baetidae, Caenidae) and caddisflies (Hydropsychidae and others) increased in the Arga. Oligochaeta, terrestrial prey and fishes increased in the Guadarrama and Ebro.
- Trophic diversity was lowest in the largest class (>150 mm) and highest in the 100–150 mm class.
- Feeding strategy plots showed a generalist strategy in all size classes and sites.

**Conservation implications**

- The authors conclude that the black bullhead could negatively affect native Iberian fish through direct predation and competition. Diet similarity with co-occurring native species may displace them to suboptimal food.
- Egg predation could not be excluded, because only bony remains were identified.
- Habitat degradation (river regulation, lentic creation, pollution, altered flow and thermal regimes) appears to favour exotic limnophilous species. Downstream reaches are the most vulnerable.
- Dam and weir removal in restoration programmes should account for barriers that may limit upstream expansion of exotics. Restoring natural flow and thermal regimes may favour native species.
