## Executive Summary
This 1990 trip report documents monitoring of two electrical fish barriers installed by the Salt River Project (SRP) at the heads of the South Canal and Arizona Canal, downstream of Granite Reef Dam, Arizona. These barriers were designed to prevent nonnative fish—particularly [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*)—from migrating upstream from the Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal system into the Salt River above the dam. During scheduled annual canal dewatering events, task force biologists from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and [[Salt River Project]] sampled fish trapped in pools immediately upstream of each barrier. While no striped bass were found, an unexpectedly large and dense population of [[Tilapia]] was documented at the Arizona Canal site, alongside large predatory [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]], with a notable scarcity of small forage fish. The findings suggest the barriers were effectively concentrating fish rather than allowing free downstream passage, and that predation pressure was suppressing forage fish numbers in the impounded pool.

Many individuals collected in the Arizona Canal, particularly catfish and suckers, were notably large-bodied, suggesting an accumulation of mature adult fish in the impounded pool.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context**: Both barriers were situated in irrigation canal segments diverted from the Salt River immediately below [[Granite Reef Dam]], within a broader system connected to the Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal — a known vector for nonnative fish introductions including striped bass.
- **Thermal behavior of Tilapia**: During the Arizona Canal dewatering, tilapia displayed thermoregulatory aggregation behavior, congregating near warm-water inflows leaking through the dam gates (measured at 12.2°C) versus the ambient pool temperature (10°C). This aligns with tilapia's known lower lethal temperature threshold of 6.0–6.5°C, with mortality onset near 10°C.
- **Predator-prey dynamics**: The Arizona Canal pool showed a striking absence of typical forage species ([[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Threadfin Shad]]) despite their known presence upstream of Granite Reef Dam. Only four [[Red Shiner]] individuals were collected. This scarcity was attributed to intense predation pressure from the concentrated population of [[Largemouth Bass]] and catfish species.
- **Barrier-induced concentration effect**: Fish were flushed downstream through the Granite Reef Dam gates into the canal pools but were unable/unwilling to pass the electrical barriers, resulting in unnaturally high fish density and biomass immediately upstream of the barriers — a "trap and concentrate" dynamic rather than a true exclusion of upstream passage from downstream sources.
- **Avian predation link**: A pair of [[Bald Eagle]]s was observed foraging in the partially dewatered canals near Granite Reef Dam, indicating a trophic link between the concentrated fish populations and local apex avian predators.
- **Human harvesting pressure**: Members of the public had already harvested stranded fish from the dewatered canal sections 24–48 hours prior to formal sampling, indicating an informal fishery exploiting canal dewatering events.

## Key Findings
- No [[Striped Bass]] were detected in either canal sample during the 1990 monitoring, supporting (with limited sampling) the assumption that the electrical barriers may be preventing upstream movement of this target invasive species.
- [[Tilapia]] were unexpectedly the dominant species collected, comprising ~56% of the South Canal sample and ~82% of the Arizona Canal sample — far exceeding prior expectations for this reach of the Salt River system.
- This report documents an important early distributional record for tilapia in the Salt/Verde River system: first recorded in the Verde River below Horseshoe Dam (~fall 1987, per Larry Riley, AGFD), and first reported in the Salt River system in 1988 (Saguaro Lake, via bald eagle diet study by Biosystems Analysis Inc.). A dead tilapia was also found ~1 mile upstream of Granite Reef Dam on December 17, 1990.
- Sampling methodology combined seine hauls (35×6 ft, 1/4-inch mesh) and backpack electrofishing (AGFD Coffelt unit), with the Arizona Canal effort using both methods and the South Canal relying solely on seining — the latter hampered by slow drainage, large rocks, and sediment islands, yielding only an incomplete census (433 fish, 8 species).
- Researchers estimated actual fish abundance in the Arizona Canal pool could have been up to 5 times greater than the recorded sample, underscoring the scale of fish concentration caused by the barrier.
- The scarcity of forage fish species in the Arizona Canal pool, despite their known upstream presence, is interpreted as a predation-driven effect from the unusually dense assemblage of predatory catfish and bass.
- Recommendations for future monitoring include: combining seining and electrofishing methods, accounting for longer-than-expected canal drainage times, and constructing a rigid mesh frame (1/2-inch mesh) to reliably block canal outlets during sampling.
- The report raises the possibility of salvaging native fish (e.g., [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]]) stranded during dewatering events for potential use in native fish reintroduction programs.
