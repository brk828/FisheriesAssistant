# Scorpions of the Puerto Peñasco Area (Cholla Bay), Sonora, Mexico

#arachnology #marine-biology #vejovidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 taxonomic survey by [[Williams and Hadley]] documents the scorpion fauna of the [[Puerto Peñasco]] area on the [[Gulf of California]] coast of Sonora, Mexico. The authors recorded five species in two families, described one new species, [[Vejovis Baergi]], and provided a regional identification key. They also reported range extensions for three species and discussed habitat preferences in relation to local geography and vegetation. The work was based on collecting trips in 1966 and 1967 centred on [[Cholla Bay]].

## Background & Description

**Study rationale.** Before this work, the scorpions of Baja California and the upper Gulf of California had received little systematic attention. Earlier contributions came from [[Pocock]] (1902), [[Hoffmann]] (1931–32), [[Gertsch]] (1958), and [[Gertsch and Soleglad]] (1966), the last on the [[Paruroctonus Subgenus]] of the genus *Vejovis*. The present study aimed to report the diversity and abundance of the scorpion fauna around Puerto Peñasco.

**Study area.**
- Puerto Peñasco (Punta Peñasco = Rocky Point) lies at about 31°18′ N on the Gulf of California, roughly 63 miles southwest of Sonoyta, Sonora.
- The headquarters site was Punta la Cholla, about 5 miles west-northwest of the town, with a large tidal flat to the north and granitic hills to the southwest. The highest of these, Cholla Peak, rises to 408 feet.
- The climate is hot and dry, with less than 5 inches of rain annually. Average July temperatures exceed 86°F and January averages range from 50 to 68°F. Strong Gulf winds have formed extensive dunes.
- The area lies in the Lower Colorado Valley subdivision of the [[Sonoran Desert]]. Soils are sandy and vegetation sparse. Dominant and notable plants include bur-sage (*Franseria*), saltbush (*Atriplex*), cholla (*Opuntia prolifera*, used as an indicator of how far ocean water reaches inland at high tide), ironwood, brittlebush, ocotillo, and [[Coldenia Palmeri]].

**Methods.** Collecting trips occurred in May, October, and November 1966 and January 1967. Four methods were used:
1. Turning surface objects such as rocks, debris, and trash
2. Buried [[Pitfall Trapping]]
3. Excavation of burrows
4. [[Ultraviolet Light Detection]] at night

Total length was measured from the anterior carapace to the sting tip. Carapace width was taken at the median eyes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Species richness.** The authors considered the fauna rich in both species number and abundance. Five species from two families were found within an area about 5 miles in diameter during three brief trips. The authors compared this with [[Gertsch and Allred]] (1965), who recorded nine species in two families at the [[Nevada Test Site]], an area of more than 1000 square miles.
- **Distribution.** Distribution was patchy and tracked the availability of surface shelter. Sand flats appeared to act as barriers to dispersal. Suspected habitat more than 10 miles inland from the Gulf was not occupied by [[Centruroides Sculpturatus]].
- **Habitat associations.** [[Vejovis Mesaensis]] was most abundant in coastal sand flats and dunes, and its numbers peaked where [[Coldenia Palmeri]] was common, since many individuals rested beneath these low plants at night. [[Vejovis Confusus]] dominated dunes both inland and coastal. [[Hadrurus Hirsutus]] and [[Vejovis Baergi]] were most frequent on sandy dune soils but were not dominant there.
- **Burrowing behaviour.** [[Hadrurus Hirsutus]] is a burrower that can use mammal burrows or dig its own. [[Vejovis Baergi]] appeared to be mainly a burrowing species, with adults found on the surface only at night. Its activity pattern resembled that of the obligate burrower *Anuroctonus phaeodactylus* (Williams, 1966).

## Key Findings

**Fauna and identification**
- Five species in two families were recorded: four in Vejovidae and one in Buthidae.
- The regional key separates species by sternum shape, cheliceral denticles, telson bristles, pedipalp form, and pectine tooth counts.

**Species accounts (specimen counts as reported)**

| Species | Family | Specimens | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Centruroides sculpturatus* | Buthidae | 65 | Triangular sternum, subaculear tooth, reddish-orange; dominant under rocks and in coastal dumps |
| *Hadrurus hirsutus* | Vejovidae | 20 | Large, hairy, one long dark cheliceral tooth; a southeastern range extension |
| *Vejovis baergi* | Vejovidae | 30 (17 ♂, 13 ♀) | New species; pale yellow; collected in sand lot and pitfall traps |
| *Vejovis confusus* | Vejovidae | 34 | Smooth cheliceral margin; a new southern range extension |
| *Vejovis mesaensis* | Vejovidae | 159 | Generally dominant species; previously the only species reported from the area |

**New species: *Vejovis baergi* Williams & Hadley**
- Named after Dr. William J. Baerg of the University of Arkansas.
- Holotype (male) total length 46.0 mm. Allotype (female) total length 41.0 mm.
- Carapace length 5.1 mm in both sexes. Carapace width at the median eyes 3.9 mm (male) and 4.1 mm (female).
- Pectine teeth: holotype 20/20 and allotype 15/16. Middle lamellae: 16 and 12.
- Paratypes: 16 males measured 42–48 mm and 12 females measured 38–52 mm. Pectine teeth were 18–23 (mode 22) in males and 14–15 (mode 15) in females.
- Holotype and allotype are deposited in the type collection of the [[California Academy of Sciences]].
- It is placed in the *Paruroctonus* subgenus and is closely related to *Vejovis bantai*, from which it differs in keel granulation, metasomal keels, and lack of pigmented markings. It also resembles *Vejovis intrepidus*, but differs in pectine structure, pigmentation, size, and distribution.

**Range extensions**
- *Hadrurus hirsutus*: a southeastern range extension.
- *Vejovis confusus*: a new southern range extension.
- *Centruroides sculpturatus*: a southern range record, with the Puerto Peñasco population distinct from darker, more reddish Black Mountain specimens.

**Abundance and patterns**
- Over 100 *Vejovis mesaensis* were collected in 2 hours at night on a Cholla Bay sand flat covering less than 3500 square yards.
- *Centruroides sculpturatus* was very abundant in a coastal dump 0.1 miles from the high tide mark, but absent from a dump at a dune 5 miles inland.

**Conclusions**
- The Puerto Peñasco area supports a rich scorpion fauna with one new species and several range extensions.
- Distribution is strongly patchy and linked to shelter availability, substrate type, and proximity to the coast.
