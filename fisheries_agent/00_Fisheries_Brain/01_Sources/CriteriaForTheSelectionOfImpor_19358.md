# Criteria for the Selection of Important Sites for Freshwater Fish in the British Isles

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Peter S. Maitland]] proposes national criteria for selecting freshwater fish sites in the British Isles for notification as [[National Nature Reserves]] or [[Sites of Special Scientific Interest]]. The author argues that many important fish sites remain unprotected because clear selection criteria are lacking. Six criteria are defined: rare species, local races, pristine stocks, unusual communities, community diversity, and community type in relation to habitat. These are presented as a summary questionnaire (Appendix) for conservationists. The paper also sets out regional species-richness thresholds, habitat-based guidance, and recommendations for protecting and re-establishing rare fish populations.

## Background & Description

The paper builds on earlier work by Maitland (1974) on the status of all British freshwater fish and Maitland (1979a) on rare species and genetic strains. The author notes that the freshwater fish resource has value beyond sport and commercial fisheries, including amenity, educational, scientific and genetic significance. The principal trend identified is a shift from natural, stable, mixed fish populations toward artificially maintained, unstable stocks of a few sporting or commercial species. Rarer, sensitive species with poor dispersal are being replaced by commoner, more robust species.

The [[Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981]] prompted a review of site evaluation in Great Britain, creating the need for national criteria. Only native species are considered in a conservation context; introduced species are excluded, as several are potentially dangerous to native stocks.

Rare native freshwater fish in the paper are defined as species with only a few known British populations, or native species whose populations are individually unique and declining. Table 1 lists eight rare species, including [[Sturgeon]], [[Allis Shad]], [[Twaite Shad]], [[Whitefish]], [[Pollan]], [[Vendace]], [[Houting]] and [[Burbot]]. Sturgeon and houting are vagrants that do not breed in the British Isles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Post-glacial isolation and local races.** Most native species have been isolated since the end of the last Ice Age (c. 10,000 years ago), and some have developed characteristic local races. Native fish are described as far more unique than birds such as the [[Osprey]], whose populations are continuous with mainland Europe. Examples of local races include:
- Spine-deficient [[Three-Spined Stickleback]] and [[Ten-Spined Stickleback]] populations in the Uists (Outer Hebrides).
- The "golden" trout of Loch Crocach, a distinct [[Brown Trout]] race whose genetics are unknown.
- Several distinct [[Arctic Charr]] forms in Scotland, including large colourful charr in Loch Inver and dwarf profundal charr in Loch Eck. Several Scottish charr populations have become extinct in recent years.

**Pristine stocks.** Extensive introductions and translocations over many centuries make the status of pristine stocks uncertain. Rare species such as whitefish and less common species such as arctic charr are likely to be pristine, whereas common species are uncertain. Species of angling value have been moved most. [[Ruffe]], previously absent from Scotland, was recently found in Loch Lomond and was probably introduced by anglers.

**Introduced species.** More than 20 species have been introduced to the British Isles, of which 12 are successful with viable populations (Table 2). Examples include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Charr]], [[Common Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Wels Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Pikeperch]]. Introduced species are excluded from assessments, and the grass carp is being introduced to a number of waters.

**Regional richness.** The main constraint on diversity is the south-to-north gradient in species numbers, shaped by the island's biogeography and post-glacial recolonisation. Species counts per 100-km square exceed 30 in much of southern Britain but fall below 10 in the extreme north (Fig. 1). Four geographic areas were defined, following major watersheds (Fig. 2):
- **Area A:** Highlands and islands of Scotland; typically 6–8 species.
- **Area B:** Northeast highlands, central and southern Scotland, Isle of Man and Ireland; typically 8–12 species.
- **Area C:** Northern England, western Wales and southwest England; typically 12–16 species.
- **Area D:** Eastern Wales, central and southeast England; typically 16–20 species.

**Habitat.** Three parameters (geology, size and altitude) determine fish communities (Table 4):
- The smallest pools and trickles usually lack fish because they dry out or freeze.
- Upland lakes and streams are chemically poor and often dominated by [[Salmonids]].
- Species richness increases with decreasing altitude, with lowland communities often dominated by [[Cyprinids]].
- The largest British lakes lie near sea level.

**Marine-affinity species.** Of the 35 common species in Table 3, 12 have strong marine affinities. These are confined to upper estuaries and lower river reaches, or can reach upstream only where barriers are absent. Examples include [[Sea Lamprey]], [[River Lamprey]], [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[European Eel]] and [[Flounder]]. Natural waterfalls and artificial barriers such as weirs and pollution restrict this movement.

## Key Findings

- **Selection criteria:** Six criteria are proposed: rare species, local races, pristine stocks, unusual communities, community diversity, and community type in relation to habitat.
- **Regional thresholds:** Sites are of interest if they exceed species thresholds in their area. Area A exceeds 6 species (exceptional above 8), Area B exceeds 8 (exceptional above 12), Area C exceeds 12 (exceptional above 16), and Area D exceeds 16 (exceptional above 20). These values exclude introduced species.
- **Rare species:** Eight native species are rare. Allis shad and burbot require more distribution data. Sturgeon and houting occur only as vagrants.
- **Unusual combinations:** Only two lakes, Loch Eck and Haweswater, hold both charr and whitefish. Llyn Tegid is the only lake with whitefish and grayling. Loch Meallt on Skye holds only charr and three-spined stickleback, possibly the original post-glacial community.
- **Barrier effects:** Removing downstream barriers can potentially double species numbers. The River Clyde has about 10 species at present, and the number could reach at least 18 as pollution barriers are reduced.
- **Fishless waters:** Some naturally fishless waters are desirable because they allow plant and invertebrate communities to develop. Such sites should be assessed for their other biota.
- **Protection of rare species:** Major sites should be protected through reserve or SSSI status. Local water authorities should be informed so that pollution control can be prioritised. Where there are fewer than five well-protected populations, new ones should be established.
- **Population establishment:** New populations should be created from fertilised eggs collected at spawning time, with parent fish returned. Egg collection should cover many adults across the spawning period to maximise genetic diversity. Stock should come from the nearest British source, and introductions from abroad are a last resort.
- **Reintroduction:** Original sites should be considered first. Alternative sites should match the species' full habitat requirements, lie close to the original site, and preferably be in the same watershed.
- **Legislation and museums:** Only the burbot is named in the Wildlife and Countryside Act (1981). The author recommends legal protection for other endangered species and a published list of museum specimens of rare species, including the Rostherne smelt, which may be the only surviving examples of an extinct population.
- **Conclusions:** Rare fish require urgent attention. The author calls for public promotion of fish conservation, suppression of the spread and introduction of common species, and active management of rare species.
