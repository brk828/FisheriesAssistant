# Proposed Listing of the Virgin River Chub as an Endangered Species with Critical Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 *Federal Register* proposed rule, issued by the U.S. [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Department of the Interior), proposes listing the [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended, and designating its critical habitat. The subspecies is endemic to the Virgin River in Arizona, Nevada and Utah and is threatened chiefly by habitat alteration through water diversion, desalinization, urban growth, impoundment, pollution and sedimentation, plus competition and predation by exotic fishes. Its very limited range makes it especially vulnerable. The package also includes a cover letter and a state clearinghouse review form, both requesting comment on the proposal and its economic impacts.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** First collected and described near Washington, Utah, by members of the [[Wheeler Survey]] (Cope and Yarrow 1875). It was later treated as a subspecies of *Gila robusta*. Holden and Stalnaker (1970) and Minckley (1973) supported its validity as a subspecies, and Smith et al. (1977) confirmed this with extensive taxonomic analyses.
- **Morphology:** A silvery, medium-sized minnow, generally less than 6 inches (about 15 cm) in total length. Small, deeply embedded scales on the back, breast and belly are difficult to see and may be absent in some individuals, which gives the subspecific name *minudo*.
- **Related form:** A closely related, undescribed subspecies occurs in the [[Moapa River]] in Nevada, a former Virgin River tributary now drained to [[Lake Mead]].
- **Diet:** Omnivorous, eating algae, aquatic and terrestrial insects, organic detritus and crustaceans (Cross 1975).
- **Habitat preference:** Most common in deeper areas with swift but non-turbulent water, usually associated with boulders or other cover. It occurs over sand and gravel substrates in water below about 32 °C (90 °F) and tolerates high salinity and turbidity (Deacon and Holden 1977).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution:** The present range is the mainstem Virgin River from Mesquite, Nevada, upstream to La Verkin Springs near Hurricane, Utah. Historically it was abundant in the river and ranged farther, but it has declined since about 1860–1900, when most present water diversions were built and the valley was heavily altered by agriculture. There is only one record from a tributary (Cross 1975).

**Land ownership:** Riverside lands are managed by the [[Bureau Of Land Management]] (BLM), the states, and private owners. In Arizona about 80–90% of riverside land is BLM-administered. In Utah about 13 miles are BLM land, and in Nevada the lands above Mesquite are private.

**Population status:** Surveys found very few young-of-the-year and few adults over seven inches standard length, indicating poor recruitment. The [[Woundfin Recovery Team]] reported good reproduction in 1983 but no evidence of successful reproduction in 1984. Electrofishing gave higher catches than seining but does not show population trends, so long-term monitoring is needed.

**Threat hierarchy:** Habitat modification is the major threat. Cross (1975) found the chub in unmodified habitat 80% of the time, in slightly modified habitat 20% of the time, and only rarely in extensively modified habitat.

**Water demand:** Washington County, Utah, grew from 13,889 (1970) to 26,065 (1980), up from 9,269 in 1940. Water is expected to become a limiting factor, and future water use may reduce flows to the detriment of the chub. Five major diversions remove nearly all flow from long stretches of the river during summer irrigation, and three lie within the chub's range. Downstream effects include higher temperatures, crowding, competition, predation, disease and pollution.

**Exotic fish barrier:** The naturally high salinity, temperature and turbidity, along with highly fluctuating flows, have limited exotic fish invasion. [[La Verkin Springs]] (discharge about 11 cubic feet per second, temperature 100–109 °F, salinity 9,650 mg/L) and [[Littlefield Spring]] keep the river saline. Impoundment or desalinization could remove this barrier. The [[Red Shiner]] is moving upstream from Lake Mead into Utah and threatens early life stages.

**Protected and planned projects:** Existing reservoirs (Gunlock, Kolob, Ash Creek) have not individually caused major harm but add cumulative effects. The Quail Creek Water Reclamation Project is expected to maintain minimum flows of 5.6 cubic feet per second (as given in the source) and was judged unlikely to jeopardize the [[Woundfin]]. Several potential projects (Warner Valley Energy System, now dropped, Dixie Project, La Verkin Springs Unit, and Lower Virgin River Unit) and USDA Soil Conservation Service projects could affect the chub.

**Regulatory status:** Listed by Arizona as Group 2 (Threatened Native Wildlife), by Nevada as sensitive, and by Utah as threatened. None of these listings protect habitat. Utah and Nevada water law has no provisions for instream water rights.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed action:** Endangered status and critical habitat for the Virgin River chub, with critical habitat in the main channel of the Virgin River in Mohave County (AZ), Clark County (NV) and Washington County (UT). Listing would provide full ESA protection.
- **Primary constituent elements of habitat** (as listed in the proposed rule): deeper pools and runs with cover in the mainstem channel, swift but turbid water, sand-and-gravel substrates, few or no exotic fish species, and periodic flooding to maintain habitat quality.
- **Threats:** Habitat alteration and loss (diversions, impoundment, desalinization, urban growth, sedimentation, pollution), exotic predators and competitors, inadequate regulatory mechanisms, and vulnerability from a restricted range.
- **Reproduction concern:** Poor recruitment is a key cause of the population decline, and the future of the subspecies depends on successful spawning.
- **Data gaps:** No long-term population trend data existed, and the effects of several Soil Conservation Service and Bureau of Reclamation projects were unknown.
- **Public process:** Comments were due August 28, 1986, and hearing requests by August 8, 1986. Contact was Gerald Burton, Endangered Species Biologist, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Albuquerque, New Mexico.
- **Clearinghouse review:** The Arizona State Clearinghouse forwarded the proposal to the Arizona Game and Fish Department (Region IV) for review, with a 17-working-day response period. The form asks reviewers to rate consistency with agency goals, overlap with other programs, and legal compliance.
