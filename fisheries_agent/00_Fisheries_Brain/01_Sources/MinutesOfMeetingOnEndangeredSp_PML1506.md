# Ozone Treatment Feasibility for Nonnative Fish and Aquatic Biota Control in the CAP Canal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #percidae #ictaluridae #ozone-toxicity

## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled file covering two linked issues: (1) a 1990s consultation between the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] and the Arizona Game and Fish Department over the introduction of nonnative fish from the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) into the Gila River Basin, and (2) a 2001 inquiry into whether [[Ozone Treatment]] could eradicate nonnative aquatic species in the CAP canal at Lake Havasu. Correspondence with ozone vendors, a vendor technical memo, an EPA-based ozone summary with cost estimates, and an ECOTOX toxicity appendix support the second issue. The ozone reviewers concluded that application to the CAP canal "does not appear technically or economically practical."

## Background & Description

**Fish-transfer consultation (1989–1993).** Water levels in Lake Lake Carlos (Sea Carlos) Reservoir dropped to a significant fish-kill risk. To preserve the fishery, Reclamation agreed to supply 30,000 acre-feet of CAP water in lieu of Gila River water. Concern then centered on nonnative fish moving from the Colorado River through CAP deliveries into the Florence-Casa Grande Canal and potentially the Gila River. Reclamation built fish barriers on the Pima Lateral and at Chiai Wash (Chila Wash in the scan). A [[Fish Transport Study]] by the USFWS Cooperative Fish Research Unit (Tucson) concluded that nonnative fish transported by CAP could pass into the Gila River above Coolidge Dam, but that the costs of precluding this were probably not commensurate with the probable benefits.

**Ozone inquiry (2001).** Carol Pacey (ASU) asked ozone suppliers whether an outdoor ozone system could kill fish, crustaceans, plants and algae in the CAP canal at Lake Havasu, which carries about 3,000 CFS (canal roughly 60 ft wide by 16 ft deep). Manual and chemical removal were described as too labor intensive and ineffective.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Ozone chemistry:** Ozone (O3) is unstable, strongly oxidizing (second only to fluorine among halogen oxidants), corrosive and toxic. Its odor is detectable above 0.01 ppm. It decomposes to oxygen, leaving no persistent residue, but it can form toxic byproducts such as aldehydes, ketones, acids and brominated compounds; bromate is a concern when bromide is present.
- **Biological effects:** Ozone-produced oxidants (OPO) damage gills and red blood cells, causing hypoxia and methemoglobin formation. Reported thresholds include:
  - Cutthroat and steelhead trout eggs and larvae: high mortality at 0.5 ppm; 0.1 ppm had no effect on fry.
  - Blue crab juveniles tolerated OPO better than larvae.
  - Grass shrimp tolerated 0 to 3 ppm ozone without stress; shrimp died after 12 minutes at 4 ppm (personal communication).
  - Zebra mussel control in pipes: dissolved ozone of 0.1 to 0.3 ppm.
- **Sensitivity pattern:** [[Burton Et Al 1981]] found early life stages of American oyster and striped bass most sensitive to OPO, with sensitivity decreasing during development. Adult white perch recovered from sublethal exposure because gill tissue repairs itself.
- **Human health:** Ozone irritates the respiratory tract; 1 ppm irritates mucous membranes, and high exposures can cause pulmonary edema.

## Key Findings

**Fish transfer (1990–1993 consultation)**
- The CAP fish-transport study concluded nonnative fish could transit CAP into the Gila River above Coolidge Dam.
- Reclamation's Biological Assessment was transmitted to FWS in February 1993, built on the study; the FWS draft Biological Opinion was received in May 1991.
- Meeting attendees agreed to a 50-day period for the biological review and a 90-day consultation deadline.

**Ozone feasibility (2001 correspondence)**
- Bollyky (BAI) judged an ozone system "very likely" feasible but recommended a pilot study. An order-of-magnitude requirement of about 16,000 lb/day was given, explicitly as an approximate guideline.
- Smith (Cyclopss) noted ozone would be wasted on total organic load, recommended filtration first, and cited a CT value of 0.55 ppm-min at 20 °C for Cryptosporidium. A five-minute contact time implies a contact chamber of roughly 300 m³ for 3,000 CFS (derived by Smith's own calculation, approximately 1,500,000 cubic meters of treatment-plant volume referenced).
- Largest ozone water treatment plant in the US: Los Angeles, about 930 CFS; world's largest: Moscow, about 2,500 CFS.

**Cost estimates (Los Angeles Aqueduct Filtration Plant, 600 MGD / 930 CFS)**
- Original capital cost: $13.4 M (about $140 M for the whole LAAFP, per the source).
- Upgrade capital: about $1.5 M.
- Production cost: about $0.50/lb ozone.
- Annual maintenance about $500K; power about $750K; total operation and management about $1.3 M.
- Availability 97.1% (1986–1996).

**Conclusion on the CAP canal**
- Scaling capital cost by capacity, treating the full 3,000 CFS canal would cost more than $40 M, with annual O&M near $4 M (unadjusted for inflation).
- The reviewers concluded ozone application to the CAP canal "does not appear technically or economically practical," citing capital cost, annual expense, use hazards, health risks and the absence of industry experience at this scale.

**ECOTOX toxicity appendix**
- Appendix 1 summarizes ozone toxicity records from eleven sources, retrieved from the EPA ECOTOX database. Fish records include American shad, white sucker, channel catfish, [[Bluegill]], [[White Perch]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Fathead Minnow]] and others. Zebra mussel LC50 and LC95 records range from roughly 260 to 560 µg/L in 1-day to multi-day exposures.
- Bluegill LC50 values range from below 100 µg/L to about 670 µg/L depending on exposure duration and study, with most records being mortality endpoints.
