## Executive Summary
This 1989 news report documents a sharp, unexplained decline in "young-of-the-year" [[Colorado Squawfish]] in Utah's Green River, observed by [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] biologist Steve Cranney during a fall 1989 survey. Fewer than 100 juvenile squawfish were found across a 110-mile stretch of river (Dinosaur National Monument to Sand Wash/[[Desolation Canyon]]), representing less than 10% of the expected population following a record-breaking prior year. A leading hypothesis under discussion is a link to a June 2, 1989 oil spill on the [[Yampa River]], in which ~13,000 gallons of light crude oil leaked from a ruptured Amoco pipeline near Craig, Colorado, coinciding with the squawfish's spawning migration. Experts caution that causation is not confirmed, citing confounding factors such as anomalously low water levels, and note that reproductive shortfalls were also seen far downstream at [[Gray Canyon]], albeit to a lesser degree.

## Background & Description
- Historically grew up to **6 feet (1.8 m)** in length, formerly the apex predator of the Colorado River basin food chain ("ruled the river like a lion rules the jungle").
- Capable of migrating up to **500 miles** to reach preferred spawning sites.
- Relies on a **sensitive olfactory (smell) system** to locate breeding grounds, likely detecting chemical odor cues from tributary streams, springs, or seeps — a trait shared with other migratory freshwater fish (e.g., [[Salmon]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historic role**: Top predator in the [[Colorado River]] food web.
- **Current range**: Small remnant populations persist in the [[Yampa River]], the [[White River]] (eastern Utah), the main stem of the [[Green River]], and a few individuals in the lower [[San Juan River]].
- **Spawning behavior**: Widely dispersed most of the year; congregates in early summer within pools and riffles of the lower Yampa River to breed.
- **Co-occurring rare native fish** in the Yampa River ecosystem:
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] — federally endangered
  - [[Humpback Chub]] — federally endangered
  - [[Bonytail Chub]] — federally endangered
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] — proposed for endangered species listing
- **Threats to population**: Dam construction, habitat destruction, and introduction of exotic/non-native fish species have drastically reduced squawfish numbers historically, independent of the 1989 spill event.

## Key Findings
- **Population anomaly**: 1989 fall survey found <100 young-of-year squawfish in the 110-mile Green River study reach — less than one-tenth of the anticipated number, despite the prior year (1988) being the best recorded reproductive year.
- **Oil spill event**: On June 2, 1989, a 6-inch steel pipeline crossing the Yampa River near Craig, Colorado ruptured, releasing ~13,000 gallons of light crude oil. The oil dispersed quickly in air and water; no dead or dying fish were observed by monitoring biologists, and only limited water/fish/sediment/insect samples were collected with no detailed follow-up studies conducted.
- **Timing overlap**: The spill occurred during the peak adult squawfish migration period toward Yampa River spawning grounds, raising concern about potential interference with reproduction.
- **Proposed mechanism — olfactory disruption**: Dr. Douglas Martin ([[Pacific Environmental Technologies]]) cited salmon research showing that **1–10 parts per billion (ppb)** of dissolved volatile crude oil compounds can disorient migrating fish by temporarily numbing/anesthetizing olfactory sense, impairing their ability to follow spawning-ground odor cues. Disoriented fish often reverse direction downstream in an apparent attempt to relocate the lost scent trail; duration of this olfactory impairment is unknown.
- **Supporting literature**: Dr. Walter Pearson ([[Battelle Marine Research Laboratory]]) noted established literature on crude oil's disruption of "biologically relevant odor cues" used by fish and other animals for breeding and feeding.
- **Behavioral avoidance precedent**: Prof. Tom Quinn (University of Washington School of Fisheries) referenced post-[[Mount Saint Helens]] eruption research showing migrating salmon paused at the mouths of ash-contaminated streams, apparently awaiting improved water quality before continuing to spawning beds — suggesting an analogous avoidance behavior could apply to squawfish in oil-contaminated water.
- **Confounding variable**: A parallel reproductive shortfall was recorded at a squawfish breeding site in [[Gray Canyon]] of the Green River, located hundreds of miles downstream of the spill site — though the decline there was notably less severe than near the Yampa confluence, weakening (but not eliminating) a clean causal link to the spill alone.
- **Alternative explanation**: Unusually low water levels in the Yampa River during the relevant period are cited as a plausible independent or contributing cause of reduced reproductive success.
- **Scientific consensus in report**: All experts interviewed agreed the oil-spill hypothesis is plausible and worth investigating, but insufficient data exist to confirm or refute a definitive causal relationship between the spill and the squawfish decline.
