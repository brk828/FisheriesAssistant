# A Review of the Japanese Salmons: *Oncorhynchus masou* and *O. rhodurus* with Particular Reference to Their Potential for Introduction into Ontario Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Ontario Department of Lands and Forests Research Information Paper (Fisheries) No. 37 (February 1970, by W. J. Christie) reviews two native Japanese Pacific salmons, the [[Cherry Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus masou*) and the [[Redspot Salmon]] (*O. rhodurus*), as candidates for introduction into the [[Great Lakes]]. The review draws on a site tour of Japan in September and early October 1966 and on the literature. The author concludes that the advantages of the fish "far outweigh" the risks, chiefly because both species are the only naturally land-locked piscivorous Pacific salmons, combining large size, high productivity and sport value. The paper also flags serious gaps in knowledge and significant risks, including competition with [[Rainbow Trout]] and uncontrolled spread before assessment.

## Background & Description

**Motivation.** Declining premium fish stocks and ongoing [[Eutrophication]] in the Great Lakes prompted Ontario to survey exotic fishes. Christie argues that restoring the historical faunal balance is no longer realistic, so new species better suited to changed conditions are needed. Earlier surveys covered European fishes ([[Martin]], 1967). The [[Ayu]] (*Plecoglossus altivelis*) was also examined as an algae-grazer but was judged unpromising, since it cannot survive Great Lakes winters and feeds on algae only in streams.

**Geography.** Japan's four main islands lie between 24°N and 46°N. [[Hokkaido]] has a cool temperate, humid continental climate comparable to southern Ontario. Japan has few lakes: only 20 exceed 2,500 acres, and natural freshwater lake area totals 700 sq. mi., or 0.5% of land, compared with 10% in Ontario. Lakes are largely volcanic in origin, and 37% of 626 lakes and ponds listed by Horie (1962) lack outlet rivers. Rivers are short and steep, subject to monsoon flushing and scouring.

**Taxonomy.** Japanese workers recognize seven *Oncorhynchus* species in the Far East, including [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Coho Salmon]], [[Chum Salmon]], [[Pink Salmon]], [[Sockeye Salmon]] (including [[Kokanee]]), and the two focal species. Taxonomy remains contested. Behnke, Koh and Needham (1962) propose a single species with three subspecies, and the author retains the seven-species list for ecological purposes. Christie treats the two focal species together because differences in their ecology are small. The English common name "cherry salmon" reflects *masou*'s flowering-season return, while Ricker and Loftus (1968) proposed "redspot salmon" for *rhodurus*.

**Morphology.** Juveniles of both species have deep bodies, distinct parr marks and dorsal black spots. Smolts become silvery and elongate. Redspot salmon are distinguished by red spots that persist into the river-resident [[Amago]] form. Hikita's (1962) meristic counts show that the two species are morphologically similar. Cherry salmon have a ventral fin ray count fixed near 10, versus a mean of 11 in redspot. Oshima (1957) showed through hybridization that red spotting is genotypic and recessive to the masou coloration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life-history flexibility.** Both species occur as sea-run, river-resident and lake-resident forms. River-resident cherry salmon are called [[Yamabe]]. In rearing experiments, 40 to 60% of fry from anadromous cherry salmon runs became Yamabe and failed to smolt, and males made up up to 75% of Yamabe. Hikita (1962) and Rounsefell (1962) placed these species in an "adaptively anadromous" category. Christie argues that this flexibility may help them survive the rigorous monsoon-driven conditions of Japanese rivers.

**Habitat threats.** Christie judges endemic salmons to be "in danger." Dams obstruct most major salmon rivers, gravel mining damages spawning and juvenile habitat, and pollution is increasing where lakes receive river flows. The official response relies heavily on large-scale [[Hatchery Culture]]. Dams and gravel removal are blamed for declining yields.

**Distribution.** Cherry salmon range from 24°N to 55°N, with the main concentration on the Sea of Japan side. Their range extends to Vladivostok and the Amur River, and they are most abundant in Hokkaido. Redspot salmon occur on Shikoku and the Pacific side of Honshu, from Tokyo Bay south-westward to northern Kyushu. Where ranges overlap, the two are not captured together. Redspot may tolerate warmer water than cherry salmon.

**Movement.** Sea-run cherry salmon show the most restricted oceanic movements of Japanese Pacific salmons. Their southward seasonal pattern follows the Polar Front and the 10°C surface isotherm. In [[Lake Biwa]], juvenile and older redspot inhabit the hypolimnion in summer. Most fish descend to the lake as fry rather than remaining in streams.

**Feeding.** Fry feed on microcrustaceans and progress to aquatic insects and then to small fish. Smolts do not feed. Adults in streams eat only fish. Lake Biwa redspot feed on the goby *Chaenogobius isaza* and Ayu. Cherry salmon in Uryu Reservoir fed almost entirely on [[Pond Smelt]]. Cherry salmon also eat kokanee fry, so the two are not planted together.

**Temperature and thermal tolerance.** Cherry salmon are found in streams reaching 20°C in late summer. Spawning temperatures vary (peak 15°C in the Shiribetsu River; generally 5 to 12°C elsewhere). Both species were held at 1.5°C for three months or more at the Glenora Fisheries Station, and their acclimation to falling temperatures is slightly slower than kokanee.

## Key Findings

**Biology and life history**

- **Age and maturity:** 90% of Hokkaido anadromous cherry salmon spawn at age three (one freshwater winter, one ocean winter). Lake Biwa redspot males mature at age 3 or 4, and females at 4 or 5.
- **Size:** Hokkaido adult cherry salmon measure 36 to 71 cm total length in rivers. Smolts are 11 to 12 cm after descending as 18-month-old fish. Lake Biwa spawners measure 44.9 to 48.2 cm. Back-calculated lengths for Lake Biwa redspot are 24.0 cm (year 2), 36.5 cm (year 3) and 42.0 cm (year 4).
- **Fecundity and eggs:** Hokkaido anadromous females average about 2,500 eggs at 2.5 kg, while Yamabe spawn 200 to 400 eggs. Eggs average about 5 mm.
- **Spawning:** Redds in one redspot study averaged 1,400 cm² and were 10 to 30 cm deep (mean 22 cm). Current velocities averaged 13.9 cm/s. Males guard the redd and females dig it.
- **Incubation:** 430 to 480 centigrade degree-days above 0°C, or 55 to 60 days at 8°C.

**Fisheries and abundance**

- **Catch:** Annual cherry salmon catch is 2,000 to 5,000 metric tons, about 5% of Japan's total salmon catch. The U.S.S.R. harvests about 1,000 t per year.
- **Productivity:** At Lake Towada, annual catches approximate 1,000,000 kokanee, 100,000 cherry salmon and 200 rainbow trout. The Japanese rank kokanee most productive, cherry salmon second and rainbow trout third.
- **Decline:** Cherry salmon production has declined, though the extent is unknown. Chum catches fell by two-thirds between the turn of the century and the 1950s.
- **Hatcheries:** Propagation costs at Lake Biwa equal 20% of the annual redspot yield value, and runs continue to fall despite propagation.

**Introduction considerations**

- **Advantages (author's view):** Ontario lies within the climatic range of both species; the fish are piscivorous and can use Great Lakes smelt and alewife; they are large, high-quality game fish; and fry stocking is more economical than the smolt plantings required for coho and chinook.
- **Drawbacks:** Possible competition with rainbow trout; unknown triggers for fry descent (if fish stay in streams to smolt, productivity is lost); fall spawning may reduce egg and fry survival through floods and ice-scour; and supply limits may force brood stocks into reservoir lakes, raising the risk of uncontrolled spread.
- **Egg transport:** In autumn 1965, 50,000 eyed cherry salmon eggs were shipped to Ontario, but only 25,000 survived because of unsuitable packaging. Three small redspot egg shipments arrived with excellent survival.
- **Egg supply:** Lake Biwa's redspot spawn-take might yield up to 100,000 eggs per year, though this is not guaranteed indefinitely. Lake Chuzenji may provide several thousand eggs annually in exchange for whitefish and lake trout eggs.
- **Choice of source:** Christie favors the Lake Biwa redspot because of lower cost, shorter shipping time and freshwater origin, but recommends testing both species in experimental quantities.
- **Coho and chinook comparison:** Christie notes that neither species has formed self-sustaining land-locked populations. In one Georgetown Lake, Montana trial, only about 15% of eggs from hatchery-reared coho hatched. Pink salmon in Lake Superior are cited as evidence that selection can produce acclimatized populations.

**Recommendations and conclusions**

- Test planting is required to resolve questions of competition, productivity and fry-descent behavior.
- Introductions should be tightly controlled, with coordinated, interstate and international programs to avoid mutual damage among agencies.
- Gaps remain in knowledge of parasites and diseases, for which "virtually no information" was available.
