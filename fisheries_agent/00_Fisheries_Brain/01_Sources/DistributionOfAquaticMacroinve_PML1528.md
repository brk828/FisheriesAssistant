# Distribution of Aquatic Macroinvertebrates in Three Sonoran Desert Springbrooks

#macroinvertebrates #freshwater-ecology #springbrooks

## Executive Summary

This study examines the occurrence and distribution of aquatic macroinvertebrates in three springbrooks in south-east Arizona, near Bylas, within the [[Sonoran Desert]]. Taxonomic richness was low at spring sources and increased in middle and lower reaches, which the authors attribute to harsh physico-chemical conditions at the sources that ameliorate downstream. The study found 53 taxa, including 46 insects. Sonoran Desert springs support far poorer invertebrate communities than desert streams, and springs elsewhere also often have depauperate faunas.

## Background & Description

Aquatic macroinvertebrate communities of the [[Sonoran Desert]] were poorly known at the time of publication. Earlier work mostly addressed individual taxa, faunal extensions, or taxonomic revisions, and most ecological studies focused on intermittent or permanent streams. Springs are unique aquatic habitats, typically characterized at their sources by constancy in temperature, discharge, and dissolved solids. Many desert springs have high temperatures, high dissolved solids and free carbon dioxide, and low dissolved oxygen and pH, all stressful to many aquatic organisms.

The study sites were three springbrooks with these features:

- **[[First Spring]]**: fed by two distinct sources, 'Bos' and 'Medicine' springs, which join into a common brook.
- **[[Second Spring]]**: a single channel that disappears into the alluvium through evapotranspiration and percolation.
- **[[Salt Creek]]**: studied most intensively; flows reached up to 1.0 km during 1981–1982.

All three systems are 0.2–2.0 m wide, with current velocities up to 0.5 m/s, and join the [[Gila River]] during large floods. Maximum observed flow distances during 1981–1982 were 0.8 km (First Spring), 0.4 km (Second Spring) and 1.0 km (Salt Creek). Ephemeral lower portions receded several hundred meters during dry periods.

Aquatic plants included spikerush ([[Eleocharis]] spp.), naiad ([[Najas]] sp.), pondweed ([[Zannichellia Palustris]]), and filamentous algae. Riparian vegetation included lizard tail (*Anemopsis californica*), velvet mesquite (*Prosopis velutina*), and dense stands of introduced [[Tamarix Chinensis]] (saltcedar). The surrounding vegetation is typical [[Creosotebush Scrub]] (Lowe, 1964).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physico-chemical conditions.** Temperature, dissolved oxygen, and pH were measured intermittently from August 1980 to March 1982. Conductivity and total alkalinity were measured in the laboratory, and dissolved carbon dioxide was calculated from pH and alkalinity.

- Temperatures at the four sources ranged from 29.0 to 32.2 °C and decreased downstream, with extremes of 17.0 °C and 36.7 °C both recorded in [[Second Spring]].
- Dissolved carbon dioxide was high at three spring sources and diminished quickly downstream. [[Dissolved Oxygen]] followed the opposite pattern.
- Most sources had low pH, which rose to between 8.5 and 9.0 downstream. [[Medicine Spring]] had a consistently high pH along its entire length.
- [[Conductivity]] was high, ranging from 3400 to 5050 µmhos/cm, with no consistent longitudinal gradient. Medicine Spring was distinctly lower, at 1350–1700 µmhos/cm, indicating a different water source.

**Sampling.** Macroinvertebrates were collected with dip nets, aquarium nets, and an [[Ekman Dredge]] in March, April, and July 1982 at each springhead and middle section, and at lower sections of First Spring and Salt Creek. Samples were sieved through 0.991 mm and 0.244 mm mesh, preserved in 10% formalin, and examined under macro- and microscopes. The downstream reach of First Spring was dry after initial sampling, so it was sampled only once.

**Comparison with other systems.** The authors cite earlier work showing that headsprings with low dissolved oxygen and high carbon dioxide support few taxa, and that temperature limits aquatic life in some springs. They note that springs elsewhere, including Florida springs and North American hot springs, are often depauperate, possibly due to constant or harsh physico-chemical environments.

## Key Findings

- **Total richness:** 53 taxa were recorded, including 46 insects. Taxonomic richness was low at headsprings and higher in middle and lower reaches.
- **Gradient driver:** The authors attribute low headspring diversity to unfavorable physico-chemical conditions at the sources, with conditions ameliorating downstream. Gains in richness downstream came mostly from [[Coleoptera]] and [[Diptera]], with some [[Hemiptera]] and a mayfly, [[Baetis]] sp., which was never found at springheads.
- **Widespread taxa:** Four taxa occurred at all permanent water sites, including planarians, the amphipod [[Hyalella Azteca]], and two [[Hydrobiidae]] snails.
- **Rare taxa:** Most taxa (about 55%) were rare, occurring at only one or two sites.
- **Headspring specialists:** Three species (two [[Elmidae]] beetles and one [[Hydrophilidae]] beetle) were found only in Bylas headsprings, each represented by a single specimen.
- **Spring versus stream:** Sonoran Desert springs support far poorer invertebrate communities than desert streams.
- **Conclusion:** Constant and/or harsh spring environments likely limit invertebrate diversity at sources, and downstream ameliorating conditions allow richer communities to develop. The authors call for further study of the Bylas springs and of springs more broadly.
