# Endangered Species Act (as Amended by Public Law 97-304)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-law

## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled text of the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 as amended, printed for the Senate Committee on Environment and Public Works (cover date garbled in the scan, likely February 1983). It incorporates the Endangered Species Act Amendments of 1978 and the 1982 amendments (Public Law 97-304). The Act provides a legal framework for identifying, listing, and protecting endangered and threatened fish, wildlife, and plants, including marine and aquatic species, and for conserving the ecosystems they depend on. It does not report field data; its content is statutory, covering findings, definitions, listing procedures, federal consultation duties, prohibitions, exceptions, penalties, and funding.

## Background & Description

The Act's findings (Sec. 2) state that various species of fish, wildlife, and plants have become extinct because of economic growth, and that many others are in danger of or threatened with extinction. The stated purposes are to conserve the ecosystems on which listed species depend, to provide a program for their conservation, and to meet the nation's treaty obligations. Treaties cited include:

- Migratory bird treaties with Canada, Mexico, and Japan
- The Convention on Nature Protection and Wildlife Preservation in the Western Hemisphere
- The International Convention for the Northwest Atlantic Fisheries
- The International Convention for the High Seas Fisheries of the North Pacific Ocean
- The [[Convention On International Trade In Endangered Species]] (CITES)

Key definitions (Sec. 3):

- **Endangered species:** any species in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of its range.
- **Threatened species:** any species likely to become endangered within the foreseeable future.
- **Fish or wildlife:** any member of the animal kingdom, including invertebrates, mollusks, crustaceans, fish, and birds, as well as their eggs and parts.
- **Critical habitat:** specified geographic areas essential to a listed species, which may need special management.
- **Take:** to harass, harm, pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, capture, or collect, or to attempt any of these.
- **Conserve:** to use all methods and procedures needed to bring a species to the point where protection is no longer necessary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Act is built around ecosystem-level conservation. Its listing criteria (Sec. 4(a)) consider threats including habitat destruction or modification, overutilization, disease or predation, and inadequacy of regulatory mechanisms. The Secretary must also consider efforts by other nations to protect species, including on the high seas. Species are managed under two federal authorities: the Secretary of the Interior for most fish and wildlife, and the [[Secretary Of Commerce]] for certain marine species. Interior determinations involving fish or wildlife require notice in the Federal Register and a period for state comment before species are added or removed.

Aquatic and marine relevance includes:

- Listing and critical-habitat provisions applying to fish and marine invertebrates
- Prohibitions on importing, exporting, and taking species on the high seas (Sec. 9)
- Interagency consultation covering federal actions that could affect aquatic habitat (Sec. 7)
- Foreign assistance and cooperation for fisheries conservation, including the [[North Pacific High Seas]] and [[Northwest Atlantic Fisheries Convention]] (Sec. 8)

## Key Findings

The text contains statutory provisions rather than study results. Its main provisions are:

1. **Listing and critical habitat (Sec. 4).** Listing decisions must rest on the best scientific and commercial data available. Critical habitat is designated with the listing where prudent. Economic and other relevant impacts are considered, and an area may be excluded if its benefits of exclusion outweigh the benefits of designation. Petitions receive a finding within 90 days. Species status is reviewed at least every five years. Recovery plans are required, with [[Recovery Plan]] provisions for implementation. Listed species are reviewed through public notice, state comment, and formal rulemaking.

2. **State cooperation (Sec. 6).** The Secretary may enter cooperative agreements with states that maintain adequate and active conservation programs. Federal cost shares are up to 75 percent, rising to 90 percent where two or more states share a common interest in an endangered or threatened species.

3. **Federal agency duties (Sec. 7).** Each federal agency must ensure that its actions are not likely to jeopardize listed species or destroy or adversely modify critical habitat. This is achieved through [[Section 7 Consultation]] and a [[Biological Opinion]]. Consultation is normally concluded within 90 days.

4. **Endangered Species Committee (Sec. 7(e)–(p)).** A seven-member committee, the [[Endangered Species Committee]], may grant exemptions from the jeopardy requirement. The Secretary of Defense may obtain an exemption for reasons of national security. The Committee's decisions are reviewable in the U.S. Court of Appeals.

5. **Prohibited acts (Sec. 9).** Unlawful acts include importing, exporting, taking, possessing, selling, or transporting listed species, and violating related regulations. Plant provisions are narrower. Exemptions exist for species held in captivity before the listing.

6. **Exceptions (Sec. 10).** The [[Incidental Take Permit]] process allows incidental take for scientific purposes or to enhance propagation or survival, subject to a conservation plan. Hardship exemptions are limited to one year. Subsistence and Alaska Native provisions allow certain takings. Scrimshaw and handicraft exemptions are included.

7. **Penalties (Sec. 11).** Civil penalties are up to $10,000 for knowing violations of major provisions, $5,000 for other regulation violations, and $500 for other violations. Criminal penalties for knowing violations reach fines of up to $20,000, imprisonment of up to one year, or both. Violators may also face forfeiture of equipment and vessels and loss of licenses.

8. **International cooperation (Sec. 8 and 8A).** The Secretary of the Interior serves as the Management Authority and Scientific Authority for CITES, carried out through the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]]. An International Convention Advisory Commission provides scientific recommendations.

9. **Funding and authorization (Sec. 15–16).** Appropriations are authorized for the Departments of the Interior, Commerce, and Agriculture, and for the Endangered Species Committee, for fiscal years 1983 through 1985. Exact dollar amounts are not reliably legible in the scan.

10. **Related amendments (Sec. 13–17).** The Act amends the Migratory Bird Conservation Act, the [[Marine Mammal Protection Act]], and pesticide control law to reference the revised Endangered Species Act. It repeals the 1969 Endangered Species Conservation Act. The Marine Mammal Protection Act takes precedence where it is more restrictive.
