# Coronado National Forest Plan Five Year Review Report Executive Summary

#forest-management #wildlife-habitat #ecosystem-health

## Executive Summary

This executive summary presents the results of a five-year review (October 1991 through September 1992) of the [[Coronado National Forest]] Land and Resource Management Plan, approved August 1986. The review evaluates progress toward desired future conditions, resolution of original issues and concerns, monitoring effectiveness, and emerging management challenges. The Forest encompasses isolated mountain ranges ("sky islands") across southeastern Arizona and New Mexico, supporting exceptional biological diversity including nearly 200 threatened, endangered, and sensitive (TE&S) species. The review concludes that substantial progress has been made toward Forest Plan goals despite funding constraints (47% in 1987 increasing to 79% by 1991), though significant work remains. Key challenges include managing increasing recreation demand, protecting riparian ecosystems, addressing conflicts over livestock grazing, implementing effective monitoring programs, and adapting to growing emphasis on ecosystem-based management rather than single-species approaches.

## Background & Description

The [[Coronado National Forest]] Plan was developed under the National Forest Management Act (NFMA) of 1976, with implementation beginning in fiscal year 1986. The Plan covers approximately 1.5 million acres of suitable grazing land (87% of total), 13,729 acres initially suitable for timber harvest (reduced to 5,000 acres by 1989), and includes designated wilderness areas totaling 211,540 acres as of the 1984 Arizona Wilderness Act. The Forest features exceptional biodiversity due to its unique geographical position, with ecosystems ranging from desert grasslands to coniferous forests. Major watersheds number 21, with 8 rated unsatisfactory due to 535,000 acres in poor condition. The Forest supports over 3,000 miles of roads and 1,000 miles of trails, serving both local populations and tourists seeking outdoor recreation opportunities year-round.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Coronado's "sky islands" create isolated mountain ecosystems supporting unique assemblages of plants and animals. Riparian areas were identified as special management areas (Management Area 7) requiring protection and restoration. Watershed condition assessments indicated 84% of lands (1,266,512 acres) in satisfactory range condition, with 16% (244,049 acres) unsatisfactory. The Forest shares a border with Mexico, creating unique management challenges including international wildlife corridors and drug enforcement issues. Key ecological concerns include:

- **Riparian Ecosystem Health**: Major channels outside wilderness mapped and managed under specific prescriptions (7A & 7B). Long-term monitoring initiated in 1993 to track recovery trends.
- **Fire Ecology**: Historical fire suppression altered vegetation composition, increasing fire hazard and reducing forage production. Prescribed fire use constrained by air quality concerns and urban interface proximity.
- **Wildlife Habitat**: Nearly 200 TE&S species present, highest number for any National Forest. Seven indicator species groups established to monitor biological community health, though this approach under revision toward ecosystem-based monitoring.
- **Cave Resources**: Significant cave resources inventoried and managed under Amendment No. 6 (January 1992), with full-time cave specialist hired.

## Key Findings

### Progress Toward Desired Future Condition

The Forest made substantial progress implementing Plan direction despite funding limitations. Congressional appropriations increased from 47% of full implementation needs (1987) to 79% (1991), supplemented by volunteers, partnerships, and alternative funding. The land's ability to respond to future needs has not been impaired beyond 1986 conditions and has improved in some areas. However, conflicts between users wanting preservation versus increased access continue to intensify, with public challenges to management decisions growing across all program areas.

### Resolution of Original Issues

**Recreation and Visual Quality**: Four recreation concept plans completed or in progress, five more scheduled. Off-Highway Vehicle (OHV) management strategy developed for FY 1993. Arizona Initiative and America's Great Outdoors Initiative provided additional funding for facility rehabilitation. Cave resources inventoried with full-time specialist hired. Visual resource integrity maintained through integrated resource management process.

**Wilderness**: Three Wilderness Study Areas (Bunk Robinson, Whitmire Canyon, Mt. Graham) evaluated with recommendations forwarded to Chief's Office. Wilderness Implementation Schedules being developed for all wilderness areas, to be completed by 1993.

**Wildlife and Fish**: Biological evaluations now standard practice. Arizona Wildlife and Fisheries Comprehensive Plan developed (1990) with species-specific goals. Habitat improvement targets exceeded in most areas except prescribed burning (constrained by air quality and cost concerns). Wildlife program funding increased but distribution equity problems persist. Shift toward conservation strategies for TE&S species rather than project-by-project evaluations recommended.

**Range Management**: Allotment management plan updates prioritized for unsatisfactory conditions and declining allotments. Number of allotments without plans reduced to 35. Actual use below estimated capacity since 1987, with improving management practices allowing higher sustainable capacity than anticipated. Expected to have current plan for each allotment by 1996.

**Timber and Forest Products**: Suitable timber land base reduced from 13,729 to 5,000 acres (Amendment No. 4, 1989) due to [[Mt. Graham Red Squirrel]] habitat needs. Last commercial timber sale completed 1989. Re-evaluation of timber program suitability planned due to below-cost sale concerns and changing wildlife habitat objectives (Mexican spotted owls, goshawks).

**Watershed and Soil**: Approximately 18,000 acres improved toward 33,000-acre first-decade target. Ecosystems in satisfactory condition confirmed satisfactory; some unsatisfactory areas (especially desert grasslands, broadleaf woodlands) in poorer condition than originally reported. [[Redrock Canyon]] watershed subjected to ecosystem analysis approach, with activity needs identified and implementation underway.

### Monitoring Results and Concerns

**Implementation Monitoring**: Conducted routinely by Forest and District personnel, documented in project files. Review indicates this occurring forest-wide, though written documentation sometimes lacking.

**Effectiveness Monitoring**: Conducted by specialists on limited basis. Not consistently applied or documented. Need identified for additional monitoring to establish long-term trends, particularly for riparian recovery rates.

**Validation Monitoring**: Limited to watershed and rangeland needs. Permanent plots established but not checked regularly.

**Critical Monitoring Gaps**:
1. **Wildlife and Plant Monitoring**: Indicator species concept under revision. Shift toward monitoring "healthy ecosystems" and species assemblages rather than individual species. Neotropical Migratory Bird Program example of new approach.
2. **Ecosystem Monitoring**: Integrated ecological approach to riparian monitoring proposed as pathway to ecosystem monitoring process. Riparian ecosystems positioned as barometers of upstream/upslope conditions.

### Future Issues and Emerging Concerns

**Ecosystem Health**: Agency moving from "New Perspectives" trial period to policy implementation. Challenge to identify appropriate ecosystem scale for planning and project implementation.

**Roadless Areas**: Approximately 325,000 acres in 16 areas remain roadless outside designated Wilderness/WSAs. Re-evaluation planned during forest plan revision.

**Wild and Scenic Rivers**: Five stream segments identified in 1991 Arizona Rivers Coalition proposal. Extensive eligibility determination conducted Fall 1992. Additional studies pending as part of Plan revision.

**Special Area Designations**: "Sky Islands" National Recreation Area explored 1991-1992. Dry Canyon (Whetstone Mountains) under study for Geologic Special Interest Area status. Atascosa Mountains proposal for Botanical Special Interest Area to protect wild chile pepper gene pool.

**Mining Area Reclamation**: Numerous old mining operations with open shafts and waste dumps pose hazardous material concerns. Identification and rehabilitation efforts initiated.

**Law Enforcement**: Dramatic increase in activity due to drug enforcement role (Mexico border proximity) and need to protect resources/users from growing population impacts.

**Livestock Grazing**: Under increasing public scrutiny. Approximately 80% of Forest determined suitable for some grazing level. New information since 1986 should supplement suitability determinations to support allotment-level decisions.

**Mineral Resource Management**: Updated mineral information from Bureau of Mines and USGS expected to increase mining interest. Need to identify lands where mineral extraction should receive high priority alongside sensitive areas requiring withdrawal.

**TE&S Species Management**: Nearly 200 species, highest number for any Forest. Habitat locations and needs must be identified to make responsible decisions regarding all resource uses.

### Recommendations

The interdisciplinary team made 14 key recommendations:

1. Clarify desired future condition (DFC) for each Management Area before forest plan revision
2. Identify DFC for each major project proposal early in IRM process
3. Develop forest-wide integrated monitoring approach measuring ecosystem condition effectiveness
4. Re-evaluate suitability of lands for commercial timber and fuelwood programs
5. Identify and map areas showing little improvement; conduct integrated watershed/ecosystem analysis
6. Re-evaluate prescribed fire role given increased environmental/social constraints
7. Determine carrying capacities for specific recreation areas; increase use monitoring
8. Evaluate need to modify OHV management direction
9. Develop conservation strategies for TE&S species as alternative to listing
10. Evaluate wildlife habitat improvement work priorities given fire use constraints
11. Review and revise Recreation Opportunity Spectrum (ROS) mapping
12. Modify Chapter 6 monitoring direction for more realistic, integrated approach
13. Use Resource Access/Travel Management process to refine transportation needs area-by-area
14. Use new mineral studies to re-evaluate lands for mineral development versus withdrawal

### Proposed Forest Plan Changes

Ten amendments proposed including: removing activity schedules; developing DFC statements for each management area; updating suitable timber harvest acres and ASQ; supplementing direction from wilderness implementation schedules; amending monitoring direction; modifying fire suppression zone map; updating management area map; establishing objective to acquire priority private lands by 2000; identifying areas needing mineral withdrawals; and reviewing recreational special use suitability where conflicts occur.
