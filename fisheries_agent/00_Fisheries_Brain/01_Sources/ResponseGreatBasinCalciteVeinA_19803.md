# Devils Hole Calcite Vein and the Pleistocene Time Scale

#paleoclimatology #stable-isotopes #groundwater

## Executive Summary

This technical comment exchange, published in [[Science]] in October 1989, debates whether the [[Devils Hole Calcite Vein]] (DH 2) oxygen-isotope record supports or contradicts the conventional [[Pleistocene Time Scale]]. [[Winograd Et Al.]] (1988) proposed that the DH 2 record, dated to 50–280 ka, implies that isotopic peaks precede glacial terminations, challenging the [[Deep-Sea Core]] chronology. [[Johnson And Wright]] argue that, after a 16,000-year [[Ground Water Residence Time]] correction, the DH 2 peaks coincide with marine glacial minima, and they attribute the peaks to glacial-period moisture-source and fractionation effects. [[Winograd And Coplen]] reply that continental proxy evidence and internal inconsistencies undermine that reinterpretation, and that the marine chronology appears incorrect for [[Glacial Termination Ii]].

The fish-free nature of this material is noted: the text contains no information on fish, invertebrates, or aquatic biota. Its relevance to aquatic science lies in groundwater and paleohydrological isotope archives.

## Background & Description

**Study system:** The DH 2 calcite vein was deposited by [[Great Basin]] ground water at a depth below the water table (the numeric value is illegible in the scanned source). The vein was sampled for oxygen-18 (δ¹⁸O) to build a well-dated profile covering roughly 50 to 280 ka.

**Original claim (Winograd et al.):** Because global precipitation δ¹⁸O correlates with surface air temperature, the DH 2 δ¹⁸O peaks were interpreted as temperature maxima during interglacial and interstadial periods. The DH 2 interglacial peaks have amplitudes up to 2 per mil and precede glacial terminations II and III in the deep-sea record by about 15,000 and 20,000 years, respectively. Critical data for the last glacial extreme (~18 ka) were missing.

**Johnson and Wright's replot:** Shifting the DH 2 data back 16,000 years produces broad positive peaks that align with major minima in the deep-sea δ¹⁸O record, corresponding to the [[Illinoian Glaciation]] (~150 ka) and an earlier glaciation (~270 ka).

**Reply's counterpoint:** Continental records and the internal logic of the proposed mechanisms are disputed (see Key Findings).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The discussion centers on the factors controlling precipitation isotopes in the western United States during glacial and interglacial periods:

- **Source water composition:** Average ocean δ¹⁸O at glacial extremes was about 1.3 per mil more positive.
- **Storm track position:** Paleoclimate model results (CLIMAP/COHMAP) indicate that under the maximum continental ice sheet, the [[Jet Stream]] crossed the coast at 30–35°N, versus about 50°N today, bringing moisture from lower-latitude ocean water.
- **Evaporation and temperature:** Warmer low-latitude temperatures (3–7°C warmer) would have produced vapor 0.7–1.6 per mil more positive.
- **Shorter continental path:** Less fractionation over a shorter path across lower-altitude land.
- **Combined effect:** Johnson and Wright estimate glacial Great Basin precipitation may have been enriched by up to about 4 per mil.
- **Humidity effect:** Lower humidity during desiccated interglacials such as the present could lower condensation temperatures and shift precipitation δ¹⁸O upward.
- **Holocene moisture-source shift:** Deuterium data from wood in Holocene lake sediments in the [[San Juan Mountains]] show a strong negative trend from 9.5 ka to the present, attributed to a change in moisture source rather than temperature.

## Key Findings

**Johnson and Wright (comment):**
- The DH 2 peaks likely reflect glacial-period conditions after a 16,000-year residence-time shift; the revised mirror-image profile supports deep-sea chronology.
- Several mechanisms (ocean δ¹⁸O, lower-latitude moisture source, evaporation, reduced fractionation) could together raise glacial δ¹⁸O.
- They favor low-latitude Pacific moisture with a short travel distance, driven by a displaced jet stream.

**Winograd and Coplen (reply):**
- Continental evidence contradicts the glacial-peak interpretation. Ground-water, [[Speleothem]] fluid-inclusion, and pedogenic CaCO₃ studies show that δ¹⁸O (and δD) peaked in the Holocene and was lightest in the late [[Wisconsin Glaciation]].
- The only contrary continental study (tree cellulose δD) was later judged unreliable for this purpose, with subsequent studies finding the heaviest values in the Holocene.
- A conceptual inconsistency is identified: the proposed mechanisms should produce isotopic troughs during interglacials, not peaks during glacials. Also, DH 2 values are lightest around [[Marine Isotope Stage 4]], tens of thousands of years after the marine interglacial peak near 125 ka, so the two records are not mirror images.
- Summer monsoonal precipitation is argued to contribute little to recharge in major Nevada mountains, so it cannot explain the discrepancy.
- Replication of the published age for termination II is reported, and the DH 2 δ¹⁸O and δ¹³C series have been extended to about 550 ka.

**Consensus outstanding:** The authors disagree on whether the DH 2 peaks coincide with interglacial or glacial periods, and on whether the marine [[Oxygen Isotope Ratios]] chronology is accurate for termination II. Both sides cite the [[Vostok Ice Core]] record for comparison of terminations I and II.
