# Results of Fish Monitoring of Selected Waters of the Gila River Basin, 1999

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report presents the results of fish monitoring in selected streams and canals of the [[Gila River]] Basin, Arizona, during 1999 (with January 2000 sampling of the Salt River Project Arizona Canal). The work was required under a reasonable and prudent alternative in the [[Biological Opinion]] on delivery of [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water, which was issued because the project threatened four listed fishes through the possible spread of non-native species. Across the monitored waters, 28 taxa were captured, but only 4 were native. Assemblages were dominated by non-native species, and no species were recorded for the first time in 1999. The report is a summary only; a full analysis of 1995–1999 data was deferred.

## Background & Description

The CAP is a system of aqueducts and pipelines that carries [[Colorado River]] water from [[Lake Havasu]] to central and southern Arizona. Construction was largely finished by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] in 1993. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) 1994 Biological Opinion concluded that CAP water transfers would jeopardize four threatened or endangered fishes: [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loachminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The FWS also found adverse modification of critical habitat for the last three. The main concern was that non-native fishes and other aquatic organisms from the lower Colorado River could enter Gila Basin drainages through the CAP.

The reasonable and prudent alternative required Reclamation, working with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AZGFD) and FWS, to develop and implement a baseline study and monitoring of non-native fish presence and distribution. Monitoring began in October 1994. The six target reaches were:

1. CAP Canal
2. Salt River Project (SRP) canals
3. Florence-Casa Grande (FCG) Canal
4. Salt River between Stewart Mountain Dam and Granite Reef Dam
5. Gila River between Coolidge and Ashurst-Hayden dams
6. San Pedro River downstream from the U.S.–Mexico border

Sampling followed the 1996 long-term monitoring plan (Clarkson 1996, revision No. 2). Quantitative sampling was done at fixed stations, mainly by [[Electrofishing]] in streams. Qualitative sampling was used to find rare species. Canal sampling was opportunistic, often during low flow or dry-up periods. The CAP canal was sampled at pumping plant forebays, and SRP and FCG canals were sampled during water outages using seines, dip nets and entanglement gear.

Field work was shared among agencies. Reclamation's Phoenix Area Office was responsible for CAP sampling and oversaw the whole program. AZGFD sampled the Gila, Salt and San Pedro rivers, and [[Arizona State University]] sampled the SRP and FCG canals. AZGFD also led a multi-agency sampling effort in the SRP canals above the [[Electrical Fish Barrier]] system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Waters monitored**

- **[[San Pedro River]]:** Sampled by electrofishing, with 200 m stations. This was the only system where complete sampling of all habitat was practical.
- **[[Gila River]]:** Upper reaches were monitored, and some sites were in dry-up condition during sampling. The Gila River site at the San Pedro confluence (site 2-3-1) was dominated by San Pedro River fishes, which likely explains its biological affinity with the lower San Pedro.
- **[[Salt River]]:** Monitored with electrofishing, supplemented by a backpack shocker in shallow habitats.
- **[[Central Arizona Project]] Canal:** Sampled at pumping plant forebays, with fixed stations.
- **[[Salt River Project Canals]]:** The South Canal and Arizona Canal were each split into reaches above and below the Electrical Fish Barrier. Canal dry-up periods were used for sampling. The South Canal dry-up began October 28, 1999. The Arizona Canal dry-up began January 2, 2000.
- **[[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]]:** Sampled above and below the electrical barrier at China Wash, with headgates at Ashurst-Hayden Dam closed on October 24, 1999.

**Water management and barriers**

Canal fish communities reflect water management. Fish richness in the CAP Canal has declined over time as more water is moved for storage in Lake Pleasant or downstream delivery. Electrical barriers on the SRP and FCG canals separate reaches, and fish moving downstream past the barrier during dry-up periods are hard to quantify.

**Non-native species**

Non-native species were present in every water body. [[Channel Catfish]] occurred in all streams and canals (though not at every reach or station). [[Red Shiner]] was found everywhere except the Salt River, and [[Green Sunfish]] was found everywhere except the FCG Canal. [[Common Carp]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] were among the species widespread across reaches. Sunfish hybrids were common in some canal stations and dominated the lowermost San Xavier pumping plant station in the CAP Canal.

## Key Findings

**Overall results**

- A total of **28 taxa** were captured in 1999 (with Tilapia spp. counted as one taxon), including samples from January 2000.
- Only **4 native species** were recorded: roundtail chub, longfin dace, desert sucker and Sonora sucker.
- Percentage native fish ranged from **0%** (CAP and FCG canals) to **50%** (upper San Pedro River).
- No new species were captured that had not been recorded before.
- Assemblage structure varied strongly among waters.

**Species richness by water body**

| Water body (sampling area) | Species count |
|---|---|
| SRP Arizona (North) Canal, above barrier | 17 |
| Gila and Salt rivers | 16 |
| SRP South Canal, below barrier | 14 |
| CAP Canal | 13 |
| SRP South Canal, above barrier | 12 |
| SRP Arizona Canal, below barrier | 11 |
| San Pedro River | 9 |
| FCG Canal, above barrier | 3 |
| FCG Canal, below barrier | 2 |

Total species richness in the canals with electrical barriers was 18 in the Arizona Canal, 18 in the South Canal, and 3 in the FCG Canal.

**Native species by water body**

- Gila River: longfin dace, desert sucker and Sonora sucker (3 native species)
- Salt River: roundtail chub, desert sucker and Sonora sucker (3 native species, reported as 3 of 16 species)
- San Pedro River: longfin dace, desert sucker and Sonora sucker (3 native species)
- SRP canals: roundtail chub, desert sucker and Sonora sucker (3 native species)
- CAP and FCG canals: no native species

**Central Arizona Project Canal**

- The uppermost station (Bouse Hills Pumping Plant) had only two centrarchids and striped bass.
- Richness totals were 9 species in the upper reach, 5 in the middle reach and 11 in the lower reach. A maximum of 7 species was found at any single station, and 13 species overall.
- Common carp, grass carp, largemouth bass and striped bass were the only species found in all three reaches.
- Centrarchids (especially sunfishes) were numerically dominant overall. Red shiner dominated at Little Harquahala Pumping Plant, and sunfish hybrids dominated at San Xavier Pumping Plant.
- Every species captured from the CAP Canal in 1999 was non-native.

**Gila River (Coolidge to Ashurst-Hayden dams)**

- 16 species were collected. Upper reaches had the most species, and Keamy site (3-2) had the fewest.
- Red shiner, green sunfish and mosquitofish dominated the catch. Mosquitofish was the only species found at every station.
- Native species were sporadic and mostly rare. Longfin dace was most common near perennial tributaries.
- Sonora sucker dominated the native catch at Dripping Spring Wash. The reported native relative abundance figures are partly illegible in the source text and should be checked against the original.

**San Pedro River**

- 9 species were captured. A maximum of 8 species was taken at a single station.
- Longfin dace was the most abundant species overall, and it accounted for nearly all fish at Swinging Wash, which had about an order of magnitude more fish than other sites.
- Mosquitofish occurred at all stations in the upper and lower reaches but was absent from the middle reach.
- Red shiner was confined to the lower reach.

**Florence-Casa Grande Canal**

- Only 3 species were captured in total: red shiner, young-of-year channel catfish and mosquitofish.
- Above the barrier, red shiner, mosquitofish and channel catfish were the species present, in decreasing order of abundance.
- Below the barrier, only red shiner (7 individuals) and mosquitofish (11 individuals) were captured.
- No native species were found.

**Salt River (Stewart Mountain to Granite Reef dams)**

- 16 species were captured in 1999, with 11 at the upper station and the rest at the lower stations. Native species comprised 3 of 16 species.
- Roundtail chub, desert sucker, Sonora sucker and rainbow trout were among the species captured.
- A large collection of mosquitofish came from Blue Point (site 1-2).
- Green sunfish was the only remaining species that made up more than a rare share of the catch.

**SRP South Canal**

- 18 species were captured in total: 12 above the electrical barrier and 14 below.
- The three native species, channel catfish and Tilapia spp. dominated the assemblage above and below the barrier, followed by largemouth bass.
- Fish numbers above the barrier were about three times higher than below, but the difference is not well explained.

**SRP Arizona Canal**

- This canal had the highest species richness of any stream or canal: 17 above the barrier and 11 below.
- A total of 18 species was recorded across both reaches.
- A grass carp was captured above the barrier.
- An electrical failure of the barrier occurred on July 23, 1999. Largemouth bass and threadfin shad were captured in the canal below the barrier after the failure.

**Management implications**

- The monitoring program is intended to establish baseline data and detect changes in non-native fish composition and distribution, as required by the 1994 Biological Opinion.
- The statistical design, assumptions and data analysis were recently reviewed (Allison 2000; Abarca and Allison 2000). Those recommendations will be applied to a revised monitoring plan and to a full analysis of 1995–1999 data.
- This report presents only very general conclusions about assemblage structure.
