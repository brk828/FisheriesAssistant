# Un-Dedicating Dams: Bruce Babbitt on Dam Removal and River Restoration

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This is a first-person essay by [[Bruce Babbitt]], former U.S. Secretary of the Interior, arguing that the United States has built too many dams and should selectively remove them to restore migratory fish runs and river ecosystems. It is an opinion and policy piece, not a research study. It uses a series of case studies, including [[Quaker Neck Dam]], [[Edwards Dam]], the [[Elwha River Dams]] and the [[Lower Snake River Dams]], to show that dam removal can restore fish passage, and it argues that the social, economic and cultural value of wild fish runs must be weighed against the modest benefits of many aging structures. The essay concludes that the nation has "a long way to go toward a better balance" and that decisions will mostly be made dam by dam, with local and state stakeholders leading.

## Background & Description

The essay opens with a contrast between the New Deal era, when [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] and [[Harold Ickes]] dedicated dams, and the late twentieth century, when Babbitt tours the country to "un-dedicate" them. He estimates that the nation has roughly 75,000 dams, "the equivalent of one every day since Jefferson wrote the Declaration of Independence."

Babbitt traces the growth of dam building from local projects to a national enterprise. He cites early hydroelectric development associated with [[Thomas Edison]] and [[Charles Steinmetz]], then large federal projects such as [[Bonneville Dam]], [[Grand Coulee Dam]], the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] and [[Shasta Dam]]. He argues that benefits were overstated and costs, including fish losses and financial costs, were understated. The essay also describes the [[Hetch Hetchy Dam]] controversy in [[Yosemite National Park]], the Dinosaur National Monument fight, and the building of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]].

Key narrative examples include:

- **Quaker Neck Dam** (Neuse River, North Carolina): about six feet high, non-generating. Removed in the year before the essay, after which shad were reported spawning about 70 miles upstream, up to Raleigh.
- **Edwards Dam** (Kennebec River, Augusta, Maine): built in 1837. By 1997 it produced less than one half of one percent of the city's electricity. Removal was agreed upon after 157 years.
- **Elwha and Glines Canyon Dams** (Olympic Peninsula, Washington): erected early in the twentieth century to power Port Angeles. The Park Service completed an environmental impact study in 1996, and a removal decision was signed in 1997.
- **Lower Snake River dams** (Washington, Idaho and Oregon): Ice Harbor, Lower Monument, Little Goose and Lower Granite, completed by 1975.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Babbitt describes a range of documented or claimed ecological effects of dams:

- **Fish passage:** loss of migratory runs, including American shad, striped bass, Atlantic salmon and "six other species of migratory fish" on the Kennebec. On the Elwha, one chinook salmon run was eliminated and survivors are held in hatcheries. Fish are also killed passing through turbines and are preyed upon in slack water.
- **Hydrology and temperature:** downstream temperature changes, sediment accumulation behind structures, downstream erosion, and altered flow regimes. Below Glen Canyon Dam the water became cold and clear, and daily fluctuations followed power demand in Phoenix and Los Angeles.
- **Riparian and delta ecosystems:** loss of cottonwood and willow sandbars and die-off of riparian trees; the Colorado River delta was reduced to salt flats; the Platte River shrank, threatening sandhill crane flocks.
- **Watershed-scale losses:** the 75,000 dams block about 600,000 miles of formerly free-flowing river, roughly 17 percent of the nation's river mileage. The Grand Coulee Dam alone cuts off more than 1,000 miles of salmon streams.
- **Native fish:** Babbitt describes Colorado River native fish as driven into a few isolated tributaries and threatened with extinction.

Babbitt also contrasts sites where fish persist. The John Day River in eastern Oregon still supports viable salmon runs, while the Grande Ronde, a tributary of the Snake entering above the four lower dams, is "virtually devoid of fish."

## Key Findings

- **The dam inventory is large and poorly documented.** Babbitt states that roughly 75,000 dams exist and that there is "no comprehensive inventory" of them, nor adequate knowledge of benefits and environmental costs for each.
- **Dam removal is feasible and can restore fish.** The Quaker Neck case is presented as evidence: shad returned upstream within about a year of removal.
- **Many dams provide marginal benefits.** Edwards Dam produced under 0.5 percent of Augusta's power; the Elwha dams served a community that was later connected to a regional grid with a power surplus.
- **Salmon declines are linked to dam expansion.** Sockeye returns to Redfish Lake fell to one fish in 1988 and six in 1989, with few seen afterwards. Babbitt attributes this partly to the Snake River dams, whose justification shifted to an Idaho inland seaport that was already served by rail.
- **Mitigation has largely failed.** Fish ladders, hatcheries, sea lion removal, bird control and squawfish bounties have been tried for over two decades "with little sign of success," according to Babbitt.
- **Tribal treaty rights are at stake.** The 1855 treaties with Columbia River tribes reserved fishing sites and equal salmon harvest rights, so tribes are asking the United States to protect and restore runs under its trust responsibilities.
- **Dam removal is a national debate, not just a regional one.** Babbitt frames the Snake River controversy as a test of whether stewardship extends to whole watersheds.
- **Decisions will be made case by case.** Babbitt expects that dam removal will proceed "on a dam by dam basis, with states and community stakeholders making most decisions," and notes that about a dozen structures had been removed by the time of writing.
