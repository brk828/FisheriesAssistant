# Fish Vertebrae and Scales in a Sediment Core from Esthwaite Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This short paper reports the first fish remains recovered from a freshwater sediment core taken by the [[Freshwater Biological Association]]. Four consecutive caudal vertebrae and about 40 scales were found at 450 cm below the mud surface in a core from Esthwaite Water, English Lake District. Pollen evidence places the surrounding sediment in the late-glacial period, and the authors estimate the remains at roughly 10,000 to 12,000 years old. Scale and vertebral morphology point to the Salmonidae, most likely [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) or [[Arctic Char]] (*Salvelinus alpinus*), but the evidence could not separate the two with certainty.

## Background & Description

- **Discovery:** [[Mackereth]] (Freshwater Biological Association) and Dr W. Pennington found the remains while examining a core taken on 24 March 1959.
- **Core setting:** The core came from the middle of Esthwaite Water, where the water is 14 m deep.
- **Stratigraphy:** The fish layer lay in grey clay-gyttja directly above pinkish-grey laminated clay, interpreted as the basal deposit from the last main episode of the [[Quaternary Glaciation]].
- **Disturbance:** The core was affected by slumping, so no continuous sample series was taken from it.
- **Reference framework:** The authors used the British pollen zonation scheme (Zones I–III late-glacial; IV–VIII post-glacial) and a normal deep-water core taken 18.5 m away for comparison.
- **Fish remains:**
  - Four vertebrae, each 5.0 mm long, almost black, well preserved, and fitting together as consecutive caudal vertebrae.
  - About 40 scales, 1.2–1.7 mm long, light brown, fragile, and mostly incomplete.
  - Plate I illustrates the vertebrae (anterior and posterior views) and scales, including one scale showing a possible annulus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stratigraphic disruption:** Above the fish layer, the usual succession was missing. Dark brown organic mud lay directly over the fish-bearing clay, and a disturbed clay (390–426 cm) with organic-mud chunks sat above it. The missing Zone II and Zone III deposits point to an unconformity.
- **Pollen evidence:** The fish-bearing clay contained a late-glacial pollen assemblage (*Betula, Pinus, Salix, Juniperus, Filipendula ulmaria, Chenopodium, Thalictrum, Artemisia, Plantago*, Compositae). Counts were too low for a significant analysis. Overlying organic mud at two levels, and at 390 cm, matched Zone VI pollen.
- **Interpretation:** The fish-bearing deposit probably formed in situ. Later slumping into a lake-floor hollow in Zone VI likely removed the overlying succession and produced the mixed clay.
- **Regional parallel:** Similar slumping was reported from [[Ennerdale Water]] and [[Wastwater]].
- **Age:** Using radiocarbon-based boundaries (Zone I/II at 10,000 BC; Zone III at 8,750 and 8,300 BC), the authors give 10,000–12,000 years as the most probable age. The fish probably lived before birch woodland spread in Zone II.
- **Lake conditions:** The authors state there is little evidence of conditions in the lake at that time.

## Key Findings

- **Identity:** Scale structure points to Salmonidae. The authors could not decide between *Salmo trutta* and *Salvelinus alpinus*.
- **Size estimate:** Plotting vertebral diameter (6.0 mm) against fish length for char and trout gave a fish of about 40–45 cm. This corresponds to roughly 2¼ lb for a trout or 1 lb 14 oz for a char.
- **Vertebral position:** The four vertebrae are probably among the most anterior caudal vertebrae, where the haemal arch carries a median spine.
- **Expert opinion:** [[Trewavas]] (British Museum, Natural History) found the vertebrae matched trout skeletons well and were structurally similar to small char of *Salvelinus colii*, *S. grayi*, and *S. perisii*. She also noted that a notched zygapophysis in one small char was probably individual variation, not a generic character.
- **Scale evidence:** Core scales lay closer in size to char than to trout, but the difference was not decisive. Most scales were torn or replacement scales. One scale showed a char-type annulus, but the evidence was insufficient to rule out trout.
- **Local significance:** Esthwaite Water holds trout but no char and has no historic record of char. A char identification would therefore be notable.
- **Scientific significance:** These are the first fish bones recorded from a freshwater mud core. The only earlier comparable record is a Cyprinodont and Cyprinid scale find from Linsley Pond, Connecticut (Lagler & Vallentyne, 1956), dated to about 7,500 years.
- **Deposition:** The remains are held by the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] and the [[British Museum (Natural History)]].
