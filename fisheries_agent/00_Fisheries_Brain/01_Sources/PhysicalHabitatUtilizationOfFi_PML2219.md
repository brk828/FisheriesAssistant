# Physical Habitat Utilization of Fish in a Sonoran Desert Stream, Arizona, Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

Rinne (1992), published in *Ecology of Freshwater Fish*, quantifies the physical habitat (water depth, velocity and substrate) used by seven native fish species in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a perennial stream in the [[Sonoran Desert]] of southeastern Arizona. The study tests whether the species occupy significantly different habitats and finds that they do. Three minnows (Longfin Dace, Speckled Dace and Loach Minnow) share similar depths and velocities, the larger pool-dwelling species (Sonora Sucker and Roundtail Chub) use deeper, slower water, and Spikedace closely matches Desert Sucker. The paper is framed by conservation concern: two of the seven species are federally listed as threatened, and riparian habitat across the Southwest has been heavily reduced.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The seven species are all native to the [[Gila River Basin]], Arizona-New Mexico, which historically held 17 native fishes. Five are minnows (Cyprinidae) and two are suckers (Catostomidae), all in the order Cypriniformes:

- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), federally listed as threatened
- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), federally listed as threatened
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*)
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)

**Study site.** Aravaipa Creek is an Upper Sonoran Desert stream with a gradient below 1%, a width of 3 to 5 m and a gravel-pebble substrate. Modal discharge is 0.5 to 1.0 m³/s, with the highest flows in winter. Late-summer convectional storms can cause sudden flash floods. A canyon-bound central reach separates open upper and lower reaches. The perennial reach lies within [[Riparian Forest]] and woodland. The stream has an extensive database spanning 30 years.

**Methods.** Data were collected in the upper reach. Up to 10 randomly located habitat transects were sampled. Fish were captured by [[Electrofishing]] with a 1 m wide block seine, and the sampling sequence was designed to limit displacement. Each sample area (about 1 to 2 m²) was characterized with a meter-transect habitat sampler. Twenty-one depth, velocity and substrate measurements were taken per area and averaged. Velocity was measured 5 cm above the substrate with a flow meter. Substrate was classed as sand, gravel, pebble, cobble or boulder, and a [[Substrate Index]] was calculated. Sampling ran five times from April 1983 to July 1984. Sample sizes were winter n = 90, spring n = 94 and summer n = 142. Because no seasonal difference in habitat use was detected, the data were pooled. Only juvenile (>1 year) and adult fish were analyzed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper places the species within a broader story of decline. Desert fishes have evolved to tolerate flood and drought cycles, but dams, diversions, groundwater pumping and [[Non-Native Fishes]] have sharply altered Southwestern hydrology and fish communities. The author cites a loss of more than 95% of historic riparian-stream habitat in the Southwest. Quantitative habitat requirements for many desert fishes were previously lacking, and the author argues that such data are needed for recovery planning under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

Aravaipa Creek is described as relatively intact. It retains the greatest percentage of the historic Gila Basin native fish fauna, is protected in part by the [[Bureau of Land Management]] as a primitive area, is partly held by the Defenders of Wildlife, and has instream flow designations. The author nonetheless warns that the stream depends on flow maintenance and protection of the upstream [[Sulphur Springs Valley Aquifer]]. Five of the seven species occupy shallow riffles, so any flow reduction could cause intermittent surface flow, and only the chub and Sonora Sucker would be likely to survive such conditions.

The [[Red Shiner]] is a concern. It is present in the Gila River, has been reported in the lower reaches of Aravaipa Creek (1990), and shows habitat use similar to Spikedace, so it is a potential competitor.

## Key Findings

**Habitat differences among species.** The seven species occupied significantly different depths (ANOVA, P < 0.0001, F = 32.5, df = 273) and velocities (ANOVA, P < 0.0001, F = 10.6, df = 273). Groupings were assigned using Tukey's multiple range test. Results are visualized as notched box plots.

- **Shallow, fast riffles (Loach Minnow, Speckled Dace):** Both used shallow (14 to 15 cm) riffles with velocities near 40 cm/s over pebble-cobble substrate.
- **Shallow riffles of moderate velocity (Longfin Dace, Spikedace):** Both were most often captured in riffles shallower than 20 cm, with velocities near 35 cm/s over pebble-gravel substrate. Longfin Dace also grouped with Spikedace and Desert Sucker in deeper (16 to 22 cm) water.
- **Riffles with deeper water (Desert Sucker):** Desert Sucker used velocity and substrate similar to Loach Minnow and Speckled Dace but occupied slightly deeper riffles.
- **Pools (Sonora Sucker, Roundtail Chub):** These species used pools of greater depth (about 30 cm; deepest areas 32 to 34 cm), lower velocity (15 to 25 cm/s) and sand-gravel substrate.

**Overall pattern.** Loach Minnow, Speckled Dace and Desert Sucker use swift, turbulent riffles over gravel-pebble. Spikedace and Longfin Dace use smoother, laminar, moderate-velocity riffles over gravel-sand. Sonora Sucker and Roundtail Chub occupy slow or zero-velocity pools over sand. Spikedace overlapped with Desert Sucker in all three habitat variables and was broadly similar to most species except the pool-dwelling chub and sucker.

**Comparison with other studies.** Loach Minnow observations from other authors (nests and spawning on cobble and gravel; spawning on "rocks" in New Mexico) agree with the Aravaipa results. Speckled Dace spawning in New Mexico occurred on 10 to 55 mm substrate. The Spikedace riffle velocity of 25 cm/s reported elsewhere by Rinne (1991) is slower than the Aravaipa values.

**Food and niche partitioning.** The author suggests that species may partition resources. Desert Sucker scrapes algae and diatoms; Speckled Dace feeds mostly on invertebrates. Speckled Dace food overlapped 80% with Longfin Dace, Spikedace and Loach Minnow, but Longfin Dace and Speckled Dace are separated by habitat. Roundtail Chub, the top carnivore, overlapped less than 80% with Loach Minnow. Rinne notes that the abundance of Loach Minnow fluctuates widely, and overlap in habitat and food with Desert Sucker and Speckled Dace suggests density-independent factors such as space and food could limit its population. Underwater observations were cursory, but suggested Loach Minnow is a substrate-interstice dweller, while Speckled Dace occupies the substrate surface to open water.

**Conservation implications.** Maintaining flow is critical, because 5 of 7 species depend on shallow riffles. Natural population cycles of the threatened Spikedace and Loach Minnow, combined with flow loss, could cause local extirpation. Introduced non-native fishes from upstream livestock tanks or the Gila River are described as an equal or greater threat.
