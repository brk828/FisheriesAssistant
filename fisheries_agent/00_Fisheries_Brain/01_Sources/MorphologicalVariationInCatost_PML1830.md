# Morphological Variation in Catostomid Fishes of the Grand Canyon Region, Middle Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report to the [[National Park Service]] (Grand Canyon National Park), dated 20 December 1980 and authored by W. L. Minckley of Arizona State University, quantifies morphological variation in suckers of the genus *Catostomus* (sensu stricto) from the middle [[Colorado River Basin]]. The study focuses on the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] ([[Catostomus Latipinnis]]), whose nomenclature had been regarded as stable. Measurements from 12 population samples across the [[Virgin River]], [[Little Colorado River]], mainstream Colorado River in [[Grand Canyon]], and upper Colorado River basins show that the group is highly variable and does not match the uniform concept of *C. latipinnis* in the literature. Only the Virgin River mainstream population closely fits that concept. The author describes the Grand Canyon fish as a "*Catostomus latipinnis* complex" and calls all conclusions tentative. Citation is discouraged without the author's permission.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic context:** [[Smith 1966]] treated the mountain suckers as subgenus *Pantosteus* within *Catostomus*. Minckley considers *Catostomus* and *Pantosteus* distinct genera. Earlier work on western catostomids included biochemical and morphological studies by [[Smith and Koehn 1969]].
- **Biology:** Catostomids are long-lived, reach large sizes, change body shape with age, and show pronounced sexual dimorphism. Their variation is linked to geographic isolation in periodically disrupted drainages and possibly to local environmental conditions. "Swift-water" forms are elongated and big-finned, while tributary forms are thick-bodied.
- **Description of the type:** The original description (Baird and Girard 1854) gives a subfusiform body, a head about 5.5 times in standard length, small eyes, a small mouth with large fleshy lips, expansive fins, and medium-sized scales. The lateral line runs mid-side.
- **Methods:** Counts and measurements followed [[Hubbs and Lagler 1970]]. Specimens are housed in the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes. Data were analyzed with analysis of variance for unequal sample sizes. All morphological values are expressed as per mille of standard length. Allometry was assessed by least-squares regression of standard length against proportional measurements. Field observations of living specimens recorded color and fin shape.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat contrasts:** Canyon-bound mainstream and swift-water habitats select for elongated, big-finned forms. Quieter tributary waters favor thick-bodied forms. The Little Colorado River suckers inhabit clear, cold, relatively stable headwater streams generally above 1,500 m elevation.
- **Impoundment effects:** Closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Lake Powell]] in the early 1960s changed the Grand Canyon reach. Discharge became nearly tidal, temperatures dropped and were stabilized, water chemistry changed, sediment transport was curtailed, and the water became consistently clear (Kubly and Cole 1979). The impoundment of [[Lake Mead]] in 1935 isolated parts of the Virgin River drainage.
- **Faunal context:** The middle Colorado River region has a distinctive fauna, including the endemic minnow genus [[Lepidomeda]], thermal-spring fishes, *Gila robusta* ([[Gila Robusta]]) subspecies, and a *Pantosteus* complex including [[Pantosteus Discobolus]].
- **Introduced species:** The [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*) has been introduced into the upper Colorado River basin, where it hybridizes with *C. latipinnis*. *C. ardens* has been recorded from Lake Mohave but is too coarse-scaled to explain the variation observed.

## Key Findings

### 1. Reliability of Characters and Allometry
- Eight of the measured characters changed predictably with size. Larger fish had relatively smaller heads (by 7.0 to 9.9%), smaller orbits (41.0 to 48.1%), shorter depressed dorsal fins (3.5 to 13.6%), and shorter lips (7.0 to 34.3%) ([[Allometry]]).
- Predorsal length was proportionally greater in smaller fish (2.3 to 7.7%) in all but the upper Colorado tributary samples.
- Caudal peduncle length was relatively greater in large Virgin River mainstream fish (about 6.5%) and tended to shorten with size in Little Colorado River fish.
- Meristic counts showed little allometry. Lip papillae counts were assessed only in adults because small fish have low lips.

### 2. Sexual Dimorphism
- [[Sexual Dimorphism]] was evident in some characters, notably depressed dorsal fin length and dorsal fin base, where males had larger fins. The differences were not large enough to influence taxonomic decisions.

### 3. Body Shape, Head and Lips
- Head length was about 23 to 26% of standard length with no clear geographic pattern. Head differences were cranial rather than opercular.
- Snout length tended to be greater in Virgin River fish, which also had larger orbits and shorter postorbital lengths.
- Fish from within Grand Canyon and lowermost Glen Canyon had narrower heads and isthmi.
- Lip length was greatest in Virgin River and above-Grand-Canyon samples and shortest in Santa Clara River, Silver and Chevalon creek, and Grand and Glen canyon samples.
- Papillae counts on the upper and lower lips did not differ significantly among populations ([[Lip Papillae]]).

### 4. Fins and Scales
- Only [[Virgin River]] populations were distinctive in fin-base measurements, indicating more expansive fins.
- [[Dorsal Fin Rays]]: Virgin River fish were extreme, with a mean of 13.1 ± 0.9 (n = 57, range 12–15). Most other samples fell within the literature range for *C. latipinnis* (11 to 13), with means from about 10.9 to 13.0.
- [[Lateral Line Scales]]: Virgin River mainstream fish averaged 97.7 ± 11.6 (n = 59, range 90–111). Upper Colorado River samples had very high counts, such as 112.0 ± 10.0 (n = 28, range 100–120) in the San Juan River.

### 5. Qualitative Observations
- Little Colorado River and Santa Clara River suckers are sharply bicolored, dark above and silvery to white below.
- Virgin River mainstream suckers have expanded, falcate dorsal fins and enlarged caudal fins with pronounced procurrent rays.
- Grand Canyon and upper Colorado River specimens are leathery in appearance, with deeply embedded scales and thickened skin, possibly a response to abrasive canyon habitats.

### 6. Population Assessment
- Only the Virgin River mainstream population fits the conceptual *C. latipinnis* in recent literature.
- The Santa Clara River population resembles Little Colorado River fish in several head, lip and fin traits, but shares other features with Virgin River and Meadow Valley Wash fish.
- Little Colorado River tributary populations are highly variable among themselves, which masks any distinctiveness of the group. This casts doubt on [[Minckley 1973]]'s proposal that these fish are an undescribed species.
- Creek-mouth samples from Grand and Glen canyons are unique in head shape, small fins and high lateral-line counts. They tend to be intermediate between Little Colorado River and upstream fish, though they were also the largest specimens examined.
- Upper Colorado River populations share large heads, lips and fins with Virgin River fish but have very high lateral-line counts and fins that are elongate but not expansive.

### 7. Hypotheses for the Variation
The author lists four alternatives:
1. A middle Colorado River catostomid fauna containing one or more undescribed forms.
2. A *C. latipinnis* "superspecies" of environmentally induced and maintained morphs.
3. Introduction and spread of a non-native catostomid, with hybridization and introgression.
4. A combination of the above.

The author favors a combination of the first two alternatives, with changes driven by human modification of the river. Hybridization and introgression after the impoundment changes are proposed to explain the intermediate traits of Grand Canyon fish. Little Colorado River suckers are considered "pre-adapted" to post-dam conditions and could have invaded the mainstream. Grand Canyon suckers generally spawn in tributaries, which would promote hybridization.

### 8. Recommendations
- Examine the type material of *C. latipinnis* from the San Pedro River, southern Arizona.
- Study comparative material from geographic extremes of the Colorado River basin.
- Collect specimens from the middle Colorado River region taken before impoundment.
- Until these studies are complete, designate Grand Canyon *Catostomus* as members of the "*Catostomus latipinnis* complex."
