# Review of the National Research Council Draft Report on Glen Canyon Environmental Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This is a 1987 letter-review by [[Minckley]], Professor of Zoology at Arizona State University, of a draft report by the [[National Research Council]] committee evaluating the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES). The GCES program was a research effort conducted largely for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR) after the closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Minckley answers the committee's review questions, finds the committee report generally sound and candid about the inconclusive nature of GCES results, and identifies major weaknesses: an in-house research approach, inadequate historical perspective, poor literature integration, and unsupported management assumptions about thermal and flow manipulations. He concludes that the committee's recommendation of "continued study" rather than policy action is appropriate.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Professional review correspondence (letter dated 24 October 1987) addressed to Sheila D. David, [[National Research Council]] Water Science and Technology Board, with an attached page-by-page commentary.
- **Subject:** Draft committee report on GCES, an assessment of ecological, hydrological, sediment, operational, and biological studies on the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]].
- **Reviewer's stated position:** Close familiarity with the biological components of GCES and earlier regional work, and a stated support for the committee's overall findings, with reservations noted below.
- **Review framework:** Answers to eight questions from the "Guidelines for Review of Reports" (responsiveness to charge, evidence base, uncertainty, clarity, completeness, relation to published literature, fairness and conflict of interest, and policy handling).
- **Agencies mentioned:** [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (principal agency), [[National Park Service]] (NPS; interest in "naturalness"), [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD; trout fishery), [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS; endangered species).
- **Key operational events cited:** Closure of [[Hoover Dam]] (Boulder), [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], and [[Glen Canyon Dam]]; the 1983 flood.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Powell as a point source:** [[Lake Powell]] integrates watershed climatic, geologic, and hydrologic variability. A deep hypolimnetic release moves the effective headwaters of the river to the head of Glen/Grand Canyon, creating a single major input for ecosystem definition.
- **Tributaries:** Apart from the [[Little Colorado River]], tributaries to the study reach are described as short and small.
- **Fish fauna and thermal effects:** Warmer releases might benefit [[Humpback Chub]] and allow reintroduction of [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The same warming could favor non-native competitors and predators, including [[Channel Catfish]], bullhead catfishes, [[Red Shiner]], [[Plains Killifish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Striped Bass]]. These non-natives could occupy creek mouths and backwaters, which the reviewer notes are important to native fishes.
- **Trout fishery:** Trout are accessible to anglers in only about 15 miles of an almost 300-mile canyon reach. Management focused on this minor reach could lead to manipulations harmful to native fishes.
- **Flooding:** Recent evidence from uncontrolled rivers indicates that floods tend to select against non-native fishes and enhance natives. Floods also promote riparian vegetation germination and suppress [[Saltcedar]].
- **Invertebrates:** Substantial ecological data exist on [[Gammarus]] species in eastern North America and Europe, apparently not reviewed by GCES researchers. A mention of "mosquito larvae" in the report is questioned as unlikely in canyon mainstreams.
- **Sediment and hydrology:** Sediment is described as a potentially near-finite resource given loss of upstream input, with possible surpluses from tributaries if the dammed river cannot transport sediment. Sediment management may become a driving factor in future manipulations.
- **Historical context:** Reviewer cites regional studies on the [[Gila River]], [[National Park Service]] Grand Canyon studies (mid-1970s to 1981), and [[Colorado River Fisheries Project]] work (since 1979) as underused sources.

## Key Findings

**Responsiveness and charge**
- The charge was well stated but initially too broad. The report addresses all charge items but its complexity is considerable.
- Reviewer recommends less "light treading" on: lack of defined objectives; in-house research by sister agencies (right-of-first-refusal approach); assignment of junior and intermediate researchers, some hired from national pools with little river or southwestern experience; and underuse of local expertise for oversight and review.

**Evidence and uncertainty**
- The report meets high standards given the data the committee considered, but substantially more information exists.
- The committee's recognition that GCES results are inconclusive and not suitable for policy formulation is its strongest aspect.
- Historical data documentation should be strengthened.

**Clarity and completeness**
- The "Law of the River" section is confused and overly wordy. Reviewer recommends a table of apportionments early in the document.
- The 1922 total mass allocation between upper and lower basins and the 1928 limitation for Arizona should be presented early.
- Specific corrections: p. 1-10, "400,000 = 4,400,000 on line 2."

**Literature integration**
- Neither the GCES report nor the committee report provides adequate references for those unfamiliar with Colorado River problems.
- Reviewer cites several unreferenced bodies of work: Gila River phreatophyte studies (including work by Burkham), NPS-supported Grand Canyon studies, [[Colorado River Fisheries Project]] studies, earlier upper basin fishery and invertebrate work, and BOR-funded lower basin riparian, mammal, bird, reptile, water quality, and fish studies.
- A comprehensive ecological review of the system should have preceded the final report.

**Fairness and conflict of interest**
- Reviewer finds the committee report fair and objective but questions whether BOR's policy intent can be separated from the interests of sister agencies (NPS "naturalness," AGFD trout fishing, USFWS endangered species).
- Reviewer questions how results can be integrated when none of the studies objectively tested an original hypothesis.

**Thermal and flow manipulations**
- Thermal models of Lake Powell existed and were field-verified as early as the 1960s.
- Reviewer objects to the report's statement (p. 4-17) that disadvantages of higher temperatures "cannot be evaluated" on available information. This should be expanded or dropped, since the committee's emphasis on thermal manipulation could be used by BOR to justify penstock modification.
- Reviewer argues that prescribed flooding may be necessary to maintain a "natural" state and productivity, analogous to prescribed burning.

**Biological methods**
- Reviewer is skeptical that [[Habitat Evaluation Procedures]] (HEP) and [[Physical Habitat Simulation]] (PHABSIM) developed elsewhere will apply to desert canyon streams, and notes they have been failing in the upper Colorado River basin.
- Monitoring should extend well beyond "fish reproduction and feeding habits" and be oriented toward a comprehensive monitoring plan.

**Terrestrial studies**
- Study selection was based on available personnel, ongoing interests, and subjects that might show "adverse" effects of the [[1983 Flood]].
- Native and non-native biota were not separated, and some non-natives were implied to enhance "naturalness."
- The committee should further critique undefined "value" and "goals."

**Policy**
- The committee's conclusion that no recommendations beyond continued study should be based on the GCES report is "an excellent handling of policy," according to the reviewer.
- The report is described as "more descriptive of how not to approach a problem area" than a source of hard data, but worth the effort if it prompts reassessment of objectives and continued study.
