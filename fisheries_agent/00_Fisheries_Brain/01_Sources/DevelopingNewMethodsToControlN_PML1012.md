# Developing New Methods to Control Nonnative Aquatic Species in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #nonnative-species-control

## Executive Summary

This internal briefing memo, dated May 17, 2007, was prepared by Shaula Hedwall of the [[Flagstaff Sub-Office]] for a presentation to the Regional Director. It argues that new, selective methods are needed to control [[Nonnative Aquatic Species]] in Arizona, because many native fishes and aquatic herpetofauna are declining or have been extirpated, and the tools currently available are limited and often harm non-target species. The memo summarizes several promising approaches under development, including taxon-specific piscicides and [[Genetic Biocontrol Methods]], and recommends that the Service take a leadership role, coordinate with the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], and seek partnerships to fund research.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Many native fishes and aquatic herpetofauna in Arizona and the Southwest have declined or been listed as threatened or endangered, largely due to the introduction and spread of nonnative species.
- **Current limitations:** Practical and effective options for controlling nonnative species are limited. Existing methods, such as broad-spectrum chemical treatments, can kill native species along with the target nonnative fishes.
- **Purpose of the briefing:** To provide background for the presentation "Need for Developing New Nonnative Aquatic Species Control Technologies" and to build support for a research and development program.
- **Contact:** Steven Spangle, Supervisor, Ecological Services Field Office, Phoenix, Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native aquatic fauna at risk:** The memo focuses on native fishes and aquatic herpetofauna in Arizona streams and rivers, which are threatened by competition, predation, and habitat changes linked to nonnative species.
- **Non-target impacts:** A central concern is that control methods must avoid harming native species, so selectivity is a core requirement for any new technology.
- **Gila River basin:** The genetic-methods research cited in the memo was specifically aimed at nonnative fish control in the [[Gila River Basin]].
- **Scale of need:** Developing new control tools is expensive and slow. The memo estimates that development could cost millions of dollars and take a decade or more, which is why coordinated, multi-agency effort is recommended.

## Key Findings

### 1. Taxon-Specific Chemical Controls
- [[Dawson and Kolar (2003)]] evaluated the integrated use of existing technologies to control nonnative fishes while minimizing non-target impacts. Their final report was prepared for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], Phoenix Area Office.
- Recent work suggests high promise for chemicals that can target specific taxa. [[Ward (2005)]] screened the toxicity of Supaverm as a candidate species-specific piscicide, showing that selective removal of nonnative fishes is feasible in principle.

### 2. Genetic Biocontrol Methods
- [[Kapuscinski and Patronski (2005)]] examined genetic methods for biological control of nonnative fishes in the Gila River basin. Their work focused on chromosome set manipulation and DNA-based approaches that target a specific biological pathway.
- The memo notes that both genetic and chemical approaches can avoid non-target impacts, but that substantial research is still needed before they can be used in the field.
- Genetic techniques were also described as potentially applicable to other nonnative species, including an Asian fish species (the source text is partly garbled at this point).

### 3. Development Requirements and Barriers
- Both approaches require lengthy, costly research: development could cost millions of dollars and take about a decade.
- The memo argues that these and other tools should be pursued as quickly as possible if the Service hopes to recover native aquatic species.

### 4. Recommended Actions
- **Leadership:** The Bureau Perspective section states that the Service should take an active leadership role in developing a program for nonnative aquatic species control, especially in the Southwest.
- **Coordination:** The Service should confer with the U.S. Geological Survey's [[Upper Midwest Environmental Sciences Center]], which conducted the integrated-management and genetics work.
- **Funding partnerships:** Potential partners include federal agencies, states, nongovernmental organizations (such as fisheries societies), and competitive research awards to universities.
- **Priority setting:** The memo recommends elevating the issue to the Service's Directorate to set priorities and designate a lead office to start the effort.
