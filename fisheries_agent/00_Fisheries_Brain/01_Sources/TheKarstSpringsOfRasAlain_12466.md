# Karst Springs of Ra's al-Ain and the Khabur River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This illustrated feature article, written by a fish researcher from the [[Senckenberg Museum]], describes the karst spring system at [[Ra's Al-Ain Karst Springs]] in northeast Syria and the [[Khabur River]] that drains it. The springs, fed by mountain catchments and rising through fractured Eocene chalk, form one of the largest karst systems on Earth and support an unexpectedly rich freshwater community in an otherwise arid steppe. The article also frames the region's ecology within its archaeological history in [[Mesopotamia]]. The text is descriptive rather than quantitative: it gives spring discharge figures and depth observations, and describes the fish, snail, crab, insect and amphibian fauna qualitatively.

## Background & Description

The article begins with the regional context of [[Mesopotamia]], the land between the [[Euphrates River]] and [[Tigris River]]. The author notes that the landscape has changed little since the time of Xenophon (circa 400 BC), though onagers, ostriches and gazelles have declined. Interest in the region's zoology grew from archaeology: [[Hartmut Kuhne]] began excavating the Assyrian provincial capital [[Dur Katlimmu]] (modern Tall Shaikh Hamad) on the lower Khabur more than ten years before the article was written. The excavation raised the question of how a population exceeding 10,000 people lived there more than 4,000 years ago in an area that today can barely support 2,000. Geomorphologists, hydrologists, botanists and zoologists worked with archaeologists to reconstruct the past environment.

The author, [[Friedhelm Krupp]], heads the research department Fischkunde 1 at the Senckenberg Museum in Frankfurt-am-Main and has travelled extensively in the Middle East since 1972.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and geology.** The Khabur is the lifeline of an area of steppe with very low rainfall. Near Ra's al-Ain, an oasis of willows, poplars, tamarisks and reeds surrounds a karst spring system. In an area of about 3 km² there are 13 springs, mostly circular depressions where water rises under artesian pressure. Water is fed mainly by the Mardin mountains and the Karaca Dag in the southern Taurus and percolates through layers of Eocene chalk. Karst forms where flowing groundwater meets soluble rock; the massive chalk itself is impermeable, so tectonic faults and cracks provided the pathways. Continued dissolution causes surface subsidence, and the town of Ra's al-Ain is threatened by it.

**Discharge.** The average annual output of the system is 39 m³/s. This exceeds [[Silver Spring]] in Florida (23 m³/s), the largest karst spring in North America, and the [[Friaul Spring]] in Italy (37 m³/s), the most productive European spring. The author notes that only spring systems spread over a considerably larger area have greater output.

**Water chemistry.** Most springs have relatively low dissolved salt content. The exception is [[Ain Kabrid]], the "Sulphur Spring", where sulphates are converted by [[Sulphur Bacteria]] into elemental sulphur. This produces the turquoise colour of the water and releases enough heat that Ain Kabrid is 3°C warmer than the other springs. It supports no higher life forms and has a strong smell of hydrogen sulphide. The article also notes that most springs on the river terraces supply saline groundwater, so salinity rises and animal numbers in the river fall downstream.

**Spring habitats.** [[Ain Banus]] is a slightly oval spring about 20 m in diameter, with water clear enough to see snails on the bottom at 12 m. Vegetation (hornwort, *Ceratophyllum*, and water lilies, *Nuphar*) is restricted to the shallow upper layer, ending at about 3 m. Below this, walls descend steeply to 12 m. At 10 m, a cave system discharges water into the spring with variable current strength. The article describes the fauna in three zones: the plant-covered shallows, the sparsely populated steep walls, and the twilight caves.

**Downstream course.** Below the springs, the Khabur meanders through the steppe between narrow bands of reeds, tamarisks and willows, rarely more than 30 m wide. Shortly before joining the Euphrates, about 200 km southwest of Ra's al-Ain, the river widens and freshwater from the Euphrates dilutes the salinity.

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna of the springs and caves**

- The most common fish in the Ain Banus caves is the [[Cyprinion Macrostomum]] (large-mouthed barb), which the author describes as found in such large numbers nowhere else. It scrapes algae from cave walls with a sharp-edged horny lower jaw and feeds almost exclusively on algae.
- Large shoals of [[Alburnus Caeruleus]] (blue silver fish), which reach barely more than 10 cm, hunt small crustaceans and insects in the open water.
- Among the plants, the [[Golden Barb]] (*Barbus luteus*) and [[Sheik's Barb]] (*Barbus schejch*) prey on freshwater shrimps. The [[Black Spot Barb]] (*Barbus subquincunciatus*) specialises in snails, cracking their shells with powerful pharyngeal teeth.
- Male [[Aphanius Mento]] defend territories aggressively; during courtship they become deep blue/black with iridescent silver-white spots.
- [[Garra Rufa]] competes with snails for sparse algae on the steep spring walls.
- The [[Mesopotamian Spiny Eel]] (*Mastacembelus mastacembelus*), mainly nocturnal, is about one metre long and can be approached within about 30 cm. It is the most northerly representative of its family in Asia and occurs in the Euphrates and Tigris systems. Its body is grey in the caves and chequered blue/grey and ochre elsewhere.

**Large river fish**

- The [[Pike Barb]] (*Barbus esocinus*) and the large-scaled barb (*Barbus grypus*) each reach almost 2 m. A photograph caption records a fisherman's catch of a 1.60 m pike barb.
- The [[Mesopotamian Catfish]] (*Silurus triostegus*) grows to more than 2 m.
- The upper course is described as very rich in fish, and fishermen catch large specimens in fixed nets. The fish market at Hasaka, the largest town on the Khabur, displays fresh river fish daily.

**Invertebrates and other fauna**

- The [[Jordan Snail]] (*Theodoxus jordani*) is widespread across the Middle East and highly variable in colour and pattern, which once led to its being classed as several species.
- Snails cluster on water lily leaves and chalk walls.
- The [[Freshwater Crab]] (*Potamon potamios*) peers from crevices in the rocks.
- [[Calopteryx Intermedia]] males (steel blue) gather on overhanging willow branches; females (metallic green) prefer less exposed perches.
- Amphibians on the banks include the [[Oriental Leaf Frog]] (*Hyla savignyi*), which is less timid than the Caspian armoured swamp toad (*Maurinus caspica*).

**Conservation and human context**

- The article contrasts past and present: the region once supported more wildlife and far larger human populations, and the author links this to long-term environmental change.
- Subsidence threatens Ra's al-Ain, and water is central to the local economy, with women and children carrying water from the river by donkey in some villages.
- The article also includes a brief, separate photo feature on Guinea, where Susu women and girls fish with hand-made nets, catching mainly small tilapia. A caption also shows a saratoga (*Scleropages jardinii*) from the Fly River in Papua New Guinea. These examples are not part of the Syrian study.
