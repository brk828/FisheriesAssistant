# Winning the War for the West

#rangeland-ecology #livestock-grazing #riparian-zones #public-lands

## Executive Summary

This 1999 *Atlantic Monthly* feature by Perri Knize examines the conflict over livestock grazing on [[Public Rangelands]] in the American West. It argues that ranchers and [[Environmental Groups]] are increasingly finding common ground on [[Riparian Restoration]], even as litigation, budget cuts and [[Rapid Western Growth]] threaten the [[Ranching Way of Life]]. The piece is a persuasive essay rather than a research study. Its fisheries content centers on the [[Muddy Creek Allotment]] and its [[West Slope Cutthroat Trout]] habitat, which is used as a case study of how grazing rules can affect both ranchers and aquatic habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Format:** Long-form magazine article, July 1999, by Perri Knize.
- **Core dispute:** Whether grazing on federal land benefits or damages the environment, and who should pay for the [[Federal Grazing Program]].
- **Opening event:** In June 1998 in Natrona County, Wyoming, vandals calling themselves "Islamic Jihad Ecoterrorists" cut more than 150 fences, causing about $100,000 in damage.
- **Key actors:** [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM), [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[Natural Resources Defense Council]], [[National Wildlife Federation]], [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]], [[Oregon Natural Resources Council]].
- **Policy context:** The [[Taylor Grazing Act]] (1934) first restricted grazing and imposed fees on BLM lands. Later the [[Beaverhead Riparian Guidelines]] and the 1993 grazing plan set stricter standards, following a push by Interior Secretary Bruce Babbitt for "range reforms."
- **Central figure:** Dean Welborn, a Montana rancher whose herd on the Muddy Creek Allotment was cut by 72 percent after he bought the ranch in 1992.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Claims by critics**
- Domestic livestock on the public range, including 88 percent of western sheep and roughly half of western cattle, were said to be defecating in trout streams, trampling banks, and removing forage and cover for wildlife.
- Federal outlays for managing grazing lands were said to exceed rancher permit fees.

**Counterclaims and data**
- In fiscal year 1998 the BLM and Forest Service spent at least $75 million on grazing and collected about $20 million in fees.
- Many scientists reportedly said that no definitive case could be made for or against grazing. The [[National Research Council]] stated there is no consistent field data to test general claims about grassland health.
- A [[General Accounting Office]] review found that 29 percent of BLM rangelands were in excellent to good condition, 43 percent in fair to poor condition, and 28 percent unclassified.
- Livestock numbers on western public lands fell from 28.6 million in 1934 to 10.3 million in 1994.
- Rangeland rated good or excellent has more than doubled since the 1930s, and acreage rated poor has been halved, according to the BLM.
- Riparian zones were still considered degraded. Restoration tools included water tanks to divert cattle, selective exclosure fencing, and rotational grazing. The GAO called the results "dramatic."

**Wildlife interactions**
- Elk, deer, wild sheep and antelope prefer the young shoots stimulated by cattle grazing, which the article compares to the role once played by bison.
- Hart Mountain National Antelope Refuge in Oregon saw an antelope decline after cattle were removed. Local ranchers attributed this to loss of cattle-grazed forage. Managers attributed it to coyote predation on fawns.
- Ungrazed grasslands were said to burn more often, and rodent populations to decline, if cattle were removed.

## Key Findings

1. **Grazing debate is contested.** Both the BLM's claim of improvement and the environmental groups' "unsatisfactory" rating were said to rely on the same underlying data. Neither side can be fully verified.
2. **Ranching economics are weak.** Ranchers reportedly earn about a 2 percent return on their operations. Cattle prices are near 20-year lows and land and operating costs have risen sharply.
3. **Permit value is disputed.** About 90 percent of ranchers with allotments were said to have paid full value for their leases, though the money went to previous permit holders, not the government. Only the roughly 10 percent still on original homesteads were described as receiving a true subsidy.
4. **Cost comparisons are mixed.** Economic studies were said to show that, on average, ranchers pay about the same for public and private forage. Agricultural landowners get back 21 cents of local public services per tax dollar, versus $1.36 for low-density residential areas (1990 USDA study).
5. **Removing livestock has costs.** Full removal of the roughly four million cattle on BLM and Forest Service land was predicted to lower cattle prices, raise private forage costs by about 10 percent, and lead to subdivision that could harm wildlife winter range.
6. **Cookbook standards draw criticism.** Critics say rules such as moving cows off a stream bank after a set number of animal tracks, or off the grass at a set stubble height, ignore ecosystem complexity.
7. **Muddy Creek case.** The allotment (about 25,000 acres) was recommended for a 1989 Stewardship Award and given a 15 percent increase in 1990. After the 1992 transfer to Welborn, herd numbers were cut by 72 percent. Welborn then grazed cattle on his own deeded land, which holds much of the prime [[West Slope Cutthroat Trout]] habitat. The remedial plan was appealed, withdrawn, and replaced by a 1993 standard.
8. **Collaboration is emerging.** The Six-Six Group of ranchers and environmentalists, the [[Malpai Borderlands Group]] (about 20 ranchers and partner agencies managing a million-acre area), and the [[National Riparian Service Team]] (1996) all show cooperative models.
9. **Conservation tools.** The Malpai Grassbank and [[Conservation Easements]] protect ranches from subdivision. The Nature Conservancy paid $4.6 million to keep a 5,167-acre deeded property working, with 250,000 acres of grazing allotments, including 42 miles of riparian area.
10. **Social stakes.** Author Aaron Harp, a University of Idaho rural sociologist, argues that ranching depends on long-standing local relationships. The article links the loss of ranches to the loss of community and habitat. Western population grew 14 percent since 1990, and urban growth consumed more than two million acres since 1982.
11. **Core conclusion.** The author argues that ranchers and environmentalists share goals of clean water, healthy wildlife and sound ecosystems, and that polarization by extremists on both sides is the main threat.
