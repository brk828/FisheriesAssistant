# Stocking, Propagation and Recovery of Trout and Colorado Squawfish in Colorado Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of Colorado fisheries management and hatchery reports. It covers three related topics: (1) guidelines for establishing a wild, self-sustaining [[Rainbow Trout]] population in large streams, based on stocking studies; (2) the recovery and propagation program for the endangered [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] (GBN), including brood stock management and disease control; and (3) intensive culture research on the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] at a federal hatchery. The stocking results also compare trout populations in several rivers and show how stocking method, size and timing affect survival.

## Background & Description

The text is a compilation of several reports and program summaries, not a single study. Its main subjects are:

- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*): stocking strategies for streams and survival of fingerlings.
- **[[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus clarkii stomias*): a federally listed subspecies. Recovery depends on a disease-free hatchery brood stock.
- **[[Brown Trout]]** (*Salmo trutta*): a competitor and predator of stocked rainbows, especially in the [[Blue River]].
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): an endangered cyprinid. The text covers intensive larval and fry culture.

Agencies and institutions named include the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] (CDOW), its Fish Hatchery Research and Culture Program (FRH), the [[Rocky Mountain National Park]] (RMNP), the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH) in Roswell, New Mexico, and the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rainbow trout stocking and river conditions**
- Stocking studies were run in the Gunnison, Rio Grande, Blue and Animas rivers.
- The Gunnison study found that rainbow trout density and biomass were lower in the upper Gunnison than in the Blue River, which had brown trout densities and biomass two to three times higher than the other study rivers.
- Growth rates are very slow in the Blue River because the winter season is long and cold, which increases natural mortality.
- Water temperature matters for egg survival. If temperatures in the target stream are below 6°C (42°F) for a significant period from April through June, the incubating eggs are unlikely to develop beyond the eyed stage.

**Greenback cutthroat trout habitat and disease**
- Source populations were chosen for genetic purity and diversity from isolated historical populations. Source streams included Corno Creek, Hunter Creek, Bear Lake, the Poudre River, Apache Creek, and Arkansas River stocks.
- A fungal infection is a persistent problem in hatchery stocks. Malachite green was used under a special-use permit to reduce the fungus, and the FDA was asked for a drug exemption permit.

**Colorado Squawfish culture**
- Wild adult squawfish were collected from the Colorado River near Grand Junction, Colorado, for brood stock.
- Fry culture depends on water temperature, feeding behavior and tank design.

## Key Findings

### 1. Guidelines for a Wild Rainbow Trout Population
Based on the stocking studies, the report recommends the following:
- Use only **wild, disease-free rainbow stocks** from sources such as the [[Oncorhynchus mykiss]] strain from the Gunnison River.
- **Stock fingerlings** at 7 to 12 cm (about 3 to 5 in) to reduce predation and improve survival.
- **Avoid hatchery rearing beyond 15 cm.** Rearing to this size is counterproductive. Production costs rise at least linearly, while long-term survival in the stream declines in a negatively exponential way.
- **Stock from July through September,** after spring runoff, to significantly increase survival.
- **Restrict size and bag limits** to reduce angler harvest of immature fish and maximize survival to spawning size and age.
- **Check the thermal profile** of the target stream from April through June.

### 2. Gunnison River Stocking Results
- Stocking of Greenback Cutthroat (CRR) fingerlings in the upper Gunnison River since 1986 significantly altered the trout population.
- Density, biomass and numbers of rainbows at least 35 cm (14 in) increased significantly (P < 0.001) in the 1986–92 period, compared with the 1981 base year.
- In 1990, at least 20% of CRR fingerlings died during transport because of hatchery truck problems.
- The Blue River trout population is more heavily influenced by brown trout predation, which reduced the value of stocked fingerlings.

### 3. Animas River Stocking Method
- Boat distribution of rainbow and brown trout fingerlings in the Animas River through the study period significantly increased trout populations (P < 0.001).
- Rainbow and brown trout density, biomass and the numbers of brown trout over 40 cm changed significantly in two study sections where fingerlings were boat-stocked compared with years when fingerlings were point-stocked from fixed sites.

### 4. Greenback Cutthroat Trout Recovery and Brood Stock
- Recovery depends on a federally managed hatchery brood stock, produced from wild, genetically pure sources.
- Brood stock fish are disinfected and isolated, and eggs from each source population are reared in separate, disease-free facilities until certified.
- Table 1 lists acquisitions from wild and on-site sources for several study years (1989–1991). Entries include the number of fry stocked per year, source population and number of eggs taken, with the note that eggs were taken from RMNP streams.
- Some stocks had heavy losses to disease. In the 1989 stock, a large share of fish were lost to fungus.
- In spring 1992, the remaining 1989 brood stock was spawned. This provided the first in-state source of disease-free greenbacks for supplemental stocking.

### 5. Colorado Squawfish Intensive Culture (1989–1990)
- The study's goal was to develop an intensive culture technique for squawfish fry. Brood stock came from DNFH and wild adult fish from the Colorado River.
- **Temperature:** a minimum of 18°C (64°F) is needed for fry development and productive growth. Higher temperatures improve growth, within limits.
- **Feeding:** fry are fed live brine shrimp (*Artemia*) and encapsulated dry diet (Biokyowa, then Biodiet). Enclosed feeding areas with aeration improved water quality and feeding response.
- **Density:** during the first 5 days, density should stay below 1 lb per cubic foot. After 90 days, feeding behavior changes and higher densities (up to 6 lb per cubic foot) are satisfactory.
- **Loading and oxygen:** the optimum loading range is 0.8 to 3.6 lb/gal/min. Squawfish need about 0.84 lb of oxygen per lb of feed (value partly garbled in the source scan).
- **Mortality:** most mortality occurred after fry began to mature sexually. Deeper water (1 ft) gave better growth than shallow water (6 in).
- **Brood stock:** six fertile females were artificially spawned using hormones. Eggs were split into seven groups and fertilized with sperm from seven males, giving 21 lots. Four lots produced no surviving eggs.
- **Tagging:** one third (1,148) of the squawfish from the 1991 study year (progeny of wild plants) were PIT-tagged and transported to DNFH for future brood stock. The remaining fish from the 1991 study year were to be tagged and moved to ponds in Grand Junction and Grand Valley, Colorado.
