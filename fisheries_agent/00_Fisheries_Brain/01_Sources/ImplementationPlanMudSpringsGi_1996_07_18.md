# Mud Springs Gila Topminnow Habitat Improvement Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #freshwater-conservation #Gila-topminnow

## Executive Summary
This 1996 Implementation Plan, developed by the Tonto National Forest in cooperation with the [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], addresses habitat degradation for an isolated, introduced population of [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*) at Mud Springs. Originally introduced in 1982 into open surface water, the population became confined to a concrete livestock drinker by 1989, creating an unstable, marginal habitat. The plan outlines construction of engineered "potholes" (small deep ponds) within a new livestock exclosure to create stable, open-water refugia, alongside continued maintenance of the original spring infrastructure, supplemental stocking, and a multi-year monitoring program. This is a case study in applied conservation hydrology and habitat engineering for an endangered livebearer fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historical/Current Habitat**: Originally occupied open surface water at Mud Springs; since 1989, confined almost exclusively to a **concrete drinker** (livestock water tank) and adjacent flooded overflow areas — a marginal, artificial habitat with limited stability.
- **Target Habitat (post-project)**: Four constructed rectangular "potholes" (excavated ponds) within a livestock exclosure, ranging from ~7×5 ft to ~35×5 ft, approximately 5 ft deep, designed to:
  - Minimize surface area (reduce evaporative water loss).
  - Maintain three vertical walls (limits emergent aquatic vegetation encroachment, preserving open water).
  - Include one sloped side (human/wildlife safety access).
  - Fill naturally via near-surface groundwater within a few months of excavation.
- **Microhabitat features**: One pothole will include underwater shelves (1–2 ft deep, ~2 ft wide) along vertical walls, experimentally designed to test whether shelf structure improves population size, recruitment, or population structure — an explicit habitat-complexity experiment.
- **Riparian vegetation**: Willow (*Salix* sp.) cuttings will be planted 3–5 ft from pothole edges in autumn (root establishment over winter), assuming 30% cutting mortality; placement avoids root damage to pond banks while providing riparian structure/shade.
- **Threats/Limiting factors**:
  - Livestock access and trampling (addressed via exclosure fencing).
  - Evaporative water loss in an arid spring system.
  - Encroachment of emergent vegetation reducing open-water habitat.
  - Population isolation limiting gene flow (addressed by moving fish between pothole/drinker habitats as needed).
- **Co-located infrastructure**: A **new springbox, pipe, and drinker** will be constructed outside the exclosure for livestock use; Gila topminnow will **not** be introduced there, keeping the two water systems ecologically and functionally separate. The original drinker will be phased out for livestock use after FY1997 but maintained for fish for at least 4 years.
- **Interacting taxa**: Emergent aquatic vegetation (unspecified species) is treated as a competing/successional threat to open-water habitat, managed by periodic mechanical removal.

## Key Findings
- **Population origin**: The Mud Springs Gila topminnow population is a **translocated/introduced** population established in 1982 under a 1981 Memorandum of Understanding — not a natural relict population.
- **Habitat bottleneck**: Confinement to a single concrete drinker since 1989 represents a critical habitat bottleneck threatening long-term population viability and necessitating active intervention.
- **Adaptive management structure**: The plan includes an explicit **4-year decision point** — if the pothole population is stable and self-reproducing, drinker maintenance will cease; if unstable, the original spring infrastructure will be reconstructed and the plan amended.
- **Experimental design embedded in habitat construction**: Shelf-equipped potholes serve as a controlled comparison for evaluating whether structural microhabitat complexity benefits topminnow demographics before wider implementation.
- **Multi-agency, multi-year monitoring protocol**:
  - *Implementation monitoring*: on-site fisheries biologist verifies construction specs.
  - *Post-project habitat monitoring*: water depth and drinker function checked monthly (3 months), then quarterly (1 year), then annually (6 years).
  - *Post-stocking biological monitoring*: presence/absence and relative abundance via seining/electrofishing; CPUE metrics (seine haul length/width, shock seconds) recorded monthly for 3 months, then each summer for 6 years.
  - *Depletion surveys*: attempted at year 4 post-stocking if population numbers are sufficiently high and can be conducted safely; used to calculate population estimates and fish density (fish/ft³) across differing pothole geometries.
- **Handling ethics/safety constraints**: Protocols explicitly limit seine hauls and electroshock duration to avoid overstressing fish and unauthorized take; depletion surveys are foregone if unsafe or if fish numbers are low.
- **Supplemental stocking strategy**: Multiple stocking events planned (1997, possibly 1998/1999+), with inter-habitat fish transfers to manage genetic diversity/gene flow across the fragmented pothole/drinker system.
- **Land management integration**: Habitat restoration is tightly coupled with livestock grazing management — a new exclosure fence excludes cattle from the fish habitat, while a separate new drinker maintains livestock water access outside the exclosure, exemplifying multiple-use conflict resolution in [[Endangered Species]] habitat management on National Forest lands.
- **Institutional collaboration**: Reflects coordinated endangered-species habitat engineering among [[Tonto National Forest]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], with contributions from range, hydrology, soils, and riparian specialists — illustrating an interdisciplinary approach to single-species habitat recovery.
