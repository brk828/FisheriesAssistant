## Executive Summary

This document is a **station profile** (Exhibit 1) for the **Spring Creek National Fish Hatchery**, a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service facility located on the Columbia River near Underwood, Washington. Established in 1901, the hatchery's primary mission is the **mitigation of [[Fall Chinook Salmon]]** populations impacted by hydroelectric and flood control development, operating under the Mitchell Act of 1938 and Flood Control Act of 1950. The profile reports strong broodstock returns and egg production for the reporting year, alongside multi-year data (1991–1995) on funding, staffing (FTE), and fish distribution numbers across federal, state, and private waters in Washington and Oregon.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide direct morphological data (e.g., length, weight, meristic counts) for [[Fall Chinook Salmon]] or [[Spring Chinook Salmon]]. Life-stage information is limited to hatchery production metrics:

- **Egg eye-up rate:** 96.3% (indicating high egg viability and successful fertilization/incubation conditions)
- **Life stage released:** Unfed fry (early post-hatch developmental stage, prior to exogenous feeding)
- **Adult return size:** Over 9,000 adults returned to the hatchery, exceeding broodstock needs

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The hatchery operation reflects the anadromous life history of [[Chinook Salmon]], which migrate between freshwater natal streams and the [[North Pacific Ocean]] via the Columbia River system:

- **Freshwater rearing habitat:** 44 holding and rearing ponds, supported by a dedicated hatchery water supply, distribution, and water reconditioning system
- **River connectivity:** A substation on the **Big White Salmon River** (a Columbia River tributary) provides supplemental water intake (30 CFS water rights), diversion rack infrastructure, and two additional raceways, extending the hatchery's rearing capacity into the natural river corridor
- **Migratory infrastructure:** A fish ladder facilitates adult upstream passage to the spawning building
- **Mitigation role:** The hatchery functions as compensatory mitigation for habitat/passage losses caused by upstream dam and flood-control infrastructure (per the Flood Control Act of 1950), situating the facility within the broader [[Columbia River Basin]] salmon management framework

## Key Findings

- **Egg production:** Over 24 million eggs taken from returning adults in the reporting year, with an exceptional eye-up rate of 96.3%
- **Fry release:** Over 7.6 million unfed fry released as part of the mitigation program
- **Adult returns:** Exceeded 9,000 adults, surpassing hatchery broodstock requirements
- **Distribution trends (Numbers of Fish Distributed, excluding eggs/fry):**
  - FY 1991: 14.9M (FWS) + 0.6M (other) = 15.5M total
  - FY 1992: 23.5M (FWS) + 1.1M (other) = 24.6M total
  - FY 1993: 22.0M (FWS) + 0.7M (other) = 22.7M total
- **Species distributed:** Primarily [[Fall Chinook Salmon]], with a secondary component of [[Spring Chinook Salmon]]
- **Distribution states:** Washington and Oregon
- **Infrastructure upgrades:** Electrical rehabilitation completed on the standby generator and transfer breaker, including installation of a new PLC (Programmable Logic Control) and UPS (Uninterrupted Power Supply) system, improving operational reliability
- **Staffing trend:** FTE count increased steadily from 10.12 (1991) to an estimated 11.3 (1994–1995), reflecting stable-to-growing operational capacity
- **Funding sources:** Cost-recoverable funding from the **Army Corps of Engineers (COE)** and **National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS)** supported operations, with NMFS contributions rising from $180.1K (1991) to an estimated $325.0K (1994–1995)
- **Operations budget decline:** Notable drop in "Operations" funding from $46.0K (1991) to $1.0K (1993–1995 est.), suggesting a shift in budget categorization or funding mechanism (e.g., increased reliance on cost-recoverable COE/NMFS funds rather than direct operations line items)
