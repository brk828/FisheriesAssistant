# Dispersal of Exotic Species from Aquaculture Sources, with Emphasis on Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter, by Courtenay and Williams, examines how aquaculture facilities have repeatedly released or allowed escape of exotic aquatic organisms into open waters, particularly in the [[Contiguous United States]]. The authors report that of 46 established exotic fishes in the contiguous U.S., 22 result from aquaculture activities, and that the pet (aquarium) industry has been the source of more introductions than food, sport, or other culture. The chapter argues that escape is virtually inevitable for any cultured exotic, that introductions can cause ecological, economic and disease impacts, and that future food aquaculture could become a major introduction source unless precautions are taken early. It concludes with recommendations for escape-proof culture, regulation, and shared responsibility between aquaculturists and conservation agencies.

## Background & Description

The chapter situates aquaculture as an ancient practice, noting pond culture of carps in China from the fifth century B.C. and the medieval European culture of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), native to the Danube. Common carp now has a nearly global distribution; Welcomme (1988) ranks it third among international transfers, behind [[Mozambique Tilapia]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], and reports successful release into 59 countries. Pullin (1986) estimated roughly 250,000 metric tons of carp are produced annually by aquaculture.

The authors define aquaculture as "the growing of aquatic organisms under controlled conditions" (Bardach et al. 1972), but restrict the term in this chapter to culture for food, sport, biological control, and aquarium purposes. A central contrast with agriculture is that most cultured aquatic species remain feral and can return to a wild state if released, whereas most agricultural livestock cannot.

The chapter's main species groups are:
- **Food species**: common carp, [[Mozambique Tilapia]], [[Blue Tilapia]], [[Nile Tilapia]], rudd, ide, tench.
- **Sport species**: brown trout, bairdiella, orangemouth corvina, zander.
- **Biological control species**: [[Grass Carp]], [[Silver Carp]], [[Bighead Carp]], and several tilapias.
- **Aquarium species**: [[Convict Cichlid]], [[Walking Catfish]], [[Goldfish]], and many cichlids, poeciliids and characins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Escape and release pathways.** Escape from culture is described as nearly universal: Shelton and Smitherman (1984) state that "escape is virtually inevitable" for any exotic fish used in culture. Causes include carelessness in facility construction and operation, flooding, effluent discharge, employee removal, fishermen's bait-bucket dumping, and occasionally deliberate release to evade prohibition.

**Impacts on native biota.** Reported impacts include:
- Predation and competition, including behavioral and spatial competition that is harder to measure than food competition.
- Common carp uprooting vegetation, raising turbidity and lowering oxygen, and eating eggs of native fishes.
- Tilapias causing overcontrol of vegetation, crowding out native spawning sites (especially centrarchids), and producing stunted populations.
- Hydrilla (*Hydrilla verticillata*), a plant released from the aquarium trade, clogging waters and costing millions of dollars annually in control measures.
- [[Melanoides Tuberculata]], an aquarium-trade snail that is an intermediate host for the Oriental lung fluke and liver fluke, posing a possible public health threat in Florida.

**Habitat alteration synergy.** The authors argue that habitat alteration often stresses native fauna first, making establishment of aliens easier, and that introductions then act synergistically with earlier human-caused changes.

**Case studies.**
- **Brown trout** in the Kern River system, California, seriously affected native [[Golden Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus aguabonita*), the state fish of California.
- **Blue tilapia** from a Dade County, Florida, facility invaded Taylor Slough in [[Everglades National Park]].
- **Blue tilapia** in the lower Susquehanna River, Pennsylvania, were killed in December 1986 when condenser cooling water was released at lower temperatures.
- **Convict cichlids** in Nevada's Pahranagat Valley apparently contributed to the decline of the endangered [[White River Springfish]], and were introduced into Hiko Spring in 1984, apparently maliciously, to disrupt springfish reestablishment.
- **Walking catfish** escaped into Florida in the late 1960s, leading to the only federal listing of a prohibited fish under the Lacey Act. Its impact in Florida has been limited so far.
- **Salton Sea** introductions of bairdiella and orangemouth corvina are described as well-planned releases with no likely adverse impacts.

**Parasites and disease.** Importation for aquaculture poses a threat of introducing exotic parasites and pathogens, with the aquarium industry now using prophylaxis of imported stock (Shotts and Gratzek 1984).

## Key Findings

- **Source attribution:** Of 46 established exotic fishes in the contiguous U.S., 22 result from aquaculture; approximately 60% of the established species are known or presumed to originate from the aquarium trade.
- **Historical pattern:** Common carp was widely regarded as a mistake by the early 1900s; it was later replaced as a preferred international transfer species by tilapias (1950–1979) and Chinese carps (1960–1980) (Welcomme 1988).
- **Rudd risk:** Rudd is being cultured in Arkansas and Virginia and sold to bait stores in at least 16 central and eastern states, and has been taken in open waters of at least eight states. Six states (Alabama, Connecticut, Florida, Louisiana, Texas, Virginia) have passed import bans. Possible hybridization with native [[Golden Shiner]] is also a concern.
- **Grass carp establishment:** Escaped grass carp from Arkansas government facilities in the early 1960s led to documented reproduction in the lower Mississippi basin, and reports of spawning in the Trinity, Red and Missouri rivers. Populations appear to be increasing.
- **Biological control failure:** The authors argue that fishes are not monophagous, so biological control with fish will always affect non-target species, unlike many successful insect biocontrols.
- **Blue tilapia spread in Florida:** Introduced for weed control at Pleasant Grove Research Station in 1961 after Auburn University found no sport value. It was established in 12 counties before 1968 and occupied 18 counties by the time of writing. Dispersal occurred through Commission personnel and fishermen removing young from nests.
- **Nile tilapia:** Recorded from a reservoir on the Tallapoosa River in Alabama, draining fish culture ponds at Auburn University. The authors warn that permitting its use amounts to condoning introduction when escapes occur.
- **Mariculture:** Atlantic salmon net pens in Pacific Northwest inshore waters are prone to stock escape, and US mariculture regulation of non-native species is described as "essentially non-existent."
- **Recommendations:** Culture of non-native species should be confined through intensive closed systems, diking, sand-and-gravel effluent filtration, and screening; permits should be granted only when a market is demonstrated and protection is assured; agencies should require notification of facility closure or bonding for stock disposal; interagency conflicts should be avoided.
- **Regulatory stance:** The authors state that regulations protect natural resources, that no state or federal agency is opposed to aquaculture, and that some states (e.g., Alabama, Arkansas, Mississippi) strongly promote it.
