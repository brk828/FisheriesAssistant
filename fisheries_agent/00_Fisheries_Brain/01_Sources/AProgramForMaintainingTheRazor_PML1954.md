# A Program for Maintaining the Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper describes an active management program to maintain the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an Endangered catostomid, in [[Lake Mohave]], a regulated reservoir on the [[Colorado River Basin]] formed by [[Davis Dam]] on the Arizona-Nevada border. Lake Mohave holds the last large population of the species. Adults spawn successfully, but almost no young survive to adulthood, and the population is aging and expected to disappear within a few years unless recruitment is restored. The [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), a cooperative team of state, federal, and university biologists, adopted a "quick response" approach instead of a conventional recovery plan. Its core strategy is to collect naturally spawned larvae, rear them in predator-free backwaters and coves to about 25 cm total length (TL), tag them, and release them into the reservoir. The stated target is a minimum of 10,000 young adults released over five years.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker has been reduced to less than 5% of its former range (Minckley et al. 1991). Nearly all individuals collected in the past two decades were adults, and the species is nearing extinction in the upper Colorado River basin. Lake Mohave supports the largest remaining population, and it has declined substantially over the past five years (Marsh 1994). The scanned source gives the endpoints and percentage only partly legibly, so exact figures are not reproduced here.

Federal recovery efforts began in 1976 with the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team, and the Colorado River Fishery Project started in 1979. The recovery program for the remaining 10 years of a 15-year plan was estimated at US$84-134 million (USFWS 1993). Under a cooperative agreement, nearly 12 million small razorback suckers were stocked into Arizona streams between 1980 and 1989, but this failed to establish self-sustaining populations. The species was listed as Endangered in 1995 (the scan reads "19S"; the source text gives the year as 1995 in the paper's context of the decade's events). The authors also discuss the [[Endangered Species Act]] framework within which these actions occurred.

The program's co-authors note that other long-lived species, including the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), are also declining. Bonytail are now considered extirpated from the upper basin, with fewer than five captured in the last 10 years. The paper uses this as a warning that the "hands off" recovery approach is failing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Razorback suckers spawn successfully in reservoirs and other lentic waters, and eggs incubate and hatch. However, young fish survive only where nonnative predators are absent or rare (Minckley et al. 1991). Predation on larval razorback suckers, though not quantified, is considered the single most important factor limiting recruitment in Lake Mohave (Minckley 1983; Marsh and Langhorst 1988). Over 40 nonnative fish species have been introduced into the Colorado River basin. Key predators in Lake Mohave include [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*). Predation by ictalurid catfishes has been shown to deter re-establishment of introduced razorback suckers (Marsh and Brooks 1989).

The fish evolved in a river system with spring flood pulses. Before channelization and reservoir construction, larvae dispersed into large, productive floodplain nursery areas, where a small predator community was held in check by seasonal low flows. Reservoirs have since flooded some nursery areas, and razorback suckers colonized the new reservoirs before nonnative predators became established. The authors conclude that introduced sport fishes were probably the decisive factor in the species' decline. Habitat restoration and nonnative fish removal from the wider basin were judged unlikely, so the program focused on reservoir-based rearing.

Lake Mohave has a 5 m vertical fluctuation zone, and its surface area is about 11,400 ha at the reservoir's operating elevation. Its shoreline contains naturally occurring backwaters that become isolated seasonally as the reservoir falls. Wave-driven gravel berms close these backwaters, but water still moves through them, which allows their use as rearing areas.

## Key Findings

**Program design**
- The NFWG has no dedicated budget. Work is shared among the Arizona Game and Fish Department, Arizona State University, Bureau of Reclamation, USFWS, National Biological Service, National Park Service, and Nevada Division of Wildlife.
- The group meets every four months and works from a flexible research and management plan first drafted in 1990.
- The strategy is to use the reproductive potential of thousands of reservoir spawners, rather than mass-producing fish from a small captive broodstock.
- Rearing stocks were chosen to be genetically representative of the reservoir population. Stocking fish at 25 cm TL was intended to let them escape most predators.

**Rearing areas**
- Nine small ephemeral backwaters (0.1-0.8 ha) were developed for seasonal rearing from 1990 onward. They are flooded from spring through late summer and drained in autumn, which prevents resident fish from establishing and aids recovery of reared fish.
- [[Davis Cove]] (1.2 ha, maximum depth 4 m at minimum pool) was closed in 1991 with a 10 mm bar-mesh net and flotation boom, later replaced by an earthen berm in fall 1994. It was not poisoned, to test whether razorback suckers can survive where nonnative fish are reduced but not eliminated.
- Other options included [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], which was being modified to rear 5-15 cm fish, and municipal ponds in Boulder City, Nevada, where a Memorandum of Agreement was signed.
- [[Yuma Cove]] was the site of several propagation trials.

**Propagation methods tested (1991-1994)**
1. Stocking reservoir adults into backwaters before spawning, at a ratio of one female to two males. This was successful in only one of two years.
2. Collecting spawners, stripping gametes, and returning fish to the reservoir. About 250,000 eggs from 24 females and about 60 males were collected, and approximately 200,000 were dispersed in Yuma Cove. The reservoir dropped about 1 m shortly afterward, undermining the trial.
3. Capturing naturally spawned larvae at night with [[Larval Light Trapping]] (hand-held spotlights, dip nets, and light traps). Larvae are phototactic.
4. Using hatchery-reared fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], which maintains a Lake Mohave broodstock. The hatchery supplied 10,000 fish for Davis Cove in June 1992.

**Results: production and survival**
- In January 1991, 100 adults (33 females, 67 males) were stocked into Yuma Cove. No young survived, for reasons unknown.
- In January 1992, 88 adults (28 females, 60 males) produced 296 young. Juveniles averaged 35.4 cm TL in November 1992.
- Genetic analysis (mitochondrial DNA and allozymes) of 15 juveniles showed they were produced by only 5 females, and 8 came from a single female, although 28 females had been introduced. This shows low effective genetic contribution from the spawners.
- In 1993, 420 laboratory-reared fry (26 mm) were stocked into Yuma Cove, producing 386 juveniles (91.5% survival).
- In 1994, larval collection from January to March yielded over 11,000 larvae. Larvae were reared to 2 cm TL and distributed to nine backwaters at 4,000-10,000 larvae per ha.
- Six of nine backwaters produced young. The three failures were attributed to berm failure or poor water quality. Survival in backwaters ranged from 12% to 76%. Approximately 1,200 young were harvested, tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]], and released in fall 1994.

**Results: Davis Cove**
- Electrofishing and gill netting removed over 600 kg of nonnative fish, including largemouth bass, striped bass, green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*), bluegill, channel catfish, yellow bullhead (*Ameiurus natalis*), and common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*). Scuba divers removed more with spear guns and judged that few large (>30 cm) predators remained.
- On 24 June 1992, the cove was stocked with 10,000 hatchery juveniles (average 6.8 cm) and 143 cove-reared fish (>12 cm).
- Predation by sunfish and juvenile largemouth bass was observed on smaller hatchery fish. Over the following 18 months, only 2% of hatchery fish were recovered, compared with over 50% of the larger cove-reared fish.

**Recovery of released fish**
- Spring monitoring recaptured 5 of 153 cove-reared fish released in autumn 1992. All five were males, and they were recaptured as far as 34 km from their release points.
- In 1993, 487 more fish were released, bringing the total stocked into Lake Mohave to 640. Ten fish (52.9 cm TL) were recaptured during the 1994 spawning period.
- One male taken in 1993 had reached adult size (50-55 cm) in just two years.
- Survival of released fish is still being estimated. The authors note that 15 of 640 returns was higher than expected and represents the largest number of subadults collected from Lake Mohave, and possibly the Colorado River, in 20 years. A total of 2,880 fish had been released by the time of writing.

**Monitoring and genetics**
- [[Arizona State University]] has monitored the reservoir population for nearly 20 years. Spring sampling in March targets spawners in shallow water, and 1,000-2,000 adults are typically tagged each year.
- Backwaters and Davis Cove are sampled bimonthly. Piscivorous fish removed from Davis Cove during sampling are culled.
- Lake Mohave razorback sucker mitochondrial DNA diversity is high compared with upstream relict populations (Dowling and Minckley 1993). The reservoir population descends from a large, genetically diverse river population.
- The NFWG adopted natural larval collection as its preferred method, based on the recommendations of Dowling and Minckley (1993), in order: (1) collect naturally produced larvae, (2) artificially collect gametes, (3) stock backwaters with spawning adults, and (4) use hatchery-produced fish. Natural larval collection is judged to preserve more genetic diversity than the other options.

**Conclusions and commitments**
- The authors conclude that recovery is not realistic in the short term. The Lake Mohave effort is a "modest step" toward conserving an existing population rather than a full recovery.
- The NFWG commits to releasing a minimum of 10,000 young adults (25 cm TL) into Lake Mohave and monitoring their survival. This is described as the first step in a long-term management commitment.
- The authors argue that active, long-term management is needed for other relict populations. Their reasoning is that technology and resources are available to sustain endangered populations; the critical question is political will.
