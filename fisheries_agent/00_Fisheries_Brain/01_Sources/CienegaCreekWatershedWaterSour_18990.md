## Executive Summary

This 1991 BLM report inventories water sources across the Cienega Creek watershed (Santa Cruz River drainage, southeastern Arizona) to evaluate threats of non-native fish contamination to one of the last pristine populations of the federally endangered **[[Gila Topminnow]]**. Cienega Creek also supports the **[[Gila Chub]]** (federal Category 2 candidate) and **[[Longfin Dace]]**. Of 246 identified water sources in the Empire/Cienega Planning Area (plus ~60 more on adjacent private/USFS land), 86 were sampled. No non-native fish were found in Cienega Creek itself or in any springs/seeps, but two isolated private earthen ponds (Northwest Reservoir and Masek Pond) contained established populations of **[[Largemouth Bass]]**, **[[Smallmouth Bass]]**, **[[Bluegill]]**, and **[[Goldfish]]**. The study concludes that the overall risk of exotic fish spreading into Cienega Creek from within the watershed is low, due to the ephemeral nature of most stock ponds and springs, but recommends ongoing monitoring, a contingency removal plan, public education, and evaluation of several springs/ponds (including the Cinco Ponds) as potential **[[Gila Topminnow]]** reintroduction sites.

## Background & Description

- **Cienega Creek** is a perennial, spring-fed desert stream forming part of the **[[Santa Cruz River]]** drainage in Pima and Santa Cruz Counties, Arizona. It is regarded as the finest remaining natural habitat for the federally endangered **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*).
- Native fish community: **Gila Topminnow**, **[[Gila Chub]]** (*Gila intermedia*, federal Category 2 candidate), and **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*).
- Initial 1988–1990 surveys sampled 12 sites along ~10 miles of creek (headwaters to "the narrows"), recording:
  - 10,339 Gila topminnow
  - 1,588 longfin dace
  - 27 Gila chub
  - **Zero non-native fish detected**
- Primary regional driver of native fish decline: introduction of non-native fishes (e.g., **[[Western Mosquitofish]]**, *Gambusia affinis*) that predate on or outcompete natives; topminnow are typically extirpated within a few years of mosquitofish introduction elsewhere.
- Cienega Creek is notable as one of very few sites in the region **not yet contaminated** by non-native fishes.
- Water rights filings documented >130 stock ponds (earthen tanks) and ~40 springs/seeps in the watershed, representing potential (if often ephemeral) habitat for fish, native or exotic.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study Area**: Empire/Cienega Planning Area (BLM) plus adjacent private and U.S. Forest Service lands.
- **Master water source inventory**: 246 sources within the Planning Area — 128 earthen stock tanks, 32 springs/seeps, 6 ponds, 80 well sites (including cement/metal tanks and troughs); ~60 additional sources (11 wells, 33 earthen tanks, 12 springs) outside the Planning Area on private/USFS land.
- **Sampling methods**: Gill nets, seines, dip nets, minnow traps, and visual observation; measured pond dimensions (length, width, max depth), temperature, pH, conductivity; dissolved oxygen (DO) sampled at sites with reintroduction potential or existing fish. Invertebrates, amphibians, and aquatic plants noted. Interviews with local landowners supplemented field surveys.
- **Prioritization scheme**: Sites ranked high/medium-high/medium/low-medium/low/non-priority based on location, water permanence, distance to Cienega Creek, flood-overflow potential, and size.
- **86 waters sampled** (57 earthen stock ponds, 18 seeps/springs [15 undeveloped, 3 developed], 3 cement tanks, 6 natural ponds). Two "tanks" on aerial photos were actually headcuts; two filed sources could not be located.
- **Water permanence**: 35 of 57 earthen tanks were dry at time of visit; most surviving tanks are seasonal, dry annually unless pumped for livestock — a pattern corroborated by local resident interviews (Sonoita area, Empire Mountains).
- **Habitat quality in dry/ephemeral tanks**: No aquatic vegetation (except occasional algae); invertebrate community dominated by rapid colonizers (backswimmers) or flighted taxa (dragonflies, mayflies); no amphibian evidence. Snails found only in **Section 6 Dirt Tank** and **Oil Well Reservoir**.
- **Cinco Ponds** (6 natural ponds adjacent to Cienega Creek): 4 of 6 supported aquatic vegetation (*Typha*, *Scirpus*, *Hydrocotyle*); 2 dry annually with no vegetation. Snails present at one perennial pond; leopard frogs historically observed at all Cinco Ponds. No fish detected. Flagged as candidate **[[Gila Topminnow]]** reintroduction site, but low dissolved oxygen levels warrant further water-quality monitoring before reintroduction.
- **Springs/seeps**: No fish found in any, including **Nogales Spring** and **Little Nogales Spring**, both previously stocked with topminnow in 1988 (stocking apparently unsuccessful or fish not detected). Most springs/seeps lack sufficient water volume to sustain fish, often reducing to moist soil in late spring/early summer.

## Key Findings

- **Exotic fish contamination risk is low** within the watershed (excluding Cienega Creek mainstem) because nearly all non-mainstem waters are non-permanent and cannot sustain fish populations.
- **No [[Western Mosquitofish]]** (*Gambusia affinis*) were found anywhere in the watershed.
- **Two private earthen ponds contained exotic fish**, both isolated from Cienega Creek:
  - **Northwest Reservoir** (~5 mi from creek, isolated, no public access): **[[Largemouth Bass]]**, **[[Bluegill]]**, **[[Goldfish]]**.
  - **Masek Pond** (~6.5 mi from creek): **[[Smallmouth Bass]]**, **[[Bluegill]]**; landowner reported possible **[[Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus* spp.), though none captured; also supported ≥2 snail species and freshwater clams.
- **Primary threat pathway** is illegal/unauthorized transfer of exotic fish by humans directly into Cienega Creek, rather than downstream flood transport — floodwater from Masek Pond has been observed to deposit fish into a roadside ditch, not into a drainage connected to the creek.
- **Non-permanence of stock ponds is ecologically beneficial**: it reduces the likelihood of unauthorized fish stocking, indirectly protecting the native fish community.
- **Management recommendations**:
  - Maintain non-permanent status of livestock stock ponds; use small drinking troughs (not large earthen tanks) for any permanent livestock or wildlife water development to discourage illicit stocking.
  - Develop small ground-level troughs/guzzlers (not large open tanks) for wildlife water to minimize both fish-stocking risk and evaporative loss.
  - Public recreation growth is identified as the greatest future risk factor for exotic introduction — BLM should implement public education on native fish conservation.
  - Establish an **annual monitoring plan** for Cienega Creek fish populations and a **contingency/removal plan** (with environmental assessment) for potential future exotic fish contamination.
  - Complete watershed inventory over the next two years, focusing on unsampled private-land sites; resampling of confirmed non-permanent waters is not considered necessary unless conditions change.
- **Candidate Gila Topminnow reintroduction sites** requiring dry-season follow-up assessment of water availability, quality, and habitat suitability: **Apache Spring**, **Empire Spring**, **Smitty Spring**, **Rockhouse Spring/Well**, **Chimenea Well/Seep**, **Cinco Ponds 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7**, **Mattie Canyon Seeps**, and **Mattie Horizontal Well**.
- Prior **1988 topminnow stocking** at **Nogales Spring** and **Little Nogales Spring** did not yield detectable populations during this survey, suggesting insufficient habitat volume/permanence at those sites.
