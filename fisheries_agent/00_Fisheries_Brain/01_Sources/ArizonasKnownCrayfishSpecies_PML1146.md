# Arizona Crayfish Species Survey and Introduction Risk

#carcinology #freshwater-biology #cambaridae #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This document is an informal survey of crayfish species known in [[Arizona]], separated into species collected from wild populations and species found in pet stores and private aquaria. The author stresses that collection is ongoing and that not every region of the state has been surveyed. Two wild species have been collected: [[Procambarus Clarkii]] and [[Orconectes Virilis]]. Two pet-trade animals were also documented: [[Procambarus Alleni]] and a crayfish sold as [[Procambarus Paeninsularus]] that was misidentified. The author concludes that Arizona has no native crayfish, that the two wild species have established permanent populations, and that further introductions from the aquarium trade and research on ecosystem impacts are needed.

## Background & Description

**Scope and method.** The author divides Arizona's known crayfish species into two categories: those in wild populations, and those sold in pet stores and therefore present in private aquariums. Collection efforts are incomplete, which is why the word "known" is used.

**Wild species.**
- [[Procambarus Clarkii]] (red swamp crayfish) has a native range from northeastern Mexico and south-central USA west to Texas, east to Alabama, and north to Tennessee and Illinois ([[Holdich and Lowery 1988]]). It is among the most widely distributed crayfish in the United States and has been introduced into fourteen countries. It is eaten as food in the southern US, but it is treated as a pest in places such as Japan, where it damages rice seedlings, taro roots, and fishing nets. Its burrowing can damage earthen dikes and water control structures. Its habitat includes lentic and lotic waters with fine silt to gravel substrates.
- [[Orconectes Virilis]] (northern crayfish) is described as the most widely distributed Canadian crayfish species. Its native range runs from Saskatchewan to Ontario, east to Maine, and from Montana to Utah, east to Arkansas. It has been introduced into several states from the east coast to the west coast. It is smaller than *P. clarkii* and has been popular as a [[Bait Fish]], which the author suggests may explain its expanded range. It occupies lentic to lotic waters with substrates from silt to cobble.

**Pet-trade species.**
- [[Procambarus Alleni]] was bought at Pets Inc., a small store in Tempe at the northwest corner of Mill and Southern, where it is sold as forage for oscars and other large fish. Small white translucent egg-shaped sacs were attached to the thorax of the animals: some had about 20 to 30, others 5 to 6. The purchased specimen had only a few sacs and ruptured during preservation. The sacs remain unidentified. Staff could not give the genus, species, or common name. The species' entire range is within [[Florida]], where its numbers are reported as stable. It lives in both lentic and lotic habitats and prefers to burrow.
- A crayfish advertised as *Procambarus paeninsularus* at Petco, at the southeast corner of McClintock and Warner Roads in Tempe, was sold as forage and as an aquarium novelty. On identification it was [[Procambarus Clarkii]]. The author notes this mislabeling may mean the store or its distributor does not know what species it handles. The author also cautions that *P. paeninsularus* should not be assumed absent from the trade. Its native range is Alabama, Florida, and Georgia, with no known introductions elsewhere.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeography.** Arizona has no native crayfish species, which the author describes as unusual for the contiguous United States and most of North America. Among states that border Arizona, 13 crayfish species and 3 subspecies are listed, but only *P. clarkii* and *O. virilis* have been found to border Arizona. Mexico has 44 crayfish species within its boundaries, but none of them border the state. The subspecies count for Mexico is partly illegible in the scanned text.

**Introduction pathways.** Aquarium releases are the main concern for further introductions. The author notes that the only crayfish prohibited by Arizona law are the Australian crayfishes of the family [[Parastacidae]]. Other US (domestic) crayfish are therefore not restricted, and the author considers the door "seemingly wide open" for their introduction. Possible parasites carried with aquarium animals are also flagged.

**Impacts.**
- *P. clarkii* is known to damage ecosystems, including earthen water control structures and commercial crops ([[Holdich and Lowery 1988]]).
- *O. virilis* has been implicated in declines of [[Lake Trout]] in the [[Great Lakes]] (Savino and Mller 1991, who studied crayfish feeding on young lake trout and the effect of rock size).
- *O. virilis* appears to have caused the trout fishery at Utah's [[New Castle Reservoir]] to crash (Hepworth and Duffield).

The author argues that two species with this damage potential justify further study, particularly given the fragility of [[Native Fish Populations]] in Arizona.

## Key Findings

1. **Wild crayfish in Arizona:** Two species have been collected from wild populations: *Procambarus clarkii* and *Orconectes virilis*. Both are considered to have taken up permanent residence.
2. **No native crayfish:** Arizona has no native crayfish species of its own.
3. **Pet-trade risk:** *Procambarus alleni* is sold as fish forage in a Tempe store. Its egg-bearing individuals are unidentified, and staff lacked species information.
4. **Misidentification:** A crayfish sold as *P. paeninsularus* at a Tempe Petco was identified as *P. clarkii*, showing that retailers and distributors may not know what they sell.
5. **Legal gap:** Only Australian Parastacidae are prohibited in Arizona. Domestic US crayfish introductions are not restricted.
6. **Ecological risk:** *P. clarkii* damages water infrastructure and crops. *O. virilis* has been linked to trout declines in the Great Lakes and at New Castle Reservoir.
7. **Research recommendation:** Future studies should examine the effects of *P. clarkii* and *O. virilis* on Arizona's aquatic ecosystems, native plants, and native animals.
