# Predation by Odonate Nymphs on Larval Razorback Suckers (*Xyrauchen texanus*) Under Laboratory Conditions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study tests whether [[Odonata|odonate]] nymphs can limit recruitment of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] in the [[Lake Mohave]] backwaters. Damselfly (*Enallagma* sp.) and dragonfly (*Tramea* sp.) nymphs, the two most common odonates in the study area, significantly reduced survival of larval razorback suckers in aquaria over 7 to 8 days. The authors argue that odonate predation is a plausible contributor to the recruitment failure that has persisted in the Lake Mohave population for nearly four decades, and that it has been largely overlooked in favor of fish predators.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** The razorback sucker is an endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] endemic. Lake Mohave holds its largest remaining population, which is composed mainly of old adults. Spawning is successful each year and larvae are abundant, but juvenile recruitment has not been detected in almost four decades.
- **Augmentation history:** Adults were moved from Lake Mohave to an adjacent fishless backwater, where they spawned naturally. Larvae grew to sub-adult size in 1985, but later propagation attempts suffered high mortality in fish under 20 mm long. Odonate nymphs were judged abundant enough in 1992 to pose an apparent threat (unpublished light-trap collections).
- **Predator groups:** Odonate nymphs are known voracious predators of invertebrates and sometimes consume small fish. Most predation research on fish larvae has focused on piscine predators.
- **Predators tested:**
  - *Enallagma* sp. (damselfly, Coenagrionidae): head width 3.5 to 3.6 mm; body length 14.2 to 14.4 mm.
  - *Tramea* sp. (dragonfly, Libellulidae): head width 5.8 to 6.8 mm; body length 15.2 to 19.4 mm. This was the most common nymph in Lake Mohave backwaters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Experimental conditions:** Aquaria were continuously aerated, with a 12L:12D photoperiod. Water was unfiltered Lake Mohave water, at about 19 °C in Experiment 1 and 23 °C in Experiment 2. Larvae were fed brine shrimp (*Artemia*) nauplii, and later mixed zooplankton filtered (80 µm) from Lake Mohave.
- **Vegetation effects:** Dense growths of [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) and spiny naiad (*Najas marina*) provide perches for ambush predators, which may increase predation by giving nymphs access to more of the water column.
- **Historical context:** Before dam construction, seasonal flooding and drying limited both macrophyte growth and predatory insect populations. Isolated backwaters now retain water year-round, which favors large odonate populations. Lacking fish that feed on odonates, these backwaters allow nymphs to build up, while insectivorous fish keep nymph numbers low within the main lake.
- **Other predators:** Most aquatic insect taxa are native or widespread and are not considered a novel threat. The authors did not quantify how much predation comes from odonates versus other predaceous insects in the backwaters.

## Key Findings

### Experiment 1: 12-day-old larvae (initial TL 11.8 ± 0.7 mm; 100 per aquarium; 7-day exposure)

- Design: 2 aquaria with damselfly nymphs, 2 with dragonfly nymphs, and 2 fish-only controls, with 20 nymphs per predator aquarium.
- Larval survival differed significantly among treatments (F = 39.4, df = 2,3; P = 0.0075). Controls had greater survival than both dragonfly and damselfly treatments (Tukey tests, P = 0.01 and P = 0.009).
- Dragonfly and damselfly treatments did not differ significantly (P > 0.5).
- Reported consumption: damselflies consumed about 87% and dragonflies about 76% of larvae, compared with about 12% mortality in controls.

### Experiment 2: 35-day-old larvae (estimated 14 to 15 mm TL, range 11 to 17 mm; 75 per aquarium; 8-day exposure)

- Design: one aquarium each for damselfly and dragonfly predators and one control, with no replication. Water was reduced to 26 L to keep fish density at 2.9 fish/L.
- Because the two predator treatments did not differ in Experiment 1, they were pooled and compared with controls using a two-sample t-test. Survival was significantly higher in controls (82%) than in pooled odonate treatments (47%) (t = 5.43, df = 2; P < 0.05).
- Larger larvae were less susceptible than small larvae. Larvae of 14 to 15 mm TL showed 53% mortality with predators, versus 18% in controls.

### Survival and size

- Survival of small larvae was 24% with dragonflies and 19% with damselflies, compared with 88% in controls.
- Final mean TL did not differ among aquaria within either experiment (Experiment 1: F = 1.01; Experiment 2: F = 0.84; P > 0.5). Surviving fish averaged about 13 mm TL in Experiment 1 and 17 to 18 mm TL in Experiment 2.
- Odonate losses occurred through unknown causes, possibly cannibalism, but most were likely emergence, supported by shed exuviae and winged adults.

### Behavioral observations

- Newly hatched larvae cannot swim for sustained periods, lack a functional gas bladder, and spend most of their time on the bottom, where they are most vulnerable.
- Larger larvae stayed in the water column and reacted faster to nymphs, often escaping.
- Nymphs were mostly confined to aquarium bottoms and sides, so the lab setup may not reflect predation in vegetated field conditions.

### Management implications

- The authors propose several changes to reduce predation:
  - Selecting new backwaters that dry completely each season, which kills macrophytes and prevents overwintering of nymphs.
  - Stocking larvae after nymphs have had time to appear, so that fish are too large to be taken before odonates mature.
  - Rearing larvae to 20 mm TL before release. In 1993, these fish showed about 25% survival, compared with 1% or less for eggs and newly hatched larvae (unpublished observation).
- Fish stocked at 30 mm TL showed more than 90% survival in one backwater.

### Conclusions

- Odonate nymphs can destroy substantial numbers of larval razorback suckers under experimental conditions, and smaller larvae are the most vulnerable.
- Predation by odonates is a plausible factor in the lack of juvenile recruitment in Lake Mohave. Its relative importance compared with other predators in the field remains unquantified.
