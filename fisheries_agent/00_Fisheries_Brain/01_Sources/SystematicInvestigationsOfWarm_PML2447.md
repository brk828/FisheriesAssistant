# Systematic Investigations of Warm-Water Fish Assemblages in the San Pedro and Babocomari Rivers, Arizona, 1990–2003

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report by [[Jerome A. Stefferud]] and [[Sally E. Stefferud]] summarizes 14 years (1990–2003) of annual spring fish monitoring in the [[San Pedro River]] and [[Babocomari River]] within the [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] (SPRNCA) in southeastern Arizona. The work was performed under a volunteer agreement with the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM), Sierra Vista office, and was submitted on May 26, 2003. Six permanent sampling sites were monitored, five on the San Pedro and one on the Babocomari. The monitoring tracked fish assemblage composition and abundance (as catch per unit effort) in relation to drought, flow loss, riparian change and [[Livestock Grazing]] removal. The dominant native species was the [[Longfin Dace]], and nonnative species were common at several sites. The authors conclude that flow regime and drought exert a stronger influence on fish assemblages than the habitat changes associated with cattle removal.

## Background & Description

The SPRNCA was designated by Congress to protect the primary riparian ecosystem of the San Pedro River, which supports a high diversity of wildlife. Management by the BLM had included reduction of [[Livestock Grazing]] and vehicle use, and the monitoring program was designed to document whether these actions affected fish assemblages. The letter accompanying the report (addressed to BLM biologist Bill Childress) was copied to BLM state and field offices, the Bureau of Reclamation, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the Arizona Game and Fish Department and Arizona State University.

**Methods.** Sampling followed established protocols (Stefferud and Stefferud 1989, 1990). Fish were captured by [[Electrofishing]], identified to species, counted and measured for length. Habitat and substrate were recorded, and each year a channel map was sketched, habitat types noted and photo points established and rephotographed. Abundance was expressed as [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (CPUE): number of individuals captured per 15 minutes of shocking time. Discharge was taken from the [[USGS]] continuous-recording gage at Charleston. Rigorous statistical analysis was not performed; the authors state that more in-depth analysis was beyond the scope of the data. Summary statistics given in the report include means and coefficients of variation (CV).

**Site descriptions.**
- [[Hereford Site]]: southernmost site, about 1,000 feet south of the Hereford bridge; wetted width about 19 (units as in source) and sand/fine gravel substrate; fringed by Goodding willow and Fremont cottonwood.
- [[Highway 90 Site]]: about 0.9 miles north of the Highway 90 crossing, about 800 feet long; mix of riffles, runs, glides and deep pools; wetted width about 20.
- [[Charleston Site]]: a permanent site on the San Pedro near the Charleston gage, about 1,000 feet long; the greatest diversity of native fish was recorded here.
- [[Fairbank Site]]: immediately south of the Babocomari mouth, extending upstream to Walnut Gulch; about 1,050 feet long; dried annually in summer.
- [[Babocomari Site]]: established on the Babocomari about 300 (units illegible in scan) upstream of its confluence with the San Pedro; dried regularly in summer.
- Highway 90 gill net site: an additional site referenced in the table of contents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The San Pedro is a desert river whose flow is strongly affected by groundwater pumping, rural and urban development and drought. Surface flow at the monitored sites varied from perennial to completely dry in summer and, in drought years, in winter as well. The report describes these fluctuations as the main driver of assemblage structure. Species that recolonize dry reaches quickly, such as longfin dace, were favored, while larger-bodied nonnative fishes (e.g., green sunfish, largemouth bass) were reduced during drought and recovered more slowly.

Riparian conditions also changed. A 1998 flood removed deep-water habitat and killed many cottonwood and willow trees at the Fairbank site. Beaver were reintroduced to the San Pedro, a colony established near the Highway 90 site, and a small dam formed above the mouth of Picnic Wash in 2001. Recreational use by bird watchers, vehicles and immigrant traffic affected the Hereford and Highway 90 sites, and cattle sign was first noted at Charleston in 2003.

Previously recorded species that were not confirmed in this study include channel catfish and several other species reported in earlier surveys (unpublished data by the authors). The authors note these might occur in the upper San Pedro but that the likelihood is low.

## Key Findings

- **Monitoring scope:** Six permanent sites were sampled annually over 14 years (1990–2003). Five sites were on the San Pedro River and one on the Babocomari River.
- **Dominant native species:** Longfin dace was the most abundant native fish at most sites. At Fairbank it comprised 87% of the total catch and was present in every year of the study.
- **Desert sucker:** Present in 8 of 14 years at Fairbank, comprising 5% of the catch. Abundance was generally low and irregular. The authors reported a downward trend from the mid-1990s, followed by an upward trend after 2002, and attributed the variation to environmental conditions.
- **Nonnative species:** Western mosquitofish, green sunfish, fathead minnow, black bullhead, largemouth bass and common carp were recorded. At Fairbank, mosquitofish comprised about 4%, fathead minnow about 2%, and black bullhead under 1% of the catch.
- **Hereford:** Native fish relative abundance varied widely between years (mean 77.4%, CV 0.31, as reported). Mosquitofish, green sunfish and fathead minnow were the main nonnative species.
- **Highway 90:** Mean total CPUE was reported in the text; longfin dace, desert sucker and black bullhead were the most consistent species, while green sunfish, largemouth bass and fathead minnow were relatively scarce (CPUE about 3.7, 3.0 and 0.2, respectively, for the species given).
- **Charleston:** Showed the greatest diversity of native species of the five San Pedro sites; relative native abundance was generally high, though it dropped in some years.
- **Babocomari:** Longfin dace predominated, and the mean total CPUE across 1990–2003 was reported as around 15.8 (CV 4.25; value partly illegible). Desert sucker was found in the Babocomari only occasionally. The authors concluded that the Babocomari site was highly unlikely to support a resident population because it dried annually; fish there colonized from the San Pedro.
- **Drought effects:** Drought reduced surface water to intermittent or no flow, reducing habitat and favoring fast-recolonizing species. Nonnative fishes were reduced in dry periods and recovered after flows returned.
- **Flood effects:** A 1998 flood at Fairbank reshaped the channel and killed riparian trees, changing habitat for fish.
- **Cattle removal:** Removal of livestock grazing at Babocomari improved riparian vegetation, with denser cover and more organic litter. However, these changes did not produce measurable increases in water retention or surface flow, which the authors attributed to drought and upstream groundwater pumping.
- **Management implication:** The authors concluded that flow regime, rather than riparian habitat restoration alone, controls fish assemblage structure in these reaches, and that long-term monitoring is needed to separate the effects of drought, flood and management.
- **Program status:** The volunteer monitoring program was to continue under agreement with the BLM.
