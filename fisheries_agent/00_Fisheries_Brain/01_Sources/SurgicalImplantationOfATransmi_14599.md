# Surgical Implantation of a Transmitter Package for Radio-Tracking Endangered Hellbenders

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cryptobranchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 *Wildlife Society Bulletin* article by Stouffer, Gates, Hocutt and Stauffer of the [[Appalachian Environmental Laboratory]] (University of Maryland) evaluates whether surgically implanted radio transmitters can track individual [[Hellbender]]s (*Cryptobranchus alleganiensis*) in stream habitat. The species is listed as endangered in Maryland and is declining across its range. Existing marking methods (toe-clipping, ear tags, Floy T-tags) require searchers to disturb rocks and habitat, and marked animals are often never relocated. Five adult hellbenders were captured in French Creek, Pennsylvania, held in a stream pen, surgically fitted with transmitters, and released into Bear Creek. The authors report that the method successfully tracked animals for an average of 2.8 months and could support studies of daily and seasonal movement and release programs.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Hellbender (*Cryptobranchus alleganiensis*), a large aquatic salamander that hides under large, flat rocks during daylight, which makes repeated relocation difficult.
- **Conservation status:** Endangered in Maryland; declining throughout its range (Williams et al. 1981a).
- **Previous marking methods:** [[Toe-Clipping]], mammalian ear tags, and Floy T-tags. Each requires locating animals, which is inefficient and alters habitat. Snorkeling and electroshocking are less destructive but require searching large areas.
- **Rationale:** Surgical implantation was chosen to minimize behavioral interference and the risk of skin infections associated with external attachments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Captive holding:** A covered 1 m x 2 m x 1 m pen of galvanized wire, lined with woven polyethylene mat and weighted with flat rocks, was placed in 25-50 cm of water at the [[Maryland Department of Natural Resources]] Fish Rearing Station on Bear Creek, Garrett County. The site had cool, well-oxygenated water.
- **Capture:** Five hellbenders captured on 1 July 1981 in French Creek, Venango Township, Crawford County, Pennsylvania, using electroshocking and turning rocks, with seines and dip nets. Lengths were 450-560 mm total length; weights 445-810 g; all considered adults.
- **Transport:** 6 hours in 75.7-liter trash cans aerated by aquarium pumps and cooled with ice.
- **Acclimation and diet:** 13-28 days in the pen. Diet was [[Crayfish]] (*Orconectes* spp.), [[Mottled Sculpin]] (*Cottus bairdi*), and [[Blacknose Dace]] (*Rhinichthys atratulus*), offered on alternate days. No change in physical appearance was noted.

## Key Findings

**Surgical method**
- Anesthesia by immersion in MS-222 (tricaine methanesulfonate) at 1:3 (g/l) and 15 °C. Mean induction time was 23 min (±3.4 SE, n = 4).
- A 2-8 cm incision was made perpendicular to the ventral midline, about 3 cm from the midline and about 5 cm anterior to the left hind leg, a site chosen to reduce suture stress.
- The transmitter was a sealed cylindrical unit, 4.8 cm long x 1.6 cm diameter, weighing 11.6 g, with a loop antenna occupying about one-quarter of its length. Frequencies were 150-151 MHz, with an estimated life of 5-6 months. It was placed in the pleuroperitoneal cavity, aligned parallel to the intestine and gonads.
- Closure used absorbable gut suture with 5-6 stitches. Operations took 15-20 min. Mean recovery time was 25 min (±2.0 SE, n = 4).

**Post-operative and release**
- After 19-23 days of observation with no complications, animals were released in Bear Creek, 9.3 km downstream of the Fish Rearing Station, on 2 August (2 individuals) and 21 August 1981 (3 individuals).
- The authors note that a nonabsorbable suture might have allowed immediate release, since the absorbable suture risked incision opening.

**Tracking performance**
- Animals were located with a handheld 4-element Yagi antenna, headphones and a 20-channel receiver, then pinpointed with a handheld bi-directional loop antenna at about 15-20 m.
- Monitoring lasted an average of 2.8 months (±0.52, n = 5; range 1.4-4.0 months). Animals were tracked about three times per week, and visual checks confirmed survival.
- Most transmitter failures were attributed to moisture seepage through the wax and dental acrylic packaging.
- Streamside vegetation and road distance (up to 100 m) attenuated the signal, while roadside elevation of 10-15 m improved reception.

**Conclusions**
- The technique successfully monitored daily and seasonal movements of individual hellbenders and could evaluate the success of re-establishment release programs in suitable stream habitat.
- Moisture-proofing of the transmitter package is the main improvement needed.
