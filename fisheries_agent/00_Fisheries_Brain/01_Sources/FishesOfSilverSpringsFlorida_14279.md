# Fishes of Silver Springs, Florida

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae #Cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1943 paper by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[E. Ross Allen]], published in the *Proceedings of the Florida Academy of Sciences* (Vol. 6, pp. 110–130), provides the first authoritative inventory of the fishes of [[Silver Springs]], a large, clear-water spring system in north-central Florida. The authors document a fauna of roughly 40 species or subspecies, provide taxonomic revisions for several groups, describe fish behavior observed by snorkel and glass-bottom viewing, and argue that a changed environment, not predation, parasites, or overfishing, was the main cause of a decline in fish numbers.

## Background & Description

Silver Springs lies about five miles east-northeast of Ocala, Marion County. Its main flow emerges at about 72° F from a funnel-shaped depression more than 35 feet deep, with a combined discharge estimated at 22 million gallons per hour. The overflow, Silver Springs Run, meanders seven miles before entering the Oklawaha River, a tributary of the St. Johns River.

Water chemistry (parts per million) included silica 32, iron 0.06, calcium 70, magnesium 11, sodium and potassium 4.4, bicarbonate 205, sulphate 43, chloride 7.6, nitrate 0.86, and total hardness as calcium carbonate 220. The spring pond covers about 2 acres, with current ranging from none to moderate (about 4 miles per hour at the source). Coarse-leaved *Sagittaria* dominates the aquatic vegetation, and the bottom is marl and cavernous limestone.

Data were gathered during collecting trips from 1935 to 1943, including an initial inventory with [[Samuel F. Hildebrand]] in August 1935 and a joint observation session with the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists on April 5, 1941. Collecting used a face mask and a rubber-slingshot spear, which the authors state kills fish at about twenty feet. Collections are held at the [[United States National Museum]], the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]], the University of Florida, and the [[Florida Reptile Institute]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat change and fish decline.** In 1929 the pond was largely surrounded by productive marsh. Since then much of this marsh has been filled, the spring edges cleared, and a stone wall built on the northeast side. The authors consider these changes the likely controlling factor in declining fish numbers, and speedboat waves may have destroyed nests and frightened off guarding males.

**Factors ruled out.** No fishing is allowed in the spring or outlet, fish appeared healthy and largely free of parasites, and few mass mortalities were seen (except in threadfin shad). Predators such as fish-eating birds, raccoons, and softshell turtles were uncommon or did not feed on fish in the springs. The giant freshwater shrimp *Macrobrachium jamaicense*, once common and a nocturnal predator, had not been seen for about seven years.

**Species declines.** *Mollienisia latipinna*, *Micropterus salmoides*, *Esox niger*, *Amia calva*, and *Elassoma evergladei* had become scarce. Sunfishes, fed daily with bread as a tourist attraction, were thriving.

**Marine-derived and transient visitors.** Several fishes that normally live in marine or estuarine waters, such as the [[Southern Hogchoker]], the [[Striped Mullet]], and the [[Atlantic Needlefish]], were recorded in fresh water. The paper also notes that threadfin shad arrived in large schools from the St. Johns River in some years, including 1933 and 1941.

## Key Findings

- **Inventory:** The report documents about 40 species and subspecies across about 18 families. Taxa are summarized here rather than listed in full.
- **Gar:** The common Florida gar is identified as *Lepisosteus platyrhincus*, distinct from *L. platostomus*. The longnose gar (*L. osseus osseus*) grows to about four feet and is less common than in central Florida streams.
- **Threadfin shad:** [[Signalosa petenensis vanhyningi]] appeared in dense schools in 1941, died in numbers, and then left the springs. The authors note a possible cyclic pattern and remark on schooling behavior, citing [[Albert Eide Parr]].
- **Catfish:** The authors treat *Ictalurus punctatus* and *I. lacustris* as likely the same species, with sexual dimorphism in adult males. They also recognize *I. catus* as distinct, with large "Catfish Hotel" aggregations below the head spring.
- **Mudfish and eels:** Bowfin (*Amia calva*) were uncommon, with a record weight of 10.5 pounds. American eels (*Anguilla bostoniensis*) were nocturnal and fed in shallow mud.
- **Sunfishes:** The bluegill is identified as *Lepomis macrochirus purpurescens*, the dominant sunfish. The eastern shellcracker, the largest sunfish in Florida, guards nests in *Sagittaria*. The stumpknocker (*Lepomis punctatus*) spawns in groups in June 1943.
- **Largemouth bass:** Common at various depths, with the largest estimated at 12 pounds or more. Bass followed the authors to catch shrimp dislodged from *Ludwigia*.
- **Smallmouth bass:** Possible but unconfirmed. Smallmouths were planted in Lake Weir, which connects by canal to the Oklawaha River.
- **Taxonomic changes:** *Fundulus dispar lineolatus* replaces the name "nottii" for the southeastern form. *Trinectes maculatus fasciatus* is applied to the southern hogchoker. *Pomoxis nigromaculatus* is validated over *P. sparoides*.
- **Notable records:** A bulldog pickerel (*Esox americanus*) was not seen in the spring pond but occurs in outlet shallows. A Crawlabottom darter (*Hadropterus nigrofasciatus*) was found in swift springs currents. The Everglades pygmy sunfish (*Elassoma evergladei*) is one of the smallest spiny-rayed vertebrates.
- **Conclusion:** Environmental alteration, rather than predators, parasites, or overfishing, is the most likely cause of fish decline in Silver Springs.
