# Movements of Marked Walleyes in the Red Lakes, Minnesota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 study reports a three-year jaw-tag mark-recapture program on [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) in the two connected [[Red Lakes]] of northern [[Minnesota]]. The lakes support the [[Red Lake Fishery]], a commercial operation that is a major economic resource for the [[Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians]]. The authors tagged 9,739 walleyes at spawning runs in 1949, 1950 and 1951 and recovered 1,572 tags (16.1%). Of these recoveries, 314 fish (20.6%) had moved between the lakes. The study concludes that the two lakes hold a single, homogeneous population that should be managed as one stock. It also finds that mature males are fully vulnerable to the gill nets, while most females are caught for one year before they mature.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** The [[Red Lakes]] comprise about 275,000 acres, mostly within the [[Red Lake Indian Reservation]]. They are two roughly oval, shallow basins joined by a channel about three-quarters of a mile wide. The upper lake has a maximum depth of 20 feet and a mean of about 8 feet. The lower lake has a maximum of 35 feet and a mean of 18 feet. Waters drain to the [[Red River of the North]] through the Red Lake River, and a dam at the outlet maintains lake level.

**Fishery.** The fishery has operated continuously since 1917 and averaged 1,095,000 pounds of marketable fish per year over 1932–1951. Harvest is controlled by an annual quota on walleyes and [[Northern Pike]]. Fish are marketed through the [[Red Lakes Fishery Association]], a cooperative operating under rules set by the U.S. Department of the Interior and approved by the [[Minnesota Department of Conservation]]. Between 180 and 250 fishers work from outboard skiffs using gill nets of 3½-inch mesh. Fishing is confined to summer and autumn, and the commercial season begins only 6 to 8 weeks after spawning.

**Research motivation.** The studies sought information to guide annual quotas, mesh size, and season timing. The central question was whether the walleye stocks in the two lakes are distinct or homogeneous. The work was run under a cooperative agreement between the [[University of Minnesota]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], with support from the [[U.S. Bureau of Indian Affairs]]. Secondary species studied were [[Yellow Perch]], [[Lake Whitefish]] and [[Goldeye]].

**Marking method.** Fish were tagged with No. 2 monel-metal strap tags on the left mandible, with serial numbers and return instructions. Tagging took place at spawning-run sites:

| Year | Site | Lake | Males | Females | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1949 | [[Blackduck River]] | Lower | 3,374 | 1,298 | 4,697 |
| 1950 | [[Tamarack River]] | Upper | 1,839 | 145 | 1,995 |
| 1951 | Tamarack River | Upper | 2,525 | 519 | 3,047 |

Tagged fish were handled to minimize air exposure and released immediately. Most females were stripped of eggs and milt before release. The 1950 tagging was affected by high water and flooded backwaters that increased post-release losses.

**Recovery method.** Because funds could not pay a reward for each tag, recovery depended on voluntary cooperation. Publicity included posters, instructions to game wardens and fishery staff, and direct letters to commercial fishers. Tags were returned through truck drivers who collected fish at fishing camps, or directly to the fishery building. Anglers mailed tags to the university or gave them to game wardens. Commercial returns supplied only date and location for most fish, so complete biological data came mainly from specimens examined by the investigators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** The lakes are moderately alkaline, with total alkalinity of 120–135 ppm. They have no thermocline. Strong winds mix the water so that temperature follows mean air temperature closely. Summer temperatures in the lower lake ranged from 62°F to 72°F at all depths.
- **Substrate and watershed:** The shores and much of the bottom are sand. Surrounding soils are bog, sand and glacial till. Inflow comes from seepage and streams draining the watershed.
- **Spawning migration:** Walleyes ascend tributary streams such as the Blackduck and Tamarack rivers to spawn in spring. Flooding and late-season runs increased hazards for fish that continued upstream after release.
- **Fishing pressure:** Pressure is heavier in the lower lake. In 1951 total commercial effort on both lakes was about 75% of the 1950 level, and effort in the upper lake was very light.
- **Comparative literature:** The authors cite prior walleye tagging work in Michigan, Wisconsin, Iowa, Tennessee, and Lake Erie, which shows that walleyes can move considerable distances and disperse rapidly after spawning.

## Key Findings

**1. Recovery and movement.**
- Tag returns totalled 1,572 (16.1% of 9,739 tagged). Of these, 1,352 came from commercial fishers and 180 from anglers, and 40 had incomplete data.
- Recovery declined over time. For the 1949 tagging it fell from 15.6% in year one to 3.2% in year three.
- Of recovered marked fish, 314 (20.6%) moved between the two lakes. The share of recoveries that crossed lakes was 7.5% for the 1949 marking, 52.6% for 1950, and 71.1% for 1951.
- The longest documented movement was 60 miles in 68 days, a fish taken 20 miles east of Thief River Falls in the Red Lake River. An unverified report suggested roughly twice that distance.
- The fastest verified movement was 41.5 miles in 33 days, from the Blackduck River mouth in the lower lake to the Tamarack River mouth in the upper lake.
- Marked fish were broadly distributed across the fishing area by the start of the commercial season, which ranged from June 16, 1951 to July 9, 1949.

**2. Population homogeneity.** The authors conclude that the two lakes hold one population, to be managed as a single stock. Their evidence:
- **Movement:** Substantial interchange between lakes, with 20.6% of recoveries crossing.
- **Growth:** Growth rates of 1,608 fish sampled in spring 1951 showed no significant difference between the lakes.
- **Condition:** Mean condition factor (K) was 1.59 in the lower lake and 1.57 in the upper lake, based on 248 fish per lake sampled July 13–19, 1949.
- **Catch rates:** Commercial catch rates showed no consistent difference between lakes.
- **Age structure:** Younger age groups were somewhat more abundant in the upper lake. The authors attribute this to downstream drift of older fish, though the size-selection tests did not confirm this.

**3. Vulnerability and maturity.**
- Most mature males are fully available to the 3½-inch nets. Males become available at about 14.0–14.3 inches, usually in the fifth growing season, and most mature the spring after that season.
- Females become available at a similar size but mature later, usually in the spring after the sixth growing season.
- As a result, most females are fished for one year before maturity, whereas only a small minority of males are caught before maturity. The authors suggest this partly explains the sex-ratio imbalance typical of spawning runs.
- Many large females exceed the size of maximum net availability, so older females experience lighter fishing pressure.
- The highest recapture rates fell in the 14.0–20.3 inch range. The most heavily exploited sizes were 13.6–16.7 inches, which made up about 75% of the commercial catch.

**4. Homing and spawning behavior.** Returns to the natal stream appeared more frequent than movement to other streams, but the authors could not demonstrate a true homing instinct. Fish were recovered in Mud Creek that had been tagged in the Blackduck and Tamarack rivers. The authors suggest that fish often spawn in the nearest suitable stream.

**5. Effects of tagging.** Jaw tags may inhibit growth. Among recaptured fish, many formed no annuli within two years, and maximum growth over three years was 2.4 inches. Males generally grew more than females, although the sample was small. The condition of recaptured fish did not visibly differ from untagged commercial fish, and only a few showed irritation around the mouth.

**6. Management conclusions.**
- Manage the Red Lakes walleye population as one homogeneous stock.
- Commercial returns and tag data indicate that the fishery takes most mature males and many young females before they spawn, which should be considered in quota and mesh decisions.
- Lighter fishing pressure in the upper lake may support a denser population and slower growth there, although the data did not confirm this clearly.
