# Canada's Game Fishes: A Sportsman's Guide to Angling Across the Provinces

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 booklet, issued under the authority of the Minister of Northern Affairs and National Resources and written in the voice of [[Gregory Clark]], is a tourist and angling guide to the principal game fishes of Canada. It describes the sporting value, size records, range, seasons and tackle for each major species, from [[Atlantic Salmon]] in the east to [[Tyee]] and [[Steelhead Trout]] in the west, and it covers saltwater big-game fishing for [[Bluefin Tuna]] and [[Swordfish]] off Nova Scotia. It closes with practical advice on travel, licences, equipment and the game-fish opportunities of Canada's national and provincial parks. The document is promotional and largely anecdotal, and it contains no original research data.

## Background & Description

The booklet opens with a foreword praising Canada's remote wilderness, which the author argues is protected by its rock, muskeg and mountain terrain from the economic exploitation that depleted American forests. It states that the wilderness is "full of fish, game and wild life" that can be maintained with enlightened management, and it frames conservation as a shared responsibility of government and sportsmen.

Fish and game are administered by the provinces, not the federal government. The booklet therefore directs readers to provincial departments and tourist bureaus for maps, guides, outfitters and cost estimates, and to the national Canadian Government Travel Bureau in Ottawa for referrals. Its species accounts are organised by group: Atlantic salmon, ouananiche, brook trout, lake trout, black bass, pike and muskellunge, walleye, Pacific trout and salmon, saltwater tuna and swordfish, and a final miscellany.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes the distribution and habitat preferences of each species in general terms:

- **Atlantic salmon** survives mainly in the maritime rivers. Mills, dams and nets have reduced it across its former range, including the New England rivers and the St. Lawrence to Lake Ontario, which Niagara Falls blocked.
- **Eastern brook trout** requires "cold and pure" water and ranges from the Atlantic to the Hudson Bay and Arctic. The booklet notes that it "survives marvellously" despite heavy fishing pressure and is easily propagated.
- **Smallmouth bass** prefers rock-bound lakes and rivers and does not thrive in the cold trout waters of the north. It was planted in Long Lake in 1901.
- **Largemouth bass** favours weedy, lily-pad-filled water and is widespread in the cultivated districts of Ontario.
- **Northern pike** is at its best in cold, rocky northern lakes, contrary to textbook descriptions of slow, weedy habitat.
- **Walleye** is a roving species of large lakes with tributary rivers, and it is abundant in the northern lakes of Quebec and Ontario.
- **Lake trout** is found from coast to coast. It stays in deep water in summer and comes to the shoals in spring.
- **Pacific species** include [[Kamloops Trout]], which is largely confined to the interior of British Columbia, and the coastal [[Coho Salmon]] and Tyee.

The booklet also notes the pressures on these fish. The musky has been "hunted so hard for over a century," and the brook trout has been "hammered harder than any other fish in the east." Recent introductions, including rainbow trout in eastern Canada, are described as successful.

## Key Findings

**Size records and notable catches (as stated in the source):**
- Atlantic salmon: average 12–15 lb on fly, with frequent 20-lb fish and occasional fish in the thirties. The record, 52½ lb, was taken from the Old Bridge Pool, Margaree River, Nova Scotia, on 30 August 1927.
- Ouananiche: average about 2 lb, rarely over 8 lb. Landlocked (sebago) salmon averages 10 lb and is reputed to exceed 35 lb.
- Brook trout: six-pounders are "not at all uncommon" in northern Ontario and Quebec.
- Lake trout: a 10-lb fish is described as a serious fight on light tackle in spring.
- Smallmouth bass: an officially weighed Ontario specimen reached 7¼ lb.
- Largemouth bass: fish of six pounds are not unusual.
- Muskellunge: reaches over 60 lb. The Lake of the Woods has produced record fish in the 50- and then 60-lb range.
- Northern pike: thirty-pounders are reported from north of the Height of Land. A ten-pounder is described as an event in the Great Lakes basin.
- Steelhead: a confirmed Canadian record of 23¼ lb, with reports up to 30 lb.
- Kamloops trout: reported to 40 lb.
- Tyee: up to 100 lb. The Tyee Club sets a 30-lb minimum for a "Tyee" and calls smaller fish "spring salmon."
- Cutthroat trout: record 41 lb, taken by trolling in a lake.

**Saltwater big-game fishing (Nova Scotia):**
- The 1948 season recorded 806 bluefin tuna, totalling 305,306 lb, taken on rod and reel. Catches ran from 3 July to 16 October.
- In 1950, Commander Duncan M. Hodgson landed a 977-lb tuna in St. Ann Bay, Cape Breton. This was the largest tuna taken on rod and reel.
- A foul-hooked 792-lb tuna was fought for 62 hours by six anglers in rotation.
- Swordfish reach 13 ft and more than 600 lb. About 400 schooners and power boats operate from Glace Bay, Louisburg and Ingonish. Michael Lerner landed two swordfish in one day, weighing 535 lb and 601 lb.

**Comparative judgements:**
- The author rates the steelhead and Kamloops trout as the premier freshwater fighters, with the Atlantic salmon. The Cutthroat trout is considered a worthy game fish, but it is overshadowed by the steelhead and Kamloops. The Dolly Varden is described as weak in fight.
- The smallmouth bass is considered a more vital fighter than the largemouth, while the largemouth strikes artificial lures more eagerly.
- Panfish (bream, crappie, sunfish, perch, catfish) are described as "practically an untapped resource" in Canada. The author notes that in the United States, ten people fish for panfish for every one angling for bass or trout.

**Equipment and logistics:**
- Salmon are fished with 11–13 ft fly rods, though many native sportsmen still prefer 15 ft.
- Brook trout: a 9-ft, 5-oz fly rod is recommended, with size 8–14 trout patterns.
- Steelhead reels should hold 300 ft of backing.
- Non-resident fishing licences can usually be bought at border crossings, but each province sets its own rules.
- Anglers may bring fishing gear, boats, tents and cameras into Canada duty-free if they report them on entry and re-export them within six months.
- Provincial regulations vary and no uniform national rules exist. Regulations are published annually, usually before the end of March.

**Parks and regions:**
- The booklet lists game fish in the national and provincial parks, including six trout species in Jasper and Banff, pike over 15 lb in Prince Albert National Park, and lake trout reaching 35 lb in the larger lakes of Riding Mountain.
- Yukon and Northwest Territories offer lake trout, whitefish, rainbow trout, Arctic grayling, northern pike and Arctic char. Development of the Yellowknife area is in progress.

**Conservation message:**
The concluding theme is that laws alone are not enough. The author states that "the intelligence of our game laws is vain without the intelligent cooperation of all who share the treasure."
