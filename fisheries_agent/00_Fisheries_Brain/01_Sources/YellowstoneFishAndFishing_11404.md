# Yellowstone Fishes and Fishing: Species, Ecology and Management in the World's First National Park

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 booklet by F. Phillip Sharpe, published by the [[Yellowstone Library and Museum Association]] with the [[National Park Service]], describes the fishes of [[Yellowstone National Park]], their ecological relationships, and the history of fishery management. It identifies 11 native species in the park's roughly 2.2 million acres, plus five exotic species and one hybrid, and presents the park's guiding aim: preserving native fish and aquatic life in a natural state while offering quality angling for wild fish. The central message is that the park's [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] fishery has persisted because management shifted from artificial stocking and egg taking to habitat protection, regulation and catch-and-release angling.

## Background & Description

The booklet is organized into chapters covering the fishery, history, ecology, [[Thermal Features]], parasites, native and introduced fish, fishing, and species accounts. It opens with a narrative of the 1871 [[Hayden Survey]] camped at [[Yellowstone Lake]], used to show that a natural ecosystem, including human participation, could remain intact over a century.

Key background points:

- **Designation:** Yellowstone became the world's first national park in 1872. The U.S. Army was caretaker from 1886 until the [[National Park Service]] was established in 1916.
- **Native fish:** Yellowstone Lake and River Basin, from the Upper Falls to Two Ocean Pass, is described as containing perhaps the largest native cutthroat population in the Western Hemisphere. Isolated populations were called "museum specimens in museum streams."
- **Fish composition:** Total of 16 full species (including two cutthroat forms) plus one hybrid, for 18 forms. The text also says there are 11 native species, a count the booklet does not reconcile with the five exotics.
- **Sport and forage fish:** Seven species are sport fish (cutthroat, rainbow, brown, lake and brook trout, grayling, whitefish). Nine are classed as forage fish, named for their role as food for sport fish rather than for their feeding habits.
- **Families covered:** [[Salmonidae]] (trout, chars, whitefish, grayling), [[Catostomidae]] (suckers), [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows) and [[Cottidae]] (sculpins).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food web.** The booklet depicts a three-tier food triangle. Producers (phytoplankton, nutrients from lake fallout and inlet streams) support consumers such as zooplankton, aquatic and terrestrial insects, and forage fish, which in turn support cutthroat and specialized predators. Predators include [[White Pelican]], [[Osprey]], mergansers, otters, mink, bears, parasites and humans. Changing one population indirectly affects the others. For example, removing angler predation would likely shift cutthroat numbers and size rather than total biomass.

**Barriers and isolation.** [[Yellowstone Falls]] (109 feet, 12 miles downstream of the lake) and other falls, including [[Hellroaring Falls]], [[Gibbon Falls]], [[Bechler Falls]], [[Cave Falls]], [[Osprey Falls]], [[Lewis Falls]] and [[Tower Falls]], restrict fish movement. The [[Continental Divide]] at Two Ocean Pass separates Yellowstone Lake cutthroat from [[Snake River Cutthroat Trout]] populations. The booklet says the two forms show little intermixing.

**Thermal waters.** Heated spring runoff, for example into the [[Firehole River]], [[Gibbon River]] and [[Gardner River]], affects fish distribution. Trout avoid temperatures above roughly 80 to 85°F, but the booklet notes that brown trout in the Firehole were vigorous and in good condition, and that warmer water extends the growing season for aquatic organisms. Witch Creek, at about 80°F, excludes trout in summer but supports a rich community at its mouth in Heart Lake.

**Parasites.** The tapeworm [[Dibothriocephalus Cordiceps]] is the most common internal parasite of Yellowstone Lake trout and depends on fish-eating birds for its life cycle. The external parasites [[Freshwater Leech]] (*Piscicola salmositica*) and [[Fish Louse]] (*Salmincola* sp.) are most noticeable in June. Humans are not at meaningful risk when trout are thoroughly cooked.

**Introduced species.** Stocking introduced competitors and hybridizing species. [[Brown Trout]] displaced native cutthroat in the Madison and lower Gibbon drainages, and [[Rainbow Trout]] hybridized with cutthroat wherever the two occurred together.

## Key Findings

### Management history

- **Early hatchery era:** A hatchery opened at West Thumb in 1901, and a Bureau of Fisheries hatchery followed in 1903. More than 817 million cutthroat eggs were collected from Yellowstone Lake from 1903 to 1953. In 1930 there were as many as six egg-collection stations on the lake.
- **Research findings:** From 1949 to 1961, [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] biologists found that egg taking and restocking were unnecessary and that excessive egg removal harmed natural reproduction. The hatchery was closed in 1953.
- **Single later stocking:** The only later experimental plant was 10,000 grayling fry in Ice Lake in 1961.
- **Life history of Yellowstone Lake cutthroat:**
  - First spawning at an average age of four years.
  - 48 percent of spawners died during spawning.
  - Few lived beyond six years.
  - Strong homing to natal streams.
- **Predation:** The white pelican colony on Molly Islands probably consumes 300,000 to 400,000 fish per year.
- **Survey scope:** The biological surveys covered more than 300 lakes and streams, recording fish species, abundance, water chemistry and food supply.

### Visitation and fishing pressure

- Visitors numbered 5,438 in 1895, 64,144 in 1943, and over two million by 1969.
- In 1969 there were over 370,000 man-days of fishing, and the ecosystem was described as beginning to be strained, prompting more restrictive regulations.

### Parasite and health data

- In 1964 an estimated 75 percent of Yellowstone Lake trout were infested with cordiceps.
- In the 1930s an investigator ingested 14 tapeworms (7 to over 70 mm). No tapeworm development was found afterward.

### Thermal and environmental change

- Trout were not harmed until temperatures exceeded about 80 to 85°F.
- South Twin Lake held cutthroat and grayling in 1962, but in 1966 no fish were found after a radical change in water chemistry. The cause was never determined.
- The 1959 earthquake dramatically changed the behavior of several thermal features.

### Introduced and exotic species

- **Yellowstone Lake:** Five fish species in total. Three were introduced: [[Redside Shiner]], [[Longnose Sucker]] and [[Lake Chub]]. Native species are longnose dace and Yellowstone cutthroat.
- **Brown trout** in Duck Lake were eradicated in 1967.
- **Yellow perch** in Goose Lake were removed by chemical treatment in 1938.
- **Landlocked salmon** planted in 1908 were not recorded again.
- **Mountain whitefish** introduced above Yellowstone Falls in 1889 did not survive.
- **Policy:** Introduction or transplanting of non-native species is prohibited in national parks. Managers aim to restore the Yellowstone cutthroat to its original waters where feasible.

### Species highlights

- **Yellowstone cutthroat trout:** Average fish from Yellowstone Lake weighs about one pound and is 14 inches long. Spawning runs occur from late May to mid-July.
- **Rainbow trout:** First introduced in 1889 in the Gibbon River. Can exceed seven pounds, though two pounds is considered a good catch.
- **Brown trout:** Introduced in 1890 in Lewis and Shoshone Lakes. Can exceed six pounds. Spawns October through February.
- **Brook trout:** Introduced in 1889. Average eight inches; up to 18 inches and three pounds in large waters.
- **Lake trout:** First planted in 1890 in Shoshone and Lewis Lakes. Can exceed 30 pounds.
- **Arctic grayling:** The most limited sport-fish distribution in the park. Average under 12 inches. Hooks smaller than #10 are needed.
- **Mountain whitefish:** Average about 12 inches and just over half a pound. Deposit about 8,000 eggs per pound of female.
- **Utah sucker:** Up to 22 inches and five pounds. Mainly a lake species, concentrated in Heart Lake.
- **Longnose sucker:** Probably introduced into Yellowstone Lake in the early 1930s by anglers using live bait.
- **Utah chub:** Up to 18 inches. Major food for lake trout. Probably introduced into Lewis and Shoshone Lakes by bait anglers.
- **Mottled sculpin:** Up to five to six inches. Males guard nests of eggs under logs or stones.

### Fishing guidance and policy

- **Catch-and-release:** The park encourages releasing fish to preserve quality wild-trout angling for less expert fishermen.
- **Lures and flies:** Light spoons, spinners (silver or gold size #1 and #2 with red, white or yellow tail spinners) and dry flies (stonefly imitations in July) are recommended. Woolly worms and muddlers are favored wet flies.
- **Bait:** The booklet argues that regulations should prohibit live bait, citing the longnose sucker introduction.
- **Overall aim:** Fishing should continue in a way that sustains native fish, nesting birds and wildlife that visitors can see without disrupting natural relationships.
