# Biological Problems in Water Pollution: Third Seminar (1962)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-biology

## Executive Summary

This is the transactions volume of the Third Seminar on Biological Problems in Water Pollution, held in 1962 and published in 1965 by the [[Robert A. Taft Sanitary Engineering Center]] as Public Health Service Publication No. 999-WP-25. The seminar, planned by [[Clarence M. Tarzwell]], focused on the environmental requirements of aquatic organisms as the scientific basis for [[Water Quality Criteria]]. The volume combines a keynote by [[James M. Quigley]], a panel debate on the value of criteria, and technical papers on algae, diatoms, invertebrates, fishes and wildlife, radionuclides, pesticides, eutrophication, fish kills and bioassay apparatus.

The central argument is that water reuse depends on knowing the water quality each use requires, and that criteria must rest on research into organism requirements rather than on committee consensus. Panelists disagreed on whether criteria should guide enforcement or whether case-by-case field work should come first.

## Background & Description

The seminar series began in 1956 to bring together researchers in sanitary biology, share new methods, and train future workers. The 1962 meeting was timed to precede the 14th meeting of the International Association of Theoretical and Applied Limnology at Madison, Wisconsin, allowing foreign scientists to attend both. Registration totaled 436, including 88 attendees from outside the United States representing 26 countries. Canada had the largest foreign delegation, and 43 US states plus the District of Columbia were represented. Universities formed the largest single group, followed by conservation agencies.

The transactions contain six main parts:

- **Role of the aquatic biologist** (Quigley, keynote)
- **Value and use of water quality criteria** (panel discussion, Berger chair)
- **Plankton algae** (Fogg chair; diatoms, extracellular algal products, toxic blue-green blooms)
- **Freshwater invertebrates** (Chambers chair; bacteria-protozoa indicators, protozoa, Crustacea, Tubificidae)
- **Marine invertebrates** (Carriker chair; oysters, crabs, shrimp, estuarine molluscs)
- **Aquatic insects** (Macan chair; Plecoptera, Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Chironomidae)

Additional sessions cover fishes and wildlife, radionuclides in the food chain, informal discussions on biological indicators and pesticides, land use, fish diseases and parasites, fish kills, eutrophication, and bioassay apparatus. The contents list about 70 papers and discussions. Only part of the volume is reproduced in the source text provided. The abstracts section is listed but not included.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality as a use-based concept.** Tarzwell defines water pollution as "the addition of any material or any change in character or quality of a water that interferes with, lessens, or destroys a desired use." Toxicity is quantitative: several trace elements (including copper, zinc, manganese and selenium) are essential at low concentrations but toxic above certain levels.

**Quigley's six aims of abatement** include safe domestic supplies, agricultural and industrial water, recreation, and "water of the quality needed for survival, growth, reproduction, and the general well-being of fresh-water, estuarial, and marine life." He stresses that extremes, not averages, define environmental suitability: "Frogs cannot be raised in a clover field, trout in an eroded arroyo, or bass in a cesspool."

**Water demand (Kimball, National Wildlife Federation).** Using US Geological Survey and Senate Select Committee figures, Kimball reports that withdrawals of 270,000 million gallons per day (mgd) occurred in the US, of which only 61,000 mgd was consumed and about 77 percent was returned to source, often degraded. Waste dilution was the largest single use of water in 1954 (512.4 mgd, projected at 332.2 mgd in 1980 and 446.5 mgd in 2000 under scheduled abatement). A proposed [[Potomac Basin]] plan included 3 billion gpd, roughly two-thirds (about 2 billion gpd) for pollution dilution.

**Diatom habitats.** Round describes a continuum from acid peat-pool and cold alkaline spring waters (relatively simple, low-diversity floras) through saprobic zones to sterile, metal-toxic or coprozoic waters. Diatoms require silica, and blanketing silica sources can halt diatom growth entirely.

## Key Findings

### Policy and criteria debate

- **Quigley** argues that aquatic biologists are "a recognized member of the team" but must translate findings into terms that enforcement officials, judges and courts can use.
- **Murray Stein** (Federal enforcement) argues that case-by-case field work, documenting what harms legitimate uses, should precede criteria, warning that committees can "fritter away" time and resources on allowable limits.
- **Milton P. Adams** (former Michigan Water Resources Commission) concludes criteria are "useful tools for a state agency for planning and administrative purposes" but may not be effective enforcement aids. Michigan's Kalamazoo River DO criterion (2.5 ppm at 10-year low flow under a pro-rata 39 percent load reduction) and a boundary-water case had failed to produce results. He also noted that "lack of knowledge" is the main obstacle.
- **Roy F. Weston** (industrial viewpoint) supports criteria that are "reasonable, equitable, measurable, enforceable, and enforced," proposing that the discharger bear the burden of proof and that allocation of allowable loads be tied to production.
- **Tarzwell** argues criteria "can be developed only through research" and that "water cannot rise above its source." He contends that without known requirements, waste treatment requirements rest on opinion and change with administrators.
- **Thomas L. Kimball** calls for enforced standards backed by funded research, noting that "the dependable supply of water" can be increased by reducing waste dilution.
- **Dr. Ronald Allanson** (South Africa) describes a system in which the Water Act of 1955 requires return of used water, with national standards (for example, no more than 100 fecal E. coli per liter, ammonia not above 10 ppm, BOD not above 10 ppm) and negotiated, time-limited exemptions.

### Diatom and algal ecology

- **Reimer** compiled physico-chemical data for five diatoms using 41 streams and rivers (computer series) and 59 areas (manual series). For *Melosira varians*, pH data came from 132 composited samples, each with at least 8,000 diatoms counted, and the species had to occur at least six times. About 20 to 40 percent frequency was suggested as high enough to indicate large populations and possible organic enrichment.
- Reimer classifies *M. varians*, *Nitzschia amphibia* and *Navicula confervacea* as having wide tolerances. *N. ingenua* was recorded for the first time in the US in 1958 (Tennessee River), later in the Savannah River (1960) and Lower Three Runs Creek.
- **Round** argues that an algal pollution index requires a standard unpolluted flora, that sediment (epipelic) and attached diatoms are better indicators than plankton, and that the *Weser* study recorded some 615 species, of which only 17 were classified as saprophytic.
- **Fogg** reports that extracellular fixation of radiocarbon ranged from 5 to 90 percent of total photosynthetic fixation in lake phytoplankton, and that glycollic acid was present in lake water (Windermere April 1961: 0.29 mg/l; October 1961: 0.045 mg/l). Concentrations near 1 mg/l of [[Glycollic Acid]] removed the lag phase in *Chlorella pyrenoidosa* growth.
- **Gorham** identifies at least two toxin types in [[Microcystis Aeruginosa]] blooms: a fast-death factor (FDF, an endotoxin released by decomposition; mouse intraperitoneal MLD 0.47 mg/kg; death in 30–60 minutes) and a slow-death factor produced by associated bacteria (death in 4–48 hours). Of 10 *Microcystis* waterblooms tested, 7 contained FDF; of 19 isolates, 8 produced FDF.
- In [[Anabaena Flos-Aquae]], a very-fast-death factor (VFDF) killed mice within 1–10 minutes; 8 of 14 cultured strains produced it. Gorham found no fast-death toxin in [[Aphanizomenon Flos-Aquae]] samples. Historical cases include a cow dying within 25 minutes after drinking 6 gallons of bloom, and 79 hogs killed at Fox Lake, Minnesota.

### Invertebrates, protozoa and toxicology

- **Hutner et al.** propose the Winogradsky column as a scaled microcosm for testing degradability and toxicity, noting that finding microbes to degrade synthetic chemicals is difficult and that little is known about freshwater inocula.
- Protozoa and bacteria were used as sensitive indicators of metabolic lesions: triparanol toxicity to *Ochromonas danica* and *Tetrahymena* (fatty acids and sterols reversed it); primidone's folic-acid-responsive effects (Crithidia fasciculata); and thalidomide toxicity to protozoa, reversed by nicotinic acid or menadione.

### Fishes, fish kills and wildlife (partly in contents only)

- The contents list papers on dissolved oxygen requirements of fishes (Doudoroff and Warren), centrarchid requirements (largemouth, smallmouth and spotted bass, Bennett), salinity tolerance of *Cyprinodon maculatus*, avoidance of pollutants by young Atlantic salmon, sublethal zinc and copper effects on Atlantic salmon migration, and fish kill causes. Full texts are not included in the source provided, so their findings cannot be reported here.
- **Tarzwell** states that "the criteria must be based on a thorough knowledge of the environmental requirements" of the organisms protected, and that "we have been doing [case-by-case fish-kill studies] for 50 years and we haven't come up with the answers."
- **Stein** and **Tarzwell** debate whether bioassays with resistant adult fish can show that exposure is safe; Tarzwell argues short-term survival does not establish safety for reproduction and productivity.

### Other findings

- **Eutrophic and bloom conditions:** blue-green blooms occur in eutrophic water during warm weather, and bloom toxicity varied even when species composition did not change.
- **Nitrogen and diatoms:** [[Melosira Varians]], [[Navicula Confervacea]] and [[Nitzschia Amphibia]] are reported to increase with elevated nitrogen, but Reimer cautions that their indicator role is "not yet clear."
- **Reuse philosophy:** Tarzwell concludes that future freshwater needs "can be met only by re-use," which requires each user to know the water quality required for the next use.

### Limitations of the source

The text ends mid-paper within the invertebrate section (Hutner et al.). Papers on fishes and wildlife, radionuclides, pesticides, eutrophication, fish kills and apparatus appear only in the contents list. Findings from those sessions are therefore not reported.
