# Age and Growth of Four Fish Species from Flathead Lake, Montana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #percidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study, published in the *Proceedings of the Montana Academy of Sciences*, reports the age and growth of four fish species in [[Flathead Lake]], Montana. Fieldwork ran from summer 1961 through spring 1962 and was conducted by the [[Montana Fish and Game Department]]. The original plan was to sample all species, but limited equipment and time restricted the work to [[Dolly Varden]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Peamouth]], and [[Northern Squawfish]]. Ages were estimated from scales, and mean lengths at each annulus were calculated. The study found that Dolly Varden grew fastest after shifting from stream to lake residence, and that the three cyprinid and percid species grew more slowly. The oldest fish recorded were 8 years (Dolly Varden), 7 years (yellow perch), 13 years (peamouth), and 17 years (squawfish).

## Background & Description

**Study context.** Earlier work on Flathead Lake covered limnology, invertebrates, and microbiology, but little had been published on its fishes. Earlier fish studies included work on [[Lake Whitefish]], [[Kokanee]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], and a [[White Sturgeon]] record. The present study targeted the northern portion of the lake, roughly 40 square miles north of a line from Woods Bay on the east shore to Angel Point on the west shore. Six collection sections were established.

**Lake description.** Flathead Lake is the largest natural lake in Montana. It has a surface area of 120,320 acres, a shoreline of 127 miles, and a maximum depth of 421 feet. The main tributaries are the [[Flathead River]] and Swan River, which enter at the northern end. [[Kerr Dam]] sits at the southern outlet and largely controls lake level. The lake is described as oligotrophic, a "deep, pure, cold mountain lake with a correspondingly low productivity of plankton and benthos."

**Fish community.** The lake supports eight game species (lake whitefish, kokanee, mountain whitefish, cutthroat trout, rainbow trout, Dolly Varden, [[Lake Trout]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]). Other species include peamouth, northern squawfish, redside shiner, longnose sucker, largescale sucker, pumpkinseed, and yellow perch.

**Study species.**
- *Dolly Varden* (*Salvelinus malma*)
- *Yellow perch* (*Perca flavescens*)
- *Peamouth* (*Mylocheilus caurinus*)
- *Northern squawfish* (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Fish were taken by [[Gill Netting]], seining, [[Pirate Trap]] sets, trolling, and [[Angling]]. Some data came from the [[Creel Census]] of the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife. Gill nets included experimental, coarse-mesh, and fine-mesh types. Total length (nearest 0.1 inch) and weight (nearest 0.01 pound) were recorded for every fish. Scales were removed from the area between the dorsal fin base and the lateral line.

**Age determination.** Annuli were measured with a scale projection machine ([[Scale Projection Method]]). Lengths at each annulus were calculated with a nomogram, and a linear scale-radius-to-body-length relationship was assumed because samples were small. The authors cite [[Whitney and Carlander]] (1956) on the error associated with this assumption.

**Spawning and early life (from literature cited in the study).**
- Dolly Varden spawn in September and October. Eggs were eyed by November 15 in the North Fork of the Flathead River (Block 1955). Scales appear at 1.6 to 1.8 inches total length (Bjornn 1961).
- Yellow perch spawn in early May in Thompson Lakes, Montana (Echo 1954).
- Peamouth spawn in May (Hill 1958). The annulus forms in May in Flathead Lake.
- Squawfish spawn in May and June in the Big Blackfoot River drainage (Hill 1958). Annulus formation occurs in late May.

## Key Findings

### Dolly Varden (*Salvelinus malma*)
- Ages were determined for **289 fish**, ranging from 8.0 to 34.5 inches.
- The youngest fish had three annuli. This age group had 44 specimens, ranging from 8.0 to 11.0 inches.
- Annual growth increments for the first two years were 1.7 and 2.7 inches. Ages III to VIII grew 4.5 to 6.0 inches per year.
- The authors attribute slow early growth to stream residence and faster later growth to lake residence. Bjornn (1961) described stream-type scale development in early years, followed by widely spaced annuli indicating a shift to lake life.
- The **oldest fish was 8 years** (34.5 inches, 20 pounds).
- Summary growth rates: 2.2 inches per year for the first two years and 5.3 inches per year from age III to VIII.

### Yellow Perch (*Perca flavescens*)
- Ages were determined for **243 specimens**, ranging from 1.4 to 12.0 inches.
- Only **10 young-of-the-year** were collected, measuring 1.4 to 1.8 inches. Annulus timing could not be determined because spring samples were few.
- Lengths at ages V, VI, and VII matched those reported by Kennedy (1949) in Manitoba. Overall mean lengths were lower than those reported for several other regions.
- The **oldest fish was 7 years**.
- Summary growth rate: 1.5 inches per year (range 0.9 to 1.9 inches).

### Peamouth (*Mylocheilus caurinus*)
- Ages were determined for **192 fish**, ranging from 1.4 to 14.0 inches.
- Young-of-the-year (collected September 10) measured 1.4 to 2.8 inches. No age class I fish were captured. Age classes II, III, and IX through XIII each had 10 or fewer specimens.
- Mean lengths were lower than those reported by Hill (1958) from Placid Lake and by Clemens et al. (1939) from Okanagan Lake.
- The **oldest fish was 13 years** (14.0 inches), compared with 9 years reported by Hill (1958) and 5 years by Clemens et al.
- Summary growth rate: 1.0 inch per year (range 0.4 to 1.4 inches).

### Northern Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*)
- Ages were determined for **200 fish**, ranging from 1.2 to 21.2 inches.
- Young-of-the-year (collected September 10) measured 1.2 to 1.8 inches. No age class I fish were captured.
- Mean lengths were lower than those reported by Hill (1958) from Alva Lake and by Clemens et al. (1939) from Woods Lake.
- The **oldest fish was 17 years** (21.2 inches). Other reported maxima were 15 years (Hill 1958), 13 years (Jeppson 1957), and 11 years (Clemens et al. 1939).
- Summary growth rate: 1.1 inches per year (range 0.9 to 1.3 inches).

### Methodological and Management Notes
- Fine-mesh and experimental gill nets caught most fish, but angling took most of the game fish.
- The study area covered only the northern portion of the lake, so results may not reflect the full lake.
- The authors note that a linear scale-length relationship was used because of small sample sizes, and that this assumption carries some error.

### Reference List
The paper cites about 50 works, including Young (1935) on lake productivity, Brown and Logan (1960) on warmwater game fish growth in Montana ponds, and Crowe (1950) on trap-net construction.
