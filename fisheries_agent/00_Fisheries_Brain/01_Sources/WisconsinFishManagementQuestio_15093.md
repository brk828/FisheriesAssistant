# Wisconsin Fish Management: Questions and Answers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This Wisconsin Conservation Department pamphlet (Publication 205-52, a revision of Publication 205 by G. E. Sprecher) answers common public questions about how the state's [[Fish Management Division]] produces sustained yields of desirable fish. It covers fish propagation in [[Fish Hatchery|hatcheries]] and [[Rearing Pond]]s, stocking and distribution, [[Great Lakes Commercial Fishing]], the [[Sea Lamprey]] problem, [[Rough Fish Control]], [[Fishery Biology]] surveys, and [[Watershed Management]]. The document is a reference aimed at schools, clubs and conservation organizations rather than a research study, so most figures are descriptive or historical.

## Background & Description

Fish management is defined as controlling a lake or stream to produce a sustained yield of the desirable fishes best suited to that water body. Productivity depends on water fertility, temperature, depth, plants, food organisms, calcium carbonate content and dissolved oxygen, and management also weighs fishing intensity, natural reproduction, the need for planting, and the control of [[Rough Fish]].

The pamphlet gives the scale of the task: about 8,000 lakes and 9,000 miles of trout streams in Wisconsin. Program size is set by the budget, which is funded by fishing license revenue.

Historical context given includes:
- Fish propagation began in ancient China about 2,500 years ago.
- Carp farming in Central Europe dates to the 18th century.
- Artificial stripping and fertilization of trout eggs was recorded in France early in the 19th century.
- State propagation in Wisconsin has been practiced since 1874.

Wisconsin operates 22 hatcheries, 13 of which propagate trout, in two types: jar-type hatcheries for warm-water species and trout hatcheries. Regional area headquarters (Northwest, Northeast, Southern) coordinate fish management under area coordinators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hatchery and rearing biology**
- Walleye and musky eggs are incubated in glass jars with constant water flow for dissolved oxygen. Walleye fry swim up immediately; musky fry remain in jars for several days.
- Incubation period depends on water temperature. The pamphlet's table lists 120 days for lake trout, 21 days for other trout and walleyes, and 8 days for muskellunge at the listed temperatures.
- Hatch success is usually 65% to 75%. Walleyes begin feeding about 3 days after hatching and trout about 4 weeks after.
- Infertile eggs are removed at the "eyed egg" stage. Newly hatched fish with a yolk sack are "sac fry"; fish older than sac fry and under one year old are "fingerlings."
- Wild broodstock for warm-water species are taken from lakes in frame or fyke nets and returned after spawning. Tagging work shows the same walleyes can be spawned year after year.

**Spawning seasons**
- Most lake species spawn in spring and early summer.
- Brook trout, brown trout, lake trout, whitefish, cisco and herring spawn in fall and winter.

**Winterkill and oxygen**
- Snow cover blocks light, aquatic plants die and decompose, and dissolved oxygen falls below what fish need.

**Watershed effects**
- Trees shade streams, stabilize temperature and reduce silt. Forest fires remove shade, expose topsoil to runoff, and release acids and lye.
- Heavy grazing damages stream banks and keeps water turbid.

**Lake productivity**
- Estimated potential ranges from about 50 lb/acre in northern bog-type lakes to about 500 lb/acre in fertile southern lakes.
- A harvest of 10 to 15 lb/acre/year is described as very heavy. Some small northern lakes yield under half a pound to one pound per acre per year.

**Great Lakes lake trout**
- Adults roam widely, while most young fish under 1.5 lb stay within about 25 miles of home waters. One Lake Superior adult moved from Michigan to Minnesota, at least 236 miles.

## Key Findings

**Fish planting and distribution**
- Stocking follows biological findings and is set by area meetings held each October or November with coordinators, biologists, wardens and [[Conservation Congress]] representatives.
- Planting is legal only in publicly accessible waters under Section 29.50 of the Wisconsin Statutes and Conservation Commission Order F-126.
- Restocking is not mandatory everywhere. Some waters are inaccessible, some already support good populations, and some need removal of unwanted species.
- Lakes where spawn is taken receive additional fish to compensate.
- Plantings are logged on form FI-76.
- Fish are transported by oxygen tank trucks.
- Federal fish from the [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]] are allocated under a cooperative agreement that prevents stocking unsuitable waters and introducing undesirable species.

**Species propagated**
- The state propagates brook, brown, rainbow, lake and other trout, muskellunge, northern pike, walleye, largemouth and smallmouth bass, and suckers.

**Lake trout and [[Sea Lamprey]] impacts**
- Lake trout first mature at about seven years or older, and virtually none under 22 inches are mature.
- A marked-trout planting experiment began in Lake Michigan in 1944 with 411,894 hatchery-marked fish. The marking scheme ran from 1944 to 1946 by clipping different fins. Rewards of $2.00 (undersized) and $4.00 (17 inches or over) were offered.
- Trout catch in Lake Huron fell from 1,345,000 lb (1939) to 41,000 lb (1946). Lake Michigan fell from 2,850,000 lb (1944) to under 30,000 lb (1950). The document states that further study is needed for a firm conclusion.
- In a spring 1947 Lake Michigan study, 21% of lake trout had one lamprey scar, 24% had more than one, and 55% were unscarred. 67% of fishermen believed scars reduced market quality.
- Over 16,000 adult lampreys were trapped in Wisconsin in 1950 and over 30,000 in 1951, using screen traps and weirs in tributary streams.

**Rough fish removal**
- Rough fish are species that provide little sport, damage habitat or have poor food quality. Removed species include carp, garfish, dogfish, sheepshead, buffalo, quillback and burbot.
- Carp were introduced in Wisconsin in 1882.
- Rough fish cannot be eradicated except in small waters, but persistent removal controls them.
- Game fish caught in removal operations are returned uninjured.
- Removal is said to improve water clarity, weed growth and fishing.

**Fishery biology and surveys**
- Lake and stream surveys record fish populations, growth, spawning areas, vegetation, forage fish and water chemistry.
- Scales are sampled for aging. Scales regenerate, so the fish are not harmed.
- Creel censuses estimate harvest rates and migration.
- Population balance and dynamics are central to planning.
- Stunting of panfish results from high reproductive potential when numbers are not controlled.

**Trout lake conversion**
- Some lakes with suitable cold, oxygenated deep water are chemically treated and converted to trout lakes. The document reports that total poundage of fish yielded to anglers is higher than before conversion.

**Watershed management**
- The program aims to restore habitat and promote good land use. Methods include contour plowing, strip-cropping, terracing, fencing livestock, cover planting and stream devices (wing and V-deflectors, small dams).
- Nine demonstration areas are named, including Black Earth Creek, Dell Creek, Brule River and Plover River.
- Stream fishing improvements are expected immediately after habitat work, and vegetation benefits within five years.

**Public information**
- Propagation and distribution records are available at the Madison office or any area headquarters.
- The pamphlet also lists references on minnows and fish and identifies 18 types of watershed cooperators, from state agencies to youth groups.
