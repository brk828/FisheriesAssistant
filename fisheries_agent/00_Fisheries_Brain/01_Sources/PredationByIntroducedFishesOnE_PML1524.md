# Predation by Introduced Fishes on Endangered Humpback Chub and Other Native Species in the Little Colorado River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined predation by five introduced predatory fishes on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and other native fishes in the [[Little Colorado River]], Grand Canyon, Arizona. Fish were sampled about monthly from July 1991 to June 1995 in the lower 15 km of the river, and 408 digestive tracts were examined. Predator diets were low in diversity and dominated by algae (mainly [[Cladophora]]), aquatic insects, and fishes. Humpback chub was found in 13 (3.2%) of the 408 stomachs, and native fishes overall in 3.7% of the 219 stomachs that contained food. The authors conclude that predation by introduced fish may significantly deplete native populations and reduce recruitment, although the population-level effect could not be fully quantified.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America has a historically depauperate fish fauna of 36 species, many of them polytypic. Roughly 70 nonnative species have been introduced to the basin, intentionally or accidentally. Together with habitat alteration from water development, these introductions have contributed to the extinction of 3 native species and the listing of 22 others as endangered or threatened. Predation and competition by introduced fishes are widely implicated in such declines, both in the basin and globally.

Humpback chub is a medium-sized minnow that persists in only seven reaches of four rivers in the basin. Its decline reflects habitat loss and modification, hybridization with congeners, and interactions with nonnative fishes (USFWS 1990). The largest remaining population occurs in the Little Colorado River, a major tributary of the [[Colorado River]] in the [[Grand Canyon]].

The five predators examined were [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Black Bullhead]], and [[Yellow Bullhead]]. Channel catfish predation on humpback chub had been suggested previously from stomach contents and from crescent-shaped bite marks on chub, but no published data existed on predation of native fishes by the introduced species. [[Common Carp]] and [[Striped Bass]] were also noted as occurring in the system, though striped bass is uncommon in the lower reaches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and sampling.** The lower 15 km of the Little Colorado River was sampled roughly monthly (July 1991 to June 1995) using three methods:
- **[[Hoop Net]]s** (0.8 or 0.7–2.2 m diameter as recorded in the original, 2.4 or 3.0 m long, 4 or 6 hoops) set in all available habitats deeper than about 0.4 m.
- **[[Trammel Net]]s** (7.6–45.7 m long, 1.8 m deep) set in water deeper than 0.5 m near the Little Colorado–Colorado River confluence and occasionally near Powell and Salt canyons, 3.0 and 10.6 river km upstream.
- **Angling** with baited hooks and artificial lures for supplementary collection.

Native fish were released alive near the capture site. Nonnative fish were killed, and their guts were either fixed in 10% formalin or, for whole fish under 100 mm, preserved in 95% ethanol. Prey were identified in the laboratory, and pharyngeal tooth counts helped separate cyprinid prey. Stomach contents were also examined microscopically for larval fish.

**Diet context.** The authors attributed the low diversity of food items to a general scarcity of food in the Little Colorado River. Diets were broadly consistent with earlier reports from the Colorado River basin and elsewhere. Earlier studies had concluded that channel catfish are opportunistic omnivores for which fish form a minor part of the diet.

## Key Findings

**Predator presence and prey remains**
- Humpback chub remains occurred in 13 (3.2%) of 408 digestive tracts examined.
- Remains of native fishes occurred in 3.7% of the 219 stomachs that contained food. (The abstract gives this figure as "13.7%" in OCR-affected text; the Results section and arithmetic support 3.7%.)
- Other native and nonnative prey in predator stomachs included common carp, speckled dace, fathead minnow, bluehead sucker, flannelmouth sucker, and unidentified fish. These were found in 51 predators, 5 of which had also eaten humpback chub.
- No larval or small postlarval fishes were found, which the authors attributed to the fragility and rapid digestion of this stage.

**Predator-specific patterns**
- 3 of 174 rainbow trout contained humpback chub (prey 40–45 mm TL). Rainbow trout otherwise ate other fishes in 22 cases.
- 2 of 12 black bullheads contained humpback chub (49 and 55 mm TL).
- 2 of 10 brown trout contained speckled dace or unidentified fish.
- 2 of 10 yellow bullheads contained unidentified fish remains.
- 8 of 202 channel catfish ate 1–7 chub each (85–200 mm TL). Channel catfish that ate fish averaged 500 mm TL, larger than those that did not.
- Mean TL of humpback chub in stomachs was 102 mm (N = 27), not significantly different from other ingested fishes (92 mm, N = 74) by two-sample t-test.

**Diet composition (frequency of occurrence)**
- Rainbow trout: algae (primarily *Cladophora*) were most common, at 47%. Other items included vegetation, amphipods (*Gammarus lacustris*), aquatic insect larvae, and terrestrial invertebrates.
- Brown trout: mostly terrestrial invertebrates (20%), with fish and fish eggs also present (20% and 10%).
- Black bullhead: detritus, aquatic insects (16%), and fish (17%).
- Yellow bullhead: detritus and odonate naiads (10% each) and fish (20%).
- Channel catfish: fish were the most common food of the non-trout predators at 10–20% across species.
- The proportion of empty stomachs ranged from 34% to 70% among species (OCR-read values).

**Population-level implications**
- About 3% of rainbow trout and channel catfish ate an average of 2.3 humpback chub.
- The authors modelled a hypothetical predator population of 1,000 individuals eating one average meal of 2.3 chub per week. Annual consumption would total 3,588 humpback chub.
- Predatory fish in the system were estimated to number in the thousands.
- Recent adult (>150 mm TL) humpback chub estimates, from five models, were 4,508–14,444 (Douglas and Marsh 1996).
- Predation may therefore limit recruitment by removing juveniles, and may also increase adult mortality, since channel catfish consumed chub up to 200 mm TL.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Predation by introduced fishes poses a threat to humpback chub in the Little Colorado River and may have a major negative effect on the population.
- Although the Little Colorado River retains a natural hydrologic character thought to favour native over introduced fishes (Minckley and Meffe 1987), predation impacts may still limit native populations.
- The long-term effect of predation on early life stages cannot be assessed until methods to quantify this predation are developed.
- Rangewide, humpback chub is represented by several sustaining populations.
