# Humpback Chub Propagation Plan for the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) planning document, prepared by Diann M. Smith of the [[Research Branch]] in February 199X (year truncated in scan), proposes methods for collecting reproductive products and adult broodstock of the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) from the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), Coconino County, Arizona. The gametes are intended for temperature shock and tolerance studies and otolith age verification, which will be described in separate documents. Three earlier field spawning attempts on the LCR population had failed, and the plan lists likely causes and corresponding solutions before proposing a spawning effort for spring 1993.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is an endangered cyprinid of the Colorado River system. Adults from the LCR and from the Colorado River at [[Black Rocks]] have previously been held in captivity for a year or more and spawned successfully through hormone-induced gonadal maturation (Hamman 1982a, 1982b; Marsh 1985). A field spawning effort on the Black Rocks population also succeeded (Valdez and Valdez-Gonzales 1991). Three field spawning attempts on the LCR population, reported by Minckley (1989), Raisanen et al. (1991), Angradi et al. (1992) and Hines (1992), were unsuccessful.

The document is organized into Introduction, Methods (spawning procedure, egg transport and care, LCR on-site incubation, larval care), Literature Cited, and two appendices: Appendix A (supplies, equipment and personnel) and Appendix B (contingency plan for obtaining adult broodstock).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key environmental parameters addressed in the plan include:

- **Water temperature:** Bubbling Ponds water is to be adjusted to near ambient LCR water but kept between 18°C and 23°C. Bubbling Ponds water of 17–20°C year-round is described as suitable for rearing humpback chub (Bulkley et al. 1981; Hamman 1982; Marsh 1985). Eggs must not undergo temperature change of more than 1–2°C per day during shipment.
- **Sediment and turbidity:** Excessive sediment in LCR water was a suspected cause of egg oxygen deficiency. Water is to be de-silted in insulated sediment basins (3 ft x 2 ft) and turbidity measured by nephelometer 2–4 times daily.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** DO is to be measured in the water supply, and oxygen is supplied to contingency buckets by oxygen stones.
- **Water quality monitoring:** A Hydrolab records temperature, conductivity, pH and DO hourly in the LCR and in incubation inflow. Flow is checked every 4 hours and monitored by an alarm.
- **Site setting:** Collection and spawning are based at Salt Trail camp on the LCR, with transport by helicopter (Grand Canyon Tours) and a 16-mile dirt-road drive to Highway 89.

## Key Findings

**Likely causes of past spawning failures and proposed remedies**

1. **Sperm diluent:** Possibly caused problems. Use diluent of known freshness (Santa Fe Animal Health product #5184-01); test a small subsample with no diluent.
2. **Sediment-related oxygen deficit:** Fertilize and treat eggs in Bubbling Ponds water and transport the same day, or de-silt LCR water for on-site incubation.
3. **Inefficient flow through Heath trays:** Use complete Heath tray apparatus that properly directs flow, and measure DO of the water supply.
4. **Handling sensitivity:** Eggs and larvae are critically vulnerable to handling and transport. Transport as eggs after water hardening and within 24 hours of fertilization.
5. **Mechanical trauma in transit:** Airlift reproductive product directly to Highway 89 to avoid the rough dirt road; pack a small subsample moist but not submerged.
6. **Temperature fluctuation in transit:** Use styrofoam egg shipping crates with sufficient insulation.

**Spawning procedure**

- Adults are collected by hoop net, PIT-tagged (instruments sterilized in Betadine), and held in flat-bottomed, covered live-cars in low-velocity water near Salt Trail camp.
- Spawning proceeds only once at least 2–3 ripe males per female are confirmed available.
- Unripe females receive intraperitoneal injections of 4 mg/kg acetone-dried carp pituitary daily for 1–3 days until ripe. Final maturation may take 20–24 hours; females are checked at 8:00 a.m. and noon the following day.
- Ova from one female and milt from 2–3 males are stripped into diluent-containing bowls, with a small subsample (about 10%) stripped without diluent. Bentonite is added to reduce adhesiveness, and eggs are rocked in water to clean them and then water-hardened for 1–2 hours.
- Naturally ripened and induced eggs are kept separate to compare hatching success.

**Transport and incubation**

- Eggs are shipped submerged in tempered Bubbling Ponds water in double bags with injected oxygen, in insulated crates. About 10% of eggs are drained and shipped moist to test for water-movement damage.
- Eggs are acclimated for 1 hour under subdued light before transfer to Heath incubators. Daily monitoring removes non-viable and fungused eggs; about 5 viable eggs are preserved daily in 5% non-buffered formalin.
- On-site Heath incubation at the LCR serves as a backup if weather prevents same-day transport or Bubbling Ponds incubation fails.

**Larval care**

- Larval baskets of under 1 mm mesh catch larvae at Heath tray outflows, and trays are checked 3–4 times daily.
- Larvae are held in aerated aquaria and fed zooplankton produced in an outdoor tank fertilized with inorganic (ammonium phosphate) and organic (Consomix trout starter) fertilizers, with later transition to other diets, and moved to other aquaria to prevent overcrowding.

**Contingency broodstock plan (Appendix B)**

- Roughly 20 unspawned adults (12 females and 8 males) are to be transported to Bubbling Ponds and held in six 6-ft circular raceways with screen tops, with disease-free rainbow trout as companion fish and Bio-Kyowa feed (2700 ppm) provided daily.
- Chub are treated for Lernaea (manual removal), Bothriocephalus (1.0 ppm Droncit for 24 hours), and Ichthyophthirius (167 ppm formalin every other day for seven treatments, plus prophylaxis).
- The raceways are in a shaded enclosure with a drain field to prevent escape of larvae and eggs.

**Personnel and supplies**

- Staff named: Diann Smith, Phil Hines (Fish Culturist), Rob Clarkson (Project Biologist), Mark Lupher (Administrative Assistant), plus one full-time and one part-time wildlife assistant.
- Major equipment includes 15 hoop nets, 6 live-cars, 2 Hydrolabs, a nephelometer, a flow meter with alarm, 6 Heath incubation racks, and 50 turkey feathers.

**Planning status**

An interoffice memo dated 24 February 1993 circulated the draft to the Aquatic Coordination Team and interested agencies (including U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Glen Canyon Environmental Studies, Bio/West and Grand Canyon National Park), requesting comments by 12 March so the plan could be finalized in time for the spring 1993 spawning season.
