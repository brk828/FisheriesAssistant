# Causes and Effects of Short Emergence Periods in Insects

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ephemeroptera

## Executive Summary

This 1950s conference paper by T. T. Macan (Ambleside, England), with a discussion section, examines why the adults of many aquatic insects (Plecoptera, Ephemeroptera, Odonata, Diptera) emerge only during a short part of the season. Macan assumes these restricted windows are maintained by [[Natural Selection]]. He reviews four proposed causes (ease of mate-finding, temperature, oxygen, and competition) and then focuses on the consequences of short emergence for the population. His main case is [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]], whose adults emerge over about a month, while its nymphs hatch and grow across roughly six months. This prolonged hatching makes standard growth and production calculations unreliable. The paper also uses [[Salmo Trutta]] data from New Zealand to show how production studies can be distorted when hatching is not synchronous.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** A comparative, conceptual paper rather than a single controlled experiment. It draws on the author's field studies, literature, and discussion remarks.
- **Opening analogy:** Macan compares insect emergence windows with the encystment rhythm of the planktonic ciliate [[Strombidium Oculatum]], which lives in rock-pools. Its encystment is timed to high tide. In captivity, without tides, the rhythm becomes progressively more variable, which suggests that natural selection maintains the timing.
- **Life-history adaptation:** The caddisfly [[Apatania Muliebris]] (printed as "Apatidea muliebris" in the source), believed to be a glacial relict, has a short late-spring flight season, rapid summer growth, and winter quiescence. Macan, citing Anker Nielsen (1951), notes this suits high latitudes with short summers but is poorly matched to the cold springs of Denmark. Closely related species, thought to be mutants of the relict, are on the wing all summer and active as larvae in winter.
- **Key study species:**
  - [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]]: the focal mayfly (Heptageniidae, per the source's "Ecdyonurus" comparison).
  - [[Ecdyonurus Torrentis]]: a similar species that emerges "in driblets" throughout summer, unlike the mass emergence of R. semicolorata.
  - [[Habroleptoides Modesta]]: a stream mayfly that emerges earlier in warmer streams and seasons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Four proposed causes of short emergence periods**

1. **Mate-finding:** Restricted emergence may aid the sexes in finding one another. Macan notes this has not been proved. He also observes that similar species differ in emergence pattern (mass versus dispersed), which weakens the idea.
2. **Temperature:** Following [[Ide 1935 Study|Ide (1935)]], certain species may pass the summer as eggs and die at lethally high temperatures if they emerge too late or hatch too early. Pleskot (1958) argues that temperature acts indirectly.
3. **Oxygen:** Pleskot proposes a critical pre-emergence period when oxygen demand is high and oxygen transfer from water to nymphal tissues is difficult. Above a threshold temperature, even saturated water may be insufficient, so emergence must occur before that temperature is reached.
4. **Competition:** In some genera of Ephemeroptera (Ide 1935) and Plecoptera (Illies 1952), species succeed one another through summer. Competition may prevent any one species from extending its emergence period.

**Consequences of short emergence**

- Macan's second assumption is that staggered hatching benefits herbivores. If young hatch at different times and grow and change diet, environmental resources can be exploited more fully.
- Macan's own data on R. semicolorata (Macan 1957) show adults on the wing from mid-May to mid-July or later, abundant for just over a month. The first nymphs appeared in August. Tiny nymphs (1–2 mm) were found every month through January, a span of six months.
- The likely explanation is wide variation in egg-stage duration, though this was not yet proven.

**Study-site contexts**

- Macan notes his streams at Ambleside "never freeze over."
- Caspers reports that small ponds near Hamburg show winter fauna reduction, mainly from oxygen depletion under ice.
- Johnson describes a Malayan (equatorial) setting where temperature and oxygen variation is too small to explain restricted emergence, and proposes flood-related water dilution as an alternative.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth and production calculations are biased by asynchronous hatching**

- Standard growth estimates (sampling at intervals, averaging lengths, and differencing successive means) are invalid if part of the population is still in the egg stage at the first sample, because it depresses the later mean.
- Production studies that ignore species individuality may also be badly wrong. Macan cites [[Allen 1951 Trout Study|Allen (1951, 1952)]], who found that [[Salmo Trutta]] apparently consumed "seventeen times more than there was present for it to eat."

**2. Trout population data illustrate the scale of early mortality**

- Of 1000 trout fry at the start of the season, 15 were alive six months later. The 15 survivors weighed about 1½ lb (value garbled in the scanned source). The total flesh produced by the fish that died was reported as about 3½ lb (also scan-garbled).
- After three years, one survivor remained from the original 1000, and a total of 10 lb of flesh had been produced.
- Macan argues that much production occurs in early life stages, which many sampling methods fail to catch. Allen could safely base mortality on the 985 deaths only because all eggs had hatched by the first sample.
- If hatching is spread over six months, as in R. semicolorata, productivity estimates could contain "an enormous error."

**3. Discussion contributions**

- **Johnson:** Restricted emergence occurs in Malayan species despite little temperature or oxygen variation. Flood-related dilution may be a factor.
- **Le Cren:** Asked about physiological mechanisms behind variable egg-hatching time. Macan cited [[Gros 1923 Egg Development Study|Gros (1923)]], who found [[Ecdyonurus Forcipula]] eggs hatched in 15 days on an aquarium bottom but took 46 days in the axils of [[Fontinalis]] leaves, possibly because light affects development.
- **Hynes:** [[Leuctra Inermis]] shows a steady decline in numbers over winter despite recruitment from hatching eggs.
- **Caspers:** Winter faunal reduction under ice in small ponds (see Context above).
- **Brook and Morgan:** Larger and smaller larvae of algae-feeding forms (e.g., [[Leptocerus Aterrimus]]) may eat the same food at the same time. Feeding differences may matter more in carnivores and macrophyte-feeders.
- **Morgan:** [[Caenis Horaria]] nymphs were found from August to about April, while adults emerged only in June and July. This parallels the prolonged hatching seen in R. semicolorata.

**Overall conclusion:** Short emergence periods are real and probably selected, but their causes remain unresolved. The more practical lesson concerns methodology: prolonged hatching can invalidate growth and production estimates unless it is measured and accounted for.
