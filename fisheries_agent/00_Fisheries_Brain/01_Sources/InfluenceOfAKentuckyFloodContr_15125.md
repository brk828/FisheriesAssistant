# Influence of a Kentucky flood-control reservoir on the tailwater and headwater fish populations

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Centrarchidae #reservoir-ecology #tailwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study (Swink and Jacobs, 1983) examined the effects of the [[Green River Lake]] flood-control dam (completed 1969, Kentucky, USA) on fish communities both downstream (tailwater) and upstream (headwater) of the impoundment. Comparisons of pre-impoundment (1960–1962, rotenone) and post-impoundment (1979–1980, electrofishing) fish samples revealed that reservoir-dwelling species — notably [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*) and [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) — migrated both downstream into the tailwaters and upstream into the unregulated river above the reservoir, becoming abundant in both zones. Conversely, riverine species such as [[Black Redhorse]] (*Moxostoma duquesnei*) and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) declined in the tailwaters, likely due to reduced hypolimnetic discharge temperatures. The study demonstrates that straightforward headwater-vs-tailwater comparisons can be confounded by bidirectional fish migration out of reservoirs, and cautions against using such comparisons to infer pre-impoundment tailwater conditions for migratory reservoir species.

## Background & Description

This is a community-composition and population-ecology study rather than a morphological analysis; however, size-structure data were reported for one focal species:

- **White Crappie (*Pomoxis annularis*)**: Length-frequency analysis comparing reservoir cove samples (August 1979) to tailwater Station 1 samples (November 1979) showed two size cohorts.
  - Reservoir (August): peaks at 65–89 mm and 141–166 mm.
  - Tailwater (November): peaks at 90–114 mm and 166–190 mm.
  - The shift in peak lengths (~25 mm increase) is consistent with growth over the intervening ~3 months, supporting the hypothesis that tailwater crappies originated from the reservoir population rather than representing a distinct cohort.

No other detailed morphometric or meristic data were reported; the paper focuses on relative abundance (density, percent composition) across sampling stations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study System:**
- **Location**: [[Green River]], Kentucky, USA (Ohio River drainage); watershed area 22,896 km².
- **Impoundment**: [[Green River Lake]] Dam — 43.6 m high, forming a 3,322-ha flood-control reservoir; completed 1969; drainage area 1,766 km².
- **Sampling stations**:
  - Tailwater Stations 1, 2, 3 — located 1.5, 10.5, and 22.5 km below the dam, respectively.
  - Headwater Station A — located 19.0 km above the reservoir (unregulated river).
  - Two reservoir coves (north arm ~10 km from dam; south arm ~5 km from dam), sampled via rotenone.
- **Substrate composition**: Predominantly gravel (60–80%) at tailwater stations, with boulder/bedrock and cobble components; Station A substrate was more heterogeneous (40% boulder/bedrock, 30% gravel, 25% cobble, 5% silt).
- **Habitat features**: Station 1 had sparse cover (boulders, riffles, shallow <0.5 m over 40% of area); Station 2 had abundant submerged log cover, few riffles; Station 3 had diverse habitat (pools, riffles, large rocks, submerged logs); Station A had two large pools separated by a deep riffle.

**Thermal Regime Alteration:**
- Hypolimnetic discharge reduced average maximum summer tailwater temperatures by as much as 6°C (from 25°C to 19°C in July) post-impoundment — a key driver of community change, enabling a put-and-take [[Trout Fishery]] but disadvantaging warmwater riverine species.

**Flow Regime:**
- Minimum regulated flow: 2.4 m³/second; observed range during study: 0.5–179.7 m³/second.
- Abundance of [[White Crappie]] and [[Gizzard Shad]] at Station 1 was positively correlated with reservoir discharge volume (Spearman's rank correlation: r = 0.7619 and r = 0.9583 respectively, N = 8, P < 0.05), supporting a discharge-driven migration mechanism out of the reservoir.

**Community Structure — Pre-Impoundment (1960–1962):**
Dominated by (in decreasing abundance): minnows ([[Cyprinidae]]), [[Redhorse]] (*Moxostoma* spp.), [[Longear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis megalotis*), darters ([[Percidae]]), and [[Madtoms]] (*Noturus* spp.). Other common taxa: [[Rock Bass]] (*Ambloplites rupestris*), [[Spotted Bass]] (*Micropterus punctulatus*), [[Spotted Sucker]] (*Minytrema melanops*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*), [[Northern Hog Sucker]] (*Hypentelium nigricans*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and [[Channel Catfish]]. Crappies and gizzard shad were rare; [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) were never collected.

**Community Structure — Post-Impoundment (1979–1980):**
- Tailwater fish densities: Station 1 = 187.3 fish/ha; Station 2 = 135.0 fish/ha; Station 3 = 170.9 fish/ha.
- Headwater Station A density = 249.1 fish/ha (highest of all stations).
- Reservoir cove samples were dominated by gizzard shad (64%) and sunfishes (33%).

## Key Findings

1. **Bidirectional Reservoir Fish Migration**: Reservoir-associated species (white crappie, gizzard shad, common carp) moved both downstream into tailwaters and upstream into headwater reaches, complicating simple upstream/downstream comparisons as indicators of impoundment effects.

2. **Discharge-Correlated Abundance**: White crappie and gizzard shad abundance at tailwater Station 1 (43% of total catch) was significantly positively correlated with reservoir discharge volume, implicating turbine/spillway passage as a recruitment source for tailwater populations.

3. **Length-Frequency Evidence of Origin**: Matching bimodal length distributions between reservoir cove and tailwater white crappie samples (with an appropriate growth increment) provided direct evidence that tailwater crappies originated from the reservoir population.

4. **Decline of Riverine Species in Tailwaters**: [[Black Redhorse]] and [[Channel Catfish]] were substantially less abundant in the tailwaters than in the headwater reach, likely due to reduced post-impoundment water temperatures (hypolimnetic release effect), consistent with patterns reported at other tailwaters (e.g., Norfork Reservoir, Arkansas).

5. **Community Composition Shifts**:
   - Increased: gizzard shad (<1% → 5%); common carp (0% → 10%); sunfishes (12% → 51%, driven mainly by crappies).
   - Decreased: minnows (58% → 8%); suckers (21% → 17%); catfishes (5% → 1%).
   - Seven cyprinid species reportedly eliminated from the tailwater since impoundment (citing Hoyt and Robison 1982).
   - Post-impoundment catfish catches lost the madtom component present pre-impoundment.

6. **Habitat-Driven Micro-Distribution**: Species distributions among tailwater stations correlated with local habitat features — e.g., greenside/rainbow darters and logperch favored riffle-rich Station 1; smallmouth bass favored rocky, silt-free substrate at Station 1; golden/shorthead redhorse and bluntnose minnows favored deep pools and vegetation at Station 3.

7. **Two Mechanisms of Tailwater Community Change**: (1) downstream migration/recruitment of reservoir species, and (2) temperature-driven reduction of native riverine species abundance.

8. **Management Implications**: Cold hypolimnetic releases enabled establishment of a put-and-take [[Trout Fishery]] in the tailwater, partially offsetting economic/recreational loss of native redhorse and catfish populations, though at higher management cost than a self-sustaining warmwater fishery. Potential future hydropower retrofitting could reduce reservoir fish emigration and further alter tailwater community dynamics, with turbine passage mortality a concern (per Turbak et al. 1981).

9. **Methodological Caution**: The authors emphasize that headwater-tailwater comparisons are valid primarily for species that seldom migrate upstream; for highly mobile reservoir-derived species, such comparisons risk erroneous conclusions about impoundment effects.
