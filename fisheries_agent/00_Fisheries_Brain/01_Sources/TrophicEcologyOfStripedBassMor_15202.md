# Trophic Ecology of Striped Bass in Lake Texoma, a Freshwater Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the diet of introduced [[Morone Saxatilis]] (Striped Bass) in [[Lake Texoma]], a 36,000 ha reservoir on the Oklahoma-Texas border. Analysis of 1,845 stomachs collected year-round over five years shows that the predator consumed a very narrow range of prey. Its diet was dominated by shad of the genus [[Dorosoma]] and by aquatic insects. The only marked diet change was a seasonal shift from shad to insects in spring and early summer. The bass did not switch to the many other fish species available in the reservoir, even during shad scarcity and starvation-level condition in 1982. The authors describe this fidelity as an unresolved "enigma," since general foraging theory predicts broader diets when prey is scarce.

## Background & Description

[[Morone Saxatilis]] is a large, highly mobile piscivore (to more than 15 kg) native to the Atlantic and Gulf coasts of North America. In its native range it is a generalist feeder on small fishes in nearshore and estuarine waters. Where introduced, its diet varies by setting. In some freshwater reservoirs it specializes on clupeids, while in others it is more catholic in prey use. The species has been stocked in more than 100 large North American reservoirs, where it is a prized gamefish.

Concern has been raised that the species might prey on juvenile [[Black Basses]] (*Micropterus* spp.) or compete with them for food. Several earlier reservoir studies are cited as contrasting cases: [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]] (Stevens, 1958, 1969, 1979) and [[Lake Mead]] (bass shifted to crayfish after shad and trout declined).

Study area: Lake Texoma is a shallow impoundment (to 18 m deep in the study area) of the Red and Washita rivers. Sampling was concentrated near the University of Oklahoma Biological Station, about 40 km uplake from the dam. Three habitats were sampled:
- [[Mayfield Flats]], a shallow cove of 4-6 ha with submerged stumps and dead willows, protected from prevailing south winds.
- [[Buncombe Creek]], a tributary bay about 1 km wide and 10-12 m deep at the collecting site.
- The main channel, about 18 m deep and 2-3 km wide.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Overnight [[Gill Netting]] was conducted from April 1981 to July 1984, usually twice monthly, using six experimental nets, with surface and bottom sets in each habitat. In March-October 1985, four to five nets were fished in the cove and in Buncombe Creek. The main channel station was dropped that year. Stomachs were taken from a minimum of 25 adult or subadult bass (over 200 mm TL) per date, plus all bass over 2 kg. Food items were identified, counted, and volume-estimated. Prey availability was assessed with weekly [[Shoreline Seining]] at four littoral locations, plus data from the Oklahoma Department of Wildlife Conservation (Mauck, 1987). Diet similarity between months and years was compared using [[Morisita's Index]].

**Prey availability.** Shad abundance varied strongly between years. Dorosoma were abundant in warm months of 1981, 1983 and 1985, but scarce in 1982 and 1984. A winter kill in January 1982 nearly eliminated [[Threadfin Shad]] (*D. petenense*) from the study area. Other forage fishes were common in the littoral zone, including [[Inland Silverside]] (*Menidia beryllina*), minnows (Cyprinidae), and sunfish (*Lepomis*). The authors estimate that most littoral fish collected over the previous decade were 20-100 mm TL, which is a suitable prey size.

**Environmental variability.** Two major floods during the study raised the reservoir to its second and third highest levels in recorded history. These floods were followed by long periods of high turbidity. Turbidity also prevented underwater observation of feeding behaviour.

## Key Findings

**Diet composition**
- Of 1,845 stomachs examined, shad of the genus *Dorosoma* (gizzard and threadfin shad, which were not separated in stomach samples) made up 33.9% of the total stomach volume.
- *Dorosoma* comprised 83.3% of the volume of fish identifiable to at least genus.
- [[Inland Silverside]] made up 4.3% of volume and [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) 2.1%.
- Only one small [[Largemouth Bass]] was found, despite angler concern about predation on black basses.
- No other fish species constituted more than a trace of the diet.
- Mean percent volume of *Dorosoma* was significantly greater than that of all other identifiable fish combined (ANOVA, F = 38.39; P < 0.01).
- Freshwater drum in stomachs were typically juveniles under 125 mm TL.

**Insects and the spring switch**
- Aquatic insects, mainly [[Mayfly Nymphs]] (Ephemeroptera) and [[Chironomid Larvae]] (chironomid larvae and pupae), were important in spring and early summer, at 29-45% of volume in those periods.
- Insects were a substantial diet component for 2-4 months each year. Their share dropped sharply after June or July.
- Insect percent volume differed significantly among seasons, peaking in spring (F = 13.53; P < 0.01). Fish volume was lowest in spring (F = 14.15; P < 0.01).
- The shift to insects did not simply track insect availability. [[Hexagenia]] mayflies were abundant in autumn, when they were virtually absent from bass stomachs (Sublette, 1953).
- Insect species dominating the spring diet varied by year. Chironomids dominated in 1981 and 1984, mayflies in 1982 and 1983, and the two were roughly equal in 1985.

**Lack of switching**
- In 1982 and 1984, when shad were scarce, bass still did not switch to other available fish.
- The only exception was April-June 1984, when freshwater drum made up 11.1% of the diet, against 6.6% for *Dorosoma*. Chironomids, however, made up 31.6% of the diet in that period.
- Morisita's index showed diets were highly similar (≥ 0.74) between all adjacent months from July to February. Ten of 11 instances of similarity below 0.60 occurred between March and July.

**Condition and starvation**
- Mean condition coefficients (K) for mid-August to mid-September differed significantly among years (F = 37.99; P < 0.01). The lowest value was 0.84 in 1982, when shad were scarce. Other years ranged from 0.99 to 1.05.
- In 1982, most bass were emaciated, with sunken abdomens and little remaining musculature. Despite this, they did not shift to other fish.
- Condition also declined in spring-early summer, when bass were eating mostly insects.

**Prey size**
- Modal length of prey fish in stomachs was 20-60 mm under all conditions.
- Dorosoma over 120 mm were scarce in stomachs, even though *D. cepedianum* over 200 mm were abundant in gill nets.
- The authors hypothesize that large shad from the previous year's spawn are too big for subadult bass in late spring and midsummer, while young-of-year shad are too small until early June.
- Carlander (1969) reports a mean length of 130 mm TL for *D. cepedianum* at age 1.

**Interannual and seasonal diet similarity**
- Diets were highly similar between adjacent years in autumn, winter and summer (Morisita's index above 0.76, with one exception).
- Spring diets differed markedly between years, with similarity values of 0.41-0.77 (Table VII).

**Comparison with other systems**
- Stevens (1979) found non-clupeid fish in only 20 of about 3,600 bass stomachs at Santee-Cooper Reservoir. About 40% of the bass there starved rather than switching to other prey.
- The authors argue this pattern is poorly suited to reservoirs where primary prey is vulnerable to environmental disasters, such as cold winters.

**Proposed explanations and limitations**
- Shad are schooling, silvery and slab-sided, which may make them visually attractive prey. Fishermen have reported bass attacking shad schools, but this has not been documented in the literature.
- Offshore, the authors believe bass have a limited set of available prey (shad, freshwater drum, and silversides). Inshore, [[Lepomis]] sunfish, crappie (*Pomoxis*), minnows, and juvenile [[White Bass]] are common, yet bass showed little use of them.
- The authors conclude the enigma of bass "starving amidst plenty" will remain unresolved until direct feeding observations and experiments are possible.
