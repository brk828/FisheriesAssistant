# Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) Management and Recovery Planning Memorandum (1980)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service memorandum (Region 2, Albuquerque, October 3, 1980) concerns the review and revision of a draft [[Razorback Sucker]] management plan. The species was not formally listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]], so no recovery team or official recovery plan existed; an ad hoc group was instead developing a management plan for an unlisted species. The memo reports a declining range and population across the [[Colorado River Basin]], the absence of any maintenance attempts beyond six adults held for five months in 1965, and a multi-year agenda (through FY 1982 and beyond) covering a Lake Mohave survey, hatchery propagation, habitat protection and public relations. The source also includes a species account (morphology, past distribution, decline, life history, and recovery objectives) and year-by-year program tables.

## Background & Description

**Morphology.** Adults have an elongated head and a laterally compressed, triangular body in cross-section. A sharp dorsal keel is supported by enlarged neural spines. Lateral-line scales number 66 to 87. The dorsal fin has 13 to 16 rays (usually 14 or 15). The mouth is inferior and surrounded by papillose lips. Adults may reach lengths near a meter and weights above five kilograms. Breeding males develop nuptial tubercles and enlarged heads, and their coloration intensifies. Young are difficult to distinguish from other catostomids such as the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]].

**Nomenclature.** The name "razorback" was applied by anglers and is a literal translation of the genus name *Xyrauchen*. The common name "humpback sucker" is noted as a less preferred alternative.

**Life history.** Spawning adults move upstream in spring. Spawning occurs over gravel in shallow water, with one female accompanied by two to twelve males. Eggs are transparent and adhesive. Feeding includes zooplankton, diatoms and midge larvae, and feeding behavior was observed under floodlights at night. No data exist on growth rates, sex ratios or longevity.

**Captive observations.** The six adults held at Arizona State University in 1965 were easily handled, fed readily and showed no disease or parasite problems in a small 3 × 0.3 × 0.3 m raceway.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical distribution:** Before the 1880s the species ranged through the upper Colorado basin, into Wyoming and Colorado, and into northwest Mexico. It likely also occupied tributaries such as the San Juan, Virgin and upper Gila Rivers. Barrier falls such as Grand Falls and Quartzite Falls may have limited its range in some streams.
- **Habitat preference:** Adults favored quiet backwaters and sloughs of larger rivers. In the 1880s the species was abundant enough in the San Pedro River to support a commercial fishery, and some populations supported commercial fishing into the late 1800s.
- **Causes of decline:** Decline coincides with river modification beginning in the early 1900s, reduced stream flow from decreased precipitation, channelization of the lower Colorado, and arroyo cutting in the late 1800s. Introduced fishes from the Mississippi River valley, increasing sharply from about 1930, may also have contributed.
- **Present status:** The species is extinct in the Gila basin. The last collection of young-of-the-year from the lower basin was in 1950 near Willow Beach. Populations persisted in the Verde system until at least 1954. Surviving individuals were reported in Lake Mead and Lake Mohave, and a small group was reported near Dinosaur National Monument. Hybridization with the Flannelmouth Sucker was observed below Glen Canyon Dam, which the memo attributes to habitat disturbance.
- **Legal status:** The species was protected by state law in California and is recognized as a threatened species in several Colorado basin states.

## Key Findings

1. **Management plan status:** The 1975 draft plan by [[W.L. Minckley]] and Gary Gibbons is largely out of date. The author requested that it be salvaged where possible and edited for a new draft to be produced for review.
2. **Protection status:** The species was not federally listed, so no recovery team existed. An ad hoc group of state and federal agencies was developing a management plan, with a target of an official plan by FY 1982.
3. **Immediate actions (January 1981):** Plans were made to coordinate netting of razorback suckers and bonytail chubs at Lake Mohave, with the four adult fish held at Dexter National Fish Hatchery (New Mexico) to be transported. Transport plans included a fish tanker, boats and a one-acre pond.
4. **Recovery objectives:** Restoration within the native range was deemed nearly impossible at the full basin scale. Stated goals were to identify unmodified river segments, quantify pristine habitat conditions, locate potential reintroduction sites, study the species in nature and in hatcheries, and eventually release hatchery young into the wild.
5. **Dual management goal:** The plan envisions a multiple-use fishery, including a game fish for anglers and divers, and potentially a commercial fishery, managed alongside other fishery programs at a sustained yield.
6. **Five-year agenda (FY 1974 to FY 1980 and extended program):** Actions include:
   - Completing a survey of the Lower Colorado River, with standardized methods and segmented responsibilities among agencies
   - Life history studies and artificial propagation via university contracts
   - Establishing a hatchery and rearing facility
   - Releasing young fish into suitable habitat and monitoring survival
   - Reviewing water projects that could affect habitat
   - Public relations through news releases, magazines and radio
   - Building a display center at the Phoenix Zoo, which would also hold razorback suckers
   - Extending these activities through FY 1980 in an extended management program
