# Fishes of the Kansas River System in Relation to Zoogeography of the Great Plains

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater-fish #zoogeography

## Executive Summary

This monograph by [[Artie L. Metcalf]] (University of Kansas Publications, Museum of Natural History, Vol. 17, No. 8, pp. 23–189, published March 24, 1966) inventories the fishes of the [[Kansas River Basin]] and interprets their distributions in relation to the physiography, geology, and preglacial drainage history of the [[Great Plains]] and western Interior Lowlands. The report has three stated aims: to determine which species occur, to relate their distributional patterns to environmental conditions, and to connect these patterns to the history and development of the Basin and the zoogeography of Plains fishes.

The author reports 71 species of fishes thought to be native to the system, seven species believed introduced by man, six with inconclusive native status, and eight historically recorded species no longer known from the system. Fish diversity is highest in clear, cool upland streams of the [[Flint Hills]] and [[Shawnee Escarpments]], and lowest in turbid, silty streams over glacial till, loess, and the Dissected High Plains. The fauna is interpreted as a mixture of stocks derived from three preglacial drainages: the Plains Stream System, the north-flowing Missouri (Hudson Bay) system, and the Teays/Mississippi system.

## Background & Description

The Kansas (Kaw) River System drains about 61,440 square miles and extends roughly 480 miles linearly from eastern Colorado to its confluence with the [[Missouri River]] at Kansas City. The mainstream forms at Junction City, Kansas, from the [[Smoky Hill River]] and [[Republican River]]. Major tributaries include the Saline, Solomon, [[Big Blue River]], [[Wakarusa River]], and Delaware rivers. Only the lower mainstream remains largely unimpounded; numerous reservoirs (e.g., [[Tuttle Creek Reservoir]], Cedar Bluff, Kanopolis, Harlan County) have been built, mainly in the western Basin.

The report is organized around physiographic provinces: Glacial Till-plains, Osage Cuestas, Douglas Lowlands, Shawnee Escarpments, Wabaunsee Group uplands, Flint Hills, Dissected High Plains, Ogallala Formation exposures, and Loess Plains with Pleistocene sands and gravels. Each province is linked to stream character (gradient, turbidity, bottom type) and to its fish fauna. The author also reviews pre-settlement and post-settlement accounts, including explorer journals (e.g., [[Fremont]], [[Thomas Say]], [[Joseph Tidball]]) and early ichthyological collections by [[Hay]], [[Cragin]], and [[Graham]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography and water permanence.** Flint Hills streams have high gradients, rubble bottoms, and clear water, and support the most diverse fauna in the Basin. A 1958 survey of the Blue River in Kansas recorded 53 species (Minckley, 1959), compared with 83 species recorded for the entire Kansas River Basin in this study. The Ogallala Formation, where it contacts underlying [[Niobrara Chalk]] and [[Pierre Shale]] aquicludes, feeds perennial springs that sustain cool, clear streams. Dissected High Plains and Loess Plains streams are typically intermittent and silty.

**Springs and aquifers.** The Ogallala Formation was described as "of great economic importance throughout the Great Plains." Springs issuing at its base supply Wallace County, Kansas, and Dundy County, Nebraska, where a combined 30 species were recorded, a surprisingly high number for this longitude.

**Pollution and sedimentation.** Municipal and industrial pollution was largely controlled except in the Kansas City area. Intensive cultivation has increased turbidity and siltation. Siltation is implicated in the decline of [[Pleurocera acuta]], a snail intolerant of silt, which appears extirpated from the Basin.

**Drought and climate.** Recurrent drought affects flow and fish survival. In 1954, sixty-one municipal water systems in the Kansas portion were inadequate.

**Beavers.** Beaver ponding in western tributaries creates habitat for fishes on small, low-gradient streams. Repopulation by beavers was judged beneficial, especially for less tolerant species.

## Key Findings

- **Species inventory:** 71 native species; 7 introduced; 6 with uncertain native status; 8 historical species no longer present. Sixty-plus species are individually treated in the accounts section.
- **Diversity hotspots:** Flint Hills and Shawnee Escarpments (clear, rubbly streams) and spring-fed western streams (Ogallala contact zones and Pleistocene gravel areas) support the most diverse faunas. Species found chiefly in these areas include [[Notropis topeka]], [[Notropis rubellus]], [[Chrosomus erythrogaster]], [[Hybopsis biguttata]], [[Noturus exilis]], and [[Etheostoma spectabile pulchellum]].
- **Low-diversity areas:** Glacial till, Loess Plains (Nebraska), and Dissected High Plains yield few species. Ubiquitous species here include [[Notropis lutrensis]], [[Pimephales promelas]], [[Ictalurus melas]], and [[Lepomis cyanellus]].
- **Historical decline:** Early records show species such as [[Hybopsis biguttata]], [[Chrosomus erythrogaster]], and [[Notropis topeka]] formerly occurred more widely. Metcalf attributes contractions to watershed modification, siltation, and increased intermittency. Several species have been reduced to relict populations, most notably in Mill Creek, Wabaunsee County.
- **Introduced species:** Including [[Cyprinus carpio]], [[Pomoxis annularis]], [[Stizostedion vitreum]], [[Perca flavescens]], [[Salmo trutta]], and [[Salmo gairdneri]].
- **Zoogeographic origins:** Three preglacial drainages are proposed: the Preglacial Plains (Ancestral Plains) Stream, which flowed south west of the Flint Hills; the Preglacial Hudson Bay (north-flowing Missouri) system; and the Teays/Mississippi system. Glacial deflection, stream capture, and Pleistocene mingling of stocks produced the present mixed fauna.
- **Big-river fishes:** Species such as [[Hiodon alosoides]], [[Lepisosteus osseus]], and [[Polyodon spathula]] reached the Basin from the east along the Missouri/Mississippi system; the [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus platorynchus*) is common in the lower river, and the [[Pallid Sturgeon]] (*S. albus*) is rarer, with no Lawrence records since 1952.
- **Morphological variation:** Plains populations often show a smaller eye, fewer and smaller scales (more scales in some western forms), chubbier heads, and more decurved snouts than eastern populations. Metcalf interprets these as partly genetic and partly environmental, and considers many differences clinal rather than taxonomically significant.
- **Taxonomic proposals:** The report recognizes [[Hybognathus placitus]] and [[Hybognathus nuchalis]] as distinct species, and treats the Plains–eastern forms of [[Etheostoma spectabile]] and [[Notropis stramineus]] as subspecies, while declining to recognize subspecies for the clinal [[Semotilus atromaculatus]] and [[Campostoma anomalum]] variants.
- **Methodological note:** Identifications and locality records draw on museum material (KU, USNM, UMMZ, MCZ, ANSP, KSU) and on extensive 1950s–1960s field surveys. Metcalf notes that some historic localities (e.g., "Platte River near Fort Riley") are likely erroneous, and that a number of Cope-described species from the Bryan expedition likely came from outside the Basin.
