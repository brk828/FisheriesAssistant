# Returns From Tagged Channel Catfish in the Des Moines River, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 study by Harry M. Harrison, published in the Iowa Academy of Science proceedings, reports mark-recapture results for [[Channel Catfish]] tagged in the [[Des Moines River]] from 1947 to 1949, with recoveries continuing through 1952. A total of 4,032 fish were tagged at 28 stations along roughly 500 miles of stream, and 101 tags (about 2%) were recovered. The study concludes that internal tagging is of very limited value for stream population inventories, that catfish move short distances (most recaptures were at or near their release point), and it provides preliminary growth generalizations.

## Background & Description

The [[Channel Catfish]] was identified by [[Speaker (1948)]], through a survey of 1,800 Iowa anglers, as the most important sport fish in Iowa's inland streams. The study set three objectives:

1. Test whether tagged fish could be used to inventory stream populations.
2. Determine the extent of catfish movement in Iowa's inland streams.
3. Obtain factual data on growth rates in presumed good catfish habitat.

**Tagging method:** An internal bar-type tag, made from the serial-number portion of small metal opercular fish tags (about 1/32 inch thick, 3/16 inch wide, 1/2 inch long), was inserted through a small belly incision ahead of the pelvic fin. The adipose fin was clipped on all tagged fish to aid recognition. Fish were tagged and released at the capture site, and date, location, length, weight, water stage, and tag number were recorded. Recovery information was filed on 4x6 cards. The metric system was used for finer accuracy in weight changes.

**Tag limitations:** Tags were often embedded in visceral fat or walled off by peritoneal-like tissue, making them hard to find. Some tag expulsion occurred, possibly shortly after marking, and no tag was found in about 10% of recaptured fish. The advantage was that internal tags did not impede normal behavior.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** The [[Des Moines River]] drainage, including the main stream to the Minnesota state line, the East Fork to Algona, the Boone to Goldfield, and the Raccoon upstream to Auburn.
- **Physical character:** The river consists of alternating deep and shallow water areas. The author suggests shallow reaches may act as movement barriers. The [[Mississippi River]], by contrast, has a nine-foot navigation channel offering deep-water passage.
- **Dams and fishways:** Modified [[Denil-Type Fishway]] traps at dams, notably at Fort Dodge and Humboldt, were operated throughout the open-water season and produced most recoveries from 1948 onward.
- **Current and attraction:** Catfish are attracted to current at the foot of fishways. The deep water below dams may itself attract catfish, which the author argues reflects limited movement rather than a dam barrier.
- **Comparison studies:** [[Bangham and Bennington (1938)]] reported that Ohio stream fishes were acclimated to their habitat and rarely moved long distances, a finding the present work supports. The [[Upper Mississippi Conservation Committee]] tagging work (382 tags) found greater movement in the Mississippi, with journeys over 50 miles common and one fish moving 180 miles.

## Key Findings

**Tagging effort and returns**
- 4,032 channel catfish were tagged: 2,484 in 1947, 652 in 1948, and 895 in 1949.
- 101 tags (about 2%) were recovered: 12 (1947), 25 (1948), 52 (1949), 6 (1950), 2 (1951), and 4 (1952).
- 16 tags were returned by fishermen; 85 were taken in regular netting or at fishway traps. 69 of the 85 came from fishway traps, mostly at Fort Dodge (35) and Humboldt (32).

**Inventory value**
- About 45,202 catfish were examined for tags over six years, and only 87 (0.002%) were tagged.
- Tagged fish were too rare to support statistically meaningful stream population estimates. At fishways, about 3.2% of 1,918 tagged fish were retaken.
- The author concludes tagging has very limited value for population studies, even on short reaches.

**Movement**
- Only 33 of the 101 recovered fish had moved from their tagging site, and 15 of these moved no more than one mile.
- Maximum downstream movement was 32 miles (tagged 387 days). Maximum upstream movement was 28 miles (tagged 34 days).
- Of 25 recoveries from fish with no dam association, nine had not moved, four moved no more than one mile, three moved under five miles, seven moved under ten miles, and three moved more than ten miles. Twelve moved upstream and four moved downstream.
- Most Des Moines River fish were released within 100 yards of capture.
- Of 76 fishway recoveries, 59 were retaken at the same dam, with little upstream retrial effort after release.
- Several hundred fish tagged above dams were not caught at the next dam upstream, even though it was only 5.5 miles away.
- A local population of about 10,000 finclipped fish in a pool between two dams (about five miles apart) moved freely within its pool but rarely crossed a shallow one-mile reach to the adjacent pool.

**Growth (preliminary generalizations)**
1. Length increase is usually most rapid in fish under 12 inches total length.
2. Length increases for younger fish may be fairly uniform in natural waters.
3. Annual gains under stream conditions may run 3-4 inches for fish under 12 inches.
4. For fish over 12 inches, annual gains are much reduced; about 1 inch per year is average.

The author notes the growth sample is small and the methods do not meet the standards of formal growth studies.
