# An Early Hepatoma Epizootic in Rainbow Trout, Salmo gairdnerii

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #fish-pathology #aquaculture

## Executive Summary
This paper (Wales & Sinnhuber, 1966) documents a previously under-reported **1936–1942 epizootic of hepatoma (liver cancer)** in domesticated [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) at two California state hatcheries — **Hot Creek Hatchery** and **Mt. Shasta Hatchery** — predating the more widely publicized 1950–1960 nationwide epizootic. Both outbreaks were temporally and causally linked to the introduction of **dry plant and animal meal-based diets** (particularly cottonseed meal, salmon egg meal, and wheat middlings) replacing traditional fresh slaughterhouse by-product diets. The authors systematically rule out chlorinated pesticides, fungicides, and solvent-extraction residues as causes of the earlier outbreak on chronological grounds, and instead implicate **oxidized lipids/sterols** and, most strongly in retrospect, **aflatoxin contamination of cottonseed meal** (from mold growth, e.g. *Aspergillus flavus*) as the likely carcinogenic agents. This paper is historically significant as an early piece of evidence in what became the seminal discovery linking **aflatoxin** to hepatocarcinogenesis in vertebrates.

## Background & Description
- **Species affected:** Domesticated [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*), originating from a fall-spawning, selectively bred brood stock traced to Missouri/Utah hatchery lines domesticated for >50 years.
- **Growth characteristics:** The domesticated brood stock exhibited growth rates up to **twice that of wild trout**, a factor the authors link to increased carcinogenic susceptibility (paralleling findings in rapidly-growing laboratory mice).
- **Pathology — Hepatoma:**
  - Presents as **liver tumors (hepatocellular neoplasms)**, first histologically confirmed via tissue sent to the Rockefeller Institute (1937–38).
  - Minimum developmental time: **6–8 months** at 56°F (Hot Creek); approximately **2 years** at 49°F (Mt. Shasta) — indicating strong **temperature-dependence of tumor development rate**.
  - **Sex-differential susceptibility:** Females suffered notably higher mortality than males (e.g., October 1937 Hot Creek die-off: 30/815 females vs. 6/226 males died, all confirmed hepatoma-positive on necropsy).
- **Susceptibility comparison:** Rainbow trout are described as **far more susceptible to hepatoma** than other domestically-reared fish species; susceptibility of wild, migratory (steelhead) rainbow was not established.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Land-based hatchery aquaculture systems (not natural wild populations), representing an anthropogenic/captive rearing context rather than a natural ecological niche.
- **Site comparison:**
  - **Hot Creek Hatchery:** Warmer water (~56°F), with notably **high boron and fluoride content** — initially suspected (but largely discounted) as contributing to hepatoma and [[Furunculosis]] susceptibility.
  - **Mt. Shasta Hatchery:** Cooler water (~48–49°F), chemically dissimilar from Hot Creek; the marked water chemistry differences between sites were used to argue **against** water chemistry as a primary causal factor, since hepatoma occurred at both despite dissimilar chemistries.
- **Diet as environmental driver:** The critical "ecological" variable was diet composition — shift from fresh slaughterhouse by-products (beef liver, heart, lung, horse meat, fresh ocean fish) to dry meal-based pellet diets (cottonseed meal, salmon egg meal, abalone meal, wheat middlings, dry skim milk, kelp meal).
- **Co-occurring disease:** [[Furunculosis]] was also observed in the Hot Creek brood stock alongside hepatoma, suggesting compounded environmental/husbandry stress.

## Key Findings
- **Two documented epizootics** of rainbow trout hepatoma in California hatcheries: **1936–1942** (subject of this paper) and **1950–1960** (previously more widely publicized).
- Both epizootics **closely coincided with introduction of dry meal diets**, especially those containing **cottonseed meal** and/or **fish meal**.
- **Chronology (Hot Creek Hatchery):**
  - 1933: Eggs from selected fall-spawning rainbow received from Missouri/Utah stock.
  - 1934: No dry meals in diet.
  - 1935: Diet = 30% beef liver, 35% salmon egg meal, 35% abalone meal.
  - 1936: Cottonseed meal added (~27% each of abalone, cottonseed, salmon egg meal by 1936).
  - 1937: Hepatoma first noted; cottonseed meal later replaced by wheat middlings due to poor brown trout egg fertility.
  - 1939: Dry meals discontinued.
  - 1942: Hepatoma no longer a serious problem.
- **Chronology (Mt. Shasta Hatchery):**
  - 1936: Wheat middlings, salmon egg meal, abalone meal, dry milk, cottonseed meal added to fresh meat diet.
  - 1937: Fingerlings transferred from Hot Creek to establish new brood stock; cottonseed meal removed from brood diet in December due to egg viability concerns.
  - 1939: Hepatoma first noted (in 3-year-old brood fish).
  - 1942: Hepatoma no longer a serious problem.
- **Causal reasoning:** By process of elimination (removing wheat middlings, salmon egg meal, and fresh fish as suspects based on later feeding trials — Wolf & Jackson, 1963), the authors implicate **cottonseed meal** as the most probable carcinogen source in both hatcheries.
- **Pesticides/fungicides ruled out:** No chlorinated hydrocarbons were used pre-WWII; only pre-war arsenical cotton sprays were considered plausible but unverified. **Solvent-extraction residues are impossible as a cause** for the 1936-42 epizootic since the first solvent-extraction cottonseed plant only began operating in 1948.
- **Oxidized lipid hypothesis:** Dry meals (especially fish meal, rich in cholesterol and unsaturated lipids) undergo lipid oxidation; oxidized sterols/cholesterol derivatives have demonstrated tumorigenic effects in mice (Fieser et al. 1955), and heat/oxidation-altered fats can enhance carcinogen potency (Sugai et al. 1962).
- **Aflatoxin hypothesis (key retrospective link):** Poor storage of dry meals in warm, moist conditions likely allowed growth of *Aspergillus flavus* and other molds. **Aflatoxin**, their toxic metabolite, is a known carcinogen in warm-blooded animals and was subsequently confirmed to produce hepatoma directly in trout (Ashley et al., 1964). A companion study (Sinnhuber et al., 1965) found **aflatoxin in a commercial trout ration** and in cottonseed meal itself, producing microscopic liver tumors in 6 months and gross hepatocarcinoma in 7 months in feeding trials — strongly supporting cottonseed meal contamination as the historical causative agent.
- **Strain/growth-rate hypothesis:** Rapid growth in selectively-bred rainbow trout may parallel findings in mice (Heston, 1963) where fast-growing strains show higher spontaneous hepatoma incidence, suggesting a possible growth-rate/hepatoma linkage requiring further study.
- **Viral and water chemistry/temperature causes:** Considered but found to lack supporting evidence; water chemistry differences between the two hatcheries argue against a water-chemistry-based cause, though temperature clearly modulates the **rate** of tumor development, not necessarily causation.
- **Resolution:** As meal use declined in the early 1940s (partly due to egg-quality concerns), hepatoma incidence declined and disappeared by 1942 — only to re-emerge on a larger, nationwide scale after commercial dry pellet diets became widespread in the mid-1950s, culminating in the well-known 1960 epizootic.
