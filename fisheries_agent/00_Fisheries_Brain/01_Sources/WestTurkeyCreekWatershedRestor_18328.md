# West Turkey Creek Watershed Restoration and Enhancement Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1995 grant application to the [[Arizona Water Protection Fund]] (AWPF) requests funding for a two-year project to quantify and monitor the effects of gabion-based erosion control on the West Turkey Creek (WTC) watershed in Cochise County, Arizona. The applicants, owners of [[El Coronado Ranch]] (ECR), have built hundreds of rock retaining structures since 1987 to slow runoff and retain water. The project aims to (1) map and document past erosion-control work using [[Geographic Information System]] (GIS) methods, (2) establish permanent sampling stations, and (3) sample and monitor hydrology, water quality, soil moisture, and biota, including two native fishes. The total budget is $141,100, of which $84,700 is requested from AWPF and $56,400 is applicant funding and in-kind support. The work is also intended to support a future [[Habitat Conservation Plan]] (HCP) for the [[Yaqui Chub]] and [[Longfin Dace]].

## Background & Description

**Project setting.** The watershed covers 15,193 acres, of which 13,273 acres are [[Coronado National Forest]] land and 1,920 acres are deeded ranch land. The project area includes about 8 miles of WTC mainstem and spans elevations from 9,000 ft down to 5,200 ft. Average annual precipitation is 19.0 inches. Vegetation includes oak/pine woodland, pine forest, mixed-conifer forest, and riparian woodland.

**Applicants and personnel.**
- Josiah and Valer Austin own and operate ECR. Austin's erosion-control program received a 1993 "Conservationist of the Year" award from the Willcox-San Simon Natural Resource Conservation District.
- [[Wendell L. Minckley]], Ph.D. (Arizona State University), will oversee research, data collection, and analysis. He led the Desert Fishes Recovery Team and helped produce the [[Fishes of the Rio Yaqui Recovery Plan]].
- Dale Pontius, an attorney and consultant, will advise on endangered species and legal issues, including the HCP.
- A graduate research assistant (not yet named) will serve as project and data manager.

**Erosion-control system.** Structures are mostly rock retaining walls (gabions) across ephemeral tributaries. The applicants report that surface water now appears in formerly dry canyons, riparian plants have expanded, and flow regimes are more stable. The system uses naturally available stone, so the main cost is labor. A [[Rattlesnake Fire]] in July 1994 (27,500 acres, about 75% of it in designated wilderness) caused severe post-fire erosion, flooding, and sedimentation.

**Stated objectives.**
1. Compile and document erosion-control and riparian activities in a GIS map and narrative, including watercourses, canals, ponds, and sensitive-species habitat.
2. Establish permanent sampling stations, with paired modified (gabioned) and unmodified (control) sub-watersheds.
3. Sample and monitor four components: streamflow volumes and durations, water quality, soil moisture and water table levels, and resident biota (flora, fauna, native fishes, and aquatic invertebrates).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fishes.** WTC is the last known native stock of the Sulfur Springs Valley drainage of longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*). Southern populations are listed as a USFWS Candidate 2 species. Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*) is federally and state-listed as endangered and was reintroduced to the WTC drainage in 1986. Both species are at the northern limit of their ranges on ECR. The "Fishes of the Rio Yaqui Recovery Plan" identifies WTC as a key recovery locale. The applicants hold federal and state permits for work on these species through Minckley's program.

**Other biota.** The application notes additional unique, imperiled, or otherwise concerning species in the riparian zone, but does not list them individually. Other wildlife on the ranch includes white-tailed deer, javelina, black bear, mountain lion, and turkey. A study of [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] is also underway. Some ranch ponds are being managed to expand Yaqui chub habitat in cooperation with USFWS.

**Fire and erosion.** Flooding after the Rattlesnake Fire carried ash and sediment downstream. It damaged ECR ponds and water-distribution systems, and fish kills occurred in WTC and connected ponds, affecting both native and non-native fishes. Critics attribute the fire's intensity to decades of [[Fire Suppression Policy]] in the Chiricahuas. Rucker Lake, a 5-acre fishing lake in the Chiricahuas, was filled with sediment. The Forest Service reported spending about $8.5 million on firefighting and roughly $100,000 on post-fire restoration. A federal draft fire policy released in 1995 recognizes fire as a "critical natural process," but suppression remained the rule in the upper Chiricahuas at the time.

**Grazing and land management.** The Turkey Creek Allotment is permitted for 66 cattle (March 1 to February 28) and 25 cattle (September 15 to December 15), totaling 867 animal months. The [[El Coronado Ranch Land Management Plan]] sets goals that include reducing erosion by 90%, reducing bare soil by 90%, raising water tables, and restoring native perennial grasses. Management uses a rotational "best pasture management system" and restricts grazing in the main Turkey Creek canyon during summer recreation periods.

## Key Findings

**Statutory compliance.** The application meets all five Minimum Statutory Criteria. It requires no property acquisition or eminent domain. It describes its relationship to existing plans, includes provisions for inspection and evaluation, and proposes accounting methods. The SHPO certification was signed (27 July 1995), with no cultural resources or pre-1945 structures reported in the project area.

**Monitoring design.**
- Minimum of 5 modified and 5 unmodified sub-watersheds sampled over 2 years for paired comparison.
- Five additional climate stations are planned beyond the one existing ECR station.
- Photographic records taken from four cardinal directions at fixed posts.
- Baseflow measured weekly using V-notch weirs and a standard width × depth/velocity formula.
- A minimum of 10 groundwater wells (2-inch PVC), including 10 wells on ephemeral tributaries (5 modified, 5 unmodified).
- Fish sampled by seines, traps, and electrofishing. Invertebrates sampled by Surber sampler, dredge, sweep nets, and light traps.

**Expected outcomes.** The applicants expect longer, more stable discharge; less turbid, more soil-filtered water; and higher soil moisture and groundwater as gabions mature. They also state that results may be equivocal, since Arizona's variable climate makes conclusive answers within two years unlikely.

- Administrative costs charged to AWPF: $2,400 (2.8% of request).
- Consultant costs ($70,800) are excluded from the year-by-year cost forms.
- Major items include GIS base mapping ($17,000, first six months), gabion construction ($17,000 over two years), and an electrofisher ($2,600).
- Capital equipment totals $19,800, including GPS receiver, GIS field unit, software, V-notch weirs, and weather-station equipment.

**Planned deliverables.**
1. Interim report at the end of Year 1, with a final report covering results, trends, design recommendations, and full data.
2. A long-term baseline database for WTC and ECR.
3. A biological inventory and monitoring program to support the HCP, recovery plans, and avoidance of listing for longfin dace.
4. A statistical evaluation of whether gabions retain water and improve local ecosystems.

**Central argument.** The applicants frame the project as a test of headwater restoration. They argue that upstream conditions drive downstream outcomes, as the Rattlesnake Fire demonstrated, so improved upstream management can benefit the whole system. They present the project as a potential model for other Southwestern watersheds, with the caveat that ecosystem effects require a long time frame.
