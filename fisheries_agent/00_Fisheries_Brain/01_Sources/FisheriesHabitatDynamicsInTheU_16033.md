# Fisheries Habitat Dynamics in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Colorado-River-Endemics

## Executive Summary

This 1985 study by Carter et al. investigates the relationship between river discharge (flow) and the surface area of distinct physical habitat types in a 2-mile reach of the upper [[Colorado River]] near Parachute, Colorado. Rather than focusing on the morphology of a single species, the paper establishes quantitative, predictive equations linking river flow (cfs) to habitat availability for a suite of endemic and endangered fishes, including the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The research identified critical flow thresholds—particularly near 10,000 cfs—where dramatic, non-linear shifts in habitat area (especially backwaters and flooded woodlands) occur, with direct implications for river management, water allocation, and endangered species recovery.

## Background & Description
*Note: This paper is a habitat/hydrology study rather than a morphological analysis. Species-specific morphological data are not provided; however, the paper documents the following taxonomic and life-history context relevant to habitat use:*

- **Basin Endemism**: The Colorado River Basin exhibits 73% fish endemism, the highest of any river basin in the United States (Miller 1961), attributed to long isolation and extreme environmental conditions (fluctuating flow, temperature, turbidity).
- **Species Guild Composition**: 13 native species occur in the upper Colorado River, including 4 listed/candidate endangered species and 9 other natives, alongside introduced exotics ([[Tyus et al. 1982]]).
- **Life-history linked traits**:
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] spawn in riffles at the upper end of resting pools, analogous to the northern squawfish of the Columbia River.
  - [[Roundtail Chub]] congregate at "eddy fences" (current/backflow interfaces) to feed on drifting insects.
  - [[Speckled Dace]] and young suckers associate strongly with shallow riffle/rubble-flat habitats.
  - Native larval fishes (e.g., larval [[Colorado Squawfish]]) rely on shoreline embayments and concavities as critical nursery habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Classification (12 types mapped):**
Backwater, eddy, pool, isolated pool, embayment, concavity, riffle, river (main channel), rapid, rubble flat, shoreline, and flooded woodland.

**Flow-Responsive Habitats (7 types):**
- **Backwaters**: Exhibited the most dramatic response — a ~50-fold increase in area at ~10,000 cfs as the river inundated old side channels and gravel pits. Backwaters were consistently 1–5°C warmer than the main channel, potentially aiding reproductive maturation via degree-day accumulation. Serve as critical nursery/resting/spawning habitat for [[Colorado Squawfish]] and other native fish, especially during the descending limb of spring runoff.
- **Flooded Woodlands**: Also surged in area near 10,000 cfs; function as feeding/shelter zones for small fish escaping main-channel turbulence.
- **Rapids & Runs**: Area increased directly with discharge; rapids only formed above 10,000 cfs. Runs serve as feeding/possible spawning habitat for suckers and dace. Few fish were observed using rapids.
- **Riffles**: Area varied *inversely* with rapids/runs (decreasing as discharge rose); important for spawning/feeding suckers and dace.
- **Eddies**: Area increased linearly with discharge; function as feeding stations for chubs and suckers along "eddy fences."
- **Rubble Flats**: Only present within a narrow flow window (~5,000–12,000 cfs); important for dace and young suckers.

**Flow-Independent Habitats (5 types):**
Embayments, concavities, pools, shorelines, and isolated pools showed no predictable relationship to discharge — driven instead by localized geomorphology (shoreline depressions, substrate type).
- **Shorelines**: Area remained relatively stable regardless of flow; hypothesized to support the majority of riverine fish populations due to consistent, low-turbulence conditions.
- **Isolated Pools**: Form via disconnection from the main channel during flow recession; disproportionately trap non-native fish, while native fish appear adept at avoiding stranding.
- **Pools**: A single deep (~15 ft) pool below the Battlement Mesa Bridge converted to a run at higher velocities — a hydraulic transformation rather than an area-based one.

**Critical Threshold**: A major hydraulic regime shift occurs near **10,000 cfs**, transitioning the river from a turbulent, high-velocity system that concentrates fish into marginal shoreline habitats (backwaters, flooded woodlands) toward a more diverse, moderate-flow system supporting a wider range of microhabitats.

## Key Findings

1. **Quantitative Flow-Habitat Models**: Regression equations (Table 1) were derived for all 12 habitat types across discharge ranges of 1,710–28,300 cfs, several with strong statistical support (e.g., Rapid: r²=0.985, p<0.01; Run: r²=0.949, p<0.01; Flooded Woodland: r²=0.980, p<0.01).
2. **Non-linear Threshold Effects**: Backwater and flooded woodland habitats displayed abrupt, non-linear expansion at ~10,000 cfs, rather than gradual scaling with discharge — a key management threshold.
3. **Total Habitat Peaks at High Flow, But Quality Is Marginal**: Total habitat area peaked during the late-June flood peak, but this was dominated by unstable, high-competition/predation habitats (deep runs, rapids), while lower flows produced more diverse and stable habitat mosaics.
4. **Species-Habitat Associations**: Distinct habitat preferences were reinforced for spawning ([[Colorado Squawfish]] in riffles above resting pools), nursery use (larval fish in embayments/concavities and backwaters), and feeding (chubs/suckers at eddy fences and runs).
5. **Management Application**: The described flow-habitat relationships provide a novel quantitative tool for setting flow recommendations to protect critical habitat for the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]], though the authors caution that equations may vary by river reach due to differing channel morphology.
6. **Non-native Fish Vulnerability**: Isolated pools disproportionately trapped and killed non-native fish species in prior studies (Valdez et al. 1982), suggesting native species possess superior flow-stage sensing/avoidance behavior.
