# Hydrology, Geomorphology and Management: Implications for Sustainability of Native Southwestern Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review compares fish assemblages in two desert rivers of the American Southwest, the [[Upper Verde River]] in Arizona and the [[Upper Gila River]] in New Mexico-Arizona, over 7 to 12 years of monitoring. The authors argue that native southwestern fishes have declined sharply in range and numbers, driven mainly by habitat alteration and the introduction of non-native species. They identify three primary influencing factors: hydrograph (peak flow, flow variability, flow volume), geomorphology (broad-scale canyon versus alluvial settings and reach-scale habitat), and human management (fisheries stocking, grazing, dams and diversions). The authors conclude that sustaining native assemblages requires land managers to address all three factors through administrative and legal mandates.

## Background & Description

The southwestern United States has a low-diversity native fish fauna, about 95% of which is cypriniform (minnows and suckers). All native species have declined in range and numbers over the past 50 years, and most are federally or state listed. Two federally threatened species, [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]], are restricted to the [[Gila River Basin]] and are a special focus of the study.

Research has traditionally been species-by-species, but the authors argue that assemblage-level study is needed. Monitoring began on the Verde in 1994 at seven established sites, and on the upper Gila in spring 1999 with five major reaches sampled through 2005. Sites (150 to 300 m long) spanned four macrohabitat types: high-gradient riffles (HGR), low-gradient riffles (LGR), glide-runs (GRUN), and pools. Fish were collected by backpack electrofishing, seining, and trammel netting in pools deeper than 2 m. Hydrograph data came from USGS gages at Paulden (Verde) and Gila.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** The Gila River sustained much greater mean flow, flow variability, and peak flows than the Verde. Mean annual streamflow in the Gila/Cliff Valley reach was almost four times that of the Upper Verde. Monthly discharge range was 19 m³/s on the Gila versus 0.57 m³/s on the Verde. Only 5 of 13 years (1993–2005) had Gila peak flows below 57 m³/s, versus 9 of 13 years on the Verde. The last flood greater than 86 m³/s on the Verde occurred in 1995; a flood exceeding 513 m³/s on the Gila in September 1997 (from Hurricane Linda) was the fourth-highest peak recorded since 1928.

**Geomorphology.** Canyon-bound reaches of the Gila Wilderness have more and deeper pools (>2 m), while broad alluvial valleys sustain fewer, shallower pools. Mean gradient was 0.8% in the canyon-bound Reach III, versus 0.4% and 0.3% in the alluvial Reaches IV and V. The Upper Verde has almost no HGR habitat (≥90 cm/s), which the authors link to loach minnow absence there.

**Management.** Livestock grazing was removed from the Upper Verde in 1997, after which riparian and instream vegetation increased and the channel narrowed and deepened. Stocking has introduced many non-native sport species; about 100 species have been introduced into Arizona since the late 1800s, and about half have established. The Upper Verde has only Sullivan Dam (no permanent pool) and one minor diversion, whereas the Upper Gila has three large diversions (Reaches III, IV, and V), including the Sunset (Virden) and Fort West Ditch diversions, which completely dried the river in summer 1999 and 2001.

## Key Findings

- **Verde River decline:** Total fish abundance in Reach I fell dramatically from 1994 to 2005. Native fishes made up over 80% of the assemblage from 1994 to 1996 but were over 70% non-native from 1997 onward. [[Longfin Dace]] fell from 1,300 individuals in 1994 to five in the past five years of sampling. [[Speckled Dace]] had none collected at the seven sites from spring 2001 to 2005. Spikedace were absent from the seven sites after 1997.
- **Large native species:** [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]], and [[Roundtail Chub]] show poor recruitment and steady decline. Current (2005) numbers are less than 1% to 3% of 1994 levels, following large floods in winter 1992–1993.
- **Non-native species:** [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Green Sunfish]] increased until 2003 before declining. [[Mosquitofish]] peaked between 1997 and 2000 and were absent by spring 2005. Six young [[Flathead Catfish]] were collected in 2001, more than in the previous seven years. [[Red Shiner]] was the most abundant and cyclical non-native species. No more than 24 [[Common Carp]] were recorded in any year.
- **Gila River 1999 assemblage:** Spikedace and loach minnow were present in Reach I (West and East Fork sites), absent from Reaches II and III (canyon-bound), most abundant in Reach IV, and declined again in Reach V. Reach IV contained almost half of its native catch as spikedace and loach minnow (49%), and non-natives were nearly absent there.
- **Hydrology as the primary driver:** Peak flow, flow variability, and flow volume are all linked to assemblage structure. The authors suggest variability and volume are equally or more important than peak flow alone. Non-native fishes became dominant on the Verde after the flood-free period, while the Gila's higher-variability flows appear to have sustained natives.
- **Pools favor predators:** Removing pools from the analysis substantially improved native/non-native ratios. At the Redrock site (Reach V), large catfish captured in deep pools were associated with reduced native fish numbers.
- **Habitat mosaic:** A mix of HGRs, LGRs, and GRUNs without deep pools appears optimal for native fishes. Spikedace are normally captured in LGR and GRUN habitats.
- **Management recommendations:** The authors call for restricting future non-native introductions, limiting flow modification (damming, diversions, groundwater pumping), ensuring grazing is compatible with fisheries goals, and conducting controlled grazing experiments (e.g., 1–2 km reaches on the Upper Verde). They caution that simple one-to-one relationships will not give managers the answers they need.
