# Exotic Fishes of the Genus Tilapia in the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cichlidae #exotic-species #hybridization

## Executive Summary

This study documents the introduction, establishment, and taxonomic status of African cichlid fishes of the genus [[Tilapia]] in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] of the American Southwest. Between 1961 and 1982, multiple [[Tilapia]] species were imported and stocked throughout [[Arizona]] and southern [[California]], primarily for food production and aquatic vegetation control. The closely related [[Tilapia mossambica]] (Mozambique Tilapia) and [[Tilapia hornorum]] have largely hybridized into a single reproducing complex. [[Tilapia aurea]] (Blue Tilapia) has become dominant in Arizona waters and is now hybridizing with the T. mossambica × T. hornorum complex in drainage canals near [[San Luis, Arizona]]. Additional species present include [[Tilapia zilli]] (Redbelly Tilapia) and tentatively [[Tilapia rendalli]], with possible genetic contributions from [[Tilapia nilotica]] (Nile Tilapia). The study employed principal components analysis (PCA) and discriminant function analysis (DFA) on morphometric and meristic data from 203-263 specimens per taxon to resolve taxonomic identities.

## Background & Description

African cichlid fishes were imported to the United States in the 1940s-1950s following recognition of their potential for aquaculture and biological control. Taxonomic instability complicated early introductions: [[Tilapia aurea]] was in synonymy with [[Tilapia nilotica]] until 1965, and [[Tilapia hornorum]] was separated from [[Tilapia mossambica]] only after both had been introduced. The "Malacca hybrid" (T. mossambica × T. hornorum) was produced experimentally for pond culture due to its predominantly male offspring, which are desirable for aquaculture operations.

**Morphological Characteristics:**

- **T. mossambica complex**: 15-16 (rarely 17) dorsal spines, 26-29 total dorsal spines + rays, 14-20 gill rakers (typically 15-19). Breeding males develop black coloration on head, body, and vertical fins with bright red dorsal and caudal fin margins, reddened pectoral fins, and whitened lower head regions. Males develop broad, thickened, elongated jaws.

- **T. hornorum**: 16-18 dorsal spines, 28-30 total dorsal spines + rays, 18-26 gill rakers (usually 19-24). Breeding males are black with red dorsal fin margin and scattered silver-white patches on opercles and flanks.

- **T. aurea**: 15-16 (rarely 14 or 17) dorsal spines, 27-30 total spines + rays. Caudal fin either unmarked, with vague irregular dark patches, or dark reticulum on clear background. Breeding males have blue-gray coloration intensified on head with vermillion dorsal fin margin. Males do not develop pronounced jaw modifications.

- **T. zilli**: 11-16 gill rakers, 14-16 dorsal spines, 10-13 dorsal rays. Caudal fin has light-colored round markings on dark background with characteristic "Tilapia mark" in dorsal fin. Dark fins with lighter marginal fringe.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] provides subtropical climate conditions suitable for African cichlids. Initial introductions occurred at the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) hatchery at [[Papago Park, Tempe]] in March 1961, with fish identified as [[Tilapia mossambica]] imported from Hawaii. The [[Arizona Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]] (ACFRU) in Tucson began experimentation with the "Malacca hybrid" in 1962, using T. mossambica stock from [[Tishomingo National Fish Hatchery]] (Oklahoma) and T. hornorum from the [[Tropical Fish Culture Institute]] in Malacca, Malaysia.

By 1964, cichlids had established at the north end of the [[Salton Sea]], presumably from fish farm escapees. After 1970, indiscriminate transfers occurred throughout the region, with a major event in 1971 when drainage of a canal near [[Yuma]] provided thousands of Tilapia that were transported basin-wide. Low-elevation habitats including canals, reservoirs ([[Painted Rock Reservoir]], [[Alamo Reservoir]], [[Mittry Lake]]), and the [[Gila River]] and [[Colorado River]] mainstems were primary establishment sites.

Environmental conditions conducive to hybridization include high turbidity from agricultural runoff, water level fluctuations from irrigation operations, and year-round intensive cultivation. Turbidity is particularly significant for cichlids, which depend heavily on visual cues in pre-mating behavior.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic Resolution:**

Arizona specimens exhibited meristic features consistently intermediate between [[Tilapia mossambica]] and [[Tilapia hornorum]]. Mean dorsal spine count was 16.3 (limits 14-18), intermediate between species. Total gill rakers averaged 21.5 in males with T. mossambica pigmentation and 19.8 in males with T. hornorum pigmentation, both outside expected ranges for pure species. The authors conclude most specimens represent hybrids between the two species, now referred to as the "T. mossambica complex."

**Statistical Analyses:**

Principal components analysis (PCA) of 203 specimens separated juvenile and adult fish but detected no subgroupings within the T. mossambica complex. The first eigenvalue (PC1) accounted for 24.8% of variance with heavy loading on total gill rakers; PC2 accounted for 13.5% of variance as a "shape" variable. Step-wise discriminant function analysis (DFA) correctly classified 99.3% of specimens referred to T. aurea versus the T. mossambica complex using jacknife classification.

**Hybridization at San Luis:**

A unique population at [[San Luis, Arizona]] drainage canal exhibited a mosaic of characters. Of 92 adults analyzed, PCA detected no subgroupings. DFA classified 83 fish as T. mossambica and 9 as T. aurea. Morphometric data alone placed 26.8% with T. aurea. Critically, many specimens combined T. aurea body morphology and pigmentation with the elongate, flattened, duck-like jaws characteristic of the T. mossambica group—a feature apparently unknown in the T. nilotica group. Chi-squared analysis showed San Luis fish differed significantly from T. mossambica in degree of dorsal and caudal fin banding (p < 0.01) but not in overall fin pigmentation. The authors interpret this as introgression from T. aurea × T. mossambica hybrids into the mossambica complex, with differential inheritance of character sets possibly under selection.

**Distribution Changes:**

[[Tilapia aurea]] has rapidly expanded since 1975, becoming dominant in southwestern Arizona and displacing the T. mossambica complex. T. aurea now ranges from east of [[Phoenix]] into Mexico and upstream along the [[Colorado River]] to at least [[Imperial Reservoir]]. The T. mossambica complex, formerly occupying most of the [[Gila River]] from Tempe to its mouth and the lower Colorado River from [[Imperial Dam]] to the [[Gulf of California]], now has spotty distribution. Specimens with T. hornorum characteristics are most common along the mainstream Colorado River, while most other areas support fish resembling T. mossambica.

**Additional Species:**

Two specimens from [[Alamo Reservoir]] on [[Bill Williams River]] exhibited darkly fringed dorsal fins and alternating light-dark caudal fin bands characteristic of [[Tilapia nilotica]], but had 15 dorsal spines (below T. nilotica minimum) and 25-28 combined dorsal spines + rays (also below species range). This may reflect genetic contamination of T. aurea populations by T. nilotica. [[Tilapia zilli]] is established but uncommon except in urban lakes where stocked. Specimens tentatively identified as [[Tilapia rendalli]] were collected near [[Blythe, California]], distinguished from T. zilli by unicolored dorsal and caudal fins with dark margins.

**Evolutionary Implications:**

The authors propose these hybrid swarms represent potential evolutionary events. Differential introgression of meristic features versus body morphology and pigmentation suggests certain character sets may be under differential selection. The T. mossambica-T. hornorum complex and the San Luis population demonstrate that not all hybrids exhibit simple blending inheritance, and backcrossing can produce populations trending toward one parental species while retaining diagnostic features of the other.

**Sample Sizes:**

Statistical analyses included 94 specimens from San Luis, 203 from the T. mossambica complex, 263 from T. aurea, 2 tentatively identified as T. nilotica, 40 T. zilli, and 10 T. rendalli. Collection localities spanned 1964-1982 across Arizona and California portions of the Lower Colorado River Basin.
