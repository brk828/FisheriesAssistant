# Life History and Ecology of the Gizzard Shad (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) in Elephant Butte Lake, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This study reports the life history, population dynamics and ecological role of [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) in [[Elephant Butte Lake]], a mainstream reservoir on the [[Rio Grande]] in south-central New Mexico. The analysis draws on 7,477 fish collected between June 1, 1964, and December 31, 1970, using [[Experimental Gill Nets]] and supplementary methods. The work was funded through the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish under federal aid programs administered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the National Marine Fisheries Service, as part of a [[Commercial Fisheries Project]] on rough fish species.

The authors conclude that gizzard shad are the dominant forage base for the lake's game fishes, but that the population is stunted, slow-growing and in poor condition, with its growth rate improving as predation by introduced [[Walleye]] and [[White Bass]] reduced numbers. The document also extends the published western range boundary of the species and documents mass die-offs linked to low temperatures and drawdown.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and classification.** The species belongs to order Clupeiformes, family Clupeidae, genus *Dorosoma*. It is distinguished from its congener [[Threadfin Shad]] (*D. petenense*) by an immaculate anal fin (29 to 35 rays, versus 17 to 25 in threadfin) and a subterminal, notched-jaw mouth. Miller (1960) recognized three nominal subspecies, but none were considered valid. The common name "gizzard shad" was adopted by the American Fisheries Society (1970).

**Morphology.** Adults are deep-bodied and laterally compressed, with a saw-like ventral ridge of bony scutes. Juveniles are slender and nearly cylindrical, and attain adult form within two to three months. Sexual dimorphism in external features was not evident, and the authors infer that larger fish were mostly female (75 percent of 228 fish of 260 mm or longer in 1970).

**Range.** The authors propose extending the western range boundary to include the Great Plains of Wyoming and Colorado, the Rio Grande drainage of north-central Chihuahua, and New Mexico drainages east of the [[Continental Divide]]. In New Mexico the species is native only to waters east of the divide, and its distribution closely follows reservoirs.

**Introductions.** Gizzard shad were successfully established in [[Alamogordo Reservoir]] in 1963, while introduced [[Threadfin Shad]] failed to persist there after a 1960–61 winter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Elephant Butte Lake.** The reservoir was first impounded in 1915, has a potential capacity of 2,150,000 acre-feet and a surface area of about 40,000 acres, and is operated by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation. Storage during the study ranged from 246,000 to 600,000 acre-feet, and the lake is subject to extreme seasonal drawdown for irrigation. The lake is morphometrically oligotrophic and essentially dimictic, with density currents preventing a true thermocline. Water chemistry is stable (dissolved oxygen 5.5 to 8.5 mg/l; pH 7.5 to 9.2, usually near 8).

**Plankton and benthos.** Both are sparse. The mean standing crop of net plankton in 1970 was 4.62 ml/m³, with zooplankton comprising 96 percent of organisms. The authors hypothesize that a dense stunted gizzard shad population consumes plankton faster than it can accumulate, a possible biological cause of the sparse crop.

**Fish community.** A checklist records 27 species in six orders and 10 families, including game fishes such as [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Walleye]], and rough fishes such as [[Carp]] and [[Smallmouth Buffalo]]. From April 1970 through March 1971, gizzard shad made up 71.2 percent of the number and 23.6 percent of the weight of fish caught in experimental gill nets.

**Habitat and movement.** Shad were found over all substrates but most consistently over sand and gravel in shallow water. Distribution was governed more by water clarity than turbidity: large numbers were caught where Secchi visibility was as low as six inches. Shad moved to deeper water and became largely inactive in fall as temperatures approached 14°C, returning to littoral areas in spring.

**Mortality.** Winter die-offs occurred in Caballo, Conchas and Elephant Butte lakes when temperatures fell below about 3.3°C (38°F). Summer die-offs occurred in 1964, 1968 and 1971 when storage fell to low volumes. The 1964 event was attributed to nitrogen gas bubble disease caused by the coliform bacterium *Aerobacter aerogenes* in fish stressed by crowding.

**Predators and forage role.** Gizzard shad were the primary forage for all major game fishes except flathead catfish, which fed mainly on carp. Common predators included [[Mergansers]], which took 88.9 percent shad in their diet on the lake, and [[Bald Eagle]] in other waters.

## Key Findings

**Growth and age.** Growth was determined from scales using the [[Lee Method]] (corrected direct-proportion). The body-scale regression was L = 30.14 + 0.785S (r = 0.983). Growth has increased since Patterson's 1965–66 study, but remains slower than in most reported waters. The fastest-growing reference population was western Lake Erie and Lake Newnan, Florida. The time for shad to reach 100 g was 4 years at Elephant Butte Lake, one year sooner than in Patterson's 1965–66 study (5 years), and longer than in any other water compared except for the 5-year figure above.

**Length-weight.** Relationships were calculated using the [[LeCren Method]] on 2,256 fish across three samples. Sexes did not differ in length or weight. Grand mean weight at Age Group III was 54.6 g.

**Condition.** Condition was assessed using the [[Hile Method]], with a grand mean coefficient of condition of 0.867 across the six-year period (1965–70). This is the lowest reported for any water compared. Annual values ranged from 0.965 (1965) to 0.814 (1967). Monthly condition dropped sharply in June after spawning.

**Size structure.** Length-frequency analysis of 7,477 fish (10 mm groups) showed alternating strong year classes. The authors concluded that length-frequency peaks overlapped multiple age groups and were not a reliable age indicator.

**Reproduction.** Spawning occurred from early May through late June at 17.8 to 23.9°C, with a possible second spawn in late summer. Spawning was mostly in shallow water, though some occurred at 15 m depth. Males matured at Age Groups II–III and females at III–IV. Males were hyperactive during spawning, leading to post-spawning mortality and shorter lifespan. Male-biased sex ratios were observed in April–June (74.9 percent males).

**Fecundity.** From 30 females, Age Group III fish (n = 26) averaged 40,500 ova (range 28,545–70,874). Fecundity was lower than in Lake Erie shad, and declined with length and age.

**Diet.** From 20 fish, phytoplankton comprised 36.8 percent and zooplankton 31.2 percent of food items by number, with unidentified organic residue at 28.4 percent and higher plant material at 3.6 percent. Zooplankton contributed the greatest biomass. The authors describe the species as a "living tow-net" feeding largely incidentally.

**Parasites and disease.** The species was relatively free of parasites. Single specimens of an ectoparasitic copepod (*Argulus*) and an acanthocephalan (*Gracilisentis*) were found, and fungal infection (*Saprolegnia parasitica*) was observed on injured fish.

**Population trends.** Shad increased from 41.1 percent to 71.2 percent of relative density in gill-net catches (1964–65 to 1970–71), and from 6.2 to 23.6 percent of biomass. Catch per net-unit fell 62 percent (26.9 to 10.2), while mean shad size increased. The authors attribute the change to predation by introduced walleye and white bass, which relieved intraspecific competition and stunting.

**Management conclusions.** Shad are more valuable as forage than as a commercial species. Commercial use is limited to frozen "cut-bait" for trotlines, at a retail value of $0.15 per pound. The population is still sufficiently abundant to support additional predatory game fish populations, and is not expected to reach levels detrimental to game fish under current management.
