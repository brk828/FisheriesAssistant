## Executive Summary

This document summarizes a February 1991 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service workshop convened to determine whether **Population Viability Analysis (PVA)** and **risk analysis** could be used to refine recovery goals for four endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (now Colorado pikeminnow), [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Experts Lynn Maguire (Duke University) and Peter Brussard (University of Nevada) presented decision theory/risk analysis and PVA frameworks, respectively, followed by a group exercise conducting a qualitative PVA for Colorado squawfish in the Yampa/Green subbasin. The workshop concluded that razorback sucker and bonytail chub populations were declining too severely for a formal PVA, whereas Colorado squawfish and the Little Colorado River population of humpback chub were stable enough, with sufficient data, to support formal PVA modeling. Numerous critical data gaps were identified regarding habitat distribution, genetic stock structure, mortality sources, and population dynamics.

## Background & Description

The source document does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of the species. Only life-history and population parameters relevant to viability analysis are reported:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: Large piscivorous cyprinid; sex ratio at spawning grounds heavily skewed (~15 males captured per female; overall catch data show ~2 males per female in the wild). Age of maturity is a function of body size. Susceptible to *Ichthyophthirius* (Ich), to which it is noted as "very sensitive."
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Remnant Lake Mojave population individuals are nearly **50 years old**, indicating no recent recruitment.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Approximately 1,000 old individuals remain in the Green River; Lake Mojave individuals are also nearly 50 years old. Reproduction documented, but no evidence of successful recruitment to the population.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]**: Distinguished in this document primarily by its bifurcated population structure — populations in the Lower Colorado River Basin and Upper Basin no longer exchange genes (gene flow interrupted).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat extent**: Currently-occupied Colorado squawfish habitat totals ~1,200 km across the Yampa/Green subbasin, Upper Colorado River subbasin, and San Juan subbasin. Historically-occupied but currently-unoccupied habitat includes three ~100 km stretches in the Salt, Verde, and Lower Colorado rivers.
- **Suitable habitat criteria** (Colorado squawfish):
  - River stretches >100 km long with historical flow/temperature regimes and historical spawning areas.
  - Non-spawning adults prefer wide, slow, meandering reaches (>5 km) — catch rates ~10 fish/km vs. ~1 fish/km in narrow canyons.
  - Spawning adults favor less-altered reaches with higher spring flows.
- **Population estimates** (professional judgment, by subbasin):

| Life Stage | Yampa/Green | Upper Colorado | San Juan | Lower Colorado River Basin (LCRB) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adults | 3,000 | 500 | 50 | ? |
| Sub-Adults | 7,000 | 700 | ? | ? |
| Age 0–1 | 50,000 | 2,000 | 100 | 1,600 |

- **Systematic (deterministic) mortality factors**:
  - Human-induced: angler harvest (~5%/yr, range 5–20%, based on tag/radio-tag return data of ~10% harvest); flow regulation impacts (temperature alteration, larval/YOY stranding, cold-shock, nursery habitat inundation, migration barriers); research sampling mortality (electroshocking, radio-tagging, larval collection); contaminants (pesticides such as Mormon cricket spraying, selenium).
  - Natural: predation (native roundtail chub and up to ~20 nonnative species preying on eggs, 10 on larvae, 5 on juveniles); choking on channel catfish; agonistic behavior; disease/parasites (nonnatives such as fathead minnows act as Ich vectors); food limitation linked to altered flow regimes, reservoirs acting as nutrient sinks, and competition with nonnatives.
- **Birth-rate determinants**: migration barriers blocking spawning runs, loss of clean cobble spawning substrate, reduced size/age of spawning females, spawning frequency, and age of maturity (function of size).
- **Growth-rate determinants**: food supply, backwater availability/quality, and crowding.
- **Stochastic factors**:
  - Demographic stochasticity: considered unimportant given presumed large population sizes; ~3 subpopulations (stocks) believed present in Yampa/Green.
  - Genetic stochasticity: effective population size ratio (**Ne/N**) estimated at ~0.1, based on breeder sex ratio, progeny variance, and overlapping generations.
  - Environmental stochasticity: runoff magnitude/timing/duration/frequency strongly shapes native vs. nonnative fish success (high spring flows favor natives; low spring flows favor nonnatives; low summer/fall flows increase disease, crowding, predation, and passage obstruction for natives).
  - Catastrophic risks: oil spills, forest fires, sustained drought, disease epidemics, contaminant spills, extreme flow events.
- **Metapopulation structure**: Genetic exchange among the ~3 Yampa/Green subpopulations appears limited, based on radio-tagged fish showing fidelity to specific spawning areas; evolutionary history suggests low colonization rates of new spawning sites.
- **Nonnative species pressure**: 42 nonnative fish species have been introduced into the Colorado River system, 10 of which are abundant; these compete for food, prey on native early life stages, and serve as disease vectors.

## Key Findings

1. **Species status (1991)**: Bonytail chub is virtually extirpated in the wild except for an aging (~50-year-old) remnant population in Lake Mojave. Razorback sucker shows documented spawning but no successful recruitment; only ~1,000 old individuals remain in the Green River, with Lake Mojave fish also nearly 50 years old.
2. **Genetic fragmentation**: Gene flow between Lower Basin and Upper Basin [[Humpback Chub]] populations has been interrupted, raising concerns about genetic isolation.
3. **PVA feasibility assessment**:
   - Razorback sucker and bonytail chub: too actively declining for formal PVA; reversing deterministic mortality should be the top priority.
   - Colorado squawfish and the Little Colorado River humpback chub population: stable enough, with sufficient data, to support a **formal PVA** requiring stochastic and deterministic modeling by a PVA expert.
   - A **qualitative PVA** (expert-elicitation based) is recommended in the short term for all four species.
4. **Decision/risk analysis framework** (Maguire): a nine-step process — establishing objectives, defining measurement criteria, identifying uncertainty sources, listing decision alternatives, building decision trees, assigning probabilities/values, assessing trade-offs, conducting sensitivity analysis, and testing whether new information changes decisions — was proposed as complementary to PVA for prioritizing recovery actions and evaluating water development trade-offs.
5. **Extinction process model** (Brussard): widespread/abundant populations decline due to systematic factors (habitat loss, overexploitation, fragmentation), after which stochastic factors (demographic, environmental, genetic, catastrophic) accelerate decline to extinction — this is the "extinction vortex." Recovery must reverse this via both crisis management and preventive measures.
6. **Qualitative PVA exercise (Colorado squawfish, Yampa/Green)**: Organized around three tiers — (I) Habitat and Population Distribution, (II) Single Population Dynamics, (III) Interacting Population Systems (metapopulation/genetic exchange). The exercise clarified existing knowledge and major data gaps but did not resolve how to formally integrate information into a persistence-probability estimate.
7. **Critical knowledge gaps identified**, including:
   - Comprehensive habitat maps (spawning, nursery, adult) across the Basin for native and nonnative fishes.
   - Squawfish status in Lake Powell and the Dirty Devil River.
   - Genetic stock structure and migration rates among stocks.
   - Sex ratio of actual spawners and proportion of migratory adults.
   - Quantification of predation impacts, dietary overlap with nonnatives, sampling-induced mortality, and selenium tolerance.
   - Spawning-site fidelity and locations used by previously stocked hatchery-reared fish.
8. **Recommendations**: (1) reverse deterministic mortality drivers for razorback sucker and bonytail chub; (2) conduct qualitative PVA for all four species; (3) contract a formal PVA for Colorado squawfish; (4) apply risk analysis to prioritize recovery actions; (5) fund targeted studies to close the data gaps listed above.
9. **Conceptual glossary terms** established for the recovery program include [[Minimum Viable Population]], [[Effective Population Size]] (Ne), [[Genetic Drift]], [[Inbreeding Depression]], [[Metapopulation]], [[Demographic Stochasticity]], [[Environmental Stochasticity]], and [[Catastrophic Stochasticity]] — foundational concepts for future viability modeling of Colorado River fishes.
