# Gila purpurea and Gila ditaenia: Yaqui Chub and Sonora Chub Species Accounts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This note combines two species accounts from a North American freshwater fish atlas compiled by W. L. Minckley (July 1978). Both accounts cover small cyprinid fishes of the genus [[Gila]] from northwestern Mexico and adjacent southern Arizona. The [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) is nearly extirpated in the United States and survives mainly in Mexican streams of the [[Rio Yaqui]] system. The [[Sonora Chub]] (*Gila ditaenia*) is restricted in the United States to [[Sycamore Canyon]] in Santa Cruz County, Arizona. Both species are small, rarely exceeding about 100 mm and 76 mm standard length respectively, and both persist in pools and springheads where predation and drought are significant pressures.

## Background & Description

### Yaqui Chub (*Gila purpurea*)

- **Taxonomy:** Described by Girard. Miller (1945) considered it related to *G. ditaenia* of the [[Rio De La Concepcion]] (Rio Asuncion), Sonora, and to *G. orcutti* of the Los Angeles Plain, California, and associated drainages.
- **Adult size:** Rarely exceeds 100 mm standard length. A voucher female from the [[Rio Matape]] measured 117 mm SL (NCSM), a transplanted population specimen.
- **Appearance:** Males are steely blue for much of the year. Females are drab yellow-brown, a contrast that aids sex identification.
- **Reproduction:** Spawns in March. Young reach 40–50 mm SL by the following year.
- **Diet:** In springhead habitats, algae, terrestrial insects, and arachnids. Aquatic insects and small fishes (*Poeciliopsis*) are eaten when available.

### Sonora Chub (*Gila ditaenia*)

- **Type locality:** Rio Magdalena, about 0.5 km west of La Casita, roughly 40 km south of Nogales, Arizona, Sonora, Mexico. The coordinates in the source are garbled by scanning and are not reproduced here.
- **Taxonomy:** Placed in subgenus *Tentaculina* (Barbour and Miller 1978). Miller (1945) associated it with *G. purpurea* of the Rio Yaqui and smaller basins to the south and east, and with *G. orcutti* of the Los Angeles Plain.
- **Adult size:** Rarely more than 76 mm SL. A voucher specimen from Sycamore Canyon measured 83 mm SL (NCSM).
- **Reproduction:** Spawns in early spring (February–April), inferred from young in collections and adults in reddened breeding coloration.
- **Diet:** Aquatic and terrestrial insects and algae in early summer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Distribution and Habitat

- **Yaqui Chub:** Occurs in smaller streams of the Rios Matape, Sonora, and Yaqui systems in Sonora, Mexico. It is nearly extirpated in the United States, persisting in one artesian well in the [[San Bernardino Creek]] drainage (McNatt 1974). It was introduced and established in [[Leslie Creek]], Swisshelm Mountains, Arizona, in 1969. Minckley (1973) noted that reported records from Morse Canyon in the northern Chiricahua Mountains, Arizona, are not supported by specimens. It lives in deeper pools of small streams near undercut banks or debris, often with higher aquatic plants.
- **Sonora Chub:** Found in Rio Altar and Rio Magdalena tributaries that form the Rio De La Concepcion, which typically sinks into its sandy channel before reaching the [[Gulf Of California]], about 260 km south of the Colorado River Delta. In the United States it is restricted to Sycamore (Bear) Canyon west of Nogales, Santa Cruz County, Arizona. There it inhabits pools near cliffs, boulders, or other channel cover, and also headsprings and seeps.

### Seasonal Dynamics and Threats

- **Drought concentration:** During seasonal drought, Sonora Chub concentrates in restricted pools, where predation by [[Belostomatid Hemipterans]] (giant water bugs) is severe.
- **Limited dispersal:** Adults move minimally from these pools, even during periods of high flow (Minckley and Deacon 1968; Minckley 1973). This limits their ability to recolonize habitat after local losses.
- **Restricted range:** Both species are confined to small, isolated habitats. Yaqui Chub is nearly extirpated in the United States, and the Sonora Chub US range is one canyon system.

## Key Findings

1. **Restricted distributions:** The Yaqui Chub persists mainly in Sonora, Mexico, with a nearly extirpated US population in one artesian well. The Sonora Chub is limited in the US to Sycamore Canyon, Arizona.
2. **Taxonomic relationships:** Miller (1945) linked both species with *G. orcutti* and with each other, and the Sonora Chub is placed in subgenus *Tentaculina* (Barbour and Miller 1978).
3. **Small adult size:** Rarely above 100 mm SL (*G. purpurea*) and 76 mm SL (*G. ditaenia*), with the voucher specimens of 117 mm and 83 mm SL exceeding these typical sizes.
4. **Reproductive timing:** Yaqui Chub spawns in March, with young reaching 40–50 mm SL the following year. Sonora Chub spawns in early spring (February–April).
5. **Sexual dimorphism:** Male Yaqui Chub are steely blue for much of the year, while females are yellow-brown. Sonora Chub adults show reddened breeding coloration.
6. **Diet:** Both species consume algae and insects. Yaqui Chub also eats small fishes when available, and springhead populations rely heavily on terrestrial insects and arachnids.
7. **Habitat preference:** Both species use deeper pools with cover (undercut banks, debris, boulders, cliffs) and springhead or seep habitats.
8. **Drought and predation risk:** Sonora Chub concentrates in shrinking pools during drought, where belostomatid predation is severe.
9. **Low dispersal:** Adult Sonora Chub rarely leave their pools, even during high water, which constrains recolonization.
10. **Introduction history:** Yaqui Chub was introduced to Leslie Creek, Arizona, in 1969. Minckley (1973) questioned unverified Morse Canyon records because no specimens supported them.
