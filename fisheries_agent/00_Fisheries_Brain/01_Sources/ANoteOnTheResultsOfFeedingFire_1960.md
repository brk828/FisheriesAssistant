## Executive Summary
This 1960 study by E. E. Prather (Auburn University) was conducted in response to sensationalized newspaper reports blaming imported fire ants (*Solenopsis saevissima richteri*) for mass fish die-offs in Alabama ponds. In the absence of empirical data, controlled aquarium feeding trials were designed to test whether fire ant consumption is harmful to [[Bluegill]] and [[Redear Sunfish]]. Both voluntary feeding trials and forced feeding trials (up to 4,000 ants per fish over 4 weeks) were performed. No mortality or observable ill effects were recorded in any test fish over a subsequent 10-week observation period, leading the author to conclude that fire ant ingestion is highly unlikely to be the cause of the reported fish deaths.

## Background & Description
- **Test subjects:** 14 [[Bluegill]] and 3 [[Redear Sunfish]] (family [[Centrarchidae]]).
- **Total length:** 6–7 inches.
- **Weight range:** 0.10–0.15 lb.
- **Prey item morphology:** Wingless form of the imported fire ant (*Solenopsis saevissima richteri*) used exclusively in all trials; ants were immobilized via CO₂ exposure for handling and recovered within ~5 minutes post-exposure.
- No morphological abnormalities, lesions, or physical ill effects were observed in fish following ant ingestion, whether voluntary or force-fed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context:** Fish were held in glass aquaria (40 liters each) simulating pond conditions typical of Alabama fisheries where imported fire ant colonies (an invasive terrestrial species) were expanding rapidly in the mid-20th century.
- **Trophic interaction:** The study examines an unusual predator-prey interaction between freshwater [[Centrarchidae|centrarchid]] sunfishes and a terrestrial invasive insect, *Solenopsis saevissima richteri* ([[Imported Fire Ant]]), which likely enters aquatic systems via flooding, mound disturbance, or incidental contact near pond margins.
- **Feeding behavior:** Fish were first conditioned to accept dropped food (commercial earthworm bait) for 2 weeks before introduction of fire ants, establishing a feeding response baseline.
- **Voluntary acceptance was low:** The majority of test fish ate fewer than 10 ants/day even after repeated exposure, suggesting fire ants are not a preferred or highly palatable natural prey item under these conditions.

## Key Findings
- **Voluntary feeding trial (2 weeks):** Most fish ate <10 fire ants/day; only 2 bluegills exceeded this (45 and 60 ants in a single day). Both fish remained healthy over a 10-day follow-up observation.
- **Forced feeding trial (4 weeks):**
  - One [[Bluegill]] and one [[Redear Sunfish]] each received 100 ants/day, 5 days/week → total 2,000 ants per fish.
  - One [[Bluegill]] and one [[Redear Sunfish]] each received 200 ants/day on the same schedule → total 4,000 ants per fish.
  - Four control bluegills received empty gelatin capsules on the same schedule.
- **Delivery method:** Ants were CO₂-immobilized, counted, and loaded into size 00 gelatin capsules (perforated for air) before being manually inserted past the esophagus into the stomach using a plastic rod.
- **Outcome:** Across a 10-week post-feeding observation period, **no mortality or adverse health effects** were observed in any experimental or control fish, regardless of ant dosage (2,000 or 4,000 ants total).
- **Conclusion:** The data strongly suggest that ingestion of imported fire ants is **not directly toxic or harmful** to bluegill or redear sunfish, contradicting contemporary media claims attributing pond fish kills to fire ant predation/ingestion.
- **Significance:** This is one of the earliest empirical rebuttals to public claims linking [[Imported Fire Ant]] proliferation to freshwater fish mortality events in the southeastern United States, highlighting the need for controlled experimentation over anecdotal media reporting in fisheries management.
