# Growth and Longevity of the Cui-ui and Other Catostomids and Cyprinids in Western North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 study by G. Gary Scoppettone (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, [[Reno Substation]]) validates annulus formation on opercula of the endangered [[Cui-Ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), a catostomid endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. Using two marker year classes (1969 and 1950) followed over eight years (1978–1986), the author confirms that the annual addition of opercular annuli is reliable and that cui-ui can reach at least 36 years by validated ages, with a maximum of 41 years recorded. Opercular aging also revealed that 15 additional large catostomids and cyprinids of western North America are considerably longer-lived than previously reported. The paper argues that scale-based ages have systematically underestimated longevity, with consequences for the management of these species.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** The cui-ui is a large, endangered sucker endemic to Pyramid Lake. Earlier estimates of its longevity (Koch 1972; Sigler et al. 1985) were two to three times lower than the validated ages reported by [[Scoppettone et al. 1986]].
- **Aging problem:** Scales have been the primary source of age underestimation in fishes, since annuli were often used without adequate validation (Beamish and McFarlane 1983). Fin rays and otoliths have been tested as alternatives, with fin rays proving unsuccessful for older cui-ui.
- **Methods:** Opercula were cleaned by boiling or scalpel scraping. Annuli were identified using the [[Casselman Method]] for hard tissues, interpreting transparent bone (winter) and opaque bone (summer). Hidden annuli were exposed by grinding away fenestrated reinforcement bone.
- **Samples:** Fish were gillnetted at the spring prespawning aggregation near the mouth of the [[Truckee River]], collected as spawning-run mortalities (including those dropped by [[White Pelican]]s or killed during handling at [[Marble Bluff Dam]]), or obtained from the [[Pyramid Lake Fishery]] and museum collections. Juveniles of 60–360 mm fork length were newly available from tribal and seine collections in the mid-1980s.
- **Growth modeling:** Composite growth curves were fitted by least-squares regression with quasi-Newton minimization, separately for males and females, using a two-part model (0–10 years and >10 years).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Recruitment history:** Two predominant year classes (1969 and 1950) were separated by 18 years of virtually no recruitment, followed by a decade of weak year classes in the 1970s. The author attributes this to water diversion and the inability of cui-ui to reach Truckee River spawning habitat in most years.
- **Juvenile scarcity:** No juveniles below 350 mm fork length had been captured between 1939 and 1981. Juveniles became relatively numerous after several high-water years in the 1980s.
- **Other taxa:** Similar patterns of unrecognized longevity were found in the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) of Lake Mohave, which had shown virtually no successful recruitment for about 30 years.
- **Growth cessation:** Late-life growth cessation may bias age estimates. Comparable patterns are reported for [[Lake Whitefish]] and [[Sablefish]].

## Key Findings

1. **Annulus validation:** Annuli on cui-ui opercula were validated over 8 years (1978–1986) by following the 1969 and 1950 year classes. The 1969 class was aged 9, 13 and 17 years in 1978, 1982 and 1986; the 1950 class was aged 28, 32 and 36 years in the same surveys. Ages up to 36 years were thus validated.
2. **Maximum age:** The oldest opercular-aged cui-ui was 41 years, while its scale-based age was 11 years.
3. **Hidden annuli:** Zero to three annuli were hidden under supporting bone. Generally none were hidden in fish under 6 years, one in most 6-year-olds, and females carried more hidden annuli than males over age 6. For example, about 50% of female 9-year-olds had two hidden annuli, versus 20% of males.
4. **False annuli:** These were rare and occurred only in the first six years of life, forming in late summer or early fall, and were specific to particular year classes.
5. **Scale unreliability:** Scales became unreliable around sexual maturity. The youngest spawners were 6 years old, with first spawning at 9–10 years in some fish and as late as 12. Scale ages of fish 28 years and older ranged from 7 to 12 years.
6. **Growth periods:** Most older cui-ui showed three growth periods: rapid growth in the first 9–10 years, slower growth at ages 11–20, and very slow or nil growth after 20 years.
7. **Growth sample:** The dataset comprised 28 ages from 1 to 41 years, including a complete annual series from ages 1 to 17, drawn from 19 year classes.
8. **Growth equations (fork length, mm):** Females: FL = 105.6 × Age^0.714 for age <10 (n = 233, r = 0.988); FL = 402.7 + 29.6 × Ln(age) for age >10 (n = 458, r = 0.626). Males: FL = 110.9 × Age^0.670 for age <10 (n = 243, r = 0.989); FL = 448.7 + 29.6 × Ln(age) for age >10 (n = 259, r = 0.426).
9. **Age–growth correlation:** Strong for the first 10 years of life but weaker after maturity, possibly partly due to small sample sizes for older males.
10. **Other long-lived species:** Maximum opercular ages of the 15 additional species (16 listed in Table 1, including cui-ui) were highest for the [[Lost River Sucker]] (43 years), [[June Sucker]] (42 years) and cui-ui (41 years). The oldest cyprinid was the [[Tui Chub]] from Eagle Lake, California (32 years), and the second oldest was the [[Colorado Squawfish]] from the Colorado River (26 years). The tui chub age exceeded the previously reported maximum by a factor of six.
11. **Management implications:** The author concludes that perceptions of life history and population management for these suckers and minnows must change. Misaging may have led to mismanagement, and the paper aims to prevent this for North American catostomids and cyprinids.
