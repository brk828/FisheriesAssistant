# Human-Induced Changes to Native Fishes in the Virgin River Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

Gregory and Deacon analyze roughly 17 years of native fish data, supplemented by historical records, to document declines in the distribution and abundance of the native fish community of the [[Virgin River]] Drainage. The river rises in southwestern Utah, crosses northwestern Arizona, and enters the Overton Arm of [[Lake Mead]] in southern Nevada. The authors attribute losses to a sequence of human actions: reservoir flooding, trout fishery development, cattle grazing, invasion by introduced [[Red Shiner]], and off-stream reservoir construction. Native fish retain near-natural abundance only in [[Zion National Park]], a short reach downstream, and a segment of the Santa Clara River above [[Gunlock Reservoir]]. Future water development threatens these remaining areas, and the paper frames the conflict as an ethical problem of living within resource limits.

## Background & Description

The Virgin River has a mean annual flow of 170,600 acre-feet (water years 1930–1992) measured at Littlefield, Arizona. The mean daily flow peaked at 697 cubic feet per second (cfs) in 1983 and fell to a low of 100 cfs in 1991. Instantaneous flows ranged from 38 cfs (May 1, 1975) to a peak of 61,000 cfs (January 1, 1989) (USGS 1993).

The native community includes several species of conservation concern:
- [[Woundfin]] (listed as endangered)
- [[Virgin Spinedace]] (candidate or threatened status)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (endangered)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]]
- Speckled dace and bluehead sucker (sources list these as native taxa)

The paper quotes [[Aldo Leopold]] on the ethics of inhabiting a river without disrupting its life.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic and habitat drivers.** Flow regulation, diversion, sewage discharge, and irrigation return flows changed the river. Irrigation diversion dams built of rock, dirt, and brush were repeatedly washed out by floods until a diversion was completed at Washington Fields in 1891. Later water development shifted from agricultural to municipal and industrial needs.

**Biological invasion.** The [[Red Shiner]], a bait minnow introduced into [[Lake Mead]] in the 1950s, spread upstream. Other invaders recorded include channel catfish, black bullhead, bluegill, green sunfish, mosquitofish, and rainbow trout. Dominance in the mid-1980s appears linked to upstream hydrologic changes, especially a record summer flow in 1983 that allowed dispersal through the Virgin Narrows, which sewage and irrigation return flows later maintained.

**Federal listing and flow standards.** The [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] project required a non-jeopardy biological opinion from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to protect woundfin. The opinion set a bypass of 86 cfs or natural flow, whichever was less. Mean daily flow records show this standard was not met. The operator instead maintained 86 cfs at a point about 9 miles below the LaVerkin Power Plant, leaving reaches between the power plant and the reservoir outlet with lower flows and reduced fish populations. The Service reopened consultation under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Key Findings

- **Lower Mainstem (1935–present):** Native fishes in the lower 25 miles were eliminated after [[Hoover Dam]] flooded the river, creating Lake Mead in the 1930s.
- **Tributaries (1960–present):** Construction of [[Schroeder Reservoir]] (about 20 surface acres, Beaver Dam Wash) in 1960 led to the decline of Virgin spinedace. Upstream dispersal of rainbow trout and fish poisoning efforts further reduced native populations. Virgin spinedace downstream were lost from about 18.3 miles of historic habitat.
- **Middle Mainstem (1985–present):** Red shiner dominance followed upstream invasion. Poisoning eliminated red shiner between Washington Fields and Johnson diversions, but native fishes did not recover, and red shiner have since re-established.
- **Zion National Park and downstream:** Native communities remain near historic abundance in the East and North Forks and the mainstem to the Quail Creek area. Non-native fishes occur but are not dominant. The authors warn that these stream habitats may not resist future domination.
- **Santa Clara River:** A segment above Gunlock Reservoir is also relatively unaltered.
- **Size structure:** Most speckled dace sampled measured under 30 mm or over 65 mm total length, which the authors read as evidence that reproduction occurs but recruitment is limited.
- **Management conflict:** Competing demands include Federal Reserved Water Rights, endangered species protection, sport fisheries, irrigation rights, and urban growth. The authors argue that the health of the river is linked to the health of the human community.
