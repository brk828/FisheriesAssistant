# Biological Survey of Streams and Lakes of Coconino National Forest, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1935 report by M. J. Madsen, Temporary Assistant Biologist for the [[U.S. Bureau of Fisheries]] (Department of Commerce), documents a field survey of the streams and lakes of the [[Coconino National Forest]] in north central Arizona. The survey was conducted from May to August 1934, during a severe multi-year drought, and was intended to provide the physical, chemical and biological data needed to select the species, size and number of fish to stock. The report concludes that most streams are intermittent and cannot support a general improvement plan, that a few streams merit targeted work, and that the shallow, mud-bottomed lakes require closed seasons, habitat structure and systematic stocking.

## Background & Description

The survey party consisted of M. J. Madsen (biologist in charge), Paul Klingenberg and Harry G. Moseley (assistant biologists), and Hudspeth (camp attendant). Fieldwork ran from May 16 to July 25 and again from August 15 to August 27, 1934. The [[Arizona State Fish and Game Commission]] supplied deputy game agents and boats, and the [[U.S. Forest Service]] supplied maps.

**Stated purpose.** The survey aimed to (1) document biological, chemical and physical conditions; (2) use these data to select fish species, size and number; (3) show where environmental alterations could increase production; and (4) flag problems needing more intensive study. The authors state it was a survey of conditions, not a complete study of any stream or lake.

**Setting.** The forest covers about 3,000 square miles and holds headwaters of two drainages: the [[Colorado River]] system (about 2,000 square miles, northeast) and the [[Verde River]] system (about 1,000 square miles, south), which is the source of most permanent waters. Elevations range from 12,600 feet at the state's highest point to about 4,500 feet on northeastern plateaus, with most waters near 6,800 feet. The city of [[Flagstaff]] is the forest's transport and activity hub.

**Methods.** Streams were sampled in 200-yard stations; lakes were sampled from an anchored boat. Physical measures included widths, depths, gradient (from USGS maps), water and air temperatures, lake depth and temperature profiles (Negretti-Zambra reversing thermometer), and turbidity (a 10 cm white disc). Field chemistry covered pH (colorimetric, bromthymol blue), free carbon dioxide (phenolphthalein titration against N/44 NaOH), dissolved oxygen (a modified [[Winkler Method]]), and alkalinity (titration with 0.02 N sulfuric acid). Biological sampling used gill nets, seines, dip nets, wire traps, angling, a square-foot sampling net for stream bottom fauna, an [[Ekman Dredge]] for lake bottoms, and a plankton net of 11 cm opening with #20 bolting silk.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Streams.** Most streams are rapid upstream and sluggish downstream. Gradient never exceeded 800 feet per mile; the average was about 150 feet per mile. Upper-reach temperatures peaked near 70°F, while lower reaches flowing through [[Semi-Desert]] conditions reached about 88°F. Drought had left most streams at low flow. Only [[Oak Creek]] had not suffered a water shortage and showed normal pool-riffle structure. Streams were judged chemically suitable for fish overall.

**Lakes.** The lakes lie on [[Anderson Mesa]] at about 7,000 feet, are isolated, shallow (none deeper than 20 feet), lack permanent inlets or outlets, and depend on storm runoff. Many originated from grazing livestock trampling grassy basins until they held water. Water level fluctuated 1 to 8 feet per year in some lakes. Turbidity ranged from 2 to 56 inches, and summer surface temperatures spanned about 26°F (54–80°F). The authors judged these conditions unsuitable for both bass (too cold most of the year) and trout (too warm at times, muddy, no spawning inlets).

**Drought effects.** Many lakes went dry in 1934; the report lists these as dry in August 1934: Ashurst, Long, Boot, Cow, Horse, Mud, Marshall, Soldier and Vail. Among those dry in early June were Dax, Deep, Duck Nest, Fisher and Fry, Hay, Myrtle, Potato, Pickett, Rogers and Red lakes. Only five lakes (Mormon, Mary, Stoneman, Annex, Kinnikinick) remained permanent. Evaporative concentration killed animal life in some lakes, most visibly in Ashurst.

## Key Findings

**Stream survey**
- Oak Creek was the healthiest stream: excellent pools, very good riffles, and the most abundant food supply (with West Clear Creek). Its maximum food count was 667 organisms per square foot.
- Overall, the average food count was 212 organisms per square foot. Mayflies averaged 76 per station, caddisflies 64 and stoneflies 20. The lowest count was 45 per square foot on Beaver Creek.
- Game fish, ranked by abundance: rainbow trout, brown trout, rock bass, catfish and bluegill sunfish. Suckers and bonytails occurred in all streams.
- Fish disease and parasitism were uncommon; suckers from West Clear Creek carried a small flatworm on the pectoral fins.
- Recommendations: Oak Creek should remain unchanged; Beaver, West Clear and main East Clear creeks should not be improved because floods make work impractical; smaller East Clear tributaries are the best improvement candidates, using check dams to create deeper pools and conserve water. All streams need systematic stocking.

**Lake survey**
- Fish observed in order of abundance: yellow perch, golden shiner, mud catfish, crappie, largemouth black bass, bluegill sunfish, rock bass and rainbow trout.
- Growth data from scales: perch took about four years to reach a six-inch fish, so the authors judged perch poorly suited to these lakes. Rainbow trout grew well, while bass grew poorly. The authors called for more data before firm conclusions.
- Stoneman Lake was the only permanent lake with abundant plant life; food organisms included midge larvae, oligochaetes, nematodes, snails and plankton.
- Free-floating algae in August was judged beneficial, as a food source for small fish organisms.
- Recommended management for permanent lakes: a closed season on black bass from May 15 to July 1; removal of yellow perch size restrictions; spawning boxes for bass; artificial cover; more aquatic and shore vegetation (except Stoneman); wave-check dikes; enlarged drainage channels; and systematic stocking.
- Lake Mary specifically: stabilize water level by filling underground drainage channels or building a permanent dam, after consulting a geologist.

**Stocking framework**
- Stream stocking should weigh water temperature, bottom type, food organisms, stream dimensions, chemistry, fishing pressure, prior stocking, natural spawning success, accessibility and existing species. The recommendations build on a planting table by [[G. C. Embody]].
- Lake stocking is based on area and food richness. The table gives three-inch trout per acre by average fish weight (for example, 600, 374 and 150 fish per acre for 1/2 lb fish, across Excellent, Average and Below Average production classes).
- Warm-water recommendations and the full stocking table are summarized in the report's Tables 3, 7 and 8 rather than reproduced here.

**Bird predators:** Great blue herons, kingfishers and loons were present but not numerous enough to harm game fish. The authors recommended controlling herons only if they became too numerous, not exterminating them.
