# Ecology and Population Dynamics of Chironomus plumosus in Lake Winnebago, Wisconsin

#aquatic-ecology #limnology #chironomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by William L. Hilsenhoff (Department of Entomology, University of Wisconsin, Madison) examines why [[Chironomus Plumosus]] populations fluctuate so strongly in [[Lake Winnebago]]. The midge is abundant enough to be a serious nuisance. Over about 3½ years, the author sampled four buoyed sites for water and mud chemistry and for benthic fauna. Chironomid numbers varied greatly between years and between sites, but no water characteristic clearly tracked them. Mud chemistry, wind during adult emergence and egg-laying, diatom-driven emergence cues, disease, parasites, fish predation, and leech predation were all identified as influential. The author concludes that the exact causes could not be confirmed without controlled experiments.

## Background & Description

*Chironomus plumosus* is a nonbiting midge whose larvae live in the profundal mud of [[Lake Winnebago]]. The study aimed to identify the factors controlling population size and distribution and to assess options for biological or cultural control. Its proposed control approaches included [[Biological Control]] using disease organisms.

**Study design**
- Four sampling sites (North, Central, West, South buoys) were marked on the lake (Fig. 1).
- Sampling frequency varied by year: weekly in 1961, every 10 days in 1962, and every 2 weeks in 1963 and 1964. Winter sampling was limited by ice and deep snow.
- Water samples were taken just above the bottom with a [[Kemmerer Water Sampler]]. Mud was collected with a 6-in. [[Ekman Dredge]] and sieved through 60-mesh screen.
- Water variables: [[Dissolved Oxygen]] (via the [[Winkler Method]], Azide modification), suspended solids, pH, nitrate, ammonium, phosphorus, [[Secchi Disc]] visibility, depth, and wind.
- Mud variables: pH, [[Redox Potential]], ammonium, phosphorus, percent water, and temperature.
- Benthos were subsampled (five 1-ml subsamples under a stereomicroscope, plus the remaining 190 ml examined at 7X). Early instars were counted only in subsamples, and very small organisms passed through the sieve and were not quantified.

**Life cycle (from Hilsenhoff 1966)**
- Two generations per year, with major emergences in mid-May and late July or August.
- Larvae spend 3-4 weeks in the first three instars and at least 3 weeks in the fourth.
- Pupation and emergence need an external stimulus. Adults fly with the wind to the lee shore, and females return to lay eggs about 3 days later.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions**
- Lake water was usually well mixed by wind. Water chemistry showed no significant differences among sites, except for winter dissolved oxygen.
- Dissolved oxygen stayed near saturation, averaging 8 ppm in July to 14 ppm in December. Under ice, readings fell to 2.8 ppm at the South Buoy and 3.2 ppm at the Central Buoy. Summer calm periods dropped it to 4.0 ppm.
- Under ice, surface water stayed near 0°C and bottom water ranged from 1° to 3°C. Maximum summer mud temperatures reached 23.0°-25.0°C.
- Water pH followed a seasonal cycle, peaking at about 8.6 in late July and reaching a highest recorded value of 9.2.
- Under ice the lake was clear (Secchi 8-10 ft, suspended solids under 5 mg/L). Spring turnover dropped Secchi readings to 3 ft or less.
- Nitrate was often absent but occasionally exceeded 1.0 ppm. Ammonium in the water reached about 1.0 ppm.
- Water depth was regulated by a dam on the [[Fox River]], which was lowered in winter to allow spring flooding.

**Mud chemistry differences among sites**
- Mud differed far more among sites than water did.
- Four-year mean mud ammonium (ppm): North 15.5, Central 30.0, West 38.4, South 21.0.
- Mean mud redox (mV): North -15, Central 8, West -6, South 16.
- Mud at the West Buoy had lower pH and higher ammonium than the other buoys from May 1963 to June 1964.
- The mud at the North Buoy was the densest, had the lowest redox potential, and had the least ammonium.

**Circulation**
- The [[Fox River]] enters at Oshkosh, flows to the northeast corner of the lake, then west to the outlet at Neenah-Menasha. This current governed nutrient and oxygen distribution, especially under ice.

**Benthic community**
- Dominant benthos included [[Copepoda]] and [[Ostracoda]], which the author considered the main food for predatory chironomids.
- Predatory tanypod midges ([[Procladius Freemani]], [[Coelotanypus Concinnus]], and possibly *Procladius denticulatus*) were common.
- Clams (Sphaeriidae) were abundant at all sites, and snails were much more common at the North Buoy.
- Leeches included [[Helobdella Stagnalis]] (a known predator on fourth-instar larvae), *H. nepheloidea* (which does not feed on *C. plumosus*), *Placobdella montifera*, and *Glossiphonia complanata*.

## Key Findings

**1. Population fluctuation.** *C. plumosus* varied strongly across years and sites. Mean fourth-instar counts per square foot were North 42.7, Central 84.4, West 101.8, and South 79.1. Yearly means were 130.6 (1961), 43.4 (1962), 54.0 (1963), and 80.0 (1964) (Table 2).

**2. Chemistry and water.** No water variable clearly explained population size. Mud chemistry, particularly at the West Buoy, appeared to matter. Fourth-instar larvae were virtually absent at the West Buoy from May 1963 to June 1964, then repopulated after the mud pH and ammonium returned to normal in spring 1964. The author judged that some unknown factor, not low pH or high ammonium directly, was the cause. Pollutants from Oshkosh were proposed as one possible source of the chemical changes.

**3. Diatoms as emergence cue.** A feeding stimulus is required for pupation and emergence. In Lake Winnebago this is normally supplied by [[Diatom Blooms]] (*Melosira* and *Stephanodiscus*). Spring blooms depend on nutrient runoff. The open winters of 1957-58 and 1963-64 had little runoff, so no May emergence occurred in 1957-58, and only about 5% of overwintering larvae at the South Buoy emerged in May 1964. Almost all remaining larvae there emerged after diatoms bloomed in early August 1964.

**4. Wind and dispersal.** Wind direction and velocity during [[Emergence Stimulus]] and oviposition strongly controlled where adults laid eggs. Strong winds could blow flies far inland. Prevailing southerly winds in May and June tended to displace the population northward.

**5. Larval migration.** Larvae may leave the mud when oxygen is depleted or when they develop in poor substrates. A large fourth-instar population appeared at the South Buoy in August 1963. The author attributed this to migration from the rocky southern fifth of the lake, carried north by wind-driven currents.

**6. Sampling bias.** Below about 6°C larvae stop feeding and burrow deeper, so winter samples likely underestimate the population. The author judged the error small because larvae probably cannot penetrate much below the dredge sampling zone.

**7. Disease.** In June 1962 very large early-instar populations at the Central Buoy failed to reach the fourth instar. This was attributed to a disease epidemic, likely bacterial, fungal, or viral. The author noted that a cultured pathogen could be an effective [[Biological Control]] agent.

**8. Microsporidian parasite.** A *Thelohania* [[Microsporidia]] parasite infected up to 50% of larvae, but normally fewer than 5%. It is fatal only to fourth instars and is unlikely to explain early-instar losses, though it may contribute to fourth-instar declines.

**9. Fish predation.** Fish stomach analyses showed that all species in the lake eat *C. plumosus* at least occasionally. Those feeding heavily were [[Lake Sturgeon]], [[Freshwater Drum]], carp, [[Yellow Perch]], and various catfish. Exclusion cages showed predation was heavy in warmer months. Year-class size, alternate prey such as minnows, and feeding location also mattered.

**10. Leech predation.** *Helobdella stagnalis* is an important predator on fourth-instar larvae, especially in summer. It has a one-year life cycle. Its populations were highest at the North Buoy and lowest at the South Buoy. Mortality was high, and numbers fell rapidly by spring, except in 1964.

**11. Tanypod predation.** In a replicated 30-day laboratory test, neither *Coelotanypus* nor *Procladius* changed fourth-instar numbers. Early instars probably form an important food source for them. The emergences of *C. concinnus* and *P. freemani* followed first-instar appearance, suggesting that predation on first-instar *C. plumosus* may cue their emergence. In late 1963 and early 1964, *C. concinnus* increased while *P. freemani* declined, suggesting competition for the same niche.

**12. Overall conclusion.** Many interacting factors (chemistry, weather, migration, parasites, predators, and disease) controlled *C. plumosus* abundance. The author stated that their relative importance could only be determined through future controlled laboratory or field experiments.
