# The Noble Muskellunge: A Historical Review of Knowledge and Exploitation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This review traces the nomenclature, prehistory, commercial exploitation, sport-fishery history and research development of the [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy*), a large freshwater predator of North America. The author argues that the species was recognized late, has a confused taxonomic and naming history, and was heavily depleted by a poorly documented commercial fishery that ended in 1936. Biological knowledge grew slowly until the 1950s–1960s, when the volume of published information roughly doubled. The paper calls for better coordination of research, population-specific management, protection of older fish, and new non-lethal study techniques.

## Background & Description

**Nomenclature.** The accepted scientific name, *Esox masquinongy* Mitchill 1824, rests on a citation that could not be verified. The original article, supposedly in *The Mirror* (1824), was never located, and later authors (Jordan 1877, 1882; Jordan and Evermann 1896–1900) reported that it was absent. The name was retained largely through established usage. Early workers confused the species with the [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), producing many doubtful names (e.g., *E. estor*, *E. nobilior*, *E. salmoneus*). Jordan and Evermann treated the species as *Lucius masquinongy* with three forms, and a trinomial persisted in the Ohio River populations.

**Common name.** Weed (1927) counted at least 40 legitimate spellings of the muskellunge name and 94 names when other vernacular names and modifiers were included. Proposed derivations include Latin, Québécois and Algonquian (Chippewa, Ojibway, Cree, Menomini) origins. Bloomfield (1975) derived "muscalonge" from words meaning "spotted" and "pickerel," which fits the spotted Great Lakes pattern.

**Morphology and pattern.** The typical Great Lakes race has small, dark brown or black spots on a silvery to green or golden ground colour. The author notes that this pattern provides poor camouflage in vegetated still waters. The [[Amur Pike]] (*Esox reicherti*) is the only other living esocid with black spots, and it lives mainly in rivers among rocks.

**Size.** The species most often approaches its potential maximum size in large, running waters such as the [[St. Lawrence River]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fossil record and distribution.** Possible fossil records include the Miocene (about 25 million years before present) of Oregon, the lower Pliocene (about 7 MYBP) of Oklahoma, and several Pleistocene records from Oklahoma, Kansas and Michigan. Indefinite prehistoric records exist for Ontario (about 700 YBP) and the St. Lawrence River (about 600 YBP). The author suggests a pike-like ancestor, possibly *E. tiemani*, and a formerly much wider range.

**Postglacial history.** The present distribution is attributed mainly to events during and after the [[Wisconsin Glaciation]], to habitat loss from settlement and agriculture, and to human-assisted range extension through stocking. The species probably survived the glacial maximum in a Mississippi refugium and then spread north via the Ohio River and early outfalls of the [[Great Lakes]]. It may have reached the Upper Mississippi no earlier than 9,000–10,000 YBP, apparently not using the Red River–Lake Agassiz route.

**Habitat and interactions.** The author proposes that the muskellunge was originally a riverine fish and became a secondary lake-dweller. In large rivers it coexists with the northern pike, while in still waters the pike usually has the advantage (Harrison and Hadley 1978). Spawning in large rivers tends to occur at greater, safer depths than in Central Ontario lakes, where shallow-water spawning exposes eggs to fluctuating levels and temperatures.

**Commercial harvest.** The commercial fishery was poorly documented. Landings were usually recorded by weight (often in barrels of 200 lb), and no numbers were recorded, so the author applied a mean weight of 15 lb per fish. Key findings include:

- **Ontario:** approximately 13.2 million lb ($589,467) landed, 1868–1936; peak of 774,320 lb in 1898.
- **Quebec:** approximately 2.8 million lb ($266,110); peak of 209,415 lb in 1886; landings ended in 1936.
- **Canadian total:** over 16 million lb, estimated at 1,069,958 fish.
- **Lake Scugog:** 3,840,550 lb in 1883–1901 (mean 202,134 lb/yr, about 13 kg/ha/yr on 7,030 ha).
- **Trent River and Rice Lake:** 1,703,717 lb in 1881–1901.
- **Lakes Simcoe and Couchiching:** 445,680 lb in 1881–1901 (0.12 kg/ha/yr on 77,368 ha).
- **New York:** 89,000 lb in 1894 and 106,130 lb in 1895 (about 13,000 fish); no muskellunge reported from 1900 onward.

Several U.S. states reported no commercial fishing, which the author considers doubtful given the New York and Ohio records. A 1917 Yukon record of 1,100 lb of "maskinonge" is considered unreliable.

**Sport fishery decline.** Reduced abundance was recognised by 1850 (Buss 1960) and again in the 1930s (Harkness 1934). The 1980 angler harvest on Lake Scugog was 720 fish (3,190 kg), far below late nineteenth-century levels.

## Key Findings

**1. The species was recognised late and its naming is unstable.** The 1824 description is unverified, and confusion with *Esox lucius* produced many invalid or composite names.

**2. Commercial exploitation was large and largely undocumented.** Canadian landings alone represent millions of pounds, and the fishery ended in Quebec in 1936 and in Ontario in 1901 (records) with state-level bans following. The effects on population size and angling success have never been properly examined.

**3. Hatchery culture became central to conservation.** Culture began in New York (1890), Wisconsin (1899), Ontario (1927) and Quebec (1950). Pituitary extracts were used to induce gamete ripening by 1940. The author questions the long-term contribution of hatchery releases relative to natural reproduction.

**4. Knowledge grew unevenly.** Early work was taxonomic and descriptive. Major growth came in the 1950s–1960s ("Wisconsin years"), and the author notes that data acquisition roughly doubled from 1950 to 1960 in many subject areas.

**5. A subject index shows where effort has gone.** Of 28 subject categories (Crossman and Goodchild 1978), the author examined 14 (Table 2). Culture and stocking dominate with 188 citations, followed by management (72), age and growth (65), population studies (43), hybrids (33), habitat (31) and pathology (30). Behaviour (5) and physiology were the least studied.

**6. Angling has shifted toward trophy selection.** Specialist anglers increasingly release fish above minimum size, so the author argues that survival to greater ages should be a major management aim. Slot-size and population-specific minimum-size regulations are proposed.

**7. Management must be race- and population-specific.** The author warns that Great Lakes data should not be used directly for Ohio or western races, and that populations differ in maximum size and size at maturity.

**8. Research needs are identified.** Neglected topics include homing to destroyed spawning habitat, time-trend analysis of natural fluctuations, the biology of very old fish, angling stress, and the mismatch between hatching and prey availability for young fish. The [[Cleithrum Aging Project]] at the [[Royal Ontario Museum]] is noted as a valuable repository for age estimation.

**9. Hybrid stocking is a risk.** The author opposes widespread introduction of the [[Tiger Muskellunge]] (hybrid) where pure muskellunge populations can persist, and recommends local indigenous stock for hatchery use.

**10. Research funding and coordination need improvement.** The paper recommends better communication among agencies, possibly through interactive workshops, newsletters and priority-setting committees such as the Ontario Esocid Advisory Committee. It also suggests that user fees (for example, the Ontario muskellunge stamp) may help fund research, and that non-lethal sampling techniques are needed as fewer fish are killed.
