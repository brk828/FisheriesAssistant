# Spikedace (Meda fulgida) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This agency review draft is a [[Recovery Plan]] for the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), a small stream-dwelling cyprinid listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The plan was prepared by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) for the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] Region 2 and dated July 20, 1988. The document is explicitly a draft, not an approved government document, and its goals are contingent on appropriations and priorities.

The species, endemic to the [[Gila River]] system, has declined sharply in range and abundance. Its remaining populations are restricted to a few stream reaches in Arizona and New Mexico. Declines are attributed to habitat destruction and alteration together with predation and displacement by non-native fishes, especially the [[Red Shiner]]. The plan's primary objective is to protect existing populations, restore depleted ones, and reestablish the species in its historic range so that it can become non-endangered and self-sustaining, which would justify delisting.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and morphology.** Spikedace is described as the only species in its genus (a "monotypic genus") and is a slender, laterally compressed minnow with scales reduced to small embedded plates. Diagnostic traits include two spinose dorsal-fin rays, seven dorsal rays, usually nine anal rays, large eyes and mouth, no barbels, and pharyngeal teeth in two rows (formula 1,4-4,1). Coloration is bright silvery with vertically elongated black specks. Breeding males become golden or brassy, especially on the head and fin bases.

**Life history.**
- **Reproduction:** Breeding occurs April–June and is triggered by a combination of stream discharge and water temperature. Males patrol shallow, sandy-gravelly riffles, and up to six males may approach a receptive female. Fecundity ranges from 90 to 250 ova and is correlated with length and age. Ova are about 1.5 mm in diameter and are adhesive and demersal. No fertilized ova have been recovered.
- **Growth and longevity:** Young fish reach 35–40 mm standard length (SL) by November. Fish average about 40 mm SL at the end of year one and 50–63 mm SL at the end of year two. Maximum size is about 45 mm SL in [[Aravaipa Creek]] and 48 mm SL in the upper Gila River. Longevity is typically one to two years, with a few fish reaching age three and rare individuals age four.
- **Diet:** Spikedace are carnivores feeding mainly on drifting aquatic and terrestrial insects, with larval mayflies (Ephemeroptera), hydropsychid caddisflies (Trichoptera), and chironomid midges (Diptera) most important. Diet diversity is greatest in small fish, while adults specialize on drifting nymphal and adult ephemeropterans.

**Taxonomic note.** The plan gives a length conversion of SL = 0.85TL − 0.12 (r² = 0.99, n = 100).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Historically, spikedace was widespread in moderate-sized, intermediate-elevation rivers of the Gila system upstream of Phoenix, including the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers and the Agua Fria, San Pedro, and San Francisco systems. Transplanted populations in Sonoita Creek (1968) and Sycamore-area 7-Springs Wash (1970) were later extirpated. Today, Arizona populations occur only in:
- [[Aravaipa Creek]] (tributary to the [[San Pedro River]])
- [[Eagle Creek]] (tributary to the Gila River)
- the upper [[Verde River]]

In New Mexico, the species is restricted to the mainstem Gila River and its East, Middle, and West forks. Only the Cliff-Gila Valley population was judged comparable in abundance to earlier years. Undiscovered populations may exist on remote tribal lands, Forest Service land, and in Sonora.

**Habitat.** Spikedace occupy flowing water usually less than one meter deep. Adults aggregate in shear zones along gravel-sand bars, eddies below riffles, and shallow areas above bars. Young fish favor quieter pool margins with soft, fine-grained bottoms. In large rivers, they are often found near tributary mouths. Habitat use varies seasonally, geographically, and with age.

**Co-occurring fishes.** Native associates include [[Loach Minnow]], speckled dace, Sonora sucker, desert sucker, and longfin dace. Non-native fishes present include red shiner, channel catfish, flathead catfish, [[Largemouth Bass]], smallmouth bass, green sunfish, and introduced trout at higher elevations.

**Causes of decline.**
- **Habitat alteration:** Dewatering, impoundments, altered flow regimes, channelization, bank stabilization, groundwater pumping, and downcutting have extirpated or depleted populations. Spikedace do not persist in reservoirs.
- **Watershed impacts:** Grazing, mining, agriculture, timber harvest, and development increase erosion, sedimentation, and contamination. A wastewater spill at the [[Cananea Mine]], Sonora, killed all fishes across a 100-km reach downstream (Eberhardt 1981).
- **Non-native fishes:** Catfishes, centrarchids, and salmonids prey on native fish. The red shiner is considered particularly important because it appears to displace spikedace from suitable habitat.

The plan concludes that the relative importance of habitat change and non-native fishes is not yet established, so both must be managed.

**Legal status.** Spikedace is federally listed as threatened. Arizona classifies it as Group 3 threatened, and New Mexico classifies it as Group 2 endangered. Proposed [[Critical Habitat]] was deferred until 18 June 1987 and remained unfinalized at the time of the draft, so it provides only limited protection. State listings protect spikedace from take except by angling in Arizona, but do not otherwise protect the species or its habitat.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective.** Protect existing populations, restore depleted and extirpated populations and their habitats, and ensure the species can become non-endangered and self-sustaining. Quantitative delisting criteria cannot be set until more information is available.

**Recovery stepdown outline.** The plan organizes recovery into nine major actions:
1. **Protect existing populations:** Identify and prioritize populations, designate critical habitat, enforce laws, curtail non-native introductions, insure natural flows, and acquire key lands and water rights.
2. **Monitor status:** Establish standard locations and techniques, define natural variation in abundance and age structure, and conduct biannual (spring and autumn) population estimates.
3. **Assess non-native interactions:** Study direct (predation, displacement) and indirect interactions through field, experimental, and laboratory work.
4. **Quantify habitat effects:** Define responses to substrate, velocity and depth, temperature, and their interactions.
5. **Enhance or restore habitats:** Identify target areas and implement improvements, including non-native fish removal where needed.
6. **Reintroduce populations:** Reestablish spikedace in historic streams. The [[San Pedro River]] system in Arizona is identified as the most amenable recipient, with Aravaipa Creek as a likely source stock.
7. **Define self-sustaining populations:** Set criteria for absolute numbers, age structure, reproduction, recruitment, [[Minimum Stock Size]], and environmental variables.
8. **Captive propagation:** Currently judged unnecessary, but recommended as a contingency and as a source of fish for reintroductions.
9. **Information and education:** Inform the public, media, Mexican agencies, and professionals, including through peer-reviewed publication.

**Critical habitat.** The plan recommends adding a segment of Eagle Creek (about 38 km, from the Phelps Dodge diversion dam upstream to Sheep Wash) to the proposed designation. This population was undiscovered when critical habitat was first proposed. Proposed reaches include about 24 km of Aravaipa Creek, about 57 km of the Verde River, and about 73 km of the Gila River in three New Mexico sections. Sycamore Creek was proposed but will not be included in the final rule.

**Implementation schedule.** Tasks are ranked by priority:
- **Priority 1:** Actions essential to prevent extinction (e.g., protecting populations, enforcing laws, curtailing non-native introductions).
- **Priority 2:** Actions needed to maintain current population status.
- **Priority 3:** Actions needed for full recovery (e.g., acquisition, reintroduction, and captive propagation).

Cost estimates are USFWS expenditures only. Examples include about 1,000 to 1,500 per year for law enforcement and habitat-protection tasks, 5,000 for three years of population status work, and 10,000 per year for community composition and non-native interaction research. Several tasks have unknown costs, such as land acquisition, instream flow purchases, and habitat implementation.
