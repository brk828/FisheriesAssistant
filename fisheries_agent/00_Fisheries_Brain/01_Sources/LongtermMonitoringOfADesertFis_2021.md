# Long-Term Monitoring of a Desert Fish Assemblage in Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #desert-fishes #long-term-monitoring

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] supports seven naturally occurring native fish species: [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*, federally endangered), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*, federally endangered), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarkii*). This represents the only water body in Arizona with all seven species naturally co-occurring. Five additional native species historically occurred: [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*, endangered, extirpated), [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*, endangered, extirpated), [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*). The latter three large-river species historically occurred in the [[San Pedro River]] and may have seasonally occupied Aravaipa Creek. Specific age, maturity, fecundity, and growth parameters are not reported in this monitoring summary.

**Habitat Associations**: Aravaipa Creek is a perennial desert stream with average monthly mean flow varying between 0.28 and 0.85 m³/s (10-30 cfs). The system experiences regular flood disturbance: winter cyclonic and summer monsoonal storms produce floods with peak discharges of ~76.5 m³/s (2,700 cfs) approximately 2 of every 3 years and ~343 m³/s (12,100 cfs) once every 10 years. Exceptional floods occurred in October 1983 (~897 m³/s, 30,000 cfs) and August 2006 (~779 m³/s, 27,500 cfs). These flood events massively alter physical and hydrologic characteristics. The creek flows through three distinct reaches (upper, canyon, lower) with varying habitat quality and riparian conditions. [[Speckled Dace]] exhibits the most restricted and patchy distribution of any native species. [[Spikedace]] currently occurs mainly in upper reaches, while [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*, nonnative) dominates lower reaches.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Not reported in detail. Native fishes demonstrate behavioral responses to flood events, moving to stream margins to avoid displacement, whereas nonnative species lack these adaptive behaviors and are more readily displaced downstream.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: [[Longfin Dace]] was the most abundant native species throughout 54 years of monitoring, accounting for 71.6% of all native fish during the 1974-1976 peak abundance period. [[Red Shiner]] is believed to compete directly with native fish for both space and food resources. [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*) were regularly captured (hundreds of individuals) when electrofishing and were found throughout the creek; their impact on native fishes is poorly understood but they are nocturnal predators. [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) were present as nonnative predators, though largemouth bass are no longer found at monitoring sites.

**Environmental Drivers**: Native fish abundance showed no significant relationship with flow volume (linear regression, 2002-2012), indicating resistance to displacement by elevated flows, likely due to evolutionary adaptation to flood-prone desert streams. In contrast, nonnative fish species responded negatively to elevated flows (p < 0.0006), with populations reduced by up to 99% following major floods (e.g., red shiner declined from 596 individuals in fall 2004 to 1 individual in fall 2006 post-flood). Lack of flow variability during drought periods (2001-2004, defined as annual water volume less than lower 95% confidence interval of mean annual volume) resulted in increased nonnative fish abundance: red shiner increased from 5 individuals in 2000 to 903 in 2004. The 2006 flood resulted in positive response by spikedace, with 2,666 individuals counted in 2008 (the third-highest count on record), likely due to sediment mobilization, allochthonous inputs, and nonnative displacement creating favorable habitat.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Total native fish counts from all sites and both seasons combined showed two major peak periods: 1974-1976 (15,735 to 33,462 individuals, peak 33,462 in 1975) and 1991-1992 (22,494 and 12,797 individuals respectively). From 2002-2017, peak abundance was 8,333 individuals in 2009, representing less than 25% of the 1975 peak and approximately 65.1% of the 1992 peak. At no time since 1992 have more than 10,000 individuals been counted. [[Spikedace]] showed greatest counts in 1991 (n=7,481) and 1992 (n=4,567), far exceeding any other years. Coefficients of variation (CV) for 2002-2016 indicated highest population variability in [[Speckled Dace]] (CV spring=85.1, fall=153.1, combined=161.3), [[Spikedace]] (CV=78.4 combined), and [[Loach Minnow]] (CV=60.8 combined). Population variation between successive years was greater than variation between fall and spring surveys within any given year. Fish assemblage showed high temporal stability: Morisita's index (IM) values from 1986-1987 through 2016-2017 were always greater than 0.90, indicating high year-to-year similarity. From 2002-2017, mean IM values were 0.93±0.07 for spring surveys, 0.93±0.05 for fall surveys, and 0.97±0.02 for combined seasons.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: The freshwater fish fauna of the desert Southwest is one of the most imperiled groups of vertebrate organisms in North America. Primary threats include: (1) exploitation and manipulation of extremely limited water resources, (2) introduction of nonnative species, (3) habitat modification and degradation, and (4) climate change. Eight nonnative fish species have been detected in Aravaipa Creek during monitoring; three persist above the fish barrier since 2002: [[Red Shiner]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Yellow Bullhead]]. Red shiner was first recorded in 1990, spread throughout the system by 1998, and currently accounts for 94.8% of all nonnative individuals collected. Since 2001, 80.7% of 5,939 red shiners collected were found in the lower reach, with only 3 individuals in the upper reach. [[Spikedace]] was present in the lower reach in early 1960s and regularly found there from 1975 until the 1990s when red shiner was first detected; the two species now have different distributions, with spikedace in upper reaches and red shiner in lower reaches. [[Green Sunfish]] maintained a source population in Horse Camp Canyon tributary but was successfully eradicated through targeted removal efforts using fish traps; none detected since 2014. Proposed water extraction during flood flows represents an ongoing threat; maintenance of the natural hydrograph is critical to survival of the intact native fish fauna.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: (1) Continue biannual monitoring at nine permanent stations to detect critical changes in populations and presence of new nonnative species, enabling rapid and effective management responses. (2) Maintain the double fish-movement barrier installed by US Bureau of Reclamation in 2001 at the lower end of Aravaipa Creek (~8 km from confluence with San Pedro River), which has effectively prevented establishment of additional nonnative species (common carp, black bullhead, fathead minnow not detected upstream for 15 years post-installation). (3) Implement targeted removal programs for nonnative species, particularly [[Red Shiner]], which should be considered given its concentration in lower reaches and potential for upstream expansion. (4) Prevent water extraction proposals that would alter the natural hydrograph; both base flows and flood flows are essential for maintaining native fish fauna by sustaining necessary habitat and critically displacing nonnative fishes. (5) Use monitoring data to determine sustainable harvest numbers for [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] translocation efforts, as Aravaipa Creek serves as an important source population for restoration throughout the [[Gila River Basin]]. (6) Maintain clear, well-defined sites, goals, and protocols for monitoring programs, and share all original data including copies of original data sheets in commonly used repositories.

**Biological Reference Points**: Not reported. The document emphasizes that processes and detectable changes in desert fish populations occur over temporal spans of decades or longer, exceeding the brief scales of weeks or months typical of many scientific studies. Peak native fish abundances (33,462 in 1975; 22,494 in 1991) provide historical reference points, though current abundances (peak 8,333 in 2009) are substantially lower. The natural flow regime includes average monthly mean flows of 0.28-0.85 m³/s, flood events of ~76.5 m³/s every 1.5 years, and ~343 m³/s every 10 years, representing critical reference conditions for native fish persistence.
