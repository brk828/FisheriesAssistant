# Untying the Western Water Knot

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This article, written by [[Ken Wiley]] for a [[The Nature Conservancy]] publication, argues that the survival of many Western aquatic species and ecosystems depends on keeping water flowing in rivers and streams. Because most land in the West is already protected, the Conservancy's main tool is acquiring [[Water Rights]] and converting them to [[Instream Flow]] protection. The article traces the legal foundation of Western water, the [[Colorado Doctrine]] of [[Prior Appropriation]], and describes case studies in Arizona, Colorado, Nevada, and Idaho. A second, truncated piece on Brazil's [[Atlantic Forest]] follows, but it does not address aquatic species.

## Background & Description

The article opens with the history of the [[Union Colony]], founded in 1870 on the [[Cache la Poudre River]] in northeastern Colorado. Greeley's colonists built ditches and canals, and their water conflict with the upstream [[Fort Collins Colony]] during the drought of 1874 led to the first state constitution's water provisions. The Colorado Doctrine, adopted in 1876, still shapes Western water allocation.

Riparian rights in the eastern United States differ from appropriative rights in the West. The article identifies three principles of prior appropriation:

- **First in time, first in right:** the earliest diverter holds senior rights.
- **Beneficial use:** water not put to beneficial use can be lost. Uses such as fisheries, recreation, and wildlife habitat have traditionally not counted because they require water to remain in the stream.
- **Separability:** appropriative rights can be bought, sold, and transferred apart from the land.

Because instream uses were historically excluded, conservation groups needed legal tools to protect flows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Muleshoe Ranch Preserve, Arizona.** Perennial streams (Bass, Double R, and Hot Springs Canyon) in the Sonoran Desert support the [[Gila Chub]] and other rare desert fishes. Cottonwood riparian corridors also provide habitat for birds and mammals.

**Aravaipa Creek, Arizona.** This stream supports the most diverse native fish assemblage in Arizona, and perhaps the entire Southwest. It contains seven native fish species, including the federally listed [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). Fish tagging is used to track seasonal migration and habitat needs.

**Upper Colorado River Basin.** The basin is the last stronghold of four endangered fish: the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The lower Yampa River reach through Dinosaur National Monument is believed to drive squawfish spawning and is prime adult humpback chub habitat. It is also being considered as a reintroduction site for the bonytail chub.

**Pyramid Lake and Stillwater, Nevada.** The [[Cui-ui]], a large native sucker, and the federally listed [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] depend on Pyramid Lake. Falling lake levels, caused by upstream diversions to the [[Truckee-Carson Irrigation District]], have sharply reduced cui-ui numbers. The Stillwater marshes are important for migratory birds, including snow geese, white pelicans, tundra swans, and canvasbacks.

## Key Findings

**Ramsey Canyon Preserve (Arizona).** In 1979 the Conservancy applied for an instream flow permit, which was approved in 1983. It was the first legal recognition in Arizona of the right to appropriate water for wildlife and recreation without diversion, and the Conservancy became the first private entity in the West granted an instream flow right.

**Aravaipa Creek (Arizona).** The Conservancy holds an 1,800 acre-foot water right dating to 1887, one of the oldest on the stream. Its field office is working to convert these irrigation rights to instream flow for wildlife protection.

**Black Canyon of the Gunnison (Colorado).** [[Pittsburg & Midway Coal Mining Company]], a Chevron subsidiary, held conditional rights to 800 cfs for a dam that would have flooded the canyon. In a 1987 donation, it gave the Conservancy a right of about 300 cfs, valued by Chevron at over $7 million, and relinquished its dam plans. Because Colorado law permits only the state to hold instream rights, the Conservancy transferred the right to the [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]]. The tentative agreement, awaiting final approval, is among the first under the 1987 law.

**Yampa River (Colorado).** The Conservancy targets instream flow protection for over 120 miles, from below Craig, Colorado, to the Green River confluence.

**Stillwater Wetlands (Nevada).** A 1910 irrigation dam diverted more than half of the Truckee's flow into the Carson River. Stillwater National Wildlife Refuge was established in 1948 with about 30,000 acres of wetlands but no water rights. Today the wetlands cover fewer than 7,000 acres. Shifting Truckee water to Pyramid Lake (about 47,000 acre-feet per year) could cut wetland acreage by as much as 80 percent. The Conservancy is optioning marginal irrigated land and transferring its water to the marshes. Its goal is to acquire 20,000 acres of irrigated farmland and 55,000 acre-feet of water at a cost of about $40 million. Stillwater has been designated a Western Hemispheric Shorebird Preserve, one of only 13 worldwide.

**Snake River Rescue (Idaho).** A freak Arctic cold front froze the Snake River after two years of drought. This blocked the food supply of 500 wintering trumpeter swans, about a quarter of those found outside Alaska. The Conservancy negotiated a one-time rental of 3,200 acre-feet from the Snake River Irrigation District, and about 80 percent of the swans were saved.

**Strategic conclusions.** The article argues that water marketing, which transfers water rights from agriculture to conservation, is central to protecting Western aquatic ecosystems. Success depends on partnerships among government agencies, tribes, agricultural interests, and cities. The author describes the Conservancy's approach as innovative in a historical context but traditional in its use of acquisition, and he invokes Horace Greeley's ideal of "the reconciling of all Interests."
