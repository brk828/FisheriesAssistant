# Distribution, Spawning and Habitat Use of the Razorback Sucker in the Green River, Utah, 1979-85

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #freshwater-ecology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This study by Harold M. Tyus (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) presents an extensive investigation into the distribution, spawning ecology, and habitat use of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Green River Basin, Utah and Colorado, from 1979–1985. Using electrofishing surveys (250 adults captured) and radiotelemetry (10 fish implanted), the study found the species heterogeneously distributed with a center of abundance in the Upper Green River flatwater sections and near tributary mouths. The species was absent from whitewater canyons. Three probable spawning areas were identified via capture of 64 breeding-condition fish and confirmed by larval collections downstream. Critically, the complete absence of small size classes (<400 mm TL) and negligible measured growth in recaptured fish (18 individuals over 1–6 years) indicate a population of aging adults with **no successful recruitment**, raising serious concerns about long-term population viability absent intervention.

## Background & Description

- **Total Length (TL) range (Green River adults):** 426–583 mm
- **Average length/weight:** 520 mm TL / 1,458 g
- **Sexual size dimorphism:** Ripe males significantly smaller than ripe females (P=0.01)
  - Males: average 500 mm TL (n=20), max reported 512–520 mm
  - Females: average 539–541 mm TL (n=14), max reported 586 mm
- **Male:Female ratio on spawning grounds:** 1.45:1
- **Age estimates:**
  - Vertebral annuli count (505 mm specimen): 12+ years
  - Otolith aging (560 mm specimen, Univ. of Michigan): ~20 years
  - Comparatively younger than Lower Colorado Basin razorbacks, estimated by [[William L. Minckley]] (1983) at 30+ years
- **Comparative regional sizes:**
  - Lower Colorado River Basin (Minckley 1983): males to 640 mm, females to 740 mm TL (old fish)
  - Upper Colorado River mainstem (McAda and Wydoski 1980): males to 520 mm, females to 620 mm TL
  - Upper Colorado mainstem (Valdez et al. 1982): average 554 mm TL, max 635 mm, no significant sex-based size difference
- **Larval identification:** Larvae measured 10.6–13.6 mm, identified via comparison with cultured *Xyrauchen* protolarvae/mesolarvae at the Larval Fish Laboratory, Colorado State University
- Length-frequency histograms were unimodal, providing no clear separation of age classes — consistent with a population lacking recruitment of younger cohorts

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution:**
- Center of distribution: Upper Green River, from the mouth of the [[Duchesne River]] to the mouth of the [[Yampa River]]
- Also found in low numbers in flatwater sections below Green River, Utah town
- Absent from whitewater canyons: Split Mountain Canyon ([[Dinosaur National Monument]]), [[Desolation Canyon]], [[Gray Canyon]] — indicating avoidance of high-gradient canyon habitat
- Also documented in Labyrinth Canyon (2 tuberculated fish) and lower Duchesne River

**Seasonal Habitat Use:**
- **Spring (breeding season):** Most abundant in shoreline habitats; captured over sand/gravel substrates and gravel-cobble bars near spawning grounds
- **Non-breeding season (summer–fall):** Shifted to midchannel habitats
  - Early spring: shoreline runs
  - Summer: strong preference for **midchannel sand bars** (coarse sand with surficial dips/ridges, presumed feeding sites) over deep holes
  - Depth range across seasons: 0.6–3.4 m (typically <2 m on summer sand bars)
  - Water velocity: averaged ~0.5 m/s on sand bars (radiotelemetry range 0.0–0.7 m/s)
  - Substrates utilized: primarily sand, with some silt and rubble

**Spawning Ecology:**
- Three identified spawning reaches:
  1. **Island and Echo Parks**, Green River (Dinosaur National Monument), including lower Yampa River
  2. **Jensen area**, Green River (Ashley Creek to Split Mountain) — most productive site (22 of 34 ripe fish captured here)
  3. **Lower Duchesne River**
- Spawning substrate: predominantly coarse sand, some gravel/cobble bars
- Spawning water temperatures: 10.5–18°C (average 15°C) at capture; ripe females specifically 13.5–18°C (average 16°C in Green River, 15°C in Duchesne River)
- River discharge during spawning period (1984): 13,800–38,500 cfs (avg. 26,100 cfs), peaking May 18
- Spawning occurs earlier in season than other native [[Catostomidae|sucker]] species
- Comparison to razorback thermal preference: hatchery-reared adults prefer 23–25°C (Bulkley and Pimentel 1983), indicating spawning occurs well below preferred thermal optimum — likely an evolved strategy tied to seasonal hydrology
- Lower Colorado Basin populations spawn at similar temperatures (12–16°C) over gravel in shallow water near stream inflows (Minckley 1983), suggesting conserved spawning cues across the species' range

**Movement Patterns (Radiotelemetry, n=10 implanted, 6 informative):**
- Largely **sedentary** during non-breeding periods — local movements only
- Breeding-to-breeding or nonbreeding-to-nonbreeding recaptures: avg. 10.3 km between capture points
- Nonbreeding-to-breeding (or vice versa) recaptures: avg. 69.1 km — indicating migratory movement specifically tied to reaching spawning grounds
- Example: one fish (tagged 1980) moved ~6 km downstream into Duchesne River, stayed <1 mile inside until June flooding, then moved to Green/Duchesne mixing zone (possible spawning), later moved ~11 km upstream in Green River

**Co-occurring Species / Community Context:**
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) — most abundant species in standardized samples (n=1,776)
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) — federally protected, co-occurring rare species (n=278)
- Numerous exotic predator species present in the system, hypothesized (but considered unlikely) to affect larval survival

## Key Findings

1. **Rarity and detection bias:** Razorback sucker were extremely rare in standardized, basin-wide sampling (5 fish over 3 years) but far more detectable during targeted spring spawning-season sampling (92–100 fish in short windows), highlighting strong seasonal/spatial capture bias and the value of targeted spawning-period surveys.
2. **Catch ratios:** Razorback:flannelmouth sucker catch ratio = 0.28% (all gear) / 0.4% (electrofishing catch rate); razorback catch was only 1.8% of Colorado squawfish catch (8.3% by electrofishing rate) — underscoring the species' extreme rarity relative to co-occurring native fish.
3. **No recruitment detected:** Absence of individuals <400 mm TL across all sampling years and unimodal length-frequency distributions strongly suggest a lack of successful recruitment into the adult population.
4. **Minimal growth in recaptured fish:** 18 fish recaptured 1–6 years post-tagging showed average growth of only 1.65 mm/year, which was **not statistically significant** (P=0.02) — consistent with an aging, non-recruiting population of large, old adults.
5. **Spawning confirmed despite recruitment failure:** Three spawning reaches identified via captures of 64 breeding-condition adults (34 with strippable sex products); larval collections downstream (31% of 42 samples containing razorback larvae) confirmed **successful spawning still occurs**, despite lack of recruitment to adulthood.
6. **Spawning phenology and site fidelity:** Fish exhibited long-distance directed movement (~69 km) specifically to reach spawning grounds, followed by sedentary residency the rest of the year — evidence of migratory spawning behavior overlaid on an otherwise sedentary life history.
7. **Age structure:** Vertebral and otolith aging suggested individuals as old as ~12–20 years, younger than aged individuals from the Lower Colorado Basin (~30+ years, Minckley 1983), but the Green River population still shows a troubling absence of younger cohorts.
8. **Hypothesized causes of recruitment failure:** Predation by exotic species was considered but deemed unlikely (exotics have coexisted longer than the razorback's estimated lifespan). The leading hypothesis implicates **habitat alteration following closure of Flaming Gorge Dam** (~20 years prior to study), consistent with the observed age structure of surviving individuals.
9. **Conservation implications:** Without recruitment, the Green River razorback sucker population is projected to decline as aging adults die off without replacement. The authors stress the urgency of federal endangered species protection to secure funding and conservation action, warning that without intervention the species may only persist in hatcheries, risking loss of genetic integrity.
