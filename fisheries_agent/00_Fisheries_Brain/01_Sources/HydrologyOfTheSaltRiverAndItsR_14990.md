# Hydrology of the Salt River and Its Reservoirs, Central Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This paper, abstracted from a doctoral thesis at [[Arizona State University]], describes the hydrology of the [[Salt River]] reservoir chain in central Arizona and considers how its flood-driven organic nutrient inputs and recent pumped-storage modifications may affect the aquatic ecosystem. The reservoirs are presented as hot-desert impoundments that are largely unstudied and ecologically distinct from better-documented temperate reservoirs. The text is primarily hydrological and limnological; it contains no species-level data and only indirect references to fish (distribution, concentrations and fisheries management).

## Background & Description

- **Historical context:** The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (established 1902) built water-storage structures in arid western lands; hydroelectric generation and later multiple-use management were added over time.
- **Study system:** The Salt River is a tributary of the Gila system within the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]. Its reservoir chain consists of [[Roosevelt Lake]] (Roosevelt Dam, 1911), Mormon Flat (1925), Horse Mesa (1927) and Stewart Mountain (1930), impounding Canyon, Apache and Saguaro lakes respectively. Horseshoe (1939) and Bartlett (1945) reservoirs lie on the Verde River.
- **Population pressure:** Human density reached 154/km² by 1940 and 772/km² by 1968 in the Phoenix metropolitan area, perhaps about 1,000/km² at writing.
- **Scope:** The study covers quantitative and qualitative hydrology, plus the expected effects of structural modifications on hydrology, biology and fisheries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Watershed and flow**
- Watershed area is slightly over 10,000 km²; annual precipitation is 25 to 89 cm.
- Average annual runoff is 7.11 cm (range 1.7 to 29.0 cm).
- Mean discharge of the Salt River is about 1,200 m³/min. The Salt River contributes roughly seven (mean flow) to ten (modal flow) times the volume of [[Tonto Creek]].
- Discharge shows a 3- to 4-year cycle of high annual flow. Winter 1970–71 flows were below average, while summer rains produced runoff comparable to 1960–69.
- Storage capacity of the four-reservoir chain is near 2.4 × 10⁶ ha-m.

**Reservoir levels and residence time**
- Canyon and Saguaro lakes stayed above 80% full since 1960, except during drainage years.
- Roosevelt Lake was mostly below 50% full-pool during the 1970–71 sampling.
- Calculated mean storage ratios were about 0.1 for Saguaro and Canyon, 5 times that for Apache and 3.5 times Apache's value for Roosevelt (about 1.65 mean). Theoretical flushing times averaged 38 days (Saguaro), 29 days (Canyon), 172 days (Apache) and 602 days (Roosevelt).

**Flood-driven organic input**
- An arbitrary discharge rate of 1.7 × 10⁴ m³/min was used as the threshold for flood conditions that move significant debris. Only 9 such days occurred between 1950 and 1965.
- The 1965–66 flood covered more than two-thirds of Roosevelt Lake's surface with debris.
- Litter accumulation is estimated at 12.5 t/ha/year in [[Chaparral]] and 5.0 t/ha/year in [[Ponderosa Pine]] forest. Estimated theoretical annual litter production for the basin is about 3.5 × 10⁶ t from these zones, and up to 7.0 × 10⁶ t for the whole watershed.
- Estimated organic input to Roosevelt Lake is roughly 5.7 × 10⁴ t per year, or 8,150 kg/ha of lake surface, a figure the author states may vary by 3 to 4 times.

**Isolated storms**
- Localized thunderstorms can deliver more than 15 cm of rain in a few hours, flushing debris into a single drainage. An October 1972 event on Canyon Lake delivered brown, humic-laden water with logs and saguaro cactus.

**Pumped-storage modifications**
- Penstock release capacity of the upper three dams increased two to five times. The middle two structures can reverse flow at two to three times their former generation capacity.
- Reverse injection of water may disrupt thermal and chemical stratification. This was observed during preliminary testing.

## Key Findings

1. **Desert reservoirs depend on episodic floods.** Organic matter and dissolved nutrients arrive in brief, massive pulses that must sustain the system for years. Dissolved solids are dilute during floods (Fig. 9).
2. **Local storms create nutrient hotspots.** Primary production and fish concentrations in isolated bays reflect localized "point input" of nutrients. Fire runoff may stimulate plankton blooms.
3. **Roosevelt Lake is the most productive reservoir,** consistent with downstream accumulation of organic inputs and with mean chlorophyll-*a* concentrations (Rinne, 1973).
4. **Desert reservoirs are ecologically distinct.** Unpredictable timing of inputs and drought-driven decomposition lags make these systems differ from temperate, mesic reservoirs.
5. **Pumped-storage effects are largely unknown.** Limnological studies of pumped-storage effects on water quality, spawning and migration of fishes are scarce. Destratification benefits documented in temperate lakes are unlikely to apply under hot-desert conditions.
6. **Recommended priority:** The author states that effects on hydrology, biology, fisheries and recreation should be the primary objective of future research on these reservoirs.
