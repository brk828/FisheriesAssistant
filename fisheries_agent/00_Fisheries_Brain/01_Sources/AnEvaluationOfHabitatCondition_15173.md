# Atomizer Falls Complex: Habitat Conditions and Fish Distribution in the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 University of Arizona M.S. thesis by William Patrick Mattes evaluates how water chemistry, physical habitat and fish distribution change along the lower 21 km of the [[Little Colorado River]] in Arizona, from Blue Springs (river-km 21.06) upstream to the [[Atomizer Falls Complex]] (river-km 11.40). The study was a subset of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program, which sought to identify preferred habitats of [[Humpback Chub]] in the river. Water chemistry (carbon dioxide, pH, alkalinity, hardness and turbidity) shows a gradual downstream gradient, with the sharpest changes between river-km 14.7 and 13.9. Fish distribution tracks these chemical gradients: [[Humpback Chub]] are absent above Chute Falls, while [[Speckled Dace]] are absent from Blue Springs itself. The author concludes that elevated carbon dioxide during base flow likely acts as a chemical barrier, and recommends carbon dioxide tolerance studies.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is a cyprinid minnow endemic to the canyons of the [[Colorado River]]. It is adapted to swift water, with large falcate fins, a nuchal hump, an inferior mouth and a dorsoventrally flattened head. The largest known population in Grand Canyon occurs at the confluence of the Little Colorado River and the Colorado River, which the author treats as essential to the species' future. The species' decline is attributed mainly to the closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]], which converted the Colorado River from a fluctuating warm-water system to a cold-water system with steadier flows. [[Hamman]] (1982) showed early life-stage survival was severely limited below 13 °C.

The study area is the lower Little Colorado River, which has a basin of about 69,807 km² and a total length of 412 km. The river becomes perennial below Blue Springs and carries about 220 ft³/s. Blue Springs water is mineralized, rich in free carbon dioxide and warmer than surface runoff. Fifteen study areas were established between Big Canyon and 60 m above Blue Springs, with the Atomizer Falls complex (three travertine dams of more than 1.5 m, at river-km 13.98, 14.34 and 14.60) separating areas 5 to 8.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Blue Springs water is supersaturated with calcium bicarbonate. Acidic groundwater dissolves limestone, and the resulting carbon dioxide and calcium bicarbonate concentrations decline downstream as carbon dioxide degasses to the atmosphere or is consumed by travertine precipitation and photosynthesis. Calcite equilibrium was not reached in the 14.7 to 13.9 km reach. Carbon dioxide and alkalinity fall, while pH and turbidity rise, downstream.

**Physical habitat.** Habitat is dynamic. The author notes that travertine dams have repeatedly been breached and re-formed, and that 1993 floods deposited sand on banks while scouring marl from pools. Clear water and the absence of travertine dams and deep pools above Chute Falls are also candidate factors limiting chub distribution.

**Physiological limits.** High carbon dioxide and bicarbonate impede gill gas and ion exchange, which can cause anesthesia and death. Fish can partly acclimate through HCO₃⁻/Cl⁻ exchange. Blue Springs water anesthetized speckled dace from above the springs within 3 minutes, while dace from below recovered.

**Historical context.** Hereford (1984) suggested higher pre-1940 discharges may have diluted Blue Springs for long periods, allowing fish to move further upstream. Chub were reported below Grand Falls before the 1940s.

## Key Findings

- **Water chemistry gradient:** pH, carbon dioxide, alkalinity, hardness and turbidity change gradually from river-km 21.06 to 11.40. The most abrupt changes occur between 14.7 km (above Chute Falls) and 13.9 km (below Atomizer Falls), a distance of 760 m.
- **Carbon dioxide levels by site:** 571 mg/l at Blue Springs (21 km), 205 mg/l at 19 km, 196 mg/l at 14.7 km, 175 mg/l at 14.3 to 14.5 km, 158 mg/l at 13.9 km and 111 mg/l at 11.4 to 12.6 km.
- **Seasonal dependence:** Monthly trends depended on flow regime: summer rain runoff (July and September 1992), spring runoff (April 1993) and base flow (June and July 1993). Monthly data did not always show the longitudinal trends clearly.
- **Humpback Chub distribution:** Absent above Chute Falls (14.7 km). Present in low densities at 14.3 to 14.5 km and in larger numbers at 13.9 km. Abundant from 11.4 to 12.6 km. Captured only in areas 1, 2, 6 and 7 in the sampling. Distribution was associated with high turbidity, travertine dams forming deep pools, and high carbon dioxide and calcium carbonate levels.
- **Speckled Dace distribution:** Present in all sampled areas except Blue Springs and about 1 km of the main channel below it. The only native fish occurring above area 7. Absence from Blue Springs appears related solely to water chemistry.
- **Other species:** [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (areas 2, 5, 7), juvenile [[Bluehead Sucker]] (area 2 only), and exotics including [[Fathead Minnow]] (areas 6, 7), [[Common Carp]] (areas 2, 7, 9) and [[Channel Catfish]] (area 7).
- **Vertical distribution:** Of 241 juvenile speckled dace observed in June 1993, 14 (6%) were in the top third of the water column, 86 (36%) in the middle third and 141 (58%) in the bottom third.
- **Habitat use vs availability:** The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test found no significant difference between habitat sampled and habitat used by speckled dace for depth, velocity or substrate.
- **June 1993 physical habitat:** Area 7 was significantly deeper than areas 6 and 8. Areas 5 and 6 had slower velocities and smaller substrates than areas 7 and 8, partly due to marl (CaCO₃) precipitate.
- **Physiological evidence:** Speckled dace from above Blue Springs became anesthetized within 3 minutes in spring water (Carothers and Aitchison 1977). Lethal carbon dioxide levels reported for other species include 260 mg/l for white sucker and 293 mg/l for fathead minnow (Black et al. 1954).
- **Main hypothesis:** Elevated carbon dioxide during base flow likely blocks upstream chub migration. Migrating fish arriving in June and July may be unable to acclimate to the chemical barrier at the Atomizer Falls complex. Short summer floods may not allow passage across the roughly 7 km to Blue Springs.
- **Recommendation:** Carbon dioxide tolerance studies for various size classes of humpback chub and other native lower-21 km fishes, under varied temperature, dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide acclimation conditions, to substantiate or falsify these hypotheses.
