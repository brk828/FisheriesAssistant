# The Bass-Bluegill Combination in a Small Artificial Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1948 Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin by [[George W. Bennett]] reports a multi-year study of Fork Lake, a 1.38-acre farm pond near Mount Zion, Illinois. The pond was rotenone-poisoned in June 1938, restocked with [[Largemouth Bass]] fry and [[Bluegill]] adults, and then cropped with [[Wing Net]]s, seines and hook-and-line from 1939 to 1942. Cropping ended when a 4-inch rain washed out the dam on July 8, 1942. The author concluded that the spread of [[Fine-Leaved Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton foliosus*) reduced the open water and the food supply, which lowered the bluegill yield and growth, despite heavy cropping.

## Background & Description

Fork Lake was about 18 years old when assigned to the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] in 1938. It had been used for fishing, waterfowl shooting and recreation. After the ban on live-decoy duck baiting in 1935, fishing declined because of large populations of black bullheads, carp and buffalo. These rough fishes were thought to limit reproduction and growth of sport fish.

A pre-treatment census (June 7, 1938) found 5,350 fish weighing 774 pounds, comprising 16 species. Rough fish (mostly carp and redmouth buffalo) made up 47.5 per cent of weight, and bullheads plus four channel catfish 41.2 per cent. Largemouth bass and panfish totaled only 6.3 per cent. The estimated yield was 539 pounds per acre, indicating moderately high natural fertility. Only 145 desirable fish large enough to interest anglers remained.

The pond was restocked June 11–18, 1938, with 270 stunted adult bluegills and 1,440 bass fry (0.15 pound), all from [[Homewood Lake]] near Decatur. Bass fry averaged 0.87 inch when stocked. By October, 74 bass averaged 6.45 inches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Cropping used [[Wing Net]]s (fyke nets) of 0.50–0.75-inch mesh, which held bass of about 5 inches and bluegills of about 3.5 inches. Nets were set in two groups of three with [[Lead Net]]s forming a drift fence. Bass were much more successful than bluegills at avoiding nets, and angling was needed to sample them.

Pond conditions changed markedly over the study:

- Turbidity fell after rotenone removal, allowing rooted plants to establish.
- Potamogeton foliosus expanded from a shore band in 1939 to fill the shallows to 5–6 feet by 1941.
- Open water shrank from 1.25 acres (1939) to 0.95 acre (1940) and 0.64 acre (1941).
- A sudden die-off of the pondweed in August 1941 was followed by an algal "bloom" of *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*, with Secchi visibility of 1.2 feet on August 19.
- The pond was thermally stratified each summer with only a trace of oxygen below 4 feet.

Turtles and crayfish were also taken in nets. Snapping turtles totaled 9, 11, 5 and 3 individuals in 1939–1942, and painted turtles 32, 8, 6 and 4.

## Key Findings

**Yield and effort**
- Fish yield fell each year: 223.4 lb (1939), 200.2 lb (1940), 129.9 lb (1941), and 60.5 lb for March–June 1942.
- Bass yield held at about 51–58 lb per year in 1939–1941, while bluegill yield dropped from 172.4 lb (1939) to 72.2 lb (1941).
- Net-days rose from 92 (1939) to 182 (1940) and 330 (1941).
- Yield decline paralleled the reduction in open water.

**Growth**
- Bluegill growth worsened with each brood. The 1938 brood averaged about 3.5 inches in March 1939 and about 6.5 inches in October, and later broods grew more slowly.
- Bass growth was slow in 1939 and early 1940–1941, but rose sharply after the August 1941 pondweed die-off, when small fish sheltered in the mats became available as prey.
- The author attributed the bluegill decline mainly to a shrinking food supply caused by pondweed spread.

**Condition**
- Bass condition followed growth, and the 1938 brood became very fat after the 1941 die-off.
- Bluegills showed a regular annual cycle, high in spring and low in November. No improvement followed the die-off.

**Scale analysis and false annuli**
- In the 1938 bluegill brood, 10.0–14.3 per cent showed clear false annuli from the summer of 1939.
- Clear false annuli appeared on 15.3 per cent of 1938-brood bass in 1940.
- The author proposed that starvation of 3 or more weeks produces false annuli. Scale abnormalities were concentrated in fish growing abnormally fast or slow.

**Reproduction and diet**
- Bass spawning was brief (May). Bluegills spawned from May through September and matured at 12 months unless stunted.
- Bass diets shifted to fish and crayfish, which correlated with growth. Bluegill diets were mainly Diptera larvae, Entomostraca and snails.

**Dam failure and fish loss**
- On July 8, 1942, a 4-inch rain washed out the dam. About 261 lb of fish were recovered (169 lb bass, 92 lb bluegills). Estimates included 5,000 small bass and at least 10,000 small bluegills stranded in the vegetation.

**Conclusions**
- The author concluded that the spread of *Potamogeton foliosus* reduced invertebrate food and likely nullified the benefit of cropping.
- Thermal stratification and low oxygen may have trapped zooplankton within the mats.
