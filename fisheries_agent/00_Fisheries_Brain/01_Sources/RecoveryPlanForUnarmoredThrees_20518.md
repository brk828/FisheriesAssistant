# Recovery Plan for Unarmored Threespine Stickleback

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Gasterosteidae #conservation #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan addresses [[Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni]], the [[Unarmored Threespine Stickleback]], an endangered freshwater fish subspecies endemic to southern California. Prepared by the Unarmored Threespine Stickleback Recovery Team (led by Shoken Sasaki, California Department of Fish and Game, with members from California State Polytechnic University Pomona, U.S. Forest Service, and Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County), the document outlines comprehensive strategies to restore this subspecies from endangered to non-endangered status. The fish is distinguished from other [[Gasterosteus aculeatus]] subspecies by having zero to minimal lateral bony plates (average 0.06-0.55 plates per individual) versus the fully-plated G. a. aculeatus or low-plated G. a. microcephalus (>6 plates average). Currently confined to the headwaters of the [[Santa Clara River]] system in northern Los Angeles County, the subspecies formerly ranged across the Los Angeles, San Gabriel, and Santa Ana river basins. The plan emphasizes habitat preservation, prevention of introgression by G. a. microcephalus, removal of exotic predators and competitors, land acquisition, public education, and establishment of new populations through transplantation.

## Background & Description

[[Gasterosteus aculeatus williamsoni]] is one of three recognized subspecies of threespine stickleback on the Pacific Coast of North America. The subspecies is readily distinguished by lateral plate counts: typical specimens have 0.06 to 0.55 plates per individual, though rare individuals may have up to six plates. Additional distinguishing features include short dorsal and pelvic spines, rounded caudal fin, less streamlined body, reduced spine denticulation, and reduced size of the ascending branch of the pelvic girdle compared to other subspecies.

Threespine sticklebacks generally are streamlined fish not exceeding 6 cm standard length. The dorsal fin consists of two isolated anterior spines plus a third smaller spine at the front of the soft-rayed posterior portion. The most striking features are the unusual pelvic girdle with denticulated spines on either side and variable numbers of large lateral bony plates (zero to about 35 depending on subspecies). Males display brilliant red nuptial coloration on the head, sometimes extending to the anal fin origin.

First described by Girard (1854) from [[Soledad Canyon]], the subspecies formerly had widespread distribution including headwaters of the Santa Clara, Los Angeles, San Gabriel, and Santa Ana rivers. By 1942, populations in the Los Angeles basin were extinct. Currently, recognized G. a. williamsoni populations are confined to the upper [[Santa Clara River]] system, with essential habitat defined in three segments: (A) from San Martinez Grande Canyon to Interstate 5, (B) Soledad Canyon from Lang to Arraste Canyon (subdivided by dry sections), and (C) two short sections in [[San Francisquito Canyon]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

G. a. williamsoni inhabits freshwater streams in the upper Santa Clara River system, coexisting with several introduced fish species including [[Gila orcutti]] (chub), [[Catostomus santaanae]] (sucker), [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquito fish), [[Lepomis cyanellus]] (sunfish), and [[Ictalurus melas]] (black bullhead). The subspecies exhibits specific microhabitat preferences critical to its survival.

**Reproductive Biology**: Males develop nuptial coloration (bright red throat, blue sides, green eyes), establish territories, and build nests of aquatic vegetation on the bottom in areas with ample vegetation and gentle water flow. After females lay eggs, males fertilize and guard them while fanning water over the eggs with pectoral fins—essential for proper development. Reproduction occurs almost year-round with a major peak in March continuing through summer and fall. Young hatch after several days of male brooding; smallest specimens captured outside nests were about 10 mm S.L.

**Habitat Requirements**: Young juveniles (<20 mm S.L.) occupy shallow stream edges with dense vegetation where water temperature is slightly elevated, potentially accelerating development. Larger juveniles and sub-adults also prefer vegetated areas with slow-moving or standing water. Adults (≥25 mm S.L.) occur throughout the stream but concentrate in slower-moving water, behind obstructions, at stream edges under algal mats (especially [[Cladophora]]), or at upstream ends of ponds where water flows in. The subspecies is not found in isolated ponds without stream connection or in turbid water.

**Population Dynamics**: The best habitat consists of small clean ponds within the stream with constant water flow. A pond at Soledad Sands Park (approximately 475 m²) supported 3.4 to 2.0 fish/m². Water cleanliness is indicated by a 2.49 diatom diversity index. Populations decline in winter due to natural mortality (fish apparently live only one year) and low winter recruitment.

**Diet and Feeding**: G. a. williamsoni is an opportunistic feeder consuming primarily benthic insects, with some nectonic and terrestrial species taken from the surface. Snails are common in the diet; worms (flatworms and nematodes) comprise a small percentage. During the 1973 dry season, the subspecies relied heavily on mayfly larvae characteristic of clean, flowing freshwater. Stickleback eggs were found in 12% of examined stomachs, all from males. [[Gambusia affinis]] may compete for some food items.

**Predation**: Known predators include belostomatid bugs (very abundant, potentially major predators especially of juveniles), garter snakes ([[Thamnophis]]), and possibly [[Ictalurus]] and [[Lepomis]] (capable but not abundant). Male cannibalism of eggs may be a major mortality factor. No other sympatric fishes have been confirmed as egg predators.

**Physiological Tolerances**: The subspecies has moderate temperature tolerance (critical thermal maximum of 30.5°C at 8°C acclimation, 34.6°C at 22.7°C acclimation) and moderate oxygen tolerance (to 2.0 ppm). As oxygen approaches 2.0 ppm, fish must increase respiratory energy expenditure, detracting from growth, reproduction, and activity.

## Key Findings

**Primary Recovery Objective**: Remove G. a. williamsoni from endangered status by establishing self-sustaining populations sufficiently widespread across three of four original drainage systems so no single catastrophic event could eradicate them.

**Reasons for Decline**: 
- Habitat destruction through channelization and stream drying from groundwater pumping
- Introduction of [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquito fish) causing competition and possible predation
- Urbanization of the Los Angeles basin with extensive stream channelization destroying quiet backwaters and side streams
- Introgression by G. a. microcephalus introduced with trout plants in some drainages (e.g., Santa Maria River)
- All populations except upper Santa Clara River have been extirpated or introgressed

**Critical Threats Identified**:

1. **Stream Channelization**: Would increase flow velocity, eliminate pools and backwaters, reduce vegetation, and potentially allow continuous water flow enabling G. a. microcephalus introgression and upstream movement of [[Cottus asper]] (prickly sculpin)

2. **Urbanization**: Increases channelization pressure, construction in floodplains, introduction risks, and water quality degradation through increased runoff, siltation, nutrients (cultural eutrophication), pesticides, and pollutants. Deformities observed in G. a. microcephalus downstream of pollution sources suggest similar vulnerability in G. a. williamsoni

3. **Introgression**: Gene flow from G. a. microcephalus could eliminate distinctive features. Currently, the dry Santa Clara River section from Lang to Interstate 5 acts as a barrier. Channelization or water releases could establish continuous flow allowing introgression

4. **Exotic Species Introduction**: 
   - Predators may cause extinction, alter appearance through selective predation on plate morphs, or restrict habitat use
   - [[Xenopus laevis]] (African clawed frog) discovered in Agua Dulce Canyon poses severe threat; eradication efforts ongoing
   - Competitors may cause extinction or phenotypic changes
   - Sources include channelization routes, urbanization, pet trade releases, and contaminated fish plants

5. **Agricultural Impacts**: Overgrazing and irrigation cause siltation covering pool bottoms. Cultural eutrophication from fertilizer and livestock waste runoff promotes excessive vegetation growth, potentially causing lethal oxygen depletion during night respiration or winter die-off

6. **Groundwater Pumping**: Excessive pumping could desiccate essential habitat. As habitat volume and flow decline, water temperature may increase to harmful levels. Particularly severe threat during dry years

7. **Off-Road Vehicles (ORVs)**: Extensively used at Soledad Station and Soledad Sands, ORVs destroy riparian vegetation, remove flow obstacles forming pools, and directly disturb nesting areas. Dirt bikes photographed racing through pools containing 12 stickleback nests

8. **Water Supply Dependency**: San Francisquito Canyon population depends on water release from Drinkwater Reservoir under LADWP-USFS agreement. Flow temporarily terminated during pipe cleaning threatens this 100 m summer habitat (1.5 km winter)

9. **Catastrophic Extinction Risk**: Southern Pacific Railroad tracks along floodplain create risk of toxic chemical spill destroying entire Santa Clara River population, leaving only small San Francisquito Canyon populations

**Recovery Plan Components**:

1. **Habitat Preservation and Protection** (Priority 1):
   - Monitor and regulate water use, quality, and land use in essential habitat
   - Prevent channelization and maintain natural flow patterns
   - Restore habitat to optimal conditions: determine past natural conditions, survey restoration needs, implement on public and private lands
   - Acquire private lands containing essential habitat (priorities: surveyed Soledad Canyon land, unsurveyed Soledad Canyon, unsurveyed land near San Martinez Grande Canyon)
   - Prevent exotic organism introductions (G. a. microcephalus, predators, competitors)
   - Remove exotic predators (continue [[Xenopus]] eradication)
   - Regulate specimen collection for scientific purposes
   - Enforce habitat protection laws
   - Monitor populations and habitat continuously

2. **Contingency Planning**:
   - Establish plan to rescue individuals threatened with immediate extermination
   - Create temporary holding facilities until habitat restoration
   - Determine maximum tolerable densities for short and long-term captivity

3. **Population Re-establishment** (Priority 2):
   - Select suitable transplant sites within former range based on absence of predatory fishes and other sticklebacks, suitable habitat, habitat stability
   - Perform transplants using 500-1,000 specimens from Soledad Canyon headwaters (least introgressed, type locality)
   - Transport in oxygenated water at <20°C, release in calm vegetated areas
   - Manage transplants: restore habitat, monitor populations, inform public, enforce laws

4. **Public Education and Information** (Priority 3):
   - Media exposure emphasizing uniqueness, native status, ecosystem role, human-caused decline
   - Install informational signs at essential habitat and public gathering places
   - Produce audio-visual programs and brochures
   - Establish nature center
   - Advise public and private organizations on activities affecting populations
   - Emphasize habitat preservation over legal enforcement alone

5. **Research Priorities**:
   - Establish water requirements and determinants of distribution/abundance
   - Determine precise habitat parameters and microhabitat preferences
   - Survey for additional populations (reports south of Santa Clara River)
   - Determine movement and dispersal patterns (critical for transplant numbers)
   - Determine crowding tolerance for captive maintenance
   - Assess effects of specific predators on plate morph selection
   - Identify fish fauna in imported water sources (Bouquet and Drinkwater reservoirs, Lake Piru, Castaic Lake)
   - Continue literature review on [[Gasterosteus aculeatus]] research

**Management Considerations**:

- Essential habitat extends laterally from stream bed to include flood plain supporting or capable of supporting riparian vegetation
- Critical management area includes all Santa Clara River system upstream of San Martinez Grande Canyon not in essential habitat; requires management ensuring adequate water quality and quantity
- Private land ownership creates financial burden on owners; recommend property tax adjustments, conservation easements, or public acquisition
- Water additions (treated sewage, imported water) could benefit or harm depending on amount, quality, and biological contents; require careful analysis and trial basis implementation
- Small impoundments with well-circulated water and abundant edge vegetation support highest densities (e.g., Pond 1: 456 m²); large impoundments without vegetation (e.g., White Rock pond) are unsuitable
- Number of suitable nesting sites may limit population size
- Natural mortality from flooding washing fish from favorable habitat

**Cost Estimates** (FY 1977-1980): Detailed budget provided for activities including population monitoring ($2,000-2,050/year), habitat restoration on private land ($7,500/year), land acquisition ($100,000+ for Soledad Canyon parcels), research ($2,900-5,000 for specific studies), public education materials ($15,000-16,000), with many ongoing activities incorporated into regular agency work projects.
