# Isolating Mechanisms in the Speciation of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted symposium paper by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] (Scripps Institution of Oceanography) examines why fishes that can interbreed in aquaria so rarely hybridize in nature. Speciation is framed as a fluctuating conflict between forces that unify the gene pool (chiefly interbreeding) and forces that isolate populations genetically. Hubbs emphasizes extrinsic ecological and behavioral factors over intrinsic genetic mechanisms. He argues that segregation is usually geographic in origin, but that [[Sympatric Speciation]] may occur in lakes with explosive evolution, and that [[Genetic Swamping]] is resisted by pure stocks well adapted to their habitats.

## Background & Description

The paper treats speciation as a long-continued conflict between integration and segregation, using fishes as the lowest vertebrate example. Hubbs states he belongs to the school that places the environmental potential at the centre of speciation and therefore gives more weight to ecological than genetic mechanisms. The text draws on decades of aquarium crosses and field collections, notably work on [[Xiphophorus]] and [[Mollienesia]] (Poeciliidae), darters ([[Etheostoma]]), sunfishes ([[Lepomis]]) and the carp–goldfish system ([[Carp and Goldfish Hybridization]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rarity of natural hybridization.** Species that cross readily in captivity seldom hybridize in the wild. In Percidae, interspecific hybrids are rare except among some primitive darters. Among Xiphophorus, many thousands of specimens have yielded no natural hybrid, although [[Xiphophorus Hellerii]] and [[Xiphophorus Maculatus]] cross thousands of times in aquaria. In Mollienesia, fewer than ten natural hybrids were found between the sphenops and latipinna groups, which hybridize freely in aquaria. Hubbs attributes the blocks mainly to subtle behavioral and habitat segregation.

**Hybridization and environmental disturbance.** Blocks break down when previously separated species are brought together, for example suckers introduced through an Owens Valley aqueduct spillway and Mississippi-drainage species established in the Colorado River system. Disturbed habitats and unbalanced abundance (a rare species among an abundant one) also promote crossing. Hybridization is reported as frequent mainly among freshwater fishes of northern regions affected by glacial and pluvial events, and almost unknown in tropical seas.

**Resistance to genetic swamping.** Several examples show that pure populations persist despite interbreeding:
- Siphateles obesus (stream and lake forms, Lahontan Basin) forms intergrades, yet the two forms remain distinct over the basin.
- Gambusia affinis subspecies (holbrookii and affinis) intergrade in a coastal zone, but headwater populations stay pure, with fin-ray numbers controlled by a single genetic factor.
- Etheostoma nigrum subspecies (nigrum and eulepis) occupy different habitats (open sand and mud versus vegetated habitats), and selection reconstitutes them after Glacial lake recolonization.
- Mollienesia mexicana subspecies in Laguna de Petén occupy marsh and open-water habitats, with a band of intergrades between them.

**Geographic versus sympatric segregation.** Allopatric segregation (stream capture, drainage disruption, closure of straits such as the Isthmus of Panama, island isolation, Postglacial refugia) is considered the usual antecedent of differentiation. Hubbs disputes the view that the African cichlid radiations in shallow, temporarily isolated ponds explain the diversity (see [[Lake Victoria]]), and considers [[Lake Tanganyika]], [[Lake Nyasa]], [[Lake Lanao]] and [[Lake Titicaca]] to be sites of genuine in-situ diversification.

## Key Findings

1. **Interbreeding potential exceeds natural hybridization.** Many species that interbreed freely in aquaria seldom hybridize in nature, and behavioral and habitudinal differences are the likely cause.
2. **Mollienesia hybrid patterns.** Sphenops-group and latipinna-group hybrids have reduced female fertility. Hybrids between Mollienesia and Limia have infertile females, and Mollienesia–Lebistes hybrids are infertile in both sexes. No hybrids were produced between tribe Poeciliini and other tribes.
3. **Sunfish hybrids are mostly sterile males.** Hybrid sunfishes (Lepomis) are almost invariably sterile and predominantly male with abnormal spermatogenesis.
4. **Blocks scale with divergence.** Isolating barriers generally increase with systematic differentiation, though exceptions exist (for example, [[Notropis Spiloptera]] crosses with the less closely related N. lutrensis but not with its near relative N. whipplii).
5. **Carp and goldfish.** The two species do not naturally cross across much of Asia, yet they hybridize in great profusion in the Lake Erie drainage, where hybrids may outnumber the parental species. The paper also notes that the genus Carassiops may reflect ancient hybrid origin.
6. **Hybrid swarms in desiccating habitats.** Hesperoleucus and Lavinia fused into a hybrid swarm in a drying section of the San Juan River, although they normally remain distinct.
7. **Homing and in-situ speciation.** Homing behavior, initially nongenetic, may provide the first step toward genetic segregation and sympatric differentiation, especially in salmonids. Oral gestation in cichlids may favor sympatric differentiation.
8. **Summary position.** Hubbs concludes that genetic isolation increases with phyletic differentiation, that extrinsic ecological factors often outweigh intrinsic genetics, and that sympatric speciation is plausible especially in lacustrine explosive radiations, though it cannot yet be tested experimentally.
