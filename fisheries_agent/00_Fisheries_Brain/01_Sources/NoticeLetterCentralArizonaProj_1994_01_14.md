## Executive Summary

This document is a legal notice letter (not a primary scientific study) sent to the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service alleging violations of the Endangered Species Act (ESA) stemming from operation of the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The core scientific claim is that the CAP — an aqueduct and reservoir system transporting Colorado River water from [[Lake Havasu]] into central Arizona — facilitates the introduction, spread, and reproduction of non-native fish species into the [[Gila River Basin]], thereby harming six federally protected native fish species. The letter cites testimony from ichthyologist Professor W.L. Minckley characterizing CAP-mediated invasive species introduction as a potential "final blow" to native Southwestern fish fauna. While light on quantitative ecological data, the letter provides valuable population-status statistics on the collapse of Arizona's native fish fauna due to non-native species introductions facilitated by interbasin water transfer infrastructure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat structure**: The [[Gila River Basin]] historically supported an isolated, desert-adapted native fish fauna. The [[Central Arizona Project]] artificially connects previously unconnected or discontinuous waterways within the basin, creating novel perennial hydrological linkages that did not exist prior to CAP construction.
- **Vector for invasion**: CAP aqueducts and canals transport water — and along with it, non-native organisms — from the [[Colorado River]] and other source waters directly into Gila Basin streams and rivers, both through direct canal/reservoir mixing and indirectly via return-flow discharges.
- **Facilitation of non-native fish establishment**: Beyond introducing new non-native species, CAP water conveyance is alleged to improve the ability of *already-established* non-native fish populations in the basin to survive, spread, reproduce, and hybridize/interact with native congeners.
- **Native fish decline context**: Of Arizona's 33 originally native fish species, 23 are now state-listed as threatened or endangered. Within the Gila Basin specifically, of 14 native fish species, 10 are federally listed as endangered/threatened or are candidates for federal listing; several native species have been entirely extirpated from the basin.
- **Regulatory framework**: Under [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 (16 U.S.C. § 1536(a)(2)), federal agencies must ensure actions are not likely to jeopardize listed species' continued existence, applicable broadly to "all activities or programs of any kind" (50 C.F.R. § 402.02). Section 9 (16 U.S.C. § 1538) separately prohibits "takings" of listed species, defined under 16 U.S.C. § 1532.

## Key Findings

- **Non-native species identified as the primary extinction driver**: The letter asserts, citing Professor W.L. Minckley (a leading ichthyological authority on Southwestern desert fishes), that non-native fish introduction represents the single greatest threat to native Arizona fish survival, and that CAP-facilitated introductions could constitute the "final blow" to remaining native populations.
- **Quantified native fish decline**: 23 of 33 (~70%) of Arizona's native fish species are now listed as threatened or endangered at the state level; 10 of 14 (~71%) native fish species in the Gila Basin specifically are federally listed or candidates for listing.
- **Mechanism of harm**: Harm occurs both through (1) direct introduction of novel non-native organisms via interbasin water transfer, and (2) enhanced survival, dispersal, and reproductive success of non-native fish already present in the Gila Basin, mediated by CAP-created perennial hydrological connections.
- **Legal/procedural failure alleged**: The Bureau of Reclamation allegedly failed to consult with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under ESA Section 7 regarding CAP's impacts on the six listed species, and operated without an incidental take statement or permit, constituting an alleged Section 9 violation.
- **Precedent cited**: Referenced case law (*TVA v. Hill*, 437 U.S. 153 (1978); *Thomas v. Peterson*, 753 F.2d 754 (9th Cir. 1985); *Lane County Audubon Society v. Jamison*, 958 F.2d 290 (9th Cir. 1992)) establishes that agency actions requiring formal ESA consultation must halt pending completion of that consultation — directly relevant to whether CAP operations affecting [[Native Fish Decline in the Southwest]] should have been suspended.
- **Broader ecological framing**: The letter situates this case within a general pattern wherein interbasin water transfer infrastructure across the arid Southwest acts as a conduit for aquatic invasive species, compounding existing threats (habitat loss, water diversion, drought) faced by native desert fish assemblages such as those documented in [[Gila River Basin]] and [[Colorado River]] fish conservation literature.
