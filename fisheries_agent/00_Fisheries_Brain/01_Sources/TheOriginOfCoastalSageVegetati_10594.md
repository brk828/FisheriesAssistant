# Origin of Coastal Sage Vegetation, Alta and Baja California

#botany #plant-ecology #coastal-sage-scrub

## Executive Summary

This paper by Daniel I. Axelrod (Department of Botany, University of California, Davis) reconstructs the age and origin of [[Coastal Sage Scrub]], a shrub vegetation type of Alta and Baja California. The author argues that coastal sage is a new, pioneer-type vegetation that spread widely only after the Early [[Quaternary]]. Its taxa were drawn from the dry, open borders of [[Chaparral]], forest, woodland and arid tropic scrub, and they moved into dry sites created by climatic change, [[Tectonism]] and [[Mass Movement]]. Most of its present area is attributed to the hot, dry [[Xerothermic]] and the period since the last glacial, with human disturbance expanding it further.

This document contains no fish or aquatic data. It is a terrestrial plant paleoecology and biogeography study.

## Background & Description

Coastal sage scrub is patchily distributed from Oregon into Baja California. It occupies the driest sites, including steep south slopes with thin soil, rocky areas, sea bluffs, coastal and river terraces of coarse alluvium, and coastal dunes. The author notes that the vegetation is being rapidly lost to urbanization. Earlier work on it, including [[Epling and Lewis 1942]] on floristic relations, had largely not addressed its age or origin.

**Physiognomy.** Coastal sage is dominated by soft-stemmed, mostly non-sclerophyllous shrubs 0.5 to 1.5 m tall, described by Jepson (1925) as "soft chaparral." It is less dense and easier to penetrate than chaparral. Life forms shift from north to south. Northern stands include evergreen forest-border shrubs and winter- and drought-deciduous shrubs. Southern stands have more drought-deciduous taxa, evergreens such as *Malosma* and *Rhus*, and stem succulents near the Mexican border and on the Channel Islands.

**Divisions.** Coastal sage is grouped into several regional divisions, shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1:
- Northern: [[Franciscan Sage]] and [[Lucian Sage]]
- Interior north: [[Diablan Sage]]
- Southern coastal: [[Venturan Sage]] and [[Diegan Sage]]
- Interior south: [[Riversidian Sage]]

The author prefers these geographic names over species-based names, because communities within each division vary with exposure, soil depth, successional stage and local climate. Table 2 lists shrub contributors to each division (about 100 taxa across the table, summarized here rather than reproduced). Several taxa, including *Artemisia californica*, *Baccharis pilularis*, *Encelia californica* and *Salvia mellifera*, contribute to two or more divisions, so the divisions intergrade.

**Ecologic occurrence.** Over much of its range, coastal sage occupies sites that were recently grassland, and it is often found as a patchy element within grassland, oak woodland, closed-cone pine forest and chaparral. Where it is the climax cover on south of Monterey or in northern Baja California, relict grassland remains on deeper ridge soils. Coastal sage is confined to the driest sites within woodland and forest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate.** Precipitation decreases southward and eastward, in the lee of the Coast Ranges. Summer rain becomes more frequent near the Mexican border. Table 4 gives mean annual rainfall for each division, but the OCR is garbled and those figures are not reproduced here. The author attributes the spread of coastal sage to a progressively drier climate, the loss of summer rain, and the development of a severe [[Mediterranean Climate]] after the Early Quaternary. Fig. 2 compares thermal parameters (days above threshold temperatures and an equability index) for northern and southern divisions.

**Floristic relationships.** Using the [[Sorensen Similarity Index]] on species presence or absence (Table 3):
- Franciscan and Lucian: 81% similar to each other
- Venturan and Diegan: 61% similar
- Diablan and Riversidian: 67% similar
- Venturan shares about half its species with each of the other divisions, which the author links to its central geographic and climatic position.

Diegan sage has the most unique taxa, including *Acalypha californica*, *Agave shawii*, *Aesculus palmeri*, *Fraxinus trifoliata*, *Malosma laurina*, *Rhus integrifolia* and many cacti. Many of these extend into the [[Sonoran Desert]]. Diegan sage also has more life-form diversity, which the author attributes to warmer winters and more frequent summer rain.

**Source taxa.** Epling and Lewis (1942) and Raven and Mathias (1960) suggested that coastal sage species such as *Fraxinus trifoliata* and *Satureja chandleri* came from chaparral ancestors. *Fraxinus trifoliata* is ancient, appearing in essentially modern form in the Miocene. *Aesculus palmeri*, *Baccharis sergiloides*, *Malosma laurina* and *Rhus integrifolia* also have Miocene and Pliocene relatives. Their present restriction to coastal sage does not, by itself, prove great age. The author notes that chaparral is also not an ancient zonal vegetation.

## Key Findings

1. **Coastal sage is a young, regional vegetation type.** It spread widely only after the Early Quaternary. Its taxa were preadapted from Neogene dry-border habitats of forest, woodland and dry tropic scrub.

2. **Neogene coastal sage was local.** Fossil floras show that equivalents occupied restricted dry sites such as south slopes, thin soils and well-drained gravels. Examples include the Mint Canyon flora (about 12 m.y.), the Tehachapi flora (about 17 m.y.), the Mt. Eden flora (about 7 m.y.) and the Anaverde flora (about 10 m.y.). Palynological records of semiwoody taxa (*Artemisia*, *Agave*, Chenopodiaceae, Compositae, *Opuntia*) are consistent with this, but the author considers them insufficient evidence of zonal coastal sage.

3. **Late Pleistocene floras show a wetter past.**
   - San Bruno flora (dated 10,000 years B.P.): a Douglas fir forest, with present-day rainfall of about 460 mm versus 810 mm at the forest edge today.
   - Santa Lucia travertine flora (10,000 or more years old): a forest of *Pinus radiata*, *Cupressus* cf. *goveniana* and *Pseudotsuga menziesii*.
   - Pt. Sal flora (28,000 years B.P.) and Carpinteria flora (over 40,000 years, revised).
   - Rancho La Brea flora (40,000 to 25,000 years B.P.): closed-cone pine forest and woodland.
   - Coastal sage was therefore much more restricted during the Wisconsin than today.

4. **Early Pleistocene and Late Pliocene data show drier and wetter phases.** The Soboba flora (Early Pleistocene) indicates forest on the San Jacinto Valley floor where rainfall now totals 305 mm. The San Joaquin flora (Late Pliocene, 4.0 to 3.5 m.y.) indicates 500 to 635 mm of rainfall, against 150 mm today. The McKittrick flora (about 38,000 years old) indicates 380 to 500 mm, against 125 mm today.

5. **Drivers of origin.** The author identifies four:
   - A summer-dry Mediterranean-type climate, which favored drought-deciduous and water-storing shrubs
   - [[Tectonism]] during the Late Pliocene and Quaternary, which created steep, dry, thin-soiled slopes and alluvial terraces
   - Episodic torrential rain, which caused slumping, mudflows and sheetflood erosion that exposed dry substrates
   - Human activity (cattle grazing, fire, clearing), which expanded coastal sage into grassland and chaparral belts

6. **Maximum area was reached in the Xerothermic and after.** Distributional evidence for the [[Xerothermic]] (8,000 to 4,000 years ago) includes coastward movement of desert and chaparral taxa and the invasion of the Channel Islands by Diegan sage. The author concludes that coastal sage attained most of its present area after the last glacial (about 12,000 years ago), and that it continues to spread.

7. **Parallel with chaparral and desert.** The author concludes that the history of coastal sage parallels that of chaparral and desert vegetation in its source taxa, age, diversity of adaptive types, and timing of maximum area.
