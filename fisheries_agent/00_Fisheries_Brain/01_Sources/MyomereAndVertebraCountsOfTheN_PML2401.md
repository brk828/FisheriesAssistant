# Myomere and Vertebra Counts of North American Cyprinids and Catostomids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper compiles myomere and vertebra counts for North American minnows ([[Cyprinidae]]) and suckers ([[Catostomidae]]) to support larval fish identification. Myomere counts had been reported for only about 20% of the roughly 260 species in these families, but vertebra counts exist for most species, and myomeres correspond closely to vertebrae. The author finds that total vertebrae can reasonably approximate total myomeres, and that the ratios of preanal, postanal and total myomeres separate the two families well. A worked example from the Upper Colorado River system shows how these counts can narrow an unidentified larva to a genus.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Summarize myomere and/or vertebra counts for many cyprinids and catostomids, then compare and characterize the two families.
- **Why counts matter:** Myomere counts are important in larval fish taxonomy. Myomeres are directly associated with vertebrae, so vertebra counts (reported for most species) can stand in for them.
- **Author:** Darrel E. Snyder, [[Larval Fish Laboratory]], Department of Fishery and Wildlife Biology, [[Colorado State University]], Fort Collins, Colorado.

### Methods

- A literature survey (acknowledged as incomplete) was supplemented with unpublished data from several researchers.
- Vertebra counts were either assumed or adjusted to include the [[Weberian Ossicles]].
- Preanal and postanal counts followed the [[Siefert Method]] (1969): all myomeres posterior to the posterior margin of the vent are postanal; the remainder are preanal.
- Adjustments relied on accurate drawings where available. Some counts were verified with personal reference specimens. A few highly unlikely counts or extremes were discarded.
- Percentages and proportions were calculated from the median values of each species' typical range.
- Total counts were grouped into uniform range intervals (e.g., 35–40, 40–45).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vent position shifts:** Relative vent position changes during larval and early juvenile development, so counts from different stages may not be directly comparable.
- **Hypural complex:** The most posterior myomeres in protolarvae and early mesolarvae may be associated with the forming [[Hypural Complex]] and may disappear or become indistinct later.
- **Technique and identification:** Variation in counts reflects differences in counting technique, difficulty in locating the most anterior and posterior myomeres, developmental stage, and possibly misidentified specimens.
- **Caution:** The author advises caution, especially where total myomeres differ notably from total vertebrae (e.g., in *Catostomus* and *Ictiobus*-type cases noted in Table 1).
- **Regional relevance:** The worked example focuses on the [[Upper Colorado River]] system, where the native fish fauna includes rare and endangered taxa.

## Key Findings

### 1. Myomeres and vertebrae are closely linked
- Extreme ranges for total myomeres were entirely included within the extreme ranges for vertebrae, or vice versa, in about 70% of cases with both ranges available. The remaining cases were at least partially overlapping.
- The author concludes there is a nearly direct, one-to-one correlation between total myomeres and total vertebrae (Weberian ossicles included).
- Total vertebrae can therefore be used, with reasonable confidence, to approximate total myomeres.

### 2. Total count ranges
- Total myomeres or vertebrae for 70 cyprinid species range from 28 to 51, a range that practically includes the 32 to 52 range for 27 catostomids.
- Over 75% of cyprinids fall within 34 to 45. Over 75% of catostomids fall within 39 to 49.
- Within catostomids, the subfamily [[Ictiobinae]] falls within 33 to 38, and [[Catostominae]] within 41 to 49.
- Extremes: the low end of the cyprinid range is driven by one genus (name garbled in the scan). Several cyprinid genera, including [[Gila]] and [[Ptychocheilus]], make up the upper end. Within catostomids, [[Ictiobus]] (Ictiobinae) and [[Catostomus]] give the lower and upper extremes, respectively.

### 3. Preanal and postanal counts
- **Cyprinids:** preanal 19 to 31; postanal 10 to 18.
- **Catostomids:** preanal 25 to 42 (30 to 42 excluding Ictiobinae); postanal 5 to 12.
- Over three-quarters of cyprinids have 27 or fewer preanal myomeres and postanal counts greater than 11.
- Over three-quarters of catostomids have 27 or more preanal myomeres and 11 or fewer postanal myomeres.

### 4. Diagnostic proportions (median-based)
| Ratio | Cyprinidae | Catostomidae |
|---|---|---|
| Postanal : preanal myomeres | about 1 or greater (48–78%) | 1/3 or less (20–35%) |
| Preanal : total myomeres | 2/3 or less (57–69%) | 3/4 or more (73–82%) |

- The postanal-to-preanal ratio gives the greatest separation between the families.
- Preanal snout-to-vent length relative to total length is typically less than two-thirds in cyprinids and about two-thirds or more in suckers, with some overlap.

### 5. Genus-level identification
- Genera within each family have more-or-less distinctive total count ranges (Figure 2), which can help identify cypriniform larvae to a restricted group of genera, and occasionally to species, with awareness of exceptions.
- **Worked example:** An unidentified mesolarva with 29 preanal and 16 postanal myomeres (total 45) from the Upper Colorado River system has a postanal-to-preanal ratio of 55% and a preanal-to-total ratio of 65%. These place it in Cyprinidae.
- Of the nine cyprinid genera known from that system, only three have total ranges that include 45. Tentative eliminations leave [[Gila Cypha]] (humpback chub, rare and endangered) and a second, common *Gila* species as the most probable identities.

### 6. Conclusions
- Total vertebra counts are a practical proxy for total myomeres.
- Postanal:preanal and preanal:total ratios are the most reliable family-level discriminators.
- Results should be applied with caution given variation from technique, developmental stage, and possible misidentification.
