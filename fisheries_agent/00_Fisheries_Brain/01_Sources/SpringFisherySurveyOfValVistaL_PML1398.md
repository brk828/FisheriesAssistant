# Spring 1998 Fishery Survey of Val Vista Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-assemblage

## Executive Summary

This report, submitted by Paul C. Marsh, Ph.D., to the [[Val Vista Lakes]] Community Association, presents the results of a spring (April) 1998 fishery survey of the lake system in [[Gilbert, Arizona]]. Eleven fish species were recorded overall, including one species taken only by seining. The community was dominated by [[Threadfin Shad]], followed by [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]] (including [[Koi]]), [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Yellow Bass]]. Results were remarkably similar to the 1997 surveys.

No young-of-year fish were captured, so reproduction could not be confirmed. Ripe adults of threadfin shad, common carp and yellow bass were found, and spawning was observed or verified for some of these. The survey concludes that channel catfish and largemouth bass populations are healthy and should be managed as status quo. Stocking of forage fish is not justified.

## Background & Description

The management goals for Val Vista Lakes are to (1) provide a quality recreational angling experience and (2) use biological agents to help control nuisance algae, aquatic plants and noxious insects. The system is stocked periodically with hatchery fish. [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]] are stocked for anglers. [[Grass Carp]] are stocked to control aquatic plants. "Tilapia" are stocked in hope they will eat algae. Common carp and Koi (a polyploid variant of common carp) were suggested for midge control.

Before 1997, the fish community had not been monitored systematically. The 1998 survey was the third, following spring and autumn surveys in 1997. Its objectives were to (1) determine the current status of fish, (2) evaluate fish health, condition and size, and (3) recommend future management actions.

Access was arranged by Sharon Wenzel, General Manager of the Val Vista Lakes Community Association. Field assistance was provided by researchers from Arizona State University.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Two types of entanglement nets were used:

- [[Gill Nets]]: 125 ft long, 6 ft deep, with five 25-ft panels and mesh sizes of 1/2 to 2 in. They capture a wide size range, are size-selective, and often kill entangled fish.
- [[Trammel Nets]]: 75 to 150 ft long, 6 ft deep, with 1/2 to 1 1/2 in inner mesh and 12 in outer mesh. They target larger fish and generally do not kill them.

Two nets were set overnight on 23–24 April 1998 in each of four water bodies: North, South, West and Regatta lakes. Total effort was 1,050 linear ft of netting across eight sets. The same sites were used in earlier surveys. Sites were chosen to be representative and inconspicuous, and to avoid boat lanes. The habitat was described as monotonous.

[[Seining]] was used as a supplementary method. Nets 6 to 15 ft long and 4 ft deep, with 1/4 or 3/8 in mesh, were pulled through shallow water near the West Lake boat ramp. Seining effort was low, at two hauls.

Fish were identified, counted and examined. Selected species were measured for total length. Most fish were released alive, and no fish were sacrificed for age, growth or diet analysis.

**Seasonal and habitat factors.** Small-bodied fish that overwinter often stay in deep water and are hard to catch until the water warms. Young of large-bodied species do not become abundant until spawning and hatching occur, and this had not yet happened in 1998. The channel catfish population is maintained by periodic stocking, but a connecting [[Salt River Project Canal]] feeds the system, and threadfin shad and yellow bass are common in that canal.

**Condition.** All fish appeared healthy, with no sign of disease or external parasites. Spring condition factor is typically elevated by pre-spawning physiology, and all specimens were judged to be in excellent condition for the season.

## Key Findings

**Overall catch (entanglement netting)**

- A total of 755 individuals of 10 species were captured. All of these had been recorded in earlier surveys, and no new species were found.
- [[Threadfin Shad]] was the most abundant species, at about 60% of the catch. Juveniles and adults were present, and active spawning was observed, with eggs laid over the nets. The population is likely sustained by in-lake reproduction.
- [[Channel Catfish]] was second at 18.1% of the catch. Most fish were adults about 12 in long. The largest fish was nearly 24 in. No ripe fish were found and no spawning was detected.
- [[Common Carp]] plus [[Koi]] made up 11% of the catch. Common carp were large, running-ripe adults that were probably spawning. No ripe Koi were found.
- [[Yellow Bass]] made up 5.0% of the catch. Fish were small, averaging 6.6 in, but most were sexually mature.
- [[Bluegill]] (2.8%) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (2.5%) were the most common of the stocked species. [[Black Crappie]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Blue Tilapia]] each made up 0.4% or less.
- Largemouth bass averaged 12.6 in and ranged from about 8 to 18 in. Most were mature adults.

**Seining**

- Five [[Red Shiner]] were captured in two hauls at West Lake, the only seine catch.

**Results by water body**

- **Regatta Lake:** 84 fish of six species. Channel catfish were 58% of the catch, and carp plus Koi were 15%.
- **West Lake:** 99 fish of eight species. Channel catfish were the most abundant species and averaged 15.3 in, significantly larger than in the other lakes. Threadfin shad were 18%, bluegill 11%, and common carp 10%.
- **Val Vista Lake (North and South, combined):** 572 fish of nine species. Threadfin shad were 74% of the catch, followed by carp plus Koi (10%) and channel catfish (7%).

**Program assessment**

- Channel catfish are exceptionally abundant compared with other private lakes in the region. Largemouth bass are adequate for a modest sport fishery and survive well after stocking.
- Black crappie were rare (3 fish). Their net survival does not appear to justify continued stocking.
- Blue tilapia were rare. Fewer than a dozen were captured in the last two surveys, although about 12,000 were stocked in March 1997. Possible causes include low catchability in cool water, poor survival, and no in-lake recruitment.
- Grass carp, flathead catfish, Mozambique mouthbrooder and redbelly tilapia, which had been recorded in earlier surveys, were not collected in 1998. Grass carp were observed.

**Recommendations**

1. Consider stocking largemouth bass only if angler satisfaction declines. If needed, stock up to 2,500 fingerlings per spring each year, or 5,000 every other year.
2. Do not stock channel catfish unless angler satisfaction declines. If needed, stock no more than 1,000 fish every other year, in winter or spring.
3. Stock [[Biological Control]] agents only where beneficial effects are demonstrated. Keep complete records of species, number and source.
4. Do not stock forage species, including minnows and crayfish.
5. Set up a voluntary [[Angler Catch Reporting]] system for resident anglers. Reports would be sent to a central clearinghouse, summarized annually and shared through local media. Prizes could encourage participation.
