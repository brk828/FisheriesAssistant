# Aquatic Weed Control: Draft Environmental Statement for Apache National Forest Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Forest Service draft Environmental Statement, prepared under Section 102(2)(c) of the National Environmental Policy Act (Public Law 91-190), evaluates methods for controlling excessive submersed aquatic vegetation in five lakes on the [[Apache National Forest]]: [[Nelson Reservoir]], [[Luna Lake]], [[Crescent Lake]], [[Quemado Lake]] and [[Woodland Lake]]. Three treatment options are compared: mechanical cutting, chemical control with the herbicide [[Diquat]], and a combined treatment. The stated aims are to improve fishing, which is in short supply in the Southwest, and to reduce vegetation-driven high pH and winter oxygen depletion. The statement concludes that no significant impacts to the physical or biological environment will result, while acknowledging short-term water-use restrictions and human-health precautions.

## Background & Description

The document is a draft statement circulated for public and agency comment. It lists federal, state and local agencies and roughly 90 organizations and individuals to whom it was mailed, and it names the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] as the cooperating agency furnishing Diquat for most lakes. Responsible official: the Forest Supervisor, Apache National Forest, Springerville, Arizona. Transmission to the Council on Environmental Quality is dated March 1974.

The project continues a program begun in 1971 on Nelson Reservoir, Luna and Crescent Lakes, repeated in 1973, and proposed again for summers 1974, 1975 and 1976.

Target plants include:
- **American milfoil** (*Myriophyllum exalbescens*): a rooted perennial, low-value duck food.
- **Coontail** (*Ceratophyllum demersum*): a rootless submersed herb forming large masses.
- **Horned pondweed** (*Zannichellia palustris*): rated good duck food.
- **Waterweed** (*Elodea bifoliata*): a slender perennial rooting at nodes, low-value duck food.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake characteristics.** Surface areas are: Crescent Lake 178 acres; Nelson Reservoir 75–105 acres; Luna Lake 130 acres; Quemado Lake 136 acres; Woodland Lake 19 acres. Elevations range from 6,900 ft (Woodland) to 9,030 ft (Crescent). Treatment is limited to no more than 20 percent of each lake's surface area per year.

**Limnology and pH.** Summer plant growth drives pH from neutral to about 10, a level the document links to fish kills. Winter oxygen depletion occurs under ice cover, when dormant plants and decaying vegetation consume dissolved oxygen. Water temperatures seldom exceed 70 °F. The lakes are relatively soft and poorly buffered.

**Geology and soils.** The White Mountain lakes arose from volcanic activity 1 to 5 million years ago. Soils are largely volcanic and highly erodible in places, with the valley-bottom soils at [[Luna Lake]] and [[Nelson Reservoir]] showing siltation. The document states that soils contain enough clay to fully adsorb Diquat.

**Climate.** Annual rainfall is about 22 inches, with roughly half falling June–September. Mean annual temperature is about 41 °F.

**Vegetation and wildlife.** Surrounding vegetation ranges from native grassland to ponderosa pine, pinyon-juniper and Douglas fir. Mule deer, elk, bear, mountain lion, turkey and antelope are present, and the lakes support ducks and teal. The major game fish is stocked [[Rainbow Trout]].

**Human context.** Recreation is heavy in summer. Nearby communities include Alpine (about 200 people), Springerville-Eagar (about 3,000) and Quemado (250). Major southwestern cities lie 200–250 miles away.

## Key Findings

**Purpose and need**
- Vegetation reduces fishable water, interferes with harvest, lowers natural beauty and drives high pH.
- The statement finds that cutting alone is ineffective and costly, so the preferred approach is a combination or targeted chemical treatment.

**Diquat chemistry and fate**
- Diquat (Ortho Diquat, Chevron Chemical Company) is applied at 2 gallons per surface acre, below the surface, on calm days (wind below 5 mph), from a slow-moving boat, after June 1.
- It binds tightly to clay and organic soils, becoming biologically unavailable and persistent but inactive.
- Its photodecomposition product has an LD50 above 5,000 mg/kg in rats.
- Registered up to 5.4 ppm for lake weed control.

**Toxicity and safety**
- Ortho Diquat LD50 is 400 mg/kg, compared with 2,4,5-T at 300 mg/kg and 2,4-D at 500 mg/kg.
- Toxicity tests on several fish species, including rainbow trout, indicate application at two to four times the needed rate is tolerated, but excessive rates may be toxic to fish.
- Treated water must not be used for human consumption for 14 days, or for irrigation or livestock for 10 days.

**Alternatives assessed**
- Mechanical-only: the most satisfactory from a safety standpoint, but reduces treatable area and may be less effective.
- Chemical-only: rejected as illogical given its stated hazards.
- Treating entire lakes: rejected because of probable fish kill and loss of productivity.
- No project: leaves weed, pH and winter kill problems unresolved.

**Impact conclusions**
- No significant impacts on the physical or biological environment are expected.
- Adverse effects that cannot be avoided include a temporary (about 14-day) reduction in water quality, minor noise and exhaust from the 6-horsepower cutter, and human-health risk if precautions fail.
- Favorable effects include improved fishing and recreation, better shore access and potentially lower summer pH.

**Management requirements (summary)**
- Pre-treatment lake surveys, public notice, coordination with irrigators and permittees, signage, use of waterproof gloves and aprons, and disposal of containers in an approved sanitary landfill.
- Quemado Lake treatment depends on cooperative studies with the New Mexico Game and Fish Department.
