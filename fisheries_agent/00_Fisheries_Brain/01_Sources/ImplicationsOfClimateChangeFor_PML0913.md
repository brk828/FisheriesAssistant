# Implications of Climate Change for Stream Communities

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[Nancy B. Grimm]] argues that climate change will reshape stream communities mainly by altering the hydrologic disturbance regime, which in turn shifts the balance between disturbance and [[Biotic Interactions]] in controlling ecosystem function. The author draws heavily on long-term work in [[Desert Streams]], where climatic influence on hydrology is especially transparent. The chapter also proposes a [[Patch Dynamics]] framework for studying heterogeneous streams, and sets out three recommendations for future research: expand the scales of investigation, conduct long-term descriptive and comparative studies, and merge abiotic and biotic perspectives in conceptual models.

## Background & Description

Streams are transport systems that receive and move materials from terrestrial landscapes, but they also transform these materials through biotic processes and influence downstream systems, including estuaries and oceans. Stream ecosystems share several defining features:

- A linear shape and unidirectional flow
- Disturbances that are primarily hydrologic, at both dry and wet extremes
- Lateral, longitudinal, and vertical connections with adjacent systems
- Organisms with short life spans
- Diverse communities

Because precipitation and evapotranspiration set the hydrologic regime, which provides the physical template for stream processes, climate should strongly shape stream character. The chapter focuses on small to mid-sized lotic systems and uses desert streams as its main examples, though stream ecology has historically centered on temperate forest streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic regime.** Changes in total and seasonal runoff and in streamflow variability will influence stream communities. The author argues that changes in extremes will matter more than changes in means, because floods and droughts define disturbance in lotic systems. Regional hydrologic change is hard to predict because of uncertain effects of warming on precipitation, vegetation feedbacks, and soils. [[Poff]] (1992) provides a regional review for the continental United States.

**Thermal regime.** Increased water temperature can directly shift biogeographic distributions. [[Sweeney]] et al. (1992) found that genetic variability (allozymes) in six mayfly species along a latitudinal transect in eastern North America was lowest at high latitudes, where warming is expected to be greatest. Declining oxygen solubility with warming may make high-oxygen-demand species especially vulnerable. Temperature effects can cascade through food webs, for example via changes in invertebrate predators and trout.

**Riparian linkages.** Riparian tree species may respond individualistically to warming. Shifts in riparian species composition can change the quality of leaf litter entering streams, since leaf species differ in decomposition rate and food value for macroinvertebrates. Riparian loss would have far-reaching effects, as streams depend on trees for food, shade, and habitat structure. Thermal effects may be masked by existing human disturbances such as canopy removal, channelization, logging, flow regulation, and pollution.

**Stream classification.** [[Poff and Ward]] (1989) classified streams of the continental United States along axes of intermittency, flood frequency, and flood predictability, predicting whether communities are controlled mainly by abiotic or biotic factors. The chapter notes that this scheme still retains the abiotic/biotic dichotomy and relies on single-point gauge records.

## Key Findings

1. **Disturbance regime as the main lever.** The author contends that the major impact of global climate change in streams will be a shift in the degree to which disturbance or biotic interactions control ecosystem functioning. This may result from altered spate frequency and magnitude, timing of high and low flows, drying frequency, or prolonged drying.

2. **Sycamore Creek case study (Arizona).** Analysis of a 29-year record (flows >1 m³/s treated as floods) compared the 5 driest and 5 wettest years:
   - Wet years had significantly more spates, significantly shorter maximum and average interflood intervals, and spent an average of 128 days (35% of the year) in early to middle successional stages.
   - Dry years spent an average of 168 days (46% of the year) in late successional stages (>200 days since flood), likely with accelerated drying conditions.
   - Monthly means of daily discharge were continuous flow for an average of 10 months in dry years, 5 months in average years, and 2 months in wet years.

3. **Biotic control after flooding.** In the [[Sycamore Creek]] system, algae in pools with [[Largemouth Bass]]-type predators were green, while pools without predators had sparse algae because of grazing by the snail *Campostoma* (Power et al., 1985). Predators reduced grazer densities, showing that biotic control of primary productivity shifts in space and time.

4. **Nitrogen as an organizing link.** Desert stream algae are often nitrogen limited. Nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria dominate late successional communities and support productivity. Spates can reduce algal standing crops by more than 80%. Some invertebrate successional sequences "crashed," which was linked to declining detrital food quality under nitrogen limitation.

5. **Alternating control.** Streams are better described by alternating periods of disturbance and biotic control than by continuous successional trends. Examples include [[Rattlesnake Creek]], where high winter flows reduce standing crops and later biotic competition and predation shape community composition.

6. **Community stability.** Invertebrate community structure was largely resistant to floods, though whole-system variables (biomass, density) responded strongly. A notable exception was a three-year shift toward snails (*Physella*) and fewer mayflies and midges, associated with higher temperature, algal biomass, and reduced discharge and nitrogen flux during prolonged drying.

7. **Drying as a stress.** Drying can be more severe than flooding because it reduces the aerial colonization source for aquatic insects and concentrates organisms in shrinking habitat. Arid-region streams are especially sensitive, since small precipitation changes can produce large changes in streamflow.

8. **Seasonal systems.** In snowmelt- and glacier-fed streams, and in Mediterranean-climate streams, long dry seasons may allow biotic interactions to dominate. Examples include [[South Fork Eel River]], where the alga *Cladophora glomerata* is grazed by tuft-weaving midges in summer, and competition in some California stream insects that is only seasonally important.

9. **Patch dynamics.** Streams are heterogeneous, so the chapter recommends first identifying spatial patches, then studying the mechanistic interactions within patches and the links among them. Beaver ponds, debris dams, and algal mats are examples of ecologically important structures. Desert riparian zones depend on stream productivity, so linkages between adjacent ecosystems may be altered by climate change and habitat fragmentation.

10. **Keystone and structuring species.** Species matter when they have unique abilities (such as nitrogen fixation in Epithemia-type diatoms), act as keystones (grazing fishes such as *Campostoma*, a "strong interactor"), or provide physical structure (wood debris, beaver dams, macrophytes). Biotic effects are often mediated by the physical environment, so biotic and abiotic factors are often inseparable.

11. **Recommendations for research.**
   - Expand the scales of investigation using whole-system experiments and creative in-system manipulations, since caging is often inappropriate in running waters.
   - Conduct long-term descriptive studies alongside experiments.
   - Use comparative methods, such as spatiotemporal gradients and historical records, and study human-affected ecosystems.
   - Merge abiotic and biotic realms by viewing ecosystems as spatial aggregations of patches, asking what each patch does in context (source or sink of nutrients, organic matter, or predators).
