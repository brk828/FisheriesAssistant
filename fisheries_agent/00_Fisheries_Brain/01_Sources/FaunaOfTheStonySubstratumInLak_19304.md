# Fauna of the Stony Substratum in English Lake District Lakes

#benthic-macroinvertebrates #limnology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

Macan and Maudsley surveyed the fauna living on stony substrata in the shallow littoral of [[Windermere]] and then extended the work to other English lakes in the [[Lake District]]. The study identified two distinct biocoenoses on the stony bottom of Windermere: a "non-insect community" dominated by flatworms, leeches, molluscs, amphipods and isopods, and an "insect community" of stoneflies, mayflies and caddisflies. The two communities merge gradually. Their distribution is linked to sewage enrichment, wave exposure and trophic status, but no single factor explains it. The authors argue for a serial, not categorical, approach to lake classification and question rigid saprobic systems.

## Background & Description

The English lakes lie in glacial basins. Windermere (17 km long) is the largest lake in the study. Reed beds are generally restricted to deeply indented bays, so most shallow-water substratum is stony. Two substratum types are distinguished following [[Moon 1934]]: fragmented, slate-like rock debris from rock destruction, and rounded stones from moraine erosion. Boulder clay deposits between rock ridges contribute to both.

The lakes form a trophic series from unproductive oligotrophic mountain lakes ([[Ennerdale]], [[Wastwater]], [[Buttermere]]) to the eutrophic [[Esthwaite Water]] (after Pearsall, 1921).

**Sampling method:** The collector lifted stones by hand and netted animals that did not cling to them. Each two-minute collection was taken 50 m apart, and five collections (10 minutes of effort) made one station. Windermere was sampled at 34 stations, 22 of which are numbered in the text. Fig. 3 reports catches for each lake over 100 minutes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Windermere distribution:**
- *[[Polycelis Nigra]]* and *P. tenuis* were moderately numerous at the north end, scarce at station 8, absent at station 4, and abundant across the south basin.
- Other flatworms, *[[Asellus Aquaticus]]*, *Physa* and the American immigrant *[[Crangonyx Pseudogracilis]]* followed a similar pattern.
- *[[Diura Bicaudata]]*, two other stoneflies and three mayfly species occurred mainly where Polycelis was scarce or absent.
- *[[Polycentropus Flavomaculatus]]* (a net-spinning caddis) was most numerous in the north basin, with a few stations in the south basin.
- Insect species were most common on the south sides of promontories, exposed to the prevailing south winds.
- *[[Agapetus Fuscipes]]* and *[[Erpobdella Octoculata]]* were the most uniformly distributed species.

**Sewage enrichment:** Ambleside effluent enters the north end. Houseboats and the Ferry House contribute to enrichment among the islands. The large Bowness and Windermere works discharge into the south basin. Several "non-insect community" species were especially abundant on either side of this outfall (stations 19 and 20). The authors call these works too efficient and the dilution too great to justify the term "pollution". The proposed mechanism is that a small prey organism becomes more abundant in enriched water, supporting more flatworms, which then prey more heavily on insects. Egg predation by omnivores and herbivores may also contribute. Insects scatter their eggs haphazardly, while non-insects protect theirs in cocoons, jelly masses or brood pouches.

**Other lakes:** Results are ordered by productivity. Coniston and Bassenthwaite, like Windermere, receive significant sewage effluent and showed different communities in different parts of the lake. Esthwaite is enriched but uniform, probably because it is small. [[Thirlmere]] is a reservoir with a surface fluctuation five or six times greater than other lakes. In Esthwaite, *[[Gammarus Pulex]]* and *Erpobdella* were more numerous, *Lymnaea* and *Physa* fewer, possibly due to leech predation. *[[Nemoura Avicularis]]* was unexpectedly common in productive lakes and scarce in unproductive ones. The authors suggest it has less affinity with other Plecoptera and Ephemeroptera than thought.

**Depth:** Collections did not extend deeper than an arm's length. March 1967 collections by the Newcastle University Sub-aqua Club at 15, 60, 120, 180 and 240 cm at three standard stations (15 two-minute collections at 15 and 240 cm, 20 at the other depths) showed that some taxa, such as *Leptophlebia marginata*, were absent from the standard samples. Shallow sampling therefore gives a biased picture of the fauna.

## Key Findings

1. **Two biocoenoses:** Windermere's stony substratum supports a "non-insect community" and an "insect community" that merge gradually. Strong wave action appears to favour their coexistence.
2. **Sewage correlation:** Enrichment by sewage is correlated with the non-insect community. The mechanism remains unknown.
3. **Trophic gradient:** Numbers of the first five species in Fig. 3 declined from productive to unproductive lakes, but not in the same pattern. *Polycelis* dropped sharply, *Gammarus* more gradually, and *Physa* in three steps.
4. **Anomalies:** Mid-series anomalies in [[Ullswater]] and [[Coniston]] are unexplained, though both drainage areas have had extensive lead and copper mining.
5. **Classification:** No sharp division exists between communities, so rigid classification is not possible. A serial approach, with one community as a reference point, is proposed.
6. **Saprobic systems:** The observations weaken the basis of any "Saprobiensystem". The north-basin centre is presumably oligosaprobic and the south basin mesosaprobic, but wave exposure, sewage and other factors interact.
7. **Sampling bias:** Shallow-water collections are biased. Underwater sampling at many sites and seasons is needed.

**Discussion points (Bousfield, Morgan, Macan):**
- Bousfield noted that lake outflows often have more plankton feeders and asked whether including the outflow area would improve lake comparisons. Macan agreed.
- Morgan asked whether sublittoral fauna, less affected by waves, would be better for classifying lakes. Macan disagreed.
- Macan cited [[Valovirta]], who showed that mud fauna changes with distance from the inflow.
