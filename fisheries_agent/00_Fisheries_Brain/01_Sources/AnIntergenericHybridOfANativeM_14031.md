# Golden Shiner x Rudd Intergeneric Hybrid

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1991 study by [[Burkhead]] and Williams (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, National Fisheries Research Center, Gainesville) describes an artificially produced intergeneric hybrid between the native [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*) and the exotic [[Rudd]] (*Scardinius erythrophthalmus*). It is reported as the first known nonsalmonid, intergeneric hybrid of an exotic and a North American native fish, and the first valid, viable hybrid involving the golden shiner. Hybrids were produced from male golden shiner by female rudd crosses. Morphological diagnostics were examined, and the authors conclude that the rudd poses an unacceptable genetic risk to native golden shiner populations, particularly given the rapid, bait-trade-driven spread of the rudd across the USA.

## Background & Description

- **Rudd:** A wide-ranging Eurasian cyprinid (supratemperate and temperate Europe, Asia Minor, interior Russia). The earliest verified U.S. introduction was in 1916, though it may have reached [[Central Park Lake]] (New York City) as early as 1897. Early introductions failed to establish. Populations existed in New York and Maine before the recent surge.
- **Recent dispersal:** From the early 1980s, rudd spread explosively after [[Arkansas Fish Farming Industry]] marketed it as a hardy, colorful bait minnow. It was distributed to 14 states and captured in public waters of 8 states. The authors compare this pace to that of [[Common Carp]] in the 1880s. Culture in Arkansas for 4-6 years and bait-dealer distribution for 2-3 years preceded awareness by fisheries agencies. Large rudd are popular bait for [[Striped Bass]] anglers.
- **Phylogenetic affinity:** Golden shiners are morphologically distinct from other North American cyprinids and are considered closest to the cultrate cyprinids of Eurasia. Recent cladistic work places them with the [[Leuciscinin Cyprinids]], which include the rudd.
- **Prior hybrid records:** Golden shiner hybrids with native cyprinodontids and atherinids failed to gastrulate. Earlier literature citing golden shiner hybrids with several North American cyprinids was judged erroneous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Golden shiners are broadly sympatric with about 31 native cyprinid genera, yet no natural hybrids with these had been found.
- Both species share a spawning guild of open-water and plant-associated broadcasters, unlike the nest-builder-associated [[Chub]] hybrid systems (*Nocomis*, *Semotilus*) common in nature.
- Rudd have also hybridized with European broadcasters, including [[Bream]] (*Abramis brama*) and [[Roach]] (*Rutilus rutilus*).
- Hybrid spread could threaten the genetic integrity of golden shiner populations in contact zones. The authors note that the risk may be low across the species' large range but unacceptable under a conservative native-genome protection philosophy.
- Commercial distribution threatens fishes and ecosystems in 26 states of the Mississippi River basin. The authors argue that the industry already has a native bait species, the golden shiner, with long acceptance by anglers and agencies.
- The rudd is an undesirable exotic under the [[Kohler and Stanley Protocol]]. Other biological questions (ecological interactions, dispersal, habitat effects, disease vectoring) remained unaddressed.
- Clinal variation in golden shiners and regional color differences may make natural hybrids harder to identify in southern parts of sympatry.

## Key Findings

**Methods (summary):**
- Parents came from Arkansas fish farms. Females were injected with LHRH-A (10 µg/kg), and six fish per cross were placed in 1,600-L tanks at 18-20°C in June 1989. Only the male golden shiner × female rudd cross yielded viable progeny. Larvae were reared in plankton-rich tanks and then in 0.2-ha ponds.
- Meristic (15 variables) and mensural (22 variables) data were drawn from 30 adults of each taxon. Analyses included principal component (PC) analysis, Scheffé's statistic, and [[Hubbs Hybrid Index]] scoring.

**Morphological diagnosis:**
- The diagnostic external character was the cultrate abdomen. Golden shiner keels are naked, rudd keels are fully scaled, and the hybrid keel is mostly scaled with a small fleshy posterior ridge. This was present in every hybrid.
- Hybrid pharyngeal tooth formulae were intermediate, though the inner tooth morphology resembled the rudd more closely.
- Hybrid life colors favored the golden shiner phenotype, lacking the rudd's orange-red fins.

**Meristic results:**
- 47% of meristic traits were intermediate. 27% (4 of 15) were supernumerary, with values outside parental ranges.
- PC1 explained 47.0% and PC2 14.2% (61.2% cumulative) of meristic variation. The hybrid lay between parents, closer to the golden shiner.

**Mensural results:**
- PC1 explained 96.2% and 97.6% cumulatively by PC2 of mensural variation. The hybrid polygon overlapped the golden shiner and was intermediate.
- Only 3 of 22 mensural characters (14%) were intermediate by the hybrid index. Nine (41%) favored the golden shiner, one favored the rudd, and nine were hyper- or hypomorphic.
- Mensural inheritance was significantly skewed toward the golden shiner phenotype.

**Anomalies and cytology:**
- Five hybrid female ovaries contained vacuole-like structures, suggesting female hybrid sterility (tentative, since no F2 hybrids were obtained).
- Ten hybrids showed double dorsal fins with associated postdorsal vertebral deformities. These were excluded from analyses.
- Both parents and the hybrid have a diploid number of 50 chromosomes, but karyotypes differ. The hybrid had 83 chromosomal arms, compared with 86 in the golden shiner and 80 in the rudd. Nucleolar dominance differed in F1 hybrids.

**Interpretation:**
- The authors attribute the skewed and disrupted inheritance to developmental instability and poor canalization of the hybrid genome. Any heterosis was judged negligible, with differences under 1% of standard length.
- The authors conclude that the hybrid's sterility or fertility should not determine whether it is released. The rudd's risk to native genomes applies equally to the hybrid. Fertile hybrids might backcross with golden shiners.

**Management implications:**
- Commercial marketing of the hybrid as bait is discouraged. Sterility is uncertain, and an uncontrolled release would be risky.
- The rudd entered river systems in states with contrasting exotic-fish policies. The authors note that practical management to protect native species "may already have been compromised" in the upper and middle Mississippi basin.

**Phylogenetic note:** The authors caution that hybridization ability is probably a primitive trait and does not show close relatedness. They do not interpret the chromosomal data as indicating sister taxa.
