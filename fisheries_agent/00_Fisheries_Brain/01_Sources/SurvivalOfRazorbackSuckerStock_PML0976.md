# Survival of Hatchery-Reared Razorback Sucker in Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report by Paul C. Marsh, based on work at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, documents the survival of hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) during pond culture in 1985. The species is a threatened [[Catostomidae|catostomid]] fish of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], and its wild populations are small and declining. The study tracked survival from fry and subfingerling stages through pond rearing, and it examined feeding with commercial trout starter diet and [[Alfalfa Pellets]]. The author concludes that hatchery techniques have improved and that the results can guide stocking and recovery efforts.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is a large sucker. The document notes that the species is known to reach large sizes, with weights of roughly 5 to 6 kg reported in the scanned text. Wild populations are small and have been described as in decline, which led to the species being treated as threatened and to the need for [[Hatchery Propagation]] for recovery.

The species' reproductive biology and early life history have become better understood, though the document notes that some early information was never formally published. Propagation began in 1974 at a hatchery in Arizona, where researchers developed techniques for spawning and rearing the fish (Toney 1974). The program was transferred to New Mexico in 1981, and the Dexter facility took over rearing.

The document also cites related work on induced spawning and hatching (Marsh 1985), on Colorado River fish survival, and on the historical status of the species (Minckley 1973; Minckley 1983; Minckley and Gustafson 1983).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study took place in earthen ponds at Dexter, which are used to grow fish for stocking. Pond-based culture allows fish to feed on natural food, such as zooplankton and invertebrates, while receiving supplemental feed. The document's water quality data are partly illegible due to scanning, but it reports the following ranges:

- Water temperatures of approximately 8.3 to 21.6 °C during subfingerling rearing.
- Dissolved oxygen (as percent saturation, approximately 87.9 to 99.6%).
- Pond depths of roughly 0.9 m (3.0 ft) in the fry and subfingerling ponds.

The document also contains references to natural food availability and to the use of commercial trout starter diet, which was not included in calculations because artificial feed was present in the ponds.

## Key Findings

**Fry stocking (1985):**
- Fry were stocked at a density of 276,200 individuals in February 1985 in ponds with depths of about 0.9 m. The exact stocking density per hectare is garbled in the scan and should be verified against the original.
- Fry were grown for 69 to 78 days. Survival and weight data are given in tables, which are partly illegible. Weights were reported in grams and pounds, with survival rates reported per pond.

**Subfingerling rearing:**
- Three ponds of about 0.4 ha (0.98 acre) were stocked with subfingerlings at densities varying from about 136,000/ha, with survival reported across ponds.
- Subfingerlings were reared for 51 to 70 days, with water temperatures from about 8.3 to 21.6 °C.
- Survival for different groups ranged from about 39% to 85%, based on the scanned data.
- A single application of alfalfa pellets at 112 kg/ha (100 lb/acre) was used in one pond before stocking. Feeding rates for other ponds were also reported, with alfalfa pellets used at 1.12 to 2.0 kg/ha in some cases.

**Stocking and feeding:**
- Fish were stocked in ponds, with feeding of commercial trout starter diet and alfalfa pellets used to supplement natural food.
- A single pond (0.15 ha) received alfalfa pellets at 112 kg/ha for the first 70 days.

**Survival and hatchery performance:**
- Survival was compared with 1983, 1984, and 1985 fertilization rates, showing improved hatchery performance over the past several years.
- The author concludes that hatchery methods have improved, and that results can support stocking of the species in its native waters.

**Conclusions from the acknowledgments:**
- The results are intended to help recovery efforts by supporting stocking of the species in former habitats and by supplementing wild populations.
- Additional information on reproduction, early development, and rearing requirements may help manage the species and increase survival.

**Reference scope:** The paper cites about 15 sources, including Deacon et al. (1979) on endangered fishes of North America, Toney (1974) on propagation, Marsh (1985) on incubation and hatching, and Minckley (1973, 1983) on the status of the species in Arizona and the Colorado River basin.
