# Annotated Bibliography of the Fishes of Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This June 1986 Special Report from the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] is an annotated bibliography of the fishes of Colorado, compiled by Charles M. Haynes and Karen Hamilton. It is meant as a foundation for understanding the state's fish fauna, past and present, and as a springboard to the literature for students, scientists, and naturalists. The bibliography contains 417 selected entries dating from the late 19th century to 1985, covering reports, publications, theses, and presented papers on 169 fish taxa. It is explicitly not exhaustive: literature focused mainly on fisheries techniques was omitted. The work is indexed by key word, county, and species, and it was funded by Colorado Nongame Income Tax Check-off donations.

## Background & Description

The bibliography was designed to precede a new text on Colorado fishes. The two earlier state books, Beckman (1952) and Everhart and Seaman (1971), were judged in need of updating. Recent Division of Wildlife publications by Wiltzius (1985) and Woodling (1985) are cited as complementary resources.

Coverage is organized around five principal drainages used as key words: the [[Colorado River Drainage]], [[Platte River Drainage]], [[Arkansas River Drainage]], [[Rio Grande Drainage]], and [[San Juan River Drainage]]. Specific rivers, streams, and water bodies are found through the County Index. The Species Index retains taxonomic names exactly as the original authors used them; the compilers did not verify or update them. Museum holdings from the University of Colorado at Boulder, the University of Michigan, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Fort Collins), and the Smithsonian Institution are listed under the key word "Collections."

The volume includes maps of Colorado's principal rivers and streams and of its principal reservoirs and lakes. Figure 2 lists 44 reservoirs and lakes. The front matter also contains a foreword by Jim Bennett, Wildlife Program Specialist, and acknowledgments to the agencies and individuals who supplied records, maps, and editorial help.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The bibliography documents several recurring themes in Colorado fish ecology:

- **Endemic Colorado River fishes in decline.** The [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] are the most frequently treated species. Many entries attribute their decline to dams, flow alteration, and habitat change, with [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and the [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] system recurring as study sites.
- **Native trout subspecies.** The [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]], and [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]] are repeatedly analyzed for taxonomy, purity, and recovery. Several entries note hybridization with introduced trout.
- **Introduced and exotic species.** Brook, brown, rainbow, and lake trout, along with carp, channel catfish, northern pike, and kokanee, were widely stocked from the 1870s onward. Entries show that many native communities shifted toward introduced species.
- **Reservoirs and urban waters.** Many studies cover reservoirs such as [[Horsetooth Reservoir]], Dillon Reservoir, and Blue Mesa Reservoir, and urban lakes in the Denver area, examining limnology, stocking success, and creel data.
- **Water quality and contamination.** Entries address mining drainage, uranium mill effluent, dissolved solids, temperature, and bioaccumulation of radionuclides in trout.

## Key Findings

The bibliography's main points and the notable findings it reports:

- **Scope and size.** 417 entries, 169 fish taxa, 1880s to 1985. The Species Index and Key Word Index allow searches by taxon, drainage, and topic.
- **Historical foundations.** The earliest entry, Abbott (1861), provides the first description of the razorback sucker. Later 19th-century and early 20th-century work includes Jordan's survey of the Rio Grande (1889) and Juday (1914), who reviewed the fishes of Colorado from 1912 to 1913 collections.
- **Bibliographic resources.** Cope (1977) indexes 1,105 Division of Wildlife references for 1941 to 1975. Wydoski et al. (1980) list 1,109 entries on the upper Colorado River ecosystem. Olive (1953) and Ecology Consultants (1977) are additional bibliographies. Wiltzius (1985) covers stocking from 1872 to 1978, with nearly 80 fishes mentioned.
- **Endangered-fish monitoring.** Wick et al. (1981) report 391 endangered fish specimens captured in the Yampa, White, Colorado, and Gunnison rivers during 1979 and 1980. Haynes and Muth (1982) found that introduced species outnumbered native species in their Yampa and Colorado samples, with redside shiners and fathead minnows predominating.
- **Yampa River significance.** Holden and Stalnaker (1975) describe the Yampa as an important refuge and spawning site for rare fishes. Haynes (1980) documented a single humpback chub in Cross Mountain Canyon in August 1982, the first Yampa River record since 1962 and the most upstream capture on record.
- **Introduced species and native declines.** Holden and Stalnaker (1975) collected 29 mainstream species on the Yampa, Green, lower Gunnison, and Colorado rivers, 19 of which were introduced. The authors attribute native decline to high dams and exotic introductions.
- **Thermal preferences.** Black and Bulkley (1985) found acute temperature preferenda of 21.9, 27.6, and 23.7 °C for yearling squawfish acclimated to 14, 20, and 26 °C, respectively, with a final preferendum estimated at 25 °C.
- **Predation risk.** Pimentel et al. (1985) showed that young squawfish can die by choking on channel catfish, a mortality factor linked to exotic prey.
- **Developmental abnormalities.** Haynes and Muth (1985) reported that 10 to 16.5% of young-of-year roundtail chub collected from the Yampa in 1980 and 1981 showed spinal curvature (lordosis).
- **Parasites.** Mpoame and Landers (1981) described a tapeworm, *Ophiotaenia critica*, as a new species infesting squawfish at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery. Mpoame and Rinne (1983) report 22 parasite species from 12 native fish species.
- **Fathead minnow tolerance.** Andrews (1970) documented a viable fathead minnow population at 3,034 m and spawning at 12.8 °C. The species requires vegetation cover and water velocities below 15 cm/s.
- **Contamination effects.** Nolting (1956) concluded that uranium mill effluent at Uravan virtually depleted the lower San Miguel and Dolores rivers of aquatic life for more than 7 miles, with an estimated 5-year recovery period after cleanup.
- **Range changes in the Arkansas drainage.** Loeffler et al. (1982) found the Arkansas darter in five southeastern counties, considered the speckled chub extirpated from Colorado, and recorded the first Colorado sighting of the redbelly dace.
- **Species inventories.** Zuckerman (1984) covers 36 Rio Grande species. Woodling (1985) describes 50 species not commonly sought by anglers. Propst (1982) reports ecological observations on 33 Platte River species.
- **Management outcomes.** Babcock (1971) found little effect of a 10-inch size limit on the Trappers Lake cutthroat trout population. Klein (1972) evaluated special regulations at Parvin Lake.
- **Recovery and propagation.** Hamman (1981, 1982) reports hatchery spawning and culture of squawfish, bonytail, and humpback chub at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery. Recovery plans for squawfish (1978) and humpback chub (1979) are cited.
- **Collections.** Museum holdings are listed for the Smithsonian (43 species and subspecies recorded from Colorado), the University of Michigan, and the University of Colorado at Boulder.
- **Compilation limits.** The compilers note that some older records required judgment about location, and that name changes for small streams complicated county assignment.
