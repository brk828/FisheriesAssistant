# Fish remains from archaeological sites

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 paper by [[Robert Rush Miller]] identifies fish remains recovered from archaeological sites in Arizona, principally [[Catclaw Cave]] on the lower [[Colorado River]]. The bones show that Indigenous peoples relied on large native fishes, namely the [[Humpback Sucker]] and the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], as food, and that a third species, the [[Humpback Chub]], was also eaten. The study also documents the decline of the native fauna after the construction of [[Davis Dam]] and the creation of reservoirs.

## Background & Description

The lower Colorado River was formerly a swift, silt-laden stream with large fluctuations in flow and turbidity. Dam construction, reservoir formation, and competition and predation by introduced game and forage fishes have decimated its native fish fauna. Only three native species (a sucker and two minnows) were known to be adapted to the extreme conditions of the lower river, and the paper argues that a third minnow once lived there. A further sucker, *Catostomus latipinnis* (Flannelmouth Sucker), probably also occurred there, as it lives in upper tributaries and the [[Gila River]] basin.

Catclaw Cave, on the east bank of the Colorado River about 15 miles below Hoover Dam, was excavated in 1949 by the Department of Anthropology of the University of Arizona. Stratigraphic dating was not possible because the cultural level had been churned by wind and animals, but cultural remains were attributed to about A.D. 750 to A.D. 1100 by comparison with a site at [[Willow Beach]]. The cave is now inundated by [[Lake Mohave]].

The collection comprised more than 375 bones and fragments, well preserved, of which about 100 were readily identified. Two families were represented: Catostomidae and Cyprinidae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pre-dam river:** Strong currents and gradient maintained swift water for at least 20 miles below [[Boulder Canyon]] (Hoover Dam). Below Topock the river broadened and lost gradient, so a permanent humpback chub population there is considered unlikely.
- **Post-dam river:** Transformed into deep, placid reservoirs connected by clear, controlled-flow channels.
- **Sturgeon record at Snaketown:** Earlier bones attributed to sturgeon (*Acipenser*) were judged misidentified. The genus is rare south of San Francisco Bay and does not enter fresh water south of the Sacramento system. The author attributes the bones to Colorado Squawfish and possibly Humpback Sucker.
- **San Pedro River erosion:** Severe channel erosion beginning about 1880 radically altered the San Pedro and other southwestern streams, after which the squawfish probably no longer inhabited the river.

## Key Findings

**Catclaw Cave (Lower Colorado River)**

- **[[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** Attains about 3 feet and commonly 10 to 14 pounds, occasionally 16. Remains indicate at least seven individuals. Charred bones confirm consumption by Indigenous people. Neural complex bones are unusually modified, and the largest fish was estimated at about 2 feet and 10–12 pounds. Ike Dowell reportedly took six tons from Lake Sahuaro in 1949.
- **[[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):** One of the largest minnows in the world. Ellis (1914) gives 5 feet and nearly 100 pounds. Testimony from a 1950 survey suggests a maximum length of about 6 feet. Remains from at least six individuals were found. The skull (USNM No. 27408) was estimated to belong to a fish about 4 feet long, and the largest salmon was at least 5 to 9 feet.
- **[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*):** Identified solely from two pharyngeal arches, each from a different individual. Arch lengths of 24.0 mm and 26.3 mm imply total lengths of about 17 to 18 inches. The author suggests it may be an extreme form within a series of subspecies of *Gila robusta*, which requires further study.

**Other sites**

- **[[Snaketown]] (Hohokam site, Gila River):** Historic bones were lost. The sturgeon identification was judged erroneous, and the remains were reattributed to Colorado Squawfish and possibly Humpback Sucker.
- **[[Quiburi]] (Sobaipuri site, San Pedro River, occupied c. A.D. 1150–1760):** Thirty-one squawfish vertebrae (largest 34.5 mm) suggest a total length of about 3 feet. A single humpback sucker vertebra (10 mm) was also identified. A report from 1846 describes "salmon trout" 3 feet long in the San Pedro, implying the species once occurred there.

**Food use and significance**

- The three native fishes were the largest and most important food fishes for Indigenous peoples. The humpback chub was less desired because it is very bony.
- Squawfish were caught by dipnets after sudden river rises, by bow and arrow, or by pitchfork spearing, and they readily take hook and bait at all seasons.
- Where edible fishes such as sturgeon and Pacific salmon are absent, as in much of the Colorado River, the genera *Xyrauchen* and *Ptychocheilus* gained food importance out of proportion to their value elsewhere.

**Conclusions**

- Humpback Sucker, Colorado Squawfish, and Humpback Chub were all eaten by people at Catclaw Cave.
- Dam construction and introduced species have caused the decline of these native fishes.
- Taxonomic status of the humpback chub as a distinct species remains open to question.
