# Investigation of the Roseau River Fish Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This fisheries report by Paul C. Marsh and Ira R. Adelman (University of Minnesota) documents a fall 1978 survey of fish in the [[Roseau River]], Minnesota. The work was funded by the [[U.S. Army Corps Of Engineers]], which needed the data to supplement an [[Environmental Impact Statement]] for a revised [[Roseau River Flood Control Project]]. Sampling used [[Electrofishing]] and [[Seine Netting]] across eight river reaches between October 7 and 18, 1978. The objectives were to measure length, weight, species composition, population size and habitat of the river's fish.

Sixteen species were collected. [[Walleye]], [[Northern Pike]] and [[White Sucker]] dominated the catch. Very few young-of-year fish were captured, and the walleye catch lacked age I fish. Population estimates were mostly unreliable because few marked fish were recaptured.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Design changes to the flood control project created a need for current fish data on the Roseau River. The [[Minnesota Department Of Natural Resources]] cooperated and supplied earlier survey results.
- **Study reaches:** Eight designated reaches spanned roughly river mile M138.0 to M93.0 and included a flooded oxbow (Reach 5), which could not be navigated and was not sampled.
- **Electrofishing gear:** A boat-mounted Smith-Root type VIA electrofisher supplied 4.0–5.0 amps at 404 volts (as printed) in pulsed DC.
- **Seining:** A 3.0 x 0.9 m minnow seine with 6.35 mm mesh was used. At least two hauls were made along most reaches. Reaches 2 and 5 could not be seined because of depth, vegetation or substrate.
- **Marking and tagging:** Fish were marked by clipping the dorsal lobe of the caudal fin. Some large northern pike and walleyes received yellow Floy tags. Tagging was stopped because of high post-tagging mortality.
- **Age determination:** Scales from northern pike and walleyes were pressed onto cellulose acetate impressions and examined under a magnification of 40.6X. Annuli were counted to assign ages. Walleye scales were read twice to check for systematic error.
- **Population estimates:** Estimates used a modified [[Mark-Recapture]] formula, N* = (M+1)(C+1)/(R+1), with bias and confidence intervals from Ricker (1975).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reach habitat:** Upstream reaches 1–2 and downstream reaches 8–9 were high-gradient sections with solid substrates, many rocks and minor vegetation. Intermediate reaches 3–7 were slow, had soft substrates and considerable aquatic vegetation, including [[Elodea]] and Potamogeton beds.
- **Cover:** Brush piles, downfalls and rocks occurred in all reaches. Log jams were common in several reaches.
- **Weather:** Conditions were mostly mild with fair skies. Some days were inclement, with freezing air temperatures, rain, snow or high winds. Visibility was usually fair.
- **Effort:** Miles fished ranged from 1.5 (Reaches 2 and 8) to over 5 (Reaches 1 and 4). Total boat hours ranged from 1 (Reach 8) to 7 (Reach 1). Electrofishing effort ranged from 0.26 hours (Reach 8) to 3.20 hours (Reach 4). The number of runs per reach ranged from 1 to 10 (Reach 3).
- **Fish distribution:** Larger walleyes and white suckers were associated with pool habitat. Northern pike were found in shallow vegetated water. Smaller white suckers occupied riffles, and small pike and walleyes used stream-margin vegetation. Fish were rarely found near brush piles or log jams, which may reflect gear inefficiency there.

## Key Findings

1. **Species composition:** Sixteen species were collected by electrofishing and seining. Electrofishing catch by number was led by [[White Sucker]] (47%), [[Walleye]] (29%) and [[Northern Pike]] (22%). Northern redhorse, trout-perch, [[Black Bullhead]], carp and [[Sauger]] made up the remainder. By weight, white suckers contributed 42%, northern pike 29% and walleyes 24%.
2. **Game fish dominance:** Walleyes and northern pike were the most abundant game species. The river had a greater proportion of game fish than most comparable waters.
3. **Catch per unit effort (CPE):** Numerical CPE was highest in downstream reaches (7, 8 and 9) and lowest in the middle reaches. Weight-based CPE was highest in Reaches 1 and 7–9.
4. **Size structure:**
   - Most northern pike measured 175–199 mm.
   - Most walleyes measured 275–324 mm.
   - Most white suckers measured 174–224 mm.
   - There was a distinct paucity of walleyes between 150 and 249 mm. Scale analysis suggested these missing fish were age group I, and Minnesota DNR data showed the same scarcity.
5. **Age structure:**
   - Northern pike ranged from age 0 to age VII, with most in age group 0. Mean lengths were 189 mm (0), 343 mm (I), 414 mm (II), 466 mm (III), 543 mm (IV), 610 mm (V) and 820 mm (VII). No age VI fish were collected.
   - Walleyes ranged from age 0 to XII, with most in age group II. Only 12 fish were age I. Mean lengths were 129 mm (0), 270 mm (I), 286 mm (II), 309 mm (III), 347 mm (IV), 374 mm (V) and 411 mm (VI).
6. **Population estimates:** Most estimates were unreliable because of very few recaptures, which produced strong negative bias. Four estimates were considered statistically valid. Abundance appeared greater in downstream Reach 7.
7. **Tagging outcome:** 59 northern pike and 15 walleyes were tagged. Only 44 pike and 8 walleyes survived capture and handling. No tagged fish were recaptured.
8. **Seine results:** Seine catches were extremely low. Seven species were unique to seine collections: [[Common Shiner]], johnny darter, blackside darter, fathead minnow, longnose dace, rock bass and central mudminnow. Seven species recorded by the DNR (including sand shiner, creek chub, silver redhorse, stonecat, tadpole madtom, burbot and freshwater drum) were not found in this survey. Black bullhead was found only in this survey.
9. **Very few small fish:** Few small fishes were collected by either seine or electrofishing.
10. **Study limitations:** The authors noted that low recapture rates limited population estimates. Electrofishing efficiency may have been reduced around brush and log jams.
