# Colorado River Endangered Fishes Recovery Program: Correspondence, Meeting Records and Work Plan (1980–1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #colorado-river-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a bundle of U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) and stakeholder records from the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for endangered fishes of the Colorado River. It covers the listing status of the [[Razorback Sucker]], the draft [[Colorado Squawfish]] recovery plan, the [[Recovery Implementation Committee]] and [[Management Group]] meetings, the FY 1989 work plan and budget, a draft cooperative agreement with the [[National Fish and Wildlife Foundation]], and water rights acquisition for instream flows. The main recurring issues are the status of the razorback sucker, fragmentation of habitat, the setting of Upper Basin recovery goals, and funding shortfalls.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River system supports several endangered native fishes. The document names the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the [[Humpback Chub]] and the [[Bonytail Chub]], which are federally listed as endangered. The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was a candidate for listing. Its historical abundance in the lower river declined after construction and operation of seven dams, channelization, and predation and competition from introduced species.

The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] was signed on January 21–22, 1988, by the governors of Utah, Wyoming and Colorado, the Western Area Power Administration and the Secretary of the Interior. It set up the [[Recovery Implementation Committee]], which oversees FWS implementation. Its members include the three states, FWS, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], Western Area Power Administration, water development interests and conservation groups. The [[Recovery Team]] provides scientific advice on recovery plans.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Razorback sucker status (1980 letter):** The fish were caught in the upper Green River only with special effort and less often than Colorado squawfish. No razorback reproduction had been documented in the upper basin. In the lower basin, a 1979–80 study of [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] found viable eggs and larvae from two spawning areas, but no young-of-the-year or yearlings. Two young fish (12"–15") were captured in the Parker Valley canal system in early 1980.
- **Spawning habitat:** Spawning areas had a slope of about 30% and clean, loose gravel (1/4"–2" diameter). Annually renewed gravel bars in the lower river had declined significantly since dam construction. Only the Parker and Mohave divisions and Lakes Mohave and Havasu were judged to have comparable gravel substrate.
- **Reservoir operations:** Water level fluctuations in February and March 1980 killed eggs and larvae at Senator Wash. Dam and reservoir operations during spawning months were identified as a possible critical factor in lower river egg and larval mortality.
- **Predation:** Channel catfish followed gravid females and fed over spawning sites. Carp, bluegill and largemouth bass were consistently recorded in spawning areas, though predation was not observed. Illegal introductions of smallmouth bass and tilapia at Senator Wash threatened to spread into the lower river. Flathead catfish increased from Mexico to Parker Dam and were first documented above Parker Dam in Lake Havasu in June 1980.
- **Squawfish spawning:** The draft plan named suspected spawning reaches at Black Rocks to Loma (RK 217–233) and Grand Junction to Clifton (RK 257–290). Pitts stated that no larval squawfish had been found in the Grand Junction–Clifton reach since 1982, and that low temperatures were a major impediment to spawning there.
- **Fragmentation:** The Recovery Team unanimously agreed that fragmentation of the squawfish range, which can result in loss of habitat, is a serious threat to recovery and possibly survival.
- **Non-native stocking:** The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources proposed stocking [[Rainbow Smelt]] into [[Lake Powell]]. The Recovery Team asked that the agency demonstrate "the absence of potential conflict with rare or endangered species."

## Key Findings

**1. Razorback sucker listing and status**
- In 1980 the Recovery Team asked FWS to propose listing the razorback sucker as threatened, citing no evidence of significant recruitment since the 1978 proposal.
- In 1989 the team reaffirmed support for formal listing but recommended endangered status instead, because numbers were considerably lower than those of squawfish and humpback chub and no recruitment was occurring.
- A Sierra Club Legal Defense Fund petition to list the razorback sucker as endangered throughout its range was on the April 1989 agenda.
- The Ten-Year MOU for stocking razorback suckers in the Lower Basin in lieu of listing expires May 29, 1991.

**2. Recovery plan disputes**
- Tom Pitts (water users representative) argued that the draft [[Colorado Squawfish]] recovery plan wrongly set specific Upper Basin recovery goals. He argued that setting them is the responsibility of the Implementation Committee under Section 2.0 of the program.
- He also objected to delisting criteria that refer to "significant fragmentation" and to the White River passage language tied to [[Taylor Draw Dam]].
- Pitts proposed general Upper Basin recovery goals until the Implementation Committee adopts specific ones, and asked that the issue go to the February 21, 1989 committee meeting.

**3. Carlin tagging and handling**
- The Recovery Team found continued [[Carlin Tagging]] valuable in certain areas (San Juan and Little Colorado River). It said tagging should occur only within approved study designs.
- It recommended that FWS evaluate electrofishing, netting and handling methods to minimize trauma and ensure survival.

**4. Leadership and recommendations**
- The Recovery Team nominated Steve Petersburg (National Park Service) as Team Leader and proposed Reed Harris (Bureau of Reclamation) as Team Secretary.
- Team Leader A. St. Amant announced his resignation after serving as a member since 1974 and as Leader since 1980.

**5. FY 1989 work plan and budget**
- The final FY 1989 request exceeded available funds by approximately $268,000. The shortfall came from increased project requests and a shortfall in FWS cash contributions.
- The revised work plan totals $2,324.7 thousand, compared with $2,303.2 thousand in the August 1988 approved plan. Funding is spread across FWS, Bureau of Reclamation, Colorado, Utah and Wyoming.
- Five projects were deferred, including "Establish Recovery Goals" ($30.0K), the "Issue Paper – Habitat Development Options" ($10.0K) and "Razorback Management Plan" ($10.0K).
- Major increases included FWS Program Management ($70.0K to $157.0K), Yampa River flow recommendations ($72.5K to $140.5K) and Flaming Gorge hydrology support ($30.6K to $42.0K).
- Major decreases included Colorado River flow recommendations ($176.5K to $133.0K) and Gila taxonomy ($90K to $53K).
- The San Juan River Recovery/Management Plan was moved from March 1, 1989 to September 30, 1989.

**6. Funding and cost-recovery mechanisms**
- Recovery Program implementation is estimated at $58.5 million over 15 years. Water project proponents are expected to contribute about $9–10 million.
- Proponents would pay $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion, with 10% due at the federal action and the balance at construction or before depletion starts.
- Section 5.5 of the program calls for contributed and donated funds to be held in an interest-bearing account, such as one administered by the [[National Fish and Wildlife Foundation]].
- The Foundation draft agreement comes in three versions, which differ in whether new funds, existing funds and congressional appropriations go to the Foundation. The agreement runs until January 22, 2003, and the Foundation may charge 2% administrative overhead.
- The Nature Conservancy agreed to help with real estate work only if broad consensus support continues. It declined to endorse the $10 per acre-foot approach or a general congressional endorsement of the program.

**7. Water rights and instream flows**
- A January 3, 1989 public hearing in Craig, Colorado, drew 75–100 people. About one third supported keeping the Yampa River free-flowing, and one third supported developing the [[Juniper-Cross Mountain Reservoirs]].
- On January 10, 1989, the Craig City Council voted 7–0 to support continued negotiations on a 100,000 acre-foot reservoir and long-term use of Juniper-Cross water rights for instream flow.
- FWS was skeptical of a lease, since it provides no permanent legal protection and would be hard to sell to Congress.
- Options for protecting [[Ruedi Reservoir]] releases through the [[15-Mile Reach]] included a state instream flow right and use of the [[Orchard Mesa Check]]. The reach needs supplemental flow when flows drop to about 622 cfs.

**8. Upcoming actions**
- The February 21, 1989 [[Recovery Implementation Committee]] agenda covered budget and work plan items, Section 7 consultations (Sandstone and Muddy Creek), San Juan River participation, Grand Canyon humpback chub studies, instream flow determinations, and the Uintah Mountain Club lawsuit.
- The April 6–7, 1989 Management Group meeting was scheduled to review the razorback petition, the Colorado squawfish plan and Ruedi releases.
