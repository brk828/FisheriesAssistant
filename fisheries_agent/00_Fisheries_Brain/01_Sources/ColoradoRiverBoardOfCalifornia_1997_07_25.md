# Colorado River Board of California Comments on FWS Final Biological and Conference Opinion (Lower Colorado River O&M)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #ConservationPolicy #FreshwaterEcology

## Executive Summary

This 1997 letter is a formal regulatory response from the [[Colorado River Board Of California]] to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's (FWS) Final Biological and Conference Opinion (BO) on Lower Colorado River (LCR) operations conducted by the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]]. While primarily a legal/administrative critique of the BO's scope, discretion analysis, and use of the environmental baseline, the document contains substantive ichthyological content regarding the population status, recruitment limitations, and conservation programs for two federally listed endemic fishes of the LCR: the [[Razorback Sucker]] and the [[Bonytail Chub]]. The letter argues that non-native fish predation and competition — largely a legacy of historical FWS and state agency stocking practices — is the primary driver of native fish recruitment failure, rather than Reclamation's current discretionary water operations. It also documents contemporaneous data on the endangered [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] as supporting evidence against claims of "catastrophic" habitat loss.

## Background & Description

- **Razorback Sucker ([[Razorback Sucker]])**: A large-bodied catostomid native to the Colorado River Basin. Current headstart/grow-out conservation programs (in Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu) rear juveniles to **10–12 inches** in length before release, a size threshold identified as necessary to reduce vulnerability to predation by non-native piscivores.
- **Bonytail Chub ([[Bonytail Chub]])**: A large cyprinid, also subject to headstart rearing programs targeting the same 10–12 inch size class for post-release survival.
- Prior reintroduction efforts (Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers) used smaller-bodied stocked fish, which the document notes resulted in **zero survivors**, implicating insufficient size-at-release as a critical morphological/life-history bottleneck rather than habitat suitability alone.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Mainstem Lower Colorado River and associated reservoirs — [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]], and [[Lake Havasu]] — plus historic reintroduction sites in the Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers.
- **Trophic interactions**: The dominant ecological pressure on native fish recruitment is identified as **predation and competition by non-native fishes**, corroborated by the USFWS's own 1994 Critical Habitat Designation Biological Support Document, which cites this as "the single most important limiting factor" for native LCR fish reproductive success.
- **Non-native species implicated**:
  - Stocked historically by FWS: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], sunfish, [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]]
  - Stocked historically by state wildlife agencies: [[Striped Bass]], [[Flathead Catfish]]
- **Recruitment paradox**: Despite documented in-river spawning success — over **100,000 wild razorback sucker larvae harvested in 1996–97** by the Native Fish Work Group in Lake Mohave — the vast majority of larvae and juveniles are subsequently lost to predation by these established non-native populations, effectively decoupling spawning success from population recruitment.
- **Historical context**: Native "trash fish" populations (the historical characterization of native catostomids/cyprinids) were actively suppressed by FWS and state agencies in favor of stocking trout and sport fish, compounding present-day non-native dominance.
- **Co-occurring listed species**: The [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], a riparian passerine, is discussed as an indicator of broader LCR ecosystem/habitat dynamics — surveys at Topock Marsh, the Virgin/Muddy River deltas, and the lower Grand Canyon documented flycatcher use of non-willow habitats (e.g., saltcedar, agricultural drainage riparian corridors), challenging the BO's assumption that only willow/cottonwood habitat supports successful nesting.

## Key Findings

1. **Recruitment limitation misattributed**: The BO attributes native fish recruitment failure to hydrologic variability ("too little or too much water"), but the Colorado River Board argues predation/competition by non-natives — a legacy of federal and state stocking programs — is the dominant limiting factor, per the USFWS's own prior findings.
2. **Successful in-river spawning demonstrated**: Wild razorback sucker larval production (>100,000 in 1996–97, Lake Mohave) demonstrates that spawning is not the primary bottleneck; post-hatch survival through predation is.
3. **Headstart program rationale**: Current lakes Mohave/Havasu grow-out programs (targeting 10–12 inch release size) are explicitly designed as **bridging conservation measures** to prevent extinction while long-term recovery solutions are developed — not to establish self-sustaining wild populations in the mainstem, consistent with both the completed [[Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan]] and the draft [[Razorback Sucker Recovery Plan]].
4. **Failed historical reintroductions**: Reintroduction attempts in the Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers failed entirely (no survivors), attributed to insufficient fish size at release relative to predation pressure — informing the current size-based headstart strategy.
5. **Regulatory/scientific basis dispute**: The Colorado River Board contests FWS's reliance on an unadopted, non-peer-reviewed preliminary draft "Lower Basin Fisheries Management Prospectus" (1995) rather than the peer-reviewed, finalized Bonytail Recovery Plan and draft Razorback Sucker and aquatic ecosystem recovery plans, raising concerns about the scientific rigor underpinning specific Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives (RPAs).
6. **Species conservation responsibility**: The letter emphasizes that responsibility for native fish decline should be shared among FWS, state wildlife agencies (as historical stocking entities of competing non-natives), and Reclamation, rather than placed disproportionately on Reclamation's narrow, discretionary water-operation actions.
7. **Flycatcher habitat flexibility**: Contrary to BO assumptions, southwestern willow flycatchers were found successfully nesting and fledging young in saltcedar-dominated habitat (Topock Marsh) and agriculturally-associated riparian corridors (Mesquite area), and in previously "extirpated" locations (Virgin/Muddy Rivers), undermining the characterization of Lake Mead Delta habitat loss as "catastrophic."
