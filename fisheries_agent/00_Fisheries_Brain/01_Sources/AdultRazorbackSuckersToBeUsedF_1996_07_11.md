## Executive Summary
This 1996 internal memorandum from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service documents a management crisis involving a batch of adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) broodstock slated for a radio-telemetry implantation study led by the [[Hualapai Tribe]]. Sixteen fish were delivered to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] on 12 June 1996 for planned surgical implantation and prompt release into [[Lake Mohave]]. Due to delays and lack of coordination between the Hualapai Tribe and Service facilities, the fish were held far longer than intended, resulting in four mortalities and visible decline in the remaining twelve individuals. A fish health specialist found no definitive pathological cause of death, and the surviving fish were urgently repatriated to Lake Mohave rather than proceeding with telemetry surgery. The document highlights critical husbandry, timing, and inter-agency coordination failures affecting an endangered, long-lived broodstock population.

## Background & Description
While this document is administrative rather than morphometric, it contains indirect biological data points relevant to the [[Razorback Sucker]] population involved:

- **Age**: Fish were reported to be **over 30 years old**, indicating long-lived broodstock individuals — consistent with known longevity records for this species.
- **Life stage**: Adults, maintained long-term as [[Broodstock]] (likely sourced from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] or wild-caught from Lake Mohave).
- **Condition**: Fish were described as "weakened" and "not doing well" following prolonged captivity, though no gross pathology was identified upon veterinary/fish-health examination.
- No direct measurements (length, weight, fin ray counts, coloration) are provided in this correspondence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the lower [[Colorado River]], serving as critical habitat for wild and reintroduced [[Razorback Sucker]] populations.
- **Thermal requirements**: Release protocol specified that fish be transported downlake, tempered en route, and released only into water **at least 75 °F (23.9 °C)**, indicating species/individual sensitivity to thermal shock during handling and reintroduction.
- **Population role**: The affected fish were part of a **captive broodstock program**, underscoring the species' status as endangered and the reliance on hatchery-supported (e.g., [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]) conservation efforts to sustain genetic reserves for the wild population.
- **Conservation context**: The species is federally listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and this document reflects direct management actions tied to its recovery program in the [[Colorado River Basin]].

## Key Findings
- **Mortality event**: 4 of 16 adult Razorback Suckers died after prolonged holding at Willow Beach Hatchery following delivery on 12 June 1996; remaining 12 fish showed signs of stress/decline.
- **No definitive cause identified**: Fish Health Specialist [[Jerry Landye]] examined the fish on 10 July and found **no obvious pathological cause** for death or poor condition, contradicting an earlier suspected cause cited in a memo from [[John Seals]] (Willow Beach) to [[Ben Zimmerman]] (Hualapai Game and Fish).
- **Management failure**: The intended protocol — implant, stabilize briefly, and release within about one week — was not followed; fish were held nearly a month without progressing to telemetry surgery.
- **Corrective action**: Following consultation between USFWS ([[Parker FRO]]) and [[Clay Bravo]] (Hualapai Game and Fish), surviving fish were released back into Lake Mohave on 12 July 1996, prioritizing fish welfare over completion of the study.
- **Study status/conditions**: USFWS (AZFRO) stated that continued support for the Hualapai radio-telemetry project required fish to be **implanted, stabilized, and released quickly**, with **no extended holding** before or after surgery.
- **Broodstock value**: Fish were noted as valuable long-term broodstock (>30 years old, maintained "at a cost of thousands"), amplifying the significance of the mortality event for the species' captive genetic reserve.
- **Coordination gap**: The incident revealed a broader **communication and coordination deficiency** between the Service, its hatchery facilities, and the Hualapai Tribe, prompting calls for improved inter-agency protocols on future endangered species research projects.
- **Future stocking consideration**: Any replacement fish for the telemetry study would need to originate from **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] broodstock** or **wild-caught individuals from Lake Mohave**.
