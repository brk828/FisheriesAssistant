## Executive Summary
This document is an internal USDA Forest Service (Tonto National Forest) memorandum, dated 22 January 1999, transmitting a summary data sheet of fish surveys conducted on the **upper [[Verde River]]** during spring and autumn 1998. The surveys were led by fishery biologist **Rome A. Stefferud** and **John Rinne** of the [[Rocky Mountain Research Station]], with field support from multiple state and federal cooperators (AGFD, USFWS, BoR, ASU, Prescott and Tonto National Forests). All fish captured during electrofishing/seining surveys were released alive at their point of capture, indicating a catch-and-release monitoring protocol consistent with native fish conservation concerns in this system.

This note therefore documents the survey design, site locations, and target species assemblage rather than quantitative catch results, which are absent from the extracted text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The survey documents the fish community structure of the **upper Verde River**, a Sonoran Desert perennial stream system in central Arizona, at seven discrete sampling sites:

- Burnt Ranch (T17N R1W Sec 7)
- FR638 (T17N R1W Sec 4)
- Duff Spring (T17N R1E Sec 6)
- Bear Siding (T18N R1E Sec 28)
- Perkinsville (T18N R1E Sec 36)
- Black Bridge (T18N R2E Sec 32)
- Sycamore (T17N R5E Sec — partially illegible)

This site array spans a longitudinal reach of the upper [[Verde River]] mainstem, sampling a mixed assemblage of:
- **Native fishes**: [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Spikedace]] — several of which are federally listed or Species of Concern in Arizona due to habitat loss and competition/predation from introduced species.
- **Nonnative/introduced fishes**: [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] — all known predators/competitors implicated in the decline of Southwestern native cyprinids and catostomids.

The co-occurrence of native cyprinid-catostomid fauna with nonnative centrarchids, ictalurids, and poeciliids reflects the broader ecological conflict characteristic of Southwestern U.S. streams, where introduced sport and forage fishes threaten endemic desert fish communities. The catch-and-release protocol (all fish "returned alive to the point of capture") suggests this was a population-monitoring effort rather than a removal/eradication study.
