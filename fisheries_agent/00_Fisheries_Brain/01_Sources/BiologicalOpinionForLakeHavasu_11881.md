# Biological Opinion for Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Partnership Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species-act #section-7-consultation

## Executive Summary

This biological opinion, issued February 18, 1993, by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Arizona Ecological Services Field Office, addresses formal Section 7 consultation for the [[Lake Havasu]] Fisheries Improvement Partnership Program. The Bureau of Land Management proposed installing artificial fish habitat structures in 42 coves covering approximately 90 acres (0.4% of the reservoir) to enhance non-native sport fisheries, along with shoreline access improvements. Critically, the project includes establishing up to ten native fish rearing coves to release approximately 30,000 [[Razorback Sucker]] and 30,000 [[Bonytail Chub]] into [[Lake Havasu]] by 2003. The Service concluded the project is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of bonytail chub or razorback sucker, nor adversely modify proposed critical habitat, and concurred with findings of no effect to [[Bald Eagle]], [[Brown Pelican]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], and [[Yuma Clapper Rail]].

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) was listed as endangered April 23, 1980, and belongs to genus *Gila* along with [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*) and [[Virgin River Chub]] (*G. seminuda*). Its distinctive feature is an extremely thin caudal peduncle. Once found throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]], recent captures are limited to six locations. In the lower basin, bonytail chub are taken annually in [[Lake Mohave]] with a small population persisting in [[Lake Havasu]]. Successful recruitment has not been positively documented in the lower basin, though Dr. Paul Marsh believes some wild-spawned fish exist in Lake Mohave alongside hatchery-stocked individuals. Adult bonytail chub occupy pools and eddies in large rivers; capture records from Lake Mohave are from lacustrine areas. Diet includes floating terrestrial and aquatic insects in riverine habitats, and plankton, stocked rainbow trout fry, and algae in Lake Mohave.

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large catostomid first described in 1861, was listed as endangered November 22, 1991. Its distinctive sharp raised keel behind the head gives the species its common name. Historically abundant throughout the Colorado River Basin, the largest remaining population (estimated 60,000 individuals) is in Lake Mohave. Natural spawning is widely documented, but recruitment of juveniles has been extremely limited. In the Green River, the last successful recruitment is estimated to have occurred in the 1960s. Captures from the 1980s-present indicate some wild-bred razorback suckers survived to juvenile stages in irrigation canals below Lake Havasu, though spawning location is unknown. Adults occupy main channel flatwater areas, sandbars, nearshore runs, backwaters, and eddies. Larval razorback suckers feed on small diatoms, phytoplankton and detritus; adults are both benthic and planktivorous feeders.

The proposed action involves placing 67,482 bass shelters, 54,724 catfish houses, 3,484 Fish 'N Forests, 1,050 tire towers, and 11,800 brush bundles into 42 coves adjacent to rooted aquatic vegetation in 10-25 feet of water. Shoreline access improvements include parking lots, trails, fishing docks, restrooms, and fish cleaning stations. Implementation would occur over 5-10 years as funding becomes available. The project also includes creating up to ten native fish rearing coves where juvenile fish from federal or state hatcheries would grow to 300 mm before release into the reservoir—a size believed beyond vulnerability to non-native predator fish. Cove development would occur over five years with a ten-year period to achieve objectives. Monitoring of coves and reservoir to locate released fish is included.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River Basin]] has experienced profound physical and biological changes over the past century. Physical alterations include dam construction, water diversions, channelization, altered flow regimes, and floodplain development. Large reservoirs release cold, clear water from depth, dramatically altering downstream temperature and turbidity conditions. Continued water reuse increases salinity and introduces pesticides and contaminants from agricultural, municipal, and industrial return flows. These changes have significantly reduced and fragmented historic native fish habitats.

Introduction of non-native fish species, both deliberate and accidental, has contributed substantially to native fish decline. Non-natives compete directly and indirectly with native species, prey on native fish (especially early life stages), and may introduce new diseases or parasites. Physical habitat changes likely facilitate non-native success by creating conditions more suitable for introduced than native fauna.

Both bonytail chub and razorback sucker populations have been eliminated from much of their former ranges. The bonytail chub is considered the most endangered native fish in the basin, with the recovery plan's short-term goal being prevention of extinction from the wild. Populations in the upper basin are very small and isolated; successful recruitment has not been documented for many years. The small Lake Havasu population is not known to have reproduced successfully in many years. Lake Mohave has the largest remaining population, but it is not self-sustaining. Both lower basin populations and probably most upper basin populations are dominated by old adults. As a relatively long-lived species, existing populations are likely to reach senescence within a few years.

The razorback sucker, though numerically larger than bonytail populations, faces similar threats. The large Lake Mohave population consists of old fish close to senescence; researchers estimate this population will likely be lost or significantly reduced in 10 years. Successful recruitment in the upper basin has not been documented in recent years. Lower Colorado River populations outside Lake Mohave are small and not self-sustaining. Introduced razorback suckers in the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]] have not established self-sustaining populations. Developing strategies to improve larval and juvenile survival is critical to species survival in the wild.

## Key Findings

**Consultation Determination**: The Service concluded that implementation of the proposed project is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of bonytail chub or razorback sucker, nor adversely modify or destroy proposed critical habitat for bonytail chub. The Service concurred with BLM's finding of no effect to bald eagle, brown pelican, peregrine falcon, and Yuma clapper rail.

**Direct and Indirect Effects**: Placement of fish habitat structures in 42 coves will affect non-native fish populations by providing cover (currently limiting) and attracting fish from adjacent areas. Increased angler use and fishing pressure in treated coves is expected. Structures may increase juvenile fish survival by providing cover, though increased predatory fish concentrations could counteract this benefit. Structures will likely provide increased food resources (algae and invertebrates). Effects on adult bonytail chub and razorback sucker are uncertain—if attracted to structures, risk of angler capture increases; if adults spawn near structures, predation on eggs, larvae, and juveniles may not differ significantly from present levels. Young endangered fish may benefit from cover or face greater predation risk due to higher predatory fish densities at structures.

**Incidental Take**: The Service authorized incidental take under two categories: (1) angler take not to exceed twenty individuals each of bonytail chub and razorback sucker per calendar year (including both released alive and killed); (2) mortality of stocked fish in rearing coves, estimated by BLM at 85%, with consultation reinitiation required if mortality exceeds 95% for two consecutive calendar years. Mortality from transport and stocking is not considered take for this project.

**Reasonable and Prudent Measures**: Three measures required: (1) inform anglers of endangered fish presence through posted information at all recreational facilities; (2) reduce potential mortality of endangered fish taken by anglers through clear identification aids and release instructions; (3) minimize mortality sources in rearing coves through adequate monitoring of barrier nets, physical features, posted closure information, and biological monitoring to detect higher-than-expected mortality.

**Conservation Benefits**: The project provides critical recovery opportunities by establishing refuge populations in Lake Havasu, enabling hatcheries to produce large numbers of young fish from different parents to maintain genetic diversity. Substantial wild populations provide buffer against loss of smaller populations and supply wild-adapted fish for transplant. Research opportunities will result from these populations, addressing critical knowledge gaps in life history and habitat requirements needed for survival and recovery actions.

**Proposed Critical Habitat**: [[Lake Havasu]] was proposed as critical habitat for bonytail chub on January 29, 1993. The Service concluded the project does not result in destruction or adverse modification of proposed critical habitat. While structures may alter physical and biological attributes of treated coves, they are not permanent and could be removed if research indicates major adverse effects. The small proportion of Lake Havasu affected (0.4%) and benefits provided by rearing coves were considered in this determination.

**Population Status**: Both species face precarious status. Bonytail chub populations are extremely small, isolated, and dominated by old adults approaching senescence. Lake Mohave has the largest population but it is not self-sustaining. Razorback sucker populations, while numerically larger, face similar age-structure problems with the Lake Mohave population estimated to be lost or significantly reduced within 10 years. Recruitment failure throughout the basin threatens both species with near-term extinction from the wild without intervention.

**Monitoring and Reporting**: Annual reporting required on: (1) number of bonytail chub and razorback sucker reported taken by anglers; (2) stocking and release from rearing coves, including problems encountered and estimates of numbers released. Consultation reinitiation required if incidental take limits are exceeded or if monitoring reveals higher-than-expected impacts.
