#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary
This 1969 field experiment by T. B. Bagenal tested whether egg size in [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) influences fry survival under natural, competitive conditions. Using colour-marked (carotenoid-pigmented) large and small eggs planted together in stream gravel troughs in Black Brows Beck, Lancashire, the study found that fry derived from larger eggs survived significantly better than fry from smaller eggs when reared together under overcrowded, territorial conditions. The results support [[Svärdson's Hypothesis]] (1949) that larger eggs, though fewer in number, compensate via higher offspring survival — providing an evolutionary check against mutations favouring higher fecundity at the cost of egg size, at least in territorial trout populations.

## Background & Description
- **Egg size classes**: Large eggs (mean dry weight 35.0 mg) vs. small eggs (mean dry weight 28.4 mg), from the same stripping/fertilization batch (17 November 1964 cohort used in starvation trial).
- **Identification method**: Natural red/orange carotenoid pigmentation of eggs persists through the alevin stage into the fry, concentrating in the adipose and other fins — allowing large- and small-egg progeny to be visually distinguished even after hatching.
- **Fry morphometrics**: Length and dry weight were measured for all recovered fry; neither length nor weight alone reliably separated the two egg-origin groups, but plotting length against weight together produced two distinguishable clusters, cross-validated against carotenoid pigmentation.
- **Growth/size relationship**: Larger eggs produce larger fry (per [[Dahl 1918-19]]), and larval/embryonic growth rate is proportional to size (per [[Gray 1926]], [[Gray 1928]]), with maximum body weight reached later in fry derived from larger eggs ([[Smith 1958]]).
- **Starvation resistance data** (Table I, alevins from 17 Nov 1964 fertilization, hatched 1 Feb 1965):

| Temperature | Egg size | 50% dead | 100% dead |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5°C (approx.) | Small | 25 April | 26 April |
| 5°C (approx.) | Large | 28 April | 2 May |
| 10°C | Small | 10 April | 17 April |
| 10°C | Large | 16 April | 24 April |

Large fry from large eggs consistently survived longer without food than small fry from small eggs at both constant temperatures tested.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Territoriality**: Young [[Brown Trout]] in Lake District becks are territorial, with a maximum stable density of ~7–10 fry/m² ([[Le Cren 1965]]); higher initial stocking density does not raise the eventual density — implying strong density-dependent mortality via territorial competition.
- **Redd/gravel life stage**: Larger fry (from larger eggs) may remain longer in the gravel of redds relying on yolk reserves, while smaller fry may emerge and begin feeding — and thus claim territories — earlier, complicating simple assumptions about "larger eggs = automatic survival advantage."
- **Experimental habitat design**: Fertilized eggs were placed in Vibert Boxes buried in sieved gravel (5–10 cm deep) inside wooden aluminium-lined troughs (236 × 66.5 × 28 cm), themselves buried in the streambed of Black Brows Beck, with controlled flow via upstream dam/sluice, mimicking natural redd conditions while allowing full fry recovery.
- **Stocking density**: 30 large + 30 small eggs per trough (four Vibert boxes) equated to ~38.2 eggs/m², deliberately exceeding the natural equilibrium fry density (7–10/m²) to induce competition for territories.
- **Timing sensitivity**: Mortality from territorial competition is concentrated between 20–40 days after fry begin feeding ([[Le Cren 1965]]); troughs emptied too early caught alevins still bearing yolk sacs (non-competitive stage), while troughs left too long risked losing colour-based group distinction or eliminating one group entirely.
- **Comparative context across taxa**: Contrasts with [[Blaxter and Hempel Herring Studies]] (1963, 1966), where large herring (*Clupea harengus*) eggs confer both greater larval size and longer starvation survival, and with [[de Ciechomski 1966]], where egg size in Argentine anchovy (*Engraulis anchoita*) had little influence on larval starvation survival — indicating egg-size effects on survival are not universal across fish taxa.

## Key Findings
- Across 7 trough-cohorts analyzed by chi-squared test (1966–1969 seasons; excluding two batches emptied too early with yolk-sac alevins still present), the number of surviving fry derived from **large eggs was highly significantly greater** than expected under an equal-survival null hypothesis, and fry from **small eggs were highly significantly fewer** than expected.
- Table II summary (observed vs. expected fry counts under equal survival assumption) across multiple trough trials (1967–1969) consistently showed large-egg fry over-represented and small-egg fry under-represented among survivors.
- The findings **experimentally confirm** [[Svärdson's Hypothesis]] (1949) — building on [[Lack 1948]]'s work on avian clutch size — that larger eggs' survival advantage compensates for a fecund female's reduced egg number, at least in *Salmo trutta*.
- The proposed mechanism is **territorial competition**: differential survival is mediated by size-based competition for limited stream territories rather than direct differences in viability, starvation resistance, or genetic quality alone.
- Because most fish species are not territorial at early life stages, the authors caution that this precise mechanism (territoriality) may not generalize, though analogous density-dependent mechanisms could produce similar fecundity–egg size trade-offs in other species.
- This directly counters a purely deterministic assumption (common in earlier literature, e.g. [[Pope et al. 1961]] on Atlantic salmon fecundity, [[Parrish and Saville 1965]] on herring fecundity) that more fecund parents' smaller eggs necessarily suffer higher unconditional mortality — instead demonstrating this mortality differential empirically under realistic competitive field conditions.
