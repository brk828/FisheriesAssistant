# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Phase I - 1992 Annual Report: Trout Studies

#ichthyology #salmonidae #rainbow-trout #tailwater-ecology #flow-management

## Executive Summary

This 1992 annual report from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] documents comprehensive research on [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) populations in the [[Lee's Ferry]] reach of the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] from November 1989 through February 1993. The study collected over 18,000 length-weight records to assess impacts of fluctuating flows on trout spawning, stranding mortality, age and growth, and behavioral responses. Key findings include dramatic reductions in stranding mortality under new interim flows (from 15,000-20,000 adult deaths in 1990-1991 to only 4 deaths in 1991-1992), widespread parasitic nematode (*Bulbodacnitis ampullastoma*) infestation affecting 88-100% of the population, declining relative weight indices from 1984-1991, and evidence that spawning occurs primarily in shoreline areas rather than traditional cobble bars. The research employed electrofishing, radio telemetry, binary coded wire tags (BCWTs), and detailed spawning habitat surveys to evaluate trout population dynamics under varying flow regimes.

## Background & Description

The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Phase II investigated how dam operations and fluctuating flows affect the tailwater trout fishery. [[Rainbow Trout]] were introduced to this reach following dam closure in 1963, creating an artificial ecosystem with exotic species including the amphipod *Gammarus lacustris* as a primary food source. Historical records indicate that stocked trout comprised 73% of the adult population during high steady flows in 1984-1985, though Phase I studies suggested 27% natural reproduction within the system.

The study area extended from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (river mile 0) downstream approximately 15 miles to [[Lee's Ferry]]. Researchers divided this reach into three habitat types: Habitat #1 (riffle-like, 34% of reach) characterized by cobble bars, high velocities, steep gradients, and *Cladophora* beds; Habitat #2 (run-like, 34%) with intermediate velocities and sand substrate; and Habitat #3 (pool-like, 32%) with deep, slow water, fine sediments, and rooted macrophytes.

Multiple strains of rainbow trout were stocked over the years, though records from 1963 through the early 1980s are incomplete. The genetic composition and fitness characteristics of the population remained largely unknown until electrophoretic analyses were conducted by the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] tailwater below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] represents a highly modified aquatic ecosystem where flow regimes are controlled by hydroelectric operations. Water releases create daily fluctuations that expose and inundate shoreline habitats, affecting spawning success, stranding mortality, and food availability. Water temperatures remain consistently cool due to hypolimnetic releases from [[Lake Powell]], potentially affecting the formation of annuli in fish hard structures and complicating age determination.

The spawning season extends from August through May, considerably longer than typical for rainbow trout, with peak activity varying by year. During 1990-1991 and 1991-1992, spawning was delayed and reduced in intensity, possibly due to research flows, food limitations, or parasite stress. The 1992-1993 season appeared more typical with earlier onset and greater intensity.

Trout utilized diverse spawning habitats beyond the initially identified 5-10 cobble bars. Direct observations revealed that most spawning occurred in shoreline areas with acceptable substrate size and current velocity, including deep-water sites not subject to dewatering. Major spawning bars were surveyed at river miles -4.0, -6.1, -8.9, -13.5, and -14.0, with the -4.0 mile bar identified as the largest and most significant spawning site.

Stranding occurred primarily in ten major backwater areas that became isolated at specific discharge levels. Two sites (-11.9R and -14.8R) isolated at 4,000 cfs and appeared to be year-round trout habitat with dense *Cladophora* growth. Other sites were shallow spawning areas used seasonally, suggesting stranding is primarily a spawning-related phenomenon. Water quality parameters (temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity) rarely reached lethal limits except during unusual low-flow events.

The discovery of the parasitic nematode *Bulbodacnitis ampullastoma* in virtually 100% of examined trout represented a significant finding. This endoparasite, found in pyloric caeca and intestinal junctions, was present in preserved specimens from 1984, indicating it was not a recent invader. The type locality for this species is Hot Creek State Fish Hatchery in California. Laboratory studies by Hiscox and Brocksen (1973) demonstrated that parasitized trout exhibited reduced growth rates under normal feeding and 60% higher mortality under starvation conditions.

## Key Findings

**Work Item 2.1 - Spawning Habitat and Redd Placement:**

- Spawning occurred over a much broader area than initially assumed, with most activity in shoreline areas rather than traditional cobble bars
- The -4.0 mile bar was the most significant spawning site; -14.0 mile bar had no spawning, -8.9 mile bar had negligible spawning, and -6.1 mile bar became unsurveyable under interim flows
- Five spawning bars were surveyed for elevation contours using Total Station equipment, with steel rebar stakes placed as reference points
- Wetted perimeter measurements were made at 3,000, 5,000, and 8,000 cfs constant discharges
- Current velocity matrices were constructed at approximately 7,000 and 12,000 cfs, with plans for 16,000 and 20,000 cfs measurements
- A new methodology adopted in March 1992 focused on annual "snapshot" redd surveys at peak spawning to establish a relative index of spawning activity
- Deep-water spawning areas not subject to dewatering were extensively used by trout
- Spawning activity during 1990-1991 and 1991-1992 was delayed and reduced compared to "normal" years, possibly due to research flows, food limitations, or parasite stress

**Work Item 2.2 - Stranding and Mortality:**

- Estimated 15,000-20,000 adult trout (mean length 437 mm, SD=53) died from stranding February 1990 to February 1991
- Under new interim flows (1991-1992 and 1992-1993), only 4 stranded adult trout were found in 21 surveys, with 3 likely to survive until water levels rose
- New minimum flows and reduced ramping rates virtually eliminated stranding as a mortality factor for adult trout
- Stranding was primarily a spawning-related phenomenon; five Floy-tagged trout released from a stranding pool returned and perished when it dewatered again
- Trout fry stranding occurred in equal or fewer numbers than adults
- Water quality rarely caused mortality; deaths resulted from dewatering or predation
- During 5,000 cfs research flow weekends, fish died from high temperatures or oxygen supersaturation on the second or third day
- Bank retention, ultimate minimum flow, and down-ramping rates all influenced dewatering rates of stranding pools

**Work Item 2.3 - Age, Growth, and Population Dynamics:**

- Over 18,000 length-weight records collected from approximately 6,000 captured trout
- Mean total length declined significantly from 397 mm (±5.29 SE) in 1989 to 285 mm (±3.13 SE) in 1992 (F₃,₁₀₆₁ = 389, P <0.001)
- Significantly larger fish were captured from mid-river sections (river miles 6-12), with mean lengths of 379-397 mm compared to 308-314 mm in upstream sections
- Winter seasons consistently produced larger fish (mean 378 mm ±3.08 SE), likely due to spawning condition
- Relative weight (W_r) showed significant stepwise degradation from 1984 to 1991, declining from approximately 100 in 1984 to 64 in 1991
- W_r improved slightly in 1992 (mean 82 ±2.45 SE) compared to 1991 (mean 64 ±1.56 SE)
- Length-frequency distributions shifted from predominantly unimodal (1989-1990) to distinctly bimodal (1991-1992), suggesting two cohorts
- Creel statistics showed dramatic declines: catch per hour dropped from 0.27 (1983) to 0.03 (1992), mean weight from 1,153 g (1980) to 499 g (1992)
- Angler hours declined from 242,432 (1991) to 110,392 (1992), with harvest dropping from 10,076 to 3,216 fish
- Percent of fish released increased from 30% (1980) to 92% (1992)

**Binary Coded Wire Tag (BCWT) Results:**

- 89,000 rainbow trout fingerlings (50-125 mm) were tagged with BCWTs at [[Page Springs Hatchery]] in May 1992
- 70,000 BCWT trout stocked May-June 1992; 8,000 held at hatchery and stocked September-October 1992
- Tag retention was 95-96% at one to two weeks post-implantation and just before stocking
- In August 1992, wire-tagged fish comprised 22% of trout <200 mm, suggesting 78% natural reproduction (opposite of the 27% reported by Maddux et al. 1987)
- Hatchery-held trout (mean TL 180 mm ±2.36 SE) showed significantly greater growth than ex-hatchery stock at Lee's Ferry (147 mm ±2.12 SE) by August 1992 (F₁,₄₅₃ = 893, P <0.001)
- Initial stocking size in May 1992 was mean TL 92 mm ±0.74 SE
- BCWTs successfully distinguished wild from hatchery trout, enabling future estimates of growth rates, mortality, and contribution to the fishery

**Parasitology and Fish Health:**

- The nematode *Bulbodacnitis ampullastoma* was found in 88-100% of all trout examined from 1990-1993
- Parasites were located in pyloric caeca and intestinal junctions
- Historical examination of preserved specimens from 1984 confirmed the parasite was present, not a recent invader
- Anecdotal reports suggest "skinny" trout were observed as early as 1980-1981
- In 1992 surveys, 88% of fish had moderate to heavy nematode concentrations
- Trout stocked three months prior to August 1992 sampling were nematode-free and had 70-90% estimated body fat
- Trout from May 1992 stocking were infected by August; 42% had no fat reserves and 33% had <50% fat
- Other parasites found rarely: *Clinostomum minimum* (yellow grub), *Gyrodactylus* sp. (skin), *Ambiphyra* sp. (gills)
- *Yersinia ruckeri* (ERM-1) bacterial infection was extremely rare
- Virtually all trout examined in December 1990 contained no body fat reserves
- Laboratory studies suggest the nematode may mechanically interfere with fat absorption or enzymatically convert it to unusable form
- Under stress conditions (fluctuating flows, reduced food), parasitized trout may experience abnormally high mortality

**Work Item 2.4 - Radio Telemetry and Behavior:**

- Ten adult trout (40-50 cm TL) were implanted with radio transmitters in November-December 1990
- Initial design using surveyed stakes for triangulation had limitations; angular errors increased with distance, achieving only 66% accuracy within 5 m
- Modified methods using different antennas and boat-based triangulation achieved 95% accuracy within 5 m for current position
- Difficulty remained in accurately mapping successive movements over time without surveyed reference points
- Plans developed to modify methods for measuring behavioral responses under new flow regimes
- Small-scale movement measurements remained problematic with existing experimental design

**Methodological Developments:**

- Standardized electrofishing protocol: 2,000 seconds per 640 m site, three sites per habitat type, five sites per night over three consecutive nights quarterly
- Fish processed assembly-line style after MS-222 anesthesia (50-125 mg/l for 2-5 minutes)
- All fish received numbered Floy spaghetti tags, weight (nearest gram), total length (nearest mm), sex determination, maturity assessment, and injury documentation
- Since August 1992, appropriate size classes examined with magnetic detection wand for BCWTs
- MULTIFAN computer simulation planned to estimate ages from length-frequency distributions using maximum likelihood or chi-square methods
- Conventional aging methods (scales, otoliths) appeared ineffectual, possibly due to consistent cool water temperatures
- Plans to re-evaluate multiple bony structures (cleithrum, operculum, sagittal otoliths, vertebral centrum) using different preparation methods
- Relative Weight (W_r) index adopted as superior to traditional condition factor (K) because it is length-independent
- W_r calculated as 100 × W/W_s, where W_s is standard weight for a given length based on 75th percentile regression

**Data Management and Analysis:**

- Over 18,000 length-weight records plus additional data to be incorporated into database management system
- Historical data from 1984 to present (over 80,000 records) planned for MULTIFAN analysis
- GIS integration planned with GCES coordinator assistance
- Outside consultant needed to standardize and collate survey data from three different crews in multiple formats
- Plans to generate predictive models of redd density and relative abundance using regression analyses
- Recapture data may be used to estimate population abundance using Lincoln Index or Seber-Jolly and Zippen open fishery models

**Literature Review and Genetics (Addendum to Work Item 2.2):**

- Literature review of trout strains completed November 1991 (Davis 1991)
- Electrophoretic analysis of trout populations and hatchery stocks completed by Illinois Natural History Survey (Claussen and Philipp 1991)
- Review planned by Dr. Mike Musyl to make recommendations based on genetics report
- Goal was to identify commercially available strains with fitness characteristics suitable for Lee's Ferry operating regimes
- Similar tailwater fisheries and their associated strains were identified for comparison
