# Razorback Sucker and Colorado Pikeminnow Reintroduction and Monitoring in the Salt and Verde Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department Technical Report (No. 147, August 1999) documents the reintroduction and monitoring of two endangered big-river native fishes, [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), in the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] of central Arizona. Both species had been extirpated from these rivers through habitat alteration and the introduction of nonnative fishes. Over a five-year period (July 1994 to June 1999), hatchery-reared fish were stocked using a revised protocol of larger fish (≥300 mm). Stocking was concentrated in the lower Verde River, while the upper Salt River was dominated by nonnative [[Flathead Catfish]]. The authors conclude that the Verde River shows high potential for supporting reintroduced populations, but that stocking numbers must increase and nonnative predators must be controlled before success can be assured.

## Background & Description

**Colorado Pikeminnow.** A member of the minnow family Cyprinidae and the largest of four *Ptychocheilus* species, it can reach six feet and weigh up to 80 pounds (figure as given in source: "as much as io porrnas," read as 80 lb per standard references in OCR). It is the top native predator of the Colorado River system. Historically abundant enough that farmers used it as fertilizer, it now occupies less than 25% of its original range (Karp and Tyus 1990). It was first listed as endangered in 1967 and received full protection under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. Lateral line count is 80–95 scales, with nine rays in the dorsal and anal fins.

**Razorback Sucker.** A member of the sucker family Catostomidae, named for the ridge extending from the head to the middle of the back. Adults reach over three feet and weigh up to 15 pounds; females are typically larger. Spawning males develop tubercles and females darken in color. The species has seven anal rays and 68–87 lateral line scales. It was listed as endangered on October 23, 1991, and its populations have declined sharply over the past 50 years.

**Earlier reintroduction history.** Reintroduction began in the mid-1980s with large numbers of small hatchery fish, which proved unsuccessful: recapture of stocked fish was low and recruitment into the adult population did not occur. A shift to larger fish (>300 mm) followed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Colorado Pikeminnow is a habitat generalist, using deep low-velocity pools, swift runs, and shallow shoreline areas. It migrates long distances upstream to spawn, and its decline is linked to river alteration and dam construction.
- **Study reaches, Upper Salt River (four reaches):** Reach 1 (US Highway 60 bridge to Gleason Flat) is steep and boulder/bedrock dominated; Reach 2 (Gleason Flat, RM 32.5 to 29.9) is a wide, low-gradient alluvial expanse; Reach 3 (Gleason Flat to Cherry Creek) resembles Reach 1; Reach 4 (Cherry Creek to Roosevelt Diversion Dam) is wide with a lower gradient.
- **Study reaches, Verde River (three reaches):** Reach 1 (Beasley Flat to Childs) is bedrock-dominated with steep drops; Reach 2 (Childs to Horseshoe Lake) is the most variable, influenced by the East Verde River and Fossil Creek alluvial fans and by Horseshoe Dam.
- **Water quality:** Upper Salt River conductivity exceeded 3,000 µS, hampering electrofishing in earlier periods.
- **Critical habitat and status:** Portions of the Verde and Salt rivers were designated critical habitat for razorback sucker on March 21, 1994. Colorado Pikeminnow was reintroduced as an experimental non-essential population (Section 10(j)).
- **Nonnative fishes:** [[Flathead Catfish]] were stocked into the Salt River above the Roosevelt diversion dam in the early 1970s and now make up 80% of fish collected between Horseshoe Bend and Highway 288 (up from under 5% at the start of reintroduction in 1981). Native species have been severely reduced in this reach.

## Key Findings

**Stocking protocol and propagation**
- Stocking shifted from fry and fingerlings (1981–1990) to larger fish (>300 mm), presumed beyond the acceptable prey size of most predators.
- Since November 1996, at least 2,000 fish of each species (≥300 mm) have been produced per year at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Bubbling Ponds State Hatchery]]. Razorback Sucker takes about 24 months to reach 300 mm; Colorado Pikeminnow about 36 months.
- Verde River reintroductions were concentrated at or below Beasley Flats, to improve survival and reduce monitoring area.

**Stocking totals**
- Verde River: 13,250 razorback suckers since 1994 (all but two stockings ≥300 mm; the other two averaged 245 and 254 mm); 8,537 Colorado Pikeminnow since December 1995, all ≥300 mm.
- Salt River: a single razorback sucker stocking of 2,046 fish (mean 285 mm) at Horseshoe Bend in December 1996, sourced from the Utah state hatchery at Monticello.
- Tagging: coded wire tags were implanted in all Verde River releases; 346 razorback suckers and 54 Colorado Pikeminnow received PIT tags; 303 of the Utah-sourced fish were PIT tagged.

**Monitoring results**
- Verde River razorback sucker recaptures rose steadily: 12 in 1996, 10 in 1997, and 61 in 1998. Surveys were limited in 1999 due to flow and funding constraints, with 17 razorback suckers and 31 Colorado Pikeminnow recaptured (figures as given in source).
- Recaptured adults were found in spawning condition, and some had moved many miles from stocking sites.
- Salt River: only one stocked razorback sucker was recaptured (Roosevelt Lake, February 1997). A 290 mm razorback sucker was found in a flathead catfish stomach in May 1994.

**Management conclusions and recommendations**
- Long-term data are needed to assess whether stocking about 2,000 sub-adults per year can contribute to recovery. Consistent multi-year stocking appears to allow survival.
- Stocking should continue in the Verde River; the authors suggest increasing numbers to 10,000 or more per species.
- Stocking in the upper Salt River should not resume until agreements are reached among the White Mountain Apache Tribe, San Carlos Apache Tribe, Arizona Game and Fish Department, US Forest Service, and US Fish and Wildlife Service, and until nonnative predators, especially flathead catfish, are reduced.
- Future work should include larval and juvenile surveys to document natural reproduction and recruitment in the Verde River.
- Recent regulation changes allow unlimited harvest of nonnative fishes above Horseshoe Dam and the Roosevelt diversion dam, and prohibit transport of live baitfish. Anglers have also been reporting Colorado Pikeminnow, prompting an outreach program on identification and safe release.
