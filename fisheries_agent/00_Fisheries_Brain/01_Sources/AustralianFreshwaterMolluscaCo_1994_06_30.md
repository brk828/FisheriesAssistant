## Executive Summary

This paper by W.F. Ponder addresses conservation priorities for Australian freshwater invertebrates, with a primary focus on the gastropod family **[[Hydrobiidae]]** as indicator taxa for identifying biologically significant, long-term permanent freshwater habitats. While the paper is invertebrate-centric, it notes that Great Artesian Basin (GAB) springs harbor **endemic fishes** alongside endemic invertebrates, and highlights that exotic fish (including introduced trout) pose a predation threat to native mollusc and invertebrate faunas in Tasmania. The core argument is that poor-dispersal, desiccation-intolerant invertebrates (hydrobiids, flatworms, amphipods, isopods) can serve as proxies for identifying refugial, long-term permanent water bodies of high conservation value — including the [[Great Artesian Basin]] mound springs and the perennial streams of SE Australia and Tasmania — which are currently under-protected relative to their biodiversity significance.

## Background & Description

- **[[Hydrobiidae]]** (family-level): Small freshwater gastropods; despite pronounced genetic divergence and species-level speciation (via allozyme electrophoresis), sympatric cryptic species at [[Wilsons Promontory]] were "with one exception... morphologically indistinguishable," indicating that morphology alone substantially underestimates true species diversity in this group.
- **Ancylastrum** (Planorbidae): Described as a "large limpet-like planorbid" gastropod, restricted to a few lakes in central Tasmania; listed as endangered.
- An unnamed **lymnaeid gastropod** is known only from the lower Franklin River, Tasmania — narrowly avoided extinction when a dam proposal was defeated.
- Three independent hydrobiid radiations are documented in the GAB springs, occurring in largely endemic genera:
  1. Lake Eyre region, South Australia (two genera)
  2. Dalhousie Springs, South Australia (endemic genus)
  3. Western Queensland springs (endemic radiation)
- Endemic genera and subfamilies in some spring systems are hypothesized to be relicts of an early Pleistocene/late Tertiary (or older) inland freshwater fauna.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat specificity**: Hydrobiids and other poor-dispersing invertebrates (flatworms, amphipods, isopods, ostracods) are tightly associated with **long-term permanent aquatic habitats** — springs and perennial streams — because they lack desiccation-resistant life-cycle stages.
- **Indicator species role**: Because these taxa cannot survive ephemeral drying events (such as those induced by Pleistocene aridity), their presence indicates water bodies that have maintained permanent water throughout much of the Pleistocene and Holocene, making them useful proxies for identifying centers of endemism analogous to the way indicator fish species are used in aquatic ecosystem assessment.
- **Microendemism and gene flow**: At Wilsons Promontory (Victoria), 4 genetically distinct sympatric hydrobiid species were found within a 15 km area (75 populations sampled). Counterintuitively, gene flow between geographically distant, isolated GAB artesian springs was found to be *higher* than between closely spaced streams at Wilsons Promontory — attributed to bird-mediated dispersal being more effective across arid, open spring habitats than through dense temperate vegetation.
- **Interaction with fish fauna**:
  - GAB mound springs support **endemic fishes** in addition to rare flora and endemic invertebrates.
  - In Tasmania, **exotic fish species** that prey on native fish and invertebrates have become established even within protected World Heritage Areas and are "almost impossible to control."
  - The endangered planorbid *Ancylastrum* in central Tasmanian lakes is "severely threatened by hydro-electric developments and predation by trout" — a direct fish-invertebrate conservation conflict.
  - The introduced hydrobiid **[[Potamopyrgus antipodarum]]** acts as an exotic competitor within Tasmanian freshwater systems.
- **Threats to habitat**: Water extraction from the GAB has caused cessation of flow in many mound springs over the last century; nearly all springs in NW New South Wales are now dry, with widespread loss continuing in Queensland. Tasmanian lotic systems face threats from damming (e.g., Gordon Dam, the defeated Franklin Dam, the destroyed Lake Pedder) and mining pollution (King River).

## Key Findings

- More than 90 Australian hydrobiid taxa are slated for inclusion in the next IUCN Red List edition — none had prior listing status.
- Only one South Australian spring group and one minor Queensland group currently have legislative protection; the remainder of GAB springs sit on unprotected pastoral land.
- Localized, undocumented/unnamed hydrobiid species are vulnerable to extinction from even minor local disturbances (e.g., farm dams), since their restricted ranges are often unrecognized due to lack of taxonomic documentation.
- Historic land clearing for agriculture in SE Australia has likely already caused undocumented extinctions among restricted-range hydrobiid populations; conversely, retention of even small patches of native vegetation around water sources has allowed faunas to persist, suggesting that minimal-intervention conservation can be effective.
- The paper argues for a dual conservation strategy: while broad **habitat conservation** is essential (as previously emphasized for Australian aquatic fauna generally, e.g., [[Michaelis 1986]]), it must be paired with **explicit identification of endemism hotspots**, since strictly habitat-based approaches can overlook narrow-range endemics such as hydrobiid snails.
- Fish-related conservation conflicts identified: endemic fish coexist with endemic invertebrates in GAB springs (shared conservation value), while exotic/introduced fish (including trout) represent a direct predation threat to endangered native mollusc species in Tasmania, illustrating cross-taxon management tensions between fisheries interests (e.g., trout stocking) and invertebrate conservation.
- Overall, artesian water extraction and hydro-electric development are identified as the principal drivers of habitat loss for both endemic invertebrate and fish faunas in these systems, underscoring the need for integrated water resource management policy.
