# Revised List of Euryhaline Fishes of North and Middle America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #euryhaline-fishes

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Gordon Gunter]] (Gulf Coast Research Laboratory, 1956) revises and extends an earlier annotated list of fishes that tolerate both fresh water and pure sea water. The revised list contains **150 species** from North and Middle America, north of the southern boundary of Panama. The author replaces the earlier four-part annotated list with a straight list grouped by life-history category (catadromous, anadromous, and fishes of fresh-water or marine ancestry). The paper also analyzes how these groups are distributed across taxonomic lineages and latitudes, and it argues that euryhalinity is concentrated in lower, less specialized fish groups.

## Background & Description

Two patterns of salinity movement are distinguished:

- **Anadromous and catadromous fishes** make regular migrations between sea and fresh water, as in spawning runs.
- **Sporadic invaders** enter the other environment irregularly, with no apparent physiological necessity. Marine fishes are the main group in this category.

The paper defines a euryhaline fish as one recorded from both fresh water and pure sea water by competent observers. This definition is deliberately conservative. It excludes fishes that enter only low-salinity bay waters and fishes recorded only with ancillary evidence, which were placed in a separate "probably euryhaline" group not treated here. The author notes that the definition is arbitrary, that some listed species may not survive indefinitely in either environment, and that this can only be settled by experimentation.

Additions to the earlier list include:

- [[Alosa Alabamae]] (Gulf shad), an anadromous species
- *Pomolobus chrysochloris* (Mississippi Valley alewife)
- Several [[Brevoortia]] species (menhaden)
- [[Fundulus Similis]] and [[Gambusia Affinis]]
- [[Gadus Macrocephalus]] (Pacific cod), recorded from fresh water in the Naknek River, Alaska
- *Membras martinica* and *Gillichthys mirabilis*
- One eel and six gobies from the Gulf Coast, based on salinity measurements by Bailey, Winn and Smith (1954)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salinity of fresh waters.** The author notes that "fresh water" is ambiguous. Rain near the sea may carry about 10 ppm chloride, soft fresh water contains roughly 50 to 70 ppm of salt, and hard fresh water may reach 0.30 ppt. On low-lying coasts, some salt in fresh water comes from the sea, so marine-fish records from such areas are suspect.

**Florida as a special case.** [[Odum (1953)]] showed that much of the Florida peninsula has waters with chloride of about 100 ppm or more, and that some sites with marine fishes have chlorinity of 570 to 900 ppm, which falls in the [[Oligohaline Zone]]. Because these waters fall outside the list's basic concept, Florida records were largely excluded. Carr and Goin (1955) nevertheless documented several marine species in Florida fresh waters that had not been reported from fresh water elsewhere.

**Known inland records.** Marine fishes occur more than 160 miles upriver in the [[Atchafalaya River]] of Louisiana (Gunter 1938b). They also occur at [[Homosassa Springs]], Florida (Herald and Strickland 1949), though Bailey, Winn and Smith (1954) questioned some of these records.

**Latitude and lineage.** All anadromous species occur in the temperate zone and northward. The two most southerly are the [[Petromyzon Marinus]] (sea lamprey, St. John's River, northern Florida) and [[Roccus Saxatilis]] (striped bass, Louisiana). The eel [[Anguilla Rostrata]] is the only known catadromous species.

## Key Findings

- **Total:** the revised list includes 150 species, grouped as catadromous, anadromous, fresh-water fishes of marine ancestry, marine fishes of fresh-water ancestry, and marine fishes that enter fresh water.
- **Anadromy:** nearly all anadromous fishes belong to lower groups (Cyclostomata, Chondrostei, Isospondyli), with two acanthopterygian exceptions in the family Moronidae. The author suggests this holds across the Northern Hemisphere and possibly worldwide.
- **Fresh-water ancestry moving to sea:** only [[Cyprinodontidae]] and three catfishes (Ariidae) have permanently moved into salt water. The cyprinodontids are characteristic of bays and brackish water.
- **Marine ancestry moving to fresh water:** [[Gobiidae]] are the chief group in this transition. The author writes that gobies have been more actively invading fresh water than any other group in the recent era. Gasterosteidae (stickleback) and some Syngnathidae appear to be in transition too.
- **Established freshwater groups:** some [[Atherinidae]] and [[Osmeridae]] species are firmly established in fresh water and no longer return to the sea. The author suggests certain North American atherinids may be further along in invading fresh water than gobies.
- **Fresh-water fishes that enter the sea:** only eight North American species do so. Most are uncommon in salt water, but the gar *Atractosteus spatula*, the gizzard shad *Dorosoma cepedianum*, and two [[Mollienisia]] species are commonly taken in salt water.
- **Invasion imbalance:** marine fishes that enter fresh water outnumber fresh-water fishes that enter the sea by more than ten to one. Günther (1880) reached a similar general conclusion without presenting data.
- **Lower versus higher fishes:** using only the euryhaline species, there are 63 lower (non-acanthopterygian) and 47 acanthopterygian euryhaline species north of Mexico. South of the United States, there are 30 lower and 58 acanthopterygian species. The author argues this pattern is shaped by ecology, physiology, paleontology and evolution, and the reasons remain unresolved.
- **Relative prevalence:** the lower 12 percent of North American fishes contain about 40 percent of the euryhaline species. The author suggests this parallels thermal adaptation, in which poikilotherms are also of lower order, and raises the possibility that less specialized fishes of past eras were more euryhaline.
- **Size and age of invaders:** the author's earlier work showed that marine fishes invading fresh water are mostly small or young. The author argues this does not indicate that fishes originated in fresh water. Instead, young marine fishes may follow ancestral behavior patterns for reasons unknown.
- **Caveats:** the list is conservative, and many species' ability to survive indefinitely in either environment still requires experimental confirmation. Some published records (notably Florida and certain Gulf Coast sites) remain under question.
