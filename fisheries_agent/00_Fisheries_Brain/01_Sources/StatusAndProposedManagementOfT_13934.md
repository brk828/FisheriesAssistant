# Status and Proposed Management of the Rough Sculpin (Cottus asperrimus) in California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game special publication (Special Publication 82-1, February 1982), by [[Robert A. Daniels]] and [[Louis A. Courtois]], reviews the status of the [[Rough Sculpin]], a small freshwater cottid restricted to the middle [[Pit River]] system of northeastern California. The species was designated biologically rare by the [[California Fish and Game Commission]] in 1973. The authors conclude it is not currently endangered or threatened, but face potential threats from habitat degradation, shoreline development, land-use change and introduced nonnative fishes. They propose protection and monitoring measures, centered on managing the [[Essential Habitat]] as wild trout streams.

## Background & Description

- **Identity:** The smallest cottid in California, recognizable by its relatively narrow body and very rough skin. The specific name *asperrimus* is Latin for "most rough."
- **Taxonomic history:** First described by [[Cloudsley Rutter]] (1908) from five specimens (one holotype, four paratypes) collected from the Fall River at Dana in the 1890s. Snyder (1917) synonymized it with *C. gulosus*, but this was not widely accepted, and Bailey and Dimick (1949) restored it as a valid species.
- **Morphology:** Finely mottled gray to light-brown body with four or five lateral blotches; white venter; barred dorsal and pectoral fins; white pelvic fins. Meristics (Moyle 1976) include 5–7 first dorsal spines, 17–19 second dorsal rays, 13–17 anal rays, 14–16 pectoral rays and 19–29 lateral-line pores. No sexual dimorphism was detected in color, meristics or morphometrics.
- **Similar species:** Most similar to the slender sculpin (*C. tenuis*) of the [[Klamath River]] drainage, which may reflect a former connection between the two drainages. Distinguished from the marbled, Pit and riffle sculpins in the Pit system by body shape, color, size and axillary prickles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and associations.** Rough sculpin are generally found in cool, deep, clear, rapidly flowing water associated with rooted vegetation, fine substrate and runs. Major populations occur in spring-fed systems arising at the base of large lava flows, such as [[Fall River]], lower [[Hat Creek]] and Crystal Lake. The species also occurs in turbid, warm Big Lake (summer temperatures above 30°C) and in small, spring-fed South Fork Mallard Creek. Cover, either aquatic vegetation or rubble, was important at all sites.

**Biogeography.** The authors interpret the species as a relict of a large [[Pleistocene Lake]] that once connected the Klamath basin, Fall River Valley and Big Valley. Other shared relict fishes include the marbled sculpin, [[Tui Chub]], Pit-Klamath brook lamprey and speckled dace.

**Threats.** Nonnative fish introductions (predation, hybridization), shoreline development (soil disturbance and siltation), upstream logging and farming, channelization or damming, and chemical treatment for unwanted fish species. Chemical treatment would kill resident sculpin directly and reduce their insect food base indirectly.

**Life history**
- Ambush predator feeding mainly on chironomid and ephemeropteran larvae; feeding peaks at dawn and dusk.
- Oviparous, low fecundity. Males guard nests under stones or logs, with up to 3,000 eggs; incubation about 2–3 weeks.
- Maximum lifespan about 5 years; reaches 30–35 mm standard length in its first season, then grows 10–16 mm per year.
- Most males and some females mature in their second year; all mature by the third.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution:** Historically confined to Fall River, lower Hat Creek and possibly the Pit River below the falls. In 1974 the species was recorded at 20 sites; the 1978 resurvey found no new sites, and population sizes at repeat sites appeared unchanged.
- **Abundance:** In the Fall River system the species comprised up to 80% of fish sampled. Lower Hat Creek held 30–50%, and all other Pit River sites under 10%.
- **Range:** The total range is approximately 80 stream km (50 miles), the basis of its rarity.
- **Sampling:** The 1,007 specimens collected in 1977–78 ranged from 6 mm to 81 mm standard length.
- **Status:** Designated biologically rare (not endangered or threatened) because of limited range and low density.
- **Essential habitat:** The entire Fall River system (excluding Bear Creek), lower Hat Creek below PG&E Powerhouse 2 and Crystal Lake, the tail of Britton Reservoir, and parts of the Pit River including Sucker Springs Creek and Tunnel Reservoir.
- **Recommendations for protection:**
  - Manage the middle Pit River as wild trout streams; prohibit stocking and live bait.
  - Limit shoreline development and protect watersheds.
  - Avoid chemical treatment; if unavoidable, relocate sculpin temporarily.
  - Study the effects of power boating on nesting near Big Lake and Horr Pond; restrict such boating until evaluated.
  - Publicize the species through public displays.
  - Consider land acquisition as private property enters the market.
  - Assess reservoirs downstream of Hat Creek, including Britton Reservoir.
  - Monitor annually in early summer by snorkel transects, reducing frequency to every 5 years after a 2-year baseline.
- **Research needs:** Study cottid interspecific relationships (electrophoresis, karyotyping), behavior, early life history and microhabitat requirements.
- **Conclusion:** The authors do not seek to expand the range or abundance; the aim is to prevent future listing as endangered or extinct.
