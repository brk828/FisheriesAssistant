# Predation by Ictalurid Catfishes as a Deterrent to Re-establishment of Hatchery-Reared Razorback Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study examines whether predation by two non-native [[Ictalurid Catfishes]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]], limits the re-establishment of hatchery-reared juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] stocked into the [[Gila River]] in eastern Arizona. Field work in autumn 1984 and 1985 and winter 1986 tracked dispersal and predation following [[Hatchery Stocking]] of juveniles into a 10-km reach. Estimated losses in a 2.5-km study reach reached up to 900 individuals/km in autumn within a 2-day post-stocking period. Predation was lower when the average size of stocked fish increased from 68 to 113 mm standard length (SL). Extrapolated winter losses were about one-sixth of autumn losses. The authors conclude that autumn juvenile stocking may be too costly for local re-establishment, and that cold-weather stocking of larger fish may improve [[Post-Stocking Survival]].

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is an imperiled fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Reintroduction into its historic range in Arizona has been ongoing since 1981, under an agreement between the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] and the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Region II). The authors note that predation by resident piscivores has repeatedly been implicated in the poor survival of stocked hatchery-reared fishes of several species, and that recent recovery plans for threatened or endangered fishes often recommend stocking to re-establish depleted populations.

The two predators are large, non-native, generalist piscivores now widespread across much of western North America:

- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*)
- [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*)

The authors argue that predation may be an acceptable cost in [[Sport Fishery Management]] programs, but that recovery efforts for endangered species must focus on methods to reduce these losses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The study area was a 10-km reach of the Gila River near Clifton, [[Greenlee County]], Arizona. Conditions recorded during the study were:

- Average stream width: 12 to 22 m
- Mean depth: 0.5 to 0.9 m, at discharges of 1.1 to 4.0 m³/s
- Mean current velocity: about 0.2 m/s during each site visit
- Substrate: mostly sand and gravel; coarser materials in shallow runs (0.5 m deep) and short riffles
- Deeper pools (locally over 1 m) occurred along canyon walls
- Aquatic vegetation was sparse, and riparian vegetation was largely absent along wetted margins

**Stocking events.** Approximately 5,000 hatchery-reared juveniles were released in aggregate in each of three events:

| Release | Date and time | Size (SL, mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 21 September 1984, 0600 h | Mean 113 ± 19 (n = 25 measured); full range partly illegible in scan |
| 2 | 13 September 1985, 0900 h | 45 to 142 mm, mean 68 ± 17 (n = 100) |
| 3 | 1986 (date partly illegible), 1200 h | 73 to 156 mm, mean 92 ± 19 (n = 83) |

The fish were released in aggregate rather than dispersed evenly, which allowed the authors to compare survival across size classes and seasons.

**Predator diet.** The authors examined stomach contents of the two catfish species. The diet categories included other fishes (including *Xyrauchen texanus* itself), aquatic insects (Ephemeroptera, Odonata, Hemiptera, Megaloptera, Trichoptera, Coleoptera, Diptera), terrestrial invertebrates, plant material, algae, detritus, and inorganic material. Numerical values in the diet table are too degraded in the scanned text to transcribe reliably, so the exact proportions are not reported here.

**Seasonal behaviour.** Channel catfish were reported not to feed actively in winter, which is consistent with the much lower winter predation rate.

## Key Findings

- **Heavy autumn predation.** Juvenile razorback suckers suffered intensive predation by channel and flathead catfish. Estimated losses in the 2.5-km study reach were up to 900 individuals/km over a 2-day post-stocking period in autumn.
- **Implication for stocking.** If losses are typical, predation may be high enough to preclude local re-establishment of the species using juvenile stockings made at that time of year.
- **Fish size matters.** Predation in autumn was lower when the average stocked size was increased from 68 to 113 mm SL.
- **Winter losses are lower.** Extrapolated total winter losses were about one-sixth of autumn losses, when channel catfish did not feed actively.
- **Recommended mitigation.** Cold-weather stocking of larger razorback suckers, around 300 mm long, may be one way to enhance post-stocking survival.
- **Broader management message.** Predation impacts may be tolerable in sport-fishery programs, but recovery programs for endangered fishes need methods to reduce predation losses.

**Limitations noted in the text:** Losses were extrapolated from short post-stocking windows, and the 300 mm recommendation is a suggestion from the authors rather than a tested treatment in this study.
