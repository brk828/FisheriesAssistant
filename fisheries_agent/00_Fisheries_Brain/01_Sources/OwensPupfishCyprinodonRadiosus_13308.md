# Recovery Plan for the Owens Pupfish, Cyprinodon radiosus

#ichthyology #cyprinodontidae #desert-fish #endangered-species #conservation

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan addresses the [[Owens Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon radiosus*), an endangered species endemic to [[Owens Valley]], California. Originally abundant throughout shallow marshy areas and springs along the [[Owens River]] from [[Fish Slough]] (Mono County) to [[Owens Lake]] (Inyo County), the species was believed extinct for 14 years until rediscovered in 1956. The plan establishes criteria for delisting: five populations, each with minimum overwintering population of 500 individuals, established in five separate areas, free of threats for five consecutive years with no foreseeable future threats. Currently three populations exist in two man-modified refugia: [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]] (OVNFS), [[BLM Spring]] in Fish Slough, and [[Warm Springs]]. Recovery requires securing existing populations, establishing three new refugia, controlling exotic species (particularly [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Crayfish]]), managing habitat vegetation, and ensuring water quality and quantity.

## Background & Description

### Taxonomy

*Cyprinodon radiosus* was originally misidentified as *C. macularius* (Kennedy 1916). Miller (1948) described it as a distinct species endemic to Owens Valley, with type locality at northwest spring of Fish Slough. The species shows closer relationships to pupfishes of the [[Amargosa River]] and [[Death Valley]] areas during the Pliocene than to *C. nevadensis* or *C. salinus* (Miller 1981).

### Morphology

The Owens pupfish is a small, deep-bodied, laterally compressed fish. Maximum total length reaches 60 mm (2 3/8 in), though most individuals measure 30-45 mm (1¼ to 1¾ in). Distinguishing characteristics include:

- Very anteriorly positioned dorsal fin (predorsal length averages 53.3% of standard length in males, 54.8% in females)
- Thickened first dorsal fin ray
- Higher average fin ray counts: dorsal (x̄ = 11.1), anal (x̄ = 10.4), pelvic (x̄ = 6.9)
- Slender head (averages 21.9% of SL)
- Large caudal peduncle (averages 28.6% of SL in males, 26.2% in females)
- Lack spine-like projections on scale circuli; interspaces possess prominent reticulations

### Sexual Dimorphism and Coloration

The species exhibits sexual dichromatism. Adult males display blue color overlying olive dorsally and slate ventrally, with deep purplish lateral bars and some gold among posterior bars. Lower head parts are silver and blue. Dorsal and anal fins are blue with orange-amber borders. Females show deep olive coloration with brown lateral blotches and vertical bars shading toward purplish, yellowish fins with pale margins on dorsal and anal, and an ocellus on posterior portion of dorsal fin. Males possess deeper body, head, and caudal peduncle than females, with longer caudal peduncle and larger fins, particularly anal.

### Reproductive Biology

Males are territorial and larger than females. Spawning season extends April to October, beginning when water temperature exceeds 20°C. Territorial males become bright blue during spawning and defend territories from other male pupfish and all other fish species. Females entering male territories are courted. Each female spawns 50-200 eggs per season, rarely laying more than one or two eggs at a time (Barlow 1961). Eggs are transparent, demersal, approximately 2 mm diameter, hatching in 7-10 days depending on water temperature. Larvae are large enough to feed on small food items (brine shrimp nauplii size) from hatching day (Crear and Haydock 1971). Fry inhabit shorelines where warm water supports abundant food. Fish mature in 3-4 months, permitting one spawning prior to first overwintering period.

### Feeding Ecology

Owens pupfish are omnivorous opportunists feeding on algae, aquatic insects, crustaceans, plankton, terrestrial insects falling into water, and even their own dead (Bunnell 1970). Kennedy (1916) analyzed stomach contents of 14 pupfish, finding primary food item (90%) was [[Chironomid Larvae]]. The species is not piscivorous but predacious on their own eggs. Small size and tolerance to temperature and salinity extremes permit foraging in habitats too hostile for most other fish species, typically nursery grounds for insects providing excellent food supply unavailable to other native adult fish species. [[Mosquitofish]] can effectively compete in these shallow areas. Male pupfish can harass other species to death if they trespass into established territories (Hiscox 1978).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Requirements

Basic habitat requirements consist of small amounts of good quality water, some vegetation, and silt- or sand-covered bottom. Historically, pupfish were collected near margins of large tule marshes in wide, shallow (8-15 cm) sloughs with abundant aquatic vegetation and in springs along the Owens River (Miller and Pister 1971). Typical habitat includes shallows with sparse [[Cattails]] (*Typha*) and [[Bulrush]] (*Scirpus*) and sand-silt-detritus bottom. These shallows associate with relatively deeper water where adult pupfish establish territories and spawn. Dense tule stands exclude pupfish, but shallow areas adjacent to Owens River probably never became densely vegetated due to annual flooding and drying cycles.

### Historical Distribution and Decline

Owens pupfish originally occurred in Owens River and adjacent springs and sloughs from springs at Fish Slough (Mono County) south to, but not in, Owens Lake (Inyo County), and in springs around the lake. Miller (1948) and Miller and Pister (1971) described progressive decline from 1916 through 1941. In 1934 survey, Carl L. Hubbs found only few fish in pools along big bend areas of Owens River northeast of Bishop where Kennedy observed large schools in 1915. The Hubbs party found no Owens pupfish along Owens River between Laws and Bishop. During 1930s, Fish Slough springs provided last refugium. By 1937, Fish Slough was primarily inhabited by [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) and largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*). Surveys in 1942 failed to find any Owens pupfish; species was presumed extinct until 1956 when California Department of Fish and Game personnel discovered small population in shallow ponds along east side of Fish Slough. This population was largely forgotten until "rediscovery" in 1964, when population was estimated at only 200 individuals.

### Endemic Fish Fauna

In addition to pupfish, [[Owens Sucker]] (*Catostomus fumeiventris*), [[Owens Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor snyderi*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus* spp.) are endemic to Owens Basin. Owens sucker has remained common, but Owens tui chub has declined substantially and was proposed for endangered status in 1984 (Federal Register 45:10959-10962). One or more local forms of speckled dace appear to have been extirpated, though taxonomy is uncertain.

### Causes of Decline

Shallow marshy areas adjacent to Owens River were destroyed as upstream impoundments and diversions prevented normal flooding cycles. Dams and canals diverted most Owens River water into [[Los Angeles Aqueduct]] and out of Owens Valley. Other dams and diversion structures distributed water for agricultural use within valley. These impoundments and diversions drastically altered pupfish habitat.

Introduction of exotic species contributed to decline. Largemouth bass, [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*), and other exotics were introduced. Largemouth bass, brown trout, and crayfish (*Procambarus clarki*) are predators on Owens pupfish. Mosquitofish probably compete for food and space and may predate on younger individuals. Crayfish are effective predators on pupfish eggs.

Currently pupfish are not secure in any three refugia. Each refugium is vulnerable to disruption from various land uses. Groundwater pumping from agriculture could threaten aquifers supplying water to remaining springs. Largemouth bass, mosquitofish, and crayfish have been planted in refugia by individuals or agencies unaware of or unconcerned with pupfish plight. Vandals have destroyed habitat or structures built to create and maintain habitats.

### Current Refugia Status

As of July 1984, three Owens pupfish populations exist in two man-modified refugia:

**Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary (OVNFS)**: During 1968-1969, California Department of Fish and Game and Los Angeles Department of Water and Power created 8.5 hectare (21 acre) fenced sanctuary in Fish Slough containing 2.3 hectare (5.6 acre) pond. Officially declared Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary by Fish and Game Commission in 1970. Provides protected habitat for Owens Valley form of speckled dace, Owens tui chub, Owens sucker, and Owens pupfish. Although designed to prevent exotic fish invasion, largemouth bass have been introduced above fish barriers and invaded slough, preying extensively on pupfish and greatly reducing numbers. In 1980, second dam was added 200 m below original structure, creating second impoundment.

**BLM Spring**: Located in southeast portion of Fish Slough. Second dam downstream from original structure was rebuilt October 1981. Two shallow pools were created on either side of stream channel upstream of dam to provide pupfish habitat.

**Warm Springs Refugium**: Small pond formed by single small dam, fed by thermal spring. Located on Department of Water and Power property north of Big Pine adjacent to [[White Mountains]]. Has been free from introduced fish predators; however, crayfish and mosquitofish have required control measures to protect pupfish population. Mosquitofish were successfully eradicated in 1979 but have sporadically reappeared, presumably through illegal introductions.

### Fish Slough Ecological Significance

Fish Slough is unique wetland in arid environment. University of California seeks to include Fish Slough area in its Land and Water Reserves System as Ecological Study Area. Uniqueness extends beyond Owens pupfish. [[Fish Slough Milk Vetch]] (*Astragalus lentiginosus* var. *piscinensis*) is known only from Fish Slough. Several other rare plant species or disjunct populations occur: *Calochortus excavatus*, *Centaurium namophilum* var. *nevadensis*, *Fimbristylis thermalis*, *Spartina gracilis*, *Dodecatheon pulchellum*, and *Astragalus argophyllus* var. *argophyllus*. Undescribed mollusk species, [[Fish Slough Snail]] (*Fontelicella* sp.), occurs in OVNFS spring outflow and Northeast Spring. Bureau of Land Management has proposed Area of Critical Environmental Concern status for 35,926 acres encompassing Fish Slough and surrounding hydrologic basin. In conjunction with U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, California Department of Fish and Game, University of California, and Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, BLM is preparing management plan for Fish Slough area.

In December 1982, U.S. Congress passed HR 2475, providing for 82 hectare (202 acre) land exchange between private party and BLM, placing last parcel of private land within Fish Slough into public ownership, making maintenance of Fish Slough integrity attainable.

### Water Quality

Present refugia provide warm water of good quality (Courtois and Tippets 1979). Natural fluoride levels of 1.5 mg/l at BLM Springs may be potential problem as this is at reported maximum acceptable fluoride level for aquatic life (McKee and Wolf 1963). Sufficient habitat for recovery can be created and managed if there is adequate water and protection from exotic fishes.

## Key Findings

### Recovery Objectives and Criteria

**Primary Objective**: Improve status to permit removal from List of Endangered and Threatened Wildlife. Delisting can occur when:
- Five populations established in five separate areas
- Each population maintains minimum overwintering population of 500 individuals
- Populations have remained free from threats for at least five consecutive years
- No foreseeable threats in future

**Interim Objective**: Restore to threatened status by:
- Expanding and assuring preservation of existing populations
- Establishing three additional populations
- Minimizing threats to refugia

### Management Actions Required

**For Existing Refugia (OVNFS, BLM Spring, Warm Springs)**:

1. **Exotic Species Management**:
   - Eliminate exotic fishes (largemouth bass can be concentrated during drawdown and selectively removed)
   - Control bullfrogs (predators on juvenile and adult pupfish)
   - Control crayfish through trapping, seining, or chemicals to reduce egg predation
   - Prevent reintroduction through informational signs and fencing between outflow and lower impoundment waters

2. **Habitat Management**:
   - Prepare multiagency management plan emphasizing protection and restoration of natural/semi-natural habitats
   - Control submergent and emergent vegetation (drawdown facilitates control)
   - Maintain dams and outflow structures to provide proper water levels
   - Reduce or eliminate grazing and fishing near refugia (grazing eliminates marsh vegetation and causes water pollution; fishing leads to centrarchid introductions)

3. **Warm Springs Specific Actions**:
   - Manage crayfish as required
   - Control mosquitofish when they appear
   - Contact local mosquito abatement office to prevent reintroduction
   - Control cattails and bulrush to prevent pool encroachment
   - Install cage-like structure over outflow to prevent vandalism
   - Redesign outflow to prevent clogging
   - Install suitable fence with stiles or walk-ways to reduce vandalism while allowing access

### New Refugia Establishment

**River Spring Lakes (Adobe Valley)**: Located approximately 10 miles northwest of Benton (T1N, R30E, Sec. 24; T1N, R31E, Sec. 19). Outside Owens Valley but nearby and historically devoid of fish. Actions required:
- Eliminate [[Amargosa Pupfish]] (*C. nevadensis amargosae*) population (not threatened in native range; must be removed to prevent hybridization)
- Eliminate other exotic species threatening Owens pupfish
- Control vegetation simultaneously with exotic pupfish treatment
- Develop management plan for habitat improvement and maintenance
- Evaluate and acquire water source and property rights (part on private land; requires agreement or conservation easement)
- Introduce Owens pupfish after exotic elimination and management plan approval
- Requires Director of Fish and Wildlife Service approval for introduction outside historic range

**Additional Potential Sites**:
- [[Little Lake]] (south of Owens Lake, T23S, R38E, Sec. 17, 18, 20): Already owned by California Department of Fish and Game, within presumed historic range
- Springs and seeps around periphery of Owens Lake
- [[Black Lake]] (Adobe Valley, T1S, R31E, Sec. 2, 32; T2S, R31E, Sec. 5): Outside presumed historic range
- [[Antelope Spring]] (Adobe Valley, T1S, R31E, Sec. 8)

**Site Evaluation Requirements**:
- Evaluate water quality (chemical and physical properties)
- Evaluate physical habitat suitability
- Evaluate potential impact on native invertebrates
- Evaluate habitat protection options regarding property ownership and water rights
- Evaluate extent and long-term security of source aquifer
- Assure protection of property and water rights
- Prepare and implement management plan addressing habitat modification and maintenance
- Select transplant stock from refugia most similar in water quality

### Monitoring Program

Each population must be periodically monitored twice annually:
- **Population size**: Census early spring (overwintering level) and near end of summer (peak population size)
- **Reproductive success**: Assess ability to successfully reproduce through several seasons as indicator of population viability and transplant success
- **Habitat condition**: Monitor for factors harmful to Owens pupfish

### Research Needs

Refugia provide opportunities for ecological studies. Basic biology aspects are unknown or based on laboratory studies. Priority research:

1. **Habitat requirements**: Determine substrate and vegetation requirements for feeding, spawning, territory establishment and survival
2. **Mosquitofish interactions**: Determine interactions between Owens pupfish and mosquitofish due to apparent similarity in foods and habitat
3. **Mosquito control suitability**: Determine suitability of Owens pupfish as mosquito control agent within Owens Valley (use beyond historic range should not be considered)

### Law Enforcement and Public Education

**Enforcement**:
- Enforce provisions of Endangered Species Act of 1973 (as amended) and other applicable laws and regulations
- Provide enforcement personnel from all agencies with maps denoting refugia locations and access points
- Make enforcement personnel and land managers aware of activities detrimental to pupfish and habitat
- Review adequacy of existing regulations and enforcement every two years; recommend needed changes

**Public Education**:
- Provide media coverage of recovery events through press releases (well-written, factual, educational)
- Prepare and distribute informative brochure discussing pupfish plight
- Prepare articles for popular magazines describing pupfish plight
- Make research results available to scientific and lay communities
- Install information signs at all refugia on public lands (serves as public education tool and deterrent against vandalism and exotic species introduction)
