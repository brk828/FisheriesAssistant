# Petition to List the Huachuca Water Umbell as Endangered (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cienega-ecosystem #freshwater-wetlands #Apiaceae #ConservationBiology

> **Note:** This source document is a botanical/ecological ESA petition centered on a semi-aquatic plant (*Lilaeopsis schaffneriana* subsp. *recurva*), not a fish species per se. However, it provides critical habitat and ecosystem context for freshwater fish species — notably [[Gila Chub]] — that co-occur in the same imperiled cienega habitats. This note synthesizes the aquatic/wetland ecological data as presented.

## Executive Summary

This 1993 petition, filed by the Greater Gila Biodiversity Project and co-petitioners, sought Endangered Species Act protection for the **Huachuca Water Umbell** (*Lilaeopsis schaffneriana* subsp. *recurva*), a rare semi-aquatic plant restricted to **cienega** (desert wetland/marsh) habitats in southeastern Arizona and adjacent Sonora, Mexico. The petition documents severe historical decline — from an estimated 15+ historic locations to only **10 remaining populations** (7 in the U.S., 3 in Mexico), most of which are unstable or in decline. The document is significant to aquatic/ichthyological interests because it identifies the plant as an **indicator species** for cienega ecosystem health, a habitat type shared with several imperiled aquatic vertebrates, most notably the **[[Gila Chub]]** (*Gila intermedia*).

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy**: *Lilaeopsis schaffneriana* subsp. *recurva*, family Umbelliferae (Apiaceae). First described as a full species by Hill (1927); reclassified as a subspecies by Affolter (1985).
- **Morphology**: A perennial, semi-aquatic herb with hollow, pale-green, septate leaves (3–20 cm long, 1–3 mm diameter) growing from shallow, mat-forming white rhizomes. Produces small umbels (3–10 flowers) with maroon-tinted petals and globose fruits (1.5–2 mm diameter).
- **Reproduction**: Flowering primarily observed in April (peak) with low-frequency flowering March–October; fruiting May–September. Reproduces both via seed germination and vegetative rhizome spreading.
- **Distribution**: Historically ranged from Tucson south to Cananea, Sonora, and from Sonoita Creek east to the Rio San Bernardino watershed. A disjunct sister subspecies (*L. schaffneriana* subsp. *schaffneriana*) occurs on the central Mexican plateau, separated by the continental divide — effectively isolating the two populations' main dispersal vector (water flow).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat: The Cienega Ecosystem
- *Lilaeopsis* is an **obligate cienega dweller**, found in wet soils and shallow, slow-moving perennial waters, generally between **4,000–6,500 ft elevation**.
- **Mid-elevation cienegas** (flatter valleys, wide canyons with reduced flood scouring) support the most stable populations. Populations at low elevations (<3,000 ft) and select high-elevation sites have been extirpated or remain marginal due to susceptibility to catastrophic, human-exacerbated flooding.
- Cienegas are characterized as among **North America's most endangered habitat types**, historically degraded by overgrazing, water diversion, groundwater pumping, pond dredging, and dam construction. A 1987 Arizona Nature Conservancy survey found that historic cienega sites had declined by an estimated **70%**.

### Indicator Species Role & Co-occurring Aquatic Fauna
Per Warren et al. (1991), *Lilaeopsis* is considered a **bioindicator** for cienega community health, given its sensitivity to hydrologic disturbance. It co-occurs with, and its condition is correlated to habitat suitability for, several other sensitive cienega-dependent species:

| Species | Common Name | Taxon |
|---|---|---|
| *Gila intermedia* | [[Gila Chub]] | Fish (Cyprinidae) |
| *Pyrgulopsis thompsonii* | Huachuca Springsnail | Mollusk |
| *Ambystoma tigrinum stebbinsi* | Huachuca/Sonora Tiger Salamander | Amphibian |
| *Rana chiricahuensis* | Chiricahua Leopard Frog | Amphibian |
| *Thamnophis eques* | Mexican Garter Snake | Reptile |
| *Spiranthes delitescens* | Canelo Lady's Tresses Orchid | Plant |

All seven associated species were, at time of writing, either federal listing candidates, subjects of separate ESA petitions, or state-listed as threatened/endangered in Arizona — underscoring the cienega habitat's broader significance to imperiled aquatic and semi-aquatic biodiversity, including freshwater fish conservation in the [[Greater Gila Ecosystem]].

### Threats to Habitat
- **Water diversions, pond dredging, and groundwater pumping** identified as primary drivers of habitat loss (directly relevant to fish habitat connectivity and stream permanence).
- **Urban growth** (e.g., Cananea, Sierra Vista) projected to further strain water supplies in the Santa Cruz and San Pedro basins.
- **Altered flood regimes** — cattle grazing and dam construction have intensified flood scouring beyond natural historic levels, degrading cienega/stream habitat structure relevant to both plant and fish survival.
- **Competition from invasive/aggressive vegetation**: *Sorghum halepense*, *Equisetum* spp., *Eleocharis*, and other graminoids threaten habitat structure.

## Key Findings

1. **Population status**: Only 10 populations of *Lilaeopsis* remain (7 U.S., 3 Mexico); **6 of 10 are classified as unstable**, and all 3 Mexican populations are unstable.
2. **Historical extirpation**: At least 4 populations have been extirpated — 1 before 1953, 2 since 1965, and 1 in 1988 — largely due to dredging, flooding, and stream channel loss (e.g., Santa Cruz River, San Pedro River, Zinn Pond, Rio San Bernardino).
3. **Watershed breakdown**:
   - **Santa Cruz Watershed**: 4 historic populations, 2 extirpated (Santa Cruz River at Tucson, Monkey Spring), 1 unstable (Cottonwood Spring/Sonoita Creek), 1 stable (San Rafael Valley springs).
   - **San Pedro Watershed**: 7 populations; 2 extirpated (Zinn Pond, San Pedro River proper); several in Huachuca Mountains (Garden Canyon, Scotia Canyon, Bear Canyon) remain stable; Turkey Creek unstable.
   - **Rio San Bernardino Watershed**: Black Draw (USFWS, San Bernardino NWR) unstable; Rio San Bernardino, Mexico, extirpated following 1988 flood.
   - **Rio Sonora Watershed**: Ojo de Agua de Cananea population unstable, threatened by domestic water extraction for the town of Cananea.
4. **Flood dynamics**: Population resilience is tied to **flood intensity and frequency** — species requires *intermediate* periodic flooding to suppress competing vegetation but cannot tolerate scouring floods, which have increased in frequency/intensity due to anthropogenic watershed alteration (grazing, damming).
5. **Regulatory status at time of petition**: Listed only as a Category 2 (candidate) species by USFWS (no ESA protection) and as "Sensitive" on Coronado National Forest (USFS) without a management plan; no protections existed on Fort Huachuca Military Reservation populations or under Arizona state law.
6. **Petition request**: Formal request for federal endangered listing under the ESA (16 U.S.C. 1531 et seq.) and concurrent designation of **Critical Habitat** encompassing both occupied sites and unoccupied areas suitable for restoration.
