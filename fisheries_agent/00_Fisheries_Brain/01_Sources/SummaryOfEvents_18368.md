# Colorado River Delta-Upper Gulf of California Workshop Summary

#marine-biology #conservation #ecosystem-restoration #binational-cooperation #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This document summarizes a binational workshop held October 30-31, 1997, in Mexicali, Baja California, Mexico, focused on the ecological and social restoration of the [[Colorado River Delta]] and [[Upper Gulf of California]]. Organized by the [[Pacific Institute]], [[International Sonoran Desert Alliance]], and [[Defenders of Wildlife]], the workshop brought together U.S. and Mexican NGOs, academic researchers, and government representatives to address water management, habitat restoration, fisheries decline, and indigenous community impacts. Key presentations covered current restoration activities in the [[Rio Hardy]] region, connections between river flows and upper gulf fisheries, disturbance regime modeling for ecological restoration, and the cultural history of the [[Cucapa]] indigenous peoples. The workshop aimed to strengthen binational networks, coordinate conservation strategies, and develop action plans for delta restoration.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Delta]] historically encompassed approximately 1,000,000 hectares of wetlands but has been reduced to approximately 60,000 hectares due to upstream water diversions and dam construction. The workshop addressed the ecological consequences of reduced freshwater flows to the delta and [[Upper Gulf of California]], including impacts on commercial fisheries, wetland habitats, and indigenous communities.

The [[Upper Gulf of California and Colorado River Delta Biosphere Reserve]] was established by Mexican presidential decree on June 10, 1993, in response to concerns from fishermen about declining shrimp and fish catches. The Reserve aims to regulate fishing activities and promote ecosystem restoration through research and management.

Workshop presentations highlighted several key ecological relationships:

- **Wetland Restoration**: Carlos Valdes Casillas ([[ITESM Guaymas]]) and Ed Glenn ([[University of Arizona]]) reported on restoration activities in the [[Rio Hardy]] region, noting that 60,000 hectares of wetlands currently exist in the delta, with 200,000 hectares remaining undeveloped. Significant riparian habitat including [[Cottonwood]] and [[Willow]] has regenerated along the lower Colorado River channel due to flood control releases from the U.S.

- **Fisheries-Flow Connections**: Salvador Galindo-Bect ([[Instituto de Investigaciones Oceanologicas]], [[UABC]]) presented research demonstrating correlations between Colorado River flows and commercial fishing success in the upper gulf. His work focused on breeding areas within the Biosphere Reserve and the formation of salinity barriers between saltwater and fresh-brackish water areas that historically protected breeding zones from predators. During periods of no freshwater flow, this barrier breaks down, potentially increasing predation on juvenile fish and shrimp. Charts showed direct correlations between fishing production and [[El Niño]] events, with the greatest fishing in San Felipe's history occurring during high-water years.

- **Disturbance Ecology**: Bill Fagan ([[Arizona State University]]) presented modeling work on the Colorado River's historical disturbance regime. Tree ring studies covering 450 years revealed at least three flood events as large or larger than the 1907 flood, with some years experiencing flows as low as 5 million acre-feet. The Colorado River historically discharged 6-7% of total annual flow in a seven-day period, compared to 1-3% for the Missouri and Ohio Rivers, demonstrating extreme variability. Fagan proposed that the delta served as a critical refuge and source of colonists for restocking the ecosystem after major flood disturbances, with the delta region being 2.5 times the size of the entire lower Colorado River riparian corridor.

- **Cultural Impacts**: Anita Williams ([[University of Baja California Museum]]) documented the history of the [[Cucapa]] indigenous peoples, whose population declined from approximately 5,000 in the mid-1800s to fewer than 200 currently living in the delta. The loss of water and associated habitat that these communities depended upon for food is identified as the most significant cause of their decline.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The workshop identified multiple ecological and environmental challenges facing the [[Colorado River Delta]] and [[Upper Gulf of California]]:

**Water Quantity and Quality**: Reduced freshwater flows have resulted in increased salinity levels in the delta, with the delta portion of the river experiencing higher salinity than the ocean itself. Water quality concerns include the introduction of toxics, pesticides, and other pollutants that may affect metabolic systems of organisms, though more studies are needed.

**Habitat Loss**: The reduction from 1,000,000 hectares to 60,000 hectares of wetlands represents a 94% loss of historical wetland habitat. Loss of sediment delivery with low flows has led to increased delta erosion and changes in vegetation abundance.

**Fisheries Decline**: Commercial fishing and shrimp harvesting have declined significantly, correlated with reduced freshwater flows. During high-flow periods, shrimp had larger breeding areas leading to higher survival rates and increased fishing success. The breakdown of salinity barriers during low-flow periods allows predators to access breeding areas.

**Species Adaptations**: Historical Colorado River species evolved as "disturbance dependent" organisms with high reproductive capacity, dispersal ability, and resilience to periodic flooding. Many species require renewal of young [[Willow]] and [[Cottonwood]] habitat and were dependent on disturbance events. The current regulated flow regime has eliminated most natural flooding, disrupting these evolutionary adaptations.

**Binational Management Challenges**: The [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (MSCP) was initiated by U.S. states and water users concerned about future water availability under [[Endangered Species Act]] requirements. The 50-year conservation plan focuses on U.S. endangered species and does not include measures to protect listed species and habitat south of the border. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] has taken the position that they have no legal discretion to consider impacts of their operations in Mexico.

## Key Findings

**Workshop Objectives and Outcomes**:

The workshop aimed to identify regional problems, develop connections among various interests, strengthen binational networks, and develop coordinated strategies for delta restoration. Participants included representatives from U.S. and Mexican NGOs, academic institutions, government agencies, and indigenous communities.

**Scientific Research Priorities**:

Salvador Galindo-Bect identified the need for long-term analysis to determine:
- How much freshwater is needed to sustain upper gulf fisheries
- Impacts of toxics, pesticides, and pollutants on the food web
- Biological considerations including recruitment, vegetation, and predation
- Chemical characterization of habitat and contaminant diagnosis
- Geological assessment of habitat availability versus habitat lost
- Effects of hydrology on sediments
- Historic conditions before [[Hoover Dam]] construction

**Restoration Opportunities**:

Current restoration activities focus on the [[Rio Hardy]] wetlands and the nucleus zone of the [[Biosphere Reserve]]. Dredging in May removed silt and sediment that had hindered water entrance to the area, though the zone is now dry and habitat has been lost. Potential water sources for restoration include effluent from the upgraded Mexicali I wastewater treatment plant and proposed Mexicali II facility, expected to be approved by [[BECC]] and financed by [[NADBANK]].

**Fishermen Survey Results**:

A survey of local fishermen by Peggy Turk-Boyer ([[CEDO]]) revealed:
- Main management suggestions: stricter fishing seasons and better management of shrimp trawlers
- Release of freshwater into the gulf viewed as very important, especially by San Felipe and el Golfo fishermen
- 90% of San Felipe fishermen agreed sustainability is viable
- 60% of el Golfo and Puerto Penasco fishermen thought sustainability viable
- 90% of Puerto Penasco fishermen willing to participate on interdisciplinary problem-solving council
- 60% of el Golfo and 30% of San Felipe fishermen interested in participating

**Long-term Goals Identified**:

- Identify social and economic benefits of bringing more water to the delta-upper gulf
- Establish water management-conservation ethic in northern Mexico
- Enhance opportunities for [[Cucapa]] fishing
- Examine organic farming opportunities
- Develop better awareness in U.S. of delta concerns and needs
- Explore need for new institutional mechanisms
- Analyze water quality in the region
- Develop more efficient water use systems and different use scenarios
- Expand interests involved in water management decisions
- Explore ways for releasing more freshwater to delta-upper gulf
- Give priority to protecting existing reserves
- Develop water allocations for wildlife and habitat restoration
- Provide adequate water for human needs
- Develop environmental education programs
- Promote ecotourism in the delta
- Educate media on delta issues

**Short-term Action Items**:

- Participate in [[BECC]] process for Mexicali wastewater treatment plant
- Review impact of [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] on Mexico
- Develop options for [[Yuma Desalter]] to assure continuous water supply for [[Cienega de Santa Clara]]
- Produce and disseminate minutes of Puerto Penasco meeting
- Investigate creation of website and listserve for information sharing
- Organize media tour of delta region
- Write letter to government agencies emphasizing that Colorado River system is one ecosystem requiring integrated management
- Mexican NGOs to express concern that entire ecosystem be considered in MSCP planning
- Participate in proposed spring tour of delta region by MSCP Steering Committee

**Institutional Coordination**:

The [[Department of Interior]] (DOI) and [[Secretariat of Environment, Natural Resources, and Fisheries]] (SEMARNAP) signed a Letter of Intent on May 5, 1997, focusing on binational cooperation and data collection for managing protected natural areas in the border region. The [[Delta-Upper Gulf Biosphere Reserve]] was identified as a pilot project area. An implementation committee of agencies and NGOs was established, with [[Sonoran Institute]] and [[International Sonoran Desert Alliance]] volunteering as lead NGOs.

**Water Management Issues**:

The [[Yuma Desalination Plant]] was discussed as potentially providing better quality water for portions of Mexico, though concerns exist about resulting loss of supply for the [[Cienega de Santa Clara]]. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] indicated the plant is not economically viable to operate, sufficient water is available to meet U.S. commitments to Mexico, and no plans exist to run the plant in the foreseeable future. Participants reached consensus on investigating a position paper regarding the Yuma desalter incorporating water issues of the Cienega and boundary concerns.

The workshop concluded with plans for a follow-up meeting in five or six months, potentially coinciding with the next planned MSCP tour in April or May 1998.
