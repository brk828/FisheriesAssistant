# Molecular Genetic Identification of a Mexican Onza Specimen as a Puma (Puma concolor)

#mammalogy #felidae #puma-concolor

## Executive Summary

This 1996 paper reports molecular genetic tests on tissue from an alleged Mexican [[Onza]], a large cat shot in the western [[Sierra Madre]] of [[Sinaloa]] in 1986. The authors compared protein markers ([[Isoenzyme Electrophoresis]] and [[Albumin]] [[Isoelectric Focusing]]) and [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) restriction fragments, plus a sequence of the [[ND5 Gene]], against nine other cat species. The Onza matched North American [[Puma Concolor]] on every test. The authors conclude that the 1986 specimen was a puma and not a distinct, new, or relict species.

## Background & Description

- **Species in the Americas:** The introduction recognizes two documented large cats: *Puma concolor* (known as puma, mountain lion, cougar, panther, or leon in Mexico) and *Panthera onca* ([[Jaguar]], or tigre south of the U.S. border).
- **Local tradition:** Locals in the western Sierra Madre speak of three big cats: leones, tigres, and onzas.
- **Historical references:**
  - In 1519, [[Bernal Diaz Del Castillo]], a member of Cortez's army, described "tigers" and two kinds of "lions" at Montezuma's zoo.
  - 18th-century Jesuit missionaries described a cat longer and leaner than the puma.
  - Modern hunters describe the Onza as having a puma-like coat, a leaner body, longer legs, and an aggressive nature.
  - Interest was revived in the 1960s by [[Robert Marshall]]'s 1961 book on the Onza.
- **The 1986 specimen:** On January 1, 1986, rancher Andres Rodriguez Murillo shot a cat at night in Sinaloa. He first thought it was a jaguar, but it seemed different from a puma. Neighbor Manuel Vega identified it as an Onza, and it was placed in fisheries cold storage in Mazatlan. The paper describes it as the first complete supposed Onza specimen collected in over a decade.
- **Examination:** In February 1986, [[Richard Greenwell]], University of New Mexico mammalogist [[Troy Best]], and graduate student Ned Gentz examined the animal at the Regional Diagnostic Laboratory of Animal Pathology (Mexican Ministry of Agriculture). Tissue samples were taken to the United States.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Range:** The paper places the alleged Onza in the western Sierra Madre of Mexico, where local people report three big cat types.
- **Competing hypotheses on the Onza's identity (from Greenwell 1987):**
  1. A new species closely related to the puma.
  2. A puma subspecies distinct from the described regional subspecies *Puma concolor azteca*.
  3. A puma × jaguar hybrid.
  4. A relict population of the Pleistocene North American cheetah *Acinonyx trumani* (proposed by Helmut Hemmer; based on paleontologist Daniel Adams's work, and believed extinct).
  5. A puma adapted to the western Sierra Madre environment, or possibly an emaciated puma.
- **Relevance of mtDNA:** The paper notes that mtDNA is a cytoplasmic chromosome of about 17,000 nucleotides, used in forensic and genetic identification. Mitochondrial DNA is maternally inherited, so a puma-female × jaguar-male hybrid could not be ruled out by mtDNA alone.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- **Samples:** Frozen liver, heart, and blood tissue from the specimen. DNA was extracted from the heart (Sambrook 1989).
- **mtDNA restriction fragment analysis:** The Onza was compared with nine cat species: lion, tiger, leopard, snow leopard, clouded leopard, jaguar, puma, cheetah, and bobcat. Several individuals per species were used (n = 2-10). Twenty-eight restriction enzymes were applied, with southern blots hybridized to a radioactively labelled domestic cat mtDNA clone.
- **ND5 sequencing:** The mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase subunit-5 gene (ND5) was amplified by [[Polymerase Chain Reaction]] for 30 cycles, using 2.0 mM MgCl2 and a 49°C annealing temperature. Sequencing used the PRISM dye primer procedure on an ABI 373A DNA Sequencer.
- **Protein analysis:** Isoenzyme electrophoresis, and isoelectric focusing of tissue homogenates on pH 5-6 gradient gels, followed by Western blotting with anti-bovine serum albumin.

**Results**

- **mtDNA fragment counts:** Each felid species yielded between 120 and 158 mtDNA restriction fragments.
- **Table 1: Fragments shared with the Onza**

| Comparison | Total fragments scored | Fragments shared | Percent shared |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onza : cheetah | 120 | 34 | 28.3% |
| Onza : jaguar | 135 | 24 | 17.7% |
| Onza : puma | 122 | 122 | 100.0% |
| lion : leopard | 148 | 54 | 36.4% |
| lion : puma | 131 | 28 | 21.3% |
| lion : jaguar | 155 | 36 | 23.2% |
| lion : tiger | 158 | 52 | 32.9% |
| tiger : snow leopard | 143 | 60 | 41.9% |

- **Puma identity:** The Onza's mtDNA restriction pattern was identical to that of four North American pumas in the study. The next-closest pair was tiger : snow leopard (41.9%).
- **ND5 sequence (245 bp):** Sequences came from the Onza, more than 50 western North American pumas (including two from southern Mexico), one jaguar, and one cheetah. All western North American puma sequences were identical.
  - Onza vs. puma: 100% identical.
  - Onza vs. cheetah: 87% similar.
  - Onza vs. jaguar: 83% similar.
  - Cheetah vs. jaguar: 85% similar.
- **Hybrid check (nuclear markers):**
  - Texas Tech University compared 19 isoenzymes and found no differences between the Onza and west Texas pumas (Richard Greenwell, personal communication).
  - An earlier National Cancer Institute isozyme study had found mobility differences at 16 isoenzymes between Texas pumas and jaguars. Hybrid protein products would therefore have been detected.
- **Albumin isoelectric focusing:**
  - The Onza's banding pattern was consistent with 40 puma standards and inconsistent with five jaguar and three cheetah standards.
  - Its albumin isoelectric point differed from all other cats tested: lion, tiger, snow leopard, clouded leopard, Geoffroy's cat, jaguarundi, serval, fishing cat, lynx, bobcat, and domestic cat.

**Discussion and Conclusions**

- **Hypothesis 4 eliminated:** Greenwell's examination of *Acinonyx trumani* skulls showed that the 1986 Onza was not a Pleistocene cheetah.
- **Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3 eliminated:** Albumin and isoenzyme data showed no difference from North American pumas and no evidence of hybridization. mtDNA identity also eliminated hypotheses 1 and 2.
- **Supported hypothesis 5:** The authors conclude that the 1986 Onza specimen was a puma, "and did not represent a distinct, new, or relict species."
