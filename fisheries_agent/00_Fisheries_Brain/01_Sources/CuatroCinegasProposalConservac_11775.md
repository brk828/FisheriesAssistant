# Conservación y Ecología de Terrapene coahuila: Propuesta de Manejo de una Especie Amenazada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #emydidae

*(Note: the source concerns a freshwater turtle, so the taxon tag is #emydidae and the fish/marine tags reflect the template; the content is herpetological and freshwater.)*

## Executive Summary

This Spanish-language management proposal, prepared by biologists Francisco Soberón Mobarak and Rolando Mendoza Trejo, addresses the conservation of the endemic aquatic box turtle [[Terrapene Coahuila]] (Bisagrita) in the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], Coahuila. The species is poorly known, is judged to be under pressure from agricultural development and commercial collection, and its status in formerly abundant populations is unknown. The project proposes a two-year field program combining population census, natural history studies and conservation actions, including protected areas, captive breeding and reintroduction, with a budget of US$42,800 for the first year and about US$36,075 for the second.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** The Cuatro Ciénegas valley (roughly 30 × 40 km, planicie near 740 m a.s.l., surrounding ranges up to 2,500 m) lies in the [[Chihuahuan Desert]], with annual precipitation below 200 mm and summer temperatures up to 44 °C.
- **Aquatic setting:** Ancient, long-lived spring-fed water bodies range from fast-flowing rivers to marshes, lagoons and canals. Isolation has driven explosive adaptive radiation, high speciation and high endemism.
- **Taxon:** [[Terrapene Coahuila]] belongs to Emydidae and is a secondarily aquatic box turtle. Unlike typical aquatic turtles (e.g., [[Trionyx]] or [[Pseudemys]]), it lacks a flat profile and interdigital webbing. McCoy called it an "adaptive paradox." Most *Terrapene* species are terrestrial woodland turtles; *T. nelsoni* of Sonora is the exception, living in tropical thorn scrub.
- **Related turtles:** The endemic *Trionyx ater* (Tortuga negra) is similar in habits to *Trionyx emoryi*, which is widely distributed in northern Mexico.
- **Research history:** E.G. Marsh's 1939 expedition provided the type series. The University of Kansas began intensive work in 1958, led largely by W.L. Minckley. W.S. Brown's 1974 study is the most important ecological account of the turtle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biogeographic overlap:** The basin sits on a boundary between the Tamaulipan biotic province and the Chihuahuan herpetofauna, producing interdigitation of endemic and widespread elements (McCoy, 1979).
- **Anthropogenic change:** The basin was relatively undisturbed until 1959, when pumping from La Becerra spring for the town and irrigated fields began. Subsequent pressures include mining of gypsum dunes, extensive agriculture and ranching that the authors consider largely unproductive, and goat introduction that threatens vegetation cover.
- **Study areas:** Three sites less than 8 km apart, with similar climate, soils and vegetation, represent different conservation states:
  1. **[[La Becerra Spring]]**: the originally typical habitat, now degraded by piping, pumping and use as a bathing resort (deteriorated zone).
  2. **El Mojarral and Nadadores**: marshes with *Typha* cover, clear pools and the Río Nadadores, with palapas and grills; fishing and turtle capture are prohibited and enforced by SEDUE. Described as lightly altered.
  3. **Rancho Orozco–Poza Tío Cándido**: extensive marshes and pools with almost no agriculture and little livestock (some horses and recently goats), considered ideal for study.
- **Land-use assessment:** An INEGI land-use map (1974) classifies most of the valley in the highest, least-usable category, suited only to wildlife. The authors argue that attempts at extensive agriculture have failed.
- **Commercial pressure:** Although the species is absent from reptile dealer price lists, locals capture turtles as "pets," and the trade appears to run overland into Texas, probably partly through Mexican animal traffickers.

## Key Findings

**Objectives**
- **Part I, Demography and Ecology:** obtain an updated census; quantify conserved and degraded areas and their carrying capacity; compile natural history data.
- **Part II, Conservation:** evaluate human impact; delimit urgent protection areas and rehabilitate habitat; propose land, water and species management programs; use captive breeding to reintroduce and reinforce populations.

**Methods**
- **Capture-recapture:** manual searches; funnel traps (minimum ten per locality) baited for diurnal and nocturnal activity; unbaited traps with drift fences (about 30 m long, at least five per area) to track movement; stomach-flushing to recover diet; marginal scute notching for marking.
- **Demography:** population density estimates across the three sites.
- **Natural history:** reproduction (hormone sampling, temperature-dependent sex determination), growth in semi-captive fenced marsh enclosures compared with zoo data (Brownsville, Dallas, Jersey Wildlife Preservation Trust), radiotelemetry of adults (at least three checks per week), an experimental radiotracer method, and behavioral ethograms including observation hides and testosterone-induced courtship.
- **Microhabitat:** associated fauna, epibionts (leeches, epibiotic algae), intestinal and cloacal parasites (non-lethal sampling), and seed dispersal through excreta, testing scarification effects on germination.

**Conservation Proposals**
- Establish a national park or biosphere reserve as a state priority, with protected areas of two types: restricted-access areas open to the public but guarded against collection, and strict core zones for researchers only.
- Regulate collection and access, control entry and exit with SEDUE checkpoints, and require environmental impact assessment before any water management or land use that could damage soils or the water table in critical areas.
- Conduct a survey of local uses and turtle-related beliefs.
- Establish a captive breeding colony at Cuatro Ciénegas for reintroduction, research stock, and possible commercial captive breeding to reduce wild collection.
- Reintroduce or reinforce populations using captive-bred offspring (marked for survival studies), captive-raised subadults and adults, confiscated trafficked turtles, and animals rescued from degraded sites. Monitor through regular censuses.
- Plan private-land measures to be negotiated with ranch owners after research results are available.

**Key Threats and Assessments**
- Irreversible alteration of parts of the basin, with severe pressure on remaining individuals of many endemic species.
- The authors estimate that perhaps 60–70% of the area could be recovered.
- Proposed saline extraction near Rancho Orozco was a threat to one of the few well-preserved sites; it appears to have been abandoned.

**Budget**
- Year 1: about US$42,800, including salaries for two biologists (US$1,000/month each, US$24,000 total) and a student (US$3,600), field expenses, traps, drift fences, radio equipment and photographic gear.
- Year 2: about US$36,075, excluding a vehicle, which the authors estimate at roughly 50,000,000 Mexican pesos (note: the figure as written in the source).
