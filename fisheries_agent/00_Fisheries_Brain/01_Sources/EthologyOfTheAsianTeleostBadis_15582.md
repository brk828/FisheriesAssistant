# Ethology of Badis badis: Locomotion, Maintenance, Aggregation and Fright

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Badidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 ethological study by George W. Barlow describes laboratory observations of the small Asian teleost [[Badis Badis]] (maximum standard length about 75 mm). The paper is the first of a planned series and focuses on locomotion, comfort movements, feeding, aggregation and fright behavior, rather than hydrodynamics. The author argues that the species is poorly understood in the literature and suggests, to be shown in a later paper, that it may be misclassified in family and suborder. Observations were made on fish kept in planted aquaria under controlled water conditions.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Badis Badis]] (Hamilton-Buchanan), a popular aquarium fish whose biology the author describes as "remarkably poorly known."
- **Stock and maintenance:** Fish were obtained from an aquarium shop in Munich, and most reported animals were laboratory-bred. Aquaria of 30, 60 and 120 liters were densely planted with a fine gravel substrate.
- **Water conditions:** Hardness about DH 4-6, pH around 6-7, temperature 26-28 °C, circulated through a filter reservoir; lighting by 25-watt fluorescent tubes.
- **Methods:** Direct observation supplemented by [[Strobe-Light Photography]]. Tempo of pectoral fin beats was tallied (10 samples of 50 beats per fish).
- **Terminology:** The author created his own behavioral units rather than adopting the [[Anabantid Fishes]] terminology of Forselius (1957), while noting the approximate correspondence of units to [[Fixed Action Patterns]].
- **Funding:** Post-doctoral fellowship (MF 8244) from the [[National Institute of Mental Health]], US Public Health Service.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Locomotion and posture**
- At slow speeds, forward thrust comes from [[Pectoral Fin Sculling]], vertical waves passing down and back along the fins, with alternating phasing between right and left fins.
- Fast swimming uses lateral body undulations, with the head initiating waves that grow in amplitude posteriorly.
- Backward swimming uses alternate paddle-like pectoral beats; the caudal and dorsal lobe act mainly as steering and rudder structures.
- The fish spends much of its time [[Hovering]], where pectoral sculling is opposed to opercular water jets.
- Fast starts are prepared from an S-shaped posture ("S-shape"), while a "Sickle-shape" gives directional accuracy over short darts (less than one body length).

**Feeding ecology**
- Badis badis is strictly carnivorous and eats only live, moving prey, with feeding visually mediated.
- Post-larvae feed first on microfauna (probably rotifers and large protozoans), then on small cyclops and *Daphnia* in open water, and on benthic animals on the bottom and leaves.
- Juveniles and adults prefer "worm-like" prey such as *Tubifex*, *Enchytraeus* and aquatic insect larvae over crustaceans. Large leeches were attacked but escaped; small leeches are probably eaten.
- Adults and juveniles pull worms free of the substrate by backing up, darting forward, or lying on one side to use the body as a lever.

**Aggregation**
- The species is essentially non-aggregating. Post-larvae are solitary until about 8-10 mm standard length, when barred coloration appears and juvenile interactions begin.
- Gravid females aggregate when deprived of males; this is possible because fighting is suppressed.
- Schooling, a fright-induced behavior, occurs in females and sub-adults placed in a bare aquarium, with fish moving in saltatory hops in a loose group.

**Fright behavior**
- Fright is expressed through three conflicting outputs: [[Avoiding]] (flight), [[Concealing]] (movement suppression) and [[Aggregating]] (schooling), with [[Nestling]] as an intermediate shelter-seeking behavior.
- Movement suppression is stronger when predators or novel objects are near, or when the fish is in open space.

## Key Findings

1. **Locomotion:** Pectoral sculling is the primary slow-speed propulsion and hovering mechanism; fast swimming shifts to lateral undulation.
2. **Hovering tempo as an arousal index (Table I):** Pectoral beat rates ranged from 3.9 to 6.1 beats/s (grand median 5.0). Ripe females at 25-30 °C-range trials had medians of 4.9-5.4 beats/s, while sub-adults at 20-22.5 °C-range trials had medians of 4.2-4.4 beats/s. Tempo rose on approach to a male and peaked on direct confrontation, which indicates that a faster tempo reflects greater excitation.
3. **Respiration in hypoxic water:** Under low oxygen, all twelve juveniles to young adults tested rose to the surface, tilted about 45°, and inhaled surface film over the gills, without trapping air bubbles.
4. **Comfort movements:** Fin flickering, chafing, flexing, coughing and yawning were described, with yawning as a rapid three-stage water-flushing sequence.
5. **Prey capture:** Capture varies with satiation. A hungry juvenile approaches, fixes on prey, forms a Sickle-shape and darts forward. Fed fish show progressively reduced, less complete behavior, and adults often skip elements altogether.
6. **Energy-based hunting threshold:** Hunting motor patterns drop out faster than eating after repletion, so the author proposes a rising motor threshold for hunting with greater energetic cost.
7. **Solitary species:** Adults are essentially solitary, with aggregation limited to juveniles, gravid females lacking males, and fright-induced schooling.
8. **Fright model:** A hypothetical hierarchy (Figure 4) places flight, concealing and aggregating under a single fright motivation. Fright behavior is usually an integrated mix of these components rather than a single outcome.
9. **Systematic claim (unverified here):** The author states that behavioral evidence will show Badis badis is misclassified in family and suborder, which is planned for a subsequent paper and is not tested in this document.
