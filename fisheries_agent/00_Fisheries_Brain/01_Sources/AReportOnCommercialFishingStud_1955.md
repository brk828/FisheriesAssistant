# A Report on Commercial Fishing Studies Conducted in the Tidal Streams of Alabama

## Executive Summary
This 1955 fisheries management report, authored by I.B. Byrd and W.L. Minckley of the Alabama Department of Conservation, documents a controlled experimental commercial fishing program conducted in fourteen tidal streams of Alabama during 1953–1954. The study was initiated to resolve a management conflict between commercial fishermen — who sought access to tidal streams with small-mesh [[Trammel Net|trammel nets]] — and sport fishermen concerned about impacts on freshwater game fish and [[Speckled Trout]] populations. Across 426 net sets, researchers recorded 65,839 fish, turtles, and crabs totaling 56,129.01 lbs. Freshwater game fish constituted a negligible 0.75% of total catch weight, while [[Striped Mullet]] (67.68%) and [[Gar]] species (20.25%) dominated the catch. The findings suggested that small-mesh trammel netting posed minimal direct risk to freshwater game fish stocks but raised specific seasonal concerns regarding bycatch of the ecologically and recreationally important [[Speckled Trout]].

## Background & Description
This report is a fisheries catch-composition study rather than a morphological survey; however, it catalogs an extensive species inventory representative of Gulf Coast estuarine and tidal-freshwater ichthyofauna. Notable taxa recorded (with scientific names) include:

**Commercial/Rough Species (dominant by weight):**
- [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*) – 67.68% of total catch weight
- [[White Mullet]] (*Mugil curema*)
- [[Gar]] complex (Alligator, Longnose, Shortnose, Spotted — *Lepisosteus* spp.) – 20.25%
- [[Blue Catfish]] (*Ictalurus furcatus*) – 3.23%
- [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) and [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Signalosa petenensis*) – 1.94%
- [[Buffalo Fish]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*) – 0.84%

**Sciaenids and Estuarine Species:**
- [[Speckled Trout]] (*Cynoscion nebulosus*) – 3.21%
- [[White Trout]] (*Cynoscion nothus*)
- [[Red Drum|Redfish]] (*Sciaenops ocellata*)
- [[Black Drum]] (*Pogonias cromis*)
- [[Atlantic Croaker]] (*Micropogon undulatus*)
- [[Spot Croaker]] (*Leiostomus xanthurus*)
- [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*)

**Freshwater Game Fish (returned live per regulation, collectively <1% of catch):**
- [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*) and [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*)
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- [[Shellcracker]] (*Lepomis microlophus*)

**Other notable taxa:** [[Southern Flounder]] (*Paralichthys lethostigma*), [[Sheepshead]] (*Archosargus probatocephalus*), [[Hardhead Catfish]] (*Galeichthys felis*), [[Gafftopsail Catfish]] (*Bagre marinus*), [[Silver Perch]] (*Bairdiella chrysura*), [[Pinfish]] (*Lagodon rhomboides*), [[Hogchoker]] (*Achirus fasciatus*), [[Sea Robin]] (*Prionotus* sp.), [[Menhaden]] (*Brevoortia patronus*), [[Jack Crevalle]] (*Caranx hippos*), [[Ribbonfish]] (*Trichiurus lepturus*), [[Paddlefish]] (*Polyodon spathula*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), and non-fish bycatch including turtles and crabs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The study area encompassed fourteen tidal streams draining into Mobile Bay and the Gulf of Mexico, spanning Mobile County (Dog, East Fowl, West Fowl, Little Rivers) and Baldwin County (Fish, Bon Secour, Magnolia Rivers, and several smaller creeks/bayous: Hammock, Palmetto, Soldier, Wolf, Graham Creeks, Graham Bayou, Stone Quarry).

Key ecological dynamics documented:
- **Seasonal mullet migration**: Large schools of [[Striped Mullet]] migrate from Mobile Bay and Gulf waters into tidal streams during fall and winter, driving commercial fishing pressure and interest in these habitats.
- **Diel catch variability**: Night sets were roughly twice as productive by weight (38,559.58 lbs) as day sets (17,569.43 lbs), suggesting nocturnal activity patterns or net avoidance behavior differences among species relative to trammel net visibility.
- **Speckled trout seasonal pulses**: [[Speckled Trout]] exhibited highly variable, pulse-like migrations into tidal streams, comprising up to 12.31% of monthly catch weight in December 1954 versus near-zero in other months (e.g., 0.00% in December 1953), indicating inconsistent environmental or climatic drivers of estuarine habitat use.
- **Freshwater/estuarine interface**: Freshwater game fish were essentially absent from 8 of 14 streams, reflecting a salinity or habitat gradient separating true freshwater sport fish assemblages from the euryhaline/marine-dominated tidal stream community.
- **Competitive release hypothesis**: The report speculates that high biomass of "rough" competitor species (mullet, gar, shad, buffalo) may suppress growth of freshwater sport fish through resource competition, implying netting could provide ecological release for game fish populations.

## Key Findings
- **Total catch**: 65,839 individuals (fish, turtles, crabs) weighing 56,129.01 lbs across 426 trammel net sets in 14 streams (1953–1954).
- **Catch composition by weight**: Commercial/rough species = 96.04%; [[Speckled Trout]] = 3.21%; freshwater game fish = 0.75%.
- **Dominant commercial species**: [[Striped Mullet]] (67.68%), [[Gar]] (20.25%), [[Blue Catfish]] (3.23%), shad species (1.94%), [[Buffalo Fish]] (0.84%).
- **Day vs. night efficiency**: Night sets (240 total) were ~2x more productive by weight than day sets (186 total); freshwater game fish percentage was higher during day sets (1.09%) vs. night sets (0.56%), while speckled trout percentage was ~2.5x higher at night (3.94% vs. 1.62%).
- **Stream-level variation**: Freshwater game fish were absent in 8 of 14 streams; among streams where present, percentages ranged from 0.06% (West Fowl River) to 1.50% (Magnolia River). Speckled trout percentages were highest in Dog River (5.06%) and West Fowl River (5.52%).
- **Monthly/seasonal trends**: Freshwater game fish peaked in February (1.02%) and March (1.06%) 1954. Speckled trout peaked sharply in November (7.73%) and December (12.31%) 1954 — roughly 7x higher than the same species' contribution during the November 1953–March 1954 period (1.16%).
- **Extreme single-event catch**: One night's netting in Dog River in December 1954 yielded 544.00 lbs of speckled trout alone — exceeding the cumulative trout catch of the entire November 1953–March 1954 sampling period.
- **Management conclusion**: Data suggested minimal risk to freshwater game fish from small-mesh trammel netting (all such fish are required to be released live under Alabama law), but raised concern that unregulated netting during peak trout migration months (November–December) could significantly impact [[Speckled Trout]] sport fisheries. The authors proposed classifying speckled trout as a protected/game species specifically when caught via commercial gear, requiring release similar to freshwater game fish, as a compromise between commercial and recreational fishing interests.
