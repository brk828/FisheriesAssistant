# Predation by Jellyfish on Larval Fish: An Experimental Evaluation Using In Situ Enclosures

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[De Lafontaine and Leggett]] (1988, *Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences*) used large in situ enclosures to measure predation on yolk-sac [[Capelin]] (*Mallotus villosus*) larvae by four jellyfish species. Daily instantaneous larval mortality rose linearly with predator density and was independent of initial larval density, implying a linear (Type I) functional response. Alternative zooplankton prey did not significantly change larval mortality. Predator body mass, rather than feeding-appendage morphology, best explained mortality. The authors conclude that larval survival depends largely on the timing of larval emergence relative to jellyfish growth and abundance.

## Background & Description

Starvation has long been proposed as the main cause of early larval death in fishes, but solid evidence is scarce despite more than 60 years of study. Predation by planktonic invertebrates, especially jellyfish, has therefore been suggested as a major mortality source. Earlier predation estimates came mainly from gut-content analyses and small laboratory containers, whose results depend strongly on container size.

The study tested three hypotheses:
1. Larval mortality is directly proportional to predator abundance.
2. Larval mortality is inversely related to initial larval density.
3. Larval mortality is significantly reduced when alternative food is present.

Experiments were run in summers 1982–1984 in 3.2 m³ [[In Situ Enclosures]] moored in Bryant's Cove, Newfoundland. Enclosures were made of Dacron with 53 µm Nitex walls and filled by water diffusion. Each enclosure received two to five predators, giving densities of 0.6–1.5 m⁻³, close to the natural range (0.1–10 m⁻³). Predators were [[Aurelia Aurita]], [[Staurophora Mertensi]], [[Cyanea Capillata]] and [[Catablema Vesicarium]]. Larval densities ranged from 12 to 780 larvae·m⁻³.

Predation rates were calculated with a depletion-corrected instantaneous mortality equation (after [[Arditi 1982]]), which avoids bias from prey depletion during the roughly 36–46 h experiments (mode 39 h).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Capture and handling:** Mortality from capture and handling was under 5% in the first 96 h, so it was considered negligible.
- **Enclosure condition:** Temperature, salinity and oxygen inside the enclosures matched outside waters. Over 90% of larvae recovered from enclosures were alive at termination.
- **Predator life cycle:** Aurelia aurita and Staurophora mertensi have a one-year cycle. They grow rapidly during the medusa stage, typically from 1–2 cm to 15–17 cm in diameter over the period of larval emergence, and die after reproduction near the end of summer.
- **Larval release:** Onshore wind events release capelin larvae over 30–40 days, so the timing of emergence relative to predator growth varies from year to year.
- **Comparison with field densities:** Natural predator densities of single species are typically 0.005–0.5 m⁻³. Blooms can exceed 10 m⁻³. Larval densities in this study stayed within natural ranges, and the authors note that in situ densities approaching the 7,000–10,000 larvae·m⁻³ used in some laboratory studies have not been reported.

## Key Findings

- **Linear functional response:** Daily larval mortality was linearly related to predator number (Z = −0.0014 + 0.1266P, r = 0.98, p = 0.0001, n = 4 for *A. aurita*). Per-predator mortality did not depend on predator number (ANOVA F = 0.19, p = 0.90). This contrasts with the Type II responses reported from small containers.
- **Larval density:** Mortality was not significantly related to initial larval density for any of the four species. Per-capita predation rate was therefore linearly proportional to the number of larvae stocked.
- **Alternative prey:** Adding zooplankton did not significantly change mortality (for example, *S. mertensi* residuals: Mann–Whitney p = 0.72). Alternative prey did not alter the linear response, and a Type II shift was not seen.
- **Mortality range:** Daily larval mortality rates ranged from 0.015 to 1.58 depending on predator species.
- **Mean mortality per predator (Table 8):**
  - *Catablema vesicarium* (n = 16, mean diameter 1.70 cm, wet weight 2.17 g): Z = 0.055
  - *Aurelia aurita* (n = 77, diameter 5.34 cm, wet weight 9.59 g): Z = 0.092
  - *Staurophora mertensi* (n = 64, diameter 9.54 cm, wet weight 38.35 g): Z = 0.152
  - *Cyanea capillata* (n = 7, diameter 10.60 cm, wet weight 127.8 g): Z = 0.969
- **Body mass over morphology:** Mortality rose with mean predator body weight. The authors suggest total predator biomass is a better predictor of losses than numerical abundance.
- **Predator size effect:** Mortality rates were dome-shaped against predator size, with small predators causing more mortality. Regression models explained 49% (*S. mertensi*) and 72% (*A. aurita*) of variability.
- **Larval size and vulnerability:** Larger larvae experienced higher mortality, especially at the transition from endogenous to exogenous feeding. The authors caution that this does not yet show that starvation is the cause.
- **Timing and mortality:** Mortality was estimated at 5–20% per day, depending on the emergence timing of capelin larvae relative to jellyfish growth. Mortality was minimised when larvae emerged while jellyfish were small (1979) and increased when releases were delayed (1984).
- **Conclusion:** Macroinvertebrate predation has the potential to be the primary regulator of larval survival. Reduced losses are achievable only by timing larval release to periods and water masses where jellyfish are naturally scarce.
