# An Eighteen-Point Fish Movement Recorder

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

W. H. Moore (1961) describes an electronic apparatus for recording the movements of individually caged trout on the bed of [[Lake Windermere]]. The system comprises six cages, each with three sets of [[Conductometric Electrodes]] (eighteen detection points in total), a [[Bridge Circuit]] driven by a common oscillator, a cumulative-reaction [[Switching Amplifier]], a servo-driven [[Automatic Balancing Circuit]], a graphical recorder and electromechanical counters. The design aimed to detect fish passage without the animals being aware of the detector, using a low applied voltage (no more than 2 V between electrodes).

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The apparatus was devised to detect and record the movement of trout living under conditions as nearly natural as possible. Fish were individually caged and kept on the lake bed in water 2-4 metres deep.

**Detection principle.** Experiments showed that the passage of a fish 15-18 cm long could be detected conductometrically between electrodes 30 cm apart, using no more than 2 volts between electrodes. Three electrodes per set therefore allowed a cage width of 60 cm, and a 2.4 m cage length was chosen as suitable for the fish size.

**Cage construction.**
- Light wood frame, 2.4 m × 60 cm × 60 cm, joined with brass screws and painted with bitumastic paint.
- Sides covered with dark green 14-mesh "Tygan" screen cloth (TS 5034); top and bottom covered with "Nyak" No. 9 netting, 1-inch mesh.
- Small removable trap door on the top side at one end.
- Removable concrete anchor blocks, each weighing 27.0 kg, hung at each end.
- Four light nylon lifting ropes joined to a nylon buoy rope.

**Electrodes.**
- Three sets of detectors, each of three electrodes made from 0.47 cm diameter stainless steel rod, 60 cm long.
- Electrodes fixed into ebonite end-pieces (1.25 cm × 3.75 cm × 60 cm) at 25 cm centres, held vertically.
- Sets placed centrally and 45 cm from each end of the cage.
- Wooden caps over electrode ends outside the top netting reduced end-effects and counts from fish swimming across the top.

**Circuit.**
- Common oscillator at approximately 1325 c/s, feeding a 1.8 V signal to a bridge network whose arms include the water-filled electrodes.
- Out-of-balance voltage triggers a switching amplifier, which energises a relay, a marking pen on the recorder, and a counter.
- Electrodes connected via 100 yards of 3-core rubber-covered flexible cable.
- Each bridge arm pair places the central-to-lateral resistances in opposite arms, giving partial self-compensation for conductivity changes.
- Only one valve is needed for the detection and switching stage.

**Recording and power.**
- Two Autovisor Operations Recorders (type R.O.1.V., speed 1 in./hour), each built as a ten-point recorder, so three cages share one recorder with one channel left for hourly time marking.
- Two electromechanical counters per cage; a 48-hour time switch alternates them so that one can be read while the other counts. Counters switch over at midnight G.M.T.
- Four power supplies (two for the six oscillators and eighteen switching amplifiers, two for the automatic balancing circuits) so that no single supply fault stops all recording. H.T. should lie between 320 V and 350 V.

**Automatic balancing.** A phase-sensitive relay servo drives a reversible d.c. permanent-magnet motor through a total reduction of 3,600:1 (a 60:1 train from an ex-R.A.F. Pilot's Repeater Compass, a 6:1 epicyclic drive, and a worm gear). The epicyclic stage gives slip protection if the motor over-runs the potentiometer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Deployment:** Cages were placed on the lake bottom at 2-4 m depth, spaced at least 3 m apart (preferably further), and submerged for at least 24 hours before balancing.
- **Natural-water effects:** The balancing circuit was needed because electrode changes alone could not compensate for variations in natural waters.
- **Rain interference:** During early trials a shower of rain blocked the switching amplifiers within less than thirty seconds; the effect lasted until late the next day. The author did not know whether this was due to static charge in the rain or to temperature/conductivity changes. Not all rain produced the effect, even during a thunderstorm.
- **Hunting:** Automatic balancing circuits occasionally hunted; the author attributed this mainly to changes in the lake rather than the equipment, excluding times when a fish lay close to an electrode.
- **Mains interference:** A 50 c/s signal from an electricity mains earth-plate within 50 m of the six cages caused interference, cured by an isolating transformer on the recording equipment mains supply.

## Key Findings

- An 18-point recorder was built for six cages, each holding one bottom-feeding trout, with three electrode sets per cage.
- Fish passage is detected conductometrically, with no more than 2 V applied between electrodes; a 15-18 cm fish was detectable between electrodes 30 cm apart.
- Cage dimensions: 2.4 m × 60 cm × 60 cm; anchor blocks of 27.0 kg each.
- Oscillator frequency approximately 1325 c/s; bridge output fed to a cumulative-reaction switching amplifier.
- Sensitivity test: placing a 100 kΩ resistor momentarily across the electrode leads reliably triggers counting.
- Typical bias 3.0-4.0 V; tripping voltage about 2.5 V; latch-off around 1.2 V (Test Point to earth).
- Balancing reduced the bridge signal to around 100 mV (as low as 60 mV) after capacitive adjustment; the valve-voltmeter may initially read 10-20 V.
- Mains transients from other equipment (e.g., a mains-operated electromechanical counter) can trip switching amplifiers; an isolating transformer resolved the 50 c/s interference.
- Split power supplies and dual counters ensure that a single fault does not stop all recording and that daily totals can be read without a fixed-time visit.
