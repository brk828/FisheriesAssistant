# Arizona Riparian Inventory and Mapping Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) report, submitted in response to the 1992 Waters - Riparian Protection Program (ARS 45-101), documents the first phase of a statewide inventory of riparian vegetation. The work combined [[Landsat Thematic Mapper]] satellite imagery with [[Multiple Resolution Aerial Videography]] and a [[Geographic Information System]] to map riparian vegetation along perennial streams, and it summarizes land ownership and land use, riparian wildlife value, a proposed [[Hierarchical Designation System]], and existing protection mechanisms. The inventory covered 4,628.95 miles of perennial streams and mapped 266,786.39 acres of riparian vegetation. Initial ground verification found only about one-third of sampled polygons correctly classified, and the maps were still being refined toward an 80% accuracy target.

## Background & Description

The legislation mandated four tasks: (1) a riparian classification system with a hierarchical designation based on functions and values; (2) identification and mapping of riparian areas, prioritizing perennial waters; (3) mapping of land ownership and current land uses; and (4) identification of existing protection options. The report was prepared for the Governor, the President of the Senate, the Speaker of the House and the Riparian Area Advisory Committee, with a reporting deadline of December 1, 1993.

Riparian areas are defined in the legislation as areas supported by "the presence of deep-rooted plant species that depend on having roots in the water table or its capillary zone." The project adopted the hierarchical [[Brown Lowe and Pase Classification System]] (1979), which arranges biotic communities from realm and formation down to series (fifth level) and association (sixth level) and is open-ended and computer-compatible.

The project was led by Ruth A. Valencia (Ecosystems Program Manager, Nongame Branch) with co-authors from AGFD's Nongame, Research and Habitat branches. The vegetation mapping was contracted to Dr. Lee Graham at the [[University of Arizona]] Advanced Resource Technology Laboratory (UA-ART) through the Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit. The project was funded largely through the Heritage Fund.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian ecosystems.** Riparian areas cover a small share of the landscape but are among the most biologically productive lands. They form an ecotone between upland and aquatic systems and act as linear wildlife travel corridors. Their composition is shaped by flood frequency and intensity, climate gradients, upland disturbance and lateral channel migration. Vegetation changes with elevation, from scrub willow and wet meadows in upper reaches, to mixed broadleaf and cottonwood-willow communities in mid-reaches, to cottonwood-willow and mesquite bosques on broad lower floodplains such as the [[San Pedro River]] and lower Verde River.

**Hydrology and stream classification.** No comprehensive statewide map of perennial, intermittent and ephemeral streams existed. The base map was built from the [[USGS Digital Line Graph]] hydrography, which had never been coded perennial, and was refined by reviewers from AGFD, ADWR and ADEQ, along with earlier AGFD perennial-stream efforts (Brown et al. 1981; Silvey et al. 1984). A minimum perennial segment length of one-half mile was adopted. Only about 250 USGS stream gages exist in the state, and many historically perennial reaches have been altered by pumping, diversion, dams and effluent.

**Wildlife values.** Riparian habitats are critical for vertebrates. Ohmart and Anderson (1986) report that more than 60 percent of vertebrates in the arid Southwest are obligate riparian users, with another 10 to 20 percent facultative. Fish depend on riparian vegetation for shade, cover and food, and riparian leaf fall and insects provide much of the food base. Amphibians depend on water for reproduction, and some reptiles and birds are restricted to riparian areas. For mammals, running water is sometimes more important than vegetation; river otter, beaver, muskrat and water shrew depend on perennial water. Cottonwood-willow communities support key nesting birds, mesquite bosques support high avian diversity, and tamarisk provides poor habitat.

**Land use pressures.** Commercial grazing, wood harvesting, urban and agricultural lands, recreation and mining all intersect the stream network. Private and tribal lands could not be mapped for most activities because no comprehensive data sets exist.

## Key Findings

**Mapping inventory**
- 4,628.95 miles of perennial streams inventoried, out of 5,022.47 miles identified: 3,961.26 miles unregulated, 972.95 miles regulated, and 88.26 miles effluent-dominated.
- 266,786.39 acres of riparian vegetation associated with perennial waters mapped, about 0.4% of the state's land area.
- An additional 393.52 miles of Colorado River within Grand Canyon National Park were not inventoried because of flight restrictions.
- Most riparian vegetation in Arizona is supported by intermittent waters, which were not mapped in this phase. On a San Pedro River case-study segment, perennial reaches supported 12,238 acres of vegetation versus 19,159 acres along intermittent reaches.

**Land ownership of perennial waters (miles, with overlap noted by AGFD)**
- Federal: 2,510.79 miles (National Forests 1,573.50; National Parks 611.90; BLM 289.07).
- Tribal: 1,408.80 miles.
- Private: 856.67 miles.
- State and municipal: 254.58 miles.
- Totals exceed 100% because stream banks can have different owners.

**Vegetation communities**
- 17 riparian community types (series level) and approximately 85 vegetation associations were identified.
- Leading categories by share of mapped acreage include tamarisk, mesquite, arrowweed, conifer, mountain meadow, oak and cottonwood-willow. Flood-scoured areas (7.4%) and unlabelled areas (5.4%) were also significant.

**Accuracy and verification**
- Ground verification covered southeastern Arizona: 1,444 polygons (59,615 acres), of which 286 polygons (17,079 acres) were sampled, requiring 1,651 plots.
- Initially only about a third of sampled polygons were correctly classified (33.7%), with a 48.1% incorrect rate.
- On a revised San Pedro River sample, accuracy rose by about 20 percentage points (correct classification from 34.8% to 54.3% of polygons), mostly from fewer upland-as-riparian misclassifications. The target is 80% accuracy.
- Major error sources included upland mesquite boundaries, understory species not visible from above, and confusion of ash and walnut with cottonwood and mesquite with tamarisk.

**Land use (acres and perennial stream miles intersected)**
- Commercial grazing: 31,021,328 acres and 1,729 miles; no commercial grazing 9,051,411 acres.
- Open to commercial wood harvesting: 8,056,906 acres and 901 miles.
- Urban lands: 2,728,273 acres (297 miles); agricultural 1,568,855 acres (63 miles); mixed use 1,099,185 acres (88 miles); industrial 118,593 acres (9 miles).
- 10,341 mining sites recorded in AZMILS (August 1993); 103 active metal and nonmetal mines (November 1992); 582 sand and gravel mine locations.
- Mineral potential: 2,625,757 acres highly favorable and 14,775,563 acres favorable.
- 352 developed recreation point locations were mapped.

**Hierarchical designation system (proposed)**
- Step 1: assess functional condition of each riparian unit, using the BLM Proper Functioning Condition approach and the COE hydrogeomorphic approach.
- Step 2: categorize into A (properly functioning), B (functional-at-risk, recovering), C (functional-at-risk, degrading), D (nonfunctional) or E (condition unknown).
- Step 3: rank values (wildlife, recreation, aesthetics) as high, medium or low within each category.
- Step 4: prioritize by the number of high values rather than a numerical average, with open questions about whether at-risk or fully functional areas should receive priority.

**Wildlife value index**
- Anderson and Ohmart's index uses vegetation community, structural type, altitude and location, with values from about +3 (highest) to about -3. It was developed for forest and scrub habitats below 3,500 feet, excludes aquatic fauna and wetlands, and requires field testing at higher elevations.

**Protection options**
- No regulatory program at any level of government is specifically designed to protect riparian areas. Existing mechanisms are often narrow or indirect, and Section 404 of the [[Clean Water Act]] covers only low-flow channels and delineated wetlands, leaving most riparian areas outside its jurisdiction.
- Protection mechanisms are grouped into regulation, policy, acquisition, planning, restoration, incentives, technical assistance and research, and are tabulated by land ownership category (federal, state, tribal, private).

**Next steps**
- Continue map refinement toward 80% accuracy, expected by June 1994.
- Begin mapping intermittent-water riparian areas.
- Use an EPA Clean Water Act 104(b)(3) Wetlands Grant to develop a functional assessment method and a statewide status-and-trends methodology.
- Form an interdisciplinary, interagency team to identify reference areas.
