# Age and Growth of White Crappies in Reelfoot Lake (1938 and 1948)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pomoxis

## Executive Summary

This Report of the [[Reelfoot Lake Biological Station]] (Contribution No. 79) by [[Robert J. Schoffman]] compares the age and growth of [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*) in [[Reelfoot Lake]] between 1938 and 1948. Scale samples from 595 fish (391 from 1938, 204 from 1948) were aged, and mean length and weight were calculated for each summer of life. The 1948 fish were longer and lighter than their 1938 counterparts at each size group. The paper also argues that commercial fishing should not be restricted without evidence of harm, and it cautions against the proposed 1951 Conservation Code. A second section, on trematode cercariae in snails, is included in the same issue. The remaining pages are news and literature lists from Tennessee science institutions.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** A study begun in 1937 aimed to determine the [[Age and Growth]] of game and rough fish in Reelfoot Lake. In 1947 the author checked [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) growth to see whether it had changed over a ten-year period. White crappie scales were collected at the same time to test whether other species followed the same pattern as bluegill.

**Sampling methods.**
- Fish came from commercial catches taken with [[Hoop Net]]s of 1.5-inch square mesh.
- The 1938 collections also used [[Wire Set Net]]s. These were basket-type nets of 1.5-inch poultry netting, set in hollow stumps.
- Wire set nets were prohibited in 1941, so no 1948 specimens came from them.
- Fish were weighed on a spring scale of two-pound capacity, read to quarter ounces. Lengths were measured to the nearest 1/8 inch.

**Aging method.** Ages were expressed as *summers of life*, not years. A fish in age group 4 is in its fourth summer, is three years old, and has three annual rings on its scales. Rings were counted with a polarized-light magnifying apparatus described in an earlier paper (Schoffman, 1939). The author did not attempt to assign calendar years to the outer scale margins.

**Regulatory context.** The legal length was 9 inches in 1938 and was reduced to 8 inches in 1947. [[Size Limit]]s were removed in May 1949. In 1949 commercial fishers were limited to 20 crappies per day, the same creel limit as sportsmen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat and changes.** The author reports no clear change in the length of the growing season or in food supply between the two periods. Two changes did occur: the water level rose after channels were dug to open all parts of the lake to free flow, and wire set nets were abolished.
- **Population status.** Over the ten-year interval the crappie population "held its own," and the size and weight of crappies caught increased. Size-limit reduction had no apparent effect on catch numbers or fish size.
- **Fishing conditions.** Boat landing owners and guides at the north end of the lake reported that fall 1949 was the best crappie fishing that end of the lake had experienced. No formal [[Creel Census]] was available.
- **Competition.** The author warns that more crappies could compete with other fish, especially [[Sunfish]], for food and spawning grounds, and could alter the lake's balance.
- **Trematode cercariae.** A separate section examines larval trematodes (cercariae) emerging from snails, including *Physa gyrina* and *Helisoma trivolvis*. The excerpt does not name its author. The results are summarized under Key Findings.

## Key Findings

### Age and growth of white crappies

- **Sample:** 391 fish in 1938 and 204 in 1948 (595 total).
- **Dominant age groups:**
  - 1948: age group 3 was the most numerous, at 41% (about two-fifths) of the catch.
  - 1938: age group 4 was the most numerous, at 39% (more than one-third).
  - Age group 4 was also the largest group in 1948, at 31%.
- **Age range:** 1938 fish reached age group 9 (2.5% of the total). The 1948 oldest group was age group 8 (2%). Age group 3 was absent from 1938 samples because the legal length was then 9 inches.
- **Time to legal size:** In 1938 the average white crappie reached 9 inches late in its third summer. In 1948 it reached 8 inches late in its second summer.
- **Length growth:** Using the 1938 ninth-summer mean length of 14.17 inches as 100%, 66% of total length growth occurred in the first summer of life (60% in 1948). Yearly increments were much smaller thereafter in both years.
- **Weight growth:** Using the 1938 ninth-summer mean weight of 30.12 ounces as 100%, 21% of total weight was gained in the first summer (20% in 1948). Cumulative 1938 weight percentages were 29% (fourth summer), 43% (fifth), 56% (sixth), and 79% (eighth). For 1948 they were 20% (third summer), 28% (fourth), 45% (fifth), 64% (sixth), and 69% (seventh). Weight was still increasing steeply in the oldest groups, so these percentages would be lower if older fish had been included.
- **1948 vs. 1938:** Crappies caught in 1948 were longer and lighter than 1938 fish in each size group.
- **Table 1 examples (mean length / mean weight):**
  - 1938, age 9 (8 fish): 14.17 in / 30.12 oz.
  - 1948, age 8 (4 fish): 14.19 in / 24.25 oz.
  - 1948, age 6 (17 fish): 12.19 in / 15.41 oz.

### Management position

- The author concludes that white crappies were being caught during a rapid-growing period, which he describes as desirable and as evidence that the crop was being harvested.
- He opposes the proposed 1951 Conservation Code, which would abolish commercial fishing for game fish. He sees no valid reason from existing data to limit or prohibit commercial fishing.
- He argues that improving fishing conditions is preferable to abolishing commercial fishing, which could upset the lake's balance.

### Trematode cercariae (snail study)

- **Species recovered:** 21 species of larval trematodes emerged from snails.
  - 5 matched previously described species.
  - 3 were probably identical in morphology to already described cercariae.
  - The rest had peculiarities that prevented assignment to known species. These included 8 Strigeid cercariae, 7 Niphidiocercariae, and 1 Echinostome cercaria.
- **Infection rates compared with Byrd (1940):**
  - *Physa gyrina*: 9.3% emerging cercariae (Byrd reported 28%).
  - *Helisoma trivolvis*: 8% (Byrd reported 12%).
- **Interpretation:** The author regards neither figure as an accurate infection percentage for either snail species. He argues that infection rates vary too much, especially when pooled from many separate collections, to be meaningfully expressed as a percentage.

### Remaining pages

The rest of the issue contains "News of Tennessee Science" items, mainly 1950 journal citations from [[Oak Ridge National Laboratory]] (ORNL), the University of Tennessee, Vanderbilt University, and Emory University. Topics include radiation physiology, biochemistry, and a thermistor study of summer temperatures at Mountain Lake, Virginia. A short taxonomic note reports millipede species from Kentucky and Great Smoky Mountains National Park, and a new caddisfly species from Gatlinburg, Tennessee. A bibliography of Reelfoot Lake studies by Byrd and colleagues (1940) is also included.
