# Largemouth Bass Tagging at Clear Lake, Lake County, California

## Executive Summary
This 1957 study by J.B. Kimsey (California Department of Fish and Game) reports on a mark-recapture tagging program conducted on [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) at [[Clear Lake]], California, between 1953 and 1956. The primary objectives were to quantify angling and natural mortality rates, characterize seasonal fishery dynamics, and assess movement patterns to inform sensible angling regulations. A total of 441 bass were tagged (333 with retained double-wire tags used in final analysis), yielding a 28.2% three-year tag return rate. The study concluded that natural mortality (36%) substantially exceeded fishing mortality (20%), indicating the fishery was not overexploited and that harvest could potentially be increased without conservation concern.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Micropterus salmoides* (Largemouth Bass), family [[Centrarchidae]]
- **Fork length range of tagged fish**: 8.0 to 20.25 inches
- **Mean fork length overall**: 9.4 inches
- **Mean fork length by tag type**:
  - Staple-tagged bass: 9.92 inches (skewed smaller; tagged earlier in season)
  - Disk-dangler tagged bass: 12.0 inches (tagged later, after growth of cohort; includes large commercial-seine catches averaging 16.2 inches)
- **Tag placement anatomy**:
  - Strap/jaw tags: lower jaw, midway between epiphysis and angle of mouth (discontinued — caused poor retention/performance)
  - Staple and disk-dangler tags: placed between soft and spinous dorsal fins, midway between lateral line and mid-dorsal line
- **Tagging-related injury**: Staple tags caused disk displacement, wire abrasion, and persistent sores; disk-dangler tags occasionally caused transient sores at the knot site that healed over with scale/skin regrowth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: [[Clear Lake]] is a seminatural, 40,000-acre lake with maximum depth ~50 feet, situated at 1,325 feet elevation in northern California. Outflow via Cache Creek, a tributary of the [[Sacramento River]]; lake level partially regulated since 1915 by a dam.
- **Spatial structure**: The lake is divided into upper and lower basins separated by a constriction called "The Narrows," near which the primary sampling site (Paradise Valley beach) was located (64% of fish captured here).
- **Seasonal behavior**:
  - Two peak angling success periods: **spring** (57.6% of tag returns) and **fall** (26.1% of returns).
  - **Summer** fishing was poor for shallow-water bass; fish moved to deeper water and were caught via deep trolling or still-fishing with minnows rather than in shallows (13.1% of returns).
  - **Winter** returns were minimal (3.2%), likely due to reduced angler effort in inclement weather; seining confirmed very few fish present in shallows during winter.
  - Tag returns tracked mean daily air temperature: returns ceased below 45°F, resumed above 45°F, remained strong until >70°F triggered the summer slump, then resumed as temperatures fell back below 70°F.
- **Movement/migration**: Generally undirected movement pattern.
  - Average distance traveled: 4.5 miles (straight-line minimum estimate)
  - 17 of 80 analyzable fish moved <1 mile or not at all
  - Maximum recorded movement: ~18 miles (Point Land Ranch/Rodman Slough to Glenhaven) in 99 days
  - Fish tagged at The Narrows (64% of total) showed a directional tendency toward Clearlake Oaks/Glenhaven, likely influenced by angler headquartering patterns rather than true fish behavior bias
  - Fish tagged in Rodman Slough, Long Tule Point, and Clearlake Oaks Canal areas were comparatively sedentary (mean distance 2.3 miles), suggesting northern/shallower lake sections host more resident subpopulations
- **Forage base management**: [[Golden Shiner]] (a lake-spawning forage fish) was introduced to compensate for reduced stream flow (due to irrigation demand and climate) that had been limiting native stream-spawning forage fish populations.
- **Fishery context**: Prior work by Murphy (1951) established baseline population and angling ecology; this study's findings supported existing moderate angling regulations and suggested further relaxation might be feasible.

## Key Findings
- **Total tagged**: 441 largemouth bass overall; 333 fish tagged with double-wire tags (251 staple, 82 disk-dangler) were used in final vital-statistics analysis. Jaw/strap tags (108 total) were discontinued after August 26, 1953, due to poor performance and excluded from analysis.
- **Tag return rate**: 94 of 333 tags (28.2%) returned over three years:
  - Year 1 (0–365 days): 54 returns (57.4% of total returns)
  - Year 2 (366–730 days): 37 returns (39.4% of total returns)
  - Year 3 (>730 days): 3 returns (3.2% of total returns) — sharp dropoff attributed to natural mortality reducing the tagged population, not tag loss
- **Tag type comparison**: No statistically significant difference in return rate between staple (26.7%) and disk-dangler (32.9%) tags (chi-square, p < 0.45); data pooled for analysis.
- **Tag reliability**: Evidence strongly against tag shedding for either wire-tag type; only one unverified report of tag loss.
- **Survival/mortality estimates** (via [[Ricker Method]], Ricker 1948):
  - Estimated annual survival rate: 44%
  - Annual expectation of death (all causes): 56%
  - Rate of exploitation (fishing mortality): 20%
  - Natural mortality rate: 36%
- **Population estimate**: ~89% of tagged fish were in year classes I–II at tagging; by 1956 (year classes IV–V), only ~80 of the original 333 tagged fish were estimated to remain in the population — explaining the steep decline in third-year returns in a large 40,000-acre lake.
- **Fishery management implication**: Fishing pressure (20% exploitation) was well below natural mortality (36%), indicating no overharvest problem. Angler take could plausibly be doubled without risk to the population, though authors caution this conclusion rests on limited data.
- **Cross-validation**: Seasonal catch patterns from tag returns matched independent creel census data (Pintler 1955): 65% of 1954 annual catch occurred in spring, 7% summer, 19% fall, 9% winter — closely paralleling tag-return seasonality.
- **Comparative context**: Reported annual harvest rates for largemouth bass elsewhere ranged from 9–41.6% (Dequine & Hall 1950; Manges 1950; Fisher 1953; Carter 1955; Chance 1955; Stroud & Bitzer 1955), though those studies used jaw tags with only first-year return data, limiting direct comparability to this study's multi-year wire-tag dataset.
