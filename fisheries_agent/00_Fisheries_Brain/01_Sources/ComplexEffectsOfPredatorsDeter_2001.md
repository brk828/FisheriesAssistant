# Complex Effects of Predators: Determining Vulnerability of the Endangered June Sucker to an Introduced Predator

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered-species #predator-prey-dynamics #conservation-biology

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[June Sucker]] (*Chasmistes liorus*) is an endemic catostomid fish of [[Utah Lake]], Utah, USA. The species once numbered in the millions but now has fewer than 1000 individuals. The population consists almost entirely of large, old adults with little evidence of natural recruitment. Mean total length of larval June suckers at swim-up is 13.3 mm (SD = 1.2). Mean total length at the end of the first year is 111 mm (SD = 39.65), estimated from otolith annuli. Body depth relates to total length by the equation: body depth = 0.19(total length) – 0.5 (R² = 0.99). The species exhibits indeterminate growth. Age and maturity parameters are not reported in this document. Spawning is cued by high spring flows in the [[Provo River]].

**Habitat Associations**: June suckers spawn in the Provo River, with peak larval drift occurring in spring. Small June suckers preferentially occupy vegetated habitats in [[Utah Lake]], particularly in the presence of adult predators. The species historically dominated the Utah Lake ecosystem. Of the original seven native fish species in Utah Lake, five have disappeared, and at least 12 introduced species have become established, dramatically altering the ecosystem structure.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Larval June suckers drift downstream from spawning areas in the Provo River to Utah Lake. Recent data suggest larvae seldom survive beyond approximately 30 days post-hatch. Timing of spawning and larval emergence can be influenced by manipulation of spring flow regimes via upstream dam operations.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: The primary predator-prey interaction examined is between young June suckers and [[White Bass]] (*Morone chrysops*), an introduced predator. White bass exhibit opportunistic feeding behavior and consume small fish. Adult white bass prefer open-water environments and avoid emergent vegetation, whereas juvenile white bass (age-0) significantly select vegetated habitats (mean number in vegetation = 3.2, t = 2.49, df = 11, P < 0.0004). This habitat selection by juvenile white bass is enhanced in the presence of adult white bass (mean = 4.0, t = 4.95, df = 11, P < 0.0004), creating substantial habitat overlap with young June suckers. Other introduced predators in Utah Lake include [[Walleye]] (*Stizosteodon vitreum*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), all of which may contribute to mortality of young suckers.

**Environmental Drivers**: Water temperature influences spawning timing for both June sucker and white bass. Average hatch date for white bass is 15 April, coinciding with appropriate water temperatures. June sucker spawning occurs later in spring, cued by high flows. Growth rates of both species are assumed linear through the first growing season, with growth ceasing by mid-October when water temperatures decline significantly. Environmental conditions that favor early hatch dates and rapid growth of both species result in extended periods of vulnerability for June suckers.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: June sucker populations have declined from millions to fewer than 1000 individuals. The species is listed as endangered by the US Fish and Wildlife Service (1995). The proximate factor leading to decline appears to be an extreme decline in survival rate of young suckers, with the current population consisting almost entirely of large, old adults and little evidence of natural recruitment.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Predation by introduced fishes, particularly white bass, is hypothesized as the major cause of increased mortality for young June suckers. The vulnerability analysis reveals that young June suckers are most vulnerable to predation by young white bass immediately after swim-up, with vulnerability declining as summer progresses. Under mean growth scenarios, June suckers experience 19 days with vulnerability >0.5. The period of high vulnerability ranges from 6 days (fast June sucker growth/slow white bass growth) to 72 days (slow June sucker growth/fast white bass growth). Fast growth rates for both species result in increased vulnerability periods (30 days) compared to mean scenarios. Young white bass constitute, on average, 76% (range = 32–98%) by number of white bass population samples in Utah Lake, representing a numerous potential predator occupying the same habitats as young June suckers. Conditions favoring early hatch dates and rapid growth of both species result in extended vulnerability periods because white bass attain larger sizes before June suckers hatch.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: (1) Re-evaluate the proposed addition of emergent vegetation to the lower Provo River, as this may increase predation on young June suckers by small white bass and other vegetation-favoring introduced species such as green sunfish, and may increase survivorship of juvenile white bass, potentially increasing adult predator populations. (2) Focus conservation strategies on the first month post-hatch, when vulnerability is highest; experimental studies on older, larger June suckers may have limited relevance for improving recruitment. (3) Manipulate timing of high spring flows via upstream dam operations to promote earlier June sucker spawning, which should decrease vulnerability by reducing the size differential between young white bass and newly hatched June suckers in any given year. (4) Design predator-free grow-out areas based on estimates of relative vulnerability to various predator sizes; exclusion periods for young white bass should be determined by their relative size at June sucker spawning time, with shorter exclusion needed when white bass are relatively small (slow growth scenarios). (5) Consider all size classes of introduced species as potential predators when developing conservation strategies.

**Biological Reference Points**: Vulnerability = 0.5 serves as a threshold for comparing predation risk across different growth scenarios (analogous to LD₅₀ in toxicity studies). Mean total length at swim-up: 13.3 mm (SD = 1.2). Mean total length at end of first year: 111 mm (SD = 39.65). White bass mean total length at hatching: 2.5 mm (estimated SD = 0.5). White bass gape size relates to total length by: gape = 0.12(total length) – 1.09 (R² = 0.703, n = 127). The parabolic consumption function c(x,y) = 4(x/y)(1 – x/y) describes probability of successful predation given encounter, where prey of size 0 or y (gape size) have consumption probability 0, while prey of size y/2 have consumption probability 1.
