# Memo from Jim Brooks on Topminnow Recovery Criteria

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary
This internal U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memo (1 December 1989), authored by Jim Brooks of the Dexter Fishery Assistance Office and addressed to Sally Stefferud (Ecological Services, Phoenix), critiques the biological criteria being used to define recovery and delisting standards for the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]]. Brooks argues that existing definitions of "wild" vs. "captive" populations, "historic range," "success" of introductions, and required "site longevity" are overly simplistic and inadequate to ensure long-term species protection. He advocates for criteria that account for habitat stability, biological integrity (risk of invasive species introduction), and realistic multi-year monitoring timeframes, rather than short-term presence/absence assessments.

## Background & Description
The memo addresses management and regulatory definitions used in the recovery planning process for the [[Gila Topminnow]], a small livebearing fish native to the [[Gila River Drainage]] of Arizona and Sonora, Mexico. The species had been the subject of repeated reintroduction and monitoring efforts by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, including stocking programs into both native and non-native drainages.

Key definitional issues raised:
- **Captive vs. Wild Habitat**: Brooks proposes that a population should be classified as "wild" if both water source and delivery are natural, and the habitat (natural or manmade) is secure from catastrophic disruption. A "captive" designation applies when water source and/or delivery to the habitat are artificial (e.g., pipes, manmade channels vulnerable to flow disruption). Notably, a site can be structurally artificial (e.g., [[Walnut Spring]]) but still be classified as "wild" if it is secure.
- **Historic Range**: Brooks recommends restricting introduction efforts to the [[Gila River Drainage]] specifically, criticizing prior stocking of topminnow and [[Pupfish]] into the [[Bill Williams River Drainage]] as a mistake that should not be repeated or counted toward recovery goals. He cautions against overly narrow definitions of "historic range" limited only to documented occurrence sites, since many such historic sites failed naturally (e.g., due to desiccation), which would preclude any meaningful reestablishment efforts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
Brooks highlights several ecological factors critical to species recovery:
- **Habitat instability**: Desert spring and stream habitats in the [[Gila River Drainage]] are inherently prone to desiccation, periodic drought, and anthropogenic disturbance (e.g., habitat renovation for water storage).
- **Biological integrity**: Site suitability should account for the likelihood of introduction of competing or predatory species, referencing an approach favored by researchers **Simons and Hendrickson**, which weighs water source stability and biological integrity over simple presence/absence metrics.
- **Population size dependency**: The carrying capacity and viability of a topminnow population is dependent on the physical size and habitat complexity of a given site.
- **Long-term habitat decline**: Brooks notes that surface water habitats in the region are expected to continue declining due to both human-caused factors and natural drought cycles, meaning recovery timeframes need to be biologically realistic yet tempered by non-biological (e.g., regulatory, practical) considerations.

## Key Findings
- **Definitions of Wild vs. Captive**: A population is "wild" if water source and delivery are natural and habitat security is present (even if manmade); "captive" designations apply when artificial delivery mechanisms are involved.
- **Historic Range Recommendation**: Recovery and reintroduction efforts should be confined to the [[Gila River Drainage]]; sites established in the [[Bill Williams River Drainage]] should not count toward recovery metrics.
- **Success Criteria Critique**: Prior monitoring reports used an overly simplistic binary success/failure metric (fish present or absent) that failed to assess long-term population and habitat health.
- **Preferred Success Framework**: Success should be judged using the stability of the water source and the biological integrity of the site (risk of non-native species introductions), following the approach of Simons and Hendrickson.
- **Site Longevity Standards**: The existing 3-year monitoring baseline is deemed inadequate for assessing population viability; Brooks suggests 10 years as a more reasonable minimum, with 20–40 years being preferable, while acknowledging practical constraints.
- **Restocking Policy**: Populations that require supplemental restocking should not automatically be disqualified from recovery consideration — especially when population loss stems from habitat modification (e.g., enlarging water storage capacity) rather than fundamental site unsuitability. Brooks recommends the site be protected and restocking decisions made on a case-by-case basis.
- **Overall Assessment**: Original recovery and delisting criteria were insufficient to protect the species, but overly strict application of current proposed criteria would leave very few viable sites for consideration — underscoring a need for balanced, pragmatic recovery standards.
