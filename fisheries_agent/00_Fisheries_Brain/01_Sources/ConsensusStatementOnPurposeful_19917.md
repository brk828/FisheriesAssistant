# Consensus Building for Fish Introductions in Fisheries Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #exotic-species

## Executive Summary

This document discusses the controversial practice of fish introductions in fisheries management, ranging from stocking native species to introducing sterile or hybrid exotics. It addresses the tension between preserving natural ecosystems and managing altered aquatic environments for recreational fishing, a multibillion-dollar industry. The text advocates for a formalized consensus-building process involving scientific review to evaluate potential impacts of exotic species introductions before implementation. Key concerns include ecological impacts on resident fishes, threatened or endangered species protection, accidental introductions, and the emerging issue of genetically altered fish species.

## Background & Description

Fish introductions represent one of the most debated activities among fisheries managers. These introductions encompass several categories:

- Stocking native species into waters where similar species exist
- Introduction of exotic fish species
- Use of sterile or hybrid exotics
- Genetically altered fish species

Natural resource agencies have statutory authority to preserve, protect, enhance, and manage aquatic resources for state residents and visitors. However, these charges create contradictions when preservation and protection are interpreted as prohibiting all stocking activities, especially exotics. Fisheries managers have utilized exotic fish introductions as a viable management option for over a century, with this trend accelerating due to:

- Increased requests from organized sportsmen groups
- Advanced fishing equipment
- Technological advances in fish culture
- Alteration and degradation of aquatic ecosystems

Recreational fishing represents a multibillion-dollar industry covering a very broad base, with many local economies dependent on it. Sport fish management programs are funded by sport fishermen through license fees and taxes on fishing tackle and boats, following a user-pay concept that focuses on sought-after sport fish species whether native or not.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Resident fishes are ecologically impacted by introductions, even when introduced fishes are from progeny of that population. The document acknowledges several ecological considerations:

**Ecosystem Impacts**: When introducing new species, potential impacts to the ecosystem must be estimated for both positive and negative effects. Generally, introductions are made when most impacts are positive and there are no outstanding negative ones.

**Threatened and Endangered Species**: If threatened or endangered species can be impacted, introductions should not be made. Designation as threatened or endangered gives recognition to non-game species, though legal constraints generally result in inadequate balance being placed on management activities favoring only protected species at the expense of sport species.

**Accidental Introductions**: Accidental introduction of exotic species (those not made by resource agencies) has caused the most environmental problems. State agencies generally have difficulty enforcing regulations to prohibit these accidental introductions, as they are difficult to prevent.

**Altered Ecosystems**: There is tension between managing ecosystems in a pure natural state versus an altered state for enjoyment of residents and non-residents. The document notes that alteration and degradation of aquatic ecosystems contribute to increased pressure for fish introductions.

## Key Findings

**Opposition to Introductions**: Introductions are opposed by individuals who haven't been involved in the planning process and by ecological purists who advocate no change of any kind for any reason, viewing all exotics as "garbage in the environment."

**Management Tools**: The use of sterile or hybrid fish has provided additional management options. However, it can be several decades before full impacts of an introduction are assessed, if they can be assessed at all.

**Historical Context**: Much relevant data is unpublished and not available to many state biologists. Many introductions were made without proper pre-evaluation due to this limited information sharing.

**Success and Failure**: Planned introductions that have been successful and beneficial are generally not recognized except by a few fisheries managers. Introductions that are not beneficial are generally disasters that are not reversible and have created management problems.

**Need for Formalized Process**: The document advocates for a more formalized process where information can be easily assessed between academicians, ichthyologists, and fish biogeographers who understand fish life histories and food webs. An [[Introduced Fish Section]] with guidelines and a transfer system would ensure decisions are made scientifically and managers don't have to reinvent the process.

**Timely Decision-Making**: Decisions need to be made in a timely manner. Although not completely free from political pressure, most managers use available best biological data when making decisions that will meet management objectives.

**Target System Evaluation**: Evaluation should be limited to those systems where some assurance of meeting objectives exists. Unique waters should be left in their natural state insofar as possible, even if all the "niches" seem not to be filled.

**Regulatory Approach**: An alternative approach is to develop lists of species that are prohibited, rather than focusing solely on preventing accidental introductions. This shifts the burden of risk and provides clearer guidance for industry.

**Genetic Engineering Concerns**: Some genetically altered fish species are nearly impossible to evaluate, as this technique provides the ability to alter species faster than evaluations can be made of the "new" species.

**Consensus Building**: A formalized critical scientific review and consensus-building process would be helpful to predict impact of exotics before introduction. An improved process should involve all affected entities in decision-making, providing opportunity for input based on sound scientific principles.

**Management Accountability**: Managers who make decisions are responsible for the outcomes, either good or bad. A formalized process ensures care is taken, though the document acknowledges that recently made introductions remain to be fully evaluated.
