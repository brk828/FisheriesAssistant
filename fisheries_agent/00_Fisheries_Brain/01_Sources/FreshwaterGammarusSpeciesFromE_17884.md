# Freshwater Gammarus Species of the Gammarus pulex-Group (Europe, North Africa, Asia Minor)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gammaridae

*Note: the source is a taxonomic revision of freshwater amphipod crustaceans, not a fish study. The taxon tag is applied accordingly.*

## Executive Summary

This 1977 revision by [[Gordan S. Karaman]] and [[Sjouk Pinkster]] treats the freshwater members of the amphipod genus [[Gammarus]] that belong to the [[Gammarus pulex-Group]]. The authors describe a complex of closely related, partly intersterile species whose morphological differences are small but stable. The paper provides a key to the (sub)species, diagnoses and descriptions of new taxa (including [[Gammarus inberbus]], [[Gammarus laticoxalis]], [[Gammarus crenulatus]], [[Gammarus osellai]], [[Gammarus frater]] and [[Gammarus pseudosyriacus]]), material lists, distribution maps and ecological notes. The study combines morphology, hybridization experiments and distributional data.

## Background & Description

The genus Gammarus is among the amphipod genera with the most epigean freshwater taxa, with more than 100 freshwater species recognised in the study area. The genus has a complicated nomenclatural history: [[Rivulogammarus]] was rejected under the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature, and Gammarus remains the oldest valid name for freshwater forms. Freshwater species are divided into three artificial groups: the [[Gammarus roeseli-Group]] (with dorsal carinae), the [[Gammarus balcanicus-Group]] (no dorsal carina, poorly setose pereiopods 3-4 and uropod 3) and the Gammarus pulex-group (no dorsal carina, dense setation on pereiopods 3-4 and uropod 3). The pulex-group is the subject of this part of the revision.

Key diagnostic characters include:
- Structure of the mandibular palp (a stable character)
- Presence of calceoli on the male antenna 2 (variable in some species)
- Dorsal armature and setation of the urosome (variable)
- Shape of epimeral plates, uropod 3 ramus ratios and telson armature

The type species, [[Gammarus pulex]], was figured completely, and other taxa are illustrated only where they differ from it. Sexual dimorphism is marked: females are smaller, have shorter appendages, lack calceoli and brush-like antenna 2 setation, and have reduced gnathopod spination.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat range:** Species occupy streams, rivers, springs, lakes, wells, subterranean waters and caves. [[Gammarus pulex]] is common in middle and lower reaches of streams and tolerates organic pollution; it has been recorded from mineral springs with salinities up to 25,370 mg/l.
- **Upper-reach species:** [[Gammarus fossarum]] inhabits the faster, cooler upper reaches and can be displaced downstream by competitors.
- **Intermediate species:** [[Gammarus wautieri]] occupies intermediate microhabitats between G. fossarum and G. pulex.
- **Lake and mountain species:** [[Gammarus lacustris]] usually inhabits mountain and glacier lakes and is susceptible to temperatures above 20 °C.
- **Calcium-rich waters:** [[Gammarus gauthieri]] appears to prefer waters with raised calcium content and is interpreted as a relict of a formerly wider Iberian and North African distribution.
- **Subterranean adaptation:** Several populations show blindness or reduced eyes, for example [[Gammarus pulex polonensis]] (subterranean waters in Poland) and [[Gammarus vignai]] and [[Gammarus microps]] (cave species from Turkey and Morocco).
- **Extreme habitats:** [[Gammarus pseudosyriacus]] is found in wells and oases where temperatures reach 34 °C, a temperature fatal to almost all other gammarids.

## Key Findings

1. **Diversity and grouping:** More than 100 freshwater Gammarus species are recognised; the revision covers the pulex-group only, with the other groups planned for later papers.
2. **Reproductive isolation:** Hybridization experiments confirmed that [[Gammarus pulex]], [[Gammarus fossarum]], [[Gammarus wautieri]] and [[Gammarus monspeliensis]] are good species. Morphologically distinct forms such as [[Gammarus pulex gallicus]] and [[Gammarus pulex araurensis]] interbreed freely and are treated as subspecies of G. pulex because geographical barriers maintain their identity.
3. **Varieties within species:** Many morphologically distinct populations of G. fossarum are mere varieties of one species, since no clear geographical barrier was found (Goedmakers, 1972).
4. **Problem of "blind" species:** Eyeless or reduced-eye forms are either new species (G. vignai, G. microps) or partially isolated subspecies (G. pulex polonensis).
5. **Sympatric species:** G. fossarum and G. p. pulex can be morphologically very similar (e.g., G. fossarum and G. wautieri) yet remain reproductively isolated.
6. **Revised status of names:** Several earlier names were reassigned. [[Gammarus komareki]] records from Romania were shown to belong to [[Gammarus arduus]]; [[Gammarus wigrensis]] was shown to be immature G. lacustris; G. fossarum bodanicus is subadult G. lacustris; and the Polish forms previously called G. wautieri belong to G. fossarum.
7. **Syriacus confusion:** The original description of [[Gammarus syriacus]] by Chevreux mixed two forms; a neotype was selected and [[Gammarus laborifer]] and G. pseudosyriacus were separated as new species.
8. **New species described:** Gammarus inberbus (Lake Issyk-Kul, Kazakhstan; known only from that lake), G. laticoxalis (Syria; characteristic dilated first coxal plate), G. laborifer (Syria, Lebanon, Turkey), G. pseudosyriacus (Near East), G. osellai (central Asia Minor), G. frater (SW Bulgaria), G. crenulatus (Greece, the only pulex-group species with crenulated metasome margins), G. birsteini (nomen novum) and G. vignai.
9. **Key to species:** An identification key based on adult males separates the (sub)species mentioned, using characters such as eye size, antenna 2 flagellum shape, epimeral corners, urosome elevations, setation of pereiopods 3-7 and uropod 3 ratios.
10. **Material base:** The study examined material from major European and Asian museums and the second author's fieldwork. Over 2,800 samples were examined for G. p. pulex alone, more than 900 for G. fossarum, and about 300 for G. lacustris.
11. **Distribution maps:** Three maps show the distribution of 10, 7 and 11 (sub)species respectively across Europe, Asia Minor and northern Africa.
12. **Limitations:** Identification of several Asian species was hampered by inaccessible Russian literature and type material (e.g., Martinov, Derjavin, Birstein), so some names may be synonyms.
