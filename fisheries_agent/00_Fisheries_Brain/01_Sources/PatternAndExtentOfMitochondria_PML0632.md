# Patterns and Extent of Mitochondrial DNA Introgression Among Members of the Gila robusta Complex (Cyprinidae) from the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines hybridization among three endangered minnows of the [[Colorado River]] system, [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), using mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) markers. The authors combine [[Restriction Endonuclease Analysis]] (RE) and [[Single-Stranded Conformation Polymorphism]] (SSCP) analysis of the [[Cytochrome B Gene]] (cytb) and [[NADH Dehydrogenase Subunit 2]] (ND2) genes. Across 384 individuals from the middle and upper basin, only *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* mtDNA was found. No *G. robusta* mtDNA was detected. The authors argue that this pattern reflects introgression that predates human influence, and they make recommendations for captive broodstock management and reintroduction.

Document type: a major research article (Genetics, development, and morphology section), with three tables, two line-art figures, and a literature list. The manuscript is dated January 2000 in the header. The work was supported by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Contract #4-EC-40-15700).

## Background & Description

The three focal species are large-bodied, endangered minnows native to the Colorado River. Earlier morphological and allozyme studies documented hybridization among them, which raised concern that human modification of the river had eroded species distinctiveness. The three forms have also been implicated in the origin of a hybrid taxon, *G. seminuda* ([[DeMarais et al. 1992]]).

Hybridization among cyprinids is common and has been treated in two ways in the literature. It can reduce diversity through genetic swamping, especially when habitats are disturbed or one species is rare. It can also transfer adaptations or generate new forms. The authors position this work within the broader [[Introgressive Hybridization]] literature.

The study had three aims:
- identify diagnostic mtDNA features for each taxon;
- quantify the extent of hybridization among them in the upper Colorado River drainage;
- provide information for management of these endangered fishes.

Taxonomic note: the source spells the genus with the common Western usage *Gila*; OCR errors have been corrected silently.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and abundance.** *G. robusta* was relatively common in the upper Colorado drainage. *G. cypha* was locally abundant in canyon-bound reaches. *G. elegans* occurred in backwater and eddy habitats of the mainstem Colorado, Green, and Gila rivers. The three species have been widely distributed across much of the basin, so they co-occur over large areas. This makes hybridization hard to study.

**Rarity of *G. elegans*.** The species is endangered and was considered extirpated from much of the upper basin. A hatchery stock is maintained at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH) and is used for stocking throughout the basin. Because of this, it is difficult to distinguish "introduced" from "wild" individuals. The hatchery stock has only one mtDNA haplotype and was founded by as few as three or four individuals.

**Allopatric and sympatric definitions.** Because no truly unaffected reference sites exist, the authors define:
- *allopatric*: sites where the three taxa never or rarely co-occurred;
- *sympatric*: sites where the taxa and/or putative hybrids were taken regularly.

Allopatric reference populations were drawn from the Gila and Bill Williams rivers (*G. robusta*), the confluence of the [[Little Colorado River]] (*G. cypha*), and [[Lake Mohave]] (*G. elegans*).

**Historical biogeography.** Cooler water temperatures likely prevented persistence of these fishes in the upper Colorado River during the Pleistocene. Populations there were probably founded recently by individuals moving upstream. As the species expanded northward, they would have come into contact in the Grand Canyon, which likely drove hybridization.

**Isolated populations.** *G. robusta jordani* in the [[Pahranagat Valley]] has been isolated for approximately 14,000 years. Isolated samples from the upper [[Little Colorado River]] have been separated from the mainstream for approximately 250,000 years.

## Key Findings

### Methods

- **Sampling:** Samples came from lower (allopatric) and upper (mostly sympatric) basin localities. Lower basin sites included Gila-system streams (*G. robusta*), Lake Mohave (*G. elegans*), and the Little Colorado River confluence (*G. cypha*). Upper basin sites included rivers and canyon reaches across Colorado, Utah, Wyoming, and Nevada, including the Moapa and Virgin rivers (*G. seminuda*) and the Pahranagat Valley (*G. robusta jordani*).
- **Molecular markers:** Genomic DNA was extracted from muscle tissue. Restriction enzymes AccI, HinfI, and ScrFI were chosen from computer searches of cytb sequences. Each species differed from the others by at least four RE site changes, which makes misidentification by convergence unlikely. SSCP analysis of four cytb and ND2 fragments was used when RE analysis failed. Each SSCP variant was sequenced to confirm species assignment.

### Results

- **Lower basin (allopatric) samples:**
  - All individuals from eight *G. robusta* populations in the Gila River drainage showed typical *G. robusta* RE patterns.
  - Ten bonytail chub from Lake Mohave carried *G. elegans* RE patterns. (The Discussion states 16 individuals from Lake Mohave; the source is inconsistent.)
  - Of 31 *G. cypha* from the Little Colorado River confluence, 28 had typical *G. cypha* haplotypes and three had *G. elegans* haplotypes.
- **Middle and upper basin (RE):** Of 231 individuals analyzed by RE, 34 carried *G. elegans* patterns, and 197 carried typical *G. cypha* haplotypes. No *G. robusta* haplotype was found.
- **Virgin River:** All specimens of *G. seminuda* from the Virgin River drainage carried *G. elegans* mtDNA. (The text gives 32 specimens in one section and 25 in another.)
- **Moapa River:** All 16 *G. seminuda* carried *G. elegans* mtDNA.
- **Pahranagat Valley (*G. robusta jordani*):** Carried *G. cypha* mtDNA only. Neither *G. elegans* nor *G. robusta* mtDNA was found.
- **SSCP (additional samples):** SSCP analysis added 153 individuals, for a total of 384 specimens examined by SSCP and/or RE. Where both methods were used, results agreed across genes, fragments, and RE patterns.
- **Total *G. elegans* mtDNA in the upper basin:** 50 individuals (41 *G. seminuda* from the Moapa and Virgin rivers; 2 from Desolation Canyon; 1 from Cataract Canyon; 6 from Westwater Canyon). The authors estimate the frequency of *G. elegans* haplotypes at approximately 5–10% in the upper basin.
- **Absence of *G. robusta* mtDNA:** Not found in 384 upper-basin individuals, including large samples from morphologically "pure" *G. robusta* populations (e.g., Debeque–Rifle area and Gunnison River, N=55). It was also absent from relatively small, isolated drainages historically inhabited only by *G. robusta*, such as Chevelon Creek and the San Juan and San Rafael rivers.

### Interpretation and Conclusions

1. **Extensive introgression:** The complete absence of *G. robusta* mtDNA in the upper basin indicates that introgression has been extensive and predates human influence. The authors consider that human activity cannot explain it, because replacement of *G. robusta* haplotypes would take far longer than humans have occupied the region.
2. **Possible explanations:** The pattern may reflect extensive in situ introgression or postglacial colonization by individuals of mixed ancestry. The authors propose that a founder effect, directional introgression, and/or selection may account for the loss of *G. robusta* mtDNA. Distinguishing these requires experiments on the fitness of hybrid classes in different environments.
3. **Comparative cases:** The authors compare the pattern to *Luxilus chrysocephalus* in the eastern United States, where populations carry only *L. cornutus* mtDNA, and to European house mice (*Mus musculus*), where Scandinavian populations carry only *M. domesticus* mtDNA. Both cases are interpreted as remnants of past hybridization followed by range shifts.
4. **Taxonomic recognition:** Because *G. r. jordani* has persisted as a unique mosaic of characters (allozymes from *G. cypha* and *G. robusta*, with *G. cypha* mtDNA), the authors argue it represents a separate hybrid taxon and should be recognized as *G. jordani*.
5. **Remnant *G. elegans*:** The relatively high frequency of *G. elegans* haplotypes in the upper basin is notable, since the species was presumed extirpated there. The authors recommend morphological and allozyme examination to determine whether these individuals are pure *G. elegans*. If they are, they should be incorporated into captive management programs.

### Management Recommendations

- Make greater efforts to obtain more specimens from the upper basin for the captive management program, since they may carry genetically important variation.
- Because of the hybridization potential among the three species, carefully consider the numbers of individuals stocked and the stocking sites, to minimize disruption of the "precarious balance" among these fishes.
- Add individuals to the broodstock to enhance genetic variation, since the DNFH stock has very low diversity. Incorporate wild individuals occasionally captured in Lake Mohave and the upper basin.
- Select reintroduction sites carefully, and base stocking numbers on past abundance at each location.
- Avoid adding large numbers of hatchery *G. elegans*, because this could shift the genetic composition of the complex and harm *G. cypha* and *G. robusta* as well.
