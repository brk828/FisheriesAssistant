# Biological Opinion for Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Partnership Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species-act #habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal Section 7 Biological Opinion issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Arizona Ecological Services Field Office]] on February 18, 1993, responding to a consultation request from the [[Bureau of Land Management]] [[Yuma District Office]]. The opinion addresses the [[Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Partnership Program]], a multi-faceted project combining recreational fishing habitat enhancement with endangered native fish recovery efforts on [[Lake Havasu]], a mainstem reservoir of the lower [[Colorado River]]. The Service concluded that the proposed action is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) or [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), nor adversely modify proposed critical habitat for the bonytail chub. The Service concurred with BLM's finding of no effect to [[Bald Eagle]], [[Brown Pelican]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], and [[Yuma Clapper Rail]]. The project includes installation of artificial fish habitat structures in 42 coves, construction of shoreline recreational facilities, and establishment of up to ten native fish rearing coves to release approximately 60,000 razorback suckers and bonytail chubs into [[Lake Havasu]] by 2003.

## Background & Description

### Species Descriptions

**[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): Listed as endangered April 23, 1980. The bonytail chub belongs to genus *Gila* along with the [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*) and [[Virgin River Chub]] (*G. seminuda*). Its distinctive feature is an extremely thin caudal peduncle and small head with predorsal hump. Once found throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]], recent captures are limited to six locations. In the lower basin, bonytail chubs are taken annually in [[Lake Mohave]] and a small population persists in [[Lake Havasu]]. Successful reproduction and recruitment have not been positively documented in lower basin reservoirs for many years. Eleven juvenile bonytail chubs have been captured from [[Lake Mohave]] since 1981; one from the 1978 year class was likely wild-born, though most are believed to result from hatchery stocking programs initiated in 1981. Adult bonytail chubs have been reported from pools and eddies in large rivers. In [[Lake Mohave]], captures are from lacustrine areas, not river-like habitat below [[Hoover Dam]]. Diet includes floating food items (terrestrial and aquatic insects in riverine habitats), plankton, stocked rainbow trout fry, and algae in reservoirs. Young chubs likely feed on plankton, algae, chironomid larvae, and mayfly nymphs, shifting to floating food as they mature.

**[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Listed as endangered November 22, 1991. First described in 1861, this large catostomid fish has a distinctive sharp raised keel behind the head. It belongs to the monotypic genus *Xyrauchen*. The [[Lake Mohave]] population shows little evidence of intergradation with other native suckers and is genetically diverse, making it critically important for species survival. Once found throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]] where it was abundant or common, the species now persists in small numbers in approximately 1,200 kilometers of the Colorado, Green, San Juan, and Yampa Rivers and portions of [[Lake Powell]] in the upper basin. In the lower basin, razorback suckers occur from the [[Grand Canyon]] to [[Imperial Reservoir]]. The largest population, estimated at 60,000 individuals, is in [[Lake Mohave]]. Reintroduction efforts in the lower Colorado, Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers have not yet established self-sustaining populations. Natural spawning is widely documented with eggs and larvae collected from both upper and lower basin populations, but recruitment of juveniles has been extremely limited. In the [[Green River]], the last successful recruitment is estimated to have occurred in the 1960s. Extensive work on [[Lake Mohave]] and other lower basin habitats between 1963 and 1988 found little evidence of recruitment. Some wild-bred juveniles have been captured in irrigation canals below [[Lake Havasu]] in the 1980s-present, though spawning location is unknown. Adults occupy diverse habitats including main channel flatwater areas, sand bars, nearshore runs, backwaters, eddies, and open water. Flooded shallow backwaters may be important feeding areas for adults and recently hatched young. Young fish utilize backwaters, shorelines, and shallow areas for at least the first few weeks before moving offshore or downstream.

### Consultation History

The BLM requested a threatened and endangered species list for [[Lake Havasu]] on June 16, 1992; the Service responded August 7, 1992. A biological evaluation dated July 21, 1992, concluded the project would not affect the razorback sucker. The Service did not concur, informing BLM that a "may affect" situation also existed for the bonytail chub. Subsequent meetings between the Service, BLM, and other agency representatives led to inclusion of a program to increase existing populations of bonytail chub and razorback sucker in [[Lake Havasu]] as part of the proposed project. On July 10, 1992, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] requested concurrence for construction of a recreational facility at the [[Havasu Pumping Plant Inlet]]. The Service concurred July 14, 1992, contingent upon displaying posters identifying razorback sucker and bonytail chub as endangered with instructions for anglers who catch these fish, and discouraging use and release of live bait fish. The 90-day formal consultation period began January 7, 1993.

### Proposed Action

The BLM and cooperators propose to install artificial fish habitats in 42 coves on [[Lake Havasu]]: 67,482 bass shelters, 64,724 catfish houses, 3,484 Fish 'N Forests, 1,950 tire towers, and 11,800 brush bundles. Structures would be placed adjacent to rooted aquatic vegetation in 10-25 feet of water, treating approximately 90 acres of lake bottom (0.4% of the lake). The project includes shoreline access enhancement through parking lots, trails, fishing docks, restrooms, and fish cleaning stations. Facilities and structures would be installed over 5-10 years as funding becomes available from public agencies and private groups.

The proposed action also includes creation of up to ten native fish rearing coves in [[Lake Havasu]] with the objective of releasing approximately 30,000 razorback suckers and 30,000 bonytail chubs by 2003 to restore depleted populations. Juvenile fish from federal or state hatcheries would be placed in barriered, renovated coves or coves adjacent to [[Lake Havasu]] and grown to 300 millimeters before release into the reservoir. At 300 mm, native fish are believed beyond vulnerability to predation by non-native fish. Development would occur over five years with adaptive management to incorporate new information. Monitoring to locate released fish is part of the action.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River Basin]] encompasses seven states and a portion of Mexico, divided at [[Lees Ferry, Arizona]] into upper basin states (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming) and lower basin states (Arizona, California, Nevada). The endangered fish species considered were once found throughout the basin. Their decline is attributed to habitat alterations and introduction of non-native fish species.

### Habitat Alterations

Basin rivers have been significantly affected by construction of dams and impoundments, water diversions, and channelization projects. Rivers have been rendered seasonally dry or had normal seasonal flows altered. Floodplains have been shut off from rivers or developed for human uses. Daily, weekly, and seasonal flow levels are determined by water or power needs rather than runoff or surface flow. Warm, sediment-laden water enters large reservoirs; water released downstream is cold and clear. Temperatures and flows below large dams like [[Flaming Gorge]] or [[Hoover Dam]] may make riverine habitats less suitable for bonytail chub and razorback sucker. Continued water use and reuse increases salinity and affects water quality downstream. Pesticides and other contaminants are carried in return flows from agriculture, municipal, and industrial uses. These changes have significantly reduced and fragmented historic native fish habitats.

### Non-Native Species Impacts

Introduction of non-native fish and other organisms, both deliberately and accidentally, have contributed to native fish decline throughout the basin. Non-native organisms may compete directly or indirectly with native species. Predation on native species, especially early life history stages, by non-natives is well documented. Non-natives may be sources of new disease or parasite introductions. Physical changes to basin rivers likely contribute to non-native success by creating habitats more suitable for non-native than native fish fauna.

### Population Status

Populations of bonytail chub and razorback sucker have been eliminated from much of their former ranges. Both physical and biological habitat changes are responsible. Surviving populations are in areas where physical habitat remains somehow suitable, at least for some life stages. Even in these areas, non-native fish presence has adversely affected spawning and recruitment.

The bonytail chub's status in the wild is precarious. The recovery plan states this species is the most endangered native fish in the basin. The recovery plan short-term goal is to prevent extinction of the species from the wild. Populations in the upper basin are very small and isolated. Successful recruitment has not been documented for many years. Areas of suspected recruitment have hybrid species present and identification of young is difficult. In the lower basin, the very small [[Lake Havasu]] population is not known to have reproduced successfully in many years. [[Lake Mohave]] has what is believed the largest remaining population, but it is not self-sustaining. Both lower basin populations and probably most upper basin populations are dominated by old adults. Bonytail chubs are relatively long-lived, but existing populations are likely to reach senescence within a few years. Successful hatchery rearing and small refugia efforts exist, but habitat restoration for self-sustaining wild populations has not progressed.

The razorback sucker was recently listed as endangered after unsuccessful recovery efforts. The species is still declining throughout its range. Numerically, razorback sucker populations are significantly larger than bonytail chub, but numbers give a false sense of security. The large [[Lake Mohave]] population consists of old fish close to senescence. Researchers estimate the population will likely be lost or significantly reduced in 10 years. Successful recruitment has not been documented in recent years. Upper basin populations are already small. In the lower basin, [[Colorado River]] populations outside [[Lake Mohave]] are small and not self-sustaining. Introduced razorback suckers in the Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers are not self-sustaining. Developing strategies to improve larval and juvenile survival is critical to species survival. Razorback suckers have been raised in hatcheries and refugia, but little progress has been made toward providing habitats for self-sustaining wild populations.

In summary, both species' status is poor. Little or no natural habitat remains for wild populations. Both species have shown they can survive in reservoir habitats. Due to skewed age structure from years of unsuccessful recruitment, remaining wild populations are likely lost in the near term. Some populations may already be below reproductive viability levels due to low numbers. Species survival depends on maintenance of existing habitat and providing for successful recruitment. Interim steps may be necessary to ensure survival while larger questions are addressed.

## Key Findings

### Direct and Indirect Effects

Placement of fish habitat improvement structures in 42 coves will affect non-native fish populations in treated coves. Cover is currently limiting; structures will attract fish from adjacent areas. Spatial dispersal within coves would be affected as structures attract and concentrate fish. Angler use of treated coves is assumed to increase, resulting in higher fishing pressure and likely increased catch rates near structures. This increased catch may reduce fish concentrations at structures, with overall effects on total cove fish populations. Species not sought by anglers would likely maintain higher local concentrations.

Fish population sizes in treated coves may also be affected. Juvenile fish in areas with sufficient cover are extremely subject to predation. Additional cover at structures may increase juvenile survival, though increased predatory fish populations may counteract some of this potential. Reduced predatory fish levels elsewhere in coves as a result of concentrating fish at structures may influence juvenile survival in untreated areas. Structures will provide additional food resources (algae and invertebrates), building an increased prey base, though enhanced populations of crayfish and crustaceans may be harder to catch.

Adult bonytail chubs and razorback suckers may or may not be attracted to increased cover offered by structures. If attracted, there may be increased risk of angler capture. If adults remain near structures to spawn, potential for predation on eggs, larvae, and juveniles might not differ significantly from present levels. If young endangered fish utilize structures, they may be at greater risk from higher predatory fish levels around structures. Reduction in predatory fish populations away from structures may also reduce predation on young endangered fish in those areas.

Construction of recreational facilities associated with the project is likely to have only minor impacts to bonytail chub and razorback sucker. Providing increased fishing access increases risk of anglers catching these fish. Some local habitat changes through shading or additional cover may provide changes to fish population concentrations. Since all recreational facilities are at treated coves, this effect is additive. Fish species not sought by anglers, especially carp, are known to concentrate around shoreside recreational facilities. Carp are known to consume razorback sucker eggs.

### Native Fish Augmentation

The project includes provisions to reintroduce 60,000 bonytail chubs and razorback suckers to [[Lake Havasu]] through operation of rearing coves. Fish would be hatchery-born from parent stock taken from [[Lake Mohave]] and raised to approximately 300 mm in coves before release into the reservoir. Stocked and released individuals would be fully protected under the Act. This action does not include designation for an experimental population. Augmentation of existing bonytail chub and razorback sucker populations in [[Lake Havasu]] will provide a source of fish for reintroduction efforts, research, and other management or recovery programs.

### Incidental Take

The primary purpose of the proposed project is to create habitat for non-native sport fish in [[Lake Havasu]]. Non-native fish species have been identified as causing native fish decline through competition and predation. Available habitat in [[Lake Havasu]] already contains these non-native fish species. Local densities likely vary tremendously in response to local habitat conditions. The proposed action would alter non-native fish densities in 42 coves. Proposed improvements would cover approximately 0.4% of the reservoir (90 acres of 20,400 acre reservoir). Changes in non-native fish density could extend beyond 90 acres. The degree to which these density changes would affect bonytail chub and razorback sucker adults and young is not known.

Conversion of up to ten coves for bonytail chub and razorback sucker rearing facilities will require cove renovation to remove as many non-native fish as possible to minimize predation risk on young native fish. This project component provides rearing habitat for young native fish that is extremely rare in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Development of rearing coves was intended to offset potential adverse effects to bonytail chub and razorback sucker from placement and operation of fish enhancement structures.

Rearing coves will receive large numbers of young bonytail chubs and razorback suckers from hatcheries. Some percentage of mortality will occur due to transport and stocking effects. This mortality is not considered take for this project by the Service. Mortality of young fish in rearing coves from natural causes, including some predation by non-native fish, will also occur. The percentage of stocked fish lost from rearing coves is not known but is estimated by BLM to be 85%.

Angler take of bonytail chubs and razorback suckers may increase over the project life. Populations of both species in the reservoir will increase significantly with releases from rearing coves. Released fish may move to reservoir areas used by anglers. If fish enhancement structures attract bonytail chubs or razorback suckers, there may be accidental taking of these species in treated areas. The existing angler take level is very low. The degree to which it could increase over the project life is not known. Fish taken by anglers may or may not be returned alive, making mortality assessment difficult. The Service sets a limit of twenty each of bonytail chubs and razorback suckers taken by anglers per calendar year. Individuals caught and released alive and those killed by anglers are both included in the limits set.

### Reasonable and Prudent Measures

The Service believes the following reasonable and prudent measures are necessary and appropriate to minimize incidental take:

1. Measures will be taken to inform anglers using [[Lake Havasu]] of the presence of two endangered fish species in the reservoir.
2. Measures to reduce potential mortality of any endangered fish taken by anglers will be implemented.
3. Measures will be taken at rearing coves to minimize potential sources of mortality to young endangered fish.

### Terms and Conditions

To implement reasonable and prudent measure 1: Information about bonytail chub and razorback sucker presence in [[Lake Havasu]] will be posted at recreational facilities used by anglers around the reservoir (displays, posters, pamphlets, or other appropriate formats).

To implement reasonable and prudent measure 2: Posted information will contain clear photographs or drawings to assist angler identification; information will instruct anglers to immediately release any bonytail chub or razorback sucker caught and provide proper handling and disposal of fishing gear; information on where anglers may report catching and releasing a bonytail chub or razorback sucker will be provided.
