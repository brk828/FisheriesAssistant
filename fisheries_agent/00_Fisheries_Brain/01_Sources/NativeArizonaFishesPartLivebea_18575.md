# Native Arizona Fishes Part I - Livebearers

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Poecilidae #conservation #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This article documents the native livebearer fishes of Arizona, specifically two subspecies of *[[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]]*: the [[Gila Topminnow]] and the [[Sonora Topminnow]]. The [[Gila Topminnow]] has experienced catastrophic population decline from widespread distribution throughout the [[Gila River]] basin to only two naturally occurring populations by 1969. The primary cause is identified as competition and displacement by the introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*[[Gambusia affinis]]*), which was intentionally spread for mosquito control beginning approximately 40 years prior to publication. Conservation efforts include captive breeding stocks and attempted reintroductions.

## Background & Description

The family [[Poecilidae]] is predominantly tropical, with largest concentrations in Mexico, Central America, and northern South America. Only the genus *Poeciliopsis* is native to Arizona's fish fauna.

**Gila Topminnow** (*P. o. occidentalis*):
- Historical range: entire [[Gila River]] basin from Frisco Hot Springs, New Mexico west to Yuma, from [[Santa Cruz River]] in northern Sonora, Mexico to [[Tonto Creek]] north of [[Roosevelt Lake]]
- Current distribution (1969): only two spring-fed areas - one in [[Santa Cruz County]], one north of Bylas on [[San Carlos Apache Reservation]]

**Sonora Topminnow** (*P. o. sonoriensis*):
- Range: [[Rio Yaqui]] basin, [[San Bernardino Ranch]] southwest of Douglas in [[Cochise County]]
- Status: presently abundant in restricted range

**Physical identification**: Males distinguished by relatively long anal fin. Female [[Gambusia]] identified by dark tear-drop marking below eye, absent in *Poeciliopsis*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical habitat**: smaller creeks, marshes, larger backwaters throughout southern [[Colorado River]] drainage. As late as the 1930s, *Poeciliopsis* was considered one of the commonest fishes in the region, long after initial habitat changes from water diversion and drainage.

**Habitat degradation timeline**:
- Late 1800s: initial decline from water diversion, creek drying, marsh drainage
- 1930s: still abundant despite habitat changes
- ~1929 (40 years before 1969): [[Gambusia affinis]] established
- Rapid decline following *Gambusia* spread through natural movement and intentional transfers by agencies for mosquito control

**Competition mechanisms**: *Poeciliopsis* and *Gambusia* are similar in size, shape, and habits. Competition likely involves food resources and living space. *Gambusia* characteristics:
- Exceedingly aggressive and prolific
- Tolerates wide range of environments
- Known to suppress population growth of larger species (goldfish, [[Largemouth Bass]]) by preying on young

**Documented displacement events**:
- [[Arivaca Creek]] (southwest of Tucson): native species abundant in 1957, totally replaced by *Gambusia* by 1959
- Artesian springs near Safford: *Poeciliopsis* abundant 1962, *Gambusia* appeared 1963, *Poeciliopsis* gone by 1966

## Key Findings

1. **Catastrophic population collapse**: [[Gila Topminnow]] declined from widespread abundance throughout [[Gila River]] basin to two natural populations, representing near-extinction in wild.

2. **Primary threat identified**: Introduction and spread of [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquitofish) is the proximate cause of rapid decline. Changes were "rapid and complete" following *Gambusia* establishment.

3. **Timeline of decline**: Species remained common through 1930s despite habitat degradation, but experienced precipitous decline after *Gambusia* establishment ~40 years prior to 1969.

4. **Reintroduction challenges**: Attempts to re-establish *Poeciliopsis* in native range met with "varying success." Failures attributed to:
   - Flash floods in smaller creeks
   - Pesticide blowover in metropolitan areas
   - Reintroduction of *Gambusia* by public health agencies for mosquito control

5. **Conservation status (1969)**: Three or four isolated populations considered "safe" for present. Breeding stocks maintained at [[Arizona State University]], [[University of Arizona]], and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] Deer Valley office.

6. **Conservation advantage**: Livebearing reproductive habits and tolerance for smaller spaces make *Poeciliopsis* relatively simple to maintain in captivity.

7. **Management goal**: Protection of vanishing species with plans for reintroduction into suitable springs and smaller creeks to perpetuate as part of Arizona's native heritage.
