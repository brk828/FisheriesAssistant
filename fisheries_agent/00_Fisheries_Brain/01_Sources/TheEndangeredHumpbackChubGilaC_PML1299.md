# Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in Grand Canyon: A Review of Past Studies and Suggestions for Future Research

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft report from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Research Branch, Phoenix), prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Upper Colorado Region) in February 1990, reviews the grey literature and agency data on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the Colorado River and its tributaries between [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and Lake Mead. The review was undertaken during the [[Section 7 Consultation]] on dam operations. Its objectives were to review the ecology of the species, compile existing data into a computerized relational database, and identify knowledge gaps. The report concludes that data are sparse, statistically limited by rarity and gear inconsistencies, and that the species' persistence depends heavily on the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) population and on the thermal and flow regime created by dam operations.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Gila cypha* (Humpback Chub), a cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River basin. The species was first described by Miller (1946) from specimens collected in Grand Canyon.
- **Listing history:** Officially designated as endangered on March 11, 1967 (Federal Register vol. 34, p. 4001), owing to its restricted, fragmented distribution, small population size, and threats from water development.
- **Dam context:** Construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in April 1963, with hydroelectric production beginning the following year. Early Fish and Wildlife Service impact evaluation focused on sport fisheries and mentioned native suckers and the [[Colorado Squawfish]] only in passing.
- **Regulatory history:** Reclamation requested Section 7 consultation in 1977. A proposed peaking-power operation was dropped in 1979 after public opposition. In 1982 the Commissioner of Reclamation directed study of operational impacts, which led to the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program.
- **Scope:** The report covers only the humpback chub, but the Department states this does not imply lack of concern for other threatened or endangered species in the region.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and hydrology**
- The study reach spans nearly 485 km (300 mi) from Glen Canyon Dam to the headwaters of Lake Mead. Reaches were designated Reach 10 through Reach 50 following [[Maddux et al. 1987]].
- Pre-dam flows peaked in June with snowmelt. Post-dam flows show reduced seasonal variation, with daily fluctuations up to about 31,500 cfs and stage changes up to 13 vertical feet.
- In 1983–1986 high releases were steady and high; under subsequent low runoff the median daily discharge fell to about 12,000 cfs, and the coefficient of variation rose to 40%.

**Tributaries**
- Tributaries fall into two groups: large, sediment-laden systems ([[Paria River]], LCR, Kanab Creek) and smaller, clear, spring-fed streams ([[Bright Angel Creek]], Tapeats Creek, Deer Creek, Havasu Creek).
- The LCR receives perennial base flow of approximately 227 cfs from [[Blue Springs]], which is high in dissolved solids and carbon dioxide and calcite-saturated.

**Thermal and chemical regime**
- Deep hypolimnetic releases keep the mainstream perennially cold. Water temperatures at Lee's Ferry vary by only about 1.2°C; temperatures about 250 river miles downstream seldom exceed 16°C.
- Backwaters and tributary mouths warm considerably more under steady flows (up to about 25°C in some backwaters, versus about 10°C in the mainchannel).
- Total dissolved solids rose by about 50 mg/L after impoundment, to roughly 600 mg/L on average.
- The LCR contributes sediment and phosphorus during floods, but suspended sediment limits light and mainstream primary production.

**Productivity and food base**
- The only primary productivity estimates (Cole and Kubly 1977) are rough and come from a single August 1976 river trip.
- Benthic invertebrate densities decline with distance from the dam. Backwater ponar samples (1988–1989) yielded 77 taxa, with densities ranging from 2 to nearly 19,000 ind/0.1 m². Oligochaetes made up more than 95% of the mean benthic count in 1988–1989.
- Zooplankton densities were higher in backwaters (mean 1,363 ind/m³) than in the mainchannel (341 ind/m³), with cladocerans markedly more abundant in backwaters. Lake Powell is considered the source of most tailwater zooplankton.

**Riparian zones**
- Riparian vegetation is confined to the Old High Water Zone (pre-dam mesquite and catclaw acacia) and the New High Water Zone (tamarisk, coyote willow, seep-willow, arrowweed, desert broom). The New High Water Zone's position is thought to be controlled by fluctuating dam flows.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and abundance**
- Only three pre-dam individuals are documented in the study area. Post-impoundment records extend from just below Glen Canyon Dam to 373 km (232 mi) downstream.
- Mainstream collections are concentrated in the reach from about 60 to 70 river miles (RM 60–70), which held 77.5% of the 466 chub captured across all gear types. Above the LCR, catches were negligible.
- Tributary records include the LCR, Bright Angel, Shinumo, Kanab, and Havasu creeks.
- Population estimates: 2,000–8,000 adults (>200 mm TL) in the LCR and adjoining Colorado River in 1980–1981 (Kaeding and Zimmerman 1982, described as a "ball park" figure); 5,783 (SE 679) and 7,060 (SE 574) in 1987 and 1988, by Minckley's Peterson estimates; and maximum lower-LCR estimates of about 1,800 and 2,900 in 1987 and 1989.
- Authors caution that estimates are highly questionable and possibly biased, given lack of geographic closure.

**Gear and statistical limitations**
- Trammel net catch rates were 0.5–2.0 fish/12 hr (Carothers et al. 1981) and 0.00–0.95 fish/12 hr (Maddux et al. 1987). Hoop and fyke nets proved highly efficient for capturing a wide range of sizes.
- The 1987–1989 LCR trammel net dataset (430 samples) was highly skewed, with 193 samples (45%) containing humpback chub. The percentage of zero-catch samples ranged from 22% (1987) to 52% (1988), with 44% in 1989.
- Year effects were significant (ANOVA F = 7.188, p < 0.001; Median Chi-square = 11.191, p < 0.01), with 1987 significantly higher than the other years.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning is inferred to occur from late spring into July, during or shortly after peak spring flows, at water temperatures of roughly 12–23°C.
- Kaeding and Zimmerman (1983) suggested that some spawning occurs in the mainstream, but no humpback chub under 145 mm TL were collected from above the LCR. Young-of-the-year were found mostly in the LCR.
- Fecundity estimates from [[Hamman 1982]]: nine LCR females produced about 30,000 eggs; Black Rocks females produced 0–5,445 eggs (average 5,262 eggs per body-weight unit).
- Only one recorded spawning observation was noted. Eggs appear to be broadcast over cobble substrate and adhere to it.

**Early development and thermal tolerance**
- Hamman (1982) found that at 12–13°C, hatching took 340–475 hours with 72% success; at 20–21°C, hatching took 102–146 hours with 79% success.
- Marsh (1985) recorded complete embryo mortality at 5°C and 30°C, with highest hatch (60%) at 21°C.
- Field thermal-shock experiments (1988–1989) showed that transport of larvae from the LCR to colder mainstream water significantly increased short-term mortality in mixed native larval fish assemblages (1988). Humpback chub alone did not show significant responses, possibly because of small sample size.

**Growth and age**
- Hatchery-reared LCR fish grew to a mean 36.9 mm at 56 days, versus 47.5 mm for Black Rocks progeny under similar conditions.
- Von Bertalanffy fits gave asymptotic lengths of 443 mm (regression) and 435 mm (tag-recapture; see [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Equation]]). The authors note few fish have exceeded 450 mm TL.
- Preliminary otolith analysis of 49 fish (104–476 mm TL) indicated ages from about 1 to 22 years.

**Movement and survival**
- Between 1984 and 1986, 1,009 chub were tagged and 41 recaptured (about 4%). Most recaptures were less than 0.2 km from the tagging site, with a maximum movement of 10 km.
- Between 1987 and 1989, 522, 723, and 808 chub were tagged each May, with same-year recapture rates of 16.7%, 16.6%, and 10.4%. Seven recaptures documented movement between the LCR and mainstream.
- Median distance moved in the LCR was 27–192 m; differences among years were not significant.

**Biological interactions**
- The parasitic copepod *Lernaea cyprinacea* was found more frequently on LCR fish than mainstream fish, suggesting tributary-mainstream movement.
- Channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*) are a potential predator; 12 of 17 stomachs from LCR and Kanab Creek catfish were analysed, with three containing fish. Striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*) are an emerging concern.
- Adult chub stomachs (n = 12 with content) were dominated by filamentous algae (*Cladophora glomerata*, 77% mean volume), with chironomid larvae also present.

**Research recommendations**
- Investigate factors affecting reproductive success and early survival, including habitat availability and suitability in tributaries and mainstream.
- Determine susceptibility to, and effects of, thermal shock on early life stages.
- Determine relationships among growth, survivorship, and fecundity under controlled thermal regimes.
- Examine how flood magnitude, frequency, and timing in the LCR affect reproduction and survival.
- Evaluate proactive management options: modifying mainstream hydrology or temperatures, habitat augmentation, and introductions into new habitats.
- The report notes that recommendations are not fully developed and will be expanded in a final report.
