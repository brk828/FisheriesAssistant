# Chemical Control of Submerged Water Weeds with Sodium Arsenite

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-macrophytes

## Executive Summary
This Michigan Department of Conservation Fish Division pamphlet (No. 16, by F. F. Hooper and A. B. Cook, Jr.) summarizes practical guidance on using liquid sodium arsenite to control rooted submerged flowering weeds in ponds and along lake shorelines. It covers target and non-target plants, dosage (5–10 p.p.m. arsenic trioxide), volume and chemical calculation, application equipment, timing, effects on weeds, fish and fish-food organisms, hazards to people, livestock and land plants, safety precautions, and the Michigan permit requirements. The pamphlet notes that the role of aquatic vegetation for fish is not well understood: limited vegetation is beneficial, while dense growth may be detrimental to game-fish production and fishing.

## Background & Description
- Rising recreational use of Michigan lakes and new subdivisions with weedy beaches created demand for weed control; runoff from fertilized fields sometimes stimulated weed growth.
- The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] has long recommended [[Sodium Arsenite]] for farm and hatchery ponds and small lakes; the method has also been used on shorelines of large lakes in Wisconsin, Minnesota and Michigan.
- **Susceptible plants:** rooted or attached submerged flowering plants, including [[Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton*), [[Elodea]], [[Water Milfoil]] (*Myriophyllum*), [[Coontail]] (*Ceratophyllum*) and [[Water Crowfoot]] (*Ranunculus*).
- **Not controlled:** [[Stoneworts]] (*Chara*, *Nitella*; muskgrass), which are algae with cylindrical whorled branches and a gritty, lime-encrusted texture. These can be treated with [[Copper Sulfate]] and other algaecides.
- **Emergent plants** (cattails, bullrushes, water lilies) are not easily eliminated; [[2,4-D]] may be used, though it rarely kills roots.
- Only the liquid form is recommended, made by dissolving arsenic trioxide in an alkaline solution. A 4 lb/gal solution equals 40 per cent arsenic trioxide.
- Key references cited: Mackenthun (1950), Surber (1932), Surber (1949, Fishery Leaflet 344).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Limited vegetation gives cover and food for young fish; dense weeds may reduce game-fish production.
- Treatment is impractical in waters fed by large streams, because the chemical is diluted and flushed out; controllable outflows can be lowered beforehand to limit loss for two to three days.
- Wave action, wind and current greatly dilute the chemical, so unprotected shorelines require calm weather and higher doses. Treating water deeper than 8–10 feet is generally inadvisable because the chemical is hard to introduce at depth.
- Decaying weeds return nutrients to the water, which can cause a temporary algal bloom and murky water.
- Mass decay depletes dissolved oxygen, with [[Fish Suffocation]] risk typically at night or early morning when photosynthesis is minimal.
- Sodium arsenite also kills land plants (trees, shrubs, grass) via spray drift or shoreline contact.

## Key Findings
**Dosage (arsenic trioxide, by weight per million parts water)**
- General range: 5–10 p.p.m.; higher for larger, deeper, densely vegetated waters. The chemical must stay in contact with plants for one or two days.
- Small ponds up to 10 acres and 10 ft deep or less: 5 p.p.m., calculated on the entire pond volume. Very small, lightly congested ponds treated during rapid growth: 3–4 p.p.m. has worked.
- Unprotected shoreline areas: 10 p.p.m.; sheltered bays and coves: 7½ p.p.m. Ponds treated in sections: 7½ p.p.m.

**Calculation**
- Volume (cu ft) = area × average depth; × 62.4 lb/cu ft gives water weight; divide by 4 million and multiply by p.p.m. to get gallons of 4 lb/gal solution.
- Shortcut: gallons per acre = 0.7 × average depth (ft) × p.p.m. Example: a 4-acre pond averaging 6 ft deep at 10 p.p.m. needs 0.7 × 6 × 10 × 4 gallons.
- Volume errors above 10–20 per cent are serious: underestimates dilute the dose below effectiveness, while overestimates at high doses may be highly toxic to fish and other organisms. A [[Hydrographic Map]] is the best basis for measurements.

**Application and timing**
- Dilute at least ten parts water to one part chemical for uniform coverage and operator safety. Use hand sprayers for small plots or a gasoline-powered centrifugal pump sprayer for large areas, ideally throwing 40–50 feet; spray downwind.
- Divide areas into sections and calculate each separately. Treat shallow shorelines first, since fish and other animals are repelled and move to deeper water.
- Sectional treatment with five to seven days between sections minimizes suffocation risk. Early-season treatment is best, when young shoots have begun growing but not reached the surface (May to early June in southern Michigan).
- Warning sign of suffocation: fish congregating at the surface in early morning; remedies include removing fish, adding fresh water or running an outboard motor over the surface.

**Effects on weeds**
- Plants go limp, sink, then turn brown and black as they decay, usually within 5–7 days; midsummer treatment may take 10 days to two weeks.

**Effects on fish and fish-food organisms**
- Toxicity to fish is not determined for all conditions. Laboratory tests suggest avoiding concentrations above 10 p.p.m., though field experience shows tolerance up to 12 p.p.m.; species likely differ.
- Laboratory work reported 2 p.p.m. toxic to several food organisms, but most insects and other fish-food animals survive 8–10 p.p.m. in the field, possibly due to short exposure through diffusion and absorption by plants and bottom mud.

**Hazards and precautions**
- Minimum lethal dose of arsenic trioxide for farm animals: 0.008 to 0.16 ounces per 100 lb body weight; keep livestock from the pond for at least three days. Livestock may be attracted to the salty taste on shoreline vegetation.
- The [[U.S. Public Health Service]] drinking water standard permits only 0.05 p.p.m. Keep children out of treated areas and post no-bathing signs for three to five days.
- Concentrated solution severely burns skin: wear rubber gloves and goggles, protect exposed skin with cold cream or vaseline, use coarse droplets with the nozzle close to the water, and wash any contact immediately (eyes: water, then boric acid, then a doctor). Remove contaminated clothing at once. Accidents often stem from faulty equipment or unfamiliar operators.

**Regulation in Michigan**
- Responsibility lies with the Director of the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]]. Permits from the Chief of the Fish Division are required for chemical control on public waters or waters connected to them; completely landlocked private waters need none.
- Permits for sodium arsenite are ordinarily granted only if (1) a state-approved professional operator is employed, or (2) the applicant demonstrates qualification and equipment and submits detailed specifications (area dimensions, weed type, chemical kind and quantity, equipment, safety plans), with treatment supervised by the District Fisheries Supervisor or representative.
