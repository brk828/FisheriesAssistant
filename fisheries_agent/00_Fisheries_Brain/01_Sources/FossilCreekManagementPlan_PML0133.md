# Fossil Creek Management Plan and Fish Community Studies (2001)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document, centered on a [[Fossil Creek Management Plan]] dated January 31, 2001 by Paul C. Marsh, addresses the restoration of a 14-mile spring-fed tributary of the [[Verde River]] in central Arizona after the decommissioning of the [[Childs-Irving Hydroelectric Project]]. Full flows of about 43 cfs are scheduled to return by December 31, 2004 under a settlement with [[Arizona Public Service]]. The plan evaluates eight fisheries management alternatives and recommends Alternative 8: a native fish stream with flies-and-lures, no-harvest angling for chubs, supported by a downstream fish barrier and chemical renovation of nonnative fishes (Option C). Appendices include fish survey data (1994–1999), a Stehr Lake water-supply memo, creel and angler-support records, a barrier feasibility study, and a pesticide literature list.

## Background & Description

Fossil Creek is fed by [[Fossil Springs]], which has provided a consistent base flow of about 43 cfs since records began in 1912. Water was diverted for hydropower from 1908 onward, leaving only about 2 cfs from the diversion dam and 3–5 cfs from the Irving plant under low-flow conditions. Fisheries management has therefore been limited.

Springs are supersaturated with calcium carbonate, forming [[Travertine Formation]]s that create dams, terraces and plunge pools. Travertine deposits are associated with rich algal growth and increased macroinvertebrate surface area, which together support fish food webs. Historical accounts from 1891 (Lummis) and 1904 (Chamberlain) describe large travertine basins that were later destroyed by floods.

The native fish community includes:
- Chubs (the exact species, reported as [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Headwater Chub]], remained uncertain pending genetic analysis)
- Desert sucker, Sonora sucker, speckled dace and longfin dace
- Historically possible but now absent: [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]]

Nonnative fishes include smallmouth bass, green sunfish, yellow bullhead, channel and flathead catfish, and common carp. Crayfish and Asian clam are also present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream fish surveys (1994–1999):** Surveys show that above the diversion dam the native community has persisted without nonnatives. Below the dam, nonnatives have expanded upstream since about 1994. Smallmouth bass were first reported in 1996 and by 1999 were found in all sections below the diversion. Preliminary 2000 data showed only one young-of-year chub below the Irving plant compared with many young smallmouth bass. Mean sizes of chubs and Sonora suckers were increasing over time, suggesting poor [[Fish Recruitment]].

**Subhabitat use:** Roundtail chub favored runs with about 49% cover, while smallmouth bass were more often found in deeper, faster water (mean 1.56 m/s and 455 mm depth). Smallmouth used pools in 1994 and runs by 1995. Electrofishing underestimated smallmouth because many were in pools too deep to shock.

**Crayfish:** Not found in 1996 surveys but present in the lower 12 miles. A waterfall barrier 1.5 miles above Irving blocks upstream movement.

**Stehr Lake:** A 28-acre regulating reservoir for the Childs plant, with largemouth bass, channel catfish and bluegill, and about 200 angler days per year. It will likely dry once flows return, eliminating about 28 acres of wetland and the fishery. A January 2000 memo estimated that a pump or well to maintain the lake would cost roughly $500,000 to $1,000,000, and that losing the lake would cost $20,700–$34,500 in revenue annually. APS proposed stocking a replacement fishery at [[Tremaine Lake]].

**Fishing pressure:** Historically under 300 angler days per year, limited by poor access. A 1999 creel survey on Verde River tributaries found only 6% of anglers targeting warm-water species and no anglers targeting them on three of four creeks.

## Key Findings

**Management alternatives evaluated (eight total):**
1. Naturally reproducing rainbow trout stream (rejected: summer water temperatures about 72°F at springs, poor spawning habitat, stocked trout previously failed)
2. Winter rainbow trout put-and-take fishery (rejected: low use, poor access)
3. Naturally reproducing Gila trout stream (requires renovation and barriers in wilderness; feasibility limited until Gila trout are downlisted)
4. Smallmouth bass and catfish fishery (No Action; threatens listed endangered fishes)
5. Half native/half nonnative stream (barrier and separate reaches)
6. Native fish stream with no angling (greatest native protection, loss of sportfishing)
7. Native fish stream with chub angling and no promotion
8. **Recommended:** native fish stream with flies-and-lures, single barbless hooks, no harvest and promotion of the fishery

**Recommended management options for Alternative 8:**
- **Option A:** Barrier plus periodic mechanical removal. Rejected: very low success, mechanical methods rarely remove more than 50% of fish, and requires annual work along 14 miles.
- **Option B:** Barrier relying on floods to flush nonnatives. Rejected: nonnatives are river-evolved and unlikely to be flushed out.
- **Option C (recommended):** Fish barrier near the Verde River confluence, chemical renovation of sections from the diversion dam to the barrier using antimycin or rotenone, collection and restocking of native fish, monitoring, and public promotion. Renovation must finish before December 31, 2004. The option has the highest probability of success but generates opposition to chemical use and will kill some native fish.

**Barrier feasibility (Bureau of Reclamation, Phase 1, November 2000):**
- Two sites were assessed: an upper site (rock slots, costs about $159,000 with helicopter-delivered concrete) and a lower site (costs about $225,000–$367,000 depending on the method). A third site was rejected.
- All sites lie in the Mazatzal Wilderness, so mechanized equipment would require waivers.
- NEPA (about $20,000), Clean Water Act permitting (about $20,000) and ESA consultation (about $10,000) were estimated, with 6–12 months each for NEPA and CWA.
- Phase 2 design costs: about $35,000 (lower site), $33,000 (upper site), and $49,000 (both).

**Historical and biological findings:**
- The 2000 agreement with APS commits to full flows by December 31, 2004, with a removal and restoration plan due by December 31, 2009.
- A 1998 "Fall Fish Count" by Grand Canyon University compared 1996 and 1998 catches. Roundtail chub, desert sucker and Sonora sucker counts were significantly lower in 1998 (χ² = 29.73, p < 0.001). No roundtail chub were captured in 1998, and the mean length of desert suckers increased from 148 mm to 221 mm, indicating absent juveniles. Smallmouth bass made up 35.5% and 60.0% of the two 1998 samples, compared with none in 1996.
- The authors recommended stabilizing flows, removing smallmouth bass, building a lower barrier, and educating anglers.

**Stakeholder support:** The Arizona Flycasters Club and the Northern Arizona Flycasters supported full flows and native-fish management with catch-and-release, fly-and-lure-only regulations. Forest-level and agency workgroup members (AGFD, USFS, USFWS, Bureau of Reclamation, ASU) recommended Alternative 8 and Option C.

**Outstanding research needs:**
- Smallmouth bass–roundtail chub interactions (predation, competition, habitat displacement)
- Macroinvertebrate diversity before renovation
- Timing of renovation to avoid amphibian eggs and larvae
- Effects of increased flows on nonnative populations
- Whether headwater and roundtail chub co-occur in Fossil Creek
