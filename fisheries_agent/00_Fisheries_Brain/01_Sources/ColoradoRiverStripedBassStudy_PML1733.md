# Striped Bass Study of the Lower Colorado River and Lake Havasu

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game (CDFG) report presents a multi-year field study (March 1984 to December 1986) of the [[Striped Bass]] population inhabiting the lower [[Colorado River]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. The study aimed to provide baseline information on spawning, nursery use, population dynamics, life history, and angler exploitation, to support management of a trophy striped bass fishery. Components included egg and larval surveys, a juvenile survey, a subadult gill net trend index, tagging studies, and a creel census. Findings show that striped bass spawn in both the river and reservoir, that spawning onset is tied to rapid water temperature increases, that juvenile captures were nearly absent, and that subadult abundance shows a roughly two-year cycle linked to high flows. Anglers increasingly targeted striped bass by 1985, following a 1985 regulation change removing the minimum size limit.

## Background & Description

The population originated from a deliberate introduction effort. In 1953 California, Arizona and Nevada agreed to introduce [[Threadfin Shad]] as forage. Threadfin shad were planted in [[Lake Havasu]] in 1954 and 1955 and spread rapidly through the lower Colorado River and irrigation canals. Striped bass were subsequently stocked in the river system from 1959 onward. Stocking records (Table 1) list 1959 to 1964 introductions, the first plant involving fish from the Atlantic coast, with the largest individual numbers in the thousands. Fish grew rapidly; a fish of 5 kg was recorded by 1962 and 9.1 kg by 1966, and a 27.2 kg fish was caught in 1977.

Two populations exist in the lower river. The [[Lake Havasu]] population is the most publicized and is the subject of this study. A second population occurs in the river from [[Parker Dam]] to Laguna Dam, lacks access to large deep reservoirs, and is partly supplemented by fish passing through Parker Dam from Lake Havasu.

Lake Havasu lies downstream of Parker Dam, is roughly 43 km long, and includes the Topock Gorge and the [[Bill Williams Arm]]. Major water diversions occur within the reservoir, including the Los Angeles Metropolitan Water District pumping plant and the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), which is authorized for up to 1.2 million acre-feet annually.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning biology (from the literature cited):** Striped bass spawn in flowing water at or near the surface at temperatures of roughly 10 to 24 °C, with peak spawning usually between 15 and 20 °C. Spawning is triggered by a rapid increase in temperature. Pelagic eggs are semibuoyant, hatch 29 to 80 hours after fertilization depending on temperature, and require current or turbulence to stay suspended. Albrecht (1964) suggested a minimum current velocity of 30.5 cm/s for suspension, while the river exceeded this value and reservoir velocities were 3.0 to 13.4 cm/s.

**Hydrology:** Flow in the lower river is regulated by [[Hoover Dam]] and [[Davis Dam]]. In 1983 an unusually high snowpack led to flows at Davis Dam reaching a record 461,200 cfs on July 2, 1983, with monthly releases three to five times normal. Flows also increased sharply on May 25, 1985, coinciding with a 2.4 °C temperature drop and cessation of spawning.

**Water quality and productivity:** Lake Havasu sampling showed the lower portion of the reservoir and Bill Williams Arm to be mesotrophic and eutrophic, respectively, while the rest of the lake, including the upper section where river-spawned larvae could develop, was oligotrophic under present discharge. Station 5, where most larvae were estimated to hatch, had the highest zooplankton concentration, suggesting favorable growth conditions.

**Other species:** Incidental catches in the gill net survey included channel catfish, bluegill, green sunfish, largemouth bass, threadfin shad, and a single razorback sucker, the latter being the first recorded for the survey.

## Key Findings

**1. Spawning and egg/larval surveys (1984–1986)**
- In 1985, 655 viable striped bass eggs were collected; 63.2% came from the reservoir and 52.7% of river catches were associated with rapid water temperature increases.
- Spawning in 1985 began in late April, with spawning peaks between April 24 and May 22 at 18.3 to 21.1 °C.
- In 1986, 572 eggs were collected in the reservoir (39.3% viable); spawning peaks occurred between April 24 and May 22 at 18.8 to 20.6 °C.
- Only 20% of river-collected viable eggs were under 4 hours old and suitable for back-calculating spawning sites, indicating that much of the study area's river spawning was not precisely located.
- Egg density per tow ranged from 0 to 22; spawning was observed at all hours of the day, with slightly more than half of eggs estimated to have been spawned during darkness.

**2. Larval collections**
- Only four larvae were collected in 1985. In 1986, 37 larvae were collected, mostly at station 5, ranging from 4 to 11 mm total length (mean 5.4 mm), consistent with newly hatched fish.

**3. Juvenile survey (1984–1986)**
- No juvenile striped bass were captured during summer efforts in 1984–1986 (38 mm target size). Eight striped bass were taken in December 1986 with a 5-month-old fish sampling effort, with 28 young-of-the-year fish collected in total. The authors concluded that recruitment into the juvenile size class was poor and that gear was ineffective for this size.

**4. Subadult gill net trend index (1979–1986)**
- Catch per unit effort (fish per 12-hour set) fluctuated cyclically: 0.43 (1980), 0.34 (1981), 0.07 (1983), 0.14 (1984), 0.03 (1985), and 0.11 (1986).
- A roughly two-year cycle was apparent, with high indices followed by lower ones. The authors suggested possible cannibalism by strong year classes and noted that high flows were associated with declines.
- Condition factors of the 1982 cohort remained stable throughout the surveyed period.

**5. Tagging (1984–1985)**
- A total of 295 striped bass were tagged in 1984 (220 non-reward, 75 reward) and 336 in 1985 (251 reward, 85 non-reward).
- First-year tag returns were 34 of 295 (11.5%) in 1984 and 33 of 336 (about 10%) in 1985; second-year returns were 7 in 1984.
- Estimated angler response was 0.214 for the combined 1984–1985 first-year returns. Estimated exploitation was 0.281; estimated survival was 0.21 (Jolly-Seber-type estimate, 1984–1985) to 0.32 (Ricker estimate).
- Instantaneous fishing mortality (F) was 0.45 and natural mortality (M) was 0.59. Population estimates were not possible due to small tagged numbers.
- Most fish tagged at Davis Dam migrated downstream to the Bill Williams Arm of Lake Havasu, indicating movement from the river into the reservoir.

**6. Creel census (1984–1985)**
- In 1984, 538 anglers were interviewed, who fished 2,461 hours and caught 1,429 fish. In 1985, 1,150 anglers were interviewed, who fished 7,455 hours.
- Striped bass were preferred by most anglers in 1985, while largemouth bass were preferred in 1984.
- Striped bass anglers kept most of their catch; largemouth bass anglers released most of theirs (about 71–97% depending on season).
- Estimated total catch in 1985 was about 38,947 fish, including roughly 25,000 striped bass, of which about 27 percent were kept by striped bass anglers.
- Striped bass caught in the lower third of the reservoir were larger on average than those caught farther upstream.

**7. Management implications and recommendations**
- The study provided baseline data and methods for future studies; the CDFG planned an expanded roving creel census starting in 1987.
- The authors recommended modifying the gill net survey to use more selective panel sizes and to standardize catch per 12-hour set.
- Because striped bass became fully legal in 1985, the authors recommended no further regulation changes until sufficient data justify them; the full impact on trophy fishery recovery would take several years to assess.
- They recommended that future tagging concentrate on specific areas and that CAP diversions be evaluated for effects on striped bass and forage fish, since CAP draws up to 3,000 cfs directly from the Bill Williams Arm.
