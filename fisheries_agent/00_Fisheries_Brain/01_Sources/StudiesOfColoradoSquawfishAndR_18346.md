# Habitat Use and Conservation of Endangered Fishes in the 15-Mile Reach of the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 final report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Colorado River Fishery Project evaluates how the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] use the 14.2-mile [[15-Mile Reach]] of the [[Colorado River]] in the [[Grand Valley]], Colorado. The study ran from May 1986 through December 1988 and was funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under a 1985 agreement tied to the Green Mountain and Ruedi Reservoir water sales. It combined [[Electrofishing]] surveys, [[Radiotelemetry]] of adults, seining for larvae and young-of-the-year (YOY), and microhabitat measurements.

The reach is important to both species, and adults move among three adjacent reaches to meet seasonal needs. Successful reproduction, however, appears very limited. The authors conclude that the 15-Mile Reach should be maintained through adequate summer flows, since low summer flows are the critical period for both species.

## Background & Description

The range of the two fishes has been reduced by about 75% since the turn of the century. Colorado Squawfish is federally listed as endangered, while Razorback Sucker, described as considerably rarer, was not. Both species are restricted to the upper basin above [[Glen Canyon Dam]], apart from a remnant population of old adults in [[Lake Mohave]].

The upstream limit of both species in the Colorado River is the [[Price Stub Dam]]. The [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] on the [[Gunnison River]], 2.2 miles above the confluence, blocks upstream fish movement there. The 15-Mile Reach lies between the Grand Valley Diversion (RM 185.1) and the Gunnison confluence (RM 171.0). Irrigation withdrawals reduce flow during August-October, when natural flows are already low.

Relevant study methods included:
- **Adult abundance:** catch per hour of electrofishing, expressed as fish caught or seen per hour.
- **Telemetry:** fish longer than 550 mm TL were implanted with radio transmitters. Battery life was estimated at 150-245 days for the smallest tags and 547-940 days for the largest.
- **Larval sampling:** 0.5-mm-mesh hand seines in backwaters, mid-July to late August. Identification was by the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University.
- **YOY sampling:** 3-mm-mesh beach seines, with catch expressed per 100 m².
- **Habitat:** habitat type, depth, column velocity, substrate and temperature at fish locations, using eight categories (backwaters, gravel pits, eddies, pools, shorelines, runs, riffles, rapids).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use.** Runs were the most common habitat used by adult squawfish year-round. Pools dominated winter use (November-February), and ice covered low-velocity areas in January. Backwaters and flooded [[Gravel-Pit Ponds]] peaked in use during May-June spring flooding. Razorback Sucker showed a stronger preference for deep water, especially sites deeper than 6.0 ft from November through April.

**Temperature.** Upstream seasonal temperatures are generally below the physiological optimum (25 C) for squawfish. Backwaters and flooded gravel pits were used in spring and can be up to 10.5 C warmer than the main channel. Temperatures suitable for spawning (minimum 20 C) occurred in the 15-Mile Reach, though often not until mid-summer.

**Water clarity.** Clarity rises between runoff events. Secchi visibility was positively but weakly correlated with depth used by squawfish (r = .29; P < .01; n = 204). Clear summer water may increase predation risk for young fish from sight-feeding predators.

**Introduced species.** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Common Carp]] are predators or competitors of concern. Gravel pits may trap stranded adults and expose young to introduced predators.

**Angling.** Angling mortality is a potential serious threat in the reach, given its proximity to Grand Junction and the popularity of [[Channel Catfish]] fishing. A radio-tagged squawfish was presumed killed by a fisherman.

## Key Findings

**Relative abundance (spring electrofishing)**
- Historical standardized surveys (1979-81) captured 22 adult squawfish from seven of eleven reaches. The Grand Valley reach (Palisade to Loma) yielded 3 adults (0.10/mile), close to the average of 0.12/mile.
- Razorback Sucker: 53 of 70 individuals (76%) captured river-wide during 1979-85 came from the Grand Valley.
- In spring, squawfish catch rates in the 15-Mile Reach were about 3.2 times the [[18-Mile Reach]] in 1986, 6.1 times in 1987, and 1.5 times lower in 1988. Combined, relative abundance was about 2.0 times higher in the 15-Mile Reach.
- Razorback Sucker shocked in the 15-Mile Reach over the three years were about 5.9 times more than in the 18-Mile Reach.
- In 1988, extremely low flows in the lower Gunnison apparently precluded squawfish use; none were found there July-October.

**Movement (radiotelemetry)**
- In 1982-85, 15 of 34 radio-tagged squawfish (44%) tagged upstream of Black Rocks occupied the 15-Mile Reach during May-October.
- Movements ranged from local to long distance; one fish moved 198 miles from Lake Powell in 1982, and another moved 120 river miles in 1985.
- Some fish use the reach year-round, others only during winter or spring, and some only as a wintering area.
- The range of one fish extended to Price Stub Dam, and several spent part of the year in the Gunnison River. A few moved downstream as far as RM 130.

**Spawning and reproduction**
- Estimated spawning dates: 26 July-5 August 1986; 4 July in both 1987 and 1988, based on single larvae.
- No squawfish larvae were found in the 15-Mile Reach in 1986-88. Only two larvae (< 25 mm) and no YOY had been collected from the reach since 1982. A single 105-mm yearling was seined from a backwater at RM 174.5 in 1986.
- Larvae were collected from the 18-Mile Reach: 8 in 1986 (mean 0.14/sample), 1 in 1987 (0.02/sample), and 1 in 1988 (0.02/sample).
- YOY squawfish: 29 in 1986 in the 18-Mile Reach (mean 4.02/100 m²; SD 7.87), 13 in 1987 (2.75/100 m²; SD 9.34), and 1 in 1988 (0.12/100 m²).
- Catch rates indicate 1986 was a considerably better year for squawfish reproduction than 1987 or 1988.

**Razorback Sucker**
- Spawning migrations into the 15-Mile Reach occurred in May-June. A ripe female was captured 3 June 1986, and a ripe male eight days later about 30 m away.
- Of 57 razorbacks captured in the Grand Valley during 1979-88, 50 (88%) came from flooded gravel-pit ponds (Walker Wildlife Area, Connected Lakes, Clifton Pond).
- No young or juvenile razorbacks were found in the Grand Valley, and no larvae or YOY were identified in the study. Local residents reported thousands of razorbacks spawning in a flooded pasture at RM 175.6 in the 1930s and early 1940s, which was later filled and converted to an orchard in 1945.

**Community changes (1986-88)**
- Native species declined overall. Bluehead Sucker (YOY) fell sharply, and Speckled Dace declined in both larvae and YOY. Roundtail Chub larvae were most abundant in 1987.
- Introduced species increased. Fathead Minnow showed the most dramatic increase. Sand Shiner and Red Shiner also rose, particularly in 1988.
- Peak spring and summer flows declined from 1986 (wet) to 1987 (average) to 1988 (low). Whether this trend will continue is unknown.

**Environmental limits**
- Within the reach, runs remain common, so their availability is probably not a current limit.
- Winter ice cover was associated with shallower use. Squawfish at ice-covered sites averaged 3.9 ft (n = 14) versus 5.7 ft (n = 10) at non-ice-covered sites (P < .005).
- Gravel-pit ponds and large backwaters are limited in number, and their use in spring may be a limiting factor for adult squawfish.

**Management recommendations**
- The 15-Mile Reach should be maintained through adequate flows. Summer is the critical period, when flows are near seasonal lows and physiological demands are high.
- Managers should recognize that contiguous habitat combinations are needed to meet life-history requirements.
- Possible enhancement measures include temperature augmentation to promote earlier spawning and high spring flushing flows to control introduced fishes.
- Gravel pits could be sealed from the river or replaced with graded backwaters that drain naturally.
