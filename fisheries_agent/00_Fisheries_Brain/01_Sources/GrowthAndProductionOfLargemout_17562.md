# Growth and Production of Largemouth Bass in a Small Pond

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study, reprinted from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 92, No. 4, pp. 391-400), examines the relationship among [[Standing Crop]], growth, and [[Surplus Production]] of [[Largemouth Bass]] in a fertile 2.8-acre pond in central Pennsylvania. Adult bass were stocked and allowed to spawn naturally in 1958 and 1960, and the resulting yearling cohorts were studied in 1959 and 1961. Both cohorts were heavily harvested, which produced two contrasting density levels. The high-density year (1959) showed slow growth and poor condition, whereas the lower-density year (1961) showed faster growth, higher condition, and greater surplus production. The authors conclude that a smaller standing crop can yield more production, expressed as fewer but much larger fish, which is more favorable to the pond manager.

## Background & Description

The authors note that concurrent estimates of vital statistics for a single largemouth bass population are rarely available, despite the species' popularity as a sport fish and its wide geographic distribution. Published standing-crop values for largemouth bass ranged from 5.2 to 82.7 pounds per acre (after [[Carlander 1955]]), and the authors cite [[Bennett 1962]] to underline how limited production-versus-standing-crop data were at that time.

**Study site: [[Breon's Pond]].** The pond is a 2.8-acre impoundment with a maximum depth of 6 feet and total alkalinity of about 100 ppm. Summer water temperatures ranged from 70 to 85 °F (May to September), with about 3 months of winter ice cover. Algal blooms and dense pondweed indicated high fertility and caused winterkill in some years. The pond was originally made by damming a trout stream for a grist mill and was later modified with inlet and outlet pipes and valves for water-level control.

**Definitions used:**
- Standing crop (W): abundance of the population in number or weight at a given time.
- Yield (Y): number or weight removed by people.
- Growth (G): rate of growth of individual fish.
- Surplus production (P): in the sense of [[Ricker 1958]], new weight produced by a fishable stock, including recruitment, minus natural mortality. Here it was estimated as yield plus the change in standing crop between time periods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Prior history.** A previous owner heavily fertilized the pond to control pondweeds. This was followed on one or two occasions by midsummer kills of largemouth bass and [[Bluegill]]. In spring 1958 the pond was drained and 760 pounds of slow-growing bluegills (271 pounds per acre, roughly 35,000 fish per acre) were removed. Bluegills were the only species present.

**Stocking and reproduction.**
- May 1958: five pairs of adult bass (12-15 inches total length) were stocked. This produced a large 1958 year class, but the 1959 spawn failed. The authors suggest predation by bluegills and possible suppression by the previous year class, though direct evidence was lacking.
- A complete kill in winter 1959-60 was attributed to low oxygen. All adults stocked in 1958 died, and no 2-year-old bass were recovered from seining in 1960.
- May 1960: four pairs of adults were stocked. They spawned successfully in 1960, but again produced no young in their second year.
- Golden shiners were introduced as forage but never exceeded about 10 pounds per acre.
- In July 1961, 2,000 bass fingerlings were introduced after natural reproduction had failed.

**Stocking benchmark.** The recommended bass fingerling stocking rate is about 100 fish per acre (Swingle and Smith, 1940). Surviving yearlings from natural reproduction were 7,158 per acre (1959) and 1,961 per acre (1961), both far above this benchmark in the year of the first cohort.

**Diet.** Stomach analyses in both years showed a diet dominated by midges and damselflies, meaning the bass were primarily invertebrate feeders.

## Key Findings

**Population estimates (Petersen mark-and-recapture, with drawdown checks):**
- The April 1959 Petersen estimate was 13,181 (95% CI 10,900-15,600). It was judged a gross underestimate, because the fall 1959 drawdown catch (8,192) plus harvest (11,851) gave a minimum of 20,043 fish.
- The April 1961 estimate was 5,490 (95% CI 4,800-6,200). It was accepted as the starting number because it agreed well with the drawdown and harvest checks.
- Fall 1961 drawdown estimate: 831 (95% CI 801-868).

**Likely cause of the 1959 underestimate:** Behavioral segregation or cold inactivation of fish in April (water below 60 °F), with seine inefficiency. Net selectivity was judged a minor factor. The authors now wait until water is above 55 °F before spring estimates.

**Harvest and standing crop:**
- 1959: 11,851 yearlings removed (107.6 lb/acre, 80.8% of the 133.1 lb/acre spring standing crop).
- 1961: 4,676 yearlings removed (104.6 lb/acre, 95.3% of the 109.8 lb/acre spring standing crop).

**Natural mortality:**
- 1959: judged low, based on few dead fish found and at most 10 adult bass present.
- 1961: the calculated mortality ranged from -0.3% (point estimates) to 11.7% (confidence-limit extremes), so the true value is uncertain.

**Growth and condition:**
- 1959 cohort: growth from 3.4 to 4.0 inches over the summer, with condition factors seldom above 45 ("poor" by [[Bennett 1948]]'s scale).
- 1961 cohort: fish averaged 8.4 inches at the end of the season (vs. 4.0 inches in 1959). Growth accelerated about two months later than in 1959 and condition was higher.
- Comparison fish: Florida fish from Iowa stock reached 13.1 inches in 12 months, with condition factors of 59.6-66.1 ("very fat"). Norris Reservoir (Tennessee) bass had condition factors of 49-54, while Michigan bass (Beckman, 1948) had factors of 45-47.
- The authors propose condition as a predictor of growth, though the evidence is correlational.

**Surplus production:**
- 1959: 51.0 lb/acre per year. Production peaked at 62.3 lb/acre on August 12 and declined to 50.8 lb/acre by drawdown as mean weights fell.
- 1961: 75.5 lb/acre per year, with a possible increase of about 12 lb/acre from the July fingerling plantings.
- Conclusion: the lower-density, faster-growing 1961 population produced more surplus production, expressed as fewer but larger fish.

**Management implications:**
- Heavy harvest at low density can improve growth and yield per unit of production, which benefits pond owners.
- The authors cite [[Boccius]]'s 1841 observation that growth increases as competition for food decreases, and they note that production of 50-70 lb/acre per year is high relative to the few published records.
- Further work is needed on surplus production at smaller standing crops to identify the factors that maximize it.
- Fish too small to be caught, and production lost to natural mortality, are both treated as wasted. Replacing natural mortality with yield could be substantial in small waters.
