# Peer Review of the Biological Opinion for Lower Colorado River Operations and Maintenance

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 1997 review letter by Paul C. Marsh, Faculty Research Associate at Arizona State University (Department of Biology), commenting on the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) and Bureau of Reclamation (USBR) Biological Opinion (BO) on [[Lower Colorado River Operations and Maintenance]]. The review focuses on the two listed endangered fishes, [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and touches on the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]. The reviewer argues that the BO relies too heavily on the unfinished [[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]], that augmentation and research provisions lack measurable benefit criteria, and that several Reasonable and Prudent Measures (RPMs) are either unachievable or unlikely to benefit the fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Formal correspondence (letter) responding to a request for review and comment on the BO, reference LC-2000 / ENV-7.00.
- **Author:** Paul C. Marsh, Faculty Research Associate, Arizona State University, Tempe, Arizona, dated 25 July 1997.
- **Recipient:** U.S. Department of the Interior, Bureau of Reclamation, Lower Colorado Regional Office, Boulder City, Nevada.
- **Scope:** The reviewer comments only on sections concerning the two listed fishes, since these are his primary expertise. Comments on the flycatcher, Discretion, Progress Evaluation, and failure provisions are explicitly outside his expertise, and the Take section received no comment.
- **Target species:**
  - [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), an endangered cyprinid.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered catostomid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Native fishes can complete life histories in artificial or highly modified habitats, provided [[Non-Native Fishes]] are absent. The reviewer states this has been repeatedly demonstrated for both target species.
- Bonytail and razorback sucker reportedly cannot complete their life cycles where established non-native fishes are present in the Colorado River, which the reviewer considers to make comparisons of habitat suitability moot.
- Knowledge gaps noted: very little is known of bonytail spawning and nursery habitat; swimming capability as a function of size and temperature is unknown for both species.
- Razorback sucker can spawn in a variety of places, and young appear to thrive wherever non-native fishes are absent.
- Some razorback suckers are entrained into canals diverting water from the lower Colorado River, but no evidence shows this is a significant mortality factor. Canals may offer recruitment opportunities unavailable in open river waters.
- Channelization and comparable systems raise questions about feasibility of [[Ecological Restoration]] on the lower Colorado River.
- Recruitment of razorback sucker in [[Lake Mead]] is questioned; the reviewer notes other plausible explanations for the presence of fish of any size or age there.

## Key Findings

**Overarching concerns**
- The BO's exclusion of external consultants caused an apparent disparity in the depth of coverage between the fishes and the flycatcher, and omitted relevant current information on the fishes. The reviewer recommends correction in future processes.
- Tying long-term RPA requirements (BO page 157) to the uncertain MSCP is ill-advised, since the plan had not been formalized or endorsed by all participants, and responsibilities remain unclear. The reviewer asks that RPM and MSCP not be co-dependent.

**Short-term provisions**
- **Augmentation:** Monitoring must measure both quantity and quality (including genetic diversity) of repatriated fish. Targets framed only as "50,000 fish" for each species are insufficient, since survival could be 0% while the target is still met.
- **Review and evaluation:** This duplicates work already required under the Endangered Species Act and should be stated and redundancy removed, or the omission justified.
- **Impoundments:** Off-channel habitat should consist of numerous smaller water bodies rather than a few large ones, for manageability, reduced loss of the fish community, and to mimic pre-development conditions. Sizes should reflect presumed pre-development flow scenarios.
- **Research funding:** The reviewer considers the proposed studies of dubious applied value. Funds should instead go to proactive, on-the-ground projects with direct benefit to the target species.

**Long-term provisions**
- **Reintroduction:** Long overdue and should be fully supported and implemented.
- **Recruitment:** Unrealistic and should be deleted, because the conditions for successful recruitment are unknowable, cannot likely be recreated in [[Lake Mohave]], and evidence of recent recruitment in Lake Mead is lacking.
- **Ecological restoration:** Reasonable only if restoration is mandated and implemented. Three preliminary questions should be answered: whether comparable river systems exist, whether channelization removal has been done or planned there, and whether removal is realistic on the lower Colorado. If implementation cannot be assured, applied experiments are preferable.
- **Agreements:** Reasonable, subject to the RPA–MSCP caveats.

**Reasonable and Prudent Measures (RPMs)**
- **Stranding:** Cannot be completed meaningfully, since spawning and nursery habitat knowledge is minimal. Quantifying stranding potential is considered futile.
- **Dams (RPM 2):** Potential loss can be modeled only with swimming-capability and velocity data not currently available. Actual loss depends on many interacting factors and has not been demonstrated. Diverting resources to RPM 2 is judged irresponsible when direct benefit activities are available.
- **Entrainment:** Information gathering may have inherent value, but its capacity to measurably benefit the fishes is limited.

**Conclusion**
- The reviewer urges FWS and USBR to direct limited resources to applied, direct-benefit experiments and on-the-ground projects rather than superfluous studies, and to consult species and system experts both within and outside their agencies.
