# Fish, Wildlife and Pesticides

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pesticide-toxicology

## Executive Summary

This 1966 U.S. Department of the Interior, [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) publication reviews how [[Synthetic Pesticides]] affect fish, wildlife, and their aquatic and coastal environments. It argues that controlled pesticide use is necessary, but that persistent chemicals pose subtle, delayed, and food-chain-mediated threats to reproduction, survival, and the commercial fishing industry. The document summarizes the production and use of pesticides, their persistence and global distribution, species sensitivities, reproductive and food-chain effects, the [[President's Science Advisory Committee]] (PSAC) report, federal responsibilities, and public recommendations for reducing harm.

## Background & Description

The publication opens by distinguishing acceptable, controlled chemical use against crop pests and disease-carrying animals from the duty to protect wild fish, birds, and mammals. It frames pesticides as pollutants comparable to automobile exhaust, pulp and paper mill wastes, and sewage.

**Chemical classes described:**
- **[[Chlorinated Hydrocarbons]]:** Introduced with [[DDT]] in 1945. Includes dieldrin, aldrin, endrin, toxaphene, lindane, methoxychlor, chlordane, and heptachlor. Chlorinated weed killers such as 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T are also mentioned. These compounds are stable and persist in soil for years.
- **[[Organic Phosphorus Compounds]]:** Used mainly against insects and mites that parasitize plants and animals (e.g., parathion, malathion, phosdrin, TEPP). Most decompose more quickly than chlorinated compounds.
- **Prewar pesticides:** Mostly non-persistent natural products such as pyrethrins, nicotine sulphate, and rotenone, plus persistent exceptions such as lead arsenate and Paris green.

**Scale of use (as reported):**
- About 500 chemical compounds mixed into more than 60,000 formulations by 8,000 U.S. manufacturing firms.
- 1964 production: 783 million pounds, about three-quarters for domestic use (insecticides, rodenticides and fumigants 444 million; herbicides 226 million; fungicides 113 million).
- About 1 acre in 10 in the continental U.S. treated annually, averaging nearly 4 pounds per acre.
- Federal government uses under 5 percent; about 15 percent of domestic volume goes to home and garden use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Persistence and global distribution**
- Chlorinated residues exceeding 176 pounds per acre accumulated in heavily treated soils, mostly in the top few inches. Persistence ranking: DDT, dieldrin, toxaphene, lindane, chlordane, heptachlor, aldrin.
- Pesticides travel by air currents, runoff, dust, and living organisms. Residues were found in Antarctic penguins and crab-eater seals.
- DDT occurred in oil of fish from offshore waters and coasts of four continents, ranging from under 1 p.p.m. to over 300 p.p.m.
- Of 56 bald eagles found dead or incapacitated (1960–1963, 20 states and 2 Canadian provinces), all but one (from Alaska) contained DDT.
- The U.S. Public Health Service reported chlorinated hydrocarbon traces in every major U.S. river system.

**Sensitivity of aquatic organisms**
- Generally, crustaceans, shellfish, and fish are most sensitive, followed by amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals.
- Plankton exposed to 1 p.p.m. of most chlorinated hydrocarbons for 4 hours showed 50–90 percent reductions in growth and reproduction.

**Coastal fisheries**
- Ten marine animal groups make up 80 percent of the $381.2 million U.S. fishery. Five use estuaries and are vulnerable: shrimp, mollusks, Pacific salmon, crabs, and menhaden.

**Food chains**
- In Clear Lake, California (1957), DDD applied at 0.02 p.p.m. was concentrated by plankton to 5 p.p.m., by fish to hundreds to over 2,000 p.p.m., and reached 1,600 p.p.m. in grebe tissue after grebes ate fish and died.
- Many robins died from DDT used against the beetle carrying Dutch elm disease; brain tissue contained up to 240 p.p.m.

## Key Findings

**Direct and delayed toxicity**
- Aldrin at 2 ounces per acre killed nearly a third of young waterfowl on a treated area.
- Fish deaths can be delayed: after one DDT application, fish began dying 3 months later, with mortality reaching nearly 100 miles downstream.
- Fish caught alive with high residues had accumulated them in fat over time; stress that forces fat reserve use could be fatal.
- Brain residue levels in eagles fed differing DDT doses were very similar, suggesting residue analysis of vital organs may help interpret cause of death.

**Reproduction and growth**
- High residues in fish impaired reproduction (including complete failure), slowed growth, and weakened disease resistance.
- New York State trout study (4 years): heavy fry mortality occurred when eggs contained about 3 p.p.m. DDT or higher.
- A 1962 California Department of Fish and Game report found riceland pheasant eggs treated with DDT and dieldrin yielded fewer healthy 1-month-old chicks.

**Shellfish and crustaceans**
- One part DDT per billion parts water killed blue crabs in 8 days.
- Brown and pink shrimp exposed to 0.3–0.4 ppb of heptachlor, endrin, or lindane were killed or immobilized in 48-hour tests.
- Oysters at 10 parts per trillion DDT stored it, reaching levels 70,000 times the surrounding 0.1 ppb concentration after 40 days. Exposure reduced shell growth and pumping activity.

**Policy**
- PSAC report ("Use of Pesticides," May 15, 1963) recommended measuring pesticide levels in people and the environment, reviewing food tolerances, developing safer controls, tightening laws, and informing the public.
- The Department of the Interior reduced persistent pesticide use on its 550 million managed acres, prioritized nonchemical methods, and monitors residues in fish, wildlife, and shellfish. It participates in the [[Federal Committee on Pest Control]].

**Recommendations to citizens**
1. Use pesticides only after weighing need, expected results, and harm.
2. Use only registered chemicals and follow label instructions.
3. Prioritize safety over cost.
4. Limit treatments to target areas and protect waterways.
5. Use selective chemicals at minimum rates and avoid large-scale use of persistent, bioconcentrating compounds.

**Research directions**
- FWS research on reproduction, behavior, residue significance, and food-chain concentration.
- Malathion identified as relatively safe to birds and mammals at insecticidal rates.
- Alternatives under study: more specific chemicals, short-lived pesticides, biological control, sterile male insect release (which eradicated the screwworm fly in the Southeast and Texas), sex attractants, and bacterial breakdown of chemicals in water.
