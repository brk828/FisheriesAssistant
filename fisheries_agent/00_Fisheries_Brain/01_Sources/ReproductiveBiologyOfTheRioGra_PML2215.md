# Reproductive Biology of the Rio Grande Sucker and Rio Grande Chub in a Montane Stream, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the reproductive biology of the [[Rio Grande Sucker]] (*Catostomus plebeius*) and the [[Rio Grande Chub]] (*Gila pandora*) in the Rio de las Vacas, a third-order montane stream at about 2900 m elevation in northern New Mexico. Specimens were collected from March 1985 to June 1987 and analyzed for sex, size, gonadal index, fecundity, tuberculation and breeding coloration. Both species showed clear sexual dimorphism and a spawning peak in early summer, with a possible weaker autumn spawning period. Fecundity in suckers varied significantly with season, peaking in autumn. The paper was written in response to a scarcity of prior reproductive data on these species, which had long been treated as "trash fish."

## Background & Description

- **Rio Grande sucker:** First described by [[Baird and Girard]] in 1854 from the Rio Grande system of southern New Mexico. Historically described as a bottom feeder consuming algae and small invertebrates, and sometimes regarded as a competitor with trout and as a bait fish.
- **Rio Grande chub:** Previously confused with the [[Chihuahuan Chub]] (*Gila nigrescens*), which is found in the Rio Grande drainage, and with other Gila species. Its taxonomic status was clarified by [[Hubbs]] and others, and it is commonly a pool-inhabitant that associates with undercut banks and brush cover.
- Both species were previously poorly documented; this study was conducted as part of a grazing-effects project in the same stream ([[Rinne]] 1985, 1988).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** The Rio de las Vacas is affected by multiple uses including logging, recreation and livestock grazing. Parts of the stream were fenced to exclude grazing, and riparian vegetation (willow-alder community) was more abundant inside these exclosures.
- **Habitat condition:** Beaver are present and dam the stream. Streambanks are unstable in grazed reaches, and fine sediment content of the substrate increases where grazing occurs.
- **Comparative context:** Spawning timing in related catostomids (e.g., [[Desert Sucker]], [[Santa Ana Sucker]], [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]]) varies with latitude, elevation and water temperature, with spring and early summer spawning most common, and in some cases late winter or autumn.

## Key Findings

**Sample and methods**
- 395 suckers and chubs were collected from March 1985 to June 1987 (Table 1 and figures summarize the sex distribution; suckers slightly outnumbered chubs).
- Fish were collected by backpack electrofishing, preserved in 10% formaldehyde-sugar solution, measured, and gonad volume determined by displacement.
- Gonadal index = gonad volume / fish volume × 100. Fecundity was estimated by counting eggs in ovaries.

**Sexual dimorphism**
- Female suckers and chubs were significantly larger than males (P < 0.001).
- Female gonadal indices were significantly greater than male indices in both species (P < 0.001). No significant differences were found between species in gonadal index.
- Breeding color (presence of color) was significantly more common in females than males (P < 0.01) in both species. Color was related to fish size and month of collection, but regression models explained only about 15% of variation.
- Tuberculation was more pronounced in females: on the caudal peduncle, anal and caudal fins (chubs), and on the head and gular region and pectoral fins (suckers).

**Reproductive timing**
- Both species showed a spawning peak in June, with a possible smaller spawning period in September.
- Chubs displayed a prolonged spring spawning peak (April-June), followed by a briefer autumn period. Suckers peaked in early summer (June-July) with a less marked autumn increase.
- Gonadal indices differed significantly among sample dates (P < 0.001).

**Size at maturity**
- Suckers appeared to mature at about 190 mm TL, and chubs at about 90 mm TL, based on regression of gonadal index on total length.

**Fecundity**
- Mean fecundity was approximately 1,511 eggs in 21 female suckers and approximately 17,000 eggs in 14 female chubs (values as reported in the OCR text).
- Fecundity was not related to fish length, volume or gonadal index. Gonad volume explained only about 11% of variation in chub fecundity and about 56% in suckers.
- Sucker fecundity varied significantly with season (F = 4.7, P < 0.05), being highest in autumn and lowest in spring. Chubs showed no seasonal pattern.

**Interpretation and conservation implications**
- Results are consistent with the variability described for the genus, but the authors note that most prior information was qualitative.
- Suckers were historically regarded as "rough" or "trash" fish, which contributed to the lack of biological study. The authors argue that baseline biological data should be collected before such species become rare or endangered, as has happened to other suckers.
