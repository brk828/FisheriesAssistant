## Executive Summary

This 1995 opinion article by Maurice Kottelat is not a primary species-description paper but a methodological and philosophical treatise on the practice of ichthyological systematics amid a global biodiversity crisis. Drawing on his fieldwork in South and Southeast Asian freshwater systems, Kottelat argues that traditional, slow, exhaustive taxonomic methods are incompatible with the urgent pace of habitat loss in the tropics. He calls for a "pragmatic" reorientation of taxonomic practice — prioritizing rapid, functional identification tools and species descriptions over comprehensive monographic treatments — to keep systematic output in step with the rate of field discovery and habitat destruction. The paper is frequently cited in discussions of the [[Pragmatic Species Concept]] and remains a touchstone in debates over taxonomic efficiency versus rigor.

## Background & Description

While this is not a species-level morphological study, Kottelat addresses methodological issues around morphological data collection in taxonomic practice:

- **Diagnostic vs. exhaustive description**: Argues that abbreviated diagnoses based on unique, consistently observable character sets are sufficient for rapid species recognition, rather than exhaustive morphometric tables for large series of similar species.
- **Case-specific character validity**: Diagnostic characters may be restricted in scope (e.g., visible only in adults, or only in one sex), and such restrictions should be pragmatically acknowledged rather than treated as grounds for indefinite delay in description.
- **Example taxonomic group discussed**: The [[Nemacheilinae]] (loaches, order [[Cypriniformes]]) from Thailand, Myanmar, Laos, Cambodia, and southern Vietnam, subject of Kottelat's 1990 monographic revision, illustrating the tension between monographic completeness and rapid taxon description.
- **Nomenclatural case study**: The rainbow trout, historically *Salmo gairdnerii*, reclassified as *[[Oncorhynchus mykiss]]* — cited as evidence that even economically and scientifically important, intensively studied species can undergo major nomenclatural revision without practical disruption, undermining arguments for rigid nomenclatural conservatism.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's ecological content centers on the **conservation urgency** framing biodiversity documentation in Southeast Asian freshwater ecosystems:

- **Geographic scope**: Tropical South and Southeast Asian freshwater habitats (with explicit reference to Singapore, Sri Lanka, and the [[Mahakam River]] system in East Borneo, Indonesia).
- **Habitat threat context**: Field data collection is described as racing against rapid habitat degradation; the "poor prognosis for the future of most habitats in tropical areas" is presented as the central driver for methodological reform.
- **Taxonomic groups affected**: Freshwater fishes, along with co-occurring crabs and prawns, are highlighted as chronically under-documented faunal components, despite their importance as food sources.
- **Conservation-taxonomy link**: Formal species naming is argued to be a prerequisite for conservation attention — an unnamed, "merely mentioned" distinct population is argued to attract far less conservation research and legal/policy protection than a formally described species.

## Key Findings

- **Scale of undescribed diversity**: Approximately 1,400 freshwater fish species were known from Southeast Asia as of the paper's writing, with Kottelat predicting ~600 additional new species would be discovered within the following decade of active fieldwork.
- **Recent discovery rate**: Over three years of fieldwork (partly in collaboration with colleagues in Singapore and Sri Lanka), roughly 200 new species and genera of fishes, crabs, and prawns were discovered — indicating that even "well-known" taxa harbor comparable numbers of unresolved systematic problems.
- **Proposal of a "Pragmatic Species Concept"**: In the absence of feasible detailed phylogenetic analysis, populations that are consistently and unambiguously diagnosable by a unique character set and occur in discrete geographic areas should be pragmatically treated and named as species, sidestepping unresolvable debates over population/subspecies/species rank. This is noted to converge practically (if not philosophically) with the [[Phylogenetic Species Concept]] (Cracraft, 1989).
- **Critique of publication systems**: Major systematics journals are criticized for multi-year publication delays (1–2 years between acceptance and print; sometimes 5–10 years for full communications) and pay-to-publish models that disadvantage researchers from developing countries where fieldwork is concentrated.
- **Critique of nomenclatural procedure**: The formal mechanisms of the International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature are characterized as slow (often adding 2+ years to resolve a naming dispute) and frequently used defensively by authors to preserve their own junior synonyms rather than to serve genuine taxonomic stability.
- **Critique of "unproductive" taxonomist behavior patterns**: Introduces two informal syndromes hampering systematic output — the "Black Hole Syndrome" (researchers who accumulate specimens/data but never publish) and the "Sleeping Beauty Syndrome" (researchers who claim ongoing work indefinitely without output).
- **Amateur systematics**: While acknowledging valuable contributions from some non-professional systematists, the paper cautions that untrained or ego-driven taxonomic publication (particularly prevalent in groups like [[Mollusca]]) generates additional corrective workload for professional systematists and should be discouraged in the absence of adequate competence.
- **Recommended reform**: A call for alternative publication formats analogous to rapid-communication models used in biochemistry and molecular biology, allowing quick dissemination of new taxa/names ahead of full monographic treatment, paired with editorial and reviewer tolerance for this pragmatic style.
