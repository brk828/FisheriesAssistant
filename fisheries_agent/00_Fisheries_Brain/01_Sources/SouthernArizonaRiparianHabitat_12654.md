# Southern Arizona Riparian Habitat: Spatial Distribution and Analysis

#botany #plant-ecology #riparian-vegetation #phreatophytes

## Executive Summary

This 1975 University of Arizona bulletin (Lacey, Ogden and Foster) inventories and assesses riparian vegetation along four southeastern Arizona drainages using high- and medium-altitude aerial photography, with a test of satellite-based automated mapping. It was produced under a [[NASA]] remote sensing grant and was intended to supply basic data for state land-use legislation proposed in 1974 (Senate Bill 1049) on water courses and riparian environments on state-trust lands. The study maps nine plant communities, quantifies acreages by township, range and section and cover class, reviews historical and recent losses, and summarizes the products, uses and water-use controversies surrounding riparian vegetation. Fish are mentioned only in the historical section. The document's central conclusion is that southern Arizona's native riparian communities have declined sharply, with much of the floodplain converted to agriculture and replaced by the introduced [[Tamarisk]].

## Background & Description

**Objectives.** The study had seven objectives: mapping riparian vegetation on four stream reaches; testing automated mapping with [[LANDSAT-1 Satellite Imagery]] computer tapes; locating existing vegetation maps; summarizing riparian products and uses; documenting recent change on the San Pedro River; reviewing historical change; and listing sources of aerial photography.

**Definitions.** Riparian vegetation follows [[Lowe]] (1964) as plants occurring in or adjacent to drainageways and floodplains with life forms different from surrounding nonriparian climax vegetation. [[Phreatophytes]] (deep-rooted plants that draw water from the saturated zone, per [[Meinzer]] 1923) were treated as riparian when growing in channels and floodplains. The authors note that separating phreatophytes from riparian vegetation is largely academic.

**Methods.** Riparian communities were delineated on color-infrared and black-and-white aerial photographs (scales 1:25,000 to 1:125,000) following [[Horton]] et al. (1964), using the [[Brown and Lowe Classification]]. Boundaries were checked on the ground and by low-level flights, and acreages were read with a dot grid over [[USGS Topographic Maps]]. The authors caution that at 1:125,000 scale, 1/8 square inch represents about 40 acres, so small associations were often lumped together. A 36-year comparison on the San Pedro used 1936 Soil Conservation Service photographs against 1973 NASA imagery.

**Community classification.** Nine communities were recognized (Table 2):
- [[Cottonwood-Willow Community]] (gallery forest on the most mesic sites)
- [[Mesquite Bosque]] (mesquite dominant, typically on floodplains above the channel)
- [[Tamarisk]] (saltcedar, in lowest bottomlands and on recent floodplains)
- Seep Willow-Broom, Mixed Scrub and Burrobrush communities
- [[Sacaton Grass Community]]
- Mixed Grass-Scrub (San Simon Valley)
- Saltbush (terraces along the upper Gila and San Simon)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site dynamics.** Mesquite establishment is intolerant of shallow groundwater, so some channel cutting that lowers the water table benefits it. Mesquite roots were mostly within 25 ft of the surface, coinciding with groundwater at 45 ft or less (Zimmerman 1969). Saltcedar seeds from March through October, establishing during spring and summer flows, and it germinates in saturated soil. Seep willow needs sustained flow and is replaced by mesquite as water tables fall.

**Study areas.** The San Pedro River, San Simon Creek, Pantano Wash-Cienega Creek and the upper Gila River are dynamic, aggrading and degrading in different reaches. The lower Gila study site near Dome lies in the Sonoran Desert, where annual rainfall is about four inches and riparian plants depend largely on agricultural tailwater.

**Human pressures.** Clearing for agriculture, ditching, pumping and channel cutting have reshaped floodplains. The Reclamation Act of 1902 expanded irrigation. Groundwater in the Sierra Vista-Fort Huachuca area has declined about 30 ft in 25 years, with a cone of depression expected to expand (Roeske and Werrell 1973).

**Historical biota.** Accounts cited in the document report native fishes including [[Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a large minnow sometimes weighing 100 lbs and reaching six ft, and the [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), sold commercially in Tombstone. Mountain lions, beaver and grizzly bears were recorded in riparian woodland in the 1800s.

## Key Findings

**Community extent by drainage**
- **San Pedro River (Cochise County):** 18,700 riparian acres. Nearly 60% is mesquite, with about 3,600 acres of mesquite at more than 75% cover. [[Sacaton Grass Community]] covers 2,800 acres, mostly south of Highway 90. Saltcedar covers 870 acres.
- **San Simon Creek:** Mesquite dominates nearly 45% of riparian vegetation, and saltcedar about a fifth. No bosques were mapped on the main channel. About 2,000 acres of Mixed Grass-Scrub lie behind the fan drop structure, and about 1,000 acres of Saltbush were mapped as riparian.
- **Pantano Wash-Cienega Creek:** Mesquite is shrub-like on Pantano Wash but forms bosques on upper Cienega Creek. Creosote bush terraces were excluded from the riparian classification. Table 6 totals 7,959 acres.
- **Upper Gila River:** Mesquite again dominates nearly 45% of riparian vegetation. Cottonwood-Willow stands are small and isolated, the largest near Duncan.

**Automated mapping test (LANDSAT-1 digital data, Lower Gila study area)**
- Four vegetation classes were computer-classified, plus one natural and four agricultural classes (nine categories total).
- Overall performance was 96.9% on the lower 15-mile reach and 85.3% on the upper reach.
- The drop was attributed to saltbush, which has spectral signatures similar to creosote-mesquite. Only 33.9% of creosote-mesquite training pixels were correct in the combined analysis.
- The computer's riparian estimate (11,348 acres total) differed greatly from the reference map (3,838 acres). The authors concluded that standardized training sets are unlikely to work and that further research is needed before automated mapping of riparian communities can be relied upon.

**Historical loss**
- **San Pedro (22-mile reach, St. David to Cascabel Road):** Riparian communities declined from 10,690 acres in 1936 to 5,500 acres in 1972, a reduction of nearly 50%. That is about 144 acres per year, or 1.4% of the 1936 base annually.
- **Lower Gila:** Haase (1972) mapped 16,363 riparian acres within an estimated 111,360 acres of floodplain. The authors estimate about 85% of riparian communities have been converted to agriculture since 1860. Excluding saltcedar, only 5,285 native riparian acres remain, about 5% of the theoretical 1860 base.
- **Upper Gila:** Saltcedar rose from 146 acres (1937) to 1,006 acres (1964) on a 2,360-acre study site.
- **Irrigated acreage, San Pedro Valley:** Rose from 2,700 acres (1890) to 12,500 (1966), then fell to 9,700 (1970).

**Statewide estimates and their disagreement**
- Robinson's statewide phreatophyte inventory totals 279,600 acres, with 102,700 acres of saltcedar and 120,600 of mesquite and paloverde.
- The Interagency Group's riparian estimate is 106,000 acres.
- Sayers (1974) reported 1,407,584 acres of mesquite bosques statewide, a figure the authors consider high because of a broader definition of bosque.
- Only about 9,000 of 103,000 riparian acres (about 10%) occur on State Trust Land.

**Water use and salvage**
- Robinson (1958) estimated phreatophytes cover about 16 million acres in 17 Western States, discharging 25 million acre-feet annually.
- Estimated salvage yield is 1.5 to 2 acre-feet per acre cleared. Ffolliott and Thorud (1974) calculated that converting all 300,000 acres of Arizona riparian vegetation could add 600,000 acre-feet of annual yield, but noted many constraints.
- Clearing costs were about $50 per acre in early estimates and may have reached $350 per acre later (Gilluly 1971).

**Uses and economic value**
- **Firewood:** Mesquite firewood sold for about $55 per cord. Cost estimates were $37.50 per cord for two-ft wood and $27.50 for four-ft wood delivered. Charleston plot yields were 7.04 cords per acre at 721 ft³/acre, Fairbanks 2.18 cords (immature), St. David 6.18 cords.
- **Bees:** Arizona had 53,000 colonies producing $562,000 in honey and beeswax in 1971, with riparian habitat important as nectar source and as refuge from insecticides.
- **Doves:** Only 70,000 acres of suitable white-winged dove habitat were found among 26 million acres of southern Arizona (Wigal 1973). Mesquite bosques were historically important, and doves increasingly nest in saltcedar.
- **Wildlife:** Riparian vegetation supports fauna out of proportion to its acreage. Over 50% of 197 vertebrate species in Chevelon Canyon depend on riparian associations. Cattail marsh supports about 13 bird species nesting along the lower Gila, including the endangered Yuma clapper rail.
- **Forage:** Mesquite beans are palatable and digestible, comparable to alfalfa when ground. Sacaton is valuable green feed in spring. Bermuda grass produces over 6,000,000 lbs of seed annually, mostly from Yuma County.

**Recommendations and conclusions**
- The authors believe riparian areas should be protected under state land-use planning, and that remote sensing can provide the base inventory.
- Riparian vegetation should be classified and mapped at a scale suited to the community, and mesquite bosques should be defined using the Pettinger et al. (1970) definition (tree-sized mesquite completely dominating the aspect).
- Brown and Lowe recommend setting aside representative areas for investigation and controlling grazing.
- The authors conclude that riparian woodlands may be irreversibly lost on the lower Gila and consider native riparian communities a nonrenewable resource in many areas.
