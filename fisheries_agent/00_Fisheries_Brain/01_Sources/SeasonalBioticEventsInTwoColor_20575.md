# Seasonal Biotic Events in Two Colorado Alpine Tundra Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1959 limnological study by [[Eugene H. Schmitz]] (University of Colorado, Boulder) describes two small permanent alpine tundra ponds, Washboiler Pond and Dead Hat Pond, at 3,582 m near Loveland Pass, Summit County, Colorado. Sampling ran from July 18 to October 19, 1957, covering the entire open season at 5 to 11 day intervals. The focus was the benthic fauna and zooplankton, with routine physical and chemical measurements. No fish were reported; the biota consisted of invertebrates and plankton. The ponds were poor in species, dominated by the [[Pisidium-Tendipedid-Daphnia Community]], and were limited by low dissolved solids, low organic content, cold temperatures and a short open season.

## Background & Description

- **Study sites:** Washboiler Pond (about 2 × 6 m, maximum depth 0.56 m) and Dead Hat Pond (about 4 × 9 m, maximum depth 0.50 m), 30 m apart, in a glaciated valley on the southwest foot of Mount Sniktau at 39°40′N, 105°52′W.
- **Setting:** Above timberline, well above the [[Alpine Limnological Zone]] lower limit of about 3,200 m suggested by [[Robert W. Pennak]]. The valley was probably formed in the late Wisconsin by glacial plucking.
- **Basins:** Saucer-shaped depressions in alpine meadow, fed by melting snow banks and frequent showers. Dead Hat Pond has a northeast bay (0.24 m deep) and an island with Carex growth. Both have outlets.
- **Bottom deposits:** Black, decaying, putrid sediments that emit hydrogen sulfide. Classified as sapropel, with coprogenic gyttja. Dead Hat Pond's deposits are thinner and mixed with sand and pebbles in its eastern half.
- **Ice regime:** The ponds are aestival, holding water all season and freezing to the bottom in winter. Ice was gone by mid-July, and by late October they had ice thick enough to support a person.
- **Methods:** Grid-based bathymetric mapping; surface and bottom temperatures; color (platinum-cobalt method); pH (Hellige discs); free and bound CO₂ by titration; residue and sediment loss-on-ignition; benthos sampled with a 12 × 12 × 2.8 cm scoop (1/70 m² per sample), sieved through a number 20 screen; zooplankton from 2.7 L bottle samples counted in a 1 cc Sedgewick-Rafter chamber; three-point running averages to smooth irregular visit intervals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions**
- Temperatures were generally higher in Dead Hat Pond. Thin ice films on October 12 and 19 produced lower surface than bottom temperatures.
- Temporary microthermoclines formed because the ponds are sheltered and small, which limits wind mixing.
- Water color was a weak tea color: Washboiler Pond stayed at 100 until late in the season, falling to 60; Dead Hat Pond fell from 70 to 50.

**Chemistry**
- pH stayed slightly acidic: 6.0–6.4 (Washboiler) and 6.0–6.8 (Dead Hat).
- Free CO₂ was low; the seasonal high was 3 ppm in Washboiler Pond on August 9. Bound CO₂ averaged 3.2 ppm and 3.7 ppm respectively, with a high of 9 ppm on October 5 in Washboiler.
- Total ash ranged 23.1–27.8 mg/L (Washboiler) and 18.2–19.9 mg/L (Dead Hat). Total organic matter was 11.1–19.3 mg/L and 9.5–9.9 mg/L.
- Substrate organic matter was 52.1% (Washboiler) and 51.6% (Dead Hat).
- The waters are "soft" and young, reflecting insoluble local bedrock with low carbonate content.

**Vegetation**
- Only emergent *Carex aquatilis* grew, at depths of 0.1 m or less. No rooted aquatic plants occurred deeper. The greatest variety of metazoans occurred in these shallow Carex zones.

**Comparative context**
- The study cited [[Neldner and Pennak]] (1955) on Trail Ridge Pond, which had phyllopods that were absent from these ponds.
- Higher-altitude [[Medicine Bow Mountains Moraine Ponds]] (Blake, 1945) had longer open seasons and warmer water.
- [[Churchill Manitoba Tundra Ponds]] (McClure, 1943) lacked Pelecypoda but had up to 80,000 metazoans per cubic foot.

## Key Findings

**Benthic fauna**
- Three groups dominated: Diptera (Tendipedidae), Pelecypoda (Sphaeriidae) and Oligochaeta (Tubificidae).
- *Tendipes* sp. was the only dipteran in the superficial sediments. Free-ranging larvae peaked at 350/m² in Washboiler Pond on August 31, and cocoons peaked at 630/m² on October 19 in Washboiler Pond. The authors conclude it overwinters in cocoons and has one generation per year.
- *Metriocnemus* sp. was bimodal, peaking at 315/m² (July 18) and 175/m² (October 12) in Washboiler, and at 140/m² and 232/m² in Dead Hat. It produces two generations per year. Its midseason low coincided with peak *Tendipes* numbers.
- Tubificidae ranged 70–490/m² in Washboiler Pond but were nearly absent from Dead Hat Pond (0–35/m²). The thicker organic deposits likely explain the difference.
- *Pisidium casertanum* was least abundant overall but was more abundant in Dead Hat Pond, peaking at 163/m² on July 26.
- Mean open-season standing crops were 388 lb/acre (Washboiler) and 184 lb/acre (Dead Hat). Comparisons: Chautauqua Lake (374 lb/acre, from [[Townes]] 1938) and Lake Texoma (111 lb/acre, from [[Sublette]] 1957). The author judged the ponds "normally rich" by Townes' standard.

**Zooplankton**
- [[Daphnia pulex]] was dominant. Washboiler Pond peaked at 91 individuals/L on September 28, against only 16/L in Dead Hat Pond on October 5.
- *Chydorus sphaericus* peaked at 9.8/L in Washboiler and 0.7/L in Dead Hat.
- Copepods *Canthocamptus sinuus* and *Cyclops varicans* were recorded regularly. These are likely new records for the Colorado mountain area.
- Rotifers and protozoans were nearly absent: one *Keratella cochlearis* and two *Actinophrys sol* specimens.
- Ephippial (resting) eggs of *Daphnia* appeared as early as August 16 in Washboiler Pond, in anticipation of winter.

**Phytoplankton and miscellaneous fauna**
- Quantitative counts were not made. Genera from Chrysophyta, Chlorophyta and Cyanophyta were observed.
- Large greenish colonies of *Polycystis (= Microcystis) incerta* formed "marbles" around pond edges, eventually forming continuous rings.
- Shoreline taxa included *Limnephilus* (caddisfly), *Notonecta undulata*, *Aedes* (mosquito), *Agabus* and *Hygrotus* (beetles), *Mesostoma* (rhabdocoel), *Limnesia* (water mite), a nematode and an ostracod.

**Conclusions**
- Both ponds were species-poor. Low dissolved solids, low organic content, physiography and climate limit the fauna.
- Absent groups include phyllopods, amphipods, mayfly and odonate nymphs, and snails.
- The most conspicuous community is the Pisidium–Tendipedidae–Daphnia association.
- Because the biota is dormant for nearly nine months a year, succession in these ponds is slow.
