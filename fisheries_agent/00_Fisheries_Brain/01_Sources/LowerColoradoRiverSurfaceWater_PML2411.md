# Lower Colorado River Surface Water and Shoreline Change, 1903 to 1982

#aquatic-habitat #hydrology #lower-colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This document is a cover letter dated July 5, 1983, from [[Southwest Natural History Association]] to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Regional Office), together with a set of reach-by-reach tables and map-based worksheets. It transmits data required under Purchase Order No. 3-PG-30-09150. The data compare the mapped extent of surface water, shoreline, islands, canals, and channels along the [[Lower Colorado River]] in 1903 with the same features in 1982. The letter also offers a proposal for an expanded analysis of historic change on the river.

No fish species, invertebrates, or biological sampling are addressed. The work is a physical habitat inventory, so the species fields are recorded as N/A.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and authors.** The letter is signed by Mrs. Loucella B. Brown, President of the Southwest Natural History Association, Phoenix, Arizona. It states that the tables and maps are "based largely on 1903 survey data," with 1982 data as the comparison.

**Definitions used in the data:**
- **River:** the normal river channel(s) and all water areas connected to the main channel, including reservoirs.
- **Lakes:** water bodies completely separated from the main channel but within the Colorado River flood plain. Connected water bodies joined only by canals or channels were classed as lakes in the methods note.
- **Islands:** land completely surrounded by the main channel or by lakes.
- **Shoreline:** both shores of the main channel, secondary channel lengths, and lake and island shorelines.
- **Canals:** man-made water conduits, identified on 1982 maps by straight form and dashed blue lines. This includes major canals such as the [[All-American Canal]].
- **Channels:** water courses of apparently natural origin, marked with solid blue lines.

**Methods notes.** The worksheets state that water levels differed by map period. Some reaches are reported on high-water, low-water, or mean-of-both levels. Conversion factors were used to convert map measurements, given for the 1903 maps and the 1982 maps. The 1982 maps appear to show less of the river valley than the 1903 maps, so the authors judged that 1982 canal lengths were comparatively underestimated. The 1982 maps also show clearly fewer channels, particularly in Divisions 1 and 11.

**Structure of the data.** The river was divided into 15 reaches, from [[Davis Dam]] south to the Sonora border. Each reach has a summary table of water area (river, lakes, total), canal length, shoreline length (river, lakes, islands, total), and island area, with 1927 (1903 survey), 1982, and change columns. Worksheets for the numbered divisions break the same data down by map sheet.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The river and its margins contain several features of ecological interest, most of them engineered:

- **Dams and diversions:** [[Davis Dam]], [[Laguna Dam]], [[Palo Verde Diversion Dam]], [[Headgate Rock Dam]], [[Imperial Dam]], and [[Morelos Dam]] divide the river into reaches with different water and shoreline histories.
- **Wetlands and refuges:** the letter cites stabilized wetlands upstream, specifically the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]], as a major offset to losses downstream.
- **Confluences and delta areas:** the [[Bill Williams River]] and its delta are included in Reach 6, with 4.34 km of the river noted as part of that reach. The [[Gila River Delta]] is measured as a separate feature in Reach 14.
- **Reservoirs and impoundments:** Reach 1 includes an industrial waste reservoir built for a power plant, which the table notes separately.
- **Named localities:** the reaches reference [[Topock]], [[Cibola Lake]], and the Sonora (Mexican) border.

## Key Findings

1. **Shoreline length is nearly unchanged overall.** The letter states that total shoreline in 1982 is "almost exactly the same" as in 1927 (1903 data). Losses in the lower reaches were offset by greater shoreline along upstream reservoirs. The exact figure is garbled in the scan.
2. **Surface water increased substantially.** The letter reports an overall gain in surface water of "almost 50%" between 1903 and 1982. Losses below Laguna Dam were more than offset by increased mean flows, reservoirs, and stabilized upstream wetlands.
3. **Island area declined the most.** The authors identify the major overall loss as islands. Both island shoreline and island area decreased considerably over the interval.
4. **Canal length nearly quadrupled.** The letter states that canals "gained almost fourfold," although the 1982 canal values are likely an underestimate because of map differences.
5. **Channel mapping differs between periods.** The 1982 maps show far fewer natural channels than the 1903 maps, which the authors say is especially true of Divisions 11 and 12. Some change figures therefore reflect map differences as well as physical change.
6. **Reach-level variation.** Reaches differ sharply. For example, Reach 4 shows no canal length in either year and a large increase in river area, while Reach 5 shows a large gain in water area and shoreline. Reach totals should be read alongside their water-level basis, since some are based on high water, others on low water, and others on the mean of both.
7. **Data limitations.** The summary tables are partly unreadable in the scan, and several values cannot be recovered with confidence. The 1903 data are the baseline, and the authors note that map differences limit the comparison.
8. **Future work offered.** The authors state that they "enjoyed doing the work" and propose a larger study: an expanded treatment and explanation of historic changes on the river, if the Bureau is interested.
