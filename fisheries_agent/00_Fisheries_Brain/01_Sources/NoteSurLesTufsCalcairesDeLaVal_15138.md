# Note on the Calcareous Tufas of the Hoyoux Valley

#bryophytes #phycology #calcareous-tufa #limnology

## Executive Summary

This short 1950 botanical note by J. J. Symoens, with contributions from P. Duvigneaud, J. Dewit and C. Krwak, reviews and extends knowledge of calcareous tufa (tuf calcaire) deposits in the [[Hoyoux River]] valley of the Condroz, Belgium. Tufa forms when aquatic plants remove carbon dioxide from calcium-bicarbonate-rich water, precipitating [[Calcium Carbonate]]. The author collates earlier geological and palaeontological records, reports new observations of active tufa formation, and lists the mosses, cyanobacteria and diatoms found in the encrustations. The note concludes that the tufa is largely biogenic and still forming today.

## Background & Description

- **Biogenic decalcification:** In waters rich in calcium bicarbonate, photosynthesis by plants converts Ca(HCO3)2 to CaCO3, water and CO2. The CO2 is taken up for chlorophyll assimilation, and the poorly soluble CaCO3 accumulates as tufa. This process is linked to [[Photosynthesis]].
- **Forms of tufa:** Deposits occur as sediments on lake or pond floors, as continuous crusts, as small transverse dams in stream beds built from encrusted pebbles, twigs and mosses, and as steep rock-like formations in fast-flowing runnels or on hillsides.
- **Stream setting:** The Hoyoux rises near the hamlet of Buzin, near Verlée. It is a large brook that becomes a small river with a steep gradient of 7.6–8.3 ‰ in its lower course. Its water is rich in calcium bicarbonate.
- **Fossil record:** Earlier authors reported mostly still-living mollusc shells, plus remains of [[Red Deer]] (Cervus elaphus) and [[Eurasian Beaver]] (Castor faber). Dumont (1832) noted abundant tubular structures, plant branches and leaves. Stockmans (1947) found plant imprints in an old tufa quarry about 550 m west of the Régissa railway station.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mechanism at the historic deposit:** Van den Broeck, Martel and Rahir (1910) argued that tufa deposition at the old site was not caused by a lake or stagnant water. It was caused by agitation and aeration of water falling from a rock barrage that once crossed the river.
- **Modern precipitation:** Tufa is still forming in the river bed. Encrustations were seen on small artificial weirs near the water installations of the Compagnie Intercommunale des Eaux de l'Agglomération Bruxelloise at [[Petit-Modave]].
- **Cascade sites:** The best-known deposits lie between Barse, Royseux and Régissa. They form a series of picturesque cascades described by Lohest and Forir (1904), Lesperneux (1904) and Van den Broeck et al. (1910). See [[Barse Cascades]] and [[Royseux Mill]].
- **Biogenic origin:** The tufa is generally attributed to mosses and algae. Carpot examined moss specimens from the cascades and provisionally assigned them to a form derived from [[Cratoneuron Filicinum]], closely related to var. Vallis-Clausae.
- **Riparian vegetation:** Plates show [[Petasites Hybridus]] vegetation growing on tufa downstream of the Royseux Mill.

## Key Findings

1. **Active tufa formation:** Calcareous tufa is still precipitating in the Hoyoux. Encrustations occur at Petit-Modave and in the cascades of Barse, Royseux and Régissa.
2. **Bryophytes:** The author recorded [[Brachythecium Rivulare]] and [[Fissidens Crassipes]] in the Hoyoux cascades. Plate 2 shows Fissidens crassipes encrusted with calcium carbonate at the Royseux Mill (January 1947).
3. **Cyanobacteria:** The dominant encrusting alga is a [[Lyngbya]] species, accompanied by other Cyanophyceae, notably [[Schizothrix Coriacea]].
4. **Diatoms:** About 13 diatom taxa were identified among the moss encrustations, including Diatoma vulgare, Meridion circulare, Synedra ulna, Cocconeis pediculus, Rhoicosphenia curvata, Gyrosigma attenuatum, Navicula gracilis (marked CC, meaning common) and Gomphonema olivaceum. Most of these were also reported from the tufas of [[Consdorf]] in Luxembourg (Symoens and Van der Werff, 1951).
5. **Causal mechanism:** Agitation and aeration of falling water, not stagnation, are considered the main drivers of tufa deposition at the historic barrage site.
6. **Fossils:** Mollusc shells, Cervus elaphus and Castor faber remains, tubular structures and plant material have been documented from the tufa.
7. **Bibliography:** The note cites about 11 references, from Davreux (1833) to Van den Broeck et al. (1910). Forir's 1904 paper appears in the text, but the bibliography dates his 1897 excursion report differently, a discrepancy present in the source.
