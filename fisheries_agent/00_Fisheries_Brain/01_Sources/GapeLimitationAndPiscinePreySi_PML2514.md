# Gape Limitation and Prey Size-Selection by Yellow Perch in Southern Lake Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined trophic links between [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) and two non-indigenous prey species, [[Alewife]] (*Alosa pseudoharengus*) and [[Round Goby]] (*Neogobius melanostomus*), in the extreme southern area of [[Lake Michigan]] during summer 2002. Yellow perch stomachs were sampled and gape and prey size were measured to determine whether size selection was constrained by morphology. Yellow perch consumed prey that were consistently smaller than the maximum sizes their gapes could physically accommodate, indicating that behavioural factors, not gape alone, limit prey choice.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Yellow perch were historically an important sport and commercial fish in southern Lake Michigan, but their abundance has fluctuated sharply. The Indiana commercial fishery closed in the mid-1990s and sport bag limits were reduced to protect the stock.
- **Alewife:** A non-indigenous planktivore established in Lake Michigan in 1949, linked in earlier studies to declines in yellow perch recruitment.
- **Round goby:** A non-indigenous benthic fish first found in the St. Clair River in 1990 and established in southwestern Lake Michigan by the early 1990s. Its aggressive behaviour and high recruitment potential have allowed it to spread widely, and it has nearly extirpated [[Mottled Sculpin]] and [[Johnny Darter]] populations in the region.
- **Gape theory:** Gape size is a widely recognised constraint on predator-prey size relationships, though prey morphology and behaviour can also matter. Prior work on adult yellow perch was limited.
- **Study objectives:** (1) Determine whether yellow perch selected fish prey by species and size relative to availability, and (2) relate this to theoretical maximum prey size limits set by gape.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling area and design**
- Lake Michigan covers roughly 57,850 km² and is one of the five [[Laurentian Great Lakes]]. Sampling sites were located near Michigan City and Gary, Indiana, in state waters (sites G, K and M).
- Night-time bottom trawls (active gear) and gillnets (passive gear) were used from 6 June to 21 August 2002. Trawling ran for six 10-minute tows per site-night, and gillnets were set for 12 to 13 hours, totalling 6 net-nights per site.
- Fish were placed on ice immediately after capture to slow digestion and prevent regurgitation.

**Diet analysis**
- Only yellow perch of 100 mm total length or larger were analysed, since smaller fish were not consuming alewives or round gobies and the ontogenetic shift to piscivory typically begins at 150 to 200 mm.
- Stomach contents were corrected for formalin shrinkage (factor of 12.22%) and linear regression equations converted measured body parts to total length and volume.

**Population context**
- Earlier diet studies (1972 and 1984) found mottled sculpin and johnny darter as dominant prey. By 2002, round goby and alewife had become major prey.
- Alewife abundance in 2002 was among the highest observed in two decades, and southern Lake Michigan experienced several consecutive years of poor yellow perch recruitment attributed to alewife.

## Key Findings

**Sample sizes**
- 2,212 yellow perch were captured; 1,293 stomachs were sub-sampled and 268 contained alewife or round goby.
- 148 yellow perch contained round gobies (all over 100 mm TL) and 120 contained alewives (all over 175 mm TL).

**Prey size selection**
- Round gobies consumed were 7 to 47% of predator total length (mean 23%). Round gobies were found in 21% of stomachs and made up 40% of food by volume.
- Alewives consumed ranged from 18 to 46% of predator total length (mean 32%). Alewives occurred in 17% of stomachs and made up 45% of food by volume.
- Round gobies and alewives together made up 79% of the diet by volume.
- Prey consumed were significantly smaller than those available in the trawl catch within months for round goby, and for alewife in June and July. No alewives were consumed in August despite their availability.

**Gape limitation**
- Vertical gape was used as the limiting dimension because it was the smaller of the two gape measurements for both sexes. Males and females did not differ in gape (ANCOVA, P > 0.05).
- Maximum ingestible prey size, based on gape and prey morphology, was consistently larger than the prey actually consumed.

**Key interpretation**
- Yellow perch shifted from historic prey to round goby, a shift attributed to the round goby invasion and declines in benthic invertebrates. This supports the view that yellow perch are generalist feeders.
- Smaller yellow perch (150 to 250 mm) increasingly relied on round gobies, since alewives were only eaten by fish over 175 mm.
- Round gobies over 100 mm were rarely present in yellow perch stomachs when available, suggesting a size refuge. Behavioural factors such as round goby aggression and handling time, as well as stomach fullness, are likely contributors.
- The authors suggest these findings may apply to other waters where yellow perch, round goby and alewife co-occur.
