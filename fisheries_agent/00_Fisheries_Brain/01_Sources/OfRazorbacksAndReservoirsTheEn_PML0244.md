# Of Razorbacks and Reservoirs: Section 7 Violations at Flaming Gorge Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document is a legal article from the *Pace Environmental Law Review* (Vol. 11, 1993, pp. 62–63 legible), titled "Of Razorbacks and Reservoirs." It is a legal analysis, not a field study. Much of the scanned text is OCR-corrupted, so the summary relies on the legible passages (roughly pages 15–17). The author argues that the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BR) violated Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] in operating [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], building and running the [[Strawberry Aqueduct]], and operating the [[Jensen Unit]] in the [[Colorado River Basin]].

## Background & Description

The article concerns endangered fishes of the upper [[Colorado River Basin]], which the title associates with razorbacks. The fishes are protected by a [[Biological Opinion]] process under [[Section 7 Consultation]]. Three projects depend on Flaming Gorge reoperation as their [[Reasonable and Prudent Alternative]] (RPA):

- Flaming Gorge Dam operation
- Strawberry Aqueduct
- Jensen Unit

FWS issued [[Jeopardy Opinion]]s for these projects, and for four [[CUP Projects]], in the early 1980s. The author treats the opinions as based on incomplete information.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water releases:** Flaming Gorge Dam continued normal or nearly normal operation for more than a decade after its jeopardy opinion. The released water was not dedicated to endangered fish.
- **Instream flows:** The [[Flaming Gorge Final Draft Opinion]] (Feb. 11, 1992) states it is "critical" that BR secure legal protection so water stays instream in the Green River below the Duchesne River confluence, to offset depletions from Strawberry Aqueduct.
- **Flow changes:** BR reportedly slightly increased flows during Aqueduct operation. No formal biological opinion confirms these releases are adequate, and there is no proof they benefit endangered fish rather than downstream [[Water Appropriators]].
- **Legal status of flows:** The author states BR had apparently taken no steps to protect instream flows as of the article's writing.

## Key Findings

1. **Section 7(a)(2) violation by FWS:** The author argues FWS violated the substantive ban on jeopardizing endangered species while preparing the Flaming Gorge biological opinion. Jeopardy opinions had already been issued for the three dependent projects. Operating those projects without an RPA in place was therefore a clear violation.

2. **Jeopardy opinions as conclusive evidence:** Citing the [[North Slope Case]] district court, the author states a jeopardy opinion is conclusive evidence that an agency action is likely to violate Section 7(a)(2). The North Slope case had a different outcome, because NMFS had found Interior's lease sales unlikely to jeopardize endangered whales.

3. **Section 7(d) "irreversible or irretrievable commitment" violation:**
   - The ban applies until a complete biological opinion is in place.
   - The RPA, Flaming Gorge reoperation, had not been fully implemented. "Normal operation," which led to the jeopardy finding, continued until 1984.
   - BR nonetheless built Strawberry Aqueduct and the Jensen Unit and put them into operation before the RPA was in place.
   - Federal spending on construction is "the archetypical irretrievable commitment" of resources.

4. **Water as a Section 7(d) resource:** The author argues "resources" need not be monetary. Water in the Colorado River Basin is valuable, and each year of release has irretrievably committed impounded water to uses other than endangered fish protection. The release also forecloses implementing the RPA, because the proposed RPA was release of that same water.

5. **Research defense rejected:** BR argued its releases were part of a study of endangered fish habitat requirements. The author notes the North Slope court suggested research commitments generally would not violate Section 7(d). The author argues the necessary condition in North Slope was that the lease sales were not likely to jeopardize species survival, which these projects do not meet.

6. **Summary conclusion:** The author concludes BR violated Section 7 by continuing construction and operation of the CUP projects and Flaming Gorge Dam before being reasonably certain the RPA would protect the species.

## Related Cases Cited

- *North Slope* (district court analysis of Section 7(a)(2) and 7(d))
- *Nebraska v. Rural Electrification Administration* (D. Neb. 1978), on Section 7(d) irretrievable commitments
- *Pacific Coast Federation of Fishermen's Associations v. Lujan* (E.D. Cal. 1992), which offered a similar Section 7(d) argument
