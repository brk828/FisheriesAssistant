# Surface Waves and Subsurface Particle Movement due to Waves

#limnology #physical-limnology #wave-hydrodynamics

## Executive Summary

This report by [[Aivars Bergs]] (University of Waterloo, Department of Mechanical Engineering), issued as Pub. No. 13 of the [[Great Lakes Institute]] (University of Toronto) research series, documents a 1964 field study of wind-generated surface waves on [[Lake Huron]]. Waves were recorded with a [[Pressure Type Wave Recorder]] deployed near the [[Douglas Point Nuclear Power Reactor]], and six record samples were analyzed by [[Harmonic Analysis]]. The study tests whether [[Classical Wave Theory]] describes real lake waves. Wave heights from the records broadly agree with hindcast predictions, but the recorder could not resolve waves below about 1 m, and measured wave lengths, velocities and subsurface particle movements departed clearly from classical theory.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The work was part of a hydrodynamic research program on the Great Lakes run by the [[Water Resources Institute]] (University of Waterloo) with the [[Great Lakes Institute]] (University of Toronto). It had two aims:
1. Determine the wave amplitude-frequency spectrum.
2. Examine subsurface pressure pulses and orbital motion to judge how useful simple classical wave theories are.

The study was organized into three groups: (1) surface waves inferred from subsurface pressure, (2) relations among observed wave "period," "length" and "velocity," and (3) movement of individual water particles or suspended sediment under passing waves.

**Field setting.** Fieldwork was carried out in summer 1964 at the [[Great Lakes Research Station]], Baie du Doré, near Kincardine, Ontario. The study was deliberately conducted on natural waves rather than under laboratory control, which limited its scope.

**Instrumentation.**
- The sensor was installed about 1200 m offshore, 5.5 m below Still Water Level (SWL), in 23 m deep water.
- Installation was completed on 24 June 1964; removal began on 2 September 1964.
- The Wave Recording System Mk II, supplied by the [[National Research Council]] (Ottawa), uses two differential pressure transducers (one for wave-induced pressure, one for submergence depth) connected by a 4-lead cable to a shore cabinet.
- The chart recorder was 10 inches wide, advancing at 6 inches per minute.
- The system sampled every 3 hours, each sample about 8 minutes long and containing about 60 waves (as stated in the Measurement section).
- The stated linear response is reliable below about 0.3 CPS; above that, inertial and viscous damping makes the record unreliable.

**Fetch.** The longest fetch was 240 km. The six analyzed records came from waves generated by winds from the northwest (Fig. 1).

**Theoretical framework.** The classical equations (first-order, small-amplitude sinusoidal theory, per Lamb 1945, Stoker 1957, Wiegel 1964) are summarized in the paper:
- Pressure response (attenuation) factor K, relating pressure at depth to surface pressure, through [[Pressure Attenuation Factor]].
- Wavelength from depth and period, with deep-water limits (L₀ and C₀ relations).
- Horizontal particle displacement under a progressive wave, a = (H/2)·cosh(…)/sinh(…), with a simplified deep-water form for measured period T.

The author notes that real wind waves in deep water are irregular "humps and hollows," not long-crested sinusoids, so the classical assumptions (small height relative to wavelength; crest length several times wavelength) do not hold. To make the theory applicable, the surface is treated as a superposition of many simple sine waves, following [[Pierson-Neumann-James Method]] (1955).

**Definitions used.** "Period" means the interval between successive crests passing a fixed point; the true period is the minimum interval after which a record repeats exactly. The same distinction applies to wavelength and speed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a physical limnology and wave-mechanics study. It contains no biological, fish, or species data. Its environmental relevance lies in the physical setting:
- Waves on the open lake are generated by northwesterly winds over fetches up to 240 km.
- Subsurface water particle motion and suspended sediment movement under waves are addressed as a route to understanding nearshore and benthic physical conditions, including the effects on objects near the lake bed (the particle test was 1.2 m above bottom in 12.8 m of water).
- Sensor installation near a nuclear power reactor's offshore intake/outfall area is noted only as the instrument site.
- A test for [[Surf Beats]] (low-frequency wave energy) found no evidence at this location.

## Key Findings

**1. Wave spectra and significant wave heights**
- Six record samples were analyzed by harmonic analysis over 0.003 to 0.33 CPS. Samples 1 and 2 were from two different storms; samples 3, 4, 5 and 6 came from a third storm taken at 3-hour intervals.
- Each sample extended about 4 minutes and contained about 30 waves (as stated in the Harmonic Analysis section).
- Wave spectra and co-cumulative spectra (C.C.S.) were calculated. Spectral energy for each component was computed as A²/f.
- Wave amplitudes above about 0.22 CPS were erratic (instrument noise plus high pressure multiplication factors) and were discarded.
- Significant wave heights from the analysis compare reasonably well with the hindcasting methods of [[Pierson-Neumann-James Method]] (1955) and [[Bretschneider Method]] (1958). Example values (Table 1, partly illegible in the scan): sample 2 analysis 1.27 m, P.N.J. 1.31 m, Bretschneider 1.52 m; sample 4 analysis 1.39 m, P.N.J. 1.52 m, Bretschneider 1.58 m; sample 5 analysis 1.38 m, P.N.J. 1.40 m, Bretschneider 1.22 m.
- Samples 1 (low wind speed, 4.5 m/s) and 6 (singular) were excluded from the main comparison. Wind was measured 10 m above ground, 50 m inland, at the end of the fetch and averaged over 6 hours.
- The Pierson et al. hindcast assumed a fully developed sea; Bretschneider's values are for fully developed conditions for comparison, so both wave heights and frequencies may be higher than true values.

**2. Accuracy of the pressure record**
- Observed waves (1/2 to 1 m) were compared with the chart record using two methods: radio-relayed readings against a marked pole (6-inch graduations), and frame-by-frame analysis of movie film.
- Pressure fluctuations at 5.5 m below SWL were too small to register for the film-tested waves, and the classical pressure model did not match the observed pressure response.
- Sensitivity is clearly lacking for waves below 1 m in height.
- Waves above 1 m could not be checked because of rough-sea limitations.

**3. Subsurface particle movement**
- A neutrally buoyant painted table-tennis ball on a 4-ft nylon line served as the water particle. Movies were taken from above and below.
- Surface waves of 30 to 45 cm were observed. Individual wave-to-movement correlation was not possible because the underwater timing watch was unreadable.
- Measured horizontal particle movements (Fig. 4, 1.2 m above bottom in 12.8 m water) were clearly higher than predicted by the classical equation, and the author suggests a possible departure from classical theory, pending more measurements.

**4. Wave lengths and velocities**
- Wave lengths were measured with a buoy on a calibrated rope, and periods with stop watches.
- Measured values were "completely divorced" from classical theory (Figs. 5 and 6). The author attributes this mainly to the irregular, short-crested surface of a choppy sea.

**5. Conclusions**
- The [[Pressure Type Wave Recorder]] is efficient and reliable within its limits, but its record is a poor replica of short-crested surface waves up to 1 m and of irregular periods.
- Records of significant wave height above 1 m agree fairly closely with hindcasts, so the analyzed samples are considered representative of high-energy surface waves.
- The observed wave length/velocity relations are incompatible with classical theory.
- Particle movement suggests a possible departure from classical theory; further measurements are needed to establish it.
