#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary
This study by Royal D. Suttkus (1955) examines age and growth of a "stunted" population of [[Smallmouth Black Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu dolomieu*) inhabiting [[Fall Creek]], a tributary of Cayuga Lake in Tompkins County, New York. Based on 966 specimens collected in 1948-1949 via angling, seining, and electric shocking, the study found that despite exhibiting classic signs of growth stunting (reduced length-at-age compared to other populations, near-complete absence of fish beyond age V), the Fall Creek bass were paradoxically in good physical condition (average coefficient of condition K=2.35-2.41), suggesting food availability was not a limiting factor. Annuli formation occurred in late May/early June. No significant sex-based difference in growth rate was detected. The findings support the hypothesis that small-stream environments intrinsically constrain smallmouth bass growth and longevity relative to lakes and large rivers.

## Background & Description
- **Length conversions** (fork length ↔ standard/total length; n=195 specimens, range 69–316 mm FL):
  - Fork length : Standard length = 1 : 0.862
  - Fork length : Total length = 1 : 1.044
- **Scale-Fork Length Relationship** (second-degree parabola, n=489):
  - Y = 3.5008 + 0.3365X + 0.000543X² (Y = scale radius ×35 in mm; X = fork length in mm)
- **Length-Weight Relationship** (n=488):
  - log W = 4.28802 + 2.85425 log L (W = weight in grams, L = standard length in mm)
- **Coefficient of Condition (K)**:
  - Average K = 2.41 (using Tate's 1:0.854 conversion ratio) or 2.35 (using this study's 1:0.862 ratio)
  - K increased steadily with fork length, from ~2.16–2.28 in smaller size classes (30–99 mm) to ~2.42–2.56 in larger size classes (220–279 mm)
  - Compared to [[Coefficient of Condition]] values reported elsewhere: Stroud (1948, Norris Reservoir, TN) K=2.08–2.35 (avg 2.21); Bennett (1938, Wisconsin) K=1.96–2.45 (avg 2.20); Tate (1949, Iowa streams) K=2.49
  - Fall Creek bass were relatively plump despite stunted growth, indicating adequate food supply
- Age determined via scale impressions (cellulose acetate roller-press method), viewed under microprojector at 35× magnification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: [[Fall Creek]] spans ~18 miles from the village of McLean (elevation 1,120 ft) to Beebe Lake (elevation 800 ft), a drop of 320 ft, flowing into [[Cayuga Lake]].
  - Upper 3 miles: rubble bottom, alternating pools and riffles
  - Middle ~7 miles: broad valley, meandering course, mud/clay bottom, pools dominant with occasional riffles
  - Lower ~8 miles: steep gradient near Etna to Beebe Lake, large boulders and exposed bedrock
  - The stream is described as more suitable for [[Trout]] through much of its length than for bass.
- **Capture methods**: Angling (146), 10-ft seine (52), 25-ft bag seine (10), electric shocker (758); total n=966.
- **Trophic context**: Extensive bottom fauna surveys by the author and R.M. Roecker indicated no apparent food limitation for the bass population, supporting the plump condition factor despite stunted length.
- **Population structure**: Age composition heavily skewed toward young fish (ages 0 and I comprised ~76% of the 488-specimen age-determined subsample); very few fish reached age IV or V, and only one specimen reached age VII (an outlier also noted in comparable Iowa streams by Tate 1949).
- **Comparative populations**: Contrasted with [[St. Lawrence River]] smallmouth bass (Stone, Pasko & Roecker 1954), which can reach age 14, and with populations in the [[Raquette River]] watershed, [[Upper Hudson River]], [[Delaware River]], and [[Schoharie Creek]] (also noted as stunted).

## Key Findings
- **Annulus formation timing**: Occurred during late May and early June.
- **Growth increments** (average calculated fork length increments per year of life, mm): Year I = 71, Year II = 61, Year III = 34, Year IV = 38, Year V = 20. Maximum annual growth increment occurred in the first year of life.
- **Age structure** (n=488, excluding one 7-year-old outlier): Age 0 = 20.3%, Age I = 55.7%, Age II = 12.1%, Age III = 5.5%, Age IV = 3.5%, Age V = 2.9%.
- **Maximum age**: 5 years was the typical maximum age limit in Fall Creek (with one exceptional 7-year-old individual), compared to 4 years (with one 7-year-old exception) in Iowa streams (Tate 1949).
- **Legal-size attainment**: Only 50% of age V fish and 35.3% of age IV fish reached legal size (10 inches total length), far below rates reported for the [[Raquette River]] watershed (94.8% at age V, 56.1% at age IV), reinforcing the stunted classification.
- **Sex-based growth comparison**: Statistical test (n=213) on Age groups I and II showed no significant difference in growth rate between sexes (t=1.577 for Age I, odds 1.6:1 against chance deviation; t=0.899 for Age II, odds 8:1).
- **Stunting pattern**: Unlike [[Schoharie Creek]] bass, which showed conspicuous stunting concentrated in specific growing seasons (3rd or 4th year), Fall Creek bass showed retardation distributed evenly throughout life, reflected in consistently close annuli spacing rather than a single stunted season.
- **Comparative growth rate**: Fall Creek bass grew similarly to bass from the South Branch Potomac River and Cacapon River, West Virginia (Surber & Seaman 1949), both considered poor-to-mediocre bass streams, but grew more slowly in absolute length-per-year than Iowa stream populations (Tate 1949), though increment patterns were similar, suggesting curvilinear vs. linear regression treatment explains much of the apparent discrepancy.
- **Weight-for-length**: Fall Creek bass weighed relatively less per unit length than Iowa stream bass (Tate 1949), despite comparable or higher K values, an apparent inconsistency attributed to methodological differences in length conversion.
- **Proposed causes of stunting**: Possible explanations include intrinsic small-stream growth constraints, differential mortality/removal of older fish via angling (cf. Greeley 1940 on lower Hudson streams), or downstream emigration of older individuals (cf. Tate 1949)—though no tagging data exist to confirm movement in Fall Creek.
