# Production of Bottom Fauna in the Provo River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #benthic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by [[Arden R. Gaufin]] (Iowa State College Journal of Science, Vol. 33, No. 3) examines the stream-bottom invertebrates of the [[Provo River]], a typical mountain stream of the Intermountain region known for [[Trout Fishing]]. Fieldwork ran from September 1946 to May 1949. The aim was to quantify invertebrates as potential trout food and to relate their productivity to physical, chemical and biological factors, with special attention to winter conditions. The river was graded as an exceptionally rich stream by the [[Hazzard Productivity Standards]]. Productivity rose steadily from the headwaters to [[Utah Lake]], peaking below [[Deer Creek Reservoir]].

## Background & Description

The Provo River rises in the high [[Uinta Mountains]] of northeastern Utah and flows about 72 miles southwest to Utah Lake. The [[Main Fork]] originates in mountain lakes and is joined at Stewart's Ranch by the [[South Fork]]. The Main Fork drains relatively insoluble quartzite with some shale, while the South Fork drains [[Mississippian Limestone]]. Below the junction the river flows through [[Deer Creek Reservoir]] and then down [[Provo Canyon]], receiving [[Deer Creek]], [[Aspen Grove Creek]] and [[Vivian Park Creek]]. These tributaries drain limestone-rich terrain and contribute alkaline water.

Water originates mainly from melting snow and springs. Flow peaks in spring, rising 40 to 150 percent from April to June, and sudden cloudburst floods can occur from July to mid-September. Vegetation changes with elevation, from [[Spruce-Fir Forest]] at high altitude through [[Lodgepole Pine]] and [[Aspen Forest]] to [[Oak Brush]] chaparral, foothill juniper-pinyon, and agricultural valley floors.

Methods combined a reconnaissance survey, nine major stations, and six supplementary tributary stations. Quantitative bottom samples used a [[Surber Sampler]] (384 square-foot samples in total). Qualitative collections used a [[Needham Hand Screen Sampler]]. Physical and chemical variables included velocity, discharge, temperature, dissolved oxygen (Winkler method), pH, free carbon dioxide, alkalinity, and electrolytic conductance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Mountain streams are harsh, unstable environments. Seasonal spates strip populations, and rapid changes in habitat, from torrential runs to placid pools, create isolated "biotic islands." The Provo River's flow ranged from 1.6 cubic feet per second (Jordanelle, September 1947) to 2,290 cubic feet per second (Vivian Park, May 1948). Velocity ranged from 0.33 to 9.6 feet per second. Water temperature ranged from 32.2°F (winter, Stewart's Ranch) to 71°F (Heber-Midway, August 1947), with a mean annual temperature of 51.8°F.

Dissolved oxygen never fell below 6.8 ppm and stayed near saturation. Free carbon dioxide was low and absent at many lower stations. The river was alkaline except during spring runoff in the upper 17 miles, where pH fell to about 6.8 to 6.9. Bound carbon dioxide (carbonate alkalinity) ranged from 4.5 ppm at the Upper Bridge to 222 ppm at Heber-Midway. Carbonate content was about six times higher in the South Fork (144 ppm) than in the Main Fork (25 ppm), reflecting differences in bedrock geology.

Turbidity was low (under 7 ppm) at most times but reached 90 ppm during spring high flows and over 200 ppm after muddy irrigation waste water entered the channel at Heber-Midway. Color was typically below 10 ppm.

Human activities altered the system, including irrigation diversion, reservoir impoundment, overgrazing and dredging. Dredging of the [[Heber-Midway Bridge]] reach in 1947 and 1948 removed marginal vegetation and reduced post-dredge productivity to 18 organisms per square foot.

## Key Findings

- **Overall productivity:** Of 384 square-foot samples, 79,298 organisms were collected, averaging 193 per square foot with a mean volume of 2.69 cc per square foot. The river qualified as "exceptionally rich" under the Hazzard standards.
- **Dominant taxa:** Five insect orders (Trichoptera, Ephemeroptera, Diptera, Plecoptera, Coleoptera) made up 95.2% of organisms and 94.2% of volume.
- **Key species:** The caddis fly *Brachycentrus occidentalis* was the most abundant species (12.3% of all organisms). The stonefly *Pteronarcys californica* made up 22.4% of total volume. Other notable taxa include the mayflies *Baetis* spp. and *Ephemerella grandis*, and the beetle *Heterlimnius quadrimaculatus*.
- **Seasonal pattern:** Numbers were lowest from April to June (106 to 194 per square foot, depending on year) because of spring flooding and stonefly emergence. Numbers and volume peaked from December to March. Late summer populations were also high (423 per square foot in August–September 1947).
- **Winter effects:** The 1948–49 winter, the most severe in 75 years, reduced populations to 146 per square foot in February–March 1949 (versus 619 in January–March 1948). Anchor ice at −20°F wiped out large numbers of organisms at high altitudes.
- **Altitudinal gradient:** Productivity increased from the headwaters downstream. Of 119 samples from the upper 17 miles of the Main Fork, only 3 were exceptionally rich. Of 100 samples from the lower 19 miles below Deer Creek Reservoir, 73 were exceptionally rich.
- **Most productive station:** Deer Creek Park (average 372 organisms per square foot, 6.3 cc) had the most stable conditions. Lemon's Grove averaged 421 per square foot, though 42.1% of these were one species.
- **Least productive stations:** Upper Bridge (72 per square foot, 0.88 cc) and North Fork (poor rating) were the least productive, with soft, slightly acidic water and boulder-rubble substrates.
- **Controlling factors:** Greater flow uniformity, more stable temperature, alkaline pH (7.7 or above) and high carbonate content were the strongest drivers of productivity. Color, turbidity, dissolved oxygen and free carbon dioxide differences were not significant.
- **Management implication:** The study frames these invertebrates as the food base for trout populations. Human disturbance (dredging, diversion, grazing) reduced productivity, though recovery was notable where habitat stabilized.
