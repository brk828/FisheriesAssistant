# Comparative Evaluation of Two Tilapias as Pondfishes in Alabama

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cichlidae #aquaculture #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study by H.S. Swingle (Transactions of the American Fisheries Society) evaluates the [[Java Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*) and [[Nile Tilapia]] (*Tilapia nilotica*) as pondfish species in Alabama, comparing their production efficiency against native and introduced American pondfish species — the [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Speckled Bullhead]]. Conducted from 1956–1959 at Auburn, Alabama, the research measured total production, feed conversion efficiency, growth rates, reproductive dynamics, and suitability as both commercial food fish and sport fish under temperate (non-tropical) pond conditions. Key experimental variables included stocking with brood adults vs. fingerlings, supplemental feeding rates, and predation control via [[Largemouth Bass]] to manage tilapia overpopulation.

The Java tilapia emerged as a highly efficient pondfish, rivaling or exceeding production of established American species, though its prolific reproduction generated large numbers of undersized fish. The Nile tilapia showed comparable production with a naturally higher percentage of harvestable-sized individuals due to lower fecundity, but was less readily caught by anglers.

## Background & Description

- **Java Tilapia (*Tilapia mossambica*)**: A cichlid; mouthbrooding reproductive strategy; adults reach 5+ lbs in tropical climates; in Alabama, brood stock were 3–6 inches at stocking; fingerlings reached 6–8 inches within 3 months when adequately fed under supplemental feeding regimes.
- **Nile Tilapia (*Tilapia nilotica*)**: Similar cichlid body plan; grew to larger individual size than Java tilapia under Alabama conditions but was less prolific.
- **Comparative species**: [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Speckled Bullhead]] (Ictaluridae) and [[Common Carp]] (Cyprinidae) served as production benchmarks; only fish of harvestable size were counted for these species (unlike tilapias, whose totals included large numbers of small juveniles).
- **Thermal tolerance**: Java tilapia ceased feeding below 60°F and died at 48°F (Kelly, 1957); Chimits (1957) cites death point at 46.4°F (8°C). This precludes overwintering outdoors in the southeastern U.S. north of central Florida, effectively preventing establishment as an invasive nuisance species in Alabama.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Fertilized freshwater aquaculture ponds, both with and without supplemental pelleted feed (Auburn No. 1/No. 2 fish feed, 3/8-inch pellets).
- **Trophic ecology**: Java tilapia young feed on crustacea and unicellular algae; older fish shift to filamentous algae and decaying organic matter (Vaas and Hofstede, 1952) — an herbivorous/detritivorous niche that confers high conversion efficiency in fertilized ponds.
- **Reproductive behavior**: Mouthbreeding cichlid; females produce 100–300+ young per hatch, with successive broods every 30–40 days in tropical climates. In Alabama, first spawning occurred late April to early May.
- **Predator-prey interactions**: [[Largemouth Bass]] introduced into tilapia brood ponds reduced tilapia numbers by 50–75%, raising the Ar (harvestable fish) value but often reducing total production due to conflicts with heavy feeding regimes (feeding could kill bass or wasn't efficient early season). Delaying bass stocking until August (after young tilapia grew too large to be eaten) produced the best combination of predation control and harvestable yield.
- **Interspecific competition**: When brood tilapias were introduced into established [[Bluegill]]-bass populations, spawning occurred but nearly all young were lost to bass predation — indicating tilapia recruitment is tightly controlled by existing predator populations, consistent with field observations in Indonesia (Hofstede and Botke, 1950).
- **Density-dependent reproduction**: Evidence of a possible **repressive factor** (per [[Swingle 1957a]]) suppressing reproduction at high stocking densities — analogous to mechanisms described in carp culture and speckled bullhead commercial culture. This effect was more pronounced in Nile tilapia than Java tilapia.
- **Sport fishery potential**: Tilapias support a short-season (August–October) sport fishery in Alabama due to inability to overwinter; Java tilapia were readily caught (mostly via worm bait), while Nile tilapia were markedly less catchable despite standing biomass.

## Key Findings

**Production Rates (Brood Stocking, Table 1)**
- Java tilapia total production ranged from **1,477.1 lbs/acre in 168 days** (no bass) to **4,383.9 lbs/acre in 191 days** (with bass, delayed stocking).
- Without feeding, fertilized ponds yielded only **347.4–1,477.1 lbs/acre**, versus much higher Asiatic yields (270–2,500 lbs/acre reported in Formosa, Java, Malaya).
- Maximum Nile tilapia production: **4,003.7 lbs/acre in 208 days** (from 4,461 lbs feed) — comparable to Java tilapia's 4,198.4 lbs/acre in 196 days.
- Recalculated to a 365-day tropical growing season, Alabama production reached up to **7,809 lbs/acre/year**, still well below Thailand's reported 12,460–16,465 lbs/acre/year (Pongsuwana, 1956), attributable to ~3× higher feeding rates and year-round growing season in Thailand.

**Fingerling Stocking (Table 3)**
- Java tilapia: production increased from **771.1 lbs/acre (4,000 fingerlings/acre, 123 days)** to **2,945.7 lbs/acre (20,000 fingerlings/acre, 111 days)**.
- Percentage of harvestable-sized fish (Ar value) **declined** with increasing stocking density: from **98.7 (2,000/acre)** to **67.7 (20,000/acre)** — direct evidence of density-dependent reproductive suppression.
- Nile tilapia showed **no reproduction** at 16,000/acre stocking and minimal reproduction at 8,000/acre, suggesting stronger susceptibility to the repressive factor than Java tilapia.
- Nile tilapia at 16,000/acre produced **70 lbs more harvestable fish/acre** than Java tilapia at 20,000/acre, with a much higher Ar (91.9 vs. 67.7).

**Comparative Species Efficiency (Table 2)**
- Maximum harvestable-fish production per acre in one growing season: **2,453.1 lbs (Java tilapia, 191 days)**, **2,233.6 lbs (Nile tilapia, 113 days)**, **2,363 lbs ([[Channel Catfish]], 252 days)**.
- Channel catfish, Java tilapia, and Nile tilapia were **approximately equally efficient** in feed conversion (S conversion values ~1.2–3.0) and all **superior** to [[Common Carp]] and [[Speckled Bullhead]] in production and conversion efficiency.

**Predation & Management Strategy**
- Bass predation combined with periodic doubling of feeding rate could substitute for continuous predation control, achieving Ar values of 40–85 in several ponds (F-157, F-1757, S-1258, F-658).
- Natural balanced populations typically show Ar > 33 when predation is adequate — a benchmark from Swingle's earlier balanced-population framework ([[Swingle 1950]]).
- Best combined outcome (high harvestable yield + predation control): bass added in **August** to Pond S-1157, yielding **2,453 lbs/acre harvestable fish**.

**Sport Fishery Performance**
- Java tilapia sport ponds (stocked with 80 brood/acre, April 15–20): catch of **593.6 lbs/acre (1957)** and **590.6 lbs/acre (1958)** — over twice the average catch from comparable [[Bluegill]]-bass ponds.
- Nile tilapia sport pond: only **128 lbs/acre caught** despite standing stock of **1,621 lbs/acre** of harvestable-sized fish — indicating lower angler catchability despite larger individual size.

**Overall Conclusion**
Both tilapia species are highly efficient, fast-growing pondfish suitable for short-season temperate aquaculture, rivaling the best American pondfish (channel catfish) in production and feed conversion. Management of their prolific reproduction — via predation timing, feeding rate manipulation, or exploitation of a density-dependent repressive reproductive factor — is critical to maximizing harvestable yield rather than biomass dominated by undersized juveniles.
