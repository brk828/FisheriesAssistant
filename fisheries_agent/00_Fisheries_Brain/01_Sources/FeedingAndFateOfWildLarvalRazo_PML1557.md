# Feeding and Fate of Wild Larval Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Marsh]] (1988, *Environmental Biology of Fishes* 21(1): 59-67) examines the feeding and survival of wild larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen texanus]]*), an imperiled endemic of the [[Colorado River]] basin, in [[Lake Mohave]] and the adjacent [[Arizona Bay Backwater]]. Larvae in the reservoir apparently disappeared shortly after hatching, and the author tests whether food shortage, emigration, or mortality explains this. Diet and zooplankton availability were similar in the two habitats, and food supply did not account for the loss. Sampling ruled out migration within the reservoir. Mortality, most likely from [[Non-Native Fishes]], is the leading explanation: after a berm breach in April 1985, introduced fishes invaded the backwater and all larvae there were lost by 5 May.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Razorback Sucker]], a large catostomid of the big rivers of the American Southwest, now listed as imperiled. Declines have been attributed to physical habitat modification and to [[Predation]] and competition by introduced fishes.
- **Study question:** Why do wild larvae disappear from Lake Mohave, where recruitment is lacking? Three explanations are weighed: migration to inaccessible habitat, [[Down-Lake Transport]] out of the area, and mortality.
- **Study area:** Lake Mohave lies immediately downstream of [[Davis Dam]] on the Arizona-Nevada border (Figure 1). Larvae were sampled by calm-weather collection along shoreline areas, and the isolated Arizona Bay backwater was a second site.
- **Methods:** Zooplankton in the water column and larval stomach contents were compared using volume-frequency distributions and the linear selection index of [[Strauss]] (1979). Larval diets were analyzed alongside [[Zooplankton]] community composition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Mohave (reservoir)**
- Zooplankton were dominated by [[Rotifera]], [[Cladocera]] (notably [[Bosmina]]), and [[Copepoda]].
- Water column sample: total zooplankton n = 354 potential food items; larval stomachs n = 165 food items.
- [[Bosmina]] water column n = 176; larval stomachs n = 149.

**Arizona Bay Backwater**
- Zooplankton density averaged 377 animals m⁻³, only about 25% of the reservoir value.
- Rotifers made up more than 50% of zooplankton, and copepods were less abundant than in the reservoir.
- Zooplankton species richness was higher than in the reservoir, but the most abundant taxa were the same in both habitats.
- Water column sample: total zooplankton n = 382 potential food items.

**Larval growth and timing**
- Larvae were first captured in the backwater on 7 March, 5 to 8 weeks after adults were stocked. Numbers rose quickly and stayed high into April.
- Mean total length (TL) started near 10 mm, similar to reservoir fish, and exceeded 16 mm by late April. The largest specimen measured 20.3 mm.
- Backwater larvae reached about twice the size of the largest wild reservoir larvae, which were early postlarvae.

**Disturbance event**
- High reservoir level (196.8 m) and storm-driven waves breached the berm separating the backwater from Lake Mohave on 4 April 1985.
- Non-native fishes that invaded within days included [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Bluegill]].

## Key Findings

1. **Diet overlap:** Zooplankton communities and larval diets were similar in Lake Mohave and the backwater. Larvae are opportunistic consumers that track the available prey.
2. **Backwater diet selection:** Larvae positively selected cladocerans, especially [[Bosmina]], and strongly avoided rotifers. Preferred zooplankters were 50-399 × 10⁻⁴ mm³ in size, with strongest positive selection for rotifers of 25-29 × 10⁻⁴ mm³, *Daphnia* of 100-299 and 1,000-4,999 × 10⁻⁴ mm³, and copepods of 100-299 × 10⁻⁴ mm³.
3. **Size and feeding:** No evidence was found that the mean size of ingested food increased as larval TL rose from about 12 to 20 mm.
4. **Food supply not limiting:** Availability of food of suitable type, number, or size does not explain the total apparent mortality of reservoir larvae.
5. **Selection differences:** Taxa and sizes preferred in the backwater were more abundant in the reservoir, yet reservoir larvae showed neutral or negative selection for them. The author found no behavioral, distributional, or habitat differences that explain this.
6. **Migration rejected:** Extensive collections over the 1970s and later, covering all habitats except the deepest profundal zone, caught thousands of other species of similar size but no small razorback sucker. Juveniles reintroduced to Arizona streams were captured readily by the same methods.
7. **Down-lake transport untested:** Razorback populations downstream (Lake Havasu and below) are very small, and only sporadic individuals have been recorded in recent years. If transport removes larvae, mortality must be high. The hypothesis needs testing.
8. **Predation as cause:** Following the 1985 breach, about 40% of [[Green Sunfish]] captured over 24 hours contained an average of four razorback larvae. Larvae could no longer be collected by 5 May, and total mortality in the backwater is attributed to predation.
9. **Supporting evidence:** Predation on razorback larvae has been documented in [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] and Lake Mohave, and ictalurid catfishes are a likely predator in the middle [[Gila River]]. Brooks (unpublished) found predation losses of young suckers directly related to green sunfish density in experimental ponds at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Papoulias (unpublished) found larval survival was independent of pond productivity at the same hatchery.
10. **Methodological barrier:** Prey identification in predator stomachs is limited by mastication and digestion. [[Serological Techniques]] (ELISA) may help but must be used with close-order sampling.
11. **Management implications:** Testing whether reduced predation allows self-sustaining populations would be a key step. [[Ichthyocide Reclamation]] to remove catfishes from selected Arizona streams, followed by stocking, is proposed as a test. Stocking larger juveniles may reestablish adults but will not ensure recruitment if larval predation continues. Recovery is most feasible in backwaters, oxbows, and smaller tributaries where non-native fishes can be repeatedly removed.
