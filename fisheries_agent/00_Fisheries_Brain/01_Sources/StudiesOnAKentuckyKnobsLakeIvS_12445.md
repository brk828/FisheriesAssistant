# Winter Limnological Conditions in Tom Wallace Lake, Kentucky

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #winter-limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1959 limnological study by Krumholz and Cole, published in *Limnology and Oceanography* (Vol. IV, No. 4), examines physical and chemical conditions in [[Tom Wallace Lake]], a small artificial impoundment in the Kentucky Knobs region. The lake was observed through three intermittent, complete ice covers over 49 days (January 5 to February 23, 1958), during an unusually cold winter. The authors combine a [[Heat Budget]] calculation, vertical and longitudinal [[Thermal Stratification]] profiles, and measurements of [[Dissolved Oxygen]], [[Total Alkalinity]], [[Total Iron]], and [[Total Solids]]. The central conclusions are that the water mass beneath the ice was turbulent and non-horizontal in its isotherms, that single-station heat budgets can be misleading, and that freezing, melting, rainfall, and subsurface seepage shaped chemical and thermal patterns. The paper contains no fish or invertebrate population data.

## Background & Description

**Study site.** Tom Wallace Lake lies at 38°05′N, 85°40′W, about 600 ft above mean sea level, in a wooded ravine in the conical hills of the [[Kentucky Knobs]] physiographic province. It is an artificial impoundment with a single concrete spillway at the northeast corner. Two inlets enter at the western end: an intermittent brook and a previously unrecorded "lesser inlet" that is usually dry.

**Morphometry (Table 1, from a new 2.34-ha-area survey based on 320 under-ice soundings):**

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Surface area | 2.34 ha |
| Volume | 90,647 m³ |
| Shoreline length | 880 m |
| Maximum depth | 8.75 m |
| Mean depth | 3.9 m |
| Volume development | 1.88 |
| Shoreline development | 1.62 |

The maximum-to-mean depth ratio is 2.24. The authors compare this with [[Hayes 1957]], who reported an average ratio of 2.35 across 500 lakes. They conclude that the man-made lake shows no unusual morphometric features.

**Mixing classification.** The lake is borderline: it is monomictic in mild winters and dimictic when brief ice covers form. The authors argue that [[Polymictic Lakes]] is the most useful descriptor for the interval between the 1957 and 1958 summer stagnation periods, even though multiple mixing events do not match the [[Hutchinson and Löffler Classification]] definition.

**Prior work.** Earlier studies on the lake (Distler 1952; Cole 1954, 1957; Neff 1955) covered environmental factors, the bass problem, net plankton, and aquatic Nematocera.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate.** January and February 1958 were well below normal in Louisville, about seven miles away. February was the coldest in Louisville since 1905, and the coldest month of any kind since January 1948. Only nine days in January and February had air temperatures at Standiford Field that did not fall to 0°C. The authors note that conditions at the lake were probably more severe, with below-freezing temperatures at the lake on some days when Standiford Field was above freezing.

**Ice cover history.** The lake froze completely in early January, partially opened around January 19, and was ice-free by February 1. It refroze on February 3–4, thawed after heavy rain on February 6, refroze by February 11, and persisted, growing to at least 6.5 in thick, until it became unsafe around February 23–26.

**Water sources.** The brook inlet delivered about 430 m³/day and the lesser inlet about 195 m³/day. The lesser inlet was frozen solid for about seven days. Seepage of groundwater probably contributed substantially, as suggested by rusty, gelatinous ferric hydroxide deposits on shallow margins. The Rosewood Shale, which dominates the drainage basin, contains limonite concretions.

**Sediments.** The soft profundal sediments overlying the original basin are only about 7–8 cm thick.

## Key Findings

1. **Heat budget.** The Birgean heat budget for January 5 to February 23 showed a net loss of 80 g-cal/cm², with distinct warming and cooling phases. Warming periods were January 5–19 (gain of 263 g-cal/cm², 18.5 g-cal/cm²/day) and February 4–16 (gain of 618 g-cal/cm², 51 g-cal/cm²/day). Loss periods were January 19–February 4 (1,016 g-cal/cm²) and February 16–21 (161 g-cal/cm²).

2. **Ice energetics.** Freezing and melting were major energy sinks and sources. Melting of the February 1 ice required 812 g-cal/cm², and melting of the February 27 ice required 1,320 g-cal/cm².

3. **Non-horizontal isotherms and turbulence.** Longitudinal and transverse profiles showed that isotherms were not horizontal. Warm water moved away from the sampling station, so single-station heat budgets gave a false impression of cooling. For example, mean water-column temperature at the station rose from 4.74°C (January 12) to 4.84°C (January 14) while the longitudinal profiles showed general warming.

4. **Heat flow direction.** Heat moved upward from the sediments on every sampling date. Beneath the ice, the upper 1–2 m showed reversing or inverse gradients, and at mid-depths (especially 5 m) most temperature change was attributed to lateral heat transport. Sediment and 8-m temperatures fluctuated at nearly equal rates (0.128°C/day in mud; 0.132°C/day at 8 m), suggesting a thin, rapidly warmed and cooled layer rather than a store of summer heat.

5. **Density anomalies.** On several days the temperature profile could not be reconciled with water density. Bicarbonate-based density corrections (18 × 10⁻⁷ per mg/L, after Hutchinson 1941) explained some cases. Five cases at 8 m and two at 4 m remained unexplained, possibly reflecting temporary instability or underestimated total solids. Westphal balance measurements generally matched the temperature-based densities.

6. **Oxygen decline at depth.** Dissolved oxygen at 8 m fell at about 0.6 mg/L/day from early January, reaching about 63% saturation. Surface and 4-m waters stayed near 90% saturation. Oxygen at 7 m was 11.9 mg/L on February 21, while the 8-m value was 7.8 mg/L, showing no evidence of extensive upward displacement of poorly oxygenated water.

7. **Alkalinity.** Mean total alkalinity was 30.1 mg/L at 0 m (SD 5.9), 33.6 mg/L at 4 m (SD 4.1), and 34.2 mg/L at 8 m (SD 2.3). Surface values dropped by 12.4 mg/L between January 11 and 12 following melting, and reached a low of 14 mg/L on January 14 after rain. Melted ice had only 3.2 mg/L alkalinity. Freezing-out effects raised surface alkalinity above 8-m values on some occasions.

8. **Iron.** Mean total iron (unfiltered) was 0.5616 mg/L at 0 m, 0.6177 mg/L at 4 m, and 0.8620 mg/L at 8 m. On February 21, ferric iron made up only 3.7% of total iron at the surface, 4.2% at 4 m, and 37.5% at 8 m. Much of the surface iron was particulate. Inflow iron was roughly 50 g/day. The spillway outflow averaged 0.4944 mg/L total iron.

9. **Total solids.** Mean total solids were 113.3 mg/L at 0 m, 108.2 mg/L at 4 m, and 117.6 mg/L at 8 m. Excluding an anomalous surface value of 367 mg/L on February 11, the surface mean drops to about 90 mg/L. Spillway solids averaged 78.8 mg/L, and inlet solids averaged 90.8 mg/L (SD 16).

10. **Flushing.** Continuous spillway outflow flushed the lake. Mean alkalinity was lower in February than in January, and the decline continued into April (surface water at 28 mg/L on April 4, against 27 mg/L in the brook inlet).

11. **Conclusion.** Winter thermal and chemical structure in this small, shallow lake was driven by ice formation and melting, solar heating through ice, inlet and seepage density currents, and lateral heat transport. Single-station data can therefore produce misleading heat budgets. The authors conclude that the calculated diffusion coefficients (A values) are not useful, because they vary too widely even over small vertical ranges.
