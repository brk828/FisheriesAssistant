# Utility of Habitat and Biota for Identifying Potential Land Use Effects on Montane Riparian-Stream Systems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review, by [[John N. Rinne]] of the Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station (Arizona State University, Tempe), evaluates whether selected physical stream habitat features ([[Sedimentation]]) and aquatic biota ([[Aquatic Macroinvertebrates]] and [[Trout]] populations) can reliably detect the effects of forest land uses, especially [[Livestock Grazing]], on montane riparian-stream systems. Prior research on these effects has produced contradictory results, partly because studies were incomplete, poorly designed, or short-term. The paper integrates the published literature with recent comparative data from two headwater creeks in central Arizona, Pine Creek (ungrazed reference) and Horton Creek (intensively grazed). The author concludes that these indicators are useful, but only when study design accounts for natural variability, temporal cycles, and interacting environmental factors.

The document is a journal manuscript with reviewer comments and a reviewer recommendation form appended. The reviewer's written notes are largely illegible in the scan, and the recommendation boxes cannot be reliably determined from the text.

## Background & Description

- **Policy context:** [[National Forest Management Act]] (1976) required accountability for multiple-use resources, including wildlife, fisheries, timber, recreation and grazing. The [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (1969) and the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) increased interest in how forest management affects fish and wildlife.
- **Problem:** Management plans often set conflicting goals (for example, increasing recreation and fish populations while increasing timber harvest and grazing), and literature on riparian effects, especially grazing, is inconsistent.
- **Scope:** The paper reviews physical habitat (sediment) and biota (macroinvertebrates and fish) as indicators, then presents new Southwestern montane stream data.
- **Study streams:** Pine Creek (ungrazed, pristine-like watershed) and Horton Creek (multiple-use, intensively grazed). Both were studied for sediment, macroinvertebrates, and trout (Rinne and Medina, 1989 and related work).
- **Indices:** The [[Biotic Condition Index]] (BCI, Winget and Mangum, 1979) integrates invertebrate, physical, and chemical parameters. The [[Hilsenhoff Biotic Index]] (HBI) is a related tolerance-based index. The U.S. Forest Service adopted the BCI, and the Aquatic Ecosystem Analysis Laboratory in Provo, Utah processed samples (111 samples from 20 forests in 1984; 800 samples per year for the Bureau of Land Management, as stated).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sedimentation.** Fine sediment is delivered to streams by erosion driven by geology, topography, vegetation, weather, and land use. Human disturbance, and sometimes natural events such as wildfire, can increase fine sediment beyond what streams can flush. Excess fine sediment can reduce substrate interstices, harming fish reproduction and food supply. Grazing causes streambank trampling and vegetation removal, increasing sediment input.

**Light and temperature.** Tree canopy removal increases solar input and can raise productivity and invertebrate biomass. Cooler, shaded streams may have lower autochthonous production. Solar radiation can override other factors, such as sediment and gradient, complicating interpretation.

**Macroinvertebrates.** Short life cycles and differing tolerance to pollution make invertebrates sensitive, rapid indicators of both degradation and recovery. Tolerant taxa generally increase and intolerant taxa decrease under impact. The caddisfly *Culoptila* strongly influenced BCI values because of its high density and its preference for slightly warmer water.

**Fish.** Trout, as top predators in many stream food webs, are considered valuable indicators. However, fish responses can be confounded by stocking, angling removal, and species-specific responses (rainbow trout in Pine Creek, brown trout in Horton Creek).

## Key Findings

**Sediment (Pine vs. Horton Creek)**
- Sediment in Horton Creek was significantly greater than in Pine Creek in both winter and summer, with no significant seasonal differences within either stream.
- Summer means were 1.35 versus 1.52 grams per box, and winter means were 1.17 versus 1.14 grams per box (as reported in the scanned text).
- The author attributes the difference to intensive livestock grazing in the Horton Creek watershed and riparian area.

**Macroinvertebrates**
- Densities did not differ significantly between streams (ANOVA, p < 0.05 reported as not significant), but Horton Creek had much higher and more variable densities.
- Density in Pine Creek was 10,467 per square meter, with one outlier of 12,454 per square meter. Excluding this outlier, the range was 4,167 to 16,700 per square meter (per the scanned values).
- Horton Creek BCI values were highly significantly greater than Pine Creek BCI values, and BCI variation was greater in Horton Creek (range 78 versus 21 in Pine Creek).
- Grazed reaches in the earlier Rio de las Vacas study showed higher invertebrate densities, but lower BCI because of more tolerant taxa.

**Trout**
- Trout density in Pine Creek (mean 71 fish per 50 meters) was significantly greater than in Horton Creek (mean 20 fish per 50 meters) (p < 0.001).
- Trout biomass was significantly greater in ungrazed Pine Creek (mean 1.72 kg per 50 meters) than in grazed Horton Creek (1.91 kg per 50 meters as written in the source; the values are internally inconsistent, so verify against the original figures).
- Year-to-year differences in trout numbers and biomass were not significant in either stream, and variances were homogeneous.

**Literature synthesis**
- Most early logging-effect literature was descriptive and unquantitative. Only 7 of 117 articles on logging effects on fish were quantitative (as reported).
- Of 20 grazing studies reviewed by [[Platts]] (1991), 9 were rated "fair" and 10 "poor," with only one rated "good."
- A Canadian study (Brant et al., 1996) found that only 2 of 10 streams showed decreased salmonid biomass after logging.

**Research recommendations**
- Establish baseline data on reference and impacted watersheds over at least 5 to 10 years before manipulation.
- Use multiple indicators, and measure species-specific responses rather than total fish numbers alone.
- Account for natural variability, temporal cycles, stocking, angling, and interacting factors such as solar radiation.
- Evaluate management at the watershed level, over extended periods, for long-term conservation of riparian forest ecosystems.

**Study status:** The Arizona work was preliminary and part of an ongoing relocated research program on six contiguous headwater streams on the Tonto National Forest.
