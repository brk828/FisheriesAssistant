# Chlorine: Its Toxicity to Fish and Detoxification of Antimycin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ecotoxicology #ictaluridae #salmonidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by [[Leif L. Marking]] and [[Terry D. Bills]] at the [[Fish Control Laboratory]] in La Crosse, Wisconsin, measured the acute toxicity of [[Chlorine]] to 12 fish species and tested whether chlorine can detoxify the piscicide [[Antimycin]]. Static [[Static Toxicity Test|toxicity tests]] gave 96-h [[LC50]] values from 0.156 mg/l for [[Channel Catfish]] to 1.41 mg/l for [[Black Bullhead]]. Water pH, temperature, and hardness had little influence on chlorine toxicity. Chlorine at 0.25 and 0.5 mg/l detoxified antimycin at pH 6.5, 7.5, and 8.5, with estimated [[Half-Life]] values of 1.1 to 1.5 h. The authors state that the work was motivated by the need to protect nontarget fish when treated water may enter municipal supplies.

## Background & Description

Chlorine is widely used to disinfect municipal water and effluents and is occasionally used to sterilize [[Fish Hatchery]] water supplies and culture ponds. Its residual presence therefore poses a risk to nontarget aquatic organisms, including fish, invertebrates, and plants. [[Brungs (1973)]] recommended that total residual chlorine not exceed 0.2 mg/l for more than 2 h per day for the more resistant fish species.

The authors note that toxicity data in the literature are hard to compare because studies often omit water chemistry, temperature, chlorine demand, solubility, complex formation, and exposure time. [[Merkens (1958)]] argued that toxicity depends on the chlorine remaining in solution, not the amount added, so test conditions should be fully reported and standardized.

Because chlorine is a strong oxidizing agent, it can also neutralize oxidizable toxicants. Earlier work by [[Dawson and Marking (1974)]] found that 0.5 mg/l of chlorine in soft water at pH 7.5 reduced 10 µg/l of antimycin to below 0.2 µg/l within 2 h, based on [[Green Sunfish]] bioassays. Antimycin is already nonpersistent in the environment, but faster detoxification is considered necessary where treated water may reach municipal supplies.

**Methods (summary):**
- **Chemicals:** Technical-grade antimycin from Ayerst Laboratories (Rouses Point, New York), dissolved in acetone and then diluted in water. Chlorine was prepared from calcium hypochlorite (commercial HTH, 70% available chlorine), with test concentrations expressed as active chlorine.
- **Test water:** Reconstituted from deionized water to total hardness (as mg/l CaCO3) of 12 (very soft), 44 (soft), 160 (hard), and 300 (very hard). pH was buffered to 6.5, 7.5, 8.5, or 9.5. Test pH was checked daily.
- **Fish:** Obtained from federal hatcheries; 10 fish of 2 to 5 cm total length per concentration; loading did not exceed 1 g per liter.
- **Endpoints and analysis:** Mortality was recorded at 1, 3, and 6 h on day 1 and at least daily afterward. LC50 values and 95% confidence intervals were calculated by [[Litchfield and Wilcoxon Method]] (1949). Chi-square tests indicated acceptable data. Half-lives came from [[Deactivation Index]] curves on semilogarithmic plots.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Chlorine toxicity was tested across a range of fish groups. The three coldwater species ([[Coho Salmon]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Lake Trout]]) were generally more sensitive than the warmwater species, the exception being channel catfish. Temperature and chemistry affected the outcome in different ways:

- **Temperature:** After 1 h, rainbow trout were more sensitive at 12 and 17 °C than at 7 °C. This reversed by 24 h, and at 96 h the coldest water was most toxic, which the authors attribute to chlorine persisting longer at low temperatures.
- **Hardness:** At a constant pH of 8.0, water hardness did not change chlorine toxicity to rainbow trout.
- **pH:** Chlorine toxicity was influenced little by pH, though there was a general trend toward lower toxicity at higher pH.
- **Chlorine dissipation:** In a 96-h test at 12 °C, chlorine concentrations fell more quickly in tanks holding fish (0.75 g/l green sunfish) than in tanks without fish. Little or no chlorine remained after 24 to 72 h in fish tanks.

## Key Findings

1. **Species-specific chlorine toxicity:** 96-h LC50 values in soft water at 12 °C ranged from 0.156 mg/l (channel catfish) to 1.41 mg/l (black bullhead) across 12 species. Other notable values include rainbow trout at 0.172 mg/l, lake trout at 0.200 mg/l, and bluegill at 0.555 mg/l.
2. **Acute timing:** At 7 or 8 mg/l of chlorine, mortality occurred within 1 h in all species except goldfish, carp, fathead minnows, and black bullheads. LC50 values changed little after 24 h.
3. **Limited environmental modifiers:** Water pH, temperature, and hardness had minor effects on chlorine toxicity, with the temperature effect reversing over time (see above).
4. **Antimycin toxicity depends on pH:** The 96-h LC50 for antimycin alone to green sunfish rose from 0.107 µg/l at pH 6.5 to 16.2 µg/l at pH 9.5. The drop was sharpest between pH 8.5 and 9.5.
5. **Chlorine detoxifies antimycin:** At pH 6.5, 7.5, and 8.5, the toxicity of antimycin fell significantly as soon as chlorine was added. At pH 7.5, the 96-h LC50 went from 0.164 µg/l (antimycin alone) to 6.90 µg/l (with 0.5 mg/l chlorine, zero interaction time).
6. **Half-lives:** Estimated half-lives of the antimycin–chlorine combination were 1.1 h at pH 6.5, 1.3 h at pH 7.5 (from the Figure 1 curve, 0.5 mg/l chlorine, 12 °C), and 1.5 h at pH 8.5 and 9.5. The text reports no 1.5 h value at pH 9.5 for the detoxification curve, but the Conclusions list 1.5 h at pH 9.5.
7. **pH 9.5 anomaly:** At pH 9.5, adding 0.5 mg/l chlorine increased toxicity (96-h LC50 fell from 16.2 to 4.45 µg/l). The authors attribute this to an additive effect rather than detoxification, and they report that detoxification occurred only with longer interaction time.
8. **Comparison with other detoxifiers:** Antimycin decays without added chemicals (half-lives of 310 h at pH 6.5 to 1.5 h at pH 10.0, per [[Marking and Dawson (1972)]]). Chlorine accelerates this, but more slowly than [[Potassium Permanganate]] (Marking and Bills 1975).
9. **Kinetics:** Decay without detoxifiers was first-order. With chlorine, the curve was nonlinear because chlorine is lost during interaction through reduction, volatilization, and adsorption. The authors note that antimycin is generally nontoxic after one or two initial half-lives.
10. **Practical conclusion:** Chlorine "destroys the biological activity of antimycin within a suitably short time" at the tested pH range, but is less rapid than permanganate.
