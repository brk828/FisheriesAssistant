# Channel Catfish (Ictalurus punctatus) in Inland Fisheries Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This chapter (no. 59 of a 1966 California Department of Fish and Game inland fisheries management volume, edited by Alex Calhoun) reviews the biology, management and role of the [[Channel Catfish]] in California. It covers identification, habitat, food, growth, reproduction, disease, stocking, harvest and mortality, drawing heavily on work from Oklahoma, Iowa, Missouri and Kansas. The species was first introduced to California in 1874. Self-sustaining fisheries have since developed in the [[Lower Colorado River]] and the [[Sutter Bypass]] area of the [[Sacramento River]]. Stocked fish in southern California reservoirs mainly provide a trophy fishery. A short start of the next chapter (60, [[Brown Bullhead]]) is also present at the end of the text.

## Background & Description

- Distinguished from other California catfish, including the [[White Catfish]] (*Ameiurus catus*), by a deeply forked tail, a smaller subconical anal fin (24–29 rays) and a more deeply forked tail than the white catfish. Smaller individuals often have black spots.
- Native range: southern prairie provinces of Canada south through the Great Lakes and Mississippi Valley, including all Gulf states and Mexico, but not the Atlantic coast ([[Trautman]], 1957). It is widely introduced and is important in sport and commercial fisheries in the Midwest, South and Southwest, including flooded fallow rice fields in Arkansas, Louisiana and Texas.
- California introductions: 1874 at an unknown locality; Sacramento River region 1891 and again between 1925 and 1930, with the first authentic record in 1942. Fish have been taken in the Colorado River since about 1920. Recent introductions have occurred in many southern California reservoirs.
- Sexing: males have a broader head, swollen above and behind the eyes at spawning, and a genital papilla extending posteriorly. Females have an inflamed, swollen vent at spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat**
- Native habitat is moderate to swiftly flowing streams. The species is also abundant in sluggish streams, lakes and large reservoirs. It prefers sand, gravel or rubble bottoms and seldom lives in dense vegetation.
- It prefers warm water and does not grow well below 70° F. It tolerates muddy water; the fatal turbidity in aerated aquaria was 85,000 ppm.
- Young fish live in shallow riffles near sand bars. Yearlings and subadults overwinter under boulders. Adults rest by day in deep holes, under logs or in other shelter, and move to shallows at night.
- Lethal dissolved oxygen levels for fish exposed to rapidly decreasing oxygen were 0.95, 1.03 and 1.08 ppm at 25, 30 and 35° C. (the 35° C value is inferred from the garbled scan); gradually acclimatized fish tolerated lower levels.

**Movement**
- Tagged fish in the Colorado River moved downstream in the fall. In the Sacramento Valley movement is restricted to the lower Sacramento and adjacent waters, with a tendency to move into the [[Sutter Bypass]]. Upstream movement may be limited by cold water releases from [[Shasta Dam]], and downstream movement by seasonal salinity increases in the lower Delta.
- Activity peaks just before sunrise and sunset.

**Food habits**
- The species is omnivorous, feeding by sight and taste, mostly near the bottom, and heavily at night, especially from dusk to shortly after midnight.
- In the [[Des Moines River]], Iowa, fish under 4 inches ate mainly Diptera. Fish 4–12 inches ate insects, small fish and plant seeds. Fish over 12 inches ate largely small fish, large insects and elm seeds. Mature fish stopped feeding during spawning.
- Large fish (average 5.0 lb) in [[Santee-Cooper Reservoir]], South Carolina, ate mainly fish. Fish were present in 75.7 percent of stomachs with food.
- *Hexagenia* mayflies made up 68 percent of stomach contents in fish from the Mississippi River near Keokuk, Iowa.
- In California, the diet in the Colorado River and Imperial Valley canal included chironomids, Odonata, Trichoptera, plants, terrestrial insects, filamentous algae and crayfish. Threadfin shad were heavily eaten in San Vicente Reservoir in June.

**Disease**
- Outbreaks of *Ichthyophthirius*, gill flukes, external protozoan parasites, and bacterial and columnaris disease occur under hatchery conditions.

## Key Findings

**Growth**
- Reported lengths at ages 1, 3 and 6 range from 1.8 to 5.4 inches, 6.1 to 14.4 (as scanned) inches, and 10.4 to 21.9 inches, respectively.
- In Oklahoma and Kansas the species seldom lives beyond 7 years.
- Stocked 3–4 inch fingerlings in southern California commonly reach 10–11 inches, sometimes 15 inches, in one year. Fish in the Sutter Bypass grew extremely rapidly: some reached 18–19 inches at 4 years. A 13-inch tagged fish was 21 inches when recaptured about 2 years later.
- Oklahoma growth factors: reservoir age (faster growth in reservoirs under 4 years old), turbidity (slower and more stunting in turbid water), and the presence of reproduction (faster growth in small lakes and ponds without reproduction). Intraspecific competition probably causes stunting more than interspecific competition. In New York farm ponds, growth was inversely correlated with density.
- Fingerling growth in Oklahoma hatchery ponds was best in clear water (under 25 ppm turbidity).
- Typical weights: about 1/4 lb at 10 inches, 1 lb at 15 inches and 4 lb at 22 inches. Length-weight relation (W in pounds, L in inches): log W = -5.3846 + 3.4069 log L (Oklahoma); -4.2738 + 3.66 log L (Mississippi River, Iowa); -1.6561 + 3.1334 log L (Des Moines River).

**Reproduction**
- Most Louisiana males and females mature at 10.5 and 12.0 inches, respectively. In Iowa, females mature at 13–16 inches, and males at a somewhat smaller size.
- Spawning occurs at 70–85° F (about 80° optimum), in secluded, semi-darkened sites such as hollow logs and under rocks. In Missouri it runs from late May to mid-July, peaking in early and late June. Females spawn once a year, males perhaps several times.
- The male selects and cleans the nest, guards and aerates the eggs by fanning with the pelvic fins. Eggs do not develop below about 69° F and hatch in about 5–10 days depending on temperature. Fry remain on the bottom before rising to feed.
- Young survive best in turbid water (Secchi disk readings under 20 inches), probably because predation is reduced. Dense weeds harbor predaceous insects.

**Management**
- Management aims to maintain populations through stocking, spawning devices, controlling competitors, encouraging harvest and sound angling regulations.
- Ponds are stocked with fingerlings at about 50 per acre together with [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]. With supplemental feeding, up to 500 per acre is possible. Clear ponds need artificial spawning devices (two 18-inch nail kegs joined together). Survival of young in clear ponds with bass and bluegill is often low due to predation.
- Many Oklahoma reservoirs have stunted turbid-water populations. Partial rotenone treatments have been used, though effectiveness has not been evaluated. Oklahoma has no bag or size limits.
- Catchable hatchery fish are stocked in several states. Returns of tagged catchable fish in New Mexico ranged from about 17 percent upward.

**Harvest and angling**
- Angler success rates reached 0.24 fish per hour in one study. Catfish contribute little to the total catch in most reservoirs. In southern California they provide an interesting trophy element, since catfish are almost always found with other gamefish.
- Annual harvests in 49-acre Peña Blanca Lake, Arizona, were 12, 20 and 1.60 (as scanned) pounds per surface acre in 1959–61. In San Vicente Reservoir the catfish yield was 1.17 lb per surface acre against a total fish yield of 26.3 lb per acre.
- Mortality: 20 percent of 308 tagged fish in the Colorado River were returned within 1 year, so the annual exploitation rate probably exceeds 30 percent. In the Sacramento–Feather River area, of 791 tagged fish, estimates were annual survival 0.44, expectation of death from fishing 0.30 and from natural causes 0.25 (as scanned).

**Role in California**
- About 1953, catfish (mostly channel catfish) were harvested from the Colorado River, and the Sutter Bypass fishery supports up to 1,000 (as scanned) anglers per day at peak.
- Fingerlings rescued from the Coachella Valley Irrigation District reservoir in 1953–55 provided the first stock for general planting. Annually, 15,000 to 20,000 (as scanned) were released in southern California reservoirs, including [[Puddingstone Reservoir]], [[Big Bear Lake]], [[San Vicente Reservoir]] and Piru Reservoir. Puddingstone and Piru produced several 10- to 28-pound specimens, and some reproduction is known to have occurred there.
- Catches from Folsom Lake (1962) and Pine Flat Lake (1963) were negligible. Hatchery production of fingerlings began in 1958 at Central Valleys Hatchery. Production in 1963 was 376,448 fingerlings.
- The success of the southern California stocking program is not well documented, and little is known of the contribution to warmwater fisheries elsewhere in the state. A proposed warmwater hatchery in Los Angeles County would initially produce about 100,000 ten-inch fish.
