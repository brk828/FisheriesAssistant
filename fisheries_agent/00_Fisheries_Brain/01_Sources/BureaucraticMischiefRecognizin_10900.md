# Bureaucratic Mischief: Recognizing Endangered Species and Subspecies

#conservation-policy #taxonomy #mammalogy #ornithology #felidae #canidae

## Executive Summary

This 1991 *Science* perspective by [[Stephen J. O'Brien]] and [[Ernst Mayr]] argues that the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) is being misapplied because of inadequate taxonomy and a rigid [[Hybrid Policy]] that treats hybrids of listed taxa as unprotectable. The authors examine four endangered groups: the [[Florida Panther]], the [[Gray Wolf]], the [[Red Wolf]], and the [[Dusky Seaside Sparrow]]. They propose that the [[Biological Species Concept]] (BSC) be used to define species and subspecies, and they recommend that the Hybrid Policy be kept for species but dropped for subspecies and threatened populations.

## Background & Description

The Endangered Species Act, as amended, protects three categories of biological taxa: species, subspecies, and populations. The authors state that unclear operational definitions, poor taxonomy, and periodic [[Hybridization]] between species and subspecies have produced confusion and misinterpretation by government officials. Listing has also encouraged molecular genetic and field studies, and some of their results contradicted morphology-based taxonomy. Because traditional designations still determine management and eligibility for protection, the authors call this a significant problem. Protection also brings financial resources for recovery.

The paper gives four case studies:

- **Florida panther** (*Felis concolor coryi*): Fewer than 50 individuals survive in southern Florida, with physiological and reproductive impairments likely caused by [[Inbreeding Depression]]. [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) and allozyme analysis found two genetic stocks. Seven animals from a captive stock, later shown to be a mixture of *F. c. coryi* and South American founders, were released into the Everglades between 1957 and 1967.
- **Gray wolf** (*Canis lupus*): Severe demographic decline followed agricultural habitat loss. mtDNA surveys found coyote mtDNA in wolves, but not the reverse, in a region from northern Minnesota to southern Quebec. Farm Bureaus of Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho petitioned to remove the species from endangered and threatened lists.
- **Red wolf** (*Canis rufus*): Its taxonomic status has long been disputed, as either a species or a subspecies of gray wolf. Molecular and morphological data suggest it may be a hybrid of gray wolf subspecies and [[Coyote]]s.
- **Dusky seaside sparrow** (*Ammodramus maritimus nigrescens*): A melanistic coastal subspecies from eastern Florida. Five birds were taken into captivity in 1980 and crossed with [[Scott's Seaside Sparrow]] (*A. m. peninsulae*). A 1981 Solicitor's Office opinion stated that hybrid production would not serve the Act. The subspecies became extinct in 1987.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's main ecological argument is that [[Reproductive Isolation]] protects species genotypes. Under the BSC, [[Species]] are "groups of actually or potentially interbreeding populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups." Natural hybrid zones between species do not destroy species integrity. Subspecies, by contrast, are geographically defined aggregates that can interbreed when in contact, so hybridization between them produces gene flow and genetic mixing.

Mayr (1940) is cited for the observation that most species occupy distinct ecological niches, and that this ecological distinctiveness is central to evolution. The authors add that subspecies may carry ecologically relevant adaptations and can evolve into new species over time, which the authors cite as grounds for protection.

The authors note that the Florida panther example shows genetic mixing may benefit an inbred population, and that the Hybrid Policy blocks such benefits. They also note that the red wolf's surviving captive animals may be the only available descendants of a historic gray wolf subspecies.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomy drives enforcement:** Traditional taxonomic designations determine protection eligibility, and molecular data can contradict them. The authors argue that taxonomy is therefore central to Endangered Species Act enforcement.
- **Hybrid Policy is contested:** Three Solicitor's Office opinions from the U.S. Department of the Interior ruled that hybrids between listed taxa cannot be protected. The authors argue this policy is counterproductive for subspecies.
- **Recommended policy split:**
  - Keep the Hybrid Policy for interspecies hybrids, to discourage capricious interbreeding in captivity and the introduction of species into habitats of closely related species.
  - Drop the Hybrid Policy for subspecies and threatened populations, because subspecies interbreed naturally and retain the potential to become new species.
- **Proposed subspecies criteria:** Subspecies share a unique geographic range or habitat, a set of phylogenetically concordant phenotypic characters, and a unique natural history. Under the BSC, they are allopatric and show recognizable phylogenetic partitioning.
- **Four fates of an allopatric subspecies:** It may go extinct, exchange genes and become a new subspecies, change through drift or selection into one or more new subspecies, or become a new species if effectively isolated.
- **Case-by-case outcomes under BSC principles:**
  1. *Florida panther*: Continued protection, since it clearly qualifies as a subspecies. The population may benefit from new genes.
  2. *Gray wolf*: The coyote hybrid zone is narrow and recent, and does not affect the genetic integrity of the species elsewhere, so protection should remain.
  3. *Red wolf*: Its status is difficult, since it is extinct in the wild. Captive survivors are argued to be the only available descendants of a historic subspecies, which supports protection.
  4. *Dusky seaside sparrow*: Molecular data supported revising the seaside sparrow complex into Gulf Coast and Atlantic Coast subspecies. Protection could be considered if either group becomes rare.
- **Hybrids as a last resort:** When only genetic information from threatened species can be recovered through hybrids, a case-by-case assessment of benefits versus disadvantages is needed.
- **Conclusion:** Legal opinions should distinguish species from subspecies. Biological species do not form hybrids that disintegrate population genetic organization, but subspecies may.
