# Monitoring in Recovery Plans for Threatened and Endangered Species Under the U.S. Endangered Species Act

#conservation-biology #endangered-species #recovery-planning #monitoring

## Executive Summary

This paper evaluates how [[Monitoring]] is incorporated into [[Recovery Plan]]s for 181 species listed as threatened or endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of the United States. The authors assessed both the monitoring tasks proposed in each plan and whether those tasks were actually implemented. Monitoring of species population trends was the most commonly proposed and implemented activity, while monitoring of [[Habitat]] and exotic species, the threats most frequently identified, was comparatively neglected. The authors caution against an overemphasis on [[Focal Species]] monitoring and recommend that species-specific attributes guide monitoring design.

## Background & Description

The ESA of 1973 requires that a recovery plan be prepared and implemented for each listed species, with the goal of increasing populations to the point of delisting. The authors argue that monitoring is essential for four reasons: it establishes current population status (many plans lack population estimates or rely on guesses), it supplies missing biological data, it allows managers to learn how species respond to uncertain management actions, and it is required to satisfy the ESA's demand for measurable delisting criteria. Because listed species are by definition in danger of extinction, careful monitoring is also warranted on its own terms.

The review was a questionnaire-based analysis of recovery plans for 181 federally listed species, supported by [[National Center for Ecological Analysis and Synthesis]], the [[Society for Conservation Biology]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. The questionnaire contained 24 questions on monitoring and 43 (text also states 48) on recovery criteria. Monitoring was split into two classes: biological and ecological factors relevant to decline, and criteria used to measure recovery success. The first class was subdivided into focal species, associated species (predators, competitors, prey, exotics), and habitat monitoring. Focal species monitoring had four subcategories: presence/absence, population size and trend, demographic information, and other information.

Analyses used chi-square and Fisher's exact tests, phi coefficients for association, and Cochran-Armitage trend tests for ordinal variables. Task proposal and implementation were dichotomized, and a category counted as implemented if at least one of its actions was underway or completed. The authors did not correct for experiment-wise error and reanalyzed the data using only the 122 single-species plans to check for bias from multi-species plans (59 species were addressed in 14 plans).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Threats to species should drive monitoring design. Recovery plans identified habitat modification (destruction, degradation, or fragmentation) as a principal threat for 167 of 181 species (92.3%), and exotic species as a principal threat for 83 species (45.9%). The authors note that threats from habitat loss and invasive species are widely documented among endangered species, so their poor monitoring is a notable gap. Problems with exotics and habitat loss have immediate consequences, and a bias toward focal species monitoring leaves them without attention or resources.

The paper also considers the role of taxonomy, knowledge of species biology, range size, and [[Recovery Priority]] (USFWS scores ranging from 1 to 18). Taxonomic bias appears to reflect a broader, process-wide bias toward animals and vertebrates in the listing and recovery process. The authors suggest that multi-species and ecosystem plans may be effective only if their monitoring addresses the specific situations of each species.

## Key Findings

- **Monitoring proposals and implementation:** At least one focal species monitoring task was proposed for 97.2% of species (the text reports this as 97.2% in one place and 97.9% in another; the source gives 9.5% in the scanned text and is inconsistent). Habitat monitoring was proposed for 64.6% and associated species monitoring for 49.7%. Roughly 37.1% of species had tasks in all three categories, and only 18.8% involved focal species monitoring alone.
- **Focal species subcategories:** Population-level monitoring was proposed for most species, demographic monitoring for 66.9%, and presence/absence monitoring for 56.7%. Presence/absence alone was monitored for only 2.8% of species.
- **Implementation:** Focal species, habitat, and associated species monitoring were implemented for 81.5%, 76.1%, and 65.6% of species respectively. Proposed tasks were typically implemented (>73%) across focal species subcategories, and proposal and implementation were strongly associated.
- **Threat monitoring:** Habitat monitoring was proposed for only 65.9% of species threatened by habitat modification and implemented for 52.1%. Exotic-species monitoring was proposed for 48.2% of species threatened by exotics and implemented for 26.5%.
- **Biology knowledge and priority:** Proposals and implementation were largely independent of how well a species' biology was understood, although demographic monitoring was slightly more likely for better-understood species in the single-species subset. Recovery priority had negligible influence.
- **Taxonomy:** Plants and animals differed little. Associated species monitoring varied among animal taxa (χ² = 9.525, P = 0.023): 66.7% of mammal species versus 19.1% of invertebrate species had such tasks proposed. Demographic monitoring was most frequent for birds (87.5%) and least for invertebrates (38.1%).
- **Range size:** Monitoring showed no pattern relative to range size.
- **Recovery criteria:** Criteria were most often specified for population (82.3% of species), followed by habitat (45.3%), demographic viability (24.9%), and securement (35.9%). Monitoring of criteria was proposed more often when criteria were defined, and proposed criteria-related tasks were uniformly 60 to 75% likely to be implemented. Habitat criteria were defined for less than half of the species.
- **Overall conclusions:** The authors call for monitoring that is tailored to each species, with basic knowledge acquisition prioritized for poorly understood species, population monitoring included in all plans, threat monitoring not overlooked, and clearly defined recovery criteria. They caution that an emphasis on focal species monitoring should not reduce monitoring of other factors.
