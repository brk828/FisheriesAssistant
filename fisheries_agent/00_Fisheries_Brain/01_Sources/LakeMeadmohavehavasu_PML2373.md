# Lake Mead, Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu: Sportfisheries of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sport-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This popular-science article by Bill Sizer, part of the *Wildlife Views* series, describes the sportfisheries of three [[Colorado River]] reservoirs: [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. The Arizona Game and Fish Department's Colorado Region contains more water than the rest of the state combined, and the article argues that its fisheries are among the most important in the Southwest. Each lake is described by its fish community, its angler success and the management challenges it presents, based largely on [[Creel Census]] data from 1961. Bass, [[Black Crappie]] and [[Channel Catfish]] dominate the harvest on Mead, while trout and bass share the catch on Mohave and bass dominate on Havasu. The article also reports on the introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]], proposed [[Striped Bass]] stocking and [[Kokanee Salmon]] planting.

## Background & Description

The author frames the region's importance around water area and angler demand. The Colorado Region, as defined by the Game and Fish Department, includes the three lakes and the river sections between them. Because the reservoirs are large and shared among states, management is more restricted than on smaller waters. Eradication of unwanted fish is generally impractical, so most past efforts have focused on studying existing conditions with an eye to improving the fisheries.

A major management event described was the introduction of threadfin shad several years earlier. According to the article, the shad has materially increased forage and productivity in the lakes, and the benefits are said to be supported by earlier reports and articles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Mead** is the largest and reportedly most productive of the three reservoirs. Formed by [[Hoover Dam]] (formerly "Boulder Dam"), it extends about 115 miles into the lower [[Grand Canyon]] and has a shoreline of roughly 550 miles. Fisheries biologists generally agree that its fish populations follow three natural areas:

- **[[Las Vegas Wash]]**: nearest the dam; generally produces lower angler success, similar to that on Mohave, which the author attributes either to lower productivity or to angler effort. Heavy recreational use (picnicking, boating, swimming) means fishing there is often incidental.
- **[[Overton Arm]]**: lies entirely in Nevada.
- **[[Temple Bar]]**: the most popular access point for Arizona anglers.

Some intermixing occurs at the edges. The author links the three-population structure to Mead's relative lack of strong fishing cycles, since one or two good seasons are typically followed by a slower period, while the angler can usually find fish by changing location.

**Lake Mohave** is the second reservoir in the chain. It is mainly a bass lake but supports a mix of species. Water levels are managed by [[Davis Dam]], and the lake's fishery is affected by [[Hatchery-Reared Trout]] stocking below the dam, where the article notes that natural trout spawning is unreliable because of fluctuating water levels. Trout dominate the catch near the northern end, while bass take the lead toward the south end and in warmer months. Anglers averaged a catch rate that was slightly lower than Mead's.

**Lake Havasu**, formed by [[Parker Dam]], is described as a warm-water lake that receives a steady supply of water from the Bill Williams River, which contributes nutrients. Its water level is kept relatively constant for the [[Los Angeles Aqueduct]] supply. The lake is increasingly popular with water skiers, and parts of it are zoned to allow this activity, which the author suggests may reduce fishing effort and lower catch rates per angler-hour.

## Key Findings

### Lake Mead

- Creel census data from three major landings in 1961 showed an average catch of fish per hour (the exact rate is garbled in the source scan), with roughly 70% of anglers successful.
- Bass made up nearly 70% of the catch, making Mead primarily a bass lake.
- Channel catfish were the second most important fish, most often sought at [[Pierce Ferry]].
- Black crappie accounted for about 8½% of the 1961 catch. Crappie are mainly a seasonal fishery, peaking from late February through April, when they form a large share of the catch.
- Crappies school, so catches are highly variable. Most anglers take four or five fish per stringer, but a lucky angler may land 75 to 100 fish, which can heavily skew creel records.
- The article notes that catch records for crappies, trout and other species may not reflect true population abundance, since they depend on whether a lucky angler is checked.
- Crappie populations on Mead are not as cyclic as those in smaller lakes, such as the Salt River chain, because anglers can usually find fish by moving to different areas.

### Lake Mohave

- Mohave is primarily a bass lake, but it has a mixed fishery: trout are important in the river sections and in cooler months, while bass lead the catch when the lake is warmer.
- Rainbow trout in the river below Davis Dam are hatchery-reared. The article describes this area as a long-standing trout fishery.
- Creel data show that the proportion of trout to bass changes from north to south along the lake.
- Mohave's average catch rate per angler-hour was slightly below that on Mead.

### Lake Havasu

- A limited creel census in 1960 found an average catch of roughly 0.22 fish per hour (the source value is partly garbled). Of the fish caught, 63% were bass, about 12½% were channel catfish, and the remainder were panfish and rough fish.
- Havasu has received less fisheries study than the other lakes, but the article reports that it appears to produce its share of bass and black crappie.
- Night fishing near lighted boat docks is noted as an especially productive way to catch crappie.
- Surface-plug bass fishing is reported to be more productive on Havasu than on other lakes, although the reason is not explained.
- The author suggests that water-skiing and general recreation may reduce fishing effort and lower catch rates.

### Introductions and Management

- **Threadfin Shad**: introduced several years before the article; the author reports that the shad has materially increased productivity and forage, which earlier reports and articles support.
- **Striped Bass**: a sea-going fish reported to reach about forty pounds, which was introduced into the Santee-Cooper reservoirs of South Carolina. The article states that conditions there suggest Havasu offers similar habitat. Arizona and Nevada biologists have been jointly considering stocking striped bass in Havasu, although no formal approval for planting had been granted in the article's time frame. Stripers need a long stretch of free-flowing river above the lake to spawn, and the author stresses that this river section is important for the species' reproduction and survival.
- **Stripers already planted**: young striped bass have been planted in the river sections, and the article reports that good-sized fish have recently been caught, indicating that the introduction appears to be succeeding.
- **Kokanee Salmon**: planted in Havasu. The article says the planting is consistent with the lake's current stocking plans, and that the species may be expected to produce well.
- **Water level management**: Lake Mead's productivity may be affected by low water levels, which reduce the amount of shoreline and nutrient-rich habitat available for fish production.
