# Gape Limitation, Prey Size Refuges and the Top-Down Impacts of Piscivorous Largemouth Bass in Shallow Pond Ecosystems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #trophic-ecology #predator-prey-interactions

## Executive Summary

This experimental pond study examined the top-down effects of piscivorous [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) on nutrients, turbidity, phytoplankton, zooplankton, and fish communities in shallow eutrophic ponds. The fish assemblage included [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), representing a range of body sizes and feeding guilds. Results showed that largemouth bass significantly reduced mosquitofish biomass and shifted bluegill size distributions toward larger individuals, but did not cascade effects to plankton communities. Turbidity and chlorophyll *a* were elevated in ponds containing fish (with or without bass) compared to fishless controls, but did not differ between fish treatments. The study demonstrates that gape limitation and the dominance of large-bodied benthivorous fish can prevent piscivore effects from cascading to lower trophic levels.

## Background & Description

[[Largemouth Bass]] is a gape-limited piscivore found throughout North America and introduced worldwide. The species' mouth gape size restricts the sizes and body depths of prey fish it can consume. This study tested predictions that piscivore effects would be strongest at the top of food webs and dampened at lower trophic levels, particularly in systems containing deep-bodied fish species capable of reaching size refuges from predation.

The experiment was conducted in 11 ponds (0.18-0.56 ha surface area, maximum depth 1.2 m, mean depth 0.6 m) at Eagle Mountain Fish Hatchery, Fort Worth, Texas. Ponds contained macrophytes including *Najas guadalupensis*, *Chara* spp., *Potamogeton* spp., *Nelumbo lutea*, *Myriophyllum spicatum*, and *Ceratophyllum demersum*.

Three treatments were established with 3-4 replicates each:
1. **NF**: Fishless ponds (n=3)
2. **FC**: Fish community without largemouth bass (n=4)
3. **FCB**: Fish community with largemouth bass (n=4)

Initial stocking rates reflected typical southern U.S. reservoir fish communities:
- Largemouth bass: 21.2 kg ha⁻¹
- Western mosquitofish: 1.7 kg ha⁻¹
- Bluegill: 43.7 kg ha⁻¹
- Channel catfish: 18.6 kg ha⁻¹
- Gizzard shad: 78.2 kg ha⁻¹
- Common carp: 69.7 kg ha⁻¹

Fish were stocked June-July 1995, and ponds were fertilized every 4-6 weeks (9.4 L ha⁻¹ of 10N:34P:0K) to maintain eutrophic conditions. Sampling occurred every 4-6 weeks from July 1995 to June 1996 for water chemistry, phytoplankton, and zooplankton. Ponds were drained March 1997 for complete fish census.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fish community represented diverse feeding guilds:
- **Western mosquitofish**: Zooplanktivore/insectivore, shallow-bodied, highly vulnerable to piscivory
- **Bluegill**: Carnivore consuming zooplankton and macroinvertebrates, deep-bodied, vulnerable as juveniles
- **Channel catfish**: Benthic carnivore, deep-bodied, capable of size refuge
- **Gizzard shad**: Benthic detritivore/omnivore, deep-bodied, capable of size refuge
- **Common carp**: Benthic carnivore, deep-bodied, capable of size refuge

Water temperatures ranged from 6°C (January 1996) to 31°C (July 1995-1996). Total phosphorus ranged from 20 to 1254 μg L⁻¹ and total nitrogen from 200 to 1500 μg L⁻¹, confirming eutrophic conditions throughout the experiment. Ponds were shallow (mean depth 0.6 m), typical of many southern U.S. aquatic systems.

Benthivorous fish (bluegill, channel catfish, gizzard shad, common carp) dominated fish community biomass in both FC and FCB treatments. These species increase turbidity through sediment resuspension, excrete benthically-derived nutrients into the water column, and can enhance phytoplankton biomass.

## Key Findings

### Water Chemistry and Phytoplankton

- **Temperature**: Marginally significant treatment effect (p=0.06), with two FCB ponds ~2.3°C cooler during summer, likely due to shading by American lotus (*Nelumbo lutea*)
- **Nutrients**: No significant treatment effects on total phosphorus (p=0.32) or total nitrogen (p=0.30)
- **Turbidity**: Significant treatment effect (p=0.02). FC (p=0.01) and FCB (p=0.02) ponds consistently more turbid than NF ponds. FC and FCB ponds did not differ (p=0.74)
- **Chlorophyll *a***: Significant treatment effect (p=0.02) and treatment×time interaction (p=0.01). FC (p=0.01) and FCB (p=0.03) ponds had higher chlorophyll *a* than NF ponds, but FC and FCB did not differ (p=0.67). Differences between fish and fishless ponds only occurred when water temperature >11°C. Chlorophyll *a* difference (fish minus fishless) was a significant positive function of water temperature in both FC (r²=0.46, p=0.031) and FCB treatments, with combined regression: y=1.2x-10.8 (r²=0.50, p<0.001)

### Zooplankton

- **Cyclopoid copepodids**: Significant treatment effect (p=0.04). Densities higher in FC (p=0.03) and FCB (p=0.003) than NF ponds. FC and FCB did not differ (p=0.08)
- **Copepod nauplii**: Significant treatment effect (p=0.01). Densities higher in FC and FCB than NF ponds. FC and FCB did not differ (p=0.935)
- ***Daphnia* spp.**: Marginally significant treatment effect (p=0.082). FC ponds had higher densities than NF (p=0.014), but FCB and NF did not differ (p=0.100), nor did FC and FCB (p=0.157)
- No significant treatment effects on *Bosmina* sp. (p=0.206), *Ceriodaphnia* sp. (p=0.103), *Diaphanosoma brachyurum* (p=0.118), calanoid copepods (p=0.251), or non-predatory rotifers (p=0.646)

### Fish Community Responses

- **Total fish community**: No significant effect of largemouth bass on total biomass or density (excluding bass)
- **Western mosquitofish**: Significantly reduced biomass (p=0.03) and density (p=0.03) in FCB ponds. All individuals had body depths <1 cm, making them vulnerable to bass predation throughout their life cycle
- **Bluegill**: No significant difference in total biomass (p=0.57) or density (p=0.28) between FC and FCB ponds. However, size distribution shifted toward larger, deeper-bodied individuals (5-8 cm body depth) in FCB ponds, while FC ponds had peak biomass at 2 cm body depth
- **Channel catfish**: No significant effect on biomass (p=0.20) or density (p=0.43). Body depths ranged 5-10 cm with peak biomass at 6-9 cm in both treatments
- **Gizzard shad**: No significant effect on biomass (p=0.69) or density (p=0.67). Body depths ranged 7-13 cm, exceeding maximum bass gape (6 cm)
- **Common carp**: No significant effect on biomass (p=0.40) or density (p=0.51). Body depths ranged 6-22 cm with majority >12 cm. Dominated total fish biomass (57-82%) in both FC and FCB ponds
- **Largemouth bass gapes**: Ranged from <1 to 6 cm, with greatest biomass proportion at 5 cm size class

### Gape Limitation and Size Refuges

The distribution of largemouth bass gape sizes (maximum 6 cm) relative to prey fish body depths revealed clear size refuges:
- Western mosquitofish: No size refuge (all <1 cm body depth)
- Bluegill: Partial refuge (individuals >6 cm body depth invulnerable)
- Channel catfish: Substantial refuge (5-10 cm body depth, mostly invulnerable)
- Gizzard shad: Complete refuge (7-13 cm body depth, all invulnerable)
- Common carp: Complete refuge (6-22 cm body depth, nearly all invulnerable)

The study demonstrates that in shallow pond ecosystems dominated by large-bodied benthivorous fish, the top-down effects of gape-limited piscivores like largemouth bass are most intense at the top of the food web and do not cascade to plankton communities. This contrasts with systems dominated by small, shallow-bodied prey fish where trophic cascades to phytoplankton have been observed. The context-dependent nature of piscivore effects challenges the generality of largemouth bass as a keystone species and suggests that fish community composition and size structure are critical determinants of whether top-down control extends to lower trophic levels.
