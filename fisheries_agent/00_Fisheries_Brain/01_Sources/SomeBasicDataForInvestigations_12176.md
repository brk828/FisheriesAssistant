# Basic Data on Aquatic Macrophytes: Biomass, Composition and Productivity

#aquatic-macrophytes #marine-biology #primary-production

## Executive Summary

This review, presented at the I.B.P. Symposium on Primary Productivity in Aquatic Environments (Pallanza, Italy, April 1965), by [[Westlake]] of the River Laboratory, Freshwater Biological Association, compiles "[[Basic Data]]" needed to plan productivity studies of aquatic macrophytes. It covers biomass and net production, criteria of biomass (volume, fresh and dry weight, ash, carbon, energy), the proportion of biomass underground, the relation between biomass changes and production, and losses from death, damage, disease and grazing. The central message is that macrophytes are highly productive where they occur, that underground parts are often a major fraction of biomass and must be sampled, and that a single biomass measurement does not reliably indicate production.

## Background & Description

The paper responds to a recognised shortage of simple but detailed data on the chemical composition, morphology and phenology of aquatic organisms (the "basic data" of [[Lund]] 1964). Its focus is freshwater macrophytes, with comparisons to marine seaweeds, salt marsh and [[Phytoplankton]]. Data come partly from the literature and partly from the author's own observations, and were analysed in collaboration with colleagues at the Freshwater Biological Association.

Key methodological topics include:

- **Volume:** a poor measure because density depends on internal air spaces. Plant volume was estimated by water displacement after repeated [[Spin-Drying]] and extrapolation to zero spin time.
- **Fresh weight:** best obtained by spin-drying. Air-draining on lines takes 30 minutes to over 3 hours depending on weather.
- **Dry weight:** oven-dried at 105 °C to constant weight. Air-dry and cooled weights run 5-10% high.
- **Ash:** ignition at 550 °C, with corrections for [[Calcareous Deposits]] (carbonates) where present.
- **Organic carbon:** determined by dry combustion after removing carbonates with sulphur dioxide.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Relative productivity.** Macrophytes are generally more productive per unit area than phytoplankton under comparable conditions. However, in lakes deeper than about 10 m (depending on transparency), in oceans and in deep turbid rivers, macrophytes are confined to the littoral zone and total production is usually lower than that of phytoplankton. Emergent communities are often more productive than adjacent submerged ones, though this depends on local conditions such as wave exposure.

**Annual net primary production on fertile sites** (Table 1, after Westlake 1963, in metric tons dry organic matter per hectare):

| Community | Production (t/ha/yr) |
|---|---|
| Marine phytoplankton | 1-45 |
| Lake phytoplankton | 1-9 |
| Freshwater submerged macrophytes | 4-20 |
| Marine submerged macrophytes (seaweeds) | 25-40 |
| Marine emergent macrophytes (salt marsh) | 25-85 (queried) |
| Freshwater emergent macrophytes ([[Reedswamp]]) | 30-85 |

Gross production is probably 1.5-3 times net production.

**Biomass.** Phytoplankton biomass is typically 10-100 g dry weight/m², against 0.2-10 kg dry weight/m² for macrophytes on fertile sites. [[Periphyton]] biomass can reach about 100-500 g dry weight/m² in luxuriant cases, though such populations are often short-lived.

**Underground biomass.** Roots and rhizomes can dominate biomass:

- [[Littorella Uniflora]]: roots form 46-55% of biomass.
- [[Thalassia Testudinum]]: 75-85% of biomass in the substratum.
- [[Eichhornia Crassipes]]: roots were 48% of biomass in open water but 21% near the bank (Marion County, Florida).
- [[Elodea Canadensis]]: roots only 2.6% of biomass (Borutskii 1950).
- In [[Glyceria Maxima]], [[Scirpus Lacustris]] and [[Typha Angustifolia]], much of the plant is buried. For example, Scirpus lacustris rhizomes were reported at 95% of the whole plant by Seidel, though the author considered this high for summer samples.

**Annual regrowth and turnover.** Many temperate submerged macrophytes behave as plants of [[Annual Regrowth]], dying back to small perennating organs. Some always die down ([[Potamogeton Pectinatus]], P. natans), others persist (Ranunculus pseudofluitans, Elodea canadensis), and some vary with conditions (Callitriche stagnalis, Myriophyllum spp.).

**Grazing.** Most aquatic macrophyte production is consumed by bacteria, fungi and detritus feeders. Molluscs and other invertebrates on submerged weeds mostly browse periphyton and dead material. Emergent plants are more heavily grazed, but losses are often small; in a Glyceria maxima reedswamp, mollusc and leaf-miner consumption was less than 1% of maximum biomass. Large vertebrates (grass carp, turtles, swans, manatee, coypu) can cause extensive losses, and cattle graze bank-side Glyceria intensively. Excluding grazers from experimental plots may underestimate productivity, since grazing can stimulate growth.

## Key Findings

1. **Dry matter content** of submerged higher plants is generally 5-20% of fresh weight (up to about 25% with calcareous deposits). Plants with floating or emergent leaves average about 15%, totally submerged plants about 10%. Mature reeds, rushes and sedges range from 14% to over 40%, with a living-plant average near 25%. Nitella may contain as little as 4%.

2. **Ash content** of submerged macrophytes is usually 15-25% of dry weight (over 50% in very calcareous material; calcareous Characeae up to 70%). Softer emergent plants usually contain 10-20%, while harder reeds, rushes and sedges contain 2-12% in green shoots.

3. **Organic carbon** (ash-free dry weight basis) is tightly constrained, with most values between 43% and 48% and a mean of about 46.5%. The common assumption of 45% is considered unsound because of variable ash content. In calcareous material, the true factor may be below 30%.

4. **Energy content** of most aquatic macrophytes is 4.3-4.8 kcal/g organic matter (mean 4.6). Harder emergent plants are generally below average. Hybrid Typha gave 4.34 kcal/g by bomb calorimetry; Glyceria maxima gave 4.5-4.8 kcal/g.

5. **Criteria of biomass** must be inter-calibrated before comparing studies. Volume is unreliable, and spin-drying gives consistent fresh weights, supported by petroleum-ether checks.

6. **Underground sampling is essential** for emergent and rosette plants. Rhizomes and roots must be sampled to avoid underestimating biomass.

7. **Stubble** can be a large fraction of harvested biomass. Cutting Typha angustifolia at about 25 cm left over 20% of biomass as stubble (50% of green shoots).

8. **Biomass change is a valid measure of production only over short periods** or where losses are negligible. A single biomass determination is not a measure of current productivity. The terminal biomass, sampled after losses, underestimates average productivity.

9. **Annual production versus maximum biomass:** Borutskii estimated that annual production of Elodea canadensis in Lake Beloie could exceed five times its seasonal maximum, though the calculations contained errors. For Phragmites communis, annual crop was about 2% above maximum standing crop. Estimates for the River Ivel (0.85 kg/m² production in 1959, versus a 0.52 kg/m² maximum in 1958) and for Ruppia maritima in Borax Lake (50-70 g/m² production versus 64 g/m² maximum) suggest maximum biomass is close to annual production in those cases, though exact values are uncertain.

10. **Losses before maximum biomass** are generally small. Harper and Borutskii estimated 2-10% of final biomass. Dead Glyceria leaves reached 10-20% of maximum biomass, though some may have persisted from the previous year. Terrestrial evidence suggests losses are unlikely to exceed 15% of current production by maximum biomass unless signs of damage are visible. Seaweed losses to wave action can be very large.

11. **Rhizome longevity** varies by species: Phragmites communis rhizomes persist at least 3 years; Scirpus lacustris at least 3 years, probably more; Arundo donax about 4 years; Sparganium erectum about 18 months; Glyceria maxima and Typha latifolia less than 2 years. Roots of most reedswamp plants probably live less than a year. Soil conditions may affect longevity.

12. **Research needs.** The author concludes that very few complete studies of biomass change in relation to losses and metabolism exist, and hopes that the I.B.P. will stimulate them. Future work should also target [[Potamogeton Pectinatus]] (underground storage organs) and [[Ranunculus Pseudofluitans]] (anchorage roots in strong currents). Methods based on [[Box Technique]] planting have limits, since planted individuals are free from natural grazing and competition.
