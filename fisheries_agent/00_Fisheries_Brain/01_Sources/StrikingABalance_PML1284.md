# Striking a Balance: Conservation Biology Between Problem Definition and Implementation

#conservation-biology #adaptive-management #research-evaluation

## Executive Summary

This commentary by [[Devra G. Kleiman]] examines the balance of activities presented at the 2002 meeting of the [[Society for Conservation Biology]] (SCB) in Canterbury. The author argues that, despite frequent references to [[Adaptive Management]], conservation biology focuses more on defining problems, setting priorities, and planning than on implementing and evaluating conservation actions. The argument rests on a content analysis of about 25% of the 2002 conference abstracts (161 abstracts reviewed). The document does not address fish, aquatic species, or any specific ecosystem. Its subject is the research culture of conservation science.

## Background & Description

The commentary was published in *Conservation Biology* (volume 17, no. 2, April 2003). Its author observed a pattern at the SCB meeting: many papers used the term "adaptive management," but few reported real cases of evaluating or implementing "lessons learned." The author links this to [[Whitten et al. 2001]], who made a similar point about the field's emphasis on planning over action.

**Method:** To obtain a roughly random sample, the author selected the first full abstract on each page of the Conference Abstracts (about four per page). Each was placed into one of seven categories:

1. Description and definition of a problem, often through statistical analysis of data
2. Development of a model
3. Directed research to find solutions
4. Basic biological monitoring (descriptive surveys, biological comparisons, basic research)
5. Implementation (application of results to management)
6. Evaluation of activities
7. None of the above

Category 7 was used for abstracts that could not be classified.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document is not an ecological study, but it places conservation research within a wider crisis framing. The author writes that "we are doing research as forests burn and species go extinct." The commentary contrasts the field's focus on describing problems with the need for hands-on action in threatened habitats. [[Habitat Restoration]] is discussed as an example where the lessons from restoration practice remain unclear. The author argues that restoration projects, like other conservation actions, need careful evaluation to determine which approaches are cost-effective.

## Key Findings

**Distribution of abstract categories (n = 161 reviewed)**

- The two largest categories were:
  - Description and definition of the problem: **25%**
  - Basic biological monitoring: **28%**
- The two smallest categories, excluding category 7, were:
  - Implementation (application of results to management): **4%**
  - Evaluation of activities: **8%**
- This pattern held even though an entire session, "Scientists and Managers," featured several papers on evaluation models and the history of evaluation.

**Interpretation:** The author concludes that conservation biologists continue to define problems through basic research and data analysis while giving less attention to implementation. This supports [[Whitten et al. 2001]].

**Alternative explanations the author considers:**

1. Implementation and evaluation take many years, and monitoring and basic research also require long timeframes.
2. Abstracts may not reflect the talks actually given, and speakers rarely mention implementation or evaluation in abstracts.
3. Conservation biologists may not see the annual SCB conference as the right venue for implementation and evaluation papers.
4. Conservation programs may have poorly defined goals, making true evaluation impossible.
5. Many conservation biologists come from academia, which is more hypothesis-driven. Support flows to basic research and model development, not to implementing recommended actions. Some biologists have said they should not be involved in implementation at all.
6. Conservation organizations rarely fund evaluation ([[Redford and Taber 2000]]). Because they are donor-driven, practitioners may avoid experimentation and self-censor their results.

**Structural pressures:**

- [[Academic Research Funding]] rewards publication in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at meetings, such as those funded through the [[National Science Foundation]]. Peer-reviewed research receives little comparable support or prestige when it concerns implementation or evaluation.
- Funding dependence and the rush toward new crises may push practitioners away from evaluating past work.

**Recommendations and conclusions:**

- Conservation biologists should engage more with implementation, including hands-on work in areas where they are not familiar.
- The field needs better [[Evaluation]] practices, and organizations should use evaluation results to guide future work rather than treating criticism as failure.
- Practitioners should share lessons from both successes and failures, including through workshops and conferences.
- Donors and academic institutions need to be educated about the value of long-term [[Long-Term Monitoring]], experimentation, and the reporting of unsuccessful outcomes.
- The author questions whether the field needs more descriptive work on problems that are already well documented, and calls for a shift in emphasis toward [[Implementation]] and evaluation.
