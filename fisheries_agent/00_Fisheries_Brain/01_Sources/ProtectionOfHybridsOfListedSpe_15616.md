# Legal Memorandum on Protection of Hybrids under the Endangered Species Act

#endangered-species #conservation-policy #legal-interpretation

## Executive Summary

This 1981 legal memorandum from the Assistant Solicitor for Fish and Wildlife to the Associate Director of Federal Assistance addresses whether hybrids of listed species receive protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The memo defines hybrids as offspring of two animals or plants where each parent is from a different species or subspecies and at least one parent is listed under the ESA. The conclusion reaffirms a previous 1977 opinion that hybrids generally should not receive protection under the Act, as such protection would contravene the Act's purpose of preserving genetic purity and diversity of disappearing species.

## Background & Description

The memorandum responds to an inquiry dated January 21, 1981, specifically referencing management programs for the [[Dusky Seaside Sparrow]] and the [[Southern Sea Otter]]. The hybrid issue had been addressed in two previous, differing memoranda from the Office, each responding to conflicting biological information based on specific species problems. The Office of Endangered Species provided a memorandum dated October 3, 1980, presenting general observations about hybrid conservation merits and concluding that hybrids generally should not receive ESA protection.

The legal analysis is based on consultation with interested offices regarding biological implications of hybrid conservation, conversations with the requesting office, and review of relevant statutes and legislative history.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The memorandum presents several biological concerns regarding hybrid protection:

- **Genetic Integrity**: Congress intended to preserve the genetic purity and diversity of disappearing species through the ESA
- **Gene Pool Disruption**: Protection of hybrids would lead to disruption of a listed species' gene pool
- **Competitive Advantage**: Hybrids are frequently larger, more vigorous, and more adaptive than their purebred parents
- **Habitat Competition**: Uncontrolled hybrids may take over habitat or environments essential to purebreds' survival
- **Extinction Risk**: Hybrid protection might encourage the extinction of endangered or threatened species

## Key Findings

**Primary Legal Conclusion**: Hybrids of listed species do not receive protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. This conclusion reaffirms the August 2, 1977 opinion without modification.

**Rationale**: Protecting hybrids would contravene the purpose of the ESA—the conservation and protection of truly endangered and threatened species—by disrupting genetic purity and diversity that Congress intended to preserve.

**Application to [[Dusky Seaside Sparrow]]**: 
- A hybrid of the Dusky would not be protected under the ESA
- Expending funds to produce hybrids would be difficult to justify as conservation or recovery under the Act's definitions (16 U.S.C. 1532(3) and 1533(g))
- Exception: If the hybrid itself could be separately listed as an endangered or threatened species, then funds to produce additional hybrids would be appropriate under Section 4(g)

**Application to [[Southern Sea Otter]]**:
- Augmentation of a listed subspecies with an unlisted subspecies would not satisfy legal requirements for recovery
- Simply adding a similar but separate subspecies does not change the biological status of the listed subspecies as measured by Section 4(a) criteria (16 U.S.C. 1533(a))
- Addition of hybrid offspring to a population of listed species would not change the biological status of the listed species
- Such augmentation would not bring the species "to the point at which measures provided pursuant to [the] Act are no longer necessary" (16 U.S.C. 1532(3))

**Policy Implication**: The memorandum establishes that recovery plans must focus on the listed subspecies or species itself, not on augmentation through hybrids or unlisted subspecies, to meet ESA recovery objectives.
