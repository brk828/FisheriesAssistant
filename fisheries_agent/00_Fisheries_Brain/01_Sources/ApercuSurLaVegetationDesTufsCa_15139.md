# Calcareous Tufa Vegetation of Belgium

#bryology #phycology #limnology #tufa-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This survey by Symoens, Duvigneaud and Vanden Berghen (Bulletin de la Société Royale de Botanique de Belgique, 1951) reviews the calcareous tufas (travertines) of Belgium and the plants that build them. Tufa forms when [[Biogenic Decalcification]] occurs: photosynthesis strips dissolved calcium bicarbonate from water, precipitating [[Calcium Carbonate]]. The authors combine a literature synthesis with their own field observations. They describe fossil and active deposits in ponds, alkaline fens, streams and slope seepages, with the Belgian [[Crons]] of the Lorraine belge as the most detailed case. The paper does not address fish, except one remark on the salmonid fauna of the [[Hoyoux River]].

## Background & Description

Tufas are carbonate rocks formed by plant-mediated precipitation from bicarbonate-rich waters that circulate through limestone terrain. Some are fossil and preserve Quaternary plant remains, which makes them valuable for palaeobotany. Others are still forming and offer a window onto present-day biological processes.

Many organisms precipitate or accumulate carbonate:

- **Algae:** freshwater taxa such as [[Oscillatoria]], [[Cladophora]], [[Spirogyra]] and [[Batrachospermum]], as well as the cyanobacteria [[Lyngbya Calcarea]], [[Phormidium]], [[Rivularia]] and [[Schizothrix]]. Marine taxa include [[Lithothamnium]] (maerl formers) and [[Halimeda]].
- **Charophytes:** [[Chara]] can be so mineralised that its ash contains up to 95% CaO.
- **Bryophytes:** [[Cratoneurum Commutatum]], [[Eucladium Verticillatum]], [[Philonotis Calcarea]] and [[Drepanocladus Revolvens]].
- **Vascular plants:** submerged macrophytes such as [[Potamogeton]], [[Myriophyllum]] and [[Elodea]] often become encrusted.

The authors also note that calcareous ponds, fens and streams in Belgium each give rise to distinct tufa types, which they classify below.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Biogenic decalcification consistently changes water chemistry. Calcium concentration falls, while alkalinity, conductivity and pH shift. The cited experiments show how fast this happens:

- Photosynthesis by mixed algae and macrophytes raised pH from 7.4–7.5 to about 9, and sometimes to 10.2–10.4 (Dahm, 1925).
- In [[Hubert]]'s experiment, 100 g of Elodea in 4.5 L of tap water raised pH from about 8.3 to 9.5 by 15:30 and changed alkalinity and conductivity over the same period.
- At Lahage (Lorraine belge), pH rose from 7.5 at the spring to 7.9 after flowing over moss and to 8.5 in water saturating [[Cratoneurum Commutatum]] tufts.
- On the [[Haumont Stream]], pH rose from about 7.06 at the source to about 8.00 at 500 m. Calcium and alkalinity fell over the same reach, then partly reversed after an affluent joined.

Lake systems also show this effect. In [[Lake Zurich]], spring plankton blooms precipitated calcium carbonate crystals, and water transparency fell from 6 m to 1.60 m.

Calcium carbonate that a plant precipitates may stay attached to it. Encrusting cyanobacteria harden into compact thalli, while [[Oscillatoria]] filaments can look like strings of beads. The deposit is eventually permanent in every case: it accumulates as lake sediments, stream dams, fen peat or slope tufa.

Water conditions for the Belgian crons are cool and basic. Spring water at the [[Crons]] runs about 8–8.3 °C at pH 7.3–7.5, with 94 mg/L CaO recorded at Montauban. Springs issue from Sinemurian marls and are often forested. The [[Hoyoux River]] carries alkalinity up to 4.95 and pH 7.9, with a steep gradient (7.6–8.3 ‰) and a fauna described as exclusively salmonid, in the trout zone.

## Key Findings

**1. Four types of tufa-forming environment in Belgium**
- Ponds and eutrophic marshes, such as the Put pond at Lillo and calcareous lake sediments near Tronchiennes. Calcium carbonate content there reaches 70%, in layers over 30 cm thick.
- Alkaline fens, notably the [[Berg Marsh]] (Schoenetum nigricantis), where encrusting mosses and cyanobacteria build calcareous peat.
- Stream tufas, classified into two types:
  - **Type 1** (algal-dominated, without vascular plants): [[Haumont Stream]], [[Vroenenbosch Stream]], [[Larenbeek Stream]], [[Vallées Stream]], [[Annevoie Stream]] and [[Fonds de Ry Stream]], all built mainly by [[Lyngbya Calcarea]].
  - **Type 2** (moss-algal, colonised by vascular plants): the [[Hoyoux River]] cascades at Royseux, Barse and Régissa. The main encrusting alga there is a Lyngbya, with [[Schizothrix Coriacea]] also present. Vegetation on the emergent tufa is dominated by [[Petasites Hybridus]].
- Slope tufas, the [[Crons]] of the Lorraine belge and the Mosan region.

**2. Fossil and recent deposits**
- The Bonlez deposit in the Train valley is a Holocene tufa. A 1.5 m white layer, a 1.75 m yellowish layer and a grey peaty base of about 1 m lie beneath 1.5 m of colluvial loam. Its formation probably ended in historical times.
- The Hoyoux tufa at Marchin is exploited to about 10 m. Its origin is disputed: lacustrine (Forir, Lohest) or aerated water falling over a rock barrier (van den Broeck et al.).

**3. Cron dynamics**
- Active crons are built mainly by [[Cratoneurum Commutatum]], with [[Lyngbya Nana]] as the epiphytic cyanobacterium that drives precipitation.
- [[Sesleria Coerulea]] is the key grass. It builds stepped, cascading terraces, and its roots grow in almost pure carbonate. The authors suggest the tufa acts as a physiological drought for these normally xerophytic species.
- Crons are cyclical. Dying terraces revert to Sesleria steppe, forest or bare limestone, and new tufa can re-form when water is diverted back.
- Light is not necessary for precipitation. Deposition occurs in shaded seepages, and active crons can clear forest by killing trees at their base.

**4. Records**
- Verhulst (1914) listed 13 cron sites in the Lorraine belge. Van Oye and Hubert (1937) listed 12, and the authors found a further series in the same region.
- Other tufa localities are listed in the Belgian survey (Liège, Namur, Dinant, Jodoigne, Brussels, Mons, Tournai).

**Conclusion:** The authors argue that tufa formation is an ecological process driven by plants and their interaction with water chemistry, not a purely geological one. Continued study of biogenic carbonate deposits in Belgium would deepen palaeobotanical and hydrochemical understanding.
