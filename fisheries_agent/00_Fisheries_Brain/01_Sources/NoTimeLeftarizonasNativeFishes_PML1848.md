# No Time Left: Arizona's Native Fishes, Going, Going, Gone!

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document argues that Arizona's native freshwater fishes are in severe decline and that their loss signals broader damage to the state's aquatic ecosystems. Of the 31 freshwater fish kinds originally present in Arizona, 26 are listed by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] as Endangered, Threatened, or candidates for listing, and one is extinct. The author states that four of five kinds face extinction and that this happened within the last 50 years. Most of these species occur nowhere else in the world except western American rivers.

The text attributes the decline to physical and biological habitat damage from human activity, including grazing, timbering, water development, and the introduction of non-native species. It proposes managing remaining natural habitats for "naturalness," meaning a self-sustaining, [[Lotic Ecosystem]], and treating native fishes as inexpensive indicators of environmental change. The [[Aravaipa Creek]] watershed serves as the central case study.

## Background & Description

The document is an unsolicited commentary and set of recommendations on long-term conservation of the [[Aravaipa Creek]] system, published in the *Arizona Conservation Voter* in autumn 1993. The author, [[W. L. Minckley]] of the Zoology department at [[Arizona State University]], writes from long involvement in the study and management of streams and fishes.

The paper is directed mainly at agencies and organizations active in the system. It emphasizes the aquatic component (the creek, its tributaries, and its aquatic and riparian biota), while treating the whole watershed as the unit of concern, because a stream "receives and transports essentially all the things that enter (or happen) within its watershed."

Responsibility for the system is fragmented. The text notes that no fewer than 18 local, state, and federal agencies, one non-governmental organization ([[The Nature Conservancy]]), and numerous private landowners have a stake in it (Table 2). Relevant bodies include the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], the [[Bureau of Land Management]], the [[Forest Service]], and the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]].

The document includes several planning tables that divide conservation responsibilities among agencies by geographic area, ecological similarity, and habitat type. The author describes the species rankings as arbitrary and capricious in relation to endangerment. The tables cover species such as [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Apache Trout]], [[Spikedace]], and [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]], among others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical (pre-technological) conditions.** The text describes the system before major human alteration as follows:
- Rain and snowmelt ran off slowly from undisturbed watersheds, with much of the water entering the water table.
- Groundwater seeped downslope into porous valley alluvium, sustaining reliable summer baseflow.
- Violent floods were infrequent, and stream channels were complex, scarcely or only locally incised.
- Substrates were well sorted (rock, gravel, and sand), and pools were common near tree roots, boulders, and banks.
- Riparian vegetation ranged from cottonwood-willow forest and dense brushland to [[Cienega]] marshes where groundwater reached the surface.
- Groundwater exchange and riparian shading moderated water temperatures.
- Native fishes co-occurred and coevolved with their predators and competitors over millennia.

**Modern conditions.** The author contrasts these with present conditions:
- Rapid runoff from grazed or timbered watersheds produces frequent flash floods.
- Flood power concentrates downward, and erosion and sediment transport increase in narrow, downcut channels.
- Streams have cut deeper into their floodplains, transported sand homogenizes bottoms, and fewer pools remain.
- Water tables have dropped through stream cutting and pumping, uprooting or killing riparian plants during drought.
- Summer flow is often reduced to a small creek across barren sand, with rapid and extreme heating and cooling.
- Non-native fishes from artificial ponds and other refuges eat or compete with remnant native fishes.

**Timeline of impacts.** Timbering and livestock grazing had altered vegetation by the 1800s. Water development began drying streams and changing flow by the early 1900s. Stocking of non-native animals and plants since the 1940s restructured aquatic communities.

**Conceptual framework.** The paper defines the overriding goal as maintaining "naturalness," accepting the objective of conserving a self-sustaining lotic ecosystem. Naturalness is treated as a relative, not absolute, condition, since complete exclusion of human influence is impossible. Table 3 organizes concerns into three categories:
1. **Watershed conditions** (factors affecting inputs): grazing, timbering, mining, agriculture, habitation, water diversion, roads, human access and recreation, and industry.
2. **In-stream conditions** (factors affecting processing, survival, and perpetuation): sediment quality and quantity, transport, nutrients, toxicants, water volume and hydrographic patterns, biotic invasion, and genetic integrity of native biota.
3. **External influences** (outside threats): loss of recolonization sources for native biota, and global factors such as acid rain and the [[Greenhouse Effect]].

## Key Findings

- **Scale of loss:** 26 of 31 native Arizona freshwater fish kinds are federally listed as Endangered, Threatened, or candidates; one is extinct. Four of five face extinction, and this occurred in roughly the last 50 years.
- **Endemism:** 29 of the 31 kinds live nowhere else in the world but western American rivers.
- **Causes:** Decline reflects physical and biological habitat damage from grazing, timbering, water development, reservoirs, canals, and non-native species. Native fishes also disappear where natural flows are replaced by artificial ones.
- **Non-native species:** Introduced fishes thrive in stable human-made habitats (ponds, reservoirs, canals), then spread into natural habitats to eliminate remaining native populations.
- **Indicator value:** Native fishes are described as natural and inexpensive indicators of environmental change. A decline in a native species suggests a system-level change that will likely affect water quality or quantity.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Manage remaining natural habitats through sustainable grazing, mining, and water-use approaches, which would protect both native fishes and water supplies.
  - Regulate stocking and other activities that spread non-native fishes, with native species in mind.
  - Pursue refuge areas and proactive management as public understanding increases, "assuming any are left to perpetuate."
- **Conservation principle:** Intervention should allow natural adjustments "neither faster nor slower than natural," and should reverse, regulate, or prohibit human actions that alter the system's natural trajectory. The author cautions that a short record (for example, a century of [[Aravaipa Creek]] data) may be too brief to define what the system should look like, so maintaining a fixed state is "preservation rather than conservation."
- **Existing protections:** Land acquisition by non-governmental and federal agencies, wilderness designation for part of the stream and watershed, and federal and state protection of certain biota have already begun.
- **Responsibility division:** Planning tables propose dividing conservation duties among agencies by geographic area (mainstem Colorado, Gila, and Mexican drainages), ecological similarity (long-lived, short-lived, and high-elevation species), and habitat (big-river, intermediate-sized creeks, marshes and springs). The author calls the rankings of species by likelihood of endangerment arbitrary.
