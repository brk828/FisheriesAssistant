# Effects of Interim Flows from Glen Canyon Dam on Lower Colorado River Fishes (Trip No. 1, 1992)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Quarterly Report (Trip No. 1, June 24 – July 2, 1992) by [[BIO/WEST Inc]] for the [[Hualapai Wildlife Management Department]] documents the first field trip of a study of aquatic resources in the 60-mile reach of the [[Colorado River]] from Diamond Creek (RM 226) to below Pearce Ferry (RM 286), at the inflow to [[Lake Mead]]. The study monitors how [[Interim Flows]] from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] affect fish populations, habitat, non-native fish interactions, and food resources, under the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Interim Flows Monitoring Program.

Sixteen fish species from nine families were captured. Only two are native to the Colorado River Basin; the other 14 are non-native or introduced. The endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were not seen or captured.

## Background & Description

The reach was divided into four longitudinal strata reflecting different habitat complexes (Table 3):

- **Stratum A** (RM 226–239): steep, narrow, swift canyon; boulder, cobble and gravel substrate; includes Diamond Creek and Travertine Canyon.
- **Stratum B** (RM 239–252): steep canyon with sedimentary lake deposits; tributaries Separation, Spencer, Surprise and Lost Creek.
- **Stratum C** (RM 252–265): wide canyon, vegetated lake sediments (willow, tamarisk, *Baccharis*); major tributary Quartermaster Canyon.
- **Stratum D** (RM 265–286): large open, braided alluvial delta with backwaters and isolated pools; tributary Emery Falls Canyon.

Four camp sites (Travertine, Spencer, Lost Creek, Quartermaster, Pearce Ferry) supported sampling. Pre-impoundment, eight native fish species occupied Grand Canyon. Three of these (Colorado squawfish, roundtail chub, bonytail) are extirpated from the canyon; humpback chub and razorback sucker are federally endangered.

Seven gear types were used, yielding 144 sample sets (Table 4): electrofishing (21), gill nets (28), trammel nets (65), hoop nets (5), minnow traps (13), seines (11) and angling (1). Trammel nets (including the sweeping trammel net) were the most numerous.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish community.** Common carp and channel catfish dominated the mainstem. Red shiner, fathead minnow and mosquitofish dominated tributaries, notably Spencer, Surprise, Lost Creek and Quartermaster canyons. Many introduced species were stocked as sport fish or forage after the reservoirs formed (Hoover Dam 1935; Glen Canyon Dam 1963). Lake-dwelling species (threadfin shad, black crappie, walleye, striped bass) appear in the inflow largely as migrants or from the lake.

**Water temperature.** Mainstem maxima ranged from 17.0 °C at Travertine Canyon to 20.0 °C at Grand Wash Cliffs; the Pearce Ferry surface reached 24.5–26.0 °C, attributed to lake impoundment. Tributary temperatures were higher: Spencer Creek 28.5–29.0 °C and Surprise Creek 26.5 °C.

**Stage change.** Interim flow fluctuations were measured at 60 cm (Spencer Canyon, over 12 hours), 54 cm (near Lost Creek) and 19 cm (Quartermaster Canyon, attributed to steady weekend releases). Unreliable 35 cm data at Travertine came from a surging eddy.

**Drift.** Thirty drift samples were collected (12 at Spencer Canyon, 12 near Lost Creek, 6 at Quartermaster), using 560-micron mesh nets. Drift analysis was not reported in this trip.

**Algae.** [[Cladophora Glomerata]] clogging limited gill and trammel net sets to 4–6 hours before cleaning.

**Parasitism.** Channel catfish near Spencer Canyon carried large masses of tapeworms, possibly the Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*).

## Key Findings

**Fish composition and abundance**
- 16 species, 9 families; 2 native (speckled dace, flannelmouth sucker), 14 non-native.
- Most abundant captures: fathead minnow (1,572), red shiner (1,474), common carp (119), channel catfish (45), mosquitofish (76), flannelmouth sucker (16), striped bass (13), threadfin shad (13), largemouth bass (6), plains killifish (5).
- Single captures: speckled dace (1), black bullhead, yellow bullhead, walleye, green sunfish, black crappie.
- Electrofishing, gill and trammel nets were most effective on large and medium fish; seines and minnow traps on small fish; live-bait angling caught striped bass and channel catfish.

**Native fishes**
- [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were not captured. Stratum A and D habitats were judged suitable for both.
- Flannelmouth sucker: only two adults in the mainstem, but numerous young-of-the-year in Spencer and Surprise canyons, suggesting local recruitment.
- Bluehead sucker was not found but is expected in Stratum A.

**Non-native predators and competitors**
- Striped bass (13 captured, all small, maturing males) and walleye (one specimen containing a threadfin shad) were noted as potential predators.
- Red shiners were most numerous in tributary lower reaches (lowest 300 m); their upstream limit remains undetermined.
- Spawning red shiners were observed in Spencer Creek.

**Management implications.** The report recommends extending each trip from 9 to 13 days, intensive seining for red shiners, 300-ft large-mesh gill nets for striped bass and flannelmouth sucker, permanent sampling stations in each stratum, and identification of the catfish tapeworm. It also recommends sampling Lost Creek slough for razorback suckers in early spring and monitoring YOY suckers in Spencer and Surprise canyons.

**Data status.** Hydrolab water-quality data were not yet available and were deferred to the Annual Report. Drift samples were still to be sorted and reported as dry weight per 100 m³.
