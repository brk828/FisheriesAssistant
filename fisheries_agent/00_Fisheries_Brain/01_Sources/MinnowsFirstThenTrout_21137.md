# Minnows First, Then Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #systematics #cladistics #Ostariophysi #Cypriniformes #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by C. Lavett Smith (American Museum of Natural History) is a methodological/taxonomic essay rather than a field or morphometric study. It argues that classifications of freshwater fishes—specifically the ordering of [[Minnows]] (Cyprinidae), [[Suckers]] (Catostomidae), and [[Catfishes]] (Ictaluridae) relative to [[Trout]] and [[Salmon]] (Salmonidae)—should be based on rigorous [[Cladistics]] rather than traditional, subjectively "primitive-to-advanced" arrangements. Smith defends his decision in *Inland Fishes of New York State* (1986) to list the [[Ostariophysi]] (minnows, suckers, catfishes) before the [[Salmonidae]], contrary to the sequence used by the American Fisheries Society's *List of Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada* (Robins et al. 1980). The paper traces the history of higher fish classification (Gill, Jordan and Evermann, Greenwood/Rosen/Weitzman/Myers 1966) and explains Hennigian cladistic principles: shared derived characters (synapomorphies), outgroup comparison, and the rejection of overall-similarity-based classification. It concludes with a diagrammatic demonstration that minnows, trout, and perches can be legitimately sequenced in several equivalent orders, but that minnows can never be placed *between* trout and perch in a list, since that would misrepresent the branching (phylogenetic) topology.

## Background & Description

The paper identifies specific **shared derived characters (synapomorphies)** used to resolve the branching relationships among freshwater fish lineages, rather than describing gross external morphology of a single species:

- **[[Weberian Apparatus]]**: A specialized modification of the first four (or five) vertebrae connecting the swim bladder to the inner ear for sound transmission. This complex, unique structure unites [[Minnows]] (Cyprinidae), [[Suckers]] (Catostomidae), and [[Catfishes]] (Ictaluridae) into the single monophyletic group **[[Ostariophysi]]**. Presence of this character alone does not resolve internal relationships within the group.
- **Caudal fin supports**: Presence of paired stegural outgrowths of the first uroneural — a derived feature uniting [[Salmonidae]] and "higher fishes" (e.g., [[Percidae]]), but absent in Ostariophysi.
- **Acellular bone**: Present in parts of the skeleton of salmonids and higher teleosts; used as another synapomorphy separating this lineage from the Ostariophysi.
- **First vertebra articulation**: In salmonids and higher fishes, the first vertebra articulates with three cranial bones (basioccipital + two exoccipitals); in ostariophysans and other "lower" fishes, it articulates only with the unpaired basioccipital.
- **Notochord**: Shared primitive feature uniting [[Lampreys]] and jawed fishes (used as an example of a character that is uninformative at certain taxonomic levels because it is plesiomorphic).
- **Jaws**: A derived character separating all jawed fishes (Gnathostomes) from [[Lampreys]] (Agnatha).
- **Anal spine number in Centrarchidae** (worked example): Black bass and sunfishes have 3 anal spines (primitive condition, confirmed via outgroup comparison with grunts, snappers, groupers); rock bass, fliers, and crappies have 5+ (derived condition).
- **Limbs vs. fins** (worked example of outgroup comparison): Limbs are the derived condition uniting cats and catbirds relative to catfish (which retain fins); confirmed by reference to the outgroup Agnatha (lampreys), which possess fins, not limbs, supporting fins as the primitive vertebrate condition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This paper is a systematics/methodology essay and does not report original ecological, habitat, or life-history data. However, it situates the discussed taxa within the freshwater fish fauna of eastern North America / New York State, explicitly referencing:

- **[[Darters]]** and **[[Bullheads]]**/**[[Madtoms]]** as examples of why like-with-like family-level grouping matters for practical fishery work (identification, life history, habitat requirements, population management).
- The **[[Ostariophysi]]** (minnows, suckers, catfishes) and **[[Salmonidae]]** (trouts, salmons) as co-occurring freshwater lineages in northeastern U.S. waters whose relative phylogenetic placement affects how field guides and checklists should be organized for practical comparative use by fishery biologists.
- Broader unresolved questions flagged by the author as relevant to fish classification and, by extension, ecological grouping: relationships of pikes/pickerels to salmonids, nearest relatives of sculpins, monophyly of topminnows (one vs. two families), and nearest relatives of suckers.

## Key Findings

- **Cladistics (Hennig 1966)** provides a rigorous alternative to classifications based on "overall similarity," which historically produced inconsistent results among different workers using the same data.
- Only **shared derived characters (synapomorphies)** are informative for classification; primitive characters and autapomorphies (unique derived features in a single lineage) are not useful for resolving relationships.
- A character's status as "derived" or "primitive" is relative to the taxonomic level being examined (e.g., possession of a backbone is derived for uniting vertebrates but uninformative/primitive when classifying catfish vs. cats vs. catbirds).
- **Outgroup comparison** is a key technique for determining polarity (which character state is primitive vs. derived), illustrated via centrarchid anal spine counts and tetrapod limbs vs. fish fins.
- The **Weberian apparatus** unambiguously unites [[Ostariophysi]] (minnows, suckers, catfishes) as a monophyletic group.
- The [[Ostariophysi]] are the **sister group** to a lineage including [[Salmonidae]] and other higher teleosts (e.g., [[Percidae]]), based on three synapomorphies of the latter group (caudal fin support structure, acellular bone, tri-partite first-vertebra articulation).
- A simple dichotomous branching diagram does not specify sequence — left-right order of two sister branches is arbitrary and interchangeable (e.g., "minnow-trout-perch" and "trout-perch-minnow" convey identical phylogenetic information).
- **Critical constraint**: a taxon can never be listed *between* two lineages that together form a sister-group pair relative to it. Thus, minnows (Ostariophysi) must be listed either before or after **both** trout and perch as a block — never sandwiched between them — because doing so would misrepresent the branching topology.
- For general reference works (field guides, AFS checklists), Smith recommends listing minnows/suckers/catfishes **before** trouts and salmons because the Ostariophysi possess comparatively fewer specializations (i.e., following a "smallest/least inclusive branch first" convention), consistent with treatments by Rosen (1973), Fink and Weitzman (1982), Lauder and Liem (1983), and J. Nelson (1984), and contrary to Robins et al. (1980, AFS checklist).
- Historical context: Higher-level fish classification was largely stagnant from Jordan and Evermann (1896–1900) until Greenwood, Rosen, Weitzman, and Myers (1966) reignited interest, coinciding with the broader adoption of Hennigian cladistic methodology.
- The classical **Linnaean hierarchy** (kingdom–phylum–class–order–family–genus–species) is predicted to become less emphasized as branching-sequence (cladistic) classifications gain prominence.
- Numerous higher-level relationships remain unresolved as of this paper's writing (1988), including the affinities of pikes/pickerels to salmonids, nearest relatives of sculpins, monophyly of topminnows, and nearest relatives of suckers — indicating continued instability in fish higher classification.
