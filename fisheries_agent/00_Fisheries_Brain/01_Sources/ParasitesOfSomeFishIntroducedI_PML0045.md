# Parasites of Introduced Fishes in Lake Mohave, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined metazoan parasites of six nonnative fish species from [[Lake Mohave]], an impoundment of the [[Colorado River]] in northwestern Arizona. Ninety fish were collected by [[Trammel Netting]] in March 1994. Five of the six species were infected, yielding eight parasitic taxa: seven helminths and one leech. Seven of the eight records were new for Arizona. The authors attribute most parasites to the [[Parent Fish Introduction|introduction of fishes]] from the [[Mississippi River Basin]] around the turn of the century. The most abundant parasite, a nematode of the genus [[Hysterothylacium]], also infected and killed two native [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), showing that introduced parasites can spill over into threatened native fishes.

## Background & Description

Arizona's aquatic habitats hold a depauperate native fish fauna of about 28 freshwater species, plus an equal but growing number of nonindigenous fishes. At least 30 nonnative taxa are naturalized. Before this study, parasite records for these fishes were sparse and mostly incidental to other biological work. Earlier studies had reported parasites of native species such as the [[Razorback Sucker]], Colorado squawfish, woundfin, and endemic trouts, as well as of introduced [[Brown Trout]] and buffalofish.

Lake Mohave formed in the early 1950s when [[Davis Dam]] closed on the Colorado River mainstream. The reservoir is 108 km long, 6.4 km wide at its widest (Cottonwood Basin, where collections were made), and 30.5 m deep at the dam face.

Fish sampled and their total lengths (cm, mean in parentheses):

- [[Rainbow Trout]]: 21-27 (24). Stocked by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service within one week of sampling; none were infected.
- [[Common Carp]]: 39-60 (45)
- [[Channel Catfish]]: 36-57 (46)
- [[Bluegill]]: 13-17 (15)
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: 24-43 (35)
- [[Striped Bass]]: 19-60 (31)

Fish were dissected at a lakeside facility. Parasites were fixed in AFA (alcohol-formalin-acetic acid), whole-mounted, and deposited in the Harold W. Manter Laboratory (HWML) at the University of Nebraska State Museum.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Nearly all recovered taxa have indirect life cycles. Their presence therefore implies intermediate and definitive hosts in the Lower Colorado Basin, including copepods, cladocerans, amphipods, oligochaetes, small fishes, and fish-eating birds such as herons. The parasites that infect channel catfish, bluegill, and largemouth bass also naturally infect the same hosts within their native [[Mississippi River Basin]] range. The likely introduction route was the stocking of these fishes, with their parasites, into the Colorado basin in the late 1800s and early 1900s.

The authors note that parasite diversity in introduced fish assemblages tends to be lower than in native ranges, a pattern first described by Dogiel (1948). Acanthocephalans were conspicuously absent, possibly because of narrow intermediate-host specificity, the lack of native amphipod or isopod intermediate hosts, or host introductions timed outside infective seasons.

Cienega Creek, the site of the topminnow losses, is a thermally fluctuating stream in the Gila River basin. It supports only two other native fishes, the [[Gila Chub]] and the longfin dace. No nonnative fishes are known in the watershed, but unauthorized stockings from outside it were considered a likely route for parasite introduction.

## Key Findings

- **Parasite records:** Eight taxa were found in five of six host species. Seven taxa were new state records for Arizona. The exception was *[[Ornithodiplostomum Ptychocheilus]]*, which had already been reported from Arizona.
- **Cestodes:**
  - *[[Atractolytocestus Huronensis]]*: three gravid worms in two common carp, the first Arizona record. The authors suggest this worm is host-specific to common carp and was introduced with it before 1885.
  - *[[Corallobothrium]] fimbriatum* and *C. giganteum*: coinfected channel catfish. Prevalence and intensity were "rather high." These are new state records.
  - *[[Proteocephalus Ambloplitis]]*: adults in the intestine of largemouth bass and plerocercoids in the liver of bluegill. These are new definitive and intermediate host records for Arizona. The authors suggest the parasite spread with stocked sunfish and bass between 1935 and 1942.
- **Trematodes:**
  - *[[Ornithodiplostomum Ptychocheilus]]*: metacercariae in bluegill, more prevalent and heavier than *[[Posthodiplostomum Minimum]]*.
  - *Posthodiplostomum minimum*: metacercariae in bluegill, the first Arizona record.
- **Nematode:** *Hysterothylacium* sp. third-stage larvae were encysted in large numbers in largemouth bass and channel catfish, and rarely in common carp and striped bass. It was the most heavily infecting parasite in the sample. This is the first published Arizona record.
- **Leech:** *[[Myzobdella Lugubris]]* infected only channel catfish, in moderate numbers, usually around the mouth and paired fins. This is apparently the first Arizona record. In one Mississippi basin locality, channel catfish carried up to 185 leeches on one fish (Amin, 1981).
- **Native fish mortality:** Two female Gila topminnows from Cienega Creek, collected under permit to establish a captive stock, had body cavities infected with *Hysterothylacium* larvae. The infection killed both fish. One worm protruded through the body wall. The source of this infection was not determined.
- **Introduction pathways:** Channel catfish were introduced to the Colorado basin in 1892-1893, and were probably the main vehicle for *Myzobdella* and the cestodes. Striped bass were first stocked in the lower Colorado River in 1969 and became abundant in Lake Mohave after 1983. They are a plausible vector for *Hysterothylacium*.
- **Public and veterinary health:** Some *Hysterothylacium* species can penetrate the mammalian alimentary tract, so the authors flag possible health concerns.
- **Management conclusion:** The authors argue that translocating fishes and their parasites into sparsely populated western waters threatens susceptible native fish populations. Managers should carefully weigh the impacts of transplanting exotic parasites.
- **Dispersal success:** The authors propose that generalist parasites spread most readily. *Proteocephalus ambloplitis* and *Hysterothylacium* appear to be such generalists. Host-specific parasites like *A. huronensis* can still succeed because their hosts disperse widely, and *A. huronensis* is triploid with a parthenogenetic reproductive cycle that can sustain it when hosts are rare.
- **Knowledge gap:** The high rate of new state records (seven of eight) reflects the poor state of knowledge of fish parasites in Arizona. The extent to which these parasites have spread into native fishes remains unevaluated.
