# Final Rule To List the Rio Grande Silvery Minnow as an Endangered Species

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #Rio-Grande

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service published a final rule on July 20, 1994, listing the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus amarus*) as an endangered species under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. This cyprinid fish, historically one of the most abundant and widespread fishes in the [[Rio Grande]] basin, now occupies only approximately 5% of its known historic range. The species currently exists only in a 275 km (170 mi) reach of the middle Rio Grande from [[Cochiti Dam]] downstream to the headwaters of [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]], New Mexico. Primary threats include dewatering and flow regulation for irrigation, channelization, diminished water quality from municipal and agricultural discharges, and competition or predation by introduced non-native fish species. The final determination on critical habitat designation was delayed up to one year to complete required economic analysis.

## Background & Description

The [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] is one of seven species in the genus *Hybognathus* found in the United States. First described by Girard in 1856 from specimens collected near Fort Brown, Cameron County, Texas, the species is a stout silvery minnow with moderately small eyes and a small, slightly oblique mouth. Adults may reach 90 mm (3.5 in) in total length. The dorsal fin is distinctly pointed with the front located slightly closer to the tip of the snout than to the base of the tail. Life color is silver with emerald reflections, the belly is silvery white, fins are plain, and barbels are absent.

Historically, this species was one of the most abundant and widespread fishes in the [[Rio Grande]] basin, occurring from Espanola, New Mexico, to the Gulf of Mexico. It was also found in the [[Pecos River]], a major tributary, from Santa Rosa, New Mexico, downstream to its confluence with the Rio Grande in south Texas. The species has been completely extirpated from the Pecos River and from the Rio Grande downstream of [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]]. Collection data from 1993 indicate the species presently occupies about 5% of its historic range.

The species is recognized as distinct at the species level by the American Fisheries Society. Cook et al. (1992) used starch gel electrophoretic methods to confirm that *H. amarus* is distinct from *H. nuchalis* and *H. placitus*, with which it was previously grouped.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] inhabits the middle Rio Grande, where water flow is controlled by multiple agencies including the Rio Grande Compact Commission, International Boundary and Water Commission, Bureau of Reclamation, and U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Land bordering the river is owned by the Middle Rio Grande Conservancy District, six Native American Pueblos, and various federal and state agencies.

Recent spawning studies reveal that spawning activity occurs during peak spring and early-summer flows. Fertilized eggs drift with the current for approximately 24 hours before hatching. Larval fish continue to drift downstream until swept into calm backwater and edge areas where food is abundant and they can continue to grow. This reproductive strategy makes the species particularly vulnerable to flow modifications.

The species' decline in the [[Pecos River]] is attributed to the introduction of the [[Plains Minnow]] (*H. placitus*), probably from bait bucket releases around 1968. The replacement was complete in less than one decade, with hybridization believed to have occurred. The last known collections of *H. amarus* from the Pecos River were made in 1968 near Roswell, New Mexico.

Between 1987 and 1988, in 50 fish collections made between Bernalillo and [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]], the Rio Grande silvery minnow was the second most abundant species, comprising 18% of total fish collected. From 1989 to 1992, 56 collections were made in the same area and only 3 Rio Grande silvery minnows were collected, representing a dramatic population decline.

## Key Findings

**Habitat Destruction and Modification (Factor A):**
- Annual dewatering of large percentages of habitat due to irrigation diversions threatens the species
- During below-average water years, the river channel may be dry from [[Isleta Diversion Dam]] downstream about 179 km (111 mi) for two months or more
- Six mainstream dams (Elephant Butte completed 1916, plus Cochiti, Angostura, Isleta, San Acacia, and dams on Rio Chama) have modified natural flow regimes
- Dams permit artificial flow regulation, prevent flooding, trap nutrients, alter sediment transport, and create reservoirs favoring non-native fish species
- Over 100,000 individual Kellner jetties occupying more than 2,000 ha (5,900 ac) have been installed since 1951, channelizing the river
- A 97 km (60 mi) long low-flow conveyance channel from San Acacia to Elephant Butte Reservoir, operated since 1958, diverts all flow less than 63 cubic meters per second
- Downstream of Elephant Butte Dam for 325 km (200 mi), the river is fully controlled by reservoir releases; meanders and oxbows have been eliminated
- Water diversions provide irrigation for approximately 248,000 ha (620,000 ac) in Colorado and 24,400 ha (61,000 ac) in New Mexico above the Middle Rio Grande Valley
- Poor water quality near Albuquerque during low flows correlates with low numbers of *H. amarus* and reduced fish community diversity

**Overutilization (Factor B):**
- New Mexico Department of Game and Fish controls scientific taking through permits
- Licensed commercial bait dealers may sell bait minnows only within the drainage where collected
- Recreational collection for bait minnows could occur through misidentification

**Disease and Predation (Factor C):**
- Non-native predatory species introduced include [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*), [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*), [[White Bass]] (*Morone chrysops*), black and brown bullheads (*Ameiurus melas*, *A. nebulosus*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieui*), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- Native predators include [[Rio Grande Chub]] (*Gila pandora*) and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- Confinement during low flow increases susceptibility to parasitic disease, notably the protozoan *Ichthyophthirius multifilis* and copepod *Lernaea*
- Avian and mammalian predation increases when fish are confined to small clear-water pools

**Inadequate Regulatory Mechanisms (Factor D):**
- New Mexico lists *H. amarus* as endangered (Group 2) under the Wildlife Conservation Act, but this only prohibits taking, not habitat destruction
- New Mexico water law does not include provisions for acquisition of instream water rights for fish and wildlife protection
- State regulations allow use of live minnows from other drainages for sport fishing, facilitating spread of non-native species
- Two native Rio Grande fish species have become extinct under existing regulatory framework

**Other Factors (Factor E):**
- Complete replacement of *H. amarus* by introduced *H. placitus* in the Pecos River between 1968 and late 1970s
- Suspected hybridization between the two congeners
- Habitat alteration and flow modification contributed to extirpation

**Conservation Measures:**
- Listing provides Federal protection under the Endangered Species Act
- Section 7(a) requires Federal agencies to ensure actions do not jeopardize the species
- Federal agencies involved include International Boundary and Water Commission, Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Environmental Protection Agency, and Bureau of Indian Affairs
- Prohibitions against take (harass, harm, pursue, hunt, shoot, wound, kill, trap, collect) apply
- Permits may be issued for scientific purposes, species propagation/survival, or incidental take
- Recovery planning process will be initiated
- Critical habitat designation delayed until March 1, 1995, pending completion of economic analysis

**Effective Date:** August 19, 1994

**Public Comment Summary:**
- 40 written comments received; 13 supported listing, 14 opposed, 13 neutral
- Two public hearings held (Albuquerque and Socorro, New Mexico) with 95 total attendees
- Major concerns included water rights, treaty obligations to Mexico (60,000 acre-feet annually), economic impacts, adequacy of scientific data, and effects on agriculture
- Service addressed all concerns, affirming that listing determination based solely on best available scientific and commercial data per Section 4(b)(1)(A) of the Act
