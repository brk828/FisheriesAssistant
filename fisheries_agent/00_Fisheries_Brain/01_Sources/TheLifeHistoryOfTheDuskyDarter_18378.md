# The Life History of the Dusky Darter (*Percina sciera*) in the Embarras River, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey Biological Notes report (No. 69, September 1970, by Lawrence M. Page and Philip W. Smith) documents the [[Life History]] of the [[Dusky Darter]] (*Percina sciera*) in the middle [[Embarras River]], Illinois. Over a 2-year study (August 1967 to July 1969) the authors made 37 collecting trips and secured 300 specimens, supplemented by 526 preserved specimens examined in total. The study covers habitat preference, reproductive cycle, spawning timing, embryonic development, growth, population density and composition, migration, territoriality, diet, and interactions with other organisms. The species is the most abundant darter at the study site, and the report provides some of the first detailed life-history data for the subgenus [[Hadropterus Subgenus]], whose reproductive habits, growth, and demography were previously largely unknown.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described as *Hadropterus scierus* from Bean Blossom Creek, Monroe County, Indiana (Swain 1884). Later reassigned to *Percina* as a subgenus. The species is currently recognized as two subspecies: the northern dusky darter (*P. s. sciera*) and the [[Guadalupe River Dusky Darter]] (*P. s. apristis*), which is endemic to Texas.
- **Subgenus:** *Hadropterus* included *P. nigrofasciata* and *P. sciera* until *P. lenticula* (Richards & Knapp 1964) and *P. aurolineata* (Suttkus & Ramsey 1967) were described.
- **Morphology:** Adults reach 2 to 4 inches (occasionally 5 inches) total length. The body is light olive-green dorsally with black or brown markings and nearly white ventrally. A dark band runs from the snout through the eye and across the opercle, connecting to a broken lateral band of 7 to 12 dusky blotches. A distinctive partially fused bar lies at the caudal fin base. Males have a midventral row of large, strongly toothed scales and a smaller, less rounded genital pore.
- **Distribution:** Ranges from the Elk River of West Virginia and the Tennessee River system southward to the Gulf Coast, and westward to central Texas, southeastern Oklahoma, and southeastern Missouri. In Illinois it is restricted to the Ohio-Wabash drainage and is common only in the Embarras River, the [[Middle Fork of the Vermilion River]], and their tributaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Embarras River is a tributary of the [[Wabash River]] that drains about 2,400 square miles over an approximately 180-mile course. It supports 14 darter species, with *P. sciera* the most abundant. The river is small to medium-sized in the study area: channels were 15 to 30 feet wide at low water, but the floodplain was inundated to nearly 1 mile wide during floods. The principal sites near Greenup had gravel and sand bars, gravelly and sandy raceways, rubble riffles, and silt-bottomed pools.

**Water chemistry** (Illinois State Water Survey data, Camargo, October 1965 to April 1968; average values):

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Total phosphates | 1.2 ppm |
| Nitrates | 25 ppm |
| Sulfates | 69.7 ppm |
| Ammonium compounds | 0.1 ppm |
| Alkalinity | 199 ppm |
| Hardness | 285 ppm |
| Total dissolved minerals | 376 ppm |

Turbidity ranged from 2 to 138 ppm. The authors describe water quality as unusually high with minimal pollution.

**Habitat.** Darters were most often found over gravel bottoms in rapid or fairly rapid current at depths of at least 1 foot, and were rarely found over other substrates or in quiet pools and shallow water. Accumulations of branches and leaves in gravel probably served as hiding or resting sites. Rooted plants were absent from the study area, yet the species was abundant.

**Associated fauna.** A [[Electric Seine Sampling]] quantitative sample of 507 square yards (average depth 1 foot) yielded 750 fishes of 18 species. The minnows *Pimephales vigilax*, *Notropis whipplei*, and *N. spilopterus* made up 78 percent of the total. *Percina sciera* ranked seventh (2.5 percent of fish), and was more than five times as abundant as the second most abundant darter, [[Slenderhead Darter]] (*P. phoxocephala*).

## Key Findings

### Reproduction
- **Males** were usually mature by their first spring. Breeding colors intensified to a peak in late May, when large males became almost uniformly black. Breeding males did not develop tubercles.
- **Females** matured at about 1 year and at roughly 40 mm standard length. Yearlings under 40 mm lacked mature eggs.
- **Ovarian development:** Ova were categorized into three size classes (under 0.5 mm white, 0.6 to 1.0 mm yellow, 1.1 to 1.5 mm dark yellow or orange). Differentiation began in early October, and 64 percent of ova in an April 28 specimen were in the smallest class. Between 500 and 2,000 ova were produced per female per season, but only about 10 percent reached mature size; mature egg counts ranged from 80 to 196.
- **Spawning timing** varied by year, likely with climate and river conditions. In 1967 spawning probably occurred in July. In 1968, high water delayed spawning until about early July. In 1969, peak spawning occurred between May 27 and June 8, with spawning concentrated in shallow (1 to 3 feet) gravel raceways. The authors conclude that June spawning is preferred at this latitude.
- **Mating:** Actual spawning was not observed. Based on morphology and related species, the authors infer that eggs were scattered over gravel, fertilized, and deposited with no elaborate behavior.

### Development and Growth
- Eggs stripped on June 8, 1969 were transparent, spherical, and adhesive, averaging 1.5 mm in diameter with a 0.6 mm oil droplet.
- Embryos developed at 74 to 78°F. Hatching began at 90 hours and was complete by 108 hours. Hatchlings averaged 5.5 mm total length.
- Fry fed on prepared food at 4 days and died on the 6th day in captivity.
- Growth was rapid in the first months of life. Males averaged larger than females in nearly all samples. Average standard lengths were 57.5 mm (females) and 61.4 mm (males) for ages 1 to 2 years, and 62.3 mm (females) and 73.3 mm (males) for ages 2 to 3 years.
- The largest specimen examined was a 108 mm standard length male from the [[Little Wabash River]], collected November 1, 1968, and aged at 4 years (OCR-garbled in the source; reported as "42-year-old" but consistent with the text's "444 years").
- Young fish were pelagic and active, while older fish were typically benthic and quiet.

### Population Characteristics
- **Density:** One dusky darter per 25.3 square yards of the total sampled area, or one per 6.3 square yards of optimal gravelly raceway habitat (about one-fourth of the sampled area).
- **Age composition:** Across all specimens, 82.8 percent were young-of-the-year, 13.7 percent yearlings, 2.7 percent second-year fish, and 0.7 percent third-year fish.
- **Sex ratio:** Young showed a 1:1 ratio (117 males, 124 females in the sample). Among fish aged 2 years or older, 28 of 35 (80 percent) were male, suggesting greater male longevity, although females reach at least 3 years.
- **Population control:** Predation was not documented, so the authors attribute population control to a combination of predation, limited longevity, and the harsh physical habitat, especially fluctuating river levels.

### Migration and Territoriality
- Specimens became scarce in November 1967 and December 1968 despite repeated seining. Catch per effort fell from 31 darters in 20 minutes on October 3, 1967 to 3 darters in 2 hours on November 4, 1967.
- The authors found winter movement out of the channels, spring immigration back in, and fall emigration from tributaries as the only migratory patterns.
- Males are inferred to defend territories because of their pronounced sexual dimorphism. Males were antagonistic toward other males, but no aggression was observed toward females.

### Diet
- Of 108 stomachs examined, all contained only current-dwelling insect larvae: midge larvae and pupae, black fly larvae, caddisfly larvae, snipe fly larvae, and mayfly and stonefly naiads. The average was 12.5 food items per fish.
- Midge and black fly larvae were the principal food. Caddisfly larvae dominated the summer diet and became the main food in fish of 65 to 80 mm standard length. Heaviest feeding occurred in May, before spawning.

### Interactions
- **Competition:** Competition with *P. phoxocephala* for food was suggested but not shown to limit populations, since the species had full stomachs year-round. Habitat partitioning with most other darters reduced competition. The two most abundant darters, *P. sciera* and *P. phoxocephala*, may compete for habitat and breeding space.
- **Predation:** No predation was found on dusky darters. The [[Spotted Bass]] (*Micropterus punctulatus*) was the most abundant large predator, but among 114 bass examined, the only darter remains were three *Etheostoma nigrum*.
- **Parasitism:** A high incidence of the leech [[Piscicolaria Reducta]] was found from June to November: 109 leeches were removed from 249 darters. Mean leech burden was 0.39 per young fish (215 fish), 0.57 per yearling (31 fish), and 2 per 2-year-old (3 fish). No other external parasites were noted.
- **Hybridization:** Natural hybrids were recorded with *P. nigrofasciata* (Pearl River, Louisiana), *P. caprodes* (San Gabriel River, Texas), and an intergeneric hybrid with *Etheostoma spectabile* (San Marcos River, Texas). Laboratory intergeneric and interspecific crosses have also been produced.

### Phylogenetic Notes
- Within *Hadropterus*, *P. sciera* and *P. aurolineata* are the most morphologically similar. The gas bladder is relatively large in *P. sciera* (about 16 percent of standard length in three specimens), suggesting limited phyletic advancement. The authors place *Hadropterus* closer to primitive subgenera.
