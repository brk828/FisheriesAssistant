# Sonoran Topminnow Habitat Restoration and Reintroduction at Middle Spring (S2), Bylas Springs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This proposal (28 May 1996) from the [[Arizona Fishery Resources Offices]], the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] and the [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]] seeks to restore the spring system of S2 at [[Bylas Springs]] and reintroduce the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]], also called the Gila topminnow. The S2 population disappeared in 1990 after surface water depletion, increased vegetation after [[Livestock Exclusion]], increased evapotranspiration and sediment infilling. The plan combines removal of fencing, vegetation and [[Salt Cedar]], construction of a series of cement troughs that act as secondary [[Fish Barrier]]s, transplanting [[Yerba Mansa]], reintroduction of S2-lineage fish from [[Hassayampa Refuge]], and one year of monthly monitoring. A supporting memorandum from local elders (April 1996) provides historical descriptions of the springs.

## Background & Description

**Species.** The Sonoran (Gila) topminnow, *Poeciliopsis occidentalis*, is endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico and to the Concepción and Sonoran basins of Sonora, Mexico. It is a small poeciliid fish that was once abundant in the lower Colorado River watershed. It was listed as an endangered species in 1967 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Population declines in the 1960s were attributed to predation by the introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and to habitat degradation in the Gila River basin.

**Study system.** The Bylas Springs complex is a series of three small springs (S1, S2 and S3, labeled west to east) adjacent to the Gila River on the San Carlos Apache Reservation. All three historically contained Gila topminnow. By the 1980s, mosquitofish had invaded S1 and S3. Between 1983 and 1984, fish barriers and cement structures were built to exclude mosquitofish and livestock. Later renovations removed mosquitofish from S1 and S3. S2 held only Gila topminnow in surveys from 1986 to 1989. In November 1987 and September 1988, two lots of S2 fish were stocked into the Upper and Lower Headquarters ponds at [[Roper Lake State Park]].

**Cultural and historical context.** The [[Elders' Cultural Advisory Council]] described S2, "Mesquite Round," as historically having large mesquites, increased surface flow, little underbrush and stream banks lined with yerba mansa, with few or no rushes. Their account of a more open system matches habitat-use data from recent studies at S1 and S3 and from the closely related [[Yaqui Topminnow]]. In those studies, Gila topminnow used shallow pools along the stream edge and deeply incised spring heads, and relatively high disturbance maintained each habitat type.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** The loss of S2 was linked to reduced surface water, dense vegetation after livestock exclusion, increased evapotranspiration, and sediment and debris that dried the system (Marsh and Minckley 1990).
- **Invasive species:** Mosquitofish predation and competition replaced native fish, and [[Salt Cedar]] (*Tamarix pentandra* in the proposal) was absent from the spring until about 1934 or 1935, according to elders.
- **Livestock:** The springs once watered many cattle. Elders reported that cattle and horses used the area and that children sometimes filled the spring with dirt.
- **Historical vegetation:** Elders described large mesquites, "Yerba Mansa" (*Anemopsis californica*) along stream banks, and little underbrush at S2 before disturbance. Some large mesquites were later cut.
- **Water quality and flow:** Monitoring was to measure dissolved oxygen, conductivity, discharge and temperature, with a continuous thermograph at S2.

## Key Findings

**Project objectives (S2 renovation and reintroduction):**
1. Remove fencing around the existing fish barrier.
2. Remove vegetation and silt upstream of the fish barrier.
3. Deepen and reinforce the footing on the downstream side of the fish barrier.
4. Remove introduced plant species, especially salt cedar, from the system.
5. Install a series of four cement troughs (6 ft × 3 ft × 2 ft each) below the spring head, with the most downstream trough serving as a secondary fish barrier.
6. Transplant yerba mansa from disturbed areas to other sections of the stream course.
7. Reintroduce S2-lineage Gila topminnow from Hassayampa Refuge.
8. Monitor the response of the S2 system to the treatment.

**Methods:**
- Fencing is removed manually. A backhoe removes vegetation and sediment immediately upstream of the fish barrier. Trough sites are prepared with hand tools, and the backhoe sets the troughs. The fourth trough includes a drop structure and splash pool to act as an additional barrier and a water source for wildlife.
- Salt cedar is cut and debris is removed. Stumps are treated with the herbicide [[Rodeo Herbicide]] immediately after cutting. The proposal states the herbicide binds to soil particles, does not leach into groundwater, breaks down in about 7 to 8 days, and that its active ingredient is classified by the EPA as noncarcinogenic in humans.
- Arizona Game and Fish Department personnel collect more than 130 Gila topminnow (n > 130) from existing refugia sites, mainly at Hassayampa Refuge. All fish are placed in aquaria for inspection by fishery biologists before transport and release into S2.

**Monitoring:**
- Monthly sampling for the first year after renovation and reintroduction.
- Three permanent sites: at the spring head, in the area of the trough series, and immediately upstream of the original fish barrier.
- A continuous thermograph at the S2 stream permanent locality to record hourly temperatures through the first year.
- Dissolved oxygen, conductivity and discharge measured before the project begins.

**Deliverables and reporting:**
- The principal investigators give the Bureau of Reclamation and other partners a written summary of the renovation and reintroduction, due within 60 days after the work.
- At the end of the one-year monitoring period, a written report is due covering the project's successes and failures, the status of the Gila topminnow population in S2, and recommendations for further work at this and other springs. (Due dates are partly illegible in the source.)

**Budget (renovation only):**
- Labor: 3 laborers at $40/day for 3 days = $360.
- Equipment and supplies: 4 cement troughs at $300 each = $1,200; backhoe transport and operation for 3 days; portable cement mixer rental, cement, rebar and fencing.
- The source shows a total of about $4,000, but the line items are partly illegible and the budget subtotals do not clearly reconcile.

**Elders' memorandum (29 April 1996):**
- Spring 1 ("Hot Spring") once had a large area of standing water above the spring, which is now dry, and was surrounded by grass and large mesquites.
- Spring 2 ("Mesquite Round") had much more water, which ran along a stream of similar size to today's. Only yerba mansa grew along its banks, and there were no rushes.
- Salt cedar appeared around 1934 or 1935. After that, the spring dried for an unknown period, and its flow later increased.
- Elders asked that yerba mansa be replaced where possible and that the banks not be disturbed during cleanup, and they declined to give an opinion on fencing.
