# Toxaphene as a Fish Toxicant

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Commission report, written by [[Jack E. Hemphill]], Chief of Fisheries, evaluates [[Toxaphene]], a chlorinated camphene insecticide, as a [[Piscicide]] for controlling unwanted "rough fish" in lakes and reservoirs. The work began after fish kills in Arizona fish ponds were linked to insecticide drift from agricultural crop dusting. Field trials at [[Lyman Reservoir]] (1951) and other waters showed that toxaphene could kill most rough fish, especially [[Common Carp]], while leaving some game species largely unharmed. The report also discusses the chemical's cost, safety for personnel and livestock, and its limitations in flowing streams and domestic water supplies.

## Background & Description

**Origin of the investigation.** Several fish kills occurred in Arizona farm fish ponds after nearby crops were dusted with insecticides. Ponds that were downwind of treated fields, or that received wind-drifted material, often suffered partial or total losses of fish. Populations of rough fish were most severely affected. Chemical analysis identified toxaphene as the insecticide responsible. Reports from fisheries workers elsewhere prompted a formal test of toxaphene as a possible tool for [[Rough Fish Control]].

**Laboratory benchmark.** Laboratory experiments showed that a concentration of 0.1 ppm produced a total kill of carp at 69°F in 12 hours. Carp fry from Apache County, Arizona, were selected for field trials at this concentration.

**Chemical profile.** Toxaphene is a chlorinated camphene compound made by the Hercules Powder Company. It is a yellowish waxy solid with a sweet odor, is essentially insoluble in water, and has a specific gravity of about 1.6. It is locally irritating and can be absorbed through the skin. The report gives an estimated fatal oral dose, but the figure is garbled in the scan and is not reliably recoverable here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water conditions.** Conditions at the time of the first treatment at Lyman Reservoir were:

- Surface water temperature: 70°F
- Maximum depth: 12 feet, with bottom temperature of 69°F
- pH: 8.6
- Total dissolved solids: 820 ppm
- [[Secchi Disk]] reading: 6 inches
- Surface area: reported as 88 acres (OCR-uncertain)
- Total volume: reported as about 385 acre-feet (OCR-uncertain)

**Fate of the toxicant.** Toxaphene breaks down under alkaline conditions, and higher alkalinity speeds dissipation. Waters with a pH of 8.0 or greater were found safe for restocking with desirable fish within four weeks. Acidic waters are expected to stay toxic much longer. The compound settles rapidly to the bottom when applied as a dust, which limits its effectiveness in deep water.

**Recovery and ecosystem response.** Restocking after treatment was tied to the [[Zooplankton]] bloom that follows fish removal. Fertilization from decaying carcasses boosts zooplankton, so stocking was timed to take advantage of this food supply. The appearance of zooplankton was also used as an indicator that toxaphene had dissipated. A plankton bloom followed the control measures at Lyman Reservoir and persisted through warm months.

**Fish behavior.** Fish appear to detect toxaphene on contact. Individual fish in treated areas show little activity, while untreated areas keep normal numbers, so the chemical's effect on fish behavior is not uniform across a lake.

## Key Findings

### 1. Lyman Reservoir Field Trial (August 1951)

- Lyman Reservoir holds 32,000 acre-feet and covers 1,800 surface acres when full. Total fish biomass was above 340 pounds per surface acre.
- Application was by mixed methods: part of the lake was treated with an emulsified solution, and the rest with toxaphene dust (introduced into the propeller wash of an outboard motor) or with an emulsion sprayed on the surface.
- Dosage was about 7 pounds of 40% toxaphene per acre-foot to reach 0.1 ppm (OCR-uncertain).
- Fish began to die within 12 hours, and the kill continued for 48 hours. The estimated losses were:
  - About 220 tons of carp
  - About 4,000 pounds of [[Bonytail]]
  - 29 [[Largemouth Bass]], 31 [[Bluegill]], 17 [[Brown Trout]]
  - Several hundred bullhead catfish
- A heavy rain two days after treatment added 800 acre-feet of water and largely stopped the die-off. When the lake was drained, the recovered fish included about 2,000 large carp, 6,000 bonytail, 112 largemouth bass averaging six pounds each, and 192 [[Channel Catfish]] weighing three to 14 pounds.
- No channel catfish were observed dying during the toxic period, but later treatments at higher concentrations were very effective against that species.
- The Secchi reading dropped from six inches to four feet after treatment.

### 2. Beckers Lake Spring Treatment (October, year illegible)

- The lake, a spring-fed water body near Springerville, was treated at 0.1 ppm toxaphene.
- Carp began to die within a few days, and a large number of carp and roughly 50 pounds of golden shiners were recovered in the first treatment.
- Carp continued to die for eleven days, and the dead fish could not all be collected.
- Gill nets and seines showed that the lake was clear of rough fish after two years of treatment.
- Twenty-five thousand bass were planted after the lake was cleared.

### 3. San Carlos Reservoir Experiment (Gila River)

- The experimental treatment destroyed about 800 tons of an estimated 1,100-ton carp population.
- It also killed about two million crappie, a large but unestimated number of [[Yellow Bass]], and a small percentage of the channel catfish.
- Results indicated that partial population control in large lakes may be achieved at a cost of about $0.05 per acre-foot treated.
- The findings support earlier work by Lawrence (F.F.C. 12(13)) on large reservoirs. The key limitation is that the lake must have no supply of fresh water available for distressed fish to migrate into.

### 4. Economics

- Toxaphene's cost increased as production rose. In the source, the price f.o.b. Phoenix is given as $1.65 for 40% toxaphene (the unit is likely per pound, but OCR-uncertain).
- Material cost at 0.1 ppm is given as $0.165 per acre-foot treated.

### 5. Safety and Handling

- Toxaphene proved no more dangerous than common [[Piscicide]]s such as sodium arsenite and copper sulfate. Standard precautions apply.
- Personnel must avoid inhalation and skin contact, and should wear gloves and a respirator.
- Containers should be burned or buried. Toxaphene breaks down at a low temperature (about 190°F) into nontoxic substances, so fish killed by it can be used for human consumption.
- Livestock watering from treated water (at 0.1 ppm) was found to produce toxic symptoms in cattle and horses. Animals were kept away from treated water for 60 days after treatment, until toxic symptoms disappeared.

### 6. Limitations

1. Water used for domestic purposes must not be treated.
2. Streams and lakes with large inflows will yield only partial kills.
3. Attempts in flowing streams were largely unsuccessful because of the chemical's slow action.
4. Deep lakes have not been treated, so the effectiveness of toxaphene there is unknown, since the dust settles quickly to the bottom.

### 7. Conclusions

Toxaphene can serve as an effective, relatively inexpensive tool for reducing rough fish in large, shallow lakes and reservoirs, provided that the water is alkaline and that the stocking and recovery schedule accounts for the zooplankton response. It is not suitable for streams, domestic water sources, or deep lakes without further study.
