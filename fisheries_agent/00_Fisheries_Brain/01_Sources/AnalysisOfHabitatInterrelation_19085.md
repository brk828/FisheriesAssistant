# Habitat Interrelations Between Spikedace and Red Shiner

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This first annual report (1987–88) from the [[Arizona State University]] Department of Zoology, prepared for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], evaluates how the introduced [[Red Shiner]] interacts with two threatened native minnows of the [[Gila River Basin]]: the [[Spikedace]] and the [[Loach Minnow]]. The study combined field sampling of five stream systems with habitat measurements and statistical comparisons (ANOVA) of occupied versus available habitat. The authors emphasize that the nature of interactions between red shiner and the native fishes had received little quantitative attention, and that management could not proceed without understanding these relationships.

The main results are that spikedace selects significantly slower current velocities than are available, that its habitat selection shifts when it occurs with red shiner (in syntopy), and that red shiner tends to occupy deeper, slower water when alone. The authors conclude that displacement of spikedace by red shiner through species interactions is suggested by the data but not demonstrated.

## Background & Description

**Study species**

- **Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)** and **Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)** are small minnows endemic to the Gila River Basin. Both were listed as threatened by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS 1986a, b) and are protected in Arizona and New Mexico. The spikedace is relatively well known, whereas the loach minnow is less well known because of its restricted distribution and cryptic nature. Draft recovery plans for both species were previously prepared (USFWS 1986a, b).
- **Red Shiner (*Notropis lutrensis*)** is native to the Rio Grande/Mississippi drainage complex of eastern North America. It was introduced to the desert Southwest in the early 1950s and has since colonized many low-elevation streams of the region, including many originally occupied by spikedace and loach minnow.

**Background problem**

Interactions between native and non-native fishes have long been implicated in the decline of native species (Miller 1961; Minckley and Deacon 1968). Predation is a direct negative interaction, while competition and other subtle interactions may also reduce and eliminate local populations. Minckley and Deacon (1968) noted progressive declines in the two native minnows as red shiner spread through the Gila River basin. Red shiner had recently appeared upstream in New Mexico, where substantial, unaltered populations of both native species persisted. Although circumstantial evidence pointed to red shiner in the decline of the native minnows, its specific mechanism had not been demonstrated.

**Objectives**

The contracting agencies (USFWS, Arizona Game and Fish Department, and New Mexico Department of Game and Fish) requested a multi-faceted investigation to evaluate red shiner–spikedace/loach minnow relationships. The study included field studies to quantify habitat occupation of the native species when allopatric and syntopic with red shiner, and dietary analysis of potential interactions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites**

Five stream systems were sampled, with one to three sites per reach:

1. **Upper (east) and lower (west) [[Aravaipa Creek]]**, Graham and Pinal counties, Arizona (spikedace and loach minnow).
2. **[[Sycamore Creek]]**, Maricopa County, Arizona (red shiner only, isolated from spikedace).
3. **[[Verde River]]**, Yavapai County, Arizona (spikedace and red shiner in syntopy).
4. **[[Blue River]]**, Greenlee County, Arizona (loach minnow).
5. **[[Gila River]] at Gila and Redrock**, Catron County, New Mexico (spikedace and loach minnow, plus red shiner at Gila-Redrock).

**Sampling methods**

- **Seining:** Standardized hauls used a 2.0 × 1.2 m, 0.92 cm mesh seine to sample consistent surface area (10 m reaches). Seining was ineffective for loach minnow on riffles at Aravaipa, Gila-Gila, and Blue River, so these fish were collected by electrofishing.
- **Electrofishing:** A backpack-style unit (115 VAC, 900 W gasoline generator, stainless steel electrodes on hand-held fiberglass probes) was used within 1-m sections (2.0 m wide) with 0.32 cm mesh netting. Distribution of effort was allocated in proportion to estimated area of available microhabitat types.
- **Habitat measurements:** Current velocity was measured at five points per sampled area; depth was recorded; cover (macrophytes, algae, woody debris, canyon walls, etc.) and substrate (silt, sand, gravel, cobble, boulder, bedrock, organic material) were scored.
- **Specimen handling:** Fishes were identified, counted, and classified by life stage (larva, juvenile, adult). Specimens were released or preserved in 10% formalin for later measurement (length, weight, sex) and dietary analysis.

**Statistical approach**

Environmental variables were tested for normality (log-transformed where needed) and variance homogeneity, then compared using unweighted ANOVA among streams. A second set of weighted ANOVAs weighted each sample by the number of individuals of the target species, so that habitat "preferred" by each species within a reach was emphasized. Where both species occurred together, the weight reflected the combined abundance of both fishes.

**Catch context**

Larval, juvenile and adult fishes were collected at each stream (sample sizes given as n = 100 for lower Aravaipa in May 1987, upper Aravaipa in August 1987, and Verde River in May 1987; n = 230 for Blue River in June 1987; and n = 240 for Gila River at Gila in June 1987). Catch tables list native and non-native species across all five systems, including spikedace, loach minnow, red shiner, longfin dace, speckled dace, desert sucker, and roundtail chub.

## Key Findings

**1. Spikedace habitat selection**

- Mean depth, velocity, and substrate for seine hauls without spikedace were statistically indistinguishable from the total sample. Hauls containing spikedace, however, occupied current velocities that were significantly slower, and substrates significantly smaller, than average (Table 3).
- Within individual streams, spikedace at West Aravaipa showed no significant preference for a particular velocity. At East Aravaipa (where mean current was significantly faster than at the other reaches) and Gila-Gila, spikedace occupied significantly slower currents than were available.
- Spikedace actively selected water of variable depth but with significantly slower-than-average velocities. Smaller substrate sizes may also play a role, but current velocity was the most demonstrative variable.

**2. Red shiner habitat in isolation**

- At Sycamore Creek, where red shiner occurred without spikedace, it was collected in significantly deeper and slower water than the available average (Table 5). This suggests a propensity for slower, deeper habitats, though it is based on a single system.

**3. Red shiner habitat in syntopy**

- In streams where red shiner co-occurred with spikedace (Verde and Gila-Redrock), it occupied water of variable depth but slower current speeds, over smaller substrates (Table 7).
- Red shiner in syntopy with spikedace occupied slower water velocities, similar to those used when in isolation.

**4. Shift in spikedace habitat with red shiner**

- Habitat preference of spikedace differed significantly between isolated streams (West Aravaipa/Gila-Gila) and streams where it co-occurred with red shiner (Verde/Gila-Redrock), particularly for current velocity (Table 7).
- Spikedace in syntopy with red shiner selected significantly faster currents than when in isolation. The authors note this shift may reflect displacement due to species interactions, but this is not demonstrated.

**5. Overall interpretation**

- The data are consistent with previous indications that spikedace selects slower currents. The authors conclude that some alteration of spikedace habitat by red shiner through species interactions is suggested, but the data do not confirm displacement or the mechanism of decline.
- The report is a first-year progress document. Dietary analysis and further study of interactions were planned for subsequent work.
