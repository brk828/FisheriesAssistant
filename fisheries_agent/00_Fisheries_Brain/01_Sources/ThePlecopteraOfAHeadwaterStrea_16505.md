# The Plecoptera of a Headwater Stream: Gaitscale Gill, English Lake District

#plecoptera #freshwater-ecology #stoneflies #aquatic-insects

## Executive Summary

This study examines the stonefly (Plecoptera) community of [[Gaitscale Gill]], a short, cascading, high-elevation tributary of the [[River Duddon]] in the English [[Lake District]]. The stream was chosen because it rises at high altitude, descends rapidly and is short, which was expected to reveal how benthic invertebrates are distributed with altitude. Sampling ran monthly from September 1965 to July 1966 at several stations. Stoneflies dominated the fauna. Eleven species were recorded, and [[Ephemeroptera]] and [[Gammarus]] were conspicuously absent. The author concludes that [[Temperature]] and/or food are the principal factors shaping the distribution and abundance of Plecoptera in the streams studied, and that altitude strongly influences species life cycles.

## Background & Description

The relationship between altitude and aquatic insect distribution has long been recognized, and temperature is generally considered the main driver of altitudinal zonation. The mechanisms, however, had not been demonstrated, so the author framed the work as a step toward testing them. Gaitscale Gill was studied as part of a wider invertebrate survey of the Duddon, with a complete faunal list reserved for a companion publication.

The stream lies on the south-facing slope of Ulpha Fell, rising in bog pools near Crinkle Crags and descending about 2.2 km to the Duddon Valley. It is 15 cm to 1 m wide and 2.5 to 10 cm deep under normal conditions, with mean discharge near the mouth (station 21) of about 0.05 m³/sec. The substratum is mainly bedrock and medium boulders. Dead grasses and bracken collect in debris packets beneath stones, and many invertebrates congregate there. The uppermost station (24), just below the outflow of the lowest bog pool, differs markedly: closely packed broken stones, almost imperceptible flow and abundant fine peat.

Plecoptera were chosen for special attention because they are the predominant group and their taxonomy is well known, following [[Hynes 1941 Survey]] and [[Hynes 1958 Key]].

### Methods

- **Sampling:** Stations 20, 21 and 23 in Gaitscale Gill and station 18 in the Duddon were sampled monthly from September 1965 to July 1966. No collections were made at station 23 in January, or at any station in February, because of ice and snow. Stations 22 and 24 were sampled less often.
- **Timed collections:** 5-minute samples in September to November and 2-minute samples thereafter, all expressed as numbers per 2-minute sample. A long-handled net with 265 µ mesh and a 25 cm square frame was used, with the bottom disturbed by kicking.
- **Measurement:** Total body length (excluding cerci) was measured to the nearest 0.5 mm.
- **Emergence:** A "pyramid" trap was set at station 23 and a floating box trap at station 21, both from 8 March to 19 July 1966. Trap losses were substantial; at station 21 the net was torn away several times.
- **Temperature:** Mercury thermometers were read on each visit, and maximum–minimum thermometers were buried at stations 18, 20 and 23 from January onward. Current velocity was measured with an Ott current meter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** Station 18 on the Duddon is about 3.3 m wide, averages 28 cm deep and carries about 0.4 m³/sec. Table 1 records water temperatures at station 21 from 0.0 to 14.1 °C over the year.

**Altitudinal gradient.** The principal contrast is between the high station 23 (640 m) and the low station 20 (260 m). Station 24 sits even higher, at 732 m. This gradient underlies the paper's comparison of life histories.

**Basin structure.** The Duddon drainage divides into two faunal basins. The upper basin is dominated by Plecoptera, with Ephemeroptera and Gammarus rare. The lower basin has predominant Ephemeroptera, with Plecoptera and Gammarus common. Station 13 marks the dividing point, and Gaitscale Gill lies in the upper basin, joining the Duddon between stations 18 and 19.

**Radial drainage.** Gaitscale Gill is one of several streams in a [[Radial Drainage]] pattern in the central Cumbrian Mountains. Because these streams share similar environmental characteristics, they provide a natural basis for comparative work.

**Comparative sites.** Eleven headwater streams in the same radial system were sampled in late March to early April 1966 with 1-minute, coarse-mesh collections. Ford Wood Beck, a warm lowland stream, provides a contrasting fauna.

## Key Findings

### Species composition and abundance
- Eleven Plecoptera species were collected from Gaitscale Gill, about half of the 25 species previously recorded in the Lake District.
- **Abundant:** [[Leuctra Inermis]], [[Amphinemura Sulcicollis]] and [[Capnia Vidua]].
- **Common:** [[Leuctra Hippopus]], [[Protonemura Meyeri]], [[Chloroperla Torrentium]] and [[Brachyptera Risi]].
- Total numbers in Table 2 (stations 23, 21, 20): *L. inermis* 2000, 1913, 1856; *C. vidua* 1826, 59, 34; *L. hippopus* 402, 109, 84; *A. sulcicollis* 7, 841, 854; *P. meyeri* 19, 221, 129.
- *L. inermis* was the most abundant and most evenly distributed species. Rare taxa (*Diura bicaudata*, *Nemurella picteti*, *Leuctra nigra*, *Nemoura cambrica*) were absent from one or more sites.

### Spatial distribution
- Station 24 held the most dissimilar fauna, dominated by *Capnia vidua* and *Nemoura cambrica*, with only four species present.
- *Capnia vidua* and *L. hippopus* kept the same rank relative to each other downstream to station 20, where the order reversed. *C. vidua* was absent at mainstream station 18.
- *A. sulcicollis* exceeded 10% of the Plecoptera at stations 20 and 21. *P. meyeri* was significant only at station 21.

### Life histories
- Most Gaitscale Gill stoneflies grow during cool months, emerge and complete their flight by August. Hatching continues from autumn into winter, producing the progressive rise in numbers.
- *Capnia vidua*: recruitment September to December, no recruitment after December, adults collected March to May, and no adults or nymphs in summer.
- *L. inermis*: first adults in April at both stations. The maximum size was 7.0 mm at station 23 and 8.0 mm at station 20. Recruitment ended about a month earlier at the lower site.
- *L. hippopus*: hatching did not occur at either station before August.
- Life-cycle patterns differed with altitude. The eight species most abundant at higher altitudes persisted longer at station 23 than at station 20.

### Distribution across the Duddon drainage
- Only *L. inermis* and *L. hippopus* occurred throughout the drainage. *A. sulcicollis*, *P. meyeri* and *C. torrentium* were widely distributed.
- Seven species found elsewhere in the Duddon were absent from Gaitscale Gill. All but two were confined to the Lower Duddon.
- *Capnia vidua* was the only species restricted primarily to Gaitscale Gill.
- The July collections were depleted. Only *L. inermis*, *A. sulcicollis*, *P. meyeri* and *C. torrentium* exceeded 10 specimens each.

### Headwater streams
- All species occurring regularly in Gaitscale Gill were also found in the headwater streams, but none of those streams matched Gaitscale Gill's richness.
- Lingcove Beck held 7 of the 11 species. The Upper River Esk, Mosedale Beck and Rossett Gill held 6 each.
- *Leuctra inermis* was present in all 11 headwater streams. *Isoperla grammatica* was the only species absent from Gaitscale Gill but found in five headwater streams.
- Gammarus and Ephemeroptera were absent from all sites except three.

### Comparison with Ford Wood Beck
- Ford Wood Beck, a warm lowland stream, yielded 16 species against 11 at Gaitscale Gill.
- Six of the eight species found only at Ford Wood Beck were restricted to the Lower Duddon basin. Three species occurred only at Gaitscale Gill.
- *Nemoura cambrica* made up 45.1% of the Ford Wood Beck total and under 1% at Gaitscale Gill. *Leuctra inermis* made up 56.3% at Gaitscale Gill and 7.3% at Ford Wood Beck. *Capnia vidua* made up 13.9% at Gaitscale Gill.
- The author attributes these differences most likely to temperature and food.

### Interpretation
- **Temperature:** Predominance of stoneflies over Ephemeroptera, the restriction of cold-water stenotherms such as *Capnia vidua* (a boreo-alpine species) to Gaitscale Gill, and the absence of summer-growing forms all point to temperature as a key driver.
- **Other factors:** The presence of *N. cambrica* at station 24 alone, and the absence of high-altitude forms such as *Chloroperla tripunctata*, *Perlodes microcephala* and *Protonemura praecox*, indicate that food, substratum and flow also matter.
- **Emergence:** Emergence was later in the Upper Duddon for several species, consistent with temperature control. [[Exuiorr 1967]] found *C. torrentium* nymphs late into summer after a severe winter, showing that their absence in other summers was not caused by lethal high temperatures.
- **Drift:** Drift is a major factor in seasonal abundance, and the author notes that drift, recruitment and mortality could all contribute to irregularities in population patterns.

### Conclusions
The author concludes that the principal factors affecting the distribution and abundance of Plecoptera in these streams are probably temperature and/or food. The community composition and dynamics of Gaitscale Gill appear similar to those of other local headwater streams, and possibly of cool-temperate headwater streams over a wider area. Because the headwater collections were limited, further sampling is needed before firm generalizations can be made.
