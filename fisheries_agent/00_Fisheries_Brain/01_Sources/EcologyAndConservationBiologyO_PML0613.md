# Ecology and Conservation Biology of Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Bureau of Reclamation contract 1-FC-40-1040, dated March 31, 1996; Arizona State University) compiles five years of research on endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and three other native catostomids in the Grand Canyon region of Arizona. It addresses population size, seasonal movements, predation by introduced fishes, and survivability. The work was part of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program, which was authorized to determine whether dam releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] could be modified to reduce downstream impacts. The study centered on the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) and its confluence with the mainstem [[Colorado River]].

The authors conclude that adult humpback chub are more resident within the LCR than previously assumed, that they stage at the confluence before spring upstream movement, and that long-term residency, including overwintering, may be a post-dam life-history shift. Introduced predators, especially [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], consume humpback chub. Survival in 1994 dropped sharply across size classes, apparently linked to a flood-induced change in water clarity.

## Background & Description

The report is organized into four sections:

1. **Section 1:** Population estimates and movements of *G. cypha* (Douglas and Marsh, 1996).
2. **Section 2:** Predation on *G. cypha* by introduced fishes, with notes on fish stocking in the Grand Canyon region.
3. **Section 3:** Population estimates, movements and survivability of Catostomidae (*Catostomus latipinnis*, *C. discobolus*, *Xyrauchen texanus*).
4. **Section 4:** Survivability of *G. cypha* from a five-year mark-recapture study.

Humpback chub is a moderate-sized, highly specialized minnow endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was described in 1946 from a specimen caught in 1932 at Bright Angel Creek. It is restricted to canyon-bound reaches, is patchily distributed, and was federally listed as endangered in 1967. The Grand Canyon population was the least known of all, and the species was not recognized as a reproducing population at the LCR confluence until 1975.

The report frames conservation within a broader context: the Colorado River basin has an ichthyofauna with species-level endemism near 75%, and at least three native kinds are extinct, with many others threatened. Dams, water diversion, habitat alteration and introduced fishes are the principal threats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Little Colorado River habitat**
- Drains 141,155 km² and flows 412 km to the confluence; the lower 21 km is perennial, fed by springs such as [[Blue Springs]] (discharge 6.1–6.6 m³/s).
- Base flow is saline (conductivity above 5,000 µmhos/cm) and travertine-forming, producing a turquoise color and complex water chemistry.
- Travertine falls at river km 14.9 (Atomizer Falls) mark the upstream limit of humpback chub distribution.
- Riparian vegetation includes native and nonnative species (e.g., [[Tamarisk]], Giant Reed).

**Dam effects**
- Glen Canyon Dam (completed 1963) altered temperature, flow, sediment and salinity; native fishes persisted in Lake Powell but could not reproduce.
- Of eight indigenous fishes in Grand Canyon National Park, five still persist in low to moderate numbers, usually in warmer tributaries and backwaters.
- Colorado River habitat suitable for large-river fishes was reduced from 2,400 km to 965 km.

**Introduced fishes**
- The region has about 20–26 introduced non-native species; roughly 26 million fish were stocked in the wider system, including 19.8 million into Lake Powell since 1963.

## Key Findings

**Population estimates and movement (1991–1992, Section 1)**
- Fifty-seven capture-history matrices were derived (three camps over 19 trips) and analyzed with [[Program CAPTURE]] and [[Pollock-Otis Estimator]] models.
- Best-fitting estimate for the entire 19-month study: 4,509 adults (Pollock and Otis estimator). A May 1992 estimate at the confluence was 1,320 adults, down from 1,800 (1987) and 2,900 (1988).
- Mean monthly summed estimate was 2,993 and mean monthly whole-river estimate was 2,434 (N = 19).
- Population peaks occurred in early spring (March–June) and autumn; movement was upstream in spring, with localized stasis (summer through winter) within reaches.
- In 1992, 21% of total movements were upstream and 9% downstream; about 70% of recorded movements were static.
- Adults are primarily nocturnal; they occupy deep eddies, boulder turbulence, and riprap.

**Predation by introduced fishes (Section 2)**
- Humpback chub remains occurred in 13 (3.2%) of 408 digestive tracts of five predator species.
- Eight of 202 channel catfish each held one to seven chubs (95–200 mm TL); rainbow trout and black bullhead also consumed chub.
- Mean prey length of humpback chub was 102 mm TL, compared with 92 mm for other ingested fish.
- A model: a predator population of 1,000 eating one chub per week would consume about 3,588 chub annually.
- Predation may limit recruitment by removing juveniles and increase adult mortality. Stomach contents were dominated by algae (*Cladophora*), aquatic insects and fish.

**Survivability (Section 4, 1991–1995)**
- Over 50 sampling periods, 18,626 captures were made, with 58% recaptures.
- Cormack-Jolly-Seber open-population estimates showed a steady decline after month 27 (summer 1993), from a high of 9,849 to a low of 442 at month 49 (adjusted for fishing effort).
- Survival in 1991–1993 was uniform across size classes; survival in 1994 fell markedly in four of five size classes, never exceeding 47% for any class (previous years were never below 52% for the smallest class).
- Authors suggest the decline correlates with a 1993 flood that cleared travertine deposits and increased water clarity, though predation and behavioral change are not ruled out.

**Catostomidae (Section 3)**
- *C. latipinnis* population estimates were relatively stable, with peaks in summer; *C. discobolus* declined over the 49-month study, with recaptures extremely low.
- *X. texanus* numbers were extremely small (under 12 individuals per season), and the species was rarely recorded; hybrids with *C. latipinnis* have been noted.

**Conservation recommendations**
- Continue long-term monitoring; genetic studies across the canyon; and studies of catostomid behavior and habitat use.
- Interactions with nonnative fishes are identified as the greatest threat facing indigenous fish communities.
