# Biology of Freshwater Fish: A Review of the Literature of 1967 on Wastewater and Water Pollution Control

#ichthyology #water-pollution #toxicology #environmental-science

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive literature review, prepared by [[Max Katz]] and [[Albert K. Sparks]] at the [[University of Washington]] College of Fisheries, synthesizes the 1967 scientific literature on the biological effects of water pollution on freshwater fish. The review was commissioned by the [[Water Pollution Control Federation]] and represents a focused examination of fish biology in polluted environments, having been narrowed from previous years' broader scope that included algae, fungi, radioactive wastes, and marine pollution. The document covers critical topics including dissolved oxygen requirements, domestic and industrial waste impacts, pesticide toxicity, heavy metal contamination, temperature effects, and bioassay methodology. This review serves as a foundational reference for understanding the state of knowledge regarding aquatic pollution biology in the late 1960s.

## Background & Description

The review represents a significant shift in scope from previous years. Prior to 1967, the College of Fisheries' contribution to the annual literature review included sections on algae, fungi, radioactive wastes, and marine pollution biology. Due to the "explosion in the literature on the biological aspects of water pollution," the review was divided into smaller, more focused sections assigned to different institutions. This document specifically addresses freshwater fish biology.

The review was prepared for publication in the [[Journal Water Pollution Control Federation]], Volume 40, No. 7, July 1968, as part of the journal's annual Literature Review issue. The authors note that a companion publication by [[Keup]], [[Ingram]], and [[Mackenthun]] (1967) represents "the most important recent single contribution to the literature on the biological aspects of water pollution," compiling important papers dating back to [[Kolkwitz]] and [[Marsson]]'s classical 1908 work.

The document includes an extensive errata section for the 1967 review, indicating the meticulous attention to scientific accuracy maintained by the authors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The review documents widespread degradation of aquatic ecosystems during the 1960s. [[Mills]] et al. documented biological changes in the [[Illinois River]] over seventy years resulting from human activities, showing "a drastic and undesirable reduction in the numbers and kinds of desirable plants and animals." [[Zhilenko]] reported that the 1962 fish catch in the [[Suifan River]] was only 24% of the 1942 catch due to domestic pollution and overfishing.

Major pollution sources affecting fish populations included:

**Municipal Wastes**: Domestic sewage caused complete deoxygenation in some systems, though rapid currents in rivers like the [[Ganges River]] prevented total oxygen depletion. [[Rogers]] documented biological changes in two Alabama streams receiving domestic wastes, showing clear water quality deterioration and subsequent recovery following abatement procedures.

**Industrial Effluents**: Various industries contributed specific pollutants:
- Pulp and paper mills: [[Hasselrot]] found effects extended considerable distances from discharge points, more noticeable in winter due to slower decomposition rates
- Sugar refineries: Despite pollution, some Russian facilities successfully reared [[Carp]] in waste lagoons treated with lime, NH₄NO₃, and superphosphate
- Mining operations: [[Saunders]] and [[Sprague]] documented that copper and zinc pollution from mines caused adult [[Atlantic Salmon]] to abort their spawning migrations

**Pesticide Contamination**: Biological concentration of pesticides was documented throughout food chains. In [[Green Bay]], [[Lake Michigan]], [[Hickey]] et al. found concentration factors of approximately 50 times in the amphipod [[Pontoporeia affinis]] relative to mud, with an additional 10-fold increase in fish tissues.

## Key Findings

### Water Quality and Dissolved Oxygen Requirements

[[Doudoroff]] and [[Shumway]] emphasized that complete protection of fish requires dissolved oxygen levels at or near saturation. Adequate growth, embryonic development, and activity can be limited by DO reductions only slightly below saturation. [[Stewart]] et al. found that food conversion efficiencies in [[Largemouth Bass]] were markedly reduced only below 4 mg/l DO at constant concentrations, but growth was markedly impaired when fish experienced alternating low and high oxygen concentrations over 24-hour periods.

[[Strel'tsova]] observed adaptation of [[Trout]] and [[Carp]] to various DO concentrations, with adaptation occurring after 22 days at 1-2°C and more rapidly at 16-17°C. [[Hoff]] et al. demonstrated that lethal oxygen levels were partially dependent on temperature and stress in marine and anadromous fish.

### Detergent Toxicity

[[Thatcher]] and [[Santner]] determined toxicity of linear alkyl sulfonate (LAS) detergent to five freshwater fish species in flowing water systems. [[Lake Emerald Shiners]] were most sensitive while [[Black Bullheads]] were most tolerant. With ABS, most mortality occurred during the first two days, but with LAS mortalities also occurred on days 3 and 4.

[[Chwala]] found that toxic effects of synthetic detergents result from depression of surface tension. When surface tension is reduced to 50 dynes/cm, epithelial cells of fish gills become irreversibly swollen and distorted. [[Scheier]] and [[Cairns]] observed that gill damage in [[Sunfish]] from 24-hour exposure to 18 ppm ABS was not reversible even after eight weeks in clean water.

### Heavy Metal Contamination

**Mercury**: [[Westoo]] determined mercury concentrations in North Atlantic marine fish and freshwater fish. Marine fish generally contained less mercury than FAO standards, but larger specimens contained more Hg than smaller specimens from the same location. Highest concentrations were found in freshwater fish near the Swedish Coast, probably due to industrial pollution. [[Berglund]] and [[Wretlind]] found that persons eating 200 g of fish from Hg-polluted water had blood concentrations 20-30 times greater than normal. Fish containing 1 mg Hg/kg or more were considered unsuitable for human consumption.

**Copper and Zinc**: [[Grande]] conducted experiments on effects of copper and zinc on eggs, fry, and fingerlings of [[Atlantic Salmon]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Rainbow Trout]]. Small amounts of both metals reduced hatching percentages. [[Saunders]] and [[Sprague]] documented avoidance reactions of salmon to copper-zinc pollution in their natural environment during spawning migrations.

**Cadmium**: [[Mount]] and [[Stephan]] found that in living [[Bluegills]], cadmium accumulation in gill tissue never exceeded 130 μg/g (dry weight), but fish that died of acute cadmium poisoning contained a minimum of 150 μg/g in gill tissue, suggesting this measurement could detect cadmium poisoning.

### Pesticide Effects

**Organochlorines**: [[Cope]] provided a comprehensive discussion of freshwater ecosystem contamination by pesticides. The variety of environmental features, multiplicity of chemicals and formulations resulted in interactions that were "highly dynamic and difficult to predict."

[[Johnson]] et al. found that lakes treated with 0.1 mg/l toxaphene 3-9 years prior to sampling contained: water 1-4 ppb, sediments 0.2-1 mg/l, and aquatic plants 0.05-0.4 mg/kg. [[Weatherholtz]] et al. found heptachlor residues in every examined farm pond watershed in southwestern Virginia, with residues detectable up to 25 months after application.

**Forest Spraying**: [[Kerswill]] and [[Elson]] studied effects of DDT spraying for [[Spruce Budworm]] control in New Brunswick on [[Atlantic Salmon]]. DDT-in-oil at 620 g/ha caused heavy losses of underyearling salmon and parr within 3 weeks. DDT at 310 g/ha had no apparent short-term effects on salmon parr but killed many underyearlings. Evidence of delayed mortality occurred 4-6 months after spraying, associated with onset of winter cold.

**Bioaccumulation**: [[Gakstatter]] and [[Weiss]] investigated elimination of ¹⁴C-labeled DDT, dieldrin, and lindane from fish following single exposure. Initial lindane-¹⁴C was eliminated by both [[Bluegills]] and [[Goldfish]] within 2 days. More than 90% of initial dieldrin-¹⁴C was eliminated after 32 days of recovery. Studies showed that labeled DDT and dieldrin were readily transferred from contaminated fish to uncontaminated controls in recovery aquaria.

**Sub-lethal Effects**: [[Warner]] et al. developed methods for rapid quantitative analysis of behavior changes in fish from sub-lethal pesticide poisoning. When [[Goldfish]] were exposed to toxaphene, behavioral aberrations were observed after 264 hours at 0.44 μg/l, while the 96-hr TLm value was 25 times greater at 11.0 μg/l. The nature of behavioral aberrations depended on both concentration and exposure period.

[[Ruttkay]] found that short-term exposure to sub-lethal dieldrin concentrations was harmless, but prolonged exposure even at low concentrations had marked effects on disease resistance, resulting finally in mortality. [[Lowe]] exposed [[Spot]] (Leiostomus xanthurus) continuously for 8 months to 0.05 mg/l endrin with no pathology, but 3-week exposure to near-lethal concentration (0.075 mg/l) produced systemic lesions involving brain, spinal cord, liver, kidneys and stomach.

### Phenol Toxicity

[[Luk'yanenko]], [[Flerov]], [[Volodin]] and associates conducted extensive observations on phenol effects. Resistance of [[Carassius]] to toxic phenol concentrations did not change with gradual increase of water hardness. Solutions of pH 3 or 12 were lethal, but pH 4 or 11 did not affect fish. Resistance to 50 mg/l phenol was not affected by pH changes within range of 4-11. Resistance did not change in temperature range of 5-25°C. Young trout (one-half-year-old) were more tolerant to phenol than two-year-old rainbows and brown trout.

Maximum tolerable concentration of phenol for [[Abramis]] eggs was 25 mg/l. Changes in cholinesterase activity in muscles and ammonia in brains of phenol-poisoned Carassius were measured, showing stimulating and paralyzing effects characteristic of phenol followed by inactivation of cholinesterase and consequent accumulation of acetylcholine in cholinergic synapses.

### Temperature Effects

[[Kennedy]] and [[Mihursky]] published an excellent bibliography of 1220 references on temperature effects in aquatic environments. [[Mihursky]] and [[Kennedy]] described water temperature criteria necessary to protect aquatic life for three ecosystems: (a) coldwater salmonid streams, (b) warm-water centrarchid environments, and (c) estuaries.

[[Precht]] et al. found that in [[Idus idus]], frequency of opercular movement decreases continuously over long periods if adaptation temperature is suddenly lowered. [[DeWilde]] and [[Houston]] investigated blood O₂-capacity of [[Rainbow Trout]] as function of thermal acclimation, evaluating hematological parameters including erythrocyte number, packed cell volume, hemoglobin concentrations, mean erythrocytic volume and hemoglobin content.

[[Shulyak]] found definite correlation between temperature effects and abnormalities in intestine structure of [[Carp]] fry. Most favorable condition for intestine development was a decrease in temperature during embryonic period followed by rise during post-embryonic period.

### Bioassay Methodology

[[Burdick]], [[Mackenthun]], and [[Cairns]] emphasized that results of bioassays are specific to particular water supplies and assay conditions, and may not be considered applicable to other waters and conditions. Toxicity values from one assay series should never be applied as standards to other waters.

[[Abram]] conducted rigorous treatment of fish toxicity threshold definition and measurement using four different laboratory methods with [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Harlequins]]. He concluded that while long-term experiments are best, extrapolation by plotting velocity of death (reciprocal of survival period) against logarithm of concentration is simplest to use.

[[Mount]] and [[Brungs]] devised a simplified diluter for maintaining series of constant concentrations in flowing water. Construction required less than 2 days using only readily available materials. [[Betts]] et al. described procedure for small-scale laboratory bioassays requiring less elaborate equipment, shorter testing periods, and easier control than full-scale continuous-flow bioassays.

### Cyanide Toxicity

[[Costa]] found cyanide highly toxic to all fish species but fish could detect and avoid NaCN at concentrations as low as 10⁻⁷. Most sensitive fish were [[Trout]] fry and least sensitive [[Goldfish]]. Intensity of response was affected by temperature, pH, and DO. [[Kariya]] et al. successfully detected cyanides in tissues of fish killed by these substances, with detection possible even after washing with tap water for 48 hours after death.

### Ammonia Effects

[[Hemens]] found that lower pH values substantially reduced toxicity of ammonia solutions. At pH 7.75, median survival period of [[Mosquito Fish]] (Gambusia affinis) in 120 mg/l ammonium as nitrogen was 60 minutes. At pH 6.55, no mortality occurred in 1000 minutes. Threshold concentration was assumed near 1.1 mg N/l as un-ionized ammonia. Male fish were significantly more sensitive than females at same un-ionized ammonia concentration.

[[Penaz]] tested tolerance of [[Salmo trutta]] eggs and young fish to ammonia under laboratory conditions. None of the concentrations up to 50 mg/l was completely lethal to eggs, although receptiveness increased with higher ammonia concentrations. Even at 2.10 mg/l, some irreversible damage to heart and muscle control was observed in young trout.

### Fish Kills and Natural Toxins

[[Muncy]] reported fish mortality in a Louisiana lake associated with high populations of the dinoflagellate [[Gymnodinium]]. Toxicity appeared related to increased pH, length of exposure to sunlight, and concentration of algal cells. Filtration with activated carbon removed toxic components.

[[Marasas]] et al. found a virulent fish toxin produced by the fungus [[Fusarium tricinctum]]. The LD₅₀ of purified toxin was 6.1 mg/kg in [[Rainbow Trout]]. Macroscopic pathological symptoms at 4-33 mg/kg included shedding of intestinal mucosa and severe edema.

[[Aschner]] et al. found muddy flavor in [[Carp]] caused by metabolites of [[Oscillatoria tenius]]. [[Kawazu]] et al. reported on toxic leaf extract of [[Callicarpa canadicans]], determining its chemical structure and finding toxicity to fish equal to that of rotenone.
