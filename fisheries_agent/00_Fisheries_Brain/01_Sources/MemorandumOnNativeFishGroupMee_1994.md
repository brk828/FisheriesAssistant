## Executive Summary
This 1994 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum documents outcomes of a Native Fish Group meeting held at Katherines Landing regarding conservation and recovery efforts for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]]. The document outlines an intensive larval collection program, a coordinated captive spawning strategy to preserve genetic diversity, plans to refresh the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] broodstock with wild-caught individuals, and a multi-agency field research schedule targeting different sections of the reservoir and canyon habitat in March 1994. The overarching goal is to sustain the wild population against an anticipated demographic "crash" while building a genetically robust captive broodstock for long-term reintroduction into Lake Mohave and [[Lake Havasu]].

## Background & Description
The source text is a management memorandum and does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of *Xyrauchen texanus*. However, key life-stage terminology relevant to morphology and development is referenced:
- **Larvae ("wild razorback larvae")**: Early developmental stage collected directly from Lake Mohave via targeted netting efforts.
- **Fry ("on feed")**: Post-larval stage that has transitioned to exogenous feeding; this is the benchmark stage (minimum 10,000 individuals) required before fish can be moved to growout facilities.
- **Larger fish**: Juveniles reared to sufficient size (200–300 individuals targeted) to serve as [[Dexter Broodstock]] replacements.

No adult morphometric, meristic, or coloration data are given in this document.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat use**: The [[Razorback Sucker]] population in Lake Mohave utilizes distinct habitat zones — reservoir backwaters (used as growout/nursery habitat for reared fry) and the upper canyon mainstem (historically under-sampled, targeted for spring 1994 research).
- **Reproductive ecology**: Spawning occurs seasonally across "different areas of the lake," implying spatially structured breeding subpopulations. The plan to pair individuals from different areas of the lake indicates a management-recognized need to preserve genetic structure across the population.
- **Reservoir sectioning**: The lake and adjoining canyon are divided into three operational research zones for the March 1994 survey:
  - **Arizona Bay** — worked by USBR and AGF (Arizona Game and Fish) personnel, 13–20 March.
  - **El Dorado Canyon** — worked by Service houseboat crews, 20–27 March.
  - **Willow Beach** — worked by a third field group.
- **Population dynamics concern**: Management text explicitly anticipates a future "population crash" in Lake Mohave, framing current wild larval recruitment as a closing window for capturing genetic diversity before decline.
- **Interconnection with other water bodies**: Progeny beyond broodstock retention needs are earmarked for reintroduction into [[Lake Havasu]], linking Lake Mohave's wild genetic resource to broader Colorado River basin recovery efforts.

## Key Findings
- **Larval collection target**: A minimum of 10,000 "on feed" fry is required to meet stated program goals for Lake Mohave backwater stocking; higher recovery numbers are hoped for.
- **Collection timeline**: Intensive larval collection effort planned weekly from late January/early February through April 1994.
- **Rearing pipeline**: Larvae will be reared at USBR (U.S. Bureau of Reclamation) facilities in Boulder City until reaching the "on feed" stage, then transferred to Lake Mohave growout facilities.
- **Dexter broodstock renewal plan**:
  - 2,000–5,000 additional larvae will be collected specifically to yield 200–300 larger fish for [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] broodstock renewal.
  - Regional Arizona Game and Fish personnel (Tom Liles) mandated that no further progeny be produced from the current Dexter broodstock, a position the Service coordinator endorses on genetic grounds.
  - Goal: full replacement of current broodstock with wild-caught individuals within 3–5 years, at which point Dexter would resume larvae production for reintroduction.
- **1994 spawning initiative** (Service-led):
  - Minimum of 30 breeding pairs, each spawned **three times** across the reproductive season, using individuals from different lake areas to maximize genetic diversity.
  - Pairings conducted 1:1 with **standardized egg volume per female** to control for genetic contribution.
  - 300–500 offspring retained annually as broodstock; remainder allocated to [[Lake Havasu]] reintroduction.
  - Program to be repeated for **at least 5 years**, or until the wild Lake Mohave population crashes — whichever comes first — with broodstock retained each year.
  - Post-crash, retained broodstock will support continued reintroduction under a comprehensive genetics management plan developed by **Holt Williamson** at Dexter, acknowledging these fish will have **lower genetic diversity** than the original wild parent population but deemed sufficient for maintenance purposes.
- **Spring 1994 field research design**:
  - Three concurrent/sequential field teams (USBR/AGF, USFWS, and a third group) covering Arizona Bay, El Dorado Canyon, and Willow Beach respectively.
  - Explicit effort to intensively sample the **upper canyon population**, an area not comprehensively studied in recent years.
  - Sampling schedule designed so the Arizona Bay complement is **not worked two consecutive weeks**, likely to reduce handling stress/disturbance on that subpopulation.
- **Interagency coordination**: Effort involves the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (USBR), [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGF), and [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM), reflecting a multi-agency recovery framework for this federally listed species.
