# Fish Remains in the Archeological Context

#ichthyology #marine-biology #archaeoichthyology

## Executive Summary

W. I. Follett's 1965 paper, reprinted from the *Symposium on Central California Archeology* (Sacramento Anthropological Society and Central California Archeological Foundation), argues that [[Fish Remains]] recovered from [[Archeological Sites]] can give ichthyologists significant information on the food resources of aboriginal peoples. The usefulness of such material depends mainly on two factors: the intensity of collecting and the care taken in handling the specimens. The paper is a practical identification guide, illustrating diagnostic bones and teeth of about two dozen Californian and adjacent fishes, and offering recommendations on recovery and curation.

## Background & Description

The paper is a reprint from the 1965 symposium volume, with a short errata sheet correcting plate labels for lower pharyngeals of [[California Sheephead]], [[Turkey-Red Rockfish]] and [[Pile Perch]] (Plate 5). It treats fish skeletal elements as identification tools, using photographs from the collections of the [[California Academy of Sciences]].

The central principle is that **identifiability depends on the local fauna**. A barracuda vertebra is easy to identify in California because only one barracuda species occurs there. A rockfish (*Sebastodes*) vertebra is hard to assign to species because California has about 50 rockfish species, many of which differ only slightly in vertebral characters.

Diagnostic elements illustrated include:

- **Teeth:** [[Great White Shark]] (*Carcharodon carcharias*), [[Deepwater Sand Shark]] (*Carcharias ferox*), [[Tiger Shark]] (*Galeocerdo cuvier*).
- **Preopercles:** [[California Sargo]], [[Spotted Scorpionfish]], [[Buffalo Sculpin]].
- **Opercles:** [[California Sardine]], [[Sacramento Perch]], [[Northern Midshipman]].
- **Sagittae (otoliths):** [[White Seabass]], [[Gulf Croaker]], [[Shortspine Channel Rockfish]].
- **Pharyngeals (throat bones):** [[Sacramento Sucker]], [[Sacramento Blackfish]], [[Hitch]], [[Pile Perch]], [[California Sheephead]], [[Turkey-Red Rockfish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Collecting intensity.** Recovering even small or doubtful fragments can matter. A single bone may be the only record of a species. Several incomplete specimens of one element can together confirm an identification. Intensive collecting can also recover a distinctive element that allows confident identification.

**Fine-screen recovery.** Small scales, small otoliths and small shark teeth are often missed. Mrs. Freddie Curtis's microanalysis, using a fine screen plus flotation, found small but identifiable fish scales at a site otherwise lacking fish remains. Small sharks are abundant in shallow Californian waters, and their teeth are readily distinguishable even though their vertebral centra are not. The four small edible sharks named are the [[Leopard Shark]], [[Brown Smoothhound]], [[Gray Smoothhound]] and [[Soupfin Shark]]. Their centra are "exceedingly similar", so identifications from centra alone are questionable.

**Regional distribution and species pairs.** Some identifications depend on geography:

- The [[Northern Midshipman]] spawns in the intertidal zone of San Francisco Bay, but in southern California the [[Slim Midshipman]] takes its place in muddy and sandy bays. A midshipman opercle from southern California therefore could not be assigned as easily.
- The [[Sacramento Sucker]] pharyngeal could be confused with suckers of the Klamath River, Lahontan Basin and Colorado River.
- The [[California Sheephead]] is the largest of three Californian wrasses. Its pharyngeals may exceed 19 mm, while the smaller species' do not.

**Archeological occurrence.** The paper notes that California sheephead is well represented at southern California sites, including Muwu village, San Nicolas Island, Little Harbor, Arroyo Sequit Shellmound, Scripps Estates and Century Ranch. The [[Hitch]] spawned in large numbers in Clear Lake tributaries and was probably important to the [[Pomo]] people.

## Key Findings

**Methodological conclusions**

- Fish remains can significantly improve reconstruction of the food resources of aboriginal peoples, provided collecting is intensive and material is carefully handled.
- Small elements, including scales, small otoliths and small shark teeth, should be recovered with fine screens and flotation to supplement skeletal assemblages.
- Shark identifications should rely on teeth rather than vertebral centra where small species are concerned.
- Identifications must consider the regional fauna. Some species are distinctive in one region but confusable in another.

**Handling and curation recommendations**

- Fish bone is fragile and may break or disintegrate, especially after excavation. Diagnostic structures can be lost when specimens jostle in paper bags.
- Material prone to disintegration on air exposure should be sprayed with a transparent fixative such as clear Krylon.
- Material for transport should be packed in cotton.

**Size evidence from diagnostic elements.** Several elements allow size estimates:

- A 24 mm sagitta of [[White Seabass]] represents a fish about one meter long.
- A 52 mm sagitta of [[Gulf Croaker]] represents a fish about 65 cm long.
- A 15 mm sagitta of [[Shortspine Channel Rockfish]] represents a fish of 58.7 cm, approaching the species' maximum recorded size.
- The [[Sacramento Sucker]] attains more than 50 cm total length.

**Species notes.** The [[Sacramento Perch]] is the only native sunfish (family Centrarchidae) west of the Rocky Mountains. The [[Pile Perch]] is viviparous. The [[Spotted Scorpionfish]] has venom glands associated with some fin spines. The [[California Sardine]] once occurred in myriads along the California coast under primitive conditions.
