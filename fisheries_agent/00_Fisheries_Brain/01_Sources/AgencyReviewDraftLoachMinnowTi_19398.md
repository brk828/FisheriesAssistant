# Spikedace Recovery Plan - Agency Review Draft

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #threatened-species #stream-ecology #southwestern-fishes

## Executive Summary

This agency review draft recovery plan addresses the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), a small stream-dwelling cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River]] system of Arizona and New Mexico. The species was listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act in 1986 due to dramatic reductions in distribution and abundance caused by habitat destruction, water diversion, and interactions with non-native fishes. Once widely distributed in moderate-sized, intermediate-elevation streams throughout the upper Gila River basin, spikedace are now restricted to scattered populations in relatively short stream reaches. The plan outlines comprehensive recovery actions including protection of existing populations, habitat restoration, research on interactions with non-native species (particularly [[Red Shiner]]), population monitoring, and potential reintroductions to historic habitats. Critical habitat designation and enforcement of existing regulations are identified as essential components. The primary recovery objective is to restore depleted and extirpated populations to achieve self-sustaining status warranting delisting.

## Background & Description

The spikedace is a small, sleek, stream-dwelling member of the minnow family ([[Cyprinidae]]) representing a unique, monotypic genus. The body is slender, almost spindle-shaped, and slightly compressed laterally. Scales are present only as small plates deeply embedded in the skin. Two spinose rays occur at the leading edge of the dorsal fin, the first being obviously the strongest, sharp-pointed, and nearly as long as the second. Eyes and mouth are both large; barbels are absent. The dorsal fin has seven rays and the anal fin usually has nine. Pharyngeal teeth are in two rows with formula 1,4-4,1.

Coloration is bright silvery on the sides with vertically-elongated black specks. The back is olive-gray to brownish, usually mottled with darker pigment. The underside is white. Males in breeding condition become brightly golden or brassy, especially on the head and at fin bases. Maximum size is near 65 mm standard length (SL) in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, and 48 mm SL in the upper [[Gila River]], New Mexico. Longevity typically is one to two years; a few fish reach age three and exceptional individuals may survive four years.

The species was proposed as threatened by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1985 and subsequently listed in 1986. Arizona classifies it as a Group 3 threatened species and New Mexico as a Group 2 endangered species. The species is protected from take except by angling in Arizona or by specific collecting permit in both states.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Requirements:** Spikedace occupy flowing waters, usually less than a meter deep. Adults often aggregate in shear zones along gravel-sand bars, quiet eddies on the downstream edge of riffles, and broad, shallow areas above gravel-sand bars. Smaller, younger fish are found in quieter water along pool margins over soft, fine-grained bottoms. In larger rivers, spikedace often occur near tributary mouths. The fish use shallower, strongly-flowing areas in springtime, often over sandy-gravelly substrates. Specific habitat associations vary seasonally, geographically, and ontogenetically.

**Reproduction:** Breeding occurs in spring (April-June), apparently initiated by a combination of stream discharge and water temperature; timing varies annually and geographically. Males patrol in shallow, sandy-gravelly riffles where current is moderate with no indication of territoriality. Receptive females are approached by up to six males, two of which remain immediately alongside and slightly behind the female. Gametes are presumably deposited into the water column or on or near the substrate. Fertilized ova are adhesive and demersal, likely adhering to substrates. Sex ratio among reproductive adults varies from near unity among younger fish to greater abundance of females among older individuals. Females may be fractional spawners with elapsed periods of a few days to several weeks between spawnings. Fecundity ranges from 90 to 250 ova per female, significantly correlated with both length and age. Ovum diameter at spawning is near 1.5 mm.

**Growth Patterns:** Young grow rapidly during summer and autumn, attaining 35 to 40 mm SL by November. Winter growth is reduced in some places, negligible in others. Fish average near 40 mm SL at the end of one year and 50 to 63 mm SL at the end of the second year. Growth of males and females appears similar, although differences may exist within particular year classes.

**Diet:** Spikedace are carnivores feeding mostly upon aquatic and terrestrial insects entrained in stream drift. Among aquatic forms, larval ephemeropterans, hydropsychid trichopterans, and chironomid dipterans are most important. Prey body size is small, typically ranging from 2 to 5 mm long. At times of emergence, pupal, imaginal or adult stages of benthic insects, especially ephemeropterans, are consumed in large quantities. Diversity of diet is greatest among smaller spikedace, which consume a variety of small, soft-bodied animals, while adults specialize on larger, drifting nymphal and adult ephemeropterans.

**Native Fish Community:** Among native fishes, [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) commonly occur in the same habitats as adult spikedace. [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) may also co-occur in shallow, sandy, laminar-flowing reaches. Larval and juvenile spikedace in quiet, marginal habitats may encounter small desert and Sonoran suckers, larval and adult longfin dace, and perhaps small [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*).

**Non-Native Species Interactions:** Introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) occupies similar habitat and may sometimes be taken in the same seine haul as spikedace. The red shiner now occurs at all places formerly occupied by spikedace, with possible exception of the [[San Francisco River]] above Frisco Hot Springs. The two species overlap spatially in upper reaches of both Gila and Verde rivers. Continuing contraction in range of spikedace in the Gila River has been attributed to upstream expansion of red shiner populations. Although mechanisms of interaction remain unclear, the shiner has been repeatedly implicated in declines of spikedace and other native fishes, with direct predation and displacement suggested as potential impacts. Other non-native fishes including [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*M. dolomieui*), and introduced trouts (Salmonidae) may also co-occur with spikedace.

**Historical vs. Present Distribution:** The spikedace was abundant in the [[San Pedro River]], Arizona, and probably occurred in that stream in Sonora, Mexico. Distribution in Arizona was widespread in large and moderate-sized rivers and streams, including the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers and their major tributaries upstream of the present Phoenix metropolitan area, and the Agua Fria, San Pedro, and San Francisco river systems. Distribution in New Mexico was in both the San Francisco and Gila rivers, including the East, Middle and West forks of the latter. Populations transplanted from Aravaipa Creek into [[Sonoita Creek]], Santa Cruz County, in 1968, and 7-Springs Wash, Maricopa County, in 1970 have since been extirpated.

Currently, spikedace occur in Arizona only in Aravaipa Creek (tributary to San Pedro River in Graham and Pinal counties), [[Eagle Creek]] (tributary to Gila River in Graham and Greenlee counties), and upper [[Verde River]] in Yavapai County. All three streams support at least moderate-sized, sustaining populations in relatively undisturbed reaches. In New Mexico, spikedace are restricted to the mainstem Gila River and its East, Middle, and West Forks; a few individuals may occasionally be encountered in lowermost reaches of perennial tributaries. Only the population occupying the Cliff-Gila Valley, New Mexico, is considered comparable in abundance to that of earlier years; others have been substantially diminished.

## Key Findings

**Reasons for Decline:** Both distribution and abundance of spikedace have become dramatically reduced in the past century, with major changes occurring in recent decades. Habitat destruction or alteration and interaction(s) with non-native fishes have acted both independently and in concert to extirpate or deplete spikedace populations. In the San Pedro and Agua Fria, plus major reaches of the Salt and Gila rivers, dewatering and other drastic habitat modifications resulted in demise of spikedace and most other native fishes. Downstream reaches of the Verde, Salt, and mainstem Gila rivers have been affected by impoundments and highly-altered flow regimes. Stream channelization, bank stabilization, and other instream management for flood control or water diversion have directly destroyed spikedace habitats.

Historic and present landscapes surrounding spikedace habitats have been impacted by domestic livestock grazing, mining, agriculture, timber harvest, and other development. These activities contribute to habitat degradation by altering flow regimes, increasing watershed and channel erosion and sedimentation, and adding contaminants. Activities that ameliorate natural flow regimes, such as damming and water diversion, have negative impacts on native fishes because desert fishes effectively withstand natural flooding while non-native forms apparently do not.

**Recovery Objectives and Actions:** The primary objective is to identify steps and delineate mechanisms necessary to protect existing populations, restore depleted and extirpated populations of spikedace and their habitats, and ensure opportunity for the species to become non-endangered and self-sustaining in perpetuity. The plan requires modification as new information becomes available; only then can quantitative criteria for delisting be elaborated.

The recovery plan outlines nine major action categories:

1. **Protect existing populations** through identification of all extant populations, prioritization by protection need, designation of critical habitat, enforcement of existing laws (particularly Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act), discouragement of detrimental land and water uses, insurance of perennial flows with natural hydrographs, curtailment of non-native fish introductions, and acquisition of important private lands and water rights.

2. **Monitor status of existing populations** through establishment of standard monitoring locations and techniques, determination of natural variation ranges in absolute abundance and age-class structure (including bi-annual spring and autumn population estimates), and monitoring of community composition.

3. **Identify nature and significance of interaction with non-native fishes** through field investigations, experimental manipulations, and laboratory studies addressing both direct interaction (predation, displacement) and indirect interaction mediated by other community members.

4. **Quantify effects of physical habitat modification** on life cycle completion, including research on substrate, velocity and depth, water temperature, and interactions among these factors.

5. **Enhance or restore habitats** occupied by depleted populations by identifying target areas amenable to management, determining necessary habitat and landscape improvements, and implementing habitat improvement measures.

6. **Reintroduce populations** to selected streams within historic range by identifying suitable stocks and recipient stream systems, determining habitat suitability, enhancing habitat as necessary, assessing and managing non-native fish status, installing barriers to prevent reinvasion, reclaiming areas to remove non-natives, conducting reintroductions, and monitoring success or failure with adaptive management.

7. **Determine quantitative criteria** for describing self-sustaining populations, including acceptable levels of natural variation in absolute numbers, age-class structure, reproduction, and recruitment; minimum stock size; and environmental variables (physical, chemical, and biological community characteristics).

8. **Plan and conduct investigations** on captive holding, propagation and rearing of spikedace, including determination of suitable wild stocks, development of holding and maintenance procedures, evaluation of propagation techniques, assessment of life-cycle requirements in hatchery environment, and supply of individuals for augmentation, reintroduction, research, and public education.

9. **Information and education** targeting both public sector (local media, state and national exposure, assistance to Mexican agencies, open communication among stakeholders) and professional information exchange (circulation among concerned parties, periodic meetings, presentations at scientific meetings, publication in peer-reviewed literature).

**Critical Habitat:** Critical habitat was proposed in 1985 but formal designation was deferred. The proposal includes approximately 24 km of [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Graham and Pinal counties, Arizona); approximately 57 km of [[Verde River]] and 1.5 km of [[Sycamore Creek]] (Yavapai County, Arizona); approximately 38 km of [[Eagle Creek]] (Graham and Greenlee counties, Arizona - recommended addition); and approximately 73 km of [[Gila River]] including sections of the West Fork and Middle Fork (Grant and Catron counties, New Mexico). These reaches flow through various federal (U.S. Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service), state, tribal (San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation), and private lands.

**Research Priorities:** The plan identifies critical knowledge gaps requiring research: quantitative assessment of red shiner impacts through field experiments and laboratory studies; determination of minimum habitat requirements for all life stages; establishment of population viability criteria; development of standardized monitoring protocols; and investigation of captive propagation techniques. Natural flooding appears to play a significant role in native fish life histories by rejuvenating habitats and differentially affecting native versus non-native species, suggesting that maintenance of natural flow regimes is essential for recovery.

**Implementation Considerations:** The plan emphasizes that interaction with non-native fishes and habitat modification, whether acting independently or in concert, are both contributory to decline and must both be addressed. The concomitant reduction of spikedace and expansion of red shiner provides powerful circumstantial evidence of displacement. The [[San Pedro River]] system, Arizona, is identified as potentially the most amenable to reestablishment efforts, as it is the type locality for spikedace and certain perennial reaches of major tributaries (e.g., Redfield Canyon, [[Babocomari River]]) may have potential for species reestablishment.
