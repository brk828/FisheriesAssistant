## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by G.W. Barlow investigates the role of body size in courtship and mate selection dynamics of the [[Orange Chromide]] (*Etroplus maculatus*), a cichlid species exhibiting reversed sexual size dimorphism potential relative to other cichlids. In typical pairs, males are ~10% longer than females. Through a series of controlled experiments—sequential pairing, multi-choice trials, and screened preference tests—the study examined whether size preference or social dominance hierarchies drive mate selection. Findings indicate that dominance relationships can override simple size preference, but when dominance is neutralized (via barrier separation), both sexes exhibit measurable, quantifiable biases toward larger conspecifics of the opposite sex, with sex-specific differences in the intensity and type of courtship motor patterns performed.

## Background & Description

- **Sexual size dimorphism**: Males (♂) are typically ~10% longer (standard length) than females (♀) in paired orange chromides.
- Size classes used in experimental design: 90%, 100%, and 110% of male (♂) body length for female (♀) test subjects.
- Comparison drawn to [[Tilapia Macrocephala]], a related cichlid in which the **female** is the larger sex — presented as a contrasting model of sexual size dimorphism within [[Cichlidae]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Species is a substrate/biparental-care cichlid (implied by courtship complexity and pair-bonding behavior typical of the genus [[Etroplus]]).
- Behavioral ecology centers on **mate choice**, **pair bonding**, and **dominance hierarchy formation**, which are ecologically significant for reproductive success and territory/resource allocation in cichlid social structures.
- Courtship occurs within a broader behavioral repertoire that includes comfort movements (yawning, chafing) alongside reproductive motor patterns (quivering, nipping, skimming), suggesting overlap between stress/arousal physiology and courtship signaling.

## Key Findings

### Experiment 1: Sequential Male Introduction
- 5 different males (♂) were sequentially and individually introduced to the same 15 females (♀), which had been arranged in a graded size series.
- Each successive male was proportionally larger relative to the female than in the prior trial.
- **Result**: Dominance hierarchy formation appeared to override any inherent size-based mate preference.

### Experiment 2: Single Male, Three Females (Size Choice)
- Design: 1 male (♂) with 3 females (♀) sized at 90%, 100%, and 110% of male body length, across 5 successive trials.
- **Result**: Males mated with either the **largest** or **smallest** female — the intermediate-sized (100%) females were consistently excluded from mating contention.

### Experiment 3: Screened Choice Test (Large vs. Small Female)
- Design: A male (♂) was given a simultaneous choice between a large and small female (♀) separated by transparent screens (preventing physical/overt aggression and full pair bonding).
- **Time allocation**:
  - Males spent 55% of time near large females, 45% near small females.
  - Females spent 56% of time near large males, 44% near small males.
- **Quivering** (central courtship display):
  - Males quivered more overall than females.
  - Males quivered **more toward small females**.
  - Females quivered **more toward large males**.
- **Behavioral sequence progression** (quiver → nip → skim):
  - Females came to predominate in performing these behaviors as the sequence progressed.
  - Females showed little size-based effect in these later-stage behaviors.
  - Males continued to show size effects throughout the sequence.
- **Comfort movements**:
  - **Chafing** paralleled/tracked the course of courtship activity intensity.
  - **Yawning** did not follow this same courtship-linked pattern (used as a contrasting control behavior).

### Comparative Discussion
- Findings are discussed in relation to [[Tilapia Macrocephala]], where sexual size dimorphism is reversed (female larger), suggesting that size-based mate preference mechanisms in cichlids may be tied to sex-specific dominance strategies rather than a fixed universal size preference.
- Overall conclusion: Simple linear size preference is not a wholly independent driver of mate choice in *E. maculatus*; it interacts with dominance hierarchies and is asymmetrically expressed between sexes and across stages of courtship behavior.
