# Physical and Chemical Conditions of Some East Jutland Streams

#limnology #stream-ecology #freshwater-invertebrates #ephemerellidae

## Executive Summary

This Danish study (in *Flora og Fauna*, 1962) describes the physical and chemical conditions of seven small streams and springs in [[East Jutland]], near [[Laurbjerg]], which were sampled for lower freshwater fauna. It is the first in a planned series on the distribution and ecology of different animal groups, and it establishes the environmental baseline for the study sites. The author, Bent Lauge Madsen, worked from the [[Freshwater Biological Laboratory]] at the Silkeborg branch in Virklund, under the University of Copenhagen.

The streams are chemically similar but differ clearly in temperature. Springs in the Kilde area are cool and stable, the brooks are summer-cool, and the lowland [[Lilleå]] and Kvitbæk and Garverbæk brooks are the most thermally variable. The paper classifies the streams using [[Thermal Stenothermy and Eurythermy]] categories.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The streams lie in the Laurbjerg area of East Jutland. The Kilde, Kildebækken and Skovkilden springs belong to a spring system in Haurum Skov. The Torup Skov brook rises near Skjød. Granslev A was sampled in Haurum Skov, Kvitbæk in Nørringure Skov, Garverbæk at Granslev, and the Lilleå at Bistrup bro near Laurbjerg. The author notes that the streams form a sequence of rising summer temperatures, which roughly matches the order of distance from each spring source.

**Methods (as described).**
- **Oxygen:** micro-method, after Bennike (1943).
- **Calcium:** complexometric method, after Schmitz (1952).
- **Alkalinity (acid-binding capacity):** Schmitz (1952), using a mixed indicator for sharper endpoints.
- **pH:** electrometric.
- **Conductivity:** Dionic Water Tester.
- **Current:** Ott-meter, usable only in water at least 10 cm deep, so measured only in the larger streams. Velocities were taken at the surface and 5 cm above the bottom.
- **Temperature:** monthly arithmetic means from all readings, plus maximum-minimum thermometer readings from July 1960.

**Acknowledgements.** The author thanks laboratory director Professor Kaj Berg for workspace, equipment funding and travel support.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Chemistry.** Only one measurement series is tabulated, and the commentary relies mainly on it.
- Oxygen is similar across streams, except that the spring source is slightly lower than the brook a few metres downstream. On 19 October 1959 the Kildebæk was as oxygen-poor as the spring itself, because it was covered with fallen ash leaves that decomposed and cut off air contact. Normal oxygen returned once the leaves cleared.
- Calcium is lower in the Kilde and Kildebæk than in the other streams.
- Alkalinity values were consistent with 1958–59 readings. Citing [[Hall (1955)]], the author notes that undisturbed brooks show stable dissolved chemistry even under strong seasonal changes in discharge.
- All streams are near neutral or weakly alkaline in pH.
- Conductivity rises with pollution. Foldby bæk, another stream in the Lilleå system, showed this effect.
- Overall, the streams "do not differ substantially" in chemistry.

**Flow.** Using Berg's (1948) classes, velocities above 100 cm/s are very strong, 50–100 cm/s strong, and 25–50 cm/s moderate.
- Garverbæk had very strong flow, and the rest of that stream was strong.
- Granslev A had strong surface flow (48 cm/s on 19 October 1959, as recorded in the text). Bottom velocity ranged from moderate to strong.
- In the Lilleå, surface flow was strong in places but never strong near the bottom. Most measurements showed moderate flow at both levels.
- Bottom velocities were generally lower than surface velocities, and flow was slower near the banks than in the middle. This allows narrow sand bars to form even in fast water. Sand deposits behind stones and piles support animal communities that differ from those on nearby stones.
- Flow affects animals directly and indirectly. Indirectly, it controls substrate texture and oxygen supply, because flowing water is physiologically richer in oxygen than still water (Ruttner 1952).

**Substrate.** Substrate changes sharply across a single stream because of the flow gradient.
- **Kilde:** fine sand and small stones (2–4 cm), mostly hygropetric (wetted only by a film of water), with moss on top.
- **Kildebæk:** small stones (2–4 cm), with a few larger hygropetric stones and little moss.
- **Skovkilden:** stones about 10 cm across, hygropetric, with patches of sand or gravel.
- **Torup Skov brook:** stones about 10 cm, many hygropetric, with extensive sand.
- **Granslev A:** sand at the banks, gravel elsewhere, and scattered stones 20–35 cm across. Some stones carry *Fontinalis* (often silted) and iron precipitates, and all are fully submerged.
- **Kvitbæk:** stones about 5 cm, with many larger stones of 15–25 cm. Little plant growth.
- **Garverbæk:** gravel with stones about 8 cm across, mostly submerged and covered in *Cladophora*.
- **Lilleå:** strongly uneven across the channel. One bank has fine mud, the middle has sand with *Elodea*, the other bank is gravel, and scattered stones are 10–20 cm across. Sand and mud can cover the stones at times.

**Temperature.** Monthly means were calculated from all readings. The annual amplitude (highest minus lowest monthly mean) rises from the springs downstream.
- The spring area is thermally stable, with a total amplitude up to 1.9 °C. [[Illies (1952)]] defines spring zones as having a total amplitude of at most 5 °C.
- In the brooks, total amplitude ranged from 7.6 to 14.5 °C.
- In the Lilleå, total amplitude was 20.6 °C.
- The most important feature is the position of the amplitude on the temperature scale, especially the summer maximum. The summer maximum divides the streams into three groups, and this division is clearer at summer maximum than at winter minimum. Winter minima matter less because many animals enter [[Diapause]] in winter. For example, *Ephemerella ignita* does so. Summer conditions are the most demanding for most animals.
- Warming in spring is faster than cooling in autumn, a pattern noted by Illies (1952).
- Springs on south-facing slopes are slightly warmer than those on north-facing slopes.
- The author proposes measuring mean water temperature with the inverted-sucrose method (Schmitz and Volkert 1959), which gives an effective mean temperature. Measurements from the Lilleå system using this method were planned for later publication.

**Diurnal temperature.** Table 2 and Figure 4 give maximum-minimum readings from July 1960. Diurnal amplitude is:
- Near 0 °C in the Kilde and about 1 °C in the Kildebæk.
- Very small in Skovkilden, where maximum and minimum sit close to the mean.
- Larger in Granslev A and the Torup Skov brook. Kvitbæk shows similar amplitude, but at a different temperature range.
- Largest in Garverbæk, which the author suggests could affect animals even though no exact experiments exist.
- In the Lilleå, the diurnal range sits higher on the temperature scale than in other streams, though its maximum is barely below the others' maxima.

## Key Findings

1. **Chemistry is uniform.** The seven streams differ little in oxygen, calcium, pH, alkalinity and conductivity. Calcium is lower in the Kilde and Kildebæk, and conductivity is a useful indicator of pollution.

2. **Temperature is the main difference.** The streams differ mainly in temperature, not chemistry. Total amplitude ranges from 1.9 °C at the springs to 20.6 °C in the Lilleå, and the summer maximum is the key variable for animals.

3. **Three thermal groups.** The paper classifies the streams as follows:
   - **Cool-stenothermic (stable, cold):** Kilde and Kildebæk.
   - **Summer-cool brooks:** Skovkilden, Torup Skov brook and Granslev A, with smaller diurnal and annual amplitude than the rest.
   - **Eurythermic (unstable, high summer temperatures):** Garverbæk, Kvitbæk and Lilleå. Garverbæk has the largest diurnal swings, and the Lilleå has the largest total amplitude. Kvitbæk and Lilleå are unstable for annual amplitude, but their diurnal swings are less extreme than Garverbæk's.

4. **Flow and substrate vary within each stream.** Flow near the bottom is slower than at the surface, and slower still near the banks. This creates a mosaic of substrates, including sand bars, that supports different animal communities.

5. **Temperature is more than a total.** The author argues that the position of extremes on the temperature scale, and the duration of those extremes, matter more than totals. The duration of extremes is not captured by maximum-minimum thermometers, so a thermograph or periodic readings are needed.

6. **Methodological limits.** Many measurements are single readings, and the author admits that exact means are hard to obtain. Flow measurements near the bottom are technically difficult. The sucrose-inversion method is proposed as a better alternative to thermographs.

7. **Series plan.** This article sets the environmental baseline for later papers on the distribution and ecology of the lower freshwater fauna of these streams.
