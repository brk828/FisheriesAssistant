# Preliminary Food Studies of Cyprinodon Pupfishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This brief note reports preliminary food-habit data for three [[Cyprinodon Pupfish]] taxa: [[Cyprinodon Macularius]] (the [[Desert Pupfish]]), [[Cyprinodon Nevadensis Amargosae]] (the [[Amargosa Pupfish]]), and [[Cyprinodon Nevadensis]] ([[Saratoga Springs Pupfish]]). The authors argue that basic life-history knowledge is needed for management, because these isolated populations face threats from water diversion, habitat alteration, and interspecific competition. The study combines gut-content analysis of fish from the wild with a comparison of local invertebrate communities. The results suggest that pupfish feed on a mixture of animal prey, detritus, and algae, and that diet shifts with season and site.

## Background & Description

Pupfishes (genus *Cyprinodon*) are small fishes that live in isolated desert waters in the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. The document describes them as threatened by:

- Diversion of water for other uses
- Alteration of habitats
- Interspecific competition

The three species named in the text occupy different localities:

- **Desert pupfish** (*C. macularius*): shore pools of the Salton Sea, California.
- **Amargosa pupfish** (*C. nevadensis amargosae*): the Amargosa River, several springs near Tecopa and Shoshone, California, and some isolated pools in the southern part of Death Valley.
- **Saratoga Springs pupfish** (*C. nevadensis*): Saratoga Springs, a spring system in the Death Valley region.

Specimens were collected as follows:

- Desert pupfish from a shore pool on the north shore of the Salton Sea.
- Amargosa pupfish from the Amargosa River and nearby springs.
- Saratoga Springs pupfish with unbaited traps left in place for 5 minutes in a spring pool.

All specimens were preserved in formalin. Regurgitation was noted at preservation. Both the gut and the entire fish were analyzed for food. The text notes that *Cyprinodon* lacks a distinct stomach, so the gut was treated as a single digestive tract.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Diet analysis used three criteria: frequency of occurrence, numbers, and volume. Volume was estimated by a point method, following [[Hynes Method]] procedures. The authors also described the invertebrate fauna of the study sites.

Key environmental observations include:

- **Low invertebrate diversity** in the Amargosa River compared with nearby springs.
- Seasonal variation in invertebrate abundance, with fewer taxa present in autumn.
- Diet variation among sites, which the authors attribute mainly to local conditions, such as the availability of prey and detritus.

The authors suggest that pupfish feeding habits are shaped by environmental conditions. They are not fixed traits, and they vary with the prey present at each site.

## Key Findings

**Diet composition (from gut analysis):**

- A **total of several hundred fish** from the Salton Sea were examined. The exact count is garbled in the scan.
- On one collection date (4 August), gut contents were about **56% animal matter** and **43% detritus**. Algae were also present in some samples.
- On a second date (about 40 m from the first collection site), animal matter was about **30%**, with the remainder largely detritus. The text notes that some fish had empty guts or were still digesting.
- In November, detritus formed a large part of the gut volume.
- Animal prey was dominated by **[[Chironomidae]] larvae and pupae**. Nymphulinae caterpillars (aquatic moth larvae) were also eaten on one date. Adult dipterans, large dipteran larvae, and miscellaneous fishes were minor components.
- Gut material from some fish contained many **[[Diatoms]]**. Digested diatom cells suggest that pupfish can process algal material.

**Diet breadth and taxa:**

- The Saratoga Springs pupfish ate a wide variety of food. Other populations focused more on a few prey types.
- The authors describe pupfish as opportunistic. Their diets can shift from animal prey to detritus and algae depending on the season and local prey availability.
- The mixture of animal, detrital, and algal material may reflect the low diversity of invertebrates in these habitats.

**Feeding ecology:**

- Pupfish appear to be more generalized feeders than previously assumed. The authors suggest that they may be considered omnivores rather than strict detritivores.
- The authors suggest that the diversity of food items may reflect [[Detritivory]] and feeding behavior shaped by local conditions.
- The digestive physiology of these fish is not clearly understood. The authors call for further work on how the gut processes algae and detritus.

**Additional records (Mexico):**

- The note reports new distribution records for the genus in Mexico. A female was collected near Tecpan de Galeana, Guerrero, from a shallow stream. Records from Chiapas and Coahuila are also discussed, with reference to [[Cuatro Cienegas]], Coahuila, where pupfish were found in springs.
- The authors use these records to fill gaps in the known range of the genus.
- A mist net was used at one Guerrero site to capture fish from a pool.

**Conclusions and management implications:**

- Basic life-history data, including diet, are needed to guide conservation of isolated pupfish populations.
- Water diversion and habitat change could reduce the invertebrate prey and detritus that these fish rely on. This is a particular concern for the Amargosa populations.
- Competition with other fishes or invertebrates is a further risk, especially where habitats are small.
