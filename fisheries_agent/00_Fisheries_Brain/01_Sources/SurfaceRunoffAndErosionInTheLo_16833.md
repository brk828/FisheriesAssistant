# Surface Runoff and Erosion in the Lower Chaparral Zone, Arizona

#hydrology #soil-erosion #chaparral-ecology

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service record (Station Paper 66, 1961, by Lowell R. Rich, [[Rocky Mountain Forest And Range Experiment Station]]) describes a watershed study of [[Surface Runoff]] and [[Soil Erosion]] in the [[Lower Chaparral Zone]] of [[Arizona]]. The work compared storm runoff and [[Sediment Yield]] from nine small watersheds on [[Granitic Soils]]. The study found that [[Overland Flow]] and erosion fell after woody vegetation was cut and the ground was seeded to [[Boer Lovegrass]] and [[Lehmann Lovegrass]], which established a perennial grass cover. Contour [[Hardware-Cloth Checks]] also reduced erosion, but did not add vegetation. The source is a database abstract, so it provides only the summary, not full data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The abstract places the study within the climate of [[Arizona]]. It notes that [[Intense Summer Thunderstorms]] are characteristic of the region. Where plant cover is sparse and infiltration capacity is low, storm water runs off the surface as overland flow. These flows can carry substantial sediment and generally increase erosion. The author argues that erosion control therefore depends on reducing surface runoff.

The study examined three main influences on runoff and sediment:

- **[[Vegetation Recovery]]:** the natural return of plant cover over time.
- **[[Mechanical Stabilization]]:** physical treatment of the soil surface.
- **Cover changes:** deliberate changes in plant cover, including cutting woody vegetation and seeding grasses.

Most surface runoff occurred during the summer, which matches the timing of the thunderstorm season.

## Key Findings

1. **Vegetation conversion reduced runoff and erosion.** Cutting woody vegetation and seeding to [[Boer Lovegrass]] and [[Lehmann Lovegrass]] lowered overland flow and erosion. The seeding also established a perennial grass cover.
2. **Contour checks reduced erosion but not vegetation.** Hardware-cloth checks placed on the contour cut erosion, but did not produce additional vegetation. They are a physical control and do not replace cover.
3. **Summer storms produced most runoff.** Most surface runoff in the study occurred during the summer thunderstorm season.
4. **Management target.** The author concludes that effective erosion control in this zone must reduce surface runoff. Reducing runoff is the primary aim, with cover establishment and physical structures as means to that end.
5. **Scope of the record.** The abstract reports these conclusions only. The paper's numerical results (runoff volumes, sediment yields, statistical tests) are not included in this record and would need to be checked in the full Station Paper 66.
