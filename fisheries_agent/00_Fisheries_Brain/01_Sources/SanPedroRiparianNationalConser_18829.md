# San Pedro Habitat Management Plan (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

The San Pedro Habitat Management Plan (HMP) is a 1993 wildlife and habitat planning document for the [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] (NCA) in Cochise County, Arizona. It was jointly prepared by the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) Safford District and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), under authority of the [[Sikes Act]], the [[Arizona-Idaho Conservation Act]] of 1988 (PL 100-696), and the [[San Pedro River Riparian Management Plan]] (RMP) of 1989. The overall goal is to conserve, protect, and enhance the riparian ecosystem and its aquatic and terrestrial wildlife values. The plan sets seven objectives, planned actions, monitoring, and cost estimates for the period FY1993 through 1999, with target dates extending to 2005 and 2020 for some outcomes. The accompanying Environmental Assessment (EA) concludes that the proposed action will have no significant impacts and that an Environmental Impact Statement is not required.

## Background & Description

**Planning context.** The HMP was prepared to fulfill the RMP record of decision (August 1989), comply with [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7, coordinate BLM habitat work with AGFD and the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]], and direct BLM funds for wildlife and aquatic habitat improvement.

**Vegetation.** About 100,000 acres were mapped in and adjacent to the NCA in 1987 using the [[Brown, Lowe, and Pase Classification]] system. Upland communities include:
- About 17,602 acres of desert grassland, dominated by scrub with minor perennial grass.
- 62,293 acres of desert scrub and mixed scrub-grassland, mostly in poor condition, with head-cutting and gully erosion.
- 10,243 acres of sacaton and sacaton-mixed scrub on heavy clay soils, mostly in good condition.

Riparian plant communities are described as a sequence of ecological sites (aquatic, wet streambank, woody regeneration, low terrace, mature terrace) that evolve over time. Cottonwood and willow establish on bare sand after floods and stabilize sediment. Lowering the water table through groundwater pumping or diversion reduces flood frequency, increases salinity, and stresses the native community.

**Historical change.** Woodcutting and grazing from the 1870s to 1880s caused erosion, channel entrenchment, and expansion of mesquite and acacia at the expense of grasses. Expeditions in the 1800s described the upper San Pedro as a meandering marshy creek with abundant [[Beaver]] and fish. During the Tombstone silver boom, miners stripped most woody vegetation from the river.

**Land status.** The NCA covers about 56,430 acres of public land, with approximately 3,400 acres of private land inside the boundary. The enabling legislation withdraws federal lands from mining laws, and firewood cutting is prohibited within the NCA.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Wildlife.** More than 365 bird species have been recorded in the NCA, including 33 species on the Arizona threatened native wildlife list. Twenty-two waterfowl species and twenty-eight diurnal raptor species have been recorded. Over 82 mammal species are known, with 24 bat species. About 43 reptile and amphibian species occur. Mule deer (420) and collared peccary (730) were estimated in 1988.

**Riparian obligate birds.** These include the yellow-billed cuckoo, willow flycatcher, Bell's vireo, and yellow warbler. They depend on mature cottonwood/willow forest with moderate to high midstory and understory cover, in contiguous stands of about 100 hectares (247 acres).

**Fishes.** Thirteen native fish species were recorded in the upper San Pedro watershed, but only two remain. About nine non-native species occur in the NCA, including carp, fathead minnow, black bullhead, [[Mosquitofish]], and green sunfish. The HMP groups the native fishes by habitat need:
- Small erosive desert streams: speckled dace, round-tailed chub, Gila sucker, spikedace, loach minnow.
- Quiet backwaters and cienegas: Gila chub, Gila topminnow, desert pupfish.
- Large desert rivers: razorback sucker, flannelmouth sucker, Colorado squawfish.

Exotic fish, especially red shiner, green sunfish, largemouth bass, and mosquitofish, are the main barrier to native fish recovery.

**Special status species.** The gray hawk is state-threatened and a federal candidate. The NCA holds about 30% of U.S. nesting gray hawks. The Swainson's hawk is estimated at about two nesting territories. The historic aplomado falcon, a federally endangered species, was last recorded in Arizona near Saint David in 1940. The lesser long-nosed bat is federally endangered.

**Threats.** Water pollution, stream channel degradation, and [[Bullfrog]] introduction have affected the Mexican garter snake and lowland leopard frog. Recreation use is increasing, and its effect on gray hawks is unknown but could be locally detrimental. Salt cedar has high transpiration rates and is increasing downstream of Lewis Springs.

## Key Findings

**Objectives (targets and timelines)**

1. **Riparian obligate birds:** Expand cottonwood/willow from 2,930 to 3,142 acres, increasing obligate bird numbers by 7% and densities by 3% by 2005. Planting covers about 65 acres at nine low-terrace sites. Salt cedar on 147 acres is to be reduced to zero, at roughly 25 acres per year using Triclopyr (Garlon).
2. **Aquatic habitat:** Increase pools and perennial surface water, reach 70% streambank cover, and reduce soil alteration below 25% by 2005. Beaver reintroduction upstream of Lewis Springs is planned, with Giardia screening of captured animals.
3. **Native floodplain habitat:** Restore 2,000 acres of fallow fields to mixed mesquite and sacaton scrub by 1998. Target sacaton frequency rises from 0% to 20%. Mow 80 acres south of Hereford Road to reduce Russian thistle.
4. **Special status raptors:** Increase breeding to 20 pairs of gray hawks and 5 pairs of Swainson's hawks by 2005. Use prescribed fire on 5,100 upland acres, erosion control on 5,600 acres, and manage recreation near nest territories.
5. **Re-establishment of extirpated species:** Re-establish plant, bird, mammal, and fish species by 2005 using AGFD and BLM procedures. The Young Block Ponds, old gravel pits, are to be drained, treated with [[Rotenone]] or antimycin if needed, and developed for native cienega and big-river fishes. Success requires re-establishment plans written by 2000.
6. **Wildlife water development:** Improve four wells (Summer's, Contention, Boquillas, Palominas) to aid big game distribution by 1997.
7. **Wetland improvement:** Manage 14 acres of ephemeral wetland at four sites for waterfowl and shorebird habitat by 1999. Groundwater pumping is to be suspended if significant drawdown is detected.

**Management decisions and monitoring**
- A fifteen-year livestock moratorium covers about 49,930 acres. About 6,500 acres remain licensed for grazing.
- The HMP runs FY1993 to 1999. It is to be updated as information is gained, following BLM Manual 6780.
- Monitoring includes photo points, color-infrared photography, avian transects (nine years of data, 1986 to 1994), line-intercept transects, pace frequency transects, summer raptor surveys, and fall fish counts.
- Partners include Friends of the San Pedro, Ducks Unlimited, Huachuca Audubon Society, and Arizona Conservation Corps work crews.

**Cost-effectiveness (Appendix 4)**
- Riparian planting: planned action $400 versus alternative $5,000.
- Floodplain restoration: planned action $13,000 versus alternative $179,000.
- Wildlife water: planned action $2,320 versus alternative $9,550.
- Wetland: planned action $1,500 versus alternative $6,000 for heavy equipment.

**Environmental Assessment (EA) conclusions**
- The no-action alternative would slow habitat recovery and let exotic plants such as Johnson grass and tumbleweed dominate abandoned fields.
- Rotenone and antimycin degrade within days. They are short-term toxic to zooplankton and aquatic invertebrates, and four daily fishing visits would be lost during renovation.
- Cumulative impacts from rising visitor use are offset by the planned actions.
- A Finding of No Significant Impact was issued, with a mitigation requirement for cultural resource clearance before surface-disturbing work.
