# Sinuous Stream Aquarium to Simulate Lotic Fish Habitat and Methods for Reducing Tilapia Fry Cannibalism

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two items from *Progressive Fish-Culturist* (45(2), April 1983). The main article, by [[Kurt D. Fausch]] and [[Ray J. White]], describes a sinuous, recirculating laboratory stream aquarium built at [[Michigan State University]] during 1976–81 to study juvenile stream salmonids. The authors argue that laboratory streams should be built as sine-generated meanders with riffle-pool beds, because natural streams have these features and they produce the velocity, depth and substrate diversity that stream fish require. The second item is the opening of a short review on preventing fry losses to cannibalism in [[Tilapia]] culture. It is truncated, and no experimental comparison of methods is reported in the text provided.

## Background & Description

**Stream aquarium (Fausch and White).** Earlier laboratory channels were straight, circular or elliptical, with uniform depth or abrupt riffle-to-pool transitions. The authors note that natural streams meander in sine-generated curves over homogeneous sediments, a form described by [[Leopold and Langbein]] (1966), and that their beds slope gradually and tilt from side to side at each meander.

The channel design includes:
- **Shape:** one complete sine-generated meander with two bends of different sinuosity. The upstream bend has a maximum deflection of 80° and sinuosity of 1.69; the downstream bend has 100° and 2.58.
- **Dimensions:** 7.28 m long, 30 cm wide, 30 cm deep, with no slope.
- **Construction:** 3-mm clear [[Plexiglass]] walls glued into grooves in a 6-mm Plexiglass base, with external triangular buttresses on a plywood table.
- **Substrate:** gravel of 2–3 cm diameter shaped into riffles (mean depth 5 cm) and pools (maximum depth 15 cm), with a gently contoured point bar on the inside of each bend.
- **Level floor:** the base beneath the gravel is level, so water velocity and depth can be varied independently.

**Flow generation.** Flow is produced by [[Air-Lift Pumping]] (a method described by Spotte, 1979). Compressed air forced through an airstone in the return pipe lifts water into the upstream head box, and the head-to-collector level difference drives flow. A paddlewheel was tried first but produced waves, splashed water and occupied 0.5 m of channel length. A floating plywood square damps surges. At 50 psi air pressure, discharge was 0.0026 m³/s (0.0919 cfs).

**Water quality and temperature.** Water circulates through a [[Biofilter]] at about 20 L/min. A Frigid Units D1-100 cooler holds temperature at least as low as 10 °C, with fluctuations damped to ±0.5 °C. Oxygen stays at 100% saturation. When fish are present, nitrifying bacteria convert NH₃ to NO₂⁻, changing water chemistry. Evaporation from air-lift pumping is offset with distilled makeup water.

**Lighting.** Five 2.5-kW mercury vapor lamps and ten 100-W incandescent bulbs are diffused through white plastic. A timer simulates dawn and dusk by ramping the incandescent lamps for 30 min before and after the mercury lamps. Light at the water surface ranges from 40 to 55 µE/m²/s over 90% of the stream area. Black-lined curtains with observation slits let observers watch fish while remaining concealed.

**Tilapia fry cannibalism (review opening).** The Cichlidae are generally omnivorous, herbivorous or insectivorous. Nest building and mouth brooding protect eggs and sac fry, which probably contributes to the family's reputation for prolificacy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fausch and White frame the design around the fluvial habitat of juvenile stream fish. Water velocity, depth and substrate combine in riffle-pool sequences to create the microhabitats that stream fish use. Their juvenile salmonid studies involved [[Coho Salmon]], [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]. Invertebrate drift was simulated by continuously pumping a [[Daphnia]] suspension into the upstream riffle of each stream half. Dominant fish held positions in low-velocity water at the head of pools, close to swift currents that carried the most drifting food per unit time.

The tilapia review places cichlid reproduction in the context of culture environments. Reservoirs and natural ponds were judged unsuitable for [[Tilapia Culture]] by [[King and Wilson]] (1957) because of poor control over reproduction, cannibalism and predation. [[Largemouth Bass]], a predaceous species, is discussed as a comparison case for pond-based and fry- and egg-transfer production methods.

## Key Findings

**Stream aquarium**
- Laboratory streams should be built as sine-generated meanders with riffles and pools, as natural streams are not straight or circular and have gradual, non-abrupt riffle-pool transitions.
- A level-based channel allows independent control of water velocity and depth. In a tilted channel these cannot be varied separately.
- Air-lift pumping avoids the wave and splash problems of a paddlewheel and frees channel length.
- Temperature, oxygen and light conditions are controlled to create a stable, naturalistic environment, with light at 40–55 µE/m²/s.
- Juvenile coho salmon, brook trout and brown trout responded favorably to the simulated stream and food supply. The authors consider the simulation of their fluvial environment successful, based on the appropriate behavior observed. No quantitative behavioral data or sample sizes are reported in the text.

**Tilapia cannibalism (incomplete)**
- Cichlid parental care (nest building, mouth brooding) protects vulnerable early life stages.
- [[King and Wilson]] (1957) judged pond and reservoir rearing unfeasible.
- [[Uchida and King]] (1962) reported cannibalism as a mortality cause in *Tilapia mossambica* culture, as did [[Hida et al.]] (1962).
- Silvera (1978) found that cannibalism in *T. nilotica* fingerlings increased with fingerling length.
- Snow (1975) reviewed production methods for largemouth bass, including spawning-rearing ponds and fry- and egg-transfer methods.
- The text ends before any method for reducing fry losses is evaluated, so the comparison promised by the title is not presented.
