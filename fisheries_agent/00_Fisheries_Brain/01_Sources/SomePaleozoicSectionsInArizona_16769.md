# Some Paleozoic Sections in Arizona and Their Correlation

#geology #stratigraphy #paleozoic

## Executive Summary

This USGS Professional Paper (No. 98-K, published July 17, 1916, by [[Frederick Leslie Ransome]]) correlates Paleozoic and older stratigraphic sections across Arizona. It links the well-known succession of the [[Grand Canyon]] with the very different beds near [[Globe Quadrangle|Globe]], [[Ray Quadrangle|Ray]], [[Clifton Section|Clifton]], [[Bisbee Section|Tombstone and Bisbee]], and the [[Santa Catalina Range]]. The central product is a revised stratigraphic framework for the [[Ray Quadrangle]] and the [[Apache Group]]. The paper contains no fish or biological species data. Its only fossil content is invertebrate faunas (brachiopods, corals, crinoids, gastropods, trilobite fragments) used for age determination.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** Detailed mapping of several mining districts had been done over the preceding 16 years, but investigations remained disconnected. A 1912 reconnaissance ran northwest from Globe toward Jerome, and a visit to the Santa Catalina Range was made with C. F. Tolman, jr. The paper covers (1) the topographic setting, (2) the full Ray-Globe section and less detailed sections elsewhere, and (3) correlation from the Grand Canyon to Bisbee near the Mexican border.

**Topographic provinces.** Arizona is divided into three regions:
- **Plateau region**: about 45,000 square miles of the [[Colorado Plateau]], at roughly 7,000 to over 8,500 feet, with nearly horizontal strata and the prevalent surface rock Kaibab (Pennsylvanian) limestone.
- **Mountain region**: a zone 70 to 150 miles wide of short, nearly parallel ranges (e.g., Santa Catalina, Pinal, Mazatzal). Few ranges exceed 50 miles in length or 8,000 feet in altitude. Most consist of Paleozoic or older quartzites and limestones on granitic and schistose basement, and most of Arizona's major copper deposits lie here.
- **Desert (bolson) region**: ranges separated by broad alluvial plains and lava-covered lowlands.

**Ray-Globe region.** The Ray and Globe quadrangles lie in south-central Arizona and include the Pinal Range and parts of the Mescal, Dripping Spring, and Tortilla ranges. The rocks are intricately faulted and extensively intruded by diabase. The total thickness of Paleozoic strata between the pre-Cambrian crystalline rocks and the base of the Carboniferous limestone is about 1,600 feet. No angular unconformity was detected within the Paleozoic series of the Ray quadrangle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's paleoenvironmental interpretations are inferred from sedimentary character and fossils:

- **Scanlan conglomerate and Barnes conglomerate**: formed from materials on a weathered ancient surface during an advancing sea (Scanlan), or by stream action rather than littoral or marine processes (Barnes).
- **Pioneer shale**: judged marine and deposited in shallow water, though not fossiliferous, with no mud cracks observed.
- **Dripping Spring quartzite**: deposited in shallow water, sometimes exposed to air (ripple marks, sun cracks, worm casts), and tentatively interpreted as deltaic.
- **Troy quartzite**: pebbly, cross-bedded beds suggest fluvial or deltaic deposition; the upper part grades into marine Devonian beds.
- **Martin and Tornado limestones**: marine. The Devonian beds contain brachiopods and corals.
- **Pinal Schist**: the pre-Cambrian crystalline basement, named in 1903 after the Pinal Mountains. It is the principal country rock of the disseminated copper deposits at [[Ray Mine|Ray]] and Miami.

Diabase and [[Mescal Basalt|basalt]] intrusions and flows are common and locally alter the sedimentary sequence.

### Key Conclusions

- **Apache Group redefined**: the group now comprises the Scanlan conglomerate, Pioneer shale, Barnes conglomerate, Dripping Spring quartzite, Mescal limestone, and Troy quartzite. All are apparently conformable and provisionally assigned to the Cambrian. The earlier Globe report had lumped these with the Devonian and Carboniferous limestones as the "Globe limestone," because the Mescal limestone and Troy quartzite were not yet distinguished.
- **Devonian age**: Martin limestone fauna was assigned by E. M. Kindle to the Upper Devonian, based on 18 fossil lots and species such as *Atrypa reticularis* and *Productella hallana*. Kindle noted that Middle Devonian representation was possible but that Upper Devonian inclusion seems well established.
- **Carboniferous ages**: of seven collections in the Ray quadrangle, four are Mississippian and three are Pennsylvanian (determined by George H. Girty). The Mississippian corresponds to the [[Escabrosa Limestone]] at Bisbee, and the lower Pennsylvanian to the lower [[Naco Limestone]].
- **Correlation from Bisbee**: the Naco limestone (probable early Pennsylvanian) rests directly on the Escabrosa (early Mississippian). Girty noted that the Ray quadrangle conditions are similar.
- **Pennsylvanian faunal affinities**: Pennsylvanian faunas of the Ray and Bisbee areas relate closely to the [[Magdalena Limestone]] and [[Manzano Group]] of New Mexico. The upper Naco may correlate with the Manzano and the lower Naco with the Magdalena.
- **Santa Catalina section**: a post-Carboniferous siliceous muscovite granite and contact metamorphism have altered the sequence. The Scanlan (10–12 ft) and Barnes (~12 ft) conglomerates are recognized, with the Pioneer shale metamorphosed to sericite schist. The Dripping Spring, Mescal, and Troy formations are tentatively identified. Overlying red shales are unconformable, and their age is uncertain.
- **Clifton section**: about 1,500 ft of apparently conformable Paleozoic beds rest unconformably on Pinal schist. Units include the [[Coronado Quartzite]] (200 ft, section totaling 243 ft), [[Longfellow Limestone]] (200–400 ft, possibly uppermost Cambrian to early Ordovician), [[Morenci Shale]] (75 ft limestone + ~100 ft shale, tentatively Devonian), [[Modoc Limestone]] (180 ft, Mississippian), and [[Tule Spring Limestone]] (500 ft, Mississippian and Pennsylvanian).
- **Roosevelt section**: the Salt River gorge through the south end of the [[Mazatzal Range]] exposes beds from the Mississippian down to the granite. It is treated as an excellent reference section, and the Barnes conglomerate's quartzite source is located north of Roosevelt in the Sierra Ancha and Mazatzal Range.
- **Grand Canyon comparison**: the Kaibab limestone is 400–600 ft and the Coconino sandstone 250–350 ft (per the correlation chart). These contrast with the thinner, more varied succession at Globe and Ray.
