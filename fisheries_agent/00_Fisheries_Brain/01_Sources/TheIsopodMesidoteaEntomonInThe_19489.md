# Mesidotea entomon in the Chignik Lakes, Alaska

#ichthyology #marine-biology #idoteidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 study by David W. Narver, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, documents the lacustrine biology of the isopod *Mesidotea entomon* in the two [[Chignik Lakes]] of the Alaska Peninsula, based on sampling from 1961 to 1964. The isopod, usually described as a littoral marine species with circumpolar distribution, was found in abundance in Chignik Lake but at low densities in [[Black Lake]]. The study reports diel movement (benthic by day, pelagic at night), a probable postreproductive die-off, smaller size at maturity than Arctic marine populations, and differences in fecundity between the two lakes. Its primary context is the [[Sockeye Salmon]] lacustrine research of which it was a by-product.

## Background & Description

*Mesidotea entomon* (Linnaeus) is a North Atlantic and Arctic isopod previously known from marine littoral habitats and brackish waters such as the [[Baltic Sea]]. Prior reports include freshwater populations in six lakes in the central Swedish lowlands and four lakes on Victoria Island in the Canadian Arctic. Morphology of Chignik specimens matched published descriptions by Richardson (1905), Boone (1920) and McCrimmon and Bray (1962), except that Chignik animals tended to be smaller.

The Chignik system consists of two freshwater lakes on the south side of the Alaska Peninsula, about 270 km southwest of Kodiak Island:

- **Black Lake (upper lake):** maximum depth 6 m, surface area 43 km², elevation about 15 m, sand and silt bottom with organic detritus.
- **Chignik Lake (lower lake):** maximum depth 64 m, area 24 km², elevation about 5 m, steep shores, rubble and boulder bottom with gravel, silt and organic deposits. It drains into an estuarine lagoon.

Sampling relied mainly on a 5.2 m tow net (3.24 m² opening, fished in the top 4 m at 1.4 m/s), supplemented by night gill nets (26 sets), daytime bottom trawls, and fyke nets. A total of 1,599 isopods from Chignik Lake and 58 from Black Lake were sexed, staged and measured. Fecundity was counted in ovigerous females, and analysis of covariance was used for statistical comparisons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat associations:** In Chignik Lake the isopod was most abundant over organic and mud bottoms and where salmon carcasses accumulate. Around 100,000 to 200,000 sockeye carcasses are present in the lower lake each September and October. The largest trawl catches came from a spawning area used by sockeye.
- **Diel behaviour:** No isopods were caught in daytime tow-net samples in the top 4 m, whereas daytime trawl catches from mud bottoms were large. Divers observed animals moving over detritus in daylight. The related *Idothea neglecta* does not show this diel pattern.
- **Depth:** Night gill-net catches increased with depth (geometric mean 8.9 at 0–10 m rising to 70.0 at 31–40 m), although the largest tow-net catches occurred in the shallow area near the outlet.
- **Feeding:** The species is an omnivorous scavenger. Divers saw 10 to 15 isopods consuming a dead sculpin, and a 100–200 mm pygmy whitefish (*Prosopium coulteri*) was reduced to a skeleton in a 10-hour gill-net set. In the pelagial zone it also appears to feed on zooplankton, with broken copepods and cladoceran remains in stomachs.
- **Predation:** Few lake fish prey on large isopods. Juvenile [[Coho Salmon]] take small individuals, and [[Dolly Varden]] wintering in Chignik Lake may use isopods as winter food.
- **Regional distribution:** Related populations occur in [[Becharof Lake]] and [[Ugashik Lake]], where they are common in May fyke-net catches, and possibly in the Naknek system. Records are absent from the Kvichak and Nushagak systems.
- **Origin:** Populations may stem from postglacial marine invasion or be marine-glacial relicts, following the hypotheses of Lockwood and Croghan (1957) and Johnson (1964).

## Key Findings

- **Abundance:** From 1961 to 1964, night tow nets caught 5,033 isopods in Chignik Lake (347 tows) and 351 in Black Lake (174 tows). Catches differed significantly among years, statistical areas and time periods in Chignik Lake, but not among areas, times or years in Black Lake.
- **Spatial pattern:** Geometric mean catches per 10-min tow rose from inlet to outlet (9.1, 9.4, 13.2, 13.6, 16.7 across five areas). The author links this to environmental differences such as glacial turbidity at the upper end and organic deposits near the outlet.
- **Seasonal decline:** Abundance fell from early to late summer, probably because of postreproductive mortality. Exoskeletons were common on beaches in late summer.
- **Growth and sex differences:** Males probably grow faster than females. Both sexes appear to mature at age I+, with growth ending at maturity. Length frequencies showed little modal progression.
- **Size at maturity:** Mature males in Chignik Lake were 41–65 mm (mean 55) and females 29–47 mm (mean 39). In Black Lake, males were 40–46 mm (mean 43) and females 29–38 mm (mean 33). McCrimmon and Bray (1962) reported larger Arctic marine sizes (males 65–95 mm; females 50–79 mm).
- **Reproduction:** Mature animals were found from June 30 to September 6. Over 92% of mature females in 1962 and 1963 were postreproductive (empty brood pouch). Most breeding probably occurs in early spring, with some ovigerous females still present in September.
- **Fecundity:** Black Lake females had higher mean fecundity and a steeper fecundity–length slope than Chignik Lake females. Regression equations were Y = 158.5 + 8.41(X − 39.7) for Chignik Lake (1962–63) and Y = 179.3 + 14.00(X − 34.0) for Black Lake (1963), though the Black Lake sample was small.
- **Embryo loss:** Brood pouches averaged 158.6 ova but only 91.8 larvae in Chignik Lake females, suggesting embryo loss during development. Eggs averaged 1.17 mm and newly released larvae 3.9 mm.
- **Comparison with Arctic populations:** MacGinitie (1955) counted 482–776 larvae per female in mid-July at Point Barrow, versus a Chignik mean of 91.8. The largest Chignik female was 43 mm.
