# Seasonal Changes in Spatial Distribution and Activity of Two Species of Pacific Rockfishes, Sebastes flavidus and S. ciliatus, in Lynn Canal, Southeastern Alaska

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Sebastidae #behavioral-ecology #seasonal-patterns

## Executive Summary

This study documents seasonal behavioral patterns in mixed aggregations of [[Yellowtail Rockfish]] (*Sebastes flavidus*) and [[Dusky Rockfish]] (*S. ciliatus*) observed by divers in [[Lynn Canal]], southeastern [[Alaska]] from 1965-75. The rockfishes exhibited dramatic seasonal shifts in spatial distribution and activity levels correlated with water temperature changes. During warmer months (May-October), fish were active and distributed 1-several meters above substrate; during colder months (November-April), they retreated into crevices and wreckage, becoming sluggish and nearly inaccessible. This behavioral pattern likely represents an adaptive survival mechanism combining energy conservation during food scarcity with predator avoidance during periods of reduced mobility.

## Background & Description

The study focused on three observation sites in lower [[Lynn Canal]], 21-43 km north and west of [[Juneau]], [[Alaska]]: [[Point Lena]] (sunken vessel), [[Vanderbilt Reef]] (sunken vessel), and [[Point Terese]] (boulder-strewn slope base). All sites were at depths of 13-28 m. Observations were conducted by divers from the [[Auke Bay Fisheries Laboratory]] over 81 separate diving days spanning all months across the 10-year period.

The aggregations consisted primarily of two species: *[[Sebastes flavidus]]* (yellowtail rockfish) and *[[Sebastes ciliatus]]* (dusky rockfish). Previous research (Carlson and Haight, 1972) demonstrated that yellowtail rockfish from [[Point Lena]] exhibited strong site fidelity, returning to home sites even when displaced by 22 km, indicating very restricted home ranges for these aggregations.

Water temperature at 20 m depth near study sites ranged from annual minimum of 3°C in February to maximum of 9°C in August. Transitional periods (October-November and April-May) averaged approximately 6°C with relatively rapid temperature changes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The observed behavioral patterns appear linked to metabolic changes driven by water temperature fluctuations. The seasonal distribution shift likely serves multiple ecological functions:

**Energy Conservation**: During cold months when metabolic rates are reduced and food is scarce, the fish minimize energy expenditure by remaining inactive in sheltered locations.

**Predator Avoidance**: By retreating into crevices and wreckage during winter, rockfishes become nearly inaccessible to predators such as seals and sea lions (common in the area) during the period when reduced mobility would make them most vulnerable.

**Feeding Behavior**: During May-October, fish were actively feeding, as evidenced by vulnerability to baited hooks and observed chasing/biting of small objects dislodged by divers. This active feeding period corresponds to warmer water temperatures and presumably greater prey availability.

The study sites provided different types of shelter: natural boulder crevices at [[Point Terese]] versus artificial structure (sunken vessels) at [[Point Lena]] and [[Vanderbilt Reef]]. Despite habitat differences, seasonal behavioral patterns were consistent across sites.

## Key Findings

**Seasonal Activity Patterns** (Table 1 data from 81 diving days):
- **May-October (warm period)**: Fish present and active above substrate on 61 diving days; distributed 1-several meters above substrate while maintaining visual contact with bottom
- **November-April (cold period)**: Fish inactive in contact with substrate on 18 diving days; not seen on 23 diving days; never observed above substrate during this period
- Transitional behavior observed in April (1 day active, 1 day inactive) and November (4 days inactive, 2 days not seen)

**Behavioral Characteristics by Season**:

*Warm Months (May-October)*:
- Fish hovered or milled slowly in water column
- Swam slowly into current when present, maintaining position relative to substrate
- Actively feeding (vulnerable to baited hooks, chased dislodged objects)
- Would not allow physical contact by divers

*Cold Months (November-April)*:
- Retreated into crevices between boulders at [[Point Terese]]
- Moved deeper into vessel wreckage at [[Point Lena]] (both laterally and vertically)
- Inactive when undisturbed
- Sluggish response to disturbance
- Occasionally allowed physical contact by divers

**Transitional Periods**:
- November: Aggregations "disappeared from sight" as fish moved into shelter
- April: Fish gradually emerged from overwintering sites, initially still partly within vessels/crevices
- By May: Fish fully active and distributed above substrate

**Site-Specific Observations**:
- [[Point Terese]]: Fish clearly visible retreating into boulder crevices
- [[Point Lena]]: Fish progressively moved deeper into vessel through fall; no rockfishes found during November-April period during normal diving excursions
- [[Vanderbilt Reef]]: No dives conducted during November-April period

**Comparative Context**: Similar seasonal patterns documented in other [[Pacific Rockfish]] species:
- *[[Sebastes emphaeus]]* ([[Puget Sound Rockfish]]): Moulton (1975) noted dramatic decline in sightings in fall, with fish huddled in caves by November (61 observations over 12 months in [[Puget Sound]])
- *[[Sebastes caurinus]]* ([[Copper Rockfish]]): Patten (1973) found fish moved into rock pile in winter, with only 1-2 largest fish visible outside interstices
- *[[Sebastes mystinus]]* ([[Blue Rockfish]]): Miller and Geibel (1973) concluded fish overwintered in different habitat from summer kelp beds to avoid heavy winter swell

**Adaptive Significance**: The seasonal behavioral shift represents an essential survival mechanism combining energy conservation during food scarcity with predator inaccessibility during periods of greatest vulnerability (reduced mobility at low temperatures).
