# Razorback Sucker Reintroduction and Monitoring Program, Lower Colorado River (1986)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #razorback-sucker

## Executive Summary

This April 14, 1986 interagency memorandum, written by Linda Blythe of the California Department of Fish and Game (Region 5) to the Fisheries Management Supervisor, documents the stocking of about 1.5 million hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] fry into the [[Lower Colorado River]] and proposes a first-year monitoring program (April 1986 to March 1987). Fry were raised at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico and stocked in backwaters at three river divisions. The memo sets out stocking methods, site selection criteria, a detailed schedule of sampling gear, candidate rearing sites on tribal and refuge lands, and recommendations for long-term monitoring.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a native large-bodied fish of the Colorado River system that is being reintroduced in the lower river. The memo describes the species in terms of its early life stages and its habitat needs, but it does not give a full species description, a taxonomic classification, or adult size data beyond the measurements taken during monitoring.

The reintroduction was carried out with recommendations from [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] personnel from the Dexter hatchery. The memo notes that the species' habitat requirements at various life-history stages were poorly known, which complicated the choice of release sites.

**Stocking methods:**
- Fry were stocked on March 31, 1986, at the transition from yolk-dependent to active feeding, about one week after hatching.
- Transport water was well water at a stated density of 0.2 kg/lb (units as written in the source). Water temperature was given as 17–18 (units not stated, probably °C).
- No bactericides or anaesthetics were used. Fry were transported at night and stocked in the early afternoon.
- Transport water was brought to within 3–5°F of river temperature before stocking. Fry were carried in sealed, oxygenated bags placed in ice chests. The memo warns that water temperatures should not drop below about 50°F on long transports.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study divisions and site selection.** Three divisions of the lower Colorado River were chosen: the Parker Division (I and II), from Headgate Rock to Palo Verde Diversion Dam; the Topock Division, at [[Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]]; and the Imperial Division, at [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]. Selection criteria were:
1. Physical habitat resembling the historic unchanneled lower river.
2. Reliable water of suitable quantity and quality.
3. Adequate food supply.
4. Large numbers of high-turbidity backwaters with cover.
5. Relative safety from future channelization and other river modifications.

**Why backwaters.** The memo gives five reasons for preferring backwaters over the mainstream:
- Exotic predatory fish that prey on young razorbacks are less abundant in small backwaters.
- Stocking in upper sections may offset the downstream drift of hatchery fish seen in studies of other species.
- Backwaters are warmer and more productive for phytoplankton and zooplankton.
- Higher turbidity and submerged cover may protect young fish from predation.
- Monitoring surveys are more effective in backwaters.

**Hydrology.** Recent high flows had deepened the main channel and formed sandbars at the mouths of many backwaters in the Parker Division. At flows below about 20,000 cfs, many backwaters there are essentially cut off from the main channel. The large backwaters at [[Blankenshlp Bend Backwater]] and [[Devils Elbow Backwater]] stayed deep and flushed well at low flows.

**Productivity.** A [[Bio-West Inc.]] study for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] reported that the Blankenship Bend backwater was among the highest in primary productivity on the lower Colorado River. Devil's Elbow was also believed to be similar in productive capacity. The Parker Division was described as having high fish-species diversity but the lowest exotic-fish abundance, probably because of cold releases from Davis Dam and the flushing effect of narrow channel confines.

## Key Findings

**Monitoring design (first-year program, April 1986 to March 1987).** The memo lays out gear, frequency, and effort for each site:

- **[[Egg and Larval Net]] sampling:** monthly transects (April–June) in Havasu backwaters, coordinated with the striped bass study.
- **[[Trammel Net]] sampling:** every six weeks from June 1986. Havasu backwaters received three nets each, set from sunset to sunrise and checked every four hours. The Imperial sites used four to five nets, checked every three to four hours.
- **[[Gill Net]] sampling:** every six weeks alongside trammel nets, two nets per backwater, checked every two hours.
- **[[Q-Beam Light]] and plankton net sampling:** every 10 days in April and May, using hourly passes of 15 minutes per area to count phototactic larvae.
- **[[Electrofishing]]:** one night every two months (Havasu and Parker sites) or every four months (Island Lake). Only razorbacks were measured for growth studies.
- **[[Trap Net]] sampling:** juvenile gear trials at Devil's Elbow and Blankenship Bend from July 1986, followed by monthly trap netting at Island Lake from November 1986.

**Data handling.** Captured razorbacks were measured (total length and fork length), weighed, and released. Exotic fish were measured and weighed, and stomachs with food were preserved in 10% formalin. The stated goal at Island Lake was a minimum of 30 razorbacks for growth-curve estimates.

**Site-specific plans:**
- **[[Devils Elbow Backwater]] and [[Blankenship Bend Backwater]]:** Havasu sites with the full set of egg, trammel, gill, and electrofishing sampling.
- **Parker II backwater** (three acres, below Headgate Rock Diversion Dam, California side): gill and trammel nets, with fish handled as above.
- **CRIT Agricultural Drain** (about one acre): nightly Q-Beam and plankton sampling, plus trammel and gill nets. Sampling is to continue for growth-rate studies.
- **[[Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond]]:** same program as the Agricultural Drain. It may also be used to test mark-recapture methods for later population estimates, starting in November 1986.
- **[[Island Lake Backwater]]** (Imperial Division): Q-Beam, trammel, gill, electrofishing, and trap netting.
- **[[Palo Verde Point Backwater]]** (Imperial Division): Q-Beam sampling every 10 days, with one trammel net set per month from May 15, 1986.

**Colorado River Indian Reservation (CRIT) rearing ponds and backwaters:**
- Three one-acre borrow pits (Quail Mesa) under a Memorandum of Understanding need completion. Two need brush clearing and burning. The tribe agreed to supply bentonite to line them to prevent seepage.
- At least two ponds should be finished and stocked with razorbacks held at the Blythe office by July 1986. All three should receive fry in the March 1987 reintroduction.
- A five-acre isolated backwater south of Headgate Rock on the California side is a potential rearing area. It would need chemical removal of exotics and fertilization. A plan was proposed to build 50 brush shelters to improve sport fishing in an adjacent backwater.
- The author does not expect the CRIT to permit blocking river access to any reservation backwater. As an alternative, fry could be released in groups of hundreds to a few thousand across all reservation backwaters. The survival of this approach could be tested against the 1986 method using monitoring-survey data.

**Contingency sites.** A small backwater of one to five acres, blocked at the inlet and treated to remove exotics, could be used when low flows isolate backwaters. Primary productivity could be raised by fertilization. Coordination with the Havasu refuge manager would be required first.

**Imperial National Wildlife Refuge candidates.** Six backwaters were selected from aerial photographs taken November 13, 1985, for possible stocking or rearing. Each would need ground truthing and an assessment of whether its inlet or outlet could be blocked:
- **No. 1:** long, narrow backwater in the top half of the photo (California side). Access to the top quarter may be blockable.
- **No. 2:** about one-acre backwater with river access, in the lower half of the photo.
- **No. 3:** backwater below Picacho State Park in a major heron rookery, easy to renovate.
- **No. 4:** backwater on the California side, middle of the photo, that could be blocked and renovated.
- **No. 5:** backwater south of Cable Lake, with access to the river if any exists.
- **No. 6:** approximately 1/3-acre backwater at Smoketree Wash on the refuge.

**Long-term monitoring.** The memo concludes that recovery of stocked fry and fingerlings was not seen beyond the immediate post-stocking period. The authors attribute this to the large size of the river divisions and the small number of fry stocked relative to the area. Future work may include:
- Radiotelemetry to study micro- and macrohabitat use.
- Multi-ended beach seines and other methods currently used by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] to monitor razorback reintroductions on the Salt, Verde, and Gila rivers.
- Close coordination with Drs. Minckley and Marsh of Arizona State University and with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Endangered Species Office (Region 2).
