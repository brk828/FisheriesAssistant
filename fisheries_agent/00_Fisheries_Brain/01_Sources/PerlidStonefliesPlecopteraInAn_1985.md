## Executive Summary

This study by Sheldon (1985) examines the distributional ecology of perlid stoneflies (Plecoptera: Perlidae) across 56 sites in the Little River drainage of Great Smoky Mountains National Park (GSMNP), Tennessee. Although the taxon under investigation is an aquatic insect family rather than fish, the paper explicitly draws parallels to fish community structuring along elevational and stream-size gradients (citing [[Lennon And Parker 1960]] on the stoneroller *Campostoma anomalum*, and [[Barila Et Al 1981]] on fish diversity in the Susquehanna drainage). The core contribution is methodological: a multivariate approach combining monothetic divisive cluster analysis and stepwise discriminant analysis to classify stream sites into biological (species-association) groups based on easily map-derived habitat variables (elevation, stream linkage number, gradient). The resulting classification functions and nomogram allow prediction of macroinvertebrate (and by extension, potentially fish) community composition at unsampled stream sites, with direct applications to watershed mapping, sampling design, and environmental impact assessment.

## Background & Description

While the paper is not fish-focused, general morphological/life-history notes on the study taxon (Perlidae) are provided for context:

- **Size class**: Medium to large-bodied stonefly larvae — among the most conspicuous and frequently collected Plecoptera.
- **Coloration**: Often colorful larvae, aiding field recognition.
- **Feeding habits**: Predominantly carnivorous (predatory) throughout larval development.
- **Life cycle**: Typically 2 or more years where known, with adult emergence during warmer months.
- **Taxonomic complexity**: The family harbors cryptic species complexes distinguishable only via detailed examination of adult reproductive structures and eggs; larvae of many newly described species remain indistinguishable, complicating ecological studies (see [[Stark And Szczytko]], [[Picker 1980]], [[Zwick 1976]]).

Seven species were collected in this survey:
- *Acroneuria abnormis*
- *Acroneuria carolinensis*
- *Beloneuria sp.* (probable *B. stewarti*)
- *Eccoptura xanthenes*
- *Paragnetina immarginata*
- *Attaneuria ruralis* (singleton, excluded from analysis)
- *Perlesta frisoni* (singleton, excluded from analysis)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat amplitudes (elevation range in m; stream linkage number range):**

| Species | Elevation (m) | Stream Number |
|---|---|---|
| *Acroneuria abnormis* | 300–1000 | 1–310 |
| *Acroneuria carolinensis* | 500–700 | 16–118 |
| *Beloneuria* sp. | 600–1150 | 1–8 |
| *Eccoptura xanthenes* | 500–1100 | 1–118 |
| *Paragnetina immarginata* | 300–700 | 64–310 |

- **Elevational exclusion**: Perlids were entirely absent above 1150 m; a consistent gap in occurrence was noted between 1150–1230 m and again near 1550 m elsewhere in the drainage.
- **Dominant species**: *Acroneuria abnormis* was the most frequently collected and numerically dominant species overall, except in smaller, higher-elevation streams where *Eccoptura xanthenes* formed dense populations — indicating niche partitioning along the elevation/stream-size gradient.
- **Gradient correlation**: Stream gradient (0.4–29.6%) was highly correlated with both elevation and stream linkage number in this terrain, and thus provided no independent discriminatory power — elevation and stream size sufficed as predictors.
- **Comparative context**: The elevational zonation pattern parallels that documented for [[Stoneroller Minnow]] (*Campostoma anomalum*) and other fish species in the same watershed ([[Lennon And Parker 1960]]), as well as broader water-quality gradients (temperature, pH) described by [[Silsbee And Larson 1982]] for GSMNP streams. This supports the general principle that elevation, stream size, and gradient function as robust integrative proxies for the suite of physicochemical factors (temperature, velocity, substrate, water chemistry) that structure lotic faunal communities — a pattern also reported for fish species diversity relative to stream order ([[Barila Et Al 1981]]).
- **Rarity/specialization**: *Acroneuria carolinensis* was infrequent and locally scarce even within its predicted range, illustrating the challenge rare species pose to predictive habitat modeling (small-sample cells yield wide confidence limits).

## Key Findings

- **Cluster analysis**: A monothetic divisive classification (information statistic *I*; [[Lance And Williams 1968]] method) using presence/absence data produced a binary dendrogram of faunal associations. The primary division was driven by presence/absence of *A. abnormis*; secondary divisions resolved further groups based on *Paragnetina*, *Beloneuria*, and *Eccoptura* occurrences. Six terminal groups were retained for analysis.

- **Discriminant analysis performance**:
  - Two-group split: 89% classification reliability
  - Three-group split: 75% reliability
  - Six-group split: 55% reliability
  - **Elevation** was consistently the primary discriminating habitat variable; **stream (linkage) number** contributed significantly at the three- and six-group levels; **gradient** never contributed independently due to strong collinearity with elevation/stream size.
  - Overall discrimination was statistically significant (P < 0.005) in all analyses; pairwise group separation was significant in two- and six-group cases except for two subgroup pairs at the six-group level (groups a/b and c/d were not significantly distinct, P > 0.10).

- **Predictive nomogram (Fig. 4)**: Classification functions (Table 1 coefficients for the six-group case) were mapped onto elevation × stream number space, producing separation-function boundaries usable to predict the most probable species association at unsampled sites. Each map cell carries two probabilities: a **classification rate** (probability a given faunal group falls within that environmental cell) and a **prediction rate** (proportion of sites in that cell actually holding the predicted fauna) — analogous to Type I/II error framing.

- **Management applications**: The elevation/stream-size/gradient framework, being derivable purely from topographic maps, enables cost-effective watershed mapping, optimized sampling design, management prescription, and detection of anomalous (impacted) sites that deviate from model predictions — with direct relevance to broader lotic ecosystem management, including fisheries contexts (cf. [[Platts 1979]], [[Tonn Et Al 1983]] on predicting fish fauna composition in Wisconsin lakes).

- **Methodological caveats**: 
  - Monothetic clustering carries elevated misclassification risk relative to polythetic methods.
  - Sampling intensity was insufficient to fully validate predictions (no independent validation performed); the author recommends stratified/systematic sampling designs and data-withholding validation approaches, as successfully applied to fish community prediction by [[Tonn Et Al 1983]].
  - The classification-discrimination sequence is argued to be statistically superior to ordination-based approaches for defining habitat-community associations in lotic systems ([[Green And Vascotto 1978]]).

- **Broader taxonomic context**: Additional undescribed/rare perlid taxa (e.g., *Hansonoperla appalachia*, *Paragnetina ichusa*) are known from the region but were not captured in this survey, suggesting either sampling limitations or genuine habitat specialization/rarity not resolved at this sampling intensity.
