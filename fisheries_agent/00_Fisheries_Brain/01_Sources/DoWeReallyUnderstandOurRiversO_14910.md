# Do We Really Understand Our Rivers? or Rivers in the Pooh - Semper in Excreta!

#ichthyology #marine-biology #river-ecology #fluvial-geomorphology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1996 Presidential Address to the Institute of Australian Geographers is a geomorphological and interdisciplinary critique of how poorly rivers are understood as integrated biophysical systems. Robin F. Warner argues that sustainable river management requires simultaneous understanding of (1) channel airspace/water dynamics, (2) channel–floodplain perimeter conditions, (3) ecological components (flora and fauna, including fish), and (4) human impacts on catchments. While the paper is not a fish-specific study, it discusses aquatic fauna — including native and exotic fish, crustaceans, plankton, and microfauna — as sensitive indicators of river health, vulnerable to habitat destruction, pollutant bioaccumulation, and hydrological disruption from human catchment modification. The paper's central conclusion is that rivers are inadequately understood across physical, ecological, and human dimensions, and that this knowledge gap undermines sustainable management, sometimes leading to degraded aquatic habitats ("rivers in the pooh").

## Background & Description

The address synthesizes fluvial geomorphology, hydrology, and river ecology, framed around the concept of the **valley-floor trough** — the combined channel and floodplain system that acts as a conduit for water, sediment, and pollutants toward the sea or internal base levels.

Key conceptual elements:
- **Channel airspace and water**: Rivers alternate between subaerial (dry, air-filled) and subaqueous (wet, water-filled) states, with capacity for conveyance determined by cross-sectional area and flow velocity.
- **Perimeter conditions**: The channel boundary is dynamic, with lateral migration, bank erosion/accretion, and sediment reworking altering the age and structure of alluvial deposits. Bed conditions vary systematically downstream from **armoured** to **transitional** to **mobile** to **backwater** reaches ([[Pickup 1986 Bed Classification]]).
- **Ecological components**: River zones support complex, often fragile flora and fauna assemblages linked to physical channel dynamics, riparian vegetation corridors, and flow regime variability.
- **Human components**: Catchment modification (urbanization, agriculture, mining, deforestation) has dramatically altered runoff, sediment, and pollutant delivery to channels, creating "time bombs" of stored contaminants that will be released by future erosion.

The paper references case studies from the **Hawkesbury-Nepean**, **Sydney Basin**, **Murray-Darling Basin**, and tropical river systems such as the **Fly and Purari Rivers (Papua New Guinea)** and **Ok Tedi (PNG mining impacts)**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat and Faunal Corridors
- Riparian vegetation corridors have been severely degraded by clearing field boundaries to bank tops or water's edge for stock access, and by aesthetic removal of trees near homesteads — undermining habitat and shade for both terrestrial and aquatic fauna.
- Introduction of **exotic willows** (a "British" aesthetic preference) has further compromised native riparian corridor integrity in Australian rivers.
- **[[Exotic Fish Species]]** introduced "for food and sport" are identified as a direct stressor on native aquatic fauna survival, compounding physical habitat loss.

### Flow Regime and Faunal Dynamics
- Riverine ecologies must accommodate natural high/low flow stages and periodic drying, especially in arid environments — but anthropogenic catchment changes have altered these natural regimes by increasing runoff, sediment yield, and pollutant loads.
- Barriers such as heavy sedimentation, weirs, and dams disrupt **migratory life forms** that depend on free passage.
- Regulation of flows for irrigation, urban water extraction, and cooling further modifies the natural flow regime that supports aquatic ecological function.

### Pollution and Bioaccumulation Threats
- **Nutrient loading** (phosphates, nitrates) is identified as posing the greatest threat to river ecosystems, driving deoxygenation, eutrophication, and algal blooms — with the Sydney **Centennial Park ornamental ponds** cited as a case study of chronic algal bloom problems requiring biomanipulation remediation.
- **Sediment loading** can destroy subaqueous habitats and impair photosynthesis-driven productivity.
- **Bacterial loads** threaten both human health (drinking water, body-contact recreation) and aquatic "edible life forms," with onward risk to human consumers and other infected organisms.
- **[[Bioaccumulation]]** of heavy metals and radionuclides in **crustaceans and fish** is described as slow but potentially lethal, and represents a direct threat to human protein sources (Miskiewicz, 1992).
- Toxicological impacts are noted at contaminated sites such as **Ok Tedi** (mining waste, PNG) and downstream of the **Ranger uranium mine** (Alligator Rivers Region, Northern Australia).
- **Microfauna and plankton** changes are highlighted as ecologically critical but historically under-detected compared to more visible physical channel changes — despite their foundational role in aquatic food chains.

### Sydney Waterways Case Study
- An estimated **30% of Sydney's sewage** was found not to reach treatment works (Water Board, 1994), compounding pollutant loads during wet weather when sewage overflows link previously isolated dry-weather pollution pockets into a continuous polluted flow.
- Natural channels have some capacity to assimilate pollutants via long, intermittent residence times, but **stormwater drains and concreted/lined channels** eliminate this natural buffering, delivering concentrated pollutant pulses directly and rapidly to receiving waters on every rain event (versus natural pulse frequency of ~6–10 times/year).

## Key Findings

- **Rivers are not purely physical systems**: A full understanding of riverine biophysical processes requires integration of engineering, geomorphology, and ecology — disciplines rarely combined in individual practitioners or managers.
- **Human impact is now the dominant geomorphic driver**: Catchment modification (deforestation, cropping, grazing, urbanization, mining) mobilizes excess water, sediment, and waste that overwhelm natural channel and floodplain buffering capacity.
- **"Time-bomb" contamination stores**: Buried waste in urban and mining landscapes constitutes a delayed-release threat, will inevitably be exposed by future erosion, and will deliver contaminants into aquatic/fluvial environments.
- **Ecological knowledge lags physical knowledge**: Subtle biological changes (microfauna, plankton, aquatic invertebrate and fish community shifts) have historically been detected and addressed later than visible physical changes to channels — often too late to prevent faunal loss.
- **Bioaccumulation risk chain**: Heavy metals and radionuclides move from sediments into aquatic food chains via crustaceans and fish, ultimately threatening human consumers who rely on these protein sources.
- **Salinity as an ecological and agricultural threat**: Irrigation in the Murray and Murrumbidgee systems has triggered geological salinity "time-bombs," degrading soil and water productivity (Banens et al., 1995).
- **Regulatory and management gaps**: The NSW Government (1994) explicitly acknowledged that river management is constrained by insufficient knowledge of how river regulation and water diversion affect the riverine ecological corridor, and there is little practitioner agreement on how altered flow regimes affect riverine ecology.
- **Channel engineering removes natural pollutant assimilation capacity**: The shift from natural to lined/concrete channels eliminates residence-time-based pollutant stripping, worsening downstream ecological and water-quality outcomes.
- **Interdisciplinary and applied research is essential**: The founding of the journal *Regulated Rivers* (edited by G. Petts) and collaborative forums (e.g., Hawkesbury-Nepean Trust, SISORS conferences) are cited as positive steps toward bridging geomorphological and ecological understanding of river systems.
- **Overall conclusion**: The author concludes rivers are not fully understood as integrated systems, and improved, applied, interdisciplinary understanding of physical–ecological interactions is essential to prevent rivers from being figuratively (and sometimes literally) "in the pooh."
