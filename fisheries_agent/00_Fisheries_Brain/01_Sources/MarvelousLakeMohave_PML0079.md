# Lake Mohave: Recreational Fishery and Development Overview

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This promotional insert from a wildlife and sportsman's magazine series profiles [[Lake Mohave]], an artificial reservoir on the [[Colorado River]] between [[Davis Dam]] and [[Hoover Dam]]. It describes the lake's history, its fish community, angling conditions, resort facilities, and a planned recreation development. The text is a magazine feature, not a research study, so it reports observations from fisheries biologists and anglers rather than measured survey data.

## Background & Description

- **Construction and filling:** Engineers first considered Pyramid Canyon as a dam site in 1903. Construction began in 1941 but was interrupted by World War II. Storage began in January 1950, and the project reached full completion in spring 1953.
- **Setting:** The lake lies in a dry, rugged desert of the lower Sonoran life zone. The area receives an average of five inches of rain per year. Wildlife mentioned includes [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]], beaver, rabbits, porcupines, woodrats, kangaroo rats, antelope squirrels, waterfowl, and occasional deer.
- **Management:** The lake is managed jointly by Nevada and Arizona. A master plan for recreation was funded with $25,000 from the [[National Park Service]]. The Park Service estimated that completing all proposed projects would cost about $1,984,000, excluding concessioner spending. The text describes most of these projects as still "in the dream stage."
- **Access and resorts:** Four resorts are described:
  - [[Lake Mohave Resort]] (Arizona side, near [[Katherine Wash]]), the most developed, with air-conditioned motel units and a cafe.
  - [[Cottonwood Cove]] (Nevada side, formerly Searchlight Ferry), recently under new ownership with plans for expansion.
  - [[Emery's Landing]] (Nevada side, near Eldorado Canyon), operated by Murl Emery.
  - [[Willow Beach]], the northernmost resort, reached by road from Las Vegas and Kingman and known as the center of trout fishing on the lake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water fluctuation:** Water-level changes are central to the fishery. A heavy drawdown of 20 to 25 feet below optimum operating level kills underwater plants. This reduces the shrimp, crayfish, and snails that trout feed on, and trout numbers declined sharply as a result.
- **Temperature gradient:** Water released from Hoover Dam is about 55 to 56°F year-round. The southern portion can reach 80°F in summer. A "cold water front" separates these zones and shifts seasonally. In summer it lies near the chalk cliffs above Emery's Landing, and in winter it moves about a mile to a mile and a half below Emery's. Rainbow trout generally stay north of the front.
- **Competition dynamics:** Drawdown in spring and early summer reduces competition between bass and bluegill spawning. The text states that if the lake stayed near optimum level, bluegill would dominate in about five years.
- **Downstream habitat:** The river below Davis Dam has become productive trout water. About ten miles of river below the dam supported good trout catches, and biologists expected the stretch to extend by a couple of miles as rubble scoured from the bottom.
- **Native fishes:** Bonytail, razorback sucker, and Colorado squawfish are the three native fishes of the Colorado River. Squawfish are described as practically extinct in Lake Mohave due to rising water but still found in considerable numbers below Davis Dam. Bonytails are described as forage fish for largemouth bass and as unwanted competitors for stocked trout fry. Razorback suckers occur throughout the lake but are seldom caught because of their bottom-feeding habits.

## Key Findings

**Fish community (from the text, no survey counts reported):**
- Trout once made up as much as 95 percent of the total catch, but fluctuating water levels reduced rainbow numbers substantially. Trout were first stocked in the Colorado River below Hoover Dam in 1938, and the first trout were caught at Willow Beach that year.
- Record rainbow trout: an 18-plus pound fish from Willow Beach. Biologists had recently tagged fish weighing up to 16 pounds in the same area.
- Largemouth bass increased greatly in number under the fluctuating water regime. Fishing was described as "terrific" that year. Creel checks showed catches per pound were keeping pace with those on [[Lake Mead]].
- Crappie are increasing because of favorable spawning conditions and are often caught incidentally on small bass lures.
- Channel catfish were previously rare but are increasing, with good catches reported by Murl Emery.
- Bluegill are increasing, and green sunfish (distinguished by a white edge on the gill covers) are also present.
- Bullheads are still uncommon but increasing in the lower lake. A brown bullhead was collected in a gill net.
- Carp are not yet a major problem but are expected to become one. They average about six pounds, with occasional fish up to 20 pounds.
- The Nevada Game and Fish Commission planned to stock [[Threadfin Shad]] as future forage for largemouth bass.

**Angling guidance:**
- Trolling or drift fishing is recommended because trout density is low and anglers must cover much water.
- Surface plugging is productive only in late spring and early fall. Otherwise, plugging or bait fishing at 15 to 20 feet is better.
- For larger fish, regulars recommend drift fishing close to shore and deep trolling or casting in the coves.
- Licensing: a state license costs $5, and a Special Use Stamp from the other state costs $2 for boat fishing. Both are valid for the entire year.

**Development outlook:**
- The Park Service master plan envisions campsites, tree planting, roads, vacation homesites, and new concessions, including a proposed resort on the Nevada side across from Fire Mountain and a second site across from the Searchlight Ferry landing. The text notes that most projects remain unfunded.
- The lake's recreation area was extended to include Lake Mohave as part of the broader Lake Mead recreation area.
