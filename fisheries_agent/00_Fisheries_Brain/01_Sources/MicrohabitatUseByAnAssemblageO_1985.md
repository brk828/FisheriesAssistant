## Executive Summary

This 1985 study by [[Peter B. Moyle]] and [[Donald M. Baltz]] examined microhabitat use (depth, velocity, substrate) by an assemblage of eight native California stream fishes in [[Deer Creek]], a relatively unmodified tributary of the [[Sacramento River]] in Tehama County, California. The researchers used underwater snorkel observation and electrofishing across three distinct stream reaches (valley, foothill, mountain) that differed in elevation, temperature regime, and gradient. Each species and size class exhibited a distinct but broadly overlapping microhabitat preference. Although habitat availability differed among reaches, these differences did not fully explain observed differences in fish assemblage composition — implicating other factors, especially temperature regime, as important determinants of species distribution. The study's core contribution is methodological: it argues that instream flow criteria (such as those used in the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]]) must be based on site-specific microhabitat use *and* availability data, collected across whole fish communities (not just game species), and interpreted cautiously due to confounding effects of interspecific competition and temperature.

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily an ecological/behavioral microhabitat study rather than a morphological one, it references species-specific morphological distinctness enabling reliable underwater visual identification (Moyle 1976). Size classes were defined by standard length (SL) and used as proxies for major life-history stages:

- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Salmo gairdneri*): young-of-year (<50 mm SL), juvenile (51–119 mm SL), adult (≥120 mm SL)
- **[[Sacramento Sucker]]** (*Catostomus occidentalis*): same 3-class scheme as rainbow trout
- **[[Sacramento Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus grandis*): 2 classes, split at 160 mm SL
- **[[Hardhead]]** (*Mylopharodon conocephalus*): 2 classes, split at 160 mm SL
- **[[California Roach]]** (*Hesperoleucus symmetricus*): 2 classes, split at 40 mm SL
- **[[Riffle Sculpin]]** (*Cottus gulosus*): 2 classes, split at 40 mm SL
- **[[Tule Perch]]** (*Hysterocarpus traski*): 2 classes, split at 40 mm SL
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): 2 classes, split at 30 mm SL

Introduced species (green sunfish, smallmouth bass, brown trout) were excluded from size-class analysis due to small sample sizes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Study Site Characteristics
Three reaches of [[Deer Creek]] were surveyed, differing markedly in gradient, elevation, and summer thermal regime:

| Reach | Elevation (m) | Gradient (m/km) | Summer Temp (°C) | Species Richness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valley | 9–120 | ~11 | 19–32 | 10 |
| Foothill | 510–530 | ~20 | 14–20 | 6 |
| Mountain | 1,010–1,060 | 27 | 13–18 | 3 |

The **valley reach** hosted the classic warmwater Sacramento Valley fish assemblage (sucker, squawfish, roach, dace, hardhead, tule perch, riffle sculpin, minor trout). The **mountain reach** was dominated by [[Rainbow Trout]] and introduced brown trout, with riffle sculpin rare and all cyprinids/catostomids absent. The **foothill reach** was intermediate (6 species), characterized by deep bedrock pools connected by high-gradient riffles.

### Microhabitat Use Patterns
- All species occupied a wide range of depth/velocity/substrate conditions, implying substantial potential for direct interspecific interaction/spatial overlap.
- Young-of-year and juveniles generally used shallower, slower water than adults; ontogenetic shifts toward deeper, faster water with increasing size were significant (P<0.05) for most species/size class comparisons of total depth and mean water column velocity.
- Relative depth (position in water column) was consistent within species regardless of size class — meaning depth preference scales with fish size but vertical positioning behavior remains stable.
- Focal point velocities (velocity at the fish's snout) were consistently much lower than ambient mean water column or surface velocities — fish exploit velocity refugia, likely via boundary-layer/substrate-adjacent positioning.
- Adult [[Rainbow Trout]], adult [[Sacramento Sucker]], adult [[Speckled Dace]], and both juvenile/adult [[Riffle Sculpin]] preferred faster water (mean surface velocity >40 cm/s).
- Substrate use converged heavily on cobble/boulder across nearly all species; finer substrates (sand, silt) were rare in the stream but strongly selected when available (positive electivity) by most species except rainbow trout. Boulder/bedrock was generally avoided except by adult tule perch and Sacramento sucker.
- Species occupying highest position in water column: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Hardhead]], [[Sacramento Squawfish]], [[Tule Perch]]. Species in near-continuous bottom contact: [[Sacramento Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Riffle Sculpin]].

### Interspecific Interactions
- High microhabitat overlap among species suggests competitive interactions are possible, but differing feeding habits likely reduce direct competition (see [[Niche Partitioning]]).
- [[Baltz Et Al 1982]] demonstrated that [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Riffle Sculpin]] compete directly for riffle habitat, with sculpin competitively excluding dace at low temperatures but losing dominance as temperatures rise — a temperature-mediated competitive switch.
- [[Baltz And Moyle 1984]] found no evidence of microhabitat competition between [[Sacramento Sucker]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] in other Sierra Nevada systems, illustrating that competitive outcomes are context/stream-specific.
- Presence of apparently suitable but unoccupied habitat in certain reaches suggests factors beyond physical microhabitat (e.g., temperature, biotic interactions, population dynamics) constrain species distributions — supporting the role of **macrohabitat variables**, especially temperature, in shaping community composition.

## Key Findings

1. **Distinct but overlapping microhabitat niches**: Every species and life-history stage selected a statistically distinct microhabitat (by depth, velocity, substrate), yet these niches broadly overlapped among co-occurring species, indicating space is not typically a strongly limiting resource in [[Deer Creek]].

2. **Ontogenetic microhabitat shifts**: Younger fish (young-of-year, juveniles) consistently occupied shallower, lower-velocity microhabitats than adults across nearly all species; vertical position in the water column (relative depth) remained stable within species regardless of size.

3. **Habitat availability alone does not explain assemblage composition**: Although the three study reaches differed in available depth/velocity/substrate distributions, these differences were insufficient to explain why certain species were entirely absent from reaches with seemingly suitable physical habitat — implicating temperature regime as a likely primary driver.

4. **Temperature as a key mediator of species distribution and competition**: Prior work (Baltz et al. 1982) showed sculpin-dace competitive dominance reverses with temperature; the authors argue analogous temperature effects likely explain broader patterns of species turnover along the elevational/thermal gradient of Deer Creek.

5. **Focal point velocity as a key adaptive metric**: Fish consistently positioned themselves in lower-velocity microhabitat directly around their bodies relative to ambient flow, indicating selection for energetically efficient stations (consistent with [[Fausch 1984]] profitable-position theory for salmonids).

6. **Implications for Instream Flow Incremental Methodology (IFIM)**: The authors caution against building habitat suitability curves from raw use data alone; such curves must be corrected against habitat availability, developed on-site (or in comparable streams), incorporate population density effects, include macrohabitat variables like temperature, and consider whole fish communities rather than only game species. This directly critiques the standard [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] framework used by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

7. **Interannual and inter-stream variability**: Citing [[Moyle And Vondracek 1985]], the authors note fish abundance can vary 5–21× between years, which can shift microhabitat use patterns (e.g., fish occupying marginal habitats during high-abundance years), complicating the reliability of single-year suitability curve data for long-term flow management decisions.
