# Late Cenozoic Volcanic and Tectonic Events Along the Western North American Plate

#geology #tectonics #plate-tectonics

## Executive Summary

This geoscience paper documents well- to relatively well-dated volcanic and tectonic events along the western margin of the [[North American Plate]]. Many events began, ended, or intensified at three time horizons: 16 ± 1 m.y. B.P., 10 ± 2 m.y. B.P., and 5 ± 1 m.y. B.P. The authors relate the 16 Ma events to upwelling of an elongated [[Mantle Diapir]] and to the approach of the [[East Pacific Rise]]. They relate the 10 Ma events to a change in [[Pacific Plate]] spreading direction and increased relative plate motion. They relate most 5 Ma events to the opening of the [[Gulf of California]]. The paper does not address aquatic organisms, habitats, or water chemistry. Its relevance to aquatic science is indirect, through marine tectonics and oceanic plate reorganization.

## Background & Description

The paper is a regional synthesis of [[Cenozoic Tectonics]] and [[Arc Volcanism]] along the western edge of North America. It compiles prior radiometric dating and plate-reconstruction studies. It is organized around three time horizons and presents maps for each (Figures 2–4). It also includes a present-day map of [[Crustal Stress]] in western North America (Figure 1) for comparison with earlier regimes.

Before 16 m.y. B.P., subduction of the [[Farallon Plate]] dominated the region. Volcanism migrated inland to western Montana, Wyoming, and Colorado, which implies a shallow slab dip. Contact of the [[East Pacific Rise]] with the margin at about 30 m.y. B.P. initiated a transform-fault boundary between the Pacific and North American plates. The [[Farallon Plate]] fragmented into remnant plates, including the [[Guadalupe Plate]] to the south.

## Key Findings

**Timing of events**

1. Significant volcanic and tectonic events along the western margin clustered at **16 ± 1 m.y. B.P.**, **10 ± 2 m.y. B.P.**, and **5 ± 1 m.y. B.P.**
2. Pulses of arc magmatism in the [[Cascade Arc]] and [[Central American Arc]] peaked at these times. Strong peaks occurred at 16–14 m.y. B.P.

**16 ± 1 m.y. B.P.: mantle upwelling and reorganization**

3. The authors attribute continental events to a crustal thermal disturbance from an elongated mantle upwelling. The upwelling produced volcanism unrelated to subduction from central British Columbia to northern Mexico.
4. Spreading rates at the [[Mid-Atlantic Ridge]] and the East Pacific Rise did not change noticeably at this time. The authors therefore conclude that the volcanic-tectonic episode was driven by forces other than plate-motion changes.
5. Plateau basalts erupted from fissures. The [[Columbia River Basalt]] was the most voluminous, erupting mostly between about 17 and 13.5 m.y. B.P. The [[Steens Basalt]] erupted at 15.2–14.9 m.y. B.P.
6. Bimodal volcanism in the [[Snake River Plain]] began at about 16–15 m.y. B.P. and migrated southeastward until about 10 m.y. B.P.
7. Strike-slip faults became active, including the [[Las Vegas Shear Zone]] (40–72 km of right-lateral offset) and the [[Lake Mead Fault Zone]] (about 60–65 km of strike slip, between 15 and 10.7 m.y. B.P.).
8. The [[Guadalupe Plate]] began fragmenting at about 17 m.y. B.P. The [[Cocos Plate]] separated from it by about 12 m.y. B.P. The spreading half-rate off Baja California changed from 4.3 to 3.0 cm/yr at 17 m.y. B.P.

**10 ± 2 m.y. B.P.: change in Pacific plate motion**

9. The Pacific plate spreading direction changed from east-west to northwest-southeast. The authors link this to clockwise rotation of the regional stress field by about 25°–45°.
10. Slip across the [[San Andreas Fault]] increased significantly at about 10 m.y. B.P. Some dextral shear was accommodated by the [[San Gregorio-Hosgri Fault]], which was active at the same time.
11. Strike-slip motion ceased across three of the four major shear zones that became active at 16 Ma. Uplift of the southern [[Colorado Plateau]] began between 10 and 8 m.y. B.P.
12. The Guadalupe Plate ceased independent existence and subduction beneath Baja California ended at 10 m.y. B.P. A triple junction formed by then.
13. Arc magmatism at the [[Aleutian Arc]] stopped at about 10 m.y. B.P. Cascade and Central American pulses at 11–8 m.y. B.P. were weaker than those at 16 Ma, despite increased Pacific plate motion.

**5 ± 1 m.y. B.P.: Gulf of California opening**

14. The Pacific-North American boundary jumped eastward between 5 and 4 m.y. B.P., and sea-floor spreading opened the [[Gulf of California]].
15. Spreading across the [[Gorda Ridge]] slowed from 5 to 2–3 cm/yr at about 6 m.y. B.P., and sediment subduction stopped.
16. The [[San Andreas Fault]] slip rate increased from 4 to 5.5 cm/yr at about 4.5 m.y. B.P.
17. Convergence rates slowed: Juan de Fuca–North America from 4.5 to 3.5 cm/yr, and Explorer–North America from 4 to 1.9 cm/yr (between 5 and 4 m.y. B.P.).
18. Arc volcanism increased in the Cascade and Central American arcs at about 5 ± 1 m.y. B.P. Rift-type volcanism began in northern British Columbia at 6 m.y. B.P.

**Interpretation and outlook**

19. Pulses of volcanism do not appear to be directly or solely related to changes in plate motion. The authors propose that arc magmatism and some intraplate volcanism are sensitive indicators or precursors of tectonic transition intervals.
20. Because the current strong pulses at [[Yellowstone]] and in the Cascades are less than 2 m.y. old, the authors suggest the region may presently be in a tectonic transition interval.
