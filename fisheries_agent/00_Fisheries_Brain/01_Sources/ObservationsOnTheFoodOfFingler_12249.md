# Observations on the Food of Fingerling Largemouth Bass in Clear Lake, Lake County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by George W. McCammon, Don LaFaunce and Charles M. Seeley of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] Inland Fisheries Branch, compares four August food habit studies of young-of-the-year [[Largemouth Bass]] (fingerlings) in [[Clear Lake]]. The 1948 study had proposed that the [[Sacramento Blackfish]] was the most important forage fish, and that the commercial blackfish fishery should be banned. The three later studies (1956, 1957, 1958) were designed to test that conclusion. The authors found that blackfish were not regularly important forage, that [[Bluegill]] were the dominant fish prey in later years, and that prey size and availability matter more than species preference. They also stress that short, single-site collections support only limited management conclusions.

## Background & Description

Clear Lake is a 40,000-surface-acre seminatural lake in central Lake County at an elevation of 1,325 feet. It supports native and exotic warmwater fishes. Since 1948, management has sought to improve the largemouth bass fishery through several steps:

- A minimum size limit for largemouth bass was removed.
- Night fishing for all species was allowed.
- Native cyprinid forage populations were protected, and the commercial blackfish fishery was banned in September 1948.
- A nonnative forage fish, the [[Golden Shiner]], was introduced in 1950 to supplement native minnows.
- A limited commercial harvest of mature blackfish was permitted in 1954 after an apparent population increase.

The management hypothesis came from [[Murphy 1951]], who linked the survival of bass-of-the-year to the strength of each year's cyprinid forage crop and proposed that augmenting forage would raise the bass population. The 1948 food study (Murphy, 1949) supported this, identifying blackfish as the most important prey of young bass over 2.8 inches fork length.

### Methods

- **Collection:** A 40- by 6-foot, 4-inch bar mesh [[Seine Netting]] was used along the northwest shore between Lakeport and Rodman Slough.
- **Dates:** August 10–12, 1948; August 13–14, 1956; August 21–22, 1957; August 21–22, 1958.
- **Selection:** Size selection was used in 1948 to obtain fish of all sizes. The later collections were not size-selected.
- **Preservation:** Specimens were preserved in formalin. In 1957 and 1958, fish were suffocated in water first to reduce regurgitation.
- **Analysis:** Fork length was recorded. Prey were identified, and unidentifiable fish remains were cleared and stained with potassium hydroxide and alizarin red S ([[Stomach Content Analysis]]). Frequency of occurrence, volume (water displacement) and, in 1957 and 1958, item counts were recorded. Counts of some items, such as cladocerans, were estimated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Length Distributions

Sample sizes and mean fork lengths were:

| Year | Fish collected | Mean fork length (in) |
|---|---|---|
| 1948 | 259 | 2.5 |
| 1956 | 251 | 2.1 |
| 1957 | 224 | 3.0 |
| 1958 | 261 | 3.6 |

The 1948 sample was probably biased toward larger fish by size selection. The authors note that prey choice is strongly tied to these size differences.

### Diet Shift with Size

All four studies showed a clear transition from invertebrates to fish as bass grew. Crustaceans and insects dominated smaller bass, and fish became dominant later. The shift length differed among years:

- 1948: about 3.0 inches
- 1956: about 2.5 inches
- 1957: about 3.0 inches
- 1958: about 2.0 inches

Larger bass ate more fish and smaller bass ate more insects and crustaceans. Fish made up 56 percent of food volume in 1956 and nearly 99 percent in 1958. Comparable switches occurred elsewhere. In [[Lake George]], Minnesota, bass of 20–29.9 mm contained fish remains ([[Kramer and Smith]], 1960). At [[Fort Randall Reservoir]], South Dakota, fish became the primary food after bass reached 2.5 inches (Nelson, 1962). The authors conclude that the limiting factors are the size and availability of forage species.

### Prey Composition

- **Blackfish:** In 1948, blackfish made up 65 percent of fish eaten and 54 percent of total food volume. They were absent from the 1956 and 1957 collections and appeared once in 1958.
- **Bluegill:** Only about 5 percent of fish and about 4 percent of total food in 1948. In each later study, bluegills were the majority of fish eaten and the single most important item.
- **Cyprinids:** Important in 1948 (blackfish 54.2 percent and carp 1.4 percent of volume) and again in 1958 (15.1 percent of volume). One-third of the 1958 cyprinid volume came from a single 2.5-inch blackfish eaten by a 5.0-inch bass.
- **Sculpins (Cottus):** Important in all years except 1958, when they made up 1.5 percent of volume. Because [[White Catfish]] also eat sculpins, the authors suggest they may be a consistently used forage fish.
- **Crustaceans:** Minor in most years. In 1956, cladocerans made up 11 percent and amphipods 16 percent of volume, which fits the small mean size of bass that year.
- **Insects:** Dipteran larvae and pupae were the bulk of insect food in all years except 1948, when water boatmen (Corixidae) were most important.
- **Cannibalism:** Only one case, a bass eaten in 1958.

## Key Findings

1. **Blackfish are not regularly important forage.** Blackfish were found in only one stomach after 1948, so the authors conclude they are not a consistent prey item for fingerling bass.
2. **Bluegills are important forage.** The 1948 conclusion that bluegill fry were unimportant conflicts with the three later studies and with work at [[Salt Springs Valley Reservoir]], [[Lake Havasu]], and [[Millerton Lake]]. Bluegills spawn late, so many small bluegills are present when bass switch to fish diets.
3. **Size and availability drive prey choice more than preference.** Species preference may play a part when prey are equally available, as in laboratory work by [[Lewis et al. 1961]], but size and availability are the main controls.
4. **Bass switch to fish at about 2 inches when suitable forage is available.** The 1958 data show this switch can occur below the commonly cited 3 inches.
5. **The 1948 management decision may not have been warranted.** The authors suggest the abolition of the commercial blackfish fishery may have been based on atypical data taken before the fishery closed, when blackfish were thought to be depleted.
6. **Blackfish as a buffer species is unresolved.** Neither confirmed nor refuted, because no food study of larger Clear Lake bass exists. A [[Lake Hughes]] introduction of blackfish, which degraded bass angling and required chemical removal, shows the risks of introduction.
7. **Predator-prey timing.** Age 0 blackfish reached about 2.9 inches by mid-August 1949 (Murphy, 1950), so most were too large to be suitable prey for bass-of-the-year.
8. **Short studies have limited value.** The authors stress that one-season, single-area collections can distort conclusions. Sampling should cover at least one growing season, and preferably two or more years, with better abundance estimates.
