# Radiotelemetry as a Means of Assessing Movement and Habitat Selection of Humpback Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service study, by R.A. Valdez and B.C. Nilson, tested [[Radiotelemetry]] as a tool for studying the movement, [[Habitat Selection]] and possible spawning sites of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in the Black Rocks area of the upper [[Colorado River]]. Eight internally tagged fish were tracked for 4–93 days. Tagged fish moved little (mean 0.8 km), often returned to their capture locations, and stayed within restricted river reaches. The authors conclude that radiotelemetry is feasible for this species but recommend a stronger, longer-lived transmitter for future work.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The humpback chub is an endangered cyprinid of the Colorado River system. The fish in this study measured 280–366 mm total length (TL). Black Rocks is one of the three largest known aggregations of the species in the Colorado River system (Valdez and Clemmer 1982).

**Prior work.** The study was conducted under the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] (CRFP), which aimed to supplement information on movement, habitat use and spawning. This was the first known attempt to use radiotelemetry on humpback chub. Earlier radiotelemetry studies on [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in the Green River had been funded by the Bureau of Reclamation (Tyus et al. 1982; Miller et al. 1982).

**Transmitters.** Tags were made by AVM Instrument Company (1979) and were the smallest used on this system, reflecting the small, laterally compressed body of the humpback chub.
- Padlock-shaped, about 4.5 g in weight (about 1–2% of fish body weight)
- Powered by a 1.35 V silver-oxide battery with a theoretical life of 90 days
- Expected range of 0.9–2.4 km, depending on turbidity, conductivity and obstructions
- Earlier, larger transmitters (11.0 g) used on squawfish and razorback sucker had longer battery lives

**Surgical protocol.** Fish were anesthetized in [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222), and transmitters were implanted through a 2–3 cm incision in the abdominal cavity. Surgery took 2–3 minutes per fish. Each fish was also tagged externally with a numbered [[Carlin Tags]] (plastic) for identification. Fish were held in cotton mesh pens for 72 hours before release, and no adverse effects were noted at release.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study setting.** Tracking took place in the Black Rocks area, a deep, swift, rocky reach of the upper Colorado River. The river is marked by bedrock, deep pools and fast-moving water.

**Tracking methods.** Fish were located from boats and from shore, with one aerial survey in September. Receivers and scanning receivers (RF-40 type) were used to find signals, and a Larson-Kuhrod whip antenna was used for narrow-range searches. Water depth, velocity and substrate were recorded at each location.

**Non-native fishes and flows.** The authors note that the restricted, quiet habitats used by humpback chub may be shrinking under flow changes and because of competition with, and predation by, non-native fish species. Flows in the upper Colorado River appear to affect these areas, and the limited spawning habitat may be affected by the same processes.

**Comparison with other fishes.** Humpback chub are strong, sustained swimmers in swift water but avoid it when possible, preferring quieter areas. Laboratory work on [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] (Bulkley et al. 1981) supports this observation.

## Key Findings

**Movement and site fidelity**
- Eight radio-tagged fish were released in two groups of four, on 3 May 1981 and 30 May 1981.
- Monitoring lasted 4–93 days (mean 38 days).
- Average movement per fish was 0.8 km (range 0–3.7 km).
- Of the eight radio-tagged fish, 2 (or more) returned to their original capture location, which suggests [[Homing Behavior]] and restricted [[Home Range]] use.
- No fish were found outside the Black Rocks area, and the authors suggest that transmitter malfunction is unlikely given the failure to locate fish in outside searches.

**Carlin-tagged comparison**
- Carlin-tagged fish were recaptured over a longer period (up to 434 days).
- Sixteen fish were recaptured, with an average movement of 1.63 km (range 0–23.0 km).
- Average days monitored for the 16 recaptures was 137 (range 1–434).

**Habitat use**
- Tagged fish occupied depths of 0.1–6.7 m (mean 4.7 m) and velocities of 0.2–1.3 fps (mean 0.6 fps).
- Fish primarily used sand and bedrock substrates.
- Fish were never found deeper than about 15 m, and most were in water shallower than 6 m.
- A total of 85 depth, velocity and substrate measurements were recorded during tracking.
- Radio-tagged fish frequently used swift (up to 7 fps) and deep (up to 18 m) water but preferred quieter areas.

**Diel behavior**
- Fish moved vertically on a daily cycle. They used shallow shorelines at dawn and dusk and deeper water in mid-morning and early afternoon.
- At night, fish occupied the main channel. This pattern is similar to that seen in humpback chub in other rivers.
- Fish often sheltered in eddies or pools behind submerged or emergent rocks for hours, then made brief forays into faster water.

**Spawning**
- No spawning sites were located. The lack of movement by tagged fish during the spring and early summer suggests they did not move to spawning areas during the study period.
- Spawning may occur at or near peak runoff in areas downstream of Black Rocks, and further study is needed to confirm spawning conditions.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Radiotelemetry is a feasible tool for studying humpback chub, but the species' sedentary behavior makes intensive searches throughout the river unnecessary.
- The authors recommend a transmitter not exceeding 5 g with a stronger and longer-lived signal, to track behavior in other river regions and beyond the summer months.
- Humpback chub appear to occupy restricted areas of deep, swift water and bedrock, and they rarely leave these areas.
- Quiet-water habitats may be critical for feeding, resting and possibly spawning, and they may be affected by flow changes and non-native fishes.
