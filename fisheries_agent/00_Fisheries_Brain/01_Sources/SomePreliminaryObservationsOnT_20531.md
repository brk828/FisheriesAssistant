# Role of the Telencephalon in Food-Reinforced Behaviour in the Goldfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #carassius-auratus

## Executive Summary

This study by G. E. Savage tests whether the [[Telencephalon]] (forebrain) of the [[Goldfish]], *Carassius auratus*, is needed for food-reinforced (positively motivated) behaviour, not only for [[Shock Avoidance Learning]]. Across seven experiments, fish with most of the telencephalon removed ([[Forebrain Ablation]]) were compared with normal fish. The forebrain-ablated fish showed normal responses to shock, feeding speed, hunger drive and simple visual discrimination learning. They were impaired only when reward was delayed, in both a discrimination task with a 5-sec delay and an [[Operant Conditioning]] feeding task. The author concludes that the forebrain acts as a short-term "holding" or attention system that maintains the stimulus representation until reinforcement arrives.

## Background & Description

The telencephalon of lower vertebrates is anatomically simpler than that of mammals, which makes it a useful model for analysing learned behaviour. Earlier work had shown that fish forebrain serves functions beyond olfaction. For example, [[Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) need an intact telencephalon for the correct courtship ritual (Segaar & Nieuwenhuys 1963). Several studies had linked the forebrain to avoidance learning, and Savage had proposed that it aids the formation of associations without being their storage site (Savage 1967, 1968a, 1968b; Hainsworth et al. 1967).

Earlier results on food-reinforced tasks were inconsistent. Forebrainless fish were poorer at maze learning with food reward (Zunini 1954; Hale 1956a; Warren 1961). Visual discrimination results varied: Nolte (1933) and Hosch (1936) found little impairment, while Janzen (1933) found slightly slower learning. [[Tilapia Macrocephala]] lost previously learned bar-pressing after forebrain removal (Aronson & Kaplan 1968).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The work is laboratory-based. Fish were kept in aerated, filtered tanks and tested under controlled lighting, shock, and feeding conditions. No field or natural-habitat observations are reported. The relevance to natural behaviour is inferred, and the author notes that goldfish are used because the forebrain may be simpler to analyse than the mammalian one.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- Surgery removed more than 80% of the telencephalon, verified by post-mortem histology. Sham operations were omitted because earlier work showed no performance difference.
- Fish were trained after a one-month recovery period. Hainsworth et al. (1967) had shown that response curves were nearly identical whether training began 1 day or 8 weeks after surgery.
- Apparatus included shuttle-type tanks, photocell-triggered feeders, and a Gellerman sequence for left/right positioning.
- Heart was used as the food reinforcer in Experiments 2 to 5.

**Experiment 1: Responses to shock and unconditioned fear**
- Groups of 11 fish (control and forebrain-ablated) received 10 shocks (0.5 sec, 4 V a.c.) per day for 15 days.
- Both groups showed a similar increase in response, averaging about five times the day-1 value. Overall response totals were 6960 (normal) and 7586 (forebrainless). The difference was not significant.
- In quiet conditions, forebrainless fish were much less active (430 counts vs 1825 in normals, t = 21, n = 12, P < 0.01).
- Red light slowed respiration in both groups. Mean fall in respiratory rate was 2.82 (SE 0.243) for normals and 3.27 (SE 0.294) for forebrainless fish. The author concluded that the shock-avoidance deficit is not due to reduced responsiveness or absent fear, and rejected the "catatonic fear" explanation (Regestein 1968).

**Experiment 2: Reaction times and satiation**
- Six normal and six forebrainless fish were trained with 10 feeding trials per day for 5 days.
- Forebrainless fish were on average 0.5 to 1 sec faster to food (t = 2.95, df = 10, P < 0.05).
- Neither group showed satiation; curves were roughly parallel with a slight downward trend.

**Experiment 3: Motivation under a noxious conflict**
- Food could only be reached by crossing an a.c. electric field.
- Mean threshold voltage was 4.83 V (SE 0.460) for normals and 3.83 V (SE 0.307) for forebrainless fish. No fish would cross more than 6 V.
- Reaction times did not differ significantly between the first and last trials, and trial 10 did not differ between groups (t = 41, n = 12, P > 0.05). The author concluded motivation was similar.

**Experiment 4: Simple simultaneous visual discrimination (no delay)**
- Preference tests showed no differences between groups for heart versus Plasticine. Fish did not reliably identify their original food visually.
- Shape preference was slight for the horizontal rectangle (H chosen 31/60 times for normals and 32/60 for forebrainless fish). The horizontal shape was therefore baited with Plasticine and the vertical with food.
- Over 20 days, both groups reached a plateau of 70 to 90% correct. No difference was found (t = 0.947, df = 10, P > 0.2).
- Mean reaction times were similar: 2.2 s and 2.3 s on day 1, and 2.3 s on day 20 for both groups.
- Forebrainless fish showed more persistent side preference, which may explain their slightly poorer scores.

**Experiment 5: Five-second reinforcement delay**
- The discrimination task from Experiment 4 was repeated with a 5-sec delay between response and reward.
- Groups performed similarly for the first few days. From day 4 onwards the forebrainless group fell behind.
- Over the last 100 trials, normals scored 85 to 95% correct and forebrainless fish scored 55 to 70% (t = 7.46, df = 10, P < 0.001).
- Forebrainless fish showed rising side positioning, reaching a mean of about 2.5 and up to 90% on some days. The author interpreted this as regression to early excessive positioning.

**Experiment 6: Automated operant feeding (naive fish)**
- Seven naive normal and seven naive forebrainless fish (12.5 to 18 cm) were tested in an automatic feeder driven by interruptions of a photocell beam.
- Normal fish showed steady, rising response rates. Forebrainless fish responded initially, then declined.
- The difference in total operations was highly significant (t = 7.001, df = 12, P < 0.001).
- A control group of six normal fish with unrewarded responses produced a curve similar to the rewarded forebrainless fish (t = 0.0079, df = 11, P < 0.5). The author interpreted this as failure of association, not loss of motivation.

**Experiment 7: Pre-trained operant feeding (long-term)**
- Six normal and six forebrainless fish were given time to reach regular feeding (normals 5 days; forebrainless 10 to 20 days), then recorded for 10 days.
- Feeding rates did not differ (t = 0.068, df = 10, P > 0.5).
- Weight gains were comparable and ranged from 1.6 to 13.8 g (t = 2.9, n = 12, P > 0.05), suggesting normal feeding and metabolism.
- The author concluded that the memory store is intact but harder to access, and that prolonged training can overcome the delay-based deficit.

**Conclusions**
- The forebrain is not needed for response to aversive stimuli or for normal feeding rates and hunger drive.
- Simultaneous, non-delayed discrimination learning is unimpaired after forebrain removal.
- Any reinforcement delay (whether in discrimination, operant or avoidance tasks) produces a deficit. This is consistent with a forebrain role in maintaining the stimulus representation until reinforcement.
- The author compares this with delayed-response findings in monkeys (Jacobsen 1936; Riopelle & Churukian 1958). Those showed deficits limited to spatial problems, whereas the fish show deficits in both spatial and visual tasks. The author suggests the deficit scales with task difficulty.
- The forebrain is proposed as a primitive "holding" or attention centre (Summary, point 5).
