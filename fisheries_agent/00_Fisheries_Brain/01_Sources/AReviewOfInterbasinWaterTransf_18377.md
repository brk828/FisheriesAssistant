# Interbasin Water Transfers and Biota Transfer: A Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #invasive-fishes

## Executive Summary

This review, authored by G. Padmanabhan of [[North Dakota State University]], examines how [[Interbasin Water Transfer]] schemes move aquatic organisms (fish, invertebrates, bacteria, viruses, and parasites) between drainage basins, with emphasis on [[Biota Transfer]]. The author argues that environmental concern over large transfers has grown, yet the science needed to predict impacts is still immature. Comparative case studies from South Africa, Canada, and the United States are summarized. The paper concludes that long-term baseline and monitoring data, plus better ecological forecasting tools, are required before biota-transfer risks can be assessed with confidence. The review was part of a multi-disciplinary program on biota transfer sponsored by the [[Garrison Diversion Conservancy District]] and the [[North Dakota State Water Commission]]. It appears in the proceedings of the North Dakota Water Quality Symposium (March 20–21, 1990, Fargo), which contains a broader set of papers on water quality and human health.

## Background & Description

**Scope and definition.** Interbasin water transfer is defined as the artificial withdrawal of water from a source in one drainage basin for beneficial use in another. Transfers use canals, conduits, tunnels, and reservoirs that cross basin boundaries. The author notes that thousands of such schemes have been built worldwide in the last 50 years, and that their scale has been increasing.

**Shift in decision criteria.** Until about a decade before the review, transfers were approved mainly on technical feasibility and economic justification. Environmental considerations have since become more important. Many proposals face public opposition, chiefly over water quality changes and biota-transfer effects on ecosystems.

**Core problem.** The author identifies a gap between need and capability. Forecasting biotic change is difficult because theoretical modeling techniques are lacking, information on biota transfer is limited, and baseline data are insufficient to calibrate and verify models. Scientific modeling of biota transfer was described as "still in its infancy."

**Research program.** In response, the [[North Dakota Water Resources Research Institute]] created the North Dakota Interbasin Biota Transfer Program in November 1987. The first task was a literature review of evidence, or lack of evidence, for biota transfer in existing schemes.

**Mechanisms of biota transfer.** Introduced fish can cause problems through rapid population growth, colonizing ability, and harmful interactions with native species. Competition for food and space can disrupt native fisheries, and some introduced fish have little value as game fish. Pathogens (viruses, bacteria, and parasites) can move with fish. Two questions govern any introduction: whether the species will survive and grow in the new environment, and whether it will feed on or be preyed upon by species of concern. Success depends on intrinsic factors (the species' genotype) and extrinsic factors (the receiving environment). The review distinguishes "introduced species," meaning organisms moved by people outside their native range, as a key category for management.

**Future research priorities** (after Shafland and Lewis, 1984; and Seagel, 1987) include:
- life-cycle, habitat, distribution, and trend data for organisms;
- direct and indirect pathogenicity studies;
- development of risk-analysis techniques;
- integrated, multi-disciplinary management strategies that define allowable risk.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Case study 1: Orange-Great Fish River, South Africa.** Transfer through the Orange-Fish Tunnel changed flow regimes. The upper river shifted from seasonal to perennial flow, and seasonal flow variation in the lower river was reduced. Inflow of low-salinity Orange River water diluted the highly mineralized Fish River and lowered total dissolved solids. Riffle invertebrate communities changed substantially, with only 33% of taxa common to pre- and post-transfer surveys. Dominant species shifted, but overall densities did not change. Some fish were surviving passage through the tunnel despite fatal hazards. The author notes that five Orange River species now have man-made access to the Fish River. Effects on the fish community remain too early to judge.

**Case study 2: McGregor Diversion, British Columbia, Canada.** Studies began in 1976 on a proposal to divert the McGregor River (Pacific drainage) into the Parsnip River (Arctic drainage). The transfer concern focused on fish, viruses, bacteria, and parasites. Three parasite forms not then found in the Parsnip River were expected to pose the greatest threat to its fishery. Neither total fish populations nor disease prevalence could be determined with confidence.

**Case study 3: Garrison Diversion Unit, North Dakota.** The project would carry water from the [[Missouri River]] basin to the [[Hudson Bay]] drainage for irrigation, municipal and industrial supply, recreation, and fish and wildlife. Most drainage and wastewater would flow into transboundary streams in Canada. After Canadian concern, the matter went to the [[International Joint Commission]], which created the International Garrison Diversion Study Board. The IJC concluded that the project would adversely affect water quality and biological resources in Manitoba. It also found that most impacts other than biota transfer could be reduced to insignificant levels through project modifications. The IJC's conclusions relied on existing data, and it noted that predicting biological impacts was hard because baseline data and forecasting techniques were lacking. A Biota and Fisheries Task Force was established in 1983 to monitor the distribution and biology of ten undesirable fish species identified in the IJC's 1977 report.

**Ongoing Garrison-linked studies** (North Dakota Interbasin Biota Transfer Program) include:
- evaluation of direct filtration to prevent biota transfer into the Hudson Bay drainage;
- review of distributional ecology of transferred fish between the Missouri and Hudson Bay basins;
- a survey for *Polypodium hydriforme* in fish eggs;
- effects of salinity on dispersal of [[Rainbow Smelt]] and [[Carp]];
- the [[Stonecat]] as a model invasive fish;
- the trophic structure of [[Lake Winnipeg]] and potential impacts from the Garrison Diversion Unit;
- invasive capability, thermal tolerance, and range-extension potential of rainbow smelt.

## Key Findings

1. **Biota transfer is a central environmental risk.** Fish, bacteria, viruses, and parasites are the main concern, and water quality and quantity also change in exporting, receiving, and diversion-route waters.
2. **Predictive science is immature.** No intensive modeling validated against actual data was found in the literature. Forecasting tools and baseline datasets are needed.
3. **Evidence from existing schemes is limited.** Comprehensive case studies are "almost non-existent." Discussions on biota transfer were described as largely philosophical, while economic and technical feasibility studies are abundant.
4. **Monitoring must be long-term.** The author calls for diagnostic monitoring (tracking trends) and prognostic monitoring (predicting consequences), conducted before construction, continued through operation, and used to design remedial measures.
5. **Ecological change can be substantial.** The South African case shows marked shifts in flow, salinity, and invertebrate communities (only 33% taxa shared pre- and post-transfer), with uncertain effects on fish.
6. **Known risks were identified but not fully resolved.** The IJC identified ten undesirable fish species and three parasite threats were flagged in the McGregor case, yet total impacts remain uncertain.
7. **Research response in North Dakota.** The 1987 program, Biota and Fisheries Task Force, and related studies represent an integrated research approach, but the author concludes that "much work remains to be done."
