# Zoogeography of Fishes of the Lower Ohio-Upper Mississippi Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #zoogeography #cyprinidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by Brooks M. Burr and Lawrence M. Page (in Hocutt and Wiley, 1986), consolidates distribution records for the freshwater fishes of the [[Lower Ohio-Upper Mississippi Basin]] and interprets them against [[Pliocene]], [[Pleistocene]] and Recent environments. The region holds 229 species in 74 genera and 29 families, of which 217 are native. Its fauna reflects [[Pleistocene Glaciation]], [[Drainage Evolution]] (including [[Teays River]] capture and diversion), post-glacial climatic phases, and recent human impacts, especially siltation.

## Background & Description

The basin extends from the Scioto and Licking rivers (Ohio and Kentucky) to the headwaters of the Mississippi and Minnesota rivers. It spans the [[Central Lowland]], parts of the [[Interior Low Plateau]], the [[Cumberland Plateau]] and the [[Coastal Plain]] of southern Illinois. Elevations range from about 610 m (Minnesota), 762 m (Kentucky) and 455 m (Ohio) down to 85 m at Cairo, Illinois. Mean annual precipitation ranges from about 70 cm (Minnesota, Wisconsin) to 115 cm (southern Illinois). Pre-settlement vegetation was oak-hickory and beech-maple forest with bluestem prairie; much of the land is now agricultural or urban.

Distributional data were drawn from state fish books, taxonomic revisions and earlier surveys, including [[Forbes and Richardson]] (1908) for Illinois and [[Trautman]] (1957, 1981) for Ohio. These pre-1900 surveys are especially valuable because they predate many human modifications.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage history.** The Pliocene Ohio River began in southern Indiana and joined the Mississippi in southern Arkansas. The Teays River drained much of eastern Kentucky, Ohio and Indiana and was later severed by Pleistocene glacial advances, its tributaries diverted into the ancestral Ohio River. Glacial River Warren, Lake Agassiz and the Chicago and Fort Wayne outlets created temporary connections between the Mississippi and the Great Lakes and Hudson Bay basins.

**Glacial effects.** Glaciation eliminated populations and displaced northern fishes southward. Cooler conditions allowed cool-water species to extend south, and reduced runoff favored clear-water creek and small-river fishes. Upland species were split into Ozarkian and Appalachian refugia. The Driftless Area of southwestern Wisconsin and northwestern Illinois remained unglaciated, but evidence for it as a fish refugium is weak.

**Post-glacial climate.** The post-Wisconsinan sequence was cool-warm-cool, with a Climatic Optimum (about 8,000–6,000 years ago) and a Xerothermic Period (about 6,000–4,000 years ago). Southern lowland species such as [[Notropis chalybaeus]] dispersed north during the Climatic Optimum and persist as relicts, while [[Notropis dorsalis]] is a Xerothermic relict tied to the Prairie Peninsula.

**Dispersal mechanisms.** Upland fishes crossed divides mainly through stream capture. [[St. Anthony Falls]] (21 m) formerly isolated the upper Mississippi above Minnesota, with 64 species above the falls compared with 123 below.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna size and composition:** 229 species (74 genera, 29 families), of which 217 are native (68 genera, 28 families). Twelve species are introduced. About 50% of native fishes are widely distributed across the region.
- **Dominant families:** Cyprinidae and Percidae together contain 111 native species (51%). The five dominant native families (Cyprinidae, Percidae, Catostomidae, Centrarchidae, Ictaluridae) contain 163 species (75%).
- **Biogeographic origins:** Approximately 10% of native fishes are primarily northern, 3% western, 8% southern or southwestern, and 19% eastern or Ohio Basin-restricted. About 11% are big-river specialists.
- **Refugia and endemism:** The Green River has the most unusual fauna, with five endemics (*Moxostoma atripinne*, *Etheostoma barbouri*, *E. barrenense*, *E. bellum*, *E. rafinesquei*). The upper Mississippi Basin has no endemics.
- **Diversity drivers:** Drainage area, habitat diversity and geological history. The Wabash River has the most native fishes (151). The Cache River, though small, has a richer fauna than the glaciated Skunk or Salt rivers.
- **Faunal resemblance:** Ranges from 39% (Cache River vs. upper Mississippi tributaries) to 93% (Wisconsin vs. Chippewa rivers). Lower Ohio tributaries average 77.2% and upper Mississippi tributaries 77.1%.
- **Faunal groups:** Four groups were recognized: Lowlands (18 species, 8%), Big River (24 species, 11%), Ohio Basin Uplands (41 species, 19%) and Wide-Ranging (just over 50%). About 13% do not fit clearly.
- **Habitat preferences:** 150 native species (69%) occur mainly in streams or creeks; 21 (10%) occupy only big rivers; 44 (19%) occur in both big rivers and streams.
- **Range expansions:** [[Notropis lutrensis]] has spread through Illinois and into Indiana and Kentucky, displacing close relatives. [[Phenacobius mirabilis]] first recorded in Illinois in 1876 and in Ohio in 1920, expanding eastward with rising turbidity. [[Osmerus mordax]] (rainbow smelt), introduced to the Great Lakes, now occurs in the upper Mississippi (1978 onward) and as far south as Louisiana in 1979. [[Dorosoma petenense]] was first recorded from the Ohio River in 1957.
- **Declines driven by siltation:** 32 of 41 selected species (78%) in Table 9.1 have had ranges greatly reduced by siltation. [[Ammocrypta asprella]] was last collected in Ohio in 1899, Kentucky in 1929, Indiana in 1888 and Illinois in 1901.
- **Other human impacts:** Stream channelization, wetland drainage, impoundments, pollution, pesticides and surface coal mining (acid-mine runoff) have all reduced native fish populations. The Saline River (Illinois) and Pond River (Kentucky) are now almost devoid of fishes over much of their length.
- **Commercial overfishing:** 168,739 kg of [[Lake Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser fulvescens*) were taken commercially from the upper Mississippi between 1894 and 1899. The 1947–1950 catch of [[Paddlefish]] (*Polyodon spathula*) was about one-tenth of the 1894 and 1899 level.
- **Extinctions:** [[Lagochila lacera]] is almost certainly extinct (last collected 1893). [[Noturus trautmani]], endemic to the Scioto River, was last found in 1957.
- **Stream capture examples:** Capture of Collins Fork (Cumberland to Kentucky River) and of White Oak Creek and Clark's Run (Salt River to Dix River) explain shared distributions of darters and [[Fundulus catenatus]].
- **Conclusion:** The regional fauna reflects Pliocene drainage, Pleistocene glaciation and refugia, post-glacial climatic shifts, and recent habitat degradation. Many species are less abundant or more restricted than in pre-1900 records, and this trend is continuing.
