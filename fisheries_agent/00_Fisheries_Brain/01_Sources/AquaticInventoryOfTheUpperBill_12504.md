# Aquatic Inventory of the Upper Bill Williams Drainage, Yavapai and Mohave Counties, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1979 Bureau of Land Management (BLM) Technical Note, authored by [[William G. Kepner]] of the Phoenix District Office, reports a winter 1978–79 aquatic inventory of the perennial drainages of the Hualapai and Aquarius Planning Units. The survey covered the upper [[Bill Williams River]] basin upstream of [[Alamo Lake]]. Sixty-four stations were sampled on three major watersheds: the [[Santa Maria River]], [[Burro Creek]], and [[Big Sandy River]]. The study combined museum records with field collections to document 22 fish species and two hybrids, along with 62 macroinvertebrate taxa. The work was undertaken to provide baseline data for NEPA-required management plans for terrestrial and aquatic resources on public lands.

The note concludes that native fishes occupy headwater reaches and introduced species dominate lower reaches. Water quality was generally within federal and state standards, with notable exceptions. The most serious documented threats are effluent from the [[Cyprus-Bagdad Copper Mine]] on Boulder Creek, the proposed [[Anderson Uranium Mine]] adjacent to the Santa Maria River, livestock grazing and feral burro impacts on riparian vegetation, and water withdrawals.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and regulatory context.** Under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) of 1970, the BLM must assess impacts of land use on National Resource Lands. Wildlife inventories on public lands were first implemented after an October 1973 lawsuit by the [[Natural Resources Defense Council]], which argued that BLM's general evaluation of livestock grazing did not address localized impacts. The study estimated 310 stream miles and over 11,800 acres of riparian woodland administered by BLM in Arizona (Hoeft, 1978).

**Study area.** The drainage consists of low-order desert streams with riffles, pools, runs, and intermittent reaches during low flow. The area was previously sampled only sporadically by researchers from the University of Michigan, Arizona State University, and Prescott Center College. The climate is warm and semiarid, with annual precipitation of 11 to 18 in. (Wendt et al. 1976). Rainfall is bimodal, with summer monsoonal thundershowers from the south and longer-duration winter Pacific storms. Snow usually falls from mid-November above 5,000 ft. and is rare after May 1.

**Methods.** Sampling was carried out between December 1978 and February 1979. Stations were placed at roughly 2.5-mile intervals, and each was sampled over a 60-ft reach and classified as pool, run, or riffle. Discharge was estimated by the [[Embody Cork-Float Method]]. Fish were collected by 115 V AC [[Backpack Electrofishing]] and 1/8-inch mesh seines, preserved in 10% formalin, and identified following Minckley (1973). Water chemistry was measured with Hach field kits and, at selected stations, by the Arizona Game and Fish Department Water Quality Branch. Macroinvertebrate sampling was qualitative, aimed at identifying distribution trends rather than meeting statistical requirements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Santa Maria River.** This watershed flows about 41 miles southwest into Alamo Lake. Mean width was 132.5 ft., and mean velocity was 2.5 ft./sec. Mean discharge was 37.5 ft³/sec. (USGS gage No. 4249, 1966–1977). Riparian vegetation was sparse, dominated by [[Mesquite Bosque]] with [[Seep Willow]], and the non-riparian community was [[Palo Verde-Saguaro Desertscrub]]. The fish fauna was depauperate, which the author attributed to a lack of habitat diversity rather than chemistry.

**Burro Creek.** This system flows about 57 miles southwest to the Big Sandy River, draining 687 square miles. Mean discharge was 25.5 ft³/sec., ranging from 0.02 to 195 ft³/sec. The creek has the richest aquatic community in the study and includes the [[Francis Creek]] and [[Boulder Creek]] tributaries. Hot springs in Francis Creek were recorded at 85°F at the springhead, while the creek below measured 53°F.

**Big Sandy River and Trout Creek.** The Big Sandy is a broad, shallow, sandy run with no pools or riffles. It drains about 2,123 square miles, excluding Burro Creek. Trout Creek is narrower, deeper, and more heterogeneous. Tamarisk ([[Tamarix chinensis]]) has invaded the riparian zones of both systems.

**Alamo Lake.** [[Alamo Dam]] was completed in 1968 for flood control and can store up to 1,043,000 acre-feet. Surface area ranges from 500 acres at minimum pool to 13,300 acres at capacity. The reservoir was stocked by the Arizona Game and Fish Department beginning in March 1968 and now supports a warm-water fishery.

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna**

- Twenty-two species and two hybrids were recorded from the upper drainage and Alamo Lake.
- Native fishes (Agosia chrysogaster, Rhinichthys osculus, Gila robusta, Catostomus insignis, Pantosteus clarki) occupy the headwaters. Introduced species dominate lower reaches.
- **Santa Maria River:** seven species in four families. Longfin dace made up more than 97% of the 1,364 fish collected. Two species, Gila robusta robusta and Pantosteus clarki, had no previous museum records from this river. No fish collected is listed as threatened, endangered, or sensitive.
- **Burro Creek:** nine species in four families from the creek and tributaries. Red shiner made up 43.7% of the 3,594 fish collected. Parasitism by black-spot metacercariae (Uvulifer ambloplitis) was extremely high in the upper reaches. One Catostomus insignis x Pantosteus clarki hybrid was collected, at 0.19% frequency.
- **Trout Creek:** seven species. Longfin dace made up 65.6% of the 480 fish collected.
- **Big Sandy River:** eight species in four families, which is seven more than previous museum records indicated. Longfin dace were 50.5% of the 1,094 fish collected, and red shiner was 33.0%.
- **Alamo Lake:** largemouth bass, bluegill, and channel catfish form the fishery. Carp made up 48.5% of a two-day gill-net catch. The author describes Alamo Lake as "an excellent warm-water fishery," with the bluegill fishery "probably the best in Arizona."
- Smallmouth bass (two specimens, April 1977) were judged not established.

**Water quality**

- Santa Maria River water was within Arizona warm-water fishery turbidity limits (<50 JTU), with a mean of 17.2 JTU. Mean TDS was 214 mg/l, mean pH was 8.3, and dissolved oxygen ranged from 9 to 12 mg/l.
- Total phosphate in the Santa Maria River (0.18–0.53 mg/l) exceeded the EPA not-to-exceed standard of 0.06 mg/l, a feature the author notes is typical of desert streams.
- Burro Creek sulfate averaged 20.1 mg/l above the Boulder Creek confluence and 113.8 mg/l below it. The effluent from the Cyprus-Bagdad mine into Boulder Creek measured 640 mg/l sulfate, 1,200 mg/l total hardness, and 49.7 mg/l chloride.
- Copper levels up to 8.0 mg/l were recorded by the Arizona State Department of Health Services in spring 1977, a violation of state and federal standards.
- Fecal coliform counts below the BLM campground averaged 275/100 ml, above the state full-body-contact standard of 200/100 ml.
- The Big Sandy River's fluoride (mean 1.2 mg/l) and TDS (mean 592 mg/l) made its water fair to objectionable as a drinking supply.

**Macroinvertebrates**

- Sixty-two total taxa were collected, including members of 9 of the 10 aquatic insect orders.
- Burro Creek had the greatest diversity, with 41 of the 62 taxa. Dominant taxa included two stoneflies, Mesocapnia frisoni and M. arizonensis, and the caddisfly Hydropsyche sp.
- The Santa Maria River yielded 21 taxa (4–15 per sample), a depauperate fauna associated with shifting sand substrate.
- Trout Creek yielded 18 taxa, and the Big Sandy River more than 35.
- Reaches of Boulder Creek below the mine outfall were essentially devoid of invertebrates, algae, and fishes.

**Management recommendations**

1. Limit or eliminate livestock grazing in riparian areas to preserve bank stability and streamside vegetation. The Burro Creek Allotment, licensed for 75 head, should be grazing-free.
2. Reduce or adjust the feral burro population.
3. Maintain instream flows and discourage diversions, impoundments, and groundwater withdrawals.
4. Continue monthly water-chemistry monitoring. Monitor the Santa Maria River below the Anderson Uranium Mine, and monitor Burro Creek near the Cyprus-Bagdad mine, including heavy-metal accumulation in piscivorous raptors.
5. Construct a permanent streamflow gauge on Burro Creek at the old U.S. Highway 93 bridge.
6. Manage the watershed as a native non-game fishery and discourage non-native sport-fish introductions.
7. File for water rights on public lands and obtain a priority date from the Arizona Water Commission.

**Key threats identified**

- Copper mine effluent and sedimentation in Boulder and Burro creeks.
- The proposed Anderson Uranium Mine, which the author expected to employ 350 permanent workers, with uncertain sediment, metal, and water-withdrawal impacts.
- Livestock grazing, feral burros, and loss of riparian vegetation.
- Flash flooding, which the author found did not decimate adult native fishes.
