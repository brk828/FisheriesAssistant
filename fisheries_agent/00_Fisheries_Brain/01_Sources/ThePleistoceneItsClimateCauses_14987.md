# The Pleistocene: Its Climate, Causes, Chronology, and Hydrology

#ichthyology #paleoclimatology #pleistocene #zoogeography #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1971 academic paper by John N. Rinne examines the [[Pleistocene Epoch]] from multiple perspectives: climatic causes, chronological methods, hydrological conditions, and faunal evidence. The document emphasizes that the Pleistocene was characterized by dramatic climatic oscillations, extensive glaciation, pluvial lake formation, and significant sea-level fluctuations. While primarily focused on broader paleoclimatic phenomena, the paper explicitly addresses implications for freshwater fish distribution and zoogeography. The author argues that Pleistocene conditions—including land bridge formation, pluvial lake systems, and climatic zone shifts—profoundly influenced freshwater fish dispersal and speciation, though ichthyological evidence remains underdeveloped compared to mammalian records.

## Background & Description

The Pleistocene represents a geologically recent epoch characterized by repeated glacial-interglacial cycles. The document establishes that the cause of glaciation remains "the greatest puzzle of all paleoclimatic endeavor" despite numerous theories. The epoch's duration is estimated between 300,000 years (Flint, 1957) and 2-3 million years (West, 1968), with the plio-pleistocene boundary dated via potassium-argon methods at approximately 1.5-1.75 million years ago.

The paper identifies Earth's "normal climate" as non-glacial, with Pleistocene glaciation representing an anomaly superimposed upon a secular cooling trend beginning in the [[Eocene]] (40 million years earlier). Key established conditions include: four major glacial-interglacial cycles, simultaneous glaciation in both hemispheres, maximum temperature differences of 7-9°C between glacial and interglacial periods, and rapid ice disappearance at glacial terminations.

Contemporary evidence (1855-1940) shows global air temperature increases of 0.5°C mean annual and 1.0°C mean winter, with Arctic regions experiencing 3°C winter warming (1910-1940). This modern warming trend provides context for understanding past climatic variability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Climatic Conditions

Contrary to popular conception, glacial periods were characterized by conditions warmer than present in some regions. The Mediterranean maintained minimum temperatures of 13°C during Pleistocene glaciations. However, worldwide temperature depression of 2.5°C in tropics and 3-12°C in extratropical regions occurred, with summer cooling being most effective. A 6°C temperature fall corresponds to an 800 km horizontal climatic zone displacement.

Glacial anticyclones developed over ice sheets, producing centrifugal winds and temperatures of -50 to -60°C in high latitudes. The [[Greenland Ice Sheet]] requires equivalent of 31.4 cm water per year for maintenance, with snow falling 55% of days and clouds prevailing 60% of time.

### Hydrological Systems

**Pluvial Lakes**: The [[Basin and Range Province]] of western North America contained at least 141 closed basins showing evidence of former lakes. [[Lake Bonneville]] reached 1,000 feet depth, stretched 346 miles, covered over 20,000 square miles, and overflowed into the [[Snake River]] via Red Rock Pass. Its modern remnant, [[Great Salt Lake]], is only 50 feet deep. [[Lake Lahontan]] was less than half Bonneville's area but rose 550-886 feet above its modern successors.

In Africa, the [[Sahara Desert]] supported long permanent streams including the Saora, Ighorghor, and Tafasset (the latter approximately the length of the [[Nile River]]). [[Lake Chad]] basin was filled during major pluvial periods. [[Lake Tanganyika]] connected northward with rift valley rivers and lakes. Four major pluvial periods are recognized, correlating with European glaciations (Günz, Mindel, Riss, Würm).

**Sea Level Fluctuations**: Evidence from strandlines, raised beaches, submerged valleys, and sea-floor sediments indicates sea level variations of 80-750 m (estimates vary). Mediterranean strandlines are recognized at 90-100m (Sicilian), 55-60m (Milazzian), 28-32m (Tyrrhenian), and 18-20m (Monastrian) above present levels. Submerged canyons at mouths of the [[Hudson River]], [[Elbe River]], and [[Rhine River]] extend to depths of 3,050 m, interpreted as drowned river valleys.

Modern observations (1930-1948) show 6 mm per year sea level rise, with 1 mm per year average over the last 100 years.

### Land Bridges

The [[Bering Land Bridge]] between North America and Asia is most extensively documented, exposed during Riss (Illinoian) and Würm (Wisconsin) glaciations when water depths consistently less than 100 fathoms (600 feet) became terrestrial plain. The bridge remained unglaciated and supported grassland vegetation warmed by the Japanese Current.

Other proposed bridges include: [[English Channel]] (barely 30 m deep, linking Great Britain and Europe), Sunda Sea (between [[Borneo]] and [[Sumatra]]), connections between [[Australia]], [[Tasmania]], and [[New Guinea]], and early Pleistocene link between Africa and Europe via Tunisia and Italy. The [[Isthmus of Panama]], though still existing, experienced more seasonal climate due to 2° southward movement of high pressure belts, opening it to species unable to cross modern tropical forests.

### Faunal Evidence - Freshwater Fishes

The [[Great Basin]] lakes support 12 genera (2 endemic) and 22 species in the [[Bonneville System]], 10 genera (1 endemic) and 13 species (8 endemic) in the [[Lahontan System]]. The [[Death Valley System]] contains 5 genera (1 endemic) and 10 species (8 endemic), including coastal forms [[Fundulus]] and [[Gasterosteus]].

African examples include Zambezian fauna in [[Atlas Mountains]], tropical fish [[Chromys]] and catfish [[Clarias lazera]] inhabiting Sahara water holes. Lakes Koga, Edward, George, and Victoria dried completely or reduced to swamps during interpluvials, becoming so deoxygenated that predaceous genera [[Hydrocyon]] and [[Lates]] with other Nilotic genera were exterminated. Upon refilling, new niches opened and present fauna developed. In contrast, deeper [[Lake Nyassa]] and [[Lake Tanganyika]] persisted as large water bodies even in arid times, allowing continual evolution. Tanganyika's fauna is of greater antiquity than any other African fauna with endemic genera.

British Pleistocene fish fauna includes only 10 species: perch, pike, roach, dace, tench, and rudd. The entire British fish fauna consists of Pleistocene relics and their descendants.

## Key Findings

**Causative Theories**: Five categories of glaciation theories are proposed: (1) Topographic, (2) Geophysical, (3) Atmospheric and Oceanic, (4) Planetary, (5) Cosmic. The most acceptable theory combines late Pliocene-Pleistocene uplift (averaging 300-800 m in Tertiary, with sea level drop of 100 m creating worldwide altitude increase of 600 m and corresponding 3°C temperature reduction) with atmospheric, axial, and orbital changes as contributors. Topographic uplift alone is prerequisite but insufficient; solar-topographic interaction is increasingly favored since the sun is sole energy source governing atmospheric processes.

**Chronological Methods**: Relative dating methods include fluorine analysis of bones (lower Pleistocene 2.0%, middle 1.0%, upper 0.5% fluorine-phosphate ratio), pollen stratigraphy, tephochronology, and paleomagnetism. Absolute methods include dendrochronology (3,000-4,000 year span for bristlecone pine), varve analysis (De Geer followed varves for 1,000 kilometers spanning 17,000 years, substantiated by radiocarbon), radiocarbon dating (half-life 5,568 years, applicable to 30,000-45,000 years, extendable to 65-70,000 years by isotopic enrichment), and potassium-argon dating (half-life 32,000 years, used for plio-pleistocene boundary determination).

**Precipitation Paradox**: Despite overall reduced evaporation from secular cooling, precipitation increased during glacial periods. High latitude ice sheets increased atmospheric circulation, offsetting reduced temperature effects. Poleward shift of storm tracks during 1925-1950 warming brought increased precipitation to glaciers but more importantly greater heat, causing rapid shrinkage. Reverse conditions during cooling would decrease precipitation in high latitudes while increasing it in lower latitudes, creating pluvial conditions. Precipitation was actually less during glacial maxima in major glaciated areas than at present.

**Glaciation-Pluviation Synchrony**: Glaciation and pluviation occurred contemporaneously "in phase" in both polar hemispheres. Pluvial lake expansion resulted not primarily from glacial melt but from climatic change: high pressure systems over ice sheets moved climatic belts 16° or more southward, displacing prevailing storm tracks accordingly. Steepening temperature gradients between high and low latitudes caused more active atmospheric forces, more powerful storms, increased cloudiness, decreased evaporation and temperature, and increased stream discharge into closed basins.

**Ice Sheet Self-Limitation**: Glacial anticyclones that developed and nourished ice sheets eventually starved them. Once ice sheets reached 700-1,000 miles diameter, anticyclonic winds developed preventing cyclone entry. High reflecting power and decreasing temperature deprived ice sheets of moisture, leading to their demise.

**Sea Level Impact**: Eustatic sea level changes of 70-100 m (possibly up to 250 m on North American east coast) dramatically increased land bridge frequency. A 70 m drop over 600,000 years (1/10 mm per year) is proposed. Modern sea level rise of 1 mm per year over last 100 years (6 mm per year 1930-1948) is attributed to glacial shrinkage rather than crustal warping, though isostatic uplift continues at 1 cm per annum in formerly glaciated regions and may continue for thousands of years in Scandinavia.

**Ichthyological Implications**: Study of Cenozoic fishes has lagged far behind higher vertebrates due to lack of adequate osteological collections, inadequate recovery methods for smaller vertebrates, and ignorance of major fish group osteology. Only 10 species known from British Pleistocene despite extensive mammalian records. Pluviation on the documented scale must have been of considerable importance in fish distribution. Zoogeography of freshwater fishes has been profoundly affected by Pleistocene conditions, influencing both dispersal routes and speciation rates.

**Temperature Trends**: Tertiary fish genera in West Germany indicate progressive cooling: Eocene 22°C, lower Miocene 19°C, upper Miocene 17°C, Pliocene 14-17°C. This secular cooling trend culminated in Pleistocene glaciations superimposed upon already cooler conditions.

**Research Gaps**: The author emphasizes that purely objective data on past fish dispersal routes is lacking. An interdisciplinary approach is necessary for future work. The complex nature of climatic control includes past distribution of land and water, continental height, oceanic circulation, atmospheric composition, and astronomical variations—all requiring integrated study.
