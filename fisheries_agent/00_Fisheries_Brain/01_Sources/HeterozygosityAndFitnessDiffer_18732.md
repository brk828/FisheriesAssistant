# Heterozygosity and Fitness Differences Among Remnant Populations of the Endangered Sonoran Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether genetic diversity affects fitness in [[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]], an endangered viviparous fish of the Gila River drainage and adjacent Sonora, Mexico. Three wild-derived laboratory stocks, differing in mean heterozygosity, were compared for survival, growth, fecundity, and developmental stability. The homozygous [[Monkey Spring]] stock (H = 0.0%) performed worst in nearly every measure. The most heterozygous stock, [[Sharp Spring]] (H = 3.7%), performed best, and [[Tule Spring]] (H = 1.5%) was generally intermediate. The authors conclude that Sharp Spring fish are the best choice for the [[Species Recovery Program]], while Monkey Spring should be kept as a reservoir of unique genetic variation.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) was once the dominant fish of lowland streams in southern Arizona. Over the past 50 years, [[Habitat Alteration]], [[Groundwater Pumping]], and introduced exotic fishes nearly eliminated it from the Gila River drainage. Natural populations now persist in fewer than a dozen isolated springs and small arroyos in Arizona, and the fish was added to the [[Federal Endangered Species List]]. The species remains locally abundant in Sonora, Mexico.

Isolated remnant populations tend to lose genetic diversity through [[Inbreeding]] and [[Genetic Drift]]. The authors argue that increased homozygosity for deleterious recessive alleles can depress fitness in traits such as growth, fecundity, developmental stability, and competitive ability. They cite the endangered [[African Cheetah]] as an example, where homozygosity is linked to low sperm counts, high infant mortality, and elevated [[Fluctuating Asymmetry]]. Loss of variation also limits long-term [[Adaptive Potential]].

Background from the earlier study by [[Vrijenhoek et al.]] (6) showed that most Arizona populations carry less genetic variation than Sonoran populations. Fish from Monkey Spring had been used as hatchery stock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] for reintroductions. The authors list four reasons Monkey Spring was a poor choice:

- It was homozygous at all 25 loci surveyed electrophoretically.
- It is a thermally stable habitat, so its fish may not adapt to fluctuating conditions.
- Females show low fecundity in the wild.
- Captive propagation may have further eroded genetic diversity.

The earlier study recommended substituting stock from Sharp Spring, a thermally fluctuating arroyo where fish are more fecund and coexist with the introduced predator [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish). The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] cautiously adopted this recommendation in its recovery plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study used a laboratory test of fitness in a novel environment. Wild gravid females were collected in Fall 1986 from three Arizona localities, identified as sites C (Monkey Spring), D (Sharp Spring), and E (Tule Spring, [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]). Tule Spring fish belong to a different subspecies (*P. o. sonoriensis*, Rio Yaqui drainage) than Sharp and Monkey Spring fish (*P. o. occidentalis*, Gila River drainage). They were chosen only because their heterozygosity was intermediate. No other Gila drainage population, apart from Sharp Spring, showed detectable electrophoretic variation.

A fourth population, [[Cienega Creek]] (H = 0.0%), could not be used because of heavy [[Helminth Parasite]] infection, which prevented establishing a laboratory stock.

Rearing conditions were tightly controlled:

- Females were held in 4 L containers suspended in a 1600 L circulating water bath.
- Temperature was held at 28 ± 1 °C, with a 16L:8D photoperiod.
- Females were fed frozen brine shrimp nauplii and liver paste twice daily.
- Females were acclimated for 30 days before progeny were harvested.

The authors note that the near-constant incubator conditions most closely match the thermally stable habitat of Monkey Spring. This gave that stock an apparent advantage, yet it still performed worst overall.

## Key Findings

**Genetic background**
- Mean heterozygosity: Monkey Spring 0.0%, Tule Spring 1.5%, Sharp Spring 3.7%.
- Ten gravid females per locality were used to produce broods, and the Table 1 caption gives 30 wild-caught females in total.

**Survival (12-week test)**
- Survival differed among populations (Wilcoxon rank test, χ² = 8.98, df = 2, P < 0.02).
- Monkey Spring had the poorest survival (S = 9.0 weeks, SE = 0.5).
- Tule Spring and Sharp Spring did not differ significantly (S = 10.4 weeks, SE = 0.4).

**Growth**
- Standard length was measured at the end of the 12-week period. Males and females were analyzed separately because males grow more slowly after maturity.
- Females: ANOVA F[2, 39] = 26.92, P < 0.001.
- Males: ANOVA F[2, 64] = 17.98, P < 0.001.
- Growth rank order matched heterozygosity: Sharp Spring fastest, Monkey Spring slowest.

**Fecundity**
- After correcting for female size, fecundity differed significantly (ANCOVA F[2, 30] = 6.68, P < 0.005).
- Sharp Spring > Tule Spring > Monkey Spring.
- Mean ovum diameter did not differ among stocks (ANOVA F[2, 31] = 0.15, P = 0.86), so the fecundity differences were not offset by egg size.

**Developmental stability**
- Seven bilateral meristic characters were scored on 109 first-generation offspring: Monkey Spring n = 25, Tule Spring n = 49, Sharp Spring n = 35.
- Across all seven characters, Sharp Spring had the highest developmental stability and differed significantly from Monkey Spring (Friedman χ² = 8.00, df = 2, P < 0.02). Tule Spring was intermediate and did not differ significantly from either stock.
- Three characters differed significantly among stocks in Table 1: lateral line scales (χ² = 11.31, P < 0.01), pectoral fin rays (χ² = 8.95, P < 0.02), and pelvic fin rays (χ² = 10.81, P < 0.01). The other four characters did not differ significantly.
- Table 1 reports Kruskal-Wallis tests for these comparisons, while the text cites a Friedman test.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The homozygous Monkey Spring stock "clearly is not the best choice" for the basic recovery stock.
- Sharp Spring fish are recommended for stocking reclaimed Gila River habitats. Their heterozygosity is typical of many freshwater fishes, and their high fecundity and survival favor rapid establishment after [[Founder Events]].
- The authors recommend periodic supplementation from Sharp Spring to maintain variation.
- Monkey Spring should be maintained as an isolated reservoir of potentially unique variation, because those fish persist in the wild without competition or predation from introduced exotics.
- Admixture could raise fitness, but indiscriminate hybridization risks [[Outbreeding Depression]]. The authors call for experimental studies of hybridization effects in endangered species.
- The authors argue that careful genetic surveys using non-destructive sampling should be performed rapidly, because fitness-component studies are not feasible for many endangered species.
