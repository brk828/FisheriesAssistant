# The Melanoma Cell as an Incompletely Differentiated Pigment Cell

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Myron Gordon]] (Genetics Laboratory, New York Zoological Society) synthesizes histological, genetic, embryological, transplantation, regeneration and tissue-culture evidence to argue that melanocytes in [[Platyfish-Swordtail Hybrid]] melanomas are pigment cells arrested in development rather than mature tumour cells. The paper builds a three-stage life cycle for fish pigment cells (melanoblast, melanocytes, melanophore) and proposes that melanoma is an inherited metabolic error that blocks melanocyte differentiation. The reprint also includes a transcribed discussion section with comments from several pigment-cell researchers.

## Background & Description

The review defines three cell types, summarized in a comparison table:

- **Melanoblast:** an unpigmented embryonic precursor, shown by [[Humm and Young]] (1956) to originate from the neural tube in xiphophorin hybrids. Highly migratory, actively dividing.
- **Melanocyte:** a mature melanin-producing cell, 10 to 100 µm, variously dendritic and migratory. Dominant cell type in melanomas.
- **Melanophore:** a sessile pigment-effector cell, 100 to 500 µm, subspherical, with rare or no division. Macromelanophores (up to about 500 µm) and micromelanophores (about 100 µm, rarely above 300 µm) are the two forms in platyfish.

Figure legends describe additional melanoma cell types, including pigmented hypertrophic fibroblasts (25 to 35 µm), giant cells (about 500 µm) and pigmented stroma cells (10 to 15 µm), which acquire melanin from neighbouring pigment cells rather than producing it themselves.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper concerns fishes mainly as genetic and developmental models rather than as ecological subjects. Several points bear on the wider aquatic context:

- **Genetics:** micromelanophore patterns are autosomally inherited, whereas macromelanophore patterns are incompletely sex-linked ([[Gordon 1927]]). Melanoma-prone hybrids arise when a platyfish carrying the macromelanophore gene is crossed with a swordtail.
- **Comparative pigmentation:** in the marine [[Bluehead Wrasse]] (*Thalassoma bifasciatum*), fin pigment patterns regenerate through the same stellate-to-melanophore sequence. In the [[Needlefish]] (*Tylosurus raphidoma*), juvenile dorsal-fin melanism is shed before sexual maturity, between about 100 and 300 mm.
- **Hormonal control:** in [[Killifish]] (*Fundulus heteroclitus*), intermedin stimulates propigment cell mitosis and differentiation, and prolactin promotes melanin deposition. In [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*), Chavin found that only ACTH induced melanophore formation.
- **Phylogeny:** melanocytes of platyfish melanomas resemble those of mouse and human melanomas in culture. In fish, amphibians and reptiles the melanophore is the terminal stage, whereas in birds and mammals the melanocyte is terminal.

## Key Findings

1. **Neural-crest origin confirmed in fish.** [[Humm and Young]] (1956) transplanted neural tube tissue from a Sd platyfish-swordtail embryo into a swordtail host. Macromelanophores appeared in the graft 10 days later, showing that melanophores derive from unpigmented neural-crest precursors.

2. **Three-stage life cycle.** Melanoblasts become migratory melanocytes, which then differentiate into sessile melanophores. Melanophores are the terminal stage and become senile, degenerate and are removed by [[Macrophages]].

3. **Genetic basis of melanoma.** Macromelanophore genes are necessary for melanoma production, while micromelanophore genes are not. The primary gene comes from the platyfish, and growth-modifying genes come from the swordtail. [[Albino Gene]] combinations determine whether melanoma is amelanotic or melanotic.

4. **Replacement dynamics.** In young melanotic hybrids (6.7 mm and older), melanophore formation and degeneration run continuously. Melanophore replacement outpaces loss, and the body is nearly covered by macromelanophores before the fish are half grown (Gordon and Lansing, 1943).

5. **Melanoma progression.** [[Gordon and Smith]] (1938) described three stages: melanosis (macromelanophores replace muscle), an intermediate phase (corial thickening and melanocyte increase), and a definitive stage dominated by melanocytes.

6. **Melanocyte-to-melanophore transition.** Observed in young normal platyfish aged 20 to 31 days and 8 to 10 mm. Melanocytes grow from about 30 µm to more than 100 µm and acquire dendrites and pigment.

7. **Migration.** Transplant studies by [[Marcus and Gordon]] (1954) recorded melanocyte migration of about 1.5 µm per hour. Cells become sessile once they transform into melanophores.

8. **Reversibility.** Melanoma tissue transplanted into a suitable flank environment reverted, after eight months, to almost entirely macromelanophores with innocuous melanosis. Reverse differentiation from melanophore to melanocyte was not observed.

9. **Precursors in immaculate fins.** Autografts of unspotted fins from 23-day-old Sd hybrids eventually developed macromelanophores and a melanoma pattern, suggesting precursor cells are present before visible pigmentation.

10. **Regeneration.** [[Ermin and Gordon]] (1955) found that amputated fins regenerated in 2 to 3 months with their original pigment pattern. Melanocytes migrated into the blastema and matured into melanophores.

11. **Metabolic differences.** [[Humm and Clark]] (1955) reported that melanocytes show about three times the tyrosine activity of macromelanophores and higher endogenous respiration. Glycolysis measurements in the discussion were 16.6 (normal macromelanophores), 8.9 (hyperpigmented macromelanophores) and 21.4 (melanoma melanocytes).

12. **Discussion highlights.** Commentators offered alternative explanations: terrain effects in amphibians (Lenman), enzymatic anaplasia (Mason), gene dilution by rapid growth (Greenberg), and metabolic-pathway interpretations (Riley). Gordon maintained that the melanocyte-arrest model fits his data, and that melanocytes can enlarge into macromelanophores but the reverse has not been found.

13. **Conclusion.** The author proposes that melanoma is "a manifestation of an inherited metabolic error which inhibits the normal development of pigment cells." The paper suggests testing whether pituitary hormones can convert malignant melanocytes into innocuous melanophores.
