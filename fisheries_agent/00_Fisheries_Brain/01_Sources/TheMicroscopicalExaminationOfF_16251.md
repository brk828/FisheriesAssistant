# The Microscopical Examination of Freshwater

#limnology #freshwater-biology #microscopy

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[J. W. G. Lund]] is a practical guide for water-industry staff who need to identify the small objects found in freshwater. It does not attempt full identification keys. Instead it advises readers to learn common microscopic materials by direct, mostly live, examination. The paper covers choice of [[Microscope Equipment]], collection and preparation methods, and the main categories of objects: aquatic organisms, animal and plant fragments, and commercial materials such as wood, fibres and hairs. The printed discussion records questions from water-undertaking members on filter-clogging algae, [[Nais]] in distribution mains, fibre identification in forensic cases, and quantitative counting. The document contains no fish data.

## Background & Description

The author, a biologist who trained in botany and seaweed research and later worked at the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] Windermere Laboratory, aims to help readers recognise small objects found in water. He states that a full descriptive treatment would need "a few thousand pages and several thousand illustrations," so the paper focuses on methods and references. Although it covers freshwater only, most groups also occur in marine and brackish water, with different species.

Key practical advice on equipment:

- **Cost tiers:** A research microscope costs about £500. Work on all the object classes described is possible with equipment costing about £100. Anything new under £50 is "unlikely to be of much use."
- **Objectives:** The author favours a [[Quadruple Objective Holder]] and first buys 16 mm and 4 mm lenses. A 2 mm oil or water immersion lens is the first choice for highest magnification.
- **Oculars:** An ocular of about 15x is a good investment. Perfect definition extends to roughly 1000 times the objective's numerical aperture.
- **Illumination:** Daylight is ideal but often unavailable. An opal bulb with a blue filter helps. Gelatine colour filters are much cheaper than glass ones and give more nearly monochromatic light.
- **Phase contrast:** This is recommended as the most valuable extra. It shows living bacteria, cleaned diatoms and flagella with "startling clarity."
- **Maintenance:** Dust and acid fumes are the main hazards. Lenses should be cleaned with camel-hair brushes, lens tissue or chamois, and microscopes kept covered and away from chemicals.

Object categories described in Section 3 include:

- **Aquatic organisms:** [[Algae]], [[Bacteria]], [[Fungi]], [[Protozoa]], rotifers, nematodes and crustaceans.
- **Animal fragments:** insect wings, crustacean appendages, statoblasts of *Cristatella*, liver-fluke cercariae and sponge spicules.
- **Plant fragments:** [[Pollen]], seeds, plant tissue, phytoliths and hairs.
- **Commercial products:** [[Timber and Wood]], [[Textile Fibres]], animal hairs and feathers, and [[Diatomaceous Earth]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper places the objects in a water-treatment setting. Slow sand filters are a prolific source of algae, protozoa and bacteria. Self-purification zones below polluted reaches show a succession of communities. Sewage "fungus," mainly the bacterium *Sphaerotilus natans*, forms greyish felty masses and is common in the water industry. Iron and sulphur bacteria also occur.

Interface communities at the mud–water boundary are rich in protozoa and microorganisms. The author suggests reforming them in dishes by dropping coverglasses onto thin mud layers. Rhizopods are most common in peat moss (*Sphagnum*), and ciliates are especially abundant in organically rich mud.

Pollen is a seasonal indicator. Tree pollen dominates in spring, grass pollen in summer, and grass pollen is usually shed very early in the day. Pollen in sediments is used to study the post-glacial history of lakes and bogs. The author notes that the same pollens cause [[Hay Fever]].

Fibres and hairs show human influence on water. Timber and textile waste enter from pulp factories and laundries. Discussion examples include a case where [[Nylon]] fibres caused blue stains on laundry.

## Key Findings

**Methods and advice**

- The paper's main message is to examine common, mostly living, specimens first. The author calls this "a golden rule."
- Fixation and preservation methods are compared. [[Osmic Acid]] is the best fixative for algae and protozoa, but it decomposes quickly. [[Formalin]] at about 4 per cent is the best general preservative but a poor fixative. [[Lugol's Solution]] is satisfactory for killing and preserving protozoa and algae.
- Cleaning glassware uses a sulphuric acid/dichromate solution made from 200 g potassium dichromate in 800 ml water with 1200 ml concentrated sulphuric acid. Glasses are then stored in methyl alcohol.
- Stains recommended include methylene blue, gentian violet and acid fuchsin (1 per cent aqueous), Sudan IV for fats, [[Chlor-zinc-iodine]] for cellulose, and phloroglucinol in hydrochloric acid for lignin. Ruthenium red stains algal mucilage.
- Canada balsam is discouraged. Polystyrene mountant (10 g in 40 ml xylene with plasticiser) is recommended instead.

**Diatom preparation**

- Diatoms are cleaned by incineration on thin coverglasses (No. 1 or No. 0) with hydrogen peroxide, or by acid treatment. Acid treatment uses concentrated sulphuric acid and nitric acid, followed by filtering on acid-hardened paper and washing to neutrality.
- Identification needs a mounting medium with refractive index above 1.6. Commercial mountants are often insufficient for the finest markings.

**Quantitative methods (from the discussion)**

- [[Plankton Net]] mesh of about 60–70 microns retains larger algae and protozoa. Whatman No. 541 filter paper is a convenient grade.
- The best method for counting algae is sedimentation in iodine–potassium iodide solution, using an inverted microscope. Filtration through anything other than a membrane filter is usually a poor quantitative method.
- Commercial haemocytometers hold only 0.0032 ml and are 200 microns deep, which some organisms exceed. Mr. Davis describes a homemade [[Sedgwick-Rafter Counting Chamber]] built from plastic wall tiles, holding about 0.2 ml.
- Mr. Davis's concentration method passes samples through a sintered glass crucible. Zooplankton counts are then made with a stereoscopic microscope.

**Water-industry observations (from the discussion)**

- Filter-clogging algae matter more than cell numbers suggest. Long *Fragilaria* ribbons caused problems at Hanningfield even when not one frustule appeared in the algal count.
- Filter runs depended on diatom numbers. Runs were only a few hours when diatoms (mainly *Synedra* and *Asterionella*) reached 15,000–20,000 per ml. They increased to 24 hours when numbers were 2,000–3,000 per ml.
- *Nais* appeared in significant numbers only from June to the end of September. Mr. Knight suggested a resistant form that survives high chlorine doses. Mr. English observed *Nais* in the raw water and at distant points where chlorine residual was low or absent, even though none was found where residual reached 0.5 ppm.
- Earthy tastes may relate to [[Actinomycetes]] in raw water.
- Long-term algal forecasts are possible only in general terms, for example periodicity in English Lake District lakes, and rely on past regular microscopic examination.

**Forensic and fibre identification**

- In one case, [[Nylon]] fibres from a lounge rug were identified by the Shirley Institute as identical to fibres staining laundry. Removing the rug restored the wash.
- Microscopy may help quantify man-made fibres in textile mixtures, which matters for import duty ratings. Staining methods such as Shirlastain A and B are suggested.

**Equipment and institutions**

- Lund recommends the [[McArthur Microscope]] as the best portable instrument ever made. It is robust and high quality, and its cost is comparable to a normal microscope.
- Membership of the [[Quekett Microscopical Club]] and the [[Ray Society]] is recommended. The Ray Society issues volumes on freshwater microscopic groups.
