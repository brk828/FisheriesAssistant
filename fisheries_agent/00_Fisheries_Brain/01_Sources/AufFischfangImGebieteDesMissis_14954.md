# Field Collecting in the Mississippi and Pánuco Basins, with a Garden Pond Construction Guide

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #fundulidae

## Executive Summary

This German-language article from the aquarium and terrarium magazine *Blätter für Aquarien- und Terrarienkunde* combines three items: (1) collecting records of salamander larvae and metamorphosing juveniles from Kentucky and Indiana; (2) a hobbyist's fish-collecting trip from [[New Orleans]] and near [[Tampico]], Mexico, targeting live-bearing and [[Killifish]] species; and (3) a practical guide to building a garden pond with a double layer of roofing felt. The fish content is anecdotal, with no formal sample design, but it provides dated field observations, habitat temperatures and collected-species lists.

## Background & Description

The salamander section is a short field report. The collecting trip is a first-person account by a member of an aquarium society (the author is identified as Wilhelm Riemenschneider, of Bremen). The pond section is written by a hobbyist describing a personal garden pond built to replace a lost natural retreat near a city.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Floodplain habitats:** The Indiana woodlands lie in the Ohio River floodplain and are flooded each spring. Pools and small ponds there held larvae and recently transformed salamanders.
- **Louisiana sloughs:** Ditches and pools near New Orleans were dense with submerged plants (Wasserpest, likely *Elodea*). Water moved very slowly, and the bottom was firm clay under about 20 cm of muck.
- **Pánuco tributary pools:** Flood-formed pools near Tancasnequi were fresh, heavily vegetated and believed to dry up in summer, according to local residents. The site lies about 1000 m above sea level, as the author states.
- **Temperature profiles:**
  - Louisiana ditch (midday): 21 °C surface, 20 °C mid-depth, 17 °C bottom.
  - Pánuco pools: 24 °C at the edge, 18 °C at 1 m depth about 5 m from shore.
  - Pánuco stream: 22 °C, maximum depth 60 cm.
- **Food:** The author found no live food at any collecting site. Captured fish accepted dry food and scraped meat.

## Key Findings

**Salamander records (Kentucky and Indiana)**
- 15 June, Smith Hill, Henderson County, Kentucky: 3 adults and 8 juveniles still in metamorphosis were found under logs and stumps in woodland. Four juveniles were taken to Ann Arbor, kept alive for many weeks and fed earthworms. They showed a strong tendency to hide.
- 10 July, Union Township, Vanderburg County, Indiana: large numbers of larvae were found in woodland water holes near a road and in small woodland ponds.
- Recently transformed animals were common under bark and stumps at the same site. A net sample yielded 48 gilled specimens. A further 15 gilled animals at various developmental stages were found out of water. No adults were found at this site.
- Three metamorphosing animals were found three days later in a damp woodland at the edge of the Ohio floodplain in Perry Township.

**Louisiana ditch collection (near New Orleans)**
- Initial dip-netting of ponds yielded mainly *Gambusia holbrooki* (mosquitofish) and one other *Gambusia* species, which the author rejected as unwanted.
- A winding ditch (about 2 m wide, up to about 1.5 m deep) held many fish that fled quickly. The collectors switched to a method of stirring the water upstream with clods of earth and grass, driving fish into two stationary nets (50 cm and 30 cm diameter). The author reports this worked "surprisingly well."
- About two-thirds of the catch was *Poecilia latipinna*. The author kept 8 pairs, selected for male dorsal fins. A male and three females were nearly black; their offspring were reported as very dark.
- Several *Fundulus* species were present: *F. chrysotus*, *F. notti*, *F. notatus*, *F. dispar* and *F. heteroclitus*. Three unidentified cichlid species and several large sunfish and other perches were also caught. The larger fish were released.
- On the return trip, about half the kept animals died from oxygen shortage and overcrowding, including all *Fundulus* except a few *F. chrysotus*.

**Pánuco collection (Tancasnequi, Mexico)**
- The collector found medium-sized swordtails and red platys in the pools. The pool platys were nearly white with a reddish dorsal fin.
- In the nearby stream he caught deep-bodied, bright blue platys. Most of these died on the journey home, and only one blue male and one female survived to reach the club.
- The collector reports he could not return and asked a friend to collect blue specimens at the same site.

**Garden pond construction (practical guide)**
- The author's existing pond was about 8 m long and 3 to 3.5 m wide, with a 75 cm maximum depth. It was built using roofing felt (*Dachpappe*) as a liner, and had to be cleaned and re-covered after several years because of overgrowth and brittle felt.
- Recommended construction: a sunny, open site; a kidney or elliptical outline; gently sloping sides (no vertical walls) so ice can expand; and a flat, rolled base, with footwear that does not puncture the liner.
- The felt is laid in two perpendicular layers, with about 15 cm overlap. Seams are sealed with hot bitumen (*Goudron*), applied in dry weather, and the whole surface is coated again.
- Edges are secured by pressing the excess liner into a trench and covering it with soil or turf. The bottom is lined with a thin layer of river sand, with richer soil under planting areas.
- Recommended plants include water lilies in a range of colours, planted in woven baskets placed before filling. The text also mentions marsh plants such as calamus, arum and frogbit, and notes that the pond attracted birds and provided water fleas for aquarists.

**Overall relevance**
- The fish observations show that many *Fundulus*, *Gambusia*, *Poecilia* and *Xiphophorus* populations were accessible in the southern US and northeastern Mexico, and that transport losses were severe.
- Salamander observations document seasonal flood-pool breeding and terrestrial transformation in the Ohio valley.
