# Sport Fishing Impacts on Native Apache Trout in Christmas Tree Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This contributed paper to the Wild Trout II Symposium (Mammoth, Wyoming, September 24–25, 1979) by John N. Rinne, Ben Robertson, Ron Major and Kel Harper examines a 1978 sport fishery on [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) in [[Christmas Tree Lake]], a small impoundment on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]]. The study estimated population size by [[Petersen Mark-Recapture]], measured fishing effects, screened for [[Infectious Pancreatic Necrosis Virus]] (IPNV), and tested genetic purity against hybridization with introduced trout.

The lake held an estimated 1,745 Apache trout over 228.5 mm and 746 legal-sized fish (355.6 mm or larger). A nine-day season removed about 45% of legal fish, and the authors concluded the population could be overfished quickly. No IPNV was detected in native trout, and taxonomic evidence indicated that the population had been contaminated by rainbow trout and hybrids. The authors recommended caution about opening a sport fishery and suggested using the population as brood stock.

## Background & Description

**Management history.** Christmas Tree Lake was impounded by the White Mountain Apache Tribe and filled in spring 1967. Native Apache trout were stocked there, along with stock from Big Bonito Creek and other sources, and the lake was also stocked with non-native trout. Sport fishing was allowed for only one day in 1969 and 1970, and later for a nine-day season in October 1978. Anglers were permitted to keep only fish over 355.6 mm (14 inches).

**Study objectives.** The 1978 study had four aims:
1. Estimate the population of *S. apache* in the lake by mark-recapture.
2. Determine the effects of fishing on the population.
3. Determine the incidence of IPNV in the trout population.
4. Define the genetic purity of the population.

Specimens collected in 1970, 1971 and 1976 were analyzed for taxonomic purity, and 1975 specimens were tested for IPNV.

**Research team.** Rinne was a fishery research biologist with the Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, working with Arizona State University. Robertson and Major were fishery biologists, and Harper was a fishery management biologist, all with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

**Pre-study workshop.** Before the 1978 fishing season, a planning meeting was held among the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, White Mountain Recreation Enterprises and the U.S. Forest Service.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake physical characteristics.**
- Located in mixed-conifer forest at roughly 2,500 m elevation (unit partly obscured in the scan).
- At full pool: 16.7 surface hectares, maximum depth 13.7 m, full-capacity volume 100 hectare-metres.
- Sits at the confluence of Sun and Moon creeks, tributaries of the Diamond Creek system, which drains into the North Fork of the White River.

**Water chemistry and thermal regime.**
- Surface water temperatures reached 17–20 °C in summer, and the lake froze in winter.
- The lake stratified. Dissolved oxygen in July 1978 was 8 mg/L at the surface, falling to 3.0 mg/L at depth.
- Beds of aquatic vegetation occurred in the littoral zone.

**Native and introduced trout context.** The native *S. apache* shares water with introduced [[Brown Trout]] (*S. trutta*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*S. gairdneri*) and [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*). The authors cite [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. clarkii*) lake populations as a comparison for exploitation and condition. Brook trout were a particular concern, and a fish barrier was constructed in 1979 to keep them from moving upstream.

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort and catch composition (July 1978).**
- Six days of sampling (19–29 July 1978) yielded 106 *S. apache* by [[Trammel Net]] (91 × 2.5 m, 38 mm mesh), 32 by [[Hoop Net]] and 28 by angling. Trammel nets were checked hourly to reduce mortality.
- Fish were weighed, measured, and tagged with Floy-type dart tags behind the dorsal fin. Kidney and spleen tissue were removed from mortalities for IPNV testing.
- Trammel nets and angling took similar proportions of legal fish (72% and 64%), and both gears showed similar size distributions (smallest fish about 229–234 mm).
- Hoop nets took a higher proportion of sub-legal fish (roughly 40–50% legal) and the smallest fish captured (91 mm).
- Most tagged fish were released near the dam or at the downstream end of the lake. The remainder were released in the Sun and Moon creek arms.

**Angler harvest, nine-day season.**
- Only 301 of 450 license holders (67%) actually fished.
- Anglers averaged 6.3 hours each. The lake yielded 0.25 fish per angler-hour.
- 463 fish were caught in nine days, and 38 legal fish were harvested. Legal fish were checked in, and sub-legal fish were released.
- The authors state that the estimated catch was about one fish every 4–4.5 hours.

**Population estimates (Petersen method, single mark and release).**
- Total *S. apache* over 228.5 mm: 1,745 ± 362 (95% confidence limits).
- Legal-sized (>355.6 mm) *S. apache*: 746 ± 116.
- Legal-sized exploitation rate over nine days: 333/746, or about 45%, described as "approaching half" of the estimated population.
- Removals included about a quarter of the larger fish (>228.5 mm) and 452 legal-sized fish in roughly a third of a month.

**Exploitation rates compared with other lakes.** The authors cite annual exploitation of 0.06–0.10 for cutthroat trout lake populations in the literature. Their calculated rates for *S. apache* were substantially higher, though on a shorter time basis.

**Post-fishing indicators (spring 1979).** Only 3 of 26 fish (12%) captured by angling and 12 of 56 (21.4%) captured by trammel net exceeded 355.6 mm. The authors describe this as a dramatic drop from about 70% legal-sized fish before the fishing season.

**Condition and growth.**
- Relative condition (K_TL) of *S. apache* in the lake was 1.00 in summer 1978.
- Other reported values include about 1.5 for large fish from the White River and a mean of 1.10 for a group of 30 fish from Big Bonito Creek. Cutthroat trout in the literature averaged about 1.1.
- Standing crop estimates were computed from the population estimate and average weight, though the scanned value is partly unreadable.

**IPNV screening.**
- No IPNV was detected in 1978 samples of brown or Arizona trout from the lake.
- One positive result occurred in June 1975 tests, but 50 Arizona trout and 7 brown trout tested in October 1975 were negative.
- Across roughly a dozen streams on the reservation, IPNV was not found in native trout. A low collective occurrence (about 4%) was found in brown and rainbow trout.
- The authors recommend continued IPNV surveillance and note that infection could cause high mortality in fry and fingerlings.

**Taxonomic and genetic purity.**
- Specimens from 1970 (eight fish) were rainbow in all morphometric measures but native in three meristic characters.
- Specimens from 1971 (nine fish) were native in all nine characters examined.
- Specimens from 1976 were native in the anal fin base and three meristic characters, but rainbow-like in five morphometric proportions.
- The largest sample (1978) was native in all three meristic characters, but was native only in dorsal fin depressed length. The remaining characters were hybrid or rainbow-like.
- Karyotype analysis of fish from Deep Creek showed chromosome types indicative of rainbow trout.
- The authors conclude that the lake population is genetically impure, with hybridization with rainbow trout having occurred over time.

**Management recommendations and implications.**
- The population could serve as brood stock for hatchery production or for restoring native trout to local streams. However, use would be limited by the genetic impurity of the lake fish.
- Any sport fishery should be carefully regulated, and the effects on the population should be monitored. Angler harvest appears to be the largest source of mortality.
- The authors concluded that a sport fishery on native trout at Christmas Tree Lake was "inadvisable" because the fishery could overharvest the population quickly, and because harvest could support the spread of hybrids and non-native genes.
- The authors recommended that phenotypic and genetic changes be monitored, and that IPNV be screened for before any stock is moved.
- Because the population is not yet certified as specific pathogen-free (SPF), which is required for commercial or stocking use, the authors recommend further disease testing before it is used as a source of fish.
