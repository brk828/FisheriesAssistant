# Desert Pupfish Endangered Species Listing and Critical Habitat Designation (Federal Register, March 31, 1986)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodon

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register final rule, issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Interior Department), lists the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and designates [[Critical Habitat]] in Imperial County, California, and Pima and Yuma-area Arizona sites. The rule took effect April 30, 1986. Only two historic habitat areas in the United States still hold viable, self-sustaining populations. The rule also responds to public comments from 28 interested parties. The same issue of the Federal Register includes unrelated rules from the [[Interstate Commerce Commission]] on recordkeeping waivers for low-value packages, and brief references to other Interior actions.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and morphology:** The species was described in 1853 by Baird and Girard from specimens collected in the San Pedro River of Arizona. It is a small, laterally compressed fish. Adults rarely exceed 75 mm (3 in) total length.
- **Sexual dimorphism and coloration:** Males are larger than females. During spawning, males turn bright blue on the dorsal head and sides, with yellow on the caudal fin and posterior caudal peduncle. Females and juveniles are tan to olive-backed with silvery sides. Most adults have narrow, vertical dark bars.
- **Life history:** Desert pupfish mature rapidly and may produce up to three generations per year. Spawning males defend small territories in shallow water. Eggs are laid and fertilized on a flocculent substrate and hatch within a few days. Spawning occurs through spring and summer. Individuals typically survive about one year.
- **Research value:** The species adapts well to [[Laboratory Aquaria]], making it a valued research animal for ichthyologists and physiologists.
- **Historic range:** It was once common in desert springs, marshes, and tributary streams of the lower [[Gila River]] and [[Colorado River]] drainages in Arizona, California, and Mexico. It also occurred in slow reaches of large rivers, including the Colorado, Gila, San Pedro, and Santa Cruz.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiological tolerances.** The species tolerates extreme conditions:
- Water temperatures above 43.3 °C (110 °F) (Moyle, 1976)
- Dissolved oxygen as low as 0.1–0.4 ppm (Lowe et al., 1967)
- Salinities nearly twice that of seawater (Barlow, 1958)
- Daily salinity swings of 10–15 ppt (Kinne, 1960)

Despite these tolerances, it cannot withstand competition or predation and is readily displaced by [[Exotic Fishes]].

**Threats to surviving populations.** The rule lists:
- Competition from exotic fishes for food and space
- Predation by exotic fishes, including [[Tilapia]] (*Tilapia zillii*) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- Water pollution and [[Groundwater Pumping]]
- Agricultural pesticide drift
- Stream channelization
- Habitat changes linked to floods in the [[Colorado River Delta]] in 1983 and 1984. These floods may have allowed exotic fishes access to Santa Clara Slough and improved their habitat by improving delta water quality.

**Status of remaining habitats.**
- In California, the species persists in two Salton Sea tributaries (San Felipe Creek system and San Sebastian Marsh; Salt Creek) and in shoreline pools and irrigation drains around the [[Salton Sea]]. Recent surveys suggest Salt Creek and Salton Sea drain populations may be reduced to non-viable levels.
- In Arizona, the species persists in [[Quitobaquito Spring]] within [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]].
- In Mexico, populations in the Rio Sonoyta drainage may be non-viable. The status of the Santa Clara Slough population is unknown.

**Refugia and introductions.**
- Arizona refugia: Bog Hole and Research Ranch (Santa Cruz County), Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum (Pima County), Boyce Thompson Arboretum (Pinal County), and Arizona State University (Maricopa County).
- California refugia: Salton Sea State Park, the Living Desert Reserve, and three sites in [[Anza-Borrego Desert State Park]].
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) holds fish from Santa Clara Slough for research and future reintroduction.
- Recent introductions on [[Bureau of Land Management]] land at three Arizona spring sites (Peoples Canyon, Howard Well, and Mesquite Spring). Their long-term survival is unknown.

**Land ownership.** Quitobaquito Spring is entirely on [[National Park Service]] land. Land along San Felipe Creek forms a checkerboard of federal and private holdings. Federal lands around San Sebastian Marsh are under oil and gas leases with no-surface-occupancy stipulations.

## Key Findings

1. **Listing decision:** The Service determined that the desert pupfish is in danger of extinction throughout a significant portion of its range and designated it as endangered. It found that existing conservation efforts and protective mechanisms are not adequate to ensure continued existence.
2. **Critical habitat:** Critical habitat was designated for the species in Imperial County, California, and in Arizona (Pima County and nearby areas), including Quitobaquito Spring and San Felipe Creek watersheds. Designation affords the full protection of the Endangered Species Act.
3. **Consultation:** Under Section 7, federal agencies must ensure their actions do not jeopardize the species or adversely modify its critical habitat. Consultation with the Service is required for federal activities that may affect the species.
4. **Exclusions considered:** Under Section 4(b)(2), the Service considered excluding areas from critical habitat. Irrigation drains and canals of the Imperial Irrigation District (IID) and Coachella Valley Water District (CVWD) were debated. The Service concluded that benefits of inclusion outweigh exclusion where the species' survival depends on these areas.
5. **Economic impact:** The Service concluded that no significant economic or other impacts are expected from the critical habitat designation. Land use in the critical habitat is currently compatible with the species' needs. Geothermal and oil and gas activities near San Sebastian Marsh were considered, but none were expected to adversely affect the species.
6. **Public comment:** Public notices and hearings drew 28 written comment submissions. Four commenters supported the proposal. Opponents, including IID and CVWD, questioned the scientific basis for listing and critical habitat designation and the economic effects. The Service responded to these comments in the rule.
7. **Regulatory flexibility:** The Service certified that the rule does not have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small entities.
8. **Recovery and research:** Refugia populations and reintroductions are part of the recovery effort. Continued monitoring, habitat protection, and control of exotic fishes are essential to long-term survival.
