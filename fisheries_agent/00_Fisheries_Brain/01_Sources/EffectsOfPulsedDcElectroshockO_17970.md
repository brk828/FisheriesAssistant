# Effects of Pulsed DC Electroshock on Adult Roundtail Chub from the Colorado River in Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Cowdell and Valdez (1994, *North American Journal of Fisheries Management*, Management Briefs) tested whether [[Pulsed DC Electrofishing]] causes observable injury in adult [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) captured from the [[Colorado River]]. Forty fish were shocked with a [[Coffelt VVP-15 Electrofisher]] in August 1992, then examined by [[Radiography]] and [[Necropsy]]. Two fish (5%) showed local spinal hemorrhaging, and no dislocated or fractured vertebrae were found. The authors concluded that the method was safe under the tested settings. The study was motivated by the [[Endangered Species Act]] status of related natives such as the [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]].

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), a native Colorado River cyprinid. The authors describe it as closely related and physiologically similar to [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*) and [[Bonytail]] (*G. elegans*), and it has been hybridized with them in hatcheries (Hamman 1981).
- **Conservation context:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), humpback chub and bonytail are listed as endangered under the U.S. Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended.
- **Prior knowledge gap:** Electrofishing harm is well documented in salmonids (Hauck 1949; Pratt 1955; Godfrey 1957; Hudy 1985; Sharber and Carothers 1988) and centrarchids (Spencer 1967). Little was known for nongame or threatened species and for larger cyprinids. Adams et al. (1972) reported high mortality in common shiners (*Luxilus cornutus*) needing more than 120 s to recover from DC exposure in the lab. Schneider (1992) reported no mortality in golden shiners (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*) shocked with 230-V AC in the field.
- **Objective:** Identify observable injuries in adult roundtail chub electrofished with pulsed DC under field conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Site conditions (August 1992):** Water temperature averaged 20°C; conductivity was 1,000 µS/cm.
- **Sample fish:** 40 adults, mean total length 269 mm (range 219–404 mm), mean weight 149 g (range 74–414 g).
- **Electrofishing settings:** Square-waveform pulsed DC at 120 V and 8 A, 20% duty cycle, 40 Hz.
- **Gear:** Operated from a 5.2-m aluminum boat with two 28-cm-diameter spherical anodes on a bow boom and two stainless steel cable cathodes (4.5 m long, 6.4 mm diameter) dragged about 4.5 m from the anodes. The authors observed no fish contacting the electrodes.
- **Handling:** Stunned fish were netted into a live well, examined for bruises and swimming abnormalities, euthanized with [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222), photographed, frozen, then later X-rayed and filleted.
- **Funding and permits:** U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Glen Canyon Environmental Studies); Colorado Division of Wildlife collection permit.

## Key Findings

- **No external or behavioral injury:** No external bruises or abnormal behavior were noted. Fish recovered quickly, typically in under 60 s, to regain equilibrium.
- **Spinal integrity:** Neither X-rays nor fillet examination showed spinal dislocation, fracture, or compression in any of the 40 fish.
- **Hemorrhaging:** Two fish (5%) had spinal hemorrhaging.
  - Fish 1 (TL 240 mm, 96 g): a moderate hematoma of about 1 cm on the right side below the dorsal fin, involving three vertebrae.
  - Fish 2 (TL 224 mm, 84 g): an elongated hematoma spanning 10 vertebrae, radiating through the muscle on both sides below the dorsal fin.
  - Both lesions resembled electrofishing injuries reported in rainbow trout and in [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] (Spencer 1967). Lateral X-rays showed no associated vertebral damage.
- **No other internal trauma:** The other 38 fish showed no internal trauma or hemorrhaging.
- **Field consistency:** Rapid recovery and lack of spinal injury matched the authors' prior field work in the upper Colorado River basin, where injury signs such as bruising, abnormal swimming, or gill hemorrhage were rarely seen in native cyprinids.
- **Authors' conclusion:** Electrofishing with square-wave pulsed DC at 40 Hz was judged a safe capture method for roundtail chub under these conditions, with a low injury rate.
- **Limitations:** Fish were observed only briefly and not held long-term, so chronic effects are unknown. The authors call for further evaluation of electrofishing as a sampling tool for native and endangered Colorado River fish. Extrapolation to humpback chub and bonytail was based on similarity, not direct testing.
