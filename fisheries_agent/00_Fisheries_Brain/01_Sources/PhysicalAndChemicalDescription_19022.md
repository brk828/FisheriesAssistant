# Physical and Chemical Description of Birch Creek, a Travertine-Depositing Stream, Inyo County, California

#limnology #travertine #cyanobacteria #rivulariaceae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Professional Paper by Keith V. Slack describes the environmental setting, water chemistry and travertine formation of [[Birch Creek]], a small spring-fed stream in the [[White Mountains]] of eastern California. The study was carried out in spring 1961 and summer 1962 to determine which environmental controls drive [[Travertine]] (calcite) deposition in an arid-region stream, with particular interest in the relative roles of inorganic and organic (biological) processes. The creek has perennial flow from ground-water discharge at a wet meadow, and its calcite crust is built largely by colonies of the blue-green alga [[Rivularia]]. Water chemistry shows strong daily cycles that grow stronger downstream, and differs between the two years. The author concludes that inorganic and organic controls cannot be cleanly separated, because both respond to [[Solar Radiation]].

## Background & Description

**Study setting.** Birch Creek drains about 16 square miles (41 km²) and flows southeastward into [[Deep Springs Valley]], an intermontane basin at about 5,000 ft (1,525 m). The channel starts near 10,000 ft (3,048 m) and ends on an alluvial fan at about 5,600 ft (1,700 m). It is ephemeral for most of its length. Perennial flow begins at about 6,950 ft (2,118 m) where ground water discharges from springs in a [[Wet Meadow]]. The perennial reach was about 1¼ miles (2 km) long in 1961 and roughly half a mile longer in 1962, after a wet winter. Discharge from the springs was about 0.4–0.5 cfs (14 L/s). The bedrock is metamorphosed Cambrian and Precambrian sediments, locally intruded by granite. The creek is free of cultural influence and is small enough to study in detail.

**Neighboring stream.** [[Wyman Creek]], the largest tributary of Deep Springs Valley (about 30 square miles, or 78 km², drainage), was sampled in 1962 for comparison. It has a similar but less pronounced daily chemical cycle.

**Methods.** Continuous observations were made at single points for 24–80 hours each, so the data are nonsynoptic. Stations were 0–5 on Birch Creek (station 5 at the wet meadow outflow, station 0 at the terminus of flow) and one station on Wyman Creek. Key methods included:
- [[Winkler Method]] (azide modification) for [[Dissolved Oxygen]], corrected for temperature and pressure.
- Titration for [[Bicarbonate Ion]], with [[Carbonate Ion]] and [[Free Carbon Dioxide]] computed from temperature, pH, ionic strength and bicarbonate.
- Continuous [[Specific Conductance]] recorders with automatic temperature compensation.
- A multichannel thermistor thermometer (accurate to 0.1 °C) for air, water, streambed and bank temperatures.
- A pyrheliometer for short-wave solar radiation (below 3.5 µm) in 1962.
- Calcium and magnesium accumulation on [[Plexiglass Plates]] and glass microscope slides to estimate deposition rates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate.** The area is a desert region with rain or snow, and summer thunderstorms are common. Thunderstorms caused nearly three-fourths of recorded White Mountain floods (Kesseli and Beaty, 1959). Preceding the 1961 season were about three years of deficient rainfall. The 1962 season had above-normal precipitation after heavy snowfall in January–March and a major storm on May 14–15, 1962. Peak solar radiation measured was 1.75 g cal/cm²/min at 1300 hours on July 20, 1962, at station 3.

**Morphology and flow.** Discharge is greatest near the source and decreases downstream through evapotranspiration and seepage. Discharge also varies daily. The maximum occurs between midnight and sunrise, and the minimum shortly after noon. In May 1961 the terminus of flow moved about 130 feet (40 m) over 24 hours, retreating at the warmest time of day.

**Riparian vegetation.** Stations are shaded by birch, willow, horsetail (*Equisetum*) and watercress near the seeps. Downstream stations have sagebrush, rabbit brush, thistles and some juniper and piñon pine. Algae and aquatic moss are more abundant downstream.

**Air and sand temperatures.** Near-water air temperatures during the day lay between free-air and water temperatures. At night they followed free-air temperature. Short-term reversals in air temperature, such as a 3.8 °C rise between 1900 and 2100 hours at station 5 in 1962, were probably caused by local airflow or cloud cover. Wet sand stayed cooler than air day and night. Dry sand was much warmer than air during the day.

**Relative humidity.** In 1962 relative humidity was generally below 30 percent except during rain showers. Maximums usually occurred near sunrise and minimums between 1300 and 1700 hours.

**Water temperature.** Water temperature responded quickly to solar radiation because the flow is shallow. Minimums occurred between 0300 and 0530 hours and maximums between 1100 and 1400 hours. The largest recorded 24-hour change was 20 °C at station 1 in 1961. Daily temperature range increased downstream. Water temperature range was smaller in 1962, which the author attributes to higher streamflow and more shading.

**Water chemistry.** Ground water emerged supersaturated with calcite and CO₂, with bicarbonate of about 260–270 ppm and carbonate of about 1.0–1.5 ppm above station 5. Water was alkaline, with pH above 8.0 at every station except station 5. Calcium and bicarbonate decreased downstream as calcite precipitated. Free CO₂ was lowest during the day, consistent with photosynthetic uptake, and highest at night. Carbonate ion was supersaturated with respect to calcite at all stations in both years, and supersaturation was greater in 1961. Average daily range in specific conductance was 54 µmhos (about 12 percent) for the most reliable record, and conductance dropped during rain showers. Water composition was calcium-magnesium carbonate type. Sodium and chloride rose only about 10 percent from station 5 to the lowest station, so evaporation accounted for only about 10 percent of water loss along that reach. Seventeen minor elements were analyzed. Most were near detection limits, and Wyman Creek had higher aluminum, iron, manganese, nickel, titanium and vanadium.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Dissolved oxygen was inversely related to water temperature. Minimums occurred at the warmest times and maximums near the coolest. A transient early-morning peak occurred shortly after sunrise, probably from resumed algal photosynthesis in cold, well-oxygenated water. Daily saturation ranges were greater in 1961 than in 1962, pointing to higher photosynthesis and respiration when algae were abundant.

## Key Findings

- **Travertine formation rate.** The maximum rate was estimated at 5–10 mm per year, from a glass slide that gained 1 mm of deposit in 34 days at station 3. This rate is much slower than the 25 mm per year reported for Doe Run, Kentucky (Minckley, 1963).
- **Erosion event.** High flows in 1962 scoured 4–8 inches (10–20 cm) of streambed material, including algae and the travertine crust. Below station 3 nearly all of the crust was removed. The eroded crust may represent 4–6 years of accumulation.
- **Shift in bed condition.** The streambed was completely cemented in 1961 and largely unconsolidated in 1962. Algae and their calcite deposits were much less abundant in 1962, and the crust was re-forming.
- **Biological and inorganic controls.** Inorganic controls (temperature, CO₂ loss, agitation) and biological controls (photosynthetic CO₂ removal and [[Rivularia]] calcite deposition) both promote precipitation. Separating them is difficult because both respond to [[Solar Radiation]]. The author states biological control of CO₂ at station 1 in 1961 is strongly supported.
- **Year-to-year differences.** Daytime dissolved oxygen and percent saturation were higher in 1961. Free CO₂ and bicarbonate patterns also differed, consistent with more algal activity in 1961.
- **Downstream amplification.** Daily ranges of pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen and carbonate species grew with distance downstream. Short-term compositional fluctuations were most frequent in daylight at upstream stations (stations 3–5).
- **Possible causes of short-term variability.** The author suggests cyclic biological metabolism, unsteady channel flow, release of water from algae-filled shallows, and pulsations in stage and velocity (Dement'ev, 1962). The data are not sufficient to settle these causes and call for continuous recordings.
- **Water resource relevance.** Small perennial spring-fed streams are potential surface-water sources or aquifer-recharge water. The author argues that understanding their physical and chemical behavior is essential for evaluating beneficial uses.
- **Limitations.** Sampling was nonsynoptic, and some chemical properties may have changed during storage. Chloroform was added to 1962 samples to inhibit biological activity, but this did not stop all changes in biologically active solutes.
