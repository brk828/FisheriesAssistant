# Red Danger for Desert Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This magazine feature examines the arrival of the [[Red Shiner]], an aggressive non-native minnow, in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a spring-fed desert stream in southeast Arizona long regarded as the Southwest's richest refuge for native fish. The red shiner was found in October 1990 during a routine survey by [[W.L. Minckley]]'s students. Managers debated whether to poison the stream, wait and watch, or build a fish barrier. The article frames Aravaipa as a pivotal test for desert fish management, as the region's remaining natural streams shrink under human pressure.

## Background & Description

- **Aravaipa Creek** flows about 16 miles from the arid foothills of the [[Galiuro Mountains]]. Its water emerges from an underground reservoir and runs through Aravaipa Canyon, lined with sycamores and cottonwoods.
- Only about 75 fish species have survived the region's harsh desert waters since the post-Ice Age warming of the past 10,000 years. Few desert streams contain more than three or four native species. Aravaipa supports seven, including the federally threatened [[Spikedace]].
- The native "Aravaipa seven" are the spikedace, longfin dace, Gila chub, desert sucker, Sonoran sucker, speckled dace, and loach minnow. These are relatively strong swimmers that seek cover when flash floods approach.
- The red shiner is native to the Mississippi River basin. It spread across Western waters over several decades through fishermen's bait buckets. It is suspected of preying on young native fish and out-competing them for food and cover.
- The creek also supports desert bighorn sheep and more than 200 bird species, many rarely seen north of Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Protection:** Much of Aravaipa is protected. About 10 miles lie within a federal wilderness area, flanked upstream and downstream by six miles of the [[Aravaipa Canyon Preserve]], managed by [[Nature Conservancy]]. The preserve lands are largely free of drains, diversions, and cattle.
- **Flood regime:** Seasonal floods have shaped the stream bed and its biota for thousands of years. Native fish are adapted to these floods, while slow-water species such as the [[Red Shiner]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Yellow Bullhead]] are more likely to be swept away or stranded in pools as floodwaters recede.
- **Regional decline:** Most other Southwestern streams have been heavily altered by agriculture, cities, and cattle. Within the past century, 16 species and subspecies of native arid-Southwest fish have gone extinct from human-caused pressures. The [[San Pedro River]], into which Aravaipa drains, has lost half of its perennial flow and 11 of its 13 native fish.
- **Gila River comparison:** On the [[Gila River]] in New Mexico, the [[Spikedace]] persists alongside red shiners. [[Dave Propst]] of the New Mexico Game and Fish Department has monitored this since 1980. He attributes the coexistence to the river's still-natural structure and flooding, which tends to displace red shiners.

## Key Findings

1. **Red shiner detection:** A red shiner was found in Aravaipa Creek in October 1990, the first recorded from the stream. The discovery prompted urgent debate among biologists.
2. **Competing management options:** Proposals ranged from poisoning the creek and restocking natives to waiting and observing. Propst cautioned against removing a possibly harmless species, comparing poisoning to "amputating your arm to remove a wart."
3. **Flood as a natural control:** A great flood the previous March scoured the upper creek of red shiners, and they remained scarce afterward. Managers considered flood protection the primary defense for native fish.
4. **Fish barrier proposal:** Managers considered building fish barriers designed by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. A three- to six-foot waterfall would let slow-swimming exotics pass during floods and then block their return. The proposal is controversial among wilderness advocates, though managers judged the risk of inaction greater.
5. **Underlying ecological concern:** The spread of the red shiner may reflect a broader ecological decline rather than being the sole cause of native fish loss. [[Tom Collazo]], Aravaipa Preserve director, stated, "We don't know if the red shiner is a cause or a symptom... We do have problems here in paradise." Minckley described it as "an indicator of something gone to hell."
6. **Stakes:** Collazo emphasized that Aravaipa may be one of the last refuges for native desert fish. Managers view it as a critical test for emerging [[Desert Fish Management]] approaches.
