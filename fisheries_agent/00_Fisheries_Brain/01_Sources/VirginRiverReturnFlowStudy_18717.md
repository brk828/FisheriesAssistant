# Virgin River Return Flow Study

#hydrology #water-resources #virgin-river #return-flow

## Executive Summary

This 1998 hydrologic study, prepared by [[Psomas and Associates]] for the [[Washington County Water Conservancy District]] (WCWCD), evaluates how the construction and operation of the [[Quail Creek Pipeline]] (completed 1985) changed flows in the [[Virgin River]] near Hurricane, Utah. Using [[USGS]] gauge records from two stations bracketing an 18.5-mile reach, plus water-rights diversion data and precipitation records, the study concludes that the pipeline increased river gain, particularly during irrigation months, by replacing leaky open canals that lost water to the [[Hurricane Fault]].

The document is a hydrologic and water-management report rather than a biological survey. It contains no fish, invertebrate, or aquatic-ecology data; the only biological reference is a general statement that the river has supported plant and wildlife habitat, including endangered species.

## Background & Description

- **Client and author:** WCWCD contracted Psomas and Associates (Salt Lake City) to assess impacts of the Quail Creek pipeline on Virgin River flows.
- **Study reach:** The 18.5-mile reach between the upstream [[USGS Station 09406000]] near Virgin, Utah, and the downstream [[USGS Station 09408150]] near Harrisburg Bench/Hurricane, Utah. The reach includes the WCWCD diversion dam, the former Hurricane and LaVerkin Bench canal diversions, the [[Hurricane Power Plant]], and inflows from [[LaVerkin Creek]] and Ash Creek.
- **Historical diversions:**
  - LaVerkin Canal (established 1891): 16.0 cfs right held by the LaVerkin Bench Canal Company.
  - Hurricane Bench Canal: 33.0 cfs right, later supplemented by 63.0 cfs of lower-priority water.
  - [[St. George and Washington Fields Canal]]: 86.0 cfs of irrigation rights, which had traditionally diverted nearly the entire river flow in normal periods.
- **Pipeline:** The Quail Creek pipeline now carries all LaVerkin Bench and Hurricane Bench canal water, and the old diversion structures were abandoned.
- **Power plant:** The Hurricane Power Plant holds a non-consumptive 30.0 cfs right from the pipeline. Its constant yearly flow ranges from 8 to 30 cfs, and the release also serves instream flow requirements agreed to by the WCWCD.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes the Virgin River as a desert habitat supporting plants and wildlife, including endangered species, but gives no species-level or aquatic-community data. Ecological relevance lies in the hydrology:

- **Dry reaches:** Before the pipeline, the river was dry below the LaVerkin diversion and remained dry for some distance below the St. George and Washington diversion until return flows surfaced.
- **Groundwater interaction:** Rock outcrop along the canals and the [[Hurricane Fault]] (with fracture networks and "trellis zones") channeled canal seepage to a deeper regional aquifer, so that water did not return to the river.
- **Return flow pathways:** Groundwater beneath irrigated land in the Hurricane and LaVerkin areas flows toward the river, so infiltrated irrigation water generally returns as accretion.
- **Tributary change:** [[LaVerkin Creek]] flows decreased after 1985, consistent with lower LaVerkin Bench Canal use and reduced return flow from LaVerkin irrigation.
- **Quail Creek Reservoir:** Three flumes (48-inch, 24-inch and 6-inch) show constant seepage and outflow from the dam, but the [[USGS]] 1994 seepage study found a negative gain between stations V4 and V5 around the Quail Creek confluence, so reservoir outflow contributes little to annual flow.
- **Climate controls:** NOAA precipitation records from St. George and LaVerkin showed no post-1985 increase, ruling out rainfall as the cause of higher flows.

## Key Findings

**Flow gains**
- Average monthly gain between the two stations rose from 125 cfs (pre-1985) to 379 cfs (post-1985), a difference of 254 cfs.
- Regression analysis (150 cfs upstream reference) gave a downstream flow of 151 cfs before 1985 and 165 cfs after, an annual gain increase of about 14 cfs.
- For April to October irrigation months, the same method gave 145 cfs before and 162 cfs after, an increase of 17 cfs.
- Scatter-plot R² values were 0.94 (1967-1985, annual), 0.90 (1985-1995, annual), 0.92 (irrigation months, pre-1985) and 0.97 (irrigation months, post-1985).

**Data handling**
- Upstream data were missing for 1972-1978 and downstream for 1989-1990. 1991 was excluded after the Quail Creek Reservoir dyke failure.
- Flow records cover 1965-1997 in the appended USGS files, with the analysis period noted as 1968-1996.

**Canal water use (Utah Division of Water Rights)**
- LaVerkin Bench Canal: 609.2 ac-ft/month before the pipeline versus 356.7 ac-ft/month since 1985.
- Hurricane Bench Canal: 1857.6 ac-ft/month before versus 1606.7 ac-ft/month since 1985.
- Combined decrease of 503.4 ac-ft/month, equal to 8.5 cfs over the seven-month irrigation period.

**Seepage study**
- A [[USGS]] seepage study (Herbert, 1995; November 1994 measurements) found a total gain of 10.7 cfs over 14 river miles (VR1-VR9), with 7.2 cfs gained between Ash Creek and about one mile above Quail Creek (VR1-VR4).

**Conclusions**
- Increased return flow results from the pipeline's efficiency. It delivers water without loss through fractured rock to the Hurricane Fault, unlike the former canals.
- The study concludes that the pipeline installation in 1985 is the cause of the increased Virgin River flow.
- Evaporation losses are lower in pipe systems than in open canals, though they were small in the canals studied.

**Source data:** The appendix contains USGS daily discharge files, monthly flow tables for both stations, Utah Division of Water Rights diversion records for the Hurricane Bench and LaVerkin Bench canals, and NOAA precipitation charts. Full tables are not reproduced here.
