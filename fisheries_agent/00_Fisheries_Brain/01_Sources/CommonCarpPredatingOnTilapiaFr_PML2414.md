# Carp Predation on Tilapia Fry in a High-Density Polyculture Fishpond

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This short study, attributed to P. Spataru and B. Hepher of the [[Dor Fish And Aquaculture Research Station]], tests whether [[Common Carp]] can limit unwanted reproduction of [[Tilapia]] in a [[Polyculture]] pond. Uncontrolled spawning of prolific tilapia ("wild spawning") floods the pond with fry that compete with stocked fish for natural food, lowering growth and yield. In the study, carp intestines were examined for tilapia fry. Fry were found in 16.6% of the examined carp, which suggests that carp can prey on tilapia fry and may also gain a new food source.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** In [[Polyculture]] systems, wild spawning is more damaging than in [[Monoculture]], because fry of different species compete for several food niches. Tilapia are the most problematic in this respect and can cause severe drops in fish yield.
- **Conventional solution:** Some polyculture systems include a predatory fish to control fry. Systems without a predator, like the one studied, lack this control.
- **Hypothesis:** Omnivorous carp might reduce tilapia fry numbers while using them as food.
- **Study pond:** A polyculture pond of 1.4 ha. Species stocked were common carp, Tilapia aurea, hybrids of T. aurea and a second tilapia species, and [[Silver Carp]] (*Hypophthalmichthys molitrix*). Stocking rates and average individual weights at stocking are given in Table 1, which lists approximately 2986 and 1141 carp-group entries, 5000 and 1168 tilapia entries, and 1700 and 238 silver carp entries per ha (the scan is partly garbled, so these figures should be checked against the original).
- **Husbandry:** Fish were stocked between 28 April and 3 May 1976. During the growing season they received protein-rich pellets (25% crude protein) at about 4% of body weight daily. Fertilisation was biweekly, using 60 kg superphosphate and 60 kg ammonium sulphate per ha, following [[Hepher]] (1965).
- **Sampling:** Carp were collected in July and August 1976. Length ranged from 21 to 34 cm (weight range partly illegible in the scan).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Natural diet of carp:** Earlier studies in Israel ([[Yashouv]], 1954, 1956) and elsewhere (Gerking, 1950; Harrison, 1950; Mackay, 1963; Botnariuc et al., 1964; Swee and McCrimmon, 1966; Zabolotskii, 1967; McCrimmon, 1969; Merla, 1969; Gurzeda, 1969; Szumiec, 1969) agree that carp feed mainly on benthos (chironomid larvae, oligochaetes, molluscs). As generally omnivorous feeders, carp also take zooplankton, macrophytes and supplemental feed.
- **Fish in carp diets:** Harlan and Speaker (1956) and Mauck (1967) recorded fish remains in carp guts. [[Spataru]] (1967) found fish fry in 2.5% of four-year-old carp in the Crapina-Jijila Lake System, Romania, probably because natural food was scarce in winter.
- **Density effects:** The stocking density in the study pond was higher than typical for fish farms. Because the daily ration was only 4% of body weight, the authors argue that natural food was nearly exhausted, which may have driven carp to switch to fry.
- **Carp-tilapia interaction:** [[Yashouv]] (1963, 1969, 1972) and Reich (1975) reported that carp production is somewhat higher in polyculture with tilapia than in monoculture. The authors suggest that carp consumption of tilapia fry may explain this.
- **Tilapia spawning:** Yashouv (1956a) found that intensive tilapia spawning inhibited both tilapia and carp growth. The authors suggest that larger carp might have solved this problem.

## Key Findings

1. **Fry predation:** Tilapia fry were found in the anterior intestine of **16.6%** of the examined carp. Fry were identified by fin structure (dorsal, anal and caudal fins) and ctenoid scales. Digestion was advanced, but the fry remained recognisable (Figs. 1-3).
2. **Carp size and fry size:** The amount of fry consumed depends on the size of both the fry and the carp. The authors state that the optimal size relationship for maximal fry removal and utilisation must be determined.
3. **Polyculture management:** Managers should consider not only the number of each species stocked, but also their relative sizes, especially when a prolific species such as tilapia is included.
4. **Practical implication:** Adding larger carp could reduce tilapia overpopulation in predator-free polyculture ponds, though the authors call this a hypothesis to test rather than a confirmed result.
5. **Study limits:** The paper is a short report on one pond in one season, and the authors frame their conclusions as preliminary.
