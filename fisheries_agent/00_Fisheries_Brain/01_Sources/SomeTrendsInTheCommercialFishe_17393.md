# Trends in the Commercial Fisheries of Lake of the Woods, Minnesota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1947 study (published 1949 in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*) examines six decades of commercial fishing on [[Lake Of The Woods]], beginning in 1884. The [[Lake Sturgeon]] has nearly disappeared and the [[Lake Whitefish]] has declined sharply. Overfishing is identified as the main cause for the sturgeon, while the parallel declines of several lightly fished species point to habitat change, chiefly [[Pulp-Mill Pollution]] of the [[Rainy River]] spawning beds. The author reports that [[Sauger]] and [[Yellow Perch]] are increasing and compete with [[Yellow Pikeperch]], and that current gear is selective in their favor. The paper recommends changes in fishing methods to make fuller use of saugers and yellow perch.

## Background & Description

The lake covers about 1,485 square miles (Meyer and White, 1917). Its southern portion, the [[Big Traverse]], is shallow (about 35 feet deep and roughly 25 miles across) with sand beaches and floating muskeg. The northern [[Little Traverse]] is deeper, with rocky shores and thousands of islands, and supports [[Lake Trout]] and whitefish. About one-third of the lake, including most of the Big Traverse, lies in Minnesota.

The commercial fishery began in 1884 when William Zippel settled near the mouth of the Rainy River. Pound nets were used first, with leads up to 80 rods long. [[Fyke Nets]] were used mainly in smaller bays for [[Yellow Bullhead]], yellow perch and [[Northern Pike]]. Legal [[Gill Netting]] gear used 4-inch stretched mesh from 1915. Table 1 of the source lists licensed gear from 1888 to 1946 (summarized here):

- Pound nets: roughly 60 to 80 in most years after the 1930s, with a peak of 288 in 1910–1914 and 129 in 1895–1899.
- Gill nets: first licensed in 1915 and fairly constant since then.
- Fyke nets: as many as about 100 used from 1925 to 1935, then a sharp decline.

Season lengths and minimum size limits (first set in 1905) changed repeatedly. These regulatory shifts make it hard to separate true population change from changes in fishing effort.

Water chemistry and biology are described as follows:

- No major thermal stratification in the Big Traverse, with oxygen abundant at all depths.
- Moderately soft water with total alkalinity of 45 to 68 ppm as calcium carbonate.
- Secchi-disc readings of 0.5 to 1.5 meters.
- Heavy August blooms of blue-green algae, principally *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*.
- Bottom fauna averaging 2.0 cubic centimeters per square-foot sample (114 samples). [[Midge Larvae]] and [[Mayfly Nymphs]] (principally *Hexagenia*) were the most important organisms.

Data sources included Evermann and Latimer (1910) for 1888–1909, U.S. Bureau of Fisheries records for 1913–1939, Minnesota Department of Conservation statistics, and individual fishermen's annual reports from 1932 to 1946. Field work from 1939 to 1946 included limnological observations, minnow seining, experimental gill netting, and scale collection for life-history studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Historical and recent observations describe large shifts in the fish community:

- **[[Lake Sturgeon]]:** Early explorers reported the lake "teeming" with sturgeon and whitefish. Evermann and Latimer (1910) called the lake "the greatest sturgeon pond in the world." Sturgeon made up over half the commercial catch until 1900. Over 7.5 million pounds were taken in the 1890s. Since 1920 only 25,000 pounds were reported, and only seven were seen by the author from 1939 to 1943. The species was placed on the protected list in 1941.
- **[[Lake Whitefish]]:** Decline began around 1910. In Minnesota waters the species has been commercially unimportant since 1920, with the annual catch falling from about 40,000 to 1,000 pounds. The Canadian portion held up better.
- **[[Yellow Pikeperch]]:** Production held until 1937, then declined. Pike catches fell about 70 percent over 30 years.
- **[[Sauger]] and [[Yellow Perch]]:** Evermann and Latimer (1910) called the sauger "not common," but it is now one of the most abundant fishes in the lake. Yellow perch are now abundant, versus "common" earlier.
- **Lightly fished species:** [[Goldeye]] (formerly common, now rare, with its decline starting around 1923), [[Mooneye]] (no recent records), [[Longnose Sucker]] (formerly abundant, now few), and [[Quillback]] (occasionally taken, no longer among the most abundant fishes). The [[Tullibee]] appears to have increased after about 1923, though records are incomplete.

The author reviews several explanations:

- **Overfishing:** Probably the main cause of the sturgeon collapse. Pound-net effort in 1895–1896 (193 nets in Minnesota waters) was over three times the number used after 1925. Catches of whitefish, pike and pikeperch were also low in 1900–1904.
- **Environmental change:** Heavy lumbering began about 1906, and large paper mills opened at International Falls and Port Francis in 1907, about 80 miles up the Rainy River. Pulp wastes destroyed spawning beds along the river, probably contributing to the decline of sturgeon, whitefish and longnose sucker, which fell from about 1915 to 1925.
- **Rainfall and year-class strength:** Among 1,556 saugers aged from collections in 1936, 1937, 1939, 1940 and 1941, year-class abundance correlated positively with June rainfall of the hatch year (r = 0.84; p = 0.017). The author suggests turbidity or nutrient input as possible links, but notes the correlation may not imply cause.
- **Silting:** Judged unlikely to be a major factor, since goldeye and quillback are normally found in the much more turbid Mississippi River.

## Key Findings

1. **Sturgeon collapse:** Overfishing, especially during the 1890s, appears responsible for the virtual disappearance of the lake sturgeon. The species was placed on the protected list in 1941.
2. **Whitefish decline:** Likely linked to heavy fishing around 1910–1920, though this is less certain than for the sturgeon.
3. **Habitat change:** Parallel declines of goldeye, longnose sucker, mooneye and quillback, which were not heavily fished, suggest environmental change, most likely from [[Pulp-Mill Pollution]] of the Rainy River spawning beds.
4. **Pikeperch decline not clearly pollution-driven:** Pikeperch decreased only in the last decade, much later than pollution effects would predict. The lake itself is their main spawning ground. The author argues that competition from increasing saugers and perch may be a factor.
5. **Rainfall effect on saugers:** June rainfall correlated positively with sauger year-class strength (r = 0.84; p = 0.017). Causality is not established.
6. **Illegal small-mesh nets:** Violations of 3- to 3¾-inch mesh gill nets during 1931–1937 inflated the sauger and perch catches of that period. The author notes that the later decline does not necessarily mean a population decline.
7. **Selective gear:** Legal 4-inch gill nets mainly catch [[Yellow Pikeperch]] over 15 inches fork length. Of 4,206 pikeperch measured, 84 percent were 15–18 inches long. Saugers and perch are seldom gilled.
8. **Experimental versus commercial catches:**
   - Saugers made up at least 20 percent of experimental small-mesh gill-net catches, versus 7 percent in commercial nets.
   - Perch made up at least 20 percent in experimental nets, versus 1 percent in commercial nets.
   - Pikeperch made up under 10 percent in experimental nets, versus about 30 percent in commercial nets.
   - The author concludes commercial sauger and perch catches could be increased at least fourfold without greater fishing pressure on those species.
9. **Food competition:** Stomach analyses (450 pikeperch, 297 saugers, 129 yellow perch, 1939–1941) show the three species share the same principal food items. Saugers are more piscivorous and yellow perch more insectivorous. Saugers and perch feed more on the bottom and are found in deeper water in summer, which partly reduces competition.
10. **Depth separation:** In summer 1941, pikeperch were most abundant in surface-suspended nets (8–12 feet). Saugers and perch were most common in bottom sets in 18–35 feet. The separation is not complete, but it offers opportunities for selective fishing.
11. **Carrying capacity:** Average catch per pound net for all species has changed little since the early years, suggesting that the lake's productive capacity has not changed much. A rise in one species likely means a fall in competitors.
12. **Management recommendation:** Fishing methods should be adjusted to exploit saugers and yellow perch on an equal basis with [[Yellow Pikeperch]], possibly through winter gill netting, as recommended for Norris Reservoir.
13. **Limits of conclusions:** The author acknowledges that overfishing, selective fishing and pollution do not explain all changes. The goldeye, longnose sucker, mooneye and quillback declines are only partly explained by pollution.
