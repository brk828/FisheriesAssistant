# Estimates of Water Use in the Western United States in 1990 and Water-Use Trends 1960-90

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-resources

## Executive Summary

This USGS report by Wayne B. Solley, prepared for the [[Western Water Policy Review Advisory Commission]] (August 1997), provides consistent estimates of freshwater use by category, State, and [[Water-Resources Region]] across the western United States, including Alaska and Hawaii. Freshwater withdrawals in 1990 were estimated at 179 million acre-feet, about the same as 1985 and 30 percent more than 1960. Agriculture remains the dominant use, while domestic and thermoelectric demand grew with population. The document is a water-resources accounting study rather than a fish or ecological survey; it contains no species-level data.

## Background & Description

The report draws on the [[National Water-Use Estimates]] compiled by the [[United States Geological Survey]] every five years. Trend analysis uses reports from 1960 to 1990 (1950 and 1955 were excluded for data inconsistencies). Key terms:

- **Offstream use:** water diverted or withdrawn from a surface or ground-water source and conveyed to a place of use.
- **Instream use:** water used within the river channel itself; here limited to [[Hydroelectric Power]] generation.
- **Consumptive use:** water evaporated, transpired, incorporated into products or crops, consumed, or otherwise removed from the immediate supply.
- **Reclaimed wastewater:** treatment-plant effluent diverted for beneficial use before reaching a natural waterway or aquifer.

The introduction frames the region's shift from [[Supply Management]] (large dams and conveyance systems) toward [[Demand Management and Conservation]], with new supplies coming from small reservoirs, conservation, reuse, and ground-water development.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses water quantity and allocation, not aquatic ecology. It does not report fish populations, invertebrates, plankton, habitat condition, or water chemistry. Environmental relevance is indirect: the authors note growing concern for the environment and polluted water as drivers of change, and the large hydroelectric instream volume (about 1.73 billion acre-feet in 1990) is mostly concentrated in the [[Pacific Northwest]] region (about 1.41 billion acre-feet). Withdrawal patterns, including the greater surface-water share (67 percent), bear on river flows that aquatic systems depend on, but the report does not evaluate those ecological effects.

## Key Findings

**National and regional totals (1990)**
- Total freshwater withdrawals: 179 million acre-feet (ground water 58.9; surface water 120).
- Agriculture (irrigation and livestock): 140 million acre-feet, 78 percent of withdrawals (86 percent in 1960).
- Domestic and commercial: 17.5 million acre-feet (about 8 percent in the abstract, 10 percent in the use discussion).
- Industrial and mining: 5.6 million acre-feet.
- Thermoelectric power: 16.2 million acre-feet, 9 percent of withdrawals (4 percent in 1960).
- Consumptive use: 81.7 million acre-feet, about 46 percent of western withdrawals and about 78 percent of national consumptive use.
- Reclaimed wastewater: 504 thousand acre-feet.
- Conveyance losses: 30 million acre-feet.
- Hydroelectric instream use: 1.73 billion acre-feet, more than double the 752 million acre-feet of 1960.

**State patterns**
- California withdrew the most offstream water (39.3 million acre-feet, about 22 percent of western withdrawals), followed by Texas (22.5) and Idaho (22.1).
- Surface water exceeded ground water in all western States except Kansas, Nebraska, and New Mexico.
- Irrigation and livestock dominate in all but Alaska and North Dakota; Idaho (98 percent), Wyoming (95 percent), and Colorado (92 percent) are highest.
- Texas accounts for 49 percent (8.0 million acre-feet) of thermoelectric water use in the West.

**Trends (1960-1990)**
- Total withdrawals rose 35 percent from 1960 to 1975 (population up 29 percent), then fell 2 percent from 1975 to 1990 (population up a further 35 percent).
- Irrigation and livestock withdrawals rose 28 percent (1960-75) and fell 7 percent (1975-90).
- Ground-water withdrawals peaked around 1975; surface-water withdrawals peaked around 1980.
- Domestic per-capita use rose from 129 gallons per day (1960) to 160 gallons per day (1990), then remained roughly stable over the last decade.
- Population in the West grew 75 percent from 1960 to 1990, nearly double the national rate.

**Conclusions**
- Irrigation, not population, is the main driver of total withdrawal change in the West.
- Declines in ground- and surface-water use since the 1970s and 1980s reflect more efficient irrigation, less water-intensive crops, and reduced irrigated acreage where water levels fell.
- Domestic and thermoelectric demand are expected to rise with population, intensifying competition for agriculture's share.
- Public water suppliers serve about 78.3 million people (87 percent of the western population), with ground water supplying 55 percent of that population.
