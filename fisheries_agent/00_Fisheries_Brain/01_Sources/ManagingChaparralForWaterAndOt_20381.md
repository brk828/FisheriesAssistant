# Managing Chaparral for Water and Other Resources in Arizona

#botany #chaparral #watershed-management

## Executive Summary

This paper, reprinted from the Watershed Management Symposium (ASCE Irrigation and Drainage Division, Logan, Utah, August 11–13, 1975), reviews [[Chaparral]] management in central Arizona. The authors, [[Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station]] scientists, summarize nearly 20 years of [[Arizona Watershed Program]] experiments on converting shrub-dominated chaparral to grass to increase water yield. Conversion raised streamflow on every treated watershed except Mingus, which was treated too late to be included. Average increases ranged from 28 percent to more than 300 percent, and the authors estimate that large-scale conversion could add about 0.2 acre-feet of water per treated acre at downstream points of use. The paper also weighs erosion, herbicide contamination, wildlife, livestock forage, and benefit-cost economics, and concludes that chaparral conversion has real but limited potential.

## Background & Description

- **Vegetation type:** Chaparral occupies roughly 3 to 4 million acres of rough terrain in central Arizona, largely in public ownership, mostly on or adjacent to the [[Tonto National Forest]] and [[Prescott National Forest]]. The paper later assumes 3.5 million acres.
- **Community structure:** It consists of deep-rooted, evergreen, sclerophyllous shrubs, usually dominated by [[Shrub Live Oak]]. [[Hairy Mountainmahogany]], [[Pringle Manzanita]], and other species may be locally dominant.
- **Climate and soils:** The type develops best where annual precipitation averages 20 to 25 inches, though open stands persist on as little as 16 inches. Soils are coarse textured, poorly developed, and mostly derived from granites.
- **Resource values:** Land managers rate chaparral low in commercial value, but it yields about 0.1 acre-foot of good-quality water per acre per year, provides forage and browse for big game and livestock, supports smaller wildlife, and is aesthetically valued as natural ground cover.
- **Management history:** Chaparral was long held under protective management only. Interest in converting it to grass grew during concern over a dwindling water supply in central Arizona. Research began on grazing and erosion, then expanded in the 1950s to test water-yield feasibility. The Salt-Verde drainage basin supplies about 1 million acre-feet of surface water each year to [[Phoenix Valley]] and surrounding areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fire response.** Although not a fire climax, chaparral is well adapted to fire. Cable (1975) describes it as a true climatic climax that is unusually susceptible to burning. Most shrubs resprout from root crowns, and species such as manzanita produce seeds stimulated to germinate by fire. Pond (1971) found little change in individual shrub live oak plants after 47 years. Because deep root systems exploit a large soil volume, sprouting species regenerate quickly after any control method that only topkills plants.

**Water use mechanism.** Conversion works by replacing deep-rooted shrubs with shallow-rooted grasses and forbs. Shrub roots occupy the whole soil mantle and can deplete soil water more than grasses can. Reduced interception by grass and greater seasonal dormancy also contribute, but most savings are attributed to limited water access for shallow-rooted plants.

**Seasonal rainfall.** Summer rains account for about one-third of annual precipitation in central Arizona and aid establishment of perennial grass cover.

**Stream response.** Converted headwater streams often became perennial rather than intermittent. Channel adjustments and accelerated erosion may occur during prolonged increased flow, but water-loving plants such as sedges, watercress, willow, and alder can stabilize channels where chaparral once grew.

## Key Findings

### Brush Control Methods

- **[[Rootplowing]]:** Effective where usable, but steep slopes and rocky soils limit it to less than 8 percent of chaparral (Pond 1961).
- **[[Prescribed Burning]]:** Modifies brush density and composition but kills outright only a few species such as manzanita. Most shrubs resprout vigorously, so burning must be combined with other methods for eradication. Fire can topkill mature stands and prepare seedbeds for grasses.
- **[[Herbicides]]:** Foliar sprays include the phenoxy group (2,4-D, 2,4,5-T, silvex) and [[Picloram]]. Phenoxy herbicides are the most economical for large projects but do not adequately control dominant shrubs and need several annual applications. Soil treatments such as [[Fenuron]], picloram, karbutylate, and bromacil can eradicate most shrubs in one heavy application, but most also harm grasses. Broadcasting large pellets 3 to 6 feet apart is being explored.
- **Regulatory status:** All herbicides discussed were tested experimentally and are not recommended or registered for watershed use. None of the soil-applied herbicides was registered for watershed use at the time, and phenoxy use was being challenged.

### Water Yield Results

- **Treated watersheds:** The first treatments began about 20 years earlier on the Sierra Ancha Experimental Forest. The [[Three Bar Watersheds]] were established in 1956 and burned by wildfire in 1959. [[Whitespar Watershed]] treatment started in 1967 and Mingus in 1974.
- **On-site increases:** Streamflow rose on six treated watersheds, from 28 percent (one-third inch) in open brush with low rainfall to more than 300 percent (over 5 inches) in dense brush with high rainfall.
- **Precipitation relationship:** At 22 inches of precipitation with complete conversion, the expected increase is 3.8 inches, with a 90 percent confidence interval of ±2.5 inches.
- **Recommended planning factor:** Large-scale conversions should be assumed to respond at about 80 percent of experimental results. Experiments reduced shrub cover by 80 to 95 percent. Eradicating at least three-fourths of shrubs is the suggested minimum.
- **Downstream yield:** Under stated assumptions (average 22 inches precipitation, 80 percent on-site response, and no more than 20 percent downstream loss), the expected increase at downstream reservoirs is about 2.4 inches, or 0.2 acre-foot per treated acre.
- **Shrub recovery:** After the Three Bar wildfire, streamflow was nearly back to normal within three years, when shrubs had recovered about one-third of prefire density. Near-zero shrub removal is needed for sustained gains.

### Water Quality

- **Nitrate:** Complete deadening of vegetation on a granitic watershed raised nitrate from 0.2 to as high as 56 parts per million after heavy rains, then declined during dry periods. The extent of loss and its effect on soil fertility and stream enrichment need further study.
- **Herbicides:** Highest contamination followed heavy soil applications on streamside areas. Picloram reached 370 parts per billion (ppb) and fenuron up to 420 ppb after heavy rains. Under moderate rates with herbicide kept away from channels, fenuron never exceeded 11 ppb and was undetectable after 18 months.

### Erosion

- **Postfire sediment:** Sediment production in the first 3 postfire years on Three Bar was 25 times greater than in the other 13 years of record combined (3 prefire, 10 postfire). This pattern held whether the area was converted to grass or reverted to brush.
- **Mass slippage:** A slip occurred at Three Bar in 1973 on a 65-percent slope converted to grass after the 1959 fire. Shrub eradication may have contributed to instability.
- **Slope limits:** Treatment on slopes over 60 percent or on particularly unstable soils should be avoided. Safe conversion on slopes below 60 percent depends on local soil and site factors.
- **Long-term effect:** Conversion should reduce erosion over the long run by preventing the heavy erosion cycle triggered by periodic wildfires, assuming replacement cover and careful grazing management.

### Livestock and Wildlife

- **Livestock carrying capacity:** Mature chaparral supports up to 10 animal units per square mile annually, depending on slope, access, and vegetation. Dense mature stands have very low stocking rates.
- **Forage gains:** Conversion increased herbaceous production on watershed C at Three Bar to an average of 1,200 pounds per acre, seven times the control. On the Tonto Springs beef study, annual livestock weight gains averaged 9.4 lb/acre on native chaparral versus 81.4 lb/acre on adjacent [[Weeping Lovegrass]] pastures.
- **[[Mule Deer]] and [[White-Tailed Deer]]:** Populations ranged from 4 or 5 to 20 or 30 per square mile, averaging about 10 per square mile in 1955. Mature browse made up to 50 percent of white-tailed deer diets in late fall, winter, and early spring. Mast, fruits, and berries reached 65 to 70 percent of midsummer diets.
- **Deer response to conversion:** Brush control on watershed C eliminated shrub fruits and killed palatable browse such as hollyleaf buckthorn, while forbs increased. Deer use of converted areas at Tonto Springs was lower by a ratio of 1:3, so untreated cover must remain nearby.
- **Birds and small mammals:** Species numbers were similar on treated and intact stands, but total numbers were higher on conversions.
- **Habitat design:** Small, irregular treatment openings covering about half the area keep cover and browse available nearby and create beneficial edge effects.

### Economic Feasibility

- **Method:** The [[Salt-Verde Basin]] analysis delineated chaparral areas, estimated conversion costs, quantified yield effects, and applied [[Benefit-Cost Ratio]] analysis over a 50-year horizon at a 6-7/8 percent discount rate.
- **Area screening:** Of 850,000 acres classified as chaparral, more than 500,000 were rejected for wilderness status, shrub cover below 30 percent, or slopes above 60 percent. The remaining 333,000 acres in 139 areas were reduced to 176,000 treatable acres (about 21 percent of total chaparral) by the 60 percent treatment limit.
- **Alternative I:** Conversion was economically feasible (benefit-cost ratio above 1.0) in 96 of the 139 areas, encompassing 147,118 convertible acres.
- **Alternative II:** Costlier but more assured, it was feasible in 72 areas covering 106,543 acres.
- **Per-acre benefits (Alternative I):** 0.21 acre-foot of extra runoff, 0.24 additional animal unit month of grazing, and a 34-cent reduction in firefighting costs. Water was valued at $12.50 per acre-foot and grazing at $6.54 per animal unit month (1972 prices).
- **Net return:** Average annuity benefit of $4.49 minus annuity cost of $1.98 gives a net return of $2.51 per converted acre per year.
- **Basin-wide effect:** Converting the 96 areas could increase Basin streamflow by 3 percent, cattle carrying capacity by 3.6 percent, and reduce firefighting costs by 3 percent.

### Statewide Potential

- **Assumptions:** Total chaparral is taken as about 3.5 million acres. Conversion is constrained by wilderness exclusion, avoidance of open stands below 30 percent crown cover, slope and soil limits (slopes up to 60 percent assumed), operational and location restrictions, and the 60 percent openings rule.
- **Realistic conversion:** Combined constraints limit actual conversion to about 21 percent of chaparral. After excluding areas with benefit-cost ratios below 1, only 13 to 17 percent is recommended for treatment.
- **Scenario outcome:** If large-scale techniques succeed, about 735,000 acres could be converted and maintained in grass. The expected downstream increase would average about 150,000 acre-feet per year, with comparisons to Arizona water supply and irrigation of 25,000 acres of alfalfa.
- **Caveats:** Herbicide use in critical stages of grass establishment is uncertain, public reluctance and high herbicide costs are current barriers, and unquantified impacts (recreation, aesthetics, opportunity costs) were not valued. The authors stress that the estimates discourage both over-optimistic and over-pessimistic views and that field application requires detailed analysis and investigation.
