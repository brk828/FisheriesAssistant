# Colorado River Endangered Fishes: Section 7 Consultation and the Solicitor's Opinion (1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #colorado-river-endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1989 document is a set of U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) memoranda and a Regional Solicitor's legal opinion. It concerns how [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) [[Section 7 Consultation]] applies to new water depletions in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] that affect the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The Service relies on the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] as a [[Reasonable And Prudent Alternative]] (RPA) for depletion impacts. It has found "significant uncertainty" that legal protection of instream flows will be achieved in time. As a backup, it proposes that large water projects withhold part of their annual yield for instream flows. The Solicitor concluded that this position is legally sufficient under the ESA and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). Two biological opinions were held in abeyance pending that legal review.

## Background & Description

The three species are endangered Colorado River fishes whose numbers have declined because of habitat loss and other factors. The FWS has issued more than 100 [[Biological Opinion]]s on water development projects. Since 1978 (the FWS memo) or 1977 (the Solicitor's memo; the two documents differ), it has consistently found that further water depletions likely jeopardize the fishes.

Conflict over minimum flows came to a head after a 1984 draft conservation plan set minimum streamflows across the Upper Basin. States and water users argued this conflicted with state water rights systems and interstate compacts. In August 1984 the FWS formed the Upper Colorado River Basin Coordinating Committee, which included the FWS, the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], the states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming, water development interests, and environmental groups. After nearly four years of work, the committee produced the Recovery Program, which was implemented in January 1988. One of its main goals was a process for Section 7 consultations on Upper Basin depletions.

Under the Recovery Program, the FWS considers depletion impacts offset by two measures:

- A one-time contribution of $10 per acre-foot (af) of a project's average annual depletion, adjusted annually for inflation, and
- Legal protection of instream flows under state law.

A "large depletion" is defined as any project depleting more than 3,000 af per year. Smaller depletions may be treated as large on a case-by-case basis, depending on location, timing, and impact on the fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document centers on instream flows, which the FWS says are needed to protect habitat for the fishes. Depletion impacts include flow reductions and related changes in temperature, salinity, and turbidity.

Quantifying flow needs has been difficult. Data on the fishes are scarce, and large, turbid, inaccessible rivers make field work hard. Flow needs have been identified only for some river reaches and only for some months, partly through professional judgment that water development interests have not readily accepted. A preliminary flow determination for a 15-mile segment of the Colorado River (July through September) took nearly three years. The FWS expects that "many more years" of research will be needed to quantify and agree on flow needs across the Upper Basin.

Key river reaches and places named in the document include:

- The [[15-Mile Reach]] of the Colorado River, immediately upstream of the [[Gunnison River]] confluence, identified as a high-priority area for water rights acquisition
- Water released from [[Ruedi Reservoir]], which must reach occupied habitat below the Grand Valley irrigation diversion dam
- The [[Yampa River]], where the Juniper and Cross Mountain water rights are the principal rights to acquire
- The [[Green River]], where nursery habitat and channel changes are to be monitored
- The [[Little Snake River]], where water rights are under negotiation and a sediment study is in progress

## Key Findings

**1. The FWS finds significant uncertainty about timely instream flow protection.** Even with progress under the Recovery Program, the FWS concluded that legal protection of instream flows may not be achieved in time. It cites nine factors, grouped under flow quantification, state-law progress, and cost and availability of water rights. Examples include:

- Flow needs are uncertain, and water rights acquisition depends on quantifying them. Acquisition could be delayed for years.
- The [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] is the only entity that can hold an instream flow right under Colorado law. Its progress has been slow. A Ruedi Reservoir delivery plan has been under discussion for more than 18 months and was expected to be approved within months.
- Wyoming law cannot protect instream flows within 1 mile of the state border, which may block water delivery to occupied habitat in Colorado and Utah.
- Colorado limits federal enforcement of a state instream right that the state is not defending or administering.
- A filing for an original instream flow right in the 15-mile reach is expected to be opposed. State Supreme Court resolution could take 6 years.

**2. Water rights are costly and scarce.** Rights are bought only from willing sellers, and the Program depends on a few "controlling" rights.

- Juniper-Cross Mountain water rights on the Yampa River have an estimated value of "$0 to $100 million." Their holders will sell only if a smaller reservoir is built in lieu of the Juniper and Cross Mountain reservoirs.
- Ruedi Reservoir water, the cheapest Colorado River supply, is expected to sell for $50 to $60/af/yr. Buying 50,000 af for a two-month period would cost $2.5 million annually, which would exhaust the Program's anticipated $10 million acquisition fund in five years.
- Agricultural rights sell for $200 to $1,000/af of transferable consumptive use.

**3. The Service proposes a backup "insurance policy."** Because of these uncertainties, the FWS wants additional conservation measures or RPAs in its biological opinions for new large depletion projects. Under the proposal, a project proponent would withhold a portion of its annual yield for instream flows until there is reasonable assurance that the Recovery Program will protect flows. A conservation measure is an RPA agreed to by the applicant before the final opinion.

**4. Progress to date is substantial but incomplete.** Achievements under the Recovery Program include:

- A signed cooperative agreement and resolutions of support from water user groups in Utah, Colorado, and Wyoming, plus several major environmental groups.
- $1 million appropriated by Congress to acquire water rights.
- A $2.3 million FY-89 work plan funding 39 high-priority projects.
- Finalization (near completion) of FWS flow recommendations for the 15-mile reach for July through September, and Implementation Committee concurrence on a work plan for October through June.
- A Bureau of Reclamation study of alternatives for supplying 15-mile reach flows, now complete.

However, as the Solicitor noted, no instream flows for the fishes had been legally protected under the program yet.

**5. The Solicitor's legal opinion (May 12, 1989) supports the FWS.** The opinion, by Regional Solicitor Gina Guy, concludes the following:

- The FWS's significant-uncertainty finding and its call for additional measures are legally sufficient and consistent with the ESA.
- Under the ESA, the project, not the endangered species, must bear the risk if an alternative may not be carried out. The FWS must give the species the benefit of the doubt and exercise "institutional caution."
- Under the APA standard (5 U.S.C. 706), the FWS's nine-factor record is adequate, and the decision is not a clear error of judgment.
- The opinion relies on [[Tva V. Hill]] and [[Sierra Club V. Marsh]]. In Sierra Club v. Marsh (9th Cir. 1987), an RPA to preserve 188 acres of wetlands lapsed when the county failed to acquire the land. The court enjoined the project because the agency waited too long before securing a workable alternative.

**6. Timelines and next steps.** Two biological opinions were held in abeyance pending the legal opinion, and the FWS requested a response by May 10, 1989. The memoranda were to be discussed at the June 14, 1989, Management Group meeting. The FWS expects to issue opinions on two large depletion projects (the Muddy Creek and Sandstone projects) before instream flow protection is in place.
