# Observations on nuptial tubercles in some catostomid fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Branley A. Branson]] describes the arrangement of breeding (nuptial) tubercles on several genera of [[Catostomidae]] (suckers). The observations come mainly from breeding males collected while preparing a skeletal study of the family, supplemented by data from earlier authors. The paper documents tubercle patterns across [[Cycleptus]], [[Ictiobus]], [[Carpiodes]], [[Moxostoma]], [[Minytrema Melanops]], [[Erimyzon]], [[Chasmistes]], [[Catostomus]] and [[Hypentelium]]. It then proposes, in a tentative hypothesis, that rows of tubercles on the head represent the primitive condition in the family. The paper is a descriptive morphological survey and does not perform statistical tests.

## Background & Description

Many fish groups develop horny tubercles on scales and fins during breeding, often in both sexes. These structures, called "breeding tubercles," "nuptial tubercles" or "pearl organs," serve several functions:

- Fighting and courtship
- Holding to the substrate during egg deposition
- Holding the male against the female during spawning
- Tending the nest and eggs

Earlier workers, including [[Bailey and Suttkus]] (1952), [[Robins and Raney]] (1956) and others, had shown that the arrangement of tubercles on the head and body is taxonomically important. [[Gibbs]] (1957) and [[Hubbs and Black]] (1947) considered the patterns phylogenetically valuable. The author examined breeding males from various genera and noted that many specimens were not in breeding condition at collection, which limited the data for some taxa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper links tubercle patterns to reproductive biology and collection timing. Key context includes:

- **Seasonality:** Tubercles are present only in breeding condition. A single [[Cycleptus]] specimen was collected in early April 1955 in the Neosho River, Coffee County, Kansas (KU3477). Nearly 200 fully mature Cycleptus (400 to 500 mm standard length) from [[Lake Texoma]], Grayson County, Texas, showed no tubercles because they were collected in middle and late summer.
- **Post-spawning condition:** An immediately post-nuptial [[Minytrema Melanops]] from Elk County, Kansas (KU3708), lacked organs on all fins and the body.
- **Nesting habits:** Citing [[Hubbs and Black]] (1947) for [[Pimephales]], the author suggests that reduction or loss of head tubercle lines may be a function of nesting habits, though this relationship is not tested in the Catostomidae.
- **Geography:** Ozark and Kansas populations of [[Minytrema Melanops]] differ from Ohio populations, and the author notes that intermediate and southern samples are needed before taxonomic conclusions can be drawn.

## Key Findings

### Distribution by Genus

- **[[Cycleptus]]:** Tubercles cover the head, trunk and all fins. The 70 to 80 large opercular tubercles (0.5 to 1.0 mm) and a cheek band of 180 to 200 organs are notable. Scales above the lateral line average 16 organs (range 12 to 25); those below average 9.5 (range 6 to 17). Posterior scales have nearly twice as many organs as anterior ones.
- **[[Ictiobus]]:** Many minute white tubercles form a patch on top of the head, with a ring around the nostrils.
- **[[Carpiodes]]:** Tubercles are apparently absent.
- **[[Moxostoma]] (Ozark forms):** Tubercles are mainly restricted to the fins rather than the body. In *M. poecilurum*, the pelvic and anal fins carry rows, and caudal rays bear 20 to 25 organs each on most rays. *M. carinatum* is similar, with a single large specimen (569.6 mm SL, White River, Arkansas) showing about 50 small tubercles on the distal one-third of the snout.
- **[[Moxostoma]] (southeastern and southwestern forms):** Patterns vary. *M. rupiscartes* has tubercles on the snout, head and predorsal scales, while *M. cervinum* has head patches extending onto the back. *M. mascotae* and *M. austrinum* have abundant tubercles on the anal fin and caudal peduncle. Descriptions of *M. duquesnei* largely agree with [[Trautman]] (1958), with minor differences in the caudal fin.
- **[[Hypentelium]] nigricans:** Pelvic and anal rays bear 8 to 10 tubercles each, and caudal rays carry 9 to 15. None were found on the caudal peduncle of 56 White River specimens.
- **[[Minytrema Melanops]]:** Four eastern Kansas specimens show anal fin rows of 14 to 20 organs on the first three rays, with larger tubercles toward the trailing edge. Head tubercles form four rows, two above and two anterolateral to the eye. Ohio specimens differ, with well-developed caudal tubercles and three rows on the lower cheek.
- **[[Erimyzon]] oblongus:** Only two breeding specimens (KU6105, Winter's Creek, Sevier County, Arkansas) were found. Sexual dimorphism is marked in the anal fin, which bears the most developed breeding rows in the genus. The head has three large yellowish tubercles, similar to those of [[Minytrema Melanops]].
- **[[Chasmistes]] brevirostris:** Tubercles are exceptionally well developed and numerous across the pelvic, anal, caudal and pectoral fins, the body and the head. The pectoral fins carry 7 to 15 very small organs on the upper posterior third.
- **[[Catostomus]]:** Only *C. macrocheilus* and *C. luxatus* were in breeding condition. *C. macrocheilus* has few tubercles, mostly on the anal and caudal fins. In *C. luxatus* (head only examined), the snout is densely sprinkled, with only 10 to 15 organs on the ventral head.

### Discussion and Hypothesis

- The author notes that some of the more primitive forms, [[Ictiobus]] and [[Carpiodes]], either lack tubercles or have them scattered on the head.
- [[Moxostoma]], [[Catostomus]], [[Hypentelium]] and [[Chasmistes]] often have scattered head tubercles but organized rows on the fins.
- [[Minytrema Melanops]] and [[Erimyzon]] have well-defined rows on the head and snout. Under [[Gibbs]]' (1957) reasoning, these would be primitive, yet the author believes they lie near the middle of the phylogenetic series.
- The author proposes that the catostomid prototype had several well-defined head rows, which decreased to scattered patches or to no tubercles. A hypothetical dendrogram (Figure 8) illustrates this sequence.

### Conclusions

The author concludes that the distribution of breeding tubercles in Catostomidae is consistent with the hypothesis that definite rows are the primitive condition. The paper calls for further ethological work to correlate tubercle arrangement with breeding habits, and for larger samples from southern and intermediate regions to resolve taxonomic questions, especially within [[Moxostoma]] and [[Minytrema Melanops]].
