# Controlled Propagation and Genetics Management Guidelines for Listed and Candidate Fishes (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Region 6)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #acipenseridae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document is a set of U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) policy drafts, guidelines and supporting material for the controlled propagation of imperiled fish species within the [[National Fish Hatchery System]] (NFHS), with a focus on Region 6 (Mountain-Prairie). It covers the planning sections a propagation program must contain (fish health, propagation, introduction, monitoring, disposal), the genetics management framework used to preserve wild and captive diversity, broodstock and founder-selection rules, disposition of surplus fish, NEPA and ESA compliance, and a flow chart linking recovery plans to propagation. The core policy is that captive propagation is a temporary recovery tool, used only when identified in an approved recovery plan, after risk assessment, and under sound genetic principles. Much of the text is procedural (checklists, guidelines, tables and references); the summary below captures its main requirements rather than reproducing them.

## Background & Description

The document was prepared around February 1996 as a set of Region 6 guidelines and draft policies. Its components include:

- **Controlled Propagation Program flow chart and planning guidelines** that define five required plan sections: fish health, propagation, introduction, monitoring and disposal.
- **Genetics Management Guidelines for Listed and Candidate Fish Species in Region 6** (Williamson, Wydoski and Vasscher, 1996), built on the Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Program model and an adaptive-management framework.
- **Draft Service policies** for controlled propagation (712 FW 5 and 6) and for fish broodstock (Policy 89-13), plus the Service's fish health policy (713 FW 1–4).
- **Interagency and ESA guidance**, including the joint Service/NMFS "Interagency Cooperative Policy for Controlled Propagation of Species Listed Under the ESA."
- **Appendices and references**: a glossary of genetic terms, guidance on monitoring plans, and a list of about 150 cited works.

The focal species in the genetics guidelines are endemic Upper Colorado River fishes, including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Pallid Sturgeon]], [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Bull Trout]]. Candidate-species and proposed-species tables (Federal Register, 28 February 1996) list many further taxa, including mammals, reptiles, amphibians and plants.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat loss and fragmentation**: dams, diversions and other water-development projects created barriers to passage and altered flows, temperatures, sediment and habitat. Non-native species were introduced and became competitors or predators of native fishes.
- **Hybridization**: documented in [[Colorado River Gila]] species, razorback sucker and bull trout, and possible in pallid sturgeon (with shovelnose sturgeon). Hybridization may swamp rare species if hybrids are fertile.
- **Population structure**: the document treats each randomly breeding group (a "stock") as the basic management unit. Stocks with limited interchange are managed separately until more data justify combining them. Migration varies widely; Colorado squawfish is among the most mobile, while some humpback chub stocks do not migrate.
- **Disease**: the Service's fish health policy addresses listed pathogens including IHNV, IPNV, VHSV, OMV, [[Renibacterium salmoninarum]] (bacterial kidney disease), [[Aeromonas salmonicida]] and [[Yersinia ruckeri]]. Pathogen designation codes (Class A, suspect, positive) govern transfers among facilities.
- **Water management**: the Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Program is cited as a model, consisting of in-stream flows, habitat development, native fish reintroduction, non-native fish management and research.

## Key Findings

**Program structure and policy**

- Controlled propagation is a restoration or recovery tool. Its short-term goal is preventing extinction in the wild; its long-term goal is recovery within native habitats, which depends on habitat restoration.
- Six criteria apply to every controlled propagation program (CPP): consistency with the [[Endangered Species Act]] and [[National Environmental Policy Act]]; use only when identified in an approved plan; implementation only after risk assessment; sound genetic principles; production of fish suitable for release; and annual review of introduction and monitoring programs.
- Captive broodstocks are an "insurance policy" against extirpation. Captive populations should be held in at least two separate facilities.
- Plan sections required: fish health, propagation, introduction, monitoring and disposal. Each needs Regional Director approval, a [[Section 7 Consultation]], and [[NEPA Compliance]] (an environmental assessment where controversy or unknown risks exist).

**Genetics and broodstock**

- Founder stock should be at least 50 adults (25 males, 25 females) when available and removal will not jeopardize the wild stock. This gives an effective population size of about 50 and an estimated inbreeding rate of 1% per generation.
- Where stocks are small, a 5 × 5 [[Di-allele Cross]] yields an effective population size of 10 and an inbreeding rate of 5%. Factorial matings are used when sexes are unequal.
- Four genetic risk types were identified: extinction; loss of within-population variability (drift and founder effect); loss of between-population variability; and domestication selection.
- Broodstock should be randomly sampled from throughout the spawning season, marked, and recorded in pedigrees. Matings should use fish from different year classes where possible.
- Recommended effective population size is 500 for long-term genetic drift and 50 to prevent inbreeding depression (per cited literature). The authors note the 50-fish guideline is empirical.

**Introduction and monitoring**

- Captive-reared fish may be used for refugia, research, augmentation and restoration. They should be released only where suitable habitat exists, after a stocking plan has been approved by the Recovery Team and Regional Director.
- Fish should be marked (e.g., coded wire tags) and released as equal family lots from pedigreed matings.
- Monitoring should start immediately after stocking and continue to determine survival, growth and movement. Results must be reported in a timely manner, with a standardized basin-wide survey program.
- Timing benchmarks for captive culture: about 5 years for bonytail and humpback chub, 7 years for razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish, and 8 or more years (up to 15–20) for pallid sturgeon.

**Surplus fish disposition**

- Priority order for surplus fish: recovery program needs; other research; transfer to other agencies; information and education; retention if space and cost permit; reference collections; and euthanasia (tricaine methanesulfonate, MS-222, is named).
- Surplus endangered fish become Service property. Only wild fish released after capture, or fish produced for approved stocking, may be released to the wild.

**Fish health and facility policy**

- Primary broodstock hatcheries (for example Ennis, Erwin and White Sulphur Springs NFHs) are maintained as specific-pathogen-free. Fertilized eggs must be water hardened in polyvinylpyrrolidone iodine (50 ppm for 30 min per the FDA notice, or 100 mg/L for 10 min for eyed eggs).
- Service fish health biologists inspect facilities annually; inspection reports remain valid for 365 days. Viral sample sizes are set to 95% confidence at 5% prevalence.
- Exotic disease response (OMV, VHSV, IHNV, IPNV) includes quarantine, a 5-mile quarantine radius, and destruction and burial of infected stock, with decontamination using chlorine or unslaked lime.

**Compliance and coordination**

- NEPA: the Regional Director should prepare an environmental assessment if there is controversy over collection, disposition or stocking, or if risks are unknown or substantial.
- ESA: Section 7 consultation, permits, and annual accomplishment reports are required. Field and regional geographic contacts are listed for Colorado, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska, North Dakota, South Dakota, Utah and Wyoming.
- The draft Service policy notes exceptions for species with very short reproductive windows, emergency rescue, and a 12-month time frame for existing programs to conform.

**Federal Register candidate notice (February 1996)**

- The notice lists proposed and candidate taxa, with status codes (E, T, PE, PT, C, Category 1 and 2 changes). Category 2 candidates were discontinued, and Category 3 was retired.
- Among the taxa recorded are mammals such as the [[Jaguar]] (U.S. population) and [[Bighorn Sheep]] (Peninsular Ranges population), and reptiles such as the [[Northern Copperbelly Water Snake]].
