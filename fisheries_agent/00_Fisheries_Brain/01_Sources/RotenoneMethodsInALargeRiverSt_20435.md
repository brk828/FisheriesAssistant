# Rotenone Methods in a Large River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This methods paper (Hocutt, Hambrick and Masnik, Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University) reports a sampling technique for fishes in the large main channel of the [[New River]], Virginia and West Virginia. The method combined [[Rotenone]] applied within a [[Block Net]], with [[Potassium Permanganate]] used to neutralize the toxicant downstream. The team sampled six stations in summer 1972. The approach yielded nine distributional records, including three species newly added to the main-channel fauna, and produced more species per collection than [[Seine Netting]].

## Background & Description

The main-channel New (upper [[Kanawha River]]) is described as difficult to sample. Bedrock, boulders, rubble, rapids and a large volume of water (127 m³/sec average annual discharge) limit most collection techniques. Earlier ichthyofaunal surveys were therefore mostly confined to tributaries. An intensive potamological study began in the late 1960s, and this paper reports the 1972 rotenone sampling.

Earlier authors had used rotenone in small streams and impoundments (for example, [[Boccarpy And Cooper]] 1963). Rotenone was preferred over other ichthyocides because it is less persistent than organochlorine poisons, has low toxicity to birds and mammals, allows selective kills, and can be detoxified after use. Neutralizing agents reported in the literature include chlorine, potassium permanganate and methylene blue.

**Methods summary**
- Six stations were set along the river, with total discharge averaging 70.75 m³/sec at each locality.
- A block net of 6.3 mm nylon mesh (printed as 913.6 m; likely about 91 m) was set across a section of river bed. At least one fifth of the river width (up to 36 m) was treated over 68 to 91 m downstream. Net depth was 8.6 m.
- About 3.8 L of 2.5% emulsified rotenone was applied 68 to 91 m above the net, mainly above riffles.
- About two pounds of potassium permanganate were spread in the water to neutralize the rotenone.
- The rotenoning period lasted about 30 minutes, judged by the number of fish carried by the current.
- Fish were preserved in formaldehyde, then stored in 40% isopropanol at the [[Virginia Polytechnic Institute And State University Fish Museum]].
- Diversity (SUMD) and redundancy (R) were computed with a FORTRAN IV program based on [[Information Theory]] (Wilhm and Dorris, 1968).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The river is a large, flowing system whose bottom ranges from bedrock and boulders to rubble, with rapids and deep runs. Water temperatures during sampling ranged from 23 to 31 °C. The authors note that fish sensitivity to rotenone varies by group. Darters reacted first, with erratic swimming at the surface before sinking. Clupeids were also affected early, while minnows (*Notropis*), chubs (*Nocomis*), centrarchids and suckers succumbed progressively. Catfish (Ictaluridae) and carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) were the last to be affected.

Dilution and "stretchout" of the toxicant were common, caused by eddies, coves, application method and water flow patterns. Lawrence (1956) showed that fish downstream of a sample area can exceed the catch within it if no neutralizing agent is used. The authors concluded that the rotenone concentrations used by Boccarpy and Cooper (1963) were probably excessive for large rivers.

## Key Findings

**Sampling totals**
- A total of **7,127 specimens** representing **7 families, 21 genera and 40 species** was collected.
- Rotenone catches per station are summarized in Table 1. The Results section gives a range of 23 to 81 species per rotenone collection, but Table 2 gives 23 to 30. The table values appear more consistent with the station data.

**Station records** (species count and diversity index in the source table)
- **Station 1** (U.S. 460 bridge, Ripplemead): 27 species, SUMD 3.80. A single *Cottus bairdi* was the only known main-channel record.
- **Station 2** (county road 641): 24 species, SUMD 3.75. The first known main-channel specimen of *Etheostoma osburni* was collected.
- **Station 3** (mouth of Stittuouse Branch): 24 species, SUMD 2.54. The second known main-channel record of *Richardsonius funduloides* was obtained.
- **Station 4** (south of Rich Creek): 27 species, SUMD 1.44. Downstream records were established for *Pimephales promelas* and *Noturus insignis*. *Campostoma anomalum* was unusually abundant (1,670 specimens), which lowered the diversity index.
- **Station 5** (north of Lurich): 30 species in the table (31 in the text), SUMD 3.82. A catch of 33 *Percina oxyrhyncha* (sharpnose darter) was notable, and main-channel records were established for *Nocomis leptocephalus* and *Semotilus atromaculatus*.
- **Station 6** (Glen Lyn): 23 species, SUMD 3.31. This included the first known record of *Etheostoma caeruleum* above Kanawha Falls, and *Notropis telescopus* records were established.

**Method comparison** (Table 2)
- Rotenone: 6 collections, 40 species total, mean 26 species per collection (range 23 to 30), mean SUMD 3.11, mean redundancy reported as 0.84 (range 0.17 to 0.72, so the mean appears inconsistent with the range).
- Seine: 15 collections, 4,380 specimens, 36 species total, mean 14 species per collection (range 10 to 19), mean SUMD 2.78 (range 2.02 to 3.21), mean redundancy 0.381.
- Rotenone-only species (9): *Nocomis leptocephalus*, *Ictalurus punctatus*, *Noturus insignis*, *Pylodictus olivaris*, *Cottus bairdi*, *Lepomis cyanellus*, *Etheostoma caeruleum*, *Etheostoma osburni* and *Percina maculata*. The authors state that most of these are uncommon in the main channel and unlikely to be taken by other methods.
- Seine-only species include *Alosa pseudoharengus*, *Notropis ardens*, *Lepomis gibbosus*, *Pomoxis nigromaculatus* and *Perca flavescens*. These are scattered in the main channel, and the authors suggest seasonal variation in local populations as a cause.
- Excluding Station 4, the recalculated mean SUMD for rotenone rises to 3.45.

**Efficiency and dose**
- Rotenone produced more species per collection than seining, and the authors report consistently higher diversity indexes for rotenone collections.
- The block net was very effective for collecting asphyxiated fish. Dip netting downstream collected almost no fish, and in earlier collections fewer than 10% of affected fish were taken by dip net.
- The application rate was about 0.26 L of rotenone per m³/sec of flow (one gallon per locality). This is far below the 2 L of 5% rotenone per m³/sec used by Boccarpy and Cooper (1963), yet still produced representative samples.
- Neutralization with potassium permanganate at 2.0 to 2.5 ppm successfully countered a 0.05 ppm rotenone concentration.

**Limitations and recommendations**
- The net was heavy when wet, so only stations with good road access were sampled. The full river width (up to 183 m) could not be treated.
- The net had to be set in wadable water, because boat or SCUBA cleaning was impractical. The authors argue that deep runs and pools can still be sampled with careful station siting.
- The authors suggest the block net could be shortened by about 30 m without losing effectiveness, and outfitting it with double floats and weights would improve it.
- Darter habitat should be sampled from below by dip net, as darters sink immediately and their contribution to drift is unknown.

**Other applications**
- The method was also used successfully on the [[Clinch River]] (one treatment over 187 m, 47 species) and the [[Blackwater River]] (one collection with 84 species), indicating broader applicability to large river systems.
