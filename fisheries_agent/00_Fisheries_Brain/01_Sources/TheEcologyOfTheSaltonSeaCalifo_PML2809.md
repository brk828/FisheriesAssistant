## Background & Description

**Lake program proposal (pp. 1–3).** The proposal lists possible program elements:
- Visitor presentations by keepers, nightcamp instructors, and similar staff.
- Gift-shop retail items, such as books on the Southwest, T-shirts, posters, and stuffed animals, plus a family "adopt the lake" scheme.
- Research on the [[Razorback Sucker]], the [[Bonytail Chub]], and related endangered fish. Projects would need approval from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] because of the species' endangered status. Partners named include [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and [[Arizona State University]]. The proposal also suggests limnological studies of the lake.

**Bonytail Chub species account (p. 4).** The account describes:
- **Morphology:** a streamlined body, a concave skull top, large fins, reduced scales and eyes, and a predorsal hump. The hump's function is unknown, but it is hypothesized to aid maneuvering in swift currents. Color is generally dark dorsally and lighter ventrally, varying with turbidity.
- **Distribution:** historically widespread in the Colorado River system. It is now restricted to several Colorado River reservoirs and the river upstream of the Grand Canyon, with small populations in Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu, Arizona. It is likely extinct in the Gila and Little Colorado rivers, and was reported from the now-dry Zuni River, New Mexico.
- **Habitat and diet:** prefers eddies and pools but tolerates strong currents and floods. Diet includes terrestrial insects, aquatic insects, plankton, plant debris, and algae.
- **Reproduction:** spawns May through July, depending on location, over gravel in 9 m or less of water. Females scatter eggs and males fertilize them. No documented successful wild recruitment has been recorded. Habitat change and predation or competition from non-native fishes are suspected causes.
- **Conservation concerns:** very small wild populations and a tendency to hybridize make the gene pool susceptible to genetic swamping. [[Captive Propagation]] is considered critical, and efforts aim to maintain a "pure" bonytail line.

**Salton Sea fish survey (pp. 6–7).** The survey documents fish observed at the Salton Sea, a saline inland lake. Salinity had increased greatly, and two freshwater fishes known from earlier records had disappeared. A freshwater species was occasionally seen near the east end of the Sea and at a spring inflow. The text also has a partly legible passage on plankton or biomass estimates in grams per m², but the numbers are too damaged to report reliably.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Colorado River system:** big-river fishes such as the bonytail and razorback sucker depend on swift, turbid water and on eddies and pools. Reservoirs now hold the remaining populations. Non-native fishes and altered flows are cited as threats.
- **Salton Sea:** rising salinity has eliminated freshwater species and shifted the community toward salt-tolerant fishes. The lake is a saline, inland system, not a marine one.
- **Management tension:** the lake proposal balances visitor and commercial use with the legal protections for endangered species, which require agency review of research.

## Key Findings

1. **Proposal priorities (pp. 1–3):** visitor education, retail and "adopt the lake" fundraising, endangered-fish research with agency approval, and limnological study.
2. **Bonytail distribution:** now confined to Colorado River reservoirs, the upper river, and small populations in Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu, Arizona.
3. **Bonytail life history:** spawns May–July on gravel at 9 m or less. Diet combines terrestrial and aquatic insects, plankton, and algae. Life-history data are limited by few wild fish.
4. **Recruitment failure:** no successful wild recruitment has been documented. Habitat change and non-native predators or competitors are the suspected causes.
5. **Hybridization risk:** tiny population size and hybridization threaten genetic integrity, so [[Captive Propagation]] to keep a pure line is considered critical.
6. **Salton Sea species shifts:** the survey lists common carp as the most abundant species. Striped mullet is second most abundant. Bonytail or roundtail chub, razorback sucker, and a single rainbow trout (about 16 inches, in fair condition) were recorded at lower abundance. Two freshwater fishes had disappeared as salinity rose.
