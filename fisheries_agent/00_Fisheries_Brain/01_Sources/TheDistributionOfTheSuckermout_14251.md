# The Distribution of the Suckermouth Minnow, *Phenacobius mirabilis*, in Texas

## Executive Summary
This 1955 study by Clark Hubbs and William F. Herzog documents the distribution of the [[Suckermouth Minnow]] (*Phenacobius mirabilis*) in Texas, updating records first established by Jordan and Gilbert (1886) in the Sabine and Trinity Rivers. The paper reports new locality records from the [[Colorado River]] system (Texas), including the Pedernales River and a riffle at the mouth of Shoal Creek in Austin, as well as additional records from the Sabine River and Owens Creek in eastern Texas. The authors argue that the species is native (not introduced via bait release) to the Colorado River System, part of a broader hypothesis that prehistoric floodwater connections between central and eastern Texas river systems allowed pelagic freshwater fishes to establish natural populations across drainage divides. The species is characterized throughout its range as rare but persistent in clear, swift-flowing streams, with the [[Balcones Escarpment]] marking the southwestern limit of its range, suggesting water chemistry — not simple stream divides — as the primary distributional barrier.

## Background & Description
- No detailed external morphological description is provided in this distributional paper; the focus is on locality, habitat, and biogeography.
- Specimens were collected as adults/juveniles sufficient for museum voucher deposition (Texas Natural History Collection, T.N.H.C.).
- Referenced congeneric ecological comparisons involve the darter *[[Hadropterus sclerus]]* (now *Percina sciera*), used as a comparative index of riffle-fish population abundance trends in the same collecting localities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat preference:** Clear water with rapid current; found in riffle zones.
- **Substrate variability:**
  - Colorado River (Shoal Creek mouth, Austin): boulders, heavy *Potamogeton* (aquatic vegetation) growth.
  - Sabine River (near Marshall): coal boulders scattered over sand; no vegetation.
  - Owens Creek (near Henderson): clay substrate; no vegetation.
- **Stream size:** Most confirmed records (except Owens Creek) are from large streams, though the authors note insufficient sampling to confirm a large-stream preference over small streams in Texas.
- **Co-occurring species:** Found alongside the darter *[[Hadropterus sclerus]]* in Colorado River riffle collections; both species appear to show increased abundance in the same riffle over a four-year sampling period (1950–1954).
- **Biogeographic barrier:** The [[Balcones Escarpment]] forms the southwestern range limit, implicating water chemistry rather than watershed divides as the controlling ecological factor.
- **Population status:** Described as rare throughout its entire range — consistent with Moore and Paden's (1950) characterization of the species as "nowhere abundant" in Oklahoma populations.

## Key Findings
1. **New Colorado River System records** (3 total): Pedernales River at Ranch Road 93 crossing (1951, single specimen, T.N.H.C. No. 1263); and two collections from the Colorado River at the mouth of Shoal Creek, Austin (October 1954, T.N.H.C. Nos. 3815 and 3816), plus seven additional specimens collected that same fall from under boulders in the riffle.
2. **New eastern Texas records**: Sabine River, 12 miles SSW of Marshall (T.N.H.C. No. 3202, July 1953) and Owens Creek, 9 miles south of Henderson (T.N.H.C. No. 3817, July 1953), collected during a National Science Foundation-supported field trip.
3. **Native vs. introduced status**: Despite suspicion of bait-release introduction (following [[Jurgens 1954]]'s report of four other cyprinids possibly introduced to the Colorado River), the authors argue *P. mirabilis* is likely native, based on the 1951 Riggs and Stephens record predating intensive local collecting efforts.
4. **Prehistoric flood-connectivity hypothesis**: The authors propose that pelagic freshwater fishes were historically transported between Texas river systems during prehistoric floodwater connections, establishing populations later supplemented — not solely created — by bait releases. The rarity and consistent ecological requirements of *P. mirabilis* across disjunct systems support this hypothesis over a pure-introduction explanation.
5. **Apparent riffle-fauna abundance increase**: Comparative data show low historical capture rates of *Hadropterus sclerus* (fewer than 10 specimens in 4 years vs. only 12 specimens from the entire Colorado River System reported by [[Hubbs 1954a]]), with a marked increase (11 specimens in just two collections) coinciding with the new *Phenacobius* captures — suggesting a broader increase in riffle-dwelling fish abundance in this locality.
6. **Corrected Rio Grande record**: Jordan's (1878) report of the species from the Rio Grande at Brownsville is noted as erroneous locality data, per [[Hubbs 1954b]].
7. **Distributional pattern**: The species exhibits a natural freshwater fish distribution pattern typical for the region, bounded southwestward by the Balcones Escarpment, implicating water chemistry as a limiting ecological factor rather than topographic stream divides.
