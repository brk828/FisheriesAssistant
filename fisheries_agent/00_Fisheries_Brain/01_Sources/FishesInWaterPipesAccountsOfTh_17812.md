# Fishes in Water Pipes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anguillidae #clupeidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1950 historical review, reprinted from *The American Midland Naturalist*, compiles documented cases of fishes obstructing municipal water mains and household service pipes. Eels ([[American Eel]]) are the dominant offenders, with the most numerous recent cases in the Bronx, New York City. Other reported intruders include [[Alewife]] (*Alosa pseudoharengus*) and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*, the catfish species assumed by the author). The author, [[E. W. Gudger]], assembles accounts from newspapers, utility engineers and the literature, arranged in reverse chronological order, and proposes explanations for how fishes enter pipe systems.

## Background & Description

The author notes that fishes sometimes behave in unexpected ways and that such cases are scattered through obscure publications. Although the expectation was that only long, slender fishes would enter pipes, the record shows that large-headed fishes such as catfishes also do so. The narrative draws on:

- Newspaper reports, notably *The New York Times* (1939 to 1948)
- Personal correspondence with utility engineers and superintendents
- Early and mid-twentieth-century technical papers and notes

The compilation covers New York City, Nova Scotia, New Bedford (Massachusetts), China, New Zealand, Texas and Missouri.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Eel migration.** The [[American Eel]] spawns in the sea. Elvers ascend freshwater streams in spring and mature in rivers and lakes. In autumn, adults descend toward the sea with the current. Pipe intakes and unscreened lake outlets intercepted these migrants.

**Alewife migration and landlocking.** The [[Alewife]] is a marine herring-family fish that ascends streams to spawn. Young fish, 2 to 3 inches long, seek to return to salt water in autumn. Landlocked populations in [[Kensico Reservoir]] had multiplied to become a water department problem.

**Reservoir screening.** Intakes at Kensico were screened with wire mesh of five-eighths of an inch square. This blocked adult alewives but let slender young pass into the supply. The author notes that the New York City system is doubly screened, yet adult eels may still pass through shoulders of dams and through the double screens during routine cleaning, a route the author calls the "shoulders" pathway.

**Catfish intake blockages.** Catfishes are heavily built forward with wide heads, which should prevent them from entering all but large intake pipes. The author treats their presence as largely fortuitous, noting they may have been carried down unscreened outlets or sought shelter in openings.

## Key Findings

**Documented pipe-blocking cases (New York City, Bronx):**
- **November 1948, 307 Mosholu Parkway South:** A large eel blocked the supply line, leaving 65 families without water. Water returned once the eel was expelled from the hydrant.
- **November 1948, 20 Boscobel Place:** A second eel, about 39 inches long, was removed from the eastern section's service pipe, five days after the first case.
- **November 1940, National Gypsum Co.:** A 36-inch, 3.5-inch-diameter eel blocked the boiler intake meter; pressure fell from 47 lbs to zero. A photograph of the eel, possibly the only one of its kind, appears in the paper.
- **November 1941, National Gypsum Co.:** A second and larger eel (29 inches long, 11 inches in circumference) was removed.
- **November 1939, Sunshine Farms (Bronx Terminal Market):** A 12-inch eel in a 2-inch pipe cut off supply to pasteurizers, causing a loss of 8,160 quarts of milk. The court held the company responsible because the fish was in its own service pipe.

**Nova Scotia, Canada:**
- **Dartmouth (reported 1906 by W. L. Bishop):** Eels plugged intake and service pipes from 1892 to 1904. Trouble peaked in September and October. Double screening and an intake trap ended the problem.
- **Halifax:** An eel-filled Granville Street main had to be bored out.
- **Sydney Mines, Cape Breton:** Eels obstructed two 4-inch pipes running 2,700 yards to colliery engines. One eel measured three feet six inches to four feet long.

**Other eel cases:**
- **New Bedford, Massachusetts (1883, Willard Nye Jr.):** Over 30 eels came from one pipe. The eels were believed to descend from fish trapped when the waterworks dam was built ten years earlier.
- **China (1926, reported by W. Rushton in 1934):** Eels blocked meters and mains fed from a reservoir supplied by a tributary near the sea.
- **Wanganui, New Zealand (H. C. Field, 1891):** Eels stalled a pipeline twice, in lake-fed mains connected to a sea-linked lake.

**Alewife complaints (New York City, 1925 to 1935):**
- [[Breder and Nigrelli]] (1936) analyzed complaints from 1926 to 1935. Alewives were reported in faucets mainly in September to November.
- Records show 185 complaints across September, October and November over the period. The heaviest year was 1934, with 115 complaints (40 in September, 61 in October, 8 in November, 6 in December).
- The study found the Kensico alewife to be a typical landlocked form of *Alosa pseudoharengus*.
- Control measures included stocking [[Kensico Reservoir]] in 1935 with rainbow and lake trout, which feed at depths below the basses and perches. Afterward, the alewife population fell sharply.

**Catfish cases:**
- **Wichita Falls, Texas (undated, approx. 25 years earlier):** A 56-pound, 49-inch catfish wedged in the intake gates cut the city's water take from Lake Wichita.
- **Bucklin, Missouri (June 1947):** A 30-pound catfish blocked the inlet pipe for four days. Clearing required pumping the intake tower dry and removing a reducer bend, where a catfish head was found.

**Conclusions and author's proposals:**
- Fishes in pipes are likely far more common than published accounts indicate, and the author urges others to report similar observations.
- The author judged the cost of wire screens for the New York City system as prohibitive. An appropriation proposal in 1941 was not pursued.
- The author proposes that eels in the Bronx survived in pipe systems by feeding on small fishes and crustaceans found in storage reservoirs and mains.
