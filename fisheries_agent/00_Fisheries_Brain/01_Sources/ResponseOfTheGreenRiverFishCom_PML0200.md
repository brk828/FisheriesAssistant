# Response of the Green River Fish Community to Construction and Re-regulation of Flaming Gorge Dam, 1962-1996

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by [[Kevin R Bestgen]] and [[Lawrence W Crist]] evaluates the restoration protocol of Stanford et al. (1996) for regulated rivers, using the 105 km reach of the [[Green River]] between [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and its confluence with the unregulated [[Yampa River]]. Closure of the dam eliminated most native fishes from the regulated reach between 1967 and 1978 because cold releases inhibited reproduction. Penstock modification in 1978 restored reproduction by most common native fishes, while [[Flow Re-regulation]] begun in 1992 partially restored spring peaks and lowered summer base flows. Sampling from 1994 to 1996 found native fishes numerically dominant in [[Lodore Canyon]], an increased abundance of adult [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and continued increases in non-native warm-water fishes and piscivores. Colorado pikeminnow reproduction was not detected, and the authors conclude that restoring natural discharge and summer temperature patterns is needed for further recovery.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Green River basin drains southern Wyoming, eastern Utah and northwestern Colorado. The regulated reach includes Red Canyon, low-gradient Browns Park, high-gradient Lodore Canyon, Whirlpool Canyon, Island and Rainbow parks, and Split Mountain Canyon. Flaming Gorge Reservoir stores 3.74 million acre-ft (4,624 million m3).
- **Objectives:** (1) assess the fish fauna from the dam to the Yampa River; (2) relate flow and temperature regimes to fish distribution and abundance; (3) compare these with other Colorado basin data; (4) determine whether rare fishes could benefit from temperature modification.
- **Data base:** Fish data spanned about 35 years (Vanicek et al. 1970, Holden and Stalnaker 1975, Holden and Crist 1981), with new sampling in 1994-1996.
- **Methods (summary):** Seining of low-velocity margins (1.3 and 4.6 m seines); drift netting of larvae in 1993-1995; raft-mounted electrofishing (Coffelt WP-15 and Smith-Root units; conductivity 300-700 µS); angling and trammel netting on a limited basis; PIT tagging of rare fishes; continuous thermograph records; empirical regression models of summer water temperature with air temperature and discharge as predictors.
- **Context:** Mean annual discharge changed little after closure (59 m3/s post-dam vs 56 m3/s pre-dam), but June mean annual maximum flow fell from 381 m3/s to 139 m3/s, and sediment yield dropped by 54%.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime:** Pre-dam summer temperatures exceeded 22 °C. After 1967, mean July temperature in two typical post-impoundment years reached only 6.1 °C. The 1978 [[Penstock Modification]] limited summer releases to about 13 °C.
- **Flow regime:** Pre-dam spring peaks were replaced by flat hydrographs. Re-regulation from 1992 released up to 130 m3/s in spring and set a summer base flow target of about 51 m3/s at Jensen, Utah, as required by the [[1992 Biological Opinion]].
- **Drivers of distribution:** A strong longitudinal temperature gradient and the availability of low-velocity backwater habitat appear to control fish distribution. Canyon reaches have few backwaters, while Browns Park and reach 4 of Lodore Canyon have more.
- **Reproductive cues:** In the Yampa River, [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] spawn from mid-to-late June as discharge declines and water temperature rises (16-23 °C). In the regulated Green River these two cues run in opposite directions in mid-summer.
- **Non-native fishes:** Warm-water species such as [[Red Shiner]], [[Sand Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Redside Shiner]] are common, along with predators [[Northern Pike]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]].

## Key Findings

- **Reproduction restored (1978):** Penstock modification restored reproduction by most common native fishes in Lodore Canyon, a 32 km reach. Reproduction by Colorado pikeminnow and razorback sucker was not detected. Eight native fishes reproduced in the regulated reach, and only these two rare species did not.
- **Community shift (1980 to 1994-1996):** Native species fell from 92% to 65% of the fish community at the upper Lodore site, and from 32% to 21% at the lower site. Cold-water salmonids declined, and brown trout, white sucker and redside shiner increased.
- **Colorado pikeminnow increase:** Captures and observations were 0.41 fish/h of electrofishing (0.53 fish/h in reaches 3 and 4), higher than earlier Lodore studies (0.08 fish/h in 1978-1980; 0.27 fish/h in 1987-1988). Individuals were captured in spring, summer and autumn. Recaptured fish grew about 23 and 30 mm/yr, versus a Colorado River average of 9.8 mm/yr.
- **Length-weight relationship:** For Lodore Canyon pikeminnow, weight (kg) = 0.03908 × TL(mm)^2.805 (r² = 0.98). Fish over 500 mm TL were heavier than Yampa River fish, suggesting high productivity.
- **Drift net results:** Very few fish were captured (10 larvae in 1993; 307 fish in 99 samples in 1994; 30 fish in 24 samples in 1995). No pikeminnow larvae were found, although the Yampa River yielded 124 in 1994.
- **Hybridization:** Five hybrid sucker combinations were recorded in 1994-1996, against three in earlier sampling. Hybrids made up 3.9% of all fishes and 7% of suckers captured by electrofishing. White sucker hybrids were most common upstream.
- **Roundtail chub and razorback sucker:** Roundtail chub were rare and may be declining (149 captured by Holden and Crist in lower Lodore vs six in the present study). A single pure razorback sucker (459 mm TL) was captured.
- **Non-native predators:** 13 northern pike (mean 633 mm TL), 58 channel catfish (mean 297 mm TL) and rare smallmouth bass were captured. No evidence of reproduction by channel catfish or northern pike was found.
- **Temperature model:** Empirical regression models using air temperature and discharge predicted summer temperatures at three sites reliably. Predicted temperatures at 25 m3/s base flow exceeded 19 °C for nearly four weeks, while at 37.5 and 50 m3/s they did not exceed 18 and 17 °C.
- **Restoration conclusions:** Enhancing natural discharge and temperature patterns is needed to restore reproduction by rare natives. A minimum of two to five weeks near or above 20 °C in mid-summer is suggested as a target.
- **Recommendations:** Evaluate the impacts of pike, smallmouth bass and channel catfish; study hybridization rates; manipulate dam releases toward natural patterns; monitor fish and temperature annually, including drift net sampling if temperatures are enhanced.
