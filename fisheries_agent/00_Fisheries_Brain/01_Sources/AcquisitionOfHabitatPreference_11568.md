# Acquisition of Habitat Preference Data by Radiotelemetry

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #radiotelemetry #habitat-assessment #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This paper presents methods and results from radiotelemetry studies of [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the [[Green River]] basin (1980-1985), evaluating radiotracking success in high-conductivity waters and comparing habitat data obtained via radiotelemetry versus electrofishing. The study demonstrates that radiotelemetry provides less biased habitat utilization data than traditional collection methods, particularly in turbid, high-conductivity rivers where visual observation is impossible. A total of 92 Colorado squawfish were surgically implanted with radio transmitters, achieving 47% of theoretical battery life under field conditions. The methodology is applicable to [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) and habitat suitability criteria development.

## Background & Description

Underwater radiotelemetry emerged in the late 1960s as a methodology for monitoring fish behavior in natural environments, particularly suitable for riverine systems where ultrasonic telemetry may be inadequate. Radio waves propagate through water inversely proportional to frequency, with signal attenuation increasing with water conductivity. Only radio energy contacting the air-water interface at angles less than 6° passes through; the apparent signal source appears as a small circle on the water surface.

The [[Green River]] study area extends from the confluence with the [[Colorado River]] through Desolation and Gray Canyons, characterized by conductivities ranging 200-2,000 µmho, shallow depths (<16 ft), and numerous underwater obstructions. Four radio module types were evaluated: Smith-Root lithium-powered (150-day and 300-day rated life) and AVM mercury-powered (180-day and 550-day rated life). All transmitters operated at 40.600-40.700 MHz frequency range.

Surgical implantation involved intraperitoneal placement of "antenna-less" modules (transmitting antenna sealed within coated capsule) coated twice in purified beeswax. Radio-to-fish weight ratios averaged <1% of body weight and ~10% of fish length. Fish were tracked weekly using Smith-Root Model RF-40 and SR-40 receivers with Larsen-Kulrod whip antennas, supplemented by Yagi and loop antennas for triangulation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Colorado Squawfish]] is an endangered migratory predator exhibiting homing behavior to two major spawning areas in the Green River Basin. The species undertakes annual spawning migrations (July-August) and utilizes diverse habitats including deep pools, eddies, shoreline runs, and riffles. Adults typically select eddies and runs along shorelines over sand and silt substrates, often at drop-offs adjacent to sand bars and at eddy-run interfaces.

Water conductivities during prime tracking months (May-September, 1980-85) varied from 205 to 950 µmho (at 25°C), averaging ~540 µmho. Fifteen modules experienced temperature extremes from 0 to 25°C. Radiotracking under conductivities >700 µmho was marginal, with success partially dependent on shallow water conditions that compensated for declining signal strength at low water levels.

Nine [[Razorback Sucker]] were also implanted but proved more difficult to track due to deeper habitat use in spring and fall, demonstrating species-specific challenges in radiotelemetry applications.

## Key Findings

**Radiotracking Performance:**
- Average field battery life achieved 47.3% of theoretical life across all radio types (range: 34-60%)
- No significant difference between mercury (47%) and lithium (47.5%) battery performance
- Mercury-powered modules rated for 1+ year averaged 260 days; 33% functioned >1 year (455-542 days maximum)
- Performance ratios consistent across three independent Green River Basin studies (47.3%, 47.6%, 49.6%)
- Temperature fluctuations caused frequency drift; one known transmitter failure occurred at near-0°C water temperature

**Equipment Optimization:**
- Simple whip antennas preferable for riverine work; directional antennas unnecessary until fish located
- Loop antennas adequate for triangulation after initial detection
- Coaxial cable condition and connector integrity critical; conversion from "bnc" to larger "coaxial" connectors reduced failure
- Search receivers with continuous tuning capability essential; wildlife-type receivers with separate frequency bands inadequate due to temperature-induced frequency drift
- Retuning search receivers for greater sensitivity improved contact success in marginal conditions

**Bias in Collection Methods:**
- Habitat distributions from electrofishing versus radiotelemetry significantly different (Chi-square, P<0.001)
- Electrofishing overrepresented shoreline runs; radiotelemetry revealed greater use of deep eddies
- Substrate distributions significantly different between methods (P<0.005)
- Mean depths differed significantly: electrofishing 1.23 m (SD=0.55, n=91) versus radiotelemetry 1.40 m (SD not reported, n=244) (ANOVA, P<0.04)
- Velocities not significantly different between methods (P>0.5)
- Bias attributed to fish movement into electrical field or "herding" during electrofishing

**Habitat Utilization Data (1980-81, 1,281 observations):**
- Fish selected relatively heterogeneous habitats with respect to depth and velocity profiles
- Measurements at fish locations differed from adjacent (1-2 m distant) measurements:
  - Inshore depths: 1.46 m versus 1.8 m at fish (P<0.04)
  - Offshore velocities: 0.53 m/s versus 0.3 m/s at fish (P<0.006)
  - No difference between fish location measurements (1.8 m, 0.30 m/s) and offshore depths/inshore velocities (1.85 m, 0.25 m/s)

**Spawning Habitat Partitioning:**
Two distinct spawning habitat types identified:
1. **Resting-staging habitat** (pools/large shoreline eddies): Mean depth 2.13 m, mean velocity 0.37 m/s (Green and Yampa Rivers combined, multiple years)
2. **Deposition-fertilization habitat** (riffles): Mean depth 0.94 m, mean velocity 0.48 m/s

Close agreement between years and rivers (Yampa River data: resting-staging 1981 n=68, 1984 n=45+147; deposition-fertilization 1981 n=84, 1983 n=30, 1984 n=45) validated behavioral habitat partitioning.

**Geographic Comparisons:**
- ANOVA revealed significant depth differences (P<0.01) between Green River and tributaries (White and Yampa Rivers)
- No significant depth difference between White and Yampa Rivers
- Velocity measurements significantly different (P<0.01) among all three rivers
- Differences may reflect habitat selection or available habitat variation; caution warranted in data pooling across streams

**Fish Growth and Transmitter Effects:**
- No significant difference in growth rates between implanted (n=14, mean=11.2 mm/year, SD=10.2) and non-implanted fish (n=59, mean=10.2 mm/year, SD=11.3) (Student's t, P>0.01)
- Two razorback suckers with removed implants: 2.5 mm/year versus 39 non-implanted fish: 2.2 mm/year
- Radio-to-fish weight ratio <1% and beeswax coating likely contributed to successful retention; no transmitter expulsion observed

**Implications for [[PHABSIM]] Modeling:**
- Microhabitat data at fish locations may not reflect mean cell depths and velocities used as PHABSIM input variables
- Habitat heterogeneity suggests need for finer spatial resolution (1-m rather than 10-m transect increments) or additional measurements near fish
- Visual observations in shallow water confirmed fish selection of sheltered habitats behind boulders or cover
- Eddy-run interface heavily utilized in deeper waters

**Methodological Recommendations:**
- Radio modules should be <1.5% fish body weight for long-term retention and minimal behavioral effects
- Investigators can anticipate ~47% of theoretical battery life in high-conductivity rivers with proper equipment maintenance
- Triangulation accuracy validated by visual observations in shallow water and fish disturbance at signal source in deep water
- Training with weighted transmitters recommended for new personnel
- Frequent inspection of cable linkages, damaged cables, and connector shorts essential; these were largest contributors to tracking failure
