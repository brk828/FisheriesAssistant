# The Ecology of the Salton Sea, California, in Relation to the Sportfishery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

Fish Bulletin No. 113 (California Department of Fish and Game, 1961), edited by [[Boyd W Walker]], reports the results of the UCLA Salton Sea Research Project (1954–1957) on the ecology of the Salton Sea and its prospects for a sportfishery. Before 1950 the Sea held only four fish species, two of which never exceeded two inches. Beginning in 1950, the Department introduced a "shotgun" assortment of species, mainly from San Felipe in the Gulf of California. [[Bairdiella]] and [[Orangemouth Corvina]] established self-sustaining populations, and [[Sargo]] later became abundant. The volume covers the Sea's geological history, salinity and temperature regimes, dissolved oxygen, phytoplankton productivity, invertebrates and zooplankton, the [[Pile Worm]] and [[Barnacle]], and the fishes.

## Background & Description

The [[Salton Sea]] is the largest inland body of water in California, lying in the Salton Sink, a basin below sea level (lowest point −273.5 ft) whose southern boundary is the [[Colorado River Delta]]. The Sea was formed by Colorado River floods in 1905–1907 through a broken irrigation headworks. Its surface elevation has since been controlled by irrigation inflow and evaporation, which the authors estimate at about six feet per year. In 1956 the Sea covered about 340 square miles (220,000 acres) at −235 ft and had a maximum depth near 40 ft.

The project was run from a rented motel cabin at Fish Springs by a staff of three full-time researchers plus part-time workers. Its goals were restricted to information most useful for future management. Its chapters, by author, were:

- History of the Salton Sea (Lars H. Carpelan)
- Physical and Chemical Characteristics (Carpelan)
- Phytoplankton and Plant Productivity (Carpelan)
- Invertebrate Fauna (Richard H. Linsley and Carpelan)
- Zooplankton (Carpelan)
- The Pile Worm (Carpelan and Linsley)
- The Fishes of the Salton Sea (Walker, Richard R. Whitney and George W. Barlow)
- Species accounts by Hendricks, Whitney and Jay C. Quast
- General Considerations and Recommendations (Walker, Whitney and Carpelan)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geological and hydrological history.** Lake Cahuilla, a freshwater lake, left a shoreline about 45 ft above present mean sea level and was probably filled by Colorado River water. Salinity analyses showed salts in the proportions expected from evaporated Colorado River water, not ocean water. In 1907 the new Sea had a salinity of 3.6 ‰, and by 1948 it was about 11 times that value. Its elevation was −240 ft in 1948 and −234.5 ft in 1956. Officials believed the level could be stabilized near −235 ft, but the rise slowed after 1951.

**Salinity and ions.** In 1956 salinity was about 33 ‰, just below the ocean total. The ion proportions differed from seawater: relatively less magnesium, potassium and chloride, and more sulphate, calcium and bicarbonate. Chlorinity in the main body ranged 13.3–14 ‰ (salinity 31.4–33 ‰). Salinity was predicted to rise about 0.4 ‰ per year at stabilization, reaching about 40 ‰ by the mid-1970s. Inflow from the [[Alamo River]] and [[New River]] was locally dilute and salty.

**Temperature.** Water temperatures ranged from 10 to 36 °C (50–97 °F). Thermal stratification was temporary, appearing after windless periods in summer.

**Dissolved oxygen and anoxia.** Oxygen was supersaturated in winter and at midday. Water deeper than about nine meters (roughly the −260 ft contour, about 56% of the bottom area) became anoxic in summer. Wind mixing then caused surface oxygen to drop temporarily, with occasional fish kills. Sulfide rose to 85 µg-at/L at the bottom on July 16, 1956.

**Nutrients.** Nitrate and phosphate were low in the main body. Ammonia showed a seasonal trend, reaching above 20 µg-at/L at the bottom in summer. Agricultural drainage carried much higher nutrient concentrations into the Sea.

**Primary production.** Phytoplankton was dominated by diatoms, dinoflagellates and a small green alga, *Westella botryoides*. Seasonal maxima reached over 30,000 cells/cm³. The estimated average surface carbon fixation was 0.75 g C/m²/day. Estimated annual production was about 2.7 tons dry weight per acre, though the authors consider this a rough approximation.

**Invertebrates.** The fauna was limited, with six phyla represented. [[Neanthes succinea]] was the most important fish food. It was probably introduced in 1930 with pile worms from San Diego Bay. Its standing crop in fall was estimated at about 125 lb per acre. It was excluded from deep anoxic water in summer. [[Balanus amphitrite]] barnacles, first seen about 1944, spread through the Sea and were of limited food value. [[Cyclops dimorphus]] was the dominant summer copepod and a key early food for bairdiella. Introductions of invertebrates (Table 17 of the source) include mysids, clams, mussels, oysters and crabs, with many species accidentally introduced with shoal grass from Texas in 1957.

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna.** The Sea now supports nine fish species, including introduced and native forms. The major ones are:

- **[[Bairdiella]]** (*Bairdiella icistius*): 67 fish planted in 1950–1951 at San Felipe. The first successful spawning was in 1952, and it spawned every year through 1957. It is the main forage fish for corvina.
- **[[Orangemouth Corvina]]** (*Cynoscion xanthulus*): about 272 fish or fewer planted 1950–1955. It became the chief game fish, with a 32-pound individual recorded.
- **[[Shortfin Corvina]]** (*Cynoscion parvipinnis*): survived well but showed no clear evidence of spawning in the Sea.
- **[[Sargo]]** (*Anisotremus davidsoni*): 65 fish planted March 1951. Only three were found during the project, but by 1960 it had produced a large population.
- **[[Threadfin Shad]]** (*Dorosoma petenense*): entered via the Colorado River system in 1955 and was an important food for corvina. Recruitment depended on freshwater spawning.
- **[[Striped Mullet]]** (*Mugil cephalus*): once abundant and commercially fished, now virtually extinct. The Alamo Canal connection was cut off in 1942 and most mullet were older than 14 years. About 75% of the fish had abnormalities, and 46.3% of 533 fish had calculi.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*): the only native species. It tolerated salinities above twice that of seawater.
- **[[Mosquitofish]]** (*Gambusia affinis*) and **[[Longjaw Mudsucker]]** (*Gillichthys mirabilis*): the mudsucker, from 500 fish planted in 1930, became abundant along shores and was a major bait species.

**Introduction history.** The 1950–1951 "shotgun" plantings, drawn from about 7,000 San Felipe fishes by the end of 1951, were the basis of the sportfishery. Other failed introductions included striped bass (1929–1930), silver salmon (1934), and anchovy and anchoveta.

**Recommendations and outlook.** The authors judged that the Sea could support a sportfishery, but its environment was highly variable. They emphasized that the most important variables were the stabilization elevation and the resulting salinity trend, oxygen depletion in deep water, and the continued availability of Colorado River water.

**Limitations.** The authors stated that many results were incomplete because of limited staff and that the sargo population grew after the project ended. Estimates of productivity and standing crop are rough.
