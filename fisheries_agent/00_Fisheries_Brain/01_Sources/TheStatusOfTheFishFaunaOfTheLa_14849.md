## Executive Summary

This 1951 survey by Orthello L. Wallis documents the fish fauna of the [[Lake Mead National Recreational Area]], a nearly 225-mile stretch of the [[Colorado River]] spanning Arizona and Nevada. The construction of [[Hoover Dam]] (completed 1936) and [[Davis Dam]] fundamentally altered the river's hydrology — converting a swift, heavily silt-laden, turbidity-variable river into placid, clear reservoir systems ([[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]]). This transformation has driven a decline in native, endemic fishes and facilitated the establishment and proliferation of introduced (exotic) game and forage species. The report catalogs 3 confirmed native species and 11 exotic species (with 6 additional native and 2 additional exotic species considered possible occurrences), and evaluates the sport fishery, particularly the dominance of [[Largemouth Black Bass]] in Lake Mead and [[Rainbow Trout]] in Lake Mohave. The author calls for restraint in further introductions and urgent scientific study of the declining native fauna before further loss occurs.

## Background & Description

- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Largest native Colorado River sucker; distinguished by a pronounced dorsal keel/hump. In Lake Mead, individuals exceed 26 inches in length and 7 lbs in weight.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): Largest of the American minnows (Cyprinidae); reputed historical maximum of 80 lbs and 5 feet in length, though no such large specimens taken in recent record (largest recent record ~34 lbs).
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila robusta elegans*): Native minnow closely related to the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*); locally misnamed "Gila or Verde trout."
- **Native fishes generally**: Adapted to swift-water, turbid habitats via reduced scale size and streamlined body form (per Miller 1946b) — morphology now poorly suited to the regulated, clear-water conditions created by damming.
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]**: Average 15 inches; few exceed 25 inches. Record specimen: 32.75 inches, 18 lbs (1950).
- **[[Brown Trout]]**: Single record specimen — 3 lbs 6 oz, 24.5 inches total length, age 7 years.
- **[[Largemouth Black Bass]]**: Record specimen 13 lbs 14 oz (1939), a national contest-winning fish; Lake Mead produced 13 additional national placings post-1939.
- **[[Black Crappie]]**: Average 1–2 lbs, under 12 inches.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (southern subspecies): Record 20 lbs, 31 inches (1945, Pierce Ferry).
- **[[Black Bullhead]]**: Single recorded specimen, 159 mm standard length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat transformation**: Pre-dam Colorado River = swift, heavily silt-laden, highly variable turbidity/volume. Post-dam = clear, placid, thermally stratified reservoir waters (Lake Mead) and cold tailwater (upper Lake Mohave, ~56°F, trout-suitable) transitioning downstream to warm-water conditions suitable for bass.
- **Native fish decline**: Following Hoover Dam completion, native species declined concurrently with exotic species increase — attributed to predation, competition, and altered ecological conditions (Dill 1944). The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a notable exception, reproducing abundantly and persisting well in Lake Mead.
- **Relict/endemic fauna**: The White River springfish (*Crenichthys baileyi*) and leather chub (*Moapa coriacea*) persist in nearby Warm Springs as glacial-relict remnants of the ancient White River fauna, a former Colorado River tributary system. *Moapa coriacea* is known from nowhere else — critical single-locality endemic.
- **Bait-bucket introduction pathway**: Live bait sourced from the Santa Clara River (Utah) and sold at Lake Mead docks includes flannelmouth sucker, Utah mountain sucker, western dace, scaled spiny dace, a sculpin (*Cottus bairdii*), and carp — creating an ongoing vector for range expansion of non-native (to Lake Mead) fishes via angler bait-bucket release.
- **Trophic/competitive dynamics**: [[Green Sunfish]] considered undesirable as a likely unintentional co-introduction with [[Bluegill]] (1937 stocking); noted as a food competitor with limited size potential. [[Carp]] have multiplied extensively in Lake Mead; commercial harvest permitted but seining/netting deemed ecologically impractical due to lake topography and bycatch risk to game species.
- **Thermal zonation of sport fishery**: Lake Mohave's upper (cold, dam-released) waters support trout; lower reaches warm sufficiently for warm-water game species (bass), though only trout were being stocked at time of writing.
- **Proposed water development**: 134 additional projects (irrigation, power, flood/silt control, channelization) proposed for the Colorado River Basin (Anonymous 1946), threatening further habitat alteration and native fish decline.

## Key Findings

- **Native species confirmed (3)**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila robusta elegans*).
- **Possible additional natives (6)**: [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), western dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), Utah mountain sucker (*Pantosteus delphinus utahensis*), scaled spiny dace (*Lepidomeda vittata*), and a sculpin (*Cottus bairdii*) — mostly documented via bait-bucket collections rather than direct lake capture.
- **Exotic species confirmed (11)**: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii irideus*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), Bonneville mountain sucker (*Pantosteus platyrhynchus*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus lacustris punctatus*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), [[Western Gambusia]] (*Gambusia affinis affinis*), [[Largemouth Black Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides salmoides*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Poxomis nigromaculatus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus macrochirus*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*).
- **Possible additional exotics (2)**: [[Smallmouth Black Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*, observed 1942 near Moapa River mouth) and [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*, widely used historically as bait, possibly established) — neither confirmed taken directly from Lake Mead.
- **Stocking history**: ~1 million rainbow trout fingerlings planted below Hoover Dam since 1935; 466,000 largemouth bass fingerlings released 1935–1940; 25,000 bluegill fingerlings released in 1937 (single planting); 722 adult/yearling channel catfish released 1892–1893 (earliest Colorado River fish introduction noted).
- **Fishery management recommendations**:
  - No closed fishing season deemed necessary given firm establishment of exotic game fishes.
  - Further introduction of additional alien species **not recommended**.
  - Further stocking of largemouth black bass or forage fishes in Lake Mead **discouraged** (self-sustaining populations).
  - Continued rainbow trout stocking below Hoover Dam **necessary** to meet angler demand, despite trout being unlikely to succeed as a permanent Lake Mead addition.
  - Explicit call for further investigation of the sport fishery and of native fish ecology before extinction/faunal alteration proceeds further.
- **Endemism concern**: The Colorado River Basin fauna, while species-poor, shows a high degree of endemism (per Evermann & Rutter 1895; Miller 1946a,b), and is characterized as urgently in need of research due to rapid anthropogenic alteration.
- **Single-locality endemic at risk**: *Moapa coriacea* (leather chub) known only from Home Ranch Warm Springs near Glendale, Nevada — highlighted as recoverable only through rigid protection.
