# Watershed Resources and Problems of the Upper Rio Grande Basin

#watershed-management #hydrology #range-ecology #aquatic-ecosystem #rio-grande

## Executive Summary

This 1956 U.S. Forest Service report by E. J. Dortignac, issued by the [[Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station]], assesses the watershed resources of the Upper Rio Grande Basin, which covers almost 21 million acres in Colorado, New Mexico, and Texas. It addresses climate and drought, water yield, vegetation, sediment, soil erosion, salinity, floods, livestock grazing, timber, wildlife, land ownership, and economic-social development. The central conclusion is that the basin's economy depends on water whose supply is limited, drought-prone, and increasingly degraded by sediment, so land management on the upland watershed is essential to protecting downstream water works.

The document contains no fish or aquatic species data. Its aquatic content concerns streamflow, sediment, water quality and the trout and game-fish habitat of the streams, which are treated only briefly. Because the document is not about a species, the species, family and habitat fields in this note are limited to the watershed setting.

## Background & Description

The Upper Rio Grande Basin is one of the largest in the American Southwest. The [[Rio Grande]] is among the longest U.S. rivers, with headwaters in the southern Rocky Mountains about 1,800 miles from the Gulf of Mexico. The basin drains 185,000 square miles and forms the international boundary with Mexico for more than half its length. The Rio Grande Valley is described as the oldest continuously settled region in the United States, with irrigation practiced about 1,000 years ago by Pueblo peoples.

Land cover is roughly one-fourth forest, one-fourth grassland, one-third [[Pinyon-Juniper Woodland]], one-seventh brush and shrubs, and only about one-twentieth cultivated. Topography ranges from less than 4,500 feet to 14,000 feet. The [[San Luis Valley]] lies in a rain shadow, and the [[Middle Rio Grande Valley]] is a narrow flood plain 3 to 5 miles wide extending 175 miles from White Rock Canyon to [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]].

Vegetation zones follow precipitation and elevation: [[Spruce-Fir-Aspen]] forest (9,000 to 13,000 feet, above 25 inches of precipitation), [[Ponderosa Pine]] forest (to about 7,500 feet), pinyon-juniper woodland, sagebrush, grassland and half-shrub, and desert zones such as creosotebush and greasewood-saltbush below 10 inches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and drought.** Annual precipitation ranges from 6.5 to 40 inches. Evidence of drought goes back to 1276, with seven or eight major dry cycles since then. The drought beginning in 1942 and the 1892–1904 drought were the worst recorded. Precipitation in 1950–51 was half to three-fourths of the long-term average, and runoff was less than half.

**Water yield.** Most streamflow originates in the mountains. Annual water yield ranges from less than 0.01 acre-feet per acre in semiarid lowlands to as much as 2.5 acre-feet per acre in the [[San Juan Mountains]]. In the spruce-fir-aspen zone, about 86 percent of Colorado's streamflow originates, and about 40 percent of New Mexico's comes from ponderosa pine. The [[Chama River]] and other mountain streams have large snowmelt flows, while streams from sedimentary rocks, such as the [[Rio Galisteo]] and [[Rio Puerco]], have quick runoff and little ground-water storage.

**Streamflow seasonality.** All permanent streams share similar seasonal patterns. About half the annual flow occurs in the 60 days of rapid snowmelt, mostly in May and June. Late-summer and fall flows, when irrigation needs peak, are small. This makes water-storage reservoirs and ground-water-retention land practices necessary.

**Sediment.** Sediment is the most damaging problem. Average annual suspended sediment loads were 39 million tons in 1936–41 and about 7 million tons in 1948–50. The [[Rio Puerco]] contributes about 41–47 percent of measured sediment but less than 8 percent of water inflow. Gully and arroyo trenching produce 65 percent of sediment, sheet erosion 30 percent, and wind erosion 5 percent.

**Water quality and salinity.** Dissolved solids rise downstream: 80 parts per million at Del Norte, Colorado, in 1936, and 2,100 at Fort Quitman, Texas, in 1936 (1,691 in the 1934–53 average). Salt burden exceeds half a million tons annually at San Marcial. Excess soluble salts reduce crop yields 10 to 20 percent below Elephant Butte Dam.

**Aquatic habitat.** The report notes that most lower-elevation streams are unsuitable for game fish, often because of poor watershed management or irrigation diversion. Trout thrive in high-mountain streams and lakes and in the Rio Grande above the Chama, where sediment is minor and water is clear.

**Water waste.** About 60,000 acres of nonbeneficial river-bottom vegetation, mainly [[Saltcedar]] mixed with cottonwoods and willows, consume about 240,000 acre-feet annually, roughly twice the use of most cultivated crops.

## Key Findings

- The Upper Rio Grande Basin covers almost 21 million acres. Only about one-twentieth is cultivated.
- Recurrent drought is part of the normal climate. Major dry cycles have occurred roughly every few decades since 1276.
- Average annual water production above Elephant Butte Dam is almost 3 million acre-feet. About 1.5 million acre-feet originate in Colorado, and two-thirds of that is consumed above the state line.
- Between the Colorado line and Elephant Butte Dam, more than 900,000 acre-feet are consumed annually. Downstream to San Marcial, only one-fourth of depletion is beneficially used; more than 400,000 acre-feet is wasted.
- Sediment damage above Elephant Butte Dam was estimated at $1,820,000 annually before recent construction. Channel damage was $371,000 and reservoir damage $658,000.
- Elephant Butte Reservoir's original capacity of 2.63 million acre-feet was reduced 17 percent to 2.20 million by 1947.
- The Rio Grande riverbed at Albuquerque is more than 3 feet higher than downtown, and it is still rising.
- Major floods: 16 since 1874. Direct flood damages along the main river approximate $500,000 annually, and total preventable damages about $1 million.
- Salt imbalance was adverse 17 of 20 years in the Rincon Valley and 11 of 20 in the Mesilla Valley.
- Livestock numbers in New Mexico peaked around 1900 at about 533,000 animal-units. In 1950 the total was 158,000 units in New Mexico and 460,000 cattle in Colorado. Forage supplies cover only about half of needs.
- About 85 percent of basin lands produce forage and are grazed.
- Timber: the national forests hold about 5.4 billion board-feet. The present annual cut exceeds net annual growth by more than one-fourth.
- Tourism and recreation are valued at about $50 million annually. Hunting, fishing and trapping bring direct income to residents.
- Control of sediment requires both main-channel engineering and on-site land treatment, revegetation and sound land use. The report calls for stronger basic land research, especially for areas receiving less than 15 inches of annual precipitation.
