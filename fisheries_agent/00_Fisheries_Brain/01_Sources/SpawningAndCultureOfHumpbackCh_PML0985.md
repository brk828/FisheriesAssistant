# Spawning and Culture of Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service study, conducted by Roger L. Beeman at the [[National Fisheries Center]] (Kearneysville, West Virginia), developed and tested techniques for spawning and culturing [[Humpback Chub]], a federally and state-listed endangered [[Cyprinidae]] endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. During 1981 at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona), broodstock were induced to spawn by [[Carp Pituitary Injection]] and by natural spawning on cobble substrate. Both methods produced viable progeny, and the authors conclude that the methods could support large-scale propagation for recovery.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** *Gila cypha* (Humpback Chub), one of the last freshwater cyprinids to be described in North America (Miller 1945). It was known only from the Colorado River basin, endemic to the Grand Canyon region.
- **Distribution:** Now found sporadically in canyon reaches of the upper and lower [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Conservation status:** Listed as endangered by Arizona, Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming, and by the U.S. Department of the Interior (1980). Decline was attributed to habitat loss from dams, reduced flows and fluctuating tailwaters, and [[Hybridization]] with other chubs.
- **Objective:** Test and develop spawning and culture techniques, and report on successful spawning of wild-caught broodstock and early culture of progeny.

### Broodstock
- 16 wild adults from the Colorado River (collected 5 November 1979, Black Rocks near Grand Junction, Colorado).
- 16 wild adults from the Little Colorado River (collected May 1978, October 1979, and June 1980 near the confluence with the Colorado River, Grand Canyon National Park), the "Little Colorado River strain."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning temperature:** Spawning is believed to occur when water reaches about 18 °C (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1979). Spawning in this study occurred at 19–20 °C (Little Colorado strain, May) and 21–22 °C (Colorado River strain, June).
- **Natural spawning raceways:** Two concrete raceways were recirculated and warmed to maintain temperatures believed necessary for spawning. Substrate consisted of boulders (30 to 40 cm) with a top layer of cobble (5 to 10 cm diameter).
- **Rearing conditions:** Raceways were pre-filled with inorganic (ammonium phosphate) and organic (commercial trout starter) fertilizers to produce a plankton bloom. Zooplankton, predominantly cladocerans, appeared within 10 days.
- **Water quality:** Dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, ammonia nitrogen, and pH were monitored weekly. Water quality was not a problem except for pH, which exceeded 8.5 on numerous occasions. Cold-water addition to compensate for evaporation lowered temperature by about 7 °C within 2 h.
- **Historical spawning:** Suttkus and Clemmer (1977) suggested spawning in June and July in Grand Canyon; Holden (1968) suggested May and June in Desolation Canyon. This study found lower-basin fish spawn earlier than upper-basin fish.
- **Hybridization:** The bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*) has been artificially hybridized with humpback chub at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery (Hamman 1981), posing a genetic threat in the wild.

## Key Findings

### Spawning results
- **Carp pituitary induction:** 16 females were used in the injected trial. Of these, 8 females were hand-stripped, and 9 females spawned naturally in a raceway (one female did not ovulate after three injections, as recorded in the text).
- **Natural spawning:** Nine females spawned (5–6 June 1981) within 24 h of receiving the first injection. About 30,000 eggs were spawned, all adhering to cobble. Egg diameter ranged from 2.6 to 2.9 mm (mean 2.8 mm).
- **Hand stripping:** Eight females ovulated 20 h after the first injection (14 June 1981). Egg diameter ranged 2.6–2.8 mm (mean 2.7 mm). Fecundity ranged 51–58 eggs/mL, total fecundity averaged 20,118 eggs per female (5,262 eggs/kg body weight).

- Newly hatched larvae weighed 0.8 to 0.9 mg and measured 5.7 to 7.4 mm total length (per the abstract).
- Hatching success was 88% at 21–22 °C and 83% at 12–13 °C (the latter suggested as a possible cause of population decline in cold natural waters).

### Culture performance
- Fry were transferred to raceways about 72 h after hatching: 12,381 (artificial) and 19,857 fingerlings were later recorded from the Willow Beach raceways.
- Fry doubled in length by 21–28 days.
- On day 56, 19,857 fingerlings (mean 36.9 mm, 588.5 mg) were removed from raceways A9 and A10, and 9,318 fingerlings (mean 47.5 mm, 884.7 mg) from raceways C1 and C2.
- Survival was 83% (natural spawn) and 75% (artificial spawn). Fish gained 5 to 7-fold in total length over 56 days.
- Growth rate: 40.5 mm and 584.5 mg gain over 56 days (approximately) in the recirculating system.

### Comparison of methods
- Natural spawning produced 23,910 swim-up fry and 19,857 fingerlings (83% survival).
- Artificial spawning produced 12,381 swim-up fry and 9,318 fingerlings (75% survival).

### Conclusions and recommendations
- Two propagation techniques were established: (1) injection with natural spawning, and (2) injection with hand stripping and artificial fertilization.
- The future of humpback chub may depend on hatchery spawning and culture followed by reintroduction of hatchery-reared progeny to increase wild abundance.
- The methods could be suitable for larger-scale propagation and restoration of this endangered species.
