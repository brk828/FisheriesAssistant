# Status of the Fish Fauna of Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This source contains two fisheries papers. The first assesses the present status of the fish fauna of the [[Lake Mead National Recreational Area]] on the [[Colorado River]] in Arizona and Nevada. It records 3 native and 11 exotic species, and reports that native fishes have declined as exotic species became established after [[Hoover Dam]] was built. The second paper reports age and growth of four fish species in a section of the [[Missouri River]] in Montana, based on summer 1948 and 1949 collections.

The first paper recommends further investigation before the native fauna disappears. It also advises against introducing new alien species and against further stocking of [[Largemouth Black Bass]] or forage fishes in [[Lake Mead]]. Continued [[Rainbow Trout]] planting below Hoover Dam is judged necessary to meet angler demand. The second paper finds that trout grow faster than suckers.

## Background & Description

**Lake Mead NRA fauna (Paper 1).** The recreational area covers nearly 3,000 square miles, administered by the [[National Park Service]]. It follows the Colorado River from Grand Canyon National Monument to [[Davis Dam]], a course the abstract gives as about 225 miles and the body text as nearly 200 miles. The climate is arid and semidesert, with hot, dry summers and cool, mild winters. Lake Mead, formed by Hoover Dam (completed 1936), originally stretched 115 navigable miles above the dam. Siltation has shortened its upstream limit to 85 miles. At its height it had over 500 miles of shoreline and a depth of 589 feet. [[Lake Mohave]], behind Davis Dam, was still filling when the paper was written. It was expected to be about 4 miles wide at most, with roughly 200 miles of shoreline and annual fluctuation of no more than 10 feet.

**Missouri River study (Paper 2).** The author, Joseph W. Kathrein of the Missouri Conservation Commission, studied a 35-mile reach between Holter Dam and Cascade, Montana. The upper 15 miles run through a narrow valley of meadowland. The river then passes through a canyon and an open farming valley. The gradient is low, at 3.1 feet of fall per mile. No earlier fisheries investigations of this reach were known to the author.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish adaptations and decline (Paper 1).** The Colorado River was originally swift, heavily silt-laden, and variable in turbidity and volume. Its native fishes evolved for that habitat, with reduced scale size and streamlined bodies. Dam regulation has produced clear, placid water above and below Hoover Dam. Following the dam's completion, native fish numbers declined while exotic species increased. Predation, competition with exotics, and changed river conditions are the causal factors cited. The [[Razorback Sucker]] is the exception. It appears to be holding its own and reproducing abundantly in Lake Mead.

**Projected pressure.** The 1946 comprehensive water report listed 134 proposed projects or project units for irrigation, power, flood and silt control, channel work, and municipal use in the Colorado River Basin. The author concluded that native fishes across the basin are likely to become scarce or locally extirpated.

**Bait-fish pathways.** Live bait for Lake Mead anglers comes from the Santa Clara River near St. George, Utah, a tributary of the Virgin River. Fishes collected from bait tanks at Las Vegas Wash and Boulder Beach included [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], Utah mountain sucker, [[Western Dace]], scaled spiny dace, a sculpin, and carp. The paper notes that escapees may establish populations and extend species ranges.

**Relict springs.** Near the Home Ranch Warm Springs, west of Glendale, Nevada (just outside the recreation area), the [[White River Springfish]] and the [[Moapa Leather Chub]] survive as relicts of the ancient White River fauna. The leather chub has not been collected anywhere else. Paper 1 recommends rigid protection for these two species and further survey of regional springs.

**Temperature and trout suitability.** Water in the upper reaches of Lake Mohave averages 56 °F and is suited to trout. Warmer lower reaches are expected to suit warm-water game fishes. Lake Mead is generally too warm for trout, so any trout caught there are strays from upstream plantings.

**Missouri River habitat (Paper 2).** Paper 2 gives no detailed water chemistry or temperature data. Its habitat context is limited to the reach description above.

## Key Findings

**Paper 1: Lake Mead NRA fish fauna**

- **Species count:** 3 native and 11 exotic species are known from the area, spanning six families. Six additional native fishes and two other alien forms may occur.
- **Native species:**
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): abundant in Lake Mead and less numerous in Lake Mohave. Individuals exceed 26 inches and 7 pounds. It has adapted better than other natives.
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus luctus*, as printed): rarely taken and becoming less numerous. A 27-pound fish was reported in 1945 and a 34-pound "salmon" was credited to H. Armitage.
  - [[Bonytail]] (*Gila robusta elegans*): rarely seen now, though Moffett reported large schools in 1941.
- **Possible native species not yet confirmed in the area:** [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), and [[Western Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*, reported from Las Vegas Creek). The paper also mentions a smallmouth bass and a goldfish observed or possibly established, though no Lake Mead captures were recorded.
- **Exotic species (11):**
  - Trout: [[Rainbow Trout]] is the leading sport fish below Hoover Dam. About a million fingerlings were stocked, and about 20,000 pounds were caught in 1946. The 1950 record was a fish of 18 pounds. [[Brown Trout]] has a single record, a 3-pound 6-ounce fish from Las Vegas Wash.
  - Suckers and minnows: [[Bonneville Mountain Sucker]] (one specimen, 1938) and [[Carp]], which is abundant in Lake Mead and below Hoover Dam and is commercially harvested by permit.
  - Catfish: [[Channel Catfish]] is an important game fish in Lake Mead. A 20-pound, 31-inch record was taken near Pierce Ferry in 1945. [[Black Bullhead]] has one record (1937).
  - Livebearer: [[Western Gambusia]] occurs in the Moapa River and in shoreline coves.
  - Sunfishes: [[Largemouth Black Bass]] is the principal sport fish. About 466,000 fingerlings were released from 1935 to 1940. A 13-pound 14-ounce fish won first place in the 1939 Field and Stream contest. [[Black Crappie]] is an important game fish, usually 1 to 2 pounds. [[Bluegill]] was stocked in 1937 with 25,000 fingerlings and is now abundant. [[Green Sunfish]] was probably introduced with the bluegill and is considered less desirable.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - A closed fishing season is probably unnecessary because exotic game fishes are firmly established.
  - No further alien species should be introduced.
  - Additional largemouth bass or forage fish stocking in Lake Mead is discouraged.
  - Warm-water stocking in lower Lake Mohave may be advisable after a survey.
  - Trout stocking below Hoover Dam must continue to meet angler demand.
  - More investigation of the Lake Mead sport fishery is needed.

**Paper 2: Growth of four Missouri River fishes (Montana)**

- **Sample sizes:** 478 rainbow trout, 12 brown trout, 87 longnose suckers, and 223 western white suckers were examined. The paper's condition-coefficient section cites 481 rainbow trout and 117 brown trout, so the sample sizes differ between sections.
- **Age ranges:** rainbow trout, age groups I to VII; brown trout, I to IX; longnose suckers, III to VIII; western white suckers, I to VIII.
- **Dominant age groups:** II for rainbow and brown trout, VI for longnose suckers, and III for western white suckers.
- **Peak growth:** second year for both trout species, and first and third years for longnose and western white suckers, respectively.
- **Growth comparison:** trout grow faster than suckers. Most trout fall in age groups I to III, while suckers are mainly in groups IV to VIII.
- **Condition coefficients:** 37.2 for rainbow trout (n = 481) and 34.6 for brown trout (n = 117).
