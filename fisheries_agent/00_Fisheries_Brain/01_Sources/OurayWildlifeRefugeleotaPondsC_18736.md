# Ouray Wildlife Refuge - Leota Ponds: Conceptual Alternatives for Hydraulic Connection with the Green River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This revised 1995 conceptual design report, prepared for the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) by FLO Engineering, Inc., evaluates ways to reconnect the Leota Ponds, a set of waterfowl refuge ponds near Ouray, Utah, to the [[Green River]]. The goal is to restore flooded [[Nursery Habitat]] for endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]], particularly the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]], during spring peak flows. The report is the first of three design phases, under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]. It presents six conceptual schemes, a preliminary cost comparison, and design issues to resolve in the next phase. Scheme 5, which uses two low head weirs to connect pond L-7 to the river, was the preferred option in early review.

## Background & Description

**Site and history.** The Leota Ponds are remnants of river bottomland that once flooded frequently. A constructed levee now separates them from the river. The [[Ouray Wildlife Refuge]] manages the area. Historically, spring peak flows may have flooded the bottomlands in as many as two of every three years. Upstream water development reduced flood frequency, and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] had the greatest single impact. With the existing levees, flooding may require discharge above 35,000 cfs, based on the estimated 1984 peak flow.

**Project scope.** The work included two site visits (April and late August 1994), review of 1963, 1974 and 1986 aerial photos and as-built plans, and a 1993 flyover video. A separate [[Hydraulic Feasibility Investigation]] was underway, including [[HEC-2]] modelling and [[Bureau of Reclamation]] topographic surveys. At revision, the first hydrographic data collection trip was complete, but no data analysis had been done.

**Design alternatives.** The schemes range from fully natural flooding to fully controlled inflow:
- **Scheme 1:** Natural flood inflow by removing riverside and interior levees for all four odd-numbered ponds (L-1, L-3, L-5, L-7).
- **Scheme 2:** Semi-natural inflow to pond L-7 only, through partial levee removal.
- **Scheme 3:** Semi-control of inflow to the four odd-numbered ponds, using low head weirs and gated culverts.
- **Scheme 4:** Semi-control of inflow to pond L-7, with one low head weir and gated outlet culverts.
- **Scheme 5:** Semi-controlled inflow to pond L-7 with two low head weirs and a fish harvest kettle.
- **Scheme 6:** Total control of inflow, using a gated inlet, settling pond, conveyance canal and three new ponds (about 10 acres each) fed from the [[Main Canal]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Recruitment limitation.** The report identifies loss of bottomland nursery habitat as a factor limiting recovery of endangered Colorado River fishes. This loss reduced the food base for larval fish (Stanford, 1993). Combined with predation by non-native fish (Modde and Irving, 1994), it has sharply reduced endangered fish populations. Flooded bottomlands offer cover, nutrients, warm water and low velocities. The report cites flooded bottomlands as many times more productive than the river for larval growth.

**Geomorphic change.** Channel incision of roughly 3 to 5 feet has occurred since 1963, according to field observations. Sand bars stabilised into islands and banks, which narrowed the active channel and reduced overbank flooding. Attributed causes include smaller peak discharges, lower sediment supply and vegetation encroachment (Andrews, 1986). Bank height is about 6 to 8 feet at 1,400 cfs. The river is entrenched in bedrock, so lateral migration toward the west is not possible except in an extreme flood.

**Fish and bird predation.** Uncontrolled inflow would admit non-native fish, which are expected to prey on larvae. Larger open water areas also increase [[Bird Predation]]. Shallow depths, diverse topography and emergent vegetation provide cover.

**Larval drift.** Historically, larvae drifted downstream during high flows and entered the bottomlands on the inside of a long bend. Secondary currents at Leota helped move drifting larvae toward the banks and into flooded areas. Designs try to reproduce this capture effect.

**Sediment.** Bed-load sand can be partly excluded at the inlet. Wash load cannot, and it will slowly reduce storage and form a muckier substrate. Sediment deposition near inlets could also keep them from working reliably.

## Key Findings

**Scheme comparison (qualitative).**
- Schemes 1 and 2 give the most natural, uncontrolled flooding and drainage, but offer no control over non-native fish or larval movement, and Scheme 1 requires extensive vegetation removal and regrading.
- Scheme 2 is described as relatively inexpensive compared with other schemes.
- Scheme 3 offers the largest potential nursery area (all four ponds) and exclusion of non-native fish from ponds L-5 and L-7, but requires extensive regrading along more than two miles of river.
- Scheme 4 offers controlled flooding of pond L-7 at a set water level, with fish collection facilities, but no non-native fish exclusion.
- Scheme 5 gives controlled flooding of pond L-7, a wide inlet and a drainage-based harvest system. It has no non-native exclusion and needs annual sediment dredging.
- Scheme 6 provides the most control: non-native fish exclusion, sediment exclusion and fish monitoring. Its disadvantages are no natural flooding, limited habitat restoration, a narrow inlet and high sediment-control cost.

**Preferred design.** Scheme 5 replaces about 60% of the pond L-7 levee (upstream 20%, downstream 40%) with a low head weir. The weir is overtopped at 10,000 to 15,000 cfs, creating an inlet about 3,000 ft wide. Pond L-7 was chosen because it lies on a relatively straight river reach, with minimal sediment and vegetation problems. The weir must also withstand the 100-year flood. Pond L-7 would be augmented from the [[Main Canal]] during low-flow seasons.

**Cost summary.** Costs are preliminary and comparative, and were estimated with conservative quantities. A 25% contingency applies to construction totals, which include engineering, surveying, data collection, construction inspection and permitting. Pond regrading assumed 0.5 ft of depth. Scheme 5's subtotal is $897,950. The full cost comparison (Table 1) is not legible in the source scan, so other scheme totals are not reported here. Annual monitoring and sediment maintenance are estimated separately, with sediment dredging expected every second or third year. Sediment costs were averaged over five years.

**Design issues to resolve.**
- *Channel incision* is the most critical issue. Incision of 1 to 2 feet may raise the flood discharge needed by several thousand cfs. Incision over 2 feet would induce further bank erosion and could make connection infeasible.
- *Freeboard:* The as-built plans show 2 feet of dike freeboard, which may need to rise for 100-year flows. Dike-stability review and possible overflow weirs between ponds are needed.
- *Drainage:* The main canal is overgrown and may need dredging. Outlet culverts have been undermined by scour.
- *Jetty:* A jetty or river constriction may raise water levels at the inlet, improving drift capture and reducing velocities.

**FWS comments on Scheme 5** (Vernal office):
- Avoid jetties in the active channel unless necessary.
- Prefer natural flooding and no inlet control.
- Provide no sediment exclusion, but prepare a sediment removal programme with a cost estimate.
- Minimise site disturbance.
- Add a larval fish congregation feature near the upstream inlet.
- Start with a small-scale project. Concern was raised about the fate of pond L-7 if the project fails, and about long-term sediment maintenance costs.

**Recommended tasks.**
1. Complete the [[Hydraulic Feasibility Investigation]]. Predict river and pond water surface elevations and choose a design discharge.
2. Analyse hydraulic feasibility, prepare structural designs, and assess channel scour with HEC-6 or bed scour equations.
3. Study historical sediment loads at the Jensen and Ouray gauging stations to evaluate long-term bed change.
4. Conduct a detailed site survey, including pond surface area versus stage.
5. Prepare detailed design, construction cost estimates, permits, and water rights resolution.

**Monitoring.** Annual monitoring would capture endangered and non-native fish during pond drainage, using fish kettles or collection facilities. Success would be judged by how well the ponds function as nursery habitat.
