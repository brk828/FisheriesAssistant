# Desert Pupfish Recovery Action Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This technical draft recovery action plan, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region 2 (June 1990) by Paul C. Marsh and Donald W. Sada, addresses the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*). The species is represented by two subspecies: the [[Colorado River Pupfish]] (*C. m. macularius*) and the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*C. m. eremus*), plus an undiagnosed form in the Rio Sonoyta, Sonora.

Natural Colorado River populations are extirpated from Arizona and restricted to two natural localities in California. They also occupy the Colorado River delta in Mexico. The Quitobaquito form persists in a single semi-natural spring and pool at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. Transplanted populations vary in size, security, duration, and genetic purity. Both natural and transplanted populations are threatened by habitat loss and interactions with non-native fishes.

**Actions needed:** Protecting extant populations requires land acquisition, secure water supplies, and eradication of non-native fishes where present, while preserving the local pupfish and ecosystem. Habitats must be shielded from non-native invasion. Genetic integrity must be maintained, and transplanted populations of doubtful purity must be destroyed. Re-establishment, refugium creation, genetic exchange protocols, monitoring, and public education are also required.

**Timeline and cost:** Recovery actions are expected to take five years to plan and implement, with downlisting eligibility about ten years later (approximately 2005). Cost figures are not legible in the source (they appear as placeholders, "#xxx"), so no total can be reported.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The species was described by Baird and Girard (1853) from the San Pedro River, Arizona. [[Cyprinodon Macularius Californiensis]] and *Lucania browni* are no longer recognized as valid, having been synonymized with *C. m. macularius*. An allozyme study by Turner (1983) of six populations found mean heterozygosity within the range reported for the euryhaline killifish *Aphanius dispar*, along with differences among areas and low inter-population differentiation.

**Morphology.** Adult males are thickened or laterally compressed, with a superior, highly protractile mouth and tricuspid jaw teeth. Females and juveniles are silvery with narrow vertical dark bars. Breeding males have yellow to orange-red caudal fins and iridescent light-to-sky-blue bodies. The Quitobaquito form differs in male head and body dimensions, reduced pelvic fins, and a more posterior dorsal fin origin.

**Life history.**
- **Maturity:** Individuals may mature at six weeks at 1.5 cm total length, but most breed in their second summer, at about 7.5 cm.
- **Breeding:** Males defend territories of 1 to 2 m² in water less than one meter deep. Spawning is a consort-pair behavior with a single relatively large egg (ca. 2 mm) per spawning act. Females may lay 50 to more than 800 eggs per season in the laboratory. There is no directed parental care.
- **Incubation:** About 10 days at 20 °C.
- **Growth:** Quitobaquito pupfish averaged 29.4 mm at age 1, 40.2 mm at age 2, and 48 mm at age 3 (June data).
- **Lifespan:** Highly variable, from under one year to up to three years at Quitobaquito.
- **Diet:** Opportunistic omnivores eating algae, plants, invertebrates, and detritus. Adults also eat ostracods, copepods, insects, and mosquito larvae. Individuals excavate soft-bottom "pits" to forage, and these are defended.

**Physiological tolerances.** Pupfish tolerate water temperatures up to 45 °C, dissolved oxygen of 0.1 to 0.4 mg/L, and salinity twice that of seawater. They also survive abrupt changes in salinity and temperature that are lethal to most fishes. These tolerances are unusual among freshwater fishes.

**Conservation status.** The species is listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1984), the [[International Union for Conservation of Nature]] (Miller 1979), Arizona (1988), and California (1980).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic range and abundance.** Historically, the species occupied the Gila River basin below about 1,500 m in Arizona and Sonora, the lower [[Colorado River]] and its delta, the [[Rio Sonoyta]], and the endorheic Laguna Salada basin. In California, it lived in springs and slow streams of the [[Salton Sink]]. After the Salton Sink flooded in the early 1900s, pupfish colonized the [[Salton Sea]], where large populations persisted until declines began in the early 1960s.

**Present distribution.**
- **Arizona:** Natural Colorado River-form populations are extirpated. At least 11 transplant locations held pupfish in January 1990, with some exceeding 1,000 individuals. The Quitobaquito Spring population fluctuates between about 5,000 and 10,000 individuals under normal conditions. The 0.22 ha pond supports this population.
- **California:** Wild populations are restricted to [[San Felipe Creek]] with its wetland [[San Sebastian Marsh]], and to upper [[Salt Creek]] in Riverside County.
- **Mexico:** The Rio Sonoyta form persists in several marshes near El Doctor and in [[Santa Clara Slough]]. Colorado-form populations occur on the Colorado delta, in Laguna Salada, and near the [[Cerro Prieto Geothermal Field]].

**Co-occurring fishes.** Harsh historic habitats often excluded other fishes, so pupfish lived alone. The [[Sonoran Topminnow]] was a common associate in cienegas and springs. At Quitobaquito, the [[Longfin Dace]] was the only other native fish. Other species, including Gila chub, speckled dace, desert sucker, and several large-river fishes, generally occupied deeper water and had little interaction with pupfish.

**Causes of decline.**
- **Habitat loss and modification:** Groundwater pumping, channel erosion, impoundment, diversion, and livestock grazing.
- **Non-native fishes:** These are the greatest threat. [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), sailfin molly, and cichlids are especially damaging in shallow habitats. Largemouth bass predation caused the extinction of Monkey Spring pupfish. At Quitobaquito, unauthorized stocking of [[Golden Shiner]] in 1968 or 1969 threatened the population.
- **Pollution:** Agricultural pesticide drift is implicated in the Quitobaquito decline. Mercury was detected in tilapia and clams near Cerro Prieto, with a maximum of 0.14 µg/g dry weight in fish. Effects on pupfish were not determined.

**Transplant outcomes.** Of 20 populations whose failure was documented in 1989, eight were lost to desiccation, two to invading exotic fishes, one was renovated, and nine failed for unknown reasons.

## Key Findings

**1. Recovery objective and downlisting.** The plan aims to eliminate threats and establish pupfish in secure habitats. Downlisting from endangered to threatened is justified once these criteria are met:
1. Natural populations in the US and Mexico are secure.
2. At least five populations of the Colorado River form are established in each of the lower Gila, San Pedro, Santa Cruz, and Salt drainages, the Rio Sonoyta, and the Colorado delta. Three populations are needed in California for each genetic stock (Salt Creek, San Felipe Creek, Salton Sea).
3. At least three viable Quitobaquito populations are established in refugia.
4. A genetic exchange plan is implemented.
5. A monitoring plan is implemented.
6. Populations persist for at least ten years without human intervention.

A viable population is defined as at least 500 overwintering adults (or existing numbers, whichever is greater), with a normal sex ratio and in-situ recruitment. Delisting criteria cannot yet be quantified.

**2. Protection of natural populations.** Quitobaquito lies entirely within federal land. California habitats are a mosaic of private and federal ownership. Most Mexican habitat is state, private, or local [[Ejido]] land. Acquisition, water-rights protection, and eradication of non-native fishes (followed by barriers and regulation) are required. The [[Pinacate Reserve]] expansion is proposed as a key Mexican measure.

**3. Re-establishment.** Five suitable sites must be found within each Colorado River-form drainage, and three within each California stock. The San Pedro River (BLM Riparian Preserve, Cochise County) is a priority because it is the type locality. Stocks should be spread across diverse habitat types (spring, cienega, stream, and river margin) rather than concentrated in one.

**4. Refugia for Quitobaquito pupfish.** At least three refugia are required, located close to the native range, spatially separated, and stocked directly from Quitobaquito Spring with at least 500 fish each at roughly a 1:1 sex ratio.

**5. Genetic exchange.** A [[Biochemical Genetics Study]] must determine stock uniqueness, natural heterozygosity, affinities among stocks, and exchange requirements. It must also produce a protocol specifying stocks, numbers, and timing. The protocol will use quantitative modelling and include mitochondrial DNA analysis. Edds and Echelle (1989) show that captive populations can lose rare alleles, so transplant genetics must be managed carefully. Transplants of questionable purity must be destroyed.

**6. Monitoring.** Populations are to be monitored twice yearly, before spring spawning (adult abundance after winter) and in late summer-early autumn (reproduction and recruitment). Genetic monitoring is to occur at five-year intervals. About 50 fish per population per visit should be preserved for future analysis, and data archived in a central repository.

**7. Research priorities.** Research must address habitat criteria, the biological factors controlling population persistence, the niche overlap of non-native species, and demographic parameters (r, Ne, life tables), along with extinction probability over the next century and millennium.

**8. Education and enforcement.** A public education program (displays, videotapes, pamphlets, seminars, signage) is needed. Resource managers and decision-makers must also be reminded of their duties under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**9. Coordination.** Recovery depends on coordination among US federal and state agencies, [[Centro Ecologico de Sonora]], and Mexican federal agencies (SARH, SEDUE). The [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]], led by W.L. Minckley, endorses a hierarchical approach, previously developed for the Sonoran topminnow, that protects wild populations first and then builds refugium populations for genetic exchange.
