## Executive Summary

This 1973 study by Daniel W. Coble investigated two factors hypothesized to influence prey vulnerability to [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) predation: (1) the **physical appearance** of prey and (2) the **satiation state** of the predator. Using [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) variants (tail shape and color) in tank experiments, and four prey species (carp, fathead minnow, green sunfish, bluegill) in pool experiments with pike subjected to varying food deprivation periods (0, 15-17, and 29 days), the study found that **neither physical appearance of prey nor satiation level of the predator significantly affected prey selection**. Instead, pike consistently preferred carp and fathead minnow over the centrarchids (green sunfish and bluegill), corroborating earlier findings by [[Mauck and Coble 1971]] that species-specific traits (likely behavior, not appearance) drive selective predation.

## Background & Description

### Predator (Northern Pike)
- Length range: 343–415 mm across experiments.
- Feeding modality: primarily **visual predator** beyond "leaping distance" (~10 cm), consistent with findings from [[Hoogland et al. 1956]].

### Prey Morphotypes Tested (Appearance Experiments)
| Trait Varied | Morphotypes | Species |
|---|---|---|
| Tail form | Fantail vs. Regular-tail | *Carassius auratus* |
| Color/Brightness | Gold, Natural, White (with orange flecks) | *Carassius auratus* |

- Prey fish size ~75 mm (goldfish, appearance experiments) and ~48 mm (four-species satiation experiments).
- Note: ~50% of "green sunfish" used were likely **hybrids** with bluegill, though phenotypically resembling green sunfish.

### Size-Selective Tendency
- Pike showed a tendency to consume **smaller goldfish** preferentially; mean lengths of surviving goldfish increased significantly over the course of experiments (t = -7.974, df=11, Exp.1; t = -2.785, df=13, Exp. 2&3).
- Natural-colored goldfish in Experiment 3 were significantly smaller than gold/white morphs (t = -4.196, df=58), potentially confounding color-based vulnerability results.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predator role**: Northern pike function as visually-oriented ambush predators in freshwater systems, relying on sight-based prey detection and discrimination rather than chemosensory or tactile cues at distance.
- **Prey vulnerability hierarchy**: A clear and consistent preference hierarchy was observed:
  - **High vulnerability**: [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)
  - **Low vulnerability**: [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- This selectivity pattern held true **regardless of predator satiation state**, suggesting an innate or behavior-driven preference rather than an availability-driven or hunger-driven mechanism.
- The study supports the framework proposed by [[Mauck and Coble 1971]] distinguishing prey **vulnerability** as a function of predator **preference** (innate inclination, satiation, conditioning) and prey **availability** (appearance, behavior, abundance, distribution, armament).
- Behavioral cues (manner of movement) are implicated as more critical to pike prey discrimination than static physical appearance, referencing stickleback avoidance conditioning in [[Hoogland et al. 1956]].
- Contrasts noted with [[Skipjack Tuna]] (*Euthynnus pelamis*) behavior described by [[Nakamura 1962]], where satiation influenced *size* selection of prey (whole vs. cut pieces) — a dynamic not observed in pike species-selection.

## Key Findings

1. **Tail Form Has No Effect**: Fantail and regular-tail goldfish were consumed at identical rates (29 of 90 each), indicating tail morphology does not influence pike predation risk.
2. **Color Has No Significant Effect**: 
   - Gold vs. Natural color goldfish: 19 vs. 22 eaten (χ² = 0.645, df=1) — not significant.
   - Gold vs. Natural vs. White (three-way): χ² = 3.501, df=2 — not significant, though natural color was eaten somewhat more (likely confounded by smaller size).
3. **Satiation Does Not Affect Species Selection**: Across food deprivation periods of 0 days (satiated), 15–17 days, and 29 days, pike consistently selected carp and fathead minnow over green sunfish and bluegill (Table 4 data).
4. **Species Identity Overrides Hunger State**: Even food-deprived (starved) pike for up to 4 weeks did not resort to eating less-preferred centrarchid prey (green sunfish/bluegill), which were nearly untouched across all trials.
5. **Size Effects Minimal in Satiation Experiments**: Prey lengths were consistent within experiments (Table 5), ruling out size as a confound in the species-selection satiation experiments.
6. **Support for Prior Framework**: Results reinforce the [[Mauck and Coble 1971]] vulnerability model — pike prey choice is governed more by intrinsic species-associated behavioral traits than by external appearance or predator hunger state.
7. **Visual Predation Mechanism**: Findings align with the concept that pike rely on visual cues, particularly *movement/behavior* patterns, over static appearance features (form, color, brightness) when selecting prey.
