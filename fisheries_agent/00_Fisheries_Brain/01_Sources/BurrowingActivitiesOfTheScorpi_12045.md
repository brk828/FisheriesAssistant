# Burrowing Activities of the Scorpion Anuroctonus phaeodactylus (Wood)

#arachnology #terrestrial-ecology #scorpionida #vejovidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by Stanley C. Williams, published in the *Proceedings of the California Academy of Sciences*, describes the burrowing behavior of [[Anuroctonus Phaeodactylus]], a scorpion with a wide but disjunct distribution in North and Central America. Field work was conducted in [[San Diego County]], with supplementary laboratory observations. The species shows an obligate, highly specialized burrowing habit, lives in isolated demes, and performs a cleaning routine called [[Sponge Bathing]]. The author argues that burrowing shapes the species' morphology, life history and survival.

## Background & Description

The scorpion is described as successfully adapted to a broad area of North and Central America, with burrowing considered one of its key adaptations. The stated aim was to document burrowing so that comparisons with other species could be made in relation to behavior and ecology. Comparisons are drawn with [[Tarantula]] life histories.

**Burrow structure (Table 1, 25 burrows from Lakeside):**
- Burrows are simple, unbranched tunnels with an oval entrance inclined 30–35° from horizontal, a series of turns, and an enlarged [[Terminal Chamber]] about 2 cm above the tunnel end.
- Entrance width averaged 2.2 cm (range 1.2–3.6); entrance height averaged 2.0 cm (range 1.0–3.1).
- Vertical depth averaged 29 cm (range 19–37) in females and immatures, and 15 cm (range 13–19) in four adult males.
- Burrow length averaged 32 cm (range 18–42); terminal chamber length averaged 6.2 cm (range 3.6–8.0) and width 4.0 cm (range 2.0–6.2).
- Of the 25 burrows, 21 were occupied by females and immature males and 4 by mature males. Each burrow held one scorpion.

**Construction sequence:** [[Burrow Construction]] involves five steps: site search, soil loosening, soil transport, deposition of spoil into a [[Tumulus]], and shaping and packing the walls. Soil is loosened by the chelicerae and carried by the first walking legs. Tail manipulation packs the walls about every 5 minutes. Scorpions may work for 5–6 hours continuously, and an initial excavation reached 2.5 cm deep and 1.5 cm wide within 30 minutes.

**Morphology:** Compared with roving vejovids such as [[Hadrurus]], the species has reduced pectines, thick and powerful pedipalps used for prey capture and defense, a reduced metasoma and venom apparatus, and an unusually heavy body (2–3 g is common, which would be very large for a roving vejovid).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and habitat:** The species occurs where deep, well-packed sedimentary soils coincide with arid conditions. Records cited include Utah, southern California, Virginia, Colorado, Baja California and Guatemala. In San Diego County it was found in [[Chaparral]] at coastal, foothill and mountain sites (La Jolla, Balboa Park, Dulzura, Ramona, El Cajon, Lakeside and the Cuyamaca Mountains). Populations favor sloping hillsides with sparse cover and southeast exposure. Greatest abundance occurred where chaparral had been periodically burned. Burrows are rare in valley bottoms or level ground.

**Population structure:** Populations form scattered, relatively dense colonies, or [[Deme]]s. Gene flow between demes is thought to occur mainly through the movements of a few adult males.

**Limiting factors:** Water drainage is a key constraint because the species is never found in lower drainage areas, and burrow entrances shed surface water. Soil texture and packing determine where permanent burrows can be built. Density is highest where vegetation is sparse and leaf litter is minimal.

**Burrow function:** The author identifies four functions: a trap for prey (the scorpion waits inside for arthropods that enter to escape the sun), protection from predators, protection from unfavorable weather, and [[Thermal Buffering]] of the environment. Living about 1 foot below the surface reduces daily and seasonal temperature swings, and the author states this eliminates the need for hibernation.

**Associated fauna and predators:** Spiders, [[Jerusalem Cricket]]s, isopods, beetle larvae and collembolans were found in burrows, mostly in the nest region. Only four predators were observed: [[Desert Shrew]] (*Notiosorex crawfordii*), [[Jerusalem Cricket]] (*Stenopelmatus* sp.), [[Eleodes Grandicollis]] darkling beetles, and owls. Burrows protect against *Eleodes* and shrews, which cannot fit the tunnel. *Stenopelmatus* can enter, with about a 50% chance of overcoming the scorpion. [[Barn Owl]] remains were almost entirely sexually mature males.

## Key Findings

- **Obligate burrowing:** Young begin burrowing once their cuticle hardens, burrowing continues throughout life except in adult males, and burrows are remarkably similar. The author concludes this is strongly shaped by natural selection.
- **Sex-linked activity:** A [[Pitfall Trapping]] study (61 traps, checked weekly, 4 April 1962 to 7 April 1963, Wildcat Canyon) caught 33 scorpions, all sexually mature males. No females or immature males were caught, although their burrows were common. Mature males are active migrants from April through October, probably linked to mating.
- **Permanent occupancy:** In a 1 square yard quadrat with three burrows, observed from October 1962 to February 1963 until the burrows were destroyed by hunters, occupancy was continuous. Traps around the quadrat caught no inhabitants. Abandoned burrows were described as very rare.
- **Early burrowing in the lab:** Thirteen young born 23 August 1965 molted to second instar on 9 September and first dug on 24 October, 61 days after birth.
- **Sponge bathing:** Cleaning of the pedipalps, chelicerae and first two pairs of walking legs occurs during and after burrowing and after drinking, sometimes lasting up to 1 hour, day and night. It uses a clear watery exudate, possibly from the mesenteron glands and possibly containing digestive enzymes. The author interprets it as keeping sensory-rich body regions clean.
- **Burrowing and tarantulas:** The author notes a striking similarity with [[Tarantula]] life histories, where females remain in burrows and only adult males roam during breeding.
- **Adaptation:** The author concludes that the reduced pectines, enlarged pedipalps, heavy body, permanent burrowing and sponge bathing are all adaptations to a burrowing existence.
