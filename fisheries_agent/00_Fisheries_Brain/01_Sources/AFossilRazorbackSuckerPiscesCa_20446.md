# Fossil Razorback Sucker from Southeastern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This manuscript by Geoffrey M. Hoetker and Kenneth W. Gobalet (California State University, Bakersfield) identifies a nearly complete fossil sucker, specimen SBCM A768-1 from the [[Anza-Borrego Desert]], as a [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The specimen is probably Pliocene in age, which would make it the oldest known *Xyrauchen* sample and extend the lineage back more than five million years. The authors base the identification on meristic counts, body and skull morphology, and measurements of the diagnostic predorsal keel, and they assign the fossil to *X. texanus* despite a few differences from modern material. The manuscript is a major article submitted for review under General Ichthyology.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen* is a monotypic genus within the subfamily Catostominae and tribe Catostomini. It is considered one of the more primitive catostomid lineages and is thought to be a sister group to *Chasmistes* and *Deltistes* (the Lost River sucker, *Deltistes luxatus*). All of these large-bodied genera occupy extensive river systems of the American West. The razorback sucker is one of the largest native freshwater fishes of the [[Colorado River]] system, approaching one meter in total length. Its name comes from a pronounced nuchal keel (predorsal hump) in adults.

The fossil was reported by Stewart and Roeder (1993) from the north side of the San Felipe Hills, near the western boundary of Imperial County and north of State Highway 78. It was attributed to the early Pliocene [[Diablo Formation]], part of the [[Palm Springs Group]]. The authors doubt that a more precise locality or age will become available. The skeleton lies within an elliptical, fine-grained arenitic concretion. The sandstone matrix suggests deltaic or floodplain deposition from the ancestral Colorado River, followed by northward and possibly westward drift to its present location.

The fossil was compared with prepared skeletons of modern lower Colorado River *X. texanus*, *D. luxatus*, *Chasmistes cujus*, and *Catostomus latipinnis*, a member of the sister group to the *Xyrauchen* clade.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hybridization.** Hybridization is known among many Colorado River fishes. *X. texanus* hybridizes with *C. latipinnis* and has the potential for introgression. Hybrids usually have a far less pronounced dorsal keel, or none at all. The authors argue that the fossil's exaggerated keel makes hybrid influence unlikely.

**Function of the predorsal hump.** The hump has been proposed as a stabilizer in the swift, deep waters of the Colorado River. Swimming tests on fishes with similar humps exist, but the effect of the hump on swimming performance has not been tested empirically. [[Minckley and Meffe]] (1987) reported that native fishes with pronounced humps survived flooding in Arizona and New Mexico, while introduced fishes lacking humps did not. The authors suggest that hump development may reflect an evolutionary response to past extreme hydrological conditions.

**Paleogeography and tectonics.** The authors note no fossil catostomids from the late Miocene [[Bidahochi Formation]] of Arizona. Late Miocene *Chasmistes* and *Deltistes* from the Chalk Hills Formation of Idaho (8.5 to 5.5 million years old) are morphologically modern, which suggests that the group diverged earlier. The fossil lies in a region affected by the southern extension of the [[San Andreas Fault]] zone. Its burial site may therefore differ from its discovery site. The [[Salton Sea Basin]] had intermittent connections with the lower Colorado River across deltaic deposits, and razorback suckers invaded during historic and prehistoric freshwater incursions. The authors also note that the fossil could come from the Pleistocene [[Ocotillo Formation]], which has yielded other *Xyrauchen* material.

## Key Findings

**Identification and age**
- SBCM A768-1 is identified as *Xyrauchen texanus*. The authors assign it to that species.
- The fossil is probably early Pliocene (reported as Pliocene, per Stewart and Roeder 1993). If correct, the *X. texanus* lineage extends back more than five million years and this is the oldest *Xyrauchen* sample known.
- Precise locality and age are considered unlikely to be refined, and the specimen may be Pleistocene.

**Size**
- Total length is estimated at 435 to 440 mm, and standard length at 365 mm. This is at the lower limit of adult size for *X. texanus*.

**Meristic counts**
- Dorsal fin rays and pterygiophores: 16. The typical count for *X. texanus* is 14 to 15, with variation of 13 to 16. The upper end of the range is noted.
- Pectoral fin rays: more than 9.
- Pelvic fin rays: at least 8.
- Anal pterygiophores: 7.
- Post-Weberian vertebrae: about 42, for a total of 46. This is one below the range in seven modern specimens (47 to 48 total). Comparison: *C. cujus* 46 to 47 total, one *D. luxatus* 49 total, and *C. latipinnis* 44 to 45 total.
- Deltistes, *Chasmistes*, and *C. latipinnis* typically have 12 to 13 dorsal rays.

**Predorsal keel (hump)**
- The keel is the most conspicuous feature of the fossil and is more pronounced than in comparative *X. texanus*.
- Hump measurements (Table 1, specimen SBCM A768-1): anterior height 37 mm, maximum height 47 mm, length 87 mm. As percentages of SL: 10.1%, 12.9%, and 23.8%.
- Maximum hump height is 12.9% of SL, compared with an average of 9.2% for comparative material.
- Comparative *X. texanus* specimens (ASU 14881, ASU 14882, ASU no. 435, CAS 66230) show maximum height percentages of 8.0% to 9.4% of SL.
- Six interneurals lie anterior to the dorsal fin. Three enlarged interneurals form the keel, whereas comparative *X. texanus* have 3 to 4 enlarged elements.

**Skull and jaws**
- The dentary shape and foramen position match *X. texanus* and differ from *C. latipinnis*. Greatest shaft length is 21 mm.
- The "doomed rectangle" dermethmoid shape resembles *Chasmistes*. The skull is dorsolaterally tilted, probably from compression.
- The preopercle character noted by Smith (1992a) is judged equivocal by the authors.

**Sex**
- The fossil may be male, based on the large predorsal hump and flared pelvic fins. The last anal fin ray is missing, so this cannot be confirmed.

**Materials examined**
- Comparative material includes about 20 catalogued specimens across four species, from the ASU, CAS, KWG, and UCMP collections.

**Conclusions**
- The specimen is a species of *Xyrauchen* and resembles *X. texanus*. Its differences include one fewer vertebra, one more dorsal ray than typical, and a more pronounced keel. The authors view these as evidence of greater prehistoric morphological variation than previously recognized.
