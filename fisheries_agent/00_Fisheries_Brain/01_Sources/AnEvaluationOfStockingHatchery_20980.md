# Stocking Hatchery-Reared Steelhead Rainbow Trout in the Sacramento River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game bulletin (Fish Bulletin No. 114, 1961) by Richard J. Hallock, William F. Van Woert, and Leo Shapovalov reports a study begun in 1952 to test whether releasing marked, yearling [[Hatchery-Reared Steelhead]] into the [[Sacramento River]] increases adult returns and the [[Sacramento River Steelhead Fishery]]. The study ran cooperatively with [[California Department of Fish and Game]], sportsmen's groups, and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] at [[Coleman National Fish Hatchery]]. Four annual plants of marked fish were evaluated, with field work ending in 1958.

Key results: about 2 percent of released yearlings returned as adults, with fish of eight per pound or larger returning at about 4 percent. Each adult cost roughly $3 to put in the run and about $8 to $10 to put in an angler's creel. The report concludes that hatchery stocking is a valid supplement to natural production.

## Background & Description

**Species and life history.** The study covered [[Steelhead Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii gairdnerii*), an anadromous form that spawns in fresh water and spends time at sea.

- **Age:** Of 100 scale-sampled wild fish, 70 spent two years in fresh water, 29 spent one year, and one spent three years. Age classes included 17 two-year-olds, 41 three-year-olds, 33 four-year-olds, six five-year-olds, two six-year-olds, and one seven-year-old.
- **Size:** Mean fork length was 18.1 inches (SD 3.4 inches), and average weight was about three pounds. The largest recorded fish weighed 154 pounds. Length distribution was typically bimodal, with modes at 15.5 and 20.5 inches.
- **Spawning:** Spawning runs from late December through April, with February usually peaking. Of 100 fish sampled in fall 1954, 83 spawned for the first time, 14 for the second, and two for the third.
- **Growth:** Most growth occurs in the first year at sea. Fish entering salt water after one or two years in fresh water were typically 7 to 9 inches long.

**Study design.** Adults were trapped at a wire fyke trap site near Fremont Weir, tagged with Petersen disk or tubing tags, and their population estimated by mark-recapture (Petersen and Schaefer methods). Yearlings were fin-clipped and released in four annual plants. Returns were tracked through trapping, tag returns, and creel censuses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Setting.** The [[Central Valley of California]] is roughly 400 miles long and 45 miles wide, drained by the [[Sacramento River]] and [[San Joaquin River]], which join in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]]. The upper Sacramento, above the Feather River, is the main steelhead water. [[Shasta Dam]] and [[Keswick Dam]] block anadromous migration and moderate flows. Flows at Red Bluff are usually under 11,000 cubic feet per second, and typically 5,000 to 7,000 in fall.

**Migration timing.** Adults enter the upper river from July through mid-March, with a single continuous run peaking near the end of September. Juveniles move seaward mainly in spring and during fall runoff, with a heavy yearling migration in spring.

**Competing and associated species.** Steelhead spawn on riffles with [[King Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*). Resident trout (mostly 14 to 20 inches) also occupy the upper river. Commercial gill netting for [[American Shad]] incidentally caught steelhead until 1957.

**Management context.** Before 1952 management relied on protective regulations, fishways, and screens, with no artificial stocking. A commercial salmon gill net fishery in the Delta was abolished by the State Legislature after the 1957 season.

## Key Findings

- **Survival:** The 663,240 yearlings released in the first four years produced 13,055 sea-run adult returns, about 2 percent. Fish of eight per pound or larger averaged a 4 percent return; fish at 10 to 26 per pound averaged just over 1 percent.
- **Release size and timing:** Larger yearlings and January to March releases gave the best returns. Releases during extreme high water appeared to produce fewer returns, though the evidence was not conclusive.
- **Release location:** Releases at Princeton, below the trout fishing area, produced more adult returns than upstream releases. Those adults also dispersed into tributaries, with 27 percent of the Battle Creek run and 2 percent of the Mill Creek run being hatchery fish in 1955-56.
- **Hatchery efficiency:** Each of 859 spawned females (plus males) yielded about 15 sea-run adult returns, versus about one adult per adult in natural reproduction.
- **Population:** The adult run averaged 20,542 fish (1953-54 to 1958-59), peaking near 28,000 in 1954-55 and 1955-56, then declining to about half that by 1958-59. Wild fish averaged about 18,000.
- **Growth:** Most growth occurs in the second year, adding about nine inches. Growth slows after the third year.
- **Costs:** A yearling cost an average of 6 cents. Each sea-run adult cost about $3 to put in the run, and about $8 to $10 to put in the creel, depending on the nonreturn assumption.
- **Tag returns and harvest:** Uncorrected tag returns showed a harvest of about 30 percent of the run, rising from 20.1 percent (1953-54) to 36.5 percent (1958-59). With a 20 percent tag nonreturn correction, the average harvest was 36.9 percent. About 70 percent of the catch occurred in October and November.
- **Sport fishery value:** Anglers spent about 4.1 hours per day, 21,597 angler days annually, and a minimum of about $390,000 per year (1954-1958). Anglers spent about $40 per fish landed.
- **Conclusions:** The authors found that stocking yearlings is a valid supplement to natural production, and that the sport fishery warrants periodic evaluation of management.
