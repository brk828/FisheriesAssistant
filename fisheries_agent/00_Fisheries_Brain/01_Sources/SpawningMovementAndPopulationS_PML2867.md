# Spawning, Movement and Population Structure of Flannelmouth Sucker in the Paria River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 University of Arizona M.S. thesis by Steven Joseph Weiss examines the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) in the [[Paria River]], an unstudied, sediment-laden tributary entering the [[Colorado River]] about 25 km below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The study documents spawning timing and habitat, confluence use, growth, longevity, population structure and recruitment. Spawning occurs in the lower 10 km of the Paria from mid-March to early April, in shallow water, over gravel and cobble. No juvenile fish above 329 mm were caught after 1981, and no young-of-year were found in 1993, suggesting that recruitment is limited and that adult-sized fish enter from downstream populations.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The flannelmouth sucker is the only endemic "big river" fish native to the Colorado River still commonly found throughout the [[Grand Canyon]]. Its range has been drastically reduced, and it is a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service candidate (Category 2) species.
- **Threats:** Hybridization with the introduced [[White Sucker]] in the upper basin, and with the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]], is documented. Dams and exotic fishes have severely altered the Colorado ecosystem.
- **Biology:** Eggs are non-galactic, demersal and adhesive, and are the largest of any catostomid in the Colorado Basin (3.8-3.9 mm). Diet consists largely of algae and detritus, with amphipods and midge larvae important in the Grand Canyon.
- **Study objectives:** (1) document spawning timing and location relative to flow, temperature, depth and substrate; (2) document confluence use by native fishes; (3) assess demographics and recruitment; (4) compare current flow and temperature regimes with historic conditions; (5) survey the upper Paria basin for native fishes and invertebrates (Appendix B).
- **Study area:** The Paria originates from springs at the base of cliffs in the [[Wasatch Formation]] of [[Bryce Canyon National Park]], Utah. It drains about 350 km² over roughly 89 km. Base flow is 3.5-35.3 cfs. The river is extremely turbid; mean suspended sediment at the mouth was 114,000 ppm, with flood peaks as high as 780,000 mg/l.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** Flash floods (flows above 50 cfs) are common in summer and were very common during the study. Mean daily flows exceeded 100 cfs on 21 days in 1992 and 43 days in 1993, with peak mean flows of 559 and 599 cfs respectively. Water temperatures ranged from 0 to 34 °C. Colorado River flows ranged from 5,000 to 20,000 cfs, with temperatures of 5-12 °C.
- **Confluence:** Colorado River flows strongly affected the size of the confluence area, which was inundated about 150 m up the Paria at high flows (16,000-20,000 cfs).
- **Other fishes:** [[Speckled Dace]] were found throughout the year, with CPUE varying widely between sampling events but little among reaches. Incidental catches included [[Bluehead Sucker]] (n = 3), [[Rainbow Trout]] (n = 11-24) and [[Common Carp]] (n = 1). Only speckled dace were found upstream of the rock slide near Bushhead Falls.
- **Invertebrates:** The basin survey recorded 12 orders and 54 families of aquatic invertebrates, with 58 organisms identified to genus and 17 to species. Taxa included mayflies ([[Baetidae]]), midges ([[Chironomidae]]), caddisflies, and beetles, distributed across 6 location areas.

## Key Findings

**Spawning and timing**
- Ripe flannelmouth were first caught on March 20, 1992, and 13 February 1993 for adult males entering the river. Spawning occurred from mid-March through early April. Peak spawning and CPUE occurred April 8-10, 1993.
- Spawning took place in water generally below 25 cm, over gravel and cobble, at mean column velocities of about 0.3-0.5 m/s. Mean depth at spawning sites was 15 cm.
- Spawning in 1992 ended abruptly before a flash flood. In 1993, spawning was more prolonged during relatively stable flows and peaked during receding flows.

**Size and sex**
- The mean total length of 1,053 fish caught was 495 mm. Females (mean 524 mm, n = 455) were larger than males (mean 470 mm, n = 522).
- Upstream spawners in 1993 (mean 476-478 mm, n = 246) were 53 mm larger than spawners in 1981 (mean 425 mm, n = 285), p < 0.001.
- Males outnumbered females about 2:1 above the confluence. The smallest male was 404 mm and the smallest female 442 mm in 1993.

**Confluence use**
- Mean CPUE in daylight was 0.55 fish/100 m²; CPUE after dark was 1.3 fish/100 m² (higher, p = 0.001 or similar). Seventy-three of 93 daylight seine hauls (78%) yielded no fish.
- The highest CPUE (50 fish/100 m²) occurred on April 10, 1993, at night, at a Colorado flow of 9,280 cfs.
- Adult flannelmouth were caught at the confluence throughout the year, and fish were seen in the deeper Colorado River channel when they were not caught in the Paria.

**Growth and longevity**
- Growth of adults was very slow, averaging about 7.3 mm/year (linear). Sub-adults grew exponentially (second-order polynomial, multiple r = 0.956).
- Based on recapture data, longevity may approach 30 years. Fish are considered long-lived (> 20 years).

**Recruitment**
- No young-of-year were found in 1993. In 1992, only 8 juveniles (22-45 mm) were caught.
- No sub-adults were caught in the Paria or the Glen Canyon reach after 1984. The last records of sub-adults were 186 mm and 262 mm TL in October 1984.
- Adult fish of 380-400 mm continue to enter the population each year, likely from downstream populations in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR).

**Recaptures and movement**
- 74 fish were recaptured as of the end of the study (cumulative recapture rate; 11-17% per trip over the last four trips).
- Fish tagged in the Paria were recaptured in the LCR, and one fish originally tagged in the Paria was recovered at Kanab Creek, 228 km downstream, from which fish were found to migrate upstream.

**Conservation implications**
- The Paria spawning run is considered the largest available for the species and may be important for its survival in Grand Canyon.
- Flash flooding may reduce spawning success, and the lower Paria's low structural complexity may make eggs and larvae vulnerable. Because of the species' long life span, failed recruitment in any single year may be of limited importance.
