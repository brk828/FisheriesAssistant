# Comments on the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological and Conference Opinion on Lower Colorado River Operations (LC-2000)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #ConservationBiology

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal expert commentary letter (25 July 1997) authored by Dr. Paul C. Marsh, an ichthyologist at Arizona State University, responding to a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) Biological and Conference Opinion (BO) on U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR) operations of the Lower Colorado River. The letter focuses exclusively on two federally listed endangered fish species — the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — critiquing the scientific and management adequacy of proposed Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives (RPA) and Reasonable and Prudent Measures (RPMs). While not a primary field study, the letter synthesizes substantial contemporary knowledge (as of 1997) regarding the biology, reproduction, habitat use, and — critically — the existential threat posed by non-native fishes to these two Colorado River natives.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide direct morphometric or anatomical descriptions of either species. However, indirect biological data relevant to species identity and life history are noted:

- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): A member of the minnow family (Cyprinidae); known to reproduce and recruit successfully under hatchery conditions (earthen ponds), but details of natural spawning/nursery habitat requirements remain essentially unknown.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): A member of the sucker family (Catostomidae); exhibits notable spawning plasticity, with confirmed reproduction across ecologically disparate settings — including reservoir environments (Lake Mohave) and free-flowing upper basin rivers — suggesting a generalist reproductive strategy relative to habitat structure.
- Swimming capability (a function of body size and water temperature) is explicitly identified as **unknown** for both species, limiting quantitative fish-passage and entrainment-loss modeling at dam intakes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat range**: Both species historically occupied the mainstream Lower Colorado River; today, both persist primarily in modified/artificial habitats — including hatchery ponds, off-channel impoundments, and reservoirs such as [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Mead]].
- **Non-native fish exclusion principle**: A central ecological finding repeatedly emphasized is that native [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] populations can complete full life cycles even in highly artificial or degraded habitats **provided non-native fishes are absent**. Conversely, it is stated as established fact that neither species can survive and complete life cycles in the presence of non-native fishes currently established in the Colorado River mainstream.
- **Recruitment uncertainty**: The letter disputes the BO's assumption of "successful recruitment" of razorback sucker in Lake Mead, arguing that alternative, equally plausible explanations exist for the presence of razorback suckers of varying size/age classes, and that this should not be treated as confirmed natural recruitment.
- **Canal entrainment as incidental habitat**: Razorback suckers are documented to become entrained into irrigation canals diverting water from the Lower Colorado River. Notably, the letter cites evidence/hypothesis that these canals may function as *de facto* recruitment habitat, potentially compensating for the lack of suitable recruitment opportunities in the open waters of the river itself — reframing entrainment as potentially neutral or even beneficial rather than strictly a mortality sink.
- **Dam-related mortality risk**: Fish loss through dams is governed by an interacting suite of variables — seasonal timing, diel timing, fish size/behavior, discharge rates, and spatial habitat use — meaning that a *potential* for loss (an intake velocity exceeding fish swimming capability) does not equate to demonstrated *actual* loss in the field.
- **Reclamation infeasibility**: The letter asserts there is "absolutely no potential" to reclaim the Lower Colorado River mainstream (or even portions thereof) from established non-native fish species, framing this as a foundational ecological constraint on management strategy.

## Key Findings

- **Data gaps prioritized over new studies**: The author argues a broad, sufficient scientific base already exists to manage/conserve native Colorado River fishes, and that additional "research funding" for further habitat-preference studies would be an irresponsible diversion of limited conservation resources, given that non-native species presence — not habitat quality per se — is the binding constraint on native fish persistence.
- **Recruitment provision deemed unrealistic**: The BO's long-term provision aiming to recreate "conditions that allowed for successful recruitment" in Lake Mohave is criticized as unachievable, since such historical conditions are unknowable and, even if known, could not plausibly be reconstructed in the present-day reservoir.
- **Stranding risk unquantifiable**: Because bonytail spawning/nursery habitat requirements are almost entirely undocumented in the wild, and razorback sucker spawning is highly habitat-plastic, the letter concludes that meaningful quantification of "stranding potential" in the Lower Colorado River is not scientifically feasible with current knowledge.
- **Dam-loss quantification requires unavailable baseline data**: Computing potential fish loss at dams requires species/size/temperature-specific swimming performance data that does not currently exist for either species; acquiring it would itself require a costly, narrowly useful basic research program.
- **Entrainment mortality significance unproven**: While entrainment of razorback sucker into diversion canals is documented, no evidence demonstrates it constitutes a significant mortality factor — and canal habitats may partially substitute for lost recruitment habitat in the regulated river.
- **Management recommendation — habitat impoundment design**: The author recommends numerous smaller, independently manageable off-channel water bodies over a few large impoundments, to (1) improve manageability, (2) reduce risk from catastrophic loss of any single fish community, and (3) allow a mosaic of ecological successional states mimicking pre-development river conditions.
- **Policy critique**: The letter repeatedly warns against structurally binding the RPA's long-term fish conservation provisions to the (at the time) unformalized [[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]] (MSCP), citing uncertain institutional commitment and unresolved jurisdictional questions among participating entities.
- **Cross-species disparity concern**: The author flags a substantive concern that the BO devoted disproportionately less analytical depth to the two endangered fish species compared to the Southwestern Willow Flycatcher, warning this could result in unequal regulatory protection despite comparable or greater biological jeopardy facing the fishes.
