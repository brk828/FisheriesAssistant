# Translocation as a Tool for Conserving Imperiled Fishes: Experiences in Western United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University, *Biological Conservation* 72, 1995) examines translocation, the movement of imperiled fishes among natural sites or from nature to propagation facilities and back, as a conservation tool in the western United States. The author argues that translocation is shaped as much by legal and political constraints as by biology. Key biological requirements are suitable and secure sites, genetically appropriate and disease-free stock, and adequate numbers. Success is hard to define and monitoring is often inadequate. Documentation lags far behind practice, and the author calls for peer-reviewed publication. Translocation is presented as an emergency, stopgap measure, with habitat-based management as the long-term goal.

## Background & Description

The indigenous freshwater fauna of western North America is declining rapidly. At least 20 species have gone extinct in the past century, and about 120 of the remaining 200 taxa are of special concern. Most imperiled taxa belong to three groups: cyprinoids (minnows and suckers), salmonids, and cyprinodontoids (livebearers, killifishes and allies). Causes include:

- Aridity-driven faunal reduction over geologic time, intensified by human water development.
- Dam construction (about 50 federal dams by 1930, roughly 1000 more from 1930 to 1980).
- Groundwater pumping, which lowered water tables and caused spring failures.
- Non-native species, including [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Sheepshead Minnow]], that invade or replace natives.

Conservation legislation, especially the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), provided legal protection from the 1960s onward. The paper defines key terms. Translocation and transplantation are synonyms. Reintroductions occur inside a species' native range, and introductions occur outside it. Preservation prevents extinction. True conservation also maintains evolutionary potential. The paper's guidelines (Table 1) follow [[Williams et al. 1988]] and cover site selection, stock quality, and post-stocking monitoring and documentation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site selection and reserve design.** Most planned transplants stay within native ranges. Illicit or accidental out-of-range introductions have caused invasions. Examples include non-native cyprinids that displaced native minnows west of the Continental Divide, and sheepshead minnows that hybridized extensively with [[Pecos Pupfish]]. Following [[Moyle and Sato 1991]], reserves should contain necessary habitat, support self-sustaining populations, be replicated, be protected from external effects, and be distant enough from replicates to avoid shared disasters. In practice only the first three criteria are consistently met. Small, short-lived species can usually be managed locally. Large, long-lived species such as the [[Razorback Sucker]] need large watersheds.

**Natural refugia and dispersal.** Permanent springs served as refugia during drought. Ephemeral marshes and streams allowed periodic connectivity and gene flow. Dams, diversions and groundwater mining now isolate populations, so translocation must replace natural dispersal. Impermanent sites remain useful transplant sites as long as gene flow is maintained.

**Hybridization and disturbance.** Hybridization between native and non-native taxa is a major problem. Western fishes evolved in allopatry and may lack "evolutionary experience" with close relatives. Site preparation can also cause harm. Eradication of native fishes, and habitat modification for [[Owens Pupfish]] reintroductions, have harmed endemic snails and fishes. Removing native animals before chemical treatment and then returning them has been effective.

**Parasites and disease.** Hatchery crowding can increase parasite and disease loads. Quarantine is recommended before stocking outside native range. Some stockings have been blocked by non-native parasite infestations.

## Key Findings

**Legal and political constraints**
- The 1982 ESA amendment created "experimental, essential" and "experimental, non-essential" categories. Non-essential populations and their habitats lose most ESA protection.
- Authorization for either experimental category can take 2 or more years. Stocking of [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Gila Topminnow]] on federal lands was delayed more than 5 years.
- Translocations may be used to preempt formal listing, and some stockings are emergency actions taken for expedience rather than long-term security.

**Stock selection and genetics**
- Where data are lacking, the author suggests translocating the rarest stock, the stock nearest the new site, or the most common stock.
- Source populations with the greatest genetic variability are preferred. Mixing stocks is generally ill-advised because co-adapted gene complexes may be disrupted.
- Effective population size, not the census count, should set the number translocated. Hundreds of [[Razorback Sucker]] may be needed, while a few pregnant mosquitofish can suffice.
- Allendorf and Leary (1988) suggested that 1.0% foreign genes is difficult to detect and unlikely to alter biological characteristics. Even highly introgressed stocks are irreplaceable if they are all that remains of a taxon.

**Captive propagation**
- Freshwater fishes breed readily in captivity and have low production and holding costs. Of 39 ESA recovery plans for listed fishes, 32 recommend some translocation, mostly with intervening propagation.
- The USFWS Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center (Dexter NFH), New Mexico, was dedicated to imperiled fishes in the 1970s. Between 1974 and 1989 it housed 24 taxa: 8 for propagation and translocation, 17 mainly as insurance, and 7 solely for research.
- Short-lived fishes self-perpetuate in ponds. Long-lived species such as razorback suckers require artificial spawning. A single 55-cm female may produce about 100,000 ova.
- Between 30 and 200 individuals are typically used to establish populations at Dexter NFH. Allendorf and Ryman (1987) considered 25 reproducing individuals of each sex an absolute minimum for salmonids.

**Taxonomy and documentation**
- Taxonomic uncertainty has hindered Colorado River chub recovery for almost 30 years.
- Voucher specimens or photographic records are required. Unreported localities can falsify original distributions.
- Translocated populations can change in phenotype and genotype. Devils Hole pupfish grew larger and more brightly colored in an artificial refuge.
- Documentation lags behind practice. About half of [[Hendrickson and Brooks 1991]] data on 40 taxa came from personal communications, agency files and unpublished reports. Of about 350 razorback sucker citations, roughly 50% were "gray" literature.

**Monitoring and failure**
- Monitoring should determine survival, establishment, and population growth. Reasons for failure can only be identified if monitoring is frequent enough to bracket the time of disappearance.
- One topminnow population persisted 10 years before a flood of expected once-per-century magnitude eliminated it. Restocking succeeded and the population has persisted for 14 years since.
- Survival of only 0.1% of about 15 million razorback suckers stocked in the lower Colorado River basin (1981–1989) would yield about 15,000 fish across roughly 20,550 ha, less than one fish per hectare.

**Success rates (Hendrickson and Brooks 1991)**
- More than 400 transplants of 40 short-lived taxa were reviewed. An overall success rate of 26.3% was computed for 407 sites.
- A single Gila topminnow reintroduction program at 208 sites achieved 18.3%. Excluding it, 199 sites succeeded 34.7% of the time.
- Of the taxa, 14 established at all sites stocked, 17 achieved intermediate success, and 9 failed everywhere.
- The author concludes that success cannot yet be predicted accurately and that roughly 25–35% multi-taxon success may be a realistic multigeneration expectation.

**Case histories**
- *[[Yaqui Chub]]*: About 200 chubs were moved in 1969 from a drying spring to an isolated creek. By 1976 this was the only known U.S. population. After 225 chubs were moved to Dexter NFH in 1976, and the creek resumed flow in 1977, translocations from 1980 onward (lots of about 50 to many thousands) all produced established populations.
- *[[Big Bend Gambusia]]*: Described from a spring that was dry in 1954. Mosquitofish invasions, poisoning, flooding and a 1975 cold event repeatedly disrupted populations. By 1985 the species had replaced mosquitofish. It now has full protection in a National Park, with priority over human drinking water during drought.
- *Failures*: [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]] and [[Amistad Gambusia]] went extinct despite efforts, through inadequate attention and human error.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Translocation should be an emergency, stopgap measure. Habitat-based management is the long-term goal.
- Premature reporting of success can harm programs, and delisting based on short-term success is risky.
- Wild and hatchery fish should be used in parallel. Rare taxa should be studied early so that ex situ maintenance is possible when needed.
- Reintroduction should follow removal of the original extirpating cause, or it will expose fish to the same threat again.
- Results must be published in the peer-reviewed literature so that future conservationists do not repeat past errors.
