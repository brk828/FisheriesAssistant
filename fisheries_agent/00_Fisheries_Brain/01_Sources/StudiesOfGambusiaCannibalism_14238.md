# Studies of Gambusia Cannibalism

## Executive Summary
This study by Clark Hubbs experimentally tests the widely cited claim that [[Gambusia]] adults are highly cannibalistic on their own young. Using controlled aquarium trials across dozens of Texas (and one Arizona) populations of multiple *Gambusia* species and related [[Poeciliidae|poeciliids]], the author finds that cannibalism is real but highly variable — contingent on predator sex, species identity, source population, and offspring birth weight. The prevailing literature is judged to overstate the generality of cannibalism, as it derives from insufficient sampling of the substantial population-level and species-level variation documented here. Key results: overall young survival under adult exposure is ~48%; female predators are markedly more voracious than males (33% vs. 72% survival of young, respectively); [[Gambusia Nobilis]] is far more predaceous than [[Gambusia Affinis]]; and predation rate correlates with birth weight at the species level but not at the population level.

## Background & Description
- **Study taxa**: *Gambusia affinis*, *Gambusia nobilis*, *Gambusia geiseri*, *Gambusia heterochir*, *Gambusia gaigei*, *Gambusia speciosa*, and *Poecilia* sp. — all live-bearing members of family [[Poeciliidae]].
- **Newborn size metric**: Birth weight (measured in micrograms after drying newborns ≥2 days) used as a key morphological/life-history trait varying by species and population.
  - Species means (µg): *G. affinis* ≈ 1.45 (range 0.80–2.32 across populations), *G. speciosa* 1.60, *G. geiseri* ≈ 1.8–2.2, *G. nobilis* 3.53–4.27, *G. heterochir* 3.20, *G. gaigei* 2.99, *Poecilia* sp. 2.32.
  - Notable extremes within *G. affinis*: smallest at Clymer Meadow (0.85 µg) and Cut 'n' Shoot (0.80 µg); largest at San Marcos (2.32 µg) and Heart of the Hills (2.23 µg).
- **Sample scale**: Experiments used ~26,923 young total across trials; sampling per locality ranged from as few as 22 individuals (Santa Rosa Spring) to >2,300 (Cow Creek); large-bodied species like *G. geiseri* and *G. nobilis* sampled in the low hundreds to low thousands.
- No morphological trait other than birth weight is reported (this is a behavioral/demographic study, not a detailed anatomical description).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat range**: Populations sampled span spring-fed refugia, creeks, rivers, and reservoirs across the Rio Grande, Pecos River, Guadalupe River, and Colorado River (Texas) drainages, plus one Arizona site (Bog Hole, Santa Cruz Co.) — illustrating the broad geographic and hydrological range of [[Gambusia]] species.
- **Specialized/endangered habitats**: Several species are restricted to single spring systems or refugia:
  - *G. nobilis* — Balmorhea and Diamond Y Refugium (Pecos Co.)
  - *G. heterochir* — Clear Creek (Menard Co.)
  - *G. gaigei* — Big Bend Refugium (Brewster Co.)
  - *G. geiseri* — multiple spring sites (Balmorhea, Diamond Y, Comal Springs, San Marcos, etc.)
  - *G. speciosa* — Devil's River Natural Area
  These narrow-range, spring-endemic species are relevant to conservation concerns for the broader [[Poeciliidae]] and desert-spring fish faunas.
- **Predator–prey dynamics within a single genus**: The study frames intraspecific and interspecific cannibalism as an ecological interaction shaping recruitment and possibly population regulation in [[Live-bearing Fish]] communities.
- **Sexual dimorphism in ecological role**: Female *Gambusia* function as the dominant intraspecific predator of newborns, a behaviorally-mediated ecological asymmetry with implications for population sex-ratio-dependent recruitment dynamics.
- **Population-level variability**: Predation intensity and birth weight are not correlated with geography or obvious environmental gradients, suggesting local evolutionary or plastic divergence in cannibalistic tendency across otherwise similar habitats (e.g., Lazy Pond vs. Pecos River sites, only ~1 km apart, differ notably in predation rate).

## Key Findings

**Experimental Design**
- Standard trial: ~10 newborns + 1 adult in a 49 × 15 × 17 cm aquarium, initially separated by a glass partition removed after 1 day; survivors counted after 30 additional days (31-day total).
- Trials where the adult predator was absent at the end (~4% of 3,062 experiments) were excluded as confounded by background/natural mortality rather than predation.

**Q1 – Do adults prey on young?**
- Yes, but incompletely: overall survival of young exposed to adult *Gambusia* predators across 26,923 individuals was **48%** (13,017 survived).

**Q2 – Does predator sex matter?**
- Yes, strongly: female predators reduced survival to **33%** (4,682/14,130), while male predators allowed **72%** survival (9,224/12,793).
- Across paired same-population comparisons, female predators showed higher chi-square-supported cannibalism far more often (sum χ² = 5,916), with the largest chi-square categories (50+) all favoring higher mortality under female predation.

**Q3 – Does predation rate vary by species?**
- Yes: *[[Gambusia Nobilis]]* is markedly more predaceous than *[[Gambusia Affinis]]*, per survivorship tables for both male and female predators (Tables 4–5).

**Q4 – Does predation rate vary by population?**
- Yes, substantially, within *G. affinis*:
  - Highest cannibalism observed with adults from **Fairy** (Hamilton Co.).
  - Lowest ("nicest") cannibalism observed with adults from **Big Bend National Park** (survivorship ~92%).
  - Nearby sites (Lazy Pond and Pecos River, ~1 km apart) differ in predation rate, underscoring fine-scale population divergence.
  - *G. gaigei* young show higher survival specifically against female predators compared to young of the other three species tested — an anomalous pattern.

**Q5 – Does birth weight vary by population?**
- Yes: birth weight varies considerably within *G. affinis* (0.80–2.32 µg) and among all species, with no consistent correlation to geography or known environmental factors.

**Q6 – Does offspring birth weight predict predation rate at the species level?**
- Yes: across species, larger-birth-weight offspring (e.g., *G. nobilis*, *G. gaigei*, *G. heterochir*) are associated with different (generally lower) predation rates compared to smaller-offspring species (*G. affinis*, *G. speciosa*).

**Q7 – Does offspring birth weight predict predation rate at the population level?**
- No: within *G. affinis*, birth weight and predation rate do not correlate — populations with similar birth weights show widely divergent survivorship outcomes, and vice versa.

**Overall Conclusion**
- The historical literature's characterization of *Gambusia* as uniformly and heavily cannibalistic is an overgeneralization built on limited sampling. True cannibalism rates are highly heterogeneous — driven by predator sex, species identity, and source population — cautioning against extrapolating findings from a single population or species to the genus as a whole. This has direct implications for [[Population Ecology]] and conservation management of endangered spring-endemic *Gambusia* species.
