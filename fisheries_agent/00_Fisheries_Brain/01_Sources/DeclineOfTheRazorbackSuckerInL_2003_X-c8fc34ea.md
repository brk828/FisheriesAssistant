# Decline of the Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Colorado River, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered_species #population_ecology

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is an endemic big-river fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Most remaining adults in [[Lake Mohave]] are estimated to exceed 50 years old, having been produced in the riverine environment during the early 1950s when young were abundant. Age estimation studies indicate these fish were spawned before impoundment. Sexual maturity characteristics allow field determination of sex categories: juvenile (sexually immature without secondary characters), male, female, and unknown (adult of indeterminate sex). The long-term effective female population was estimated via molecular techniques to be in the hundreds of thousands historically, likely derived from a basinwide population numbering in the millions.

**Habitat Associations**: Historically widely distributed and abundant to common in mainstream [[Colorado River]] and major tributaries from Wyoming through Colorado, Utah, Nevada, Arizona, California, and into Sonora and Baja, Mexico, extending to the Gulf of California. Currently, the largest remaining population occurs in Lake Mohave, a 105-km-long, 11,400-ha run-of-the-river reservoir impounded between [[Hoover Dam]] and [[Davis Dam]]. Spawning adults consistently visit specific shallow shoreline areas, coves, inlets, reefs, and spits at depths of 2–6 m. Primary sampling sites included Owl Cove (RK 45), Yuma and Gold coves (RK 39), Arizona Bay (RK 38), Carp Cove (RK 32), Cottonwood Cove East (RK 32), Cottonwood Cove West to Tequila Cove (RK 35), Hog Farm Cove (RK 31), Six Mile Cove (RK 30), Nine Mile to Half-way coves (RK 27), and Nine Mile Cove (RK 26).

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Adult razorback suckers have been shown to disperse great distances after release and randomly mix into the population. Spawning behavior involves adults staging and spawning at specific shoreline areas in springtime (March). Telemetry studies documented distribution, movements, and habitat use patterns in the reservoir environment.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Predation on early life stages by nonnative fishes is the primary factor precluding natural recruitment. Larval razorback suckers are consumed by introduced predatory species, preventing survival to juvenile stages. The feeding ecology and fate of wild larval razorback suckers has been documented, showing vulnerability during early development.

**Environmental Drivers**: Population decline is attributed primarily to impacts of nonnative (introduced) fishes and habitat alterations associated with regional water development. The transformation from riverine to reservoir environment fundamentally altered reproductive success. Natural recruitment has been precluded since the early 1950s, with only one record of natural recruitment documented since that time. Juveniles are absent from collections in the present impoundment.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Razorback sucker abundance in Lake Mohave declined catastrophically from historical numbers in the hundreds of thousands to 44,000–75,000 in the 1980s, 44,000 in 1991, fewer than 25,000 in the mid-1990s, and fewer than 3,000 in 2001. The 2001 population estimate was only 13% of the 1992 estimate. Over the 12-year study period (1991–2002), the population declined more than 94%. Annual single-census population estimates using the Chapman modification of the Peterson method showed: 1991 N* = 44,333 (95% CL: 30,118–68,415); 1992 N* = 20,853 (95% CL: 17,060–25,491); 1993 N* = 16,932 (95% CL: 13,549–21,162); 1994 N* = 13,517 (95% CL: 10,281–17,774); 1995 N* = 9,322 (95% CL: 7,049–12,653); 1996 N* = 6,678 (95% CL: 4,780–9,661); 1997 N* = 5,355 (95% CL: 3,190–9,735); 1998 N* = 4,506 (95% CL: 2,627–8,489); 1999 N* = 8,161 (95% CL: 1,458–81,601, with statistical bias noted); 2000 N* = 2,872 (95% CL: 1,965–4,392); 2001 N* = 2,698 (95% CL: 1,573–5,081). Data indicate a significant downward trend over time (R² = 0.67).

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Population viability analysis predicted mean time to extinction of 14.65 years (95% CL: 9.84–20.40) for a threshold of 50 individuals, 12.10 years (95% CL: 8.13–16.84) for 100 individuals, and 6.19 years (95% CL: 4.16–8.62) for 500 individuals. The 5% probability of persistence would be attained in approximately 20, 17, and 9 years for thresholds of 50, 100, and 500 individuals, respectively. Probability of ultimate extinction for all thresholds combined could be realized within 24 years. Population growth rate (λ) was estimated at 0.78 (95% CL: 0.69–0.87), predicting continued average population decrease. At current decline rates, extirpation from Lake Mohave is anticipated within the decade. The population is limited primarily to large, old adults because predation on larvae by nonnative fishes has precluded measurable recruitment for nearly half a century. Natural populations elsewhere in the Colorado River system are non-sustaining and small or have been extirpated. Only about 100 fish persist in the [[Green River]], the largest remaining upper basin population, which is also declining. The species has been extirpated from the [[Gila River]] system for nearly 50 years.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: The Native Fish Work Group initiated a repatriation program in 1991 to capture larval razorback suckers, rear them to a size relatively safe from predation (30 cm or longer), and return them to Lake Mohave. The goal is to establish a population of at least 50,000 repatriated razorback suckers to replace the aging and nearly extirpated adult stock. To date, approximately 70,000 fish have been released, with 811 recaptures documented. The intensive conservation effort based upon repatriated fish aims to perpetuate the species and its genetic variability until threats can be ameliorated or removed. Only then can self-sustaining populations be re-established into historical habitats. At this time, there is no practicable method to remove the continuing threat of nonnative predators to razorback sucker larvae. Approximately 100 fish were removed from the population during the study decade for reproductive services at national fish hatcheries or participation in telemetry and other studies. The Lake Mohave population is considered the last remaining storehouse of genetic diversity for the species, as mitochondrial DNA diversity studies show it is genetically the most diverse remaining population.

**Biological Reference Points**: The recovery goal established by USFWS is a repatriated population of at least 50,000 individuals. Extinction thresholds analyzed were 50, 100, and 500 individuals. The population estimate for marking year 2001 was 2,698 individuals (95% CL: 1,573–5,081). Population growth rate μ was estimated at −0.27, with variance σ² = 0.036. Federal listing occurred in 1991 under the Endangered Species Act.
