# Analysis of Eighteen-Year Trawl Captures of Seatrout (*Cynoscion* sp.) from South Carolina

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This Bears Bluff Laboratories contribution (No. 53), by [[G. Robert Lunz]] and [[Frank J. Schwartz]], analyzes eighteen years (January 1953 through December 1968) of standardized otter-trawl sampling at 18 stations along the South Carolina coast. It focuses on three seatrouts of the family [[Sciaenidae]]: [[Gray Seatrout]] (*Cynoscion regalis*), [[Spotted Seatrout]] (*C. nebulosus*) and [[Silver Seatrout]] (*C. nothus*). The study describes seasonal, depth and salinity distributions by size class, tests whether catch per unit effort (c/e) has changed, and compares trawl data with [[Commercial Landings]]. The authors conclude that gray seatrout use estuaries as nursery areas, move offshore as they mature, show a persistent four-year abundance cycle, and have declined over the study period.

## Background & Description

- **Economic and recreational importance:** the three *Cynoscion* species support both sport and commercial fisheries in South Carolina.
- **Regional context:** they make up a small part of the roughly 400 marine, estuarine and nearshore fish species known from the state. Among trawl catches of Sciaenids they ranked fourth, after *Stellifer lanceolatus*, *Leiostomus xanthurus* and *Micropogon undulatus* (Bearden, 1961).
- **Geographic range:** gray seatrout range from Massachusetts to eastern Florida; spotted trout from New York to Florida and through the Gulf of Mexico; silver seatrout from Chesapeake Bay to Florida and through the Gulf.
- **Motivation:** the authors note much hearsay about good and bad years and fishing spots. They aim to test these views with data on seasonal, depth and salinity preferences, population structure (compared with 1953), and future directions.
- **Species identification:** [[Gray Seatrout]] and [[Silver Seatrout]] were long confused, even among project biologists. Field identification of young fish relied on meristic counts (dorsal rays, gill rakers, lateral scales). Wittkamp's analysis of 1964–1965 data indicated the catch was 66–73% *C. regalis* and 26–33% *C. nothus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods**
- Vessel: 50 ft R/V *Anita*, a commercially rigged wooden shrimp trawler.
- Gear: 20 ft flat shrimp trawl, 1¾ in stretched mesh nylon with 1¼ in stretched mesh cod end, no liner. Each tow lasted 20 minutes at 1.5 knots, at 18 stations, weather and season permitting.
- Measurements: salinity by salinometer; surface and bottom temperature (combined) by telethermometer to 0.5 °C; depth by fathometer.
- Fish were measured in the field and grouped into 0–3, 3–6, 6–9 and 9–11 inch classes.
- Analysis: a linear regression (Y = a + bX) of annual catch against effort served as the c/e model.

**Station setting**
- Stations ran from Price Creek (north) to Bloody Point (south), within several river-fed systems with strong tidal currents (mean spring range 6.1–8.4 ft) and high turbidity.
- River and brackish marsh stations: [[Bolders Island]], [[Ashepoo River]], [[Ashley River]], [[South Edisto River]].
- High-salinity stations: [[Kiawah Inshore]], [[Kiawah Offshore]] and [[Bloody Point]].
- Remaining stations were in sounds, with bottoms ranging from mud and sand to "live bottom" sponge and soft coral (e.g., [[Price Creek]], [[Long Creek]]).
- All stations showed lower salinities and temperatures during spring runoff. Temperatures peaked in July–August and salinities in October–November.

**Life history (from literature reviewed)**
- Spawning is prolonged. In northern and southern populations, eggs hatch in about 2 days at 60–64 °F.
- Young are found mainly in inshore estuarine waters from April to November.
- Juveniles move offshore in fall and return inshore and northward in spring; this migration pattern was described by Nesbit (1954).
- Gray seatrout can live to about seven years in New York waters.
- Spotted trout are associated with seagrass (*Thalassia*, *Diplanthera*). They spawn in Florida from May to August and are vulnerable to cold spells below about 45 °F.

## Key Findings

**Catch totals and size structure (*C. regalis*)**
- 2,479 trawl samples yielded 23,025 *Cynoscion* assumed to be *C. regalis*.
- Annual catches ranged from 1 fish at Port Royal to 613 per year at Ashepoo River (1960 total 2,439; 1966 total 431).
- Size classes: 3–6 in = 15,987 (69.4%); 0–3 in = 5,081 (22.6%); 6–9 in = 1,832 (7.9%); 9–11 in = 125.
- Stations with the most young fish: Ashepoo River (3,560) and Calibogue Sound (3,151).

**Seasonal and salinity patterns**
- Most captures occurred in July (6,368) and August (5,508). The fewest were in February (93) and March (82).
- Fish of all sizes were found at all stations in all seasons.
- Most individuals were caught at 20–30‰ salinity. The authors interpret this as a seaward shift with growth, which is consistent with earlier reports.
- Fish reached about 6 in by the end of their first winter, and 9–11 in by their second autumn. They returned to spawn at age three.

**Population cycles and catch per unit effort**
- A consistent four-year cycle of abundance was seen at most estuarine stations. The authors project 1969 as the next good year.
- The c/e regression is Y = −136.5 + 0.16X. The correlation was r = 0.5211 for all years and r = 0.726 with 1966 excluded.
- The authors conclude that catches show an overall decline despite fluctuations.

**Commercial landings (Table 1)**
- Gray seatrout landings ranged from 29,000 lb (1966) to 590 lb (1968).
- Spotted trout landings peaked at 223,000 lb (1956) and fell to 1,687 lb (1967).
- Good trawl catches of young fish were often followed by good commercial catches one to two years later. The authors describe the relationship as a time lag.

**Spotted and silver seatrout**
- 207 young *C. nebulosus* were captured over 18 years, most in 1959 (34) and none in 1953 (three were recorded that year, per the text).
- The South Edisto, Point of Pines and Calibogue stations held the most spotted trout.
- At least 30% of the commercial catch is believed to be silver seatrout, though identification problems limit conclusions.

**Incidental species**
- 95 species in 51 families were taken.
- Sciaenids (10 species), Carangids (7) and flatfishes (10 across Bothidae, Soleidae and Cynoglossidae) were most frequent.
- Ten species were present at all stations, including [[Brevoortia tyrannus]], [[Cynoscion nebulosus]] and [[Stellifer lanceolatus]].
- Species richness per station ranged from 39 to 59 with no clear salinity trend.

**Management recommendations**
- Protect estuaries from pollution, dredging, filling and development.
- Apply regulations to South Carolina, neighboring and international fishers so adult spawning stocks can persist.
- Consider larger trawl mesh to reduce capture of small fish; Daiber (1956) suggested this for trawl fisheries.
- The authors warn that the seasonal spawning and migration cycle, if disrupted, could eliminate seatrout from South Carolina fisheries.
