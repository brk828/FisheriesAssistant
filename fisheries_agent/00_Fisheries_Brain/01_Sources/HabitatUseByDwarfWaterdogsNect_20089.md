# Habitat Use by Dwarf Waterdogs (*Necturus punctatus*) in South Carolina Streams, with Life History Notes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #proteidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 study in *Herpetologica* by Gary K. Meffe and Andrew L. Sheldon examines habitat use and life history of the [[Dwarf Waterdog]] (*Necturus punctatus*) in first- to third-order [[Blackwater Streams]] tributaries of the [[Savannah River]] on the [[Savannah River Plant]], central South Carolina. Sampling was conducted at 49 sites between 15 October and 19 November 1985. Waterdogs were found at 15 sites (30.6%) and were most common in deeper, wider, muddier reaches with slow current and undercut banks. Specimens were examined for maturity, reproduction, tail dimorphism, and diet, with over half of the 50 captured animals having empty stomachs.

## Background & Description

*Necturus punctatus* (Proteidae) occurs in coastal plain streams from southern Virginia to south-central Georgia (Conant, 1975). Prior to this work the species had received little study, with most knowledge drawn from systematic reviews and from congeners such as [[Neuse River Waterdog]] (*N. lewisi*). An earlier unpublished thesis (Fedak, 1971) compared the life histories of *N. punctatus* and *N. lewisi* in North Carolina.

Collections were made with block seines, quantitative habitat surveys, and backpack electrofishing (five passes, depletion sampling). Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin and later transferred to 70% ethanol for measurement (snout-vent length, SVL; tail length, TL), dissection, stomach content analysis, and gonad examination. Habitat variables were analyzed with univariate tests, [[Principal Components Analysis]], ANCOVA, linear regression, and a Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test, all at α = 0.05.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study streams:** Blackwater tributaries selected for an apparent absence of nuclear or industrial impact, considered representative of natural coastal plain streams.
- **Associated fauna:** Only one [[Two-Toed Amphiuma]] (*Amphiuma means*) and one siren (*Siren* sp.) were captured across the 49 collections. Waterdogs made up 10.3% of the fish-plus-*Necturus* fauna at occupied sites (range 1.6–21.5%).
- **Univariate habitat differences (Table 1):** Sites with waterdogs had significantly greater mean depth (27.6 vs. 14.3 cm), maximum depth (52.9 vs. 28.2 cm), mean width (4.4 vs. 2.7 m), percent mud (9.1 vs. 3.2%), and percent undercuts (2.1 vs. 0.4%), and significantly less percent sand (43.7 vs. 58.0%). Mean velocity did not differ (12.1 vs. 14.8 cm/s, P > 0.2).
- **Principal components:** Sixteen habitat parameters were reduced to four components explaining 59.8% of variance. Component I was a current velocity gradient (fast, sandy and clear vs. slow, silty and leafy). Component II was a size gradient (deep, wide, muddy and undercut vs. small and clear). Components III and IV (substrate and cover gradients) showed no discrimination by waterdogs.

## Key Findings

**Habitat use**
- Waterdogs were most abundant in larger, deeper, muddier sites with reduced current, silty leafy bottoms, and undercut banks.
- They were absent from smaller, sandier, faster-flowing sites.
- Usage did not change detectably along the substrate and cover axes (PCIII and PCIV).
- A total of 50 waterdogs was collected (17 females, 19 males, 14 juveniles).

**Size and maturity**
- Captured animals ranged from 26 to 116 mm SVL.
- Individuals under 60 mm SVL were juveniles; those over 75 mm SVL were adults; intermediate sizes were juveniles or newly mature.
- Maturity was reported at 70–80 mm SVL.
- Somatic weight was positively related to SVL on a log-log scale (Y = 2.98X − 11.05; R² = 0.979), with no sex difference at a given length (P = 0.808).

**Sexual dimorphism**
- Tail length increases with SVL. Females had significantly longer tails than males for a given body length (Wilcoxon, P < 0.0001).
- Mean tail proportion of total length was 35.7% in juveniles, 34.4% in females, and 31.85% in males.
- A smaller tail for a given SVL suggests a male, though not with complete reliability for field sexing.

**Reproduction**
- Clutch size increased linearly with SVL, but only 70.6% of mature females carried yolked ova; five mature females had none.
- Oocyte diameters ranged from 1.78 to 2.43 mm (mean 2.02 mm, n = 65). One female collected 20 February 1986 had a mean oocyte diameter of 3.99 mm.
- Ovaries were usually asymmetrical (11 of 13 females with the more loaded ovary on one side). Asymmetry averaged 0.136 (SD 0.152) and correlated with total oocyte number (r = 0.626, P < 0.05), suggesting physical constraint by the digestive tract.
- Males had significantly greater reproductive allocation than females at this time of year (ANCOVA, P = 0.003).
- Oocyte vitellogenesis and testis ripening in October–November indicate winter mating, with egg laying likely March–May.

**Diet**
- Over half (54%) of specimens had empty digestive tracts.
- Stomach contents across all specimens: unidentified digested material (16%), earthworms (11%), unidentified insects (7%), mayflies (4%), chironomids (2%), salamanders (2%), crayfish (2%), and plant material (2%).
- A salamander in the diet could not be identified, so cannibalism cannot be ruled out.
- The authors suggest low autumn feeding, possible nocturnal feeding, and reduced feeding before or during breeding, consistent with earlier observations by [[Fedak Study]] and [[Folkerts Study]].

**Conclusions**
- *N. punctatus* is a common inhabitant of small blackwater streams in the central Savannah River area.
- The authors note that its secretive habit and preference for leafy cover match laboratory observations by Fedak (1971).
