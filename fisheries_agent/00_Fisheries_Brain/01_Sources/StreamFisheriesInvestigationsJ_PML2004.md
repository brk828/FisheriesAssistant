# Stream Fisheries Investigations: Pteronarcys Californica Naiads and Colorado River Water Temperature (Federal Aid Project F-51-R, Job 6)

#freshwater-ecology #plecoptera #pteronarcyidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife job final report (Federal Aid Project F-51-R, Job No. 6, July 1986 to June 1987) examines the [[Aquatic Invertebrate]] community of the [[Colorado River]] between Granby and Kremmling, Colorado. Its two objectives were to test whether [[Pteronarcys Californica]] (yellow fly, a large stonefly) naiad density correlates with [[Water Temperature]] and flow regime, and to quantify the importance of this stonefly in the diet of [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]. Six years of data (1982 to 1987) show that the stonefly is the dominant food item of trout in this reach. Temperature and [[Dissolved Oxygen]] stress tests indicate that thermal and oxygen conditions produced by [[Windy Gap Reservoir]] are unlikely to push the species to its mortality threshold. The report concludes that the largest long-term threat is loss of the interstitial habitat that naiads depend on, primarily through siltation.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Pteronarcys californica, a member of the stonefly order Plecoptera. The document describes it as one of the more primitive stonefly forms and notes that the lineage has persisted in essentially unchanged form for millions of years.
- **Life stages:** Naiads (aquatic nymphs) live in the interstitial spaces beneath cobble and rubble in riffles and chutes. Adults emerge in June, and eggs are deposited in the first weeks of July. Egg hatching is reported to occur within about 90 days of deposition.
- **Sampling:** Benthic invertebrate samples (five 1/2-square-meter samples per site) were collected at each site in April 1986 and 1987. Naiads were measured for body length (from the front of the head to the tip of the abdomen, excluding tails and cerci). Sex was determined from the terminal abdominal segments of males and females of 25 to 30 mm and larger. Immature females were separated from immature males by the presence of a vaginal "ovipositor" mark on the ventral segment, which the authors describe as a potentially useful but imperfect criterion.
- **Laboratory tests:** Naiads were acclimated at 15 °C and 7.5 mg/L dissolved oxygen (or higher) before testing. Each test used 10 naiads per replicate, and temperature and dissolved oxygen were monitored daily. Naiads were sized before testing so that individuals at similar developmental stages were compared.
- **Temperature monitoring:** Continuous Peabody/Ryan thermographs were installed at five sites from 1980 to 1986 (see [[Temperature Monitoring Sites]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Trout diet.** Stomach-content analysis has shown that naiads are the principal prey of trout between Granby and Kremmling. Naiads are the dominant food item by number and volume from April through October.

**Benthic habitat.** Naiads are closely tied to the interstitial spaces between cobbles and boulders. Earlier work (Nehring and Anderson 1985; Anderson, Nehring, and Winters 1984) indicated that water velocity is the critical factor in maintaining the integrity of this preferred microhabitat. Fine sediments must be kept out of interstitial spaces to maintain habitat integrity.

**Temperature regime.** Windy Gap Reservoir reduces daily temperature fluctuation below the dam and modifies seasonal temperature patterns:
- Monthly mean highs below the dam are cooler in summer and warmer in fall than above the dam.
- Monthly mean lows below the dam are warmer in winter, consistent with the thermal behavior of a large, deep reservoir.
- The river about 2 km below the dam (Chimney Rock Ranch) appears to have warmed by about 1 °C compared with pre-dam temperatures, based on 1984–86 data.
- Largest daily temperature swings occur at [[Cos Richards Ranch]], especially in drought years.

**Flow and drought.** Abnormal temperature fluctuations in drought years (e.g., 1981) were attributed to water releases from [[Williams Fork Reservoir]] to supply downstream users. In July 1981 monthly means at Cos Richards Ranch were 4 to 6 °C higher than in any other July in the 1980–86 record.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Predicted from Knight and Gaufin (1964), naiads would reach their mortality threshold only if dissolved oxygen fell to about 4 mg/L at 25 °C.

**Cumulative impacts.** The report cites Ward (1984) as estimating that construction of Lake Granby reduced annual flow of the Colorado River by 91 percent. The authors argue that changes in sediment transport and microhabitat accumulation are of greater long-term concern than thermal extremes.

## Key Findings

### Trout Diet

- **1980–1983 stomach samples:** 72 stomachs were analyzed from April through October (except August). [[Pteronarcys Californica]] naiads made up 42–95% of food volume in these samples.
- **Byers Canyon, April 1983:** Naiads comprised 77.6% of invertebrate numerical density, 70.3% of dry-weight biomass, and 94.1% of invertebrate volume (values as reported in OCR text; interpretation of some digits is uncertain).
- **Parshall, April 1983:** Naiads comprised 39.3% of numerical density, 58.6% of dry-weight biomass, and 53.6% of invertebrate volume.
- **Trout stomachs, April 1983:** Sixteen rainbow and brown trout stomachs were examined. Naiads made up 45.9% of food volume in individual trout (as reported), and 75% of food volume when all 16 stomachs were combined. Only one stomach contained no naiads.
- **October 1983:** Of 15 stomachs, only three were empty. Naiads made up 0–97% of food volume in individual fish and 94% of total food volume.
- **September 1980:** Naiads made up 94% of food volume.

### Density and Biomass

- Maximum reported naiad biomass was 56 g/m² (Anderson, Nehring, and Winters 1984).
- Numerical densities exceeded 1,000 per m² in some samples.
- In 1982, naiads accounted for 5.4% of invertebrate density but 45.5% of total invertebrate volume at Parshall (OCR figure interpreted as 5.4%).

### Population Dynamics and Life History

- **Life cycle:** Length-frequency and size analyses of April 1987 samples indicated a four-year life cycle, in contrast to the three-year cycle reported by Knight (1965) and Poole (1981).
- **Cohorts:** Strong 1981 and 1982 cohorts were evident in 1983 histograms at Parshall and Byers Canyon. Strong adult cohorts in 1985 and 1986 produced the largest 1986 cohort, the strongest in the six-year record.
- **Post-emergence sampling (July 1987):** Collections found young-of-the-year (YOY) naiads, which contradicts a three-year cycle and supports a four-year cycle or longer.
- **Flow and density:** After six years of data, there appeared to be an overall depressing effect of high-discharge years on naiad density in the following spring. The correlation coefficient between peak discharge and subsequent density was r = -0.47. This was not statistically significant but was considered biologically meaningful.
- **Sex ratio:** Adult sex ratios varied from 46:54 (male:female) at Parshall in 1985 to 68:32 at Byers Canyon in April 1987. Poole (1981) reported a 60:40 ratio.

### Temperature and Dissolved Oxygen Stress

- Temperature and dissolved oxygen stress tests (Shuler 1986; Nehring 1986) showed that naiads tolerate dissolved oxygen near 4–5 mg/L when temperatures are elevated to 23–26 °C for several days.
- In a test at 25.4–26.5 °C with dissolved oxygen at 4–5 mg/L, both II+ and III+ naiads suffered 50–75% mortality.
- First mortality occurred at 24 hours in one test and 39 hours in the other (total test durations were 120 and 260 hours).
- Older (III+) naiads were more quickly affected by temperature and oxygen stress, as predicted by Knight and Gaufin (1964).
- The report concludes that the temperature and dissolved oxygen profiles produced by Windy Gap Reservoir are unlikely to push naiads to their mortality threshold.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- The stonefly is "clearly the most important food item" for trout between Granby and Kremmling.
- The species is "totally dependent" on clean interstitial spaces among cobble-boulder substrates; loss of these spaces through siltation is the greatest threat to the species in the upper Colorado River basin.
- Naiads are likely to survive after trout die from temperature and oxygen stress, but chronic habitat degradation could cause long-term decline.
- The Windy Gap project is one of several cumulative water developments that have altered the upper Colorado River since 1950; further development should be assessed for its effect on trout food supply.
- Three to four more years of research were judged necessary to determine whether peak runoff or parent-cohort density is the primary controller of naiad density.
