# Man, the Desert and the Conservation of Natural Resources

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Spanish-language essay by Biol. M.Sc. Salvador Contreras Balderas argues that [[Desert Fishes]] are scarce, highly peculiar and often known from only one or a few springs or small streams. The author holds that their evolutionary and biogeographic traits record the hydrographic history of their basins. He also argues that the extinction of desert fishes is an indicator of human-caused ecological imbalance, driven mainly by poor land-use planning, invasive fish introductions, water alteration and persistent pesticides such as [[DDT]]. The text closes with a call to protect ecosystems as a condition for human survival.

## Background & Description

The author describes desert fishes as:

- **Scarce and peculiar**, usually restricted to a few springs and small streams.
- **Evolutionary indicators**. Their affinities with fishes of more favored regions can be established through [[Taxonomy]] and [[Biogeography]]. Their traits are directly related to the hydrographic history of the basins they inhabit.
- **Scientifically significant**, although non-specialists often regard them as mere curiosities.

Two principles underpin the argument:

1. **Divergence through basin fragmentation.** When a basin is fragmented, as happens when a region turns into desert, its fishes accumulate differences from their nearest relatives. The degree of divergence is proportional to the time of separation and inversely proportional to basin size, since smaller basins diverge faster. The author cites desert basins including the [[Rio Bravo]] and its former tributaries: the [[Nazas]], [[Aguanaval]], [[Casas Grandes]], [[Santa Maria]], [[Santa Clara]], [[Alto Yaqui]] and [[Alto Mezquital]].
2. **Low competitor diversity.** Saturated rivers may hold 20 to more than 100 species together. Deserts typically hold 1 to 2 species in springs and up to 10 in rivers. As a result, desert species have little capacity to withstand competitors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats to desert fishes**

- **Introductions.** Officials and hobbyists routinely move fishes from saturated regions into unsaturated environments. This practice, the author argues, has extinguished numerous [[Native Species]] across North America from Mexico to Canada. Together with other causes, it threatens more than 100 species with complete disappearance, about 50 of them in Mexico alone. See [[Invasive Species]].
- **Water alteration.** Desert waters are peculiar in temperature, salt content and other properties. When they are altered, their inhabitants are the first victims. Common causes include:
  - Channelization and canalization
  - Burial (*entubamiento*) of watercourses
  - Silting and desilting
  - Industrial, urban and agricultural pollution, including insecticides and poorly applied fertilizers

  The author describes these forms of pollution as chronic and severe in Mexico. See [[Pollution]].

**Extinction as an ecological signal**

When desert fish extinctions occur without evolutionary causes, the author attributes them to errors in land-use and resource planning. Such extinctions indicate an [[Ecological Imbalance]] caused by humans. The root causes he identifies are:

- Technicians in agriculture and related fields lack knowledge of basic biology, [[Evolution]] and [[Ecology]].
- Technical priorities focus narrowly on crop yield and monetary cost.
- Humans are treated as spectators of the ecosystem rather than as one of its components.
- There is a shortage of conservation biologists.

**Biosphere and nutrient cycles**

- The [[Biosphere]] contains more than one million species. The current unplanned human process uses fewer than 12,000 of them worldwide.
- Nutrient quantities in the biosphere are fixed by the planet's chemical composition. Fertilizing one site therefore restores it at the expense of the site from which the fertilizer was extracted.
- Insecticides such as DDT and other chlorinated compounds kill the microbes that normally break down waste. They persist for up to 11 years, accumulate in the environment and worsen the problem. See [[Nutrient Cycling]] and [[Chlorinated Hydrocarbons]].
- Planned management would involve using more species under biological regulation, restoring healthy cycles, replacing pesticides with [[Biological Control]], and treating sewage and industrial wastewater so nutrients return to the land.

**Water as an environmental barometer**

Water purity is essential for aquatic flora and fauna. Aquatic organisms are described as an excellent environmental barometer. In cases of general pollution, the aquatic environment shows noticeable and even lethal effects first. The author notes that environmental deterioration is reversible if it has not progressed too far.

## Key Findings

- **Desert fishes are evolutionary evidence.** Their characteristics reflect basin fragmentation history. Divergence increases with separation time and decreases with basin size.
- **Low species richness limits resilience.** Deserts hold 1 to 2 species in springs and up to 10 in rivers, compared with 20 to more than 100 in saturated rivers. Desert species therefore cannot withstand competitors.
- **Introductions drive extinction.** Introduced fishes have extinguished many species in North America. More than 100 species are threatened, about 50 of them in Mexico.
- **Water alteration is a leading threat.** Canalization, burial, silting and pollution are the most frequent causes of harm.
- **Extinctions signal human error.** Non-evolutionary extinctions indicate poor planning and ecological imbalance.
- **Pesticides are persistent and far-reaching.** DDT persists up to 11 years and has been detected 900 km from the nearest agricultural source. The author also cites an atmospheric contaminant that could circle the globe in about 8 years under optimal conditions.
- **Resolutions of the American Fisheries Society (New York meeting, September 15, 1970)** included:
  - **No. 1:** Water development programs should give equal consideration to fishes, other animals and plants, and government programs should be reviewed accordingly.
  - **No. 4:** Live fish transport is increasing and cannot be adequately controlled, so the problem should be studied and solutions recommended to government agencies.
  - **No. 7:** DDT and other chlorinated hydrocarbons reduce fish and bird populations, disrupt photosynthesis in marine algae at low concentrations, and may pose genetic and cancer risks to humans. The resolution recommends a ban on their use, research into safer pesticides, and biological control.
- **Conservation is a human imperative.** The author concludes that saving ecosystems, where wild fauna and flora are fundamental, is necessary for the survival of the human species. Damage to ecosystems is framed as damage to humanity, whether immediate or delayed.
