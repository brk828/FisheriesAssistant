# Archaeological Resources of the San Bernardino Ranch National Historic Landmark, Cochise County, Arizona

#archaeology #historic-archaeology #cultural-resource-management

## Executive Summary

This August 1982 report by Lyle M. Stone and James E. Ayres of [[Archaeological Research Services]] (Tempe, Arizona) describes and evaluates the archaeological resources of a 131.24-acre parcel of the [[San Bernardino Grant]] owned by the [[Johnson Historical Museum]] of the Southwest, Inc. The parcel contains most of the [[National Historic Landmark]] property, including the standing buildings of the [[Slaughter Ranch]]. Fieldwork took place June 5–10, 1982. The project combined document review, intensive surface survey, site mapping, and limited test excavation, and it was funded by a matching grant-in-aid from the National Park Service through the Arizona State Parks Board and the State Historic Preservation Officer.

The survey identified 24 archaeological sites (SB-1 to SB-24). Most date to the Slaughter occupation (c. 1884–1922). The most significant is SB-1, a fortified U.S. Army encampment occupied c. 1914/1915–1918/1919. Three prehistoric [[Mogollon]] sites are also present. The authors judge the historic resources to be in good to excellent condition and recommend protection, stabilization, curation, and further research.

## Background & Description

**Grant history.** The San Bernardino Grant was originally 73,240 acres in southeastern Arizona and northeastern Sonora. [[Ignacio de Perez]] acquired it from Mexico in May 1822 for $90.00. Perez abandoned it in the early 1830s because of Apache hostilities. John H. Slaughter bought the property in 1884 and built a large cattle operation in both Arizona and Sonora. An earthquake centered in Sonora on May 3, 1887, destroyed the original Slaughter ranch buildings, and most surviving structures date from the rebuilding. Slaughter served as Sheriff of Cochise County (1887–1890) and later as a state representative. After his death in 1922 the ranch was leased to Dave Hunt (1924–1936), then sold to Marion L. Williams in 1936. The Ramsowers bought it in 1968, and [[The Nature Conservancy]] acquired it in 1980. In 1982 the Fish and Wildlife Service and the Johnson Museum purchased the 2,382.86-acre Arizona holding.

**Land status.** About 180 acres were designated a National Historic Landmark in 1963. The Johnson Museum parcel (131.24 acres) excludes a 60-foot strip along the international border, which the [[International Boundary and Water Commission]] administers. The Fish and Wildlife Service administers the rest.

**Prior work.** Earlier records include Stacy's 1974 survey, which listed prehistoric and historic sites on the ranch, and the 1971 excavation of a large Salado habitation site (AZ FF:11:21) by Mills and Mills. None of the prehistoric sites Stacy recorded lies within the Landmark or Johnson Museum boundaries.

**Methods.** The study used four procedures:
1. Review of documents and site files, mainly at the Arizona State Museum.
2. Intensive pedestrian survey by a three-person team over two days, with transects spaced 25–50 ft apart.
3. Site recording by mapping, photography, and written description over three days.
4. Test excavation at SB-3 and SB-7 only. At SB-3, about 500 sq ft was cleared by hand, plus two test units totaling 14 sq ft. At SB-7, about 85 ft of alignment was exposed, plus one 15 sq ft unit.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sites sit on a mesa rising about 3,800 ft above sea level, along drainages and near a spring-fed pond. Vegetation is mainly dense mesquite, with sparse cover at many trash scatters. Several sites are on or next to the U.S.–Mexico border, so the landscape reflects both border conflict and cross-border ranching.

Environmental and occupational context:
- **Prehistoric use:** Mogollon (San Simon Branch) and later Salado groups occupied the area. Bedrock mortars at SB-17 and SB-18 reflect plant processing.
- **Spanish and Mexican presence:** The Presidio de San Bernardino (mid-to-late 1770s, abandoned 1780) and the Perez hacienda lie just across the border in Sonora. No archaeological evidence of this earlier occupation was found on the Arizona parcel.
- **Military context:** The border site overlooks a large stretch of the border. It was used during the Mexican Revolution (1911–1920) and World War I.
- **Cross-border settlement:** Several houses (SB-12, SB-14, SB-15) straddle or lie near the international boundary, which the authors use to argue for an international interpretive perspective.

## Key Findings

**Site inventory (24 sites).**
- **Military:** SB-1, a fortified U.S. Army encampment (c. 1914/1915–1918/1919) on a mesa called "Mesa de la Avanzada." It covers about 5.15 acres, with a perimeter wall about 1,400 ft long, bastions, gun emplacements, and an 80 × 170 ft corral. The authors judge it to be in excellent condition and highly suitable for public interpretation. Related trash dumps are SB-2 and SB-23.
- **Architectural remains:** Nine sites have standing or partly standing structures: SB-1, SB-3 (adobe schoolhouse, probably the "possum trot" school), SB-5 ([[Old Bat's House]]), SB-7 (stone alignments), SB-12 (Chinese laborers' house), SB-14 ([[Mormon House]]), SB-15 ([[Howell House]]), and SB-19 (rock alignment).
- **Historic trash:** Twelve sites are trash dumps or scatters. Their dates cluster in several periods: 1880s–1900, 1895–1905, 1904–1915, and 1910–1920. Many share flow blue ceramic patterns (SB-6, SB-10, SB-11, SB-12, SB-21, SB-22), which suggests they are roughly contemporaneous.
- **Prehistoric:** Three sites. SB-8 is a relatively large habitation site with San Simon Branch red-on-brown sherds, dated tentatively to AD 700–1200.
- **Bedrock mortars:** SB-17 has ten mortars and SB-18 has seven. The authors consider them probably prehistoric.

**Notable finds.**
- SB-12 is the only site with evidence of Chinese laborers employed by Slaughter, including celadon rice bowls, rice wine bottles, and gaming counters. Its tentative occupation date is 1905–1915.
- SB-3 excavation yielded a U.S. military uniform button (c. 1902–1913) and a Winchester .45 Colt cartridge, which reinforce the military and ranch timeline.
- SB-1 artifacts include a brass cartridge headstamped P C CO 4 14 (after 1887) and military buttons dated c. 1902–1913 and 1917.

**Interpretation limits.** The authors describe their dates as preliminary and based mainly on surface evidence and informant testimony. They call for test excavation to resolve conflicts, such as the early trash found at the later SB-15C foundation.

**Management recommendations (Chapter 5).** The authors describe the Slaughter Ranch as one of the most important historic site complexes in southeastern Arizona. It is one of only eight of 21 Arizona land grants (per the authors' count) successfully claimed by private parties. Their key recommendations are:
1. Review all ground-disturbing actions by an archaeologist and the State Historic Preservation Officer.
2. Prohibit uncontrolled site access and artifact collection by non-archaeologists.
3. Acquire SB-15 and SB-24 by adding a strip of about 800 ft (approximate) along the east boundary.
4. Notify the International Boundary and Water Commission about SB-1l, SB-12, SB-14, and SB-19, and suggest joint management.
5. Stabilize SB-1 and SB-5, whose standing walls are weathering.
6. Expand historical documentary research across both sides of the border.
7. Permanently curate artifacts from Johnson Museum land under standards at least equal to those on state and federal lands.
8. Target future research at questions such as the location of the pre-1887 Slaughter house, the function of SB-1's buildings, and how ethnic and socioeconomic differences appear in the trash record.
