# Vegetation and Flora of the San Bernardino Ranch, Cochise County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #botany #cienega #plant-community-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1983 Arizona State University Master of Science thesis by Gayle Ellen Marrs-Smith documents the vegetation and vascular flora of the [[San Bernardino Ranch]], a 994 ha (2,433 acre) property in the [[San Bernardino Valley]] purchased by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) mainly to protect endemic endangered fishes. The ranch is dominated by [[Chihuahuan Desert Scrub]] and [[Desert Grassland]], and contains rare freshwater marshes (cienegas) fed by artesian wells and springs. The study recorded 343 to 353 taxa (the document gives both figures in different sections), the most informative of which are the two species previously questioned for Arizona: *Ibervillea tenuisecta* and *Corchorus orinocensis*. The thesis provides baseline data for judging future vegetation change following removal of livestock grazing.

## Background & Description

The ranch lies about 28 km east of Douglas at roughly 1,158 m elevation, within the upper [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage, which flows south into the Gulf of California. Its history spans pre-contact agrarian peoples around the springs, Spanish Jesuit exploration from the early 1720s, the [[Ignacio de Perez Land Grant]] of 1820 (29,640 ha), Mormon Battalion and forty-niner travel routes, the post-1853 boundary surveys, and settlement by "Texas" John Slaughter from 1884. Slaughter drained marshland, dammed springs and farmed over 200 ha, and by 1892 to 1893 drought and overstocking had heavily reduced range condition. The Nature Conservancy acquired the ranch in 1979, and it passed to the USFWS in 1982 as part of the national wildlife refuge system.

Botanical knowledge began with travel accounts and the [[Mexican Boundary Surveys]] (Emory 1857; Mearns 1907), which describe lush meadows and cane that has since disappeared. Systematic collecting began only in 1980, with the author making 13 trips (33 field days) between June 1980 and March 1982 and about 1,000 specimens studied.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and hydrology.** The valley is a gently sloping, north-south basin enclosed by parallel mountain ranges. Surface geology is Quaternary valley fill and younger olivine basalt on the western mesa. Artesian wells (104 to 206 m deep) are fed by groundwater flowing south. Downcutting of [[Black Draw]] (historically the Rio San Bernardino) after about 1880 separated the river from the springs and lowered the water table, degrading cienega habitats. Eight spring areas currently support or formerly supported marsh vegetation.

**Water chemistry (1981).** Artesian water (n = 8) averaged 28.5 °C, with low total dissolved solids, sodium as the dominant cation, and high nitrate (1.116 ± 0.169 mg/L) but low phosphate (0.005 ± 0.001 mg/L). Black Draw (n = 2) was chemically distinct, with higher phosphate (0.127 ± 0.015 mg/L) and sulfate, and a temperature of 19.4 °C. Artesian samples were chemically identical, suggesting one shared groundwater system.

**Climate.** Winter precipitation comes from Pacific frontal systems and summer rain from convective thunderstorms. Annual snowfall averages 19 mm. Herbaceous productivity depends on winter rain and peaks in July to September.

**Plant communities.** Eight communities were recognized (after Lanning 1981), with the ranch area distributed as follows (only the four given areas are reported in the source):

- Desert Grassland: 255 ha (25%)
- Chihuahuan Desert Scrub: 385 ha (39%)
- Mesquite Bosque: 220 ha (22%)
- Marshland/Cienega and riparian types: remaining areas, with Riparian Forest/Woodland at 110 ha

Marshland and Desert Grassland have been most degraded. Grazing has promoted [[Honey Mesquite]] (*Prosopis glandulosa*) invasion and snakeweed expansion.

**Marshland zonation.** Around spring sources, dominant emergents were *Scirpus americanus* and *Bidens laevis*, with *Typha domingensis* and submergents *Zannichellia palustris* and *Potamogeton pectinatus*. The cienega proper was dominated by *Eleocharis parishii*, *Cyperus niger* and *Aster pauciflorus*.

**Aquatic taxa.** House Pond (<1.0 ha, max depth 2.0 m, outflow about 190 L/min) is covered by introduced *Nymphaea odorata* and hosts *Lilaeopsis recurva*, a species found only there. North Pond has locally abundant *Najas marina*.

## Key Findings

- **Flora:** 343 taxa (340 species, 230 genera, 72 families) in the Executive Summary and abstract; the catalogue section states 353 taxa (235 genera), and Table 7 gives 353 taxa across 72 families (Gramineae the largest family with 69 taxa). The Compositae (55 taxa) and Leguminosae (17 taxa) follow.
- **New Arizona records:** *Ibervillea tenuisecta* (Cucurbitaceae) and *Corchorus orinocensis* (Tiliaceae) were collected, both previously unconfirmed for Arizona.
- **Native grasses:** 70% of collected grasses are native to Arizona.
- **Sensitive taxa:** Several species are marked as sensitive to disturbance (for example *Cleome multicaulis*, *Lilaeopsis recurva*, *Peniocereus greggii*, *Echinopepon wrightii*, *Talinum angustissimum*). No collected plant is federally listed as endangered.
- **Transect results:** Desert Grassland was dominated by *Hilaria mutica* (1,960 plants/ha) and *Acacia constricta* (504 plants/ha). Chihuahuan Desert Scrub was dominated by *Larrea divaricata* ssp. *tridentata* (4,630 plants/ha). Mesquite Bosque was almost pure *Prosopis glandulosa* (463 plants/ha, 0.717% cover).
- **Protection recommendations:** (1) control mesquite invasion of Desert Grassland now that grazing and cultivation have ceased; (2) expand marshland habitat as proposed by the USFWS (1980); (3) apply balanced judgment to aquatic clearing, because some vegetation perceived as "choking" fish habitat, such as *Heteranthera limosa* and *Aster pauciflorus*, is uncommon regionally.
- **Core message:** The ranch is valued for its endemic native fishes and cienega habitat, so this thesis supplies the baseline needed to track vegetation responses to restoration and grazing removal.
