# Management Briefs: Surgical Skin Stapling for Striped Bass Transmitter Implants and Non-Explosive Rock Demolition

#ichthyology #marine-biology #morone-saxatilis

## Executive Summary

This document is a pair of short management briefs. The first reports a field test of a [[Surgical Skin Stapler]] to close incisions in [[Striped Bass]] implanted with [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] transmitters. Stapling shortened the closure step and was associated with lower post-surgical mortality than the conventional suture method. The second brief describes practical considerations for excavating solid rock with [[Expansive Demolition Agents]] on the [[North Thorne River]] in Alaska, where drill-hole planning and fracturing time dominate project scheduling.

## Background & Description

**Brief 1: Striped bass transmitter surgery.** Surgical implantation of [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] transmitters into the body cavity (coelom) has become a standard [[Biotelemetry]] method for monitoring fish behavior across many species. Successful implantation depends on closing the incision quickly to limit stress. The conventional method uses an atraumatic cutting needle and surgical silk, which takes 8 to 15 minutes. Researchers implanting transmitters in striped bass reported post-surgical mortalities of 21.9 to 45.5%, which the author attributed to stress from this slower closure.

The stapler tested was the Ethicon Proximate Skin Stapler, a disposable medical device with stainless steel staples. Units were priced at $122.47 per lot of 6 devices (35 staples each) to $174.57 per lot (55 staples each), with 1981 prices given. Discarded hospital staplers were noted as a possible free source.

**Brief 2: Rock excavation.** Excavating holes in solid rock, as done on the North Thorne River, is among the most difficult applications of non-explosive demolition. Few unrestricted free surfaces exist for fractured rock to expand into. The brief notes that drill-hole pattern planning is critical, that a fracturing interval is needed for each excavation, and that removing large rock masses in several lifts can make project completion time prohibitive. The author concludes that expansive agents are most useful where small volumes of material must be fractured and excavated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The striped bass study took place in Lake Whitney, Texas, a 9,535-hectare reservoir. Water temperature during surgery was kept below 15.6 °C, following guidance from C. Coutant of Oak Ridge National Laboratory. Fish were collected between November 1982 and March 1983 using monofilament gill nets or angling. Earlier work cited in the brief covered striped bass movements and habitat use in reservoirs such as Cherokee Reservoir and Percy Priest Reservoir, Tennessee, and in the Savannah River, Georgia.

The rock excavation brief addresses a rock-cutting setting on the North Thorne River within [[Tongass National Forest]], Alaska. The document does not describe aquatic biota or water chemistry at that site. Its environmental relevance is mainly the potential disturbance from channel excavation, which the brief does not quantify.

## Key Findings

**Surgical closure method (striped bass):**
- Stapling reduced the total procedure to about 10 minutes, with the surgical phase taking 4 to 5 minutes.
- The incision was about 3 cm long and closed with 8 to 10 skin staples, after removing three scale rows mid-ventrally just anterior to the urogenital pore.
- Of 36 adult striped bass implanted, 28 were tracked for about 7 months. One of these was caught by a fisherman after 5 months, and the transmitter was still functioning in that fish.
- Two fish were never found, and five were presumed dead because no movement was detected.
- The five presumed deaths followed holding in an [[Anesthetic Tub]] for 20 to 25 minutes before surgery, versus the normal 5 minutes. The author therefore links time in the anesthetic tub to mortality, not only surgical time.
- The recovered fish's incision had healed completely, and the transmitter was encapsulated by peritoneum.
- The author concludes that stapling appears superior to the suture method and should reduce stress and mortality, especially in sensitive species such as striped bass.

**Rock excavation (North Thorne River):**
- Drill-hole pattern planning is critical, and projects may need several separate excavations.
- Each excavation requires time for complete fracturing, which can make multi-lift removal of large rock masses costly.
- Expansive demolition agents suit projects that fracture and excavate small amounts of material.
- Partially legible notes on the demolition plan indicate that drill-hole diameter, spacing and depth, and working temperature (within the recommended range for the agent used) affect fracturing time. A plan should be developed early to define optimum drill-hole spacing and the desired rock configuration, and the brief notes that such plans can support schedule and cost control.
