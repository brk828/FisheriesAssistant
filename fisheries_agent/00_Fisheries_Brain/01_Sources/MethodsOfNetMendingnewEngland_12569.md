# Methods of Net Mending: New England Otter Trawl Fishery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #commercial-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service fishery leaflet (Fishery Leaflet 241, Part I of two) by [[Boris O. Knake]] describes hand methods of [[Net Mending]] used by New England [[Otter Trawl]] fishermen. It covers netting terminology, knot definitions, damage inspection and trimming, needle filling, mesh gauging, and step-by-step tying of each knot type. The document concerns fishing-gear maintenance, not fish biology. It contains no species, population, or habitat data.

## Background & Description

- **Source context:** Published under the [[Department of the Interior]] and the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] with the [[Bureau of Commercial Fisheries]]. The text states that it is Part I of a two-part article, first appearing in the March issue of [[Commercial Fisheries Review]]. The author is affiliated with the Fishery Technological Laboratory, Branch of Commercial Fisheries.
- **Rationale:** Otter trawl crews must mend torn nets by hand, often at sea and in harsh conditions (cramped space, rolling seas, decks awash, rain, snow, bitter cold). The leaflet notes that most nets are machine-made, but mending will remain a manual task.
- **Core terminology:** Netting is a sequence of loops, or bights, interwoven by a [[Sheet-Bend-Hitch]] (also called weaver's knot, fishermen's knot, or mesh knot). A single mesh is the combined upper and lower half-meshes tied midway by a hitch. Netting size is set by stretched mesh size and counted in meshes of length and depth.
- **Knot classification:** Repairs are named by knot position in the damage: [[Mesh Knot]] (half-mesh knot), [[Pick-Up Knot]], [[Sider Knot]] (on the sides of the damage), [[Starter Knot]] (starter three-legger), and [[Finishing Knot]] (finishing three-legger).
- **Tools:** The only required special tool is a [[Net Needle]] (also called a shuttle), traditionally made of ash, hickory, bamboo, or dogwood, and sometimes metal, plastic, or ivory. For otter trawl repairs, the needle is about 8 inches long and roughly 1/4 inch thick.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not address fish species, aquatic habitat, water chemistry, or ecosystem processes. Its only environmental references concern:

- **Working conditions:** Shipboard mending takes place in adverse weather and sea states, which shaped the design of the method.
- **Material behavior:** Twine shrinks when wet, so meshes mended into a wet net should be slightly larger than the wet net's meshes to match the dry twine.
- **Net stress:** Nets damaged under severe strain can deform knots, making them hard to identify. Under such conditions, sider knots may need a [[Double Hitch Sider Knot]] for added security.

## Key Findings

**1. Inspection and trimming (prerequisites to mending)**
- Stretch the damaged section to "straight twine" alignment. Avoid "[[Cross Twine]]," which causes strands to loop and leads to mis-sequenced meshes.
- Cut out all broken or damaged strands. Trim stumps in half-meshes and pick-up meshes where time allows. This reduces bulky knots and prevents confusing the half-mesh knot with the sider knot.
- Leave at least 1/2 inch of strand beside three-leggers and siders, because these knots can untie. If cut too close, a knot cannot be retied, and extra meshes must be cut.

**2. Mending sequence**
- Every repair needs a definite start and finish, both three-leggers. A simple tear uses a starter three-legger (1) and a finishing three-legger (11).
- Sider knots signal a reversal of shuttling direction, marking the end of a row. Mend from left to right or right to left depending on the starter location.
- The document's "smallest possible tear" example (Figure 22) uses every mending variation in sequence: starter knot, half-mesh knots, sider on the right, pick-up knots, sider on the left, and finishing knot.
- A wrongly sequenced mend causes the knot to be upside down or twisted, or causes the mender to fall into "getting into crosstwine," which requires cutting out and redoing the repaired meshes.

**3. Techniques**
- **Needle filling:** Single-twine filling wraps the twine over the tongue and under the heel, rocking the needle until full. Double-twine filling uses a doubled length with the middle bight over the tongue.
- **Mesh gauging:** Gauge each new mesh against the previous one using the fingers of the left hand. This limits mesh size to roughly hand width, and tautness of the twine can make small adjustments.
- **Shuttling:** For large meshes, move the needle through three hand positions (A, B, C). For small meshes, the needle stays secure between meshes.
- **Net support:** Run a support twine through the meshes one or two meshes above the starter three-legger, tie them in a bunch with a slip-double-half hitch, and secure the other end to a fixed point.

**4. Knot-tying procedures (illustrated step by step in Figures 23 to 39)**
- Starter knot, half-mesh knots (left-to-right and right-to-left), sider on the right, sider on the left, pick-up knots (both directions), finishing knot, and double hitch sider knot.
- The finishing knot is tied with two hitches, with the second placed below the first so it does not jam. Leave about a 1/2 inch stump when cutting the twine.
