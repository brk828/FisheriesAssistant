# Sacramento Squawfish (Ptychocheilus grandis)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #native-fish #predator-prey-dynamics

## Executive Summary

The [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus grandis*) is a large native minnow endemic to California's [[Sacramento-San Joaquin River System]] and select coastal drainages. Historically the top fish predator in its ecosystem, it now coexists with introduced predatory species including [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Striped Bass]]. This document provides natural history information, management context, and addresses concerns about predation on juvenile [[Salmon]] in modified river systems, particularly near the [[Red Bluff Diversion Dam]].

## Background & Description

The Sacramento squawfish is one of the largest native minnows in North America, with the record specimen measuring almost 4 feet long and weighing 32 pounds. The oldest recorded individual was aged at 9 years. Its relative, the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), is the largest minnow in North America, exceeding 6 feet in length.

**Physical Characteristics:**
- Juveniles: silvery coloration with small black spot near tail
- Adults: torpedo-shaped body with long, pointed snout
- Coloration: olive-brown backs, yellowish bellies
- Mouth margin extends beyond front margin of eye
- Like all minnows, lacks teeth in jaw (pharyngeal teeth present)
- Similar in appearance to [[Hardhead]] (*Mylopharodon conocephalus*), another California-endemic minnow

**Distribution:** Native to the Sacramento-San Joaquin River system of the Central Valley, Pajaro and Salinas rivers of Monterey Bay, Russian River, and north coast streams. Typically inhabits areas with other native warmwater fishes including hardhead, suckers, roach, and sculpin, with seasonal presence of anadromous salmonids.

**Habitat Preferences:**
- Larger individuals (>8"): deep rocky or sandy pools
- Smaller individuals: pool and riffle edges
- Prefer slow-moving water adjacent to faster currents for energy-efficient foraging

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Feeding Ecology:**
- Young fish (<8"): schooling behavior, feed on aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates
- Larger fish: solitary ambush predators
- Foraging strategy: suction feeding by rapidly opening mouth to draw in water and prey
- Prey items: sculpin, suckers, small minnows, trout, salmon, crayfish, amphibians
- Historically served as top fish predator before introduction of non-native bass species

**Reproductive Biology:**
- Annual spring spawning migrations (March-May)
- Adults move upstream to spawn in gravel riffles
- Small eggs adhere to gravel substrate
- Fry emerge and swim at river's edge within two weeks
- Entire life cycle occurs in freshwater (non-anadromous)

**Taxonomic Relatives:** Four distinct squawfish species in North America:
1. Sacramento (*P. grandis*) - Sacramento-San Joaquin system and California coastal streams
2. Colorado (*P. lucius*) - Colorado River system
3. Umpqua (*P. umpquae*) - Umpqua River, Oregon
4. Northern (*P. oregonensis*) - Oregon to British Columbia

**Cultural Significance:** Historically a major food source for local Native American tribes and early white settlers. Has very mild flavor and was called "Sacramento pike" due to pike-like shape and feeding habits, though unrelated to true pike family.

## Key Findings

**Predator-Prey Dynamics in Modified Systems:**
In natural systems, predator and prey populations maintain balance through evolved avoidance behaviors. River modifications alter this balance by:
- Increasing preferred squawfish habitat (slow, deep water)
- Disorienting, injuring, or killing young salmon during dam passage
- Creating conditions where squawfish predation on compromised prey becomes disproportionate

Studies on the [[Columbia River]] found that [[Northern Squawfish]] below dams preferentially consume young salmon that are already dead or injured, indicating squawfish are not highly efficient predators of healthy fish.

**Management Approaches in Three Systems:**

1. **Colorado River:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] is endangered due to dams preventing spawning migrations and eliminating juvenile habitat. Species adapted to high temperature, turbid water. Hatchery and recovery program established.

2. **Eel River, California:** Sacramento squawfish illegally introduced in early 1980s. Population increased in absence of native prey adaptations, creating imbalanced predator-prey relationship. Restoration efforts underway.

3. **Columbia River:** Eleven mainstem dams provide year-round squawfish habitat, increasing northern squawfish numbers. Biologists estimate approximately 15% juvenile salmon mortality at each dam from combined passage effects and predation. Dams lack operable gates for seasonal opening.

**Sacramento River Management:**
The [[Red Bluff Diversion Dam]] gates can be opened during non-irrigation season, distinguishing this system from Columbia River. Opening gates:
- Mimics natural system conditions
- Allows free fish passage
- Enables predator-prey balance to reestablish
- Identified as "single most important step for enhancing the fish community"

**US Fish and Wildlife Service Research:**
Studies conducted near the [[Red Bluff Research Pumping Plant]] examining Sacramento squawfish and striped bass populations with objectives including:
- Determining predator population size
- Analyzing food habits
- Measuring growth rates
- Assessing age structure
- Evaluating spawning condition

These data will inform understanding of the Sacramento River fish community dynamics.

**Conservation Status:** Sacramento squawfish is a unique fish species endemic to California and represents an important component of native freshwater biodiversity. State regulations prohibit waste of wildlife, and the species provides recreational fishing opportunities and food value when properly prepared.
