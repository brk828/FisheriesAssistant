# Notes from August 26, 1994, Meeting on Fish Barriers (Aravaipa Creek)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #invasive-species #southwestern-us-fishes

## Executive Summary
This document summarizes an interagency meeting held on August 26, 1994, at the Bureau of Reclamation's Phoenix Area Office to address fish barrier design on [[Aravaipa Creek]], a tributary within the [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona. The meeting was mandated by an April 15, 1994 Biological Opinion tied to the Central Arizona Project water delivery program. The core objective was to design physical barriers to prevent upstream invasion of non-native fishes into habitat supporting native Southwestern fish species, while balancing hydrological, structural, and legal (water rights) constraints. Key experts in attendance included [[W.L. Minckley]], [[Paul Marsh]], and [[John Rinne]], alongside representatives from [[Reclamation]], [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[National Marine Fisheries Service]], and [[The Nature Conservancy]]. Discussion centered on barrier design alternatives (arched vs. notched vs. Obermeyer Gate), flow dynamics (laminar vs. turbulent), and the swimming/jumping capabilities of specific invasive fish "body types" that the barriers must exclude.

## Background & Description
The meeting did not focus on detailed morphometric data for individual species but identified five functional fish "types" or body-shape categories flagged by [[W.L. Minckley]] as priorities for swimming and jumping capability research, given their relevance to barrier design (i.e., which body forms are most likely to breach a barrier):

1. **Catfish** (Ictaluridae) — benthic body form; relevant to bottom-crawling/negotiating structures during low flow.
2. **Red Shiner** ([[Cyprinella lutrensis]]) — small-bodied cyprinid; high reproductive/invasive potential in desert streams.
3. **Green Sunfish** / **Smallmouth Bass** (Centrarchidae) — laterally compressed, strong burst-swimming body forms.
4. **Common Carp** ([[Cyprinus carpio]]) — large-bodied, strong swimmer, historically difficult to exclude.
5. **Rudd** ([[Scardinius erythrophthalmus]]) — cyprinid, jumping-capable body form of concern for leaping over low-head barriers.

These categories were selected based on presumed differences in swimming performance, jumping ability, and behavior under varying hydraulic conditions, rather than granular morphological measurement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] is a perennial desert stream within the Gila River Basin supporting native Southwestern U.S. fish assemblages threatened by upstream invasion of non-native species transported via water infrastructure (Central Arizona Project) and human activity (live bait release, "bait bucket" transfers).
- **Invasion pathway**: Non-native fish reinvasion is expected to occur predominantly not at peak flood flows, but during the **descending limb of the hydrograph** — a critical hydrological-ecological insight from [[W.L. Minckley]] informing barrier operational timing.
- **Trophic/competitive threat**: Species of concern (catfish, red shiner, green sunfish, smallmouth bass, carp, rudd) represent predatory and competitive pressures on native fish populations, consistent with well-documented impacts of non-native centrarchids and cyprinids on Southwestern native fish declines.
- **Anadromous barrier precedent**: [[Ed Meyer]] drew on Pacific Northwest experience with barriers designed for anadromous salmonids, noting that species-specific biological and behavioral data (e.g., swimming ability, migration timing) are critical for effective barrier design — in the data-poor Aravaipa context, a conservative design approach was recommended.
- **Human-mediated dispersal**: Public education on the risks of live bait use and bait bucket transfers was identified as a necessary complementary management action alongside physical barrier construction.

## Key Findings
- **Regulatory driver**: Barrier design mandated by the April 15, 1994 [[Biological Opinion]] on Central Arizona Project water transport/delivery to the Gila River Basin, requiring mutual agreement among [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] with input from regional fish/hydrology/exotic-species experts.
- **Barriers are not intended to be 100% effective**; taller/larger structures would increase efficacy but risk unacceptable adverse impacts to upstream and downstream habitat — a stated tradeoff between exclusion effectiveness and habitat disruption.
- **Design alternatives debated**:
  - Movable **Obermeyer Gate** with a possible central notch to concentrate flow (proposed by [[Thomas Haider]]) — rejected by [[Gus Zebian]] due to overflow control and O&M difficulties.
  - **Arched structure**, higher at bank tie-ins than center (preferred by Reclamation/Zebian).
  - **Notched structure** — structural/flow concerns may eliminate this option.
  - **Combination electric and physical barrier** (suggested by [[Jeff Simms]]).
- **Flow regime disagreement**: [[Ed Meyer]] recommended high-velocity turbulent flow over the barrier as more effective than laminar flow; [[W.L. Minckley]] disagreed, advocating laminar flow instead.
- **Siting flexibility**: [[Ed Meyer]] suggested that tying the structure into bedrock may be less critical than assumed, and siting in a wider downstream section of Aravaipa Creek could reduce habitat impacts.
- **Reinvasion timing**: Fish reinvasion is expected to occur primarily during the descending hydrograph rather than at peak flood flows ([[W.L. Minckley]]).
- **Research directive**: Reclamation committed to researching swimming/jumping capabilities of the five identified fish body types, with potential follow-up requests to expert attendees for data.
- **Project timeline constraint**: A 3.5-year target for barrier completion was raised as a design constraint by [[Thomas Haider]].
- **Non-biological constraints**: Concerns raised by [[Phil Hedrick]] regarding upstream flooding/ponding and water rights impacts were deferred to detailed design and public involvement processes under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]].
- **Velocity analysis**: An analysis of water velocities at various flood depths was recommended to inform structural design and species exclusion thresholds.
