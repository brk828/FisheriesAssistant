# Preliminary Report of Investigations of Springs in the Mogollon Rim Region, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrogeology

## Executive Summary

This 1954 U.S. Geological Survey open-file report (SY-339, Tucson, Arizona) presents a reconnaissance of springs in the [[Mogollon Rim]] region of central Arizona, the source area for much of the water in the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]]. The region had not previously been systematically studied for groundwater occurrence. The report, by J. H. Feth with sections by N. D. White (base flow of streams) and J. D. Hem (quality of water), covers geography, stratigraphy, igneous activity, structure, surface water, groundwater, spring discharge, and water chemistry. The document contains no fish or aquatic-biota data; its aquatic content concerns hydrology and water quality.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Mogollon Rim is an escarpment about 200 miles long, trending northwest from near Clifton-Morenci to north of Prescott. The rim region covers an estimated 10,000 to 15,000 square miles and is divided into western, central, and eastern divisions on structural and physiographic grounds.
- **Purpose:** The investigation sought to (1) locate springs contributing to the Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers; (2) measure fluctuations in spring discharge; (3) assess water-bearing properties of spring-discharge rocks; (4) relate chemistry to source formations; (5) relate geologic structure to spring location; (6) identify sources of mineralized water harmful to irrigation; and (7) locate areas where spring flow could be increased.
- **Additional fields:** Investigation of artificial [[Groundwater Recharge]] and of the effect of "prescribed burning" (about 60,000 acres on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] up to 1952) on spring flow.
- **Status:** Open-file, not reviewed for editorial standards. About two-thirds of the reconnaissance was complete by December 1952.
- **Land use:** Ranching, lumbering, and local copper mining are the main industries. Cited mining districts include [[Jerome]], [[Globe-Miami]], and [[Clifton-Morenci]]. The region is a major recreation area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and water budget**
- Cold-temperate climate on and north of the rim. The rim intercepts moisture-laden southern air masses, producing relatively high precipitation along its crest.
- Assuming a conservative mean annual precipitation of 12 inches, about 6.4 million acre-feet (the text also gives 64 million in the abstract) falls on the plateau portion of the rim region each year.
- Measured spring discharge is about 140,000 to 150,000 acre-feet per year, roughly 2 percent of precipitation. About 98 percent leaves by evaporation, flood runoff, and subsurface discharge outside the study area.

**Surface water and base flow**
- A study of perennial base flow (N. D. White) covered five south-flowing streams: Salt River near Chrysotile, Oak Creek near Cornville, White River near McNary, Verde River near Camp Verde, and Tonto Creek above Gun Creek. The total median annual minimum flow is about 175 cfs (about 127,000 acre-feet per year).
- Natural lakes and cienagas, including [[Mormon Lake]], Ashurst Lake, and Pecks Lake, lose water and sometimes desiccate fully in dry years. They are considered potential recharge sites.
- Mineralization generally increases with distance downstream from headwater springs.

**Geology and aquifers**
- Major aquifers include the [[Redwall Limestone]] (Mississippian), basalt caps (Tertiary and Quaternary), the [[Coconino Sandstone]], the [[Supai Formation]], the [[Kaibab Limestone]], and the [[Verde Formation]] lake beds.
- Structure is dominated by high-angle, mostly normal faults. Faults transverse to the rim suggest blocks offset by more than 2,000 feet. The [[Verde Basin]] is interpreted as a graben.
- Sodium sulfate deposits near Camp Verde are a notable evaporite resource.

**Travertine**
- Active travertine deposition occurs at four sites: Tonto Natural Bridge, Fossil Creek, the Salt Banks on the Salt River, and Columbine Terrace on the White River. Six other terraces are inactive.

## Key Findings

**Spring discharge and aquifers**
- The [[Fossil Creek]] springs, issuing from the Redwall Limestone, have an aggregate discharge of about 18,500 gallons per minute, the largest measured along the rim. Fluctuation is negligible.
- Springs in the Oak Creek flood plain total about 22,500 gallons per minute, at 67° to 71°F.
- Columbine Terrace Spring discharges about 7,500 gallons per minute from the Fort Apache limestone member of the Supai Formation.
- Cold Spring on Ellison Creek fluctuated widely; in May 1952, discharge was about 5 times that of December 1952.
- Contact springs in basalt-covered areas range from under 1 to about 100 gpm, with a few reaching about 3,000 gpm.
- Table 7 gives total maximum spring discharge of about 142,000 acre-feet per year. Basalt sources account for roughly 14,300 gpm in the western division.
- Southward-flowing springs dominate: about ten times as much spring flow moves south as north. The western and central divisions provide about five-sixths of the south-flowing spring flow.
- Blue Spring, in the Little Colorado River canyon, discharges about 200 cfs, roughly two-thirds of all south-flowing springs measured.

**Water chemistry (J. D. Hem)**
- Pre-Cambrian quartzite springs at the Salt Banks contain up to 37,000 ppm dissolved solids, mainly sodium and chloride, with a concentration near that of sea water.
- Paleozoic-aquifer springs generally contain less than 500 ppm, mostly calcium, magnesium, and bicarbonate.
- Basalt springs near the rim yield the best water, some below 100 ppm.
- Clifton Hot Springs (105°F to 130°F, with a reported range up to 160°F) contribute about 18,000 tons of dissolved solids per year to the San Francisco River.
- The Salt Banks may add about 50,000 tons of dissolved solids per year to the Salt River. Spring inflows above Roosevelt Reservoir are estimated at 315,000 tons of soluble salt per year.

**Quality versus use**
- Most spring water is suitable for irrigation and domestic use. Travertine-depositing and saline waters present utilization problems.
- Mixing with the Oak Creek flow in the Verde River raises sodium and sulfate downstream, which the authors link to Verde formation evaporites.

**Recharge and development**
- Recharge is by direct infiltration through jointed basalt, cinders, and Kaibab limestone, with additional seepage from streams.
- Artificial recharge via marshy catchments and structures to detain snowmelt is recommended for investigation.
- Development candidates include artesian conduit springs at Fossil Creek and Sycamore Canyon. The report notes that the Fossil Creek yield might be raised by drilling wells or tunneling to lower back pressure.
- Diverting highly mineralized water at Clifton Hot Springs and the Salt Banks could improve downstream chemical quality. The authors consider this worthwhile even though the diverted volume is small.

**Origin of salinity**
- The authors find no bedded salt that could supply the springs. They tentatively propose that dissolved minerals come from leaching of igneous, metamorphic, and diabase-intruded rocks, with volcanic heat and juvenile water possibly contributing at the hot springs.

**Recommended further work**
- Broader water-quality sampling, tracing of groundwater movement, seasonal sampling of large-discharge springs, and studies of the salt-spring inflows to the Salt River.

**Irrelevance to aquatic biota**
- The document contains no fish, invertebrate, or plankton data. Its aquatic content is limited to springs, streams, lakes, and water chemistry.
