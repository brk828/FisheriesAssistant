# Federal Aid in Wildlife and Sport Fish Restoration: Internal Briefing Memorandum

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sport-fish-restoration

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) memorandum transmits background material from an Assistant Regional Director's presentation on the [[Federal Aid Program]] at Matagorda Island. It explains how the program is funded, who holds a stake in it, how the Service's role differs from that of the states, and why Federal Aid must operate apart from mainstream Service programs. The document reports Region 2 grant totals for FY 1993 and describes the four core functions of Federal Aid staff: project review and approval, grant administration, technical assistance, and compliance review. It contains no species, habitat, or field data.

## Background & Description

The memorandum is an internal policy and program-description document, not a research report. It is organized as a set of short sections, each framing one aspect of the program:

- **Stakeholders:** Manufacturers, distributors, hunters, anglers, boaters, [[State Game and Fish Agencies]], and FWS Federal Aid staff all have roles in the program.
- **Ownership of funds:** The document describes a recurring debate over whether grant money is federal, state, or sportsmen's money, and whether industry has a claim to it.
- **Partnership:** It distinguishes a true partnership, which depends on mutual respect and develops over time, from cooperative agreements that states often view as access to federal dollars.
- **Roles:** It separates the "Your business / My business / Our business" of states and the Service.
- **History:** It traces the shift in state–federal relations from early "parenting" to a more equal relationship.
- **Neutral ground:** It argues that Federal Aid must stay independent of other Service programs.
- **Federal Aid functions:** It lists four roles, each with a short explanation.

The memo's tone is informal and at times critical of attitudes within the Service. It describes the program in terms of its legal basis, particularly the [[Federal Aid in Wildlife Restoration Act]] (1937) and the [[Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration Act]] (1950).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not address ecological conditions, species, habitats, water chemistry, or aquatic ecosystems. Its only environmental references are indirect. It notes that the funds are meant to "enhance" recreational opportunities for sportsmen and that Federal Aid projects follow "sound wildlife management principles." It also contrasts Federal Aid with other FWS activities that are "Federally Conducted," including [[National Wildlife Refuges]], [[Fish Hatcheries]], [[Law Enforcement]], and [[Ecological Services]]. These are listed only as examples of Service programs and are not described in detail.

Because the memo gives no site-level or biological information, its relevance to fisheries science is limited to the management and funding framework that supports state fish restoration work.

## Key Findings

**1. Federal Aid is a user-pay, user-benefit system.**
Excise taxes on sporting arms, ammunition, tackle, and motorboat fuel fund the program. Hunters, anglers, and boaters pay these taxes through purchases and provide matching funds through license sales. The memo calls the program a "Closed System."

**2. Multiple stakeholders claim ownership of the funds.**
The memo describes competing views: federal (because the funds come from federal excise taxes), state (because the agencies use them), sportsmen's (because they pay the taxes), and industry (because manufacturers pay the taxes). The memo calls these debates "pointless" but notes that they show how much each group depends on the program's success.

**3. Region 2 grant funding for FY 1993 was substantial.**
Total Sport Fish and Wildlife Restoration funding in Region 2 was $37.7 million. The breakdown by state is:

| State | Wildlife Restoration | Sport Fish Restoration |
|---|---|---|
| Arizona | $3,518,300 | $3,934,291 |
| New Mexico | $3,218,694 | $3,498,747 |
| Oklahoma | $2,939,422 | $3,699,443 |
| Texas | $7,201,000 | $9,745,000 |
| **Total** | **$16,877,416** | **$20,877,481** |

**4. A true partnership is a relationship, not a signed agreement.**
The memo argues that Service personnel often use "partnership" to describe arrangements that states see as convenient access to federal money. A real partnership depends on respect and trust and develops over time. The memo calls for Service staff to improve their attitudes toward state personnel.

**5. States and the Service have separate roles.**
States determine restoration needs, set project priorities, develop and submit proposals, carry out approved projects, and report results. The Service ensures that proposals are "substantial in character and design," that states follow federal law, and that grant funds are not diverted. The Service does not judge which projects are important. That decision belongs to the state.

**6. The state agencies have matured, and the Service has been slow to recognize it.**
The memo describes a historical shift from law enforcement–focused wildlife management in the early 1900s to professional management after the 1937 and 1950 Acts. It states that the Service acted as a "parent" in the early years, but that state agencies are now professional equals. Most Service personnel outside Federal Aid still treat the states as they did decades ago.

**7. Federal Aid must operate on neutral ground.**
Federal Aid's role is to help states succeed, which sometimes puts it at odds with other Service programs. Under [[43 CFR Part 12]], the Service may not impose administrative requirements on grantees beyond those in codified regulations. The Federal Aid Manual was rewritten so that every requirement it contains is found in the Code of Federal Regulations.

**8. Federal Aid performs four functions.**
- **Project Review and Approval:** Staff work with states on proposals, and funds are apportioned by formula based on license holders and land area. Funds cannot be used until projects are approved. The memo states that very few proposals are rejected, because problems are usually resolved before submission.
- **Grant Administration:** States may use their own financial management systems, subject to Federal Aid review. Reimbursements are drawn electronically, usually monthly or quarterly. Federal Aid monitors these drawdowns but is not directly involved.
- **Technical Assistance:** Federal Aid may offer computer-based bibliographic services, sponsor workshops, and share cross-state information. It does not tell states how to manage their resources.
- **Compliance Review:** Staff ensure that states meet federal requirements, from handicapped access to drug-free workplace rules. Non-diversion and substantiality reviews are welcomed by states, because they protect program integrity and help officials decline politically motivated projects.

**9. Federal Aid is a Federally Assisted program, not a Federally Conducted one.**
Most FWS activities, such as refuges, hatcheries, law enforcement, and ecological services, are run directly by the Service. The Federal Aid "program" is run by the states, and the Service assists them.
