# Lake Trout Planting Experiment in Lake Michigan

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #Salmonidae #lake-trout #stocking-programs

## Executive Summary

This 1947 report by Albert S. Hazzard describes a cooperative multi-state experiment to evaluate the effectiveness of [[Lake Trout]] fingerling stocking in [[Lake Michigan]]. Between 1944-1946, 411,394 hatchery-reared lake trout (12.6% of 3,277,402 total planted) were marked by fin-clipping and released in the Fox Islands area. The study involved Michigan, Wisconsin, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, with a $2.00 reward offered for each marked fish recovered. Control experiments at the Marquette Hatchery showed minimal effects of fin-clipping on mortality (4-5% higher loss) and growth (differences <1.3%), with 91-96% of marks remaining recognizable after one year depending on which fins were removed.

## Background & Description

Prior to this experiment, the value of [[Lake Trout]] stocking in the [[Great Lakes]] was unknown despite regular plantings of millions of fingerlings. In December 1944, the Wisconsin Conservation Commission convened a conference of all states bordering Lakes Michigan and Superior, along with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, to address this knowledge gap. Conference participants agreed that fry planting (newly hatched fish) was probably of little value and that natural spawning with proper closed seasons was at least as efficient as fry stocking.

The cooperative research program focused on fingerling planting (2-4 inch fish) since hatchery facilities already existed. Three marking treatments were employed:
- **1944**: Back and fatty fins removed from 100,280 fish (9.2% of total), averaging 2.9 inches
- **1945**: Right front fin removed from 159,712 fish (12.3% of total), averaging 3.2 inches  
- **1946**: Left front fin removed from 151,402 fish (17.1% of total), averaging 3.2 inches

Fish were anesthetized using a 1% ether solution during the clipping process. Handling and clipping mortality decreased from 1.95% in 1944 to 0.11% in 1946 as techniques improved.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

All marked fingerlings were planted in a localized area around the [[Fox Islands]] in northern [[Lake Michigan]] to facilitate recovery by commercial fishermen and sport trollers, and to study fish movements. The experimental design included a critical element: no spawn fishing was permitted in 1946-1948 for lake trout egg collection, meaning no fry or fingerlings would be planted in 1947-1949. This three-year gap was intended to reveal whether artificial propagation significantly contributed to commercial catches.

The study recognized substantial but poorly quantified natural mortality of eggs and young fish in wild populations. Hatchery operations also incurred known losses during egg collection and fingerling rearing. The experiment aimed to determine actual returns to fisheries from fingerling stocking before expanding propagation programs or reallocating hatchery resources.

Control experiments were conducted at the [[Marquette Hatchery]] to assess fin-clipping effects. Equal numbers of marked and unmarked fingerlings were held in ponds, with populations reduced in March 1945 to prevent overcrowding (1,005 each of clipped and unclipped in mixed ponds, 500 in separate ponds for regeneration studies).

## Key Findings

**Fin Regeneration Rates:**
- Back and fatty fin removal (1944): 2.8% showed complete regeneration of both fins; 47.6% showed some regeneration of either or both fins; 91% retained clearly recognizable marks after one year
- Right front fin removal (1945): 2.1% showed complete regeneration; 21.6% showed any regeneration; 96% retained clearly recognizable marks after one year
- Regeneration occurred primarily within the first year, with no significant additional growth at 18 months

**Mortality Effects:**
- 1944 experiment: 5% fewer marked than unmarked trout survived
- 1945 experiment: 4% fewer marked than unmarked trout survived
- Actual mortality difference may be lower since fully regenerated fish would be counted as unmarked

**Growth Effects:**
- No significant difference between fin-clipped and unmarked fingerlings
- 1944 marked fish grew 1.3% less than unmarked individuals
- 1945 marked fish grew 0.8% more than unmarked fish

**Recovery Program:**
- $2.00 reward established for each marked lake trout reported, paid by the state where captured
- Rewards offered for both legal-sized and undersized fish
- Conservation officers instructed to collect fin scars, scale samples, weight, length, sex, date and location data
- Posters distributed to fish houses, commercial boats, and sport fishing docks around [[Lake Michigan]]

**Experimental Design:**
- Total planted 1944-1946: 3,277,402 fingerlings
- Total marked: 411,394 (12.6%)
- Uniform commercial catch reporting methods adopted across all Lake Michigan states
- Expected recoveries beginning 1947 season, continuing for approximately 10 years
- Three-year planting moratorium (1947-1949) designed to test whether stocking significantly maintains fishery yields

The study represents a pioneering effort in quantitative fisheries management, using marked fish recoveries to evaluate hatchery program effectiveness in the [[Great Lakes]].
