# U.S. Forest Service Report Hides the Disappearance of Old Growth in the Southwest

#forest-ecology #pinaceae #forest-management

## Executive Summary

This Southwest Forest Alliance report, dated November 14, 1995 and authored by Torey Silloway and Kieran Suckling of the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]], is a policy critique rather than a primary research study. It argues that a [[U.S. Forest Service]] report by M. Johnson (1995) misrepresents the causes and extent of forest change in Arizona and New Mexico. The authors contend that 120 years of [[Logging]], [[Overgrazing]], [[Road Construction]] and [[Fire Suppression]] have eliminated most [[Old Growth Forests]] in the Southwest. They state that the Forest Service report is a distorted rewrite of four earlier studies covering 1962 to 1986. The document has no fish or aquatic content. It concerns forest structure, tree size, grazing and fire history, and endangered-species policy. It closes with preliminary recommendations for [[Restoring Forest Health]].

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Advocacy report and policy critique with footnoted citations and an appendix reference (Appendix One).
- **Authors:** Torey Silloway and Kieran Suckling, Southwest Center for Biological Diversity.
- **Prior controversy:** A 1993 Forest Service report by the same author (Johnson, 1993) claimed logging had not significantly reduced large trees, recommended increased [[Clearcut Logging]], and attributed declining forest health to protection of endangered species. The authors state that this report was discredited and that the author later admitted old growth had declined regionally.
- **Forest Service report under review:** Johnson (1995), *Changes in Southwestern forests: Stewardship implications*, USDA Forest Service, Southwestern Region, Albuquerque, NM.
- **Source data:** Four earlier studies on [[Arizona Forest Resources]] and [[New Mexico Forest Resources]], including Spencer (1966), Conner et al. (1990), Choate (1966) and Van Hooser et al. (1992). These cover conditions from 1962 to 1986.
- **Historical sources cited:** Rixon (1904) and Lieberg et al. (1904) on the [[Gila River Forest Reserve]] and [[San Francisco Mountains Forest Reserve]]; Plummer (1904) on the [[Black Mesa Forest Reserve]]; Taugust and Gomez (1984) on the [[San Carlos Apache Reservation]]; and Savage and Swetnam (1990) on fire decline after sheep pasturing in a Navajo [[Ponderosa Pine]] forest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tree size and age decline**
- The Forest Service defined any tree over 17 inches dbh as "large." The authors say this redefinition hides losses in the largest size classes.
- Historical average tree sizes cited:
  - Ponderosa pine, [[Gila National Forest]] and [[Coconino National Forest]] (1905): 19.3 inches
  - Douglas fir (1905): 23.5 inches
  - Pine, [[Apache National Forest]]: 18 inches
  - [[Kaibab National Forest]] (1909): foresters did not measure trees under 18 inches
  - San Carlos Reservation (1910): trees under 24 inches were not considered merchantable
  - By 1986, average tree size had fallen to 6 inches dbh
- Ages: 400-year-old ponderosa pines were common around 1900, 300-year-old pines were "veterans," and 200-year-old trees were just reaching maturity. The Forest Service now treats 120-year-old trees as mature or old growth.

**Old growth extent**
- Early surveys estimated Southwestern ponderosa pine forests were about 75% old growth.
- Recent Forest Service inventories on the Gila National Forest found old growth ponderosa pine covering an average of 2% of the landscape.
- Table 1 lists recent timber sales on the Gila National Forest (including Eagle Peak, Buzzard, Bull Pasture and Water/Beaver) with old growth pine percentages. The OCR text of the table is partly garbled, so exact values are not reliably recoverable beyond the listed 4% and 0% figures.

**Grazing and fire**
- Overgrazing reduces competition between trees and grasses for moisture and nutrients, and removes the fine fuels that carry fire.
- Studies cited indicate forest fires declined sharply once livestock were introduced, decades before fire suppression or Forest Service establishment.
- Fire is a major source of young tree mortality, so reduced fire allowed many trees to grow into poles.
- At least five of the cited studies identify grazing as contributing to forest health problems, and two discuss it extensively.

**Endangered species**
- The Mexican spotted owl conservation measures began in 1989, after the 1962 to 1986 data period.
- Only a few hundred acres region-wide were spared for spotted owls or goshawks during 1962 to 1986.
- Mixed-conifer forests, the spotted owl habitat, have more old growth and better health than the logged and grazed ponderosa pine stands.
- [[Northern Goshawk]] populations are associated with the few remaining stands of large trees.

## Key Findings

1. **Claimed tree-size stability is disputed.** The report claims "trees in nearly every size class have increased" and that large trees "remained virtually unchanged" from 1962 to 1986. The authors say the original data show the largest size classes declined dramatically.
2. **Volume claims are disputed.** The authors say claims of increased growing stock volume in Arizona are wrong.
3. **Large-tree loss is quantified.** Between 1962 and 1986, the U.S. Forest Service cut nearly half of all trees greater than 39 inches dbh, with higher cutting rates on the largest trees (described as "highgrading"). Figure 1 shows percent change by diameter class from 1962 to 1986, using data provided by Dwayne Van Hooser.
4. **Old growth loss.** Old growth ponderosa pine has fallen from an estimated 75% of the landscape to an average of 2% on the Gila National Forest.
5. **Ongoing logging.** The Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest was cutting 20,000 ponderosa pines over 18 inches and 1,000 over 24 inches on the Hay timber sale. The [[Carson National Forest]] La Manga sale would cut through one of the few remaining large-pine stands in northern New Mexico.
6. **Causal attribution.** The authors contend that logging, grazing and fire suppression, not spotted owl protection, caused forest decline. They note that the decline coincided with the peak of logging and grazing and predated environmental protections.
7. **Grazing is underreported.** The authors say the Forest Service underplays overgrazing even though cited studies identify it as a major factor, and that the report's emphasis on fire suppression deflects criticism from grazing and logging.
8. **Methodological criticism.** The authors describe the report's redefinition of "large" trees and its statistical methods as manipulative. They compare the redefinition to defining a "wealthy person" as anyone earning over $10,000 a year.
9. **Recommendations for landscape analysis.**
   - Complete a full landscape inventory of each eco-region (old growth, grasslands, priority watersheds, mistletoe classes, dense thickets, fire), displayed in GIS format.
   - Develop peer-reviewed restoration plans for each eco-region based on pre-settlement baseline conditions, with control and experimental plots.
10. **Recommendations for ponderosa pine.**
    - Retain and invigorate mature and old growth trees, including pines that are "yellow," gnarled, or 120 years old or older.
    - Retain all pines over 16 inches dbh unless they have invaded natural parks or grasslands.
    - Invigorate stressed mature pines by thinning understory trees within a radius of one tree height.
    - Maintain snags averaging 10 per acre greater than 14 inches dbh, selected across decay classes. Most areas are currently deficient in snags.
    - Clear excessive duff around the base of mature and old growth trees where it represents a fire hazard.
11. **Recommendations for plants and wildlife.**
    - Reintroduce extirpated predator species, which regulate prey populations and distribution.
    - Restore reduced wildlife populations, especially cavity-nesting and insectivorous songbirds, whose decline is linked to rising insect populations and poor forest health.
    - Prohibit introduction of non-native species. The document names bullfrogs, red shiners, [[Cheatgrass]] and Lehmann's lovegrass as species that have harmed native species through competition and predation.
12. **Conclusion.** The authors state that management decisions should rest on science, not rhetoric. They argue that thinning may be needed in some forests, but logging alone, especially a return to clearcutting, will not restore forest health, mimic natural fire, improve eroded watersheds or restore plant diversity.
