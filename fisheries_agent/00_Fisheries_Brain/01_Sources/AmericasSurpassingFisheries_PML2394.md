# America's Surpassing Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1916 *National Geographic* magazine article, by Hugh M. Smith of the U.S. Commission of Fisheries, reviews the history, present condition, and future prospects of American fisheries and the federal role in managing them. It covers marine fishes, shellfish, whales, and fur seals, along with freshwater fish culture. The document also includes an excerpt on wild [[Blueberry Pollination]] and the contents page of the issue. Its central argument is that American fisheries are of enormous economic value but are threatened by overfishing, neglect of natural laws, and poor regulation. It argues that the federal government, through the [[Bureau of Fisheries]], should lead conservation and artificial propagation, and that private aquaculture will become essential as natural supplies decline.

## Background & Description

Fisheries shaped early European and American colonization. Tales of abundant [[Atlantic Cod]] and herring drew navigators to the western Atlantic. Normans and Bretons fished off Newfoundland by 1504, and the Massachusetts colony was founded largely for fishing. The article notes that the first exports from New England were fish, and that conflicts between English and French colonies over fisheries lasted about 150 years.

The fisheries are a mix of groups. Crustaceans and mollusks provide the bulk of the approximately $76,000,000 annual value, with marine mammals (whales and seals) also important. The article describes the major fisheries in turn:

- **Marine fishes:** Cod, haddock, hake, mackerel, bluefish, herring, and sea bass on the Atlantic coast, and [[Atlantic Menhaden]] (a major source of oil and fertilizer). Pacific waters support flounders, rockfishes, and herring.
- **Shellfish:** [[Lobster]], [[Oyster Culture|oysters]], [[Blue Crab]], shrimp, clams, scallops, abalone, and squid.
- **Freshwater and migratory fishes:** [[Shad]], striped bass, whitefish of the [[Great Lakes Fisheries]], and [[Pacific Salmon]].
- **Marine mammals:** [[Whaling Industry|Sperm and other whales]] and the [[Alaskan Fur Seal]].
- **Freshwater mussels:** Used for pearl-button manufacture, the only valuable pearls in U.S. waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article repeatedly links fishery health to environmental conditions. The [[Gulf Stream]] influenced the distribution of species such as the [[Tilefish]], which died off in 1882 when a cold inshore current replaced the warm Gulf Stream over its grounds. Its return in 1892 allowed the species to recover.

Dams, factory and city pollution, and obstructions in streams had already cut off migration routes and degraded habitat. The author argues that future freshwater supplies will depend heavily on cultivation, because natural productive capacity has declined. Estuaries such as [[Chesapeake Bay]] are the most productive oyster waters in the country. Shallow pools left by Mississippi floods trap stranded fish, which are rescued by Bureau crews (see [[Rescue of Stranded Fishes]]).

The article also describes the biology of cultured species. Oyster larvae need clean cultch to settle on, and oyster beds are threatened by starfish, drills, and other predators. Soft-shell crabs result from the normal molting of hard-shell crabs. Button-making mussels need fish hosts for their larval stage.

## Key Findings

**Economic scale**
- Total annual value of American fisheries: approximately $76,000,000.
- Oysters are the most valuable mollusk fishery. Lobsters make up most of the crustacean catch. Seals and whales provide most of the mammal returns.
- Alaska's total fishery value since it became part of the national domain is nearly $300,000,000. The 1915 peak was three times the purchase price of Alaska.
- In 1915, the Alaskan salmon catch weighed about 400,000,000 pounds.

**Whaling decline**
- During the 75 years after the American Revolution, whaling was the most important branch of American fisheries, with 500 to 700 vessels at sea.
- New Bedford alone sent out 300 vessels in one year.
- Sperm whale oil peaked in 1837 at nearly $4,500,000. In 1846, about 70,000 people depended on whaling, with 720 vessels valued at $21,000,000.
- The fishery has declined for more than 50 years.

**Lobster paradox**
- Over the past 25 years, lobster catch declined by more than 60 percent while fishermen's receipts rose by 200 percent.
- The author notes that the lobster should be a staple food but has become a luxury.
- The article blames "disregard for the future, neglect of natural laws, and indiscriminate fishing," and calls for radical state action.

**Oyster industry**
- The American oyster is more valuable than all other fishery products in other countries combined.
- The annual oyster crop is approximately 75,000,000 bushels. The OCR text is uncertain here, but this matches the printed figure.
- Virginia and Maryland have the largest output. New York and Connecticut have the highest revenue.
- About half of the quantity and 70 percent of the value come from planted grounds.
- Oyster culture involves seven steps: acquiring grounds, clearing bottoms, spreading cultch, ensuring spat set, protecting beds from predators, transplanting, and culling for market.

**Fur seal recovery**
- The [[Alaskan Fur Seal]] herd was depleted by indiscriminate killing. Under an international agreement, the herd has recovered.
- About 100,000 young seals were added in 1916. The author argues that the herd could be managed like cattle, with yearly harvests of surplus young males.
- The federal government may sell the skins in an American market rather than abroad.

**Federal fish culture**
- In the fiscal year ending June 30, 1916, about five billion food and game fishes were produced under federal supervision.
- Egg collection and hatching operated in 32 states and Alaska.
- About twenty states maintain their own hatcheries.
- Distribution required about 6,000 miles of railway travel by fish cars in 1915.
- Congress was considering a bill for eighteen additional hatcheries.

**Rescue operations**
- Each year, Mississippi floods strand food fishes in sloughs and ponds, where they die.
- Bureau crews with seines rescue these fishes from Minnesota to Mississippi and return them to streams or supply them to applicants.

**Tilefish revival**
- Tilefish was first recorded in 1879, when Captain Kirby caught several thousand pounds off Nantucket.
- A cold wave in 1882 wiped out the population.
- The Bureau of Fisheries predicted the Gulf Stream would return by 1892, and the species recovered.
- In 1915, the Bureau began a publicity campaign to create a commercial fishery. Within a month, it turned the project over to commercial interests.
- By June 1, 1916, more than 1,700 tons had been caught and sold, mostly in New York City.

**Blue crab and soft-shell crab**
- Chesapeake Bay is the leading crab fishery. In 1915, over 1,000,000 crabs were caught in that body of water, valued at more than one million dollars. The OCR text is uncertain about the exact count.
- Soft-shell crab production is a conservation and culture interest.

**Freshwater mussels**
- Mussels in interior rivers are not eaten but supply the pearl-button industry, valued at an estimated $8,000,000 to $10,000,000 per year.
- Button mussels need fish hosts for larval development. Hatcheries help maintain these hosts.

**Future outlook**
- The author concludes that federal and state cooperation, along with wise regulation, can maintain marine supplies. The article argues that the states cannot manage these shared national resources alone.
- Private fish culture on ponds can provide food for rural and suburban communities. The author states that an acre of water can yield greater returns than an acre of the best land.
- The article calls for more attention to aquaculture, including mussel and oyster planting, to offset declining wild stocks.
