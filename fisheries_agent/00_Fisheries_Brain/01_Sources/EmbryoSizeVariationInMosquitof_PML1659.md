# Embryo Size Variation in Mosquitofish: Optimality vs Plasticity in Propagule Size

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontiformes

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Gerald K. Meffe]] examines embryo size variation in [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquitofish), a live-bearing fish, using embryos from controlled laboratory experiments and from wild-caught females. Each embryo was individually weighed to (1) document the magnitude and sources of variation in propagule size and (2) test predictions of [[Optimality Models]] against a [[Phenotypic Plasticity]] explanation. Variance in embryo dry weight was high at every developmental stage, both among and within females. Fewer than half of the total variance was attributable to developmental stage, maternal size, clutch size, or treatment effects. The trade-off between embryo size and number was relatively weak, and the data did not support the existence of an optimal offspring size. The author concludes that variable propagule size may be adaptive, favoring plasticity over strong developmental canalization.

## Background & Description

**Study organism.** Mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) are live-bearing fish in the order Cyprinodontiformes (cited in the source as Prinodontiformes). Females are maintained and sampled in the study as sources of embryos at different developmental stages.

**Theoretical framework.** Offspring size is a critical [[Life History Traits]] component that affects survival, growth, and competitive ability. Because a finite amount of energy is available for reproduction, it may be allocated to many small offspring or fewer large ones. Two main hypotheses are contrasted:

- **Optimality models** ([[Optimality Models]]) predict an optimal offspring size for a given ecological setting, with other sizes at a selective disadvantage (cited work includes Smith 1974, Shaffer and Gadgil 1975, and Brockelman 1975).
- **Plasticity or variance hypothesis** holds that fitness is not a function of a single size. Instead, variable propagule size may be advantageous when environments change unpredictably. The best strategy is then to produce a spread of sizes, maximizing the number of offspring that survive to maturity.

**Methods overview.**

- **Laboratory sampling:** Female mosquitofish were held under constant, controlled conditions (temperature, photoperiod, and water quality) and fed a controlled diet of dried flake food with muscle tissue of [[Bluegill]] or [[Catfish]]. Females were given 49 days on treatment, with fish of different food treatments compared against each other.
- **Wild sampling:** Fish were collected in August 1984 from [[Southwestern Florida]] and processed in the same way as laboratory fish.
- **Measurements:** Embryos were removed from the ovaries, counted, measured for standard length (SL), and individually weighed. Embryos were assigned to developmental stages from immature ova to late-stage embryos ready for parturition. Because a normal gestation period is 3 to 4 weeks, reproduction was initiated after introduction to the experimental treatments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Developmental stage** was a key sorting variable. Stage 1 ova are immature and still undergoing [[Vitellogenesis]], while later stages are mature. Six stages were defined, ranging from immature ova to late embryos with a large amount of yolk remaining. Individual weight declined through development, but this decline was not consistent across all groups.
- **Maternal size and clutch size** were measured as possible predictors of embryo weight. Larger females and larger clutches were expected to show different embryo size patterns.
- **Environmental conditions** in the laboratory were held constant among females to minimize environmental variation. Wild females, by contrast, experienced natural field conditions, which may have increased variation in embryo size.
- **Sampling design:** Embryos came from multiple females per group, and the analysis emphasized female means and among-female variation rather than treating individual embryos as independent samples.

## Key Findings

1. **Large variance in embryo weight.** Coefficients of variation were high for all three samples (fall laboratory, spring laboratory, and wild). Variance was high both among females and within females, at every developmental stage.

2. **Sample sizes and ranges.**
   - Fall laboratory sample: 184 embryos from 19 females, ranging 0.8 to 2.8 mg.
   - Wild sample: 348 embryos from 41 wild females, ranging 0.6 to 2.6 mg.
   - Mean weights, standard deviations, and CVs by developmental stage are reported in the source's Table 1.

3. **Limited explanatory power of predictors.**
   - Developmental stage, maternal size, clutch size, and food treatment together accounted for 36.8% of the variation in embryo weight in multiple regression.
   - Clutch size accounted for most of the explained variance; food treatment accounted for none.
   - Over 63% of variance in embryo weight remained unexplained by these four factors.
   - Less than half of the total variance was attributable to developmental stage, maternal size, clutch size, or treatment effects.

4. **Weak size-number trade-off.** The relationship between embryo size and embryo number was relatively weak, so the data did not reveal a clear constraint that would favor a single optimal embryo size.

5. **Among-female differences.** Some variation was due to differences among females, but a large share of the variance remained within females. Certain laboratory-reared groups differed in mean weight, possibly reflecting differences in experimental handling or environment.

6. **Conclusion favoring plasticity.** The author argues that the lack of a strong optimum, combined with high variance, is consistent with a variable-size reproductive strategy. Under this view, producing embryos of varying sizes buffers reproduction against unpredictable environments, which is more plausible than strict developmental canalization toward one optimal size.

7. **Implications.** The results challenge optimality-based expectations for offspring size in this live-bearing fish and suggest that propagule size variation is an important, evolutionarily relevant feature of mosquitofish [[Reproductive Strategy]].
