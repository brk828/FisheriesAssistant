# Plankton Community Cycling and Recovery After Drought in Lake Merrimajeel

#zooplankton #limnology #rotifera #cladocera

## Executive Summary

This study followed the zooplankton of [[Lake Merrimajeel]], a shallow, impermanent swamp in the [[Murray-Darling Basin]] of western New South Wales, from February 1977 to January 1980. Sampling covered a total drawdown (complete drying) and subsequent refilling. Despite erratic changes in water level, temperature and turbidity, a basic annual community cycle persisted, moving from rotifer dominance in summer/autumn to crustacean dominance in winter/spring. The drawdown disrupted this cycle, which was re-established several months after refilling.

The authors propose that the high plankton diversity of flood-plain river systems depends partly on a "fugitive" existence: species that temporarily colonise basins after drawdowns and refilling, exploiting the organic detritus released on flooding. Turbidity was not uniformly depressive here. Many limnetic species were abundant under turbid conditions, which the authors attribute to the organic nature of the suspended material.

## Background & Description

The flood-plain rivers of western New South Wales hold rich planktonic faunas, with over 110 crustacean and 500 rotifer species recorded from the region. Billabongs and shallow swamps are numerous and fluctuate strongly in level, often drying completely in droughts. Quantitative zooplankton dynamics in such temporary habitats were, according to the authors, largely unrecorded.

The study addressed four questions: the composition of the zooplankton; whether major groups show seasonality despite fluctuating water levels; how the assemblage responded to drying; and how it re-established after reflooding.

**Study site.** Lake Merrimajeel is an extensively gilgaied depression of 130 ha, about 1.3 m deep, with large clumps of lignum (*Muehlenbeckia cunninghamii*), ephemeral macrophyte beds and a complex marsh edge. Upstream lie Murrumbidgil Swamp (red gum and lignum), and the reservoirs Lake Brewster, Lake Cargelligo and Wyangala Dam.

**Methods (summary).**
- Ten stations were sampled monthly, February 1977 to January 1980.
- At each station, water depth, Secchi transparency and top and bottom temperatures were recorded. The authors note that Secchi depth can be biased in very shallow lakes.
- Plankton was collected with a 60 µm mesh net (30 cm mouth) hauled through the undisturbed water column, then preserved in 6% formalin. Counts were complete except for very numerous taxa, which were counted in a 50% subsample.
- Cladocerans were identified after Frey (1982) and Smirnov & Timms (1983); copepods after Bayly (1964) and Morton (1985); rotifers by R. Shiel.
- Diversity was calculated with the [[Shannon-Weaver Index]]. Cyclopoid nauplii and copepodites were excluded from species-level analyses.
- Seasonality was tested with [[Periodic Regression]] (annual and six-month harmonics on log-transformed counts, in four-week periods), applied separately to pre-dry, post-dry and overall data.
- Community structure was examined with [[Correspondence Analysis]] on a matrix of 32 sample times by 71 taxa.

**Fish.** Fish are not a focus of this paper. Newly hatched fish were included in the plankton analysis, "Fish" appears as an unidentified taxon in the species list, and [[Common Carp]] are mentioned as sediment-disturbing bottom feeders.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** The swamp's inflow stopped in early December 1977 and it dried completely in late February 1978. It refilled in late April 1978, reaching a maximum depth of 1.05 m in August 1978, and dried completely again in February 1980.

**Temperature and transparency.** Mean temperature across stations ranged from about 8°C in winter to 35°C in summer, with surface readings above 44°C at some stations. The swamp was stratified intermittently in spring and autumn. It was turbid on most dates, clearest just after refilling and in late 1979, and most turbid before the two drawdowns.

**Water chemistry** ([[Briggs]] et al., 1985). Ionic composition was dominated by Na⁺ and Cl⁻, with mean concentrations of 77.3 and 142.2 mg l⁻¹. Carbonate and hydroxide were not detected. Mean conductivity was 677.7 µS cm⁻¹, pH 7.7 and oxygen 6.3 mg l⁻¹. Conductivity, ions and pH were inversely related to water level, reflecting concentration and dilution.

**Drying-phase chemistry** (20 Sep 1977 to 9 Feb 1978):
- Na⁺ rose from 29 to 216 mg l⁻¹
- Cl⁻ rose from 59 to 738 mg l⁻¹
- Conductivity rose from 373 to 2510 µS cm⁻¹
- Oxygen fell from 7.0 to 4.0 mg l⁻¹ by 11 Jan 1978, then became undetectable by 9 Feb 1978
- pH rose from 8.8 to 9.0, then fell to 6.8 by 9 Feb 1978

**Macrophytes.** [[Valisneria Spiralis]] was the commonest macrophyte and at times covered most of the swamp. *Myriophyllum*, *Potamogeton*, sedges, algae and grasses grew at the shallow edges, and the floating fern *Azolla filiculoides* formed dense mats.

**Water volume.** From November 1977 to February 1978 the water volume fell from 448,780 m³ to 5,400 m³.

## Key Findings

**1. Diversity and composition.**
- 30 rotifer species and 34 microcrustacean species were recorded, plus several rarer groups.
- Of 72 taxa in total, 31 were recorded on four occasions or fewer.
- "Phyllopods" (anostracans, conchostracans, notostracans) were absent or extremely rare. Twenty dip-net samples taken shortly before the first plankton sample yielded only one *Triops*.

**2. Community cycle.** Despite erratic physical change, a basic annual cycle persisted: rotifers dominated summer/autumn and crustaceans dominated winter/spring. The cycle was disrupted by the 1978 drawdown and re-established through a succession several months after refilling.

**3. Abundance and diversity through the drying phase.**
- Plankton was most abundant in 1977 and just before the drawdown.
- Species numbers and diversity peaked in the months before drying, when many rotifer species occurred.
- Rotifer density in February 1978 reached 3,769 l⁻¹, far above the 672 l⁻¹ "high" figure cited from Shiel & Koste (1983).
- Diversity was lowest in February 1978, when many species died just before the swamp dried. *Brachionus calyciflorus* and *Asplanchna sieboldi* then made up 98% of the sample.

**4. Dominance episodes.**
- *Daphnia* dominated 83% of samples in May 1978, three months after refilling.
- *Daphnia* and cyclopoid copepods made up 80–90% of samples in winter/spring 1979.
- *Bosmina meridionalis* made up 83% of the sample in August 1977.
- *Boeckella fluvialis* made up 64% of the sample in November 1978.

**5. Seasonality (periodic regression).**
- Calanoid copepods were most abundant before the drawdown and in mid-1979. Pre-dry R² was 0.901 and post-dry R² was 0.558, so seasonality was stronger before drying.
- Cyclopoids had strong annual cycles with summer/autumn maxima.
- Chydorids peaked in autumn, with a minor peak before the drying.
- Daphniidae occurred in the latter half of the year, with spring maxima.
- *Bosmina meridionalis* showed strong annual cycles with spring maxima.
- *Moina micrura* had summer maxima in the pre-dry and overall data.
- Rotifers showed strong annual cycles with late summer/autumn maxima. The pre-dry R² was 0.931, the strongest seasonality reported.
- *Asplanchna sieboldi* had strong, unmodified annual cycles with autumn maxima (pre-dry R² = 0.909).
- *Brachionus* species were commonest after the drawdown in summer 1979. *Keratella* species were commonest before it.

**6. Ordination (Correspondence Analysis).**
- Axis 1 separated post-refilling sample dates from the rest, and axis 2 separated summer/autumn from winter/spring.
- The annual cycle recurred similarly in 1977 and 1979. The 1978 drying samples lay far to one side.
- Species groups A (summer/autumn), B (widespread or opportunistic) and C (winter/spring) were identified, along with a distinct Group D.
- Group D, associated with post-filling succession in 1978, was made up mostly of rare chydorids and ostracods. Seven of its species occurred only in that period.

**7. Turbidity and drying.**
- Turbidity was not depressive in this lake. Significant correlations with turbidity were found for total plankton, Rotifera (r = 0.857) and Calanoida (r = 0.595), all negative in slope.
- These correlations were probably artefacts of shared seasonality. After removing periodic components, the relationship disappeared for all groups except Rotifera, which kept a strong negative residual relationship (r = 0.774).
- The authors conclude that turbidity had a positive effect on rotifers, possibly through organic particles.

**8. Causes of the drying-phase build-up.**
- Densities increased faster than volume decreased. Rotifer density rose as log(rotifers) = 16.869 − 1.244 × log(volume), r = 0.994. For *Asplanchna*, log(*Asplanchna*) = 16.511 − 1.175 × log(volume), r = 0.906.
- Oxygen stayed at or above 4 mg l⁻¹ until the final stages of drying, maintained by photosynthesis of dense phytoplankton.
- Salinity stayed moderate compared with African and Moroccan water bodies.
- Vertebrate disturbance (waterbirds, carp) may have released nutrients and distributed organic particles in the shrinking volume.

**9. Recovery phase.**
- Low populations persisted for many months after refilling, dominated by *Daphnia carinata*, *Boeckella fluvialis*, cyclopoid nauplii and copepodites, and *Bosmina meridionalis*.
- Adult *Mesocyclops* cf. *notius* did not appear until August 1978 and were not common until months later. The cyclopoids of the recovery phase were mainly juveniles, probably herbivorous.
- The characteristic species of the filling phase were rare littoral and benthic forms, including *Diaphanosoma excisum*, *Pleuroxus aduncus*, *Chydorus eurynotus*, *Ephemeroperus barroisi*, *Alona* cf. *davidi*, *Candonocypris* sp., *Newnhamia fenestrata* and *Australocyclops australis*.
- The authors interpret these as early successional specialists exploiting organic-rich detritus from dead macrophytes, phytoplankton and fish, and from flooded dryland vegetation.

**10. Weed beds and detritus.**
- Many *Brachionus* species, including the endemics *B. keikoa* and *B. dichotomus*, coincided with the large *Valisneria* development in early 1979.
- Chydorid peaks in 1979 (*Alona rectangula*, *Biapertura karua*, *Graptolebris testudinaria*) matched weed-bed growth. The 1977 chydorid peak instead matched open water, with *Chydorus sphaericus* and *Dunhevedia crassa* dominant.
- Detrital production supported short-lived high productivity of detritivorous chironomids (Maher, 1984; Maher & Carpenter, 1984), which in turn supported waterfowl production (Crome, 1986).

**11. Conclusions.**
- Drastic water-level fluctuation did not destroy the basic annual zooplankton cycle, though re-establishment took several months.
- The drying phase had high densities in a shrinking volume. The filling phase was characterised by rare successional or colonist species.
- Turbidity and detritus can be stimulatory or detrimental. The nature of suspended material matters: inorganic suspended material is likely depressive, while organic-rich material may suit some species.
- Lake Merrimajeel differs from small ephemeral pans and rainpools in several ways: water persists for years, colonists arrive from rivers and upstream swamps (not only resting stages), the water stayed relatively fresh and oxygenated during drying, and phyllopods were absent.
- The authors propose that the fugitive existence of species colonising briefly refilled basins, after a drawdown has eliminated the dominant plankton, helps explain the high plankton diversity of flood-plain regions such as the [[Murray-Darling Basin]].
