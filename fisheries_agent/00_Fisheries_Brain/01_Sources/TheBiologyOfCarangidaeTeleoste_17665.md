# The Biology of Carangidae in Natal Estuaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #carangidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the occurrence, biology and feeding ecology of [[Carangidae]] (jacks and trevallies) in [[Natal Estuaries]], South Africa, based on a two-year survey of seven estuaries. Of 17 carangid species recorded in estuaries (from at least 40 species in Natal waters), six were ecologically significant: [[Caranx Ignobilis]], [[Caranx Melampygus]], [[Caranx Papuensis]], [[Caranx Sexfasciatus]], [[Lichia Amia]] and [[Scomberoides Lysan]]. Only juveniles and subadults used estuaries. The authors examined recruitment, growth, diet, feeding periodicity, gill raker morphology, and salinity and turbidity tolerance. Carangids are largely predatory and occupy a high trophic position within estuarine fish communities, though their estuarine dependence varies by species.

## Background & Description

The Carangidae are described as an important group of largely piscivorous predators found in the coastal waters of all subtropical and tropical seas. Prior to this work, detailed biological studies of the family in the west Indian Ocean were limited to [[Williams 1965]] in East Africa, and little was known about the relative abundance or ecology of Natal estuarine carangids.

The study had three aims:
1. Identify carangid species of importance in the estuarine ecosystem.
2. Investigate their biology and ecological role in the community.
3. Assess the relative importance of estuaries in the life cycles of each species.

Most estuarine carangids were juveniles or subadults, consistent with patterns reported for estuarine fishes generally ([[Day et al. 1981]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study estuaries.** Seven systems were sampled, ranging from small closed estuaries (Fafa, Mdloti, Tongati) to large open and tidal systems (Mtamvuna, Mlalazi, [[St Lucia]], [[Kosi System]]). Surface areas ranged from 7.6 ha (Tongati) to 417 km² (St Lucia), and turbidity ranges varied widely across systems (for example, 1.6–86.0 NTU at Mtamvuna and 2.0–1472.0 NTU at St Lucia).

**Environmental measurements.** Turbidity was measured with a Hach nephelometer in nephelometric turbidity units (NTU), and salinity with a Goldberg optical salinometer. Species were grouped by [[Salinity Tolerance]] and [[Turbidity]] preference.

**Diet and energetics.** Prey fish energy values were measured with a ballistic bomb calorimeter, and length–dry weight regressions were derived for prey species. Diet was expressed as percentage energy contribution (%E) and percentage frequency of occurrence (%F), with [[Diet Analysis]] supported by [[Otolith Identification]] for heavily digested prey.

**Species diversity.** Carangid diversity increased from south to north, with the highest number of species (14) recorded at Kosi. Closed estuaries (Fafa, Mdloti, Tongati) were not strictly comparable to open systems, because recruitment depended on mouth-opening timing relative to spawning periods.

## Key Findings

### Occurrence and Abundance
- Six species were numerically significant in estuaries; the remainder were rare or occasional.
- Juveniles of *C. sexfasciatus* were the most abundant carangid at all sites except Kosi, and occurred in every estuary sampled.
- *C. melampygus* and *S. lysan* occurred only in the warmer Zululand estuaries, in relatively clear water.
- *L. amia* occurred mainly in turbid water, and was recorded at Mtamvuna, Mlalazi and St Lucia.

### Size Classes and Maturity
- No reproductively active adults were captured. Williams (1965) gives minimum lengths for first maturity of 540 mm (*C. ignobilis*), 400 mm (*C. sexfasciatus*) and 300 mm (*C. melampygus*), all well above the sizes captured in estuaries.
- *L. amia* subadults measured 240–545 mm; only two fish (95 mm at Mdloti and 115 mm at St Lucia) were smaller.

### Salinity and Turbidity Tolerance
- All six species are euryhaline. *C. melampygus*, *C. papuensis* and *S. lysan* were not recorded in near-freshwater conditions.
- *C. melampygus* was the least euryhaline (juveniles 6–35‰) and occurred only in clear water (≤2.4 NTU), not beyond Makhawulani Lake of the Kosi system.
- *C. sexfasciatus* tolerated the widest ranges (salinity 0.5–40‰; juveniles from 0.2 to 73.0 NTU), which the authors suggest explains its wider estuarine distribution.
- *C. ignobilis* juveniles (<200 mm) occurred in highly turbid water (>50 NTU) and in clear water at Kosi (<5 NTU).
- *L. amia* was not found below 7.5 NTU but occurred up to 76.0 NTU.
- *S. lysan* was absent from turbid water; fry and juveniles tolerated higher turbidity than subadults.

### Recruitment and Growth
- *C. ignobilis*: juveniles recruit in summer; insufficient samples allowed no growth analysis.
- *C. melampygus*: juveniles recruit at Kosi from October to March; length-frequency analysis gave no usable growth estimates, possibly due to high mobility.
- *C. papuensis*: two immature size classes (40–120 mm and 180–280 mm) suggested first and second year classes, implying growth of about 150 mm per year.
- *C. sexfasciatus*: recruitment (30–60 mm) occurs from October to April; growth of about 120 mm in the first and second years, with 1+ fish near 250 mm.
- *S. lysan*: fry enter estuaries at 20–30 mm in January–April; growth of about 120 mm per year in years one and two, with slower growth in year three (a mode of 330 mm).

### Diet and Trophic Role
- Subadults of all six species were predominantly piscivorous.
- *C. ignobilis* juveniles (40–199 mm) took penaeid prawns; subadults (200–550 mm) were almost entirely piscivorous, feeding on *Rhabdosargus sarba* and *Ambassis* spp.
- *C. melampygus* juveniles (<170 mm) fed mainly on the mysid *Mesopodopsis africana* and small penaeids; larger fish were almost entirely piscivorous.
- *C. papuensis* juveniles (40–160 mm) obtained 60% of diet energy from mysids and small penaeids; *Gilchristella aestuarius* was the major prey of subadults.
- *C. sexfasciatus* fish >200 mm were almost entirely piscivorous, especially on *Ambassis* spp.; fish of about 30 mm fed mainly on calanoid copepods (*Pseudodiaptomus*).
- *L. amia* was exclusively piscivorous, with *Mugilidae* the most common prey item, and a preference for slow-moving species such as *Thryssa vitrirostris* and *Sarotherodon mossambicus*.
- *S. lysan* fry (20–60 mm) fed on planktonic organisms and were lepidophagous (feeding on teleost scales), consistent with [[Major 1978]] for Hawaiian juveniles. Juveniles fed mainly on *M. africana* and juvenile penaeids; subadults shifted to fish, mainly *G. aestuarius* and *Croilia mossambica*.

### Gill Raker Morphology and Filter Feeding
- Scanning electron micrographs showed long gill rakers with well-developed denticles in *C. melampygus*, *C. papuensis* and *S. lysan*, suggesting [[Filter Feeding]] on mysids and penaeids.
- *C. melampygus* (110 mm): mean gill raker gap 0.1 mm, denticle length 0.02 mm.
- *C. papuensis*: mean gap 0.285 mm (97 mm fish) and 0.31 mm (216 mm fish).
- *S. lysan*: mean gap 0.085 mm (90 mm fish) and 0.285 mm (250 mm fish), with denticle length 0.03–0.12 mm, sufficient to retain all sizes of mysids.
- Subadults of *C. papuensis* and *S. lysan* retained filter-feeding structures and continued to consume mysids, indicating facultative filter feeding.

### Feeding Periodicity
- *C. ignobilis* juveniles fed during the day, peaking at 18:00.
- *C. melampygus* juveniles fed throughout the day, peaking at 09:00.
- *C. papuensis* subadults peaked at 15:00; juveniles peaked at 12:00.
- *C. sexfasciatus* juveniles peaked at 15:00; larger fish fed early morning and late evening.
- *S. lysan* juveniles and subadults both peaked at 15:00.
- Different feeding periodicities may reduce interspecific competition, especially among juveniles.

### Estuarine Dependence
- *C. melampygus* and *C. papuensis* were considered mobile, resident only briefly in estuaries, and more abundant at sea; they were judged not estuarine dependent.
- *S. lysan* appeared dependent on clear-water estuaries, or on similar low-turbidity, shallow sandy sea areas such as coral reef zones.
- *C. ignobilis* and *C. sexfasciatus* were judged well adapted to estuarine extremes and, if not estuarine dependent, occupy a very important trophic position.
- *C. sexfasciatus* is the only true piscivore in small closed estuaries.

### Shoaling Behaviour
- Juveniles of *C. melampygus*, *C. papuensis* and *S. lysan* formed mixed-species shoals; *S. lysan* juveniles and subadults were captured in single-class shoals of at least 100 individuals.
- Subadult *C. papuensis* formed large single-species shoals.
- Shoaling may facilitate capture of shoaling prey.

### Ecological Significance
- Carangids are among the most important piscivorous fishes in clear-water estuaries such as Kosi and Mlalazi, possibly together with Sphyraenidae.
- In more turbid open systems such as St Lucia, carangids were present but not among the major piscivorous predators.
