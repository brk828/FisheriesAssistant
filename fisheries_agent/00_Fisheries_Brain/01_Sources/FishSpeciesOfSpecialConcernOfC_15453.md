# Fish Species of Special Concern of California

## Executive Summary

This 1989 report, commissioned by the California Department of Fish and Game, represents a systematic assessment of the conservation status of California's native freshwater fish fauna. Of 113 recognized native species and subspecies, **65 are endemic to California**, a reflection of the long-term isolation of the state's drainage basins and the specialized adaptations required to persist in its variable aquatic environments (desert springs, flow-fluctuating rivers, shallow alkaline lakes, and sloughs).

The report documents that **5 taxa have gone extinct**, **16 are formally listed as threatened or endangered**, and an additional **44 taxa** are newly classified in this report as warranting special concern due to declining populations, restricted ranges, or insufficient data. Combined, **72 taxa (64% of native freshwater fish taxa)** require some form of protective management. The authors developed a five-tier classification system (Class 1–5) to triage taxa by extinction risk, forming the analytical backbone of the [[California Native Fish Status Assessment]].

## Background & Description

As a multi-taxon status report rather than a single-species account, morphological detail is not centrally treated; however, the report notes:

- Standardized measurement protocol: fish lengths reported as **total length (TL)**, **fork length (FL)**, or **standard length (SL)**, with SL preferred where data allow.
- Many included taxa are **morphologically and ecologically distinctive isolated populations** (e.g., subspecies of [[Tui Chub]] and [[California Roach]]) whose taxonomic status remains poorly resolved, underscoring a need for further systematic work.
- The **rainbow trout** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) complex is cited as an example where detailed morphological/genetic work (Berg 1987) has revealed that many isolated populations likely merit distinct taxonomic recognition — a pattern the authors suggest is probably widespread but under-documented in other polytypic genera such as *Gila* and *Cottus*.
- Sculpin taxonomy (genus *Cottus*) is flagged as needing revision; Hopkirk (1973) proposed at least three subspecies of [[Prickly Sculpin]] in California, with the Clear Lake population considered ecologically distinctive enough to warrant separate status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report frames California's native fish fauna within a mosaic of specialized, often extreme, freshwater habitats:

- **Desert springs**: e.g., [[Saratoga Springs Pupfish]], [[Amargosa Pupfish]], [[Shoshone Pupfish]], [[Salt Creek Pupfish]] — isolated relict populations in the Death Valley/Amargosa drainage system.
- **Alkaline lakes and closed basins**: e.g., [[Eagle Lake Tui Chub]], [[Goose Lake Tui Chub]], [[Goose Lake Sucker]], [[Goose Lake Redband Trout]] — endemics restricted to single lake basins in the Lahontan and Goose Lake systems.
- **High-gradient, flow-variable rivers**: anadromous salmonids such as [[Spring Chinook Salmon]], [[Sacramento River Winter Chinook Salmon]], [[Coho Salmon]], and [[Summer Steelhead]] depend on intact riverine connectivity now severely disrupted by damming and diversion.
- **Coastal streams and estuaries**: [[Tidewater Goby]], [[Santa Ana Threespine Stickleback]], [[Coastal Cutthroat Trout]], and [[Russian River Tule Perch]] occupy narrow coastal ranges vulnerable to habitat loss.
- **Anthropogenic threats** cited throughout: damming/diversion of streams, draining of lakes and marshes, water pollution, and introduction of non-native fish species — identified as the primary drivers of decline across nearly all taxa treated.
- Several taxa (e.g., [[Sacramento Perch]], [[Arroyo Chub]], [[Volcano Creek Golden Trout]]) persist regionally only because of successful introductions **outside** their native range, even as native populations continue to decline — illustrating a niche-displacement conservation paradox.

## Key Findings

- **Endemism**: 65 of 113 California native freshwater fish taxa (~58%) are state-endemic, driven by long isolation of drainage basins.
- **Extinction and Listing Status**: 5 taxa extinct; 16 taxa already federally/state listed as threatened or endangered (Table 1).
- **New Special Concern Designations**: 44 additional taxa identified in this report as needing protection, including 4 species/subspecies and 2 salmonid runs recommended for immediate threatened/endangered listing (Class 1).
- **Cumulative Risk**: 72 taxa total (extinct + listed + special concern) = **64% of California's native freshwater fish fauna** requires conservation attention.
- **Five-Class Risk System** established:
  - **Class 1**: Meets state threatened/endangered criteria; recommended for listing (e.g., [[Delta Smelt]], [[Sacramento River Winter Chinook Salmon]], [[Santa Ana Speckled Dace]], [[Shoshone Pupfish]]).
  - **Class 2**: Low, scattered, or highly localized populations in decline (e.g., [[Kern Brook Lamprey]], [[Goose Lake Lamprey]], [[Kern River Rainbow Trout]]).
  - **Class 3**: Uncommon but occupying much of natural range; monitor for accelerating decline (e.g., [[Klamath River Lamprey]], [[Modoc Brook Lamprey]], [[Hardhead]], [[Mountain Sucker]]).
  - **Class 4**: Declined natively but thriving from introductions elsewhere (e.g., [[Sacramento Perch]], [[Arroyo Chub]], [[Volcano Creek Golden Trout]]).
  - **Class 5**: Common/stable, excluded from special concern list, though 4 taxa flagged for status re-investigation: [[Green Sturgeon]], [[Blue Chub]], [[Lahontan Speckled Dace]], and [[Mountain Whitefish]].
- **Taxonomic Uncertainty**: Numerous undescribed or poorly described subspecies (especially within *Gila*, *Lavinia*, *Rhinichthys*, and *Cottus*) are included based on Hubbs et al. (1979) and expert judgment, highlighting systemic gaps in California ichthyological taxonomy.
- **Habitat as Proxy for Broader Decline**: The authors explicitly note that native fish decline signals broader degradation of aquatic ecosystems, likely affecting many poorly studied endemic invertebrates and aquatic plants as well — linking fish conservation to whole-ecosystem health, consistent with the goals of the [[Endangered Species Act 1973]] framework applied here.
