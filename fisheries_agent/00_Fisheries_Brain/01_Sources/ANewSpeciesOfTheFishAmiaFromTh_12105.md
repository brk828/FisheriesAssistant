# A New Species of the Fish *Amia* from the Middle Eocene of British Columbia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #amiidae

## Executive Summary

This paper describes *Amia hesperia* sp. nov., a fossil amiid fish from Middle Eocene freshwater shales of the [[Allenby Formation]], [[Princeton Group]], in south-central [[British Columbia]]. The description rests on a single partially articulated skeleton (holotype UAVP 14758) and several disarticulated skull and jaw bones. The author assigns the species to the genus [[Amia]] rather than to [[Kindleia]], because it lacks the styliform teeth and body-proportion features that characterize Kindleia. The fish is reconstructed as a deep-bodied, large-jawed piscivore. The paper also revisits the taxonomic status of Kindleia and compares the new species with other North American and European amiids.

## Background & Description

**Context of discovery.** Previously known Eocene *Amia* records from British Columbia, Washington, and Oregon were mostly undiagnostic scales, which are very common in these deposits. The specimens were found during palaeobotanical work on silicified plant fossils in a chert and coal outcrop. [[James Basinger]] discovered a trionychid turtle in the shales above the chert, and the author collected the fish in 1977 and 1978.

**Holotype and material.**
- Holotype: UAVP 14758, an almost complete, partially articulated fish preserved as a mould in hard siliceous shale, with an estimated total length of about 55 cm.
- Non-type material: five specimens (UAVP 13801, 13804, 13805, 13806, 13812), including a dentary and maxilla, extrascapular, infraorbitals, branchial tooth plates, and an opercle. All were collected within a few centimetres of the holotype.
- Etymology: the epithet comes from the Latin *hesperius*, meaning "western."

**Diagnostic and descriptive features.**
- Deep body with square parietals (parietal/frontal ratio 0.37) and long, narrow frontals (width-to-length ratio 0.41) with a shallow orbital excavation ("orbital concavity ratio" 0.117; dermosphenotic angle 136°).
- Large nasals without an anterior notch; lachrymal with a posterior notch; enlarged fourth infraorbital.
- Deep maxilla with a long, deep supramaxilla; deep mandible with a dentary bearing large, pointed teeth.
- Opercle deep relative to its length (length:height ratio 0.90).
- Parasphenoid with a posterior ramus 0.84 times the length of the anterior ramus; tooth patch restricted to the posterior part.
- Vomers with conical, sharply pointed teeth confined to a short anterior patch.
- Cleithrum with arms meeting at an obtuse angle of about 120°, unlike the right angle in [[Amia Calva]] and [[Amia Uintaensis]].
- Pectoral fin with about 16 rays; anal fin with about 9 rays; dorsal fin originating above the 17th trunk centrum.
- Scales about two-thirds as wide as long, with an apical focus and a horseshoe-shaped lamellar rim. They are thinner than those of Cretaceous and Palaeocene *Kindleia fragosa* but thicker than those of *A. calva*.

**Systematics.** The species is placed in Class Osteichthyes, Order Amiiformes, Family Amiidae, Genus *Amia*. Its skull shares specializations with *A. calva*, *A. scutata*, and *A. uintaensis*. It is distinguished from each of these by a set of features (for example, frontal length, parasphenoid tooth patch, cleithrum angle, and opercle shape) summarized in the Key Findings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Depositional setting.** The fossils come from the fossiliferous layers about 550 m above the Princeton-Black Coal in the Allenby Formation, a freshwater sedimentary unit that yields abundant fish and insect assemblages at several localities in the [[Princeton Basin]]. The fish occur in shales with carbonized plants, including stems, twigs, dicotyledonous leaves, taxodiaceous shoots, seeds, ferns, and amber.

**Associated fauna.** The fish assemblage includes:
- *Amyzon* sp. (Catostomidae, suckers)
- *Libotonius* sp. (Percopsidae, trout-perches)
- Coprolites containing fish bones
- A trionychid turtle in the overlying shales

**Habitat inference.** Earlier workers (cited in the paper as [[Wilson 1980]]) interpreted these Eocene Amia-bearing localities as warm, shallow, swampy habitats, based on lithology and associated organisms. The author notes that this matches the habitat preferences of living *Amia calva*, which is confined to freshwaters of south-eastern North America.

**Age.** The deposits are Middle Eocene, based on potassium-argon ages of about 47–50 million years for the Allenby Formation and the occurrence of the mammal genus *Trogosus* in the formation. Similar-aged amiid scales occur in British Columbia and in the [[Klondike Mountain Formation]] of Washington State.

**Diet and feeding.** The large mouth and large, sharp teeth imply piscivory. This supports the earlier association of amiid scales with fish-bone-bearing coprolites at many Eocene localities in British Columbia and Washington. In contrast, [[Boreske 1974]] suggested that *Kindleia* relied more on molluscs, which is consistent with the styliform teeth seen in that genus.

**Biogeography.** Eocene amiid species in North America show a pattern in which *A. hesperia* occurs in the far west, while *A. uintaensis* and *K. fragosa* occur further east. The author suggests this pattern reflects separation by a north–south barrier, perhaps a continental divide similar to the one that today separates western and eastern fish faunas.

## Key Findings

1. **New species described.** *Amia hesperia* sp. nov. is based on the holotype UAVP 14758 and five non-type specimens. It is the first identifiable *Amia* skeleton recovered from the many amiid scales known from southern British Columbia.

2. **Assignment to *Amia*, not *Kindleia*.** The species lacks the main Kindleia specializations, namely styliform teeth on the coronoids, dermopalatines, and vomers, and the shorter, more compact body form.

3. **Synonymy debate resolved in favour of a distinct genus.** The author agrees with [[Gaudant 1980]] that several characters shared by *K. fragosa* and *A. kehreri* are derived, but argues that *Kindleia* should be retained as a separate genus. The proposed shared derived character list is extended to include the relatively short, wide frontal. The author also notes that the enlarged fourth infraorbital is not a diagnostic Kindleia feature, because it also occurs in *A. hesperia* and in *Enneles*.

4. **Distinguishing features from congeners.**
   - Versus *A. uintaensis*: slightly shorter frontals and parietals, unnotched and interdigitating nasals, posteriorly notched lachrymals, more angular fourth infraorbitals, and more obtusely angled cleithra.
   - Versus *A. scutata* and *A. calva*: longer frontals, shorter parietals, interdigitating nasals and frontals, and more obtusely angled cleithra.
   - Versus *A. calva* in particular: longer pterotics, larger fourth infraorbitals, and more extensive cleithral ornamentation.
   - Versus *A. robusta* (a Palaeocene–Oligocene European species): longer frontals, more rectangular parietals, and a longer parasphenoid tooth patch in *A. robusta*. *A. hesperia* has more obtusely angled cleithra.

5. **Body form.** The dorsal fin originates over the 17th trunk centrum, suggesting that *A. hesperia* was long-bodied, unlike the short, stout form of *K. fragosa*. The skull, however, is deep and large-jawed.

6. **Piscivorous feeding.** The large mouth and sharp teeth indicate piscivory, consistent with fish-bone-bearing coprolites found at many British Columbian and Washington Eocene localities.

7. **Prediction for related localities.** The author predicts that amiids from other Eocene localities in British Columbia and Washington will prove to be closely related to or conspecific with *A. hesperia*.

8. **Biogeographic implication.** Eocene amiids in North America form a west–east pattern, which the author links to a north–south barrier comparable to the continental divide that separates western and eastern fish faunas today.
