# Evaluation of the Status, Life History, and Habitat Requirements of Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the Upper Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report (Part 2 of a three-part study, FWS/OBS-77/62, September 1977) evaluates the status, life history, and habitat requirements of the endangered and threatened fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It was prepared by Ecology Consultants, Inc. with co-authors from Colorado State University and BioWest for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Office of Biological Services, funded under an [[Environmental Protection Agency]] interagency agreement. The authors state that the report combines published literature with substantial professional judgment and speculation, because a comprehensive database does not exist. The core concern is a conflict between energy and water development (coal, oil shale, uranium, irrigation, and recreation) and maintenance of an endemic fish fauna. The report covers the abiotic environment, biota, species accounts, six river basin descriptions, major factors of change, and research priorities.

## Background & Description

The upper basin comprises roughly 109,000 square miles draining to [[Lee Ferry]], Arizona. Streams begin as cold, clear, first-order headwaters and become large, warm, turbid third- to fourth-order rivers flowing through deep canyons. The native fauna evolved in isolation for a long period, producing high endemism. The report counts 13 native, 7 endemic, at least 27 exotic, and 6 threatened or endangered species in the system.

The listed or sensitive species include:

- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarkii pleuriticus*)
- [[Kendall Warm Springs Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus thermalis*)

A substantial section addresses speciation and taxonomy. Historical chub nomenclature in the genus *Gila* is confused by morphological variability and apparent hybridization. Earlier authors variously treated bonytail and roundtail chubs as one species or as subspecies. [[Holden and Stalnaker]] (1970) concluded that *G. robusta* and *G. elegans* are separate species. The report accepts *G. elegans* and *G. cypha* as valid species pending studies of the extent of hybridization, while noting that *Gila* hybrids are not known to be fertile.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Abiotic conditions.** The basin historically had large fluctuations in flow, turbidity, and temperature. Native fishes are adapted to high flows and turbidity and lack lacustrine adaptations. Reservoirs trap sediment, reduce turbidity and flow, and produce cold downstream releases. Sediment loads reaching the Gulf of California have also been greatly reduced.

**Biological components.**
- *Upper zone* (cold headwaters, "trout water"): primary production in periphytic algae; benthic invertebrates support resident salmonids.
- *Intermediate zone*: warmer, more nutrient-rich water; rocky substrate; catostomids and cyprinids increasingly common.
- *Lower zone* (large warm turbid rivers): primary production nearly absent; energy derives largely from allochthonous terrestrial input; zooplankton (copepods, cladocerans, ostracods) important for young fish. Invertebrate data are sparse.

**Land and water use.** Livestock grazing began in the early 1800s and covers about 84 percent of the upper basin. Mining, forestry, channelization, irrigation diversions, and dam construction altered habitat. Exotic species, particularly trout, competed with and hybridized with natives.

**Basin-specific context.**
- *Yampa:* the least-altered major tributary; no large impoundments; a critical source of cool, spawning-temperature water for the Green River.
- *Green:* Flaming Gorge Dam eliminated listed species from about 120 miles of river; the lower Green below the Yampa mouth is the best remaining habitat.
- *White:* oil shale and coal development expected; a proposed reservoir may affect a site where squawfish were collected in 1977.
- *Upper Colorado mainstem:* heavy depletions; Colorado cutthroat reduced to a few isolated populations.
- *San Juan:* Navajo Reservoir is linked to decline of roundtail chub in the lower river; the report judges native large-river species probably extirpated.
- *Dolores:* a depauperate fauna; the report considers future development unlikely to affect rare fishes.
- *Gunnison:* heavily depleted by agriculture; a possible tail-water threat from coal mining; Colorado cutthroat status unknown and requires survey.

## Key Findings

1. **Dams and flow regulation are the principal cause of decline.** Impoundments replace lotic habitat with lentic habitat that favors introduced, lacustrine-adapted species. Cold hypolimnetic releases below Flaming Gorge and Glen Canyon dams suppressed reproduction; the report cites reproduction loss in the Green River between Flaming Gorge and the Yampa mouth (about 65 miles).
2. **Exotic fishes compete with and prey on natives.** Redside shiner first found in the upper Green in 1938 and dominant by 1959-1960, as squawfish declined. Channel catfish and red shiner were abundant in the lower basin. Non-native trout hybridize with and displace native cutthroat trout.
3. **Hybridization threatens genetic integrity.** Hybrids of razorback and flannelmouth suckers were found at varying rates (for example, 18 percent of 88 razorback-area specimens in one Green River study and 43 percent in another). Intergrades between *G. elegans* and *G. cypha* appear common, while only three of 309 *Gila* specimens were *robusta* × *elegans* hybrids.
4. **Parasitism may disadvantage squawfish.** The tapeworm *Proteocephalus ambloplites* infected up to 80 percent of squawfish intestines examined. The report suggests it is non-native and may have been introduced with exotic fishes.
5. **Livestock grazing damages riparian habitat.** Trout biomass was reported 3 to 4 times greater in protected areas; one Oregon study found 175 lb/acre in good riparian habitat versus 48 lb/acre in degraded habitat.
6. **Logging and mining raise temperature and sediment loads.** Clearcutting raised weekly maximum May temperatures by up to 13°C in one cited study; buffer strips of 50 to 100 feet prevented detectable temperature increases.
7. **Uranium milling caused severe local effects.** Fish life was eliminated from large sections of the lower San Miguel and Dolores rivers.
8. **Projected development threatens remaining populations.** The report judges that the Juniper Springs project on the Yampa would likely eliminate the main spawning habitat of the squawfish and razorback sucker in the Green. Flow reductions from energy and water projects are considered almost certain to harm rare fishes; the Green River is the last system where populations remain, so it is probably the best remaining habitat.
9. **Research gaps remain large.** Little is known about spawning, age and growth, food habits, and movements of bonytail and humpback chubs. Humpback chub has been collected only sporadically and is "considered extirpated" above the Yampa mouth. Bonytail was documented in the Green River in Utah only.
10. **Data limitations are acknowledged.** The authors state that speculation and professional judgment make up a significant part of the conclusions, given the limited database and the sensitivity of the subject.
