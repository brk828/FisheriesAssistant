# Fish Distribution in the Blue and San Francisco Rivers, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines fish distributions in the [[Blue River]] and [[San Francisco River]] of southeastern Arizona, using collections made from June 1994 to July 1995. It argues that western native fishes are under pressure from introduced species, which benefit from altered, more stable stream flows. The study describes the abundant fishes of the region, compares potential interactions among species through catch-per-effort graphs and regression, maps localities in a [[Geographical Information System]], and interprets the patterns. The author stresses that the data are limited and that correlation values should be read cautiously in a species-poor western system.

## Background & Description

**Context and conservation rationale**

- Western North American freshwater fishes are less diverse than eastern ones: about 200 species in the west versus as many as 600 in the east.
- Citing [[Minckley]] and [[Deacon]] (1991), about 122 fish species listed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as threatened or endangered, or under consideration for listing, lie west of the continental divide.
- Eastern fishes are described as "specialists" that tolerate competition, while western fishes are "generalists" with well-defined niches and weak competitive ability.
- Dams and flow-stabilizing structures have removed the high-flow flushing that once limited introduced eastern species, creating connected habitat that favors them.

**Study area**

- The Blue River rises in New Mexico, flows through canyons, receives many tributaries, and joins the San Francisco River. It is described as unaltered and remote.
- The San Francisco River rises in Arizona, crosses into New Mexico, and returns to Arizona in Greenlee County near the mining towns of Clifton and Morenci. It has deeper pools, is less remote, and is more altered by humans.

**Sampling methods**

- Collectors included Paul Marsh, Brian Bagley, Tim Hurst, Terry Inman, Glen Knowles and others from the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest office.
- Fish were sampled with a Smith-Root model 15-A gasoline-powered backpack [[Electrofishing]] unit and, rarely, a 6 × 4 ft, 1/8-inch mesh nylon seine.
- Species counts and habitat data were recorded for each locality, and catch per unit effort (CPE) was calculated.

**Species accounts (summary)**

Native species abundant in the Blue River system:

- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*): small, usually under 100 mm, found from low desert to cool conifer-zone streams.
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*): 45–63 mm; the only native fish recorded in all seven western U.S. freshwater drainages in the text.
- [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*): large sucker, up to 800 mm; hybridizes occasionally with [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), 100–280 mm.
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*): small gravel-riffle fish, about 60 mm at maturity; listed as Threatened.
- [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*): historically present in the Blue River, not recently collected there; listed as Endangered.
- [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*): historically present in the Blue River and New Mexico; listed as Endangered.

Non-native species:

- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*): widely introduced sport fish; hybridizes with Gila and Apache trout.
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*): introduced bait and forage fish with strong negative effects on natives.
- [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*): introduced predatory sport fish, most abundant in the San Francisco River.
- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*): introduced sport and commercial species, also linked to native declines in the San Francisco River.
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): widely introduced food fish, found in all waters below 2,000 m.

Species listed as potential but not found in recent collections (seven in total): smallmouth bass, spikedace, largemouth bass, mosquito fish, flathead minnow, brown trout and roundtail chub.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and flow**

- Native fishes evolved in high-variance systems with flood and drought cycles. Introduced eastern species are flushed out by high flows, but flow regulation has reduced this effect.
- The Blue River's frequent flooding is cited as preventing establishment of non-natives. The [[Lower Colorado River]] is described as heavily altered and supporting substantial non-native populations.
- Deeper pools in the San Francisco River favor large non-native fishes such as carp and flathead catfish.

**Interactions and feeding**

- Most species are opportunistic omnivores feeding on algae, detritus and invertebrates, so overlap in diet and habitat is likely. For example, speckled dace and desert suckers have overlapping feeding habitats.
- Non-native fishes are believed to compete with natives for food and space. [[Competitive Displacement]] is a central idea, with the red shiner singled out as a major threat.
- Predation by native fish is rarely reported, but [[Flathead Catfish]] are described as causing severe native declines where abundant.

**Threats to native species**

- Loach minnow now occupies about 15% of its historic range. Of approximately 2,600 km of historic stream habitat, 2,220 km no longer supports the species.
- Causes of decline cited include dams and irrigation that alter natural flow variation, habitat destruction from livestock grazing and four-wheel-drive vehicles, and non-native invasions.
- Apache trout's range is reduced to about 48 km or less, from an estimated 965 km. Hybridization with rainbow trout has substantially damaged its gene pool.
- Warming streams may have destroyed Gila trout habitat.

**Biogeography**

- The author's hypothesis is that fish are zoned longitudinally in Arizona, with a broad transitional zone between low-desert and upland faunas.
- Elevation ranges given for species include speckled dace at 2,000–3,000 m and loach minnow at 4,300–7,100 ft, with Sonoran sucker at 300 to over 2,000 m.

## Key Findings

**Graph-based statistical results** (the author notes these were calculated from limited, unequal sampling effort):

- **Graph #1, speckled dace CPE vs. elevation:** R² = 0.005, with no meaningful correlation. The author concludes that elevation changes within the drainages were not large enough to control speckled dace distribution.
- **Graph #2, desert sucker CPE vs. elevation:** R² = 0.033, a weak correlation. Desert suckers were more abundant at higher elevations, and the sampled area is described as a transitional elevation zone for them.
- **Graph #3, total CPE vs. speckled dace CPE:** R² = 0.884 (y = 12.815 + 1.1239x). Speckled dace make up the largest part of total CPE.
- **Graph #4, stream rank (by size) vs. number of species:** R² = 0.378. Larger streams held more fish species, which the author attributes to greater habitat and space.

**Distribution patterns (Discussion)**

- Species richness per site was consistent with a pattern of lower biodiversity in headwaters and downstream reaches and higher diversity in mid-reach sections.
- Speckled dace, typically found at higher elevations, occurred throughout the reach. Longfin dace, typically found in low desert, also penetrated the whole study area.

**Conclusions**

- The longitudinal zonation hypothesis was described as clearly supported.
- The author also states that local conditions alone affect fish distributions and that no consistent pattern of environmental change with altitude was found.
- No definite trends in species distribution or abundance could be derived, except that more species occur in larger habitats.

**Methodological limitations**

- Electroshocking time varied among localities, so fish abundances may be under- or overestimated at some sites.
- Correlation coefficients are considered low by conventional statistical standards. The author interprets them more leniently because western streams naturally have less competition.

**Historic records**

- The source includes numerous site-level collection records for Blue River stations, Raspberry, Coleman, Buckaloo and Strayhorse creeks, and San Francisco River sites (Evans Point, Clifton, 3,400 ft contour). These list species such as *Catostomus insignis*, *Pantosteus clarki*, *Rhinichthys osculus*, *Agosia chrysogaster*, *Salmo trutta*, *Pylodictis olivaris* and *Ictalurus punctatus*.

**Management relevance**

- Gila trout is described as a potential native game fish that could benefit from proper management.
- Continued conservation of western native fishes is identified as a priority, particularly controlling non-native species and protecting natural flow variation.
