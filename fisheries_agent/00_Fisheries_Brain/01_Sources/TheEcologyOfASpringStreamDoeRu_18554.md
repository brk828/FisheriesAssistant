# The Ecology of a Spring Stream: Doe Run, Meade County, Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Wildlife Monograph by W. L. Minckley (University of Louisville, 1963) presents a multi-year, integrated study of Doe Run, an unpolluted, partly modified spring stream in eastern Meade County, Kentucky, that drains karst limestone terrain into the [[Ohio River]]. The work covers physical flow and temperature regimes, water chemistry, algae and higher plants, benthic invertebrates, 66 fish species, and the trophic structure of the community. Research was sponsored by the [[United States Atomic Energy Commission]] under contract with the University of Louisville, and intensive sampling ended when the lower creek was impounded in July 1961. The monograph argues that streams possess their own "climates" and that ecologically equivalent communities should be compared across habitats rather than only taxonomic units. Fishes are presented as superimposed on the invertebrate and plant assemblages, and detritus is shown to be central to the food web.

## Background & Description

Doe Run is a spring stream about 9.7 miles long, with its main spring issuing from a foundered cave in the St. Louis Limestone at about 575 feet above mean sea level. It flows north-northeast and enters the Ohio River at about 374 feet, giving an average gradient of 20.7 feet per mile. The stream is about 30 feet wide, narrowing to 6 feet at some riffles and widening to over 50 feet at some pools.

Human influence has been substantial. Between 1780 and 1825, two water grist mills and a sawmill were established, along with a tannery, and dams were built on the creek (three remained at the time of study). Brine wells and natural gas production followed after 1860. Riparian clearing and earth moving accompanied impoundment of the lower creek by the Doe Valley Corporation.

Five permanent sampling stations were established: Station I at the source, Station II near Mile 1.9, Station III at Mile 3.1 (in the marl section, below a road culvert crossing), Station IV near Mile 4.8, and Station V near Mile 7.7 (subject to backwater from the Ohio River).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical environment.** Doe Run is a second-magnitude spring (average discharge 10–100 cfs). The modal discharge at all stations was 20–40 cfs, with average values inflated by two months of extreme flow. Discharge at Station I averaged about 65 cfs, about 140 cfs at Station III, and near 250 cfs at Station V. Water temperature at Station I was consistently 13.0–13.5°C at modal flow, while downstream stations reached maxima of 20.0°C (Station III) and 25.6°C (Station V). Impoundments produced surface temperatures above 25°C and density currents within the mill pools.

**Chemical environment.** Dissolved oxygen at Station I was usually 70–85 percent of saturation, with supersaturation in vegetated pools in daylight. Water at the spring had high alkalinity (200–280 mg/l) and high carbon dioxide. Between Mile 2.82 and Mile 5.3, calcium carbonate precipitated to form marl. Total iron, phosphate phosphorus, and nitrate nitrogen were measured, with nitrate declining downstream, which the author attributed to biological uptake and to fertilizer inputs from the watershed.

**Biota.** Benthic algae dominated over phytoplankton. Key taxa included [[Fissidens Moss]] (*Fissidens julianus*), *Batrachospermum*, *Lemanea*, *Cladophora glomerata*, *Vaucheria*, and *Nitella flexilis*. Higher plants included watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*), forget-me-not (*Myosotis scorpioides*), and water milfoil (*Myriophyllum heterophyllum*).

Invertebrate communities were dominated by crustaceans (*Asellus bivittatus*, *Gammarus minus*, *G. bousfieldi*) in spring-influenced moss beds, and by mayflies, caddisflies, and midges in downstream riffles. Crayfish included *Cambarus bartoni* and *Orconectes rusticus*.

## Key Findings

- **Fish fauna:** 66 species were collected. Thirteen of 31 "river fishes" maintained definite ranges in the creek, and 18 of 32 rare species were considered strays from the Ohio River.
- **Fish distribution drivers:** Stream gradient, temperature, and the marl section in the midstream controlled fish distribution. Little reproduction occurred upstream of Mile 5.3 for most species, probably due to low temperatures and marl deposition.
- **Resident fishes:** Only *Catostomus commersoni*, *Rhinichthys atratulus*, and *Cottus carolinae* maintained populations through reproduction at Station I. Sculpins comprised more than 75 percent of fish collected there.
- **Introduced trout:** Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*) stocked in 1939 and 1955 did not reproduce successfully, with one redd found in May 1960 that was destroyed by a flood.
- **Invertebrates:** About 150 macrobenthic taxa were recorded. Fissidens beds were the most productive habitat, with maxima of 18,708 and 20,004 individuals/ft² at Stations I and II.
- **Marl formation:** Marl was deposited by algal and moss activity. The author estimated rapid accumulation, with 63 annual-type rings counted in one marl block, averaging about 25 mm width.
- **Trophic structure:** Detritus, both autochthonous and allochthonous, was at least as important as primary production as a food source. Ten of 17 fishes examined ate substantial detritus.
- **Climate concept:** The author proposes that streams have climates analogous to terrestrial ones, with "climax" communities defined by stream type and that riffles can act as quasi-climax habitats.
- **Management implication:** Impoundment and clearing altered sediment, turbidity, and temperature, with turbidity at Station V rising to a mean of 80 units after clearing, compared with 12.6 units at Station III.
