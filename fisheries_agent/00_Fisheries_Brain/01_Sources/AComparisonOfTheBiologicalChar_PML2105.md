# Biological Characteristics of Sarotherodon niloticus Compared with S. aureus and Other Nile Delta Tilapia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the biology of two cultured tilapias, [[Sarotherodon Niloticus]] and [[Sarotherodon Aureus]], with two co-occurring species, [[Sarotherodon Galilaeus]] and [[Tilapia Zillii]], in the lower Nile and Nile Delta of Egypt. It was carried out as part of a resource survey for a major fish farm development. The work covers identification and distribution, age and growth from scales, spawning seasonality, fecundity, hybridization, and salinity tolerance. The central finding is that *S. niloticus* grows faster than *S. aureus* after the first year, while *S. aureus* appears to invest more energy in reproduction through a longer spawning season and multiple spawnings. No evidence of natural hybridization was found, and *T. zillii* is the most salt-tolerant species.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and identification:** The two *Sarotherodon* species were only recently separated and were often confused in earlier work. The most reliable field difference is dorsal spine count: 83.8% of Egyptian *S. niloticus* had 17 dorsal spines, and *S. aureus* had a lower proportion with that count. Dorsal fin lappets are always dark in *S. niloticus*, but red or pale in *S. aureus*. *S. niloticus* also has a completely barred tail, while *S. aureus* has a red caudal edge.
- **Range:** *S. aureus* is found in the "soudanian" regions of West Africa, the middle Niger and Chad Basin, the lower Nile, and most of Israel. In Israel, the natural range of *S. niloticus* is probably limited to a few coastal rivers, principally the Yarkon.
- **Use in culture:** Both species are used extensively for fish culture in Africa, the Middle East, and elsewhere. *S. aureus* has performed very successfully in Israeli culture.
- **Distribution in Egypt:** All four species occur in the shallow, productive northern coastal lakes ([[Lake Maryut]], Idku, and [[Lake Manzalla]]), in delta canals and drains, and in government fish farms. *S. aureus* was not found more than a few kilometres south of Cairo, nor in [[Lake Nasser]], where *S. galilaeus* is dominant. The authors conclude that *S. aureus* is confined to the Nile Delta.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites and sampling.** Fish were sampled from fishermen's catches at Lake Maryut (landing ground by the desert road), and at Damietta and Port Said on Lake Manzalla, during April and August. Standard length was measured, and scales were mounted for ring counts.

**Salinity.** Salinity is a key environmental variable in the Nile Delta. Drainage waters are often saline, and shallow ponds can reach 12–16‰ through soil salt and evaporation even when their source water is below 10‰. Ponds near Lake Manzalla may approach seawater salinity, and the Manzalla government fish farm reaches about 11‰ at times. Lake Qarun has become progressively more saline since the 1920s.

**Temperature and spawning cues.** Israeli observations indicate that display and breeding behaviour begin at about 20 °C and spawning can occur at 22 °C. In Lake Manzalla, March monthly temperatures ranged 15.6–19.1 °C and April 20.5–26.7 °C. Pond monthly averages do not exceed 20 °C until May, which helps explain the later spawning in ponds.

**Food and growth.** In Lake Maryut, phytoplankton productivity begins to rise between April and May and peaks in July. This timing coincides with the gonad-maturation period and the formation of scale rings.

## Key Findings

### 1. Age and growth from scales

- Scale rings were read from the key scale above the anus (third row on the flank) and checked against shoulder scales. Length at ring formation was back-calculated using the Tesch (1968) method.
- Rings appear to be associated with spawning. *S. niloticus* shows a peak of ring formation in April–May, matching its spawning peak. *S. aureus* shows ring build-up through summer, consistent with a more protracted spawning season.
- Two ring types were identified: an "erosion" type at the scale edge, linked to spawning, and an innermost "juvenile" ring formed by fish spawned late in the season (around August–September). Juvenile rings occurred in 76% of *S. niloticus* and 86% of *S. aureus* in Lake Maryut (as reported in the text).
- Two rings at one location in *S. aureus* suggest at least two spawnings per season.
- A length–scale relationship was derived: L = 0.267S + 1.6, where L is length (cm) and S is scale diameter (arbitrary micrometre units).

### 2. Growth rates

- Growth over the first year is very similar for both species. After the first year, *S. aureus* slows markedly.
- Student's t-tests showed no significant difference in size after the first year at either lake. In every later year, the difference was significant at both sites.
- The largest *S. aureus* recorded was 24 cm (Lake Manzalla). The text reports the largest *S. niloticus* as "82 cm", which is probably an OCR error. Fishermen state that *S. niloticus* can reach 40 cm.
- Excluding fish with juvenile rings raised the mean length of *S. niloticus* after the first winter in Lake Maryut from 11.1 cm to 12.9 cm.
- *S. galilaeus* grows at a rate similar to *S. niloticus* in earlier data, and slightly slower in Lake Manzalla. *T. zillii* grows more slowly, but its growth in Lake Maryut and Lake Qarun is very similar despite a large salinity difference.

### 3. Size and fecundity

- The size at first maturity coincides with the size at the end of the first year, which suggests that *S. aureus* allocates proportionally more energy to reproduction.
- Fecundity was estimated from ovary counts (preserved in formalin, one ovary counted and doubled). The relationship for both species, pooled, is log F = log 1.88 + 2.28 log L (abstract). Body-text variants of this equation differ in OCR, so the abstract form is used here.
- Fecundity is more closely related to the square of length than to the cube, unlike most fishes. Smaller fish therefore produce more eggs per gram of body weight.
- Fecundity of *S. galilaeus* is similar to that of the two *Sarotherodon* species. *T. zillii* is somewhat more fecund.

### 4. Spawning season

- *S. niloticus* has a discrete spawning peak in April–May.
- *S. aureus* spawns from May to September, with at least two spawnings within this period. One female was found with eggs in her mouth and mature ovaries in April.
- *S. aureus* spawns in shallow, weedy areas, building saucer-shaped nests about 40 cm in diameter and as little as 1 m apart. *S. niloticus* was not seen in these locations and, according to fishermen, spawns in deeper, more open water.
- Pond spawning starts later than in the lake. Pond fish showed no reproductive activity in February, and gonads were still developing in late April, when lake fish were mature.
- *T. zillii* also breeds in the lake (nest-building behaviour was observed in Lake Manzalla), and it probably has a lengthy breeding season as well.

### 5. Hybridization

- Hybridization can occur when males of one species are stocked with females of the other. It often produces a skewed sex ratio, sometimes approaching 100% males.
- In Israel, crossing was used for [[Monosex Culture]]. Reliability suffered from strain variability and contamination, so crosses were eventually restricted to indoor laboratories.
- Several hundred fish from the Nile Delta were examined. Only two possible hybrids were found, both from one pond at a government fish farm. The authors conclude that natural hybridization does not occur.
- Electrophoretic analysis of muscle proteins (McAndrew and Majumdar, 1983) showed clear differences between Lake Manzalla fish, supporting the species distinction.
- Two golden (probably melanin-deficient) *S. aureus* individuals were found around Lake Manzalla. If they can be used as parents, they could provide a convenient genetic marker.

### 6. Salinity tolerance

Upper estimates of salinities allowing unimpaired growth:

| Species | Upper salinity estimate (‰) |
|---|---|
| *T. zillii* | 29 |
| *S. galilaeus* | 16–20 |
| *S. aureus* | 10–16 |
| *S. niloticus* | 6–10 |

- *T. zillii* is the most salt-tolerant. It survived 29‰ in earlier work, has bred in Lake Manzalla at about 40‰ NaCl, and is the only tilapia found in Lake Qarun, where it persists despite rising salinity.
- *S. galilaeus* disappeared from Lake Qarun and from saline Lake Manzalla areas, suggesting lower tolerance than *T. zillii*.
- *S. niloticus* disappeared from Lake Qarun at about 16‰. In Lake Maryut it occurs at salinities up to about 10‰, and it is the first species to disappear along the salinity gradient of Lake Manzalla.
- Experiments with *S. aureus* in brackish ponds gave a mean mortality of 20.8% at the higher salinity, compared with about 8% in freshwater controls (Chervinski, 1961, 1966). It can survive in 50‰ seawater, though larval survival is low.
- Fishelson (1980) found that *T. zillii*, *S. mossambicus*, *S. galilaeus*, and *S. niloticus* could all be acclimated to full-strength seawater, but *S. niloticus* showed the highest mortality in hypersaline water. Hybrids were tolerant of high salinity.

## Conclusions and Management Implications

- *S. niloticus* grows faster than *S. aureus* beyond the first year and may be better suited to culture in lower-salinity conditions. *S. aureus* may be preferable in slightly more saline ones.
- The faster growth of *S. niloticus* may reflect its lower reproductive investment, with a shorter spawning period and possibly more frequent spawning. The authors note this is a hypothesis.
- *T. zillii* has slower growth and higher fecundity, which limits its value for culture. Its salinity tolerance nevertheless allows it to contribute to saline pond polyculture, as it already does with grey mullets and gilthead bream in northern coastal lagoons.
- *S. galilaeus* grows well in lakes and has high salinity tolerance, but it has been regarded as disappointing for culture.
- The lack of hybridization in Egypt means that pure *S. niloticus* and *S. aureus* stocks should be treated as a valuable resource. Care is needed to avoid genetic contamination, particularly through hybrids and transfers of stock. Widespread transport of stocks of uncertain origin could blur local species differences and complicate selective breeding for domestication.
