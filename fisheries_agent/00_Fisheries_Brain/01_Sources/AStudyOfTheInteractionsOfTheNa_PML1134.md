# Interactions of Loach Minnow with Longfin Dace, Spikedace, and Sonoran Sucker in a Laboratory Setting

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 laboratory study by Tim Hurst, [[Arizona State University]], examined how [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) interact with three native Arizona fishes: [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). Six ten-gallon aquaria were used, and interactions ("hits," defined as one fish attacking another) were tallied and analyzed with [[Analysis of Variance]] (ANOVA) and t-tests. All three species interacted with loach minnows to some degree, but spikedace produced by far the most interactions. The author attributes this to the active, aggressive behavior of spikedace and the territorial defense of male loach minnows around their undercut areas. The study is motivated by the decline of native southwestern fishes, two of which (loach minnow and spikedace) are listed as [[Threatened Species]] by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

## Background & Description

**Study purpose.** The paper states that no similar study of these four species had been done before. Previous work documented interactions between these fishes and the introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), but the literature only stated that the four species would interact in the wild without describing how. The author hypothesized that, because of similar habitat and food requirements, the fishes would interact extensively in the laboratory.

**Loach Minnow.** A small stream fish of swift riffles in water typically less than 6 inches deep. It has a small swim bladder, so it sinks unless swimming vigorously. Life span is about 24 months. It is an opportunistic omnivore feeding mostly on aquatic insect larvae, stores adipose tissue during summer to survive winter, and spawns from March to June. Both sexes guard nests, which the paper describes as rare among cyprinids and important for studying the evolution of parental care. Habitat destruction has made it rarer each year, and it occupies only a fraction of its historic range.

**Spikedace.** A small stream fish of pools and moderately swift creeks, usually in water less than 1 meter deep. It aggregates in slack zones along gravel-sand bars, eddies, and the downstream edges of riffles. It eats mainly mayfly adults and nymphs plus drifting material, spawns from April through June, and lives about 13 months, rarely more than 24. Its range has been widely reduced by dams, channel alteration, riparian destruction, water diversion, groundwater pumping, and nonnative introductions.

**Longfin Dace.** Still occupies most of its native range, including sandy-bottomed streams in Arizona below about 1,500 m (elevation given as "1,500 in" in the OCR text). It tolerates temperature extremes and low oxygen, is an opportunistic omnivore eating mainly insects and algae, and spawns from December to July. Males are highly territorial about sand nests.

**Sonoran Sucker.** A medium-sized catostomid, typically 100 to 200 mm long. It prefers turbulent water and rifles near boulders, while adults occupy deeper undercut areas. It scrapes algae and plants from stones and mainly consumes filamentous algae and diatoms. It spawns in late winter and early spring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Shared habitat.** All four species occur in shallow, swift habitats with gravel-to-pebble substrates and must compete for food and space. A prior study found the Sonoran sucker and spikedace use the same riffle habitat as loach minnow.
- **Coexistence history.** The four species have co-occurred for millennia, although habitat use by loach minnow and spikedace varies with stream morphology.
- **Key limiting factors.** Water flow, food availability, and elevation most strongly limit loach minnow distribution.
- **Human pressures.** Dams, channel alterations, riparian destruction, water diversions, groundwater pumping, and introduced nonnative fishes have driven declines across the Gila River system and other southwestern drainages. Loach minnow and spikedace are now restricted to a few streams, including [[Aravaipa Creek]], Eagle Creek, and the upper Verde River in Arizona, and the mainstem and forks of the [[Gila River]] in New Mexico.
- **Aravaipa Creek.** Several field studies cited in the paper were conducted here, and it is where spawning of longfin dace correlates with flood events and biannual rain patterns.
- **Laboratory habitat simulation.** Tanks were fitted with gravel substrate, cobble undercut areas, and a [[Powerhead Pump]] to create rifle-like current.

## Key Findings

### Methods Overview

- **Apparatus.** Six ten-gallon tanks, separated by cardboard, with small filters, powerhead pumps, gravel substrate, cobbles for undercut areas, and wire mesh covers. Lights were timed to approximate midday sun. Fish were fed live brine shrimp and bloodworms each morning. Three additional tanks held reserve fish.
- **Phase one.** Tanks 1 and 2 held loach minnows with one Sonoran sucker; tank 3 held loach minnows with one spikedace; tank 4 held loach minnows with a breeding pair of spikedace; tanks 5 and 6 held loach minnows with a breeding pair of longfin dace. Observations were made at 1.5-hour intervals.
- **Phase two.** Tanks 1 to 3 held one spikedace with a breeding pair of loach minnows; tanks 4 to 6 held a breeding pair of spikedace with a breeding pair of loach minnows. Observations were made at 30-minute intervals (four observations reported).
- **Analysis.** Hits were tallied per fish per tank. Phase one used ANOVA across dates, tanks, and pooled tanks, with pooling allowed when p > 0.05. Phase two used t-tests, with tanks pooled when no significant difference was found.

### Results

- **Phase one.** The author judged the interaction level "significant" across eleven one-hour trial periods. Interactions peaked mid-trial. Tank 3 (one spikedace, two loach minnows) produced the most hits. Tanks 1, 2, 5, and 6 had significantly fewer. ANOVAs on dates and tanks were not significant. Pooled data from tanks 1 and 2 and tanks 5 and 6 showed more interaction between loach minnow and longfin dace than between loach minnow and sucker, though both were at low levels. Tank 3 and tank 4 could not be pooled because of unequal spikedace sample sizes.
- **Phase two.** Tanks were converted to spikedace-loach minnow pairings, and hits tended to rise over time. Tank 4 had the most hits. T-tests found no significant difference between tanks 3 and 4 or between tanks 5 and 6, so data were pooled. Pooled tanks 2 and 3 showed most interactions in the middle of the experiment; pooled tanks 4 and 5 showed most in the fourth observation period.

### Discussion and Conclusions

1. **Interaction ranking.** Interactions occurred between loach minnow and spikedace and between loach minnow and longfin dace and sucker. The largest number occurred when a single spikedace was housed with two loach minnows. The next largest occurred with two spikedace pairs. Few interactions occurred with suckers and longfin dace.
2. **Spikedace behavior.** Spikedace are typically very active and may taunt other fishes when isolated with them.
3. **Loach minnow territoriality.** Male loach minnows strongly defended their undercut areas and frequently attacked spikedace that approached the rock.
4. **Competition for flow.** Both loach minnow and spikedace prefer fast water, so they competed for the powerhead current. This competition was less frequent with the sucker.
5. **Pairs reduce interactions.** Two breeding pairs in one tank kept each other occupied, producing fewer interactions.
6. **Longfin dace.** Midwater swimmers that rarely contact bottom-dwelling loach minnows, so interactions were minimal.
7. **Body size.** Males in tanks 3, 4, and 5 were significantly larger than males in other tanks, which may explain the higher interaction counts in those tanks.
8. **Courtship.** Courtship behavior of loach minnows changed little, contrary to the original hypothesis.

### Limitations and Recommendations

- Laboratory confinement may create interactions that would not occur naturally, though the author concludes that unnatural interactions were not a major factor.
- Field observations with snorkeling and longer observation periods would yield more natural and conclusive data.
- Future work should examine longfin dace and spikedace interactions, use long trough tanks to simulate streams, and study interactions with nonnative species.
- The author concludes the research is important for conserving these threatened Arizona fishes.
