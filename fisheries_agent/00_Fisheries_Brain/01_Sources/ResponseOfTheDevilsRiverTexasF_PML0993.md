# Response of the Devil's River (Texas) Fish Community to Flooding

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the fish community of the free-flowing [[Devil's River]] in southwest Texas, a desert stream that the source describes as the last remaining undammed, unpolluted major river in Texas. The author sampled 12 sites over 1974–75 and compared fish assemblages before and after a major flood on 18 September 1974 (maximum gauge depth 7.2 m). The flood reduced habitat distinctness, species diversity and richness, and disrupted most species associations. Six dominant minnows, however, retained their rank order and their ecological importance. The author concludes that continued dominance by a few species is a sound indicator of ecosystem stability in naturally stressed desert streams.

## Background & Description

The Devil's River lies within the [[Edwards Plateau]] and drains approximately 10,000 km². Although it falls within the Balconian biotic province, its proximity to the Chihuahuan and Tamaulipan provinces adds to its biotic diversity (Blair, 1950). The river arises in northwestern Sutton County and flows intermittently south into Val Verde County, where all permanent surface water occurs. The study covered about 64 km of the river, historically fed by numerous small springs and six major springs. During the study, Juno Springs were dry and Pecan Springs had been reduced from six outlets to one.

The source notes several peripheral Mexican fishes, the undescribed [[Devil's River Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon* sp.), and the [[Devil's River Minnow]] (*Dionda diaboli*), which is restricted to the Devil's River, San Felipe Creek and Las Moras Creek. The [[Largespring Gambusia]] (*Gambusia geiseri*) is restricted to the Devil's River and other clear-water Edwards Plateau streams.

Desert streams flash-flood frequently, so their fishes need mechanisms to persist in variable conditions. Earlier southwestern studies usually addressed single species or species-poor systems, while earlier Devil's River work consisted mainly of surveys and species descriptions. This study addresses species associations, dominant-species responses to flooding, and their link to total community stability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and flooding.** Thunderstorms occur mainly June–October and cause minor floods (maximum gauge depth 2.0–5.9 m). Severe storms produce major floods (gauge depth above 6 m), and at least nine major floods were recorded from 1916 to 1975. At the [[Baker's Crossing Gauge]], the mean maximum depth was 1.9 m and the mean minimum depth 0.7 m. The 18 September 1974 flood was the ninth largest on record and occurred midway through the study. The river has a mean fall rate of 1.9 m/km and passes over many waterfalls, most under 1.5 m. [[Dolan Falls]] (7.5 m) is the largest barrier to fishes in the lower river. Below Dolan Falls, [[Amistad Reservoir]] impounds the river, creating lentic conditions.

**Physical structure.** The upper river (Beaver Lake to Baker's Crossing) is intermittent and rapid-rich, with deep canyons and solution channels. Channels reach 60 m wide but only about 10 cm deep over limestone bedrock. Shallow riffles often give way to deep, gravel- and rubble-bottomed pools. Water is fed by [[Edwards Aquifer]] springs, which provide the river's perennial supply.

**Vegetation.** Submergent and emergent vegetation is most common in the upper river. Pondweed (*Potamogeton* sp.) and yellow cow-lily (*Nuphar luteum*) occur in pools, while horsetail (*Equisetum* sp.) and cattail (*Typha* sp.) line the margins. Riparian trees include pecan (*Carya illinoinensis*), willow (*Salix* sp.), live oak (*Quercus virginiana*) and sycamore (*Platanus occidentalis*).

**Water chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen, pH, calcium carbonate hardness and temperature were measured at each site with a standard thermometer and a Hach kit. The author reports that the results match earlier Devil's River studies.

## Key Findings

**Study design**
- Twelve sites were chosen to span differences in substrate, flow, turbidity, vegetative cover and depth. The most downstream site lay 12.9 km above Amistad Reservoir.
- Fishes were collected by seining the same area at each site on six occasions: May, June, August and October 1974, and January and March 1975. Night collection in June ensured nocturnal species were included. The first five collections were pre-flood and the last three post-flood (the source's count of pre- and post-flood collections does not fully match this).
- Habitat types were characterised by polar ordination using pH, dissolved oxygen, hardness, temperature, turbidity, substrate, flow and vegetative cover. The five types were springs, pools, channels, riffles and intermediate habitats.

**Community changes after flooding**
- Twenty-three species were collected in total.
- Habitat distinctness declined. Habitats became more riffle-like, and intermediate habitats increased from 5 to 6 sites.
- Species diversity (H′), richness (S) and evenness (J) all decreased after the flood. Only the decline in diversity was statistically significant (P < 0.05).
- H′ and S remained correlated before and after the flood, so richness is a good predictor of diversity (r = 0.743, P < 0.001 after the flood).
- Before the flood, diversity showed no relationship with distance from the headwaters. Afterwards, distance and diversity were positively correlated (r = 0.5972, P < 0.05), because the reservoir-proximal reach was less flood-stressed.
- In intermediate habitats, H′ and J were significantly reduced (P < 0.001). In springs, they increased, possibly because the dominance of *Gambusia geiseri* fell.
- Chi-square association analysis found 18 significant species pairs before the flood and 13 afterwards. Only three pre-flood associations persisted: *Notropis venustus* with *N. amabilis*, *N. proserpinus* with *N. venustus*, and *Cichlasoma cyanoguttatum* with *Lepomis auritus*.

**Dominant species**
- Six species dominated both regimes: [[Dionda Episcopa]], [[Notropis Proserpinus]], [[Notropis Amabilis]], [[Astyanax Mexicanus]], [[Notropis Venustus]] and [[Dionda Diaboli]].
- These six made up 75% of mean pre-flood biomass and over 93% of mean post-flood biomass.
- Their rank order by biomass was identical in both regimes (rho = 0.9341, P < 0.01), which the author takes as evidence of flood adaptation.
- *Dionda episcopa* had 2.9–3.3 times the biomass of *N. proserpinus* and was the most important small fish. It was a riffle species before the flood, and it shifted toward channels afterwards.
- *Notropis amabilis* increased in relative importance after the flood, while *N. proserpinus* became more abundant (13 times more abundant before the flood, 6 times after) and shifted into intermediate habitats.
- *Notropis venustus* declined in biomass and abundance, especially in riffles, so the author regards it as the least successfully adapted dominant.
- *Astyanax mexicanus* moved into Hudspeth Springs after the flood, where it apparently reproduced.
- *Gambusia geiseri* declined in springs after the flood.
- Four of the six dominants increased in abundance after the flood, one seemed unaffected, and one declined.

**Methodological notes**
- Abundance and biomass were negatively correlated in both regimes. Biomass and caloric value were positively correlated (r = 0.9186, P < 0.001).
- Biomass was chosen for diversity calculations because it had the least variance and avoids the pyramid-of-numbers bias.
- Dominance was measured as relative biomass.
- Some statistical values in the source scan are garbled or illegible (for example, several means and standard errors and some regression coefficients), so only clearly legible figures are reported here.

**Conclusions**
- Flooding altered habitat distinctness, species diversity and most species associations.
- The dominant fishes kept their ecological importance despite shifts in habitat use, which suggests that ecological plasticity underlies success in stressful environments such as desert streams.
- The author proposes that continued or increased dominance by a few species should be considered typical of natural ecosystem stability in such systems (Orians, 1975).
