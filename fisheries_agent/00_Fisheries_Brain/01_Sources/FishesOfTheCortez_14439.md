# Fishes of the Cortez

#ichthyology #marine-biology #serranidae

## Executive Summary

This angler's appendix is a field guide to the fishes of the [[Sea of Cortez]] (Gulf of California), intended to help anglers identify species and understand how they are related. It explains how [[Scientific Names]] and [[Taxonomy]] work, then profiles the fishes most likely to interest anglers, grouped by family. The guide covers sharks, rays, herrings, catfishes, basses, snappers, jacks, croakers, billfishes, flatfishes and others. The author notes that the Cortez holds some 650 fish species, so the listing is limited to notable and sport-relevant species. Identification features, sizes, Mexican common names, and bait or tackle tips are given throughout.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and approach.** The guide argues that knowing fish families increases enjoyment of angling, because related species often share feeding habits and mouth structure. For example, all [[Cynoscion]] croakers have tender mouth membranes and must be worked gently. Common names are mostly Mexican, since these are used regionally, and the guide retains established local names rather than imposing standard ones. "Cabrilla" is used for most basses, "pargo" for snappers, "roncador" for grunts and croakers, and "jurel" for several jacks.

**Classification.** Taxonomy follows the listing of the American Fisheries Society, with scientific names and abbreviated descriptions drawn from the checklist of Dr. Boyd Walker. Species with identical characters are grouped together. Anatomical differences separate species within a genus, and increasing differences group genera into families, then into larger aggregations.

**Identification conventions.**
- A hypothetical fish diagram labels the gill cover, pectoral fins, spiny and soft dorsal fins, finlets, and tail lobes.
- Color is used as a character only for freshly caught adults, because color and shape change rapidly and differ in young fish.
- Sizes are the largest scientific records, not world angling records. Length runs from the snout tip to the tail fork.
- Family prototypes are illustrated. Species counts and descriptions apply only to the Cortez, since the same families elsewhere may include many more forms.

**Coverage.** The guide profiles about 40 featured big-game and sport species in detail, and the text notes that dolphinfish and yellowfin tuna are described in the main text rather than the appendix.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses ecology only in passing, so the following points are drawn from its notes:

- **Habitat associations:** Many species are tied to specific settings. Examples include black snook and striped mullet in brackish or estuarine water, ariid catfishes in mud-bottom backwaters, cabrillas and other basses in rocky reefs, and creoles that mass along steep cliffs.
- **Depth:** Several records give depth ranges, such as ruby snapper at 2 fathoms in the Midriff and 40 fathoms in the south, and gold spotted bass at 25 to 60 fathoms.
- **Regional distribution:** The Midriff region is repeatedly named as a hotspot for herrings and as a location for several basses and snappers. Some species are rare or limited to specific areas, such as broomtail grouper, black sea bass (rare below Mulegé), and the totuava (native to the north half of the Cortez).
- **Trophic roles:** Herrings and anchovies are called "superabundant" forage fishes, and the anchovy is described as "more vital as a link in fish food chain." Rudderfishes and opaleyes are largely herbivorous, and surgeonfish are described as generally herbivorous.
- **Human impacts:** The guide mentions one non-native introduction, California sargo, transplanted to the Salton Sea. Milkfish are raised in ponds in South Asia. No conservation status or population trend data is given.

## Key Findings

- **Diversity:** The Cortez has roughly 650 fish species. Family counts from the text include Sciaenidae (35 species), Serranidae (34), Carangidae (28), Pomadasyidae (22), Engraulididae (16), Labridae (14), Ariidae (12), Carcharhinidae (10), Dasyatidae (10), Lutjanidae (9), Clupeidae (8), Scorpaenidae (8), Scaridae (7), Mugilidae (5), Belonidae (5), Sphyrnidae (5), Istiophoridae (5), Lamnidae (3), and Mullidae (4). The flatfishes (Pleuronectiformes) total 29 species.
- **Record-size fishes:** The Pacific manta (*Manta hamiltoni*) reaches over 3,500 lb and 22 ft across. Blue marlin (*Makaira nigricans*) reaches 2,000 lb, black marlin (*Makaira indica*) 1,800 lb, southern jewfish (*Promicrops* sp.) 1,000 lb, and broadbill swordfish (*Xiphias gladius*) 1,000 lb. Black sea bass (*Stereolepis gigas*) reaches 600 lb and totuava (*Cynoscion macdonaldi*) 300 lb.
- **Sharks and rays:** The bonito shark (*Isurus glaucus*) grows to 12 ft and jumps like a billfish. The smooth hammerhead (*Sphyrna zygaena*) reaches 12 ft and 1,000 lb and may attack divers when blood is present. Stingrays are hazardous because they bury themselves in shallow sand or mud. Caudal spines can cause a lethal sting, treated by soaking the wound in very hot water.
- **Bass family:** Serranidae are the largest sport group. Notable species include the baya grouper, pinta cabrilla, flag cabrilla, leatherfin bass, and the Pacific creole, which is recognized by its deep concave tail and bright red color.
- **Snappers and jacks:** Ruby snapper (*Lutjanus peru*) is described as among the finest foods. Jacks (Carangidae) are called "great gamesters," with the jack crevalle reaching 50 lb and the threadfin pompano 60 lb.
- **Billfish and big game:** Striped marlin (*Makaira audax*) is abundant and reaches 425 lb. Pacific sailfish (*Istiompax greyi*) reaches 275 lb and is abundant, occurring up to Punta Peñasco in spring. Yellowfin tuna (*Neothunnus macropterus*) is distinguished from the weaker-fighting allison tuna, which is miscalled "yellowfin."
- **Croakers and corvinas:** The corvinas (*Cynoscion* spp.) are notable for their tender mouths. The white seabass (*Cynoscion nobilis*) reaches 80 lb, and the Cortez corvinas are hard to tell apart, with the scalyfin corvina being a particularly difficult identification.
- **Angling guidance:** The guide gives bait and lure advice for most species, such as live bait, stripbait, ski-bait, feathers, and white jigs. Some species are noted as poor game fish or as good food fish, including the herrings, anchovies, and mullets.
- **Practical advice:** Anglers are advised that sharks and rays are generally edible, and that several species have dangerous spines or teeth. The guide's sections are intended for anglers, so its information on ecology and population status is minimal.
