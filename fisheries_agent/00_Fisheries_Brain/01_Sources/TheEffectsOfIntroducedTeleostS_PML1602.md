# Social Behavior of Cyprinodon macularius californiensis Affected by Introduced Teleost Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1981 Occidental College master's thesis by Margaret Louise Matsui examines how introduced fishes alter the social behavior and reproductive success of the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius californiensis*) at the [[Salton Sea]], California. The study combined field observations in three drainage canals with laboratory experiments that tested the effects of [[Tilapia zillii]] and [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*) on pupfish breeding behavior. The central finding is that peripherally territorial males, which occupy the shallow edges of [[Lek Breeding System]] arenas, have lower reproductive success, apparently because they spend more time on aggression toward heterospecifics and less time on courtship. The author attributes this to [[Aggressive Neglect]] and proposes that the pupfish have not yet adapted to recently introduced exotic species.

## Background & Description

*Cyprinodon macularius californiensis* is a small desert pupfish that lives in hypersaline and fluctuating environments with rapidly changing temperature, salinity, and oxygen levels. Its most conspicuous social adaptation is a lek mating system. Males gather temporarily in breeding aggregations, each establishing a territory on the substrate, displaying to attract females, and interacting aggressively with other males. Within a lek, males are either centrally territorial, peripherally territorial, or non-territorial "satellite" and "sneaker" males.

The thesis notes that lek breeding also occurs in many other taxa, including birds such as the [[Sage Grouse]] and [[Ruff]], mammals such as the [[Uganda Kob]], and insects such as [[Drosophilid Flies]]. Earlier work had shown that central males on leks often have higher reproductive success, but little was known about differential success based on position or access to preferred areas. The study was designed to clarify how exotic species affect this system.

Breeding season at the Salton Sea ran from late March through late September. Males were tagged with acrylic paint on the dorsal musculature for individual identification. Behaviors recorded included relative size, territory position, feeding, patrolling, courtship, spawning, and [[Reproductive Success]], measured as the number of spawning acts per 15 minutes of observation per day.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Field work was conducted in three drainage canals on the Salton Sea: Avenue 81 (west side), Ruth Street (Mayer Street area) on the east side, and [[Cleveland Street]] canal on the east side. These canals were fed by irrigation water stored in underground wells, which kept the water at a relatively constant 28 °C. Flow varied with irrigation pumping and rainfall, and flash floods periodically eliminated pupfish populations for several weeks. Cleveland Street, a concrete-lined canal, was the main study site.

Introduced fishes, including Tilapia zillii and Poecilia latipinna, coexisted with the pupfish in these canals. The author notes that the Salton Sea pupfish were once abundant in shallow shore pools, but after tilapia introduction they were found only in dense aggregations in peripheral canal habitats. Other threats considered included physical stress from flash floods, herbicides, and predation. The thesis notes that the effects of herbicides and pesticides on pupfish populations had not been studied.

## Key Findings

- **Position effect:** At Cleveland Street, centrally territorial males had significantly higher reproductive success than peripherally territorial males. Satellite males were not territorial and had lower success than territorial males.
- **Path analysis:** Path analysis of the Cleveland Street data showed that aggression toward heterospecifics (*Poecilia* and *Tilapia*) was negatively correlated with reproductive success in peripherally territorial males, while courtship was positively correlated with success.
- **Heterospecific aggression:** Peripherally territorial males directed significantly more aggression toward heterospecifics than centrally territorial males did. This elevated aggression may be why their reproductive success was lower.
- **Laboratory experiments:** Four treatments were tested over 70 days of observation: low density (6 male and 12 female pupfish), high density (12 male and 12 female pupfish), tilapia (6 male pupfish, 6 juvenile tilapia), and molly (6 male pupfish, 6 male and 3 female mollies). Five variables (aggression, patrol, relative size, territory size, and courtship) were significantly correlated with reproductive success (p < 0.001) across all treatments.
- **Partial correlation results:** Territory size was a spurious correlate of reproductive success in the low-density treatment. In the molly treatment, courtship and aggression toward conspecifics were positively correlated with success, and aggression toward mollies was negatively correlated.
- **Introduced predator effects:** Juvenile tilapia did not eat pupfish eggs in lab trials, but they disrupted territorial behavior by feeding within territories and increasing aggression. Adult mollies increased interference through courtship attempts and reduced pupfish courtship and spawning.
- **Aggressive neglect:** The thesis proposes that increased aggression from territorial males toward heterospecifics reduces time spent on courtship, lowering individual fitness. This matches the concept of aggressive neglect described by [[Hutchinson and MacArthur]] (1959).
- **Possible causes of decline:** The author concludes that behavioral interference is probably not the only factor. Other causes considered include physical stress, herbicides, pesticides, and predation.
- **Conclusion:** The author hypothesizes that the maladaptive behavior of peripheral males results from insufficient time to adapt to recently introduced exotic species. Aggressive neglect may be a significant contributor to the decline of *C. m. californiensis*.
