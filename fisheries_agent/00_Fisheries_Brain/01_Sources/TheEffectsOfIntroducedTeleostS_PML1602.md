# The Effects of Introduced Teleost Species on the Social Behavior of Cyprinodon macularius californiensis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1981 Occidental College [[Master Of Arts Thesis]] by Margaret L. Matsui examines whether introduced fishes alter the [[Lek Mating System]] of the endangered [[Salton Sea Pupfish]]. Field work in drainage canals showed that reproductive success depends on a male's position on the lek. Laboratory experiments showed that peripherally territorial males spend more effort fighting heterospecific intruders, and this reduces courtship time and spawning. The author frames this as [[Aggressive Neglect]] caused by [[Behavioral Interference]] from [[Tilapia Zillii]] juveniles and [[Sailfin Molly]] adults, and proposes it as one contributor to the species' decline.

## Background & Description

*Cyprinodon macularius californiensis* is a small desert pupfish adapted to rapidly fluctuating temperature, salinity and oxygen levels. Its most conspicuous social trait is a lek system in which males gather on a shared arena, each holding a display territory to attract females. Lekking occurs in birds, ungulates, anurans, bats and insects, and it departs from monogamous pair bonding and produces strong sexual dimorphism.

Earlier work had described the species' physiology and ecology but said little about social adaptations or about how position on the lek affects mating success. The study therefore set out to characterise the field social system before testing the effects of exotic species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study sites:** Three drainage canals on the Salton Sea were used: Avenue 81 (west side), Hayes Street and Cleveland Street (east side). Cleveland Street, a concrete-lined canal, supplied most observations because the other two were affected by herbicide-driven defoliation and turbidity changes.
- **Water conditions:** Canal water came from irrigation stored in underground wells and stayed at a near-constant 28 °C. Flash floods scoured canals and periodically eliminated pupfish populations for several weeks.
- **Breeding season:** Late March to late September. Field observations covered March to June and September to October of 1978 and 1979.
- **Introduced species:** Only *C. m. californiensis* was native to the sea when Barlow (1961) surveyed it. Later introductions included [[Gambusia Affinis]] (1922), [[Gillichthys Mirabilis]] (1950), [[Poecilia Latipinna]] (1957), [[Sarotherodon Mossambicus]] (1960 to 1970) and [[Tilapia Zillii]] (1971 to 1980). - **Decline:** The species was proposed for the endangered list in 1976. Three possible causes were considered: physical stress, predation by exotics, and competition with exotics (through exploitation or behavioral interference).

## Key Findings

**Field study (Chapter 1)**
- Male reproductive success varied with lek position. Centrally territorial males produced significantly more spawnings than other males (ANOVA), while peripherally territorial males did not differ significantly from satellite males. All males did better than sneaker males (p = .001).
- Centrally territorial males performed significantly fewer heterospecific aggressive acts than peripherally territorial males (p = .001). Table 2 gives mean aggression of 46.18 ± 8.36 for central males and 36.14 ± 11.45 for peripheral males (acts/male/15 min). Spawns were 4.73 ± 0.53 (central), 3.79 ± 0.42 (peripheral), 6.05 ± 0.62 (satellite) and 2.13 ± 0.43 (sneaker).
- Path analysis showed that for peripheral males, aggression toward *Poecilia* significantly and negatively correlated with reproductive success, while courtship was positively correlated.
- Satellite males used courtship as their only significant route to spawning. Sneaker males lacked nuptial coloration and spawned by interrupting territorial pairs.
- Territorial males recognise and cannibalise other males' eggs, which the author proposes provides an extra energy source that helps them hold territory.
- Populations rarely stayed on the Cleveland Street arena longer than three days.

**Laboratory study (Chapter 2)**
- Pearson correlations and partial correlations were run across four treatments: Low-density *Cyprinodon* (6 males, 12 females), High-density *Cyprinodon* (12 males, 12 females), High-density *Tilapia* (6 male *Cyprinodon*, 6 juvenile *Tilapia*) and High-density *Poecilia* (6 male *Cyprinodon*, 3 male and 3 female *Poecilia*). Each treatment ran ten 7-day replicates (70 days).
- In the High-density *Cyprinodon* and *Tilapia* treatments, aggression directed at conspecifics or heterospecifics reduced courtship time. In the *Poecilia* treatment, aggression toward *Poecilia* was negatively correlated with fitness, and territory size, relative size and patrolling were spurious correlates.
- Courtship time fell from 21.90 ± 17.68 (Low-density) to 12.87 ± 8.67 (High-density *Cyprinodon*), 11.91 ± 6.23 (*Tilapia*) and 7.96 ± 4.67 (*Poecilia*) acts/male/15 min. Eggs per male fell from 24.10 ± 7.56 (Low-density) to 5.85 ± 3.16, 12.53 ± 1.32 and 6.81 ± 0.92 in the other three treatments respectively.
- *Tilapia* juveniles did not prey on pupfish eggs in tests (4 replicates per species). Their main effect was behavioral interference through schooling and feeding in breeding areas.
- *Poecilia* males courted *Cyprinodon* females and blocked female movement in the water column, which triggered elevated patrolling and aggression.

**Interpretation**
- The author concludes that aggressive neglect, defined as a reduction in fitness caused by excessive aggression reducing courtship time, is plausible but not yet shown to be the only cause of the decline. Flash floods, herbicides, pesticides and predation were also considered.
- The thesis hypothesises that peripheral males' maladaptive behavior reflects too little time for evolutionary adjustment to recently introduced exotics.
- It notes that lekking pupfish can switch to alternative mating systems (dominance hierarchies, male groups chasing females, consort pairs), so facultative responses are possible.
