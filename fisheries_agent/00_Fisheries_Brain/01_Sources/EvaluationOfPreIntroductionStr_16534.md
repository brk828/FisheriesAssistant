## Executive Summary

This report evaluates whether **pre-introduction stress** (transport, handling, anesthesia, and radio-transmitter implantation) contributed to the near-total mortality of hatchery-reared adult [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) stocked into the [[Green River]] in 1988–1989. Fish used were F1 offspring (1981) of wild broodstock from [[Lake Mohave]], reared at Dexter National Fish Hatchery. Using pen trials with four experimental treatments (dummy transmitter implant, sham surgery/incision-only, handling/anesthesia, and control), the authors found **minimal direct mortality attributable to implantation or transport procedures**. However, overall field mortality following release into the Green River was extremely high (64 of 86 fish confirmed dead; only 6 of 86 known to survive long-term). The authors conclude that mortality was most likely driven by a **failure of hatchery-conditioned bonytail chub to behaviorally adapt to natural river conditions** (e.g., predator avoidance, foraging), rather than by handling stress, transmitter implantation, or unsuitability of the Green River habitat itself. This aligns with prior salmonid literature demonstrating that hatchery rearing reduces fitness-related behaviors necessary for survival in the wild.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Gila elegans* (Bonytail Chub), family Cyprinidae — a federally listed endangered cyprinid of the Colorado River Basin.
- **Provenance**: F1 hatchery-reared offspring (spawned 1981) derived from wild adults collected at [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona/Nevada; reared to adulthood at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- **Physical condition indicators noted during study**:
  - Fish in the high-velocity ("run") pen developed **abrasions on the snout and fins** rapidly after transfer from slow backwater habitat, suggesting physical/behavioral maladaptation to higher flow conditions.
  - One mortality (dummy-transmitter fish) showed **internal injuries and abdominal tissue damage** at the antenna exit site, attributed to antenna entanglement on the wire pen rather than the implant itself.
  - General external appearance of fish was reported as healthy at time of stocking, despite eventual high field mortality — supporting a behavioral rather than physiological/health-based explanation for mortality.
- **Tagging/marking methods**: Carlin tags used for individual identification; external radio transmitters (ATS antennas) and dummy wooden-dowel transmitters (1/4" diameter × 1.5") used to simulate implant stress without functional telemetry.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat types studied**: Backwater habitats and higher-velocity run habitats within the [[Green River]], representative of natural bonytail chub habitat in the Colorado River Basin.
- **Captive-to-wild transition**: The study is framed around the [[Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan]] (USFWS 1990), which relies on captive broodstock (maintained for genetic integrity) to produce fish for reintroduction — highlighting a key ecological tension between **genetic conservation** and **behavioral fitness** for wild survival.
- **Behavioral deficits inferred**: Drawing on comparative salmonid literature, hatchery-reared fish are hypothesized to lack:
  - Adequate **predator avoidance/fright response** ([[Vincent 1960]]; [[Johnsson and Abrahams 1991]] on steelhead trout).
  - Ability to occupy **energetically advantageous stream positions** and convert to natural diet ([[Wiley et al. Manuscript]]).
- **Comparative stocking ecology**: [[Nehring 1991]] found that stocked rainbow trout >12 cm rarely survived, while smaller stocked trout — despite higher predation risk — were more likely to establish populations, suggesting size/conditioning at release affects long-term establishment success across species.
- **Physical river conditions affecting survival**: Fluctuating river elevation (falling water levels isolating backwater pens) caused incidental mortality independent of treatment, illustrating how river hydrology can confound stocking success evaluations.

## Key Findings

- **Implantation-related mortality was low**: Of 4 fish implanted at Dexter NFH (May 11, 1988), 3 survived beyond one month post-surgery.
- **1988 pen trials**: Among treatments (dummy transmitter, incision-only, handled/anesthetized, control), only 2 direct mortalities occurred out of the initial cohort; one death was linked to antenna entanglement on the cage rather than the implant itself.
- **High-velocity pen effects**: Fish moved to a faster-flow pen in October 1988 rapidly developed abrasions; a second implanted fish died within 19 days, followed by an incision-only fish 7 days later — trial terminated November 18, 1988 due to poor fish condition.
- **1989 protocol changes**: A revised transport protocol (3 ppm furacin bath, oxytetracycline + vitamin C injection, fungicide dip post-surgery) was introduced after high mortality following June 1989 transport; despite this, **70% mortality occurred within 11 days** in pens following July 1989 transport.
- **June 1989 mass mortality**: Of 22 bonytail transported to Ouray NWR (June 22), 10 died between June 27–July 2 (6 implanted, 4 non-implanted) — no clear difference between implanted and control fish, suggesting implantation was not the primary driver.
- **Transport mortality was minimal**: Only 1 fish died in transit (June 1989) across both years of study.
- **Overall field mortality was severe and largely unexplained by handling stress**: 64 of 86 stocked bonytail chub were confirmed dead, with 16 additional fish presumed dead but unaccounted for — leaving only **6 of 86 fish** known to survive for an extended period post-stocking.
- **Primary conclusion**: The weight of evidence suggests **hatchery-conditioning effects** (reduced predator avoidance, poor habitat selection, failure to adopt natural foraging behavior) — not pre-stocking handling/transmitter stress or unsuitability of Green River habitat — best explain the catastrophic post-stocking mortality of hatchery-reared bonytail chub.
- **Study limitations**: Lack of independent replication, small sample sizes, non-independent treatment observations, and confounding cumulative effects prevented formal statistical analysis; findings are explicitly descriptive.
- **Management implication**: Supports development of **"behavioral conditioning" or predator-recognition training programs** in hatcheries (cf. [[Wiley et al. Manuscript]]) prior to reintroduction, analogous to recommendations for hatchery-reared salmonids, to improve post-stocking survival of endangered cyprinids like the [[Bonytail Chub]].
