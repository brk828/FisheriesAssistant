# Use of Hydroelectric Dams to Control Evaporation from Lake Mead

#limnology #reservoir-management #water-quality #evaporation-control

## Executive Summary

This 1981 technical report by [[Larry J. Paulson]] from the [[Lake Mead Limnological Research Center]] demonstrates that hydroelectric dam operations can significantly reduce evaporation from [[Lake Mead]] and decrease salinity in the [[Colorado River]]. The construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963 reduced evaporation by at least 93,000 acre-feet (1.2 × 10⁸ m³) annually through cold-water releases. The study proposes that operating [[Hoover Dam]] from surface rather than deep-water discharge could reduce evaporation by an additional 120,000 acre-feet (1.48 × 10⁸ m³) per year, decreasing salinity by at least 9 mg/L and saving several million dollars annually in downstream impacts.

## Background & Description

[[Lake Mead]] was formed in 1935 by construction of [[Hoover Dam]], extending 114 miles (183 km) from the mouth of [[Grand Canyon]] to [[Black Canyon]]. At maximum operating level (1227 ft, 374 m), the reservoir has a surface area of 163,028 acres (660 km²) and volume of 29,185,245 acre-feet (36 × 10⁹ m³). The reservoir is divided into Upper and Lower Basins by [[Boulder Canyon]].

[[Hoover Dam]] is equipped with intake gates at 1045 ft (319 m) and 895 ft (273 m) elevations and has operated from the lower gates since 1954. The [[Colorado River]] inflow was unregulated prior to 1963 when [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was constructed 280 miles (451 km) upstream. Annual discharges are high (8,354,000 acre-feet in 1978), with the Colorado River comprising 98% of inflow. Additional inputs come from the [[Virgin River]], [[Muddy River]], and [[Las Vegas Wash]] (secondary-treated sewage effluents).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963 drastically altered seasonal temperature and discharge cycles in the [[Colorado River]]. River temperatures increased by nearly 5°C during late fall and winter but decreased by 10°C during the rest of the year due to cold-water releases from the hypolimnion (230 ft, 70 m) of [[Lake Powell]]. Discharge temperatures from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] average about 8°C year-round, reaching only 10-11°C at [[Grand Canyon]] and 15-16°C at [[Pierce Ferry]] in summer—nearly 10°C colder than pre-1963 spring and summer temperatures.

These alterations eliminated the historical spring discharge pulse caused by upper basin runoff. Monthly discharges now show much less variation, with peak discharges typically occurring in summer during maximum power demand periods.

The temperature structure in [[Lake Mead]] shows regional variations, with the Upper Basin (Virgin Basin) generally 1-2°C colder than the Lower Basin (Boulder Basin), especially during spring and early summer. The [[Colorado River]] inflow forms a density current confined primarily to the Upper Basin, with Virgin Basin acting as a large "mixing bowl." Only during high discharge periods does the density current extend into the Lower Basin.

Water shortages and salinity problems exist throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Salt concentrations are approaching levels that severely affect municipal and agricultural uses. Estimates indicate salt concentrations at [[Imperial Dam]] will rise to 1165-1299 mg/L by 2000, causing cumulative economic damages of $1-1.5 billion.

## Key Findings

**Evaporation Reduction from Glen Canyon Dam Operations:**
- Pre-[[Glen Canyon Dam]] (before 1963): evaporation rates averaged 85.2 inches (33.5 cm) per year
- Post-[[Glen Canyon Dam]] (1965-1970): evaporation rates decreased to approximately 74 inches (29 cm) per year
- Net reduction: at least 93,376 acre-feet (1.2 × 10⁸ m³) annually
- Cold-water discharges from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] reduced net advective energy input to Lake Mead from 300-400 cal·cm⁻²·day⁻¹ (pre-1963) to only 9.04 cal·cm⁻²·day⁻¹ (1977-1978)

**Advective Energy Analysis (October 1977 - September 1978):**
- Deep discharge at [[Hoover Dam]]: net advective heat gain of 9.04 cal·cm⁻²·day⁻¹
- Projected surface discharge at [[Hoover Dam]]: net advective heat loss of -29.55 cal·cm⁻²·day⁻¹
- Net difference: -38.59 cal·cm⁻²·day⁻¹
- This translates to evaporation reduction of 26.99 cm·yr⁻¹ (10.6 inches·yr⁻¹)
- Annual water savings: 119,779 acre-feet (1.48 × 10⁸ m³) at 1978 lake elevations

**Combined Evaporation Reduction Strategy:**
- Continuing [[Glen Canyon Dam]] deep discharge + shifting [[Hoover Dam]] to surface discharge
- Total reduction: 213,155 acre-feet (2.63 × 10⁸ m³) per year
- This represents over two-thirds of Nevada's current Colorado River allocation

**Salinity Impacts:**
- 1978 dissolved solids concentration at [[Hoover Dam]] intake towers: 676 mg/L
- Evaporation reduction of 120,000 acre-feet would decrease salinity by 9 mg/L within five years
- Evaporation reduction of 213,000 acre-feet would decrease salinity by 16 mg/L within five years
- These reductions are comparable to those projected from the [[Las Vegas Wash]] Salinity Control Project (8 mg/L) and [[Grand Valley]] Salinity Control Project (19 mg/L)
- Economic benefit: each 1 mg/L salinity reduction saves $230,000 annually
- Total annual savings: $2,070,000 to $3,680,000

**Methodological Concerns:**
- Historical evaporation estimates may overestimate actual rates post-1963
- Adjustments for energy advection and storage were discontinued in October 1974, causing artificially high 1975-1976 estimates
- [[Water and Power Resources Service]] observed overall water gain in Lake Mead averaging 230,000 acre-feet (2.34 × 10⁸ m³) per year (1960-1970), possibly due to evaporation overestimation
- Temperature measurements at [[Hoover Dam]] intake towers may not represent reservoir-wide conditions, particularly in the Upper Basin influenced by cold-water inflows

**Potential Concerns with Surface Discharge:**
- Requires modification of [[Hoover Dam]] intake structures; "Morning Glory" type intake towers suggested
- Warm-water discharges could impact [[Black Canyon]] cold-water trout fishery, though may benefit aquatic insect reproduction
- Could increase evaporation from [[Lake Mohave]], though net system-wide water loss would still decrease
- Would increase nutrient retention: total nitrogen by 66%, total phosphorus by 60%
- Elevated productivity could benefit declining [[Largemouth Bass]] populations

**Additional Recommendations:**
- Conduct engineering studies on intake structure modifications
- Perform hydraulic studies to ensure surface water withdrawal
- Investigate impacts on downstream endangered fishes in [[Lake Mohave]]
- Evaluate pump-storage units (Spring Canyon and Rifle Range sites) for additional evaporation reduction through hypolimnion withdrawal and epilimnion release
