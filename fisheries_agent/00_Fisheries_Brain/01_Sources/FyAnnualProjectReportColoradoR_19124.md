# Mitochondrial DNA Variability in the Endangered Razorback Sucker: Analysis of Hatchery Stocks and Implications for Captive Propagation

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #catostomidae #endangered-species #genetics

## Executive Summary

This FY 1993 annual project report documents a genetic diversity study of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large, long-lived catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] drainage. The research examined mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variation in the [[Lake Mohave]] source population and three hatchery-produced year classes (1987, 1989, 1990) from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The source population exhibited remarkably high genetic diversity (h = 0.97, with 17 unique haplotypes in 25 individuals). While 1987 and 1989 hatchery year classes maintained comparable diversity levels, the 1990 year class showed significantly reduced diversity (h = 0.71), likely due to unequal female contributions or differential progeny viability. The study evaluates three management alternatives for preserving genetic diversity: standard hatchery propagation, natural spawning in predator-free environments, and protective custody of wild-spawned larvae. The authors strongly recommend the third option as most cost-effective for maintaining genetic variability while the species faces recruitment failure throughout its range.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large catostomid fish historically widespread and abundant throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species has been extirpated from most of its former range due to human impacts including water management practices, habitat modification, and introduction of non-native predatory species. The largest remaining populations occur in the upper [[Green River]] (Colorado-Utah, estimated at fewer than 1,000 adults) and [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada (fewer than 55,000 adults). The species was federally listed as endangered in 1991.

[[Lake Mohave]] is a mainstem impoundment created in 1954 by closure of Davis Dam. The reservoir consists of an upstream riverine reach approximately 60 km long grading into a lower basin 48 km long, with surface area of 115 km², average depth of 10.5 m, and short retention time (0.24 year). Tag-recapture data indicate a population of less than 55,000 adult razorback suckers concentrated in upper parts of the lower basin, particularly near shore during winter-spring spawning aggregations.

All remaining populations exhibit complete recruitment failure—while spawning and larval production continue, populations consist only of adults averaging 25 to more than 40 years of age. Juveniles are virtually unknown and recruitment to spawning stocks is undetected. Without intervention, remaining populations face extinction in the foreseeable future.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The recruitment failure affecting [[Razorback Sucker]] populations appears linked to three primary factors: (1) transport of larvae by water delivery and hydropower operations, (2) insufficient zooplankton resources for larval persistence, and (3) predation by non-native fishes on eggs and larvae. Evidence strongly supports predation as a critical factor—when stocked with predatory fishes, larval razorback suckers fail to survive to juvenile stage in all habitats studied, but typically mature successfully in diverse, fishless environments without food supplementation.

At least 23 species have been introduced into [[Lake Mohave]] as sportfish or forage, with approximately three-fourths known or predicted to consume eggs or larval fishes. These same predatory species are widespread throughout the Colorado River basin. The species' historical success in newly constructed reservoirs was short-lived; strong initial year classes failed to reproduce successfully, with populations persisting approximately 40 years before disappearing.

The [[Lake Mohave]] population likely hatched around 1954 coincident with impoundment, prior to prevalence of non-native predators. Based on timing of other reservoir stock collapses in the lower Colorado basin, this population faces imminent crash. The species' longevity has allowed persistence of genetic diversity long enough for conservation response, but immediate action is required.

## Key Findings

**Genetic Diversity Assessment:**
- Source population ([[Lake Mohave]]) exhibited remarkably high mtDNA diversity: h = 0.97, with 17 haplotypes in 25 individuals (0.68 haplotypes per individual)
- Eight restriction enzymes produced approximately 160 fragments, yielding 27 total haplotypes across all 56 individuals examined
- Most haplotypes (11 of 17) in wild population were unique to individual fish
- High diversity indicates population comprises direct descendants of an exceedingly large, diverse, panmictic population from pre-development times

**Hatchery Year Class Analysis:**
- 1987 year class: h = 0.89, 6 haplotypes in 10 individuals (0.60 haplotypes per individual); 55 females contributed
- 1989 year class: h = 0.91, 7 haplotypes in 11 individuals (0.64 haplotypes per individual); 14 females contributed
- 1990 year class: h = 0.71, 4 haplotypes in 10 individuals (0.40 haplotypes per individual); 17 females contributed
- Only 1990 year class showed significantly reduced diversity compared to source population (p < 0.05)
- Unique haplotypes not found in Lake Mohave sample appeared in all three year classes (4, 6, and 1 respectively)

**Factors Affecting Hatchery Diversity:**
- Reduced diversity in 1990 year class not explained by number of females used (17 vs. 55 and 14 for 1987 and 1989)
- Survivorship from fertilized ova to swim-up was only 11.1% in 1990, substantially lower than 1982-1989 minimum of 22.5% (mean 40.1%, maximum 71.4%)
- Computer simulations demonstrated haplotype diversity (h) is relatively insensitive to broodstock size above 15-20 individuals, leveling at h = 0.95
- Diversity reduction likely due to over-representation of some females, differential fecundity, differential progeny viability, or interactions among these factors
- Potential inbreeding effects from mixing F₁ broodfish with close relatives cannot be excluded

**Broodstock History and Complications:**
- Initial broodstock obtained 1981-82 from Lake Mohave: maximum 79 females (81+82-WILD)
- Most F₁ broodfish derived from only 6-8 wild females, with two contributing 95.6% of 29,815 larvae in 1981
- 71 wild females spawned in 1982 produced 1.7 million F₁ larvae (82YC-F₁)
- Wild genetic material infusion in 1985: approximately 2,000 wild-spawned larvae (85-WILD) transported from Lake Mohave
- By 1990, broodstock of 526 fish comprised mixed year classes: 81+82YC-F₁s (likely >50%), 85-WILD (likely <20%), and 87YC-F₂s (33.5%)
- Specific origins of individuals used to produce 1990 year class not identifiable, raising concerns about inadvertent inbreeding

**Management Alternative Evaluations:**

*Standard Hatchery Propagation:*
- Can produce spectacular numbers: 100 females at 45 cm SL could yield 1.43 × 10⁸ ova
- With 71.4% survival to swim-up and 10% survival to 30 cm SL, could produce 1.02 × 10⁶ fish (nearly twice estimated wild population) in one season
- Major limitation: space constraints on broodstock numbers dramatically reduce genetic variability
- Even under ideal conditions (equalizing individual contributions), can maintain only small proportion of existing variability
- Problems from unequal parental contribution, inadvertent selection, or inbreeding may further reduce diversity
- Most expensive option with least desirable outcome for genetic diversity preservation

*Natural Spawning in Predator-Free Environments (Cutoff Bays):*
- Requires removal of predators from isolated backwaters, stocking adults to spawn unattended, releasing adults and progeny back to lake
- Five one-year pilot bays used 145 females total between 1984-1992, with two of five years producing progeny large enough to escape predation
- If 10 bays established with 40% success rate, approximately 120 different females could contribute progeny annually
- Continuation over multiple years would maintain far more genetic variability than most hatchery conditions
- Disadvantages: space availability limits contributing fish numbers; lack of control over reproduction prevents equalizing contributions or ensuring all individuals reproduce
- Intermediate cost and genetic diversity preservation

*Protective Custody of Wild-Spawned Larvae:*
- Direct capture and short-term husbandry of wild-spawned larvae from Lake Mohave, followed by reintroduction
- Larval razorback suckers appear along shorelines December-January, may persist to April-May (rarely June)
- Larvae positively phototactic, readily collected manually or by light traps
- Two dedicated collectors can harvest hundreds to thousands in favorable nights
- Tag-recapture data indicate large proportion of Lake Mohave individuals reproduce annually, so larvae derive from great number of adults
- Represents far more parents and better conserves diversity than either alternative
- Requires sampling multiple time periods (early, mid-, late spawning) and widespread geographic locations over multiple years to account for potential temporal/spatial genetic structure
- Diverse "grow-out" habitats can range from hatchery ponds/raceways to cutoff bays, livestock ponds, urban lakes—all proven successful
- **Strongly recommended as most cost-effective and biologically desirable option**
- Immediate, substantial effort essential to minimize genetic variability loss as wild adults die

**Conservation Recommendations:**
- Begin immediate program of wild-spawned larval capture and protective custody
- Do NOT release progeny from current inbred DNFH broodstock into wild, especially not to replace Lake Mohave population
- Replace present DNFH broodstock with wild-caught individuals; maintain large, diverse captive broodstock as insurance
- Design future hatchery production to maintain documented genetic variability in offspring
- Continue and expand bay-culture program to provide rearing habitat for wild-caught larvae
- Locate rearing geographically near Lake Mohave to minimize costs and handling losses
- Maintain all three management options if economics allow, with continuing refinement, so alternatives available if one approach fails
- Avoid temptation to use traditional hatchery methods to quickly produce large quantities for numerical recovery goals at expense of genetic variability

**Critical Urgency:**
- Only natural recruitment can maintain existing genetic variability
- Population crash will result in significant diversity loss
- Recruitment problem unlikely solved before remaining population collapses
- Hesitation or intransigence may preclude wild larval capture in numbers sufficient to maintain genetic variation
- If old, wild population begins disappearing, opportunity for diversity preservation may be permanently lost
- Population size can be maintained through hatchery stocking, but at substantial cost to innate genetic variability with unknown future consequences
