## Executive Summary

This 1979 USFWS draft recovery plan addresses the critical conservation status of the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*), a subspecies of the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*). The plan is narrowly focused because the Yaqui topminnow faces imminent extinction risk: its last known U.S. habitat, the San Bernardino Ranch in Cochise County, Arizona, was listed for sale, with no guarantee that a future owner would maintain the artesian spring habitat. The document outlines a detailed recovery outline for the broader Gila topminnow and proposes fee acquisition of the San Bernardino Ranch (~$1,000,000) to protect one of only three remaining Rio Yaqui surface water areas in the United States, which also supports five other native fish species, several of which were endangered or proposed for threatened status.

## Background & Description

- Both the Yaqui and Gila topminnow are small members of the live-bearing family [[Poeciliidae]].
- **Males**: seldom exceed 25 mm.
- **Females**: average 30–45 mm.
- Breeding males are characteristically **blackened** in coloration.
- **Reproductive differences between subspecies**:
  - Yaqui topminnow: brood size 6–49 offspring; gestation period 12–15 days.
  - Gila topminnow: brood size 2–16 offspring; gestation period 24–28 days.
- Breeding occurs January–August, though pregnant females and newborns have been documented year-round.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Preferred habitat**: shallow water (<20 cm depth), warm temperatures (>20° C), with adults concentrated below riffles in areas of moderate current and within algal mats along margins of flowing streams. Substrate in existing habitats is generally sandy.
- **Juveniles**: more often found in thick beds of aquatic vegetation, distinct from adult habitat preference.
- **Diet**: generalized feeders consuming bottom debris, vegetal material, aquatic insect larvae, and amphipod crustaceans.
- **Ecosystem role**: Both topminnow subspecies are believed to be as effective at mosquito control as the invasive [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).
- **Historic range**: 
  - Yaqui topminnow: Rio Yaqui basin in Arizona (Whitewater and Black draws) and tributaries in Sonora/Chihuahua, Mexico.
  - Gila topminnow: Gila River drainage below 1800 m elevation, historically widespread; currently persists in Santa Cruz River, Cienega Creek, Sonoita Creek, and several springs (Cottonwood, Monkey, Sheehy, Bylas) and Cocio Wash, Arizona.
- **Current Yaqui topminnow range**: restricted to artesian-fed springs on San Bernardino Ranch and an introduced population in a 200-meter reach of [[Leslie Creek]].
- **Co-occurring native Rio Yaqui fish species** (present or historically found at San Bernardino Ranch):
  - [[Yaqui Sucker]] (*Catostomus bernardini*)
  - [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) — recommended for endangered listing
  - [[Yaqui Shiner]] (*Notropis formosus mearnsii*)
  - [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*)
  - [[Mexican Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma ornatum*)
- **Competitive exclusion**: Both topminnow subspecies are now found only in habitats free of *Gambusia affinis*, introduced to Arizona in 1926, which drove the Gila topminnow to near-extinction by the 1940s. The Yaqui topminnow's decline, however, is attributed primarily to habitat loss rather than *Gambusia* competition, since mosquitofish have not been widely introduced into the Rio Yaqui system.

## Key Findings

- **Immediate extinction threat**: The San Bernardino Ranch, containing the last significant U.S. habitat for the Yaqui topminnow, was for sale with no guarantee of aquatic habitat preservation under new ownership; agricultural groundwater pumping could dry the artesian springs entirely.
- **Historical habitat loss**: Major river downcutting in the late 1800s and irrigation/water diversion in the early 1900s destroyed extensive historic marsh and spring habitats along the Gila, Salt, and Yaqui rivers (citing [[Hastings 1959]] and [[Miller 1961]]), confining topminnows to small permanent springs and streams.
- **Case study of habitat loss**: [[Astin Spring]] was cited by Minckley (1973) as destroyed by cattle trampling in 1969, illustrating the fragility of remaining habitats.
- **Regional context**: Of three remaining Rio Yaqui surface water areas in the U.S., Leslie Canyon is nearly dry and Rucker Canyon has been dammed for a trout lake, leaving San Bernardino Ranch as the only substantial low riparian habitat remaining in southeastern Arizona.
- **Proposed acquisition costs**: Total fee acquisition estimated at $1,000,000 (land $750,000; improvements $95,000; overhead $155,000), with additional habitat renovation costs (~$20,000) for lake/spring restoration and restocking of six fish species.
- **Ongoing management estimate**: ~$40,000/year for a full-time refuge manager and maintenance staff, potentially offset through partnership with the Arizona State Historical Society given the ranch's historic designation (former land grant of sheriff [[John H. Slaughter]]).
- **Alternatives analysis**: The plan evaluated six alternatives to full fee acquisition (partial acquisition, easements, leases, future negotiation, no action), concluding that partial measures were largely impractical or uneconomical, and that failure to act risked extinction of the Yaqui topminnow and Yaqui chub in the United States within a few years.
- **Recovery outline structure**: The broader Gila Topminnow Recovery Plan Outline (Part II) establishes a three-phase strategy: (1) protect/manage existing habitats and populations, (2) restore topminnows to suitable historic range through reintroduction, and (3) determine criteria and timelines for reclassification from endangered to threatened status and eventual delisting.
- **Genetic/propagation considerations**: The plan calls for assessing genetic heterogeneity of remaining populations before using hatchery or wild stock for reintroduction efforts, noting that without a viable Rio Yaqui tributary, artificial propagation efforts (e.g., at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico) would have no suitable location for reintroduction.
