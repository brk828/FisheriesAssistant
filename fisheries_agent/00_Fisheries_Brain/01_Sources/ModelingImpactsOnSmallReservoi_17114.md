# Modeling Impacts of Ungulate Grazing on Nutrient Mobilization into Small Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #range-hydrology

## Executive Summary

This Technical Completion Report (WRRI, January 1983) by [[Richard A. Cole]] and co-authors presents models and first-approximation coefficients for predicting how grazing animals mobilize dung and soil nutrients from western watersheds into downstream waters, particularly the hypolimnia of small reservoirs. The work combines infiltrometer experiments, natural storm monitoring in twelve watersheds of Sixteen Springs Canyon, dung decay and volatilization trials, and oxygen-demand and phosphorus-release incubations. The authors frame the problem as one affecting downstream [[Fisheries Habitat]] quality, since nutrient loading and oxygen depletion alter fish growth, yield and survival. Results are presented mainly as preliminary reference tables built around the [[Universal Soil Loss Equation]] (USLE), which the authors modify to account for dung behavior.

## Background & Description

The report is motivated by the value of western inland fisheries (New Mexico's fishery valued at $90 million per year in 1975) and by the fact that about three-quarters of western watersheds are grazed. Prior work had focused on sediment and stream morphology; the authors argue that nutrient mobilization from upland dung, especially outside floodplains, had received little study.

Cattle (*Bos taurus*) were the primary focus, with [[Mule Deer]] (*Odocoileus hemionus*) and [[Elk]] (*Cervus elaphus*) studied as wild comparators. Study sites spanned five plant formations in New Mexico ([[Creosote]], Desert Grass, [[Pinyon-Juniper]], [[Ponderosa Pine]], Mixed-Conifer) and sites in Wyoming (Aspen, Lodgepole Pine). The primary field site was Sixteen Springs Canyon in the Lincoln National Forest, where twelve adjacent south-facing watersheds (23.8 to 81.6 ha) were monitored in summer 1979. Watershed slopes ranged from steep, sparsely covered upper slopes to gentler lower slopes with moderately dense pine cover.

The conceptual model (Figure 1) links rainfall erosivity, slope length, vegetative cover and soil erodibility to dung nutrient export, floatation of dried dung, nutrient penetration into soil, oxygen demand, and phosphorus availability in the hypolimnion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dung composition:** Volatile solids in cow dung averaged about 75% of dry weight (range 69.9-87.3%). Mean hydrolizable phosphorus was about 0.20% and total nitrogen about 2.5%, with nitrogen highly age-dependent. Deer dung had significantly higher organic matter than cow dung.
- **Soils and litter:** Volatile solids in creosote soils averaged about 1.7%, while mixed-conifer soils reached 15.9%. Dung contributed less than 10% of the organic matter, phosphorus and nitrogen exported from infiltrometer plots.
- **Weather and erosivity:** Annual Rainfall Erosivity in New Mexico ranged from about 20 (Creosote) to 100 (Mixed-Conifer). Summer (July-September) storms carried roughly 50-70% of annual erosivity.
- **Reservoir behavior:** Small ephemeral-fed reservoirs in New Mexico are reported to deoxygenate their hypolimnia during summer stratification regardless of elevation (Potter 1982).
- **Downstream waters:** The Rio Penasco, fed by springs, received arroyo runoff that substantially increased suspended solids and phosphorus loads during storms.

## Key Findings

**Dung and nutrient dynamics**
- Fresh dung lost nitrogen at about three times the rate of old dung; organic matter and nitrogen losses largely stabilized within about 4 days in shade and 2 days in full sun.
- Under simulated rain, fresh cow dung lost 3 to 15 times as much phosphorus and 4 to 7 times as much organic matter as dung several days old.
- Over 95% of dung mobilized during the August 1979 storms was old, dry and easily dislodged. Deer dung was 34% more erodable than cow dung.

**Watershed mobilization**
- During a 2-week period in summer 1979 (mean Rainfall Erosivity 125), recognizable cow dung declined by 65% and deer dung by 87.5%.
- Dung loss exceeded USLE-based predictions. Empirical floatation multipliers were estimated at 5.31 (volatile solids), 2.65 (phosphorus) and 18.6 (nitrogen) times the fresh-dung-on-impermeable-surface values.
- Dung deposition averaged 25 to 50% more on bare than on covered surfaces, and deer deposits were more often on bare ground.

**Oxygen demand and phosphorus release**
- Mixtures of fresh cow dung and soil (25% dung) generated an oxygen demand about five times that predicted from the separate materials; 5% dung mixtures were not similarly stimulated.
- Phosphorus release rose once oxygen fell below 1 mg/L. Soil alone released less than 11% of its phosphorus.
- In reservoir modeling, the authors predicted that hypolimnia would be deoxygenated for part of the stratification period in all hypothetical reservoirs.

**Predictive framework**
- Preliminary reference tables link stocking rate, forage production, ground cover, bare area, rainfall erosivity and reservoir loading. They are explicitly described as first approximations requiring refinement.
- The authors estimate that doubling the phosphorus associated with soil loss would occur if a Pinyon-Juniper range with 60% grass production were reduced to nearly no grass.

**Wild versus domestic ungulates**
- Deer and cattle generally use different forage and areas, so deer dung input would likely persist if livestock were removed.
- Cattle exert less pressure per unit weight than deer or elk because of their larger feet, but trampling effects depend on biomass distribution.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Models can simulate dung deposition, erosion and downstream oxygen and phosphorus effects, but coefficient accuracy limits prediction. The authors call for further research on floatation, deer dung erosion under artificial rain, soil-dung interactions, and nutrient sorption.
- Fresh dung in summer storms is the most important short-term contributor where grazing overlaps the rainy season, particularly at higher elevations. At lower elevations, the authors judged that soil-borne nutrients alone could deoxygenate hypolimnia.
- Overgrazing in southern New Mexico was expected mainly to degrade water quality in higher-elevation reservoirs.
