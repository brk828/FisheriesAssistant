# A Check List of Fishes of Oklahoma (Moore, 1952)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 check list, compiled by [[George A. Moore]] and presented as an Oklahoma fishing guide, catalogues 157 numbered fish entries from [[Oklahoma]] freshwaters across 21 families. It gives common names with scientific names and author citations, and it adds practical advice for anglers and students. The document also explains the value of scientific names and notes the lack of standardized common names. It is a regional taxonomic inventory with an educational and conservation purpose rather than a research study with measured data.

## Background & Description

The checklist opens with a note that common names are followed by scientific names. It explains that under zoological nomenclature no two kinds of animals may share a name, and it uses the [[Longnose Gar]] (*Lepisosteus osseus*, Linnaeus) as an example. Parentheses around an author's name indicate that the original author used the specific epithet in a different genus.

Common names were not standardized at the time. The compiler states that the list follows the [[American Fisheries Society]] list where possible, or at least has the approval of the committee chairman, Dr. R. M. Bailey of the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]]. The committee aimed to avoid names that suggest false relationships.

The checklist is organized by family, with the following counts (approximate, from the numbered entries):

- **Cyprinidae (minnows):** the largest group, including the Southern Redbelly Dace, the Hybopsis chubs, the Notropis shiners (about 40 species), and the Phenacobius, Hybognathus, Pimephales, and Campostoma minnows.
- **Catostomidae (suckers):** 14 species, including the [[Blue Sucker]], [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], [[Smallmouth Buffalo]], and several redhorse species.
- **Centrarchidae (sunfishes):** 17 species, including the [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[White Crappie]] and [[Black Crappie]], Rock Bass, and Flier.
- **Percidae (perches and darters):** about 33 species, including the [[Walleye]], Sauger, Yellow Perch, Log Perch, and numerous darters (*Etheostoma* and *Percina*).
- **Ameiuridae (catfishes):** 13 entries, including the [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), the Blue Catfish, and several bullhead and madtom species.

The compiler also provides a list of common-name equivalents for Oklahoma anglers, for example the "spoonbill cat" (Paddlefish, *Polyodon spathula*) and the "grindle" (Bowfin, *Amia calva*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses several environmental and management issues:

- **Pollution:** The compiler identifies pollution as one of the greatest threats to fish. Common sources include oil and salt brine from oil fields, soil erosion that causes silt in streams and lakes, and untreated sewage discharged into waterways.
- **Habitat enhancement:** Old Christmas trees and brush bundles wired together and sunk in deep water are described as good concentration sites for crappie and bluegill. This is an early example of artificial fish habitat.
- **Overpopulation:** Farm ponds should be heavily fished to prevent overcrowding and stunting of game fish.
- **Commercial fishing:** The document describes commercial fishermen as competitors of sport anglers. Removing non-game fish with commercial nets and seines is said to improve conditions for game fish.
- **Regulations:** Oklahoma waters are described as generously open to fishing, with no closed areas and no closed seasons on most game species. Bag limits apply to game fish, and the document notes that anglers should keep a record of their catch (bait, place, depth, hour, species, and weight).

## Key Findings

1. **Species inventory:** The checklist includes 157 numbered fish entries across 21 families, with the Cyprinidae containing the most species. Several entries in the OCR-scanned text are incomplete, and some species names are missing from the scan.
2. **Nomenclature guidance:** Scientific names are essential for avoiding confusion, because common names are not standardized and vary among regions.
3. **Common-name disputes:** Local names such as "spoonbill cat" for the Paddlefish and "grindle" for the Bowfin are identified as misleading and are replaced with the names used in the list.
4. **Conservation threats:** Pollution from oil and salt brine, soil erosion, and untreated sewage is identified as a major threat to Oklahoma fishes.
5. **Management recommendations:** The compiler recommends heavy fishing of farm ponds to control stunting, the use of sunken brush and Christmas tree structures as fish habitat, catch recording, and reduction of commercial netting so that game fish stocks can recover.
6. **Regulatory context:** The compiler notes that Oklahoma waters have no closed areas and no closed game-fish seasons, and that bag limits apply to game fish.
