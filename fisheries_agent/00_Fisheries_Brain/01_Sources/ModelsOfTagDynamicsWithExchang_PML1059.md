# Modes of Tag Dynamics with Exchange Between Available and Unavailable Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #scombridae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 study by [[Ray Hilborn]], published in the *Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences* (Vol. 46), develops and tests four mathematical models of [[Tag-Recovery Analysis]] for [[Skipjack Tuna]] (*Katsuwonus pelamis*). The work was motivated by a [[South Pacific Commission]] (SPC) tagging programme in which more than 140,000 skipjack were tagged and released from 1977 to 1980 in the waters of Fiji, Papua New Guinea and the Solomon Islands. The models address whether tag loss reflects fishing mortality, natural mortality, seasonal changes in vulnerability, or exchange with an invulnerable population. Model fit was tested against data from all three countries, and the results were used to estimate standing stock, throughput and fishing mortality for each fishery.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Skipjack Tuna]] (*Katsuwonus pelamis*), a pelagic scombrid fished by purse seine and pole-and-line in the tropical Pacific.
- **Research context:** Earlier tagging analyses by [[Kleiber et al. 1987]] and [[Clark and Mangel 1979]] assumed simple tag-loss dynamics. Clark and Mangel proposed a model in which skipjack are vulnerable to purse seining only when schooled, and in which an invulnerable portion of the population exists.
- **Stated aim:** To (1) develop alternative models of skipjack population dynamics using tag recoveries, and (2) determine the productivity, size and exploitation status of skipjack stocks in the study countries.
- **Data source:** Tag recovery records, catch and effort data from the SPC skipjack tagging programme, with releases spanning 1977 to 1980.
- **Terminology:** Throughput (tons per month that die, are captured, or emigrate from an area) is treated as the maximum sustainable yield under constant recruitment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biological processes modelled:** Natural mortality, emigration, immigration, recruitment and seasonal movement away from fishing grounds.
- **Seasonality:** The [[Papua New Guinea Skipjack Fishery]] showed strong seasonal changes in catch per unit of effort (CPUE), which the author linked to seasonal movement or an annual availability cycle.
- **Exchange with other stocks:** Skipjack stocks are connected to adjacent fisheries by exchange, and the author notes this exchange is poorly understood.
- **Management relevance:** Clark and Mangel's model implies a maximum stock size for the vulnerable population, with implications for overexploitation thresholds.

## Key Findings

**Four hypotheses tested:**

1. **Model I (constant mortality):** Fishing mortality is proportional to effort, with constant natural mortality. Adequate for Fiji and the Solomon Islands.
2. **Model II (fishing mortality proportional to effort):** Fishing mortality varies with effort and time-dependent gear efficiency. Gave a much better fit for Fiji and Papua New Guinea, and a small improvement for the Solomon Islands.
3. **Model III (seasonal changes in vulnerability):** Catch is directly proportional to fishing mortality. Best fit for Papua New Guinea (r² = 0.93 reported for the model fit).
4. **Model IV (invulnerable population with exchange):** Continuous exchange between vulnerable and invulnerable populations. Gave a better fit than Model III for Papua New Guinea (significant at the 0.05 level only under an assumed extra-parameter test), but the evidence was not overwhelming.

**Data set results:**

- **Fiji:** Model I and II adequate; Model IV gave no improvement. Recovery ratio of 9.2% (lowest of the three).
- **Papua New Guinea:** Only data set showing good evidence of seasonal changes in availability or vulnerability. Recovery ratio of 12.5% (highest). The catch was nearly 10 times that of Fiji. Total loss rate was roughly double that of Fiji. The model IV fit suggested no natural mortality, with all loss attributed to movement out of the fishing area.
- **Solomon Islands:** Data from 3,731 tagged fish released in June 1980. High proportion of returns and slow tag disappearance. Total loss rate about 0.1 per month, with natural mortality roughly 2–3% per month. Little seasonality detected, so Model I/II were considered adequate.

**Standing stock and throughput (Table 4 summary):**

- Fiji and Papua New Guinea stock estimates differed substantially, but their throughput estimates were nearly identical, as were their catches.
- Fishing mortality estimates were considered the most reliable output of the study.
- Estimated natural mortality plus emigration, derived by subtracting fishing mortality from total loss rate Z, showed large differences between countries. If a few percent of the Solomon Islands loss is attributed to emigration, natural mortality is about 5–6% per month, implying most loss from Fiji and Papua New Guinea was due to emigration.

**Methodological conclusions:**

- Regression (log-linear) estimation of parameters was used, with [[Weighted Least Squares]] proposed as a refinement to give more weight to early recaptures.
- Variance estimates were derived by the [[Delta Method]] and bootstrapping; a coefficient of variation of 0.1 was assumed for catch.
- The author notes that model choice has direct management consequences: using model I when model III is true can lead to underestimation of natural mortality and overestimation of stock.

**Management implications:**

- Measured throughput may be equivalent to potential yield if recruitment is independent of harvest, which parallels classic yield-per-recruit optimisation.
- Under a different model, a yield ceiling could be lower, so the choice of model affects management advice.
- The author states the model IV hypothesis could not be distinguished from throughput models using the data available, and recommends further investigation.
