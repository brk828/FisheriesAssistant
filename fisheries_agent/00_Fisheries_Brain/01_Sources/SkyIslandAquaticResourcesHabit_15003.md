# Sky Island Aquatic Resources: Habitats and Refugia for Native Fishes

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This paper examines the aquatic habitats and native fish fauna of the Sky Island region in southeastern Arizona, part of the northern [[Madrean Archipelago]]. The combination of basin-and-range topography and orographic rainfall produces isolated perennial streams, springs, and cienegas that support an un-diverse but uniquely adapted native fish fauna of 13 extant species. The majority (75%) of the landscape is under federal or state ownership, providing critical protection for aquatic habitats. Major threats include aquifer pumping, water diversions, and introduced non-native species. Several species have been extirpated, including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]] from major rivers, and an undescribed pupfish from [[Monkey Springs]]. Conservation security depends on public land ownership, species listing status, vigilant monitoring, recovery activities, and international cooperation with Mexico.

## Background & Description

The Sky Islands of southeastern Arizona represent the northern extent of the [[Madrean Archipelago]], characterized by isolated block-faulted mountains rising to over 2,200 m elevation. The [[Pinaleno Mountains]] contain the highest peaks ([[Mt. Graham]] at 3,060 m and [[Heliograph Peak]] at 3,270 m). Four major river valleys lie between the mountain ranges: the [[San Pedro River]], [[San Simon River]], [[Santa Cruz River]] (all flowing north to the [[Gila River]]), and [[Sulfur Springs Valley]] (draining south to Mexico). In geologic time, all rivers flowed south into Mexico.

The region's aquatic habitats include: (1) disjunct perennial streams on mountain ranges and their bajadas and pediments with base flows mostly less than one cubic foot per second; (2) isolated springs and spring runs at varying elevations, including artesian springs in extreme southeastern Arizona; and (3) valley streams sustained by basin aquifers. Cienegas (wet meadows) were historically more widespread but have been largely lost.

The climate features a bimodal precipitation pattern with monsoon rains in July-August from the Gulf of Mexico and winter Pacific storms. Annual precipitation ranges from less than 25.4 cm in valleys to 2-2.5 times that amount in mountains. May-June represents the driest period. Vegetation ranges from desert scrub in basins through semidesert grassland to evergreen forests on mountain tops, with riparian corridors of cottonwood ([[Populus]]), sycamore ([[Platanus]]), and other hardwoods lining streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The extant native fish fauna comprises 13 species, predominantly cypriniforms (minnows and suckers), characteristic of desert regions worldwide. The region is the center of distribution for [[Gila Chub]] ([[Gila intermedia]]) and [[Gila Topminnow]] ([[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]]). Species composition varies by stream: [[Aravaipa Creek]] contains seven native species including two federally threatened species ([[Spikedace]], [[Meda fulgida]], and [[Loach Minnow]], [[Rhinichthys cobitis]]). [[Eagle Creek]], [[Redfield Creek]], and [[Sonoita Creek]] each sustain five native species.

Several taxa have been extirpated from the region: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] from the [[Gila River]] and [[San Pedro River]]; [[Desert Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon macularius]]) from major rivers; an undescribed pupfish from [[Monkey Springs]] (extinct by 1971 due to [[Largemouth Bass]] introduction); [[Beautiful Shiner]] ([[Cyprinella formosa]]) from [[San Bernardino Creek]]; [[Yaqui Catfish]] ([[Ictalurus pricei]]) and [[Yaqui Sucker]] ([[Catostomus bernardini]]) from Rio Yaqui headwaters.

Land ownership is critical to conservation: 75% of the landscape is under federal (U.S. Forest Service 18%, Bureau of Land Management 17%) and state (31%) jurisdiction. Key protected areas include [[Coronado National Forest]], [[San Pedro National Riparian Conservation Area]], [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area]], [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (2,309 acres acquired 1982), and [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] (100,000+ acres). The Nature Conservancy's [[Canelo Hills]] and [[Sonoita Creek]] preserves protect habitats for six native species.

## Key Findings

**Habitat Loss and Alteration**: Extensive arroyo cutting commenced in the late 1800s, attributed to excessive livestock grazing and irrigation diversions. Water tables lowered up to 5+ meters in some stream reaches, causing dramatic loss of cienegas and perennial river reaches. The [[San Simon River]] and [[San Pedro River]] now flow subsurface and intermittently except during high runoff events. Irrigation diversions combined with aquifer mining caused demise of large river species.

**Introduced Species Impacts**: Introduction of [[Western Mosquitofish]] ([[Gambusia affinis]]) for mosquito control has severely impacted native [[Gila Topminnow]] through direct predation and competition. [[Green Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis cyanellus]]) is displacing [[Gila Chub]] in [[Sabino Creek]]. [[Flathead Catfish]] ([[Pylodictus olivaris]]) predation prevented [[Razorback Sucker]] reintroduction attempts in the [[Gila River]]. The undescribed [[Monkey Springs]] pupfish and unique [[Gila Chub]] form were rendered extinct by [[Largemouth Bass]] introduction in 1969.

**Conservation Status**: Two species are federally threatened ([[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]). Multiple species are candidates for listing or Forest Service sensitive species. [[Aravaipa Creek]] has instream flow rights in "permitted status." Few listed species have official, fully-funded recovery plans functioning despite legal protection.

**Management Strategies**: (1) Public land ownership provides jurisdiction over 75% of aquatic habitats; (2) Strategic acquisitions by agencies and The Nature Conservancy protect key habitats; (3) [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] manages for six Rio Yaqui native species, currently sustaining three (Yaqui chub and topminnow, beautiful shiner) with plans to reestablish three more; (4) Forest Service is drafting "Conservation Assessments" for candidate species to prevent listing; (5) Periodic monitoring is necessary for early warning of threats; (6) International cooperation with Mexico is essential as approximately half the species occur in northern Mexico.

**Critical Recommendations**: Cessation of non-native species stocking for sport and bait is essential. Barriers may be necessary to prevent upstream invasion of non-natives. Stream renovations using chemical treatment should be used only as last resort due to impacts on macroinvertebrates. Active, focused recovery efforts must move beyond listing status to on-the-ground activity. The U.S. Forest Service entered into an MOU with Mexico to foster cooperation on shared species management.
