# Knowledge, Beliefs, and Attitudes Toward Endangered Fish of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado State University project report, prepared for the [[Recovery Program for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin]], assesses public awareness of, beliefs about, attitudes toward, and support for recovering four endangered fish: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. A telephone survey of Colorado and Utah residents (N = 897) drew on four strata: elected officials (n = 168), anglers (n = 300), environmental group members (n = 125), and a general public sample (n = 304). The study found low unprompted recall of the fish, moderate-to-high general support for recovery, and substantial public ambivalence about stocking non-native sportfish. A predictive model suggested that promotional efforts should emphasize the fish's endangered status rather than agency recovery actions.

## Background & Description

The four fish are native to the Upper Colorado River Basin and are described as found nowhere else in the world. Their decline is attributed to interacting pressures: hundreds of dams and water diversions that blocked migration, altered temperatures, and flattened the historical spring-peak flow regime; competition and predation from non-native species; and pollution, parasites, fish control efforts, and hybridization. The report notes that by 1982, 76% of the 55 fish species in the basin were non-native.

The [[Recovery Program for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin]] is a 15-year, multi-agency effort (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, Western Area Power Administration, the states of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, Upper Basin water users, and environmental organizations) aimed at re-establishing self-sustaining populations while allowing continued water development. The Information and Education Committee commissioned the study to establish baseline public attitudes and to evaluate and target its outreach.

The study objectives were to: (1) measure awareness and knowledge of the fish's decline; (2) examine beliefs and attitudes toward recovery and preservation; (3) assess general and program-specific support; and (4) evaluate attitudes toward stocking non-native sportfish such as bass, catfish and tiger muskie.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report identifies the following pressures on the native fishes:

- **Flow alteration:** Historically, flows peaked in spring from snowmelt and were low the rest of the year; dams made flows more even year-round.
- **Non-native fish:** Stocked species cause competition and predation (examples given: red shiner, channel catfish, northern pike, fathead minnow).
- **Other stressors:** Pollution, parasites, fish control efforts and hybridization.

Recovery actions referenced in the survey include restoring historically flooded wetlands, stocking hatchery-raised fish, changing dam operations to provide higher spring flows, building fish passageways around dams, and constructing ponds to hold fish in protected settings. Stocking of [[Mosquito Fish]] and [[Grass Carp]] was presented as a biological control practice distinct from sportfish stocking.

The sampling frame covered the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] tributaries: Yampa, White, Gunnison, Dolores, Green, Duchesne and Colorado rivers.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Telephone survey conducted spring 1994 at Colorado State University; stratified random sampling; total N = 897 (545 Colorado, 352 Utah).
- Anglers were drawn from [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] and [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] license records. Environmental members were sampled via Survey Sampling, Inc.
- Margin of error: about ±10% at 90% confidence for the large strata; ±2% at 98% for elected officials; no interval calculable for environmental members.

**Knowledge and Awareness**
- Unprompted, Colorado Squawfish (26%) was the most frequently named endangered fish; Humpback Chub 18%, Bonytail Chub 6%; Razorback Sucker was named by no respondents. Bald Eagle (32%) was the most-mentioned endangered species overall.
- 61% said they were aware of endangered fish in the basin (elected officials 79%, anglers 69%, environmental members 68%, general public 39%).
- After prompting, 84% had heard of the four fish; 70% recognized the squawfish, 61% the razorback and humpback, and 49% the bonytail.
- Word of mouth (58% combined with newspapers as leading sources) and newspapers were most cited; educational presentations (16%), public meetings (15%) and slide shows/videos (10%) were least cited.

**Beliefs and Attitudes Toward the Fish**
- 61% believed the fish were endangered; 14% did not; 25% did not know. Environmental members (78%) were most convinced.
- 49% held a positive general attitude; 36% were neutral; 15% negative. Environmental members were most positive (70%); elected officials most negative (24% positive per the group-level report).
- Causes of decline: water pollution (79% agreement) and dams (61–62%) ranked highest; "events in nature" contributed more than human activity drew only 30% agreement.
- Basic beliefs: 94% agreed that fish have a right to live if capable of surviving; 76% supported self-sustaining populations; 60% felt fish should be managed for human benefit.
- Stereotypes were held by roughly a quarter to a third; elected officials and anglers held them more than the other groups.

**Reasons for Recovery**
- Strongest reasons: recovery is as important as recovering birds and mammals (81%); fish maintain balance in nature (73%); recover whenever possible (72%).
- Weak reasons: squawfish size (42%) and sportfish potential (38%).
- Cost (38%) and economic development were not widely seen as reasons against recovery; water use for other purposes (30%) was a modest concern.

**Recovery Program**
- 77% had not heard of the Recovery Program; awareness was highest among elected officials (42%) and lowest among the general public (12%).
- Among the 23% who had heard of it, most correctly identified program components such as altering dam operations (18%) and stocking hatchery fish (17%). Elected officials incorrectly believed the program prohibits new water development (17%).
- Two-thirds (66%) supported recovery efforts; environmental members 82%, general public 70%, anglers 61%, elected officials 60%.

**Stocking of Non-Native Sportfish**
- About one-third had heard of the stocking issue (elected officials 48%, anglers 38%, environmental members 32%, general public 23%).
- 59% believed stocking improves fishing; 75% said it should occur only if endangered fish are not harmed.
- Attitudes were split: 35% positive, 37% neutral, 28% negative. After being informed of the issue, 49% opposed stocking (environmental members 70%); 34% supported it (anglers 46%).
- 54% approved of stocking [[Mosquito Fish]] and [[Grass Carp]] for insect and weed control.

**Predictive Model (Value → Attitude → Behavior)**
- The model explained 72% of variance in support for recovery (R² = 72%).
- Basic beliefs about fish rights and existence were positively linked to decline beliefs, preservation beliefs and negatively to stereotypes; the use-oriented belief was negatively linked to preservation beliefs.
- General and specific attitudes both predicted support (β = .15 and .33). Preservation beliefs had the strongest direct effect (β = .46).
- Beliefs about the Recovery Program were unrelated to attitudes or support.

**Management Recommendations**
- Target the general public and elected officials in education efforts, since the general public had the lowest awareness and elected officials the most stereotypes.
- Newspapers are cheaper and reach wider audiences than presentations or videos.
- Inform the public of the fish's endangered status before promoting agency actions.
- Clarify the relationship between non-native sportfish stocking and harm to endangered fish.
