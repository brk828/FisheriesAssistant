## Executive Summary

This 1949 study by Richard H. Stroud (TVA) examines growth rates and condition ("K" factor) of seven game and pan fish species across three TVA storage reservoirs—[[Cherokee Reservoir]], [[Douglas Reservoir]], and [[Hiwassee Reservoir]]—and compares results to previously studied [[Norris Reservoir]]. Based on scale-aging analysis of 616 fish sampled 1945–1947 via gill nets, hook and line, and rotenone (derris root), the paper concludes that growth rates in all three reservoirs were rapid but generally slower than in Norris, the oldest and largest TVA impoundment. Growth differences were attributed less to the age of impoundment and more to growing-season length (linked to altitude), population density, food supply, pollution, and parasite loads. Condition factor ("K") tracked growth rate patterns closely, with Norris fish consistently the heaviest-for-length, Hiwassee fish the leanest, and Cherokee/Douglas intermediate.

## Background & Description

- **Total Length (T.L.) to Standard Length (S.L.) ratios** were newly established for two species not previously covered in Norris studies:
  - [[White Crappie]]: average T.L./S.L. = 1.260 (n=61), ranging from 1.279 (140–179mm S.L.) to 1.246 (220–259mm S.L.)
  - [[Sauger]]: average T.L./S.L. = 1.183 (n=121), ranging from 1.211 (140–179mm S.L.) to 1.163 (260–299mm S.L.)
  - Conversion factors: S.L. (mm) × 0.0497 = T.L. (inches) for white crappie; × 0.0466 for sauger.
- **Condition Factor ("K")** was used as a proxy for relative plumpness/heaviness, calculated via the standard length-weight formula.
  - Highest average K values were consistently recorded for [[Norris Reservoir]] fish across nearly all species.
  - [[Black Crappie]] showed the highest overall K values (2.79–3.38 across reservoirs; 3.07 in Norris).
  - [[Sauger]] and [[Walleye]] exhibited the lowest K values (1.29–1.53), consistent with their more elongate, piscivorous body form.
  - Seasonal variation strongly affects K: Norris fish are heaviest in late winter (pre-spawn) and leanest immediately post-spawn or after rapid summer growth.
- **Parasitism** noted: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Black Crappie]] in Hiwassee were heavily infected with larval bass tapeworm (*Proteocephalus ambloplites*), identified by Dr. Ralph V. Bangham. No infections were found in white crappie or walleye, suggesting a species-specific parasite burden potentially correlated with growth suppression (citing Cross, 1935).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reservoir habitat comparisons** (Table 1):
  - [[Cherokee Reservoir]] and [[Douglas Reservoir]] are comparable in area (30,000–34,000 acres), shallow, similarly aged, and drain sparsely forested, agricultural watersheds—resulting in turbid water and higher pollution/organic loading from suburban and industrial sources.
  - [[Hiwassee Reservoir]] is smaller (~1/5 the area, ~1/6 the storage capacity of Norris) but comparable in depth and forested-watershed character to Norris; it sits at a much higher altitude, producing a shorter growing season.
  - [[Norris Reservoir]] is the oldest, deepest, and largest-capacity reservoir studied, with a heavily forested, low-agriculture watershed and minimal pollution.
- **Species distribution patterns**:
  - [[Walleye]] and [[Sauger]] populations are large in Norris and Hiwassee but nearly absent (≤1% of catch) in Cherokee and Douglas, likely due to unfavorable temperature/oxygen conditions in the latter two reservoirs.
  - [[Black Crappie]] and [[White Crappie]] dominate catches in Cherokee and Douglas (88–90% of total catch), likely because the absence of piscivorous walleye/sauger removes a key predatory check on crappie population density.
  - [[Gizzard Shad]], the primary forage fish for walleye in Norris, were not part of Hiwassee's original fauna and were only introduced post-impoundment — likely contributing to slower walleye growth there.
- **Population density vs. growth rate**: An inverse relationship was repeatedly observed — reservoirs/species with higher population densities (e.g., Norris walleye post-1942 spawn, Hiwassee bass/crappie) showed depressed growth rates and lower condition factors, even under increased fishing pressure.
- **Trophic dynamics**: Walleye and sauger are described as almost entirely piscivorous, potentially serving a top-down regulatory role limiting crappie overpopulation in Norris — a mechanism absent in Cherokee/Douglas.

## Key Findings

1. **616 total fish sampled** (1945–1947): 371 from Cherokee, 119 from Douglas, 126 from Hiwassee, comprising [[Spotted Bass]] (84), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (26), [[Largemouth Bass]] (184), [[Black Crappie]] (122), [[White Crappie]] (61), [[Sauger]] (109), and [[Walleye]] (30).
2. **General growth ranking**: Norris > Cherokee ≈ Douglas > Hiwassee for nearly all species except sauger, which grew comparably or slightly faster in Cherokee/Douglas than Norris.
3. **Minimum legal length attainment ages**:
   - [[Largemouth Bass]] (9 in.): ~1 year in Cherokee/Douglas (spring); late summer as 1-year-olds in Hiwassee.
   - [[Smallmouth Bass]] (9 in.): ~2 years in Norris and Hiwassee, though later-life growth much slower in Hiwassee.
   - [[Spotted Bass]] (9 in.): <2 years in Norris; slightly over 2 years in Cherokee; ~2.5 years in Douglas/Hiwassee.
   - [[Black Crappie]] (8 in.): <2 years in Norris; ~early 3rd year in Cherokee/Hiwassee; ~3 years in Douglas despite fast first-year growth.
   - [[White Crappie]] (8 in.): <2 years in Cherokee; <3 years in Hiwassee; growth rate faster than black crappie in Cherokee/Douglas.
   - [[Sauger]] (10 in.): 2nd year (spring) in Cherokee, Douglas, and Norris.
   - [[Walleye]] (15 in.): ~1.5 years in Norris; ≥3 years in Hiwassee. Norris 4-year-olds averaged ~4 inches longer and ~22+ ounces heavier than Hiwassee counterparts of the same age.
4. **Condition factor (K) ranking mirrors growth rate ranking**: Norris highest, Hiwassee lowest, Cherokee/Douglas intermediate — reinforcing the link between growth rate, food supply/population density, and body condition.
5. **Walleye growth in Norris declined** following an exceptionally successful 1942 spawning event that increased population density, despite the elimination of the closed fishing season in 1944 (increased fishing pressure did not offset density-driven growth suppression).
6. **Sinkhole rescue data point** (Cherokee Reservoir, winter 1946): 18,000 fish rescued from a 200-acre sinkhole area yielded minimum density estimates of 54 legal-sized crappie and 36 bass (12 legal-sized) per acre — offering rare direct evidence of standing population density.
7. **Cross-reservoir comparison underscores** that impoundment age alone does not predict growth rate; growing season length (altitude-driven), food supply, population density, pollution, and parasite load are more predictive variables than reservoir age.
