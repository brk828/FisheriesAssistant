# Artificial Hybrids Between Amur Pike and North American Esocids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This study describes artificial hybrids between the [[Amur Pike]] (*Esox reicherti*), the only member of the family [[Esocidae]] not naturally occurring in North America, and several North American esocids. The work was carried out in 1973 at the [[Union City Hatchery]] and the [[Benner Spring Fish Research Station]] of the [[Pennsylvania Fish Commission]]. Of 10 possible reciprocal crosses, six were produced; five are described in detail, and a sixth (Amur pike female × muskellunge male) was successful but is not described because of a sample-integrity problem. Interspecific fertility was unexpectedly high, and survival of crosses was inversely related to the difference in potential maximum size between parent species. The authors also report a fertile F1 female, a backcross, and an F2 generation, and note that hybrids had been released into a Pennsylvania lake.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and evolution.** The family comprises five species in the single genus *Esox*. Three are strictly North American, one is circumpolar, and one (*E. reicherti*) is confined to northeastern Asia. The family has a fossil record extending back probably to the Eocene, and its karyotype (2n = 50) is the most generalized among esocoids ([[Karyotype]]). Morphology and lens-protein data (Eckroat 1974) split the family into two groups:
- **Pikes:** [[Northern Pike]] (*E. lucius*), Amur pike (*E. reicherti*), [[Muskellunge]] (*E. masquinongy*)
- **Pickerels:** [[Redfin Pickerel]] (*E. americanus americanus*), [[Grass Pickerel]] (*E. a. vermiculatus*), [[Chain Pickerel]] (*E. niger*)

**Motivation.** The earlier study by [[Crossman and Buss 1965]] produced hybrids of all possible reciprocal crosses among the North American species, but Amur pike was unavailable. The aims were to add genetic data on esocid evolution and to support identification and law enforcement, since hybrids were increasingly used in sport-fishery management. The first aim was hampered by the lack of Amur pike data, and the second was limited because Amur pike and hybrids had already been released into [[Glendale Lake]], Cambria County, Pennsylvania.

**Parental stock.** Amur pike eggs were sent from the USSR to Pennsylvania in 1968–70 and reared to adulthood in ponds at Benner Springs. North American parents were taken from:
- Muskellunge: Edinboro Lake, PA (Ohio River population)
- Northern pike: Conneaut Lake, PA (Allegheny River)
- Chain pickerel: Hankins Pond, Delaware River system, PA
- Redfin pickerel: tributary of the Lehigh River, PA
- Grass pickerel: Allegheny River, PA

**Morphological description of *E. reicherti*** (from [[Nikolsky 1956]], [[Berg 1962]], and Royal Ontario Museum specimens):
- Anal rays 15–19; dorsal rays 17–22
- Branchiostegal rays usually 5 + 8 or 6 + 7; submandibular pores usually 5/5
- Lateral-line scales 130–165; vertebrae 60–65
- Length usually to 1 m; weight to 16 kg in the USSR
- A 114 cm TL specimen was caught by an angler in Glendale Lake in 1976
- Coloration: dark spots on a golden-brown to green back and flanks, unlike the light markings on dark background of *E. lucius*. Young lack the golden mid-dorsal stripe of young muskellunge.

In general, Amur pike most closely resembles northern pike in counts, measurements, and potential maximum size, but its coloration resembles muskellunge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

In the USSR, Amur pike is not strongly associated with heavily vegetated lakes or slow rivers, unlike North American esocids. It is said to inhabit rocky areas in the central portions of moderately flowing rivers as well as lakes. The species is restricted to the Amur River system, one of the few Asian watersheds without [[Northern Pike]].

The authors conclude that none of the 10 crosses is likely to occur in nature. The Amur pike and northern pike are both Asian, but the pickerels and muskellunge evolved on a different continent (North America), so the hybrids' existence reflects artificial conditions. The authors suggest that geographic and behavioral barriers maintain species integrity in nature, since hybrid frequency is low even where two species overlap.

## Key Findings

**Crosses and sample status**
- Six of 10 reciprocal combinations were produced:
  - Amur pike ♀ × muskellunge ♂ (successful, not described)
  - Amur pike ♀ × muskellunge ♂ (described, sample integrity doubtful after a handling error)
  - Amur pike ♀ × northern pike ♂ (total length 56–144 mm)
  - Amur pike ♂ × northern pike ♀ (total length 60–188 mm)
  - Amur pike ♂ × chain pickerel ♀ (one specimen, 135 mm)
  - Amur pike ♂ × grass pickerel ♀ (five specimens, 63–154 mm)
- Three crosses failed to hatch, all involving male North American parents: Amur pike ♀ × chain pickerel ♂, Amur pike ♀ × redfin pickerel ♂, and Amur pike ♀ × grass pickerel ♂.
- The Amur pike ♀ × redfin pickerel ♂ cross was never made because no female redfin pickerel was available. The reciprocal was attempted, but no eggs hatched.

**Morphological results**
- *Amur pike × muskellunge:* Color pattern, head squamation, and vertebral number resembled muskellunge. Snout length and anal rays resembled Amur pike. Lateral-line scale count was lower than either parent.
- *Amur pike × northern pike:* Young hybrids resembled northern pike more than Amur pike in pattern, though the spot patterns were intermediate. Branchiostegal ray counts exceeded the highest values of either parent. Values for most characters were close to both parents, so it was difficult to say whether the hybrids were intermediate or resembled one parent.
- *Amur pike × chain pickerel:* The pattern (broad, dark, irregular bars) resembled neither parent. The branchiostegal ray arrangement (6 + 9 – 6 + 9) was known for chain pickerel but not Amur pike. Vertebral count was intermediate.
- *Amur pike × grass pickerel:* Pattern resembled that of the reciprocal northern pike × grass pickerel cross. Vertebral and lateral-line counts were intermediate, and opercular scalation resembled grass pickerel.
- Over 10 characters, two hybrids resembled Amur pike more and three resembled the other parent more (two northern pike and one muskellunge). Vertebral number was intermediate in four of five hybrids.

**Fertility and hatching**
- *Muskellunge crosses:* Amur pike ♀ × muskellunge ♂ hatch was under 10%, with most fry dying before yolk absorption. The reciprocal hatched about 20%.
- *Northern pike crosses:* Amur pike ♀ × northern pike ♂ had a 50–60% hatch. The reciprocal had about 20%. Both crosses produced normal-appearing survivors.
- *Pickerel crosses:* Hatch was 40–60% for Amur pike ♀ × chain pickerel ♂ and Amur pike ♀ × grass pickerel ♂, but 50% or more of fry were crippled and survival was low.
- The authors note that egg quality did not explain the failures, since the eggs used had the greater potential size in each case.

**Fertility of hybrids and backcrosses**
- A female hybrid (Amur pike × northern pike) was backcrossed to an Amur pike male in spring 1975. The offspring were viable, and 200 individuals were alive in December 1976.
- In spring 1976, the backcross offspring survived as well as F1 hybrids and were kept as brood stock.
- Eggs from the Amur pike × northern pike hybrid were fertilized with sperm from the same cross. Survival was as good as F1 hybrids, and 186 progeny were released unmarked into Glendale Lake, PA.
- [[Davisson 1971]] had earlier suggested that F1 Amur pike × northern pike hybrids were fertile in both sexes.

**Main conclusions**
- Interspecific fertility was unexpectedly high, and survival was inversely related to the difference in potential maximum size between parents. Crosses between pikes (Amur pike, northern pike, muskellunge) performed well, while crosses with pickerels performed poorly.
- Complete genetic incompatibility does not occur in the family. Fertile F1 hybrids and backcrosses can be produced both within the Nearctic species and between the Palaearctic species (Amur pike and northern pike).
- Pattern and morphology of hybrids were generally more like the other parent, except for dark fin spots, which occur only in Amur pike and muskellunge in nature.
- Further efforts to repeat certain crosses were considered unnecessary, since the results were clear enough for practical identification purposes.
