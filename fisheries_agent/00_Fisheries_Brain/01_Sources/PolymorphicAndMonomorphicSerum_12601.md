# Polymorphic and Monomorphic Serum Esterase Heterogeneity in Catostomid Fish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by [[Richard K. Koehn]] and [[David I. Rasmussen]] (Arizona State University) describes serum esterase variation in western North American suckers of the genus [[Catostomus]]. The authors report a one-locus, two-allele esterase polymorphism (Es-I) in populations of [[Catostomus clarki]] of the lower [[Colorado River]] system. The allele frequency follows a clinal pattern correlated with latitude, which the authors interpret as evidence of selective maintenance of the polymorphism. Sympatric species of the subgenus Catostomus show a monomorphic double-banded phenotype that matches the heterozygote of the polymorphic populations, suggesting a different genetic solution to the same selective pressures.

## Background & Description

- **Study focus:** Serum esterase phenotypes in natural populations of subgenera Pantosteus and Catostomus, with emphasis on evolutionary aspects of genetic systems in fish populations, which earlier descriptive serum-protein work had not addressed.
- **Prior context:** Earlier work on fish serum proteins (reviewed by [[Booke]] 1964) and on cod serum transferrins (Møller 1966) was cited as background. [[Augustinsson]] (1959, 1961) found that the predominant serum esterase in cod, eel, pike, shark and hagfish is propionylesterase.
- **Methods:** Fish were collected in 1964–1966 by nets, seines and electrofishing, and blood was analysed by horizontal [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] in tris borate buffer, with modified voltage (13.0 V/cm) and starch concentration (18%). Enzyme classification used substrates (naphthyl esters) and inhibitors ([[Diisopropylfluorophosphate]], eserine, hydroxymercuribenzoate, EDTA).
- **Nomenclature:** Gene symbol Es-I was assigned following [[Popp and Popp]] (1962) and Petras (1963) for the house mouse. Phenotypes were designated Es-I-a, Es-I-ab and Es-I-b, with Es-I^a the faster-migrating allele and Es-I^b the slower.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Geographic setting:** Populations are in connected and disconnected tributaries of the lower Colorado River. Several drainages (White River, Meadow Valley Wash, Gila River system and, in arid periods, the Virgin River) were isolated by aridity, desiccation or past sea transgression.
- **Isolation and gene flow:** Because the isolated populations cannot depend on recent migration, the authors argue that selection at the Es-I locus maintains allelic equilibrium.
- **Relict population:** The [[Preston Big Springs]] population (White River headwaters, Nevada), about 100 miles from the Colorado River and isolated since at least the Wisconsin glacial stage (~10,000 years), probably has fewer than 50 adults.
- **Desiccation of Lake Bonneville:** Following desiccation of the [[Bonneville Basin]], [[Catostomus platyrhynchus]] became isolated in disjunct remnant drainages.
- **Hybridization event:** [[Shoal Creek]] (Utah), a former tributary of Pleistocene Lake Bonneville, was colonised by C. clarki after 1938, probably via bait introduction, creating a hybrid population with the native C. platyrhynchus.
- **Mobility:** The authors note the sedentary behaviour ascribed to catostomid populations (Gerking 1959), which limits gene flow.

## Key Findings

- **Polymorphism:** Three Es-I phenotypes (Es-I-a, Es-I-ab, Es-I-b) were observed in C. clarki, consistent with a one-locus, two-allele system.
- **Sample size:** Twenty population samples comprising 717 individual fish were studied.
- **Hardy-Weinberg fit:** Phenotypic distributions did not deviate significantly from panmictic expectations in all samples except Tularosa Creek, New Mexico (locality 17; χ² = 18.34, P < 0.01).
- **Allele frequency range:** The frequency of Es-I^a (p) ranged from 0.18 in the White River headwaters (Nevada, loc. 11) to 1.00 in the San Pedro River headwaters (Arizona, loc. 15).
- **Latitudinal cline:** The Es-I^a frequency was consistently higher at more southern latitudes. Regression of frequency on latitude rejected the null hypothesis of no correlation (F = 72.3, versus F(1, 12.05) = 4.8).
- **Elevation effect:** Across two samples at similar latitude differing by about 5000 ft, the allele frequency decreased at 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ per foot, which the authors judged negligible.
- **Virgin River homogeneity:** Eight samples from the Virgin River drainage did not differ significantly (χ² = 10.44, d.f. = 7, 0.20 > P > 0.10).
- **Selection inference:** Maintenance of the polymorphism in isolated, possibly very small populations suggests selection strong enough to outweigh random drift.
- **Hybrid population:** In the Shoal Creek drainage, observed Es-I^a frequencies in Park Creek samples were 0.35, 0.37 and 0.34, close to the predicted 0.37 from the latitudinal regression. The authors took this as further evidence for selective maintenance.
- **Monomorphic species:** C. (P.) platyrhynchus is homozygous for the Es-I-b phenotype (n = 181). C. (P.) santaanae is homozygous for Es-I-a, possibly because its southern latitude favours fixation of that allele.
- **Subgenus Catostomus:** Over a hundred representatives of species including C. insignis, C. bernardini, C. latipinnis, C. tahoensis, C. macrocheilus and C. ardens showed a double-banded phenotype identical to the Es-I-ab heterozygote, which the authors describe as a "fixed heterozygote" phenotype.
- **Evolutionary models:** The authors propose that the single-locus system in Pantosteus arose through mutation and selection at one locus, whereas Catostomus probably duplicated an ancestral gene and later fixed two divergent loci. Both routes were argued to be consistent with protein divergence theory.
- **Enzyme class:** Es-I-a and Es-I-b are aliesterases (totally inhibited by DFP and resistant to EDTA and eserine), with a selective affinity for alpha-isomers of propionyl esters. The authors suggest they may be classed as propionylaliesterases.
