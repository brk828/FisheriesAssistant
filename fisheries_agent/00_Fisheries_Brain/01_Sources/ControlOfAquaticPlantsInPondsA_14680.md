# Control of Aquatic Plants in Ponds and Lakes

#aquatic-botany #freshwater-ecology #fish-culture

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Fishery Leaflet (No. 344, July 1949), by Fishery Research Biologist Eugene W. Surber, gives practical guidance for controlling nuisance aquatic plants in farm ponds, hatchery ponds and small lakes. Its central argument is that excessive submersed or emergent vegetation causes overpopulation and stunting of fish, lowers total fish yield, and disrupts the predator–forage fish balance. The leaflet covers pond mapping, mechanical removal, [[Fertilization]] as a biological control, and chemical control with [[Copper Sulfate]], [[Sodium Arsenite]] and [[2,4-D]]. It also gives application rates, mixing tables and equipment-cleaning procedures, and includes appendices listing target plant species and commercial suppliers.

## Background & Description

The leaflet is aimed at fish culturists, pond owners and operators who face weed-control problems. It separates two plant groups:

- **Submersed plants**, rooted in the pond bottom and growing to the surface, such as [[Anacharis]] (American waterweed), [[Coontail]] and many pondweeds (*Potamogeton*).
- **Emergent plants**, which start at the pond margin and build marshes, such as [[Cattails]], softstem bulrush, burreed, willows and spikerushes (*Eleocharis*).

The authors stress that weed control is essential for a proper predator–forage fish balance, for hatchery production and for recreational value. Coarse weed beds over most of the bottom reduce total fish weight and make the remaining fish smaller, so the operator's goal is to raise relatively few fish to edible size as quickly as possible.

**Area and volume determination.** Accurate surface area and average depth are needed before any chemical treatment. Irregular ponds can be mapped with a plane table or transit, with angles and distances read against a leveling rod. Data are plotted on graph paper (1/64 inch = 1 foot; each 0.5-inch square = 0.0235 acre), and volume is area multiplied by average depth. The leaflet refers readers to Welch (1948) for further mapping methods (see [[Pond Mapping]]).

**Plant biology relevant to control.** Submersed plants are hard to control because they reproduce in several ways:

- Flowers and seeds are dispersed by water, wind, animals and birds.
- Brittle-stemmed plants (Anacharis, Coontail) fragment, and each fragment can regrow.
- Underground rhizomes with nodes produce new shoots. Draining a pond or mowing gives only temporary results, because rhizomes survive in damp hatchery bottoms long after the soil surface dries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Shading by plankton.** The leaflet describes "shading out" as nature's elimination method. Fertilizers encourage microscopic "waterbloom" algae, which are cosmopolitan, spread by wind- or water-borne spores, and multiply rapidly by cell division. These algae block light from reaching the coarse plants on the bottom.

**Water chemistry.** Water hardness strongly affects results:

- In **soft water**, fertilization works well because little bicarbonate is present to buffer the system.
- In **hard water** (limestone regions), bicarbonates are broken down by plant activity, forming precipitates that carry suspended material to the bottom. These ponds tend to stay clear or clear faster, and fertilization is less effective. At Leetown, West Virginia, even 300 pounds of fertilizer per acre per application failed to produce water blooms once other plants were established.

**Oxygen depletion.** Decomposing dead vegetation consumes dissolved oxygen. Herbicide-killed plants that sink and decay rapidly are the main risk. Fish congregating at the shoreline or surface indicate oxygen depletion. The leaflet recommends fresh-water inflow, treating only half of a pond at a time when there is no inflow, and a standby pump of 200 gallons per minute or more for emergency aeration.

**Non-target effects.** The leaflet warns that some chemicals harm fish and fish food, and that copper accumulates in bottom muds. Chemical choice therefore depends on water type, plant type and the presence of fish or livestock.

## Key Findings

**1. Mechanical removal gives only temporary results.** Underwater mowers, weed saws, wires and scythes are laborious and mostly temporary. Cutting perennial emergents once, just after peak flowering and before seed ripens, was effective. A second cutting of the sparse regrowth 4 to 6 weeks later often gave practically complete control. Raising the water level 2.0 to 3.0 feet was also mentioned as a control method.

**2. Fertilization is a productivity-enhancing alternative to poisons.** Unlike chemical herbicides, fertilization raises pond productivity. For new ponds, the protocol is:

- Add about 100 pounds per acre of inorganic fertilizer at weekly intervals after filling, until the water turns green or brown (typically two to four applications).
- Then apply at roughly monthly intervals, or when water transparency falls to 12 to 18 inches (a white dish or hand becomes visible below about 18 inches).
- Fertilize only during the warm months.

Four formulae (10-6-4, 8-8-4, 6-8-4, 5-10-5) are tabulated as mixes of ammonium sulfate, superphosphate, muriate of potash and filler, so operators can blend their own fertilizer. These mixes match the grades used on cotton, orchards and gardens.

**3. Winter fertilization reclaims old ponds in the South.** Smith and Swingle (1942) recommended beginning in December or January, applying fertilizer about monthly until warm weather. Filamentous algae such as *Spirogyra* develop and then decay when warm weather arrives, and the new growth of submersed plants is suppressed. Later, applications are increased to weekly until waterbloom develops.

**4. The Deer Lake trial succeeded in a northern recreational lake.** At Deer Lake near Boonton, New Jersey (a small recreational lake of about 4.4 acres, given as "44 acre" in the scan), fertilization began on March 16, one week after ice-out, and was successful despite relatively large inflows:

- Seven applications of 5-10-5 fertilizer were made: 50 lb/acre starting March 16, then April 13, May 11, June 8, June 15, June 21 and June 28. The June 8 application was 100 lb/acre once the weather warmed.
- A bloom appeared after the first application, and good blooms were established after June 8.
- The lake serves 400 families, and [[Coontail]] (which blocked sailboating) was eliminated first.
- Decomposing Anacharis, carried to the surface and ashore by wind, had to be removed manually from beaches.
- At nearby Shongum Lake and Dixon's Pond, uprooted Cabomba (fanwort) shoots up to 12 feet long drifted ashore and were removed with forks.

**5. Fertilization fails in hard water.** Trials in hard-water ponds at Leetown, West Virginia, and Blacksburg, Virginia, only increased weeds and pond scums. Hard-water control is therefore left to chemicals.

**6. Copper sulfate controls muskgrass and pond scum.** Copper sulfate (CuSO₄·5H₂O) is cheap and was widely used at the Leetown hatchery, where water is hard:

- At 1.0 ppm (8.3 pounds per million gallons), it controls [[Muskgrass]] (*Chara*) and floating pond scum algae. Waterbloom algae usually follow muskgrass control, after which fertilization maintains blooms more easily.
- Toxicity tests by Moore and Kellerman (1905) found that sunfish withstood up to 1.2 ppm and bass up to 2.1 ppm in soft water. Tolerance was apparently much higher in hard water.
- Treatment is most effective early, when scums first appear. Spot-treating with crystals (3 pounds per 1,000 square feet, as used by Dr. O. L. Meehean at Welaka, Florida) is practical for early patches.
- Long-term use in St. Paul, Minnesota water-supply lakes apparently had no harmful effect on fish production (Moyle, unpublished). However, copper content in some Wisconsin lake muds indicated cumulative effects, so the authors advise caution in soft water.

**7. Sodium arsenite is the cheapest, most effective submersed-plant killer.** Sodium arsenite is sold as a brown or greenish syrupy liquid or as a greyish powder:

- Dose: 4.0 ppm kills even dense submersed growths in warm summer months in both hard and soft water. A 2.65 ppm dose works if weeds are not too dense.
- Safety margin: the leaflet reports that about 11 to 12 ppm is needed to kill fish.
- Conversion: 1 gallon of liquid in 64,080 cubic feet of water gives 1 ppm. For powder (75 percent As₂O₃), 5.3 pounds per 64,080 cubic feet gives 1 ppm. About 21 pounds is needed for 4.0 ppm, and 13 pounds 4 ounces for 2.65 ppm.
- Application: the simplest method is a dipper from a tub in the front of a boat (suited to ponds up to about 1 acre). Spraying at 40 to 60 psi with a gun-type sprayer and 30 to 40 gallon pneumatic tank (as at Leetown) is more thorough and recommended for larger ponds.
- Precautions: wear rubber gloves, dissolve powder fully before spraying, and exclude cattle for at least one week after treatment.

**8. Chemicals to avoid or limit.** The leaflet reviews several other agents:

- Hydrocarbon solvents (orthodichlorobenzene, trichlorobenzene, dichlorobenzene, naphtha) are toxic to fish and food organisms, killing them at lower concentrations than plants.
- Nigrosine dye works but is too expensive for hatcheries, and its biological effects were unstudied.
- Phenoxyacetic compounds (2,4-D, 2,4,5-T) are too expensive for submersed plants. 2,4-D also gives fish flesh a disagreeable flavor, making it inedible.
- Ammonium sulfamate is too expensive for submersed plants.
- Sodium chlorate is explosive.

**9. Phenoxy herbicides are effective on emergent plants.** [[2,4-D]] (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid) and 2,4,5-T are highly effective and inexpensive above the water line:

- Rates of 1 to 3 pounds per acre kill succulent broadleaf plants (jewelweed, plantains, arrowheads).
- Waxy-coated plants (waterlilies, cattails, water-hyacinth) need 0.5 to 1.0 percent solutions with oil carriers or detergents. Eradication, rather than control, is the goal for these.
- Spray volume: 20 to 200 gallons per acre, with about 100 gallons usually giving good coverage.
- Timing: apply when plants are actively growing and before fruiting, in warm, clear weather.
- Acidification: the sodium salt is more effective at pH 2 to 3 with phosphoric acid (Hamner, Lucas and Sell, 1947). Excessively high concentrations can damage tissues and block translocation to roots.
- Dr. Gladys S. King's formula, containing phenylacetic acid, was commercially successful against water-hyacinth.
- Mixing tables give 1.0 percent and 0.5 percent solutions from commercial 2,4-D acid contents of 90, 85, 80, 70 and 60 percent, plus liquid formulations.
- A commercial formulation of 35.0 percent 2,4-D with 17.5 percent phenylacetic acid, applied at 7 pints per acre (2.6 pounds 2,4-D per acre), was effective on waterlilies and most common hatchery emergents.

**10. Appendices list target species and suppliers.** Appendix A tabulates the agent and rate for about 50 nuisance species: floating plants (for example, duckweed and water hyacinth), emergents (cattail, waterlilies, bur-reed, spikerush), submersed plants (Anacharis, Coontail, muskgrass, pondweeds and naiad, with sodium arsenite at roughly 2.0 to 4.0 ppm), and woody bank species (willows, sycamore, poison ivy and others, treated with 0.5 to 2.0 percent 2,4-D or 2,4,5-T). Appendix B lists commercial suppliers of chemicals and cutting equipment. The Service explicitly neither recommends nor endorses these sources.

**11. Equipment must be decontaminated.** Spray equipment should be rinsed with kerosene after oil sprays, or with a dilute washing soda solution after aqueous sprays, then with warm water and soap. Acetone, ammonia water and trisodium phosphate (0.5 ounce per gallon) are alternative cleaners.
