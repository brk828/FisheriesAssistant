# Growth of Wild and Hatchery Strains of Brook Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Edwin L Cooper]] (The Pennsylvania State University), published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (1961), compares the growth of three wild [[Brook Trout]] populations from northern Michigan streams with five domesticated hatchery groups maintained at the [[Benner Spring Fish Research Station]]. Hatchery fish grew far faster, attaining about 13 inches by 20 months, while wild fish reached about 6 inches in 2 years. The author attributes wild-population slowness mainly to unfavorable [[Water Temperature]] and food shortage, and shows that inbreeding reduces growth in hatchery stock. The paper also tests two growth models ([[Walford Growth Model]] and [[Parker and Larkin Growth Model]]) and proposes a modified [[Relative Condition Factor]] based on hatchery weight-length data.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), a salmonid.
- **Prior work:** An earlier paper by Cooper (1953) described seasonal growth of the same three wild populations. The present study extends this by adding hatchery comparisons.
- **Wild study sites:** Three streams with hard water and abundant ground-water seepage:
  - Hunt Creek: about 20 ft wide (smallest)
  - Pigeon River: about 40 ft wide
  - North Branch of the Au Sable River: about 200 ft wide (largest)
- **Hatchery groups:** Five groups from the [[Pennsylvania Fish Commission]], held in similar concrete ponds with routine care:
  - Randomly bred: Pond 90 (Berlin, New Hampshire stock), Pond 98 (New Jersey stock), Pond 94 (Pennsylvania females × New Jersey males)
  - Inbred: Pond 92 (one female mated with 15 of her yearling sons) and Pond 96 (two females mated with two of their brothers)
- **Sampling:** 92 fish per group at the start (April 30, 1957); at least 50 per group measured at each period. Lengths were recorded to 1 mm (small fish) or 0.1 inch; weights to 0.1 g (small fish) or 10 g (fish over 12 inches). Sex was not determined.
- **Mortality:** 10–20% over 16 months in most groups; one group lost about 80% in the 16th month, ending the experiment on August 29, 1958.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hatchery conditions:** Water temperature stayed within a few degrees of 50 °F throughout the 16-month period. Fish were fed several times daily when small and at least once daily when larger. Disease and nutritional deficiency were absent until shortly before the close.
- **Wild conditions:** Growth is seasonal. Early spring offers adequate food and favorable temperatures, late summer often brings food shortage, and winter temperature limits the efficient use of food. Wild fish lost over 30% of expected weight in winter, much of which was recovered quickly in spring.
- **Temperature optimum:** The author notes that the maximum growth temperature for this species approximates 55 °F, citing [[Baldwin]] (1957) and Haskell et al. (1956).
- **Genetic context:** Heterozygosity is linked to growth capacity. Random mating maintains heterozygosity in domesticated stock, while inbreeding reduces growth.

## Key Findings

1. **Hatchery vs. wild growth:** Wild populations reached about 6 inches (5.9, 6.4, and 7.9 inches in 26 months, by stream). Hatchery trout reached about 13 inches in 20 months. Weight differences were of the order of 10-fold. The five hatchery groups grew as much in 1 year as the wild fish did in 3 years.
2. **Inbreeding effect:** Randomly bred groups were similar in size up to 20 months. Inbred groups (ponds 92 and 96) grew significantly slower from the second month onward (Table 2), with mean z values of 1.215 versus 1.52 for randomly bred groups (t = 15, 4 df, P < 0.001).
3. **Declining specific growth rate:** Growth rates fell rapidly with increasing size. In pond 92, the specific rate fell from 0.82% per day (2.67–3.36 in) to 0.16% per day (11.74–12.42 in). The overall rate for the 482-day period was 0.32% per day. Growth in length was closer to linear than exponential over long periods.
4. **Growth models:**
   - The Walford transformation was judged unsuitable. Its asymptotic lengths (21.8 in and 37.7 in) were unrealistic; the upper value corresponds to about 29 lb, which the species has never approached.
   - The Parker and Larkin parabolic model described the curves adequately and detected genetic differences. Optimum z was about 1.52 for randomly bred groups and about 1.215 for inbred groups. Analysis of variance found no significant growth differences within groups sharing z values (F = 0.18, P > 0.25).
5. **Weight-length relationships:** Groups grown under similar conditions had similar weight-length curves, but inbred groups differed markedly. A large composite sample of 5,212 Michigan brook trout (2.3–12.7 in) gave log w = −0.74925 + 2.99 log l, supporting the historically accepted cubic relationship. The authors argue that variation in b reflects environmental history as well as genetics.
6. **Relative condition factor:** The author proposes a modified LeCren relative condition factor. The standard is the weight-length relationship of the three randomly bred hatchery groups (log w = −0.82609 + 3.154 log l), treated as the optimum physiological condition. Hatchery groups stayed within about 5% of expected weight. Wild groups seldom reached 90% of expected weight.
7. **Maturity effect:** A slight growth decline appeared in autumn, after many males matured in their first year. The difference was not statistically significant (P < 0.05 and P = 0.10).
8. **Standardization recommendation:** A common table of expected weights and length conversions (standard length to total length) would help biologists compare results across studies.
