# Trout Hepatoma Research Conference Papers (1965 Portland Conference, Research Report 70)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife Research Report (No. 70, published 1967) compiles papers from the Third National Trout Hepatoma Research Conference, held in Portland, Oregon, on March 18–19, 1965. The conference reviewed the status of research on liver tumors (hepatoma) in hatchery-reared [[Rainbow Trout]] and related salmonids, arranged discussions among active research groups, and set priorities for future work. The collected papers cover [[Dietary Carcinogenesis]] from cottonseed products, dry pellet formulations, [[Aflatoxin]] identification and toxicity, liver histology classification, and biochemical features of tumors. The central conclusion is that dietary factors in dry feeds, most strongly [[Cottonseed Meal]] and aflatoxin-contaminated ingredients, are the leading suspected cause of trout hepatoma, though the exact agent and its threshold remain under investigation.

## Background & Description

The report was issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[National Cancer Institute]] as part of the Research Report series that began in 1941. The conference was sponsored by the NCI Field Studies Branch and the Division of Fishery Research of the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife. Editors were John E. Halver and John A. Mitchell (OCR text gives "Ian A. Mitchell"; the foreword identifies him as Associate Scientific Director, Etiology, NCI). The volume contains a foreword, contents, four color plates of normal and hepatomatous livers, and about 20 papers with discussions. A closing synopsis was given by Paul Kotin, NCI Scientific Director for Etiology.

The disease was first recognized as a problem at [[Hagerman National Fish Hatchery]], Idaho, in June 1960 (five 4-year-old rainbow trout examined, three with multinodular livers). Earlier outbreaks were limited to a few locations. The paper by Dollar et al. describes a related condition, [[Liver Lipoid Degeneration]] (L.L.D.), recognized in Europe, often associated with ceroid pigment in the liver.

The main contributors and topics were:

- Dollar, Smuckler and Simon: etiology and epidemiology; survey of feeds in the US, Europe, Australia and Kenya.
- Simon, Dollar and Smuckler: descriptive classification of normal and altered liver histology (Classes I–V).
- Wolf and Jackson: cottonseed meal and flour in rainbow trout and other salmonids.
- Coates, Potts and Wilcke (Ralston Purina): feeding experiments with known compounds and cottonseed fractions.
- Yasutake and Rucker: nutritionally induced hepatomagenesis at Hagerman.
- Sinnhuber: aflatoxin in cottonseed meal and liver cancer.
- Wales, Scarpelli, Novelli, Halver, Ashley, Wogan, Newberne, Phelps, Goldblatt, Falk and Sims: degeneration, ultrastructure, amino acid incorporation, aflatoxin chemistry and assay, and potential carcinogens.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most hatchery trout in the study were reared on dry pellets, often combined with fresh or frozen liver, heart, fish or meat. The authors repeatedly noted that feed quality, storage and handling were major variables. Spoiled fresh foods, rancid or oxidized fats, and poor-quality fish meal were implicated in high disease and mortality rates in Europe.

A notable comparison was the Snob's Creek Hatchery in Australia, where rainbow trout had been isolated since the 1890s and fed almost entirely fresh or frozen meat. Examined fish up to about 10 years of age showed no liver tumors, suggesting that tumors, if they occur naturally, are very infrequent. Water at this hatchery was low in calcium (about 3 ppm or less), and winter zinc rose to about 0.3 ppm, which was toxic under calcium deficiency when prolonged.

Kenya's trout culture (based on [[Surrey Trout Farm]] stock) showed a similar pattern. Its 1962 locally milled pellet, stored more than 6 months, had a free fatty acid value of 31.4 percent, suggesting oxidation. Ingredients were produced regionally in Kenya, Uganda and Tanganyika.

Experimental hatchery water temperatures ranged from about 53°F to 60°F, depending on station. Wild comparison fish came from Cabin and Corral Creeks in the Challis National Forest, Idaho, where hatchery trout had not been released for 15 years.

## Key Findings

**1. Etiology and epidemiology (Dollar, Smuckler, Simon)**
- The 1960–61 outbreak was linked to mixed dry feeds (pellets or dry feeds admixed with wet feeds). Exact diet composition was often unknown.
- In Kenya, the revised feed formula (after cottonseed meal was considered a suspect) reduced brood-stock mortality. In 1962 spawners, 713 of 1,127 died (63 percent). In 1963 spawners, 111 of 1,754 (7 percent) died during the year.
- Diets with about 38 percent protein or less, one third of it animal-derived, appeared more prone to tumor induction.
- Field experiments identified cottonseed meal as the "most critical ingredient." Two fish meals and wheat middlings failed to evoke a response when cottonseed meal was absent.

**2. Liver histology classification (Simon, Dollar, Smuckler)**
- Study based on more than 1,200 hatchery fish sampled quarterly over 2 years, plus 150 wild rainbow trout from Idaho.
- Gross liver categories A–E were defined by nodule size (none; nodules under 3 mm; 3–10 mm; over 10 mm).
- Normal liver averaged 1.4 percent of body weight (sample means 1.1–1.7 percent). Cell plates are typically two cells thick.
- Nuclear diameter increased with body weight (slope 0.00065 µm per gram; 0.65 µm per kg). Males had consistently larger nuclei than females at the 95 percent significance level (F = 5.18, 1,30 d.f.).
- Five classes were proposed: Class I (normal liver), Class II (preneoplastic dysplasia with regeneration), Class IIIa and IIIb (basophilic nodules, with or without cholangiomatous elements), Class IV (proliferating ductules with advanced tumors), and Class V (hepatocellular carcinoma).
- The authors state that cell morphology alone cannot reliably establish malignancy in the absence of metastases.

**3. Cottonseed products and other salmonids (Wolf and Jackson)**
- Diet DA (Santa Monica ration, 20 percent cottonseed meal) produced hepatomas by 5 months. By month 9, 88 percent prevalence was recorded.
- Cottonseed flour (DC) produced no hepatomas until month 9, when 5 of 25 were positive. In contrast, 22 of 25 fish on DA had hepatomas at month 9.
- Aflatoxin analysis detects levels as low as 7 ppb. Cottonseed meal contained aflatoxin at 0.71–0.99 ppm (per Table 25), while flour was negative.
- Among other salmonids, brook trout developed gross hepatomas on the DA diet. Brown trout, king salmon and silver salmon did not show confirmed hepatomas at 10 months.

**4. Nutritionally induced hepatomagenesis (Yasutake and Rucker)**
- At Hagerman, four commercial pellets (A–D) were tested. Diet A produced bizarre, eosinophilic, basophilic and anaplastic nodules. Diets B and D showed no significant pathology.
- Approximately 1,200 livers were examined over 18 months.
- Metastases to spleen and gill were found in group A fish at 18–20 months.

**5. Cottonseed meal experiments (Coates, Potts and Wilcke)**
- Experiment 1 (about 100,000 fish; Nov 1960–Oct 1961) found hepatoma at 61.11 percent with 39.65 percent cottonseed meal. Diethylstilbestrol, dienestrol diacetate and other additives were negative.
- Experiment 2 (180,000 fish) tested cottonseed fractions (meats, hulls, expeller and solvent meals, crude and refined oil, soapstock, acidulated soapstock). All were negative over about 450 days.
- Experiment 3 surveyed 34,000 fish across 17 ponds with many cottonseed sources. Several meals, including meals C, F, G and P, produced positive results with aflatoxin B1 levels reported in ppb.

**6. Aflatoxin in cottonseed meal (Sinnhuber)**
- The Santa Monica (SM) ration produced hepatoma in 20 of 20 fish at 6 months in some groups and 24 of 24 at 9 months, with aflatoxin about 37–43 ppb.
- Removing fish meal left a high incidence at 12 months.
- Diet C-3 (no cottonseed meal) still produced over 50 percent incidence. Wheat middlings were later found to contain aflatoxin (about 6 ppb contribution, estimated 24 ppb in the middlings).
- In a purified Oregon Test Diet, J and N cottonseed meal produced about 50 percent gross hepatoma at 12 months. Aflatoxin at 3.7 ppb produced a 38 percent incidence.
- Flour, glandless cottonseed meal, gossypol at 1,000 ppm and sterculic acid did not produce hepatoma. Approximately 1 ppb aflatoxin appeared near the no-response level.

**7. Degeneration and regeneration (Wales)**
- Fish on the SM ration showed degenerative changes by 84 days, 94 days before the first hepatoma node was found. Regenerating basophilic islets formed among enlarged eosinophilic cells.
- Nuclear aberrations were scored on a three-level index (No. 10 minimal; No. 12 most extensive).

**8. Ultrastructure and biochemistry (Scarpelli)**
- Tumor-bearing trout had serum protein elevations up to threefold over normal values.
- Microsomes from hepatoma incorporated more C14-lysine than non-neoplastic liver (values listed in Table 39-B, including 138, 392 and 113 CPM/cm²).
- No biochemical differences were found between adenomas and carcinomas for the enzymes tested.

**9. Amino acid incorporation (Novelli)**
- Rat liver regeneration showed a 25 percent increase in polysome area, matching a 25 percent increase in protein synthesis.
- Optimal in vitro incorporation temperature was about 23°C for trout liver preparations, compared with 37°C for rat.

**10. Crystalline aflatoxin and induction (Halver)**
- Thirteen chemical carcinogens were fed at five levels. Eleven produced hepatoma at intermediate or low doses. Tannic acid, thioacetamide and aminotriazole were included in the list tested.
- Standard test diet with no added carcinogen produced only one incipient and one small hepatoma in over 300 livers over 1 year.
- Crystalline aflatoxin B1 at 0.5 ppb produced a real tumor response in about one third of fish after 1 year. Fish weighed about 150 g, with an estimated intake near 0.2 µg per fish.
- Trout had an approximate LD50 between 1 and 3 mg/kg crude aflatoxin (50 g fish).
- The "hot" commercial diet was later assayed at 200 ppb aflatoxin B1.

**11. Histopathology of aflatoxicosis (Ashley)**
- Trout and 50 g Pekin ducklings were similarly sensitive to aflatoxin. Trout developed more massive necrosis and hemorrhage at higher doses, while ducklings showed more bile duct hyperplasia.
- Lesions were also found in skin, gills, spleen, kidney, pancreas and gastrointestinal tract.

**12. Conference summary**
- Hepatoma in trout appears to be diet-induced and is strongly associated with aflatoxin-contaminated ingredients, especially cottonseed meal. Other possible factors include oxidized lipids, ceroid, and unidentified carcinogens.
- Authors noted that the conference's stated purpose was to review status, arrange discussions, and project future research.
