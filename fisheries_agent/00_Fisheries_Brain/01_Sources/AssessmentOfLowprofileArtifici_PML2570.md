# Assessment of Low-Profile Artificial Cover for Minimizing Fisheries Impacts from In-Place Concrete Lining of Water Canal Systems (1990 Annual Progress Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 progress report, prepared by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Gordon Mueller, Environmental Division; Dr. Charles R. Liston, Environmental Sciences Section), presents 18 of 24 months of a study testing whether low-profile artificial cover in lined water conveyance canals can offset fishery losses caused by in-place concrete lining. The study uses scrap-tire reefs of two designs ([[Flat Reefs]] and [[Cellular Reefs]]) placed in two operating canals: the [[Coachella Canal]] and the [[Hayden-Rhodes Aqueduct]]. Reef-associated sections supported roughly 20 times more fish and 150 times more fish biomass than control stations, and invertebrate densities on tires were about 3.4 to 3.5 times those on canal sides in the Coachella Canal. Reefs also supported catfish spawning. No significant changes in sediment or aquatic plants and no conveyance problems were reported. The report recommends refocusing future work on longevity, spawning habitat and silt reduction rather than replicating reefs in the in-place lining prototype.

## Background & Description

The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] is working with the Coachella Valley Water District, Imperial Irrigation District and Metropolitan Water District of California to evaluate lining earthen canals without interrupting water conveyance. The in-place lining technology, which lays concrete and an impermeable membrane in the wet, was being field-tested in an 11-mile earthen section of the Coachella Canal. The [[Low-Profile Artificial Cover Study]] began on October 1, 1988, to determine whether artificial cover could offset residual fishery impacts and possibly enhance lined canals.

Study sites were selected for straight reaches free of control structures, good road access, minimal public use, and a range of operational velocities:
- **Hayden-Rhodes Aqueduct (HRA)**, formerly Granite Reef Aqueduct, part of the [[Central Arizona Project]]: maximum depth 22 ft, 80-ft flat bottom, maximum velocities about 1.2 ft/sec at full canal conditions; frequently little or no flow.
- **Coachella Canal**: maximum velocities can exceed 3.5 ft/sec; the study section lies about 5 miles downstream of a Highway 78 crossing.
- **In-place prototype section**: approximately 15 miles northwest of Niland, California; planned 48-ft flat bottom and about 9 ft depth.

**Reef construction:** Roughly 4,150 scrap tires (supplier in Las Vegas, Nevada) were inspected for size and oil contamination, bound with 2 to 4 straps of 300-pound tensile strength non-corrosive binding material, and fastened with nylon buckles. Reefs were built in Boulder City, Nevada, and trucked to the sites.
- Flat reefs: 45 tires laid flat in six adjoining rows, covering about 15.3 m².
- Cellular reefs: tires bound vertically, covering 9.0 m² with about four times the depth of flat reefs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design:** 48 sampling stations spaced 50 m apart along each 11-mile section were grouped into 16 subsections of three stations each. Each subsection contained one control, one cellular reef and one flat reef, giving 32 reefs and 16 controls per section. Reefs were placed on the canal side slope, weighted with concrete, and secured to fence posts in the levee. A test of bottom placement showed reefs could be displaced (one cellular reef moved 2.6 miles after refilling).

**Water quality (May 1989 to August 1990)** was within earlier recorded ranges:

| Parameter | Coachella Canal (range) | HRA (range) |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | 12.7 to 30.6 | 12.19 to 27.3 |
| pH | 8.1 to 8.3 | 7.4 to 10.7 |
| Dissolved oxygen (mg/L) | 7.5 to 11.1 | 6.5 to 10.1 |
| Conductivity (µmhos/cm) | 995 to 1,219 | 911 to 1,219 |
| Turbidity (NTU) | 1.4 to 4.5 | 1.3 to 1.5 |

**Hydrology:** Coachella Canal diversions reached monthly averages near 1,000 cfs, with velocities of 1.0 to 3.6 ft/sec. The [[Central Arizona Project]] reached system capacity (3,000 cfs) for the first time in June 1990. HRA was dewatered in December 1989 for an unscheduled inspection, exposing reefs for about one month.

**Sampling methods:**
- *Seining:* a 100 ft by 16 ft, ⅛-inch net with a chained lead line (160-lb weight) was positioned around each reef, which was then lifted and removed. Fish were identified, measured and weighed; large fish (>5 in) were tagged with Floy tags and released, and unmarked fish were released outside the study area.
- *Scuba surveys:* conducted on reefs and controls scheduled for seining the next quarter, without disturbing the structures.
- *Invertebrates:* 105-mm diameter disks cut from tire treads (86.6 cm²) were attached to tires, and a diver-operated modified Hess sampler (314.2 cm²) sampled control side slopes. Results are expressed per 100 cm².
- *Sediment and velocity:* measured at seven permanent transects in subsection 6, the long-term monitoring site.

**Sampling effort:** Eight quarterly trips (May, August, November and February) were planned for 1989 and 1990, with the final field effort delayed by construction.

**Physical condition and vandalism:** Reefs maintained structural integrity. Problems were limited to a few cases of strap failure, attributed to solar degradation of exposed nylon buckles and polyethylene rope, and one vandalism event. Fishing equipment and litter suggested anglers were using the sites, which the authors felt offered some security.

**Sediment accumulation:** Sediment depths were about 1 inch deeper downstream of reefs than at controls, with no significant deposition or scour. Sediment and [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*) accumulate inside the tires, which are now about half full at the bottom, complicating reef removal. A portable pump is used to flush material and small fish during retrieval.

## Key Findings

**Fish communities**
- Eleven fish species were collected in the Coachella Canal and six in the HRA. Largemouth Bass, Bluegill and Flathead Catfish were the cover-oriented species using the structures.
- Mean fish per seine haul in the Coachella Canal: 7.8 for flat reefs, a slightly higher figure for cellular reefs, and 0.2 for controls.
- HRA densities: 9.2 fish per haul on cellular reefs and 16.3 on flat reefs. No fish were captured in any of 12 control efforts.
- In the HRA, small Bluegill, Green Sunfish and Channel Catfish were about twice as abundant on flat reefs as on cellular reefs, suggesting overhead cover may be more critical for these populations.
- Coachella Canal fish biomass was 4 times that in the HRA and 150 times that at control stations, largely due to large [[Channel Catfish]].

**Scuba observations**
- Far fewer fish were seen by divers than collected by seining, largely because most fish were small and concealed in tires. Large catfish were commonly seen sheltering near the concrete anchors.

**Spawning**
- Largemouth Bass were observed on 40 percent of all reefs in the HRA.
- Catfish spawning was documented: a pair of adult Channel Catfish (female heavy with eggs) was taken from a tire cavity in May 1990, and a male was found guarding fertilized eggs in August 1990.

**Tagging**
- 33 fish were tagged in the Coachella Canal (24 Channel Catfish, 7 Flathead Catfish, one Carp, one Bluegill). One tagged Channel Catfish was recaptured twice; one was reported by an angler about 6 miles downstream.
- In the HRA, 11 fish were tagged (4 Largemouth Bass, 4 Bluegill, 2 Green Sunfish, 1 Channel Catfish); one Bluegill was recaptured.

**Invertebrates**
- A total of 220 invertebrate samples were analyzed through August 1990 (96 flat-reef disks, 96 cellular-reef disks, 48 control side-wall samples).
- Coachella Canal: reef structures supported about 3.4 to 3.5 times the total invertebrate density of concrete sides. Caddisflies (Trichoptera) were about 9 times denser on tires than on controls, and mayflies (Ephemeroptera) 3.3 to 4.0 times denser. Both are important fish food.
- Caddisflies and mayflies made up about 80 percent of invertebrates on reef disks in the Coachella Canal.
- HRA: dewatering complicated interpretation. Midges, clams and snails dominated, and flat reefs supported more invertebrates than the concrete slopes, while cellular reefs supported fewer.

**Hydraulic and sediment effects**
- Cellular reefs raise the profile and alter water velocities. Floating debris collects on their upstream side, and flow changes are visible for about 50 ft downstream.
- No appreciable sediment deposition or scour and no conveyance interference were reported.

**Structural and operational issues**
- The weight of accumulated sediment (estimated 500 to 1,500 pounds) and clams made reef removal difficult.
- Lead-line rolling during retrieval was remedied by having a diver guide the net.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Reefs appear to increase fish and invertebrate numbers and diversity, and to provide a cost-effective mitigation/enhancement option that avoids added water demand.
- Because reef results are consistent across high and low velocity sites, the authors judged that replicating reefs in the in-place lining prototype may be unnecessary. They recommended refocusing research on:
  - *Spawning features:* catfish spawning tubes, gravel beds and improved tire cavities.
  - *Overhead cover:* streamers or simulated plant stands.
  - *Small fish shelters:* wired cylinders to protect juveniles from predation by large catfish.
  - *Structural longevity:* stainless steel strapping, bolts, chain or solar-resistant synthetics, aiming for 25-year integrity.
  - *Anti-silting design:* roughly 30 percent of cavities were lost to silt buildup.
  - *Micro-wetlands:* small terrestrial stands (<1 yd²) on cellular reefs may shelter small fish and invertebrates.
- The proposed follow-up would test four reef designs with 16 reefs, sample semi- to annually, and run for a minimum of 10 years.
- Final data analysis and a draft report were expected by June 1991. The last field sampling was scheduled for January 28 to February 8, 1991.
