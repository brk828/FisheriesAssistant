# Roundtail Chub (*Gila robusta*) Stewardship Abstract

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Element Stewardship Abstract, issued by [[The Nature Conservancy]] Arizona Field Office and prepared by Stephen P. Vives (Georgia Southern University), compiles management-relevant information on the [[Roundtail Chub]], a medium-to-large minnow endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species has declined across much of its range due to [[Habitat Modification]], impoundment, water diversion, and predation or competition from [[Non-Native Fishes]]. It is federally listed as endangered only for the Virgin River subspecies (*G. r. seminuda*), with state-level protection in Arizona and New Mexico. Recovery potential is rated good only where suitable habitat is firmly protected. The abstract provides monitoring, research, and management recommendations.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The genus *Gila* in the Colorado basin grades from the streamlined *G. elegans* of large rivers, through *G. robusta* of intermediate rivers, to the thick-bodied *G. intermedia* of creeks and marshes. Several intermediate populations (*G. r. seminuda*, *G. r. jordani*, *G. r. grahami*) create taxonomic uncertainty. *G. r. grahami* is no longer considered valid, but the name is retained for problematic populations until mitochondrial DNA and protein electrophoresis studies are complete.
- **Morphology:** A relatively large minnow, 250–350 mm standard length. Fin rays usually nine (8–10 in dorsal, anal, and pelvic fins). Fully scaled (except *G. r. seminuda*, which may be partly naked). Usually 81 or more lateral-line scales (range 75–99) and 43–49 total vertebrae. Barbels absent; pharyngeal teeth in two rows (2,4–5,2). Darkened dorsally, silvery white ventrally. Orange-red coloration on ventrolateral surfaces and fins (except dorsal fin), intensifying in breeding males. Both sexes develop breeding tubercles, less extensive in females.
- **Diagnostic separation:** [[Gila Intermedia]] is more robust and darker, with eight dorsal and anal rays and larger scales. [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]] are distinguished as adults by prominent predorsal humps.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Adults occupy the largest, most permanent water in streams, favoring pools and eddies below or beside rapids and boulders in cool to warm, mid-elevation waters. They are usually found near cover. Large populations often occur in pools behind irrigation diversions. Juveniles prefer flowing-water margins and backwaters.
- **Instream flow:** In Burro Creek, Arizona, fish 76–178 mm TL preferred depths greater than 9 cm and velocities below 21 cm/s. Usable habitat in Aravaipa Creek was reported to increase with discharge.
- **Thermal tolerance:** Critical thermal maxima ranged from 30.5 to 39.5 °C and critical thermal minima from 7.7 to 1.0 °C, depending on acclimation temperature (Schumann 1977). Siebert (1980) proposed seasonal migration in Aravaipa Creek to avoid temperature extremes.
- **Abundance:** In relatively undisturbed Aravaipa Creek, roundtail chub made up 0 to about 11.7% of fish collected across habitats, with frequency of occurrence of 20% (above canyon), 68.8% (within canyon), and 50% (below canyon).
- **Associated fauna:** Typically co-occurs with speckled dace, *Catostomus* species, and *Pantosteus* species. Historically associated with woundfin, bonytail, humpback chub, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, and Gila topminnow, many now extirpated over most of their former range.
- **Riparian linkage:** Riparian vegetation influences leaf fall, energy flow, cover, temperature, and sediment deposition. Only Aravaipa Creek, where cattle were excluded from 1973, showed successful reproduction and a broadleaf riparian community.

## Key Findings

### Life history
- Matures in its second or third year. Eggs are scattered over clean gravel in runs and pools at least 25 cm deep. Males are not territorial, and females likely choose spawning sites.
- Spawning in the lower Colorado basin runs from March to May (primarily April to May). In the upper basin, *Gila* spawning occurs from mid- to late June to early July at about 18 °C.
- Fecundity in Fossil Creek (*G. r. grahami*, 100–260 mm TL) ranged from 1,000 to 4,300 ova, with a 321 g female carrying 36,959 mature ova. Bestgen (1985) counted 600 to 45,125 ova per female in the Gila basin.
- Eggs hatch in four to seven days at 19 °C.
- Age and growth: Young-of-year reached 12–28 mm SL by June. Mean TL by year class was 68, 127, 174, 217, and 321 mm for ages 0, 1, 2, 3, and 5. The largest 7-year-old female was 366 mm TL and 393 g. Maximum size exceeds 400 mm TL.

### Diet
- Likely a carnivore in morphology but often feeds heavily on algae and detritus.
- Diet varies by size and season. Juveniles under 50 mm eat diatoms and filamentous algae. Larger fish eat larval insects, ostracods, and plant material. Diet is most diverse in spring and least in winter.
- Virgin River fish were up to 90% algae in diet. Adults over 110 mm ate only algae and debris.

### Parasites
- Recorded parasites include *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* (protozoan), several trematodes, one cestode, and nematodes.

### Threats
- Historic decline documented by Minckley (1973) through comparison with 1920s–1950s field notes.
- Main threats: impoundment, channel downcutting, sedimentation, water diversion, groundwater pumping, and non-native predators and competitors such as channel catfish, flathead catfish, smallmouth bass, and red shiner.
- Flaming Gorge Dam temperature reductions reportedly impaired spawning success. Heat deaths occurred at 30.5 °C in Salt River pools.
- Roundtail chub are extirpated from the Zuni drainage and no longer taken from the Little Colorado River since 1965.

### Conservation status
- *G. r. seminuda*: federally endangered (USFWS 1989); Arizona endangered.
- *G. r. grahami*: federal "notice of review" subspecies, with further action pending taxonomic resolution.
- *G. r. robusta*: Arizona threatened; New Mexico Group 2 endangered.
- *G. r. jordani* (White River, Nevada) and an undescribed Moapa River subspecies: low populations, not federally protected.
- Overall land protection is minimal outside conservation-organization holdings.

### Recovery and monitoring
- Recovery potential is **good** only if adequate habitat is vigorously protected. Reintroduction is problematic until causes of decline are addressed.
- Priority monitoring gaps: upper Colorado basin, Sonoran Gila drainage, and tribal lands of the San Carlos and White Mountain Apache.
- Recommended: biannual monitoring in spring (breeding) and late autumn (recruitment), with standardized methods. Electrofishing with 1–2 passes in netted areas captured 99% of fish in one study (Minckley 1981). Sampling entire streams every two to three years is preferred over repeated small-area sampling.

### Research and management priorities
- Research needs include habitat preferences of all life stages, effects of non-native fishes, movement patterns, and taxonomic clarification before any hatchery or reintroduction work.
- Management priorities include protecting existing populations, maintaining perennial natural hydrographs, curtailing non-native fish introductions, building barriers, and reintroducing fish to historic streams using local stock.
- Riparian management should exclude cattle, and any controlled burns should occur during winter non-spawning periods.
- Rapidly declining populations should be secured in a hatchery such as Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico.
- Current management programs are limited. The Bureau of Land Management has prioritized riparian habitat and planned a fisheries biologist position in Safford, Arizona, in 1990.
