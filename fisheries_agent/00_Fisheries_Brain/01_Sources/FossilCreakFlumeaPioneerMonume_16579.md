# Fossil Creek Flume and the Irving and Childs Hydroelectric Plants

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-hydrology

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1966 magazine feature from the [[Arizona Engineer And Scientist]] describing the [[Fossil Creek Flume]], a historic water conduit that diverts spring-fed flow from [[Fossil Creek]] to power two small hydroelectric plants, [[Irving Plant]] and [[Childs Plant]], operated by [[Arizona Public Service]]. The article covers the project's construction history (begun 1908), its engineering details, its costs and labor, the plants' continued operation after almost sixty years, and their economic role. A second, unrelated article describes the magazine's computer-based mailing label program. The document contains no fish, invertebrate, or aquatic ecological survey data; its only biological content concerns algal growth and mineral deposition in the flume and a planned trout lake at [[Stehr Lake]].

## Background & Description

**The Fossil Creek Flume (historical engineering project)**
- The creek takes its name from the high mineral content of its water, which coats materials with [[Calcium Carbonate]] and [[Magnesium Carbonate]] so they appear "fossilized."
- Natural springs supplied 48 cfs, described as a dependable flow year after year.
- No site along the creek was suitable for a conventional dam, so the water was conveyed by a flume to more convenient locations.
- The article's author is [[Hal R. Moore]]. Supervisor [[Emmett Postle]] is pictured with the original redwood flume.

**Planning and markets**
- Growing communities of Cottonwood, Jerome, Clarkdale, and Prescott were identified as ready markets.
- [[Senator W. A. Clark]], of Montana, contracted for one-third of the entire power output of the Childs plant for the [[United Verde Mine And Smelter]] at Jerome and Clarkdale.

**Construction timeline**
- First work began in April 1908, on the [[Verde Rim Road]], which was completed to the river on July 1, 1908.
- The Childs plant and flume were built in one year by roughly 600 men.
- The Irving plant was constructed in 1914.

**Transport and logistics**
- Reports state that some 400 mules pulling 150 wagons hauled supplies and equipment.
- The Verde Rim Road drops 3,300 feet to the canyon floor and covers about eleven miles to the flume intake on Fossil Creek.

**Water conduit components (as reported in text)**
- 10,000 feet of tunnels
- 12,000 feet of reinforced concrete flume
- 1,400 feet of reinforced concrete pipe
- 4,800 feet of steel pressure pipe
- 7,500 feet of steel gravity siphon
- 2,200 feet of wooden flume
- The text totals 38,000 feet and notes a 100-foot discrepancy. The embedded table lists a total conduit length of 54,905.4 ft, which conflicts with the body text and is not reconciled in the source.
- The article also mentions an inverted siphon of more than seven miles before water reaches Childs on the Verde River.

**Transmission**
- Seventy-five miles of steel tower transmission lines with double circuits carried 45,000 volts to population centers.

**Stehr Lake storage**
- Stehr Lake covers about 28 acres and functions as a storage reservoir.
- It was a project of the [[Arizona Game And Fish Commission]] to create a trout lake.
- A tunnel three and one-half feet wide, six feet high, and 5,000 feet long conveys water from Stehr Lake to the Childs plant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish and aquatic fauna:** No fish species, populations, or aquatic fauna surveys are reported. The only fishery reference is a plan by the Arizona Game and Fish Commission to make Stehr Lake a trout lake, and general fishing at the Verde River, described as "interesting if not spectacular."
- **Algae:** Growth of algae, called "moss" by [[R. S. Masson]], was a maintenance problem in the flume. Covering open portions of the flume reduced algal growth to a minimum.
- **Mineral deposits:** Mineral deposition from the spring water is harder to control. Crews periodically chip deposits from the lengthy tunnels.
- **Vegetation:** The author notes flowers growing from fossil-like growths on the sides of the old flume.
- **Water return:** Water from the Childs plant is emptied into the Verde River, adding volume to that stream.
- **Access restrictions:** The road from the Irving plant to the flume intake is closed to the public for safety reasons. Access to the flume requires permission from Public Service.
- **Structural monitoring:** A gate tender walks about half of the Irving flume daily, watching for rocks that might roll down hillsides and for leaks that could undermine trestles. The flume descends only one foot per thousand feet.

## Key Findings

- **Plant output and economics:** [[Jack Swift]], Publicity Manager for Public Service, stated that "quantity-wise" the two hydro plants are not important sources of power. However, from a production cost standpoint they are competitive with newer gas-fueled plants, so they will be kept on the line.
- **Service area:** Power is assumed (not definitively stated) to be used in nearby communities such as Payson, Pine, Camp Verde, Cottonwood, and Prescott.
- **Staffing:** Four employees live at the Childs plant, eight at the Irving plant, and a gate tender and his wife live near the top of the flume.
- **Construction costs and wages:** Construction, including labor and materials hauled to the site, cost $1 per foot. Apache Indian laborers were paid $2 per day, and foremen $3 per day.
- **Construction pace:** Average rate was 100 feet per day, with as many as 150 feet completed in a single day when no unforeseen interruptions occurred.
- **Engineering prediction:** R. S. Masson, Chief Engineer for the [[Arizona Power Company]], predicted that reinforced concrete on rock bench "will hereafter, without the least doubt, replace all other forms of permanent gravity conduits." The article states the construction proved an unqualified success.
- **Later modifications:** Wooden portions have been replaced with steel, but much of the flume remains as originally built.
- **Technical documentation:** Masson's detailed account appeared in *Electrical World Magazine* (August 11 and 18, 1910), with reprints available from Arizona Public Service.
- **Visitor access:** Public Service maintains campgrounds at the Childs plant, reached via the Camp Verde–Strawberry road and the Verde Hot Springs turnoff.

**Unrelated article: Magazine Mailing Program (Gary E. Patrick, IEEE)**
- Author: [[Gary E. Patrick]].
- The magazine's mailing labels were printed by a [[GE-225 Computer]] at the [[Arizona State University]] Engineering Computer Center.
- Hardware: one card reader, one card punch, one typewriter, six magnetic tape units, and one high-speed printer.
- The program has eight possible phases. Records are punched onto data cards, and two master decks are maintained: an "Alpha-Deck" sorted by last name and a "Zip-Deck" sorted by ZIP code.
- Program options include printing mailing labels for specified groups, transferring ZIP-sorted data to magnetic tape, printing tape listings, printing audits of group classifications, counting people by geographic location, and printing labels on continuous envelopes for meeting notices.
- Each option is controlled by a switch. Switch number one controls mailing label printing.
- Some groups receive special issues, some rotate, and some receive every issue.
