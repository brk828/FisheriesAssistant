# Effects of Grass Carp on native fish populations in two Florida lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined how stocking [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), used for aquatic weed control, affected native fish populations in two Florida ponds between August 1972 and August 1975. Both ponds showed significant adverse effects. In [[Pasco Pond]], the [[Largemouth Bass]] standing crop fell by 91% in block-net samples. [[Warmouth]] populations were greatly reduced, several forage species disappeared, and [[Bluegill]] numbers rose sharply, with signs of overcrowding. The authors attribute most changes to the loss of aquatic vegetation caused by grass carp feeding. They conclude that grass carp use in Florida should be restricted to closed, easily managed systems.

## Background & Description

Grass carp, also called white amur, had drawn growing controversy in the United States. Concern stemmed from limited scientific knowledge of the exotic species and from a stocking program in Arkansas that exposed rivers and streams of the Central United States to grass carp invasion. The authors argue that understanding the species' biology is essential before sound management programs can be developed.

In 1972 the Florida Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission began a study in four small lakes. Two sites with dense aquatic communities were used for weed control experiments, and the other two were designated for fisheries impact studies. This report covers the two fisheries sites:

- **[[Suwannee Pond]]**: 30 acres near Live Oak. Typical of north Florida mesotrophic lakes, with a native vegetation margin, good water quality, and habitat conditions.
- **[[Pasco Pond]]**: 6 acres near Land-O-Lakes. A typical cypress pond with low pH and adjacent marsh.

Stocking densities were 120 fish/acre (298 fish/ha) in Suwannee Pond and 73 fish/acre (185 fish/ha) in Pasco Pond. Pasco fish averaged 0.82 pounds. Suwannee received mixed sizes because of limited stock availability. The stocking rate was based on earlier work by Sutton (1974), which estimated that 60 pounds/acre (69 kg/ha) of grass carp would be needed to remove a significant amount of aquatic vegetation.

The study had three phases:
- **Baseline year (1972):** natural conditions were monitored without grass carp.
- **Stocking and monitoring:** grass carp were introduced in September and October 1973.
- **Final assessment (1975):** total [[Lake Renovation]] was used to validate block-net data.

Sampling methods included [[Block Net Sampling]], [[Rotenone Sampling]], [[Wegener Ring]] sampling for shallow-water fishes, and a 125-yard trammel net used in Pasco Pond at the start of the study.

Table 1 lists the fishes found in the two ponds, about two dozen species in total. Largemouth bass, bluegill, warmouth, and lake chubsucker were the dominant or most important species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors describe both lakes as macrophyte-based systems that depend on aquatic vegetation for biological health. Before stocking, both supported healthy centrarchid populations. Bluegill and largemouth bass dominated, with killifishes, shiners, and small sunfishes as principal forage species and lake chubsucker as the major coarse species.

Rooted vegetation supports sport fish production, particularly largemouth bass, as shown in earlier Florida studies. Within nine months of stocking, grass carp eliminated the dominant submerged plants in both ponds, including *Hydrilla verticillata*. Emergent plants such as lilies, watershield, and umbrella-grass were also eliminated or substantially reduced. At study end both sites were essentially void of vegetation (Drda, 1976). The authors argue that the stocking density exceeded the carrying capacity of both lakes for native species, so the resulting loss of food, space, and habitat affected all trophic levels.

## Key Findings

### Largemouth Bass

**Pasco Pond (pre-carp):**
- Well-distributed population with good reproduction.
- 164 young-of-the-year, 34 intermediate, and 12 harvestable adults per acre.
- Standing crop of 22 pounds/acre, 11.6% of total fish weight, ranking third in biomass.
- Largest specimen was a 10-pound fish from trammel nets.

**Pasco Pond (post-carp):**
- Block-net standing crop dropped from 22 to 2 pounds/acre, a 91% reduction.
- Eight intermediate bass/acre and no harvestable adults recovered.
- Renovation data showed bass reduced from 250/acre to 28/acre. Standing crop was 7.5 pounds/acre, a 66.0% reduction. Composition fell to 5.9% by weight.
- Young-of-year production declined from an estimated 164 fish/acre to 16 fish/acre.

**Suwannee Pond:**
- Pre-carp block nets (1972) found 26 young-of-year, 25 intermediates, and 38 harvestable adults per acre, with a standing crop of 22.6 pounds/acre.
- Two years after stocking, block nets showed a 23% decline in standing crop (17.4 pounds/acre). Harvestable adults fell from 38 to 10 per acre, a reduction of roughly 74%.
- Renovation showed 13.4 harvestable adults/acre and a standing crop of 24.7 pounds/acre.
- The 1973 and 1974 year-classes were weak. The authors note that the 1972 rotenone kill may have contributed to this, but also argue that a weak 1973–1974 recruitment pattern is more consistent with grass carp effects. Good 1975 bass production occurred when the grass carp population had declined.

### Bluegill

- **Pasco Pond:** Numbers rose from 516 to 2,675 fish/acre, while standing crop stayed nearly constant (30.3 vs. 29.1 pounds/acre). Young-of-year accounted for most of the increase and may have been early signs of overcrowding. Mean condition factor (K) for adults fell from 2.38 in 1972 to 1.77 in 1975.
- **Suwannee Pond:** Bluegill shifted toward smaller fish and heavy young-of-year production. Baseline data showed over 50 bluegill/acre in the 9- and 10-inch classes, but these larger fish were largely eliminated by renovation. Adult condition factor fell from 1.60 to 1.41.

### Warmouth

- **Pasco Pond:** Pre-carp block nets recorded 19/acre, including 26 harvestable adults/acre, and 9.9% of total fish weight. After stocking, the population fell to 1.8 pounds/acre, fewer than four harvestable adults/acre, and 1.4% composition. Numbers dropped from an estimated 948/acre to about 200/acre, and the population was dominated by 1- and 2-inch fish.
- **Suwannee Pond:** A minor species. Renovation found 0.5 harvestable fish/acre and a standing crop of 0.4 pounds, compared with 2 harvestable fish/acre and 0.6 pounds before stocking.

### Lake Chubsucker and Other Coarse Species

- Lake chubsucker in Pasco Pond declined from 92.8 pounds/acre to 52.1 pounds/acre. Suwannee chubsucker increased from seven fish/acre to 25 pounds/acre.
- In Suwannee Pond, coarse species (chiefly brown bullhead and lake chubsucker) increased over baseline. They made up 47.3% of the fish population at study end, compared with third-place ranking before stocking.
- 17 grass carp (3.0 pounds/acre) were recovered at Pasco renovation. 21 grass carp weighing 96 pounds were recovered at Suwannee renovation.

### Shallow-Water Fishes (Wegener Ring Data)

- **Pasco Pond:** 18 species were recovered before stocking, with lake chubsucker, warmouth, mosquitofish, and bluegill among the dominant species. After stocking, biomass declined and species diversity dropped. Dollar sunfish and largemouth bass fry increased, but bass fry recruitment was poor (16 young-of-year/acre at August renovation).
- **Suwannee Pond:** Species diversity fell over the first year after stocking, and biomass declined substantially.
- Seven species were absent from renovation data in one or both ponds, including blackbanded sunfish, redfin pickerel, brook silverside, banded topminnow, and pygmy killifish. The authors report a close correlation between shallow-water fish diversity and the reduction of aquatic vegetation.

### Conclusions and Management Recommendations

- High grass carp densities caused significant adverse effects on native fish populations. The authors cite Sutton (1974), who found that controlling *Hydrilla verticillata* required over 200 pounds of grass carp/acre, and recommend that grass carp be combined with chemical or mechanical methods.
- The authors attribute most changes to the loss of aquatic vegetation, which altered food, space, and habitat for native species.
- Further grass carp use in Florida should be restricted to closed, highly manageable situations where fish can be easily removed. Establishment in public waters, through escapement, natural reproduction, or wholesale stocking, could harm Florida's [[Largemouth Bass]] populations and the sport fishing industry.
- The authors acknowledge that the 1972 rotenone kill confounded some data, and that more research is needed before the role of grass carp in natural systems is fully understood.
