# The Changing Fish Fauna of the Upper Ohio Basin

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #conservation #zoogeography #cyprinidae #percidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 symposium paper by Ernest A. Lachner documents the dramatic decline of fish diversity and abundance in the [[Upper Ohio River Basin]] over the preceding century. Originally supporting more than 125 species across 25 families, the basin's fish fauna has been severely degraded by human activities including deforestation, agricultural intensification, pollution, dam construction, and siltation. The paper provides historical context on the basin's rich ichthyological heritage, explains the zoogeographic origins of the fauna, and catalogs species that have been extirpated or greatly reduced. Lachner calls for an integrated interstate research and conservation program to rehabilitate aquatic habitats and manage remaining fish populations as an "agricultural crop" requiring active stewardship.

## Background & Description

The [[Upper Ohio Basin]] fauna, defined as waters above the mouth of the [[Kanawha River]] in West Virginia, originally contained more than 125 fish species representing 25 families. Several families are endemic to North America, including gars ([[Lepisosteidae]]), bowfin ([[Amiidae]]), mooneye ([[Hiodontidae]]), North American freshwater catfish ([[Ictaluridae]]), sunfish ([[Centrarchidae]]), and troutperch ([[Percopsidae]]). The fauna includes archaic groups such as the [[Paddlefish]] ([[Polyodontidae]]), represented by only one species in North America and one giant relative in Chinese rivers.

The basin's fish fauna shows closest zoogeographic affinity to Eurasia rather than South America. Families shared with Eurasia include sturgeon ([[Acipenseridae]]), minnow ([[Cyprinidae]]), pike ([[Esocidae]]), mudminnow ([[Umbridae]]), and perch ([[Percidae]]). The [[Sucker Family]] ([[Catostomidae]]) is almost entirely North American with only two Asiatic species. The minnow family, containing more than 1,000 species globally, reaches its greatest diversity in southeastern Asia.

The fauna's origins trace to ancient continental connections between North America and Asia, likely more than 100 million years ago. During warm periods, faunal exchanges occurred across these land bridges. The [[Allegheny River]] once flowed northward into what is now [[Lake Erie]]. Pleistocene glaciation profoundly affected distribution: the last ice sheet forced warm-water species southward while creating opportunities for cold-water salmonids (trouts and chars) to colonize streams. Post-glacial lake connections linked the [[Mississippi Valley]] with the Great Lakes, allowing recolonization as ice receded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Original Habitat Conditions**: Early explorers found streams "teeming with life" with crystal-clear, constant-flowing water, lush aquatic vegetation, and clean gravel, rock, and sand substrates. Dense fish schools provided abundant food resources.

**Anthropogenic Degradation**: Human activities have fundamentally altered stream ecosystems:

- **Deforestation and Agriculture**: Forest clearing and intensive farming increased soil erosion, causing massive siltation that clogs fish respiratory systems, eliminates spawning gravels, and destroys invertebrate food sources and aquatic plant refugia
- **Pollution**: Municipal sewage, industrial wastes (mill acids, mine drainage), and agricultural wastes (cannery, dairy) have rendered many streams uninhabitable. The [[Monongahela River]], which supported 40 species in 1886, has been "practically wiped out" by mining pollution
- **Hydrological Changes**: Spring flows reduced to trickles, groundwater levels lowered, many streams seasonally dry, large rivers show extreme flow fluctuations
- **Temperature Increases**: Reduced forest cover, lower water volumes, and diminished spring inputs have raised summer water temperatures, restricting cold-water species like [[Brook Trout]] to isolated headwater refugia
- **Physical Barriers**: Dams and reservoirs block migrations; grain mills with small dams numbered in the hundreds during settlement

**Habitat Associations**: Different species occupy distinct niches:
- Large river inhabitants: [[Lake Sturgeon]], [[Paddlefish]], [[Mooneye]], [[Blue Catfish]]
- Swift riffle specialists: [[Gilt Darter]], [[Bighead Darter]], [[Spotted Darter]]
- Brook/creek species: [[Blacknose Dace]], [[Creek Chub]], [[Hornyhead Chub]], [[Sculpin]]
- Glacial drift areas with clear water and gravel: [[Eastern Tonguetied Minnow]], [[Northern Redfin Shiner]]

**Biological Interactions**: Competition for food and spawning sites, predation, and commensalism affect distribution. The [[River Chub]] ([[Hybopsis micropogon]]) builds large pebble nests (>10,000 stones) that provide spawning sites for [[Common Shiner]] ([[Notropis cornutus chrysocephalus]]) and other species, demonstrating possible mutualistic relationships.

## Key Findings

**Species Extirpations and Declines**: Eighteen species formerly common in the upper basin have not been reported in recent years in Pennsylvania tributaries:

- [[Paddlefish]] (*Polyodon spathula*)
- [[Lake Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser fulvescens*)
- [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus platorhynchus*)
- [[Shortnose Gar]] (*Lepisosteus platostomus*)
- [[Bowfin]] (*Amia calva*)
- [[Goldeye]] (*Hiodon alosoides*)
- [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*)
- [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*)
- [[Highfin Sucker]] (*Carpiodes velifer velifer*)
- [[River Carpsucker]] (*Carpiodes carpi carpio*)
- [[Blue Sucker]] (*Cycleptus elongatus*)
- [[Sturgeon Sucker]] (*Catostomus catostomus*)
- [[River Shiner]] (*Notropis blennius*)
- [[Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus nuchalis*)
- [[Blue Catfish]] (*Ictalurus furcatus*)
- [[American Eel]] (*Anguilla rostrata*)
- [[Sauger]] (*Stizostedion canadense*)
- [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*)

The [[Hairlip Sucker]] (*Lagochila lacera*) is probably extinct. The [[Alligator Gar]] was last recorded near Cincinnati in 1838.

**Siltation Impacts**: In Ohio, extreme siltation has extirpated the [[Bigeye Shiner]] (*Notropis boops*) and greatly reduced the [[Creek Chubsucker]] (*Erimyzon oblongus*) and [[Blacknose Shiner]] (*Notropis heterolepis*), which require clean, vegetated streams.

**Replacement Fauna**: Tolerant species adapted to turbid conditions are expanding:
- [[Suckermouth Minnow]] (*Phenacobius mirabilis*), originally from turbid plains streams, now reaches Appalachian foothills
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) and [[Bluntnose Minnow]] (*Pimephales notatus*) show greatly increased distribution

**Introduced Species**: Seven species or groups have been stocked:
1. [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) - European introduction
2. [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) - Pacific Coast native
3. [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) - native, restocked
4. [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) - Asian species introduced ~1876, now abundant but underutilized
5. [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) - Asian aquarium species, uncommon
6. [[Ohio Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy ohiensis*) - native, restocked
7. Various warm-water species for farm ponds: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], bullheads, [[White Bass]], [[Crappie]], [[Yellow Perch]]

**Conservation Recommendations**: Lachner advocates for:
- Integrated interstate program combining public health, sanitation, game, fish, forest, and education departments
- Research programs to develop new fisheries management procedures
- Training personnel for management and conservation
- Public education through schools, radio, television, and press
- Teaching conservation as curriculum requirement in elementary and secondary schools
- Managing fish populations as an "agricultural crop" requiring active stewardship
- Recognition that stocking programs and fishing regulations alone are insufficient without habitat rehabilitation

**Socioeconomic Context**: More than 3 million anglers use the basin annually, contributing millions of dollars to gear, tackle, food, lodging, and transportation. Fishing pressure continues to increase with shorter working hours, yet fish populations have declined to "a critical minimum" relative to angler numbers. The resource provides "wholesome use of leisure time, an immeasurable value."

**Historical Documentation**: The paper synthesizes work by early naturalists including LeSueur (1817-1818), Rafinesque (1820), Kirtland (1838-1847), Cope (1869, 1881), Evermann and Bollman (1886), and numerous 20th-century ichthyologists. Kirtland noted that migratory species in the [[Mahoning River]] were eliminated by dam construction over a century ago. The [[Monongahela River]] supported 40 species in 1886 before mining pollution devastated the fauna.
