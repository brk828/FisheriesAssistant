# Fish Refugia and Captive Propagation: A Viable Aid to Conservation and Restoration

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This conference paper, presented at the National Symposium on Endangered Fishes of India (25-26 April 1992, Allahabad, Uttar Pradesh), is authored by [[Buddy L. Jensen]], Director of the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (Dexter NFH & TC), a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service facility in New Mexico. It argues that captive propagation and reintroduction are valuable, but not sufficient, tools for conserving imperiled freshwater fishes of the American Southwest, and it describes the facility's protocols for spawning, genetic management, rearing, and holding. The text is partly a generalized defense of captive propagation and partly an operational description of Dexter.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with an acknowledgement of the many staff who developed the techniques over roughly 14 years, and cites a body of literature on captive propagation, endangered species, and the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973). It frames captivity as a historically established means of protecting species, while cautioning that captive propagation "is not an end in itself" and carries risks to the species involved.

Dexter was built in 1931-1932 and for over 40 years produced warmwater game fish. In 1978 its mission changed to endangered fishes after other Fish and Wildlife Service hatcheries in Texas and Oklahoma took over game-fish production. Of roughly 88 U.S. fish species listed as threatened or endangered, sixteen (about 18%) were held and cultured at Dexter at the time of writing. A total of 29 species have been held since the program began. The facility is one of only six Technology Centers in the Fish and Wildlife Service.

Dexter lies at 3,600 ft (1,108 m) elevation in the Pecos River Valley, where annual precipitation averages 12 in (30 cm) and vegetation is semidesert scrub on the northern fringe of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. Water comes from four shallow wells, 150 ft (46 m) deep, pumping about 2,000 gpm (about 8,400 lpm). Well-head temperature is a constant 64 °F (17 °C) and pH averages 8.4-8.5. There are 48 earthen ponds, 0.15 to 1.8 acres (0.07 to 0.8 ha) and 3 to 6 ft (0.93 to 1.85 m) deep. Each pond has separate supply and drain lines to prevent mixing, since many held species are congeneric and could hybridize.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper identifies three major categories of human-caused decline:

- **Dams:** blocking migrations, converting riverine habitat to lacustrine habitat, altering flow and temperature regimes, dewatering downstream reaches through diversion, and killing fish through hydropower turbines and unnatural flow fluctuations for peak power demand.
- **Exotic species:** non-native fishes compete for food and space and prey on early life stages. Once established in large, diverse systems they are essentially impossible to remove. The authors quote Minckley and Douglas (1991) that non-native fishes "may prove a far greater problem to native fish survival than all our other environmental abuses combined."
- **Groundwater pumping:** in arid western North America this has reduced flow and dried springs and streams, causing population losses.

Secondary impacts include overgrazing and timber harvest causing soil erosion and arroyo cutting, which can dewater [[Bosques]]; industrial and municipal pollution; thermal pollution from fossil-fuel power generation; channelization; overharvest, especially of commercial and sport species; and dredging and riprap armoring of banks.

## Key Findings

**Spawning and culture protocols**
- Most species are spawned by hormone-induced ovulation, using [[Carp Pituitary]] (CP) for cyprinids and [[Human Chorionic Gonadotropin]] (HCG) for catostomids.
- CP is given intraperitoneally at 2 mg/kg of body weight (as stated in the source), and females ovulate 20-24 h later. Accurate maturity assessment is essential because CP can harm under-developed fish.
- Cyprinids with low or no milk flow receive a single dose intramuscularly at 300 IU/lb (600 IU/kg).
- Catostomids receive three intramuscular HCG injections, 24 h apart, at 150 IU/lb (300 IU/kg). Females ovulate 20-24 h after the final injection. HCG is considered gentler and less damaging than CP.
- Eggs are stripped into a saline sperm diluent with milt from two or more males, stirred with a feather, and treated with a clay slurry to remove stickiness. They water-harden for at least 30 minutes.
- Eggs incubate at 70 °F, hatch in about 96 h, and complete hatching in about 120 h. Sac fry are reared for about four days in indoor troughs before being stocked into fertilized earthen ponds at roughly 100,000 to 125,000 fry per acre.
- Ponds are fertilized with alfalfa pellets (100 lb/acre, weekly) and superphosphate (50 lb/acre, weekly) to build zooplankton populations for fry feeding, then fry move to commercial diets. Production fish are held 4 to 30 months.
- Before shipment fish are held for at least 48 hours to clear their guts, which helps maintain water quality during transport.

**Genetic management**
- Genetic "fingerprints" should be established at the outset for each species and population to create a baseline.
- Gene banking is a last resort. Priority goes to habitat and in-situ species maintenance.
- Broodstock of 150 to 500 adults is maintained where feasible. Broodfish are tagged with PIT (passive integrated transponder) tags to track individual performance in a database.
- Periodic introduction of wild fish is recommended to counter the loss of genetic diversity that results from repeated captive generations.
- Some species are very long-lived: [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] are known to exceed 40 years, and a Colorado squawfish brood stock has been held for 18 years and remains productive.

**Facility objectives**
1. **Refuge:** maintain species threatened with extinction in case a natural or human-caused disaster eliminates the wild population.
2. **Research and development:** collect life-history data and develop spawning and culture techniques for species with little known biology.
3. **Reintroduction:** produce large numbers of fish of various sizes for restoring populations in historic habitats.

**Handling and quarantine**
- Wild fish arrive in truck-mounted tanks and are quarantined and treated for disease and parasites before being moved to holding ponds. Ponds are cleaned and re-stocked at least twice a year.

The four exceptions are the Ranch Roundtail Chub, Warner Sucker, Shortnose Sucker and Lost River Sucker. Table No. 2 lists species by family and federal listing status.

**Conclusions and position**
- Captive propagation and reintroduction are "tools," not a substitute for habitat protection. The authors call for combining habitat preservation, captive propagation, and reintroduction within a "holistic approach to conservation biology."
- The authors quote Aldo Leopold (1949): "The first prerequisite of any intelligent tinkering is to save all the pieces."
- The paper concludes that loss of natural resources need not be the price of modern progress if planning is careful.
