# Maintenance and Rehabilitation of Eastern US Riparian Habitats: Inertia, Elasticity and Biological Monitoring

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-assemblages

## Executive Summary

This reprinted chapter, from a 1979 U.S. Forest Service proceedings on floodplain wetland and riparian ecosystem protection, argues that eastern U.S. riparian habitats can be both maintained and rehabilitated with existing methods. The authors, [[John Cairns Jr.]], [[Jay R. Stauffer Jr.]] and [[Charles H. Hocutt]], propose that ecological quality should be managed with the same [[Quality Control System]] logic used in industry: sensors, rapid data relay, and a decision unit able to act. They also test two indices, [[Inertia Index]] and [[Elasticity Index]], on fish data from two stream systems. The paper concludes that biological monitoring must be combined with chemical-physical monitoring, and it cites the [[Thames Estuary]] recovery as evidence that rehabilitation is economically feasible.

## Background & Description

The paper has two parts, because maintenance and rehabilitation of water quality require different strategies. The authors use [[Webster's Dictionary]] definitions to distinguish the two. Maintenance means preserving a condition, while rehabilitation means improving a damaged system to a higher level of value. The authors argue that society is more likely to accept rehabilitation in this sense than a return to original conditions, which they describe as technically uncertain and costly.

Maintenance needs three components:

- **Baseline studies** of present biological, chemical and physical condition. The authors note that most large rivers and lakes, such as the Ohio River and Lake Erie, have few pre-disturbance records, and that fish communities are poorly known.
- **[[Hazard Evaluation]]** of known potential pollutants and their estimated biological effects. The authors trace aquatic toxicity testing from [[Hart et al. 1945]] onward, moving from batch to continuous-flow tests, from acute to chronic endpoints, and from death to sensitive behavioural endpoints.
- **[[Biological Monitoring]]** for early warning. Speed of information generation is the key requirement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors argue that chemical limits alone cannot protect biological systems. Effluent that is harmless under one water-quality regime can be lethal under another, even when the discharge is unchanged. Chemical analysis also misses three things: biological effects below detection limits, the combined effects of compounds (including [[Synergistic Toxicity]]), and the modifying effect of water quality on toxicity. Because of this, regulated effluents are often kept well below no-effect concentrations, which underuses the stream's [[Assimilative Capacity]].

The paper proposes that ecosystem vulnerability has four properties, drawn from [[Cairns 1976]]:

- **Elasticity**: the ability to recover from damage, based on nearby source populations, dispersal stages, habitat condition, residual toxicants, water quality after stress, and management capability.
- **Inertia**: the ability to resist displacement, based on indigenous variability tolerance, [[Structural Redundancy]] and [[Functional Redundancy]], mixing or flushing capacity, buffering chemistry, proximity to an [[Ecological Threshold]], and regional management.
- **Resiliency**: the number of times a system can recover after displacement, which the authors say has been little studied.
- **Vulnerability to irreversible damage**.

The authors distinguish their terms from [[Holling 1973]]'s usage. They use "elasticity" for recovery from societal displacement and "inertia" for resistance to societal or technological perturbation.

### Case Study Systems

- **[[Youghiogheny River]]** (Monongahela drainage): 15 stream sections, 55 fish species. Mining occurs in the basin.
- **[[Conowingo Creek]]** (Susquehanna drainage): 4 stream sections, 27 fish taxa. The basin drains limestone and is well buffered.

## Key Findings

**Recovery and rehabilitation case histories**

- **Thames estuary**: Pollution was a concern from 1620. Salmon were still caught in 1766, the last salmon was caught in 1833, and commercial fishing ceased by 1850. At the start of Queen Elizabeth II's reign there were virtually no fish in the tidal Thames. Doxat (1977) reports 91 species now found in the tideway. The authors state that rehabilitation occurred without a depression and that economic benefits "far outweigh" costs. Cairns observed general satisfaction on two visits in 1978.
- **Lake Washington**: [[Edmondson 1977]] describes recovery from eutrophication without undue financial burden, with improved recreation and aesthetics.
- **Strip-mined lands in Ohio**: [[Riley 1977]] describes similar rehabilitation.

The authors conclude that rehabilitation is technologically feasible, not exorbitantly expensive, understandable to the public when given adequate cost/benefit information, and a source of civic pride.

**Inertia Index results**

The inertia index is the product of six categories (a through f), each rated 1 to 3 or scored on a fractional scale for redundancy and temperature. The index is calculated for each stream section.

- **Conowingo Creek**: Indices for sections 1 to 4 were 18, 48, 90 and 72 (section 4 partly illegible in the OCR, value computed from the listed categories). Section 3 had the highest inertia.
- **Youghiogheny River**: Indices ranged from 12 (section 9) to 90 (section 4). Section 9 was rated lowest for temperature because summer temperatures approach lethal limits for [[Brook Trout]] in a native population.

**Structural and functional redundancy**

- **Conowingo Creek** structural redundancy: 0.367, 0.546, 0.917 and 0.550 for sections 1 to 4. Functional redundancy: 0.358, 0.479, 0.952 and 0.471.
- **Youghiogheny River** structural redundancy ranged from 0.168 (section 8) to 0.650 (section 4). Functional redundancy ranged from 0.185 to 0.742.
- Both measures used family-level species counts for structural redundancy and trophic-level counts for functional redundancy. Trophic levels were drawn from [[Carlander 1969]] and [[Scott and Crossman 1973]].

**Elasticity Index results**

Only three elasticity categories could be calculated (a, b and f), since the others need stress information.

- **Conowingo Creek**: Indices were 4, 6, 2 and 6 for sections 1 to 4. Section 3 had the lowest elasticity and the highest inertia.
- **Youghiogheny River**: Indices ranged from 4 to 12, with section 4 the highest.

**Relationship between inertia and elasticity**

In Conowingo Creek, the section with the highest inertia had the lowest elasticity. The authors note this pattern does not hold in all cases (it does not in the Youghiogheny data). They suggest that sections with high inertia and low elasticity should be protected strictly, because they may serve as recolonization sources.

**Methodological limits**

- Neither study collected fish data for these specific analyses.
- Dispersal rates for fish are poorly known, which limits the elasticity index. The authors note that locomotion speed does not necessarily predict dispersal ease.
- The authors used fish because they occupy the top of the food chain, pass through most trophic levels, are taxonomically well studied, and have better life-history information. They stress that the indices must be tested on other groups, such as microbes, macroinvertebrates and algae, before a "best method" is established.

**Conclusions**

The authors conclude that:

1. Existing methods are sufficient to maintain and rehabilitate eastern U.S. riparian habitats.
2. Water quality should be maintained by combining biological assessment with biological monitoring, and both must be paired with chemical-physical monitoring.
3. Maintenance is more ethically and economically desirable than waiting for damage before acting.
