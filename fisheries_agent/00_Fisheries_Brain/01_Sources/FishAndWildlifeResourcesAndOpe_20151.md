# Fish and Wildlife Resources and Operations of Glen Canyon Dam - Draft Planning Aid Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #endangered-species #dam-impacts #riparian-habitat

## Executive Summary

This draft planning aid report, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under the [[Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act]], advises the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] on potential resource concerns and study needs for fish and wildlife resources in the [[Colorado River]] affected by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations. The report covers the 292-mile stretch from Glen Canyon Dam through Marble and Grand Canyons to Lake Mead, documenting impacts on aquatic ecosystems, riparian vegetation, terrestrial wildlife, and endangered species. Since dam closure in 1963, the river has transformed from warm, turbid waters with native warmwater fishes to cold, clear waters supporting a coldwater trout fishery, with dramatic alterations to flow regimes, sediment transport, and habitat structure. The document identifies critical concerns for multiple endangered species including [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*), and [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus anatum*), and recommends comprehensive assessment of operational alternatives in the forthcoming Environmental Impact Statement.

## Background & Description

The project area encompasses aquatic, terrestrial, and riparian zones of the [[Colorado River]] between [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Lake Mead]], including 15 miles of Glen Canyon and 277 miles of Marble and Grand Canyons. The [[National Park Service]] administers [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]] below the dam and [[Grand Canyon National Park]] downstream. Major portions of the [[Little Colorado River]] and adjacent areas are Navajo Indian lands.

Since dam closure in 1963, the Colorado River ecosystem has been drastically altered. Seasonal water fluctuations have been replaced by daily fluctuations driven primarily by hydropower production demands. Water temperatures dropped from warm and turbid to constant cold (approximately 10°C) and clear conditions. The native warmwater fish assemblage has been largely displaced by a coldwater trout fishery. Current operational conditions affecting the ecosystem include floods (releases >31,000 cubic feet per second), daily fluctuations, rate of fluctuations, and low flows.

The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Phase I (December 1982-1987) evaluated impacts during a period of record high flows. Between 1983 and 1986, approximately 15.4 million tons of sand were lost from Grand Canyon, and approximately 95% of marshes were eliminated due to flooding. Phase II studies were planned to analyze impacts of low and fluctuating flows in greater depth. The Bureau of Reclamation announced preparation of an Environmental Impact Statement for dam operations, with completion targeted for end of 1991.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic Ecosystem:** The filamentous green alga *[[Cladophora glomerata]]* provides the food chain basis for many organisms. The amphipod *[[Gammarus lacustris]]*, introduced by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] in 1967-1968, serves as a primary food source for [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*). During fluctuating flows, Gammarus drift increased from 15.4% to 49.2%, with juveniles more susceptible than adults. Aquatic invertebrate samples from Lees Ferry to river-mile 220 in 1985 included chironomid midges (Chironomidae), freshwater earthworms (Oligochaeta), and buffalo gnats (Simuliidae), with noticeable decline below the Little Colorado River.

**Lees Ferry Trout Fishery:** Creel surveys (January 1984-May 1986) collected data on 6,266 trout: 96.4% [[Rainbow Trout]], 3.5% [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), and 0.1% other species including [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarki*), and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*). Approximately 28% of harvested trout originated from natural reproduction. An experimental flow reduction to 5,000 cfs in October 1984 resulted in over 800 stranded trout observed, with 635 of 639 documented fish being rainbow trout, mostly large reproductive adults.

**Tributary Systems:** Eleven major tributaries were evaluated during GCES Phase I. In spring 89% and summer 96% of captured fish were native species. [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*) were caught in Paria River, Kanab Creek, and Little Colorado River. Summer tributary temperatures ranged 13.0-28.0°C versus mainstem temperatures of 6-12°C year-round. In winter, 68% of tributary fish were rainbow trout, which spawned in eight of eleven examined tributaries, typically in December.

**Sediment Dynamics:** Between 1928-1959, the Lees Ferry gaging station commonly recorded suspended sediment at 10,000 parts per million (ppm); post-dam readings dropped to approximately 200 ppm. Selective erosion removes fine sediments, leaving coarser particles exposed. Beach erosion and degradation represent the most drastic changes along the river, affecting trout spawning gravel beds, potential humpback chub habitat, and riparian habitat maintenance.

**Riparian Vegetation:** [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix ramossissima*), sand bar willow (*Salix interior*), and seep willow (*Baccharis salicifolia*) now thrive in areas where they previously existed in small numbers. By 1976, saltcedar was well-established, apparently responding to optimal daily inundation during critical seedling periods. The 1983 flood eliminated approximately 50% of riparian vegetation. Experimental studies showed that plants subject to fluctuating flows had higher survival than those completely inundated, duration of flooding had more severe impact than amplitude, and no seedlings survived more than five days of desiccation. Complete plant recovery may require over 10 years.

**Terrestrial Wildlife:** Over 30 bird species inhabit the Lower Colorado River, including 11 obligate riparian species. Regulated flows increased riparian habitats, benefiting some species while causing losses of nest sites and food resources for birds within the flood range. Reptiles and amphibians including side-blotched lizard (*Uta stansburiana*), red spotted toad (*Bufo punctatus*), and western rattlesnake (*Crotalus viridis*) inhabit the area. Mammals include [[Mule Deer]] (*Odocoileus hemionus*), [[Bighorn Sheep]] (*Ovis canadensis*), [[Beaver]] (*Castor canadensis*), and multiple bat species (Chiroptera).

## Key Findings

**Endangered Species Status:**

1. **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): The Grand Canyon population represents one of two high concentrations of this endangered species. A jeopardy biological opinion issued in 1978 exists for dam operations. Conservation measures are being developed cooperatively by FWS, AGFD, NPS, and Reclamation, with the next biological opinion expected to coincide with EIS completion.

2. **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): This endangered species was extirpated from the study area following dam closure. Suitability of reintroduction into Colorado River tributaries and historic sites is being evaluated by the Lower Basin fishery management group.

3. **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Listed as endangered by Arizona (Category 1 candidate federally). Specimens were observed in 1978 at Paria River confluence and above Bass Camp (River Mile 107). A single specimen collected in Little Colorado River in May 1989 was recently confirmed as full razorback sucker.

4. **[[Peregrine Falcon]]** (*Falco peregrinus anatum*): Surveys (1982-1987) indicate small nesting numbers on river cliffs. Recent surveys suggest Grand Canyon may contain the largest nesting population of peregrine falcons in the lower 48 states. Limited information available on food resources; surveys indicate summer feeding on white-throated swifts, swallows, and bats.

5. **[[Bald Eagle]]** (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*): Not evaluated in GCES, but periodic surveys since 1976 documented presence. In January 1985, no eagles were seen during 18-day boat survey; in December 1985, four eagles were observed at Nankoweap Creek mouth. Wintering eagle numbers have increased, apparently responding to trout availability in Nankoweap Creek during spawning season. Speculation exists that permanent populations may establish in Grand Canyon.

6. **[[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]** (*Empidonax traillii extimus*): Arizona lists willow flycatcher as endangered; FWS considers the southwestern subspecies a Category 2 candidate. The Grand Canyon population constitutes no more than a few dozen pairs but represents the highest numbers in Arizona. Numbers increased from 1982-1986 but declined in 1987, possibly due to water levels occurring below suitable habitat.

**Operational Impacts:**

- **Flow Fluctuations:** Daily fluctuations for hydropower production cause stranding of fish, desiccation of algae and invertebrates, nest flooding for riparian birds, and exposure of beaches and campsites. Experimentation with pre-emergent alevins showed fish from steady flow redds were 20 times more likely to survive than those from dewatered redds.

- **Flood Events:** Floods (>31,000 cfs) expected every 20 years cause massive habitat disruption. The 1983 flood eliminated approximately 50% of riparian vegetation and 95% of marshes, removed 15.4 million tons of sand, and caused extensive stranding of adult trout.

- **Low Flows:** Information on low flow impacts is lacking from GCES Phase I. Low flows cause fish stranding (800+ trout observed during October 1984 experimental reduction to 5,000 cfs), desiccation of algae and invertebrates, and reduced food production.

**Wetland and Backwater Habitats:** Dam closure in 1963 created wetland habitats important for [[Canada Geese]] (*Branta canadensis*), [[Bufflehead]] (*Bucephala albeola*), [[Mallard]] (*Anas platyrhynchos*), [[American Wigeon]] (*Anas americana*), and other migratory waterfowl. These limited backwater and wetland habitats are critical components of the Greater Grand Canyon ecosystem, particularly in dry western areas. Between 1983-1986, approximately 95% of marshes were eliminated due to flooding; full impact not understood.

**Recreational Resources:** The Lees Ferry trout fishery constitutes a nationally significant sport fishery resource. Millions of visitors annually utilize Grand Canyon National Park and Glen Canyon National Recreation Area. Recreationists view diverse non-game species including beavers, ring-tailed cats, belted kingfishers, bald eagles, peregrine falcons, songbirds, waterfowl, and other migratory wetland birds. Quality of outdoor experience may decrease due to periodic exposure and erosional loss of gravel bars, beaches, and campsites. Navigational hazards may increase at rapids and gravel bars from fluctuating and low flows.

**Study Recommendations:** The FWS recommends consideration of:

1. **Habitat Evaluation Procedures (HEP):** Habitat-based methodology for impact assessment and project planning to measure habitat quality for fish and wildlife resources.

2. **Instream Flow Incremental Methodology (IFIM):** Technique to adjust interim flow requirements balancing hydropower production, water needs, fisheries and wildlife protection, and recreational uses while minimizing detrimental impacts.

**Management Priorities:** All operational and non-operational alternatives in the EIS should assess: algal and invertebrate production, trout spawning bars and recruitment, tributary outlets and bald eagle forage accessibility, sediment replenishment mechanisms, vegetation seasonal flow requirements, reptile and amphibian reproduction timing, riparian bird nest site stability, backwater and wetland habitat maintenance, endangered species conservation measures, and recreational use sustainability. Stable populations cannot be expected without consistent, long-term operating criteria. If EIS schedule cannot be maintained, interim flows should be implemented to reduce negative impacts, secondary to research flow needs.
