# Gizzard Shad Removal in Lake Hamilton, Arkansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gizzard-shad

## Executive Summary

This report describes a 1957 rotenone treatment of [[Lake Hamilton]], a 7,200-acre reservoir near [[Hot Springs]], Arkansas, undertaken to reduce an overpopulation of [[Gizzard Shad]] and [[Freshwater Drum]]. The control was recommended by a three-year comparative fisheries survey and was carried out with the [[Arkansas Game and Fish Commission]], local civic groups and the [[Arkansas Power and Light Company]], which lowered the lake by ten feet to reduce its surface area. The operation applied about 2,255 gallons of [[Pro-Noxfish]] (rotenone) at a target concentration of 0.10 to 0.15 ppm. The authors estimate that roughly 650,000 pounds of fish were removed, including at least 90% of the shad and about 50% of the drum, while game fish losses were about 1% by weight. The authors describe the operation as mechanically successful but state that its long-term value must await follow-up studies.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** Lake Hamilton is one of three reservoirs on the [[Ouachita River]] near Hot Springs (the others named are [[Lake Catherine]] and [[Lake Ouachita]]). It was built in 1931 by the Arkansas Power and Light Company primarily for hydro-electric generation. A land development program later sold lots and encouraged cottages, homes, tourist courts and other facilities. The shoreline is described as one of the best developed in the state, and fishing is an important part of the resort economy.

**Survey origin.** In 1955 the Arkansas Game and Fish Commission began a three-year comparative fisheries study under a Federal Aid (D-J) Investigations Project (F-5-R). Its aim was to inform management of large reservoirs. The survey concluded that Lake Hamilton had an overpopulation of gizzard shad and drum. Sampling over three years gave an average standing population of 157 pounds of fish per acre, of which 50% was gizzard shad and 21 was reported as drum (the source text reads "21 drum", which is probably a percentage but is ambiguous). Gizzard shad were the dominant fish, and predators such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]] could no longer control them.

**Stakeholders.** The Hot Springs Chamber of Commerce, civic clubs and property owners followed the study closely and wanted the recommendations implemented. The Chamber requested a shad kill, raised money to help pay for the rotenone, and took responsibility for fish disposal. The Commission provided technical advice, applied the chemical and shared costs.

**Authors.** The report is by [[Andrew H. Hulsey]] (Arkansas Game and Fish Commission, Lonoke) and [[James Stevenson]] (University of Little Rock).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Timing and drawdown.** The Power and Light Company had planned a ten-foot drawdown in October 1957 so property owners could dredge and build. Water was lowered at six inches per day from September 1. By September 20 the lake stood at 390 feet above mean sea level, ten feet below normal pool, with about 6,090 surface acres.
- **Treatment area.** A fishery biologist mapped desirable areas. About half the lake was selected and divided into seven segments, with maps showing depth, volume and the amount of rotenone needed.
- **Logistics.** Three loading stations were set up. Eight boats each carried a 55-gallon drum of Pro-Noxfish on a low-center-of-gravity stand. Each operator received a map of an assigned section.
- **Tailwater concern.** The upper reach, about four miles below [[Blakely Mountain Dam]], was cold and fast-flowing with apparently few shad. The utility and the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] agreed to split generation among the three dams to slow the current and limit dilution.
- **Persistence.** Some treated areas stayed toxic for about a week. After five days, local anglers caught some largemouth bass and crappie, and fishing later improved.

## Key Findings

**Operation and dosage**
- Application began October 31. Chemical was fed by gravity through a rubber hose and mixed by the outboard propeller. Treatment took about eight hours, with larger garden hose used after the first five hours.
- The initial target was 0.10 ppm. Because adult shad and drum were still visible in the lower lake, the concentration was raised to 0.13 ppm and in some areas 0.15 ppm.
- Total chemical applied was 2,255 gallons, over approximately 3,000 surface acres.

**Biological response**
- Young-of-the-year gizzard shad (4 to 6 inches) showed distress about three hours after treatment began. Drum surfaced in the upstream area in a little over four hours.
- On November 1, buffalo fish of 25 to 85 pounds were killed where the concentration reached 0.15 ppm, along with bluegill, redear and longear sunfish, warmouth, black and white crappie, spotted and largemouth bass and white bass. White bass appeared more susceptible than other sportfish.
- Madtoms, darters, logperch and brook silversides were also apparently eliminated in large numbers, though their total weight was small.
- The utility dewatered the lake about one foot over 18 hours. This pushed toxic water from bays and upper reaches into untreated areas, where shad and drum began dying. Some drum died in the main channel as far as two miles below the treated area.

**Mortality estimates**
- Observations on November 3 indicated the water was still toxic to young shad over most of the lake. The authors estimated at least a 90% shad kill for the whole lake.
- Roughly 60% of affected fish sank and never floated, so the weight recovered was uncertain.
- Shoreline counts suggested about 90% of gizzard shad and possibly 50% of drum were killed. Game fish made up about 1% of the kill by weight.
- Calculations used the three-year population data: 565,200 pounds of gizzard shad and 237,000 pounds of drum (the source reads "237,00"). Estimated removals were 508,700 pounds of shad (90%), 118,700 pounds of drum (as stated in source; 50% of 237,000 is 118,500), and about 6,500 pounds of game fish. The authors give a total removal of about 650,000 pounds, while stating that the figures are "rather arbitrary."

**Recovery and public involvement**
- Hundreds of boats with spectators used dip nets during and for several days after treatment. The Chamber of Commerce set up fifteen disposal stations, publicized through newspapers and radio. Comparatively few fish reached them because most owners collected fish along their own shorelines.
- An attempt to count fish with 100 submerged baskets (four feet square, quarter-inch mesh) failed. Many baskets were empty, some were lost to boat traffic, and some were apparently emptied by people on the lake.

**Authors' conclusions**
- The mechanics of the operation were judged highly satisfactory, which the authors credit to detailed planning, efficient work by personnel and cooperation from local groups.
- The authors state that the permanent value of the project cannot be judged until follow-up studies and the future success of fishermen are assessed.
