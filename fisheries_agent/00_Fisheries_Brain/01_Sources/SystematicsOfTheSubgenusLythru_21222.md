# Systematics of the Subgenus Lythrurus, Genus Notropis (Pisces: Cyprinidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 monograph by [[Franklin F. Snelson Jr.]], published in the [[Bulletin of the Florida State Museum]], revises the systematics of the North American minnow subgenus [[Lythrurus]] within the genus [[Notropis]]. The subgenus is diagnosed, its nomenclature is traced, and its species are grouped into four complexes: fumeus, ardens, umbratilis, and roseipinnis. The roseipinnis complex, which forms the core of the paper, is revised. It contains [[Notropis bellus]] (with subspecies [[Notropis bellus bellus]] and the new subspecies [[Notropis bellus alegnotus]]), [[Notropis roseipinnis]], and the new species [[Notropis atrapiculus]]. The work relies on meristic, morphometric, lateral-line, pigmentation, tuberculation and sexual-dimorphism analyses across drainages, supported by specimen loans from many North American museums.

## Background & Description

Lythrurus is a group of small, brightly colored shiners, usually under about 60 mm standard length, most of which prefer small, clear streams. The subgenus was first described by [[David Starr Jordan]] in 1876 and was reduced to subgeneric rank under Notropis by Jordan in 1885. Its type species is now designated as [[Notropis umbratilis cyanocephalus]], an emended designation justified under the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature. The subgenus is closely related to the atherinoides series of Notropis. Its diagnostic features include smaller scales, reduced squamation on the anterior dorsolateral body, bright breeding colors, reduction of the cephalic lateral-line system, and enlarged urogenital papillae in breeding females.

The study used standardized counts and measurements, including fin rays, scale counts, pharyngeal teeth, gill rakers, vertebrae from radiographs, cephalic lateral-line pores, and a fin pigmentation index. Sexual dimorphism was tested with Chi-square and Student's t-tests. Most analyses were restricted to breeding specimens because of seasonal variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Members of the roseipinnis complex live mainly in Gulf Coastal streams. Their habitat preferences differ among species:

- **N. b. bellus** inhabits small to moderately large streams of low to moderate gradient, with clear to turbid water. It is generally absent from dark-stained, acid lower Coastal Plain waters, possibly because of competition with N. roseipinnis. It is relatively tolerant of pollution; it was the most abundant fish in Shades Creek, Birmingham, Alabama, a degraded stream lacking darters.
- **N. b. alegnotus** occupies upland riffle-pool streams with clear water, hard bottoms, and moderate to fast current. In Five Mile, Marriott, and Crooked creeks it was numerically dominant, favoring deeper, slower pool margins. Strip mining apparently extirpated one population at Mud Creek.
- **N. roseipinnis** is restricted to Coastal Plain streams with white to brown-stained water, sand or gravel bottoms, and abundant vegetation. It prefers slow, deep pools.
- **N. atrapiculus** is found in small to moderate streams of pools and runs in the Escambia, Yellow, and Choctawhatchee drainages, and both above and below the Fall Line in the Apalachicola.

Reproduction was observed in Calebee Creek, Alabama (25 May 1968). Large tuberculate males of N. b. bellus milled over sunfish (Lepomis megalotis) nests, but no spawning was seen. Water temperature was 78°F.

Anthropogenic threats are noted. Strip mining in the Black Warrior basin is continuing; reservoirs are planned on the North River; and urban encroachment threatens many streams.

## Key Findings

- **Four species complexes** were recognized in Lythrurus: fumeus (N. fumeus), ardens (N. ardens and N. lirus), umbratilis (N. umbratilis with subspecies), and roseipinnis (revised here).
- **Notropis bellus alegnotus** is described as a new subspecies from upper Black Warrior streams above the Fall Line. The holotype is CU 53346, a 42.3 mm SL breeding male from Five Mile Creek, Jefferson County, Alabama. Its fin pigmentation lacks the terminal black bands of N. b. bellus, and it has a more reduced lateral line and cephalic canals. Its status is debated; it was retained as a subspecies, and its taxonomic position is noted as questionable.
- **Intergrades** between N. b. bellus and N. b. alegnotus occur at the Fall Line in the North River and Hurricane Creek systems, and are interpreted as past gene exchange.
- **Notropis atrapiculus** is a new species, allopatric from its close relatives and intermediate between them in most characters. Its fin pigment is distinctive, forming apical blotches on the anal and pelvic fins. Specific rank was chosen because subspecific status would require arbitrary assignment to either relative.
- **Notropis roseipinnis** shows strong east-west clinal variation in morphometrics and fin pigmentation. Subspecific division was judged arbitrary and obscuring of the clines.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males of N. b. bellus were the larger sex in 57 of 73 collections (Chi-square 23.0, P much less than 0.005). Males of N. roseipinnis were larger in 91 of 112 collections (Chi-square 43.75). N. atrapiculus showed no significant size dimorphism (22 of 37 collections, Chi-square 1.32).
- **Cephalic lateral line reduction** is strongest in N. b. alegnotus, where 52% of specimens had reduced supratemporal pores. In N. b. bellus, 6% of specimens showed this condition.
- **Anterior basidorsal spot** is present only in N. ardens and N. umbratilis, and was lost independently several times within Lythrurus.
- **Evolutionary hypotheses:** Five alternative hypotheses on the origin of N. atrapiculus and N. roseipinnis were proposed. N. b. bellus is regarded as the least derived member of the roseipinnis complex. The most primitive N. roseipinnis populations are in the Mississippi Valley, which the author found difficult to reconcile with a single model.
- **Biogeography:** The Mississippi Valley populations of N. roseipinnis (Bayou Pierre, Big Black, Yazoo) are hypothesized to have entered via stream capture or lowland transfer through the Lake Pontchartrain area of the Mississippi delta.
- **Taxonomic conclusion:** N. b. bellus and N. roseipinnis are valid biological species, given strong differentiation and apparently no gene exchange where ranges meet in the lower Mobile Bay basin.
