# Refinement of Aquatic Macrohabitat Definition in the Upper Verde River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-macrohabitat

## Executive Summary

Sponholtz and Rinne evaluate whether more intensive, quantitative physical sampling can improve the definition of aquatic macrohabitats (riffles, runs, pools, glides) in the upper [[Verde River]], Arizona. The study is part of a [[USDA Forest Service]] project examining how native fishes persist relative to natural hydrology and introduced species. Physical data (depth, velocity, percent gradient, substrate) collected at five of seven permanent study reaches in autumn 1996 were compared with less intensive 1994-1995 data and with subjective, qualitative habitat designations. Classification and regression trees (CART) showed that subjective classification agreed with objective classification only about 45-73% of the time, and that gradient was the most important variable in 1996. The authors conclude that their models are not predictive and that gradient measurement should be standardized and included in habitat classification.

## Background & Description

- **Why macrohabitat classification matters:** Habitat structure governs biotic communities, and abiotic classification helps predict responses to disturbance (Brown and Moyle 1991). Native fishes of the Southwest are threatened by water diversions, dams, and introduced species, and many are formally listed as threatened or endangered (Rinne and Minckley 1991; Minckley and Deacon 1991).
- **Prior approaches:** Harding (1981) used landform subdivisions; Bisson et al. (1991) described riffle and pool types used by salmonids in western Washington; Aadland (1993) grouped fishes into habitat guilds; [[Mesohabitat]] concepts (shallow pool, run) were used by Vadas and colleagues.
- **Study context:** Fish population monitoring on the upper Verde began in spring 1994 with the [[USDA Forest Service]] Rocky Mountain Research Station in Flagstaff. Preliminary qualitative habitat definitions were given by Rinne and Stefferud (1996).
- **Objectives:** (1) compare intensive and less intensive sampling; (2) define macrohabitats from quantitative physical data; (3) test the validity of qualitative habitat definitions. The authors suggest the results could apply to other desert rivers in the Southwest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites:** Seven permanent reaches on the upper Verde River. Only five (Burnt Ranch, FR538, Bear Siding, Perkinsville, and Sycamore Creek) were sampled for physical habitat in 1996.

**Methods summary:**
- 1996 sampling used transects every 3 m, with three equidistant depth and velocity points per transect; four to six transects per habitat type.
- Percent water-surface gradient was measured with a laser survey instrument.
- Substrate was characterized by pebble counts (30 points per reach) following [[Pebble Count Methodology]].
- Velocity was measured at 0.5 of total depth with a [[Marsh-McBirney Current Meter]].
- Data were averaged across transects before analysis; a nested ANOVA and [[Tukey HSD Test]] were applied to log-transformed data.

**Hydrological context:** Base flows at the Paulden gauge range from 0.2 to 3.7 m³/s (average 1.9 m³/s), while the downstream Clarkdale gauge records 1.2 to 11.7 m³/s (average 5.7 m³/s) (Smith et al. 1993). The authors suggest that discharge differences may explain why two glides at different sites have different physical characteristics.

**Habitat descriptions (qualitative, Table 1):**
- *High-gradient riffle (HGR):* excessive surface agitation, high velocity, standing waves, boulder substrate.
- *Low-gradient riffle (LGR):* broad surface agitation, gravel/cobble substrate with boulders.
- *Run:* obvious surface velocity with some agitation; variable depth; mosaic substrate.
- *Glide:* some velocity; generally laminar; sand or gravel substrate.
- *Pool:* little or no surface velocity; deepest habitat relative to neighbours; fine sediments.

## Key Findings

**Depth and velocity by habitat (1994-1996):**
- In 1994 (n = 209 for depth/velocity at five sites in 1995 per the sampling summary), depth differed significantly among sites, habitat types, and transects (p < 0.05). Velocity showed similar results except no significant difference at the transect level.
- Pools and glides did not differ in depth or velocity. High- and low-gradient riffles did not differ in depth but did differ in velocity. Runs were deeper than riffles and faster than pools or glides.
- In 1996, glides, runs, and pools were deeper than riffles, though glides did not differ from pools or runs. All habitat types differed in velocity except runs and low-gradient riffles. Pools had the lowest velocities and gradients; riffles had the highest.
- Sample totals reported: 1,567 individual depth/velocity data points at five sites in 1995; 404 and 502 data points across seven sites in 1994 and 1995.

**Classification accuracy:**
- Subjective classification matched objective definitions in 45.4% of cases in 1994.
- Correct classification by CART was 53% (1996 habitat results) and an overall 72.8% correct in 1996 per the Table 4 summary; models using depth, velocity, and gradient reached 72.8% overall.
- Combining gradient with depth and velocity gave the highest accuracy; gradient alone gave 21.8% correct classification as a single-variable comparison (as reported in the source).
- Glides were most often misclassified as pools; low-gradient riffles were misclassified as runs and high-gradient riffles.

**Key authors' conclusions:**
- Gradient is the only single measurement that clearly defines habitat types, and it should be included in classification.
- Classification trees are not predictive and should be used cautiously; future models should weight errors by their ecological cost (misclassifying a high-gradient riffle as a pool is more serious than as a low-gradient riffle).
- Habitats may be site-specific; more sites and years are needed to test how variables change with stream flow.
- Gradient measurement procedures need standardization to reduce variability.

**Limitations noted:** Only one season of intensive sampling; uneven sample sizes; data log-transformed but not fully normal; the authors caution that the CART models are descriptive only.
