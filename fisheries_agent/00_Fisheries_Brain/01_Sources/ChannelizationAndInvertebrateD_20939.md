# Channelization and Invertebrate Drift in Some Iowa Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ephemeroptera

## Executive Summary

This 1978 study in the *Water Resources Bulletin* by David W. Zimmer and Roger W. Bachmann examines how [[Stream Channelization]] alters stream habitat and [[Invertebrate Drift]] in tributaries of the [[Upper Des Moines River Basin]] in north central Iowa. Eleven study sites (1.6 km reaches) were sampled in 1974 and 1975. Channelized streams had lower [[Sinuosity Index]] values than natural segments, and sinuosity was positively correlated with the variability of water depth and current velocity and with drift density. The authors conclude that channelization has reduced habitat variability and invertebrate drift density and has probably reduced the water stored in channels during low flow.

## Background & Description

Channelization was a central part of drainage and flood control projects across the United States. Earlier fisheries work, including studies on the Little Sioux River, Iowa, linked channelization to losses of pools, brushpiles, coarse substrate, and pool-riffle interspersion. Channelization typically alters [[Channel Morphometry]], especially sinuosity, gradient, and bank vegetation, so morphometric variables were proposed as predictors of its ecological impact.

The study area consisted of gently rolling farmland on the [[Wisconsin Drift Sheet]]. Poorly drained prairie soils were drained in the late 1800s and early 1900s, and many stream reaches were rechanneled to provide tile outlets. Channelization was restricted almost entirely to low-gradient prairie streams, while high-gradient woodland streams near the Des Moines and lower Raccoon rivers were included as unchannelized comparisons. Drainage areas ranged from 373 to 821 km².

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Objectives:** (1) determine effects of channel morphometry on habitat abundance and diversity; (2) evaluate morphometric effects on invertebrate drift density; (3) assess channelization's impact on habitat and drift.

**Methods overview:**
- Sinuosity was calculated as the ratio of channel distance to valley-axis distance from aerial photographs. Gradient was measured over 1 to 2 km as meters of drop per km.
- Habitat (depth, current velocity, width, substrate, pools, riffles, midchannel bars, shading, brushpiles, snags) was measured at low flow in July and August, with discharge of 0.002 to 0.683 m³/s. Ten random cross sections per segment were used.
- Drift was collected with 24-hour cylindrical nets (about 2 m long, 30 cm diameter, 1.2 mm mesh) placed 2 cm below the surface. Two samples per site were taken in each of nine replicates across summers 1974 and 1975. Organisms were identified to family and counted, and weighed after preservation.

**Study sites:** Sites were classified as prairie or woodland. Stations 1, 2, 3, and 7 were channelized, with drainage records placing channelization of stations 1 and 2 in 1914 to 1917, station 3 around 1918, and station 7 in 1956.

## Key Findings

- **Morphometry:** Sinuosity index ranged from 0.95 (a channelized segment shorter than its valley axis) to 1.67. Gradient ranged from 0.17 to 2.18 m/km. Channelized prairie streams had low sinuosity and low gradient.
- **Velocity variability:** Variance of current velocity (as percent of mean, log-transformed) was significantly correlated with sinuosity (r = 0.78, P = 0.05).
- **Depth variability:** Variance of depth between cross sections was significantly correlated with sinuosity (r = 0.62, P = 0.05). The authors suggest more water is stored in meandering reaches during low discharge. Depth variance on its own was not significantly regressed on sinuosity in the depth analysis.
- **Substrate and bedform:** Substrate diversity tracked gradient. Low-gradient sites were mostly sand or sand-muck; high-gradient sites ranged from sand to boulders and bedrock. High-gradient sites had more pools and riffles. Midchannel bar length was not related to gradient or sinuosity.
- **Cover:** Woodland segments had more shading, brushpiles, and snags. Both channelized and natural prairie streams had negligible cover, so channelization probably had little effect on cover in prairie streams.
- **Drift composition:** Ephemeroptera, Hemiptera, and Trichoptera made up more than 60% of drift by numbers at all sites. Drift samples contained 19 to 25 invertebrate families per site.
- **Drift density:** Mean drift weight (r = 0.65) and mean number of organisms per sample (r = 0.72) were both significantly correlated with sinuosity (P = 0.05). Drift density was not related to gradient. Summer drift was greater in sinuous than in straight channels.
- **Family-level correlations:** Caenidae were negatively correlated with gradient (r = −0.764, P = 0.05). Heptageniidae (r = 0.764) and Siphlonuridae (r = 0.886) were positively correlated with sinuosity (P = 0.05).
- **Predictive limits:** Relationships showed considerable scatter, so sinuosity is of limited use for predicting drift in any single segment. Sinuosity measured only channel meanders, not valley meanders.
- **Recovery:** Channelized segments still had very low sinuosity decades later. Published evidence indicates recovery toward natural conditions is slow and variable, depending on original channel design.
- **Conclusions and recommendations:** Channelization reduced habitat variability and drift density in the upper Des Moines tributaries and probably reduced low-flow water storage. Impacts might be reduced by designing artificial channels with some curvature.
