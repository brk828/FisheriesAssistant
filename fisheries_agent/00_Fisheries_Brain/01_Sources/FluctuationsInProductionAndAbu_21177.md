# Fluctuations in Production and Abundance of Commercial Fish Species in the Red Lakes, Minnesota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 study (published 1954 in *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*) analyzes 37 years of commercial catch statistics from the [[Red Lakes]] in northwestern Minnesota, with abundance calculated for 1930–1953. The fishery, operated by Chippewa Indians through a cooperative marketing enterprise, is dominated by [[Walleye]] and [[Yellow Perch]]. The authors find wide fluctuations in abundance with no long-term trend, and conclude that walleye abundance is governed by the strength of individual year classes rather than by fishing effort, brood stock size, competing species, hatchery planting or weather. They recommend management that predicts abundance and adjusts harvest annually, and question the value of the hatcheries and the combined walleye–northern pike quota.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The Red Lakes comprise 275,000 acres in two shallow, roughly oval basins joined by a channel about ¾ mile wide. The upper lake has a maximum depth of 20 feet and mean depth of about 8 feet; the lower lake has a maximum of 35 feet and mean of 18 feet. Shores and much of the bottom are sand, with surrounding bog, sand and glacial till. Waters are moderately alkaline (total alkalinity 120–135 ppm) and lack a thermocline; wind mixing keeps summer temperatures in the lower lake between 62° and 72° F at all levels. The lakes drain via the Red Lake River into the Red River of the North, part of the [[Hudson Bay Drainage]].

**Fish community.** Of 38 fish species in the lakes, the commercially exploited ones are walleye, yellow perch, [[Lake Whitefish]], [[Northern Pike]], goldeye, sheepshead (freshwater drum), black bullhead and white sucker. Lake sturgeon were taken early in the fishery; the last commercial report was in 1921.

**Fishery history.** Commercial fishing began in 1917 as a wartime food measure. A 1921 state–federal contract set a 650,000-pound quota on game fish (walleye and northern pike). In 1929 the [[Red Lake Fishery Association]] was incorporated by the Red Lake Band, which has since conducted the fishery and holds exclusive commercial rights. Regulations changed over time: the 3¾-inch-mesh [[Gill Net Fishing]] standard gave way to 3¼-inch mesh after 1936, the quota was raised to 1,000,000 pounds in 1946, and it was returned to 650,000 pounds after the poor 1950 season. Fishing effort rose from eight nets per fisherman after 1941 (about five nets in the 1930–1940 base period).

**Methods.** Catch and effort records were kept by the state (to 1928) and the Association (from 1929), with 1914–1937 data from federal compilations. Because a quota and variable effort make total production an unreliable index, abundance was expressed as average pounds per standard five-net lift (see [[Catch Per Unit Effort]]). The 1,500-foot overnight set was the standard effort unit. Periods were defined as 1917–1929, the base period 1930–1940, and the late period 1941–1953.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Production trends.** Total annual production ranged from 322,684 pounds (1920) to 1,533,958 pounds (1947). Walleye and yellow perch formed an average of 85 percent of the catch from 1941 onward, reaching 94 percent in some years. Perch rose from a minor species to a close second to walleye, while [[Lake Whitefish]] declined steadily, averaging 79,347 pounds (1917–1929), 61,807 pounds (1930–1940) and 30,997 pounds (1941–1952). Goldeye, abundant early and largely unrecorded before 1927, declined to a negligible catch by 1953.

**Fishing effort and [[Fall Fishing Season]].** The commercial season ran about 90 days from mid-June to mid-September. A fall whitefish-egg season (1924–1947) supported the [[Hatchery Fry Planting]] program at Redby and contributed up to 50.4 percent of effort in 1932. Average summer effort rose from 5,965 lifts (1930–1940) to 10,636 lifts (1941–1953), an increase of about 78 percent, peaking at 248.4 percent of base-period effort in 1943.

**Gear and environment.** Gill nets were set 2–5 miles offshore and lifted each morning, and fish quality suffered from this handling. Walleyes are highly mobile after spawning, and marking studies (Smith, Krefting and Butler, 1952) showed a single homogeneous population across both basins. The authors judged that availability to gear was not appreciably biased by fish behavior, although high water in 1950 may have reduced availability somewhat.

**Age structure.** Age-groups V and VI made up 75–95 percent of the annual walleye catch; age-group VII usually made up less than 10 percent. Mean total lengths of walleyes at capture (August) were 14.0–14.9 inches for age IV and 15.5–18.2 inches for older fish, in a sample set (1949–1953) that reflects growth variation between years.

## Key Findings

**Walleye abundance**
- Catch per five-net lift ranged from 30.3 pounds (1950) to 117.0 pounds (1938), averaging 76.3 pounds over 1930–1953.
- Abundance was low in 1935, 1936, 1943, 1950 and 1951, and extremely high in 1932 and 1938.
- The 1941–1953 average was 13 percent below the base period despite the large increase in effort. No sustained trend developed, and the data suggest, but do not establish, a cycle of about 7 years between minima.

**Effort and abundance**
- Walleye abundance was independent of fishing effort. Summer effort was not significantly correlated with abundance in the same, previous, or two-year-earlier period (for example, one-year lag r = −0.32, p = 0.15).
- Gear competition had no detectable effect: the correlation between total gear and catch was r = −0.07 (p = 0.74). For ten individual seasons, no significant negative correlation appeared, and four were significantly positive.

**Year-class control**
- Abundance was controlled by the strength of individual year classes, with the V group having the greatest influence. The 1950 low coincided with an extremely weak 1945 year class, and the 1952 peak with strong 1946 and 1947 year classes.

**Tested factors with no detectable effect**
- *Brood stock:* no significant correlation between brood-stock abundance and subsequent year-class strength (r = −0.07, p = 0.78; eight ranked year classes r = −0.25, p = 0.50).
- *Hatchery fry planting:* planting of 10.9 to 120.2 million fry per year was uncorrelated with later abundance (r = 0.016 for plantings five years earlier; r = 0.056 for year-class strength).
- *Weather:* spawning-period temperature and precipitation, and May and June conditions, showed no significant correlation with year-class strength.
- *Competing species:* walleye and yellow perch abundance were significantly positively correlated over 1930–1951 (r = 0.70, p = 0.0013; r = 0.538, p = 0.01 including 1952–1953). Northern pike showed no consistent relationship.

**Yellow perch**
- Perch abundance was lowest in 1930, 1941, 1950 and 1951, and highest in 1937–1939, 1944, 1952 and 1953. It fell to 21 percent of the base-period average in 1950 and reached 248 percent in 1953. Fluctuations appeared independent of fishing effort and brood stock.

**Goldeye, whitefish and pike**
- Goldeye reached 1 percent of base-period abundance in 1933, peaked at 257 percent in 1936, and averaged 43 percent of base-period abundance in 1941–1953. The authors warn of possible elimination but note recovery after the earlier 1932–1933 low.
- Whitefish abundance appears to fluctuate about a lower mean, and the fishery shifted toward smaller cull fish after the fall season ended in 1947.
- Northern pike abundance was appreciably higher in recent years, sometimes reaching 266 percent of the base-period average (1949).

**Management conclusions**
- Fluctuations are largely independent of management, except where economic demand set harvest levels.
- Regulations on catch and gear should aim at optimum size at harvest. The combined walleye–northern pike quota is "not biologically sound," since few anglers catch pike and a substantial population of larger fish exceeds what current gear can take.
- Hatchery continuation for walleyes appears "highly questionable," and closing it would probably be beneficial. Reestablishing the fall season was not supported.
- Priorities for management are predicting annual abundance, limiting gear and fishermen to ensure adequate income, and specifying gear that maximizes returns per brood year.
