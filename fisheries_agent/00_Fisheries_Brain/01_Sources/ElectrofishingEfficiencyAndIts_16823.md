# Electrofishing Efficiency and Its Influence on Stock Assessment Strategy

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This workshop paper by [[James B. Reynolds]] of the [[Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] (Columbia, Missouri) summarizes a two-year pilot study of boat electrofishing efficiency in warm water impoundments, focusing on [[Largemouth Bass]]. Efficiency was measured by comparing fish captured by electrofishing against total fish recovered after [[Rotenone]] application. The study found that species, fish size, water temperature, water transparency, cover, and catch-per-unit-effort were the main determinants of efficiency. Electrofishing could not yet be used to predict population size reliably, but it can estimate [[Proportional Stock Density]] (PSD) for largemouth bass, provided sample sizes are adequate. The paper proposes a standardized sampling strategy centred on spring and autumn night-time shoreline sampling.

## Background & Description

Electrofishing is principally used as a capture device. Quantitative uses have been mostly limited to relative abundance estimates, [[Mark-Recapture]] and, less often, [[Depletion Sampling]]. The authors noted that little was quantitatively known about how well electrofishing samples represent the source populations.

The study had three stated goals: (1) quantify electrofishing efficiency in warm water impoundments, particularly for largemouth bass; (2) identify factors affecting efficiency; and (3) develop a sampling strategy with standard procedures for assessing populations and stocks.

A 1976 mail survey (Simpson and Reynolds 1977) indicated that most biologists still used alternating current (AC) from 240 V generators with simple electrode designs, which shaped the recommended procedure. Support came from the [[Bass Research Foundation]] and several state and federal agencies, including the Central States Pond Management Work Group and the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Boat electrofishing was conducted mostly at night with 220–240 V AC, one lap of shoreline per sample, using one or two dip-netters. Meteorological and limnological measurements were taken beforehand. After shocking, rotenone was applied and fish were collected for at least three consecutive days. Efficiency was expressed as the number electrofished divided by the number collected. Because not all fish are ever collected, efficiency values are overestimated, with the greatest bias for small fish. Analyses used linear and multiple regression, with bass analysed in 4.0-inch (10 cm) groups and bluegills in 3.0-inch (7.5 cm) groups.

**Limnological and environmental factors.**
- Water temperature: efficiency for bass and bluegills generally decreased between 70–85 °F (21–29 °C); larger fish at higher temperatures move into deeper water.
- Water transparency: efficiency declined exponentially as transparency increased in both ponds and coves.
- Cover: nearly 90% of bass captures in experimental ponds occurred in sections with tire, stake, or brush shelters, suggesting cover concentrates bass.
- Time and season: daytime efficiency is assumed lower; spring is likely the highest-efficiency season because fish associate with warming inshore waters.
- Weather: not evaluated, though events such as cold fronts or full moons may affect distribution.

## Key Findings

**Species differences.**
- Centrarchids were the most vulnerable group, particularly largemouth bass; crappies were the exception.
- Efficiency was low for channel catfish, bullheads, and golden shiners.
- Mean efficiency in ponds and coves combined was 9.1% for bass versus 0.83% for bluegills.
- In 27 [[Central States Pond Management Work Group]] ponds (Table 1), bluegill was detected in 100% of populations (combined efficiency 0.5%), largemouth bass in 96% (5.6%), and crappie in only 25% (0.3%). Populations were defined by rotenone recovery of at least 30 individuals.

**Size selectivity.** Electrofishing selects strongly for larger fish. Bass efficiency was highest in the 12.0–15.9 inch and over-16.0 inch groups in combined data (about 17.9% and 18.9%), while bluegill efficiency peaked at medium sizes.

**Current type.** In a 1964 pond study, average efficiency for bass was 19.1% with unpulsed direct current (DC) and 13.3% with AC, with similar size selectivity for both.

**Predictive power.** Multiple regression explained only 50% of the variation in efficiency. Catch-per-unit-effort (fish per 100 feet or 30.5 m of shoreline) was the only consistently significant predictor, so a predictive equation for population size was not yet feasible.

**Stock assessment.**
- For largemouth bass, stock size is >8.0 inches (20 cm) and quality size is >12.0 inches (30 cm).
- Reynolds and Babb (in preparation) recommend 40–60% PSD as balanced bass stocks in small impoundments.
- Electrofishing samples give close PSD estimates because efficiency for stock-size bass is similar across size classes.
- A sample of 8–12 stock-size bass estimates PSD within ±10% at the 90% confidence level, for unbalanced stocks (PSD 30–70%).
- The binomial-based formula p ± 1.645√(pq/n) gives the confidence limits; they are widest at 40–60% PSD and narrow as sample size grows.

**Recommended sampling strategy.**
- Spring is the best season; surface temperature should exceed 60 °F (16 °C), sampling soon after dusk.
- Autumn is the second-best season, after shoreline temperatures fall from summer maxima.
- Use DC or pulsed DC where possible, though current type matters less if consistent.
- Record transparency, amperage, weather, and cover.
- One lap of shoreline is the standard unit of effort; partial laps are recorded separately, and larger waters require both shoreline distance and time.
- Use experienced dip-netters who collect all fish.
- Catch-per-unit-effort provides relative abundance; PSD is a valuable management index. Detecting unbalanced stocks needs smaller samples, and the authors argue that correctly identifying poor stock condition matters more than misidentifying a balanced one.
