# Simplified Rotary Fish Screen and Automatic Water Gate

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-engineering

## Executive Summary

This 1957 engineering paper by Marvin J. Whalls, David S. Shetter, and James E. Vondette, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 86, 1956), describes two low-cost devices from the Michigan Department of Conservation. The first is a self-cleaning, gearless, vane-type [[Rotary Fish Screen]] for stream and hatchery raceways. The second is an [[Automatic Water Gate]] that opens during floods to pass water through a [[Gear-Driven Rotary Screen]] and closes when the flow recedes. Both were developed at the [[Hunt Creek Fisheries Experiment Station]] to replace stationary [[Vertical Screen]] units, which clog with debris and disrupt raceway water levels. The paper gives construction details, hydraulic design rules, and field performance data, but no biological data on fish species.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Rotary fish screens have been used at Hunt Creek since 1949 to carry excess water past [[Fish Trap]] units. The original gear-driven screens were designed by J. D. Alexander and resemble the Saunders inner-drive design described by Leitritz (1952a).
- **Problem:** Gear-driven screens work well in the main stream but break down and need high maintenance if run constantly, and they are too heavy to handle without special lifting gear. Vertical screens in raceways clog with leaves, sticks, and other debris, which causes unnatural water-level fluctuations.
- **Prior work cited:** California screen programs (Wales 1948; Wales, Murphey, and Handley 1950; Leitritz 1952a, b), general fish screen reviews (Rounsefell and Everhart 1953), two-way weirs and rotating screens in Michigan (Carbine and Shetter 1945), a spaced-disc screen (John 1954), and a two-way trap (Whalls, Proshek, and Shetter 1955).
- **Acknowledgments:** Engineers Steve Such and Gil Stewart reviewed the screen design, and Max Bradley and William L. Cristanelli prepared the drawings.

### Simplified Rotary Screen Design

- **Operation:** Water flowing over a horizontal plate fills the fixed vanes on the downstream side. The weight of this water creates torque that turns the screen. Deflectors on the plate make the vanes fill before they reach shaft level.
- **Vanes:** Twelve large vanes caused a pulsating motion and jamming, so they were replaced with 16 smaller vanes that pass the same volume of water.
- **Dimensions (Hunt Creek unit):** The revolving drum is 24 inches in diameter and 48 inches long. The outer frame is 51.5 inches long and 31 inches high. A 16-inch fall is needed for end plates with 12-inch radii. The shaft is 0.620-inch solid steel rod, and the bulkhead is concrete, 4 by 4 by 8 feet.
- **Seals:** Front, side, and top seals are heavy-duty inner tubes. The roller seal is metal tubing covered with reinforced rubber hose and lifts on end hinges to pass debris.
- **Cost:** Engineers at the Forest Fire Experiment Station, Roscommon, Michigan, estimated the screen costs one-half as much as a gear-driven screen. Aluminum parts would lower the weight, and the steel model can be handled by two men.

### Automatic Water Gate Design

- **Principle:** The gate is a vertical barrier that pivots on a horizontal shaft. Water pressure increases with depth, so the gate stays closed when the force below the shaft exceeds the force above it. The shaft must sit below the gate's center. A centered shaft would keep the gate closed at all levels.
- **Closing:** Counterweights close the gate as the water recedes. A second cable allows the left counterweight to disengage at 45 degrees when the gate closes.
- **Materials:** Wooden gates need heavier counterweights to offset buoyancy. Welded angle-steel frames reduce buoyancy and weight, and wood with a steel bottom is a cheap option for pilot models.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper concerns hydraulic control at fish traps and raceways, not fish populations or ecology. Its environmental relevance lies in:

- **Flow regulation:** Vertical screens clog and disturb normal flow and water levels in raceways. The rotary screen passes ice, leaves, grass, and small debris without jamming.
- **Flood management:** Before the gate existed, a worker had to remove solid gates by hand during floods, day or night, to keep traps from flooding and fish from being lost. The automatic gate removes the need for that response.
- **Fish retention:** The side shields act as barriers to fish, and the seals and frame fit are meant to prevent fish loss, including when water rises above the upper frame.
- **Hydrologic conditions cited:** Normal main-stream discharge at Hunt Creek is 23.2 cubic feet per second. Flood risks named include melting snow, heavy rain, and beaver dam breakage.
- **Debris limits:** Large debris can wedge between the screen and roller seal and cause jamming. The screen still passes anything that clears the gear-driven screen.

## Key Findings

- **Simplified rotary screen performance:** The full-scale model ran for 814 hours in the Station's raceways and 215 hours in a raceway at the Wolverine Trout Rearing Ponds. Performance was satisfactory at both sites.
- **Operating output:** With the upstream water level about five inches above the shaft, the screen turns at 11 revolutions per minute and passes 720 gallons per minute. Revolutions per minute are stated in the source as "11 revolutions."
- **Design rule:** The torque from water in the downstream vanes must exceed the upstream water-pressure torque plus bearing and seal friction. Otherwise the screen reverses or stops.
- **Cost and portability:** The screen costs about half as much as a gear-driven unit and is light enough for two men to move (steel version).
- **Gate operating levels (Hunt Creek gate, 15 by 44 inches):**
  - Shaft centered 3.5 inches above the gate bottom
  - Normal operating level 5.0 inches above the shaft
  - Opens at 8 inches above the shaft
  - Closes at 1.5 inches above the shaft
- **Force balance at normal level:** The water force below the shaft (106 poundals, on 154 square inches) exceeds the force above it (70 poundals, on 242 square inches), so the gate stays closed. Table 1 gives the calculation for each water depth. The gate opens at about 0.67 foot above the shaft.
- **Design trade-offs:** A shallow gate suits small allowable water-level fluctuations. A deep gate suits larger fluctuations and higher flows. Keeping the counterweights minimal and using self-aligning bearings are key to reliable closing.
- **Limits:** The rotary screen is not a substitute for large-capacity, gear-driven screens. The authors note that a pilot gate should be built in wood and steel before a permanent steel gate is installed.
