# Development of Techniques and Methodology for the Laboratory Culture of Striped Bass

#ichthyology #marine-biology #morone-saxatilis

## Executive Summary

This EPA project summary, issued in July 1982 by the [[Environmental Protection Agency]] (Environmental Research Laboratory, Narragansett, Rhode Island), describes the development of a laboratory culture protocol for [[Striped Bass]] (*[[Morone Saxatilis]]*). The goal was a reliable supply of test organisms for water-quality and hazard assessment research, particularly [[Toxicological Testing]] of waterborne pollutants. The authors were Bruce A. Rogers, Deborah T. Westin, and Saul B. Saila of the University of Rhode Island.

The team defined four life stages (egg, larva, juvenile, adult) and, for each, specified upper and lower lethal limits and optimal ranges for temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, light, turbidity, diet, and density. A step-by-step culture procedure was written for research personnel. All life stages were maintained in the laboratory, and eggs were repeatedly reared through to the juvenile stage. Captive adults were kept for five years, but the team could not induce spawning in the laboratory.

## Background & Description

Striped bass is described as an important commercial and recreational species, with its distribution centered between the [[Hudson River]] and [[Chesapeake Bay]]. Adults migrate along the coast, and some populations ascend major rivers to spawn. The document notes that the species tolerates a wide range of conditions and is exposed to heavy pollution and habitat alteration, yet the Atlantic population had long remained abundant.

Its eurythermal and euryhaline life stages make it a useful model for toxicant studies. Because it is relatively easy to culture, striped bass has been raised in hatcheries for stocking lakes, reservoirs, and impoundments, mainly in freshwater. Earlier work had examined the culture of the species, but no reliable, complete laboratory culture protocol existed for maintaining all life stages. The authors therefore aimed to supply one, so that researchers could better study the effects of pollutants on this resilient but vulnerable species.

Developmental stages were characterized by age, length, and morphology, drawing on earlier studies (including [[Mansueti]], [[Pearson]], and [[Doroshev]]). Stages were reared at about 17 °C unless stated otherwise, and metamorphosis was documented at about 18 °C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key environmental parameters defined for each life stage (summarized from the authors' Table 3):

- **Temperature:** Larvae were optimally reared at about 16–20 °C, and postlarvae and juveniles at roughly 18–22 °C. Water temperature should stay within 1 °C among tanks.
- **Salinity:** Low salinity was used for early larvae to reduce disease risk, while adults were held in seawater. The authors judged fresh water acceptable for hatching and early larval stages, though some salinity reduction was needed.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Kept at air saturation at all stages.
- **Light:** Natural photoperiod, with low light intensity for larval and early juvenile stages.
- **Turbidity:** Kept low (below 100 mg/L for postlarvae).
- **Density:** Larvae were stocked at about 100 per liter in egg incubation, with prolarval densities of about 50–75 per liter and fewer per liter for later stages.
- **Water quality:** Temperature, dissolved oxygen, and salinity were to be checked daily and held at optimum levels.

Culture was designed to be done in fresh or low-salinity water wherever possible, which lowers costs and limits disease. Foods were matched to life stage: rotifers and newly hatched brine shrimp (*[[Artemia Salina]]* nauplii) for larvae, progressing to ground squid, prepared diets, and commercial trout feed for juveniles. Table 4 compares caloric values and lipid/protein content of candidate live foods, including *Artemia* nauplii, *Acartia* species, and *[[Calanus Finmarchicus]]*. *Artemia* nauplii were reported at about 5,800–6,000 calories per gram (ash-free dry weight).

## Key Findings

**Egg requirements and survival**
- For toxicological studies needing 20,000 prolarvae, the authors estimated a minimum of about 40,000 eggs (OCR-garbled figure, read as 40,000). The estimate allows for egg survival that is low in artificial spawning and higher in natural spawning. Antibiotics in rearing water raised survival to 40–50%.
- Eggs may be obtained from artificial spawnings using broodfish held under controlled temperature, salinity, and photoperiod, or from natural spawnings collected by plankton tow or pond net fishing during the February–May spawning season.
- Eggs collected from plankton tows must be separated before transport. Eggs should be moved by pipette or siphon, not dip nets. Transfer water temperature should be checked to avoid shock.

**Larval rearing**
- Larvae should be reared in static tanks prepared following [[Houde]] and [[Hamsey]] (1971), and graded to similar sizes to limit cannibalism.
- Feeding begins about four days after hatching at 10–20% of body weight, offered twice daily.
- Metamorphosis was documented at about 18 °C, with full scale cover and a full complement of meristic characters achieved by roughly 80 days.

**Developmental timeline (Table 1)**
- The table gives stage-by-stage developmental descriptions and lengths (in mm), from fertilization through hatching, yolk absorption, and fin differentiation to metamorphosis. Growth rates under fixed and increasing temperature regimes were compared with earlier studies (Figure 2).

**Juvenile handling**
- Juveniles were obtained by rearing from eggs or larvae, or by seining in spawning rivers. Field-collected fish were kept separate from reared fish to avoid mixing.
- Juveniles were fed frozen brine shrimp, ground squid, prepared diets, or commercial trout feed, with frozen brine shrimp preferred.

**Adults and reproduction**
- Adults of both sexes maintained in captivity for five years became sexually mature, but despite repeated attempts, spawning could not be induced in the laboratory.

**Conclusions**
- All life stages, from egg to adult, were successfully maintained under laboratory conditions.
- Environmental requirements were established empirically, approximated from environmental data, or corroborated from other workers' reports.
- A step-by-step culture methodology was prepared for future workers.
