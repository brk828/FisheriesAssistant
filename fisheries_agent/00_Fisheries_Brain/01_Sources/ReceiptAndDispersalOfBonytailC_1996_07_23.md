## Executive Summary

This document is an internal U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum (Parker Fish and Wildlife Field Office) describing the logistics of receiving and dispersing hatchery-reared **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) into net pens as part of a pen-culture rearing experiment. The memo does not present formal morphometric or ecological research data but is valuable as a primary source documenting husbandry practices, size-class categorization, transport/acclimation methods, and stocking totals for an endangered cyprinid conservation program in the Lower Colorado River Basin. The operation was a coordinated effort among USFWS, Bureau of Land Management, the Colorado River Indian Tribes (CRIT), Arizona State University (ASU), and Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge (NWR) personnel.

## Background & Description

No direct morphological, meristic, or biometric data are reported in this source. Fish were sorted qualitatively into three relative **size categories** for stocking purposes:

- **Large** — 270 individuals
- **Medium** — 432 individuals
- **Small** — 270 individuals (handled last, described as "smallest")

Total fish received and dispersed: **972 individuals** across the three size classes. A subsample of 25–30 fish from each group was preserved as vouchers and returned to **[[Arizona State University]]** for further examination (potentially for morphometric, genetic, or health assessment, though not specified in text).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The **[[Bonytail Chub]]** is a native, federally endangered cyprinid of the **[[Colorado River]]** basin, historically inhabiting large, turbid, swift-flowing mainstem river reaches. This record documents an *ex-situ conservation intervention*: hatchery-reared bonytail were transported and released into **net pen enclosures** within a reservoir/impoundment environment (referred to as "Office Cove," within or near the **[[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]]** on the Lower Colorado River near Parker, AZ) rather than being placed directly into open riverine habitat. This pen-culture approach is a recognized strategy for growing out juvenile endangered cyprinids in protected, predator-free enclosures prior to eventual release into the wild, addressing the species' vulnerability to introduced predatory and competing fishes in the mainstem river/reservoir system.

Key environmental/operational parameters recorded:
- **Truck (hatchery transport) water temperature:** 58 °F
- **Lake (receiving) water temperature:** 89 °F
- **Acclimation blend temperature (cooler mix):** ~80 °F
- Supplemental **oxygen** was introduced into transport coolers during acclimation to reduce stress and mortality during the temperature-equilibration process.

This large temperature differential (31 °F) between hatchery transport water and receiving lake water highlights a significant thermal stress risk during stocking events, mitigated here through gradual mixing/acclimation in coolers rather than direct release.

## Key Findings

- **972 total bonytail chubs** were transported, acclimated, and stocked into net pens in a single operation (23 July 1996): 270 large, 432 medium, 270 small.
- Fish were acclimated in stages: smaller individuals were transferred directly from cubitainers and floated in the cove; larger numbers were moved via dip net into a 150-quart cooler where hatchery and lake water were blended and supplemental oxygen was added.
- Large and medium size classes were processed **simultaneously but kept in separate coolers** to prevent cross-contamination of size categories in case fish escaped nets during handling — an operational detail relevant to future **[[Fish Stocking Protocols]]**.
- Distribution to net pens was conducted via two johnboats, with a continuous rotation system (one boat loading while the other delivered) enabling efficient dispersal; the entire stocking operation was completed in under 2 hours (0800–0945).
- **Mortality was minimal**, estimated at **<0.5%**, indicating the acclimation protocol (gradual temperature blending + supplemental oxygenation) was effective for minimizing transport/handling stress in this endangered species.
- A voucher subsample (25–30 fish per size group) was preserved and sent to **[[Arizona State University]]**, suggesting parallel research/monitoring objectives (e.g., growth, health, or genetic assessment) tied to the pen culture experiment.
- The operation involved multi-agency cooperation: **[[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]**, **[[Bureau of Land Management]]**, **[[Colorado River Indian Tribes]]**, **[[Arizona State University]]**, and **[[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]]** (including a Youth Conservation Corps crew), reflecting the broader collaborative structure typical of **[[Endangered Fish Recovery Programs]]** in the Colorado River Basin.
