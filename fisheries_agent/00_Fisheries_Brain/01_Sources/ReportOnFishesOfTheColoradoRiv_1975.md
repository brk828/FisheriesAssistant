## Executive Summary

This 1975 survey report documents fish fauna along the [[Colorado River]] between [[Lee's Ferry]] and [[Surprise Canyon, Arizona]], conducted by the Museum of Northern Arizona under U.S. National Park Service sponsorship. Twenty-one stations were sampled between July 7–21, 1975, though unusually high water levels and cold water temperatures (47°F–60°F) hampered collection efforts in the main river channel. The survey confirmed the continued presence of six native fish species, including two suckers (family [[Catostomidae]]) and four minnows (family [[Cyprinidae]]), alongside a growing suite of introduced exotic species. A key concern raised is the vulnerability of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and the rarity of the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), both members of the *[[Gila robusta]]* complex, whose juveniles cannot be reliably distinguished from one another in the field. The report emphasizes the ecological importance of the [[Little Colorado River]] as critical spawning habitat and flags concerns about exotic species establishment, particularly *[[Fundulus zebrinus]]* (plains killifish), and habitat loss for *[[Rhinichthys osculus]]* (speckled dace) in [[Tapeats Creek]] due to rainbow trout dominance.

## Background & Description

- **Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*)**: Diagnostic identification relies on a distinctive dorsal hump that does not develop until fish approach adult size. Juveniles are morphologically indistinguishable from other members of the *Gila robusta* complex, complicating field identification. A specimen of ~6 inches total length caught and released at River Mile (RM) 64.5 was tentatively suspected to be this species but could not be confirmed.
- **Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)**: A specimen recorded at the mouth of Havasu Creek (June 1973) measured approximately 320 mm standard length.
- **Gila robusta complex**: Encompasses *G. elegans* (bonytail) and *G. cypha* (humpback chub); preserved specimens from the survey could not be assigned unambiguously to either species due to overlapping juvenile morphology.
- **Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*)**: Multiple genetically/morphologically distinctive populations are known to exist between the Paria River and Spencer Canyon, and separately within the Virgin River basin, though no formal comparative study has been conducted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime**: Water temperatures in the main river channel between Lee's Ferry and Spencer Creek ranged from 47°F to 60°F during the survey — conditions cold enough to preclude native fish spawning in the main channel that season.
- **Spawning habitat**: The warmer tributaries, especially the lower [[Little Colorado River]], serve as the primary spawning grounds for native species, including the suspected breeding population of *Gila cypha* near its confluence with the Colorado River.
- **Range and distribution**:
  - *Catostomus latipinnis* and *Pantosteus discobolus* (suckers) remain common throughout the study area.
  - *Xyrauchen texanus* (razorback sucker) was not recorded, likely due to lack of suitable habitat in this reach, despite its known presence both upstream and downstream in the basin.
  - *Ptychocheilus lucius* historically inhabited the Little Colorado River near Black Falls; post-1920 records are exceedingly rare (single unconfirmed 1973 report from Havasu Creek).
  - *Gila cypha* has been documented sporadically since pre-1942 (holotype from Bright Angel Creek) at sites including Lee's Ferry, Vasey's Paradise, near the Little Colorado River, and the Colorado River mouth; also known from subfossil skull material at [[Stanton's Cave]].
- **Habitat displacement**: *Tapeats Creek*, which yielded *Rhinichthys osculus* in a 1968 collection, showed no dace in 1975 despite thorough sampling — attributed to competitive exclusion or habitat takeover by introduced rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*).
- **Exotic species establishment**: Introduced species recorded include threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*, Surprise Canyon only), rainbow trout, brown trout (*Salmo trutta*), Coho salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*, single 1971 record at RM 194.5), carp, fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*), red shiner (*Notropis lutrensis*), channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*), black bullhead (*Ictalurus melas*), green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*, Surprise Canyon only), and plains killifish (*[[Fundulus zebrinus]]*).
- **Proximity to Lake Mead**: Surprise Canyon (RM 248.6) lies near the upper end of Lake Mead, likely facilitating additional unrecorded exotic species incursions.

## Key Findings

1. **Native fish assemblage remains largely intact** in terms of species presence (six species: two Catostomidae, four Cyprinidae), though relative abundance and reproductive success are threatened by flow and temperature fluctuations in the regulated river.
2. **Humpback Chub conservation status**: Pure breeding stocks were known only from the Yampa River, Colorado, at the time of this report, with a possible additional breeding population in/near the Little Colorado River confluence — underscoring the critical need to protect this tributary from anthropogenic disturbance.
3. **Bonytail (*Gila elegans*) may be rarer than the Humpback Chub** in the Grand Canyon region and was reported to be in active captive propagation at the [[Willow Beach Hatchery]] at the time of writing, alongside efforts to rear humpback chub.
4. **Taxonomic/identification gap**: Lack of a preserved reference series of known-identity juvenile *Gila cypha* limits accurate field identification of young fish, hampering conservation monitoring.
5. **Local extirpation risk**: The apparent disappearance of *Rhinichthys osculus* from Tapeats Creek between 1968 and 1975 raises concern about unquantified genetic/ecological loss, especially given known population-level distinctiveness among dace stocks in the region.
6. **Exotic species spread**: *Fundulus zebrinus* has progressively colonized the Little Colorado River since 1938 and was newly detected at Parashont Wash (RM 198.5) in 1975; this species is documented to have detrimental effects on native fishes elsewhere (Texas), and eradication is considered unlikely.
7. **Overall exotic fish levels** appeared stable relative to a prior 1968 survey (Lee's Ferry to Diamond Creek), though precise abundance data and native-exotic interaction dynamics remain unknown.
8. **Colorado Squawfish** is functionally near-absent from the surveyed reach in modern records (post-1920), with only a single unconfirmed 1973 report, suggesting severe historical decline in this section of its range.
