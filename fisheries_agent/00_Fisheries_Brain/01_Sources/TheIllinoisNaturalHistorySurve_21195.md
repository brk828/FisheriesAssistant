# Illinois Natural History Survey and Its Faunistic Surveys Programme

#faunistics #entomology #ichthyology #natural-history-survey

## Executive Summary

This institutional history describes the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] (INHS), which the author describes as unique among American research agencies for maintaining an active faunistic surveys programme for more than 100 years. The article traces the Survey's origins from the 1858 Illinois Natural History Society through the [[Illinois State Laboratory of Natural History]] to the 1917 consolidation into the Survey. It details the organisation's structure, the leaders who shaped it, and the [[Faunistic Surveys Programme]], whose State reports on the Illinois fauna remain a legal responsibility of the agency.

## Background & Description

### Origins and Early History

- By the mid-19th century, interior regions of the United States, including Illinois, were becoming populated, and natural history societies formed to advance knowledge of local flora, fauna and geology.
- In 1858 the Illinois Natural History Society was formed. Its State charter required the museum it established to be free to citizens and schools of Illinois. Because the part-time scientists could not sustain it unaided, the museum became State property as the [[Illinois Museum of Natural History]] at Normal.
- A 1877 legislative act changed the museum into the State Laboratory of Natural History, which emphasised research over exhibits. A separate Natural History Museum, now the [[Illinois State Museum]], was established in Springfield.
- [[Stephen Alfred Forbes]], who also served as State Entomologist, led the State Laboratory and later became Professor of Zoology and Entomology and Dean of the College of Science at the University of Illinois.

### Establishment of the Survey (1917)

- In 1917 the State Laboratory and the State Entomologist's office were united as the Illinois Natural History Survey. Quarantine administration went to the State Department of Agriculture.
- The same Civil Administrative Code created a State Water Survey and a State Geological Survey. The three scientific Surveys were placed in the State Department of Registration and Education, under a Board of Natural Resources and Conservation.
- The Survey's mandate included natural history research, a survey of the State's natural history (favouring educational and economic subjects), resource investigation, publication of zoology and botany reports, and investigation of insects injurious to agriculture, horticulture and livestock.
- The author attributes the agency's success to its permanent, non-political professional board, its autonomy from the University of Illinois and the Department of Conservation (which it predates), its record of service and its competent leadership. Sister surveys in other States had largely declined or been absorbed.

### Leadership

- Forbes: Museum Curator (1872–1877), State Laboratory Director (1877–1917), State Entomologist (1882–1917), and Survey Chief until his death in 1930.
- [[Theodore H. Frison]], an entomologist, succeeded Forbes and was Chief until his death in 1945.
- [[Harlow B. Mills]] was appointed Chief after an acting period and served until his retirement in 1965.
- [[George Sprugel Jr]], an ecologist, was appointed Chief in 1966.

### Organisation and Facilities

- The Survey shares the Natural Resources Building on the University of Illinois campus at Urbana with the State Geological Survey, and maintains field laboratories in seven other Illinois communities.
- More than 100 full-time technical and scientific staff are divided into an Administrative Services Section and five scientific sections.
- The Administrative Services Section provides logistic, fiscal, editorial and library support. The Survey library held over 26,000 volumes, 10,000 pamphlets and subscriptions to 850 serials.
- The five scientific sections are [[Aquatic Biology Section]], [[Botany and Plant Pathology Section]], [[Economic Entomology Section]], [[Wildlife Research Section]], and [[Faunistic Surveys and Insect Identification Section]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The [[Aquatic Biology Section]] studies the ecology and management of sport and commercial fishes in lakes, ponds, streams and rivers. Its work also covers hybridisation, pollution effects, aquatic plants and invertebrates, and water productivity.
- The [[Economic Entomology Section]] (the largest section) researches insects and other arthropods of economic or health importance, including insecticide chemistry, insect behaviour and ecology, and monitoring of pests and insecticide residues.
- The [[Wildlife Research Section]] examines wild birds and mammals in Illinois' altered environments, including habitat, population fluctuations, disease transmission and the effects of pollution.
- Early surveys emphasised aquatic and insect groups. The Illinois River plankton studies of C. A. Kofoid (1897–1908) are cited as classic work.
- The author notes that repeated faunistic surveys of fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and stoneflies have documented changes in the State's animal populations and habitats over more than a century.
- With rising concern about environmental pollution, demand for the State fauna reports is increasing, because pollution researchers need correct species identifications.

## Key Findings

- **Longevity and uniqueness:** The Survey's faunistic programme has run for more than 100 years, which the author presents as unusual among American research agencies.
- **Historical foundation:** Early Illinois faunal work by Rafinesque, Audubon, Say, Le Sueur, Troost and others preceded the Survey. Forbes and Richardson's *The Fishes of Illinois* (1908) and Ridgway's *The Ornithology of Illinois* (1889) are cited as major monuments.
- **Fish collection:** The [[Fish Collection]] assembled by Forbes and associates between 1872 and 1909 holds an estimated quarter of a million specimens.
- **Insect collection:** The [[Insect Collection]] holds an estimated five million specimens.
- **Programme revitalisation:** Frison's appointment as Systematic Entomologist in 1923 revived the collections and State reporting. His plan used specialists to collect, identify and produce keys and systematic accounts, with attention to variation, distribution, ecology and host associations.
- **Expansion to all animal groups:** In 1947 [[Herbert H. Ross]] broadened the programme to all animal groups, renamed the section Faunistic Surveys and Insect Identification, and took responsibility for all faunistic collections.
- **State reports:** Examples include Frison's *The Stoneflies, or Plecoptera, of Illinois* (1935), Hebard's *The Dermaptera and Orthoptera of Illinois* (1934), Ross's *The Mosquitoes of Illinois* (1947), Smith's *The Amphibians and Reptiles of Illinois* (1961) and Stannard's *The Thrips, or Thysanoptera, of Illinois* (1968). The author notes that State reports typically needed ten or more years from survey inception to publication.
- **Current staffing:** Seven full-time systematists specialise in Coleoptera, Thysanoptera and Acarina, Hymenoptera, Diptera, Lepidoptera, Arachnida, and cold-blooded vertebrates.
- **Methodology:** Standard faunistic treatments include illustrated keys, up-to-date synonymies, descriptions or diagnoses, distributional data and bionomics. Groups poorly known or in need of revision receive priority.
- **Taxonomic revision value:** Repeated surveys and retained voucher specimens allow old species to be re-examined. The author notes that many early "species" proved to be composites of two or more currently recognised species.
- **Strategic value:** The author argues that the programme's support comes from its integration with economic entomology, botany, aquatic biology and wildlife research, which helps secure legislative funding.
