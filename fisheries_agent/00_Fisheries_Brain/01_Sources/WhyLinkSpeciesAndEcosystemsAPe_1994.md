# Why Link Species and Ecosystems? A Perspective from Ecosystem Ecology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ecosystem-ecology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Centrarchidae #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This theoretical paper by Nancy B. Grimm (Arizona State University), presented at the Vth Cary Conference, argues for greater integration between population/community ecology and ecosystem ecology. While not a primary fish-focused study, it uses a well-known case study — the interaction between the algivorous minnow **[[Campostoma anomalum]]**, piscivorous **[[Bass]]**, and stream algae — as a key example of how single species and species interactions can drive ecosystem-level processes (primary productivity, nutrient cycling). The paper also discusses desert stream ecosystems in the southwestern USA, where fish are classified functionally as "megagrazers" alongside snails, and considers how disturbance regimes (floods, drying) modulate the strength of biotic control over ecosystem function.

## Background & Description

The paper is a conceptual/theoretical contribution rather than an empirical fish study. It addresses:

- The historical and conceptual split between **evolutionary/population/community ecology** (focused on distribution and abundance of organisms) and **ecosystem ecology** (focused on processes, fluxes of energy/matter, and "ecosystem services" sensu Ehrlich and Wilson 1991).
- Competing definitions of ecology (Krebs 1985, Begon et al. 1990, Likens 1992), and the argument that ecosystem ecology draws as much from earth sciences (hydrology, geochemistry, geomorphology) as from classical biology.
- Reiners' (1986) three complementary models underlying ecosystem ecology: biogeochemical stoichiometry, energy flow (thermodynamics), and population/community structure.

The central fish-relevant case study is drawn from **Power et al. (1985)**:

- **[[Campostoma anomalum]]** (a cyprinid minnow) is described as an important algal grazer in sunlit, midcontinental North American rivers.
- In small, species-poor streams, Campostoma grazing can control macroalgal abundance and productivity.
- A piscivorous **[[Bass]]** predator feeds on Campostoma in pools where both co-occur (due to redistribution during spates, movement between pools, or experimental introduction).
- When bass are experimentally added to a Campostoma-containing pool, algae are released from grazing pressure and grow rapidly — a classic trophic cascade.
- In larger rivers, this cascading interaction is dampened due to greater spatial heterogeneity.

This example is used to illustrate the "keystone species" concept (Paine 1969, 1980) as applied to ecosystem functioning, and to demonstrate the interaction between species identity, predation, and spatial heterogeneity in determining functional redundancy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat:** Sunlit, midcontinental North American rivers and southwestern USA desert streams — lotic freshwater systems characterized by strong disturbance regimes (flash floods, drying).

**Trophic role of fish in this system:**
- Fish (along with snails) are classified as **"megagrazers"** in desert stream ecosystems, distinguished functionally from macroinvertebrate collector-gatherers.
- Omnivorous fish and invertebrates contribute organic matter (feces) that supports a microbial community associated with fine particulate organic matter (FPOM).
- The broader desert stream food web includes: nitrogen (limiting nutrient), primary producers (diatoms, *Cladophora glomerata*, *Anabaena* sp.), microbial community, macroinvertebrate collector-gatherers, megagrazers (snails/fish), and disturbance (floods/drying) as a controlling factor on successional and spatial context.

**Disturbance regime:** Desert streams are subject to frequent, unpredictable flash floods; organismal life spans are short relative to ecological investigation timeframes, enabling repeated observation of succession. Ecosystem attributes (primary producer biomass, organic matter storage, gross primary production, ecosystem respiration) increase rapidly post-disturbance, often reaching an asymptote, while periphyton community composition continues to shift toward cyanobacterial dominance (nitrogen-fixers).

**Spatial heterogeneity:** In larger rivers, greater spatial heterogeneity dampens the strength of the Campostoma–bass–algae trophic cascade compared to smaller, species-poor streams — illustrating how spatial scale modulates species-level effects on ecosystem function.

## Key Findings

- **Trophic cascade demonstration:** The Campostoma–bass–algae system (Power et al. 1985) is a canonical example showing that (1) a single grazer species can exert strong, ecosystem-level control over primary producer biomass and productivity; (2) predation on that grazer can cascade to release algae from grazing pressure; and (3) the strength of this cascade is spatially contingent (stronger in small, species-poor streams than in larger, heterogeneous rivers).

- **Keystone species concept applied to ecosystems:** Following Paine (1969, 1980), the paper argues that keystone species are those that are the sole members of a trophic, biogeochemical, or structural functional group; Campostoma functions as a keystone grazer in appropriate spatial contexts.

- **Interaction strength over identity:** The author (following Paine 1980) advocates assigning "interaction strength" to species rather than applying the keystone label broadly to groups — emphasizing the *potential* of a species (e.g., Campostoma or bass) to affect ecosystem processes, not merely its abundance.

- **Functional redundancy vs. keystone status:** Redundancy is more likely when functional definitions are broad (e.g., "grazer") and less likely when defined narrowly (e.g., a single N-fixing algal functional group); this framework, illustrated primarily with algal case studies (*Epithemia sorex*, *Calothrix* sp.), is directly analogous to how fish/megagrazer roles could be assessed for redundancy versus uniqueness.

- **Megagrazers as a functional group:** Fish and snails are grouped as "megagrazers" in desert stream ecosystem models, distinct from macroinvertebrate collector-gatherers, reflecting a functional (not taxonomic) approach to classifying consumers' roles in ecosystem nutrient and energy dynamics.

- **Context-dependence of biotic control:** Citing Fisher and Grimm (1991), the paper stresses that biotic interactions (including fish-mediated trophic cascades) are not uniformly important; their influence on ecosystem structure and function varies temporally and spatially with disturbance regime (flood/drying cycles).

- **Broader integrative theme:** Aquatic ecosystems (streams, intertidal zones) have historically achieved better integration of community and ecosystem ecology than terrestrial systems, partly because population boundaries are more easily defined and because attached, visible biota (e.g., periphyton, sessile invertebrates) have driven research emphasis on disturbance and spatial heterogeneity — factors highly relevant to fish-algae-invertebrate interactions in desert and midcontinental streams.
