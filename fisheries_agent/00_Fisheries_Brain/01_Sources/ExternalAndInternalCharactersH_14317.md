# External and Internal Characters, Distribution, Luminescence, and Food of the Dwarf Pelagic Shark *Euprotomicrus bispinatus*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #squalidae

## Executive Summary

This monograph by [[Carl L. Hubbs]], Tamotsu Iwai, and Kiyomatsu Matsubara (Bulletin of the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]], Vol. 10, 1967) consolidates knowledge of the pygmy shark *Euprotomicrus bispinatus*, one of the smallest known sharks. Whereas only 8 specimens were reported before 1951, the authors examined records for 37 specimens, most collected during recent high-seas oceanographic work. The study covers systematics and synonymy, external and internal anatomy (compared with the related [[Cookiecutter Shark]], *Isistius brasiliensis*), photophores and bioluminescence, dentition, reproduction, horizontal and vertical distribution, and stomach contents. The authors conclude that the species undertakes diurnal vertical migrations to bathypelagic depths, feeding chiefly on squids and midwater fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Size:** The largest known specimen measures 265 mm total length and weighs under 70 g. Males mature at about 170 mm; females at 233 mm or less.
- **Body form:** The body is terete, with small gill openings, and the tail is essentially diphycercal, with rounded lobes and a horizontal spinal termination. The authors describe the overall appearance as almost embryonic.
- **Skeleton:** Calcification is very limited, apparently an adaptation for neutral buoyancy. Vertebrae are few, and the upper and lower jaw teeth differ strongly in form.
- **Color:** Ranges from very light brown to brownish black.
- **Denticles:** Dimorphic, with larger denticles variably pitted.
- **Systematics:** Placed in the subfamily Dalatiinae. The genus *Squaliolus* is maintained as distinct after debate with Fowler's earlier synonymy. Numerous synonyms (including *Scymnus bispinatus*, *Scymnus labordii*, and *Euprotomicrus hyalinus*) are listed, and *Euprotomicrus* is treated as a monotypic genus.
- **Morphometrics:** 21 specimens were analyzed for proportions, with more than 1,200 measurements listed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Horizontal distribution.** Occurrences cluster in central water masses (North Pacific Central Water, Indian Central Water, and South Atlantic Central Water). The species avoids coastal waters, the California and Peru currents, the Pacific Equatorial Water wedge, and the Kuroshio. The records are associated with 200 m temperatures of roughly 18–21 °C. Surface temperatures at capture ranged from about 16 to 27 °C. Two strays were taken near Campbell Island and west of Cape Horn, close to the Antarctic Convergence.

**Physical-chemical factors.** Salinity across the range is about 33.0–36.5 per mill. Temperature does not appear to limit range directly. Oxygen depletion in Pacific Equatorial Water may contribute to avoidance.

**Depth.** Of 15 stations with definite depth records, depths ranged from 1,829 to 9,938 m, with nearly all between 3,000 and 6,000 m.

**Trophic context.** The authors link the species' low-nutrient, oligotrophic habitat to its small size, in line with the dwarfism of melamphaid fishes described by Ebeling.

**Associated taxa.** Comparisons are drawn with [[Squaliolus]] and [[Isistius brasiliensis]], which share the midwater dalatiine habit, and with luminescent squaloids such as *Etmopterus lucifer* and *Centroscyllium ritteri*.

## Key Findings

**Morphology and anatomy**
- Visceral anatomy differs strongly from *Isistius*. In *Euprotomicrus*, the stomach is J-shaped, the duodenum forms an inverted U, the valvular intestine has about 13 turns, and the liver is small and two-lobed. In *Isistius*, the liver is enormous and fills nearly the whole body cavity, and the spiral valve has 7 turns.
- Caudal and hemal arches are bulbous, possibly fluid-filled, and largely uncalcified, which the authors link to neutral buoyancy.
- Clasper structure differs in several features (table 8), supporting a long, separate phyletic history.

**Growth and morphometrics**
- The abdomen grows with positive allometry, most strongly in females.
- Caudal and anterior head regions grow with negative allometry.
- Other parts grow isometrically.
- No specific distinction was found, though a slight Atlantic differentiation is suggested.

**Luminescence and photophores**
- Thousands of minute photophores (about 50–90 µm) form a network on the ventral surface, directing light downward.
- Each organ has a single photogenic cell and lacks the iris-like structure typical of *Etmopterus* and *Centroscyllium*. The authors identify this as a second photophore type, suggesting bioluminescence evolved independently at least twice in sharks.
- Structures reported as photophores in *Somniosus* are considered not to be light organs.

**Dentition**
- Upper teeth are needle-pointed and arranged in quincunx.
- Lower teeth form a locked cutting edge replaced as a unit, with 19–23 mandibular teeth (mean 21.36 in 14 specimens).
- Tooth count does not clearly increase with age, contrary to earlier reports.

**Reproduction**
- The species is ovoviviparous, with a constant complement of 4 + 4 = 8 offspring, a rare condition among sharks.
- Embryos of 50–60 mm (and 22–25 mm in the Dickens specimen) closely resemble adults.
- The sex ratio is 16 females to 17 males (33 sexed).
- Males mature at a smaller size than females.

**Vertical migration and food**
- All specimens were caught at the surface, mostly under lights at night.
- Stomach contents from 12 specimens included bathypelagic squids (probably cranchiids), the lightfish *Vinciguerria nimbaria*, the hatchetfish *Argyropelecus* cf. *aculeatus* (which suggests descent to at least 300 m), the lanternfish *Myctophum nitidulum*, and the pomfret juvenile *Collybus drachme*.
- Crustaceans played a minor role.
- Volcanic sand grains in one stomach from the East Pacific Rise suggest occasional bottom contact at great depth.
- Trypanorhynchan cestodes (*Sphyriocephalus tergestinus*) occurred in 6 of the 12 specimens.
- The authors conclude that the species makes regular diurnal vertical migrations and is an active, predacious swimmer attracted to light.

**Conclusion**
Euprotomicrus is treated as a distinct genus and species within Dalatiinae. Its dwarfism is interpreted through several correlated traits, including limited skeletal calcification, few vertebrae and teeth, a diphycercal tail regarded as neotenic, a large head and eyes, few embryos, and reduced clasper differentiation.
