# Humpback Chub Captive Broodstock Program: 1998 Correspondence and Protocols

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of 1998 letters, internal emails, a fax-style memo, and a protocol draft concerning an endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) [[Captive Broodstock]] program. The first broodstock at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (WBNFH) died in July 1998. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) team then planned a second attempt, to be supplied with about 600 wild young-of-year (YOY) fish from [[Grand Canyon]]. The letters raise concerns about hatchery location, the lack of expert consultation, and poor communication. The memo sets out detailed handling, isolation, and reporting protocols for the second attempt.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Humpback Chub]] is a federally endangered cyprinid of the Colorado River system. Its recovery is guided by the [[Humpback Chub Recovery Plan]], which the authors state they support.
- **Related species:** The [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) is described as critically imperiled. Its only reliably known wild population is in [[Lake Mohave]], where a repatriation program is underway.
- **Letter context:** On 30 July 1998, Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University, Department of Biology) wrote to Owen T. Gorman (USFWS, Grand Canyon Fishery Resources Office, Flagstaff). The letter was co-signed by John Haskett, Dom Ciccone, Jennifer Fowler, and a Fish Health specialist at Pinetop, AZ.
- **Internal emails:** Chuck Minckley (WBNFH, 29 July 1998) and Chester R. Figiel, Jr. (WBNFH) discussed the die-off, staff notification, and the need for protocols. A notice from the Willow Beach fishery biologist (7/11/98) reported the loss of the entire broodstock.
- **Protocol memo:** Owen Gorman's memo (17 July 1998) lists the broodstock team and covers collection, transport, isolation, treatment, feeding, records, and transfer.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Mohave:** The reservoir is the only place where wild bonytail can still be reliably found. The authors worry that escaped humpback chub could hybridize with bonytail, which would compromise recovery actions. They recommend the federal facility at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, which the authors describe as renovated and as the Region 2 focal point for these activities.
- **Die-off cause:** The correspondence reports an estimated loss of "5-600" fish at the ponds (the source text is partly garbled). Aerators and a pump were running continuously. Dissolved oxygen was suspected, but the authors said they could not be certain. Disease was also being checked.
- **Source population:** Wild fish were to be collected from the [[Little Colorado River]], about 10 to 11 km upstream of its confluence with the Colorado River in Grand Canyon.
- **Research link:** The 600 YOY fish were also to support contracted research on how temperature affects growth and survival. The authors say this information is needed for managing the [[Thermal Warming]] modification of [[Glen Canyon Dam]].
- **Funding:** The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] offered funds and services for the broodstock effort.

## Key Findings

1. **Hatchery location concern:** The authors recommend against housing humpback chub at WBNFH on Lake Mohave because of the risk of escapement and hybridization with bonytail. They recommend the Dexter facility instead.
2. **Expert consultation:** The authors urge the USFWS to assemble a broad team, including experts and staff from other federal and state agencies. They note that no such group was known to exist for this program.
3. **Communication gaps:** The correspondence and internal emails repeatedly identify poor communication among principals as a major cause of the failure. Revised communication channels are described as a prerequisite for the second transport.
4. **Protocol content (capture):** Fish were to be captured with traps and seines in the [[Little Colorado River]] and held in a large in-stream enclosure. Handling was to minimize stress. A [[MS-222]] anesthetic dosage trial was planned, with doses reported as 20 and 25 (units garbled in the scan).
5. **Protocol content (transport):** Fish were to move in a 100-liter tank at about 20 °C, with a stated density limit. The transport was planned for 30 July 1998, taking roughly two hours (the source value is unclear).
6. **Protocol content (isolation and treatment):** Fish were to be held in 10-gallon aquaria in an [[Isolation Facility]] for at least two weeks. Prophylaxis included 0.3–0.5% [[Sodium Chloride]] and [[Chloramine]]. Praziquantel was to be given in the first week to treat [[Asian Tapeworm]] infection, with calculations verified to prevent over- or double-dosing.
7. **Water quality and monitoring:** [[Water Quality]] parameters (dissolved oxygen, temperature, pH, salinity) were to be recorded four times daily. Daily logs and weekly summaries were required.
8. **Handling and husbandry:** The protocols call for curtained tanks, reduced noise, overhead lighting that follows the fish's diurnal activity, covered tanks to prevent jumping, wet-hand handling, and a 2–3 hour acclimation period when moving fish between tanks of different temperatures. Feeding was to start after 24 hours, with live and artificial foods such as brine shrimp, flake food, krill, and worms.
9. **Reporting timelines:** Fish health problems were to be reported to the [[Pinetop Fish Health Center]] and the Grand Canyon Fishery Resources Office within 24 hours. Mortality reports were due to the Regional Office within 7 days. Fish must be moved to larger tanks no later than 30 days after arrival.
10. **Team and schedule:** The [[Humpback Chub Wild Broodstock Team]] includes Owen Gorman (coordinator), Richard Van Hoosen (lead native fish biologist, resident at WBNFH), Manuel Ulibarri (WBNFH manager), and Jerry Landye (fish health biologist). Assistance from other staff was scheduled through August 1998.
11. **Next steps:** New protocols for handling, feeding, disease treatment, monitoring, and reporting were to be drafted by 1 September 1998. They are to conform with existing USFWS protocols, including the "Recommended Protocols for Conducting Biological Investigations and Establishing a Captive Population of Humpback Chub."
