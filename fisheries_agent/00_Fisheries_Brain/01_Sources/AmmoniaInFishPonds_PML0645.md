# Ammonia Dynamics and Toxicity in Fish Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture

## Executive Summary

This Southern Regional Aquaculture Center publication (SRAC No. 463, January 1992), authored by [[David M. Durborow]] and co-authors (with [[Robert M. Crosby]] and [[Martin W. Brunson]] listed in the header), explains how [[Total Ammonia Nitrogen]] (TAN) builds up in [[Fish Pond Culture]] systems, how the toxic un-ionized fraction is calculated, and how to manage it. Toxicity rises with temperature and pH, and feeding practices are identified as the main lever for prevention.

## Background & Description

- Ammonia is the major end product of [[Protein Metabolism]] in fish. Fish excrete ammonia through their [[Gills]] and in feces.
- Excretion scales with feed input, increasing as feeding rates rise.
- Ammonia also enters ponds through bacterial decomposition of organic matter such as uneaten feed, dead algae and aquatic plants.
- TAN comprises two forms in equilibrium: toxic un-ionized ammonia (NH₃) and nontoxic ionized ammonia (NH₄⁺).
- The toxic fraction increases with both water temperature and pH. Each one-unit pH increase raises the amount of toxic un-ionized ammonia about 10 times.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Toxicity thresholds**
- Short-term lethal levels of un-ionized ammonia (killing fish within a few days) start at about 0.6 mg/L (ppm).
- Chronic exposure at levels as low as 0.06 mg/L (ppm) can cause gill and kidney damage, reduced growth, possible brain malfunction, and reduced oxygen-carrying capacity.

**Natural cycling and seasonal patterns**
- [[Phytoplankton]] (plankton algae) take up ammonia, reducing exposure for fish.
- Ammonia rises in fall and winter because algal populations decline and become less efficient at uptake. Cooler water also slows aerobic bacterial activity, slowing [[Nitrification]], the conversion of ammonia to nitrate (see the nitrogen cycle figure in the source).
- Algal die-offs can cause very high ammonia concentrations, but the accompanying drop in pH lowers the toxic fraction.

**Management options**
- Pumping fresh water is impractical and uneconomical for whole-pond treatment, though it can create a small refuge near the inflow.
- Maintaining high dissolved oxygen through [[Aeration]] slightly reduces the toxicity of un-ionized ammonia and can increase aerobic bacterial conversion of TAN.
- Temporarily reducing feeding rates is recommended until TAN reaches acceptable levels.

## Key Findings

- **Calculating toxic ammonia:** Measure TAN with a water quality test kit, look up the toxic fraction for the pond's pH and temperature in the source table (calculated from Emerson et al. 1975), and multiply. Worked example: pH 8.6, 30 °C, TAN 3 mg/L gives a fraction of 0.2422 and about 0.73 mg/L toxic un-ionized ammonia.
- **Table of fractions:** The source provides a lookup table of toxic (un-ionized) ammonia fractions across pH values roughly 6 to 10.2 and temperatures from 6 to 30 °C. Values were derived from [[Aqueous Ammonia Equilibrium]] calculations (Emerson et al. 1975, *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, 32:2379–2383).
- **Prevention is preferred:** Lower feeding rates and good feeding practices are the most effective means of keeping TAN low. Problems are expected when feeding exceeds 100 pounds per acre per day or when excessive feed waste occurs. Fish should consume the feed offered.
- **Economic link:** Feed is a major share of production costs, so efficient feeding also has economic value.
- **Stocking constraint:** As pond and tank stocking densities rise, organic and nitrogen loading becomes a major concern. Most ponds have a finite capacity to manage nitrogen and organic loading, which may become a limiting factor for stocking and production unless more efficient management methods are developed. [[Recirculating Aquaculture Systems]] may handle higher loads better.
