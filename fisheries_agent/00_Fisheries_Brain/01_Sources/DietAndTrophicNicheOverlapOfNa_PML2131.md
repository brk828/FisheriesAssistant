# Diet and Trophic Niche Overlap of Native and Nonnative Fishes in the Gila River, USA

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae #salmonidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study characterizes trophic linkages between [[Native Fishes]] and [[Nonnative Predators]] in the upper [[Gila River]] basin of southwest New Mexico, one of the few unimpounded drainage basins west of the Continental Divide. It combines [[Diet Analysis]] (gut contents) with [[Stable Isotope Analysis]] (δ13C and δ15N of dorsal muscle) across six sites sampled in June–July 2007 and 2008. Both native and nonnative fishes fed at multiple trophic levels. Adult nonnative [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Yellow Bullhead]] and two trout species occupied significantly higher trophic positions than native fishes, which lengthened community food chains. Predation on juvenile native fishes is a plausible threat to persistence, but high omnivory suggests impacts may be moderated by environmental variability.

## Background & Description

Negative interactions between native and nonnative fishes are common in aquatic systems. They include competitive displacement and predation, and they can alter food webs through [[Trophic Cascade]] effects. The upper Gila basin has relatively natural flow and little physical habitat modification, yet native fish persistence has declined because of established nonnative species and natural drought cycles. Earlier work by [[Propst et al. 2008]] inferred negative trophic interactions from correlative evidence only. This study tested those interactions directly by examining shared resource use.

The three objectives were to:
1. Characterize food webs in six reaches varying in nonnative predator abundance.
2. Quantify resource overlap among native and nonnative fishes.
3. Quantify the effects of nonnative predators on native food webs.

**Study area.** Sites were located on the West, Middle and East forks of the upper Gila River, the Heart Bar reach below their confluence, the [[San Francisco River]], and the mainstem Gila near Cliff, New Mexico. Catchment areas ranged from 295 to 4,828 km². Upper forks flow through canyons and largely federal lands, including the Gila and Aldo Leopold Wildernesses. The San Francisco and mainstem sites are more influenced by irrigation diversion and livestock grazing.

**Species.** Native species included Headwater Chub (*Gila nigra*), two suckers ([[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]]), and several small-bodied cyprinids. Among these, Spikedace and Loach Minnow are federally listed as threatened, and Spikedace is also listed as endangered by New Mexico. Seven native and nine nonnative species were analysed. Nonnative species included Red Shiner, Western Mosquitofish, Yellow Bullhead, Channel Catfish, Flathead Catfish, Rainbow Trout, Brown Trout, Green Sunfish and Smallmouth Bass.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods overview.**
- Fish, invertebrates and basal sources were collected by seining and electrofishing from pool and riffle macrohabitats.
- Large-bodied fishes (>100 mm total length) were divided into juvenile, sub-adult and adult classes using length-frequency histograms. Small-bodied fishes (<100 mm) were grouped together.
- Gut contents were analysed by percent area, which was assumed to be proportional to dry weight. This assumption was supported by a log–log regression across 148 individuals (slope = 0.033, r² = 0.410, P < 0.001).
- Dorsal muscle was used for isotopes because it integrates diet over weeks to months. Lipid correction followed [[Post et al. 2007]].
- Trophic position (TP) was calculated two ways: from diet, weighting prey trophic levels by proportional volume, and from δ15N, using a baseline of ephemeropteran larvae and a trophic enrichment factor of 3.4‰.
- Analyses included [[Principal Coordinates Analysis]], [[Discriminant Function Analysis]], MANCOVA, constrained analysis of principal coordinates against predator density, and [[IsoSource]] mixing models.

**Environmental drivers.** Nonnative predator density varied among sites and is linked to stream size, elevation and water temperature. Middle Fork (warmer) supported Smallmouth Bass and Yellow Bullhead, whereas the colder West Fork supported more trout. Speckled Dace was most common at high-elevation narrow sites, and was replaced by Loach Minnow downstream at Riverside.

## Key Findings

**Sample sizes.** Diets were analysed for 996 individuals of 7 native and 9 nonnative species across six sites. Tissue samples from 787 fishes were analysed for isotopes.

**Food web structure.**
- Native small-bodied fishes were mainly insectivorous. Ephemeropteran larvae made up the largest volume (12.8–53.8% per species/age-class), while chironomid and simuliid larvae were the most frequent items.
- Adult Sonora and Desert suckers were omnivores, consuming algae and detritus (16.0–74.0% by volume) as well as invertebrates in low volume.
- Headwater Chub was the only native species found to be piscivorous. Fish occurred in 18.0% of adult guts and 27.0% of sub-adult guts, while algae made up 46.8% of adult volume.
- Nonnative species consumed a greater diversity of invertebrates and more fish than native species. On average, fish made up 25% of adult nonnative diets. This varied widely among species: Yellow Bullhead 12%, Channel Catfish 6%, Green Sunfish 31%, Smallmouth Bass 23%, Rainbow Trout 87%, Flathead Catfish 84%, and Brown Trout 10%.
- Of the fish prey, 64% were suckers and 6% were minnows.
- Smallmouth Bass had the highest proportion of empty guts (44%), followed by Yellow Bullhead (22%).

**Stable isotopes.**
- Fish δ15N ranged from roughly 11.2‰ to 14.5‰ in nonnative adults and sub-adults, which was more enriched than native fishes. Basal resources were about 3‰ more depleted than macroinvertebrates, and macroinvertebrates were about 3‰ more depleted than fishes.
- Riffle-dwelling fishes (Speckled Dace and Loach Minnow) had the most depleted δ13C values (−27.4‰ to −24.1‰).
- Single-celled algae (mean δ13C −24.3‰) were most closely aligned with fish, suggesting they are a major carbon source.

**Trophic position concordance.**
- Diet- and isotope-based TP were significantly related (r² = 0.49, P < 0.001). The slope was 0.54 (95% CI 0.39–0.78), so isotope-based estimates were generally higher than diet-based ones for low-trophic-level fishes.
- Removing Western Mosquitofish and juvenile Headwater Chub, whose diets were dominated by detritus and algae, increased the explanatory power to r² = 0.62.
- Both methods agreed that adult and sub-adult nonnative predators occupied higher trophic positions than native species.

**Resource overlap.**
- Discriminant function analysis classified 61% of species/age-classes correctly across four size-groups. Nonnative large-bodied fishes were most accurately classified (74%) and were the most distinct group. Native small-bodied fishes were classified with 70% accuracy.
- Native large-bodied fishes had the lowest trophic positions. Nonnative large-bodied fishes had the highest δ15N (estimated marginal mean 10.4‰) and were significantly more enriched than native large-bodied (8.1‰) and native small-bodied (9.4‰) fishes.
- Diet and isotope overlap was high among native large-bodied, native small-bodied and nonnative small-bodied fishes.

**Effects of nonnative predators on native food webs.**
- Constrained analysis of principal coordinates found that predator density was not associated with native diet (pseudo-F = 1.02, P = 0.42).
- Presence or absence of nonnative predators did not change native δ13C or δ15N ranges, except where trout were present. There, native trophic position was higher, the opposite of the predicted reduction.
- Correlations between native trophic position and predator density ranged from −0.59 to −0.05 but were not significant (all P > 0.1).
- IsoSource models were highly variable. They did not support a constrained energy source for nonnative predators and gave inconsistent prey contributions among sites (for example, predaceous invertebrates were important for Yellow Bullhead at Middle Fork).

**Ontogenetic shifts.** Suckers shifted from higher trophic positions as juveniles, feeding mainly on insects, to lower positions as adults, feeding more on algae and detritus. Nonnative large-bodied fishes started low as juveniles and rose in trophic position with size. Adults were piscivorous and also fed on predaceous invertebrates. Juvenile nonnatives therefore overlapped with native fishes, while sub-adults and adults could prey on small native fishes.

**Conclusions and conservation implications.**
- Nonnative apex predators in this basin prey on native fishes and use predaceous invertebrates that native species do not, which may reduce competition but leaves indirect effects uncertain.
- Juvenile and age-0 suckers were the main fish prey, yet mixing models suggested they contributed minimally to nonnative diets at most sites. Predation may therefore be underestimated by gut contents alone.
- Native fishes have persisted with nonnatives for decades, but the coexistence may be fragile. The authors suggest this may reflect a threshold that has not yet been crossed, and that [[Nonnative Species Removal]] may be needed for conservation in these habitats.
- The concordance between diet and isotope methods supports using stable isotopes to estimate trophic dynamics in this system.
