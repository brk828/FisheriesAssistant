# Raising Bait Fish and Crayfish in New York Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 extension bulletin by John (J. L.) Forney, published by the [[New York State College of Agriculture]] at [[Cornell University]], describes how to plan, build, stock, manage and harvest ponds for commercial production of bait fish and crayfish. It was prompted by a sharp rise in angling demand that natural lakes and streams could no longer supply. Tests in 90 ponds showed that [[Golden Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[White Sucker]] can be raised successfully in properly designed New York ponds. The bulletin covers site selection, pond and hatchery design, species-specific rearing, fertilization and feeding, brood-stock and egg handling, harvest and grading, transport, [[Crayfish Culture]], rotenone use, legal requirements and market economics.

## Background & Description

The bulletin was produced by the [[New York State Conservation Department]] in cooperation with Cornell University, with funding from Dingell-Johnson (Federal Aid in Fish Restoration) Project F-4-R. Its main audience is pond owners and bait dealers.

Two pond types are distinguished:
- **Farm bait ponds**: usually filled by surface runoff; relatively cheap to build; many have produced profitable crops.
- **Hatchery (bait) ponds**: a compact group of ponds with a permanent water supply, which can be run more intensively and profitably than a single farm pond.

Ideal farm pond sites have broad draws with slopes of 2 to 8 feet per 100, subsoil with enough clay to hold water, a drainage area of 5 to 10 acres per acre of pond, and an adequate but not excessive water supply. Ponds of about 1/4 to 1/2 acre are most desirable, and dams and drains are designed around them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water supply and temperature**: Springs are an excellent source, but large spring flows lower water temperature and slow fish growth. Turbid runoff with silt and clay shortens pond life and reduces productivity; a vegetated drainage area reduces erosion.
- **Oxygen**: Winter kill is the main risk. Ponds fed by runoff or small springs should be at least 6 feet deep over half the area; dugout ponds are advised to reach 10 feet. Feeding and fertilization must stop before critical oxygen conditions develop, particularly in cloudy weather.
- **Vegetation and plankton**: Fertilizers increase plankton, which feeds minnows. Dense filamentous algae and weed beds block this, so they must be treated first. Shallow water favours cattails and other undesirable plants.
- **Clay turbidity**: Clay particles reduce light for plants. Turbid ponds can be cleared with manure.
- **Undesirable fish and predators**: Game or pan fish greatly reduce minnow yield. Wild fish entering from streams can seriously reduce production.
- **Pests**: Aquatic insects can kill fry; a kerosene and diesel oil spray (10 gallons per acre) is recommended before stocking. Muskrats burrow into dikes built too low.
- **Fungus**: Dead eggs can be infected with *Saprolegnia*; eggs can be treated with malachite green (1:20,000 for one minute).

## Key Findings

**Pond planning and construction**
- Hatchery ponds of 1/4 to 1/2 acre are ideal; a maximum depth of six feet is adequate if a small flow of water can be maintained through winter.
- Dikes are built with 1-to-1 or 2-to-1 slopes; a dam at the deep end should be 10 to 12 feet wide for use as a roadway.
- A combined [[Standpipe and Drain]] keeps the water level and allows full drainage. A catch basin 15 feet square and 1 foot deep suits a 1/4-acre pond.
- Supply intakes should drop water 12 to 18 inches onto a 24 to 30 mesh bronze screen set at about 5 per cent slope, which is self-cleaning.
- A small storage pond can supply a series of bait ponds; if runoff is the only source, storage should hold twice the volume of all bait ponds.

**Golden shiner**
- Spawns readily in ponds, reaches bass size (2 3/4 to 3 3/4 inches) in 6 to 12 months; stocked at 200 to 400 adults per acre, production is 2 to 4 times greater in fertilized ponds.
- Spawning begins in May at 68 to 70°F and runs to late August, with eggs attached to filamentous algae or pond weeds; straw mats can serve as spawning sites.
- In a typical fertilized pond, 10% of the young reach bass size, 40% perch size (2 to 2 1/2 inches).
- Biennial drain-and-restock increases bass-size shiners about 7-fold between years one and two; selective harvest yields 20,000 to 30,000 bass-size shiners per acre each summer.
- Restocking young at 45,000 per acre (fall) or 35,000 per acre (spring) can yield about 30,000 bass-size shiners in late summer; soybean-meal feeding has produced up to 60,000 per acre (800 pounds).
- Feeding should be delayed until the second year to avoid excess reproduction.

**White sucker**
- Reared for 4 to 6 inch bait for pike and muskellunge; suckers rarely spawn in ponds, so they must be stocked as eggs or fry collected in streams between mid-April and mid-June.
- Eggs run 30,000 to 35,000 per quart. About 30% of fry survive; stock 50,000 to 100,000 fry per acre (or 100,000 to 200,000 with supplemental feed); about 35% of eyed eggs fail to hatch.
- Fertilized ponds yield 200 to 500 pounds of suckers in the first summer; soybean-meal feeding (10 to 20 pounds per acre per day) yields 500 to 900 pounds.
- Feeding should be limited to about 5 pounds per acre per day in a conventional farm pond; overwintering suckers should not be fed from late October to late March.

**Fathead minnow**
- Tough and hardy; suitable for summer perch-size bait. Stocked in April or May at 500 to 700 adults per acre, with equal sexes.
- Spawns on boards or rocks guarded by males; eggs hatch in 4 to 6 days.
- Saleable size is 1.8 inches. Fertilized ponds produce 40,000 to 60,000 perch-size fatheads per acre in the second and later years; restocked fish at 50,000 to 75,000 per acre survive at 70 to 80%.
- Fatheads can tolerate higher fertilization than shiners or suckers; up to 10 pounds of soybean meal per acre per day has been fed without dangerous oxygen loss.

**Harvest and handling**
- Draining is the only full-harvest method; a 75-foot bag seine (8 feet deep) captures 70 to 95% of saleable minnows in four hauls in ponds under 1/4 acre.
- Golden shiners should not be handled above 75°F; minnows should be transported at 65°F or less, with oxygen through perforated tubing at 1/2 to 1 1/2 pounds of fish per gallon.
- Mechanical graders use 10/32-inch bar spacing for shiners, 9/32-inch for fatheads and 15/32-inch for suckers.
- Six pounds of minnows equal one gallon of bait; weighing in a half-filled bucket is preferred to reduce injury.

**Fertilization and feeding**
- Fertilized ponds produced greater weights and numbers than unfertilized ponds. In ten fertilized ponds over two years, wholesale value was $648 per acre versus $173 per acre in three unfertilized ponds; fertilizer cost about $60 per acre.
- A 6-12-6 fertilizer is recommended at 200 pounds per acre, every two weeks from late April until water turns green or brown, and until mid-September.
- Feeding must stop in shallow farm ponds a month early. Hatchery ponds should be flushed when transparency falls below 8 to 10 inches.

**Crayfish**
- Eight crayfish species occur in New York; the grass or paper-shell crayfish (*Orconectes immunis*) is the only species recommended for pond culture.
- Stocking with 600 to 1,000 mature crayfish per acre in fall; spring stocking uses 300 to 500 berried females per acre.
- Young hatch in May and can reach bait size by July; females carry 60 to 300 eggs through winter.
- Yields ranged from 22,500 to 63,100 crayfish per acre (89 to 352 pounds) across four fertilized ponds. Hay plus superphosphate yielded 48,000 crayfish (340 pounds) per acre in one test.
- Crayfish and minnows can be combined, but this complicates seining and game fish strongly reduce crayfish yield.

**Rotenone and regulations**
- Fish may be removed with rotenone only under a permit from a District Fisheries Management Office. Dosage is 3 pounds of 5% derris powder or 1 1/2 quarts of 5% emulsifiable rotenone per acre-foot; ponds should be restocked after 2 to 4 weeks.
- A stocking permit (no charge) is required for all fish or eggs. A net-and-sell-bait license costs 10 cents per lineal foot of net (minimum $1.00).
- Ponds over 1,000,000 gallons, watersheds over one square mile, or dams over 10 feet require a Public Works permit, except for exempt farm ponds. Farm fish ponds up to 10 acres may be licensed under Section 358 of the Conservation Law.

**Economics**
- One acre of minnow ponds costs $1,000 to $1,500 to install; nets and transport equipment cost $20 to several hundred dollars; management needs 20 to 100 hours per acre per year.
- Wholesale prices per 1,000 perch-size minnows range from $5 to $20; bass-size from $15 to $35; 4 to 6 inch pike minnows from $25 to $50. Pond-side sales return about half the wholesale price.
- Pike-size minnows are worth raising only if prices are at least three times those of bass minnows.
- The bulletin recommends bait raising for established retail dealers as a supplement to wild supply, with large-scale wholesale production considered only after small-scale experience.
