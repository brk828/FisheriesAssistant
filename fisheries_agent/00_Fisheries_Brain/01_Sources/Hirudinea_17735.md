# Cave Leeches of the Family Erpobdellidae: Taxonomy, Eye Reduction and Subterranean Distribution

#hirudinea #erpobdellidae #speleobiology

## Executive Summary

This paper by B. Sket, of the Institute of Biology, University of Ljubljana, reviews the [[Hirudinea]] (leeches) that live in caves and other groundwater habitats. It concentrates on the family [[Erpobdellidae]], which contains every known stygobitic (groundwater-adapted) leech and the only troglobitic aquatic leeches. The author argues that the accepted taxonomy of the group is unstable, that cave-leech faunas are poorly studied anatomically, and that no review of cavernicolous leeches exists. The paper combines a taxonomic discussion, a station list of troglobitic and cave-associated taxa, and a case study of the polluted [[Pivka Sinking River]] in the [[Postojna-Planina Cave System]]. It also announces a planned morpho-anatomical study of cave leeches and paired surface populations.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic status.** The author uses the widely accepted taxonomy of the group but notes that it "deserves repeated discussion." The phylogenetic weight of annulation differences within the [[Erpobdella]]–[[Dina]]–[[Trocheta]] complex is questionable, and the monophyly of Trocheta is doubtful. A revision is difficult because earlier work (Sket 1968) found strong racial, and sometimes individual, variability in the genital system within some species. By contrast, the generic status of the North American [[Mooreobdella]] is considered well founded because of a small but striking anatomical difference.

**Eye reduction.** Lack of well-structured cephalic eyes is not rare in Hirudinea. Within Erpobdellidae it is characteristic of most populations of the epigean species *Dina anoculata*. Erpobdellids with reduced eyes in caves develop normal eyes when they live outside caves. Few troglobitic taxa have been examined anatomically, and few have been compared with epigean populations from the same regions. Among the better-studied troglobites, only [[Dina Absoloni]] appears phylogenetically very distant from any epigean taxon.

**Diversity.** Over 500 leech species are known, about 60 of them in Erpobdellidae. New epigean species are still described, even in regions with long hirudinological traditions such as Europe and the USA. The author does not expect a significant increase in troglobitic taxa. Troglobitic species or populations are known so far only from the northeastern Mediterranean (sensu lato), Japan, and Texas, although the family itself is distributed worldwide.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Non-troglobitic species in caves.** The sinking river Pivka is periodically heavily polluted (α-mesosaprobic) where it flows underground, but is almost purified by the end of its roughly 9.5 km subterranean course. Species penetrate the cave to different degrees:

- [[Helobdella Stagnalis]] reaches up to 800 m underground.
- [[Erpobdella Testacea]] penetrates up to 4 km but forms a very dense population only between 0 and 500 m.
- [[Glossiphonia Complanata]] occurs, almost normally pigmented, as scarce specimens between the 7th and 9th km.
- A single specimen of [[Hemiclepsis Marginata]] was found at the 8th km, and may have been carried there by a fish.
- [[Trocheta Bykowskii]] occurs sparsely between the 1st and 9th km. Its population is heterogeneous, with normal pigmentation and some eyes only weakly pigmented.

**Troglophile status.** Trocheta bykowskii is considered a troglophile. It occurs with normal pigmentation in many spring-caves in NW Yugoslavia (Sket 1968) and in large numbers in some underground waters in France (Turquin, in litt.). Other species, such as Erpobdella testacea and [[Haemopis Sanguisuga]], enter hypogean waters much less frequently.

**Regional and station records.** The paper's station list includes about 12 localities across Eurasia and North America, among them:

- Fanagoriiskaja Cave, Western Ciscaucasia (Russia), for an Erpobdella species
- Saruta-dō Cave, Shikoku (Japan), for a Dina species
- Localities near Lovćen Mountain (Montenegro/Kosovo border area), for Dina absoloni and Dina vignai-type material
- Planinska Cave, 35 km SW of Ljubljana, for Trocheta bykowskii
- San Marcos, Texas, for an erpobdellid species
- Stara Planina Mountains (Bulgaria), for Dina material
- Turkish cave sites, for Dina vignai

**Unresolved records.** Several entries are marked as uncertain. One eyeless specimen from Gruzija (Georgia) lacks a description and has an uncertain taxonomic position. A probably new troglobitic Dina species was collected in the Mika Sinović pećina near Ogulin, but its relation to epigean populations is unknown.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomic instability:** The traditional taxonomy of Erpobdellidae, especially the Erpobdella–Dina–Trocheta complex, needs revision. Genital-system variability within species complicates this.
- **Eye reduction is not a reliable troglomorphy marker:** Reduced eyes occur in some cave populations, but normal eyes are restored outside caves. Dina anoculata is a surface species lacking eyes.
- **Limited anatomical knowledge:** Only a few troglobitic leeches have been examined in detail or compared with surface relatives.
- **Dina absoloni is distinctive:** It is the best-studied troglobite and appears phylogenetically distant from epigean taxa.
- **Pollution gradient in caves:** Along the polluted Pivka sinking river, species penetrate to different depths, with Helobdella stagnalis reaching 800 m and Erpobdella testacea reaching 4 km.
- **Trocheta bykowskii is a troglophile:** It is widespread in spring-caves and groundwater, and is not strictly restricted to caves.
- **Global distribution of troglobites:** Known troglobitic leeches occur in the northeastern Mediterranean, Japan and Texas, while the family is found worldwide.
- **Knowledge gap:** No review of cavernicolous leeches exists, and the literature is widely scattered. The author plans a morpho-anatomical study comparing cave leeches with epigean populations from the same localities.
- **Research priorities:** Future work should aim to clarify the taxonomic position of uncertain cave records and resolve the relationships between troglobitic and epigean populations.
