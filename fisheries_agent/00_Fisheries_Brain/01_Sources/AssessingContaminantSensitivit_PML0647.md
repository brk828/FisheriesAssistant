# Acute Toxicity of Five Chemicals to Endangered and Threatened Aquatic Species: Part I

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae #Acipenseridae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Dwyer et al.]] (Archives of Environmental Contamination and Toxicology, 2005, vol. 48, pp. 143–154) tests whether toxicity data from common laboratory species can protect federally and state-listed aquatic species. Three standard test species ([[Fathead Minnow]], [[Sheepshead Minnow]], [[Rainbow Trout]]) and 17 listed or closely related species were exposed in 96-hour static acute tests to five chemicals: [[Carbaryl]], [[Copper]], [[4-Nonylphenol]], [[Pentachlorophenol]] and [[Permethrin]]. No single species was most sensitive to all chemicals. Rainbow trout was equal to or more sensitive than listed species in 81% of comparisons (58 of 70), supporting its use as a surrogate. The authors propose an LC50 adjustment factor of 0.63 and a no- or low-effect factor of about 0.56, both far less conservative than a safety factor of 0.1.

## Background & Description

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) must determine whether chemicals under the [[Federal Insecticide, Fungicide and Rodenticide Act]], the [[Toxic Substances Control Act]] and the [[Clean Water Act]] pose unreasonable risk. The [[Endangered Species Act]] requires managers to estimate potential losses of individuals in listed species. The document reports 612 federally identified threatened or endangered species, 13 federally proposed, and 118 federally identified candidate aquatic or aquatic-dependent species. The TSCA Inventory lists more than 70,000 commercially usable chemicals, so exposure is likely.

Direct toxicity testing of each listed species is often impractical because some species cannot be cultured. The indirect approach uses standard test species under standardized procedures (ASTM 2003), but the authors note concern that these species may not represent listed species. The study covers acute water exposures using early life stages, with mortality as the endpoint. Companion work by [[Besser et al.]] addressed chronic copper and pentachlorophenol tests, and [[Milam et al.]] addressed freshwater mussel glochidia.

Test organisms were received as eyed eggs, young-of-year fish, or boreal toad tadpoles. Most were held in well water (alkalinity 258 mg/L as CaCO3, hardness 286 mg/L as CaCO3, pH 7.8, 18°C). Exposures used reconstituted hard water (alkalinity 110–120 mg/L, hardness 160–180 mg/L as CaCO3), and saltwater tests used natural seawater diluted to 20‰ with deionized water. Static tests used six concentrations in a 60% dilution series, with 15 L test solution in glass jars for freshwater and 3 L for saltwater. Mortality was recorded at 24, 48, 72 and 96 hours. LC50s were calculated by probit analysis, or by moving-average or nonlinear interpolation when probit analysis was inappropriate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The 17 listed or related species were chosen as endangered, threatened or candidate species, as surrogates in a [[USFWS Recovery Plan]] (for [[Pallid Sturgeon]]), or as state-listed species. Taxa represented include Cyprinidae, Cyprinodontidae, Salmonidae, Acipenseridae, Bufonidae, Catostomidae, Percidae and Poeciliidae.

Three factors shaped the results:
- **Solvent effects:** Acetone reduced survival in Atlantic and shovelnose sturgeon controls, so shovelnose sturgeon data with acetone were excluded. Atlantic sturgeon results are interpreted cautiously.
- **Low-concentration mortality:** Fountain darter showed 5–15% mortality at low exposures, which lowered its no-effect factor.
- **Salinity:** Sheepshead minnow and Leon Springs pupfish were more sensitive to pentachlorophenol, probably because of low salinity effects reported in earlier studies.

The authors also cite the [[USEPA Standard Evaluation Procedure]], which requires formal consultation when expected environmental concentrations exceed 1/2 the lowest aquatic acute LC50 (with slope) or 1/20 (without slope).

## Key Findings

- **Test design:** 3 common species and 17 listed or related species were tested with 5 chemicals across different modes of action. Only 5 chemicals were used, all tested under identical conditions.
- **Chemical toxicity:** Permethrin was generally the most toxic chemical at 96 hours, and carbaryl the least toxic. Copper and the two phenolic compounds had similar LC50 ranges.
- **Rainbow trout as surrogate:** Rainbow trout had the lowest summary rank among common species (rank 6) and was the most sensitive common species. Fathead minnow was the most tolerant (rank 16).
- **Listed species sensitivity:** Atlantic sturgeon and shortnose sturgeon were the most sensitive listed species (summary ranks 1 and 2). Apache trout and Lahontan cutthroat trout ranked among the three most sensitive species overall, with fountain darter.
- **Comparison outcome:** Of 70 comparisons with fathead minnow and rainbow trout, rainbow trout was equal to or more sensitive than the listed species in 81% of cases (58 of 70). Forty-four listed species LC50s were below the fathead minnow range (63.7%), and 14 were below the rainbow trout range (19.7%).
- **Magnitude of difference:** The geometric mean factor for listed species with LC50s below rainbow trout was about 0.63. The lowest factor (Atlantic sturgeon with 4-nonylphenol) was about 0.3.
- **No- or low-effect factor:** The overall geometric mean factor was 0.56 (fathead minnow 0.53, sheepshead minnow 0.48, rainbow trout 0.60). For listed species, factors ranged from 0.43 (fountain darter) to 0.64 (shortnose sturgeon).
- **Variability:** Only five 96-hour LC50s fell below a factor of 0.5, and three of these were sturgeon. The authors found less variability than earlier studies reported, probably because tests used identical conditions within two laboratories.
- **Temperature:** Of 44 cases where a listed species was equal to or more sensitive than rainbow trout, 32 (73%) came from species tested above 12°C. Excluding species by temperature could fail to protect warmwater species.
- **Recommendations:** Rainbow trout-based protective measures would often protect listed fish. A factor of 0.63 is recommended for LC50s, with a more conservative factor of 0.46 (1 SD) or 0.33 (2 SD) if needed. Direct testing remains the only way to provide specific protection information for listed species.
