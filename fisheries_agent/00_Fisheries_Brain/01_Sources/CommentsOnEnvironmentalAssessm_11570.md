# Ichthyological Review of Roosevelt Diversion Dam Modifications, Salt River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1 September 1987 letter from a professor of zoology at [[Arizona State University]] to the Project Manager of the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Arizona Project Office). It reviews the draft [[Environmental Assessment]] (EA) for [[Roosevelt Diversion Dam]] modifications for public safety, prepared by SWCA, Inc. for [[Salt River Project]]. The reviewer restricted comments to fishes and fisheries resources and argues that demolishing the dam would remove a barrier to non-native fish, harming native fishes, the reintroduced [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], the [[Southern Bald Eagle]] population, and the recreational catfish fishery. The letter concludes that a Finding of No Significant Impact (FONSI) is inappropriate and recommends further analysis, including an [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 consultation.

## Background & Description

**Document type:** Agency review correspondence with appended references.

**Subject project:** Draft EA, "Environmental Assessment for Roosevelt Diversion Dam Modifications for Public Safety, Gila County, Arizona," circulated by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] on 6 August 1987 for public comment.

**Reviewer's four biological concerns:**
1. Removal of the barrier will allow further passage of introduced (non-native) fishes into the [[Salt River Canyon]], causing further declines of indigenous fish populations.
2. Increased non-native predators will negatively affect experimental introductions of the Federally Endangered Colorado squawfish and the Candidate-for-listing razorback sucker.
3. Fish population changes in the Salt River Canyon may influence nesting [[Southern Bald Eagle]] pairs, including the [[Pinal Creek]] nest, and eventually upstream pairs.
4. Removing the dam will end concentration of non-native piscivorous fishes, reducing angler harvest and further enhancing those fish populations at the expense of native and other sport fishes.

**Species of concern:**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: Federally listed as Endangered (U.S. Department of Interior 1974). Stockings downstream from U.S. Highway 60 and Roosevelt Lake are classed as "Experimental, Non-essential" under 1982 Endangered Species Act amendments.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Candidate for listing; recommended in 1987 for Federal listing as Endangered by the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team.
- [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]]: large, highly piscivorous species supporting a significant angler harvest.

**Authorities cited:** Numerous works by [[Minckley]], [[Miller]], [[Moyle]], [[Meffe]], [[Marsh and Brooks]], and others (full list in the document's references).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Barrier effect of the dam:**
- Bigmouth buffalofish, threadfin shad, redear sunfish, black crappie, and yellow bass have never been recorded in the Salt River upstream from Roosevelt Lake (Minckley 1972).
- Other non-native species reached the river through stocking, transfer to ponds or tributaries, or bait transfer, not necessarily by passing the dam.
- Spring concentrations of non-native fishes below the diversion suggest the structure is effective as a barrier. Detained reproductive adults cannot contribute young upstream.

**Flood dynamics in canyon-bound streams:**
- Native fishes are most persistent in canyon-bound reaches of unregulated streams (Minckley 1985).
- In canyon-bound streams, including the Salt River, major flooding led to declines of non-native fishes and increased relative abundance of natives (Minckley and Meffe, in press).
- Native fishes showed no decline even after floods of 10-year dimensions; non-native fishes did (Meffe 1984, field and laboratory).
- A downstream barrier limits recolonization by non-natives, giving natives time to reproduce and recruit.

**Existing composition:** Non-native fishes already in the Salt River upstream of the diversion are considered harmful to indigenous forms (EA pp. 31-32). Native fishes still make up 40% of the fish in the river, a substantial proportion. The AGF data (EA Appendix E2) were mostly collected with trammel and gill nets, which are biased toward larger non-native species.

**Reintroduction context:** Both Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker formerly inhabited the Salt River and were extirpated after damming, for reasons unknown. Hatchery propagation has been successful (Rinne et al. 1984). Reintroductions in the Salt River basin began in the 1980s under agreements among the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], and other agencies.

**Predation pressure:** Predation by non-native catfishes is a major deterrent to the establishment of stocked razorback suckers (Marsh and Brooks, in prep.). Extensive predation by introduced species was also detected when Colorado squawfish were reintroduced in the [[Verde River]] (Dean A. Hendrickson, AGF, pers. comm.) and elsewhere (Harold M. Tyus, FWS, pers. comm.).

**Wilderness and future management:** Construction of a new downstream barrier is not possible upstream because of the Salt River Canyon's [[Wilderness Status]]. The present dam is therefore likely important to future population manipulation for the reintroduced species.

**Bald Eagle food web:** Eagles feed heavily on large introduced fishes (common carp, channel catfish) but also take natives. Fish concentrations below the dam enhance feeding by the Pinal Creek pair. Fishermen's cleaning offal is also provided as carrion for eagles.

**Flathead catfish fishery:**
- Spring concentrations below dams during floods are characteristic of flathead catfish, and much of the harvest occurs then.
- Reviewer's observation: perhaps more flathead catfish are caught at the diversion in a few spring weeks than elsewhere in Roosevelt Lake for the rest of the year.
- The reach below the dam is likely the most important single harvest point for the species in the system.
- The species has undergone a "biological explosion" in Roosevelt Lake and the lower Salt River and is almost certainly under-exploited. Setlines, the most effective gear in reservoirs, are not yet legal under AGF rules.

## Key Findings

1. **Barrier role:** Roosevelt Diversion Dam acts as an effective barrier to many non-native species, and demolition would allow immediate reinvasion of the canyon after floods plus a continuing influx of migrants.
2. **No direct upstream-passage data:** There are no absolute data showing any species actually invaded the Salt River mainstream by upstream passage over the dam, though some fish may ascend during certain flows.
3. **Flood effects:** Non-native fishes decline after major floods, and natives resist them, including floods of 10-year dimensions. Native populations persist best in canyon-bound reaches.
4. **Native fish status:** Native fishes still make up 40% of the fish in the Salt River upstream from the diversion, but some streams are populated only by introduced forms.
5. **Reintroduction risk:** Non-native predation threatens the Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker reintroductions. The dam may be essential for future population manipulation.
6. **Regulatory concern:** Experimental, Non-essential and Candidate species are subject only to ESA Section 7(a)(4), a non-binding conference procedure. The draft EA (p. 33) notes this, but there is no indication that conference occurred.
7. **Bald Eagle risk:** The EA (p. 34) speculates dam removal "may result in an enhanced foraging base," but the reviewer considers that speculation a sign of potential jeopardy requiring an ESA Section 7 consultation (see also EA Appendix F).
8. **Canyon-wide effects:** Without the barrier, non-native fishes would move farther upstream, populating Gleason Flat, Horseshoe Bend, and the entire canyon reach, so impacts would reach all breeding and wintering Bald Eagles on the Salt River, not only the Pinal Creek pair.
9. **Fishery losses:** Removing the dam would eliminate harvest of all but incidental residents or migrants in the reach and significantly reduce catfish harvest from the Salt River-Roosevelt Lake fishery. Angler removal of large predatory catfish currently reduces predation on native and game fishes.
10. **Quantification gap:** The reviewer did not analyze sport fishery catch statistics, and AGF did not quantify the recreational fishery in its correspondence (EA Appendix E1).
11. **Conclusion:** A FONSI is inappropriate. The EA is inconsistent and superficial, and it places "inordinate and inappropriate emphasis" on expediting demolition rather than on mitigation of environmental problems.
12. **Recommendations:** Preserve the dam; conduct an ESA Section 7 consultation; examine impacts on Bald Eagles and the fishery in depth; consider mitigation; and provide the reviewer with future reports and documentation.
