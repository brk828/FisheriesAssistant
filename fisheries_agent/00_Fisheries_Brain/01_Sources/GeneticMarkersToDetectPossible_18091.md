# Genetic Markers to Detect Possible Hybridization Between Cui-ui and Tahoe Suckers

## Executive Summary
This USFWS-funded study used starch-gel [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] to test for hybridization between the endangered [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*) and the [[Tahoe Sucker]] (*Catostomus tahoensis*) in [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. Individuals previously classified as "presumptive hybrids" (Group B) based on mouth/head morphology were genetically indistinguishable from pure cui-ui (Group A), with a genetic identity (Nei 1978) of 1.000 between the two groups. In contrast, cui-ui and Tahoe sucker showed strong genetic divergence (genetic identity = 0.541), with 25 loci fixed for different alleles and 3 nearly fixed. The study also identified diagnostic markers—particularly **Pgm-1**—capable of distinguishing cui-ui, Tahoe sucker, and true hybrid fry, despite the confounding discovery that several loci undergo ontogenetic phenotype shifts between larval and adult stages in this [[Catostomidae|catostomid]] lineage.

## Background & Description
- **Classification basis:** Field/hatchery classification of cui-ui into Group A (pure) and Group B (presumptive hybrid) relied entirely on **mouth and head morphology**, not genetics.
- **Outcome:** Despite visible morphological divergence between Group A and Group B cui-ui, no corresponding allozyme divergence was detected — genetic identity = 1.000.
- **Interpretation:** The morphological differences likely reflect a **developmental phenomenon** — either phenotypic plasticity or a polymorphism with an undetermined genetic basis (not linked to the diagnostic loci tested). This remains an open question requiring further research.
- **Catostomid genome architecture:** Catostomidae arose ~50 million years ago via a **tetraploidization (whole-genome duplication) event**; all genera retain a conserved karyotype of 2n = 100 (vs. 2n = 50 in most Cypriniformes), and DNA content is double that of typical diploid fishes (Ferris and Whitt 1977). This duplication history underlies many of the fixed allelic differences observed as gene-silencing events between the two species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Location:** Samples were drawn from [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada (cui-ui and hybrids) and [[Lake Tahoe]] (Sand Point, NV; Tahoe suckers), both within the Lahontan drainage system.
- **Conservation context:** Cui-ui is an endangered, lake-dwelling sucker endemic to the Lahontan Basin; hybridization risk with the co-occurring Tahoe sucker is a management concern for species purity and conservation genetics.
- **Life stages sampled:** Adults (liver, muscle, eye tissue) and hatchery-raised fry (whole body minus head/gut) of cui-ui, Tahoe sucker, and true experimental hybrids.
- **Genetic diversity implications:** Extremely low heterozygosity in cui-ui (H = 0.002–0.003) versus average levels in Tahoe sucker (H = 0.061) raises concern about historical population bottlenecks, reduced adaptive capacity, and compromised short-term fitness in the endangered species — an important consideration for [[Cui-ui]] recovery and habitat/water management planning.

## Key Findings
1. **No true hybrids detected in wild samples:** Presumptive cui-ui/Tahoe sucker hybrids (Group B) were genetically pure cui-ui at all 25+ diagnostic loci; genetic identity with Group A cui-ui = 1.000.
2. **Strong species divergence:** Cui-ui and Tahoe sucker are distinguished by **fixed allelic differences at 25 of 67 loci** and near-fixed differences at 3 additional loci; overall genetic identity = 0.541.
3. **Heterozygosity disparity:** Tahoe sucker H = 0.061 (average for fish generally); cui-ui H = 0.002 (Group A) and 0.003 (Group B) — indicative of possible historical genetic bottleneck(s) in cui-ui.
4. **No evidence of reproductive isolation** between cui-ui Groups A and B at the three loci polymorphic within cui-ui (Gpi-4, Idh-2, Mdh-3; G-tests, p > 0.05), though small sample sizes limit power to detect it.
5. **Ontogenetic phenotype shifts:** Four enzyme systems (Ck-3, Idh-1, Mdhp, PepB), diagnostic in adults, showed **identical, non-diagnostic phenotypes in fry** of both species and hybrids — an unexpected complication attributed to developmental changes in gene expression/tissue specificity, consistent with prior findings in other catostomids (Shaklee et al. 1974).
6. **Best fry-stage diagnostic marker — Pgm-1:**
   - Cui-ui fixed for the **180 allele** (180/180).
   - Tahoe sucker polymorphic for **100** (freq. 0.92) and **137** (freq. 0.08) alleles; no 180 allele detected.
   - True hybrids show a clear **heterozygous 100/180 (or 137/180) phenotype**, easily distinguished from either parental homozygote due to large mobility differences.
7. **Secondary fry marker — Mpi:** Tahoe sucker fixed for allele 100; cui-ui fixed for allele 106; hybrids heterozygous (both bands present), though mobility differences are smaller and harder to score reliably than Pgm-1.
8. **General protein stain (GP):** Revealed an additional band present in all fry but absent in adults, reinforcing that ontogenetic isozyme changes are widespread in this system.
9. **Evolutionary interpretation:** Many of the 25 fixed interspecific differences likely arose from **random silencing of duplicated gene copies** following the ancestral catostomid tetraploidization event, while diagnostic loci like Mpi and Pgm-1 likely reflect true structural-gene mutations. Silencing/tissue specificity appears to occur relatively late in development, raising broader questions about catostomid evolutionary genetics.
10. **Management recommendation:** Given the potential mismatch between allozyme heterozygosity and quantitative genetic variation, the authors recommend heritability analyses using captive-reared cui-ui to properly assess adaptive genetic potential, rather than relying on allozyme heterozygosity alone.
