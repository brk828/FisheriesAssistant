# Cyprinid Fishes of the Genus Gila from the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 Master of Science thesis by [[John Norman Rinne]] (Arizona State University) revises the taxonomy of western chubs of the genus [[Gila]] (family [[Cyprinidae]]) in the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. Drawing on roughly 1500 museum specimens, the author combines meristic counts, morphometric ratios, allometry checks, temporal comparisons and drainage-history reasoning. The study recognizes three species: [[Gila Elegans]] (Bonytail chub), [[Gila Intermedia]] (Gila chub, elevated from subspecies rank) and [[Gila Robusta]] (Colorado River chub), the last comprising two subspecies, [[Gila Robusta Robusta]] and [[Gila Robusta Grahami]] (resurrected). The author argues that the forms are essentially allopatric and reflect Cenozoic drainage history rather than [[Ecotypy]] within a single polymorphic species.

## Background & Description

The genus has a long and confused taxonomic history. Local differentiation is the rule in western fishes, and the chubs have been placed under six generic names in the Gila River basin alone, with more than a dozen specific synonyms. Gila elegans was once a subspecies of G. robusta but has been validated as a full species.

Diagnostic characters separate the taxa:

- **G. elegans:** streamlined body, thin caudal peduncle, 10 or more dorsal fin-rays, usually 10 anal fin-rays, and 47-50 total vertebrae.
- **G. intermedia:** chubby, dark body with large thick scales; lateral-line scales almost always fewer than 80; dorsal and anal fin-rays usually 8; 38-45 vertebrae; head/peduncle-depth ratio usually 3.0 or less.
- **G. robusta:** thick but not chubby, lighter coloration, lateral-line scales usually above 80 (grahami as low as 75), dorsal fin-rays usually 9.

Methods followed Hubbs and Lagler (1958). About 1500 specimens were examined. Counts and measurements were taken with fine-pointed dividers, converted to thousandths of standard length, and analysed with ANOVA and grouping tests on the GE 425 at Arizona State University.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat spectrum:** Each form occupies different waters, yet each spans a range from large to small streams, low to high elevation, and swift to semi-lentic conditions. G. intermedia prefers smaller quiet water but persists in the San Carlos River and the Buckeye Canal. G. robusta shows no special habitat preference, provided it is a stream.
- **Fossil Creek and Aravaipa Creek:** Aravaipa Creek (average gradient 7.0 m/km, riffle-dominated) and Fossil Creek (dropping about 500 m in 18 km) show that G. r. grahami occurs in contrasting habitats, which the author cites against an ecotype explanation.
- **Human-driven change:** Arroyo cutting, falling water tables, pumping, and major and minor dams have dried many streams and marshes over roughly 100 years. The fishes have not responded phenotypically.
- **Wet Beaver Creek:** Rotenone treatment in 1962 was followed by a morphological change in 1964-66, which may reflect invasion by Verde River G. r. robusta.
- **Introductions:** The Oak Creek population of G. intermedia is probably introduced, given the heavily managed and fished stream.
- **Geological framework:** The proposed history runs from Miocene and Pliocene drainage integration of the Colorado Plateau through the Bouse Embayment, Mogollon Highlands uplift, and Pleistocene dam breaching in the Salt and Verde systems.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomic revision:** G. intermedia is elevated to species rank. Problematic Gila River populations are recognized as a distinct subspecies of G. robusta, and G. grahami is resurrected as G. robusta grahami.
- **Ecotypy rejected:** Morphological stability over time and space, and habitat distributions inconsistent with intergradation, argue against treating the forms as ecotypes. The author states it is "highly unlikely that morphological stability of ecotypes would prevail if the animals were as plastic as ecotypic responses presumably require."
- **Separating G. intermedia from G. robusta:**
  - 97% of 390 G. intermedia specimens have fewer than 80 lateral-line scales (77% fewer than 75).
  - About 80% of 670 G. robusta specimens have 80 or more lateral-line scales, and 96% have more than 75.
  - Pelvic fin-rays are 9 in more than 95% of G. robusta and 8 or 9 in G. intermedia.
- **Subspecies separation:**
  - G. r. robusta has 80 or more lateral-line scales in 97% of specimens (range of means 83.8-91.3).
  - G. r. grahami has 70-90 scales in 98% of specimens, with 82% between 75 and 85 (range of means 77.0-82.4).
  - 81% of G. r. robusta populations have a head/peduncle-depth ratio above 3.30, against 99% of G. r. grahami below that value.
- **Current distribution:**
  - G. elegans: extinct in the Gila River system and now confined largely to the Colorado above Lake Mead.
  - G. intermedia: endemic to the Gila basin, most abundant in southeastern Arizona.
  - G. r. robusta: Verde mainstream, Salt River above Roosevelt Lake, Bill Williams River.
  - G. r. grahami: Verde tributaries, upper Gila basin, Aravaipa Creek.
- **Four indigenous forms:** The lower basin holds four indigenous forms, or five if the archaeological record of G. cypha from Catclaw Cave is included.
- **Southern origin:** G. intermedia is proposed to derive from a southern stock related to chubs of the Mexican Plateau, including G. ditaenia, G. purpurea, G. nigrescens and G. pandora. A connection to the Guzman basin is not supported by the ichthyofauna.
- **Historical conclusion:** Gila robusta or its progenitor probably occupied the lower basin by the Pliocene. Later Pleistocene dam breaching allowed the larger-river G. r. robusta to invade and displace the isolated G. r. grahami upstream.
