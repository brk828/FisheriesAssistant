# Extensions of Range for Blennioid Fishes in Southern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #blennioidei

## Executive Summary
This 1944 paper by Hubbs and Barnhart, based on specimens acquired by the [[Scripps Institution Of Oceanography]], documents range extensions for three blennioid fishes and one sculpin species along the Southern California coast. Two species — [[Cebidichthys violaceus]] and [[Anarrhichthys ocellatus]] — show **southward range extensions** into deeper water near San Diego, consistent with a pattern of **isothermal distribution**, where cold-water tide-pool species occupy progressively deeper habitats as they occur farther south into warmer latitudes. A third species, [[Ulvicola sanctae-rosae]], previously believed endemic to the Channel Islands, is shown to also inhabit **mainland kelp beds**, undermining the notion of a distinct "Channel Islands fauna." The authors also note that a sculpin, [[Montereya recalva]], previously known south of Point Conception only from Los Coronados Islands, was newly collected at White's Point near San Pedro, further eroding the island-endemism hypothesis. The paper emphasizes how poorly understood the fish fauna of giant kelp beds remained at the time and calls for further ecological study of this habitat.

## Background & Description

### Cebidichthys violaceus (Monkeyface Prickleback / "monkeyfaced blenny eel")
- Elongate, eel-like blenny body form
- Largest of the intertidal "eels" fished in central California
- Specimen from Pt. Loma: juvenile, 68 mm standard length
- Previously recorded adult (San Nicolas Island): 62 cm, caught by hand line

### Ulvicola sanctae-rosae ("kelp-eel")
- Ribbon-shaped body, blenny-type morphology
- Mainland specimens (Pt. Loma): 3 adults, 224–268 mm long
- Additional specimens from Gull Island kelp harvest: 33 individuals, mostly large young to adult
- Found alongside other kelp-associated fishes: pipefish (*Syngnathus californiensis californiensis*), kelp blennies (*Gibbonsia metzi*, *Heterostichus rostratus*)

### Anarrhichthys ocellatus ("wolf-eel")
- Large-bodied blennioid (family Anarhichadidae)
- La Jolla Canyon specimen taken at ~400 feet depth
- Additional mounted adult from Santa Catalina Island (Cabrillo Beach Museum), collected October 1937

### Montereya recalva (sculpin)
- Reef-top pool inhabitant
- Several specimens collected at White's Point, near San Pedro (Aug 2, 1943)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat associations**: 
  - *Cebidichthys violaceus* — typically intertidal/tide-pool and boulder reef habitat in central California; in southern populations appears to shift to deeper subtidal water, avoiding the warmer tidal zone.
  - *Ulvicola sanctae-rosae* — near-surface waters within [[Giant Kelp Beds]]; considered possibly the most common and consistent fish inhabitant of kelp canopy waters; regularly hauled up on kelp-harvesting barges.
  - *Anarrhichthys ocellatus* — found in deeper water southward, including submarine canyon environments (La Jolla Submarine Canyon, ~400 ft).
  - *Montereya recalva* — rocky reef-top tide pools.

- **Isothermal Distribution Principle**: The authors articulate a broader biogeographic pattern wherein temperature acts as the controlling factor for depth distribution — tide-pool species from northern (cooler) latitudes are found progressively deeper in southern (warmer) latitudes, and shallow-water cold-adapted species retreat to depth to maintain equivalent thermal conditions. This is presented as a general principle relevant to [[West Coast Fish Distribution]] patterns.

- **Channel Islands vs. Mainland fauna**: The paper challenges the assumption of a distinctive, isolated Channel Islands fish fauna, showing that several supposedly island-endemic species (*Ulvicola sanctae-rosae*, *Montereya recalva*) also occur on the mainland coast, simply undersampled previously.

- **Kelp bed ecology**: The kelp beds of the Southern California mainland and offshore islands are identified as a major, poorly studied ecological community, with kelp-harvesting operations serendipitously providing access to otherwise hard-to-sample fish fauna.

## Key Findings

1. **Southward extension for *Cebidichthys violaceus***: previously known south to San Nicolas Island / Santa Cruz Island tide pools; now documented from a kelp holdfast off Pt. Loma, San Diego County — first record south of Santa Cruz Island in tide-pool/reef habitat, notably absent from intertidal habitats farther south, implying a habitat depth shift.

2. **Mainland occurrence of *Ulvicola sanctae-rosae***: Previously considered endemic to Santa Rosa Island, Santa Catalina Island, and Guadalupe Island; now confirmed along the mainland (Pt. Loma) and further documented via kelp harvest surveys at Gull Island (Santa Cruz Island) and West Cove (San Clemente Island), suggesting it is a common, previously overlooked kelp-bed resident.

3. **Southward extension for *Anarrhichthys ocellatus***: Previous southern limit was Redondo/Venice (Los Angeles County); new record from La Jolla Submarine Canyon (~400 ft depth, Aug 6, 1941) represents a definitive southward range extension, reinforcing the deeper-water-in-south distributional pattern.

4. **Removal of *Montereya recalva* from "island-only" list**: Previously attributed south of Point Conception only to Los Coronados Islands (per [[Hubbs 1926]]); new mainland record from White's Point (San Pedro) collected August 2, 1943.

5. **Kelp-harvest species survey (Gull Island, Santa Cruz Island, Aug 6 collection, ~170 tons of kelp)**:
   - *Syngnathus californiensis californiensis* (pipefish) — 1 adult (though 3–4 dozen commonly reported per trip)
   - *Gibbonsia metzi* (kelp blenny) — 7 specimens, young to adult
   - *Heterostichus rostratus* (kelp blenny) — 3 specimens, young to adult
   - *Ulvicola sanctae-rosae* — 33 specimens, large young and adults

6. **Methodological note**: Commercial kelp-cutting/harvesting operations are highlighted as an underused but valuable opportunistic sampling method for surveying kelp-bed fish fauna, given the difficulty of direct collection in this habitat.

7. **Broader implication**: The findings collectively argue against the idea of a taxonomically distinct island fauna in southern California and support the isothermal (temperature-depth compensation) model of fish distribution along the [[California Current]] system.
