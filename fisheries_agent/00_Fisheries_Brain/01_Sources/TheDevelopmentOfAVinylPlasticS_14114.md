# Development of a Vinyl Plastic Subcutaneous Tag for Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #rainbow-trout

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper by Robert L. Butler of the [[California Department Of Fish And Game]] describes the development of a [[Subcutaneous Vinyl Tag]] for [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) in the state's [[Catchable Trout Stocking]] program. The work was part of the [[Dingell-Johnson Project]] to evaluate stocking results. Existing methods were rejected: the [[Jaw Tag]] caused tag loss, tissue damage and impaired growth, and the [[Eipper Tag]] showed very high loss. The belly-placed vinyl tag, inserted with a modified surgical scalpel handle under [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] anesthesia, was retained reasonably well when placed longitudinally, and it was readily seen by anglers. Blistering was identified as the main retention problem. Hatchery tests indicated it was not caused by vinyl toxicity, bacterial infection, or tag irritation alone.

## Background & Description

California runs one of the largest catchable rainbow trout programs in the United States. About eight million trout are planted annually at a cost above one million dollars. Fish weigh four to six per pound and are stocked in accessible streams and lakes with high angling pressure. Department policy is to plant only waters where more than 50 percent of stocked fish are expected to reach anglers' creels.

Evaluating these plantings is difficult. Most waters have multiple access points, so complete creel censuses and truly random samples are virtually impossible. Fin-clip marks are seldom recognized by anglers, so the study relied on the estimated recapture rate from voluntary returns of numbered tags, supplemented by creel census. An ideal tag had to be easy to apply, visible to anglers, and harmless to fish.

**Tag design and specifications**
- Tags are cut from soft upholsterer's yellow vinyl plastic, about 2.5 to 3 mm wide (narrow) and 20 mm long, 0.018 inch thick. The author suggested 0.010 to 0.015 inch would be preferable.
- Tags are rounded at both ends and bear a number plus "Return," and "C. F. G. Sacto." on the reverse. Two studies used "Reward $5" tags.
- Plastic was obtained from three manufacturers. Its exact properties were unknown, so each lot must be tested for retention and toxicity.

**Tagging tool**
- Built from a [[Bard And Parker Scalpel Handle]] with a No. 12 blade, filed to a flat 1 mm by 4 mm by about 30 mm blade, bent into a U-shape, and polished. The author credits [[Le Cren]] with the idea of a philatelist's tool for pocket formation.

**Placement**
- The belly was chosen because it has little black pigment, so the tag is visible, and because it has minimal body movement, fat deposits and low blood supply. Most anglers gut fish belly-up, which increases the chance a tag is seen. The incision is made left of the sagittal line, anterior to the ventral fins, no more than 4 mm long, with the blade held at no more than 30 degrees to reduce muscle cutting.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was framed around fish-stocking management rather than ecosystem ecology. Its relevant environmental factors were water temperature and anesthetic chemistry, which affected fish survival. Trials were run at hatcheries and at Castle Lake, a planted lake. Results from hatchery raceways may not transfer to lake or stream conditions, and the author notes that long-term tests in natural waters had not been done. Angling intensity was high: 80 to 90 percent of planted trout were removed within two to three weeks, which limits measures of long-term tag loss in those waters.

**Anesthesia and handling**
- [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222) at a low concentration (the scanned text is garbled; later passages imply roughly one-quarter gram per gallon at 50°F) produced anesthesia within 30 seconds. Four to five fish were treated at once.
- Higher temperatures, higher concentrations, or longer exposure increased mortality risk. Concentrations were to be kept close to hatchery water temperature.
- [[Chlorobutanol]] relaxed fish well but allowed excessive movement during tagging.

## Key Findings

**Tagging speed and technique**
- Field men became proficient within half a day and could tag about one fish per minute. Optimum speed was estimated at about 100 tags per hour. Chi-square analysis showed the technique was learned quickly, and significant differences in returns between taggers were rarely found.

**Tag retention**
- An initial broad tag (5 x 15 mm) placed transversely was lost entirely by day 135. Longitudinal placement showed 24 percent tag loss, with only about 30 percent of tags in good condition. Most retained tags were poor by day 69.
- Field application of the broad longitudinal tag in [[Castle Lake]] (980 fish planted June 15, 1955) produced 519 returns, of which 168 (32.4%) were lost tags. Loss was 57.8 percent in the 42 to 69 day period, so the broad tag was judged unsuitable.
- In a random hatchery check after 15 days, narrow tags showed 10 good, 3 fair, 1 poor, and 0 lost (14 fish). Broad tags showed 14 good, 16 fair, 8 poor, and 4 lost (42 fish). Six-month returns differed significantly, with narrow tags returning at a higher rate.
- Early loss occurred through the open incision. Later loss resulted from [[Blistering]], which weakened tissue, caused necrosis, and led to tag loss.

**Blistering investigation**
- Four hypotheses were tested: bacterial infection, tag toxicity, irritation from tag stiffness, and circulation blockage from tag width.
- Blister contents showed practically no bacteria, and control fish with incisions but no tags healed completely within two weeks.
- Two-year-old brood stock tagged with [[Aureomycin]]-covered tags still blistered in nearly every case. Eight of 10 tags examined in March 1957 were well retained.
- A soft [[Silicone]] (Silastic S-97-11) tag also caused blistering within 19 days, which indicated toxicity was not the cause.
- Alcohol treatment alone (isopropyl alcohol dip, no antibiotics) was the most effective: no blistering was observed, and tags could be read through the skin.

**Mortality**
- Longitudinal tagged group (56 fish, 69 days): 1 dead, 5 missing. Longitudinal control (56 fish): 2 dead, 3 missing. Transverse tagged (23 fish): 1 dead, 3 missing. Transverse control (23 fish): 0 dead. Unmarked control (5 fish): 1 dead. Fin-clip control (15 fish): 1 dead.
- Differences in mortality between tagged and untagged fish could not be demonstrated. At Castle Lake, three of 980 fish died during tagging, with no further losses after five days. One loss in a 1,200-fish group occurred during tagging.

**Growth**
- Over 135 days, tagged fish averaged 1.8 inches of growth (range 1.4 to 2.3).
- Over 61 days, longitudinal tagged and control fish grew similarly (1.8 inches for controls and narrow tags; 1.6 inches for incision controls; 1.7 inches for broad tags). Broad-tagged fish had a wider range (0.7 to 2.6 inches).
- Four-year-old brood stock tagged two years earlier all grew positively over the two-year period.

**Comparison with other tagging methods**
- [[Heincke Stud Tag]] (acrylic, dorsal) was retained for 135 days but produced an unsightly wound.
- Among fish, [[Le Cren]] (1954) used metal subcutaneous tags on char with detection by an electronic device (Moore and Mortimer, 1954). Dr. E. Bertelsen reported vinyl tags on [[European Plaice]]: 438 of 1,150 tagged fish were returned, and only two showed tag loss.

**Field performance and conclusions**
- Anglers returned more tags voluntarily than were recorded by creel checkers, indicating that the tag is readily seen.
- The tag did not catch on vegetation or netting, making it valuable where netting or brush contact is likely.
- Under hatchery conditions the tag did not impair growth and did not increase mortality, and fish did not outgrow the tag.
- Summary conclusions: blistering is the most serious retention problem; it is not caused by vinyl toxicity, bacteria, or irritation; the narrow tag with isopropyl alcohol gives the best retention. No long-term tests in lakes or streams had yet been completed, so long-term effectiveness remained unproven.
