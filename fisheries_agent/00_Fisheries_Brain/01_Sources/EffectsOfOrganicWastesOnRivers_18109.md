# Effects of Organic Wastes on Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This review by [[E. J. C. Curtis]] and [[D. W. Harrington]] (Water Pollution Research Laboratory, WPRL) examines how organic effluents alter the biological community of rivers, the substances and conditions that drive that change, and methods for controlling it. Organic pollution lowers [[Dissolved Oxygen]] and shifts river communities from clean-water taxa toward bacteria, [[Sewage Fungus]], chironomid larvae and tubificids. The authors stress that biological indicators, [[BOD]] and soluble organic carbon each give only partial information, and that the best control is near-complete removal of biodegradable organic matter, with pilot-channel testing where treatment is partial.

## Background & Description

Pollution of rivers by organic effluents is presented as a major problem in highly populated, industrialised countries. A 1958 survey of streams in England and Wales with a dry-weather flow of at least 1 million gal/day (about 20,000 miles in total) found that 6% were grossly polluted (five-day BOD of 12 mg/litre or more) and 21% were of doubtful or poor quality (BOD of 3-12 mg/litre). Sources include sewage, industrial effluents, farm and agricultural wastes, and run-off from land and tips. Rising effluent volumes and expanding use of rivers for water supply and recreation are expected to demand higher effluent standards.

Organic matter is naturally present in unpolluted streams from land drainage, decaying plants, algae and leaf fall, but at low concentrations tolerated by clean-water organisms. Excess organic loads cause toxicity, oxygen depletion through heterotrophic growth, turbidity and sludge deposits. Biological assessment schemes exploit this relationship. The earliest was the [[Saprobien System]] of [[Kolkwitz and Marsson]], which defined polysaprobic, mesosaprobic and oligosaprobic zones with indicator lists. The simpler [[Biotic Index]] of the Trent River Board relates pollution to the presence or absence of invertebrate groups. The authors note that community structure also depends on non-pollution factors such as bed type and turbulence, so biological methods are best combined with standard sanitary analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

A single discharge typically produces a sequence of zones (Table 1):

- **Zone 1, clean water (above discharge):** High dissolved oxygen, low organic carbon, many species, autotrophic production by algae and green plants, with fish as the dominant predator.
- **Zone 2, organic pollution:** High organic content and low oxygen. Zone 2A (gross pollution) may be nearly devoid of life apart from tolerant bacteria. Zone 2B (less pollution) holds pollution-tolerant bacteria, sewage fungus, chironomid larvae and tubificids.
- **Zone 3, semi-recovery:** Inorganic nutrients support algal growth, including filamentous forms such as *Cladophora* (blanket weed) and *Stigeoclonium*, which decay in autumn and cause nuisance.
- **Zone 4, recovered river:** Similar to conditions above the source.

Bacterial numbers rose below a sewage effluent on the River Ivel (July-August 1963). Average counts on *Berula*, *Callitriche* and *Elodea* were 640, 870 and 630 million/g dry weight above the effluent, and 5,670, 3,800 and 2,140 million/g below it.

[[Sewage Fungus]] is a mucilaginous or filamentous community of bacteria, fungi and ciliated protozoa. It can grow at low nutrient levels in flowing water, cover submerged surfaces including artificial materials, reach up to 100 g dry weight/m² of slime, and trap silt that can more than treble the layer's dry weight. Its oxygen consumption can be 10-20 times that of normal macrophytes per unit dry weight. Sludge deposits, especially in slow streams, can hold chironomid populations of up to 100,000/m².

Green plants add a daytime oxygen surplus that can push pH to lethal levels for fish, and night-time respiration that depletes oxygen and raises carbon dioxide. Depletion of dissolved oxygen also lowers fish resistance to toxicants. In fast, shallow reaerated water, slime can grow while oxygen stays above 90% saturation, whereas slow streams suffer sludge build-up and severe deoxygenation.

## Key Findings

- **Sewage fungus growth factors:** Growth is unaffected by pH between 4 and 8, fungal forms dominate below about pH 6, and growth is possible at dissolved oxygen above about 1 mg/litre.
- **Nutrients:** Phosphate at 0.05 mg P/litre produced heavy slime growth, and a tenfold increase gave no further increase. Inorganic nutrients are rarely limiting. Vitamin B12 is needed by several slime organisms but is normally present in effluents.
- **Indicator limits of BOD:** The five-day BOD test is reliable only on fresh samples and does not measure biodegradation rate. Slowly oxidised substances promote less slime.
- **Soluble organic carbon:** Useful only when carbon is readily biodegradable. In [[Table 3]], Effluent A (mostly starch) and Effluent B (non-biodegradable formaldehyde products) both produced heavy slime, with standing crops of 320 and 360 g dry wt/m² respectively.
- **Pilot-channel data:** Slime standing crops were 320 and 360 g dry wt/m² for the paper-mill effluents (conventional treatment and settlement), 50 g/m² after conventional biological treatment in the resin-manufacture case, and 10 g/m² after sand filtration of biological effluent. Corresponding BODs were 27, 26, 13 and 7 mg/litre.
- **Control options:** Removal of biodegradable organics, mainly by conventional biological treatment, is the primary approach. Sand filtration (tertiary polishing) reduced BOD, suspended solids and slime. Lagooning, spray irrigation and soil percolation can help if of adequate capacity. Partial treatment may have no effect or may trigger sewage fungus.
- **Intermittent discharge:** US experiments suggest storing effluent and releasing it as a concentrated slug can limit slime growth. The authors say this is unproven in the UK, where flows and storage are more moderate.
- **Conclusion:** Oxygen depletion is often the most serious consequence of organic pollution. Biological indicators, BOD and chemical analyses should be combined, with pilot channels used where treatment is incomplete.
