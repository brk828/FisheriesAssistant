# Rare and Endangered Fishes of the United States (1963 Survey)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontiformes #conservation

## Executive Summary

This 1963 survey, prepared at the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] and attributed to [[Robert Rush Miller]], reviews the status of rare and threatened native fishes state by state across the United States. For each species it gives known range, number of recorded individuals or populations, the main threats, and specific recommendations for habitat protection, mostly as wildlife monuments, sanctuaries or refuges. The document covers roughly 70 taxa in about 15 states. Its central message is that many species survive in only one or two localities, and that poisoning, exotic fish introductions, impoundment and spring destruction could eliminate them.

## Background & Description

The text is a conservation report rather than a research paper. It is organized alphabetically by state, with each entry giving the scientific name, author, common name, distribution, and a conservation recommendation. Many entries cite prior literature, such as [[Miller 1961]] and [[Hubbs and Miller]] (1948), and unpublished or personal communications from state agencies and colleagues. Several entries describe population counts, but most are qualitative, and many species are described only by the date of the last known specimen. The document is partly OCR-degraded and contains some incomplete passages; it is best read as a 1963 snapshot rather than a current status review.

Taxa addressed include:

- **Salmonids:** [[Dolly Varden]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Arctic Grayling]]
- **Cyprinids and catostomids:** [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], [[Woundfin]], [[Cui-Ui]], [[Shortnose Sucker]], [[June Sucker]]
- **Cyprinodontids (pupfishes and killifishes):** [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], [[Amargosa Pupfish]], [[Ash Meadows Killifish]], [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]]
- **Poeciliids (gambusias, mollies):** [[Big Bend Gambusia]], [[Amazon Molly]], [[Gila Topminnow]]
- **Percids and centrarchids:** [[Maryland Darter]], [[Guadalupe Bass]], [[Suwannee Bass]], [[Okaloosa Darter]]
- **Cavefishes and blindcats:** [[Ozark Cavefish]], [[Toothless Blindcat]], [[Widemouth Blindcat]]

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document identifies recurring threats:

- **Chemical poisoning:** rotenone and other toxins used in fish eradication or "rehabilitation" have killed populations, for example the [[Colorado Squawfish]] habitat in September 1962, the Texas [[Pecos Gambusia]] population in February 1962, and a massive kill on the Colorado River on January 15, 1961.
- **Exotic species:** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), bullfrogs, eastern crawfish and sailfin mollies compete with or prey on native fishes and may cause [[Genetic Swamping]] through hybridization.
- **Spring and habitat loss:** many taxa are restricted to warm springs, so draining, impoundment or development of a spring can eliminate a species. Examples include the [[Sharphead Darter]] (type locality inundated) and the [[Pahrump Killifish]] (two of three populations lost).
- **Water diversion and pollution:** reduced river flow prevented spawning migration of the [[Cui-Ui]], and domestic pollution and estuary filling threaten the [[Tidewater Goby]].
- **Recreational pressure:** illegal spearing of [[Suwannee Bass]] and collecting pressure on cavefish populations.

Habitat descriptors are specific where given. The [[Gambusia Heterochir]] (Clear Creek gambusia) is correlated with coontail (*Ceratophyllum*) and pH readings of 6.2 to 6.7. Many desert species depend on warm springs of up to about 100° F, and several Nevada and Texas taxa show distinct, highly localized adaptations.

## Key Findings

**Population and status records (as reported):**

- The [[Ozark Cavefish]] population in the Cave Springs town water supply, Benton County, Arkansas, was estimated at 100 individuals by T. L. Poulson (1963), the largest known population.
- The [[Gila Topminnow]] survives in only two Arizona areas, Monkey Spring and Cottonwood Spring, after being nearly eliminated from the state.
- The [[Maryland Darter]] was thought extinct since 1912, then a single young individual was collected on June 10, 1962, only the third known specimen; subsequent July and November 1962 collecting attempts failed.
- The [[Ash Meadows Killifish]] was last collected in 1918 and may already be extinct.
- The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] has a home range smaller than a backyard swimming pool and was placed in Death Valley National Monument in 1952.
- The [[Satan Eurystomus]] (widemouth blindcat) and [[Trogloglanis Pattersoni]] (toothless blindcat) are each known from only three specimens, all from artesian wells near San Antonio, Texas.
- The [[Cui-Ui]] is one of three species in its genus, all considered threatened.
- The [[Olympic Mudminnow]] was found in three Washington localities, with one 1955 collection in a mud-bottomed pool in Newman Creek.
- The [[Sharphead Darter]] (Tennessee) is evidently extinct, and the [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]] is extinct at its type locality at Fort Stockton, Texas.
- The [[Gila Trout]] has pure populations in Diamond Creek, New Mexico; New Mexico began a three-year biological study on April 1, 1962.
- The [[Brook Stickleback]] was thought exterminated in New Mexico by 1957 but persists in Vermejo Park.

**Recommendations:**

- Set aside protected segments or springs as wildlife monuments, sanctuaries or refuges, including the upper Soldier Meadows (Nevada), Dinosaur National Monument (Colorado), Sycamore Canyon (Arizona), and the Devils River basin (Texas).
- Prohibit chemical treatment and other habitat modification in protected reaches, for example East Clear Creek, Arizona.
- Remove exotic species, such as at Thurston Lake, California, before reintroducing the [[Sacramento Perch]].
- Conduct status surveys for species whose current condition is unknown, including the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Least Chub]] and [[Malheur Sculpin]].
- Consider captive or transplant programs as precautions, as was done for the [[Railroad Valley Killifish]].
