# Critical Habitat Proposal for Colorado River Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) proposed critical habitat for four endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The proposal was published in the *Federal Register* on January 29, 1993 (Vol. 58, No. 18), under a court order that required a proposed rule for the razorback sucker by January 25, 1993, using presently available information. The Service proposes 3,370 km (2,094 miles) of critical habitat across the four species, with considerable overlap among them. Biological support and [[Economic Analysis]] documents were not yet complete, and public comments were accepted until March 30, 1993.

## Background & Description

The four species were historically widespread in the mainstream Colorado River system, which drains about 627,000 km² (242,000 sq mi) in the United States plus about 5,000 km² (2,000 sq mi) in Mexico. The Basin includes seven Western States, with the Upper Basin in Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming and the Lower Basin in Arizona, California, and Nevada.

- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): once one of the most abundant and widely distributed mainstream fishes. Lower Basin populations are extirpated from natural rivers; a large remnant stock persists in [[Lake Mohave]].
- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): the only living member of its genus in the Basin, where it is endemic. Native stocks survive mainly in the Green River basin.
- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): restricted to remote, white-water canyons. The largest populations are in the [[Little Colorado River]] and [[Grand Canyon]] and at Black Rocks on the Colorado River.
- **Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*): the rarest native Colorado River fish. The wild population consists mainly of old adults (40 years or more), chiefly in Lake Mohave.

Listing history:
- Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub: listed as endangered March 11, 1967.
- Bonytail Chub: listed April 23, 1980.
- Razorback Sucker: proposed as threatened in 1978, withdrawn in 1980, petitioned for endangered listing in 1989, and listed as endangered October 23, 1991, without critical habitat.

Following a 60-day notice of intent to sue and a lawsuit, the District Court ruled on October 27, 1992, that the Service had violated the [[Endangered Species Act]] by not designating critical habitat at listing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Principal threats.** The document attributes declines to habitat loss from flow, temperature, and sediment regulation; proliferation of introduced fishes; and other human disturbance. [[Dams and Diversions]] have fragmented habitat and eliminated genetic exchange between some populations, and flood-driven nutrient exchange with floodplains has been reduced. Altered conditions favor [[Nonnative Fishes]], which have in some cases flourished at the expense of the natives.

**Recruitment and genetics.** Several populations consist mostly of old adults, and [[Recruitment]] is nearly absent in many remnant stocks. The document warns that loss of genetic diversity could increase susceptibility to environmental change and ultimately lead to extinction.

**Life history linked to flow.**
- *Razorback sucker:* spawns in spring with rising flows and temperatures; reservoir spawning occurs from January or February to April or May at 10.5 to 21 °C. Larvae prefer shallow littoral zones before dispersing.
- *Colorado squawfish:* adults may migrate up to 320 km (200 miles) to spawning areas. Spawning in the Yampa and Green Rivers is associated with declining flows and water temperatures of 22 to 25 °C. Young drift about 6 days and may travel up to 160 km (100 miles) to nursery embayments.
- *Humpback chub:* spawns soon after the highest spring flows, when water temperatures approach 20 °C.
- *Bonytail chub:* spawning has never been observed in nature. Reported spawning occurred in June and July at about 18 °C. Captive-reared bonytails in ponds have produced large numbers of young.

**Habitat concept.** The document describes these fishes as adapted to fluctuating seasonal and annual flows. Habitat includes eddies, backwaters, runs, shoreline inundated lowlands, and canyon pools, and the species move among these habitats seasonally.

## Key Findings

**Proposed critical habitat (river kilometers/miles, share of historical range):**

| Species | Length | Historical Range Share |
|---|---|---|
| Razorback Sucker | 2,935 km (1,824 mi) | 52% |
| Colorado Squawfish | 1,843 km (1,148 mi) | 29% |
| Humpback Chub | 610 km (379 mi) | 28% |
| Bonytail Chub | 544 km (344 mi) | 15% |

- The total for all four species is 3,370 km (2,094 mi), and the species' reaches overlap considerably.
- The razorback sucker is proposed in 15 reaches across the Green, Yampa, Duchesne, Colorado, White, Gunnison, and San Juan Rivers in the Upper Basin and the Colorado, Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers in the Lower Basin.
- The humpback chub is proposed in seven reaches in the Colorado, Green, and Yampa Rivers and the Colorado River in the Lower Basin.
- The bonytail chub is proposed in five reaches, including the Yampa, Green (including Desolation and Gray Canyons), and Colorado Rivers, and a Lower Basin reach from Hoover Dam to Parker Dam including Lakes Mohave and Havasu.
- The bonytail total is given as 544 km in the summary and 554 km in a later section; the document does not resolve this discrepancy.

**Primary constituent elements (PCEs)** defined in the rule:
1. **Water:** adequate quantity and quality (temperature, dissolved oxygen, contaminants, nutrients, turbidity) delivered on a hydrologic regime suited to each life stage.
2. **Physical habitat:** river channels, bottomlands, side channels, oxbows, backwaters, and other areas of the 100-year floodplain that support spawning, nursery, feeding, rearing, or access to them.
3. **Biological environment:** food supply, predation, and competition, including imbalances caused by introduced fishes.

**Razorback selection criteria:** (1) known or suspected wild spawning populations; (2) nursery habitat for juveniles; (3) occupied or historically occupied areas needed for recovery; (4) areas maintaining rangewide distribution and diversity; (5) areas needing special management to allow recovery.

**Regulatory effects (section 7):**
- Federal agencies must ensure their actions are not likely to destroy or adversely modify critical habitat, and must confer on actions affecting proposed habitat.
- Designation does not create a management plan, set population goals, or affect non-Federal actions directly.
- Exclusion decisions weigh the benefits of exclusion against designation unless exclusion would cause extinction.

**Economic analysis:** The Service planned a [[Computable General Equilibrium Model]] built on IMPLAN data, collapsed from 523 to 20 sectors, covering more than 150 counties in seven States. The analysis had not been completed at publication. It was to be based on flow scenarios, input-output models for each State, and a Basin-wide recreation survey.

**Recovery plans:** Plans exist for the Colorado Squawfish (approved 1978, revised 1991), Humpback Chub (approved 1979, revised 1984 and 1990), and Bonytail Chub (approved 1984, revised 1990). Recovery goals from these plans informed the proposed boundaries. A razorback recovery plan was in preparation by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] and was not available for this rule.

**Process and timeline:**
- Public hearings were planned in Phoenix, Arizona; Denver, Colorado; and a site in southern California, to be announced at least 15 days in advance.
- Comments were due by March 30, 1993.
- The Service's own schedule, as stated in the court filings, placed completion of the complex analyses around September 1993.
- The final rule will address exclusions, the economic analysis, and the Regulatory Flexibility Act. The Service states that the rule imposes no direct costs or recordkeeping on small entities.
