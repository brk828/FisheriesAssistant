# Acclimation of Rainbow Trout to High pH to Prevent Stocking Mortality in Summer

## Executive Summary
This study by Murray and Ziebell investigates the physiological tolerance and acclimation capacity of hatchery-reared [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) to elevated pH levels, a common summer phenomenon in southwestern mountain lakes caused by intense photosynthetic activity (eutrophication). The researchers tested both **gradual** (0.2–1.0 pH unit/day) and **rapid** (6-hour) acclimation protocols. Results demonstrated that trout could be gradually acclimated to a pH of 9.8 over 5 days with minimal mortality, whereas rapid exposure to pH 9.5 within 6 hours caused 50% mortality. However, a rapid 6-hour acclimation to pH 9.3 resulted in only temporary appetite loss and zero mortality. These findings provide a practical protocol for hatcheries to pre-acclimate trout before stocking them into high-pH lakes, preventing "pH shock" and preserving summer "put-and-take" sport fisheries.

## Background & Description
While this study is primarily physiological/behavioral rather than morphological, specific physical stress indicators were documented:
- **Total Length:** Test specimens ranged from 14–23 cm (5.5–9.0 in).
- **Corneal Opacities:** At extreme pH levels (10.0–10.3), some fish developed clouding of the eyes (corneal opacities), indicating severe alkaline chemical stress on epithelial tissue.
- **Loss of Equilibrium:** A key physical stress indicator observed at pH 10.0 and above, correlating with neurological/osmoregulatory failure preceding mortality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Context:** The study addresses conditions in freshwater [[Mountain Lakes]] of the southwestern United States (specifically Arizona), which are managed as "put-and-take" trout fisheries requiring frequent restocking.
- **Water Chemistry Drivers:** High pH (9.0–10.1) is not a natural baseline condition but an *indirect result* of nutrient loading (natural or anthropogenic) leading to excessive growth of [[Aquatic Macrophytes]] and algae.
- **Photosynthesis-Driven pH Shift:** Vigorous photosynthesis by aquatic plants strips CO₂ from the water faster than it can be replenished by respiration or atmospheric diffusion. Since CO₂ acts as an acid in water, its removal shifts the chemical equilibrium toward hydroxyl ions, raising pH (see [[Boyd 1979]]).
- **Geographic Scope:** High pH lake events are noted globally, including South Africa ([[Schutte and Elsworth 1954]]), Japan, and Manitoba, but are specifically prevalent in Arizona, where Jones (1982) measured pH >9.0 in 10 of 13 sampled mountain lakes.
- **Hatchery vs. Wild Mismatch:** Hatchery water is typically neutral (pH 7.2–7.5), creating a stark chemical gradient when fish are transferred to alkaline lake environments, triggering "pH shock."

## Key Findings

### 1. Gradual Acclimation (Long-Term)
- Trout successfully acclimated to **pH 9.8** using slow increases of 0.2–0.4 units/day.
- **Day 1-3 (pH 8.6–9.7):** Normal to slightly decreased activity; feeding continued.
- **Day 4 (pH 9.7–9.9):** Minor distress; feeding continued.
- **Day 5 (pH 9.9–10.3):** Feeding ceased; equilibrium loss began at 10.0; corneal opacities appeared at 10.1; 50% equilibrium loss at 10.2; 60% mortality at 10.3.
- **Conclusion:** The maximum pH tolerated *before onset of mortality* was **10.2**. Trout could not permanently acclimate to pH ≥ 10.0.

### 2. Rapid Acclimation (6-Hour Protocol)
- **Preliminary Tests:** Increasing pH to 9.6–9.7 in 3–5 hours caused ≥40% mortality.
- **pH 9.5 Trial:** Marked stress within 12 hours; mortality reached 50% by 49 hours. Survivors resumed feeding by 72 hours.
- **pH 9.3 Trial:** Only minor adverse reactions (reduced swimming, temporary appetite loss). **Zero mortality** across all replicates. Normal feeding resumed within 48 hours.
- **Critical Threshold:** The data pinpoints a narrow but critical safety margin between pH 9.3 (safe) and pH 9.5 (lethal) for a 6-hour acclimation window.

### 3. Management Implications & Stocking Protocol
- A viable stocking strategy is proposed: Transfer fish from neutral water directly to holding water of pH 8.5 (per [[Witschi and Ziebell 1979]]), then increase pH by slightly more than 0.1 units/hour for 6 hours to a max of **9.3**.
- Since natural lake pH increases are typically gradual (0.3–0.5 units/month per [[Jones 1982]]), trout stocked at pH 9.3 should be able to tolerate the natural seasonal rise up to pH 9.8 without further shock.
- This protocol allows hatcheries to maintain summer stocking schedules in [[High pH Lakes]], sustaining the "put-and-take" fishery model without significant post-stocking die-offs.
