# Cienega Creek Stream Restoration Project: Biological Opinion on Gila Topminnow and Southwestern Willow Flycatcher

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) biological opinion, dated June 3, 1998 (file 2-21-98-F-373), evaluates the Bureau of Land Management's proposed [[Cienega Creek]] Stream Restoration Project on the [[Empire-Cienega Resource Conservation Area]] (RCA) in southeastern Arizona. The consultation covers two federally endangered species: the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) and the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*).

The Service concludes that the action, as proposed, is **not likely to jeopardize the continued existence** of either species and **will not adversely modify designated critical habitat**. The project would remove three dikes and one levee, replace grade-control structures, reconstruct a meandering channel, plug a canal, and install a cement road crossing. It would also revegetate about 2.0 acres. The Service anticipates incidental take of both species and sets specific take thresholds, reasonable and prudent measures, terms and conditions, and conservation recommendations.

## Background & Description

### Consultation Context
- Request for formal consultation received February 11, 1998.
- Biological opinion deadline: June 26, 1998.
- The Bureau also requested concurrence with a "not likely to adversely affect" determination for the Huachuca water umbel (*Lilaeopsis schaffneriana* var. *recurva*) and "no effect" determinations for the [[Jaguar]], [[Bald Eagle]], and lesser long-nosed bat. The Service concurred with the Lilaeopsis determination, conditional on pre-construction surveys in potential habitat.
- Earlier related consultations on the RCA include a 1993 headcut repair and fencing action (withdrawn and reinitiated, with a biological opinion completed February 7, 1994) and the Cienega Creek Interim Grazing Plan (completed January 1996).

### Proposed Action Components
1. **Dike and levee removal:** Three dikes and one levee removed with heavy equipment. About 7,000 cubic yards of fill removed from all four structures. Dike #1 fill is 250 cubic yards; dike #3 fill is 1,600 cubic yards; the levee is 890 ft long (4,770 cubic yards fill). Grade-control cross-vane weirs (per [[Rosgen]] 1997) would replace the dikes. Riparian and stream-bottom disturbance totals about 1.06 acres.
2. **New channel configuration:** A meandering channel about 750 ft long and 40 ft wide, at 0.5% grade, with a bend radius of 149 ft, reconstructing the pre-canal pattern shown on aerial photos.
3. **Cement road crossing and realignment:** A 90 ft × 16 ft crossing with an 8-inch reinforced slab. The road moves about 1/4 mile downstream, with about 2,400 ft of road improved. The crossing disturbs about 0.05 acres of riparian vegetation.
4. **Canal plug:** A small dike requiring about 1,500 cubic yards of fill, blocking about 100 ft of canal. Disturbs about 0.52 acres of riparian vegetation.
5. **Revegetation:** About 2.0 acres, with approximately 2,000 plants (deer grass, big sacaton, willow and cottonwood poles). Drip irrigation at one gallon per minute is used where moisture is poor.
6. **Monitoring:** Photos at six fixed locations, six monumented cross sections, four well points, quarterly water depth and discharge, annual RACE (Riparian Area Condition Evaluation) and flycatcher surveys.

### Gila Topminnow: Biology
- A small, live-bearing poeciliid. Males rarely exceed 25 mm; females reach 51 mm.
- Fertilization is internal; brood development takes 24 to 28 days, with 2 to 3 broods developing simultaneously (superfetation). Broods contain 1 to 31 young.
- Breeding occurs primarily March through August, with some winter young.
- Diet includes bottom debris, vegetation, amphipods, and insect larvae.
- Tolerances reported: 6 to 37°C, pH 6.6 to 8.9, dissolved oxygen 2.2 to 11 mg/L, and salinities approaching seawater. They burrow into mud or vegetation when water levels drop.
- Listed in 1967 without critical habitat. Only U.S. populations are protected under the ESA.

### Southwestern Willow Flycatcher: Biology
- A small passerine (Tyrannidae), about 15 cm long and 11 g.
- An insectivore that hawks and gleans prey from dense riparian vegetation. A riparian obligate.
- Listed as endangered: proposed July 23, 1993; final rule February 27, 1995 (effective March 29, 1995). Critical habitat published July 18, 1997 (effective August 21, 1997).
- Migrates to Mexico, Central America, and possibly northern South America.
- Nests typically 0.6 to 14 m above ground, with mean heights of 1.8 to 2.1 m in native broadleaf habitat and 4.3 to 7.4 m in mixed native/exotic and monotypic exotic habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Environmental Baseline
- Cattle grazing in southern Arizona dates to the 1600s. In the 1880s, 6,000 cattle and 23,000 sheep grazed the Empire and Cienega Ranches.
- The RCA covers 36,498 acres of public land and 37,462 acres of State land. Average elevation is about 4,600 ft. Annual rainfall averages 15 inches, with high year-to-year variability.
- In the 1970s, a drag-line canal diverted floodwater around cultivated bottomlands and bisected a marsh. The unlined canal has since eroded to over 100 ft wide and 20 ft deep below a concrete ford.
- Three dikes installed in the 1970s have largely silted in; the last dike is eroding and threatens to short-cut a meander.
- Riparian area function ranges from unsatisfactory to satisfactory. RACE evaluations in 1993 indicated improving riparian health. Vegetative ground cover was 49% (1974) and 57% (1991); bare ground was 17% and 23%, respectively.

### Cienega Creek as Topminnow Habitat
- One of the last Arizona streams with an intact native fish fauna uncontaminated by exotic fish. One of nine remaining natural topminnow sites and one of three not contaminated by [[Mosquitofish]].
- Fall population estimate of about 2.5 million topminnow for 6.5 miles of perennial habitat. Another 1.1 miles in [[Mattie Canyon]] and 0.9 miles in [[Empire Gulch]] were not included. Warm groundwater discharge areas held up to 566 fish per square meter.
- Habitat composition (1989 to 1990 basin-type survey, by length): marsh 49.9%, pool 22.1%, glide 13.0%, riffle 11.6%, rapid 1.6%, backwater pool 1.1%, others 0.7%. All pool types combined equal 36.2%.
- Topminnow occur throughout Bureau-managed Cienega Creek except the intermittent project segment, where they appear seasonally in wet years.
- Other fish in the system include [[Gila Chub]] and [[Longfin Dace]].

### Flycatcher Habitat in the Action Area
- Bureau records include 30 banding records along Cienega Creek (1987 to 1993): 5 spring and 34 fall (as reported in the text).
- No breeding records from Cienega Creek. No flycatchers detected in a May 1993 playback survey or during three years of call surveys (Whetstone 1996). A June 1997 survey found no flycatchers, and the habitat was of insufficient density to hold breeding birds.
- The canal and Mattie Canyon provide about 2.1 miles (41.0 acres) of potentially colonizable habitat.

### Threats and Cumulative Effects
- **Topminnow:** dewatering, impoundment, channelization, diversion, flow regulation, erosion, and nonnative fish and crayfish (per Minckley, Miller, and Meffe et al.).
- **Flycatcher:** cowbird parasitism, nest predation, habitat loss, inundation, fire, and water diversion.
- **Future development:** Groundwater pumping, subdivision ("ranchettes") around Sonoita, nonnative fish stocking, bullfrogs, and recreation pressure.

## Key Findings

### Species Status
- **Gila topminnow:** Only 11 of 15 recent natural populations are extant, and only three are considered secure. Topminnow persist at 18 of 175 stocked wild sites. The species occurs at no more than 29 localities, many small and highly threatened. Cienega Creek is "by far the largest natural population remaining."
- **Southwestern willow flycatcher:** Rangewide population is about 454 territories (Table 3), but the Service judges the actual population to be about 300 to 500 territories, with many unmated birds. More than 75% of occupied locations hold five or fewer territorial birds.
- Unitt (1987) estimated 500 to 1,000 pairs historically (rangewide, before recent declines), and documented the loss of more than 70 breeding locations.
- Breeding groups are highly fragmented; the nearest known groups to Roosevelt Lake are about 88 km apart.
- Cowbird parasitism exceeded 90% of surveyed sites with cowbirds present (per cited surveys, "cowbirds have been documented at more than 90% of sites surveyed").

### Cowbird and Nest Biology
- Probability of a flycatcher fledging its own young from a parasitized nest is less than 5%.
- Nest predation is generally 30% to 50%.
- On the South Fork Kern River, the breeding population fell from 44 to 27 pairs (1989 to 1993) while cowbird parasitism ran 50% to 80%. After trapping cut parasitism below 20%, nest success rose from 26% to 48%, and fledged young per pair rose from 1.01 to 1.73. Numbers stabilized at 32 to 34 pairs in 1993 to 1995.
- Juveniles fledged on or before July 20 returned at 21.9%, versus 6.4% for those fledged later.

### Predicted Effects of the Project
- **Topminnow:** Risk of mortality if intermittent flow carries fish into the work area; mitigated by salvage and screening. Short-term siltation may persist up to three years. Benefit: long-term improvement of more than two miles of riparian and aquatic habitat, and prevention of headcut migration that could degrade miles of upstream habitat. Removing Dikes #1 and #3 eliminates ponding that could attract nonnative fish or frogs.
- **Flycatcher:** Pole cutting may degrade about 3/4 mile of potential habitat (11.2 acres) along the canal. Equipment activity during May and June may disturb birds. Blasting may disturb migrants. Long-term, the reach is expected to become suitable for colonization after about 3 or more years.
- **Hydrology:** Flood-flow reconnection is expected to improve perennial surface water in the project reach. Trees in the canal are expected to survive through subsurface seepage, but natural regeneration will decline without flood flows.
- The Service judged the magnitude of effects "negligible" for the flycatcher, while recognizing the risks above.

### Take Thresholds (Incidental Take Statement)
- **Gila topminnow:** Take is exceeded if (1) more than 10 dead topminnow are found in the project area, or (2) new headcuts begin at the present dike locations. An unlimited number may be captured and moved to permanent water in Cienega Creek.
- **Southwestern willow flycatcher:** Take is exceeded if surveys detect two flycatchers using the area during construction.

### Reasonable and Prudent Measures
1. Conduct all actions to minimize take of both species.
2. Monitor the fish and bird community and habitat in the project reach.
3. Maintain complete and accurate records.

### Key Terms and Conditions
- If water enters the site, work within the floodplain stops until it drains or a qualified fisheries biologist removes topminnow.
- Construct the new road crossing before removing dikes, and leave the canal open and berm in place to shunt flows.
- Blast the cement dike before September 1, 1998.
- Survey for flycatchers before June work and before recommencing on September 1. A biologist inspects active construction at least weekly.
- Cross sections monitored annually for three years after completion, then every five years until the reach is in proper functioning condition. Photos at six stations before the project starts.
- Records and annual reports due to the Service by July 1, beginning 1999.

### Mitigation Measures in the Proposed Action
- Revegetation with deer grass and riparian tree poles; irrigation to aid establishment.
- Poling of canal trees delayed until dormancy.
- Pre-June flycatcher surveys, and implementation of the Programmatic Grazing BO measures (2-21-96-F-160), including livestock exclusion and cowbird control.
- Salvage of stranded topminnow and screening of diversions.
- Pre-construction Huachuca water umbel surveys, with avoidance if found.

### Conservation Recommendations
1. Begin consultation on road maintenance in the RCA, and address road maintenance and closures in the land use plan.
2. Identify unoccupied sites on the RCA suitable for Gila topminnow, to create refugia against impacts to Cienega Creek.
3. Complete the planned riparian ecological site inventory.
4. Address strategies in the land use plan that enhance the chance of establishing a breeding flycatcher population. Establishing such a population may require reinitiation of consultation.

### Conclusion
The Service concludes the Cienega Creek Stream Restoration Project, as proposed, is not likely to jeopardize either species and will not adversely modify critical habitat. Existing critical habitat for the flycatcher lies outside the action area, and no critical habitat has been designated for the Gila topminnow.
