# Managing Riverine Connectivity in Complex Landscapes to Protect Remnant Natural Areas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-shrimp-atyidae

## Executive Summary

This plenary lecture by [[Catherine M. Pringle]] (Verhandlungen Internationaler Verein Limnologie, Stuttgart, November 2000) argues that [[Riverine Connectivity]], the exchange of matter, energy and organisms across the riverine landscape, is central to the health of [[Remnant Natural Areas]]. These protected areas are vestiges of larger ecosystems embedded in human-dominated matrices. The paper reviews four global patterns of biotic impoverishment caused by altered connectivity and examines three case studies: [[Kruger National Park]] (South Africa), the [[Caribbean National Forest]] (Puerto Rico) and the [[Braulio Carrillo-La Selva Land Corridor]] (Costa Rica). The author concludes that connectivity must be managed at landscape and catchment scales, and that research on it is urgently needed.

## Background & Description

The paper defines riverine connectivity in the longitudinal, lateral and vertical dimensions, following [[James Ward]] (Ward 1997) and [[Ward and Stanford Four-Dimensional Model]]. Remnant natural areas include national parks, forest reserves, military lands, transboundary protected areas and private holdings. The author describes them as data banks, gene pools, outdoor laboratories and source areas for native biota.

Human disturbances that disrupt connectivity include dams, channelization, flow regulation, water diversion and groundwater abstraction. Their effects propagate upstream, downstream, laterally and vertically, often with long time lags. Sediment, toxic loading and exotic species are exacerbated by altered connectivity.

The three case study sites differ in scale:
- Caribbean National Forest: ~113 km²
- Braulio Carrillo-La Selva corridor: ~500 km² (462 km² in one passage)
- Kruger National Park: ~20,000 km² (21,500 km² in another passage)

Nearly half of the world's parks and protected areas are under 100 km², and only about 2% fall in the "larger park" category (>10,000 km²).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Four global patterns of biotic impoverishment**
1. Deterioration of lower catchments, deltas, estuaries and coastal waters (longitudinal).
2. Loss of floodplains, connecting wetlands and riparian zones (lateral).
3. Deterioration of irrigated lands and connected surface and groundwaters (vertical).
4. Isolation of headwater catchments (longitudinal, insidious and often overlooked).

**Selected global figures reported**
- More than 40,000 large dams plus hundreds of smaller barriers (McCully 1996).
- Reservoir storage estimated at up to 10,000 km³, about five times the volume of all the world's rivers (Chao 1991).
- The United States and Europe have lost over half their wetlands; Asia has lost ~27%.
- Almost 90% of the Rhine floodplain in Germany has been developed.
- Water tables are falling on every continent.
- A little over 6% of the world's land is strictly or partially protected.

**Deltas and estuaries**
- Dniester, Dnieper and Don rivers: discharges reduced by about half; estuarine salinities rose 4-fold and 10-fold respectively; commercial fisheries declined 90-98%.
- Indus River discharge reduced ~80%, with mangrove dieoffs.
- Shoreline erosion near Baltim, Egypt is ~151 m per year, linked to dams such as the Aswan High Dam.

**Danube**
- The 4,420 km² Danube Delta, Europe's largest wetland, became a biosphere reserve in 1990.
- The river drains 12 countries and carries 60,000 tons of phosphorus and 340,000 tons of inorganic nitrogen annually to the Black Sea.

**Puerto Rican freshwater fauna**
- Fishes and shrimps are migratory (amphidromous or catadromous), linking headwaters with estuaries.
- Adult atyid shrimps dominate montane stream biomass and process leaf litter.

## Key Findings

**Case Study I: Kruger National Park, South Africa**
- Semi-arid, receives ~0.5 m rainfall per year; 147 mammal species, 492 birds, 118 reptiles, 34 amphibians, 49 freshwater fishes, 404 trees and shrubs, 224 grasses.
- Six major rivers flow west to east from intensively settled land through the park into Mozambique.
- The Letaba and Luvuvhu have become ephemeral due to irrigation and forestry abstraction, with prolonged no-flow periods.
- The Crocodile River suffers sewage and irrigation-driven salinity problems.
- The Sabie, the only park river that has never stopped flowing, has seen reduced flow, falling water tables and riparian vegetation mortality.
- The Olifants (catchment 54,575 km², ~2.5 million people, 30 major dams) has lost at least five fish species from high salinity, heavy metals, sulfate, fluoride and silt. Silt releases caused massive downstream fish kills, followed by decline of the Pels fishing owl.
- Herbivores live within 6 km of surface water. Groundwater-pumped artificial watering points increased herbivore numbers, which are now food-limited, producing catastrophic collapses in 1966 and 1983.
- Management options include upstream water reallocation, compensation flows, instream flow requirements per river, hydrological simulation, and new storage dams.
- South Africa's National Water Policy (DWAF 1997) and catchment management agencies are being used to involve upstream farmers; during a recent drought, farmers changed abstraction to keep water flowing through the park, though some suffered losses.

**Case Study II: Caribbean National Forest, Puerto Rico**
- The forest is the largest old-growth tract in the Caribbean, with 61 endemic tree species (26 endemic to the Luquillo Mountains) and the last natural population of the Puerto Rican parrot (*Amazona vittata*). Only native mammals are bats.
- Average rainfall is ~4 m per year. The forest is a Biosphere Reserve (1976) and an LTER site (1988).
- The forest includes the upper portions of nine catchments. A water budget recorded 21 intakes within forest boundaries and nine large intakes downstream (Naumann 1994).
- On an average day, more than 50% of drainage water is diverted before reaching the ocean.
- Saline water intrudes 2-3 km upstream in the absence of freshwater inflow.
- Puerto Rico has 404 inhabitants per km²; San Juan metropolitan region holds one-third of its 3.5 million people.
- Migratory shrimp larvae drift at night, peaking just after dusk. Larvae spend 50-110 days in estuaries before returning as postlarvae.
- Water abstraction on the lower Espiritu Santo caused 42% mortality of drifting first-stage larvae over 2 months; during low discharge, 100% were entrained.
- Modeling with a 30-year discharge record gave long-term mean daily entrainment mortality of 34-62%.
- Recommendations: 3-5 hour abstraction stoppages during post-dusk larval drift, maintenance of fish ladders, minimum flows over dams, evaluation of withdrawal systems, improved distribution efficiency, and instream flow requirements.

**Case Study III: Braulio Carrillo-La Selva Land Corridor, Costa Rica**
- The corridor spans four life zones and two transition zones, from lowland wet forest at La Selva (35 m a.s.l.) to montane forest at Barva Volcano (2,906 m a.s.l.). It is the last primary-forest tract spanning this elevation range on the Atlantic slope of Central America.
- The La Selva Biological Station (~1,536 ha) holds over 104 mammal species (~62 bats), over 1,900 plant species, 425 bird species, 450 ant species and 45 fish species.
- The park was created in 1977 for watershed protection and joined the La Selva area in 1986. UNESCO recognised the Cordillera Volcanica Central Biosphere Reserve in 1988.
- Lowland surface and groundwater are contaminated with fecal coliforms from cattle and humans. Banana plantation development raised the local population by 26,000 in just over three years.
- Highland catchments in the park now supply potable water to ~46,000 people.
- Spring water at Puerto Viejo de Sarapiqui is free of biological contaminants but geothermally contaminated, with high mineral content and up to 400 µg SRP L⁻¹.
- Over one-half of the flow of the Salto stream at La Selva is geothermally modified groundwater (Genereux & Pringle 1997), an interbasin transfer that justifies continued protection.
- Nineteen hydropower projects are proposed or existing in the Sarapiqui catchment. Two were installed without environmental impact assessment. Hydropower threatens ecotourism, including river rafting.
- Possible impacts include downcutting that could strand swamps, or flooding of low-lying areas, and altered aquifer recharge. Baseline discharge data are lacking; the only record is on the Sarapiqui mainstem.

**Summary and conclusions**
- All three areas share water quality and quantity problems driven by altered connectivity, despite differences in size, climate and governance.
- Kruger shows that large size does not protect against upstream impacts, so management must be at very large scales.
- The Caribbean National Forest is a "window into the future" for humid tropical developing regions.
- Key research questions include how lateral, longitudinal and vertical connectivity operate across landscapes, how different taxa respond, how natural and human disturbances interact, and how cumulative regional-to-global effects alter biological integrity.
