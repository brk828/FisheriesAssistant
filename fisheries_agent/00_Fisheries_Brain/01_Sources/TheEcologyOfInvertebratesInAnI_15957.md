# The Ecology of Invertebrates in an Intermittent Stream

#aquatic-ecology #crustacean-ecology #intermittent-stream #Asellidae #Crangonyctidae #stream-ecology #drought-adaptation

## Executive Summary

This dissertation investigates the ecology and physiological mechanisms enabling macroinvertebrates to survive in a small south-central Indiana stream ([[Caldwell Hollow]]) that becomes completely dry each summer. The stream fauna is dominated by two aquatic peracarid crustaceans, [[Lirceus fontinalis]] (an isopod) and [[Crangonyx forbesi]] (an amphipod), which persist year-round by surviving dry periods as small animals in subsurface seepage and water-saturated air spaces within moist soil. The study documents reproductive cycles, seasonal oxygen consumption, humidity tolerance, and the critical role of interstitial spaces in maintaining permanent aquatic populations. Other invertebrates divide into late summer-autumn and late winter-spring associations, with most permanent residents exhibiting univoltine life cycles and spring emergence patterns adapted to the intermittent flow regime.

## Background & Description

[[Caldwell Hollow]] is a tributary of [[Salt Creek]] in the [[Norman Upland]] of [[Brown County]], Indiana, approximately 23 km east of Bloomington. The stream is 2.22 km long with a watershed of 3.1 km² and an average gradient of 31.7 m/km. The stream bed consists predominantly of sandstone particles of various sizes. During October 1963 to October 1964, the stream was continuous with Salt Creek for only 46 days and was completely dry for 38 days during August-November 1964.

The two dominant crustaceans exhibit distinct characteristics:
- **[[Lirceus fontinalis]]**: An aquatic isopod that reproduces March-April with females carrying 180-270 eggs in brood pouches, incubation averaging 38 days at 18°C
- **[[Crangonyx forbesi]]**: An aquatic amphipod breeding March-May, with females producing one brood of approximately 90 eggs before dying

The stream's aquatic insect fauna includes:
- **Caddisflies**: [[Ironoquia punctatissima]], [[Lepidostoma]] sp., [[Neophylax concinnus]], [[Eubasilissa]] sp., [[Diplectrona]] sp.
- **Stoneflies**: [[Allocapnia pygmaea]], [[Isoperla decepta]], [[Perlesta placida]]
- **Mayflies**: [[Heptagenia]] sp., [[Ameletus lineatus]]
- **Beetles**: [[Hydroporus laetus]], [[Cymbiodyta]] sp., [[Limnebius discolor]]

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The intermittent stream environment presents severe ecological stresses:
- **Flowing stream** conditions in spring
- **Pond-like** conditions in early summer
- **Terrestrial** conditions in late summer/early autumn
- **Stagnant pools** in late autumn/winter with high oxygen demand from decomposing leaf material

Water temperatures ranged from near 0°C in winter to over 25°C in summer. Dissolved oxygen in isolated pools often approached depletion, with characteristic dark brown "black water" conditions from leaf decomposition.

**Critical habitat features**:
- **Interstitial spaces**: Non-angular sandstone fragments create numerous pore spaces extending to considerable depth (up to 60 cm)
- **Subsurface seepage**: Maintains aquatic environment below dry surface
- **Water-saturated air spaces**: Provide habitat above seepage level
- **Low silt load**: Pristine wooded watershed prevents clogging of interstitial spaces
- **Protection from freeze-thaw**: North-facing aspect reduces daily temperature fluctuations

The stream's physical characteristics create "Prandtl's layers" (dead-water zones) in fissures and pore spaces that protect organisms from high water velocities during floods.

## Key Findings

**Reproductive Cycles and Life Histories**:

1. **[[Lirceus fontinalis]]** exhibits an annual cycle with facultative univoltine reproduction:
   - First ovigerous females appear late March-early April
   - Two peaks of reproduction: early (April) and late (May-June)
   - All overwintering adults perish by end of July
   - New generation shows arrested growth during summer/autumn dry period
   - Rapid growth resumes in late autumn/winter
   - Average 220 eggs per female, with 105 embryos and 55 young surviving to release

2. **[[Crangonyx forbesi]]** shows obligate univoltine reproduction:
   - Ovigerous females present April-May
   - Each female produces one brood (~90 eggs) then dies
   - Males die before females
   - Sex ratio of 2.2 females to 1 male during peak reproduction
   - New generation exhibits minimal summer growth, surviving in subsurface seepage

**Physiological Adaptations**:

3. **Oxygen consumption** in L. fontinalis declined in late spring/summer when measured at 5°C but not at 18°C. When corrected for body size, animals at 18°C also exhibited lower summer metabolic rates. The weight-specific regression coefficient was -0.29 at 18°C, similar to other isopods.

4. **Humidity tolerance experiments** (water-saturated air at 13°C):
   - L. fontinalis from intermittent stream: survived up to 24 days (50% survival = 7 days)
   - C. forbesi from intermittent stream: survived up to 11 days (50% survival = 6 days)
   - L. fontinalis from permanent stream: 50% survival = 1.5 days
   - [[Gammarus minus]] from permanent stream: 50% survival < 1 day
   - Intermittent stream species showed significantly greater survival than permanent stream relatives

**Seasonal Community Structure**:

5. **Summer-autumn association** (stream dry or nearly dry):
   - Adult aquatic beetles (permanent residents)
   - Adventitious species: [[Tubifera]] sp., [[Culex]] spp., [[Anopheles]] sp.
   - Migratory corixids (temporary)
   - [[Gordius]] sp. (nematomorph)

6. **Winter-spring association** (stream flowing):
   - Five caddisfly species
   - Three stonefly species
   - Two mayfly species
   - Young [[Semotilus atromaculatus]] (creek chub, unable to complete life cycle)

7. L. fontinalis and C. forbesi consistently comprised 80-90% of total organisms except during April-May when spring fauna was abundant.

**Survival Mechanisms During Dry Periods**:

8. **Caddisflies** show highest adaptation:
   - I. punctatissima: eggs laid in October on dry stream bed, larvae appear January, aestivate as prepupae in terrestrial leaf litter
   - Lepidostoma sp.: similar terrestrial aestivation
   - N. concinnus: survives as prepupae attached to rocks, then as resistant eggs

9. **Stoneflies and mayflies**:
   - Allocapnia pygmaea: survives as very small nymphs (2 mm) in subsurface seepage with arrested summer growth
   - Isoperla decepta and Heptagenia sp.: likely survive as eggs with long hatching periods, possibly spending considerable nymphal life in hyporheic (interstitial) zone

10. **Size constraints**: Animals surviving in moist soil or subsurface seepage during peak drought:
    - L. fontinalis: averaged 3 mm (moist soil, October) to 5 mm (subsurface seepage, November)
    - C. forbesi: averaged 3 mm (August subsurface seepage)
    - Specimens found as deep as 60 cm, most between surface and 25 cm

**Soil and Habitat Analysis**:

11. **Subsurface seepage samples** contained: L. fontinalis (up to 11 mm), C. forbesi, [[Asellus stygius]], Allocapnia pygmaea nymphs, adult beetles, and occasional large specimens

12. **Moist soil samples** (water-saturated air, no free water) contained: small L. fontinalis (2-8 mm), C. forbesi (3-4 mm), adult beetles, but notably absent were A. stygius and A. pygmaea nymphs (restricted to free water)

13. **Moist leaf litter** served as temporary refuge for large animals:
    - Large adult L. fontinalis (3-14 mm) and C. forbesi (2-14 mm), many ovigerous
    - Prepupal caddisflies (I. punctatissima, Lepidostoma sp.)
    - Animals too large to follow subsiding water table

14. **Dry soil** contained very few organisms: occasional [[Gordius]] sp. and one tendipedid larva, demonstrating critical importance of 100% relative humidity

**Ecological Implications**:

15. The permanent fauna is restricted to:
    - Univoltine species (one generation per year)
    - Annual life cycles (approximately one year life span)
    - Species without major summer growth periods
    - Insects with spring emergence (except caddisflies)

16. The intermittent environment eliminates:
    - Bi- or multivoltine invertebrates (e.g., [[Baetis]] spp., [[Stenonema]] spp.)
    - Animals with 2+ year life cycles (e.g., [[Pteronarcys]] spp., [[Acroneuria]] spp.)
    - Species with summer growth and autumn emergence (e.g., [[Hydropsyche]] spp.)

17. **Interstitial space requirements**:
    - Non-angular bottom materials essential
    - Absence of heavy silt load critical
    - Protection from daily freeze-thaw cycles important
    - Depends on local geological and geographical features

18. Following rotenone poisoning of [[Salt Creek]] in autumn 1964, intermittent streams like Caldwell Hollow (completely dry during poisoning) contributed substantially to reestablishment of invertebrates in the permanent stream, demonstrating their importance as faunal reservoirs.

19. The March 1964 flood (>2 inches precipitation) completely altered stream bed configuration, scouring pools to depths >60 cm and causing substantial mortality of interstitial fauna, but the community recovered.

**Comparative Analysis**: The study proposes arranging intermittent streams by permanency of aquatic environment (reflected in fauna) rather than simple flow characteristics. Caldwell Hollow represents an extreme case where the stream goes completely dry but maintains permanent aquatic fauna through subsurface refugia, contrasting with drought-stricken permanent streams or intermittent streams with isolated pools.
