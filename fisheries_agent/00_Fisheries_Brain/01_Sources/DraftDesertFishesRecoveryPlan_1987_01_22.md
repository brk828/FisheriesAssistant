# Draft Desert Fishes Recovery Plan — Guzman Basin & Rio Grande (Big Bend Reach)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #desert-fishes #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1987 internal memorandum from the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish (David L. Propst to W.L. Minckley) provides descriptive background for a Draft Desert Fishes Recovery Plan. It characterizes two major desert drainage systems relevant to imperiled native fish conservation: the endorheic [[Guzman Basin]] of northwestern Chihuahua and southwestern New Mexico, and the [[Rio Grande]] Basin, with particular focus on the Big Bend reach where the cyprinid [[Campostoma Ornatum]] (Mexican stoneroller) persists. The document is primarily a hydrogeographic and habitat-condition assessment rather than a morphological or taxonomic study, emphasizing anthropogenic habitat degradation (irrigation diversion, riparian clearing, damming, grazing) as the central conservation concern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Guzman Basin (Endorheic System)
- Drained by Rios [[Mimbres]], [[Casas Grandes]], [[Del Carmen]], and [[Santa Maria]], terminating in Lagos [[Guzman]], [[De Patos]], and [[Santa Maria]].
- Entirely within the **Chihuahuan Desert**; xeric vegetation dominates except at higher elevations with mesic plant communities.
- **Rio Mimbres** (only Guzman Basin drainage occurring in the US):
  - Headwaters (Rio Mimbres, McKnight Creek, Gallinas Creek) originate in the Black Range (>2800 m elevation).
  - Upper reaches: coniferous-dominated riparian stands.
  - Lower reaches: mixed alder, cottonwood, and willow riparian communities.
  - Historic condition (per Antisell, 1856): described as a **lagoon-like stream system**.
  - Current condition: incised channel dominated by riffle-run habitat; pools scarce and largely confined to intact riparian stands.
  - Flow is naturally intermittent, becoming dry near Dwyer, New Mexico.
  - Degradation drivers: timber harvest, livestock grazing (watershed instability at higher elevations); irrigation diversion and riparian clearing for cultivation (lower elevations).

### Rio Grande Basin
- Originates in the San Juan Mountains, Colorado; forms the US–Mexico boundary from El Paso/Ciudad Juárez to the Gulf of Mexico.
- Major tributaries: Rios [[Chama]], [[Conchos]], [[Pecos]], [[Salado]], and [[San Juan]].
- Follows the **Rio Grande Rift** (Late Oligocene/Early Miocene fault system; Chapin, 1979).
- Habitat gradient:
  - Northern reaches: canyon-dominated, interrupted by narrow valleys.
  - South of Cochiti Pueblo: low-relief agricultural valley meanders.
  - Big Bend, Texas: re-enters constricted canyon country, emerging near Del Rio/Ojinaga before continuing as a lowland stream to the Gulf.
- **Anthropogenic alterations:**
  - Impoundments at Cochiti, Elephant Butte, and Amistad.
  - Seasonal flow elimination via irrigation diversion (Albuquerque, below Caballo Reservoir, below El Paso).
  - Widespread flow reduction/regulation and levee confinement, resulting in markedly reduced habitat diversity throughout most of the river's length in New Mexico and parts of Texas.

### Big Bend Reach — Focal Habitat for Recovery Concern
- Region spans Texas/Chihuahua and Coahuila.
- Supports persisting populations of [[Campostoma Ornatum]].
- US populations restricted to two small tributaries: **Tornillo Creek** and **Terlingua Creek**, both within [[Big Bend National Park]].
- Permanently watered reaches are short; **spring discharge** sustains flow for much of the time, functioning as critical refugia in an otherwise flow-limited system.

## Key Findings
- The [[Guzman Basin]] and [[Rio Grande]] Basin both exemplify desert riverine systems where human water management (irrigation diversion, damming, riparian clearing, grazing) has substantially reduced habitat diversity and altered natural flow regimes.
- The Rio Mimbres has transitioned from a historically lagoon-like, pool-rich stream system to an incised, riffle-run dominated channel with minimal pool habitat — a direct consequence of watershed and floodplain disturbance.
- The Rio Grande's habitat diversity has been substantially diminished by a series of major impoundments (Cochiti, Elephant Butte, Amistad) and near-total seasonal flow elimination in several reaches.
- [[Campostoma Ornatum]] persistence in the US is narrowly confined to spring-fed refugia (Tornillo and Terlingua creeks) in the Big Bend region, underscoring the importance of spring discharge in maintaining native fish populations amid basin-wide flow reduction.
- The memorandum highlights significant knowledge gaps regarding the Mexican portions of both the Guzman Basin and the lower Rio Grande, limiting the completeness of range-wide recovery planning at the time of writing (1987).
