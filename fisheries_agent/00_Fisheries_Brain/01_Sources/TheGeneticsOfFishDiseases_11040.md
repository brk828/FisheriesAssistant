# The Genetics of Fish Diseases

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review, by [[Myron Gordon]] of the Genetics Laboratory of the New York Zoological Society, argues that hereditary factors play a real and important role in fish diseases and skeletal abnormalities. Most of the evidence comes from long-term breeding experiments on [[Xiphophorus Helleri]] (swordtails), [[Xiphophorus Maculatus]] (platyfish) and their hybrids. The author also reviews genetic work on [[Oryzias Latipes]] (medaka), [[Lebistes Reticulatus]] (guppies), and the thyroid and kidney tumors of [[Salvelinus Fontinalis]] (brook trout). The paper concludes that fish genetics could match the gains made in plant and farm-animal breeding if it received comparable funding and a modern research program.

## Background & Description

The paper opens by comparing fish breeding with plant and livestock breeding. The author notes that fishes have a much higher reproductive capacity than other domesticated animals, which lets breeders multiply a strain quickly once a desirable pair is produced. Despite this, fish-culture methods for trout and other species had lagged behind, and older trout-breeding methods, such as those of [[Embody and Hayford]], needed reappraisal.

The work sits within a body of research on [[Pigment Cell Genetics]] in the xiphophorin fishes. Key background points include:

- **Color genes:** The wild [[Xiphophorus Helleri]] has an olive-gray coloration produced by two pigment-cell types, micromelanophores (black) and xanthophores (yellow). The golden mutant lost most black cells, and was shown to be a recessive, Mendelian trait. The albino mutant, first seen in aquaria around 1934, is also recessive and removes most black pigment, so the pink eye color reflects blood vessels in the iris and retina.
- **Laboratory lines:** Montezuma swordtails (*Xiphophorus montezumae*), originally collected from the Rio Panuco in Mexico, have been propagated in the laboratory since 1939. Some lines were inbred for many generations, including strain 38° (7 generations of inbreeding, the last 3 by brother-to-sister matings).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper treats environmental conditions as a major influence on disease and abnormality expression:

- Skeletal characteristics are strongly affected by conditions during early embryonic development, especially temperature. The author recommends rigid temperature control in any genetic study of skeletal traits.
- Laboratory fish were kept in conditioned fresh water reused over many years and shared among tanks of six other xiphophorin species. All seven species were fed a common diet of dried shredded ocean shrimp, liver, cereal, and living tubificids and daphnids.
- Wild Montezuma swordtails were compared with laboratory-reared fish, with wild thyroid tissue fixed at the site of capture in Mexico.
- Environmental effects complicate genetic analysis. The author attributes the inconsistent inheritance of spinal deformities in medaka and platyfish, compared with guppies, partly to environmental influence.
- Tumor susceptibility is genetic but occurs under identical maintenance conditions. Pure lines from wild populations and fanciers' stocks differ widely in tumor incidence when kept under the same conditions.

## Key Findings

**1. Melanomas in platyfish-swordtail hybrids are genetically controlled.**
- A sex-linked dominant gene from the platyfish (Sp, for macromelanophore growth) interacts with dominant modifier genes (A and B) from the swordtail. Hybrids with Sp A B are highly melanotic and tend to develop melanomas.
- Melanoma control depends on a constellation of genes, not one gene. The author states that the mechanism is consistent with standard Mendelian principles.

**2. Red-black tumors (erythromelanomas) depend on gene linkage.**
- Erythromelanomas appeared in a significant share of one brood. The cause was a platyfish male carrying the ruby-throated gene (Rt) and the spotted gene (Sp) on the same sex chromosome. When linked, they were passed together, and their offspring could develop erythromelanomas. Without linkage, no erythromelanomas were produced.
- The red component was a xanthoerythrophoroma in the head region, and the black component was a melanoma in the tail region.

**3. Ocular melanomas are heritable but separate from cutaneous melanomas.**
- Exophthalmic ocular tumors appeared in platyfish, swordtails, and hybrids during 1943 to 1946. Most affected fish were genetically related.
- The author proposed a recessive ocular-tumor gene (oc) that interacts with the albino gene (a). The author considers ocular and cutaneous melanomas genetically unrelated.

**4. Cataracts follow an irregular, undetermined pattern.**
- A female platyfish with a cataract was mated to a normal male. Of 43 second-generation fish, one male had a cataract, but he was unable to mate.
- Ten normal second-generation pairs were mated. Three pairs each produced one cataractal fish in the third generation.
- One cataractal third-generation female, mated to her brother, produced 5 broods totaling 115 fish, 20 of which had cataracts. Inheritance could not be resolved beyond the fourth generation.

**5. Embryonic lethality follows color genotype.**
- In golden-by-albino crosses, the F2 generation from wild-colored F1 parents showed fewer albinos and golden fish than the expected 9:3:4 ratio.
- Dissection of gravid F1 females showed embryos in close to a 9 gray : 3 yellow : 4 albino ratio. The author concludes that many albino and golden embryos die shortly before full term, though the cause of death is unknown.
- [[Haskins and Haskins]] (1948) reported similar results in guppies, where albinism is described as a semi-lethal autosomal mutation.

**6. Spinal deformities show inconsistent inheritance.**
- In platyfish, a dwarf hunchback carrying a black spot behind the head was studied. The black spot is a simple Mendelian dominant. Spinal deformity inheritance was obscure: one brood of 64 included one deformed fish; a brood of 70 from two hunchbacks had none; a brood of 24 had one hunchback.
- In medaka, "wavy" and "fused" spinal deformities were reported as Mendelian, but a few normal fish appeared among offspring of abnormal parents, which typical recessive inheritance would not allow.
- Guppy spinal deformities gave clear Mendelian ratios.

**7. Gene interaction produces new patterns.**
- A comet platyfish (CoCo) crossed to a non-comet swordtail produced hybrids with a new "wagtail" pattern, with black fins and extremities. The wagtail pattern requires an extensor modifier gene (E) from the swordtail, which has no visible effect alone.
- Among wagtail-F2 offspring, the ratio was 9 wagtails : 3 comets : 4 no pattern. The wagtail blackening never becomes pathological because only micromelanophores are involved.

**8. Thyroid tumors in swordtails and trout.**
- Thyroid tumors in hatchery-reared brook trout were described earlier by Marine and Lenhart (1910) and Gaylord and Marsh (1912). Tumors invaded and replaced muscle and bone.
- Thyroid tumors also occur in laboratory-reared Montezuma swordtails. Genetic history and developmental stages of these tumors were compared between inbred laboratory sublines and wild fish from Mexico.

**9. Kidney tumors have a thyroid-cell origin and are genetically variable.**
- Kidney tumors (cystic, with papillary adenoma and adenocarcinoma features) differ in incidence among platyfish strains kept under identical conditions. Estimated incidence reached 50 percent in some lines and was zero in others.
- Baker et al. (1954) reported that thyroid glands of fish with tumorous kidneys are hyperplastic and that thyroid cells infiltrate the kidney, eye choroid, pericardium, and along blood vessels, but not the liver. Radioactive iodine studies supported thyroid-kidney cell continuity.

**10. Goldfish show abnormalities relevant to fish genetics.**
- Many goldfish variants would be considered abnormal in other species, and many are inherited in complex ways. Schlumberger (1950) reported that 5 percent of goldfish in a large urban pond developed cutaneous neurofibroma, and some had polycystic kidneys. The author suggests genetic factors may be involved.

**Conclusions:** The author states that the known facts for fishes parallel those for genetic control of disease in other vertebrates, including humans. Hereditary factors in disease are generally complex and hard to detect, but their existence is "undeniable." The spontaneous thyroid and kidney tumors are genetically influenced, though the specific genes remain unidentified. Given time, funding, and a modern research program, fish geneticists could match the gains made in plant and farm-animal breeding.
