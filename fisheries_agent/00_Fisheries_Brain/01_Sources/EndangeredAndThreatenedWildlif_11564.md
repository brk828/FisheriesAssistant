# Final Rule To Determine Lepidomeda vittata (Little Colorado Spinedace) To Be a Threatened Species With Critical Habitat

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species-act #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service issued a final rule on September 16, 1987, determining [[Lepidomeda vittata]] ([[Little Colorado Spinedace]]) to be a threatened species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. This native Arizona cyprinid, generally less than 10 centimeters (4 inches) in total length, historically occurred throughout the upper [[Little Colorado River]] drainage but is now restricted to portions of [[East Clear Creek]], [[Chevelon Creek]], [[Silver Creek]], [[Nutrioso Creek]], and the [[Little Colorado River]] in Coconino, Navajo, and Apache Counties. The rule designates critical habitat totaling approximately 44 stream miles and establishes a special rule allowing take under certain conditions when conducted in accordance with Arizona State laws. The species' decline results from habitat alteration due to impoundment, water removal, channelization, grazing, road building, urban growth, introduction of exotic predatory and competitive fish species, and use of ichthyotoxins (piscicides).

## Background & Description

[[Lepidomeda vittata]] was first collected west of the 100th meridian by members of the U.S. Topographical and Geographical Survey and described by E.D. Cope in 1874 from the "Chiquito Colorado" (later defined as the [[Little Colorado River]] between the mouth of the [[Zuni River]] and [[Sierra Blanca]]). The species is a member of the family [[Cyprinidae]] and is endemic to the upper portions of the Little Colorado River and its north-flowing, permanent tributaries on the [[Mogollon Rim]] and northern slopes of the [[White Mountains]] in eastern Arizona.

The spinedace inhabits very small to moderate sized streams and is characteristically found in pools with water flowing over fine gravel and silt-mud substrates. During drought periods, spinedace are believed to persist in springs and intermittent streambed pools; during flooding they distribute throughout the stream. The species spawns primarily in early summer but continues at reduced levels until early fall. The spinedace is "troutlike in its behavior and habitat requirements."

Current distribution includes:
1. **[[East Clear Creek]]** and tributaries (Coconino County): approximately 35 stream miles from confluence with [[Clear Creek]] to headwaters near [[Polato Lake]], flowing through [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]] and [[Coconino National Forest]]
2. **[[Chevelon Creek]]** (Navajo County): approximately 8 miles from confluence with Little Colorado River upstream to [[Bell Cow Canyon]], primarily on private lands with small portion as [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]'s [[Chevelon Creek Wildlife Area]]
3. **[[Silver Creek]]** (Navajo County): approximately 20 stream miles from confluence with Little Colorado River to headwaters near town of [[Silver Creek]], primarily on private lands
4. **[[Little Colorado River]]** (Apache County): approximately 40 miles of permanent stream from [[St. Johns]] upstream to permanent headwaters in White Mountains near [[Greer]]
5. **[[Nutrioso Creek]]** (Apache County): approximately 12 stream miles from confluence with Little Colorado River upstream to near town of [[Nutrioso]]

Populations fluctuate dramatically year to year, historically reflecting cyclic drought and rainfall periods, but human water demands have adversely altered normal fluctuations, resulting in accentuated population lows and reduced population highs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Little Colorado River]] basin historically supported extensive perennial streams with riparian areas of grasses, cottonwoods, and willows. In the 1880s, extensive swamps and marshy areas existed above [[Winslow]]. The river now has perennial flow only in the uppermost 10 to 15 percent of its length.

Approximately 150 impoundments now exist in the Little Colorado basin, ranging from small stock tanks to reservoirs up to 1,400 surface acres. These reservoirs are uninhabitable by the stream-dwelling spinedace and have inundated previously occupied habitat. Impoundments result in total or partial dewatering of downstream reaches and facilitate spread of exotic fish species.

The spinedace was historically associated with few if any fish predators. Of native species, only the [[Roundtail Chub]] ([[Gila robusta]]) was a potential predator. However, several exotic predatory fish species have been introduced in the past 100 years, including:
- [[Black Bullhead]] ([[Ictalurus melas]])
- [[Channel Catfish]] ([[Ictalurus punctatus]])
- [[Yellow Bullhead]] ([[Ictalurus natalis]])
- [[Green Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis cyanellus]])
- [[Largemouth Bass]] ([[Micropterus salmoides]])
- [[Brown Trout]] ([[Salmo trutta]])

Competitive exotic species pose additional threats, particularly [[Golden Shiner]]s in upper Chevelon Creek (present in such large numbers in 1965 that the Arizona Game and Fish Department treated the stream with piscicide) and potential introduction of [[Red Shiner]] ([[Notropis lutrensis]]), which has displaced [[Spikedace]] ([[Meda fulgida]]) in portions of the [[Gila River]] system. Other exotic cyprinids such as [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Rio Grande Killifish]] may also compete with spinedace.

## Key Findings

**Listing Determination**: The Service determined that threatened status is appropriate because of the severely reduced range and many threats to the fish and its remaining habitat. The species is still extant in several locations and threats are generally localized, so it is not in immediate danger of extinction (thus not warranted for endangered status), but could reasonably be expected to become endangered within the foreseeable future if not listed.

**Critical Habitat Designation**: Critical habitat totaling approximately 44 stream miles was designated in three areas:
1. **East Clear Creek** (Coconino County): approximately 18 miles from confluence with [[Leonard Canyon]] upstream to [[Blue Ridge Reservoir]] dam, and approximately 13 miles from upper end of Blue Ridge Reservoir upstream to Potato Lake
2. **Chevelon Creek** (Navajo County): approximately 8 miles from confluence with Little Colorado River upstream to Bell Cow Canyon
3. **Nutrioso Creek** (Apache County): approximately 5 miles from Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest boundary upstream to [[Nelson Reservoir]] dam

These areas were chosen because they presently support healthy self-perpetuating populations providing all ecological, behavioral, and physiological requirements necessary for survival. [[Silver Creek]] and Little Colorado River populations are currently spotty and/or difficult to locate but may change with periodic population fluctuations.

**Constituent elements** for all critical habitat areas include clean, permanent flowing water with pools and fine gravel or silt-mud substrate.

**Threats Analysis** (Section 4(a)(1) factors):
- **Factor A (Habitat destruction/modification)**: Most significant threat. Historic habitat adversely modified or destroyed by impoundments (approximately 150 in basin), dewatering, riparian destruction, urban growth, mining, timber harvest, road construction, livestock grazing, and watershed disturbances. Proposed future threats include [[Wilkin's Dam]] (Bureau of Reclamation project at confluence of Clear and East Clear Creeks, currently inactive), potential Arizona Public Service Corporation groundwater tapping in Chevelon Creek drainage, and nine potential recreational impoundment sites identified by Arizona Game and Fish Department.
- **Factor B (Overutilization)**: No evidence of overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific, or educational purposes.
- **Factor C (Disease or predation)**: Predation by exotic piscivorous fish is major contributing factor. Reservoir construction exacerbates threat by providing desirable habitat for predatory game fishes and encouraging their spread. Parasites introduced with exotic fish may also adversely affect spinedace.
- **Factor D (Inadequate regulatory mechanisms)**: Arizona lists species as Group 3 Threatened Wildlife, regulating direct take through collecting permits, but provides no habitat protection and no management plan exists.
- **Factor E (Other factors)**: Introduction of exotic competitive fishes poses major threat. Golden shiners in upper Chevelon Creek compete with young game fish and likely with spinedace. Red shiner poses additional competitive threat and is commonly used for bait, increasing introduction probability. Use of piscicides (rotenone, toxaphene) in major game fish streams has significantly reduced populations and range. Little Colorado River was treated from [[Lyman Reservoir]] downstream approximately 10 miles in 1951; Chevelon Creek treated twice in 1965 and again several years later.

**Special Rule**: A special rule under section 4(d) of the Act allows taking without Federal permit when conducted for educational purposes, scientific purposes, enhancement of propagation or survival, zoological exhibition, and other conservation purposes consistent with the Act, provided a State collecting permit is obtained and all State wildlife conservation laws are satisfied. The rule precludes further application of piscicides that would result in take unless in accordance with an approved conservation plan. Incidental take by State-licensed recreational fishermen is not prohibited if fish are immediately returned to habitat.

**Economic Analysis**: The Service determined the designation is not a major rule under Executive Order 12291 and will not have significant economic effect on substantial number of small entities under the Regulatory Flexibility Act. Federal program costs would be minimal, incurred as cost of planning to prevent introduction of exotic species and adverse effects from logging activities. No economic impacts on individuals or State and local governments were identified.

**Public Comments**: Fifteen comment letters were received. Support for listing and critical habitat came from The Nature Conservancy, Arizona Game and Fish Department, Desert Fishes Council, Dr. R.R. Miller, and Mr. C.O. Minckley. Opposition to listing came from Phelps Dodge Corporation. U.S. Forest Service and Arizona Department of Transportation supported listing but opposed critical habitat designation. Biological information provided by commenters resulted in adjustments to Chevelon Creek critical habitat boundaries.

**Effective Date**: October 16, 1987.
