# Methods of Collecting and Hatching Buffalo-fish Eggs at the U.S. Fisheries Biological Station, Fairport, Iowa

#ichthyology #fish-culture #Catostomidae #artificial-propagation #Mississippi-River

## Executive Summary

This document presents methods developed at the [[U.S. Fisheries Biological Station]] in [[Fairport, Iowa]] for successfully collecting and hatching eggs of buffalo-fishes (genus [[Ictiobus]]), addressing previous failures in artificial propagation of these commercially important food fishes. The work, conducted by H.L. Canfield, describes spawning timing, collection techniques utilizing commercial fishermen, egg handling procedures, and hatching protocols. A secondary article by Alfred Eastgate discusses efforts to restock [[Devils Lake]], [[North Dakota]], an alkali lake where fish populations were exterminated due to overfishing and environmental changes.

## Background & Description

The [[Buffalo-fish]] comprises three recognized species of genus [[Ictiobus]] in the [[Mississippi River]] region. These fish are commercially important food species experiencing rapidly decreasing populations. Spawning occurs ordinarily between April 15th and May 15th during spring river rises when lowlands become inundated. Prior to experimental work at [[Fairport Station]], efforts to collect and hatch buffalo-fish eggs had resulted in absolute failure or minimal success.

The eggs are light amber colored, highly glutinous, and measure 180,000 per fluid quart. Newly hatched fry are approximately three-sixteenths of an inch long, with yolk sacs completely absorbed in about three days. The fry are not cannibalistic and swim up readily after hatching.

[[Devils Lake]], [[North Dakota]] comprises approximately 45,000 acres and historically supported substantial commercial fisheries, with dealers contracting for three to seven carloads of fish weekly. Fish weighing up to thirty-five pounds were common. A severe drought in the late 1880s lowered water levels, concentrating salts to approximately thirteen percent solids, while continued intensive fishing led to complete extermination of the fish population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Buffalo-fish spawn in inundated lowlands and woods adjacent to rivers and inland sloughs during spring flooding. The fish move into these temporarily flooded areas when current is present. Many fish taken during this period remain unripe, suggesting they naturally return to the river to seek more favorable spawning grounds, necessitating vigorous collection efforts during the rising stage.

Commercial fishermen catch spawning buffalo-fish primarily with fyke-nets (provided with wings) set on inundated lands where current exists, and funnel hoop-nets used along shores and entrances to inland sloughs. Seine fishing is impractical for capturing these fish during spawning migrations.

Temperature requirements for successful hatching range from 52 to 62 degrees Fahrenheit, with optimal range of 56 to 62 degrees. Roily water presents no problems, and ordinary river water at proper temperature is preferable to clear water of lower temperature from springs. The [[Mississippi River]] provides suitable natural conditions for egg development.

## Key Findings

**Egg Collection Methods:**
- Eggs successfully collected from fish taken by commercial fishermen at fish markets, representing supply that would otherwise be lost
- Fish survive removal from water for one to four hours in cool spring weather, arriving at markets alive
- Spawntakers strip and fertilize ripe eggs promptly at market facilities
- This arrangement proves economical with minimum disruption to commercial fishing operations
- No definite injury occurs to eggs from this handling method; hatch rates and fry strength comparable to eggs from freshly caught fish

**Spawning Protocol:**
- Pan rinsed with water and drained but not dried
- Female eggs ejected by gentle pressure over ovaries toward vent
- Male milt stripped similarly into pan
- Contents stirred gently with fingers at intervals for approximately five minutes to ensure impregnation
- Two tablespoonfuls of ordinary corn starch added (specific purpose not detailed in text)
- Eggs washed by repeated gentle water changes while stirring to keep eggs separated
- Eggs become water hardened during washing process

**Transportation and Handling:**
- Eggs transported in pails if hatching battery nearby
- For shipment: eggs placed on canton flannel trays, stacked in field shipping boxes
- Upper tray serves as ice hopper, lower tray as cushion
- On arrival, eggs carefully tempered and trays moistened by floating in tanks
- Eggs scraped from trays in enmassed strips using dull hand scraper
- Eggs washed and brushed through one-eighth inch mesh bobbinet-bottomed tray into clean water
- Sifting process performed with eggs slightly immersed, using fingers or paint brush

**Hatching Process:**
- Eggs placed in [[Downing Jars]] on battery shelves with running water
- Following day requires passing eggs through bobbinet tray again to separate them
- If fungus develops, eggs washed, separated, and given quick bath in weak salt solution
- Eggs "eye" in three to four days at average temperature of 62 degrees Fahrenheit
- Hatching occurs in nine to ten days
- Very fine meshed screen required for fry retaining-tank to prevent escape

**Stakeholder Cooperation:**
- Active fishermen and market operators fully appreciated propagation work
- Complete cooperation in facilitating egg collection
- Stakeholders assisted freely with time and labor for benefit of the work

**Devils Lake Restoration Challenge:**
- Water contains approximately thirteen percent solids with mixture of several salts
- Approximately ninety-seven percent of fish introduced from fresh water died immediately
- Prof. M.A. Brannon of [[State University]] conducted systematic study of water, contents, and plant/animal life
- No definitive reason found for fish mortality despite five years of investigation
- Work continued under direction of Alfred Eastgate to implement practical applications of research findings
