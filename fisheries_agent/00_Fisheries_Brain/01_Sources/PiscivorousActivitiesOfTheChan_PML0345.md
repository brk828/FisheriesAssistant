# Piscivorous Activity of the Channel Catfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 paper by Raymond L. Busbee (Cooperative Sport Fishery Unit, University of Georgia) examines at what size [[Channel Catfish]] begin feeding on other fish. The question matters because stocking ratios for mixed [[Largemouth Bass]]–[[Bluegill]] populations depend on whether catfish compete with bass and bluegill as forage consumers or act as predators. Stomachs of 354 channel catfish were examined; 55 (about 15.5%) contained identifiable fish. None of the fish under 11 inches contained fish, and the first clear increase appeared in the 15-inch group. Based on this, the author suggests catfish of 15 inches and longer be reclassified from the "F" (forage) to the "C" (carnivore) group, but states that feeding behavior must be confirmed before the shift is justified.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), a freshwater catfish stocked in farm ponds and reservoirs.
- **Classification framework:** [[Swingle Forage-Carnivore Ratio]] (1950) divides fishes into "F" (forage) and "C" (carnivore) groups. Swingle suggested channel catfish under about 2 pounds compete with bluegills and belong in the "F" group, but large individuals might belong in "C". The F/C ratio is defined as total forage fish weight divided by total carnivorous fish weight in a population.
- **Prior work:** [[Prather]] (1959, 1964) showed channel catfish could be used effectively in farm ponds and lakes with bass and bluegill. Crawford (1958) reported that Arkansas state and private hatcheries could not meet stocking demand.
- **Study aim:** Identify the point in the life cycle at which channel catfish begin to compete with recognized piscivorous species, so more realistic stocking ratios can be recommended.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites**

- **[[High Falls Lake]]** (Butts County, Georgia): a 600-acre state-owned impoundment. It was completely drained in 1961 and restocked in 1961–1962 with largemouth bass, bluegill, [[Redear Sunfish]], [[White Bass]], threadfin shad, channel catfish, and [[Flathead Catfish]]. Fish were captured with baskets, trotlines, and gill nets.
- **[[Walton County Fish Hatchery]]:** fish were collected by draining a pond.

**Forage conditions**

Both waters had an overabundance of small and intermediate forage fish. The author had conducted annual State Game and Fish Commission population surveys on High Falls Lake for two years before and one year during the study, and an overabundance was present in all three surveys. Because small forage fish were abundant in both sites, the author argues that catfish diet reflects available prey rather than a preference for fish.

**Diet composition**

Catfish in every size class used a wide variety of food types, with no single food type dominating. The author suggests diet depends on which foods are available at the time.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**

- Channel catfish were collected over a 27-month period.
- Length was recorded to the nearest 0.1 inch and weight to the nearest 0.1 pound for fish over 8 inches total length.
- Stomach contents were checked for fish bones, scales, or whole fish as evidence of piscivory.

**Sample sizes**

- Total stomachs analyzed: 354 (306 from High Falls Lake, June 1963–March 1965; 48 from Walton County Fish Hatchery, March 1965).
- Fish baskets gave the best capture results at High Falls Lake, yielding 224 specimens.
- Stomachs containing identifiable fish or fish parts: 55 of 354.
- Empty stomachs: 76.

**Size-class results**

- No stomachs in the 8- and 10-inch groups contained other fish.
- In the 11-inch group, 3 of the stomachs analyzed contained fish.
- The first detected increase in piscivory occurred in the 15-inch group.
- The count of fish containing other fish across size classes totaled 55 (Table 1, figure 1).

**Interpretation**

- Information suggests channel catfish begin to use other fish as part of their diet near the 14- or 15-inch size class.
- Under the author's proposed framework, channel catfish 15 inches and longer "should be considered a 'C' species," but only tentatively.
- The shift cannot yet be justified. If channel catfish are aggressive feeders that expend energy capturing prey, reclassification to "C" is supported. If they are opportunists that eat only dead or dying fish (as [[Swingle Forage-Carnivore Ratio|Swingle]] 1950 suggested), the shift is not justified.

**Comparison with other catfish**

- Brown and Dendy (1961) found that no [[Flathead Catfish]] under 11 inches contained fish, matching this study's pattern.
- In contrast, [[Blue Catfish]] as small as 3 and 6 inches contained fish, and 5 of 10 blue catfish analyzed in the 8-inch group contained fish.

**Recommendations**

- Future work should study the feeding habits of channel catfish to determine whether they are aggressive predators.
- Mixed-population stocking ratios depend on the size at which catfish become piscivorous, so this threshold is central to fisheries management.
