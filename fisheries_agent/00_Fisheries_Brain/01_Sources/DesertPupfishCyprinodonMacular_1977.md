# Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*)

## Executive Summary
The [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius* Baird and Girard, 1853) is a small cyprinodontiform fish historically distributed across the [[Gila River]] basin of Arizona and northern Sonora, the lower [[Colorado River]] (including the [[Salton Sea]] and its tributaries) in southeastern California and Mexico, and the [[Rio Sonoyta]] basin including [[Quitobaquito Springs]] in Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona. The species has suffered severe range contraction due to anthropogenic water management (irrigation, groundwater extraction), damming/diversion of the Colorado River, and introduction of exotic predators and competitors. It is now extinct in the Gila River basin of Arizona (except at Quitobaquito), severely reduced in the Rio Sonoyta, and badly depleted in the Colorado River delta and Salton Sea. Conservation measures include a refuge population maintained at [[Anza-Borrego State Park]] in California, with proposals for additional protective and monitoring actions.

## Background & Description
- Small-bodied member of the family [[Cyprinodontidae]] (pupfishes).
- Specific morphometric data not detailed in this status report; taxonomic distinction noted between *C. macularius* and the related species *[[Cyprinodon nevadensis]]* (per Miller 1943 reference).
- The Quitobaquito Springs population is recognized as a **distinctive subspecies**, warranting separate conservation attention.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** Warm to cool springs, marshes (ciénegas), small creeks, and lagunas.
- **Water chemistry:** Tolerates highly alkaline and moderate to strongly saline waters — indicative of considerable physiological tolerance to osmotic and thermal stress (see [[Lowe & Heath 1969]] on thermal/physiological responses).
- **Depth preference:** Typically found in depths of less than 1 m.
- **Vegetation association:** Often associated with abundant aquatic vegetation, likely providing refuge and food resources.
- **Diet:** Documented at Quitobaquito Springs (Cox 1972) — food habits studied in this population specifically.
- **Social behavior:** Documented under both field and aquarium conditions (Barlow 1961), suggesting territorial/reproductive behavioral complexity typical of pupfishes.
- **Breeding ecology:** Wild breeding rate unknown; captive breeding potential rated as **good** (Crear & Haydock 1971 demonstrated successful laboratory rearing).

## Key Findings
- **Historic range:** [[Gila River]] basin (Arizona, northern Sonora), lower [[Colorado River]] (Salton Sea and tributaries, Sonora, Baja California del Norte), and [[Rio Sonoyta]] basin (including Quitobaquito Springs).
- **Current status:** 
  - Extinct in the Gila River basin of Arizona, except at Quitobaquito Springs.
  - Severely reduced in the Rio Sonoyta basin.
  - Badly depleted in the Colorado River delta and Salton Sea populations.
- **Primary threats:** Irrigation and groundwater practices, exotic species introductions (predators and competitors), and damming/diversion of the Colorado River.
- **Conservation actions taken:** A refuge stock derived from the [[Salton Sea]] population is maintained in a concrete pool at [[Anza-Borrego State Park]], Borrego Valley, San Diego County, California.
- **Conservation actions proposed:**
  - Monitor and protect the distinctive Quitobaquito Springs subspecies, focusing on water supply security, exclusion of exotic species, and habitat maintenance.
  - Maintain and expand refuge populations at Anza-Borrego State Park (establish a second refuge).
  - Broader protective measures proposed for Arizona populations and the Gila River basin.
- **Captivity status:** Precise captive population numbers unknown; breeding potential in captivity rated as good, supported by successful laboratory rearing protocols (Crear & Haydock 1971).
- **Key research references:** Behavioral studies (Barlow 1961), ecological/breeding notes (Cowles 1934), diet studies at Quitobaquito (Cox 1972), captive rearing (Crear & Haydock 1971), thermal physiology (Lowe & Heath 1969), and taxonomic status (Miller 1943).
