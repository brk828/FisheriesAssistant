# The Frankenstein Effect: Impact of Introduced Fishes on Native Fishes in North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This review, a draft by [[Peter B. Moyle]], [[Hiram W. Li]], and [[Bruce A. Barton]] (Oregon Cooperative Fishery Research Unit, Oregon State University), examines the "Frankenstein Effect," the broad-scale and often unforeseen harm caused by fish introductions made to solve local problems. It documents the extent of introduced fishes in North America, their effects on native species, the mechanisms by which they displace natives, and whether [[Ecological Theory]] can predict introduction impacts. The authors conclude that virtually any species can establish outside its native range, that introductions frequently harm natives, and that further introductions are unlikely to be warranted in most waters.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with an analogy to [[Mary Shelley]]'s novel *Frankenstein, or the Modern Prometheus* (1818), arguing that fisheries managers often pursue narrow fixes without considering wider consequences. The first documented introduction into North America was of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) into the Hudson River, New York, in 1831, though the authors note doubt about whether those were true carp. Introductions of [[Goldfish]] and interbasin transfers of native fishes likely predate this. Introductions have grown with culture techniques, holding methods, and rapid transport. The authors identify [[Fish Introductions]] in North America as among the most extensive worldwide, with well over 140 freshwater species having ranges expanded by introduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **East of the Rocky Mountains:** introduced species usually make up less than 10% of drainage species lists, with exceptions in [[Florida]], some small coastal drainages, and the [[Great Lakes]].
- **Western United States:** introduced species make up 35 to 59% of faunal lists; percentages are lower in Canada and Alaska.
- **Depauperate systems:** introduced fishes dominate western streams and coldwater lakes, reflecting angler dissatisfaction with natives plus environmental change such as dams and diversions, rather than intrinsic traits of the fishes.
- **Estuaries:** introduced species mirror adjacent streams, being common on the west coast and uncommon on the east coast.
- **Marine:** introduced species are rare, likely because few marine introductions have been attempted.
- **Confounding change:** effects are hard to separate from habitat alteration. The replacement of the [[Colorado River]] native fauna reflects dams, water-quality change, and eradication programs as well as competition. Replacement of [[Spotfin Shiner]] by [[Red Shiner]] in Illinois tracks increasing stream turbidity.

## Key Findings

**1. Introductions are widespread and nearly any species can establish.** Virtually any species can become established outside its native range in suitable habitat, and virtually any fish-supporting aquatic environment can support introduced fishes.

**2. Documented effects fall into four categories.**
- *Elimination:* green sunfish replaced cyprinids in North Carolina headwaters; [[Lake Trout]] and large coregonids in the Great Lakes were driven to near or actual extinction by [[Sea Lamprey]] predation and [[Alewife]] competition and predation; brown trout replaced [[Brook Trout]] over 15 years in a Minnesota stream; the [[Mojave Tui Chub]] was eliminated by [[Arroyo Chub]]; peacock cichlid (*Cichla ocellaris*) caused rapid native declines in a Central American lake.
- *Reduced growth and survival:* redside shiner introduction reduced young rainbow trout growth and survival in Paul Lake, while large trout benefited; mysid introduction reduced zooplankton and hurt [[Kokanee]]; yellow perch established in a small Ontario lake, reducing trout growth and returns.
- *Community structure changes:* natives shifted niches, for example brook trout moved to faster water when brown trout arrived, and bloaters shifted to benthos in Lake Michigan with alewives present, later showing reduced gill raker counts.
- *No demonstrable impact:* some introductions, such as four Eurasian cyprinids, remained in small localized populations, though the authors caution that such "no impact" conclusions require careful study.

**3. Six mechanisms enable displacement:** competition (interference and exploitation), predation, inhibition of reproduction, environmental modification, transfer of parasites and diseases, and hybridization. These usually succeed only in human-altered environments.
- *Competition:* brown trout displaced brook trout from holding areas by aggression; creek chub limited brook trout feeding on benthos in lakes.
- *Predation:* centrarchid basses likely prevent native minnows from reestablishing in reservoirs; brown trout reduced a small native sucker; razorback sucker fails to reproduce due to predation by introduced red shiners.
- *Reproductive inhibition:* constant harassment by female mosquitofish inhibits Sonoran topminnow reproduction.
- *Environmental modification:* [[Grass Carp]] and common carp destroy plants, increase turbidity, and reduce sight-feeding native fishes, though rigorous causal testing is lacking.
- *Parasites and diseases:* [[Whirling Disease]] (*Myxosoma cerebralis*) spread from brown trout to rainbow trout and reached North America in frozen rainbow trout in 1957.
- *Hybridization:* cutthroat trout hybridize with introduced rainbow trout, and the arroyo chub eliminated the Mohave chub via hybridization.

**4. The "vacant niche" assumption is flawed.** Introductions assume unfilled niches exist, but under [[Hutchinsonian Niche]] theory a successful introduction compresses the realized niches of resident species. Apparent vacancy usually reflects unmeasured resource use.

**5. Three predictive approaches are proposed, each with weaknesses.**
- *Island biogeography:* species number depends on the species pool, isolation, system size and age, and habitat diversity. [[Tonn and Magnuson]] (1982) showed piscivore presence structures northern Wisconsin lake communities.
- *Limiting similarity:* morphological gaps may indicate available niche space, but it works poorly in complex communities and was not useful for predicting introduction impact in [[Simberloff]] (1981).
- *Food web analysis:* simpler webs are likely more stable; introductions will likely substantially alter food web structure, with outcomes sensitive to time lags, interaction strength, connectance, and trophic levels.

**6. Conclusions and management recommendations.** Introduced gamefish now support sport fisheries in many waters. The authors estimate that 25 to 50% of freshwater fishes caught in the continental United States and Canada come from introduced populations. This benefit has come at the cost of native losses, threatened species, and unstable communities. The authors recommend against further introductions in most waters until their dynamics are better understood, and urge closer collaboration between fisheries managers and academic ecologists, supporting ecologically oriented research not tied to immediate local problems.
