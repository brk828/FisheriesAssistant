# Distribution and Abundance of Plecoptera in a Regulated Rocky Mountain River

#entomology #freshwater-ecology #plecoptera

## Executive Summary

This 1989 study by [[Jack A. Stanford]] and [[James V. Ward]] examines the distribution and abundance of stoneflies (Plecoptera) along the entire 329 km course of the [[Gunnison River]], Colorado, a tributary of the [[Colorado River]]. Eleven sites spanning 2900 to 1400 m a.s.l. were sampled year-round between September 1979 and October 1980. Twenty-two species were recorded. Stonefly richness and abundance were depressed below [[Hypolimnial-Release Dams]] and recovered roughly 80 km downstream, producing the pattern predicted by the [[Serial Discontinuity Concept]] and consistent with the [[River Continuum Concept]].

## Background & Description

Stoneflies are aquatic insect larvae that are sensitive to changes in temperature and substrate, and their abundance is often greatly reduced below hypolimnial-release dams. Few earlier studies had quantified zoobenthos responses to [[Stream Regulation]] along an entire river continuum, and none had covered a whole catchment regulated by a series of such dams. The authors focus on Plecoptera; the responses of other zoobenthos groups, including [[Trichoptera]] and [[Ephemeroptera]], are reported separately.

The Gunnison River heads on the western slope of the Continental Divide at 3609 m a.s.l. and falls 2224 m to the confluence with the Colorado River at 1385 m a.s.l. The upper basin is mesic and drains relatively insoluble crystalline bedrock, while the lower basin drains mineral-rich sedimentary formations of the semiarid [[Colorado Plateau]]. Dissolved solids increase nearly two orders of magnitude from headwaters to confluence.

Regulation is provided by [[Taylor Park Reservoir]] in the upper basin, a series of three deep-release reservoirs in the middle reaches ([[Blue Mesa Reservoir]], [[Morrow Point Reservoir]]), and [[Crystal Reservoir]], a shallower reregulation unit that dampens flow fluctuations from hydropower generation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat.** Substrata are rubble-boulder and cobble along the whole profile. Armouring occurs below the dams, and sands and silts reduce interstitial space in the aggraded downstream reach. Physical habitat is otherwise considered generally suitable for stoneflies.

**Thermal regime.** Winter-warm, summer-cool modification of annual temperature patterns occurs below the dams, persisting about 15 km downstream of Taylor Park and about 80 km below Crystal Reservoir. Annual degree days and temperature ranges differ among sites (Figure 1 of the source).

**Proposed mechanisms.** The authors attribute the depressed communities to:
- Non-scouring, nutrient-rich reservoir releases that promote standing crops of the filamentous green alga [[Cladophora Glomerata]], which alters substrate conditions.
- Armouring of the substrate at regulated sites, which makes it very stable.
- Reduced thermal amplitude, which likely prevents successful emergence or hatching of many native stoneflies.
- High dissolved solids at nearly all sites.

**Sampling design.** Unregulated sites lay above reservoirs or well below dams (Site 3 was 33 km below Taylor Park Dam; Site 8 was 56 km below Crystal Dam). Regulated sites were chosen to measure the response to upstream impoundment, and the reregulated Site 7 tested the efficacy of the reregulation strategy. Tributaries entering below the dams (East River, North Fork, Uncompahgre River) were noted as potential confounders. Sampling used a [[Surber Sampler]]-type device (150 µm mesh) sampling 0.5 m² of [[Rubble-Boulder Riffles]], with three samples per site per occasion. Larvae were identified by [[Baumann et al. 1977]] and verified against adult collections and a reference collection at the [[Flathead Lake Biological Station]]. Abundance was measured as numbers and dry mass (60 °C constant mass).

## Key Findings

**Overall fauna**
- Twenty-two Plecoptera species were identified. Species and distribution patterns were broadly comparable to those of the pristine [[St. Vrain River]] system (33 species recorded there by Ward, 1982). [[Baumann et al. 1977]] reported 49 species within the Gunnison River system.
- Several species, including the perlodids *Megarcys signata* and *Kogotus modestus*, occur in the unregulated East Fork of the Gunnison, which was not sampled. The authors conclude these were probably absent or scarce at the study sites.
- Site 1 (4th order, upstream) was too far from the source to characterise the headwater stonefly community.

**Longitudinal patterns at unregulated sites**
- Species richness showed a unimodal pattern, peaking at Site 4.
- Twenty-one of the 22 species occurred in the upper basin, but only 16 in the lower basin.
- Of the 14 species abundant in the upper basin, only nine were also abundant at some lower-basin sites.
- Species found at nearly all unregulated sites were winter or early-spring emergers (*Prostoia besametsa*, *Taenionema pacificum*, *Capnia confusa*).
- Mean density and biomass in the upper basin (Sites 1, 3, 4) were about 2 to 3 times those in the lower basin (Sites 8–11): 182 versus 93 nymphs m⁻², and 382 versus 116 mg dry weight m⁻².
- Lower-basin density and biomass were almost entirely *Isoperla* spp., which reached about 200–400 organisms m⁻² in late winter and spring. Other species averaged under one organism per sample.

**Responses to regulation**
- **Headwater reservoir (Sites 1–2–3):** Diversity fell from 16 species at Site 1 to 4 at Site 2 below the headwater reservoir, then returned to near original levels by Site 3. Biomass and density below the dam were dominated by *Suwallia pallidula* and *Sweltsa coloradensis*, which were very abundant at Site 1.
- **Middle-reach dams:** Only two species occurred at Site 5 below Blue Mesa Reservoir, both at mean densities well under 1 nymph m⁻². No stoneflies were found below Morrow Point Reservoir.
- **Reregulation (Site 7, below Crystal Reservoir):** The community was much more diverse and abundant than at the other middle-reach regulated sites. *Hesperoperla pacifica* (15 nymphs m⁻²), *Skwala parallela* (5 nymphs m⁻²), and *Diura knowltoni* (8 nymphs m⁻²) were most abundant. The authors describe the shallow dam as ameliorating negative conditions, but the river remains summer-cool, extending rhithron characteristics about 10 species well into the lower river.
- **Recovery downstream:** The community recovered about 80 km below the last dam (15 species; 244 organisms; 250 mg m⁻²). At the most downstream site (11), only four species were present (four organisms; 16 mg m⁻²).
- **Abstract caveat:** The abstract's density and biomass figures for the reduced below-dam community (nine species; 35 organisms; 180 mg m⁻²) are partly illegible in the scanned text, so they are not reproduced here.

**Historical comparison**
- Knight and Argyle (1962), sampling 28 June–19 July 1961 before inundation, recorded a preimpoundment fauna remarkably similar to that at unregulated Site 4.
- *Pteronarcys californica*, *Pteronarcella badia*, *Hesperoperla pacifica*, and *Claassenia sabulosa* were common before regulation and remain so at Site 4.

**Ecosystem concepts**
- Speciosity and abundance peak at Sites 4 and 8, supporting both the [[River Continuum Concept]] (Vannote et al., 1980) and the Serial Discontinuity Concept (Ward and Stanford, 1983).
- Regulation eliminated or reduced stonefly communities, which then recovered downstream. Conditions at Site 4 probably extended only to about Site 6 before regulation, implying a downstream discontinuity of at least 68 km.
- Stonefly patterns are consistent with a broader faunal shift. Native [[Colorado River Fishes]] (Cyprinidae and Catostomidae) that inhabited the pre-regulation potamon reach (Sites 7–11) have been replaced by a productive [[Salmonid]] fishery typical of rhithron biocoenosis (Stanford and Ward, 1986).

**Conclusion**
The authors conclude that the distribution pattern is a classic serial discontinuity caused by hypolimnial stream regulation in a temperate-latitude river. Reregulation at Crystal Reservoir partly mitigates the damage, but the mainstem fauna is still reduced relative to unregulated reaches.
