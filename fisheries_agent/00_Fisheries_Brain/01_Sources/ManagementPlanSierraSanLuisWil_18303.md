# Sierra San Luis Wild and Aquatic Flora and Fauna Protection Area Management Plan

#conservation #protected-areas #biodiversity #Mexico #Sierra-Madre-Occidental #ecosystem-management

## Executive Summary

This management plan proposes the establishment of the "Sierra San Luis" Wild and Aquatic Flora and Fauna Protection Area, encompassing 59,000 hectares in northwestern Mexico along the border with the United States. The area spans the municipalities of [[Agua Prieta]] (Sonora) and [[Janos]] (Chihuahua) and forms part of the extreme northern [[Sierra Madre Occidental]]. The reserve is divided into two management zones: a Protection and Natural Resources Recovery zone (14,400 hectares) and an Extensive Use zone (44,600 hectares). The area hosts significant biodiversity including 157 plant species, 395 vertebrate species (70 under federal protection), and critical habitats ranging from mixed conifer forests at elevations up to 2,520 meters to natural grasslands. Economic activities include cattle ranching and beargrass harvesting. The plan emphasizes community-based conservation with direct management by landowners coordinated through governmental agencies.

## Background & Description

The [[Sierra San Luis]] reserve is situated between 31°05'-31°19' N latitude and 108°40'30"-108°58' W longitude, forming part of two major physiographic regions of the [[Sierra Madre Occidental]]: the subprovince of range and canyons of the North (high-site topography) and the subprovince of plains and meadows of the North (hills and plains grasslands). The area exhibits broken, irregular topography with elevations from 1,300 to 2,520 meters above sea level, encompassing three principal topographic forms: a mountainous mass (the Sierra San Luis itself, approximately 25 km long by 8 km wide), hill country in the southwest, and plains in the northwest.

The reserve contains four biotic communities according to Brown and Lowe (1980): [[Madrean Montane Conifer Forest]], [[Madrean Evergreen Forest]], Plains Grasslands, and Gran Bolson (Chihuahua Desert) Grasslands. Climate types range from dry (mean annual precipitation 334.8 mm, temperature 17°C) at lower elevations to temperate and semi-cold in mountain zones (precipitation ~500 mm, temperatures 10-12°C). The area forms part of two watersheds: the [[Rio Yaqui]] basin (Hydrologic Region No. 9) draining westward through [[San Bernardino Arroyo]] and [[Cajon Bonito]], and the [[Rio Casas Grandes]] watershed (Hydrologic Region No. 34) on the eastern flanks.

Soils are predominantly litosol (>70% of surface area) in higher elevations, regosol (~25%) in lower areas, and xerosol (<5%) in the lowest parts. The dominant geology consists of rhyolite and acid-tufa rocks from the Tertiary period (>70% of area), with Tertiary conglomerates in bajada regions and alluvial soils in specific canyon sites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flora**: The reserve contains 157 plant species across 49 families. Only [[Juglans major]] (walnut) is federally protected, though [[Coryphanta robbinsorum]] and [[Talinum marginatum]] are considered rare species. Economically important species include beargrass ([[Nolina microcarpa]]), harvested for broom manufacture. Exotic species identified include eucalyptus (Eucalipthus sp.), Bermuda grass ([[Cyonodon dactylon]]), [[Roripa nasturtium-aquaticum]], [[Oxalis corniculata]], and [[Galium aparine]].

Vegetation communities include: (1) Conifer forests dominated by [[Pinus ponderosa]], [[Pinus discolor]], [[Pinus engelmanii]], and [[Pseudotsuga mentziesii]] (Douglas fir) above 2,100 m elevation; (2) Oak-juniper forest with [[Pinus ponderosa]], [[Pinus discolor]], and [[Juniperus deppeana]]; (3) Oak forest (>70% of reserve surface) dominated by [[Quercus arizonica]], [[Q. oblongifolia]], [[Q. hypoleucoides]], and [[Q. rugosa]] at 1,800-2,100 m; (4) Chaparral with [[Arctostaphylos pungens]] (manzanita) and [[Quercus toumeyii]]; (5) Riparian/gallery forest with [[Populus fremontii]] (cottonwood), [[Salix exigua]] (willow), [[Juglans major]], [[Cupressus arizonica]], [[Prosopis pubescens]], and [[Prosopis velutina]]; and (6) Grasslands with [[Bouteloua]] species, [[Prosopis]] spp., [[Yucca baccata]], and [[Nolina microcarpa]].

**Fauna**: The reserve hosts 395 vertebrate species, with 70 under federal protection (38 threatened, 10 endangered, 13 special protection, 15 rare).

*Mammals* (76 species, 19 families): Four protected species include [[Ursus americanus]] (black bear, endangered, presence verified 1991 and 1993), [[Canis lupus baileyi]] (Mexican gray wolf, within historic range), [[Panthera onca]] (jaguar), and possibly [[Antilocapra americana mexicana]] (pronghorn antelope). Game species include [[Sylvilagus audubonii]] and [[S. floridaaus]] (rabbits), [[Canis latrans]] (coyote), [[Lepus alleni]] and [[L. californicus]] (jackrabbits), [[Odocoileus hemionus]] (mule deer), [[Lynx rufus]] (bobcat), [[Tayassu tajacu]] (collared peccary), and [[Odocoileus virginianus]] (white-tailed deer).

*Birds* (248 species): Thirty species federally protected (4 endangered, 20 threatened, 4 special protection, 2 rare). Notable species include [[Strix occidentalis]] (Mexican spotted owl, threatened), endangered falcons ([[Falco columbarius]], [[F. femoralis]], [[F. mexicanus]], [[F. peregrinus]]), and game species [[Meleagris gallopavo]] (wild turkey), [[Callipepla gambelii]] (Gambel's quail), [[Zenaida asiatica]] (white-winged dove), [[Zenaida macroura]] (mourning dove), and [[Columbina fasciata]] (band-tailed pigeon).

*Reptiles* (51 species, 11 families, 38.93% of state total): Twenty-two species protected (10 threatened, 7 special protection, 5 rare). Notable discovery of [[Crotalus willardi obscurus]] (ridgenosed rattlesnake subspecies) with restricted mountain distribution. Endangered species include [[Micruroides euryxanthus]] (Sonora coral snake), [[Lampropeltis getulus]] (common kingsnake), [[Lampropeltis pyromelana]] (Sonora milksnake), multiple rattlesnakes ([[Crotalus atrox]], [[C. lepidus kauberi]], [[C. molossus]], [[C. pricei pricei]], [[C. scutulatus scutulatus]]), and [[Terrapene ornata]] (turtle).

*Amphibians* (11 species, 29.73% of state total): Five species (46.45%) federally listed: [[Rana chiricahuensis]] (Chiricahua frog, threatened), [[Ambystoma tigrinum]] (tiger salamander, special protection), [[Rana berlandieri]] (leopard frog, special protection), and [[Rana yavapaiensis]] (lowland leopard frog, rare). The first and last species have experienced dramatic population declines and extirpations from many localities in Arizona, California, and New Mexico over the last 15 years.

*Fish* (9 species, 8 native, 1 introduced, 14.75% of state total): Seven of eight native species (87.50%) federally listed. The [[Rio Yaqui]] watershed within the reserve has experienced expansion of exotic fish species, which deplete native populations. [[Cajon Bonito]] is described as "one of most intact low-desert water courses in the American Southwest" (Rinne and Minckley, 1991), with recent studies confirming continued good condition. Sonoran populations remain in good condition despite declines/disappearances in United States sites.

**Socioeconomic Context**: The reserve has extremely low population density (~1 inhabitant per 15.5 km²), with only 6 dwellings housing 16 adults (2.7 people per residence). All 59,000 hectares are private property divided among 22 parcels, with 9 properties entirely within the conservation zone. No property boundary controversies exist.

Economic development centers on cattle ranching in northern and western areas utilizing natural pasturage. The 1993 cattle census for nine parcels within the reserve recorded 707 cows, 115 bulls, 206 heifers, 97 calves, 124 female calves, 16 horses, and 12 mules/donkeys (total ~1,249 bovines). Current exploitation averages 12.9 hectares/A.U., above the recommended 19 hectares/A.U. given current pasturage conditions. Beargrass harvesting occurs seasonally with authorization from property owners; in 1992 the municipality produced 1,500 tons valued at 225 million new pesos, with 1994 prices of 250 pesos/ton (unthreshed) and 950 pesos/ton (threshed). Harvesting employs ~9 people (5 cutters, 2 loaders, 1 trucker, 1 cook) working from mobile camps, paying landowners 40 pesos/ton.

## Key Findings

**Conservation Problems Identified**:
- Possible overgrazing damage, particularly in [[Madrean Evergreen Forest]] and conifer forest rocky areas with lowest load capacity
- Physical damage to lower vegetation strata from cattle trampling, affecting rare plant species
- Vegetation cover loss from forest fires with consequent habitat loss and reduced pasturage capacity
- Introduction of exotic flora and fauna species transmitting diseases, competing with, and preying on native species
- Decline of critical species populations, particularly documented for certain groups
- Insufficient knowledge of current species occurrence and regional biological wealth, especially flora
- Illegal hunting of deer, [[Ursus americanus|black bear]], and [[Meleagris gallopavo|wild turkey]]; irresponsible permit use including exceeding authorized limits and abandoning captured organisms

**Management Objectives**:
- Achieve long-term sustainable pasturage use through methods compatible with ecosystem conservation
- Protect, conserve, and recover vegetational cover including forest fire prevention and control
- Protect, conserve, and recover native flora and fauna species
- Protect habitats of special interest based on pristine characteristics and/or special-interest species occurrence
- Undertake/foster research projects applicable to resource management and conservation

**Management Structure**: Proposed organizational framework includes: (1) Governing Entity led by INE-SEDESOL coordinating state government, federal agencies (SARH), municipality of Agua Prieta, and property owner representatives; (2) Property Owners Council comprising all participating landowners establishing board and representative; (3) Technical-Scientific Consulting Council of national/international scholars and researchers; (4) Reserve Administration responsible for liaison and executing Annual Operating Programs.

**Management Programs**: Four primary programs established: (1) **Conservation Program** with sub-programs for flora/fauna (specific management plans for critical species, control of exotic species, reintroduction programs), soil/vegetation restoration (fire prevention/control, pasturage maintenance, erosion control, native species propagation), and inspection/patrol (monitoring compliance, communication systems, access control); (2) **Sustainable Use Program** with sub-programs for cattle ranching (rational pasturage use, agrostologic techniques, technical extension) and forest/fauna use (beargrass quotas, cynegetic use of white-tailed deer and wild turkey); (3) **Research and Monitoring Program** (biological and socioeconomic indicators, priority research projects, monitoring [[Cajon Bonito]] for exotic fish, impact evaluation of exotic species, populational status assessment); (4) **Administration and Operations Program** (organizational structure formation, property owners council establishment, liaison hiring, accounting system, Annual Operating Programs development).

**Zoning Regulations**: 
*Extensive Natural Resource Use Zone*: Beargrass harvest subject to SARH-determined volumes/techniques; harvest on owner-agreed terms; new population centers prohibited; cattle-ranching per State of Sonora law; minimum infrastructure for productive activities; predator control evaluated by Scientific-Technical Committee using least-impact methods; exotic species introduction only with management justification (fish permanently barred).

*Protection and Natural Resource Recovery Zone*: Forest harvesting beyond domestic use prohibited; mining exploration/exploitation prohibited; cynegetic use through annual-harvest programs complying with INE-SEDESOL requirements; exotic species introduction prohibited under all circumstances; new productive activities prohibited except tourism/recreation; minimum infrastructure for existing activities and resource conservation.

**Financing Strategy**: Initial stage supported through federal/state environmental program funds and private national/foreign conservation funds for minimum infrastructure, personnel, and basic management actions. Short/intermediate coordination between public/private sectors to decrease operational costs and maximize infrastructure/personnel access. INE-SEDESOL funds guarantee inspection/patrol development with complementary funding from organizations/companies. Intermediate/long-range financing from strengthened strategies and property owner actions to improve range/flora/fauna management supporting economic activities.

**Planning System**: Four-stage process per National System of Democratic Planning: (1) Formulation (this management plan); (2) Implementation (Annual Operation Programs developed/executed from management programs); (3) Control and follow-up (trimestral reports to property owners council and Governing Entity); (4) Evaluation (financial audits, annual evaluation reports analyzing programmed vs. achieved results, overall reserve changes, strategic indicators). Annual evaluations permit continuous feedback between AOPs and management plan.
