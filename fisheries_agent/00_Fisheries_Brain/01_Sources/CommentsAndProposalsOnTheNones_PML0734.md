# Comments and Proposals on the Nonessential Experimental Population Designation of Colorado Squawfish in the Lower Colorado River (1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of correspondence and review materials from October 1987 concerning a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposal to reintroduce an endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) into the lower [[Colorado River]] between [[Parker Dam]] and [[Imperial Dam]] as a nonessential experimental population under Section 10(j) of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The package includes a letter from Dr. Paul Marsh (Arizona State University) opposing the nonessential designation, a Federal Register notice, a draft [[Environmental Assessment]], a letter from the [[Desert Fishes Council]], an Arizona Game and Fish Department letter supporting the proposal, and a letter from Dr. W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) supporting an essential experimental designation.

Reviewers agreed on the goal of reintroduction but disagreed sharply on the legal status. Critics argued that a nonessential designation would exclude the population from recovery contribution, weaken protection, and permit sport harvest prematurely. The Arizona Game and Fish Department supported the proposal, with the stated expectation that the population would be managed as a put-and-take fishery.

## Background & Description

The species is an endangered large-bodied cyprinid of the Colorado River basin, historically abundant in the lower basin. The proposal would authorize a population established from hatchery-reared broodstock, with the Service's Environmental Assessment describing the action as needed to reintroduce the fish to its historic habitat in the lower Colorado River, as recommended in the [[Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan]] and by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

Key regulatory elements of the proposed special rule (50 CFR 17.84(f)) include:
- Take permitted for educational, scientific, propagation, and exhibition purposes consistent with the Act.
- Incidental take during State-permitted recreational fishing, with fish immediately returned to habitat.
- Take in accordance with State regulations during open season.
- Take in violation of State law treated as a violation of the Act; possession, sale, or transport of illegally taken fish prohibited.
- Release sites in Yuma and Paz Counties, Arizona, and Riverside and San Bernardino Counties, California.

The Environmental Assessment notes that the population would be isolated from native upper-basin populations by approximately 500 miles of mainstem river and five mainstem dams, so the two could not interbreed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Environmental Assessment describes the lower Colorado River as a warm-water stream at elevations of roughly 100 to 300 feet, with flows governed by dam discharge. Aquatic habitats in Arizona's Sonoran Desert are dominated by exotic fishes, which outnumber native species four to one. Of Arizona's 27 native fish species, 81 percent are listed by the State or the Service as endangered or threatened, and no native species remain in the lower Colorado River. Exotic species named include [[Tilapia]], [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Red Shiner]].

Commenters emphasized that remaining suitable habitat for the species is largely restricted to the mainstem, that extensive backwaters and off-channel habitats within the [[Lower Colorado River National Wildlife Refuge]] lands are highly suitable for reintroduction, and that changed fish communities since the 1970s–1940s may now permit persistence of reintroduced natives. The [[Bonytail]] was cited as an example of a native that failed when exposed to introduced centrarchids.

## Key Findings

- **Legal status dispute:** Marsh and the Desert Fishes Council argued that a nonessential designation would exclude the population from recovery contribution and would be self-defeating; both urged an essential experimental designation instead.
- **Recovery contribution:** Commenters asked how a population designated nonessential could help recovery, and what its status would be if one or more upper-basin native populations were lost.
- **Dispersal and escape:** Reviewers noted that squawfish in the upper basin and related species (e.g., [[Razorback Sucker]]) move considerable distances, so fish could escape into canals, backwaters, and tributaries, raising questions about their legal status.
- **Management plans:** Marsh and the Desert Fishes Council stated that detailed plans for monitoring, experimental stocking, and research were not provided, and that the proposal lacked a research program with clear questions.
- **Sport fishery concern:** The Desert Fishes Council considered a sport fishery premature, since management strategies to alleviate the species' endangerment had not been developed, implemented, or evaluated.
- **Law enforcement:** Marsh noted that varying State fishing regulations across river sections would require complex enforcement; Arizona's Game and Fish Department stated it would promulgate regulations allowing controlled sport take.
- **Federal protection:** Under a nonessential designation, Section 7(a)(2) consultation would not apply. Marsh argued that refuge lands would still be fully protected, and that the proposal should state whether Section 7 consultation applies to Federal lands.
- **Arizona Game and Fish position:** The Department supported the proposal but stated that the population should not count toward recovery unless it becomes self-sustaining through natural reproduction, which it did not expect given habitat conditions. It expected to manage the population as a put-and-take fishery and emphasized that broodstock would need genetic management, including regular infusions of wild-caught fish.
- **Alternatives:** The Environmental Assessment concluded that without nonessential designation the reintroduction would not occur. The essential experimental alternative was rejected because the Service judged the population would not be essential to the species' survival; Minckley argued for the essential designation.
- **Monitoring:** The Service and the State would monitor the population, with a 10-year reintroduction period planned before final success is determined.
- **Minckley's proposal:** Minckley supported an essential experimental population in the reach between Parker and Imperial dams, citing its historic abundance, suitable habitat, and near-complete federal ownership.
