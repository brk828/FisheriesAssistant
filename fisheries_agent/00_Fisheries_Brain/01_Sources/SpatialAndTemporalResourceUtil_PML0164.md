# Spatial and Temporal Resource Utilization by Cyprinid Fishes in Black Creek, Mississippi, and Von Bertalanffy Growth Rates in Mustelus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #triakidae

## Executive Summary

This Copeia (1981, No. 1) document combines two papers. The main paper by Baker and Ross examines how eight co-occurring cyprinid minnows of the upper [[Black Creek]] system in southern Mississippi partition their resources. The authors find that spatial segregation, mainly by stream size, vertical water-column position, and use of aquatic vegetation, is the dominant axis of partitioning, supplemented by temporal separation in [[Diel Feeding Periodicity]]. A shorter second paper, which is partly truncated in the scan, applies [[Holden's Growth Rate Method]] to estimate [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Parameters]] for [[Mustelus]] smooth-hound sharks from embryonic growth data.

## Background & Description

The cyprinid family dominates the ichthyofauna of most North American streams, and multispecies assemblages of [[Notropis]] minnows are common. Prior work on fish assemblages focused mainly on dietary differences, while more recent studies suggested that spatial partitioning may be of primary importance in aquatic systems. Schoener (1974) found spatial separation to be the most common form of resource partitioning but tentatively suggested it might be less important among aquatic organisms.

The study area was the [[Black Creek]] system, which rises in Jefferson Davis County about 50 km northwest of Hattiesburg and flows into the [[Pascagoula River]]. The study area covered roughly 826 km². Waters carried few suspended solids but were brownish from dissolved humic substances, with pH 5.2 to 6.8. Streams ranged from 1 to 40 m wide, and gradients ranged from over 6 m/km in some tributaries to 1.1 m/km in most of Black Creek. The area was relatively free of industrial and municipal pollution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Between September 1975 and January 1977, 84 stations were sampled, and 34 stations were used in the analysis. The total sample was 223 collections: about two-thirds (145) were made with a 6.1 m × 1.2 m, 3.1 mm mesh [[Seine Netting]], and the remaining collections came from 41 hours of [[Underwater Visual Observation]] by mask and snorkel (April to November 1976). Microhabitats were defined a priori by physical characteristics. Variables recorded included surface current velocity, mean and maximum depth, litter, cover, vegetation, substrate (scored on a phi-scale analogue from clay to rubble), and water-column position (scored 1 to 5, from surface to substrate).

**Analytical methods.** A stepwise [[Multiple Discriminant Analysis]] (using the Wilks lambda criterion) identified factors separating species spatially. Species associations were assessed with [[Hurlbert's Coefficient of Interspecific Association]]. Only species found at 20% or more of stations (eight species) were analysed, and only late juveniles were included to avoid ontogenetic habitat effects. Because visual data lacked seine-sample water-column values, the authors assigned randomly drawn water-column values to seine samples based on the observed distributions, a procedure they justified as a check on patterns rather than a statistical test.

**Stream size.** Stream order and width were highly correlated with upstream drainage area (r = 0.89 and 0.87, P < 0.01), so drainage area served as the measure of stream size. Species differed in the stream sizes they occupied. [[Notropis signipinnis]] and [[Notropis welaka]] were most common in small streams, while [[Notropis texanus]], [[Notropis roseipinnis]], [[Notropis xenustus]], [[Notropis longirostris]] and [[Ericymba buccata]] used larger streams. [[Notropis chrysocephalus]] ranged across nearly all sizes.

**Diel feeding.** Feeding periodicity was determined by the wet-weight stomach-content method from 24-hour collections at 3-hour intervals in spring and fall 1976. Most species fed diurnally, with peaks in the morning or afternoon. [[Ericymba buccata]] was the only nocturnal feeder, with stomach fullness rising after sundown and peaking at dawn, though it also appeared to feed slightly during the day.

## Key Findings

- **Species pool:** 15 cyprinid species from four genera were collected. Eight species met the 20% occurrence criterion and were analysed further.
- **Stream-size gradient:** Species differed in mean stream size occupied, and resource overlap was high across the drainage. Species addition and replacement occurred with increasing stream size.
- **Vertical water-column position** was the most important microhabitat dimension. It was followed by relative use of aquatic vegetation. Together these separated six of eight species. Exceptions were Ericymba buccata and N. longirostris.
- **Discriminant functions:** Two functions explained about 97% of among-species variance (DF I: 89.1%; DF II: 8.3%). DF I separated surface-oriented [[Notropis roseipinnis]] and [[Notropis signipinnis]] from benthic Ericymba buccata and N. longirostris. DF II separated vegetated from unvegetated microhabitats.
- **Classification success:** About 62.4% of cases were correctly classified. N. roseipinnis (90.0%) and N. signipinnis (86.4%) were the most ecologically distinct. N. texanus, N. xenustus and N. chrysocephalus were generalists, classified correctly in fewer than half of cases.
- **Microhabitat associations:** Significant associations were infrequent and mostly weak. Exceptions were N. roseipinnis with Ericymba buccata (negative) and N. chrysocephalus with N. signipinnis (positive). Pairs with similar habitat requirements were often collected together more than chance would predict.
- **Temporal partitioning:** Temporal separation effectively distinguished the otherwise similar pair Ericymba buccata and N. longirostris. The latter fed strictly diurnally, while Ericymba buccata fed at night. Authors suggest the spatial overlap and significant microhabitat association between these two species may indicate competition.
- **Geographic range and niche breadth:** Specialists (N. roseipinnis, N. signipinnis) had restricted Gulf coastal plain ranges. Generalists (N. texanus, N. xenustus, N. chrysocephalus, Ericymba buccata) had broader ranges. The authors suggest that greater niche generality may accompany wider distribution and abundance.
- **Current velocity and depth** were less important predictors of occurrence than in many earlier stream studies. The authors attribute this to focusing on a single family rather than a whole fauna. Unpublished data for the full fauna of the same system showed these variables were highly important.
- **Overall conclusion:** Spatial segregation (drainage area and microhabitat, especially vertical position and vegetation use) combined with temporal feeding activity explains coexistence among eight confamilial minnow species.

**Second paper: von Bertalanffy growth rates in Mustelus (Triakidae).** The second part, partly garbled in the scan, reviews methods for estimating elasmobranch growth. Common approaches (vertebral and spine ring analysis, length-frequency modes, and clasper growth) are labour-intensive. [[Holden's Growth Rate Method]] (1974) estimates von Bertalanffy parameters from embryonic growth rates, which is faster. The authors applied this method to estimate growth for Mustelus. Reported results include:

- Each of seven Mustelus species with known reproductive biology has a gestation period of roughly 10 to 11 months (OCR-garbled).
- Estimated von Bertalanffy growth constants (K) ranged from about 0.27 to 0.63 for Mustelus species, and from about 0.21 to 0.34 for another group (values partially illegible).
- The authors conclude that Holden's method is suitable for rapid preliminary estimation of growth rates for fishery management purposes.
