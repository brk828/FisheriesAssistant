# Complex Systems, Trade-Offs, and Theoretical Population Biology: Richard Levins's "Strategy of Model Building in Population Biology" Revisited

#population-ecology #theoretical-ecology #philosophy-of-science

## Executive Summary

This paper by philosopher Jay Odenbaugh (*Philosophy of Science* 70, December 2003, pp. 1496–1507) defends ecologist [[Richard Levins]]'s thesis that biologists building mathematical models must trade off generality, precision, and realism because biological systems are complex. [[Steven Orzack]] and [[Elliott Sober]] (1993) argue that, under explicit definitions of these properties, some models are limiting cases of one another, so the three properties can be maximized together and the trade-off thesis is false. Odenbaugh contends that Orzack and Sober misread Levins. Levins's trade-off concerns the pragmatic limits of scientists (their capacity to measure, interpret, solve, and use models), not the formal semantics of the models. The paper contains no empirical data or fish-specific material; it is a conceptual analysis of model building in theoretical population biology.

## Background & Description

**Scope of the debate.** Theoretical population biology spans population ecology (multispecies demographic and density dynamics), population genetics (genotype frequencies under selection, inbreeding, mutation, migration, and drift), behavioral ecology, and biogeography. Traditionally these fields treat ecological change and evolutionary change as separate, with environments assumed either to vary or to stay fixed.

**Levins's context.** In the 1960s, Levins, together with [[Richard Lewontin]], [[Robert MacArthur]], and [[E. O. Wilson]], sought to integrate these fields, as evidence grew that ecological and evolutionary processes are temporally commensurate. Examples cited include seasonal beak-size evolution in [[Darwin's Finches]] during severe drought, and forest succession over centuries. Levins also opposed [[Systems Ecology]], represented by [[Kenneth Watt]]'s *Ecology and Resource Management* (1968), whose universal, computer-simulated models Levins dubbed "FORTRAN ecology" and "photographically exact" approaches.

**Levins's three strategies (1966).** A "photographically exact" model would be an enormous set of coupled partial differential equations with hundreds of parameters, which Levins judged impractical. Biologists instead use three strategies:

- **Type I:** sacrifice generality for precision and realism (e.g., short-term fisheries models, testable and computable).
- **Type II:** sacrifice realism for generality and precision (e.g., idealized physics-style models whose deviations are hoped to cancel out).
- **Type III:** sacrifice precision for generality and realism (graphical models yielding inequality predictions). Levins preferred this approach.

**Levins's thesis.** Levins's thesis (Odenbaugh's label "(LT)") states that there is a necessary trade-off between generality, precision, and realism in ecological and evolutionary models, such that at most two of these can be maximized per model.

**Orzack and Sober's definitions.** Orzack and Sober (1993, p. 534) define:

- **(G)** A model applying to more real-world systems is more general.
- **(R)** A model accounting for more independent variables known to have an effect is more realistic.
- **(P)** A model generating point predictions for output parameters is precise.

They also require that compared models be both instantiated or both uninstantiated, since an uninstantiated model is always more general.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's "environment" is the epistemic and institutional setting of modeling rather than a natural habitat. Its central concern is the empirical limits on science:

- **Parameter burden.** A [[Lotka-Volterra Competition Model]] of a twenty-species community requires intrinsic growth rates and carrying capacities for each species, plus a 20 × 20 interaction matrix, which grows further with higher-order effects.
- **Short study durations.** Levins's critique notes that most ecological studies run "at most, a decade or two" (Pimm 1991), making the required measurement effort practically impossible.
- **Interpretability.** Competition coefficients are hard to interpret. Competition can arise through many mechanisms (Schoener 1983), and [[Apparent Competition]] (Holt 1977) can produce negative pairwise effects without direct interaction.
- **Analytic solvability.** Complex systems likely lack analytic solutions, so simulation depends on available computing technology.

Odenbaugh stresses that these difficulties are empirical: they follow from how the world is, and from two kinds of human limits, the inability to manipulate systems in the field and lab, and cognitive limits in storing, inferring, and abstracting with mathematical formalisms.

## Key Findings

1. **Levins's trade-off is pragmatic, not formal.** Odenbaugh argues the trade-offs arise from "our limitations," not from the semantics of the models. Levins's "contradictions" concern the conflict between complex nature and limited minds, not logical inconsistency.

2. **Orzack and Sober's counterexamples.** Using the [[Exponential Growth Model]] (dN/dt = rN) and the [[Logistic Growth Model]] (dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K)), they show the exponential is a limiting case of the logistic as N → 0, so the logistic is at least as general and realistic (it adds K). Adding an independent variable to a precise model likewise preserves precision and increases generality and realism. Their principle (OS) holds that if one model is a special case of another, no trade-off exists between them.

3. **Odenbaugh's reply.** Orzack and Sober's (OS) is consistent with Levins's thesis because trade-offs apply to models above a boundary of cognitive accessibility. Models inside the boundary can be used; models beyond it are cognitively inaccessible. A nested sequence of models may be fully maximized yet still contain an unusable model beyond the boundary, so a "necessary" trade-off persists for usable models.

4. **Graphical formalization.** Odenbaugh models the space of possible models along generality, realism, and precision axes. He defines a convex boundary separating usable from unusable models, arguing that the trade-off thesis can be reformulated (as "(LT*)" and "(LT**)") to be consistent with nested models, and that the three-dimensional case shows at most two properties can be increased beyond the boundary.

5. **Conclusion.** Levins's strategy of model building is "left unscathed." Orzack and Sober "misunderstand the nature of Levins's concerns," which address trade-offs in fallibly inquiring into complex systems, not the logical independence of model properties.

6. **Caveat on Levins's terminology.** Odenbaugh notes that Levins, a Marxist, uses "contradiction" in a dialectical sense, not to mean a necessarily false proposition (Levins and Lewontin 1985).
