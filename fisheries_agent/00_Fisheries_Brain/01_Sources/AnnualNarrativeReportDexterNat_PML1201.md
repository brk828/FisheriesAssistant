# Dexter National Fish Hatchery Annual Narrative Report 1987

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annual narrative report from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in the Pecos River Valley of southeastern New Mexico covers calendar year 1987. The hatchery's program focuses on holding, spawning, rearing and distributing imperiled native fishes of the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. In 1987 it distributed large numbers of [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Bonytail Chub]], along with smaller numbers of [[Largemouth Bass]]. The report also covers field surveys in the [[Gila River Drainage]], [[San Juan River]], [[Pecos River]], [[Virgin River]] and [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]], captive propagation of [[Woundfin]], disease problems, and cooperation with other agencies.

## Background & Description

The hatchery lies about 20 miles southeast of Roswell. Pond and building construction began in 1931 and fish rearing started in 1932. The original mission was warmwater game fish for the Southwest. Changes in federal law and Service responsibilities in 1978 redirected the program toward native and endangered fishes.

Planned work for 1987 included:
- providing refuge for 12 species of imperiled fishes;
- producing 1,000,000 fry and 100,000 fingerling razorback sucker, and 1,000,000 fry and 100,000 fingerling Colorado squawfish;
- monitoring stocked razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish;
- supplying live and preserved fishes for university studies and museums;
- supporting the Rio Grande Fishes Recovery Team.

The staff in 1987 consisted of eight permanent employees, one Cooperative student, one Youth Conservation Corps enrollee and one Intergovernmental Personnel Act (IPA) position. Table 1 of the report lists 14 species held, most with federal status. Of these, [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Woundfin]] and [[Gila Topminnow]] are listed as endangered, [[Pecos Pupfish]] as threatened, and [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Big Bend Gambusia]] as endangered or candidates depending on status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Many of the species are restricted to a few drainages. The Gila River system, including the Verde and Salt rivers and their tributaries in eastern and central Arizona, is the main recovery area. Stocking sites also included the lower Colorado River in Arizona and California.

Several environmental issues appear throughout the report:
- **Habitat and competition:** Nonnative species, especially [[Red Shiner]] and [[Channel Catfish]], were dominant or abundant at several sites and are thought to compete with and reduce native populations. At the Virgin River, the decline of [[Woundfin]] was linked to interactions with red shiner and the introduced Asian tapeworm.
- **Water chemistry:** The Bitter Lake refuge sinkholes are highly saline, with conductivity often above 10,000 µmhos/cm, and some are meromictic (layered by salinity).
- **Nonnative predators:** Catfish were identified as a major predator of stocked razorback sucker in the Salt River.
- **Hybridization and encroachment:** Sheepshead minnow threatens Pecos pupfish through hybridization and possible range encroachment.

## Key Findings

### Production and distribution
- **Razorback sucker:** 1,580,513 fish weighing 2,413 pounds were distributed. Spawning produced an average take of 84,349 eggs per female (a later passage gives 84,507). Fry were distributed to hatchery ponds, the Page Springs SFH, Buenos Aires NWR, and other sites. Of 380,974 fry reared in ponds, 244,622 (64.2%) were returned at harvest.
- **Colorado squawfish:** 391,335 fish weighing 442 pounds were distributed. Fry were stocked in the Verde River and other Arizona sites.
- **Bonytail chub:** 19,669 fish weighing 212 pounds were distributed.
- **Largemouth bass:** 760 fish weighing 240 pounds were supplied to the Fort Apache Indian Reservation.

### Woundfin propagation
- In March 1987, 235 woundfin were collected from the Virgin River near Littlefield, Arizona, and held at Dexter.
- Spawning experiments were run in raceways, a circular tank and a pond. Raceway 3 produced 176 fry, Raceway 4 produced 264, the circular tank 23, and pond 2C 50 (released to Pond 4C, which was later found empty).
- The total was 464 fry, of which about 440 survived to the end of the year.
- An outbreak of [[Lernaea]] (anchorworm) in June was treated weekly with 0.25 ppm Masoten for five weeks, which stopped most losses by mid-June.
- Ongoing plans include acquiring additional woundfin from the Virgin River in March 1988 and testing spawning substrates.

### Other species
- **Pecos pupfish and Leon Springs pupfish:** Each had more than 10,000 individuals. 250 Desert pupfish were sent to the Desert Botanical Garden, Phoenix, and 105 to the BLM office in Kingman.
- **Gila topminnow:** 450 fish were sent to the Desert Botanical Garden and 105 to the Kingman BLM office.
- **Big Bend gambusia:** The population did not survive outdoor overwintering. 200 fish were brought from Big Bend National Park to broaden the genetic base.
- **Yaqui catfish:** Infected with yellow grub and a freshwater leech; the leeches were removed.

### Recovery monitoring and research
- **Colorado squawfish marking:** 51,360 fish were marked with fluorescent pigment, half conditioned in still water and half in 0.5 cm/sec current, and stocked into the Verde River on 20 October 1987. Post-marking mortality was under 1%. Four fish were recaptured in Canyon Creek.
- **Razorback sucker dispersal:** 1,075 fish from the 1986 year class and 5,154 from the 1987 year class were marked and stocked in Eagle Creek, Arizona. Larger fish (185 mm TL) dispersed less and sought cover, while smaller fish (150 mm TL) dispersed downstream rapidly. Conditioning did not improve retention.
- **Recaptures:** 42 razorback suckers were recaptured in Gila tributaries, mostly June stocking, and 38 from the lower Colorado River.

### Survey results
- **Virgin River (March 1987):** Native fish made up 47.9% of the seine catch (321 fish) and nonnative fish 52.2% (351 fish), out of 672 total. Woundfin accounted for 35.0% of the catch.
- **Gila River drainage:** Surveys covered several tributaries. Eagle Creek held a newly confirmed population of [[Spikedace]]. Blue River survey found loach minnow in high abundance. The Salt River had a notable absence of roundtail chub.
- **San Juan River:** Two adult Colorado squawfish (796 mm TL, 5.5 kg female; 540 mm TL, 1.1 kg male) and two young-of-year were collected near the Mancos River, showing squawfish still reproduce there.
- **Pecos River:** [[Pecos Bluntnose Shiner]] made up nearly 32% of fish collected. The introduced [[Arkansas River Shiner]] is a concern for competition with native Pecos shiners.
- **Bitter Lake refuge:** Pecos pupfish was the most abundant species, and Pecos gambusia was found only in the northern part of the refuge.

### Staff and visitors
- About 31,000 people visited the hatchery in 1987.

### Recommendations
1. Continue recovery monitoring surveys in the Gila drainage.
2. Continue San Juan River studies to define squawfish status.
3. Continue Pecos River surveys, focusing on bluntnose shiner and Arkansas River shiner.
4. Inspect Bitter Lake habitats annually to monitor pupfish and gambusia.
5. Continue squawfish stocking, testing larger fish and conditioning to reduce predation and improve survival.
6. Continue razorback sucker stocking, with larger sizes in tributary habitats where nonnative predators are fewer.
7. Survey and manage Tularosa pupfish, and assist in the surveys and reintroduction of Chihuahua chub and other native species.
