# Response of Fishes to Periodic Spring Floods in a Southeastern Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Centrarchidae #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by [[Stephen T. Ross]] and [[John A. Baker]] examines how stream fishes move onto a fringing floodplain during periodic, nondestructive spring floods in [[Black Creek]], a lowland stream in southeastern Mississippi. Fishes were sampled by seine and minnow trap during five spring floods (1976, 1977, 1979), with baseline channel seining from 1975 to 1981. The authors tested whether floodplain fish assemblages were random subsets of the channel fauna, documented diel (day/night) shifts in floodplain use, and tested whether flood conditions influence population abundance.

## Background & Description

Flooding in low-gradient systems is primarily lateral expansion onto floodplains rather than erosive scour. Most prior work addressed erosive floods, whereas nondestructive floods in North American streams had received little attention. The study site lies within the Pine Hills region of the Gulf Coast Plain, where the creek averages about 10 m wide, has clear but brown-stained, acidic (mean pH 6.2), low-conductivity water, and a sandy substrate with [[Sparganium Americanum]] beds. Upstream drainage area is 199 km², and annual rainfall averages 146 cm, with elevated flows from February to April. Flood flow was calculated from channel morphology, and high correlation with the nearby Bowie Creek gauge (r = 0.85; N = 96 monthly means) was used to characterize the long-term flow regime.

The study addressed three questions: (1) whether floodplain species composition was a random sample of the channel ichthyofauna; (2) whether diel changes occurred in floodplain use; and (3) whether diel changes occurred in fish location on the floodplain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The floodplain is a seasonally inundated habitat of grassy meadow, woodland and four small permanent ponds that connect to the creek during floods. Floodplains are recognized as breeding and foraging grounds and as nursery areas for juvenile fishes, especially [[Centrarchidae]], a role also noted by Forbes (1925) and Guillory (1979). Flood-exploitative fishes were largely species of the upper water column or slack-water margins, while the only lower-column species that readily moved onto the floodplain was [[Notropis texanus]]. Turbidity rose with flood stage, reaching a peak of 65 JTU during peak flooding. Gonadal development in many exploiters coincides with the flood period, so the inundated floodplain may supply energy for reproduction.

## Key Findings

- **Sampling effort:** 5,389 fishes of 42 species were taken by seining in the channel (September 1975 to January 1981; 17 collections). During floods, 1,411 fishes of 33 species were collected from channel and floodplain combined.
- **Floodplain fauna:** 26 species were taken on the floodplain: 9 from both floodplain and channel, and 17 from the floodplain only. Thirteen species were caught only by trap, nine only by seine, and eleven by both methods.
- **Community similarity:** Morisita's Index (Im) between floodplain and channel communities was 0.6 (moderate difference) when trap and seine data were combined. With trap data alone, Im = 0.06, indicating essentially different faunas. Both areas yielded similar species richness (16 channel, 17 floodplain trap species).
- **Dominant floodplain fishes:** Fundulus olivaceus, F. notti, Gambusia affinis, Notropis welaka, N. texanus, N. roseipinnis, Lepomis macrochirus, L. cyanellus and L. marginatus.
- **Day versus night:** Most flood-exploitative species peaked in catch-per-effort on the floodplain during the day and declined sharply at night. Overall fish activity on the floodplain decreased at night. Lepomis macrochirus, L. cyanellus and N. texanus increased with distance from the channel by day, indicating movement toward the periphery; at night L. macrochirus and L. cyanellus were nearest the channel.
- **Size structure:** Larger centrarchids on the floodplain (e.g., L. macrochirus, mean standard length 34.7 mm; L. cyanellus, mean 37 mm) were juveniles relative to published minimum reproductive size, indicating a nursery role, though sampling bias against large fish is acknowledged.
- **Quiescent species:** Percina nigrofasciata and Lepomis megalotis were common in the channel but largely did not exploit the floodplain. P. nigrofasciata was collected on the floodplain only once, and its daytime catch-per-effort was negatively correlated with turbidity (r = -0.65; P < 0.01). Lepomis megalotis was never collected on the floodplain by seine.
- **Population response:** Notropis texanus abundance was higher in the 3 high spring-discharge years (1975, 1979, 1980; > 35 m³/s) than in the 3 low-discharge years (1976–1978; < 25 m³/s). Change in abundance was correlated with change in spring flow (r = 0.91; P < 0.05), supporting the hypothesis that flooding influences flood-exploiter populations. The apparent decline of N. texanus across the study was attributed to collecting effects. No significant relationship was found for P. nigrofasciata.
- **Conclusion:** Spring flooding appears to exert significant control over fish community structure, with each species responding differently. The authors note that the fish assemblage is shaped by both structural habitat suitability and stochastic flood intensity. Follow-up work on the effects of repeated collecting was planned.
