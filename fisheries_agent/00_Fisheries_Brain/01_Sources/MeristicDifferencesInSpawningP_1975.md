## Executive Summary

This study by Carscadden and Leggett (1975) investigated meristic (countable morphological) characters in spawning populations of [[American Shad]] (*Alosa sapidissima*) across four Atlantic coast river systems — the [[St. John River]], [[Miramichi River]], [[Connecticut River]], and [[Hudson River]] — with particular focus on three distinct tributaries/sites within the St. John River system (Kennebecasis River, Washademoak Lake, and Mactaquac Dam). Using univariate (Student's t-test) and multivariate (discriminant function analysis, Mahalanobis-D²) statistical approaches on five meristic characters, the authors found significant differences in 71% of pairwise population comparisons, with very low percent overlap (<1% in most cases) between populations. These findings extend prior evidence of river-specific homing in shad to a finer spatial scale, suggesting that American Shad home not only to their [[Natal River Homing|natal river]] but also to their specific natal tributary within that river system. The consistency of meristic counts across year-classes within a single population supports a strong genetic component underlying these differences, though environmental influences (particularly water temperature during early development) cannot be entirely ruled out.

## Background & Description

**Species:** American Shad (*Alosa sapidissima*), family Clupeidae — an anadromous, pelagic marine fish that returns to freshwater rivers to spawn.

**Prior evidence for homing:** Earlier studies (Fischler 1959; Hildebrand and Schroeder 1928; Hill 1959; Nichols 1966; Vladykov and Wallace 1938; Warfel and Olsen 1947) documented meristic and life-history differences between shad populations across different Atlantic coast rivers, and tagging studies (Hollis 1948; Talbot and Sykes 1958) supported the concept of [[Natal River Homing]].

**Study rationale:** The authors previously reported (Carscadden and Leggett 1975, *J. Fish Biol.*) life-history differences between shad in two tributaries and the main stem of the St. John River, suggesting isolation due to homing at the tributary level. This paper tests that hypothesis using meristic data.

**Meristic characters measured (per Hill 1959 methodology):**
- Dorsal fin ray count
- Anal fin ray count
- Right pectoral fin ray count
- Anterior scute count
- Posterior scute count

**Study sites and sampling methods:**

|---|---|---|---|
| Kennebecasis River | St. John | ~48 km | Gillnets (11.4, 12.7, 14.0 cm stretched mesh) |
| Washademoak Lake | St. John | ~88 km | Commercial gaspereau trapnets |
| Mactaquac Dam | St. John (main stem) | ~140 km | Mechanical fish lift |

Fish were aged via the scale method (Cating 1953; Judy 1961). Up to 100 fish were randomly sampled per site annually; smaller samples were retained in full.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- American Shad exhibit precise **spawning migration timing** correlated with a narrow water temperature range (13–18°C) across all Atlantic coast populations, ensuring optimal thermal conditions for egg and larval development (Leggett and Whitney 1972).
- Optimal temperatures for **egg and larval development** range more broadly, 15.5–26.5°C (Bradford et al. 1968; Leim 1924; Marcy 1969), leaving room for localized thermal variation to influence [[Meristic Variation|meristic character development]] during critical developmental windows.
- Environmental factors known to influence meristic counts in fishes generally include water temperature, dissolved oxygen (Garside 1966; Taning 1952), and light duration (Lindsey 1958), in addition to genetic (multifactorial) control (Barlow 1961).
- A **cline of decreasing mean meristic counts** was observed with increasing distance of the St. John River tributary from the river mouth (Kennebecasis → Washademoak Lake → Mactaquac Dam), suggesting either genetic differentiation or highly stable, tributary-specific environmental conditions during early development.
- The Connecticut River population exhibited the lowest mean meristic values of all populations sampled, with no obvious latitudinal trend detected across the broader geographic range studied.
- Comparisons with strictly pelagic marine species (Pacific sardine — Mais 1972; yellowfin tuna — Royce 1964) showed much *higher* percent overlap values than observed in shad, suggesting shad populations intermingle far less during reproduction than fully marine species — consistent with strong homing behavior to distinct freshwater spawning grounds.

## Key Findings

1. **High rate of significant meristic differences:** 99 of 140 possible pairwise comparisons (71%) between river/tributary populations showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in at least one of the five meristic characters.

2. **Character-specific variability in significance:**
   - Posterior scutes: significant in 23 of 28 comparisons (most variable)
   - Dorsal fin rays and anterior scutes: significant in 21 of 28 comparisons each
   - Pectoral fin rays: significant in 18 of 28 comparisons
   - Anal fin rays: significant in 16 of 28 comparisons (least variable)

3. **Low multivariate overlap:** Discriminant function analysis (Mahalanobis-D²) showed <1% percent overlap in 23 of 28 population comparisons, indicating very limited morphological intermixing between populations.

4. **No latitudinal or distance-based trend in overlap:** The magnitude of differentiation (D²) did not correlate simply with geographic distance between rivers. Notably:
   - Hudson River and Mactaquac Dam (St. John) populations were *more* similar than Hudson River and Connecticut River populations, despite Connecticut being geographically closer to Hudson.
   - Hudson River–Mactaquac Dam comparison showed the **highest percent overlap** of all comparisons, differing in only one meristic character — an anomaly the authors could not explain.

5. **High overlap exceptions:**
   - Northwest Miramichi River vs. Millbank and Southwest Miramichi River vs. Millbank showed high overlap — expected, since the Millbank sample is a mixture of both tributary populations.
   - Northwest vs. Southwest Miramichi River comparison showed a notably high overlap (11.6%), a deviation from the general low-overlap pattern, possibly due to subpopulation mixing at unknown spawning sites within the Miramichi system.

6. **Year-class stability within populations:** Between-year-class comparisons *within* the same population (Kennebecasis River, Washademoak Lake, Mactaquac Dam) revealed **no significant differences**, with relatively high percent overlap values (19.4–26.3%), indicating strong temporal consistency in meristic counts within a spawning population.

7. **Within-year-class comparisons between populations:** In contrast, when comparing single year-classes *between* different St. John River tributary populations, significant differences and very low overlap were still observed (e.g., Washademoak Lake vs. Mactaquac Dam 1969 year-class: 0.05% overlap), reinforcing that population differences are not an artifact of pooling multiple year-classes.

8. **Genetic vs. environmental interpretation:** The high temporal stability of meristic counts within populations, contrasted with strong differentiation between populations, supports the conclusion that [[Meristic Characters|meristic counts]] in American Shad are **strongly genetically influenced**, though the relative contribution of environment (especially fine-scale temperature variation during development) could not be fully excluded due to lack of environmental data for the specific year-classes studied.

9. **Overall conclusion:** The results support and extend the [[Natal River Homing]] hypothesis to a finer spatial scale — American Shad appear to home not just to their natal river, but to their **specific natal tributary** within that river system, with a level of reproductive isolation comparable between river-level and tributary-level comparisons.
