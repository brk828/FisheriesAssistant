# Caudal Neurosecretory System in Teleost Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This teaching unit introduces the [[Caudal Neurosecretory System]] of teleost fishes, a neurosecretory organ system that releases peptide hormones with biological activity in many vertebrate and invertebrate groups. Its two principal hormones, [[Urotensin I]] and [[Urotensin II]], have been the focus of endocrine research since the 1980s and have aided the characterization of peptides such as [[Corticotropin Releasing Hormone]] (CRH). The system is described as anatomically well understood but still enigmatic in its endocrine control and specific functions. Proposed roles include osmoregulation, cardiovascular regulation, reproduction and pH regulation, and the document closes by suggesting possible relevance to human endocrinology.

## Background & Description

**Historical development.** Early observations (1913 to 1930s) noted an enlarged terminal spinal cord region, a highly vascularized terminal organ of unknown function, and large secretory cells in skates. Later work (1950s) connected caudal neurosecretory neurons with capillaries in the caudal peduncle, identified a neurohemal structure, and named it the [[Urophysis]] after its similarity to the [[Neurohypophysis]]. From the 1960s to the 1980s, anatomy, histology, pharmacology and biochemistry were refined, urotensins were characterized as hormones, and their structures were determined. Physiological and mechanistic studies continued into the 1990s.

**Phylogenetic distribution.**
- Agnathans ([[Lampreys]] and [[Hagfishes]]) have caudal neurosecretory cells.
- Elasmobranchs ([[Sharks]], [[Rays]], [[Skates]]) have caudal neurosecretory cells but no urophysis.
- Holocephalans ([[Chimaeras]]) are not yet definitively characterized.
- Among actinopterygians, sturgeons, paddlefish and gars have caudal neurosecretory cells but no urophysis.
- Lungfishes show large spinal neurons, but no secretory products have been observed.
- Among more than 350 teleost species investigated, only [[Seahorses and Pipefishes]] (Syngnathidae) and ocean sunfishes appear to lack the system. All others possess neurosecretory cells that innervate a highly vascularized urophysis.

**Cellular anatomy.** Caudal neurosecretory cells lie ventrolateral to the central canal of the spinal cord, extending anteriorly to the fifth to eighth preterminal vertebrae. Their axons terminate at individual urophysial capillaries. The cells are polymorphic, have lobed nuclei, and are generally larger than other spinal neurons. Most stain acidophilic. They occur in three size classes (large, medium, small) that decrease posteriorly. Immunocytochemistry shows that individual cells can contain both [[Urotensin I]] and [[Urotensin II]] granules, with separate populations of UI and UII cells. Neurons also receive aminergic, cholinergic and peptidergic input from the reticular formation, medulla, magnocellular nucleus and medial longitudinal fasciculus.

**Vascular features.** Blood reaches the urophysis via paired dorsal segmental arteries from the dorsal caudal artery. It then passes into a capillary plexus, drains into the caudal vein at the level of the fourth preterminal vertebra, and flows into the [[Renal Portal System]]. The urophysis thus has direct access to the kidney, urinary bladder, liver and other anterior tissues.

**Development.** The system develops in late larval stages, after the [[Diencephalic Neurosecretory System]] appears. Both systems derive from neural ectoderm. After extirpation, neurosecretory cells regenerate from ependymal cells in many teleost species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Urotensins act on diverse teleost tissues but are not found in non-piscine vertebrates. They do show biological activity in avian and mammalian tissues. A recent discovery of UI in the midgut epithelia of three insect species extends the known distribution.

The system responds to environmental change. Brook trout moved from fresh water to seawater showed changes in urophysial histology and amino acid uptake, interpreted as altered synthesis of urotensins. Transfers to deionized water produced the opposite synthetic response. Monoaminergic innervation of the system also varies with salinity acclimation. Studies on brook trout exposed to acidified water and aluminum showed that UI synthesis increases with lower environmental pH and UII synthesis increases with elevated aluminum (Al3+) levels, indicating involvement in pH and metal stress responses.

Osmoregulation is central to the proposed function. In euryhaline fishes, cortisol mediates adaptation to higher salinity, while prolactin controls adaptation to hypo-osmotic water. Urotensins are thought to influence cortisol release through ACTH stimulation and to inhibit prolactin release, and the system may fine-tune homeostasis under small osmotic fluctuations. Several species retain some osmoregulatory ability after urophysectomy, which suggests compensation by other tissues or regeneration.

## Key Findings

**Hormone chemistry**
- **Urotensin I (UI):** a 41-amino-acid oligopeptide, C-terminally amidated. It shares about 50% sequence homology with ovine CRH and the frog-skin peptide sauvagine. It is synthesized from a 145-residue prepro-UI precursor. Lederis et al. (1990) suggest that prepro-UI and the 162-residue teleost prepro-CRH may be evolutionary precursors of longer mammalian forms (187 residues in rat, 190 in sheep, 196 in human, as printed).
- **Urotensin II (UII):** a dodecapeptide with sequence homology to mammalian [[Somatostatin-14]]. Six variants have been identified. Bern (1990) suggests UII may be a diverse peptide family analogous to neurohypophysial neuropeptides.
- **Urotensin IV:** indistinguishable from [[Arginine Vasotocin]] (AVT), present in only a few teleost species.
- **Urophysins:** putative carrier proteins analogous to neurophysins. Their biogenic relationship to urotensins is unknown.
- **Other substances:** acetylcholine occurs at high concentration in the urophysis, and Met-enkephalin has been isolated from its neurosecretory cells. A putative urotensin III has been proposed but not isolated.

**Biological actions**
- Both UI and UII contract smooth muscle in many tissues (gut, uterus, vas deferens, ureter, bladder and others), as summarized in a table of hypertensive and related actions across teleosts and mammals.
- UI and UII show opposing effects in some tissues, and their co-localization in the same neurons is considered puzzling.
- UI acts as a general smooth-muscle spasmogen in intestine and affects transepithelial ion transport in gill, opercular membrane, urinary bladder and posterior intestine of several species.
- UII appears to act as a vasoconstrictor and affects blood pressure in several species.
- Proposed reproductive role: UII stimulates contraction of the ovary, oviduct and sperm duct in guppy and goby, probably assisting gamete expulsion during spawning.

**Mechanisms**
- Urotensins act through membrane receptors. Proposed transduction pathways include inhibition of [[Adenylate Cyclase]] (reducing cAMP), stimulation of intracellular calcium, and a prostaglandin-mediated pathway, but these remain unconfirmed.
- UI may bind CRH receptors and vasoactive intestinal peptide receptors. UII may bind somatostatin receptors. Specific receptors for both have yet to be identified in fishes.

**Neural control**
- The system receives extensive input from the central nervous system. Plasma volume, ion concentration, and internal and external pH are hypothesized to be monitored by specialized receptors feeding into it.
- Humoral influences are indicated by reproduction-related responses.

**Mammalian relevance**
- UI is structurally similar and biologically nearly identical to mammalian CRH in its effects. UII resembles mammalian somatostatin. Pressor effects of urotensins in teleosts appear reversed in mammalian tissues.

**Summary of open questions**
- Membrane receptors and transduction mechanisms remain undefined.
- Functions of other caudal neurosecretory substances (e.g., urophysins) need study.
- The control of differential UI and UII synthesis and release, and their co-localization, are unresolved.
- Interactions with pituitary AVT remain to be explored.
- Their potential role in mammalian systems is under investigation.
