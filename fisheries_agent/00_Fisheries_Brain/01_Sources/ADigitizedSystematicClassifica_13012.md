# A Digitized Systematic Classification for Ecosystems of North America

#vegetation-ecology #ecosystem-classification #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service General Technical Report (RM-73, June 1980) by [[David E. Brown]], [[Charles H. Lowe]], and [[Charles P. Pase]] presents a hierarchical, digitized classification of natural ecosystems. It is built on biotic and climatic criteria rather than land use. The system uses a numeric code that nests from biogeographic realm down to composition-structure-phase, and it is designed to be computer compatible and adaptable to any map scale. The authors illustrate the system with a summary of the natural vegetation of North America, with most detail for the Southwest. The document does not address fish or other aquatic fauna as study subjects. Its aquatic content concerns wetland and aquatic plant communities.

## Background & Description

The classification grew out of field research in the North American Southwest, literature synthesis, and revision through feedback from users. Its fourth level underpins the 1:1,000,000 vegetation map of the southwestern United States and adjacent northwestern Mexico (Brown et al. 1977). In April 1979, the Bureau of Land Management, Fish and Wildlife Service, Forest Service, Geological Survey, and Soil Conservation Service endorsed a four-component framework (vegetation, soil, landform, and aquatic/water) for renewable resource inventories (Driscoll et al. 1978). The vegetation component is a modification of the [[UNESCO Vegetation Classification]] (1973). The two systems are broadly comparable at the formation and association levels, and differences are expected to be resolved as plant community knowledge improves.

The authors argue that most earlier schemes were regional, nonhierarchical, or based on geologic and chemical criteria that are hard to apply in the field. The system is intended for resource planning, habitat stratification, environmental impact analysis (including under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]]), and identifying [[Natural Areas]] for preservation. It is used in the [[RUN WILD]] wildlife habitat retrieval program and in the [[Arizona Resources Inventory System]].

A key advantage is that the hierarchy links plant communities to expected wildlife. For example, nesting [[Wood Duck]] (*Aix sponsa*) is expected in temperate swamp and riparian forests, whereas [[Black-bellied Whistling Duck]] (*Dendrocygna autumnalis*) is expected in tropical-subtropical swamp forests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hierarchy and coding.** The code is read as follows: 1,000 = [[Biogeographic Realm]]; 1,100 = vegetation; 1,110 = [[Formation-Type]]; 1,111 = [[Climatic Zone]]; 1,111.1 = [[Biome]] (regional formation); 1,111.11 = [[Series]] (generic dominants); 1,111.111 = [[Association]] (specific dominants); 1,111.1111 = composition-structure-phase. The first digit after the comma sorts vegetation into upland (1,100) or wetland (1,200). Cultivated lands (1,300, 1,400) are not treated.

**Biogeographic realms.** Seven realms are recognized, adapted from Wallace (1876): Nearctic (1000), Palaearctic (2000), Neotropical-Antarctican (3000), Oriental (4000), Ethiopian (5000), Australian (6000), and Oceanic (7000). Transitional areas with wide ecotones are assigned to both neighboring realms.

**Formation-types.** Upland formations are [[Tundra]], [[Forest and Woodland]], [[Scrubland]], [[Grassland]], [[Desertland]], and nonvegetated areas. Wetland formations are [[Wet Tundra]], [[Swamp-Forest]] (including riparian forest), [[Swamp-Scrub]], [[Marshland]], [[Strand]], and [[Submergent Vegetation]]. Formations respond mainly to available soil moisture. Desertland is defined as areas receiving usually less than 300 mm of precipitation per year. Savanna is treated as an ecotone between woodland and grassland, with grassland defined as areas where tree crowns cover less than about 15% of the ground.

**Climatic zones.** Four zones are defined by minimum temperature: Arctic-Boreal (or Antarctic-Austral), Cold Temperate, Warm Temperate, and Tropical-Subtropical. For example, Arctic-Boreal areas have a coldest-month isotherm of −3 °C and growing seasons generally under 100 days.

**Aquatic and wetland communities.** Wetlands are defined as periodically, seasonally, or continually submerged communities with species or life forms distinct from adjacent uplands. Riparian forests are assigned to the wetland division. Strand communities are beach and river-channel systems subject to periodic submersion and wave action. Submergent communities are dominated by submerged or non-emergent plants. The fourth level includes Marine and Inland submergent series, such as [[Phytoplankton Series]], [[Pondweed Series]], [[Eelgrass Series]], [[Giant Kelp Series]], and [[Ruppia Series]]. Figure 11 shows kelp beds with sea otters off the west coast of North America. Figure 90 shows a Sierran-Cascade alpine submergent community in a glacial pool that is the home of golden trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss aguabonita*), though the text itself does not describe fish ecology.

**Dominant plants.** Some plants are facultative and dominate several formations. [[Mesquite]] (*Prosopis juliflora*) is one example, and it also crosses climatic zones. [[Creosote Bush]] (*Larrea tridentata*) and introduced [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) are others. Generic dominants such as *Populus*, *Salix*, *Pinus*, and *Quercus* are shared among biomes, but speciation and genetic makeup usually differ between biomes (Yang and Lowe 1970).

**Mapping scale.** The hierarchy can be applied at any scale. Fourth-level biomes are most useful for mapping states, provinces, and continents. Sixth-level associations are needed for regional detail, and seventh-level units are for intensive plot-based studies.

## Key Findings

- **Hierarchical structure.** The system nests seven levels of ecosystem detail under a numeric code. It is open-ended and can be expanded or adapted to different scales and data needs.
- **Realm-based framework.** Seven biogeographic realms are used, with the Nearctic realm (1000) as the primary focus. Tropical and highland areas adjacent to the Southwest are included.
- **Dichotomous top-level division.** All natural vegetation is classed as upland (1,100) or wetland (1,200), with riparian systems placed in the wetland division.
- **Formation and climate integration.** Eleven formation-types for upland and wetland systems are crossed with four climatic zones, giving a standard matrix for North America (Tables 2 and 3).
- **Detailed nomenclature.** Table 4 lists upland biotic communities and Table 5 lists wetland communities to the fourth level, with series and association examples. Table 4 covers tundra, forest, scrub, grassland, and desert communities. Table 5 covers wet tundra, swamp-forest, swamp-scrub, marsh, strand, and submergent communities.
- **Wildlife linkage.** Because communities are tied to ecological distribution, the system can indicate which wildlife species are expected, for example wood ducks in temperate swamp and riparian forests.
- **Wetland definition.** Wetlands are defined biotically, not by geology or chemistry, which the authors state makes them easier to apply in the field.
- **Documentation.** The report contains about 90 illustrations (photos of tundra, forests, grasslands, deserts, wetlands, and strands) and an extensive literature list.
- **Acknowledged limitations.** The authors state that the classification is "not meant to be either a definitive or final classification." Knowledge of some tundra and marine strand communities is incomplete, and fifth- and sixth-level determinations will need revision as field data accumulate.
