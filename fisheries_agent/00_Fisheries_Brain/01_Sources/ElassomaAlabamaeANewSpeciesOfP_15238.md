# Elassoma alabamae, a New Species of Pygmy Sunfish Endemic to the Tennessee River Drainage of Alabama

#ichthyology #marine-biology #elassomatidae

## Executive Summary

Richard L. Mayden (1993, [[Bulletin Alabama Museum of Natural History]] No. 16) formally describes the **spring pygmy sunfish**, *Elassoma alabamae*, a small freshwater fish of the family [[Elassomatidae]]. The description brings the number of described species in the family to six. The species is distinguished from its congeners by meristic, mensural and coloration characters, most notably three dorsal fin spines, clear or white "windows" in the dorsal and anal fins of males, and 6–7 broad dark flank bars. It is known from only three springs in the [[Tennessee River]] Drainage of north Alabama, two of which have been extirpated. A single native population survives in the [[Moss Spring]] and [[Beaverdam Creek]] system, and a reintroduced population in the [[Pryor Springs]] complex is reproducing. The author regards the species as highly vulnerable and recommends state and federal endangered-species protection.

## Background & Description

The family Elassomatidae is endemic to North America and included five described and two undescribed species before this paper. Most members are under 30 mm standard length (SL); the exception is [[Banded Pygmy Sunfish]] (*Elassoma zonatum*), which is more widespread across Coastal Plain drainages from eastern Texas to North Carolina. The new species is the most geographically restricted member of the family.

The species was first collected on 5 November 1937 by Tennessee Valley Authority biologist L. F. Miller at [[Cave Spring]], Lauderdale County. Specimens were identified by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and Milton B. Trautman as an undescribed *Elassoma*, but it remained undescribed and known only by the informal name "spring pygmy sunfish" for over half a century.

**Morphology (summary):**
- Smallest member of the family; mean adult SL 17.4 mm (N = 122), maximum observed 25 mm SL (female).
- Dorsal spines II–IV (usually III); lateral scale rows 27–32 (usually 28–29); caudal peduncle scale rows 15–20 (usually 16–18); transverse scale rows usually 11–12.
- 5–8 broad dark flank bars (usually 6–7) separated by 4–8 narrow iridescent interbars.
- No scales on top of the head; no dark scapular blotches; males have a single clear window in dorsal and anal fins.
- Males are sexually dichromatic and brighter in breeding season; males are smaller than females (mean SL 17.6 mm vs 18.8 mm in the measured subsamples).

**Type material:** Holotype UAIC 10275.01 (adult male, 17.4 mm SL), collected 2 March 1992 at Moss Spring, Limestone County. Allotype UAIC 10275.05 (female, 17.4 mm SL). Paratypes include 50 specimens from the holotype locality plus series from several institutions (UAIC, INHS, SIUC, TU, UF, UMMZ, USNM, UT, AUM).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat:** Cave Spring in 1937 had clear water, dense matted vegetation, and fine sand and mud substrate, at depths of 15 cm to 1 m. Present-day Moss Spring and Beaverdam Creek/Swamp habitat resembles this. The fish is most often found among rooted submergent vegetation (*Ceratophyllum*, *Myriophyllum*, *Utricularia*, *Elodea*), in clear water over fine sand, clay, mud or limestone. Shorelines are lined with hardwoods, shrubs and marsh-like grasses. The species appears mobile and uses different spring and swamp macrohabitats seasonally.

**Associated fauna:** Twenty-six fish species were recorded alongside the spring pygmy sunfish in Cave, Pryor and Moss springs, including [[Bowfin]] (*Amia calva*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), several [[Sunfish]] (*Lepomis* spp.) and darters (*Etheostoma* spp.), and [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).

**Life history:** Mettee (1974) and later authors suggest the species is annual. Adults spawn at about one year of age in March and April, then die within days to months. By September the population consists only of the year's offspring. Females produce up to 65 eggs per spawning. Eggs are attached to vegetation above the substrate; the species does not build nests, unlike centrarchids.

**Threats:** Impoundment (Pickwick Lake, 1938), vegetation invasion and herbicide treatment (2,4-D, 1945) at Pryor Springs, channelization and agricultural pollution, and aerial pesticide and herbicide drift from surrounding pasture and farmland. Proximity to highways and livestock is also noted.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomy:** *E. alabamae* is distinguished from all congeners by three dorsal fin spines, 6–7 broad flank bars, 16–18 caudal peduncle scale rows, and cream-to-white basicaudal ocelli bounded anteriorly but not posteriorly. Its nearest congener, *E. zonatum*, differs significantly in all meristic variables (P < 0.0001), except branchiostegal ray number.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males are smaller and have longer fins, larger eyes and wider dark bars relative to interbars (P < 0.01) than females.
- **Distribution:** Known only from the Tennessee River Drainage above the Fall Line. It is the only *Elassoma* whose entire range lies above the Fall Line.
- **Population status:**
  - Cave Spring population extirpated after Pickwick Lake filled in February 1938.
  - Pryor Springs population likely extirpated in the 1940s after vegetation replacement and 2,4-D treatment (28 May 1945).
  - Species presumed extinct from 1941 until rediscovery in January 1973 by [[David A. Etnier]] and students.
- **Reintroduction:** 36 adults (11 males, 25 gravid females) moved from Moss Spring to an unnamed Pryor Branch tributary on 17 February 1984. A further 120 (37 males, 83 females) followed in 1985, and 58 males and 59 females were moved to original Pryor Spring in January 1987. Surveys in September 1992 found the species common and expanding into beaver-dammed areas.
- **Conservation recommendations:** The author considers the species endangered and advises state and federal protection, regular population monitoring, further study of its biology and phylogeny, and genetic characterization before any further translocations. An informal habitat-protection agreement among landowners, the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the Alabama Department of Conservation and Natural Resources currently exists.
- **Taxonomic framing:** The paper adds *E. alabamae* as the sixth described species in the family, based on morphometric, meristic and colour data for a sample of over 100 specimens and principal component analysis.
