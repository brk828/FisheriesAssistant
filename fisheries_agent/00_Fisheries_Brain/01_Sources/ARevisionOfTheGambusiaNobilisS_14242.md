# A Revision of the Gambusia nobilis Species Group

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This revision by [[Clark Hubbs]] and [[Victor G. Springer]] (The University of Texas, *Texas Journal of Science*, Vol. IX, No. 3, September 1957) reviews the [[Gambusia Nobilis]] species group, a set of mosquitofishes from the Pecos River drainage of New Mexico and western Texas and from the Rio Conchos system of Chihuahua and Durango, Mexico. The authors recognize six species, three of which are described as new: [[Gambusia Geiseri]] (a new name for the fish long called *G. affinis*), [[Gambusia Hurtadoi]], and [[Gambusia Alvarezi]]. The work combines taxonomy (meristics, gonopodial morphology, color), morphometric and meristic variation under laboratory rearing, distributional history, and an evolutionary hypothesis that isolation in springs and streams during drought and competition with [[Gambusia Affinis]] produced the species.

## Background & Description

The group was previously reviewed by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] (1929), who recognized four species: *G. nobilis*, *G. senilis*, *G. gaigei*, and *G. affinis*. The authors show that the name *affinis* had been applied to a fish that does not belong to this group, so the San Marcos form is renamed *G. geiseri*. Hubbs (1926) had placed the group within the subgenus *Gambusia* of the tribe Gambusiini.

Diagnostic characters are:

- **Gonopodium (male anal fin):** the distal spines of ray 3 are very long. Recurved hooks are present only in *G. geiseri*. Elbow segment number, ray 4a length, and the shape of the terminal hooks on rays 4p and 5a separate species (see the dichotomous key in the source).
- **Dorsal rays:** usually 7 to 9 across the group.
- **Anal rays:** usually 7 to 9, sometimes 10 (the authors' revised range).
- **Color:** a diffuse lateral band, dark or black markings on the female anal fin, dark scale-pocket margins, and species-specific crescents, spots, and suborbital or chin bars.

The authors deliberately omit most proportional measurements because they vary with environment. The suspensorium of the gonopodium, drawn from work by [[Luis Howell Rivero]], is treated as a key taxonomic feature in poeciliids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Five of the six species were reared in laboratory stocks, and five of these species showed that morphometric characters vary with environment. Wild *G. hurtadoi* had a caudal peduncle depth of 1.1 to 1.3 times in caudal length, versus 0.8 to 0.9 in laboratory fish. Laboratory fish were generally deeper-bodied with shorter fin rays. Gonopodial structure and color patterns did not change under laboratory conditions and are considered genetic. Environmental effects on fin-ray counts were smaller than differences between species.

**Habitat by species:**

- **[[Gambusia Geiseri]]** lives in or near the flow of large springs on the [[Balcones Escarpment]]: [[San Marcos Springs]] (flow 51 to 286 second-feet, average 153; mean temperature about 22°C) and [[Comal Springs]] (flow 235 to 420 second-feet, average 324; 23.3°C). Water is clear, calcium-carbonate-rich, and heavily vegetated. Annual temperature range is probably no more than 3°C either side of spring temperature.
- **[[Gambusia Nobilis]]** is almost entirely spring-dwelling. Springs of the Balmorhea area had flows of 53 to 104 second-feet (five major springs), Leon Spring 16 to 18, and Comanche Springs 40 to 50. [[Phantom Lake]] surface temperatures ranged 21.7°C to 25.6°C.
- **[[Gambusia Senilis]]** is the most ecologically tolerant member. It occurs in irrigation and roadside ditches, quiet weedy waters, clear springs, and turbid or polluted rivers. Observed temperatures ranged 12°C to 30°C.
- **[[Gambusia Gaigei]]** is known from very few sites. The [[Graham Ranch Warm Spring]] was 34.5°C on April 27, 1956, and its pool ranged 25°C to 32.5°C. Nearby hot springs (38°C to 40°C) had no fishes. Spring flow was less than 0.3 second-feet.
- **[[Gambusia Hurtadoi]]** lives in a warm spring with about 12 second-feet flow (June 30, 1954). Temperatures ranged 33°C in the boil to 29°C (December 31, 1954). Irrigation ditch temperatures ranged 31°C to 21°C.
- **[[Gambusia Alvarezi]]** was observed on December 31, 1955 (air temperature 3°C after a hard freeze). Spring water was 24°C and water one-third mile downstream was 19°C. Fish were absent from ice-covered side pools.

## Key Findings

**1. Taxonomy and nomenclature**

- Six allopatric species are recognized. *G. geiseri*, *G. hurtadoi*, and *G. alvarezi* are new. *G. affinis* does not belong to the group.
- The distinctness of *G. geiseri* and *G. nobilis* is supported by sympatry at Comanche Springs and Toyahvale irrigation ditches, no observed hybridization, and male preference for conspecific females in laboratory trials.
- Hubbs and colleagues had earlier shown that the nominal species *affinis* and *holbrookii* integrade and should be regarded as one species.

**2. Geographic distribution and introductions**

- ***G. geiseri*** is known from nine Texas localities, a disjunct range "no other fish species" in Texas shares. The authors judge the Trans-Pecos and Balcones records to be introductions from the San Marcos stock, which had been distributed for mosquito control and stocked with game fishes. Seven specimens from the nineteenth century were the only mosquitofish from the suspected localities before 1937.
- ***G. geiseri* type material:** holotype (17 mm male, UMMZ 168974); 435 paratypes; 3,627 additional non-paratype specimens. The species is named for Dr. Samuel W. Geiser.
- ***G. nobilis*** is restricted to the Pecos River drainage of New Mexico and western Texas. Earlier reports of the species in Mexico were erroneous. The Zimmerman Reservoir record is considered probably based on faulty locality data.
- ***G. gaigei*** persists in only one spring. The Boquillas Spring population from which the types were collected in 1928 is extinct. At Graham Ranch, nearly 95% of mosquitofish were *G. gaigei* in 1954 but approximately 95% were *G. affinis* in 1956. The senior author recommended eliminating the introduced *G. affinis* and protecting the remaining *G. gaigei* population.
- ***G. senilis*** is widely distributed in the Rio Conchos. A spotted race is recognized from the Rio Valle de Allende and El Ojo Almoloya (800-plus specimens from the spring-ditch system, 378 from the Valle de Allende). The spotted race's disjunct distribution is attributed to past irrigation diversions. The spotting is thought to be genetically controlled and correlates with poorer condition (about 10% lower displacement volume).

**3. Morphometric variation and growth**

- Laboratory-raised *G. gaigei* and *G. hurtadoi* grew larger than their wild parents. Wild *G. gaigei* females reached a maximum of 28 mm (n = 223 wild females); two held in the laboratory for one year reached 45 mm. Wild *G. hurtadoi* females reached 36 mm (laboratory survivors averaged 40.5 mm, range 38 to 45 mm).
- Laboratory F1 males of *G. hurtadoi* ranged 22 to 28 mm (mean 24.9, n = 39) and 24 to 31 mm (mean 26.5, n = 21), exceeding wild males (16 to 22 mm, mean 18.5, n = 320).
- Authors attribute the increase to better food, lower temperatures, or both. Basing species distinctions on male size is therefore unreliable.

**4. Dorsal ray variation and temperature**

Dorsal-ray counts were regressed on standard length:

| Sample | Slope (per mm) | p |
|---|---|---|
| *G. hurtadoi*, El Ojo de la Hacienda Dolores, June 30, 1954 | −0.0396 | 0.0005 |
| *G. hurtadoi*, same spring, December 31, 1954 | +0.0228 | 0.0004 |
| *G. hurtadoi*, laboratory F1 (February 1955) | +0.0261 | 0.0001 |
| *G. gaigei*, Graham Ranch, June 11, 1954 | +0.0255 | 0.0035 |

- The authors interpret the negative June slope as reflecting the effect of warm developmental temperatures on ray number in larger fish born during the previous winter. The ditch population showed no significant correlation. *G. alvarezi* showed no significant correlation.
- The authors suggest *G. hurtadoi* ray number increases with developmental temperature and *G. gaigei* decreases, while *G. alvarezi* is unaffected. Confirmation requires more precise temperature records.

**5. Sex ratio and abnormal development**

- Wild samples contain more females than males. The authors attribute this to selective netting of larger females and higher male mortality, which is consistent with earlier work on *G. affinis* (Hildebrand, 1927).
- Three large males from El Ojo de la Hacienda Dolores (among 546 females in the two collections) show sex reversal or delayed differentiation, about 0.6% of females. Their gonopodia contain structures found in other poeciliid genera. One male *G. senilis* had an aberrant gonopodium with no explanation offered.

**6. Color variation in *G. hurtadoi***

- Four color variants were recognized: the typical pattern and three variants called Spotted, Gray, and Golden.
- In a random sample of 1,342 individuals collected December 31, 1954, there were 16 Spotted and 13 Gray individuals; a single Golden individual (16 mm) was collected later. Of the sample, 502 were over 20 mm, but none of the color variants were, suggesting reduced survival for the variants.

**7. Evolutionary hypothesis**

- The authors propose that an ancestral widespread *nobilis*-type fish was isolated in springs and streams during interglacial drought. *G. affinis* entered western Texas and further isolated spring populations, which became *G. nobilis* and *G. gaigei*. *G. geiseri* evolved in the Comal and San Marcos springs, where *nobilis* stock could compete with the *affinis* ancestor.
- Speciation through isolation resulting from competition with *G. affinis* is also suggested for an endemic *G. nicaraguensis*-group species in Menard County, Texas (see [[Gambusia Heterochir]]).

**8. Conservation implications**

- Two populations of *G. gaigei* are described: one extinct, the other near extinction. The authors recommend eliminating introduced *G. affinis* from Graham Ranch and reintroducing *G. gaigei*, which succeeded at a spring near the Boquillas ford in [[Big Bend National Park]].
- Introduction of game fishes into the Big Bend endemic-fish waters is strongly discouraged.
- Springs are considered the principal refuges for all six species, and only *G. senilis* occurs frequently outside them.
