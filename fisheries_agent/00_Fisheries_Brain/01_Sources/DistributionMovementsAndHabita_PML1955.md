# Distribution, Movements, and Habitat Use of [[Razorback Sucker]] in a Lower Colorado River Reservoir, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study used [[Sonic Telemetry]] to track 10 wild adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a mainstem [[Colorado River]] reservoir on the Arizona-Nevada border, from November 1994 through July 1997. Movement rates and home ranges were similar to those reported for riverine populations. Fish returned to spawning sites used in earlier years but also visited other spawning areas, and spawning females moved far more than males during the reproductive period. After spawning, most fish moved up-reservoir to a [[Warm-Cold Mixing Zone]] that supports higher algal production, which the authors suggest is important for regaining body condition. Fish stayed mainly within 50 m of shore and occupied a wide range of temperatures and depths that varied with season. The authors argue that reservoirs, which have a high share of the remaining wild populations, offer conditions that make direct observation of spawning and movement easier than in rivers.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is the largest catostomid in North America and is endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin. It was historically common but now survives as relic and fragmented populations. Of four known wild populations with more than 100 fish, three are in reservoirs, and the largest is in [[Lake Mohave]]. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] listed the species as endangered in 1991. Earlier telemetry work focused on riverine backwaters and other lentic habitats, but river studies were limited by remote sites, few wild fish, and a spawning season lasting only 2-3 weeks.

Lake Mohave lies behind [[Davis Dam]], about 5 km upstream of Laughlin, Nevada. At full pool (surface elevation 197 m msl) it covers 11,655 ha and extends about 100 km upstream to the tailrace of [[Hoover Dam]]. The reservoir is up to 6 km wide. Cold, hypolimnetic releases from [[Lake Mead]] enter at the upstream end.

**Methods (summary):**
- **Capture and tagging:** Fish were captured with trammel nets at [[Tequila Cove]] and [[Yuma Cove]]. Each was measured, weighed, anesthetized, and surgically implanted with a sonic transmitter following [[Tyus]] (1987). Transmitters weighed about 6 g, were 16 × 55 mm, and had a life of about 14 months. They logged body temperature (±0.25 °C).
- **Tracking:** Surveys ran weekly by boat along a grid of 71 GPS way-points ("listening stations") spaced at intervals under 1600 m. Each detection recorded location, body temperature, and distance from shore. Vertical water temperature profiles were measured with a Hydrolab temperature profiler in 1995.
- **Analysis:** Movement maps and distances were built in a GIS. Shoreline use was tested with chi-square, and spawning-period movement rates with ANOVA.
- **Spawning verification:** Active spawning was confirmed by observation and by collecting newly emergent larvae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mohave is not pristine razorback sucker habitat, but the authors note that its reservoir conditions offer a broader and more stable range of physical parameters than natural rivers. Reservoir water visibility exceeded 10-15 m, allowing direct observation. Summer temperatures showed a stable but wide thermal gradient of 12-30 °C, flows ranged from 0 to 2 m s⁻¹, and depths exceeded 40 m. Fish could use both littoral and pelagic habitats.

Thermal stratification shaped fish distribution. Upstream areas stayed cold (12-16 °C) regardless of season or depth because of hypolimnetic releases from Lake Mead. Further downstream, summer stratification produced surface temperatures near 30 °C and cooler deeper water. Fish appeared to avoid warm surface water in summer, moving offshore and to deeper layers, which suggests active regulation of body temperature. Summer body temperatures were 18-22 °C, similar to thermal preferences reported by [[Bulkley and Pimentel]] (1983). Across the full study, body temperatures ranged from 12 to 28 °C.

Spawning in reservoirs occurs from January through March. This period is earlier and typically two to three times longer than riverine spawning. Adults are highly social and form spawning groups that can number in the hundreds. Males typically dominate spawning sites at a ratio of about 2:1, and females join from deeper adjacent waters when ready to release eggs. Fecundity exceeds 100,000 ova per female.

## Key Findings

**Sample and effort**
- 20 fish were implanted (10 males, 10 females). Six females and four males were tracked for the full 14-month period and form the analysed sample. The remainder were excluded because contact was lost or transmitters became immobile.
- Study fish averaged 609 mm total length (range 555-680 mm) and 2.78 kg mean weight.
- More than 1000 contacts were recorded: about 580 daily sightings, 300 hourly sightings, 800 body temperatures, and 100 vertical temperature profiles.

**Movement**
- Movement rates ranged from 0.06 to 17.85 km d⁻¹ (as printed in the abstract), and linear ranges averaged 39 km (18-72 km), similar to riverine populations.
- Mean individual movement was 527 m d⁻¹. Females were slightly more active (582 m d⁻¹) than males, but the difference was not statistically significant.
- Monthly average movement exceeded 1000 m d⁻¹ in March, April, and May of 1995, 1996, and 1997, which were the months around and after spawning.
- Mean linear range was 42 km for females (29-72 km) and 35 km for males (18-51 km).
- During spawning (31 January-15 March 1995 and 1996), females were significantly more active than males (ANOVA, p = 0.031).
- Some individuals remained sedentary for weeks, while others roamed widely, showing strong individual variation.

**Spawning and site fidelity**
- All study fish returned to spawning sites used in previous years but also visited other spawning groups.
- During peak spawning (1-28 February 1995 and 1996), most females crossed the reservoir to visit other spawning sites, while males typically stayed at one site.
- Nine of the 10 fish crossed the reservoir and joined spawning groups on both the Arizona and Nevada shorelines. In some cases the distance between spawning sites exceeded 50 km.
- The authors could not confirm that females spawned at multiple sites directly. They infer multiple-site spawning from extended spawning periods, high fecundity, and prior genetic diversity findings ([[Dowling et al.]] 1996).

**Post-spawning movement**
- Travel distances and movement rates peaked after spawning, when most study fish (70%) moved up-reservoir to the [[Warm-Cold Mixing Zone]]. There, nutrient-rich hypolimnetic water from Lake Mead fuels algal production ([[Baker and Paulson]] 1980).
- The authors suggest this movement supports earlier ideas that the species historically sought productive habitat after spawning, and that feeding in such areas helps fish recover body condition.
- Increased activity in shallow water may indicate planktonic feeding.

**Habitat use and depth**
- Fish showed a significant affinity for inshore habitat, within 50 m of shore (p < 0.001, chi-square). The affinity was strongest during spawning, when average distance from shore was under 30 m.
- Average depth varied by season, with most records between 3.1 and 16.8 m (range 0.2 to over 30 m). The mean depth in the vertical profile analysis was 9.1 m. Fish were nearer the surface in spring and autumn and deeper in midsummer.
- Fish were detected mostly around [[Big Basin]] and [[Owl Point]], which were used year-round. [[Tequila Cove]] and Yuma Cove were used seasonally, and the mixing zone was used in summer. Fish were seldom found in up-reservoir or down-reservoir canyon habitats.
- In June 1997, nine untagged fish were seen near the surface at the mixing zone.

**Context and implications**
- The authors show that reservoirs allow direct observation of spawning and reproduction, through larval collection, in ways rarely possible in rivers.
- Movement patterns matched the homing behaviour described by [[Gerking]] (1958): fish gather at specific spawning sites and then disperse over a wide area for the rest of the year.
- Conservation implications include the importance of protecting spawning sites, the mixing zone's productive habitat, and temperature-stratified refuges for recovery of this endangered fish.
