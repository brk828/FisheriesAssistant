# Man and the Changing Fish Fauna of the American Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #cyprinodontidae #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1961 review by [[Robert Rush Miller]] of the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology documents how the freshwater fish fauna of the arid American Southwest changed over roughly the past 100 years. The paper argues that modern human activity, rather than climate, is the chief driver of these changes. Habitat alteration (loss of flow, lowered water tables, arroyo cutting, vegetation loss, dams, diversion, mining and pollution) combined with the introduction of exotic fishes has reduced native populations and driven several species to extinction. The author estimates that 6 or 7 species have gone extinct and at least 13 more are locally exterminated or endangered, together about 20 percent of the aboriginal fishes of Western North America north of Mexico.

## Background & Description

Only about 100 species of strictly freshwater fishes are known west of the Rocky Mountains and north of Mexico. This fauna is depauperate and is characterized by relicts, monotypic genera and a high degree of regional [[Endemism]]. Many species have limited ranges and occupy habitats of only a few thousand gallons or about an acre. Because these isolated populations lack the defensive traits found in more saturated faunas, they are vulnerable to aggressive exotic species.

The region lies largely within the [[Basin and Range Province]], including the Great Basin, Sonoran, Death Valley and Chihuahuan deserts. The paper draws on historical accounts from 19th-century surveys, museum collections, and interviews with old-timers, and uses repeated collections at fixed stations to track change.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat modification.** Historical accounts describe a shift from clear, dependable streams to intermittent, flashy, silt-laden flows. Water temperatures rose as flow decreased and vegetation was lost. Marshes, springs, ciénegas and lagoons disappeared as the [[Water Table]] fell. Specific river histories include:

- **[[Lower Colorado River]]:** Once swift, silt-laden and seasonally variable (250,000 c.f.s. peak in 1916; 18 c.f.s. minimum in 1934). After [[Hoover Dam]] (completed 1935), temperatures narrowed to 54–62 °F below Hoover and Davis dams, while dredging removed marshes and sloughs.
- **[[Gila River]]:** Once a large, essentially permanent stream with lagoons and marshes. The lower 250 miles are now normally dry. The channel at Solomonsville widened from an average of 138.6 ft (1875) to more than 1,900 ft (1916).
- **[[San Pedro River]]:** A gully 3–20 ft deep was cut along 125 miles by 1892–93, and the river entrenched 10–40 ft by 1900.
- **[[San Simon Valley]], [[Blue River]], [[Little Colorado River]]:** Erosion, arroyo cutting and the loss of grasses and cottonwood stands transformed valley bottoms.
- **[[Santa Ana River]] and the Los Angeles Plain:** Nearly all normal flow was appropriated for irrigation between about 1875 and 1905, and springs and ciénegas were drained.
- **[[Upper Rio Grande]]:** Historically a larger, more reliable river, as shown by a sturgeon record near Albuquerque in the 1870s and blue sucker remains from about 1500.

**Causes of depletion** (summarized from [[Chamberlain]]'s 1904 report and the author's later work):

1. Destruction of vegetation by livestock grazing, which increased floods and erosion.
2. Arroyo cutting, largely between about 1880 and 1910.
3. Timber cutting in headwaters, which increased runoff and lowered the water table.
4. Dams, irrigation diversions and the loss of upstream migration routes.
5. Mining sediments, toxic chemicals, and industrial and domestic pollution.
6. Groundwater pumping, intensified after World War II.
7. Introduction of exotic species (about 36 established, most from eastern North America, mainly 1930–1950).
8. Chemical fish toxins such as [[Rotenone]] and toxaphene.

The author notes that climate has recurring wet and dry cycles, but that the severe erosion of the past century is associated with human disturbance.

## Key Findings

- **Extinctions and endangerment:** No fewer than 6 species, and probably 7, have gone extinct in the Southwest since the late 19th century. At least 13 more have been locally exterminated or are threatened. Together these are about 20 percent of the aboriginal native fishes of Western North America north of Mexico.
- **Extinct taxa include** the [[Stumptooth Minnow]] (Stypodon signifer), [[Parras Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon latifasciatus), [[Pahranagat Spinedace]] (Lepidomeda altivelis), [[Owens Valley Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon radiosus), [[Leon Springs Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon bovinus), [[Thicktail Chub]] (Gila crassicauda) (last few specimens since 1936), and [[Ash Meadows Killifish]] (Empetrichthys merriami), which may be extinct.
- **Threatened taxa** include the [[Big Bend Gambusia]], which had fewer than 24 individuals in 1960 before a captive-breeding rescue. Others are the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (estimated 100–300 individuals, with a 160-fish estimate in one survey), the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (one specimen captured in August 1960), the [[Modoc Sucker]], the [[June Sucker]], and the [[Mohave Chub]].
- **Cui-ui persistence:** The [[Cui-ui]] (Chasmistes cujus) remains abundant in Pyramid Lake, where it spawns on the shoreline.
- **Species replacement:** At Arivaca Creek, the native [[Gila Topminnow]] was completely replaced by the [[Western Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia affinis) in less than two years (1957–1959). Exotic species, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Mosquitofish]], drove many native declines through predation, competition and hybridization.
- **Hybridization:** Rainbow trout and native [[Arizona Native Trout]] (Salmo gilae) and cutthroat trout hybridized widely. The author documented native trout declines from catch rates of 100–350 per day in the 1900s to near-absence.
- **Lower Colorado squawfish:** Historically very large (possibly 6 ft and about 100 lb), it was nearly extirpated from the lower river. Numbers were so high before 1911 that fish were pitchforked from irrigation ditches for fertilizer.
- **Exotic richness:** Exotics now equal or exceed native species in some states. Examples: Colorado 54 native and 33 exotic; New Mexico 61 native and 22 introduced; Arizona 28 native and 37 stocked; Nevada 41 native and 21 exotic; California 67 native and 32 introduced.
- **Conclusion:** The author concludes that "modern man, rather than climatic change, has been the chief agent" of the observed change, with overgrazing and forest cutting as the most important causes of erosion. He recommends protective legislation to limit chemical treatments and calls for protection of the remaining native populations.
