# Fish Tagging Studies in TVA Storage Reservoirs, 1947–1949

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Daniel E. Manges of the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] (Norris, Tennessee), reports tagging studies on several TVA reservoirs from 1940 to 1949. The studies estimated how much of the game fish crop is harvested by anglers and how far fish move within impoundments. A total of 5,196 game and pan fish were tagged, and 675 (13.0 percent) were reported caught by sport fishermen. Harvest rates differed among species and years, but averages over several years appeared to even out. Walleye and sauger moved extensively, while basses and crappie moved much less. The author found no evidence that year-round fishing had seriously affected the annual fish crop in TVA storage waters.

## Background & Description

Tagging began in the fall of 1945 and was motivated by two questions: how much of the fish crop anglers harvest, and how extensively fish move through impoundments. Earlier work included a 1940 study on [[Norris Reservoir]] (Eschmeyer, 1942) and a 1946 sauger study (Haslbauer and Manges, 1947). The 1947 and 1948 results are the main focus. The 1949 data were incomplete at the time of writing.

Studied waters:
- [[Norris Reservoir]], a storage reservoir with the most data
- [[Cherokee Reservoir]], tagged over three successive years
- [[Hiwassee Reservoir]], tagged early in 1947
- [[Nottely Reservoir]], tagged early in 1948
- [[Fort Loudoun Reservoir]], the uppermost mainstream impoundment on the [[Tennessee River]]. The author notes that it resembles Guntersville and Wheeler reservoirs more than storage reservoirs, but it was included because of its proximity to them.

Methods:
- Fish were captured mainly by [[Gill Netting]] (walleye and sauger in winter), with angling, hoop nets and some seining for other species.
- Only fish in good condition were tagged, and only when water was cold or cool, never in summer. Injured fish were kept for growth and stomach studies.
- The marking device was the No. 3 [[Jaw Tag]] of Monel metal, affixed to the upper jaw.
- Sportsmen's groups offered prizes for returned tags or helped capture fish (Table 1 lists nine groups across the 1947–1949 seasons). Some unreported tags were likely, but the author believed the proportion was very small.

Waters were open to year-round fishing. Since 1943 there had been no closed season on Norris Reservoir, and since 1944 none on any TVA impoundment except Watauga Reservoir.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Harvest and fishing pressure.** Harvest rates depended on location and fishing methods. Tagging near [[Norris Reservoir]] Dam was later found to be in an area fished about twice as heavily as the reservoir as a whole, so the author judged actual harvest to be probably not over half the indicated rate there. Fishing on [[Cherokee Reservoir]] was increasing, but the author believed most of the crop was still not harvested.

**Fish movement.** Walleye and sauger travelled far, and some sauger may spawn in the reservoir while others migrate to the headwaters. Basses and crappie were much more sedentary, so local depletion near boat docks was possible. Fish tagged near Norris Dam must move upstream because the dam blocks downstream passage. Two sauger were recaptured just below the dam, one of which had likely passed through the turbines.

**Mortality and fish life span.** Tagging effects on mortality had not been studied on TVA waters since Eschmeyer's 1940 inquiry. Recaptures fell sharply after the first season, probably because tagged fish were adults nearing the end of their short lives. Growth studies (Stroud, 1948) indicated that few game fish live longer than five years in Norris.

**Species introductions.** White crappie had not been found in Norris Reservoir before 1948. In spring of that year, 210 adults were moved from [[Guntersville Reservoir]], and 205 were tagged. Two were later found dead along shore and no tags were returned, so the author saw no evidence the introduction succeeded.

### Norris Reservoir Harvest

- **1940:** 1,010 game fish tagged, more than 90 percent bass. 218 tags returned (21.6 percent), including 200 in the first season.
- **1946:** 328 sauger tagged. 20 returned (6.1 percent), including 16 in the same year.
- **Norris harvest by species, all years:** spotted bass 36.8 percent (highest), black crappie below average. Sauger returns ranged from 4.9 to 30.3 percent, walleye from 5.0 to 23.6 percent, and largemouth bass from 5.3 to 18.5 percent.
- **1949 harvest:** about 25 percent by the author's estimate, or 23.2 percent as of September 1. This was much higher than most other TVA waters. The author attributed part of the increase to deeper fishing and more use of spinners with earthworms, which were especially effective for walleye.
- **Spotted bass:** the author linked their high harvest to heavy fishing on walleye and sauger, which share their bottom-oriented habits.

### Other Reservoirs

- **Cherokee:** 1,243 fish tagged (1947–1949), 65 returned (5.2 percent). Returns rose each year.
- **Hiwassee:** 38 tagged, 10 returned (26.3 percent). The tagging site was probably among the most heavily fished areas, so whole-reservoir harvest was likely lower.
- **Nottely:** 135 tagged, mostly black crappie, 2 returned (1.5 percent). The fish were in poor condition and concentrated in the headwaters, and tagging was stopped because fish could easily be taken illegally. No crappie tags were returned.
- **Fort Loudoun:** 276 tagged in 1947, mostly white crappie. 5 returned (1.8 percent), including one fish found dead. The author noted this matched closely with Wheeler and Guntersville reservoirs.

### Recapture Timing

- Most tags were returned in the first season after tagging.
- Second-season returns were small. Norris returns were 4 sauger in 1947, 6 walleye, 4 sauger and 1 largemouth bass in 1948, and 5 sauger and 8 walleye in 1949, with 2 walleye in the third season of 1949.
- Stoudt (1938) reported 11.4 percent second-season gill-net recoveries for Minnesota walleye, far above Norris angling returns. The author interpreted this as evidence of angling's low efficiency as a harvest method.
- For some species, harvestability appeared to vary widely by year, but four- and five-year averages reduced these differences.

### Fish Movement

[[Minimum Migration Distance]] values use the shortest water path between tagging and recapture points.

- **Norris, 1940:** 195 recaptured fish (several species) averaged 3.7 miles.
- **Norris, 1946:** 16 sauger averaged 20.0 miles.
- **Norris, 1940–1949 (583 fish):** [[Sauger]] averaged 19.1 miles (259 fish) and [[Walleye]] averaged 11.6 miles (76 fish). Basses and crappie averaged far less: [[Largemouth Bass]] 4.0 miles, [[Smallmouth Bass]] 1.2 miles, [[Spotted Bass]] 3.6 miles and [[Black Crappie]] 5.4 miles (all-year averages from Table 9).
- **Extreme movers:** three sauger travelled 116.0, 144.5 and 149 miles. One sauger tagged March 16, 1949 was recaptured 24.0 miles downstream after 11 days.
- **Walleye trend:** average minimum distance rose from 4.8 miles in 1940 to 12.8 miles in 1949.
- **Seasonal pattern:** according to statistical analysis by W. E. Ricker, average distance peaked in March and April. Most recaptures were upstream of tagging points, and the return movement peaked around late April and early May. Few fish returned to the tagging point.
- **Cherokee:** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]] and crappie generally stayed within a few miles (Table 10 weighted averages: black crappie 5.0 miles, white crappie 3.8 miles, largemouth bass 4.7 miles, smallmouth bass 3.5 miles, sauger 8.7 miles from only 3 fish). The data were incomplete.

### Conclusions

- Rate of harvest varied from under 3 percent (Cherokee black crappie, 1947) to over 39 percent (Norris spotted bass, 1940).
- Recapture rates on reservoirs with multi-year studies rose gradually, probably from more fishing and more effective methods.
- Walleye and sauger are unlikely to be depleted locally by angling, while basses and crappie could be depleted near resorts.
- The author concluded there was still no evidence that year-round fishing had seriously affected the annual fish crop in TVA storage waters.
