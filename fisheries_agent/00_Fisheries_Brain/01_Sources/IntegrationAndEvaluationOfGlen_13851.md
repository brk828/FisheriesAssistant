# Integration and Evaluation of Glen Canyon Environmental Studies: Aquatic Biology Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1997 review by P. E. Brunkow, Post-doctoral Research Associate in the Department of Zoology at [[Arizona State University]], of aquatic biology project reports from the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Phase I and Phase II efforts. It was prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under Cooperative Agreement 1425-6-FC-81-05003. The review treats each report as a manuscript submitted to a journal, assessing sampling design against stated goals, the statistical analysis performed, and whether interpretations are supported by the data.

The review has two parts. The first identifies cross-study problems in mechanistic explanation, sampling, flow-regime definition, data presentation, and data analysis, and proposes areas of integration (notably [[Cladophora]] light saturation and timing of dam releases). The second gives chapter-by-chapter and report-by-report critiques of roughly 40 contractor reports, covering mainly [[Humpback Chub]] and other native and non-native fishes, plus lower-trophic-level, water-chemistry, zooplankton, and parasite studies.

The document is a review and not a research study. Its main points are methodological critiques, recommended standards, and integration priorities for future [[Grand Canyon]] monitoring of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Evaluate contractor aquatic biology reports and support eventual synthesis of aquatic biological, riparian, and sediment/hydrological GCES research (Fig. 1 in the source).
- **Scope:** Reports received by the Center for Environmental Studies at ASU or sent directly to the author by 1 June 1997. Topics outside the author's expertise (regional history, sediment/hydrology, some specialized analytical methods) were not addressed.
- **Focal species:** The endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha) receives the most attention, through the [[Bio/West]] final report (AQU0701), the [[Little Colorado River]] studies, and the five-year mark/recapture study (Douglas and Marsh, AQU0400). Other species include [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Brown Trout]].
- **Reports reviewed (by contractor):** [[Bio/West]] (AQU0701 and thesis), [[Arizona State University]], [[University of Arizona]] master's theses, [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] (AQU0200), [[National Park Service]] and [[Northern Arizona University]] (AQU0100, Cladophora and periphyton work), [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AQU0300 series and WAQ series), and several Phase I reports.
- **Method of review:** Chapters or sections were read as independent manuscripts. Follow-up correspondence with several authors (dated 11/13/96 to 12/12/96) is reported where relevant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dam effects:** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations alter flow regime, water temperature, sediment load, and light attenuation. These changes affect habitat availability, food production, and fish distribution along the river.
- **Flow regimes:** Under high, steady releases during the Phase I period, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] limited the possibility of studying fluctuating flows. Only the [[Interim Flows]] period (from August 1991) and autumn/winter fluctuating releases provided contrast in some studies. The review notes that "steady" flow effects are poorly known because few steady flows were sampled.
- **Tributary influence:** The [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) is the largest tributary in Grand Canyon and a major site for [[Humpback Chub]] spawning and rearing. Temperature and turbidity contrasts between the LCR and the mainstem are central to fish distribution hypotheses.
- **Lower trophic productivity:** [[Cladophora]] is the main primary producer in the tailwater upstream of [[Lee's Ferry]]. Downstream of the [[Paria River]], the diatom-associated alga [[Oscillatoria]] becomes important. Macroinvertebrate and algal standing crop decline sharply below roughly 70 to 80 km from Lee's Ferry (RM 42 to 48).
- **Lake Mead influence:** Fish species composition, macroinvertebrate density, and beach/sediment stability below [[Bridge Canyon]] (RM 235) may depend more on [[Lake Mead]] than on Glen Canyon Dam operations, according to one Phase I report.
- **Parasites and invasives:** The [[Asian Fish Tapeworm]] (Bothriocephalus acheilognathi) was the only internal parasite reported from chub stomach flushes. Its establishment depends on mainstem temperature (above 20 °C) and on the presence of cyclopoid copepod intermediate hosts.

## Key Findings

### Cross-study methodological issues

1. **Mechanisms are rarely proposed.** Studies describe static conditions and seldom explain longer-term changes in habitat availability, seasonal shifts between native and non-native fish dominance, or catch-rate patterns. The review recommends that future research state and test proposed mechanisms.
2. **Sampling bias in visual surveys.** The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] larval fish study (AQU0307) sampled clear water visually and turbid water with random dip nets, so the two conditions were sampled with very different intensity. Random or systematic site selection, as used by [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] (AQU0200), is recommended.
3. **Undefined flow terms.** Terms such as "fluctuating," "steady," "constant," and "variable" are used inconsistently. The review proposes that "fluctuating" means daily rise and fall, "steady" means no change on a daily time scale, "constant" means a longer period of one type, and "variable" means shifts between the two over longer periods. Effects should be stated relative to actual discharge (for example, steady 5000 cfs versus steady 15000 cfs).
4. **Non-standard data reporting.** Fish survey results are presented as frequency of occurrence averaged across dates, locations, and gear types. The review recommends the dBase IV formats developed by [[Bio/West]] for future synthesis.
5. **Inconsistent CPUE units.** Some reports express [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] per unit area, others per unit time, and some switch within a single report. Standardization is needed for long-term comparisons.
6. **Non-independent observations.** Repeated samples within transects were often treated as independent, which inflates degrees of freedom in ANOVA and linear regression and increases Type I error risk. The recommended fix is to average within transects and use the number of transects as the sample size.
7. **Habitat-use bias.** Capture rates by habitat type should be weighted by sampling efficiency for each habitat (for example, riffles versus cover-rich areas). [[Bio/West]] gear efficiency analyses (AQU0701, AQU0441) could provide weighting indices.
8. **Correlated habitat variables.** Depth, velocity, and substrate are correlated, yet several analyses treated them independently. Use of the [[Mann-Whitney U-Test]] for continuous-gradient categories and the [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]] for non-gradient categories (such as cover type) is recommended.

### Humpback Chub (Gila cypha)

- **Condition index:** Length-weight constants for condition index came from 550 fish handled in 1990-91 (the source prints "1900-91," corrected here). Condition indices were calculated for 1693 fish captured in 1990-93, while length-weight data covered 4632 fish. Selection of the 1693 fish is not explained.
- **Trout versus chub condition:** The source reports that rainbow trout have lower and more variable condition (Kn) than chub. The reviewer found that mean Kn was slightly higher for trout than for chub, with similar or lower variability, which does not support the competition argument based on condition alone.
- **Transition to the mainstem:** Back-calculated minimum size at transition from the LCR to the mainstem was 52 mm. The claim that little survival occurs below this size is weakened, because fish smaller than 52 mm may never attempt the transition. Figure 6-11B also appears to be Figure 6-11A shifted by about 15 mm, which suggests systematic error in back-calculated lengths.
- **Predation:** Brown trout reportedly do not eat chub smaller than about 80 mm SL (Fig. 6-17), so predation is unlikely to explain losses of chub below 52 mm.
- **Aggregations:** The authors describe nine mainstem aggregations that accounted for 94% of mainstem captures. Their stability should be monitored.
- **Survival (AQU0400):** The authors state that "1994 was clearly a problematic year" for G. cypha. The reviewer notes that the largest fish had clearly higher survival than other size classes in 1994 (Fig. 4-5, Table 4-5), which argues against the authors' claim that predation is unlikely.
- **Population replacement (AQU0701):** The authors estimate a survival rate of 0.097 to one year of age and a three-year survival of 0.001. The reviewer suspects the three-year value was derived by cubing the one-year rate and may underestimate survival of subadults. Adult stability implies recruitment from another source, probably the LCR, so the LCR and LCR-mainstem aggregation may need to be assessed as one population.
- **Habitat availability:** Havasu Creek shows depth, current, and substrate distributions comparable to the LCR in some years (MWU tests), and adults and subadults use shallower water (under 160 cm) even when deeper water is available. The review suggests Havasu Creek could be a candidate for a second breeding population.
- **Scale of comparison:** Comparisons between tributaries should consider absolute area, not just relative frequency, because the LCR is the largest tributary in Grand Canyon.
- **Diel activity:** Young-of-year humpback chub were significantly more active during the day, and adults more active at night.
- **Diet:** [[Bio/West]] found no fish remains in 158 adult stomachs. [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] (AQU0200) reported five young-of-year chub regurgitated by adults out of 208 handled adults in 1993. The review considers it premature to call young-of-year chub an important adult diet item.

### Other fishes

- **Rainbow trout distribution:** Trout are presumed limited upstream of the LCR confluence by turbidity, but temperature was not systematically tested. Raw capture data could show which variable better explains distribution.
- **Rainbow trout catch shift (Otis, AQU0434):** Rainbow trout relative abundance declined from 97% (1979) to 76.5% (1988) and to a maximum of 19.4% (1988 to 1992). Brown trout became dominant.
- **Flannelmouth sucker growth and spawning (Weiss, AQU0435):** Mean length of captured flannelmouth was 334 mm TL, estimated at 4 to 5 years old. The reviewer argues that the inference of successful spawning under experimental operations depends on age-growth relationships that dam operations could have altered. Negative growth values in Table 11 were retained, and the reviewer notes that many are of similar magnitude to positive values, so no usable growth estimates may be derived.
- **Spawning migrations:** The conclusion that Paria-tagged fish migrated to spawn is not supported; fish may have taken up residence in the Paria.
- **Shinumo Creek (Allan, AQU0429):** Six of 14 significant habitat differences between sampling trips disappeared after sequential Bonferroni correction. Changes in relative species abundance are attributed more to sampling technique than season. The reviewer questions the explanation of small fish size reducing competition, and notes that rainbow trout above 200 mm TL could eat fish with body depth of 20 mm or less, including young natives.
- **Bright Angel and Kanab creeks (Otis, AQU0434):** Sampling differences were due to physical stream layout, plus restrictive NPS scheduling. The reviewer notes that Otis found dead fish across a wide range of abiotic conditions in Kanab Creek and attributed death primarily to Lernaea infection.
- **Speckled dace and humpback chub (AQU0200):** Speckled dace used significantly shallower water day and night (MWU P < 0.001 for both). Substrate, cover, and corrected cover did not differ significantly between dace and chub.
- **Trout habitat bias (Trout Studies, AQU0317):** More detailed data were collected from wadeable locations, which biases trout habitat use toward shallower water and lower river elevations.

### Lower trophic levels and water quality

- **Light and Cladophora (Yard, AQU0900):** The authors assumed photoinhibition at 920 µE. Saturation depth was about 3 m at low flow and 2 to 3 m at high flow. A large fraction of submerged Cladophora may not photosynthesize, so Blinn et al. (AQU0100) production estimates may overstate actual production.
- **Light attenuation and sediment:** A linear relationship between sediment concentration and light attenuation was reported across a narrow range of sediment concentrations. Only channel width was significantly related to attenuation, and only at high discharge.
- **Cladophora recovery and desiccation (Blinn et al., AQU0100; Usher et al., PB88-183454):** Desiccation tolerance experiments were conducted at the river's edge or in the laboratory, which may underestimate the severity of exposure. Angradi and Kubly (AQU0333) found that Cladophora exposed at night still lost about 25% biomass.
- **Chironomid production (AQU0100):** Estimated at approximately 17 to 18 kg dry mass ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, using a P/B ratio of 9.5 from a small Illinois stream (Juday Creek). The reviewer considers this transfer of an external ratio unsatisfactory.
- **Benthic food web:** The reviewer argues that ecological potential of the Lee's Ferry reach depends on in situ benthic production, and that downstream fish depend more on drift and terrestrial input.
- **Dissolved oxygen (Parnell et al., WAQ0100; Hoffnagle, WAQ0303):** In Spencer Creek, oxygen varied inversely with temperature, while in the LCR and backwaters it varied positively with temperature, likely from daytime photosynthesis. The disparity suggests lower primary productivity in Spencer Creek.
- **Zooplankton (Haury, PB88-183462; Ayers and McKinney, WAQ0301):** Sampling was sporadic and spatially limited, and no statistical analyses were applied in the Haury study. In the Ayers and McKinney study, the reviewer criticizes the Chi-squared test used with expected counts of zero, and notes that 80 µm nets may underestimate rotifers.

### Flow timing and integration recommendations

- **Timing of releases:** High-water releases could be timed so that water reaches the mouth of the LCR at mid-day to maximize temperature when young-of-year native fish enter the Colorado River, or at night so that drifting Cladophora and macroinvertebrates arrive when native fish feed. This requires detailed hydrologic, temperature, and biotic response models.
- **Backwater management:** Backwater use by larval fish under steady flows depends on return-channel depth, which depends on the steady discharge selected. The [[Hoffnagle]] study (AQU0335, fluctuating versus one steady flow of 8200 cfs) covered only four backwaters over one time of year.
- **Priority integration tasks:** (a) integrate light saturation with Cladophora productivity; (b) standardize flow terms and CPUE units; (c) obtain raw data from authors to test habitat correlations and trout–chub competition; (d) define a single population unit for LCR and mainstem LCR-aggregation chub; (e) confirm copepod presence and distribution in the mainstem for tapeworm risk; (f) compare current conditions with pre-dam conditions for benthic production and the LCR.
- **Monitoring endpoint:** Monitoring of dam effects may be limited to the area between Glen Canyon Dam and Bridge Canyon, because fish composition, invertebrate density, and water quality below Bridge Canyon appear influenced by Lake Mead.
