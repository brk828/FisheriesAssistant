# Agency Recalcitrance and Evasion Regarding Compliance with the National Environmental Policy Act Relating to Glen Canyon Dam Operations

#environmental-policy #river-ecology #dam-impacts #NEPA-compliance #Grand-Canyon

## Executive Summary

This technical report documents the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]'s systematic failure to comply with the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) regarding [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations from 1970-1990. Despite multiple court stipulations, legal commitments, and documented environmental degradation in [[Grand Canyon National Park]], the BOR repeatedly evaded preparing required Environmental Impact Statements. The dam's post-1963 operations fundamentally altered the [[Colorado River]] ecosystem through flow regime changes, sediment trapping, and extreme peaking power operations. The report concludes that Congressional intervention is necessary to mandate proper NEPA compliance, interim protective flow regimes, and comprehensive systemwide environmental assessment of [[Colorado River Storage Project]] operations.

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in 1963 under the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] (CRSP), authorized by PL84-495 in 1956. The dam was designed to impound 27 million acre-feet in [[Lake Powell]] and generate 1,150 megawatts of electricity through base-loaded operations. Project purposes were statutorily prioritized as: (1) river flow regulation, (2) water storage for beneficial consumptive use, (3) reclamation of arid lands, (4) flood control, and (5) hydropower generation as an incidental purpose.

The dam's closure in 1963 immediately transformed the river ecosystem. The natural Colorado River previously carried heavy silt loads with seasonal flood flows averaging 90,000 cfs during spring runoff. Post-dam operations released clear, cold water with extreme fluctuations from 1,000 cfs to 28,000+ cfs for peaking power generation. Annual minimum releases were set at 8.23 million acre-feet pursuant to the [[Colorado River Compact]] and 1944 Mexican Treaty obligations.

Environmental changes included: elimination of natural flood cycles that previously reworked beaches and riparian zones; irreversible wind and water erosion of beach terraces; invasion of floodplain vegetation into former beach zones; extirpation of native fish species (several now extinct or endangered); accumulation of uncleared debris flows creating navigation hazards; and compromised recreational safety and viability for river expeditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The pre-dam [[Colorado River]] ecosystem in [[Grand Canyon]] was characterized by dynamic seasonal flooding that maintained beach and riparian habitat structure. Spring floods averaging 90,000 cfs reworked sediments, deposited sand and alluvium at flood recession, and cleared debris flows from side canyons. This natural disturbance regime supported native fish assemblages and maintained navigable channels.

Post-dam conditions created a fundamentally altered ecosystem. Clear, cold water releases eliminated sediment supply while extreme daily flow fluctuations (1,000-28,000+ cfs) prevented establishment of stable shoreline conditions. High ramping rates accelerated erosion of existing beaches. The higher riparian terraces, no longer flooded, experienced irreversible wind erosion and elimination. Lower beach areas eroded continuously, with sediments washing into [[Lake Mead]] due to flow instability and lack of new sand deposition.

Dense floodplain-type vegetation invaded former beach zones, representing a complete ecological succession in response to the new flow regime. The native fish community collapsed, with several species extirpated and listed as extinct or endangered. Debris flows from tributary canyons accumulated without natural flood clearing, creating progressively worsening navigation hazards threatening to completely block channels.

Operational changes through the 1970s-1980s intensified these impacts. The 1970 "criteria for coordinated long range operation of Colorado River reservoirs" shifted operations toward flexible peaking power generation to maximize revenue. The [[Western Energy Expansion Study]] (1977) proposed 250 megawatt power plant additions and 104 megawatt uprating of existing turbines. Power plant upratings completed 1979-1982 increased capacity from 1,150 to 1,336 megawatts and maximum releases from 32,000 to 33,700 cfs, further accelerating environmental degradation.

## Key Findings

**Legal History and Agency Evasion (1974-1990):**

The BOR engaged in systematic evasion of NEPA compliance despite multiple legal triggers and court stipulations:

- **1974**: *Grand Canyon Dories v. Walker* (500 F.2d 588) first challenged BOR non-compliance with NEPA regarding dam operations affecting licensed float trips. Court held NEPA issue not yet "ripe" but put DOI on notice.

- **1975-1976**: BOR prepared "Draft Assessment of Environmental Impact Regarding Operation of Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Powell" (DEA) addressing downstream impacts and alternatives for "release more oriented to recreation and boating." The effort was abandoned without completing final EA or Finding of No Significant Impact (FONSI).

- **1977-1980**: In *Badoni v. Higgenson* (638 F.2d 172), government stipulated before Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals that it had "formally decided to draft a Comprehensive EIS (CEIS) for the entire Colorado River Basin Project." Court relied on this representation in its ruling. The CEIS was initiated 1977-1978 but then abandoned.

- **1980-1981**: In *EDF v. Higgenson* (655 F.2d 1244), BOR reversed position, claiming it would not seek CEIS funding but instead address impacts through "site specific impact statements for individual projects." Court remanded for determination of legality of this shift, emphasizing NEPA compliance was required.

- **1982**: Stipulated settlement agreement filed April 20, 1982 in Federal District Court, D.C., wherein BOR agreed to prepare EIS's on Colorado River hydropower facilities addressing cumulative and synergistic impacts. Cases dismissed pursuant to stipulation. BOR failed to act on stipulation.

**Major Federal Actions Without Proper NEPA Compliance:**

- **Post-1970 Operating Criteria**: New criteria published 1970 and 1980 radically altered operations toward flexible peaking power generation, integrating systemwide hydroelectric production to maximize revenue. These constituted major federal actions significantly affecting environment, implemented without EIS.

- **Power Plant Upratings (1979-1982)**: BOR completed rewinding of generators and turbine upratings increasing capacity from 1,150 to 1,336 megawatts. Environmental Assessment (December 1982) issued FONSI despite public and professional comments citing "glaring omissions, inaccuracies, obsolete and contradictory information, false assumptions, erroneous factual and legal conclusions." Independent review by Philip Williams and Associates found EA "conceals or greatly underestimates adverse environmental impacts" and "fails to relate the Project to any coherent environmental management goals."

- **Enhanced Peaking Operations**: Flow records and WAPA power marketing plans documented extreme operational shifts to peak demand periods. "Future Flow Patterns for CRSP Power Peaking Capacity" detailed plans already being implemented without NEPA review.

- **Flood Control Actions (1983-1984)**: Record precipitation and agency failure to plan conservatively resulted in unprecedented uncontrolled flows and bypasses causing severe environmental and recreational impacts. Congressional oversight hearings (September 1983) revealed BOR/WAPA operations "driven primarily by generating and brokering hydroelectricity for maximum revenues," arbitrarily relegating congressionally stated purposes (river regulation, flood control, fish and wildlife, recreation) to lesser priorities.

**Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) as Delay Tactic:**

In December 1982, following FONSI on power plant upratings, Secretary of Interior directed "multi-agency study" rather than EIS. GCES Final Report (January 1988) explicitly disclaimed binding commitment to NEPA: "This study was not intended to nor designed to lead directly to changes in dam operations."

GCES Phase One (1983-1988) concluded: (1) dam operations affect downstream resources, (2) some resources benefited while others adversely affected, (3) some impacts not fully understood, (4) some resources could be protected through modified operations, (5) understanding of relationships "is not complete."

National Academy of Sciences and GCES Executive Review Committee (1988) determined that due to "lack of reliable detailed information and severe limitations of the study, a decision on the operations of Glen Canyon Dam Power Plant could not be made." Secretary ordered additional Phase Two and Three studies rather than implementing EIS process, further delaying compliance.

**1989 Litigation Forces Action:**

*National Wildlife Federation v. WAPA* (Civil No. 88-C-1175G, September 1989) held for plaintiffs, enjoining WAPA from issuing 15-year power contracts without EIS assessing environmental impacts of power production policies. In response, Secretary of Interior ordered BOR (October 1989) to prepare EIS on Glen Canyon Dam operations. Notice of Intent published October 30, 1989 for two-year draft EIS examining "operation of Glen Canyon Dam and its impacts on downstream natural resources within Grand Canyon National Park and Grand Canyon National Recreation Area."

**Documented Environmental Degradation:**

Report concludes that 13 years of post-NEPA threshold delay resulted in unnecessary acceleration of environmental impacts, "most notably the permanent erosion of Colorado River beaches in Grand Canyon National Park." Approximately one-half of pre-dam beach area lost during this period. The Colorado River environment in Grand Canyon, afforded highest protection as National Park and World Heritage Site, "now stands significantly more degraded than it would have been had the agency in good faith complied with NEPA."

**Recommendations for Congressional Intervention:**

1. **Immediate Congressional investigation and oversight** into BOR activities and omissions regarding NEPA compliance.

2. **Legislation directing site-specific Glen Canyon EIS** be based on "non-discretionary preferred alternative" articulating maximum technically attainable impact mitigation, involving return toward base-load operating regime.

3. **Mandate study of technologies and methodologies** for restoration and stabilization of riparian environment in Grand Canyon.

4. **Implement interim protective flow regimes** during EIS process to prevent further irreversible impacts.

5. **Congressional legislation directing DOI preparation of Comprehensive EIS** regarding operations and management of all CRSP facilities and cumulative/synergistic impacts of all developments on upper and lower Colorado River, with analysis to: (a) redefine and institutionalize project purposes and priorities, (b) give environmental mitigation and enhancement top priority, (c) rationally integrate management in light of higher priorities, (d) assess constraints of present "law of the river," (e) evaluate water and power conservation and efficient use, (f) institutionalize systemwide instream flow values. Analysis "not to be constrained by present 'law of the river.'"

Report concludes: "Nothing short of immediate congressional intervention with creative reform of the obsolete and inefficient water and power delivery schemes presently existing, as well as the inflexible and incongruent 'Law of the River,' will prevent the continued and ultimate degradation of Grand Canyon and Colorado River environmental and recreational resources and values."
