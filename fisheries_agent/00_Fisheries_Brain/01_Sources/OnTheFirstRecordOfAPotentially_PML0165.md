# First Record of Sparus aurata in the Gulf of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sparidae

## Executive Summary

This paper documents the first confirmed record of a free-swimming, healthy Mediterranean [[Gilt-Head Seabream]] (*Sparus aurata* Linnaeus, 1758) in the [[Gulf of California]], Mexico. The fish was captured on 3 October 2007 in [[Bahía de La Paz]] during experimental fishing. The individual most likely escaped from a commercial culture operated by [[Biotecmar S.A.]], which had introduced at least 300,000 individuals in September 2005 in place of the permitted seabass. The authors argue that the species is a potentially harmful exotic, that the probability of this single capture reflects a much larger wild population, and that the event is an early warning for managers and scientists in the [[Eastern Pacific Ocean]].

## Background & Description

*Sparus aurata* is a Mediterranean sparid reaching 70 cm total length and 6 kg. It is a voracious, opportunistic predator whose diet shifts with local prey availability, although [[Pita et al. 2002]] describe it as more specialized on gastropods and bivalves. Its introduction through aquaculture elsewhere has been linked to eutrophication, disease transfer, and ecological disruption.

Disease records cited in the paper include:
- Lymphocystis disease virus (viral)
- *Pseudomonas anguilliseptica* (bacterial)
- *Ichthyophonus* (fungal), see [[Ichthyophonus Infection]]
- Several protozoan parasites, including *Leptotheca sparidarum*, *Ceratomyxa sparusaurati*, *Enteromyxum leei*, *Cryptosporidium molnari*, *Polysporoplasma sparis*, *Brooklynella hostilis*, and *Goussia sparis*

**Specimen description (CIBNOR collection, code CIBN-4336):**
- Female, 300 mm total length, 242 mm standard length, 356 g
- Oblong body; scaleless preopercle; upper and lower jaws with six and four front canines followed by molariform teeth
- Four upper and eight lower gill rakers
- Dorsal fin count XI, 13 (OCR-corrected)
- Lateral line scales to caudal base: 72 (the diagnostic count is 73)
- Diagnostic coloration: a large dark patch at the origin of the lateral line, a golden curved bar across the forehead bordered by two dark zones, and a black-edged caudal fin

**Species separation:** The only other sparid in the Gulf of California is [[Calamus Brachysomus]]. It has a deeper body, lacks the diagnostic coloration, and differs in dorsal fin counts (XI, 11–13) and lateral line counts (45–52). The other two eastern Pacific sparids, *Calamus taurinus* and *Archosargus pourtalesii*, which occur around the Galápagos Islands, also differ.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regional biodiversity and exotic-species policy:** The Gulf of California is described as a "world aquarium" with thousands of marine species and many endemics. Mexico's federal biodiversity authority, [[CONABIO]], produced a checklist of 82 introduced aquatic species. Of these, 77 are fish, and all are freshwater-associated. Invasive freshwater crayfish (*Orconectes virilis*, *Cherax quadricarinatus*, *Procambarus clarkii*) have affected only local continental reservoirs. The authors argue that marine invasions may be more consequential because natural barriers operate at a larger geographic scale.

**Aquaculture pathway:** In 2004, Biotecmar S.A. received permission to culture seabass in Bahía de La Paz, located at 24°N, about 90 miles from the tip of Baja California and 50 miles north of the Tropic of Cancer. In September 2005 the company instead introduced at least 300,000 *S. aurata*, which were held in eight circular net cages. Authorities, NGOs, and academic institutions sued to force their removal. The company was ordered to extract, kill, or sell all fish by July 2007. The authors state that the enforcement appears to have been unsuccessful because sightings continued.

**Field evidence before capture:**
- From August 2007, local fishermen reported an unfamiliar fish in the bay.
- Divers reported an unknown species near an experimental bivalve culture directly in front of the Biotecmar installations (pers. comm., J.J. Ramírez Rosas and E. Calvillo).
- Local reef fishes shy away from divers, but the unknown species approached them closely.

**Reproductive biology:** *S. aurata* is a [[Protandrous Hermaphroditism|protandrous hermaphrodite]], with about 5% of individuals hatching as female according to [[Kissil et al. 2001]]. The authors use this to argue that a single capture is unlikely by chance, so the wild population is probably large.

**Expected impacts:** Predicting disruption is difficult. The outcome depends on the magnitude of escapement and on whether the species can establish self-sustaining populations (see [[Exotic Species Introduction]]). Farmed fish with established wild populations elsewhere, including Atlantic and Pacific salmon, Channel catfish, Asian black carp, and tilapias, provide precedents for ecological harm (see [[Aquaculture Escapes]]).

## Key Findings

1. **First documented record:** A female *Sparus aurata* (300 mm TL, 356 g) was captured free-swimming in Bahía de La Paz on 3 October 2007 and deposited as CIBN-4336. This is the first record of the species in the Gulf of California.

2. **Likely source:** The fish probably escaped from the [[Biotecmar S.A.]] net-cage culture, which had at least 300,000 individuals introduced in September 2005, despite the company's permit being for seabass.

3. **Wild population likely larger than one specimen:** Corroborating fisher and diver reports since August 2007, together with the species' protandrous sex change, lead the authors to conclude that many individuals are probably present in the wild.

4. **Regulatory failure:** A court-ordered removal deadline of July 2007 apparently was not met, and sightings continued after it.

5. **Unambiguous identification:** The specimen matches the diagnostic characters of *S. aurata*. It is distinguished from the only local sparid, *Calamus brachysomus*, by body profile, coloration, and fin and lateral line counts.

6. **Potential harm:** The authors warn that *S. aurata* could become a persistent exotic with ecological impacts on marine ecosystems of the eastern Pacific, based on its documented disease load and predatory feeding.

7. **Call to action:** The paper is framed as a "first alert" for decision makers and the scientific community. It calls for monitoring and assessment of the species' spread, while acknowledging that specific impacts remain hard to predict.
