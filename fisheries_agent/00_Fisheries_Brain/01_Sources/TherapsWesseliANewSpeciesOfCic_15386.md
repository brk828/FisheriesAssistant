# New Honduran Cichlid *Theraps wesseli* and Rainforest Water Chemistry for Aquarium Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #characidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document has two parts. The first is a formal description of a new cichlid, [[Theraps Wesseli]] (Honduras Mojarra), from a small mountain drainage on the Caribbean slope of northern Honduras. The species was found by amateur naturalist [[Rusty Wessel]] and is named in his honor. The second part is an aquarium-science article by Weitzman, Palmer, Menezes and Burns on maintaining and breeding forest-adapted characins, especially [[Mimagoniates]], with emphasis on how water chemistry, seasonal rainfall, and contaminants shape reproduction.

## Background & Description

**Part 1: Taxonomy.** Miller notes that the Middle American cichlids (over 100 species), mostly placed in *Cichlosoma* by Regan (1905), remain generically unresolved because phylogenetic relationships are poorly known. He uses *Theraps* for the new species, while acknowledging its limits are unknown.

The new species is based on nine specimens (3 juveniles, 4 females, 2 males). The holotype, UMMZ 231103, is an adult male of 110 mm standard length (SL). Paratypes are held at the [[University Of Michigan Museum Of Zoology]] (UMMZ).

**Diagnosis.** The species is a slender cichlid distinguished by:
- a very slender body;
- a lateral stripe running from the opercle to the caudal fin base, at or above the lower lateral line level;
- no vertical bars on the sides;
- a round or squarish black spot at the caudal fin base;
- a pointed snout set high on the head;
- an oblique mouth;
- jet-black nuptial coloration on the venter in both sexes.

Its closest relative is believed to be [[Theraps Microphthalmus]] of the [[Rio Motagua Basin]].

**Meristics (holotype).** Dorsal XVIII,11; anal VI,9; pectorals 15-15; lateral scales 30; upper lateral line 20; lower lateral line 13; cheek scales 6; caudal peduncle scales 19; vertebrae 13 + 17 = 30. Across the series, dorsal spines ranged XVI–XIX and anal soft rays 7–9.

**Part 2: Aquarium article.** The authors work on the systematics, anatomy, and reproductive biology of the eight known species of the glandulocaudine tribe. They state they have not performed controlled experiments; their husbandry methods reflect field observation and practical aquarium trial and error.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Theraps wesseli habitat.** The species was captured at the [[Rio Belleaire]] crossing near Entelina, in the [[Rio Papaloteca Basin]], Departamento de Atlantida. Recorded conditions were:
- maximum width 20 m, flow about 1 m/s;
- substrate: boulders 45%, rocky rubble 25%, riffle and sand 25%, silt 5%;
- depth about 1.8 m, little marginal vegetation, no bankside trees;
- water temperature 76°F (24.5°C).

The fish prefers boulder and rock areas. The rubber-like fold of a nearly even-height dorsal fin is interpreted as an adaptation to swift, rocky water. Known localities are three sites along CA13 (the Rio Jutiapa, Rio Belleaire, and Rio Huaron), all within the Papaloteca basin. The basin drains the Cordillera Nombre de Dios into the Gulf of Honduras, Caribbean Sea. Martin (1972) had surveyed Honduran freshwater fishes earlier, collecting about 26,500 specimens from 182 localities, so the discovery was unexpected.

**Mimagoniates and forest streams.** Weitzman et al. describe Mimagoniates as occurring mainly in coastal streams of the [[Atlantic Forest]] of Brazil. That forest is now 95–98% altered. Key points include:
- Adults live in relatively quiet but moving water near vegetation, in streams of low gradient.
- Water depth is usually less than 2 feet (about 60 cm).
- Courtship and insemination occur in open water near shore vegetation; egg-laying occurs near the bottom on adjacent vegetation.
- Natural waters are soft (about 10–50 ppm total hardness), somewhat acid to neutral (pH 5.5–7.0), well oxygenated, and low in nitrites, ammonia, and nitrates.

**Seasonal and flood effects.** In the Amazon, seasonal rainfall floods forest, bringing abundant invertebrate food and freshening the water by dilution, which likely triggers breeding. In the smaller eastern Brazilian coastal streams, temperature appears more important than rainfall for Mimagoniates, particularly in the south, where cold winters suppress courtship. The authors contend that the notion of a stable tropical forest environment is a myth.

**Water chemistry data cited:**
- Rio Negro (blackwater): pH 3.6–5.8.
- Amazon above the Rio Negro confluence: pH about 6.5–7.4.
- Venezuelan Guayana soils: pH about 3.5 (stream water not recorded).
- Rio Negro and Rio Solimões confluence: the warmer, acidic black water meets cooler, muddy white water, causing turbulence.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic findings (Theraps wesseli)**
- *Theraps wesseli* is described as new, from nine specimens (holotype 110 mm SL), and differs from its closest relative *T. microphthalmus* in lateral stripe position, body depth, caudal spot shape, and snout form (see Table 3 in source).
- Measurements show the new species is more slender than *T. microphthalmus* (for example, body depth 301–352‰ of SL in adults versus a deeper body in the comparison species).
- The species is known from three localities in the Rio Papaloteca basin, all within a restricted, mountainous range.
- Its generic placement is provisional, pending a broader revision of Middle American cichlids.

**Breeding behavior (Theraps wesseli)**
- Nuptial colors (jet-black ventral surfaces, chalky-blue fin and scale tips in males) appear about a week before breeding and last until fry reach about 25 mm.
- The female guards the eggs; the male defends the nest area against fish and other predators but leaves when a human hand threatens.
- Observations came from both a Kentucky aquarium and the wild.

**Husbandry and reproduction (Weitzman et al.)**
- Stable, soft, slightly acid water (pH about 5.5–7.0) is important for breeding many forest-stream fishes. The authors recommend pH 6.5 for [[Nematobrycon Palmeri]] after it was stressed at pH 3.5, though it survived and bred at 4.5–6.8.
- Hard water (over 300 ppm) and pH 7.8–8.2 suited [[Hyphessobrycon Scholzei]], suggesting limestone origin, which contrasts with the soft-water norm.
- Cooler water can trigger spawning. In one case, a [[Rosy Barb]] pair spawned after half their water was replaced with cooler, aged water (78°F to 74°F), yielding about 75 young. Weekly partial water changes also stimulated female [[Mimagoniates]] egg-laying.
- Rainwater substitutes are needed because urban rainwater is polluted and tap water rarely matches forest waters.

**Contaminants**
- Lead and zinc oxides used in older aquarium cements, and copper or lead pipes, can dissolve into soft, acidic water and harm eggs, sperm, or fry; [[Heavy Metal Contamination]] can block breeding even when adults appear healthy.
- Arsenic-containing white silicone bath sealants caused failed breeding of [[Corydoras Pygmaeus]]. Eggs in tanks sealed with clear silicone had a 90–95% hatch rate.
- Gold-mining mercury and sediment threaten downstream fishes in parts of Brazil, Guyana, and Venezuela.

**Conclusion.** The authors argue that aquarists should replicate the chemical and physical stability, seasonal patterns, and temperature cycles of a species' natural habitat, and test stimuli such as cooler water or freshening, since these can be the difference between successful and failed breeding.
