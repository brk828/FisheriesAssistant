# Recovery Implementation Program (RIPRAP) for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) document, revised April 1, 1999, contains two linked parts: a Section 7 Consultation, Sufficient Progress, and Historic Projects Agreement (October 15, 1993), and the Recovery Implementation Program Recovery Action Plan (RIPRAP). Together they govern how the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) serves as a [[Reasonable and Prudent Alternative]] under the Endangered Species Act for water projects in the Upper Basin. The program targets four endangered fishes: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]]. The estimated budget for FY1999–FY2005 is approximately $96 million. The plan covers flow protection, habitat restoration, nonnative fish control, genetic stocking, monitoring, public outreach and program management, organized by subbasin.

## Background & Description

The RIP aims to go beyond offsetting water depletions and to achieve full recovery of the four endangered fishes, with the overall goal of naturally self-sustaining populations and protected habitat. The recovery goals are as follows:

- **Colorado Pikeminnow** (formerly called Colorado squawfish): establish and maintain self-sustaining populations and habitat.
- **Humpback Chub**: establish and maintain self-sustaining populations and habitat.
- **Bonytail**: prevent extinction, given its critical upper-basin status.
- **Razorback Sucker**: prevent extinction in the wild, given limited evidence of successful recruitment.

The [[Recovery Action Plan]] is an adaptive management plan. It is reviewed annually by September 30, or before the annual work plan is adopted, and it guides research, recovery and budgeting. Actions are flagged by a caret (">") if they produce measurable population or habitat responses, protect flows, or reduce the threat of immediate extinction. An asterisk (*) marks actions that contribute to avoiding [[Critical Habitat]] destruction or adverse modification. The plan's structure has seven recovery elements: protect instream flows; restore habitat; reduce nonnative fish impacts; conserve genetic integrity and augment populations; monitor and research; increase public awareness; and provide program planning and support.

The agreement applies only to the four Colorado River fishes in the Upper Basin, excluding the San Juan River. It is explicitly not a precedent for other species or locations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat loss and fragmentation.** Upper Basin floodplains were historically inundated by spring runoff. Today much of the river is channelized by levees, dikes, rip-rap and tamarisk. Peak spring flows have also been reduced by upstream impoundments. Flooded bottomlands contain large numbers of zooplankton and benthic organisms, and razorback suckers use them extensively for feeding. Passage barriers have confined the fishes to 20 percent of their former range. Blockage by dams and diversions is suggested as an important cause of Colorado pikeminnow decline.

**Thermal and flow alteration.** Cold water released from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] is presumed to make the Green River below the dam unsuitable, and it may be the primary reason the endangered fishes disappeared from that reach.

**Contaminants.** Selenium, petroleum derivatives, heavy metals and uranium have been identified in the upper basin, along with suspected contaminant hot spots. Uranium processing in the 1940s–1960s severely affected the [[Dolores River]]. Selenium contamination at Stewart Lake and Ashley Creek is a source of impact to razorback suckers. Contaminant clean-up is pursued by other agencies outside the Recovery Program. The agreement excludes discharges of pollutants such as trace elements, heavy metals and pesticides from its protection scope.

**Nonnative fishes.** Of 52 fish species in the upper basin, only 13 are native. Competition and predation by nonnative species are widely assumed to have contributed to decline, though direct evidence is difficult to obtain. Northern pike are known to prey on native fishes in the Yampa River.

## Key Findings

### 1. Section 7 framework

- The RIP serves as the reasonable and prudent alternative for depletion impacts of new projects and for all past and existing impacts of historic projects, excluding pollutant discharges.
- Historic projects operating before January 22, 1988 (Cooperative Agreement execution date) may be offset if needed for recovery. Depletion increases after that date are subject to the depletion charge.
- The FWS determines whether recovery progress is sufficient, using accomplishments under the RIP as its measure. Sufficiency is evaluated separately for the Colorado and Green River subbasins.
- If progress is insufficient, biological opinions specify RIPRAP actions that must be completed on schedule (historic projects) or before the project's impact (new projects).
- The agreement takes effect on adoption of the RIPRAP by the Implementation Committee. The FWS has ultimate authority over sufficiency decisions.

### 2. Funding

- The estimated total budget for FY1999–FY2005 is approximately $96 million.
- The annual operating budget is about $3 million, totaling about $25 million through FY2005, contributed by Reclamation, FWS, and the states of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming.
- The depletion fee for new projects was $14.13 per acre-foot as of October 1, 1998, adjusted annually for inflation.
- Operation and maintenance for refugia, hatcheries and fish passage is about $7 million through FY2005.
- Capital costs are about $64 million through FY2005, covering water acquisition, fish passages, hatcheries and flooded bottomland restoration. This total is capped.

### 3. Flow protection

- Colorado uses three mechanisms: "Acquire" (water right purchase or lease), "Appropriate" (new instream filings, which take three to four years to decree and carry junior priority), and "Deliver" (reservoir reoperation).
- Utah conditions new and pending water right approvals on predetermined streamflow levels. This policy was adopted by the State Engineer.
- In the 15-Mile Reach, Colorado was granted a 581 cfs instream flow right for July–September after a 1997 decree.
- Reclamation can provide up to 31,650 acre-feet from [[Ruedi Reservoir]] (since 1990) and up to 6,000 acre-feet from Wolford Mountain Reservoir.
- Colorado filed a junior instream flow right on the Yampa River in December 1995. Forty-eight statements of opposition were filed. The recovery flow filing was withdrawn, and the base flow portion is being re-evaluated.

### 4. Habitat restoration and passage

- A floodplain habitat inventory covered 870 miles of the Colorado, Green, Gunnison, Yampa and White rivers in 1994. Restoration sites are being screened and restored through at least 2003.
- Fish passage was restored at [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] (Gunnison River, fish ladder completed 1996) and at the Grand Valley Irrigation Company diversion near Palisade. Passage at Price-Stubb is underway. Five sites in total have been identified as needing passage, and restoration could open about 50 miles of historic habitat.
- Four floodplain sites on the Colorado River have been restored, and five BLM sites plus three Ouray Refuge sites on the Green River.

### 5. Subbasin priorities

- **Green River**: the largest known population of razorback suckers occurs in its riverine habitat. A revised [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] biological opinion is due in December 1999.
- **Yampa and Little Snake rivers**: the Yampa is one of two confirmed Colorado pikeminnow spawning areas in the Upper Basin. The Little Snake supplies about 28 percent of the Yampa's flow and 60 percent of its sediment.
- **Duchesne River**: 1997–1998 surveys of the lower 33 miles captured 23 Colorado pikeminnow and 3 razorback suckers. The Duchesne Biological Opinion was issued in 1998.
- **White River**: Taylor Draw Dam (1984) blocked pikeminnow migration to upper reaches. Interim flow recommendations are due by September 1999.
- **Colorado River**: the 15-Mile Reach is a recovery focal point, where pikeminnow catch rates are roughly double other areas. Razorback reproduction is not evident. In 1993, 67 adult razorbacks were collected from isolated ponds near Debeque.
- **Gunnison River**: the Aspinall Unit biological opinion is due in 2000. Experimental razorback stocking began in 1995.
- **Dolores River**: recovery is limited to preventing escapement of nonnative sportfish from McPhee Reservoir. Pikeminnow stocking is planned to begin in 1999.

### 6. Genetics and stocking

- Stocking is treated as an interim tool, since recovery requires self-sustaining populations. Success depends on flow protection, habitat restoration and nonnative fish management.
- Four core documents guide genetics management: Genetics Management Guidelines, Genetics Management Plan, Annual Propagation Operations Plan and Coordinated Hatchery Facility Plan.
- Initial stocking focused on razorback suckers and bonytails. The new plan adds Colorado pikeminnow to the Colorado and Dolores rivers.

### 7. Nonnative fish management

- A nonnative fish control strategy prioritizes removal options through 2003.
- States and FWS adopted stocking procedures and signed a memorandum of understanding.
- Critical habitat modifications added removal of northern pike from the Yampa River and of northern pike and centrarchids from the Gunnison River and possibly Paonia Reservoir.

### 8. Critical habitat

- The final rule designating critical habitat was published March 21, 1994, and became effective April 20, 1994.
- Primary constituent elements are water (quantity and quality), physical habitat (channels, bottomlands, backwaters) and biological environment (food, predation, competition).
- Modifications were mostly refinements. They include adjudicating instream flow filings, replacing annual bulldozing of unstable Yampa diversions, and adding an entrainment structure at the Government Highline (Roller Dam) passage.

### 9. Monitoring and outreach

- Monitoring is tied to measurable population responses, but these may take many years to appear. Progress in the interim is measured by completed RIPRAP actions.
- The public information program includes a semiannual newsletter, a web page, a technical library, educational materials, and angler information. Its outreach covers local, state and national audiences.
