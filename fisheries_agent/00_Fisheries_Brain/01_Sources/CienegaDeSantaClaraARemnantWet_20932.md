# Cienega de Santa Clara: Vegetation Distribution and Effects of Water Flow Reduction

#ichthyology #marine-biology #typhaceae

## Executive Summary

This study characterizes the vegetation of the [[Cienega de Santa Clara]], the largest remaining wetland in the [[Rio Colorado Delta]] (Sonora, Mexico), and documents how an eight-month interruption of canal inflow affected it. The wetland, sustained by agricultural drainage from the [[Wellton-Mohawk Main Outlet Drain Extension]] (M.O.D.E.), is dominated by [[Typha Domingensis]] (cattail) and supports endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] populations. Vegetation distribution tracked salinity and water depth. During the dry-down, 60–70% of marsh foliage died back, yet Typha recovered from dormant rhizomes after flow returned in January 1994. The authors conclude that the vegetation is resilient to temporary disruption, but prolonged flow reduction, such as that projected for the [[Yuma Desalting Plant]], would substantially shrink the wetland and eliminate salt-sensitive species.

## Background & Description

The [[Rio Colorado Delta]] and northern [[Gulf of California]] once formed a major desert estuary bordered by freshwater and tidal wetlands. Diversion of the Colorado River and agricultural development have eliminated most of these systems, leaving small remnant wetlands that provide habitat for migrating waterfowl and rare species.

The Cienega is a brackish wetland that began forming in 1977 when M.O.D.E. drain water was discharged into a former overflow arm of the river, after [[Hoover Dam]] had left the area an unvegetated salt flat. The discharge was intended to be temporary, pending completion of the Yuma Desalting Plant, which has never been fully operated. The wetland now covers about 12,000 ha, of which the uppermost 4,200 ha is thickly vegetated. In 1993 the Cienega was placed in the core zone of the [[Alto Golfo de California y Delta del Rio Colorado Biosphere Reserve]].

Field work combined aerial photomosaics (80 photographs at 1:24,000 scale), vertical aerial videography from a Cessna 182 at 1,200 m, GPS-georeferenced zoom shots, helicopter and ground surveys (6 site visits, 1992–1994), and an [[ARC/INFO GIS Database]]. Video zoom sampling points were not random and were not analyzed statistically; presence/absence was displayed graphically.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and water quality.** M.O.D.E. discharge averaged 5.5 m³ s⁻¹ over 1977–1993 at about 3 ppt total dissolved solids (TDS). The Riito Drain added about 1.0 m³ s⁻¹ at 4 ppt TDS. Gila River floods in early 1993 brought high flows of reduced salinity and increased turbidity. Canal repairs then reduced flows to 0.0–0.5 m³ s⁻¹ from May 1993 to January 1994. Regular delivery resumed on January 18, 1994. Water entered the marsh at about 3 ppt TDS and 3.0 ppm nitrate, and both declined along a low-salinity plume that followed the [[Cerro Prieto Fault]].

**Geomorphology.** A low natural barrier separates the basin from the Gulf. Seawater crosses it during extreme tides or strong winds, forming a hypersaline (40–50 ppt) lagoon south of the vegetated area. Water depth ranged from 70–110 cm in the interior to 15–30 cm at the margins. The adjacent [[El Doctor Wetlands]] are spring-fed seeps (pozos) at the foot of the Gran Desierto Escarpment.

**Disturbance.** Residents burn Typha to open water for boat passage, promote new growth, and attract waterfowl. Cattle graze the new growth.

## Key Findings

- **Dominance and distribution.** Typha domingensis occurred in 53 of 56 video zoom samples. Scirpus spp. occurred at 47 of 56 sites, Phragmites australis at 20, Distichlis spp. at 24, Allenrolfea occidentalis at 10, Tamarix ramosissima at 13, and Juncus cooperi at 3.
- **Salinity as the main control.** Salinity was the major determinant of the vegetated area's size and of species distribution. Burnett et al. (1993) estimated the Typha salinity limit at about 6 ppt. Phragmites, which is salt-sensitive, lay mainly along the low-salinity fault plume. Distichlis palmeri, a salt-tolerant intertidal species, occupied the tidal southern and western margins.
- **Floristic richness.** A total of 24 wetland plant species were recorded in the Cienega and 29 in the El Doctor wetlands, plus three submerged aquatic species. The El Doctor wetlands' greater diversity was attributed partly to lower salinity (1–2 ppt versus a 3 ppt minimum in the Cienega). No endemic species were found in the delta region, but Distichlis palmeri is restricted to Gulf shores.
- **Exotic species.** Three non-native species were recorded: Tamarix ramosissima, Polypogon monspeliensis, and Rumex dentatus.
- **Flow-reduction impact.** Following the dry-down, net vegetation losses were 70% (upper), 67% (mid-central), and 38% (lower) relative to November 1992. The mean net reduction was 58% (S.D. = 14.4%). Large-scale living cover fell by about 70%, with green vegetation restricted to a narrow tongue along the fault line.
- **Resilience.** By March 15, 1994, the vegetation was regreening. By May 5–6, 1994, the Cienega appeared fully green. Typha recovered mainly from dormant rhizomes, and new seedlings appeared in one newly flooded location.
- **Fish and wildlife.** Desert pupfish were numerous in shallow margin sites, possibly the largest population anywhere. Abarca et al. (1993) estimated that half of the remaining breeding pairs of Yuma clapper rails are in the main body of the Cienega. The site is also a key shorebird breeding and staging area, and peregrine falcons and bald eagles were reported there in 1993.
- **Future threats.** Full operation of the Yuma Desalting Plant is projected to reduce flow by 65% and raise inflow salinity to 7.2 ppt TDS or more. This would likely eliminate Typha and other salt-sensitive species. The authors recommend planned binational water management that includes the Cienega.
