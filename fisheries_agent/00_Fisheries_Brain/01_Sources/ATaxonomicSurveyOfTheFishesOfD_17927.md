# Taxonomic Survey of the Fishes of Delta National Wildlife Refuge with Emphasis upon Distribution and Abundance

#ichthyology #marine-biology #estuarine-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Louisiana State University M.S. thesis by John Richard Kelley, Jr. (1965) surveys the fishes of [[Delta National Wildlife Refuge]], a brackish and fresh estuarine section of the [[Mississippi River Delta]], over August 1963 to January 1965. The study recorded 79 species in 33 families, quantified standing crops with [[Block-Off Net Sampling]], and tested how [[Salinity]] and [[Water Temperature]] govern distribution. Salinity limited freshwater species, while temperature and pond accessibility controlled the distribution, abundance and seasonality of marine species. The refuge functions as a nursery ground for several marine species.

## Background & Description

**Study rationale.** The author notes that the estuarine area of North America's largest river had received no prior attention, despite the value of estuaries as commercial and sport fishing grounds and as links in marine fish life cycles. Objectives were to determine species occurrence, distribution and abundance, the effect of salinity on distribution, and whether species show seasonal cycles.

**Study area.** The refuge lies in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, between latitudes 29°9' and 29°20' N, and was established by Executive Order 7229 in 1935. It covered 32,108 acres originally and encompasses 48,799.1 acres today. The land results from the opening of [[Cubits Gap]] in 1862. Main Pass is the dominant outlet, with Octave, Brant and Raphael passes smaller. Ponds are typically shallow, averaging about 3 feet deep, with [[Horseshoe Pond]] the deepest at roughly 4.5 feet. Vegetation includes roseau cane (*Phragmites communis*), oyster grass, widgeongrass and water hyacinth.

**Sampling gear.** Gear included a 16-foot otter trawl, a 50-foot bag seine (later abandoned), a boat-mounted electric shocker, gill and trammel nets, and rotenone (1 ppm). Passive nets were 40-yard gill nets, a 125-foot experimental gill net, and trammel nets. Identification followed the American Fisheries Society (1960) nomenclature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and climate.** The area has a sub-tropical coastal climate. Mean annual precipitation at Burwood (1931–1952) was 58.2 inches. Average river stages were 1.63 feet in 1963 and 2.06 feet in 1964. About 12 per cent of total river flow passes through Cubits Gap, at 94,300 cfs at a 3.6-foot stage. Tides are typically 12 to 16 inches and mainly affect the deeper passes.

**Salinity regime.** Pond salinities were generally below 5.0 ppt and lowest in spring and summer during peak river stage. Salinity rose in fall and winter as saline water entered through the passes and mixed into the ponds. Salinity increased from south to north, reflecting gulf openings, and the ponds north of Main Pass recorded the highest salinities on the refuge.

**Temperature regime.** Pond summer averages were 30.8 °C (1963) and 30.5 °C (1964). Pond water averaged 21.3 °C in October, and the minimum was 11.1 °C in January 1965. Passes ran 1 to 2 °C cooler than ponds.

**Habitat disturbance.** Oil operations and dredging altered the marshes. Canals allowed greater saltwater intrusion, but many were later plugged, which deepened some ponds.

## Key Findings

**Community composition**
- 79 species in 33 families were collected; the taxonomic checklist includes sharks, rays, gars, herrings, catfishes, killifishes, sciaenids, gobies and flounders.
- Centrarchidae (8 species) was the most abundant freshwater family; Sciaenidae was the most abundant marine family.
- Striped mullet and blue catfish were the most abundant species. Blue catfish accounted for 16.61 per cent of standing crop estimates.

**Standing crop**
- Block-off samples (28 samples, 12 locations) averaged 47.0 lb/acre in area 1 (north of Main Pass), 77.60 lb/acre in area 2 (between Main and Octave passes) and 115.5 lb/acre in area 3 (south of Octave Pass).
- Individual samples ranged from 1.22 lb/acre (area 1) to 258.82 lb/acre (area 3).
- Ponds south of Main Pass had higher standing crops than those north of it.
- Marine euryhaline species made up 15.12 per cent of ponds sampled. Their pounds per acre did not differ significantly among areas at the 0.05 level.

**Nursery function**
- Young of Atlantic croaker, spot, sand seatrout, spotted seatrout and largescale menhaden were present seasonally.
- Adult species with ripe eggs or young included Atlantic needlefish, Atlantic stingray, bluntnose stingray and gulf pipefish.
- Young-of-the-year blue catfish stay in the passes for one to two years before moving into ponds.

**Salinity and temperature effects**
- Freshwater species showed a clear relationship with salinity. Golden shiners were absent from ponds above 4.0 ppt, and river carpsucker was absent from waters above 2.5 ppt.
- Most marine species were not limited by salinity. Temperature governed their seasonal occurrence more strongly, with summer species such as flounders, sheepshead and ladyfish, and fall-winter species such as croakers, sand seatrout and menhaden.
- Accessibility to ponds, via passes and high water, was the principal control on marine fish abundance.

**Notable records**
- Bull shark (3 specimens, 31.4–35.0 inches) was recorded in Long Island Bayou and Delta Bend.
- A single gray snapper and a single goldeye were collected, probably at the edge of their ranges.
- Freshwater drum (87 specimens) and sand seatrout were widespread.

**Conclusions**
- The Mississippi delta estuary is an important nursery area for several marine species.
- Brackish ponds sustain a diverse fish assemblage, with salinity and temperature as the main controlling factors.
