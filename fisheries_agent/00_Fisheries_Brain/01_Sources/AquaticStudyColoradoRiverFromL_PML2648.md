# Distribution and Abundance of Fishes of the Lower Colorado River (1980 Special Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Phoenix Ecological Services special report, dated May 19, 1980, summarizes the historical and current status of the fish fauna of the [[Colorado River]] from Lees Ferry to the Southern International Boundary. It was prepared under a Memorandum of Agreement dated January 22, 1979 (Contract No. 9-07-03-X0066) and built from literature searches and communications with state, federal, university and independent experts. No biological inventories were undertaken. The report is a general guide, not an official endangered species list for [[Section 7 Consultation]]. It documents a dramatic shift from fewer than 25 native and non-native species around 1900 to a fauna dominated by introduced species after the [[Dam Construction Era]], and it catalogs 79 fish taxa, distribution and abundance charts, stocking records, protected species status, and museum collections.

## Background & Description

**River setting.** The Colorado River is described as the fifth longest river in the United States, flowing about 1,450 miles from the Rocky Mountains of Colorado and Wyoming through Utah, Arizona, Nevada and California to the Gulf of California. The U.S. watershed covers 242,000 square miles, plus 2,000 square miles in Mexico. The [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922 set the upper/lower basin division at Compact Point, one mile below the Paria River confluence near Lees Ferry; the lower basin covers about 141,000 square miles.

**Reach gradients.** From Lees Ferry to Lake Mead backwaters the gradient is about 8 feet per river mile through the [[Grand Canyon]]. From Hoover Dam to Imperial Dam the gradient is about 1.8 feet per mile across broad, flat valleys of the Basin and Range Province.

**Historic flow regime.** Pre-dam flows peaked in June with spring runoff and declined by August, with low flows typically from September through February. Documented extremes include an all-time maximum estimated at 400,000 cfs around 1862 and a minimum of 538 cfs above Imperial Dam on August 3, 1934. Since Hoover Dam the average discharge below it was 13,270 cfs (1935–1977). At Lees Ferry the average was 17,850 cfs (1921–1962) before Glen Canyon Dam and 12,170 cfs (1964–1977) after.

**Sediment.** The river was historically very silty, the source of its name ("Too thick to drink; too thin to plow"). Annual silt load at Lees Ferry was estimated at 100 million tons before Glen Canyon Dam closure. Lake Mead accumulated 2,716,000 acre-feet between 1935 and 1964. Silt moving past Yuma fell from about 138,000 acre-feet annually before dams to 2,070 acre-feet in 1940.

**Dams.** Construction began with Laguna Diversion Dam (1909) and proceeded through [[Hoover Dam]] (1935), Imperial Dam (1938), [[Parker Dam]] (1938, Lake Havasu), Headgate Rock Dam (1944), Morelos Dam (1950), [[Davis Dam]] (1953, Lake Mohave), Palo Verde Diversion Dam (1957), [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (1964, Lake Powell) and Senator Wash Dam (1966). Protective works (levees, riprap) and channelization were also applied for flood control, water delivery and hydropower.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Before impoundment the river was warm, swift and turbid, with little submergent vegetation and nearly no invertebrates, supporting low biotic diversity. Native fishes such as the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were adapted to these conditions. After dam construction the water cleared, flows stabilized, and the river supported greater diversity. Submergent vegetation developed, and many introduced and exotic species, both naturally migrating and stocked, thrived. Many less adaptive native fishes became locally extinct and are threatened with total extinction.

The report frames ecological change as the central management issue; the authors state that efforts were under way to understand the impacts and manage the river wisely.

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna and synonymy**
- The synonymy lists fishes known to have occurred in the mainstem from Lees Ferry to the International Boundary, arranged by order and family following the [[American Fisheries Society]] classification (Bailey et al., 1970). It includes 79 numbered taxa, spanning orders such as Acipenseriformes, Clupeiformes, Salmoniformes, Cypriniformes, Siluriformes, Atheriniformes and Perciformes.
- Notable entries include [[White Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser transmontanus*), [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Brown Trout]], [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Striped Bass]] and [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).
- Non-native taxa include [[Goldfish]], [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], tilapia and several aquarium-trade species.

**Distribution and abundance**
- Pre-dam conditions supported fewer than 25 species at the turn of the century, both native and non-native.
- After Hoover Dam was completed in 1935 the species count increased sharply, with more than 20 species introduced in the 1950s alone.
- Charts cover circa 1880 to present, organized into numbered river regions (1–5) matching USGS hydrologic units, with lettered subdivisions by dams or barriers. Occurrence is documented by the Pacific Southwest Interagency Committee river mile system (1976).
- Abundance is categorized as abundant, common or rare, taken directly from the literature without further analysis.

**Fishes of 1970–1979**
- A regional presence table summarizes species recorded in the 1970s across Regions 1–5. Examples include cutthroat trout, rainbow trout, flannelmouth sucker, and the widespread [[Largemouth Bass]], among others.

**Stocking records**
- The report chronicles federal and state stocking from circa 1880 to 1977 using hatchery and agency files from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], the California Department of Fish and Game, the Nevada Department of Fish and Game, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department.
- Records are often contradictory or incomplete. For example, Jones and Sumner report Nevada planting 106,144 rainbow trout in Lake Mohave in 1945, which Nevada's own records do not list. Some locations are recorded only as "lower Colorado River."
- Recommendation: the Lower Colorado River Management Program Work Group should develop a standardized method for recording stocking data and a centralized office should maintain the records.

**Protected and unique species (Table 2)**
- Federal listings noted include Humpback Chub, Bonytail, Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker as endangered; Arizona trout (*Salmo apache*) as threatened; and Virgin River spinedace, Moapa dace and woundfin as proposed or listed in various categories. Roundtail chub and Gila chub were under review.
- State categories (California, Arizona Groups I–IV, Nevada endangered/rare) are summarized in the report.
- The report notes that federal critical habitat proposals were withdrawn as of March 6, 1979, pending supplemental information under the 1978 Endangered Species Act amendments.

**Museum collections**
- The report lists 15 institutions holding Colorado River fish specimens, including Arizona State University, the California Academy of Sciences, Museum of Northern Arizona, Scripps Institution of Oceanography, U.S. National Museum, Stanford University, Tulane University and University of Michigan Museum of Zoology.

**Conclusions and purpose**
- The report is intended as a general management guide. Its key message is that damming transformed the lower river from a turbid, variable system with a few specialized native fishes into a clearer, more stable habitat dominated by introduced species, with many natives now threatened.
