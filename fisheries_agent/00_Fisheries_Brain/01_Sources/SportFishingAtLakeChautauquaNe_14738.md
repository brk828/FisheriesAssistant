# Sport Fishing at Lake Chautauqua, Illinois, 1950 and 1951

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #sport-fishery

## Executive Summary

This preliminary report from the Illinois Natural History Survey and Illinois Department of Conservation (Biological Notes No. 30, 1952) documents a creel census of the sport fishery at [[Lake Chautauqua]], a reflooded drainage district on the [[Illinois River]] floodplain near Havana. Recorded catches were 36,822 fish in 1950 and 56,289 fish in 1951, comprising 25 species. The study examined how water level, season, population cohorts and angler technique affected catches, and it issued practical tackle and technique recommendations. The authors describe the findings as preliminary and do not present final conclusions.

## Background & Description

The bottomland lakes of the Illinois River were once among the most productive sport and commercial fisheries in the country. Between 1900 and 1920 many were drained for agriculture, and the river fishery declined. Lake Chautauqua was drained by the Chautauqua Drainage and Levee District (organized 1916), flooded in 1926, and purchased in 1936 by the United States Biological Survey (predecessor of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]) as a migratory waterfowl refuge. Levees were repaired and control gates installed by 1940.

The lake covers 3,562 acres with an average depth of about 3.2 feet at normal pool. It has a mud bottom, narrow sandy beaches on the east shore, and little wind protection. Its turbidity and loss of vegetation followed the near-record flood of 1943. A 1950 silt survey by the [[Illinois Water Survey]] found storage capacity reduced by 18.3 per cent in 23.75 years, and sediments were highly fertile, partly due to waterfowl excreta.

Fieldwork began April 15, 1950, under a cooperative program of the Illinois Natural History Survey, the Illinois Department of Conservation and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Its aims were to value the sport and commercial fisheries, develop management to raise yields, study fish biology and estimate population dynamics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water levels:** At low, stable stages the lake is controlled by refuge gates (pool 435.0 ft, base 430.0 ft). At moderate to high river stages it connects with the Illinois River, and the lake was connected for 7½ months in 1951.
- **Vegetation:** Aquatic plants were abundant before 1943 and largely failed to re-establish afterward. The authors tentatively link this loss to the decline of [[Yellow Perch]], which they consider dependent on aquatic vegetation for spawning.
- **Structure:** Man-made brush piles 50 to 100 yards offshore and natural [[Buckbrush]] (*Cephalanthus occidentalis*) along the shore served as fish habitat and fishing sites. Fall and winter crappies congregated in brush piles.
- **Dominant-brood cycles:** Citing [[David H. Thompson]] (1941) on Lake Senachwine, the authors describe dominant crappie year-classes that periodically dominate and then deplete the population through cannibalism and natural mortality.
- **Access control:** Shore fishing was prohibited. Anglers used four boat yards, which issued free permit cards that doubled as [[Creel Census]] records.

## Key Findings

**Catch and effort**
- 1950: 36,822 fish in 10,459 fisherman-days (812 fish taken through ice in late December).
- January–February 1951 ice fishing: 14,546 fish in 1,026 fisherman-days; crappies made up 98.3 per cent.
- Whole 1951 season: 56,289 fish in 13,630 fisherman-days.
- Average annual yield was 5.6 lb per acre, with fishing pressure of 18.2 man-hours per acre.
- Over 80 per cent of anglers drove 25 miles or more, mostly from the Peoria–Pekin area (40–50 miles).

**Species composition**
- Crappies (white and black) made up 27.4 per cent of the 1950 catch and 63.0 per cent in 1951. Bluegill was 20.7 and 16.6 per cent, freshwater drum 25.7 and 7.6 per cent.
- Compared with 1941 and 1942 (Hansen 1942, one boat yard), yellow perch fell from 21.2 and 15.5 per cent to 1.4 and 0.8 per cent, and freshwater drum rose from 1.2 and 0.1 per cent.

**[[White Crappie]]**
- Dominated crappie catches; fishing was driven by the dominant 1948 year-class.
- The 1948 brood averaged 8.5 inches by September 1950 and 10.0 inches by fall 1951. The largest white crappie handled was just over 14 inches, aged 7 years.
- Spawning was very poor in 1949 and 1951 and comparatively successful in 1950.
- Tagging showed hundreds of thousands of fish disappeared between October 1950 and October 1951; no tag returns came from outside the lake, so the authors believe most died in the lake.
- Decline was predicted for fall 1952 or 1953, with a possible good fishery in fall 1955.

**[[Black Crappie]]**
- Made up a small share of catches. The 1950 brood may support fishing in 1952–53.
- Anglers were advised to fish the buckbrush for black crappies when white crappie populations are low.
- Diagnostic features: white crappie usually has six dorsal spines (black has seven or eight); in the black crappie, BC ≈ AB.

**[[Bluegill]]**
- Best fishing in late spring and summer. Peaks were probably linked to spawning, which ran late May to early August in 1951, rather than to water level.
- Recommended rig: cane pole with a small double-blade spinner, worms and bobber.

**[[Freshwater Drum]]**
- Best catches when water was rising or high. Drum made up 40.6 per cent of the summer 1950 catch and 26.3 per cent in summer 1951.

**[[Channel Catfish]]**
- Positive correlation between rising or high water and catch rate. Catfish were 2.6 per cent of the 1950–51 catch.

**[[Largemouth Black Bass]]**
- Best when water was falling or at low, stable stage.
- Technique: stiff cane pole with a live 5–8 inch chub, fished quietly in buckbrush, smartweed and around stumps. Artificial lures were judged largely ineffective.
- Experimental liberalized season (April 16–May 31, normally closed) in 1950 and 1951: 295 bass taken in 1950 (21.9 per cent of the year's catch) and 191 in 1951 (17.3 per cent).
- 1950 fall/spring evidence of spawning: 65 young bass (1.1–1.6 inches) taken in a seine haul on June 23, 1950. Spawning in 1951 appeared poor.
- The 10-inch size limit was removed in spring 1951. Of 737 bass measured in 1951, 33.4 per cent were under 10 inches.

**[[White Bass]] and [[Yellow Bass]]**
- Yellow bass made up 32.1 per cent of the spring 1950 catch and were best caught late April to mid-May. Appears not to spawn successfully every year.
- White bass were nearly absent in 1950 (20 caught, 14 of them 1950-spawned) but 805 were caught in 1951, more than 40 times as many. Fish reached 10–12 inches by fall 1951.
- Summer technique: target surface "boils" of feeding schools with a flicker spinner or small wobbler. In spring and fall, cast weighted wobblers over sandbars.
- White bass are short-lived and may decline by 1954 if spawning is poor in 1952–53.

**[[Yellow Perch]]**
- Once abundant (17.8 per cent of catch at one boat yard in 1941–42), fell to 1.0 per cent in 1950–51. The last good catches were taken in the winter of 1944–45.

**Other species**
- Carp were abundant but caught rarely (246 in 1950, 141 in 1951); commercial carp averaged 6 lb.
- Warmouth, pumpkinseed and green sunfish were minor components.

**Management and conclusions**
- A still-fishing rig with live chubs for largemouth bass and instruction in angler techniques were recommended. The authors argue a larger harvest would result if more anglers knew how to fish the lake.
- The authors conclude that Illinois River bottomland lakes can provide good sport fishing for thousands of anglers.
