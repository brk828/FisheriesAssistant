# Canyoneering the Mogollon Rim and Southwestern Desert Canyons

#ichthyology #marine-biology #canyoneering

## Executive Summary

This 1988 *Outside* magazine feature by [[Jon Krakauer]] profiles photographer and guide [[Rick Fisher]] and his exploration of the canyons of the [[Mogollon Rim]] in north-central Arizona. Krakauer frames canyoneering as a hybrid of rock climbing, river running and backpacking, and argues that the Mogollon canyons rival more celebrated desert canyons because of their geology, water features and low visitation. The article also covers several canyoneering pioneers, a Mexican expedition with a drug-trade confrontation, and advocacy for protecting canyon wilderness. The document contains no fish, invertebrate or water-quality data; its aquatic content is limited to descriptions of creeks, pools and waterfalls.

## Background & Description

- **Author and setting:** Krakauer describes a week-long trip into the upper [[Salt River]] canyon region with Fisher, four friends and two golden retrievers, starting from a crowded riverside campground.
- **Route features:** The party waded up [[Cibecue Creek]] (knee-deep, fast, clear and cold), passed a 50-foot falls, climbed a rock pillar to bypass it, and reached a "rock box" grotto and a narrow slot only about six feet wide.
- **The Mogollon Rim:** The Rim marks the southern edge of the [[Colorado Plateau]] and drops roughly 6,000 feet to the [[Sonoran Desert]]. Its 10 million-plus acres include black bears, bald and golden eagles, mountain lions, deer and bighorn sheep. Fourteen or 15 noteworthy canyons, including Salome Jug, Hell's Gate, Dry Beaver and Devil's Windpipe, cut its face.
- **Canyon origins:** Krakauer notes that the [[Colorado River]] carved a 6,000-foot-deep, 1,000-mile gash through the plateau.
- **Profiles:** Fisher's 36-year-old profile is supplemented by "Canyon Pioneers" sidebars on [[Harvey Butchart]], [[John Annerino]] and [[Ken Sleight]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and geology:** Fisher says a canyon's geology decides whether its creek keeps water. If the strata are wrong, "most of the stream's water will sink into the ground," and no pools or waterfalls form. Catchments must cover roughly 10 by 20 miles, from peaks around 8,000 feet, to carry enough flow to cut a canyon.
- **Water features:** Canyons contain long chains of emerald pools tinted by dissolved minerals, cascades from inches to over 70 feet, and "water boxes" too deep to wade.
- **Flash floods:** Three large logs jammed 60 feet overhead in West Clear Creek show the force of flood surges.
- **Protection and visitation:** Because of the lack of livestock grazing in Cibecue Creek, native riparian flora has not been displaced by cheatgrass. Remote canyons receive very few visits; West Clear Creek sees only four or five parties a year.
- **Development threats:** The Forest Service planned commercial development of the Mogollon canyons in 1984 until a wilderness coalition secured protected status from Congress. On the Colorado Plateau, canyons have been proposed for strip mining and nuclear-waste dumps.
- **Cultural resources:** Cliff dwellings built by the [[Mogollon Culture]] around 700 years ago remain largely undisturbed. Krakauer also notes the risk from pothunters.
- **Biota (non-fish):** The text mentions no fish, aquatic invertebrates or plankton. The only animal life mentioned is terrestrial or avian, plus a blacktail rattlesnake and black diamondback rattlesnakes.

## Key Findings

- **Geology drives canyon quality:** Fisher's rule is that mixed igneous, metamorphic and sedimentary geology produces multitiered cascades, deep pools and narrow slots. A uniformly igneous canyon such as "Crystal Canyon" was initially dismissed, but it proved to contain large waterfalls, clear pools and quartz crystal clusters.
- **Low visitation preserves wilderness:** Fisher argues that the difficult access of the Mogollon canyons keeps crowds away, unlike Zion Narrows, which may see 20 parties on a spring day.
- **Mini-rafts as essential gear:** Fisher's inflatable raft, which fits in a pack and carries a week's food, became a standard tool after the chairman of the Phoenix Sierra Club drowned in West Clear Creek in 1979.
- **Expedition highlights:** In 1986 Fisher made the first descents of the Sinforosa (6,002 feet) and Urique (6,136 feet) barrancas in the Mexican Sierra Madre, described as the deepest canyons in North America.
- **Salome Jug as a model canyon:** A half-mile-long canyon with pools and cascades, reached by a natural ramp system, was judged by the author the most alluring of the five Mogollon canyons he visited.
- **Advocacy and ethics:** Fisher and others favour restricting access and protecting cliff dwellings. Sleight reluctantly supports limiting access to many canyons.
- **Recommended destinations:** Aravaipa Canyon (the only free-flowing perennial stream in the Sonoran Desert, BLM permit required), the West Fork of Oak Creek, Buckskin Gulch to Lee's Ferry (35 miles), the Little Colorado River Gorge (57 miles), and Grand Gulch and Desolation Canyon (Green River).
