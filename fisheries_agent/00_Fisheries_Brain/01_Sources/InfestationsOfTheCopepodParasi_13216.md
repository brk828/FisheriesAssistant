# Infestations of *Lernaea cyprinaceae* in Native Fishes of Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Copepoda #freshwater-parasitology

## Executive Summary

This study documents the first significant outbreak of the introduced parasitic copepod **[[Lernaea Cyprinaceae]]** ("anchor worm") on native fishes of the Grand Canyon reach of the [[Colorado River]], including the first-ever reported parasitism on the endangered **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*). Surveys conducted 1977–1978 in two low-gradient tributaries — the **[[Little Colorado River]]** and **[[Kanab Creek]]** — found heavy infestations exclusively on juveniles of four native species: *Gila cypha*, *Rhinichthys osculus* (speckled dace), *Catostomus latipinnis* (flannelmouth sucker), and *Pantosteus discobolus* (bluehead sucker). Notably, no infestations were found on any of five co-occurring exotic species, nor on adult native fish, nor in the cold, dam-regulated mainstem Colorado River. The authors attribute this pattern to the thermophilic requirements of the parasite's life cycle, which restrict its viability to the warmer tributary nursery habitats used by juvenile native fish.

## Background & Description

- **Parasite**: *Lernaea cyprinaceae* Linnaeus (Crustacea: Copepoda) is a non-native fish parasite originally described on European fishes (Wilson 1918), believed introduced to North America via shipments of Asian ornamental/tropical fish (Tidd 1934).
- It causes significant mortality in pond and hatchery settings under warm, crowded conditions (Uzmann and Rayner 1958), and near-epizootic infestations have been documented in Arizona desert streams with low, warm, ponded summer/fall flows (James 1968).
- Parasitism in natural streams is generally less common than in lakes/ponds (Bulow et al. 1979).
- **Study system**: The Grand Canyon reach of the Colorado River has been heavily altered by **[[Glen Canyon Dam]]** (upstream, controls temperature/discharge via hypolimnetic release, ~7–10°C year-round) and **[[Hoover Dam]]** (downstream, forms Lake Mead). These alterations, plus invasion by 21 exotic fish species, have caused decline or extirpation of several native fish (e.g., *Ptychocheilus lucius*, *Gila robusta*, *G. elegans* now extinct in the Canyon; *Xyrauchen texanus* nearly extirpated).
- Of eight historically native fish species, only four (*C. latipinnis*, *P. discobolus*, *R. osculus*, *G. cypha*) persist with viable populations; *G. cypha* is federally listed as **Endangered** (USFWS 1973) and breeds successfully only near the confluence of the Little Colorado River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites** (sampled Nov, Jan, May, Aug, Oct 1977–1978; seines 2–6.5 m, 3.2 mm mesh):

- **[[Little Colorado River]]**: Drainage ~141,120 km²; flows 412 km from the White Mountains to confluence with Colorado River at River Mile 61.2. Spring inputs (5–21 km upstream of confluence) supply 6.1–6.6 m³/sec baseflow; travertine deposits create pools/dams. Riparian vegetation: *Tamarix chinensis*, *Salix exigua*, *Phragmites australis*. Water temps during study: 8.5–14.7°C at confluence, 15–23°C 200 m upstream.
- **[[Kanab Creek]]**: Drainage ~5,698 km² in southern Utah; flows ~105 km to Colorado River confluence at River Mile 143.5. Mean discharge 0.14 m³/sec (range: no flow to 131.1 m³/sec). Riparian vegetation: *Tamarix chinensis*, *Juncus* spp., *Vitis arizonica*, *Typha latifolia*. Water temps: 9–24°C during study.
- **Mainstem Colorado River**: Now thermally stable and cold (7–10°C year-round) due to hypolimnetic release from Glen Canyon Dam ~60 m below Lake Powell's surface (Cole and Kubly 1976) — outside the thermal tolerance range for *L. cyprinaceae* development.

**Thermal biology of the parasite**: Optimum development range for *L. cyprinaceae* is 23–30°C; below 14°C the parasite cannot complete its life cycle (Bauer 1955, Putz and Bowen 1964). This explains the parasite's restriction to warm tributary habitats and its absence from the cold mainstem river.

**Host ecology**: Adult native fish tolerate and are frequently captured in the cold mainstem, but juveniles require warmer tributary habitats for spawning/rearing — placing only juveniles at risk of exposure to the thermophilic parasite during the warm, low-flow late summer/fall period when tributaries become sluggish and pooled (optimal copepod habitat per Bulow et al. 1979).

## Key Findings

- **First documented heavy infestation** of *L. cyprinaceae* on native Grand Canyon fishes, and the **first-ever record of copepod parasitism on *Gila cypha*** in Grand Canyon.
- **Infestation confined to juveniles**: Adults of all four native species were sampled but showed **no evidence of parasitism**.
- **Temporal onset**: First observed August 1978; increased in prevalence and species affected by October 1978.
  - August 1978 (Kanab Creek): *R. osculus* 6.5% infested; *P. discobolus* 20.7% infested.
  - October 1978 (Kanab Creek): *R. osculus* 10.0%; *P. discobolus* 55.1%; *C. latipinnis* 33.3% infested.
  - October 1978 (Little Colorado River): *G. cypha* 53.8% infested; *C. latipinnis* 50.0% infested.
- **Parasite load**: Most infested fish carried 1–2 parasites; maximum was **10 copepods on a single juvenile *P. discobolus*** from Kanab Creek (October).
- **Attachment sites**: Most frequently near the base of the pectoral, pelvic, or dorsal fins — consistent with prior studies (Bulow et al. 1979; Haley and Winn 1959; James 1968).
- **No infestation on exotic species** examined (*Cyprinus carpio*, *Ictalurus punctatus*, *Pimephales promelas*, *Fundulus zebrinus*, *Salmo gairdneri*) in either tributary, despite these species being known hosts of *L. cyprinaceae* elsewhere (Hoffman 1967; James 1968; Wilson et al. 1966) — suggesting habitat segregation (preference for cooler mainstem) rather than innate resistance.
- **Historical context**: *L. cyprinaceae* was essentially absent from Grand Canyon fish collections from 1975 through mid-1978 (Minckley and Blinn 1976, unpubl. data); Suttkus (in Johnson 1976) had previously found the parasite only once each on *Gila* sp. and *Cyprinus carpio* in the Little Colorado River (1972–1976 sampling), indicating the 1978 outbreak represents a **new, more severe epizootic event**.
- **Probable origin**: Parasite likely introduced via parasitized exotic fish from the Lake Mead area or via hatchery-origin salmonids released below [[Lake Powell]].
- **Conservation implication**: Since the Little Colorado River confluence represents the **only known successful breeding refugium for *Gila cypha***, and congener *Gila robusta* is known to be particularly susceptible to *L. cyprinaceae* (James 1968), this outbreak poses a potential additional threat to the already endangered species. Survival rates of parasitized juvenile natives remain unknown.
- **Proposed protective mechanism**: Juvenile native fish may escape parasite mortality by migrating to the cold mainstem Colorado River once they reach an unknown critical size, at which point hosted copepods likely cannot survive the low temperatures.
