# Genetic Monitoring of Wild and Repatriated Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report, prepared by [[Dowling]] and colleagues at Arizona State University for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (agreement 01-FG-30-0023, dated 31 March 2004), evaluates whether the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) repatriation program for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] transmits the genetic diversity of the wild Lake Mohave population from wild adults through captive-reared larvae into repatriated adults. Using mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variation in a 364 bp fragment of the cytochrome b gene, the authors screened 2,958 individuals by [[Single-Stranded Conformational Polymorphism]] (SSCP) analysis and sequencing. They concluded that the program is effectively transmitting variation, while cautioning that reproductive performance of repatriates remains untested.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a critically endangered fish endemic to the modified main stream of the [[Colorado River]] system. Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining population. Natural recruitment there has not been documented since the 1950s, and estimated numbers fell from more than 60,000 to fewer than 2,000 in the decade before the study (Marsh et al. 2003, unpublished data). The authors note that genetic diversity in the species is as high as any vertebrate examined, which suggests current individuals are remnants of a very large population.

The repatriation program began in 1991 under the NFWG. Wild larvae are captured, reared in protective custody until large enough to escape [[Predation]] by non-native fishes, and then stocked back into the lake. Through 2003, nearly 500,000 larvae had been harvested and more than 80,000 young fish stocked. Repatriates are identified by unique 10-digit [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tags.

The document also frames the study within conservation genetics theory: small populations suffer [[Genetic Drift]], [[Inbreeding Depression]], and loss of adaptive potential.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** Lake Mohave reservoir, with four larval collection areas: Nine Mile, Tequila Cove, Wrong Cove and Yuma Cove (near [[Davis Dam]]).
- **Larval collection:** Dip netting at night after attraction to submersed lights, during the January to April spawning period.
- **Repatriate and adult collection:** Overnight trammel nets; fin-clip tissue sampled (less than 1 g).
- **Main threat:** Heavy predation on larval razorback suckers, which causes recruitment failure.
- **Reproductive biology:** High individual fecundity means a few parents can produce many progeny, raising the risk that stocked fish derive from a small, unrepresentative parental set.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sampling**
- Mitochondrial cytochrome b fragment analysed by SSCP, with representative haplotypes sequenced (ABI 377 and Big Dye v3.1).
- Wild adults: 272 individuals from the late 1980s, early 1990s and late 1990s.
- Larvae: 2,430 individuals in 99 temporal samples, 1997-2003, from four areas.
- Repatriates: 256 individuals from 11 cohorts (1992-2002).
- Historical samples from [[Dowling et al. 1996]] were combined with new samples.

**Haplotype diversity**
- Total of 28 haplotypes across all samples (sequenced haplotypes A-BB).
- Wild adults: 19 haplotypes; larvae: 27 haplotypes; repatriates: 14 haplotypes.
- 23 variable positions, with 7, 1 and 15 in codon positions 1, 2 and 3, respectively, giving eight amino acid substitutions. The authors describe this as atypical for cytb, with unusually many first-position changes.
- Homoplasy was low (CI = 0.68, RI = 0.82).

**Wild adults**
- AMOVA among the three adult samples showed no significant divergence (FST = -0.003, p = 0.55), so years were pooled as a baseline.

**Larvae**
- Gene diversity ranged from 0 to 0.82 (mean 0.55); haplotype richness (ΔN) had a mean of 0.23.
- Of 186 comparisons, 25 showed significantly lower diversity or fewer haplotypes than expected (p < 0.05). After Bonferroni correction, only eight remained (and the authors note the correction is conservative).
- Deviations showed no consistent temporal or geographic pattern (year likelihood ratio = 2.74, p = 0.46; area likelihood ratio = 7.30, p = 0.29).
- AMOVA within years was significant (FST 0.020-0.102 across years); variation was among temporal samples within areas, not among areas (FCT never significant, range -0.022 to -0.012 on the reported partitions).
- Example: a Tequila Cove 1998 sample with a single haplotype (ΔN = 0.03) was followed two weeks later by a sample with eight haplotypes, demonstrating random sampling effects.

**Repatriates**
- Gene diversity ranged from 0.135 (1995) to 0.211 (1997), with no significant departure from wild adult expectations.
- AMOVA among cohorts showed low, non-significant divergence (FST = 0.008, p = 0.134). Small sample sizes limited power.

**Adults, larvae and repatriates compared**
- Overall AMOVA was significant (FST = 0.006, p = 0.000) but biologically trivial.
- Partition between adults, larvae and repatriates was not significant (FCT = -0.001, p = 0.763).

**Conclusions and management implications**
- The program appears effective at transmitting genetic variation from adults through larvae into repatriates.
- Reproductive capacity of repatriates is still unassessed, so it is unknown whether variation will be maintained as wild adults die off and repatriates replace them (Figure 3).
- Recommended action: periodic monitoring of genetic variation in larval samples. If variation falls in repatriates, intervention may be needed to keep particular individuals from being over-represented.
- Continued stratified sampling across the full spawning season is emphasised, since pooling dilutes temporal sampling error.
