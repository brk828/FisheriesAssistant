# Systematic Review of the Greenthroat Darter, Etheostoma lepidum

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This systematic review by Echelle, Echelle, and Hubbs (published in a 1984 tribute volume to Henry S. Fitch) redescribes the [[Greenthroat Darter]], *Etheostoma lepidum*, and compares it with the geographically associated [[Rio Grande Darter]] (*E. grahami*). The species was long known from springfed streams of the [[Edwards Plateau]] in Texas, but a disjunct New Mexico population in the [[Pecos River]] drainage has declined markedly since the 1950s. The authors recognize no subspecies, document weak geographic variation, and propose a Pliocene–Pleistocene vicariance history involving stream capture by the Pecos River.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described by Baird and Girard (1853) as *Boleosoma lepida* from the Rio Leona (Nueces River drainage) at Uvalde, Texas. Type material includes UMMZ 86335 and USNM 744 (designated paralectotypes and a lectotype under the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature). Synonyms include *Oligocephalus leonensis* and *Etheostoma lepidogenys*.
- **Subgenus:** *Etheostoma (Oligocephalus)*. Lacks breeding tubercles in males, which separates it from [[Spotted Darter]] (*E. spectabile*).
- **Diagnostic traits:** Blue-green breeding coloration on branchiostegal membranes, breast, and pelvic and anal fins; first dorsal fin of breeding males with red and blue-green bands; naked opercle; lateral line incomplete, with 44–60 total lateral line scales and 19–42 pored scales.
- **Meristics (ranges, modes):** Spinous dorsal rays 7–12 (mode 9); soft dorsal rays 9–13 (mode 11); anal soft rays 5–8; pectoral rays 10–13 (usually 11 or 12); transverse scale rows 12–19 (modal 14–16); caudal peduncle scales 4–6 above and 4–7 below the lateral line.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Breeding males are brightly colored, with dark red to rust-colored sides and blue-green ventral surfaces. Females and nonbreeding males are olive to brown dorsally and white ventrally. All fin measurements are larger in males, and males have slightly longer heads.
- **Methods:** Counts and measurements followed [[Hubbs and Lagler (1958)]], with modifications. Scalation was coded from 0 (no scales) to 3 (completely scaled). Specimens came from museum collections at UNM, TNHC, OSU, and TU.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** In Texas, the species is most abundant in springfed habitats and vegetated riffles on the Edwards Plateau, and it also thrives in certain reservoirs. In New Mexico, populations are closely associated with dense vegetation, and the species favors "rocks and weeds" (Koster 1957).
- **Sympatry:** Where it co-occurs with *E. spectabile*, *E. lepidum* shifts toward gravel riffles in the Nueces River drainage, where *E. spectabile* is absent.
- **Breeding:** Texas populations breed over 10–12 months in springfed localities with uniform temperatures, with depressed breeding in midsummer. At variable-temperature sites, breeding occurs from November through April. New Mexico populations show a similar pattern, with ripe females in most months but reduced breeding in summer. Strawn (1955b) recorded repeated spawning at temperatures from 16°C (or lower) to 23°C. Developmental tolerances of 7–29°C were reported by Hubbs (1961).
- **Threats:** The New Mexico decline is attributed to channel straightening, brush removal, irrigation diversions, and pollution from oil fields, municipalities, and cattle feedlots. In Texas, groundwater pumping threatens spring flows. The Blue Spring population was historically isolated from downstream areas by a 15 m waterfall, a barrier that may have been weaker before recent human activity.
- **Current New Mexico strongholds:** Blue Spring (near Whites City, Eddy County) and the Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge (near Roswell). The authors state that these populations are well protected and not in immediate danger of elimination.
- **Salinity:** The Pecos segment separating the two species has dissolved solids often exceeding 10 ppt.

## Key Findings

1. **Disjunct range:** The New Mexico population of *E. lepidum* lies on the opposite side of the High Plains Divide from the main Texas range. Large populations now occur at only three localities: Blue Spring, Bitter Lake, and Boiling Springs on the Pecos River. Since 1961, only single specimens have been collected at three additional sites, including one in the Rio Penasco drainage that may indicate an undiscovered population.
2. **Decline:** Koster's 1940s–1950s collections included samples of 32–330 specimens from the Pecos River and its large tributaries. The species has been virtually eliminated from the Pecos River proper.
3. **No subspecies:** Geographic variation in meristic characters, anterior squamation, breeding color, and body proportions is weak or environmentally driven. Variation in the ventral blue band of the first dorsal fin shows a north–south cline in Texas, and New Mexico males lack this band.
4. **Blue Spring differentiation:** The Blue Spring population has more extensive cheek, nape, and breast scalation, and 51% of its specimens have seven or more infraorbital pores. This pattern may be maintained by restricted gene flow.
5. **Species separation from *E. grahami*:** The two species differ in male breeding colors, opercular scalation (*E. grahami* has scaled opercles), first dorsal fin height in males, and fin lengths. Meristic characters overlap broadly. Early taxonomic confusion arose from superficial resemblance and proximity.
6. **Historical biogeography:** The authors hypothesize that the two species diverged allopatrically in the Pliocene. The lower Pecos River later eroded headward, captured the middle-to-upper Pecos, and brought *E. lepidum* into the Pecos drainage. Extirpation from the upper Colorado River may have produced the present disjunction.
7. **Misidentifications:** Many early literature records of *E. lepidum* were based on misidentified *E. spectabile pulchellum*, including the erroneous statement that the species occurs in Oklahoma.
