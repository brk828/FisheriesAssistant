# Conservation and Status of Gila Trout (*Oncorhynchus gilae*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Propst]] and colleagues reviews the conservation history and status of the [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*), a threatened salmonid of the [[Gila River]] drainage in New Mexico and Arizona. Once widespread in suitable habitat, the species was restricted to a few remote headwater reaches when described in 1950. Protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) and the New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act (1974) spurred recovery work, but efforts focused on establishing additional populations in small headwater streams have had mixed results. The authors conclude that recovery requires continued protection, re-establishment of the species in larger drainages, and modified recovery strategies.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and history:** Described by [[Miller]] (1950) from [[Main Diamond Creek]] and [[Spruce Creek]], which were the only known pure populations at that time. The native status of Gila trout in the [[San Francisco River]] drainage and [[Verde River]] (Arizona) has been debated, with genetic and morphological studies supporting presence in the San Francisco drainage.
- **Range:** Historically occupied much of the Gila River drainage in New Mexico and Arizona. Arizona populations were eliminated.
- **Threats to decline:** Competition and predation by introduced salmonids, hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]] (*O. mykiss*) and [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*O. clarkii*), habitat degradation and loss, and altered water quality and quantity (Sublette et al., 1990).
- **Protection timeline:** Listed under the Endangered Species Act of 1973; state protection followed under the New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act of 1974. A 1987 proposal downlisted the species from "endangered" to "threatened"; the [[New Mexico Game Commission]] delisted the McKnight Creek population and reclassified other New Mexico populations from Group 1 to Group 2 before the federal comment period closed (26 July 1989).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Flow regimes in the study streams are highly variable. Floods, drought, and fire repeatedly scoured channels, removed cover, and eliminated young-of-year fish. Surface flow in South Diamond Creek is commonly interrupted. Water chemistry data from Main Diamond Creek (July 1989) recorded water temperature 16.3°C, dissolved oxygen 7.6 mg/l, pH 8.1, total suspended solids 29,224 mg/l, conductivity 472 µS/cm, and total hardness 1,328 mg/l. Macroinvertebrate abundance and diversity recovered after flooding in 1989, but a flood in August 1990 caused near-complete loss of the fauna.

Recovery efforts relied on translocations, chemical removal of non-native and native fish (rotenone-type treatments on [[Iron Creek]], [[McKnight Creek]], and [[Little Creek]]), and installation of log stream-improvement structures, many of which were damaged or destroyed by floods.

## Key Findings

- **Population status (pre-1989):** Eleven populations existed (five relictual and six re-established). Events in 1988 and 1989 eliminated one population and severely reduced two others.
- **Genetic concerns:** The McKnight Creek population showed mitochondrial DNA evidence of contamination with *O. mykiss* genes, which may have occurred recently. Purity of other populations remains a concern.
- **McKnight Creek:** Population estimated at 800 to 1,000 fish in July 1976 after transplants from Main Diamond Creek. A flood in August 1988 scoured the channel and eliminated nearly all young-of-year trout. The population was reduced by more than 95 percent after 1988 floods and forest fire effects.
- **South Diamond Creek:** Population estimated at about 500 fish (July 1976). Drought and forest fire reduced the population by more than 95 percent (June to November 1989). Eleven fish were observed in South Diamond Creek and 21 in Burnt Canyon in May 1990.
- **Iron Creek:** Following chemical treatment in 1981 and reintroduction of 38 survivors, counts at three sampling locations increased steadily but irregularly (for example, 1.58 fish/m in the upper reach in August 1985 to 2.38 fish/m in an upper reach in 1989; values vary by site and year).
- **Spruce Creek:** Considered pure by several workers. Approximately 96 fish were removed in October 1985 to establish a replicate population in Dry Creek.
- **Stocking results:** Populations established in Trail Canyon, Woodrow Canyon, Dry Creek, and Sacaton Creek; Mogollon Creek translocations and Little Creek populations remained uncertain or were still being stocked at the time of writing.
- **Status evaluation:** Populations in Iron and Spruce creeks appeared secure. Limited habitat and low abundance make the status of populations in Sheep Corral and Gap creeks tenuous.
- **Recovery strategy conclusion:** Reliance on many small isolated headwater streams was inadequate; the 1988 to 1989 losses showed that larger, hydrologically diverse drainages provide greater security. The authors recommend reclaiming and managing entire sub-drainages, using stock from genetically pure donor populations, and using multiple stockings to maintain genetic diversity. The loss of at least three and as many as five populations in one year was described as a "distinct possibility of extinction."
- **Authors' assessment:** The Gila trout's status is a "cautious optimism," with long-term recovery depending on protecting large, connected watersheds.
