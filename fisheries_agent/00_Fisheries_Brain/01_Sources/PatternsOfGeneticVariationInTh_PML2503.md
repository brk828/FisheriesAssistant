# Patterns of Genetic Variation in Three Southwestern Minnows

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Master's thesis from [[Arizona State University]] (1993) compares patterns of genetic variation in three small cyprinid minnows native to the lower [[Colorado River Drainage]]: [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*). The species share historical ranges and environmental history, but differ in habitat specificity, dispersal, and reproduction. Two of them have lost much of their range through habitat alteration and competition with non-native fishes, while *Agosia* remains widespread.

The study uses [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) restriction-fragment analysis and [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] to test whether genetic structure follows drainage boundaries and whether the three species differ in gene flow, as predicted from their ecology. Results support strong population subdivision in *Tiaroga* and *Meda*, with *Agosia* showing high gene flow within the Gila drainage. The Verde River and Williamson Valley Wash populations are distinctive. The author concludes that remaining populations of the two declining species still retain considerable genetic variation.

## Background & Description

**Theoretical framework.** The introduction draws on the [[Isolation By Distance]] model (Wright 1943) and the [[Stepping Stone Model]] (Kimura and Weiss 1964). Under both, reduced [[Gene Flow]] among fragmented subpopulations, together with [[Genetic Drift]] in small populations, promotes divergence and the formation of independent evolutionary lineages.

**Regional history.** Basin-and-range formation, alluviation, tectonism, and episodic climate change (including Pleistocene glaciation and Tertiary aridification) isolated river drainages and produced high endemism among southwestern fishes. Modern aridity has fragmented stream habitats, with permanent flow near headwaters and intermittency downstream.

**Species descriptions.**

- ***Tiaroga cobitis* (Loach Minnow).** A small, darter-like minnow of shallow, rocky riffles in the Gila River system. It has a reduced swim bladder, is denser than water, and is carnivorous on benthic insects. Life span is about two years, with spawning mainly March through July. It is federally listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The species persists in Aravaipa Creek and the Blue and White rivers in Arizona, and in the San Francisco and upper Gila river systems in New Mexico.
- ***Meda fulgida* (Spikedace).** A slender member of the Plagopterini, endemic to the Colorado River system. It lives in swiftly flowing water over sand or gravel. It is threatened under the Endangered Species Act and is classed as a state-listed species in Arizona and New Mexico. Its decline is largely attributed to habitat alteration and displacement by the non-native [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*).
- ***Agosia chrysogaster* (Longfin Dace).** A fusiform, omnivorous minnow of low- to mid-elevation desert streams. It prefers shallow, slower water over sand or gravel, can spawn throughout the year, and recolonizes habitats quickly after floods or drought. Its range is comparatively intact, and it occurs in Mexican drainages (Rio Yaqui and Sonoyta) as well.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predicted differences.** Because the three species share a history, the authors predicted similar genetic patterns if extrinsic factors dominated. Intrinsic traits were expected to produce differences:

- *Tiaroga* was predicted to be the most sedentary and habitat-specific, with the greatest population subdivision.
- *Meda* was expected to disperse farther, especially in high water, but to be less differentiated than *Tiaroga*.
- *Agosia* was expected to show the most gene flow and the least subdivision.

**Threats.** Impoundment, water diversion, groundwater pumping, habitat degradation, and non-native fishes have fragmented former ranges. Former ranges of *Meda* and *Tiaroga* are now more fragmented, with fewer and more widely separated populations. Genetic information on population structure is part of recovery planning for both species.

**Water level.** The author notes that stream discharge controls habitat availability, population size, and dispersal corridors. *Tiaroga* is probably the most vulnerable to falling water levels because its riffle habitat dries first. *Agosia* tolerates the widest range of conditions.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods.**

- Individuals were collected by seining from several localities in Arizona and New Mexico, frozen on dry ice, and stored at -70°C.
- mtDNA was screened with 15 six-base-cutting restriction enzymes. A subset of three or four polymorphic enzymes was then used on additional individuals (about 25 per population where available) by transfer hybridization.
- Haplotype divergence was estimated, and [[Haplotype Diversity]] and nucleotide diversity were partitioned within and among populations.
- Nuclear protein variation was assessed by allozyme electrophoresis. Allele frequencies were analyzed with [[F-Statistics]] (F-statistics, Nei 1977; Wright 1978) and clustered by UPGMA.

**Tiaroga cobitis (Loach Minnow).**

- Sixteen mtDNA haplotypes were found, with an average of four per locality. This was the most polymorphic of the three species.
- Mean θ was 0.3807, and the among-population share of nucleotide diversity was about 67% (15-enzyme data).
- Haplotypes were largely locality-specific. The Aravaipa Creek population was the most divergent group, and the Blue/San Francisco and Gila groups clustered at about nine times the divergence seen within groups.
- Only four of 15 allozyme loci were polymorphic, and all alleles were shared among localities. Hierarchical analysis showed population structure at both the locality and drainage levels, with about 47% of variation among localities.
- The authors interpret the data as evidence that the three main populations (Aravaipa, Blue/San Francisco, and Gila) are historically isolated and represent distinct lineages.

**Meda fulgida (Spikedace).**

- Ten mtDNA haplotypes were found, with 39 restriction sites detected.
- Overall θ was 0.7135, falling to 0.5768 when the Verde River was excluded.
- The Verde River population had a single haplotype (θ = 1.0). It was distinctive in both mtDNA and nuclear markers, which the authors read as historical isolation rather than recent human effects.
- Five polymorphic allozyme loci were analyzed. Mean F<sub>ST</sub> was very large, with little variation among Gila drainage samples once the Verde was removed (mean F<sub>ST</sub> = 0.032).
- Most nucleotide variation (about 85%) was distributed among localities.

**Agosia chrysogaster (Longfin Dace).**

- Ten mtDNA haplotypes were found, with 46 restriction sites. One haplotype was common to all Gila River samples but absent from Williamson Valley Wash.
- Overall θ was 0.5987 but fell to 0.0239 when Williamson Valley Wash was excluded, indicating little structure within the Gila drainage.
- Mean allozyme F<sub>ST</sub> was 0.491 with the Williamson Valley Wash sample included, and 0.038 without it.
- mtDNA divergence reached up to about 3.0%, which the authors attribute to long-term subdivision in the Gila drainage followed by a period of panmixia.

**Cross-species comparisons.**

- Population structure in *Meda* and *Tiaroga* closely tracked drainages. *Agosia* showed minimal subdivision within the Gila system.
- Phenograms from mtDNA and allozymes agreed in general pattern.
- *Tiaroga* had more haplotypes than *Meda*, which the author attributes to larger effective population sizes, faster mtDNA evolution, or a bottleneck in *Meda*.
- The Verde River sample of *Meda* and the Williamson Valley Wash sample of *Agosia* were both distinctive. For *Agosia*, the authors suggest a barrier to dispersal through a cienega that connected the wash to the Verde River only at high water.

**Conclusions for conservation.** Populations of *Meda* and *Tiaroga* retain considerable genetic variation despite severe decline. Management should preserve distinct drainage-level stocks and avoid mixing genetically different populations through restocking.
