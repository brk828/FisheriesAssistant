# Coldwater Investigations: Trout Streams and Tailwaters of Kentucky (1965–1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by [[James P Henley]], Principal Fishery Biologist with the Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources, evaluates four coldwater streams and three dam tailwaters in Kentucky from March 1965 through February 1966. The study examined water chemistry and temperature, fish populations, angler harvest and pressure, and seasonal bottom invertebrate abundance. The author concludes that these waters can support a year-round trout fishery and a [[Rainbow Trout]]–minnow fish community. Stocked trout were harvested heavily, with return rates ranging from 31% to 82%. Western Kentucky streams produced more bottom fauna and standing crop than eastern streams. The project was partly funded by Federal Aid in Fish Restoration (Kentucky Project F-27-R).

## Background & Description

The study addressed four objectives:

1. Physical and chemical characteristics of four coldwater streams and three cold tailwaters.
2. Qualitative and quantitative fish population measurements for each study stream.
3. Trout harvest and fishing pressure.
4. Invertebrate bottom fauna and its seasonal abundance in four streams.

The seven study waters were:

- **Streams:** [[Rock Creek]] (McCreary County), [[Casey Creek]] (Trigg County), [[Sulphur Spring Creek]] (Simpson County), and [[Trammel Creek]] (Allen County).
- **Tailwaters:** [[Cumberland Tailwater]] (Russell County, below Wolf Creek Dam), [[Buckhorn Tailwater]] (Perry County), and [[Rough River Tailwater]] (Grayson County).

**Methods**

- **Water quality:** Dissolved oxygen (modified Winkler method), total alkalinity (bromocreosol green titration with 0.02N sulphuric acid), pH (Beckman electronic meter), and free carbon dioxide were measured monthly from surface samples. Temperature was recorded with Ryan 15-day recording thermometers. Flow was measured with a "Pigmy" flow meter.
- **Fish populations:** Stations were 250–500 feet long and included pools and riffles. Small streams were sampled with [[Electrofishing]] (boom-type in year one, whip-type in year two). Tailwaters were sampled only qualitatively, using a boom shocker, because of their size.
- **Creel surveys:** Conservation officers counted and interviewed anglers on 63 days from April to October 1965, averaging nine visits per stream per month. An intensive five-day [[Creel Survey]] followed trout stocking in April, June, and July 1966.
- **Bottom fauna:** Samples were collected with a [[Surber Sampler]] at riffles. Animals were washed through nested screens, and recovery was estimated at 95–99%. Results were expressed as numbers and wet weight per square foot.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and temperature**

- Water temperatures stayed in the high 50s or low 70s °F during the warmer months. Streams were usually warmest from about 5:00 p.m. to midnight and coolest from about 6:00 a.m. to 1:00 p.m.
- Dissolved oxygen was sufficient for trout in all streams and tailwaters. See [[Dissolved Oxygen]] for further context.
- Rock Creek's lower 4 miles, below the White Oak Branch confluence, were polluted by [[Acid Mine Drainage]] and had no fish.
- Casey Creek and Sulphur Spring Creek were in karst or limestone terrain with farmland. Rock Creek lay within the Daniel Boone National Forest.
- Rough River Tailwater had high free carbon dioxide (up to 15.0 ppm) and a suspected hydrogen sulfide odor when flow was minimal.

**Fish communities**

- Stream fish communities were dominated by cyprinids (minnows), with stoneroller, common shiner, and banded sculpin prominent. Rainbow trout were present at low densities in most streams.
- Tailwater communities were more diverse and included sunfishes, catfishes, suckers, and some trout. Trout in Buckhorn Tailwater moved into the outlet tunnel beneath the dam during daylight.

**Bottom fauna**

- Mayflies (Ephemeroptera), caddisflies ([[Trichoptera]]), true flies ([[Diptera]]), and amphipods were the most abundant groups.
- Invertebrate abundance was generally highest in late summer and fall and lowest in spring.

## Key Findings

**Physical and chemical**

- Rock Creek: gradient 14.6 ft/mile over 15 Kentucky miles; mean flow 10.9 cfs (range 4.2–24.7 cfs); temperature 46–74 °F; dissolved oxygen 7.0–9.7 ppm.
- Casey Creek: 5.2 miles; gradient 12.6 ft/mile; mean flow 3.8 cfs; temperature 49–64 °F; alkalinity averaged 147 ppm.
- Sulphur Spring Creek: 7.4 miles; gradient 8.8 ft/mile; mean flow 5.5 cfs; temperature 41–71 °F; alkalinity 83–208 ppm.
- Trammel Creek: about 8 miles, with a trout section of 4.5 miles; gradient 7.7 ft/mile; mean flow 14.7 cfs; temperature 48–66 °F.
- Cumberland Tailwater: temperature 44–52 °F; dissolved oxygen 6.8–10.2 ppm; drawn from the hypolimnion in summer.
- Buckhorn Tailwater: temperature 42–69 °F; dissolved oxygen 7.4–12.2 ppm.
- Rough River Tailwater: temperature 46–73 °F; dissolved oxygen as low as 4.4 ppm during low flow.

**Fish populations (per acre standing crop)**

- Rock Creek: upper station 12.5 lb/acre (505 fish/acre), 10 species, with three rainbow trout; lower station 32.7 lb/acre (1,474 fish/acre), 14 species, with no trout.
- Casey Creek: upper station 44.5 lb/acre (924 fish/acre), 13 species, with eight rainbow trout; lower station 48.5 lb/acre (468 fish/acre), 13 species, with one trout.
- Sulphur Spring Creek: upper station 33.9 lb/acre (795 fish/acre), 15 species, with four trout; lower station 48.2 lb/acre (692 fish/acre), 12 species, with four trout.
- Trammel Creek: upper station 36.8 lb/acre (531 fish/acre), 13 species, with 10 trout; lower station 40.5 lb/acre (355 fish/acre), 22 species, with no trout but 10 [[Smallmouth Bass]].
- Cumberland Tailwater: upper station 25 species, including 43 rainbow trout and 13 [[Brook Trout]]; lower station 10 species, with 17 rainbow trout.
- Buckhorn Tailwater: 21 species; redhorse sucker made up 49% of the population.
- Rough River Tailwater: 13 species at the upper station and 11 at the lower station; no trout at the lower station.

**Creel and harvest**

- Rock Creek: 15,128 man-hours; 6,599 trout harvested (82% return); 0.43 fish/hour; 252 fishermen per mile.
- Casey Creek: 4,347 man-hours; 1.01 fish/hour; 209 fishermen per mile. Intensive survey: 35% return.
- Sulphur Spring Creek: 2,936 man-hours; 3,924 trout harvested (67% return); 1.33 fish/hour.
- Trammel Creek: 7,792 man-hours; 1.02 fish/hour. Intensive survey: 43% return.
- Cumberland Tailwater: 40,729 man-hours; 9,371 trout harvested (49% return); 0.23 fish/hour.
- Buckhorn Tailwater: 16,451 man-hours; 2,686 trout harvested (51% return); 0.16 fish/hour.
- Rough River Tailwater: 12,062 man-hours; 1,135 trout harvested (31% return); 0.09 fish/hour.
- Across the seven-month survey, about 50% of trout were harvested. The intensive survey showed about 38% harvested within 10 days of stocking.

**Bottom fauna**

- Mean abundance: Trammel Creek 506 organisms/ft²; Sulphur Spring Creek 382; Casey Creek 261; Rock Creek 78.
- Mean standing crop: Sulphur Spring Creek 2,371 mg/ft²; Trammel Creek 1,814; Casey Creek 759; Rock Creek 536.
- Western streams (Sulphur Spring and Trammel) were described as "excellent producers" of bottom organisms.
- Rock Creek's abundance ranged from 14 organisms/ft² in May to 113 in November.
- Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Diptera, and Plecoptera dominated the samples, though the dominant orders varied by stream.

**Conclusions**

- The four streams can support a year-round trout fishery and a rainbow trout–minnow community.
- Western Kentucky streams had higher standing crops and bottom fauna production than eastern streams.
- Trout harvest was high, and return rates varied widely among waters.
