# Life Histories of Noturus baileyi and N. flavipinnis in Citico Creek, Tennessee

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1995 study (Bulletin Alabama Museum of Natural History 18:43-69) by [[Gerald R. Dinkins]] and [[Peggy W. Shute]] documents the life histories of two rare madtom catfishes, the federally endangered [[Smoky Madtom]] (*Noturus baileyi*) and the federally threatened [[Yellowfin Madtom]] (*N. flavipinnis*), in [[Citico Creek]], a tributary of the lower [[Little Tennessee River]] in eastern Tennessee. Most observations were made between May 1981 and June 1984, supplemented by data from a captive rearing and reintroduction program running from 1986 onward. The paper covers distribution, habitat, age and growth, reproduction and nesting, larval development, diet, and parasitism. Both species are small, short-lived, low-fecundity, pool- or riffle-dwelling insectivores that guard their eggs in nests under flat rocks, and the authors conclude that these traits, combined with highly localized populations, make both species vulnerable.

## Background & Description

*Noturus baileyi* was described by [[Taylor 1969]] from five specimens collected during a 1957 stream reclamation (poisoning) project on [[Abrams Creek]], within [[Great Smoky Mountains National Park]]. It was presumed extinct until a single specimen was collected from Citico Creek on 23 July 1980 during a survey for the [[Spotfin Chub]]. It was federally listed as endangered on 26 October 1984, with Citico Creek designated Critical Habitat.

*Noturus flavipinnis* was described by Taylor (1969) from thirteen specimens collected between 1884 and 1893 at four upper Tennessee River system localities, only three of which are known with certainty. Three isolated extant populations were later found: a single specimen from the [[Powell River]] (1968), 28 specimens from [[Copper Creek]], Virginia (1969), and the Citico Creek population discovered in June 1981 by night skin-diving. It was federally listed as threatened on 9 September 1977.

Both species belong to the [[Madtom Catfish Genus Noturus]], which the authors note is imperiled out of proportion to its share of the regional fauna. Five *Noturus* species were then federally listed, and *N. trautmani* was considered probably extinct.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Citico Creek is a relatively pristine, fourth-order stream (10 to 25 m wide) rising in the [[Blue Ridge Province]] and entering the [[Valley and Ridge Province]] near its confluence. The lower 2.3 km are inundated by [[Tellico Reservoir]] (since 1979). Two small concrete dams (creek km 13.7 and 25.3, built 1973) block redhorse migration into trout-stocked reaches. Fifty-six fish species were documented in the creek, including the federally endangered [[Duskytail Darter]] (*Etheostoma percnurum*).

**Habitat partitioning.** *N. baileyi* occupies a 10.8 km reach (creek km 6.0 to 16.8) and shifts from riffle crests (May to early November, when water temperatures are above about 7-8 °C) to shallow pools in colder months. *N. flavipinnis* is a year-round pool dweller confined to a 3.6 km reach above the dam at creek km 13.7. Its absence from the lower creek is unexplained; the authors suggest sedimentation from Caney Branch, introduction by anglers, or dam-related degradation.

**Sensitivity.** The authors note that madtoms, which are mostly nocturnal, rely on chemoreception and may be harmed by small changes in water quality, citing the hypothesis of [[Etnier and Jenkins 1980]] that "olfactory noise" from trace organic chemicals may contribute to madtom declines.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and movement**
- *N. baileyi* occupies only 10.8 km of Citico Creek; *N. flavipinnis* occupies 3.6 km there.
- Tagged adult *N. flavipinnis* did not move between pools: 7 of 45 acrylic-tagged fish were recaptured 7 to 32 months later, all in their original pool, and all 13 recaptured fin-clipped fish were also in their original pool.
- Population estimates for *N. flavipinnis* in the 3.2 km occupied reach were 451 (modified Schnabel) and 549 (Schumaker), with 95% confidence limits of 312-1453; density was 1.4 and 1.8 adults per 10 m².

**Age, growth and maturity**
- *N. baileyi* lives about two years and matures in its second summer (age one); length-frequency data from 131 fish supported this.
- *N. flavipinnis* lives three to four years, with maturity at age two in the wild (captive-reared fish often matured at one year). Length-frequency data from 233 fish indicated a three-year lifespan.
- Growth of *N. flavipinnis* appears to stop near a maximum of 110-115 mm SL in Citico Creek.

**Sexual dimorphism**
- Female and male *N. baileyi* did not differ significantly in size (χ² = 0.0675 < 3.841).
- Male and female *N. flavipinnis* did not differ significantly (χ² = 0.083 < 3.841); mean standard lengths were 98.8 mm (males) and 94.8 mm (females).
- Breeding male *N. baileyi* show yellow body coloration; breeding male *N. flavipinnis* do not differ in color.

**Nesting and reproduction**
- Both species are speleophils that nest under large, flat rocks, with a single male guarding the eggs. Spawning occurs May to July in both species.
- *N. baileyi* nests averaged 36.3 eggs (four nests, 1982-1983); *N. flavipinnis* nests averaged 55 eggs (ten nests).
- Nest rocks for *N. flavipinnis* were larger (mean 50 × 34 cm) than those for *N. baileyi* (mean 25.8 × 25.8 cm), though the two overlapped.
- Between 1986 and 1994, captive-reared *N. baileyi* clutches ranged from 20 to 65 eggs (mean 30).
- Guardian male *N. flavipinnis* apparently do not feed during guarding; one had an empty stomach.

**Fecundity**
- *N. baileyi* is among the least fecund madtoms. A 63 mm SL captive female carried 653 eggs; a 52 mm wild female had 287 oocytes.
- *N. flavipinnis* females carried 382 to 456 ova (mean 408), with about 177 ripe eggs per female.

**Possible polyandry**
- Evidence suggests both species may occasionally be polyandrous, though the authors judge it inconclusive.

**Larval development**
- *N. baileyi* hatched in 16 days at 20-21 °C, with 94% hatching success in one nest of 33 eggs. Larvae lacked the adult dorsal saddles until after about 12 days.
- *N. flavipinnis* embryos were described from 6 to 12 days post-fertilization; a 24-day larva had complete fin rays.

**Diet**
- Both species are almost exclusively insectivorous. *N. baileyi* ate 15 invertebrate taxa in 13 specimens, with aquatic insect larvae making up 89.3% of food items, and Ephemeroptera nymphs 70.7%.
- *N. flavipinnis* ate 32 taxa from 16 families in 12 specimens, with Ephemeroptera (mainly Ephemerellidae) at 33.8%. Dipterans were present in all 12 stomachs.
- Ten of 12 *N. flavipinnis* were collected at night. Based on digestion timing, the authors infer that feeding peaked in the middle of the night.

**Parasitism**
- No internal parasites were observed. Three adult *N. flavipinnis* had fin erosion caused by the ciliate *Epistylis*, possibly linked to organic enrichment during low flow in summer 1983.

**Methods**
- Underwater skin- and SCUBA-diving was the most efficient method for both species and minimized habitat disturbance, while standard seining had missed *N. flavipinnis*.

**Conservation status and recovery**
- Copper Creek, once home to 78 stream km of *N. flavipinnis*, produced no specimens in standard surveys in recent years; a 1993 snorkeling effort of 52.3 hours found two specimens in one pool.
- The Powell River population yielded only three specimens since 1968.
- Conservation Fisheries, Inc. reared and released 821 *N. baileyi* and 404 *N. flavipinnis* into Abrams Creek from 1986 onward. Wild *N. baileyi* were observed at the transplant site in 1990, 1991 and 1994.
- The authors conclude that low fecundity, short lifespan, specialized nesting and habitat requirements, and restricted distributions have likely increased vulnerability and may have caused historical extirpations.
