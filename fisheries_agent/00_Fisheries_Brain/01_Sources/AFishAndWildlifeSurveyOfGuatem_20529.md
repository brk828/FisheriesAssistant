# Fish and Wildlife Survey of Guatemala (1946–1947)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #waterfowl #game-birds

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Special Scientific Report (Wildlife No. 5, June 1950) summarises a joint survey of Guatemala's wildlife and fisheries carried out in March–May 1946 and March–June 1947 under an intergovernmental agreement with the Guatemalan Ministry of Agriculture. The report's main content is an annotated catalogue of game birds and shorebirds, supported by a game-regulation proposal, a bird-banding recovery list and a mammal section. The fishery recommendations for streams, lakes and marine fisheries are indexed in the table of contents, but the body of the text supplied here covers the birds and mammals. Substantive management advice concerns seasonal closures, trapping bans, forest and soil conservation, and avoidance of introducing exotic quail.

## Background & Description

- **Mission:** Staffed by [[Saunders]] (chief, game birds), [[Holloway]] (aquatic biologist, lakes and streams), [[Miller]] (fish taxonomy, U.S. National Museum), and [[Handley]] (mammals), with three Guatemalan field associates each year from the newly formed Department of Game and Fish.
- **Methods:** Field investigations in every Department, mainly by truck; Petén and coastal areas by aerial reconnaissance. Information came from specimens, literature and interviews with resident hunters and finca owners (over fifty hunters were interviewed on waterfowl alone).
- **Legal basis:** U.S. Acts of May 3, 1939 and August 9, 1939, as part of the U.S. cooperation programme with American Republics.
- **Regions:** Seven wildlife regions were defined: [[Pacific Coastal Plain]], [[Pacific Cordillera]], the Altos highlands, Vera Paz mountains, arid interior valleys, [[Petén Lowlands]], and Caribbean lowlands (Izabal).
- **Scope note:** The report states that the full fish checklist would take several years to complete, owing to the thousands of specimens collected.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Forest loss** is the principal driver of declines. Clearing, burning, erosion, night hunting with headlights and widespread trapping have sharply reduced highland game. The authors state that wooded slopes, especially steep ones and those on light soils, should not have been cleared.
- **Tinamous** depend on dense forest and leaf litter; natural mortality was judged to be the greater control on numbers, with shooting significant only in intensively hunted settled areas.
- **Wetlands and migration:** Ducks are almost entirely migrants from Canada, Alaska and the United States. The Pacific coastal plain, with lagoons and marshes near Chiquimulilla Canal, is the only area of major importance for migrant and wintering waterfowl. Lake Retana had been dry much of the time in recent years.
- **Quail declines** are attributed to clearing, increased hunting pressure, and extensive trapping ("figure-four" traps, snares called *alzapié*, and nets called *redes*).
- **Bird migration:** Many North American species winter in or pass through Guatemala. A list of about 200 species (in the report's migrant and breeder tables) is summarised here rather than reproduced.

## Key Findings

**Game bird and shorebird inventory**
- The annotated list covers roughly 80 numbered taxa, including tinamous, curassows, guans, chachalacas, partridges, quail, turkeys, ducks, rails, gallinules, coots, shorebirds and pigeons.
- Non-game species (herons, egrets, songbirds, shorebirds) should receive full legal protection; the authors stress insectivorous birds' value to agriculture.

**Selected species notes**
- [[Central American Curassow]] (*Crax rubra rubra*): fairly common resident; declining where forest is cleared or hunting is intense.
- [[Horned Guan]] (*Oreophasis derbianus*): restricted to high-elevation forests, mainly above 7,500 ft; reported on volcanoes including San Lucas and Fuego.
- [[Ocellated Turkey]] (*Agriocharis ocellata*): Petén endemic-range species; hunters asked for a ban, which the Department was asked to consider.
- [[Black-bellied Tree Duck]] (*Dendrocygna autumnalis lucida*): numbers rise November–February with migrants from southern Mexico; a flock of 1,125 was counted near Champerico on February 11, 1947.
- [[Blue-winged Teal]] (*Anas discors*): common migrant; numerous counts up to 500 at single sites.
- [[Green-winged Teal]] (*Anas crecca carolinensis*): first Guatemalan record, from a single observation of about 400 birds near Papaturro on January 29, 1947.
- [[Pintail]] (*Anas acuta*): common migrant; 2,460 counted near Champerico on February 11, 1947.
- [[American Coot]] (*Fulica americana*): common winter resident; recommended as a game bird.

**Management recommendations**
- **Duck season:** October 1–January 31 only, to close before the spring breeding season; rifles prohibited for waterfowl.
- **Other game birds:** season September 1–March 1 for quail, partridges, tinamous, curassows, guans, chachalacas, doves, pigeons and similar species.
- **Deer:** hunting by day only, November 1–March 31 season, closed where numbers are low and during the breeding peak; licensing and record-keeping required for deer hide purchases and export.
- **Trapping and sale:** trapping of game birds and commercial sale of game birds to be banned year-round; night hunting with headlights banned.
- **Full protection:** shorebirds, herons, egrets, storks, ibises, small doves (*tortolitas*), and [[Thick-knee]] (*Burhinus*) because of scarcity; tapir, manatee, monkeys and anteaters also protected.
- **Quail restocking:** introduction of exotic quail from other countries not recommended because of disease and parasite risk; land owners and the Department should instead improve habitat, and wild birds may be live-trapped for restocking.
- **Conservation policy:** the authors view forest and soil conservation as the basic requirement for any long-term wildlife increase.

**Banding recoveries**
- About 25 recoveries are listed (e.g., Great Blue Heron, Black-crowned Night Heron, Pintail, Herring Gull, Franklin's Gull, Laughing Gull, Mourning Dove, Eastern White-winged Dove), showing that North American birds reached Guatemala from the Midwest, Canada and the Gulf coast. Most were shot or caught, and several were found in Guatemala City and along the Pacific coast.

**Fishery and other sections**
- The table of contents lists recommendations for stream and lake fisheries (Pacific and Caribbean drainages, Lake Izabal and Golfete, Lake Atitlán, Amatitlán and others), stocking, hatchery sites, and commercial marine fisheries. The detailed text for these sections is not included in the source provided, so no fishery data are reported here.
