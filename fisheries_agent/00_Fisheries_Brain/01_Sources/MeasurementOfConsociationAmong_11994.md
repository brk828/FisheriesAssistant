# Measurement of Consociation Among Fishes and Comments on the Evolution of Schooling

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1964 study by George C. Williams, published by the Michigan State University Museum (Biological Series, vol. 2, no. 7), experimentally measures [[Schooling Behavior]] in nine fish species and proposes an evolutionary explanation for schooling. Fish were tested in a visually uniform white circular tank that removed all cover. Schooling, defined as a contagious distribution produced by social attraction, appeared in all nine species, although with varying intensity. Williams argues that schools are a statistical consequence of adaptive individual behavior, not an adaptation of the group itself, and that schooling evolves from [[Aggregation]] driven by predation risk.

## Background & Description

Schooling is superficially simple to observe, yet it had resisted convincing ethological, ecological, or evolutionary explanation because no obvious "purpose" was apparent. The paper evaluates several hypotheses: conditioning of the medium, reproduction, and protection from predators. The hypothesis tested experimentally relates schooling to defensive hiding: a fish places itself among conspecifics to become less conspicuous and to put others between itself and danger.

Williams uses terminology that distinguishes causes. A school is a contagious distribution maintained by social (non-sexual) forces. An aggregation forms when individuals independently seek the same localized conditions. The paper notes that Soviet usage (Nikolsky, 1963) separates a school, in which every fish is aware of every other, from a larger shoal.

### Experimental Apparatus and Methods

- **Tank:** Cylindrical steel tank, 153 cm in diameter and 30 cm deep, filled to 17 cm. The interior was painted white, coated with paraffin, and fitted with bleached muslin liners.
- **Lighting and observation:** A ring of six 200-watt bulbs hung above the tank. A camera (Kodak Cine-Special or Ansco Memar) was fixed above a central observation hole.
- **Temperature control:** Tropical species were kept at a minimum of 22 °C with electric heaters; the room was about 18 °C. No experiments were run in summer.
- **Sampling:** Each group produced two series of 36 photographs, taken at 30-second intervals, about five to eight hours apart.
- **Metric:** Schooling intensity was measured as the median perimeter of the smallest convex polygon enclosing a group, expressed as a proportion of the maximum possible perimeter (the inscribed regular polygon, 2N sin(π/N)). The perimeter was preferred over [[Area Measurement]] because it is easier to calculate and corresponds better to intuitive judgments of group size.
- **Random baseline:** Random movement was simulated by scattering points at random within a circle (see [[Random Movement Baseline]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Williams proposes that schooling is a form of cover-seeking and should be most conspicuous in cover-deficient habitats. General observations support this: schooling is prominent in pelagic oceanic waters, scarcely detectable in weedy or rocky shallows, and intermediate in other habitats. The experimental tank was designed to be as cover-deficient as possible, so that fish had no outlet for cover-seeking except other fish.

Williams suggests that natural selection acts on vision-dependent [[Predation]]. Peripheral or isolated individuals are most exposed, so individuals with tendencies to stay among conspecifics would be favored over time. Pelagic fish would be expected to aggregate where food concentrates, such as at convergences of surface waters, and schooling would develop from those aggregations.

Williams also considers the role of [[Schreckstoff]] (alarm substance). He argues that it is probably an incidental result of tissue damage and possibly a repellent rather than a warning signal, because chemical diffusion is slow compared with visual and auditory signals, the substance is not actively secreted, and its effect has low specificity.

## Key Findings

### Schooling Across Nine Species

- Schooling was evident in all nine species. The American eel and male guppy showed weak, intermittent schooling.
- The dwarf gourami, a normally territorial fish, schooled strongly in the tank. Williams argues that this shows a capacity to school, which is suppressed in planted aquaria by the absence of appropriate stimuli.
- Median perimeters for groups of three in the tank were much smaller than random expectations:
  - Sand shiner (*Notropis stramineus*): 0.47, 0.78, 1.20, and 1.30 radii, against 2.84 radii expected for random movement. Williams concludes that this species schools consistently, with variable intensity.
  - Blunt-nosed minnow (*Pimephales notatus*): two groups gave 0.41 and 0.86 radii (mean 0.64).
  - Green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*): median perimeter estimated at about one-third of the random value.
- The flame tetra (*Hyphessobrycon flammeus*, 12 fish) and emerald shiner (*Notropis atherinoides*, 3 fish) were not quantified, but were judged to school. The flame tetra's school rarely showed more than about a third of the perimeter expected from random movement.

### Individual Movement Is Random in Most Species

- Isolated sand shiners, blunt-nosed minnows, dwarf gouramis, and female guppies showed no significant quadrant bias, supporting independent movement.
- Isolated eels did not move randomly. Their quadrant use was 0.6 in the western half of the tank (95% CI 0.55–0.65), and their median distance from the center was 0.56 radii against a random expectation of 0.707.
- Isolated dwarf gouramis had median distances from the center of 0.86–0.90 radii (early and late observations), which are higher than random. Williams judged the difference too small to be meaningful.

### Eels: Variable Schooling

- The eels (*Anguilla rostrata*, 33 elvers collected in August 1958, averaging about 80 mm) showed a mortality that Williams suspected affected their behavior.
- In three of six experiments, median perimeters were well below three-quarters of the random value, with observed/random ratios of 0.27, 0.62, 0.32, and 0.23. In one experiment the ratio was 1.06, owing to one fish circling the edge.
- Williams concludes that eels school at times, form low-cohesion schools, and sometimes do not school. He could not explain this inconsistency.

### Female Guppies and Dwarf Gouramis: Equation Fit

- Median perimeters for female guppies (companion numbers 1, 2, 4, and 11) were all well below random values. The fitted constants were a ≈ 0.68 and b ≈ 0.09. The random-movement value of a is 0.85, so the guppy regression is steeper than random, and Williams extrapolates that it would cross the random line at a companion number below 100.
- For dwarf gouramis, the fitted constants were a ≈ 0.54 and b ≈ 0.095, indicating weaker social forces than in female guppies.
- Males and females of the dwarf gourami schooled similarly, so sex was not treated as a variable.
- Williams interprets these results as evidence that the species differ in their [[Logarithmic Companion Number Function]] constants, providing a quantitative basis for comparing schooling intensity across species.

### Heterotypic Pairs Do Not School

- One-companion median perimeters for dwarf gourami and guppy were about 0.39 and 0.18 radii (as reported in the text) for homotypic pairs.
- In eight experiments with heterotypic pairs (a female guppy and a male or female dwarf gourami, matched within about 2 mm of standard length), there was no clear schooling. Median perimeters were closer to random than homotypic values were.
- Williams concludes that schooling responds to conspecific recognition cues, not to any object of suitable size.

### Predation and Other Hypotheses

- Williams reviews three hypotheses: conditioning of the medium, reproduction, and protection from predators. He rejects the first as unimportant in large water bodies and considers the second unlikely to be important because schooling does not intensify as the breeding season approaches.
- Several predator-avoidance mechanisms are reviewed, including [[Confusion Effect]], satiation (Brock and Riffenburgh, 1960), and collective mimicry (Knipper, 1955). Williams notes that schooling can also facilitate predation, citing human fisheries, thresher sharks, and swordfish.
- He argues that schools lack functional organization (no leader, no division of labor, no ordered sequence of changes) and are highly redundant with low information content, so they are not adaptive units in themselves.

### Theoretical Conclusion

- Williams proposes that schools are the "statistical consequence" of adaptive individual behavior. Four factors make up the schooling phenomenon: recognition of conspecifics, the desire to approach them, the giving of species-recognition cues, and the initiation of defensive reactions when they are detected in others.
- The schooling constants can be explained by [[Equilibrium Distance Theory]] (Breder, 1954). Schooling would give way to over-dispersion when fish are forced closer together than their preferred distance, so the crossing point with the random regression is predicted at roughly 50–100 individuals.

### Limitations Noted by the Author

- The eel and guppy data are affected by poor condition and mortality.
- Only the female guppies and dwarf gouramis have quantitative data across multiple companion numbers.
- Several species were tested with few individuals or were not quantified (for example, the flame tetra and emerald shiner).
