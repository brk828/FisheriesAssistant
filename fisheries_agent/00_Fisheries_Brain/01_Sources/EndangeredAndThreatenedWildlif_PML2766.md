# Reclassification of Spikedace and Loach Minnow to Endangered Status and Proposed Critical Habitat Designation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed rule, published in the *Federal Register* on October 28, 2010 (Vol. 75, No. 208), proposes to reclassify the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) from threatened to endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and to designate revised [[Critical Habitat]] for both species in Arizona and New Mexico. Both species now occupy a small fraction of their historical range (about 10 percent or less for spikedace; 15 to 20 percent for loach minnow). The Service finds that both species meet the definition of endangered, with the largest remaining threat being competition with and predation by [[Nonnative Aquatic Species]]. The proposal designates eight units totaling 1,168 km of stream for spikedace and 1,141 km for loach minnow (824 km overlapping).

## Background & Description

**Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)** is a small, slender cyprinid under 80 mm (3 in) long, olive-colored with black mottling. Breeding males show red to red-orange markings on fins, mouth, and abdomen. It was first collected in 1851 from the San Pedro River and described by Girard in 1856.

**Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)** is the only species in the genus *Meda*, a small slim fish under 77 mm (3 in). It has olive-gray to brownish skin, silvery sides, and vertically elongated black specks. It was first collected from the San Pedro River in 1851.

Both species were listed as threatened in 1986 (spikedace on July 1, 1986; loach minnow on October 28, 1986). Previous critical habitat designations were made in 1994, 1999, 2000, and 2007, and the 2000 designation was set aside by a federal court in 2004.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Both species occupy moderate to large perennial streams at low elevations and low gradients, with shallow riffles and runs over sand, gravel, and cobble. Spikedace favor shear zones, sheet flow, and eddies at riffle edges. Loach minnow use interstitial spaces between rocks and are rare where fine sediments fill them.
- **Diet:** Spikedace feed mainly on mayflies, with true flies, caddisflies, and stoneflies; loach minnow consume true fly larvae and mayfly nymphs. Both depend on riffle-dwelling aquatic insects.
- **Thermal and water-quality tolerances:** Summer temperatures in occupied habitat ranged from 19.3 to 27 °C. A University of Arizona study found 50 percent mortality of spikedace after 30 days at 32.1 °C, and loach minnow at 30.6 °C.
- **Flooding:** Periodic flooding is described as beneficial, removing excess sediment, reducing nonnative fish, and stimulating recruitment.
- **Distribution:** Spikedace now occur in the upper Gila River, Aravaipa Creek, Eagle Creek, and the Verde River. Loach minnow are restricted largely to the Gila, San Francisco, and Tularosa rivers in New Mexico, Aravaipa Creek, and the Blue River. Several populations were translocated in 2007 and 2008 into Hot Springs, Redfield, and Bass canyons, Fossil Creek, and Bonita Creek.

**Principal threats** identified under the five ESA factors:
- **Factor A (habitat loss):** Water diversions and groundwater pumping, [[Stream Channelization]], [[Livestock Grazing]], [[Roads and Bridges]], [[Recreation]], and [[Wildfire]] effects.
- **Factor B (overutilization):** Not a threat; collection is regulated and no commercial use is known.
- **Factor C (disease and predation):** Channel catfish, flathead catfish, smallmouth bass, and parasites (Asian tapeworm, anchor worm, *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*).
- **Factor D (regulatory inadequacy):** Existing state and federal laws do not adequately address nonnative species introductions or instream water rights.
- **Factor E (other factors):** Nonnative fish competition, especially [[Red Shiner]]; loss of genetic variation; and [[Drought and Climate Change]].

## Key Findings

- **Status:** The Service proposes reclassifying both species from threatened to endangered, concluding that both are in danger of extinction throughout their ranges. Both currently exist in a small portion of their historical range.
- **Primary threat:** Competition with and predation by nonnative species is "likely the largest remaining threat."
- **Regulatory gap:** Existing regulatory mechanisms have not halted the decline since listing.
- **Climate:** Warmer, drier, drought-like conditions predicted under climate change will further reduce available resources.
- **Proposed critical habitat (spikedace):** 1,168 km (726 mi) in eight units, including the Verde, Salt, San Pedro, Bonita, Eagle, San Francisco, Blue, and Gila rivers and their tributaries.
- **Proposed critical habitat (loach minnow):** 1,141 km (709 mi) in eight units; 824 km (512 mi) overlap with spikedace units.
- **Physical and biological features (PBFs):** Perennial flows with depths generally under 1 m, flow velocities of roughly 5 to 80 cm per second (spikedace) and 0 to 80 cm per second (loach minnow), low gradients, sand/gravel/cobble substrates, water temperatures of about 8 to 28 °C (spikedace) and 8 to 25 °C (loach minnow), an abundant aquatic insect food base, low pollutant levels, and no or sufficiently low nonnative aquatic species.
- **Lateral extent:** Each unit includes 91.4 m (300 ft) on either side of bankfull stage, used as a surrogate for the 100-year floodplain.
- **Recovery targets:** Preliminary assessment recommends downlisting spikedace after one additional stable population in the Salt or Verde subbasin and 10 occupied streams rangewide; delisting at 12 occupied streams. Loach minnow downlisting at 22 occupied streams, delisting at 28.
- **Exclusions:** Lands of the White Mountain Apache, San Carlos Apache, and Yavapai-Apache Nation, and Freeport McMoRan holdings, may be considered for exclusion under section 4(b)(2). No Department of Defense lands are in the proposal.
- **Economic analysis:** A new draft economic analysis will be prepared; the Regulatory Flexibility Act finding is deferred until it is complete.
- **Public comment:** Comments were accepted until December 27, 2010, and public hearings may be requested within 45 days.
