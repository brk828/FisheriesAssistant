# Factors Resulting in Decline of the Endangered Sonoran Topminnow *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* in the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Meffe Et Al 1983]] examines the decline of the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) in the United States, where the species now survives only in a few isolated localities in southern Arizona. Field surveys conducted between August 1979 and July 1981 at all known US populations link the decline primarily to [[Habitat Destruction]] and to the introduction of [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and other exotic fishes. The authors conclude that [[Predation By Introduced Fishes]] on juvenile topminnows is the most likely mechanism of replacement, rather than competition or abiotic change.

## Background & Description

The [[Sonoran Topminnow]] is a small (<50 mm) viviparous poeciliid native to the Sonoran Desert of the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. The authors treat the [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Yaqui Topminnow]] as part of a single species, following [[Hendrickson Et Al 1981]]. Two forms are recognized in the United States:

- *P. o. occidentalis*, formerly occupying lowland streams of the Gila River system in Arizona and New Mexico.
- *P. o. sonoriensis*, which extends north in the Rio Yaqui basin into Arizona from its wider range in Mexico.

The genus is the northernmost of *Poeciliopsis*, whose range extends along the Pacific drainage of Central America to Colombia. The introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) was first recorded in Arizona in 1926. The paper notes that the native arid-western fish fauna is depauperate and highly vulnerable to extinction, with 28 of 43 North American endangered or threatened fishes occurring in the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico.

Three hypotheses are tested:

1. **Habitat alteration (abiotic):** arroyo cutting begun in the 1880s, damming and diversion, and groundwater pumping.
2. **Competition with introduced fishes (biotic).**
3. **Predation by introduced fishes (biotic).**

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Environmental tolerance.** Water temperature, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, alkalinity, pH, and ion concentrations were recorded at most sites (Table 1). Recorded ranges are minima of what the species tolerates, since remnant habitats are far less variable than historical ones. The authors report that topminnows tolerate salinities from tap water to full seawater, temperatures from near freezing to 36–37 °C, and survival through one to two days of drying in warm mud.

**Habitat types.** Extant populations occupy:
- Isolated springs, artesian wells, and spring runs (e.g., Monkey Spring, Cottonwood Spring, Sheehy Spring), often in oak woodland or oak grassland.
- Ciénega marshland and intermittent or flash-flooded desert streams (e.g., Santa Cruz River, Cienega Creek, Redrock Canyon, Cocio Wash).
- Mesquite bosque and lowland desert washes (e.g., Bylas Springs, Salt Creek, San Bernardino Creek).

**Physical history.** Arroyo cutting drained backwater and ciénega habitats that once provided shallow vegetated refuges from native piscine predators such as chubs (*Gila* spp.). Prolonged drought over roughly 75 years, channelization, impoundments, and groundwater pumping further altered habitats, though the authors note many of these changes predate the major topminnow declines.

**Biotic context.** Native [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), and [[Desert Pupfish]]-associated fauna co-occur with topminnows in several systems. Exotic species recorded include [[Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), black crappie, and black bullhead.

## Key Findings

**Population status and decline**
- The species is classed as endangered (US Department of the Interior, 1980). It was once "one of the commonest fishes" of the Colorado River drainage (Hubbs & Miller, 1941) but declined to endangered status in under four decades.
- Stable, isolated populations (spring, artesian, and stream-segment habitats protected by falls or dry reaches over 50 km) are all on private land, with one exception protected from public access.
- Several populations were lost or nearly lost after mosquitofish invasion, and the authors report that topminnows were often reduced or replaced within one to two years of exotic introduction.

**Case studies of replacement**
- **Sheehy Spring:** topminnows were abundant in March 1977 and free of mosquitofish in the upper reach. By December 1979 they made up 58% of 377 poeciliids sampled, 6% of 270 fish in June 1980, and fell to an estimated under 3% by late July 1981.
- **House Pond, San Bernardino Ranch:** topminnows were recorded in 1943 but absent in 1964. Mosquitofish arrived in late 1979, and topminnows were restocked in May 1980 but were not observed in September. Only a few remained in the springhead by December 1980, and they were rare by June 1981.
- **Bylas Springs:** an alluvial fan barrier was breached by floods in winter 1978–79, allowing mosquitofish and red shiners access. Mosquitofish outnumbered topminnows at least 20 to 1 in Salt Creek, and almost every topminnow collected had shredded fins. No juveniles were observed.
- **Cocio Wash:** green sunfish invaded by March 1979 after floods. Only 11 topminnows were collected in June 1980, none were found in September 1980, and a few adults and juveniles reappeared in mid-August 1981.
- **Santa Cruz River:** topminnow numbers fluctuated and were generally inversely proportional to those of introduced fishes. The species was considered extirpated for 12 years (1963–1974).
- **Sonoita Creek:** topminnows and mosquitofish coexisted for at least seven years but have not been collected since 1976.
- **San Bernardino Creek:** mosquitofish were first recorded in June 1980 (three specimens). Topminnows outnumbered them roughly 10:1 through December 1980, but mosquitofish comprised at least half the poeciliids by June 1981.

**Mechanism of decline**
- **Competition (hypothesis 2)** is judged unimportant. Diets overlap little, food is abundant, and mosquitofish have strong conical teeth suited to capturing moving prey, whereas topminnows have weaker spatulate teeth suited to browsing and grazing.
- **Predation (hypothesis 3)** is proposed as the main cause. *G. affinis* is implicated in the decline of at least 22 fish species. It preys on juvenile topminnows and nips adults. Female mosquitofish are reported as dominant and aggressive, reducing topminnow female mortality and fecundity.
- Simberloff (1981) attributed 50 of 71 introduced-species extinctions (70.4%) to predation, 11 (15.5%) to habitat alteration, and 3 (4.2%) to competitive exclusion.
- **Abiotic factors (hypothesis 1)** are judged unlikely to be the main driver. Flooding rarely causes lasting harm where refugia exist, and the species tolerates wide physicochemical ranges. Abiotic change matters chiefly when surface water disappears.

**Management implications**
- Re-introductions within the natural range have largely failed, so continued monitoring, maintenance of existing populations, searching for new localities, and protection from introduced fishes are essential.
- The northern form *P. o. occidentalis* is unique and cannot be replaced by stocking the southern *P. o. sonoriensis* after local extinctions.
- Public Health agencies stock mosquitofish as mosquito control agents, although native topminnows and pupfish are also larvivorous. Bait and sport fish transport by private citizens also spreads exotics, which the authors describe as compounding the problem.
