# Up From Colorado Canon: Prospector Ben Beamer's 1892 Account of the Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-fishes

## Executive Summary

This is a transcribed 1892 newspaper interview, published in the *Denver Republican* on 17 July 1892, with [[Ben Beamer]], a prospector who had lived alone at the bottom of the [[Grand Canyon]] since February 1890. It is not a research study. Its aquatic content is anecdotal: copper-laden springs that make the [[Colorado River]] unlivable for fish, large seasonal fish runs that Beamer harvested for food, and a fish of about twenty inches with a flat hump behind the head. The species are not named. The main content concerns mineral prospects, access trails, and daily life in the canyon.

## Background & Description

- **Source type:** Newspaper article (*Denver Republican*, Sunday Morning, 17 July 1892), transcribed by Richard Ornstein from a discovery by Dove Menkes (as credited in the transcription).
- **Subject:** Ben Beamer, who came to Denver from his home at the bottom of the [[Grand Canyon]]. He describes the canyon's sandstone walls, rising about 5,000 feet, and the river's roar as preferable to the noise of towns.
- **Access:** He entered by the Tanner trail in February 1890. The Tanner trail lies twelve miles below the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]], and Hance's trail lies sixteen miles farther downstream. These are described as the only two routes to the river bed in that section of Arizona.
- **Contact:** He reported seeing no other person during his time there, until the surveyors of the [[Atlantic and Pacific Railroad]] arrived with a guide in spring 1892.
- **Property:** He held a ranch at the mouth of the Little Colorado with about ten acres of cultivable land and a cabin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Copper springs:** Springs about twelve miles above the Little Colorado mouth discharge water so heavily charged with blue vitriol (copper sulfate) that, in Beamer's words, "no fish can live in it." In June and July, when the river drops, these springs supply enough flow to float a boat, but the water is undrinkable.
- **Seasonal fish runs:** After snowmelt, the Colorado backs up into small side canyons. Beamer reports that fish arrive "in millions" to feed on a vegetable growing on the rocks, and that they can be pulled from the water by the tail, two at a time.
- **Fish absence in the Little Colorado:** Beamer says fish cannot live in the Little Colorado itself.
- **Fish description:** Fish are "about twenty inches long" with a flat hump on the back just behind the head. No scientific or common name is given. The description is consistent with, but does not confirm, a large cyprinid.
- **Geology:** The country is sandstone underlain by shale, which Beamer considered a strong indicator of copper ore.
- **Trail and landscape:** Trails are too narrow for horses. Some sections are a "mere hog-back," and a fall from the trail that winds down from the rim would be a drop of about 5,000 feet (OCR reading "51000 feet"; read as 5,000).

## Key Findings

**Mineral prospects (as reported by Beamer):**
- Copper: Ore reported at 50 to 70 per cent copper on the McDonald claim, six miles below Beamer's ranch on the south side of the river. Denver parties, including the lawyer J. N. Hughes, were said to hold it.
- Copper and silver: Hance and two partners had large copper claims further down. The Berry claim, further down still, showed similar copper percentages and yielded silver at $10 to $100 per ton.
- Asbestos: An asbestos claim below Hance was recently sold for $7,500.
- Gold and silver: Large gold and silver ledges lie farther south, though Beamer knew little about them.
- Silverado: Discoveries were made near the canyon rim, about fifty miles back from Kingman on the railroad. Water is lacking, and piping was being arranged.
- Other metals: Beamer listed gold, silver, lead, uranium, molybdenum, and indium, and noted miles of rock giving a blowpipe test for nickel.

**Access and infrastructure:**
- The [[Atlantic and Pacific Railroad]] surveyed Hance's trail in spring 1892 to secure its right of way. Beamer doubted a road would be built.
- Trails allow packing but not riding horseback. Walking along the river bank was reported as reasonably good for twenty-five miles below the Little Colorado mouth.

**Personal account:**
- Beamer lived by prospecting and by eating fish and wild goat. He noted the presence of otters.
- He said he found solitude unproblematic: "Lonesome? Not when you get used to it."

**Limitations:**
- This is an unverified, first-person newspaper account with promotional and speculative elements. Ore grades, claim ownership, and sale prices are not independently confirmed. The fish observations lack species identification, sample sizes, or measurements beyond the approximate length.
