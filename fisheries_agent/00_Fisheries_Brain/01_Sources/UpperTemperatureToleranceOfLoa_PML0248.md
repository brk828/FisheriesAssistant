# Upper Temperature Tolerance of Loach Minnow and Spikedace Under Acute, Chronic, and Fluctuating Thermal Regimes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study, conducted by [[Scott A. Bonar]], [[Cori J. Carveth]], and [[Ann M. Widmer]] with [[Jeff Simms]] of the [[Bureau of Land Management]], measured the upper thermal tolerance of two endangered southwestern cyprinids, [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). Both species are small (<80 mm TL), endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] and the [[San Pedro River]], have lost more than 85% of their range in 75 years, and were listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1986.

Four laboratory methods were compared: the [[Critical Thermal Method]] (CTM), the [[Chronic Lethal Method]] (CLM), and two versions of the [[Acclimated Chronic Exposure Method]] (ACE), one at static temperatures and one with [[Diel Temperature Fluctuation]]. The authors concluded that the static ACE test was the most conservative estimate. Peak stream temperatures should remain below about 28 °C for loach minnow and 30 °C for spikedace to avoid appreciable mortality, and sustained temperatures above these levels cause sublethal stress.

## Background & Description

Both species are small cyprinids of Arizona and New Mexico desert streams. Their decline is attributed to dams, water diversions, sedimentation, channelization, competition and predation by non-native fishes, and altered thermal regimes (Minckley and Deacon 1968; Propst et al. 1988; U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1991a,b). Thermal tolerance data existed for most North American fishes but were lacking for many native Southwestern species. Recovery plans called for quantifying thermal requirements to support habitat assessment.

Temperature governs nearly all biochemical, physiological, and life-history processes in fishes. Southwestern streams are especially vulnerable because reduced surface flow, loss of [[Riparian Vegetation]], and wider channels increase solar heating and daily temperature swings. Daily fluctuations of 15–20 °C have been recorded in summer, and maximum summer temperatures in Arizona streams have been reported at 24–40.3 °C (Deacon and Minckley 1974).

Fish were from [[Aravaipa Creek]] stock. Wild-caught fish and captive F1 fish came from the [[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]] or the University of New Mexico. Fish were held at least 3 months before testing, fed daily, and water changes kept fluctuations under 1.0 °C/d.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime change:** Laboratory fluctuation treatments were modeled on San Pedro River thermograph data. Peaks above 30 °C can persist for up to 30 d there, which set the exposure length for the static ACE tests.
- **Field mortality:** Loach minnow died in Aravaipa Creek at 30.5 °C (Deacon and Minckley 1974), and large adults were observed dying at 34.5 °C in July 2002. The authors note field data cannot give an exact lethal temperature or confirm thermal cause of death.
- **Co-stressors:** Disease, parasites, predation, competition, and poor water quality can lower tolerance in the wild. Loach minnow in Aravaipa Creek were commonly infected with *Ichthyophthirius*, and fish tested positive for *Aeromonas* bacteria.
- **Thermal refugia:** Cooler pools can be several degrees cooler than the main channel. [[Longfin Dace]] reportedly use shaded pools about 3 °C cooler. Temperature differences of up to 12 °C among habitat types have been recorded.
- **Comparative tolerance:** Spikedace and loach minnow tolerate lower temperatures than several co-occurring species, including [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Desert Pupfish]]. Higher tolerance may give non-native species a competitive edge at high temperatures.

## Key Findings

### Methods Compared

| Method | Key Feature | Main Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| CTM | Linear ramp, 0.3 °C/min | Rate unnaturally fast; overestimates lethal limit |
| CLM | Ramp of 1 °C/d | Does not measure exposure duration |
| ACE static | Slow ramp, 30 d constant exposure | Ignores daily fluctuations |
| ACE fluctuating | 30 d sinusoidal cycle | Most realistic; fish tested were a standard size |

### Loach Minnow

- **CTM:** Fish were acclimated for 2 weeks at 25 or 30 °C, then ramped at 0.3 °C/min. Mean death temperature was 36.0–36.8 °C for fish acclimated at 30 °C (the summary's reading is OCR-garbled). The summary reports death at 30.4 °C for fish acclimated at 25 °C, which conflicts with the Results ranges and should be verified against the original. Acclimation temperature significantly raised the temperature at loss of equilibrium. Larger total length also raised the temperature at death. Infection with [[Yellow Grub]] lowered tolerance.
- **CLM:** Acclimation at 30 °C, then ramping at 1 °C/d. Mean death temperature was 33.4 °C (SE 0.07) for 25–35 mm fish and 32.9 °C (SE 0.09) for 40–50 mm fish, a 0.5 °C difference (P<0.0001).
- **ACE static (30 d):** No fish survived 30 d at 32 °C, and mortality began on the first day of exposure. The 30 d LT50 was 30.6 °C (95% CI 30.3–31.1). Six fish died at 30 °C and none at 28 °C. Growth declined by about 1.27 mm per °C increase.
- **ACE fluctuating (30 d):** Treatments were 22–32 °C, 26–32 °C, and 28–32 °C. Survival exceeded 90% in the first two. Mortality was highest (83.3%) in the 28–32 °C treatment. Growth was similar across fluctuating treatments but significantly lower than at ambient 25–29 °C.

### Spikedace

- **CTM:** Ramped at 0.3 °C/min. Loss of equilibrium was 34.7 ± 0.9 °C at 25 °C acclimation and 36.9 ± 1.1 °C at 30 °C acclimation. Death was 37.0 ± 0.7 °C and 39.1 ± 0.6 °C, respectively. Infection with yellow grub had no detectable effect.
- **CLM:** Ramped at 1 °C/d from 30 °C. Loss of equilibrium occurred at 34.5–35 °C, and mean death temperature was 35.6 ± 0.4 °C. All fish died before reaching 36 °C.
- **ACE static (30 d):** This was the most conservative test. No spikedace died at 25 or 30 °C, and none survived 34 or 36 °C. The 30 d LT50 was 32.1 °C (95% CI 31.8–32.5). Growth at 32 °C was 37% of control (5.9 mm). Growth at 30 °C was 70% of control (11.2 mm), and control growth was about 16 mm. Exophthalmia, loss of equilibrium, and inactivity appeared at 30–32 °C.
- **ACE fluctuating (30 d, peak 34 °C):** Survival was 96.5% in the control. Survival was similar for 24–34 °C (10 °C swing). It was significantly lower at 28–34 °C (79.2%, 6 °C swing) and 30–34 °C (73.3%, 4 °C swing). Growth was significantly reduced in the 24–34 °C and 30–34 °C treatments (F = 4.05, P = 0.03).

### Management Implications

- Peaks should remain below 28 °C for loach minnow and 30 °C for spikedace. Below these, little juvenile or adult mortality from heat is expected in the static tests (100% survival at each threshold).
- Brief daily exposure above these levels is less stressful than constant exposure, as shown by the fluctuating trials. Fish still lose growth, however, which may reduce fecundity because spikedace live only 1–2 years.
- Stream temperature fluctuations above 30 °C may push spikedace into thermal refugia where predation and low dissolved oxygen operate.
- The study covered only upper tolerance and did not test lower lethal limits, physiological optima, or early life stages.
