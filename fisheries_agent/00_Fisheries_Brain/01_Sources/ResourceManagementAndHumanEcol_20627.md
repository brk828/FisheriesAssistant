# Human Ecology and Resource Management

#resource-management #human-ecology #wildlife-management

## Executive Summary

This conceptual essay by George B. Happ of [[The Principia College]] argues that [[Human Ecology]] offers a practical framework for [[Natural Resource Management]]. Happ holds that people are the dominant environmental factor in resource management because of their capacity to make choices, experience change, and rearrange their environment. He proposes a parallel ecological sequence, *resource-environment-relation-result* for organisms and *man-environment-relations-results* for humans, and uses tabular "charts" to organize it. Relations are classed as cooperative (cooperation, coordination, toleration) or opposing (uneven competition, dispossession, destruction). Outcomes are judged by stability, security, and survival. The essay is qualitative: it reports no measurements, sample sizes, or statistics, and relies on illustrative examples and a short historical survey of human management of forests, wildlife, waterways, and fisheries.

## Background & Description

Happ defines [[Ecology]] as the study of living entities in relation to their environment, with humans as the main living factor in human ecology. He traces the term to [[Ernst Haeckel]] (1869) and describes ecology as developing from intuitive observation of nature toward logical analysis and synthesis, following the same procedure used in the physical sciences.

The organizing tool is **Chart A**, which relates an animal or plant to its environment. Each resource is treated as a combined physical-psychical (animal) or physical-patterned (plant) entity. The chart has four columns:

1. **Resource**: physical structure (form, size, color, protoplasm), psychical or patterned state (intelligence, instinct, reaction, ignorance; or tropism, habit, reaction).
2. **Environment**: humans, other animals, plants, soil, water, and air, plus edaphic, aquatic, and atmospheric conditions.
3. **Relations**: cooperative or opposing, at a point of contact.
4. **Resulting conditions**: stability, security, and survival.

**Chart B** applies the same structure to humans, replacing instinct with intuition. Human intelligence is defined as the ability to make choices and experience changes that maintain or increase stability, security, and survival for both people and their environment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Happ develops the idea of a **pair event**, a single point of contact between a resource and its environment, which initiates a relation and a result. He compares this to the combination of hydrogen and oxygen forming stable water. Because these contacts recur and vary with changing conditions, outcomes shift over time.

**Illustrative examples from the text:**

- **Douglas-fir** in a mesophytic western environment: cooperative relations and stability, growing large and sturdy. A lower-elevation tree in a more xerophytic setting: opposing competitive relations and instability. A seedling in very xerophytic desert: destructive relations and non-survival.
- **Red-shafted flickers**: cooperative nesting in trees in a mesophytic valley, competitive nesting on telephone poles further west, and no nesting sites on the open plains.
- **Coyote and rodents** in a well-watered mountain valley: cooperative-destructive relations, producing stable subsistence for the coyote and non-survival for the rodents.
- **Digger wasp** (*Bembix* sp.): cited as an example of wild species whose structure and behavior have changed in recent years.
- **Ivory-billed woodpecker**: human-driven changes in forest environments contributed to opposing relations and eventual extinction.

**Human-driven change and management:**

- **Passenger pigeon and American bison**: unwise human predation moved these relations toward destruction, causing the pigeon's extinction and greatly reduced bison numbers.
- **Domesticated animals and plants** (horse, cow, sheep, swine, and forest plants): human contact generally shifted relations toward cooperation, with increased size, adaptability, and survival.
- **Forests**: early settlers used timber without provision for reproduction. Later, [[Theodore Roosevelt]] and [[Gifford Pinchot]] supported national forests under government control. Industrial operators then adopted selective cutting, fire-damage reduction, and tree farms.
- **Wildlife**: laws protecting migratory birds and regulating populations guard against both overdepletion (passenger pigeon) and overpopulation (Virginia deer, which at times became a nuisance or starved).
- **Mississippi valley**: managed waterfowl refuges, navigation dams, limestone quarrying, contour plowing and strip cropping, crop rotation, and scenic parkways.

**Indirect contacts** include business and industrial long-range planning, private forestry and tree farming, recreation and leisure growth, [[Migratory Bird Treaty]] cooperation with Canada, and international agreements. Examples include the Pribilof Islands fur seal management (shared by Canada, Russia, Japan, and the United States, and weakened when Russia and Japan stopped participating), the coordinated Pacific halibut catch, the whaling regulations linked to the [[League of Nations]], and the effect of wartime breakdowns on whale-protection enforcement. Local leadership also affects soil conservation district success.

## Key Findings

- **Central framework:** Resource management is best understood through a parallel ecological sequence (resource-environment-relation-result, and man-environment-relations-results). Happ presents this as increasing objectivity and precision for applied management.
- **Relation types:** Cooperative relations (cooperation, coordination, toleration) lead to stability and survival. Opposing relations (uneven competition, dispossession, destruction) lead to instability, non-survival, or extinction.
- **Human dominance:** Human intelligence, expressed as choice-making and adaptive change, is the most powerful determinant of resource outcomes. Humans can move relations toward cooperation or toward destruction.
- **Historical trend:** Happ argues that human management of forests, wildlife, and waterways has generally moved from ignorance and exploitation toward cooperation, though some cases (passenger pigeon, bison, ivory-billed woodpecker) ended in extinction or severe decline.
- **Indirect influences matter:** Economic planning, recreation, international agreements, and local leadership shape resource outcomes as much as direct management does.
- **Stated conclusion:** Maintaining cooperation between people and natural resources is "urgent and essential." Happ calls on everyone to take part in intelligent, balanced management for mutual stability and security.
- **Limitations:** The essay presents no quantitative data, sample sizes, or statistical tests. Its claims rest on conceptual analysis and examples.
