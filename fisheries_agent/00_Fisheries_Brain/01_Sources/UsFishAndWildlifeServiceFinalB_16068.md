# CAWCD Report on the 1994 Biological Opinion for Central Arizona Project Water Deliveries to the Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a report prepared by the [[Central Arizona Water Conservation District]] (CAWCD) responding to the April 1994 final [[Biological Opinion]] issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (the Service). The opinion concluded that continued delivery of [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water to central Arizona would jeopardize four threatened or endangered fishes (spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow and razorback sucker) and adversely modify critical habitat for three of them. CAWCD argues that the opinion is contrary to the best available scientific and commercial data, contradicts earlier Service consultations, exceeds the scope of the consultation requested by [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and applies a double standard compared with the Service's own stocking activities. CAWCD asks that the opinion be reconsidered and the jeopardy finding withdrawn.

## Background & Description

**The Central Arizona Project.** The CAP is a 336-mile concrete-lined conveyance system, authorized by the Colorado River Basin Project Act of 1968, that carries up to 2.2 million acre-feet per year from [[Lake Havasu]] on the Colorado River to central Arizona. Construction began in 1973, first deliveries reached central Arizona in 1985, and the water supply system was declared substantially complete in 1993. CAWCD, a political subdivision of Arizona, repays CAP costs (its share approaching $2 billion) and operates the aqueduct. The CAP was intended to reduce groundwater overdraft, which the [[Groundwater Management Act]] requires to be brought into balance by 2025.

**The Four Species.**
- **Spikedace** (*Meda fulgida*): a small silvery fish under 70 mm, short-lived (one to two years), listed as threatened in 1986. Critical habitat designated March 8, 1994 includes 15 miles of Aravaipa Creek, 35 miles of the upper Verde River and reaches of the Gila River in New Mexico.
- **Loach minnow** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): a small, bottom-dwelling fish of swift riffles, listed as threatened October 28, 1986; typically lives no more than two years.
- **Gila topminnow** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*): a very small live-bearer, listed as endangered March 11, 1967. Nine natural populations remain, eight in the Santa Cruz basin and one near Bylas; nearly 200 other locations hold stocked populations. No critical habitat has been designated.
- **Razorback sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): a large sucker, proposed for listing in 1978 and listed as endangered in 1991. Critical habitat was designated March 21, 1994. Hatchery introductions in the Verde, Salt and Gila rivers were largely unsuccessful, with predation by non-native fish, especially flathead catfish, implicated.

**Consultation history.** CAWCD states that Reclamation consulted the Service more than forty times during CAP design and construction, and that the Service earlier found no jeopardy for the Gila topminnow (1983 opinion on the Central Arizona Water Control Study) and no effect for several irrigation delivery systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Non-native fish and the CAP.** Fourteen non-native species were reported in CAP canals between Lake Havasu and Brady Pumping Plant, including threadfin shad, largemouth bass, bluegill, common carp, channel catfish, flathead catfish and red shiner. Survey data from electrofishing at pumping plant forebays show that the average catch fell from about 8.56 fish per minute in 1987 to about 0.7 in 1992, a 92 percent decline. Summer canal temperatures regularly exceed 25°C and can reach 30°C, and concrete lining offers few nutrients.

**Hydrology.** The Hassayampa River is ephemeral, normally dry for 38 miles between Morristown and Arlington. Centennial Wash flows only about four to five days per year on average. Flow to the Gila River requires roughly 800 cfs at Morristown, exceeded only about one percent of the time.

**Barriers.** CAWCD emphasises several barriers: electrical fish barriers on Salt River Project canals (each a series of six pulse generators) and on the Florence-Casa Grande Canal at China Wash; Granite Reef Diversion Dam; Stewart Mountain, Mormon Flat, Horse Mesa and Roosevelt dams on the Salt River; Bartlett and Horseshoe dams on the Verde; Roosevelt Diversion Dam; Ashurst-Hayden Dam; and Coolidge Dam. A water-to-water connection between the Gila and upper Hassayampa is expected only a few days each year.

**Stocking.** CAWCD reports that the Arizona Game and Fish Department stocked nearly 28 million non-native fish into the Gila basin since 1928, and that the Service itself stocked 2,546,902 non-native fish in the Gila and Salt drainages from 1990 to 1993. Roughly 14 million rainbow trout were stocked in the upper Salt and Verde systems.

**Striped bass.** Striped bass were treated as the most significant potential invader. CAWCD cites studies indicating they avoid temperatures above about 28°C and are unlikely to enter small streams such as the San Pedro River or Aravaipa Creek.

## Key Findings

**Findings presented by CAWCD (the report's conclusions):**

- The Biological Opinion's RPAs require a $500,000 annual transfer to the Service for 25 years, construction and maintenance of fish barriers, 100 years of non-native fish monitoring, and an education program. CAWCD estimates total compliance costs above $150 million (its footnote gives $152.056 million).
- Gila topminnow in the Agua Fria basin: the Service's 1983 opinion found no jeopardy, and Reclamation built a barrier on Tule Creek. CAWCD says non-native fish were already well established in Lake Pleasant before CAP storage began in December 1992.
- Only two CAP non-native species (striped bass and flathead catfish) were absent from Lake Pleasant; neither is expected to threaten topminnow in tributaries.
- Of 33 non-native species the Opinion cites for CAP canals, only fourteen had been reported in CAP canals, and all but redear sunfish and striped bass were already in the San Pedro River or San Carlos Reservoir.
- Non-native fish in the CAP mirror those in Lake Havasu, and most species were established in the Gila basin before any CAP water arrived.
- The Opinion's jeopardy finding depends largely on "bait bucket" transfers by anglers, which CAWCD argues are not an effect of the CAP under the regulatory definition.
- The Service applied a double standard: in its July 1994 opinion on rainbow trout and channel catfish stocking in the lower Colorado River, it required "direct evidence" of predation, distinguished stocked from wild populations, and found no jeopardy where dams or isolated waters separated stocked fish from endangered species.
- The Service's recovery plans for spikedace and loach minnow allow stocking downstream of barriers, and the Service estimated those two species' recovery at about $230,000 per year for twenty years.

**Findings from the Biological Opinion (as summarised in the document):**

- The Opinion concluded jeopardy for the four species and adverse modification of critical habitat for spikedace, loach minnow and razorback sucker. It acknowledged that the Aravaipa Creek populations of loach minnow and spikedace are at high risk and will probably be extirpated by cumulative non-native introductions over the life of the project.
- Striped and white bass are expected to become part of the fish fauna of the Gila and San Pedro rivers, where they may prey on native fish.
- The Opinion treated the CAP as creating a perennial waterway from the Colorado River to the Gila basin, and it cited the Salt and Verde barriers as "not totally effective."

**Conclusions and requests in the report:**

- CAWCD asks that the Biological Opinion be reconsidered and the jeopardy finding withdrawn.
- CAWCD argues that the RPAs were, in its view, used to fund Service research and conservation rather than to address CAP-specific effects, and that nine candidate RPAs were replaced by funding transfers to the Service.

**Limitations.** This is a stakeholder rebuttal, and its claims about the Biological Opinion and about other Service and agency actions come from CAWCD's own reading of those documents. The Opinion text reproduced in the appendix is substantially complete but partly OCR-degraded.
