# Additional Studies on the Little Colorado River Population of the Humpback Chub

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #Gila-cypha #endangered-species

## Executive Summary
This 1979 field survey by C.O. Minckley investigated the reproductive success, age structure, relative density/abundance, behavior, tagging, and ectoparasite loads of the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) population in the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence area with the [[Colorado River]], Grand Canyon. Conducted 13-15 July 1979 using trammel nets, gill nets, and seining, the study confirmed successful reproduction, documented over ten age classes, and reaffirmed the Little Colorado River's critical role as a spawning and nursery habitat for this endangered cyprinid. The survey also tracked a marked decline in [[Anchorworm|Anchorworm (Lernaea cyprinaceae)]] ectoparasite infestation compared to the prior year.

## Background & Description
- **Total length range documented:** 25 mm (young-of-year) to over 300 mm (mature adults).
- **Tagging threshold:** Fish >15 cm (150 mm) total length were tagged (Willow Beach method and Minckley method) using consecutively numbered fingerling tags.
- **Growth trajectory (per Carothers and Minckley 1980):**
  - First year of life: 86-92 mm total length
  - Ninth year of life: ~358 mm total length
- **Size class segregation observed during schooling:**
  - <100 mm
  - 100-200 mm
  - 200-300 mm
  - >300 mm
- Discrete size groupings within collections suggest potential ontogenetic or behavioral size-based schooling segregation, though preliminary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat role:** The Little Colorado River confluence and upstream reaches (~0.4 km upstream site) serve as an important **spawning and nursery area** for *Gila cypha*, corroborated by multiple independent studies ([[Carothers and Minckley 1980]], [[Suttkus and Clemmer 1979]]).
- **Spawning period:** Extends from March through mid-summer; spawning in 1979 occurred after flood recession (flows had exceeded 3000 cfs in April 1979), demonstrating reproductive resilience of native fishes to hydrological disturbance, likely influenced by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations.
- **Foraging behavior:** Adults observed actively foraging along substrate, ledges, and mid-water column; diet composition not determined.
- **Schooling behavior:** Adults observed in schools of 15-20 individuals, with possible size-based segregation within schools.
- **Sympatric fish community** (relative abundance in confluence area, July 1979):

- **Parasite ecology:** Infested by the exotic [[Anchorworm]] ectoparasite (*Lernaea cyprinaceae*), a copepod known to cause severe impacts in other native fish populations (Wilson et al. 1966; James 1968).
- **Movement/dispersal:** Tagged fish presumed to disperse into the mainstream [[Colorado River]] or move upstream within the Little Colorado River, though unconfirmed due to zero recaptures.

## Key Findings
1. **Reproduction confirmed:** 12 young-of-year fish (25-44 mm TL) collected, mostly ~0.4 km upstream of the confluence, confirming successful 1979 spawning despite spring flooding (>3000 cfs in April).
2. **Age structure:** More than ten age classes present in July collections; fish age IX+ dominated (26.4%), followed by age IV (14.9%) and young-of-year (13.8%).
3. **Relative density (YOY/juveniles):**
   - Upstream site (~0.4 km): 582 fish/hectare (down from 1063 f/ha in 1978)
   - Confluence site: 407 fish/hectare (up from 278 f/ha in 1978)
   - Both within historical range of 167-1063 f/ha.
   - Density of larger fish could not be quantitatively estimated, though qualitatively abundant.
4. **Relative abundance:** Humpback chub was the most abundant species collected (47.4%), followed by flannelmouth sucker, speckled dace, carp, and channel catfish.
5. **Tagging program:** 67 humpback chubs tagged and released in this survey, bringing cumulative Grand Canyon regional total to 216 tagged fish; zero recaptures to date.
6. **Behavioral insight:** Preliminary evidence of size segregation during schooling behavior — a novel observation warranting further study.
7. **Ectoparasite decline:** Anchorworm infestation dropped sharply from 55% (October 1978) to 2.3% (July 1979), though continued presence is flagged as a conservation concern given documented severe impacts on other native fish populations.
8. **Conservation implication:** Findings reinforce the Little Colorado River's designation as critical spawning/nursery habitat for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], with implications for Glen Canyon Dam flow management and continued long-term monitoring needs.
