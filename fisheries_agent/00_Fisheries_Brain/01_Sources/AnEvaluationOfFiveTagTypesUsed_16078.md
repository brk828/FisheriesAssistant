# Evaluation of Five Fish Tag Types in a Striped Bass Mortality and Migration Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 paper by Harold K. Chadwick of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] evaluates five external and internal tag types used in a 1958–1961 study of [[Striped Bass]] mortality rates and migrations in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin River System]] and [[San Francisco Bay]]. Tags were judged against four criteria: no effect on mortality, no effect on angling vulnerability, no shedding, and easy recognition with clear return instructions. The tags tested were [[Disk-Dangler Tags]], [[Hydrostatic Tags]], [[Dart Tags]], [[Streamer Tags]], and [[Spaghetti Tags]]. The author concluded that none fully met all requirements, and that disk-dangler tags, although widely returned, introduced biases through shedding, irritation, and reduced growth.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Tagging was the primary method for the 1958–1961 study. Earlier striped bass work relied mainly on [[Petersen Disk Tags]], which were shed rapidly and were therefore poor for mortality studies. An internal tag had been used before but was considered impossible to recover from the sport fishery.
- **Selection rationale:** Disk-dangler tags (modified Atkins design) had performed well on striped bass, white catfish, and largemouth bass, so they were chosen as the standard. Spaghetti, hydrostatic, dart, and streamer tags were added as alternatives.
- **Study sites:** Most fish were caught by gill net near Antioch in the spring spawning migration. Some were tagged at Verona, Prisoner's Point, and San Pablo Bay. Landlocked bass were tagged in San Luis Wasteway, a pond roughly 200 ft wide, 200 yards long, and up to 1 ft deep. A 1,000-gallon aquarium gave limited additional observations.
- **Recovery method:** Anglers returning tags received a postal questionnaire on flesh condition around the tag. Answers were subjective and used for comparison only.
- **Funding:** Dingell-Johnson Project California F-9-R, "A Study of Sturgeon and Striped Bass."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Tag–fish interaction:** Shedding, wire corrosion, skin healing, and irritation all depended on attachment site and material. Tags pulled through the flesh until anchored on pterygiophores and neural spines, forming a stalk of 0.5 to 1.5 inches.
- **Biofouling:** Marine organisms, especially [[Hydroids]], grew on tags in the Delta. Barnacles were less important. Hydroids sometimes reached several inches and were the likely cause of most severe sores.
- **Tissue response:** Necrotic pockets and connective-tissue sheaths formed around tags. Bacterial infection occurred in only one shed-tag case. Heavy algal growth on spaghetti tags obscured their printing.
- **Growth and vulnerability:** Tagged bass grew more slowly, and tagged bass were caught at a reduced rate for about the first month after tagging.

## Key Findings

**Disk-dangler tags (approximately 16,000 bass tagged 1958–1961)**
- Wire types (pure tantalum 0.020 and 0.025 in; Type 302 soft stainless steel 0.020 in) gave broadly comparable results. The 1958 stainless steel tags returned more than tantalum (χ² = 5.15, P = 0.02), but the 1960 pattern reversed (χ² = 3.48, P = 0.07).
- Overall returns were about 24–30% for the 0.020 wires in the Delta (for example, 1958 tags: 26% stainless vs. 19% tantalum first-year returns combined in Table 2).
- Irritation was similar for stainless and 0.020 tantalum wires. Roughly 53–57% had no sore, 33–40% a slight sore, and 7–10% a bad sore.
- Tantalum wire appeared more prone to shedding because of lower tensile strength. At San Luis Wasteway, skin had healed over the knot on only 10 of 37 tantalum tags at the end of year one. Stainless steel healed faster in the Delta (13 of 16 vs. 17 of 29).
- Shedding probably increased over time. Survival estimates from 1958 tags fell from 0.58 (1959) to 0.44 (1960), while estimates for 1960 tags were 0.44, 0.33, and 0.31 for successive tag groups.
- Growth was reduced: most deviations from expected growth were negative, indicating slowed growth.
- Tag returns were lower during the first four months after tagging, suggesting reduced vulnerability to angling. Antioch comparisons showed this effect declined over the first month.

**Hydrostatic tags (459 tagged in May 1959, alternating with 493 disk-danglers)**
- First-year returns were 16% (hydrostatic) vs. 15% (disk-dangler); total returns were 29% vs. 27%.
- Hydrostatic tags without growths showed less irritation than disk-danglers, although the difference was not significant at the 95% level (χ² = 5.6, df = 2, P = 0.06).
- The printed message offered a prize, which may have biased returns. Better instructions may improve angler reporting.

**Dart tags (Floy FT#1 and a modified skipjack design; 466 Delta tags and 64 San Luis Wasteway tags, 1959)**
- At San Luis Wasteway, 22 of 30 bass examined about a year later had shed their tags.
- Delta shedding was high. By the second year about three-quarters of tags were lost. Total returns were 9.0% (42 tags) vs. 27.5% for disk-danglers.
- Printing wore off and the Resinite plastic darkened, so most tags were illegible after two years.

**Streamer tags (299 Delta tags alternating with 300 disk-danglers, May 1960)**
- Returns were not significantly different over two years (20.4% vs. 22.7%), but streamer returns fell behind after nine months (χ² = 3.6, P = 0.06).
- At San Luis Wasteway, 5 of 26 bass had shed tags at the end of year one.

**Spaghetti tags (Temflex 105 tubing; 31 San Luis Wasteway bass, 1957–1958)**
- Shedding was low. Only one tag was shed (after two years).
- Irritation was strong: connective-tissue sheaths formed, necrosis developed, and some tags pulled out with tissue.
- Algal growth and tubing darkening made the legend illegible after 12–18 months.

**Overall conclusions**
- None of the five tags met all criteria.
- Disk-danglers had high return rates, but shedding, irritation, growth reduction, and reduced early angling vulnerability could bias mortality estimates.
- Hydrostatic tags performed similarly and may cause less irritation.
- Dart tags were unsatisfactory because of high shedding and deterioration.
- Spaghetti tags were unsatisfactory because of tissue reaction, though low shedding suggested a different plastic might work.
- Streamer tags were satisfactory for about nine months only.
