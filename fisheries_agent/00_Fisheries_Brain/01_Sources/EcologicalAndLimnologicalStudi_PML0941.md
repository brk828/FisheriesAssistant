# Ecological and Limnological Studies of the Green River, Dinosaur National Monument (1962 Field Season)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 report by [[Harold K. Hagen]] (Associate Professor, Fisheries Science) and [[Joe L. Banks]] (Graduate Student) of [[Colorado State University]] documents the 1962 field season of an ecological and limnological survey of the [[Green River]] in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. The study was contracted by the Midwest Regional Office of the National Park Service and emphasized the fishes of the area, with concurrent aquatic insect, plankton, and stomach-content work. Physical and chemical data (temperature, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, pH, alkalinity) were collected alongside catch statistics. Results were strongly shaped by a September 1962 pollution event that killed large numbers of fish, and the authors caution that their collections are not a complete census of fish populations.

## Background & Description

The project was initiated in June 1962 following earlier surveys in 1961 (Hagen, 1961, a preliminary survey of the vertebrate and invertebrate fauna). A single graduate student was placed in the field for the entire summer and fall, collecting under varying water conditions. Collections were made at several stations, including [[Lodore Canyon]] (Gates of Lodore), [[Island Park]], [[Echo Park]], [[Split Mountain]] (Split Mountain Campground), and Castle Park on the [[Yampa River]]. Stations at Beaver Creek and Dromedary Park were dropped because no fish were found at the former collecting site.

The report notes that the Green River's extreme seasonal fluctuations in volume, temperature, and turbidity likely drive fish and invertebrate distribution and have favored specialized adaptations in several rare fish forms, which occupy narrow ecological niches. Long-term physical data gathered by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Geological Survey, and state agencies of Wyoming and Colorado were not reproduced in detail.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat.** Stream gradient varies considerably. Near Lodore the fall is about 2.5 feet per mile, while the Lodore Canyon average grade increases to 18.6 feet per mile. Rapids and plunges in Whirlpool Canyon and Split Mountain Canyon reach an average near 22 feet per mile. Bottom types range from silt and sand to rubble and bedrock, with silt and sand dominating at Lodore. Backwaters and eddies were populated by fish at all seasons, though foraging was limited during high water.

**Temperature.** Temperatures in the Green River were recorded in degrees Fahrenheit throughout summer 1962, typically 65 to 70 °F during July and August, with the highest recorded value near 72 °F. The Yampa River was usually a few degrees warmer than the Green. Temperature was considered a major factor in fish distribution and in the failure to find fish at some sites.

**Turbidity and flow.** The river rose and fell repeatedly, changing by roughly 1 to 7 inches per day during July and August. Turbidity was high much of the time, and the river was described as cloudy with red-to-brown color during some periods. Depth of visibility was measured in inches (Table 1). Turbidity peaks were linked to rain and to muddy inflows.

**Water chemistry.** On the Green River, pH stayed near 7.5. Dissolved oxygen ranged from 5.8 to 8.0 parts per million (ppm), and total alkalinity from 121 to 151 ppm. The Yampa River showed dissolved oxygen of 6.0 to 8.1 ppm and alkalinity of 123 to 171 ppm. Field alkalinity readings from Lodore varied widely, with one value flagged as probably erroneous.

**Aquatic plants.** Vegetation in the Green River was sparse. Algae increased sharply after the river cleared in late summer, forming thick green mats by late September. Rains washed filamentous algae downstream, clogging throw lines.

**Invertebrates.** Mayflies (Ephemeroptera), stoneflies (Plecoptera), caddisflies (Trichoptera), midges and blackflies (Diptera), and beetles (Coleoptera) were collected with square-foot bottom samplers. Insect abundance was described as very cyclic. Average invertebrate counts per square foot were reported for four stations in 1961 and 1962 (Table 4). Bottom organism numbers declined noticeably between 1961 and 1962; the authors attributed the decline to the September pollution.

**Stomach contents and diet.** Stomach analysis was incomplete at the time of the report. Fish remains were found in stomachs of adult squawfish, chubs, carp, catfish, and flannelmouth suckers. Juvenile chubs ate only insects and plant material. The authors suggested that the fishes appear to be opportunistic rather than selective feeders.

**Parasites and disease.** Three principal external parasites were found: the copepod [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] (anchor worm), a leech, and a sporozoan causing a white-spot-like disease. Prevalence was highest on the Yampa River, with 41 to 44 per cent of fish infected (figure partly illegible in scan), followed by Echo Park at 31.3 per cent. Copepods were found on nearly all species except channel catfish, black bullhead, and carp. Copepods caused ulcerous growths, and one squawfish was blinded in one eye by copepod infection.

## Key Findings

**Fish community and key species**

- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) was present in the Lodore and Whirlpool Canyon reaches. The report noted that distinguishing [[Bony Tail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) from [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) was possible in the field. Bony tail were taken in the slower waters of narrow channels, while roundtail were taken in fast current near Lodore. Red to orange markings appeared on bony tail during spawning season.
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) was taken in all reaches and appeared to have expanded into the turbulent river. A three-inch carp from the lower Yampa River had a distinct hump, first noted in 1951, and the authors discussed whether hump formation reflects an unusual trait or hybridization.
- [[Colorado River Squawfish]] was taken in all water types, with juveniles favoring quiet backwaters. Only one adult was caught on hook and line, in Whirlpool Canyon.
- A [[Humpback Sucker]] was not observed until after the September pollution event, when many of these rare forms were collected dead from Lodore to Split Mountain. Hybrids between humpback sucker and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) were noted.
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*) reports were not confirmed in the Green River; no mountain sucker was found in the Green, although it was found in the Yampa.
- [[Green River Spring Dace]] was common in backwaters and cut-offs. Other species included [[Channel Catfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Green Sunfish]], Colorado River sculpin, redside shiner, and Idaho silverside.

**Sampling methods**

- The most effective method was a large crew equipped with electrofishing, large and small seines, and fish toxicant (rotenone) for small side channels. A rotenone-based poison at more than 2 ppm was used in one side channel near Castle Park.
- Gill nets were unsuccessful until late in summer. Hoop nets and trap nets caught little. Throw lines caught fish only within about 3 feet of shore; no fish were taken beyond the third hook (10 to 15 feet).

**Survey totals**

- October 1962 surveys used two crews (14 students total) across the sampled stations. Table 2 lists fish collections by station. Echo Park held the greatest number of species (of 19 recorded) of any single station in 1961.
- Electrofishing and seining at Split Mountain recovered only about half the fish stunned; at Echo Park nearly all stunned fish were taken.

**Pollution event (September 1962)**

- A pollution event in early September 1962 killed large numbers of fish along the Green River from Ledore to Split Mountain. Dead fish included squawfish, flannelmouth suckers, humpback suckers, carp, channel catfish, chubs, and shiners. Reports estimated about three-quarters of the dead fish in one reach were channel catfish (figure partly illegible in scan).
- The authors stated that the pollution event was the major reason for the much lower fish and invertebrate numbers in 1962 compared with 1961.

**Invertebrate and food-web changes**

- Invertebrate numbers in the bottom samples fell from 1961 to 1962 at all compared stations. The authors suggested that the decline reduced available fish food within the monument and that invertebrates would recover faster than fish.

**Water quality**

- Turbidity and temperature changes were tracked daily from July through November 1962. Daily logs, including daily river stage, temperature, pH, and dissolved oxygen, appear in an appendix.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- The authors concluded that fish were not strongly selective feeders, that the fish fauna was highly adapted to a variable river, and that the 1962 pollution event had caused serious losses of rare species.
- Future collections were recommended for summer 1963 and later years, before the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] reservoir was filled, to compare population dynamics over time.
- The report recommended a single-student annual sampling program and a genetic study of rare forms still remaining in the river.
