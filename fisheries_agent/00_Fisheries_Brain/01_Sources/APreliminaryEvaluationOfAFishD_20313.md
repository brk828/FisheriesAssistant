# A Preliminary Evaluation of a Fish Diet Based on Roasted Soybeans and Fresh Fish

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Ictaluridae #fish-nutrition

## Executive Summary

This 1973 study evaluated two experimental fish diets combining roasted soybeans with fresh [[Gizzard Shad]] (Dorosoma cepedianum) as alternatives to commercial [[Oregon Pellets]] for feeding yearling [[Channel Catfish]] (Ictalurus punctatus). Both experimental formulations—one with 50% roasted soybeans and 50% fresh fish, another with 30% soybeans, 20% corn meal, and 50% fresh fish—produced growth rates and feed conversion ratios statistically equivalent to the commercial control diet over a 6-week trial. The diets were designed for regions lacking access to commercial fish feeds and to utilize non-commercial fish species as feed ingredients.

## Background & Description

Commercial pelleted fish feeds were widely available in the United States by 1973, including complete diets for raceway and cage feeding, and supplemental diets for pond culture. The [[Oregon Pellet]], a moist refrigerated feed, had proven highly successful for trout and salmon culture. However, prepared fish feeds were not available worldwide, creating need for locally-producible alternatives.

The experimental diet formulation was inspired by recommendations from the Fish Feeds and Nutrition Workshop (September 26-28, 1972, Rapid City, South Dakota), which suggested that soybeans roasted at 218°C for 15 minutes, supplemented with fish meal, would provide a nutritionally complete and palatable fish diet. The researchers modified this approach by using fresh whole ground fish rather than fish meal, incorporating the moisture from fresh fish directly into the pellet similar to [[Oregon Pellet]] methodology but with simpler formulation and less sophisticated equipment requirements.

**Experimental Diet 1 (wet weight basis):**
- 48.5% ground roasted soybeans
- 48.5% fresh or frozen [[Gizzard Shad]]
- 1.0% vitamin mix
- 2.0% salt mixture (U.S.P. XIV)

**Experimental Diet 2 (wet weight basis):**
- 32.3% ground roasted soybeans
- 16.2% corn meal
- 48.5% fresh or frozen [[Gizzard Shad]]
- 1.0% vitamin mix
- 2.0% salt mixture (U.S.P. XIV)

The vitamin mix included 16 components: Vitamin A, Vitamin D, alpha tocopherol, ascorbic acid, inositol, choline chloride, menadione, p-aminobenzoic acid, niacin, riboflavin, pyridoxine hydrochloride, thiamine hydrochloride, calcium pantothenate, biotin, folic acid, and Vitamin B12.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was conducted in controlled laboratory conditions at the [[Fisheries Research Laboratory]], [[Southern Illinois University]]. Nine experimental tanks, each containing 200 liters of water, were maintained under continuous aeration and 24-hour lighting. Each tank was stocked with 20 [[Channel Catfish]] weighing approximately 20 g each.

The research addressed practical aquaculture challenges including:
1. Feed availability in developing regions
2. Utilization of non-commercial fish species (including [[Common Carp]] (Cyprinus carpio), [[Green Sunfish]] (Lepomis cyanellus), and [[Fathead Minnow]] (Pimephales promelas) in supplementary trials)
3. Potential use of fish viscera or processing scraps in locally-produced feeds

## Key Findings

**Growth Performance:**
- No significant difference in weight gain among fish fed the two experimental diets and [[Oregon Pellets]] (α level = 0.10, resulting P = 0.115)
- [[Oregon Pellet]] tanks: mean initial weight 397 g, final weight 1072 g, 70% gain
- Shad/soybean diet tanks: mean initial weight 409 g, final weight 1019 g, 49% gain
- Shad/soybean/corn meal diet tanks: mean initial weight 394 g, final weight 1025 g, 160% gain

**Feed Conversion:**
- No significant difference in feed conversion ratios among the three feed types (α level = 0.10, resulting P = 0.107)
- [[Oregon Pellet]]: mean feed conversion 0.81
- Shad/soybean diet: mean feed conversion 0.90
- Shad/soybean/corn meal diet: mean feed conversion 0.87
- Feed conversion calculated as dry weight of feed divided by wet weight gain

**Feed Characteristics:**
- The grinding process (four passes through a 1-horsepower Toledo Chopper with 3 mm plate) produced pellets 0.5 to 1.0 cm long and 3 mm diameter with excellent physical characteristics
- Both experimental feeds were readily accepted by [[Channel Catfish]] with feeding response similar to [[Oregon Pellets]]
- Substituting [[Common Carp]], [[Green Sunfish]], or [[Fathead Minnow]] for [[Gizzard Shad]] did not alter pellet physical state or feeding response

**Feeding Rates:**
- Approximately 3% of body weight per day for weeks 1-2
- Increased by 50% for weeks 3-4
- Doubled for weeks 5-6
- Calculated feeding rate at harvest: 2.3%

**Vitamin Deficiency Observation:**
- Fish exhibited fighting behavior within 2 weeks when vitamin mix was omitted from the shad/soybean diet
- Tentatively interpreted as symptom of Vitamin B6 deficiency
- Researchers noted this finding's potential relevance to cage culture, where fighting is a serious problem

**Protein Content:**
- [[Oregon Pellet]]: 55% protein
- Shad/soybean diet: 31% protein
- Shad/soybean/corn meal diet: 18% protein

The experimental diets demonstrated practical viability for regions lacking commercial fish feeds and potential for utilizing non-commercial fish species in aquaculture feed production. The simpler formulation and less sophisticated equipment requirements (basic food chopper) made these diets particularly attractive for local production in developing regions.
