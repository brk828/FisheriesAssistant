# Distribution, reproduction and habitat use of the razorback sucker in the Green River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Harold M. Tyus]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] evaluated the distribution, habitat use, relative abundance, and reproduction of the rare [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the [[Green River]] of Colorado and Utah, 1979-1986. The work combined electrofishing, seining, and trammel-net captures of 323 adults with radiotelemetry of 6 fish. Razorback suckers were concentrated in flatwater reaches and absent from whitewater canyons. Three spawning reaches were identified, and larvae were found downstream of them, but no juveniles were collected, indicating little or no successful recruitment. The author concludes that the population is small and aging, and that without recruitment the species may disappear in nature as old fish die.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and is among its rarest fishes. It was once abundant and widely distributed, but its riverine range is now mainly the mainstem of the Green and Upper Colorado rivers. Stocks in lower basin reservoirs, such as [[Lake Mohave]], appear to be relict populations of very old individuals. Systematic sampling by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] began in 1979 to document the distribution, abundance, and life history of [[Endangered Fishes]], and the present paper draws on that dataset.

Adult fish measured 426-698 mm total length. Ripe males averaged 503 mm (N = 37) and ripe females 544 mm (N = 23); females were significantly longer and heavier (Student's t, P < 0.01). The length-frequency distribution was unimodal, so age groups could not be separated, and all fish were assumed adult. Green River fish were smaller than the up to 650 mm (males) and 740 mm (females) reported from lower-basin reservoirs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and water conditions.** Ripe fish were captured over coarse sand and some gravel and cobble bars. Spawning-season capture temperatures ranged from 10 to 18 °C, averaging about 15 °C. Ripe fish were also taken in warm (17-19 °C) flooded bottomlands in 1986. Outside the breeding season, radiotracked fish used the main channel, depths of 0.6-3.4 m, and midchannel sand bars in summer, where water was usually under 2 m deep with velocities near 0.5 m/s.

**Associated species.** [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) was the most abundant sucker, with 1,776 adults caught in standardized sampling. [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) was also sampled, with 278 caught in standardized sampling and 175 in spring electrofishing. Introduced predators, including [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), are considered a possible cause of larval loss.

**Threats.** Water resource development, [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (completed 1962), and introduced predators are identified as factors that may reduce recruitment. Low spring water temperatures may also reduce embryo survival.

## Key Findings

**Abundance and distribution**
- 323 razorback suckers were captured. The center of distribution was the Upper Green River, from the Duchesne River mouth to the Yampa River mouth.
- Only 6 fish were collected in the lower sections (A and B). None were found in [[Split Mountain Canyon]], [[Desolation Canyon]], or [[Gray Canyon]].
- The ratio of razorback to flannelmouth suckers was 1:355 (0.28%) in standardized sampling and 1:32 (3.2%) in spring electrofishing, when razorbacks were highly vulnerable during spawning.
- Razorback catch equaled 1.8% of the Colorado squawfish catch in standardized sampling and 8.3% in spring sampling.

**Reproduction**
- 101 fish in breeding condition were captured between May 3 and June 15 (1981, 1984, and 1986). Ripe fish made up 55% (1984) and 58.7% (1986) of the catch (N = 163).
- Few fish were collected in 1983 and 1985 (N = 16), and no ripe fish were found, suggesting low spawning activity in those years.
- Three spawning reaches were identified: Island and Echo Parks in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], the Jensen area (73% of ripe fish, N = 60), and the Ouray area. The male:female ratio was 1.61:1.
- Larvae (10.6-13.6 mm) were collected in 1984 from about 31% of 42 seine samples (N = 33 larvae), downstream of spawning areas. Their identity was tentative.
- Spawning occurred over sand and gravel in shallow water near inflowing streams. Spawning temperatures were 15 to 15.5 °C, which the author notes may not be optimal, since [[Marsh (1985)]] found the best hatch at 20 °C and complete mortality at 5 and 10 °C.

**Recruitment**
- No juveniles were collected, so recruitment appears to be negligible. Two hypotheses are proposed: predation by introduced species, and habitat alteration following the 1962 Flaming Gorge Dam closure.

**Growth and age**
- Old fish are present. A 505 mm specimen had at least 12 annular rings, and a 560 mm fish was estimated at 19 years by otolith reading. Average age may be below the 24-44 years reported by [[McCarthy (1986)]].
- 39 adults were recaptured 1-8 years after tagging, with slow growth of 2.2 mm per year.

**Movement**
- 61 fish were recaptured 74 times (21% of fish captured). Of these, 24 were sedentary (moved under 10 km), and 28 were mobile, averaging 59.3 km (range 13-206 km) from release.
- 21 tagged fish were found at two or more spawning areas. 14 moved between the two Jensen-reach sites, mostly downstream.
- One radiotagged fish moved 136 km downstream and later returned 130 km upstream.

**Habitat use (radiotelemetry)**
- Of 10 implanted fish, 6 were tracked successfully. Mean depth was 1.3-1.5 m and mean velocity was 0.3-0.4 m/s.
- Fish used runs near shore in spring and midchannel sand bars in summer. Preference for these bars over deeper pools was described as unexpected.

**Conclusions**
- The author concludes the population will continue to decline as old individuals die without replacement, and that immediate measures are needed. The Green River stock may be the largest surviving in the species' native riverine environment.
