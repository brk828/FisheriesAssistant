# Temperature Tolerance of Humpback Chub and Colorado Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-cypha #ptychocheilus-lucius

## Executive Summary

This 1993 [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] annual report chapter tests how water temperature affects early life stages of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). The work responds to the depressed spring-summer water temperatures created by [[Glen Canyon Dam]], which are thought to have limited natural reproduction. Laboratory experiments showed that cold exposure caused temporary loss of equilibrium ("[[Cold Coma]]") and reduced growth, but no deaths during short observation periods. The authors also describe a field spawning and [[Fish Culture]] protocol that succeeded in 1993 where earlier attempts failed, and they recommend flow changes at the dam to restore warmer conditions.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** The humpback chub is an endangered cyprinid-group fish. The [[Grand Canyon]] population is the largest of seven known populations. The Colorado squawfish is a native large-river fish also declined in the basin. Both are native to the [[Colorado River Mainstem]].
- **Historical decline:** Closure of Glen Canyon Dam (around 1963) changed river physical conditions. Authors cite the loss of the Colorado squawfish, bonytail, and roundtail chub from Grand Canyon, and the persistence of humpback chub and other natives only in reduced numbers.
- **Key habitat:** The [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), a perennial warm tributary, is the only known site of successful humpback chub reproduction and recruitment in Grand Canyon.
- **Prior failures:** Three earlier field attempts to spawn LCR humpback chub produced no live larvae (Minckley 1989; Raisanen et al. 1991; Angradi et al. 1992). Earlier successful propagation occurred with Brack Rocks fish at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal and flow effects**
- Dam operation depressed spring-summer downstream temperatures, likely precluding successful native reproduction and causing mortality during incubation.
- Early life stages that move from the warm LCR into the cold mainstem cross a [[Thermal Gradient]], which can reduce survival, growth, and condition.
- Cold rearing has been linked in the literature to increased early mortality, delayed maturity, reduced lipid stores, elevated overwinter mortality of young-of-year fish, and lower adult fecundity.

**Field water quality (LCR and hatching buckets, 1993)**
- Dissolved oxygen in the LCR: 6.3 to 8.2 mg/L (75.5 to 97.6% saturation); full saturation maintained in aerated buckets.
- Conductivity rose from near 2800 µS (April 26) to 3800 µS (May 7), with similar levels in buckets.
- pH: 7.7 to 8.2 in the LCR.
- LCR temperature: maximum 20.4 °C, minimum 12.0 °C during the spawning period. Bucket temperatures ran about 1 °C greater in both directions.

## Key Findings

### Field spawning and incubation (1993)

- Spawning and incubation ran near Salt Trail Canyon, 10.5 km above the Colorado River confluence, using [[Hoop Nets]] (6.4 mm mesh, checked at least twice daily from April 20 to May 8, 1993).
- Approximately 75 humpback chub over 200 mm were captured. Reported sex counts in the scan are internally inconsistent and should be checked against the original report.
- Fish were tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tags. Females not readily expressing eggs were injected with carp pituitary extract (4 mg/kg) under [[Hormone-Induced Spawning]] protocols. Males were injected similarly.
- Eggs were stripped into a plastic bowl, stirred for 10 min, then water-hardened for 1 to 2 h before incubation in floating buckets and an instream hatching box.
- **Key methodological change:** [[Bentonite Clay]] (to prevent egg clumping) and sperm diluent were not used, because they were thought to have contributed to previous failures.
- Eggs were airlifted by helicopter to [[Bubbling Ponds Hatchery]] after 1 to 3 days on site.

### Egg outcomes at hatchery

- About 500 eggs were damaged or broken on arrival.
- About 200 eggs covered with silt died.
- About 1,000 eggs were killed by fungus.
- About 1,100 eggs hatched, mostly on day 6 to day 7 post-fertilization, with completion by day 8.
- Incubation water was held near 18.6 °C, pH 7.6, and conductivity about 365 µS. Dissolved oxygen ranged from 4.3 to 7.3 mg/L.

### Temperature experiments on early larvae

- Incubation at 18.6 °C, then acclimation to 20 °C, preceded experiments.
- **5 to 7 day humpback chub larvae** moved from 20 °C to 10 °C entered cold coma: they lost equilibrium and immobility for about 90 min, then regained swimming over 60 min but stayed lethargic. Heart rate fell from 100 to 120 beats/min at 20 °C to 30 to 40 beats/min at 10 °C.
- 5 to 7 day larvae moved to 12 °C became lethargic but kept position in the water column. Moves to 14 °C produced no behavioral effects.
- **11 to 13 day larvae** moved to 10 °C also entered cold coma but recovered normal behavior within minutes. Other treatments showed no obvious adverse effects.
- No mortality occurred in any treatment during the 4 h observation periods.

### Growth experiments

- **6 to 8 day humpback chub** reared 30 d at 10 °C gained about 28% in weight; fish appeared emaciated because weight gain lagged length gain. Length and weight gains at 14 °C and 20 °C were reported as higher; several values are garbled in the scan. Growth differences among all three temperatures were reported as significant.
- **13 to 15 day humpback chub:** similar pattern, but differences in length and weight between 10 °C and 14 °C were not significant. Authors suggest older fish were less affected by the coldest temperature.
- **39 to 41 day (juvenile) humpback chub** over 93 d: at 10 °C, length gain was about 24% and weight gain about 71%. At 14 °C, length gain was about 67% and weight gain about 286%. At 20 °C, length gain was about 148% and weight gain about 1,236%.
- **14 day Colorado squawfish larvae** over 92 d: final length at 20 °C was about 263% greater than initial length, about 100% at 14 °C, and about 17% at 10 °C. Growth trends matched the humpback chub results.
- A costiasis (parasite) outbreak caused static growth between days 42 and 59 in the squawfish experiment.

### Discussion and conclusions

- Earlier failures were attributed most plausibly to bentonite clay, which may have interfered with fertilization or egg adhesion. This could not be tested in 1993 because so few eggs were collected.
- The 1993 field effort yielded roughly an order of magnitude fewer eggs than the 1992 effort despite similar fish numbers, which the authors attribute to logistical constraints.
- Fish entering the cold mainstem at about 10 °C would be expected to enter cold coma for 15 to 90 min, with possible predation, drift, and reduced foraging vulnerability (see [[Cold Shock]]).

### Management recommendations

- Reduce daily flow fluctuations from Glen Canyon Dam. This would increase stability and the potential for warming mainstem rearing habitat and backwaters.
- Mimic the historic spring-time discharge pattern of the Colorado River to create warm, productive refugia for rearing native fish.
- Consider a [[Temperature Tolerance]]-informed flow regime that limits cold-water exposure of early life stages.
