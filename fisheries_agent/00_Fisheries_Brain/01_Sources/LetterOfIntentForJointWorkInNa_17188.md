## Executive Summary

This 1997 Letter of Intent formalizes cooperative conservation efforts between the United States (Department of the Interior, DOI) and Mexico (Secretariat of Environment, Natural Resources and Fisheries, SEMARNAP) for jointly managing contiguous natural protected areas along the U.S.–Mexico border. The agreement builds on prior binational frameworks, including the 1983 [[Border Environmental Cooperation Agreement]], a 1988 memorandum on protected natural and cultural heritage sites, a 1995 memorandum on scientific/biological data exchange, and the 1996 [[Trilateral Committee for Wildlife and Ecosystem Conservation and Management]] (Mexico, Canada, U.S.). It designates two binational pilot regions for cooperative management and outlines shared priorities including scientific research, species monitoring, environmental education, emergency response coordination, and joint enforcement against illegal resource exploitation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While no species-level ecological data is provided, the agreement identifies specific **transboundary ecosystems and protected habitat complexes** relevant to future biological research and conservation, including:

**Pilot Region A — Sonoran Desert / Colorado River Delta Complex:**
- [[Alto Golfo de California y Delta del Río Colorado Biosphere Reserve]] (Baja California & Sonora, Mexico)
- [[El Pinacate y Gran Desierto de Altar Biosphere Reserve]] (Sonora, Mexico)
- [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] (Arizona, USA)
- [[Cabeza Prieta National Wildlife Refuge]] (Arizona, USA)
- [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] (California/Arizona, USA)

This region is ecologically significant for its proximity to the **Gulf of California** and the **Colorado River Delta**, a habitat historically important for estuarine and freshwater fish populations (e.g., historic range of the endangered [[Totoaba]] and native Colorado River fishes), though the document itself does not discuss aquatic species directly.

**Pilot Region B — Chihuahuan Desert / Rio Grande Complex:**
- [[Maderas del Carmen Wildlife Protection Area]] (Coahuila, Mexico)
- [[Cañón de Santa Elena Wildlife Protection Area]] (Chihuahua, Mexico)
- [[Big Bend National Park]] (Texas, USA)

This region borders the [[Rio Grande]]/[[Río Bravo]] riparian corridor, a system of ichthyological relevance for native and endemic fish species monitoring, though again not detailed in this policy text.

## Key Findings

- The agreement establishes a **binational governance framework**, not a research dataset — its primary output is administrative and diplomatic, intended to guide future joint conservation and scientific activity rather than report findings.
- Designates **two pilot binational conservation corridors** spanning desert biosphere reserves and national parks/refuges on both sides of the border.
- Explicitly calls for **expansion of scientific knowledge** of protected areas through joint research, shared information systems, and **species monitoring** — implying future biological/ichthyological data collection under this framework, but none is presented here.
- Establishes cooperative mechanisms for:
  - Harmonizing conservation policy between the two nations
  - Personnel and technical knowledge exchange
  - Environmental education and community engagement
  - Joint emergency response networks (e.g., wildfire coordination)
  - Coordinated inspection/surveillance to curb illegal exploitation of natural resources
- Activities under this Letter of Intent are to be reported to the **Border XXI Program's Natural Resources Workgroup**, linking it to broader binational environmental governance structures.
- Legally, cooperation remains **subordinate to each nation's domestic laws and regulations**, and the agreement carries no formal treaty status.
