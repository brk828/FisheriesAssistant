# Observations on Woundfin Spawning and Growth in an Outdoor Experimental Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Creger and Deacon tested how [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), an endangered minnow, responded to different substrates and current speeds in an outdoor concrete stream at the [[University of Nevada Las Vegas]]. Woundfin spawned over rock substrate in a 0.6 to 0.9 m/sec current at 10 cm depth. The fish grew to about 55 to 60 mm TL within 5 months. The authors framed the work as a way to define spawning requirements for reintroduction and for assessing the impacts of water development on the [[Virgin River]].

## Background & Description

The woundfin is an endangered cyprinid. At the time of the study it occurred only in the Virgin River of Nevada, Arizona, and Utah, part of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Attempts to reestablish the species in other parts of its original range in the Virgin River Basin of Arizona had been unsuccessful. The authors note that almost 70 percent of the original woundfin habitat in the Virgin River had become unusable for part of the year because of [[Irrigation Diversion]] (Deacon 1979). Planned water projects that would alter present river flows were identified as further threats.

Knowing the substrate and current needs of spawning fish was described as essential for three purposes: predicting environmental impacts of proposed development, selecting suitable areas within the former range for [[Reintroduction]], and supporting captive propagation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental stream design.** The facility had two concrete raceways. The narrow upper raceway was about 7 m long and 45 cm wide. The wider lower raceway was about 7 m long and 90 cm wide. Both were linked to two end pools. A 115-volt centrifugal pump drew water from the west pool through a rock filter and delivered it to the top of the upper raceway. Water then flowed through the lower raceway back to the west pool. Current speed was measured with a [[Marsh McBirney Current Meter]], a hand-held paramagnetic instrument. The lower raceway's current was too slow for the study.

**Substrate treatments.** Four substrate types were placed in distinct segments of both raceways:
- Large rock, 15 to 25 cm diameter
- Rock, 5 to 10 cm diameter
- Gravel, 2 to 3 cm
- Sand

**Water quality.** No filters removed waste products. Well water was added daily to replace seepage and evaporation. The authors judged that this, together with the stocking density, was sufficient to prevent the buildup of ammonia and other harmful substances.

**Fish source.** Approximately 50 adult woundfin were collected on 26 April 1980 from the Virgin River near the confluence of [[Beaver Dam Wash]] at Littlefield, Arizona. They were transported to the stream facility and placed in it on arrival. Many were robust and in prime reproductive condition.

## Key Findings

- **Spawning conditions:** Spawning occurred on rock substrate in currents of 0.6 to 0.9 m/sec at a depth of 10 cm. Spawning groups numbered about 15 to 20 fish, and eggs were deposited under these conditions. Spawning occurred at irregular intervals, and the authors report that fish spawned at different times across the study.
- **Growth:** Fish spawned under these conditions grew to approximately 55 to 60 mm TL in 5 months. The authors also compared their results with a large length-frequency data base for wild woundfin in the Virgin River. The paper reports weekly growth increments, but the OCR of these figures is too degraded to quote them reliably.
- **Fry and survival:** Fry appeared to be in good condition. Mortality during the earlier part of the study is reported, but the scanned figures are partly illegible. Fry fed on small invertebrates, including [[Chydoridae]] crustaceans.
- **Habitat implications:** Current velocity and substrate size, specifically rock in fast flow, are the key spawning requirements. The authors present them as criteria for site selection and for evaluating development impacts.
- **Conservation context:** Failed reintroduction attempts, habitat loss from diversions, and planned flow alterations together make [[Endangered Species]] management dependent on understanding these requirements.
