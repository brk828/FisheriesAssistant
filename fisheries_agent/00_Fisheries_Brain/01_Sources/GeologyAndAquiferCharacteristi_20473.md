# Geology and Aquifer Characteristics of the Inner-Valley Alluvium in the Safford Valley, Arizona

#hydrogeology #groundwater #aquifer-geology

## Executive Summary

This Department of Geology, University of Arizona paper by J. T. Hollander examines the stratigraphy, structure, and boundary conditions of the [[Safford Valley]] alluvium along the [[Gila River]] in Graham County, Arizona. The author argues that the water-bearing behavior of the [[Inner-Valley Alluvium]] and the older underlying fill can be understood only once the geologic framework is established. The paper does not address fish, invertebrates, or other aquatic biota. Its subject is groundwater geology.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** A stretch of the Gila River in Graham County. The eastern, northern, and southwestern boundaries are the Peloncillo, Gila, and Graham (Pinaleno) and Santa Teresa mountains. The northwestern boundary lies near the eastern edge of the [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]], and the southern boundary lies between Townships 10 and 11 South, including the Cactus Flats-Artesia area.
- **Structural setting:** The valley is a northwest-trending structural trough, depressed relative to the bordering mountains. Well logs suggest the surrounding uplands supplied much of the fill.
- **Older fill ("lake beds"):** Test wells, including one drilled to 3,767 feet without reaching bedrock, show that the older fill is only partly lacustrine or playa in origin. The author states that "lake beds" is an improper blanket term because the older alluvium also contains considerable fluviatile material.
- **Recent fill:** Fluviatile in origin, typically about 20 to 110 feet thick, and composed mainly of unconsolidated silt, sand, gravel, cobbles, and boulders.
- **Prior work:** The paper draws on [[Knechtel 1938]] and the regional summary by [[Halpenny et Al. 1952]], both produced by the [[United States Geological Survey]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Groundwater occurrence.** Water in the Recent alluvium is mostly unconfined and sits at shallow depths, often about 25 feet below the land surface. Many wells encounter water in more than one zone. The water-bearing sediments are sand and gravel overlying lacustrine-type clay.

**Recharge pathways.**
- Natural: the Gila River, precipitation over the valley floor, and seepage from the older alluvium.
- Artificial: seepage from irrigation canals and from cultivated land irrigated by spreading.

**Discharge pathways.**
- Natural: evaporation, transpiration, and seepage to the Gila River.
- Artificial: pumping, which accounts for large withdrawals from the subsurface reservoir.

Groundwater pumped from the inner-valley fill is the main source for irrigation and supplements surface supplies, so annual pumping varies. The paper calls groundwater "of utmost importance to the economy of the Safford Valley."

## Key Findings

1. **Stratigraphic sequence:** Most wells show, from top to bottom, topsoil or fill, sand or sand and gravel, gravel (with or without boulders), and clay. Clay also occurs at intermediate depths in varying thickness. Drilling was usually stopped on encountering clay, assumed to be impermeable lake clay.
2. **Contact geometry:** The basal contact between inner-valley fill and older clays is irregular, probably from scour and fill, but it dips in the same direction as the present Gila River gradient. The marginal contact is locally abrupt and steeply inclined.
3. **Recharge from the older alluvium:** Seepage across the marginal contacts is probably greatest along the valley sides. The reasons given are the impervious fine materials beneath the basal contact, greater horizontal permeability, and the larger contact area along the side walls.
4. **Aquifer continuity:** Contrary to the conventional model for arid and semi-arid valley fills (lenticular, discontinuous sand and gravel beds), the well logs suggest greater bedding regularity and laterally continuous coarse layers. The author also notes an absence of coarse clastics along basin margins, which suggests recharge from the uplands may be insignificant.
5. **Sediment provenance:** Coarse basal material probably came from local terrace gravel and conglomerate and the older alluvium. Fine material (sand, silt, clay) was deposited later as the upper fill. The local share of fine sediment is unknown, but it is likely "not insignificant."
6. **Methodological plan:** The author intends to combine the geologic framework with existing hydrologic data. Proposed methods include specific capacities, empirical permeability formulae, pumping tests, and flow-net analysis using analog electrical models.
