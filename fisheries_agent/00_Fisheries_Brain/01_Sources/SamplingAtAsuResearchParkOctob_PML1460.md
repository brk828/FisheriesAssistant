# Fish Sampling at ASU Research Park, October 1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

A memorandum from the [[Arizona State University]] Center for Environmental Studies, dated 25 October 1988, reports fish sampling of the ponds at the [[Arizona State University Research Park]] on 10–12 October 1988. Overnight [[Trammel Net]] sets captured 51 individuals of seven taxa, dominated by non-native species. Two native species, [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]], were present, though the razorback sucker showed poor body condition and a reduced catch rate. Daytime [[Seining]] yielded ten non-native taxa and no natives. The author attributes declines in larger fish to high summer water temperatures and expects the surviving native adults to spawn during the 1989 season.

## Background & Description

The memo was written by Paul C. Marsh and circulated to a distribution list of agencies and researchers, including Arizona Game and Fish Department (AZGFD), CADFC, ASU Research Park staff, [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service offices in Albuquergue and Phoenix. Sampling was intended to assess stocked native fishes in the ponds. The scan is partly degraded, so some numbers are approximate or incomplete, as noted below.

Methods:
- **Trammel nets:** deployed for 12 hours (overnight).
- **Seining:** conducted during midday.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water temperature:** Water near 35 °C was measured in the south lake in August. The author considered this heat the likely cause of stress and mortality in native fish. A dozen dead razorback suckers were found near shore, and the author concluded that conditions had exceeded the tolerance of at least this species.
- **Pond variation:** Species composition, relative abundance and total catch varied substantially among ponds, which the author describes as typical of past samples.
- **Non-native predators:** Abundant non-native fishes occupy the lakes, and their interactions with native larvae were identified as a future research focus.
- **Population trend:** The October catch was among the lowest recorded since autumn 1985, which the author interprets as depletion of larger fish.

## Key Findings

**Trammel net catch (51 fish, seven taxa)**
- Catch was dominated by [[Redbelly Tilapia]], followed by [[Bluegill]] and razorback sucker. [[Gila Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] together made up a smaller share.
- A single bonytail was captured: 344 mm total length, 284 mm standard length, about 25 g. Sex could not be determined with certainty but appeared male. The fish was moderately colored and in good condition, with slight fungal growth on the dorsum and several lesions at the dorsal fin base.
- Eight razorback suckers were captured, all apparently male:
  - Total length: 408–593 mm (mean 451 mm).
  - Weight: 584–1,200 g (mean 849 g).
  - Condition: poor (emaciated), attributed to summer heat stress.
- The razorback catch rate was relatively low compared with previous autumn samples. The author considers this likely to reflect mortality, though enough adults appear to remain to reproduce.

**Seining (ten non-native taxa, no natives)**
- **North lake:** Several thousand specimens (the exact count is partly illegible in the scan), with a catch per unit effort (CPE) of roughly 51 fish per haul. Dominant taxa were red shiner, bluegill and [[Mosquitofish]].
- **Middle lake:** 262 fish from six taxa, CPE 5.7. Redbelly tilapia, bluegill and tilapia hybrids predominated.
- **South pond:** The fewest fish, with a CPE of 0.8 and five species. Bluegill was most abundant (about 31%), followed by largemouth bass (about 25%), [[Threadfin Shad]] and green sunfish (about 19% each), and redbelly tilapia (about 5%).

**Conclusions and plans**
- Larger fish appear to have declined in the ASU Research Park lakes, probably due to high summer water temperatures.
- Native bonytail and razorback sucker persist in reduced numbers, and enough stock should remain to reproduce during the 1989 spawning season.
- Larval native fish are expected to appear in winter–spring 1989. Efforts will follow to examine their relationships with abundant non-native predators.
- The next scheduled sampling, excluding larval work, is planned for spring 1989.
