# Some Effects of X-Irradiation of Different Embryonic Stages of the Trout (*Salmo gairdnerii*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Welander (Applied Fisheries Laboratory, University of Washington) reports a preliminary study of how [[X-Ray Irradiation]] affects six developmental stages of [[Rainbow Trout]] embryos, from the one-cell stage to the pre-hatching eyed stage. Eggs were exposed to graded doses (roughly 25 to 2570 r) and followed through hatching and first feeding, with mortality, growth, pigmentation, fin-ray counts and superficial abnormalities recorded. The one-cell (blastodisc) stage was the most radiosensitive, with a [[Median Lethal Dose]] (LD-50) of 58 r at yolk absorption. The other stages had LD-50 values between roughly 300 and 900 r. Sensitivity did not fall in a simple way with embryonic age. Hatching timing was unaffected. The author concludes that X-rays could be a useful tool for locating [[Critical Periods]] in fish development, particularly for meristic and pigment characters used to separate races and species.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The study sought the developmental periods in which radiation does the most damage to a salmonid embryo, and whether radiosensitivity changes across the critical hatching and feeding transitions. Because hard ionizing radiation penetrates the whole organism, selective responses of structures were expected to reveal intrinsic patterns of embryonic sensitivity.

**Literature context.** Earlier work on mammals and amphibians had found a "critical period" of heightened sensitivity. Fish embryology had received little attention. Prior X-ray work on salmonids covered gametes (Foster et al., 1949), eyed stages (Welander et al., 1948), fingerlings (Bonham et al., 1948) and adults (Welander et al., 1950). The author cites Corbella (1930) as the only earlier study of X-irradiated trout embryos.

**Why salmonid embryos.** The author lists several advantages: a long developmental period (about 50 days), a semi-transparent egg that allows staging, large eggs (5 to 9 mm), 3000 to 7000 eggs per female, standard hatchery rearing methods, and a good basic embryological literature.

**Materials.**
- Progeny of four females and eight males, from one generation of a selectively bred stock at the University of Washington School of Fisheries. Two males were used per female to ensure fertilization.
- A total of 4,011 eggs were irradiated. Each lot contained roughly 35 to 100 eggs.
- Irradiation used 200 kvp X-rays from a Coolidge tube, with 1.00 mm aluminum and 0.25 mm copper filters at 20 mA. Output at 40 cm was about 133 r per minute, measured in air.
- Eggs were transferred under water to petri dishes for exposure, which lasted 0.55 to 7.0 minutes. Controls were handled in the same way without irradiation.
- Water temperature averaged 57.7 °F (range 50.5 to 66.0 °F). Development to hatching took about 532 to 571 temperature units (t.u.), depending on the female. Stage timing followed the [[Temperature Units]] method of Wallich (1901).

**Six irradiated stages (Figures 1 to 6).**

| Stage | Approximate age after fertilization |
|---|---|
| One-cell (blastodisc) | 2 h |
| 32-cell | 18 h |
| Early germ ring (~1/8 epiboly) | 113 h |
| Late germ ring (~2/3 epiboly) | 148 h |
| Early eye | 360 h |
| Late eye (pre-hatching) | 479 h |

Post-irradiation, larvae were held in 24 compartments in a hatchery trough until yolk absorption and first feeding. The experiment ended at 54 days (about 1400 t.u.), when survivors were weighed, measured and preserved.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Developmental timing.** Temperature units were used to map stages onto time. Hatching came at roughly 532 to 571 t.u. Mortality peaks and feeding onset were then placed on the same scale.

**Environmental factors compared.** The author compares X-ray effects with those of other agents on fish embryos:
- Temperature, light and atmospheric pressure, which tend to retard growth.
- Sublethal temperatures, asphyxiants, KCN, vibration, concussion and electric shock, which produce mortality peaks mainly during blastopore closure (Vernidub, 1939; Hata, 1927; Scheminzky, 1922; Rollefsen, 1932).
- Salinity and temperature combinations, which alter fin-ray numbers in sticklebacks (Heuts, 1949).

**Handling damage.** Salmonid eggs are very susceptible to physical damage at certain stages. Handling caused mortality in nearly all lots of the early eye stage, which the author treats as a confound. Germ ring stages were especially vulnerable to handling injury.

**Comparative radiosensitivity.** Early cleavage LD-50 doses in this cold-blooded vertebrate were "surprisingly low" and comparable to doses affecting mammals in similar periods (Russell and Russell, 1954).

## Key Findings

1. **Radiosensitivity by stage.** The one-cell (blastodisc) stage was the most radiosensitive. Its LD-50 at yolk absorption was 58 r, and the author estimates it was five to fifteen times as radiosensitive as the other five stages. The other stages had LD-50 values of roughly 300 to 900 r. The early germ ring stage was the least sensitive overall. Sensitivity did not decline steadily with age.

2. **Periodic mortality.** Cumulative mortality showed four peaks:
   - about day 8 (~160 t.u.), during late cleavage;
   - about day 22 (~475 t.u.), during eye, brain and caudal development;
   - about day 31 (~709 t.u.), a rapid rise in early larval stages;
   - about day 50 (~1277 t.u.), a slight rise during first active feeding.

   Heavier doses brought these peaks forward. The author suggests irradiation may damage precursors of vital structures that are not needed until a later critical stage.

3. **Hatching not affected.** Hatching time and the duration of the hatching period were unchanged by the doses used. Only high doses in the one-cell, 32-cell, and early and late germ ring stages prevented hatching. Large numbers hatched after 2400 r in eyed stages. The author tentatively concludes the [[Hatching Enzyme]] was not affected.

4. **Growth retardation.** Growth in length was retarded after 38 r in the early eye stage, when the caudal region grows rapidly. Other stages needed about 200 r or more to show an effect, and 400 r inhibited growth after irradiation at the 32-cell stage. One-cell survivors showed no growth effect.

5. **Body proportions.** Irradiated embryos tended to keep juvenile proportions, with relatively larger heads and eyes. This was most marked in the 32-cell, late germ ring and early eye stages. A proportionately larger eye was significant after 150 r in the early eye stage.

6. **Pigmentation (parr marks).** Parr-mark number was affected at every stage. Doses as low as 25 r significantly reduced it in the 32-cell and early germ ring stages (P < .04). Other stages were affected at 50 to 300 r. Parr marks were the most generally sensitive character in the study, though trends were inconsistent in places.

7. **Fin rays.** Dorsal fin rays were reduced after 100, 200 and 400 r at the 32-cell stage, and after 75 to 600 r in eyed stages. Anal fin rays were reduced in several stages, with effects from 50 r in the early germ ring stage. Fin-ray reduction usually accompanied retarded development.

8. **Abnormalities.** Common anomalies in both controls and irradiated fish included microphthalmia, abdominal and caudal foreshortening, and abdominal lordosis. Irradiation at the 32-cell stage with 200 and 400 r produced a distinctive loss or splitting of the dorsal and adipose fins. These fins are disturbed long before their primordia form, which the author interprets as damage to precursors. Abnormality frequency rose with dose in the one-cell, 32-cell and early eye stages.

9. **Comparisons with other agents.** Irradiation resembles temperature, light and other factors in retarding development and altering meristic counts. It differs in that X-rays reduce fin-ray and vertebra numbers, whereas temperature and light tend to reduce them while accelerating development. The author concludes that X-rays act on dividing cells differently from these factors.

10. **Comparisons with other studies.** Fundulus embryos (Solberg, 1938) show a similar early cleavage sensitivity, though with less agreement on peaks. Corbella (1930) reported effects at 97 r and appreciable trout mortality at 440 r. Chinook salmon eyed stages (Welander et al., 1948) showed effects similar to these trout.

11. **Coefficient of divergence.** A multivariate divergence coefficient (Clark, 1952) combining standard length, head and eye proportions, parr marks and fin rays rose directly with dose. The pre-hatching stage generally had lower divergence than the others. The early germ ring stage at 400 r was also low and was judged an outlier.

12. **Mechanism.** The author proposes that low-dose effects on sensitivity reflect the metabolic rate or differentiation of dividing cells. Vernidub's (1939) oxygen-consumption and glycolysis data suggest metabolic rates are involved in critical periods. The author concludes that irradiation could be a useful tool for analyzing the developmental basis of [[Meristic Characters]], such as fin rays and colour, which are used to distinguish races, subspecies and species.

13. **Limitations.** The author describes this as a preliminary study. Data on several stages are sparse, and some trends are inconsistent, with possible stimulation by low doses. Further experiments with low doses are needed.

**Summary of figures in the text:** six embryonic stages, graded doses of about 25 to 2570 r, 4,011 eggs, 54-day experiment.
