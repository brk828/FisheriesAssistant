# Open the Floodgates: Restoring the Colorado River Through the Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This news feature reports on the 1996 experimental flood released from the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to rebuild sandbars and beaches in the [[Grand Canyon]], and on the second, redesigned flood planned after the first one largely failed. The 1996 flood was meant to redistribute sediment, but sediment supply ran short and the restored beaches eroded quickly. Researchers now plan floods timed to follow fresh sediment inputs, alongside a plan to protect the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] by removing non-native fish and disrupting [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] spawning. The article also covers the competing interests of the stakeholders in the [[Glen Canyon Adaptive Management Workgroup]].

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] flowed seasonally before the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in 1963. Spring snowmelt carried large sediment loads, scoured the channel and renewed the canyon's beaches. The dam now traps more than 90% of incoming sediment in Lake Powell, and it releases water from valves near its base. The resulting flows are steady and cold, and the river is clear.

The humpback chub (*Gila cypha*) evolved in the Colorado River basin over the past two million years and is found nowhere else. Its population has declined from an estimated 8,000 to around 2,000 over the past 20 years. Three native species have already disappeared from the canyon's waters.

The main actors are the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] (USGS) Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center, the [[National Park Service]], the [[Western Area Power Administration]], Native American tribes including the Hopi and Hualapai, and environmental groups such as the [[Grand Canyon Trust]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sediment:** Before 1963 the river carried an average of 57 million tonnes of sediment each year. Seasonal rains in late summer and autumn bring roughly 3 million tonnes per year down the [[Paria River]], which enters upstream of the canyon.
- **Temperature:** Pre-dam summer water reached as much as 32 °C. Water released from the dam now stays near 9 °C year round.
- **Habitat loss:** The loss of beaches and sandbars removed sheltered backwaters where chub fry once lived.
- **Predation and competition:** Trout populations have grown to 250,000 or more and act as a predatory threat. In a 1997 study, biologists found partially digested chub in the stomachs of trout and of non-native [[Channel Catfish]]. Some researchers suspect trout also eat chub eggs and fry.
- **Morphology:** The chub's prominent hump may once have helped it swim through turbulent flows, and its eyes shrank in the murky water. Today's clear, cold water has made it less competitive.

## Key Findings

**1. The 1996 flood largely failed.**
- The flood ran for seven days at 1,290 m³/s, starting 27 March 1996, through the bypass tubes of the Glen Canyon Dam.
- Sediment in floodwater declined rapidly each day, and the average grain size increased.
- Post-flood trenches showed coarse sand on top of silt, the reverse of the expected deposition pattern.
- Most restored beaches were built from sand scoured from the sandbars that formed their own foundations. This explains why they eroded so quickly.
- Analysis showed that the flood built most beaches within its first two days, and that it was eroding rather than building them by the end.
- Sediment carried in from tributaries is moved downstream within weeks or months, not stored for years as researchers had assumed. The earlier sediment budget had been calculated in error.
- The flood cost hydroelectric operators roughly $25 million in lost generation.

**2. A revised flood strategy is proposed.**
- Floods should be timed to follow fresh sediment input from the Paria River.
- A flood of about 60 hours, rather than seven days, is considered enough to build more resilient beaches.
- The revised plan calls for a flood of similar magnitude to 1996, lasting two and a half days.
- Because of a legal constraint on dam operations, the next flood cannot occur before 1 January. Low flows are planned in the meantime to retain sediment in the channel.
- Drought in 2002, one of the driest years on record, meant too little sediment entered the river to trigger the flood. The plans carry over to the next year.

**3. A two-pronged plan targets the humpback chub's predators.**
- Over two years, the USGS will remove non-native fish from a 16 km stretch of the river near chub spawning grounds, using electrofishing. Native fish will be released after recovery, and non-native fish will be killed with an anaesthetic overdose.
- Dam flows will vary during the trout spawning season, January to March, to leave trout eggs and fry stranded.
- Officials acknowledge that some adult trout will also be stranded. They argue the plan will benefit anglers by thinning crowded populations and producing larger trophy fish.

**4. Stakeholder tensions remain.**
- Hopi and Hualapai tribes object to killing the trout, which they consider ancestral. They are willing to consider a compromise in which the fish are used for pet food or fertilizer.
- Rafting guides and park managers want stable flows, while power customers need reliable supply.
- Environmental groups remain frustrated by slow progress. Litigation remains a possibility.
- The workgroup has so far avoided showstopping controversy, and its members agree that compromise is preferable to a court battle.
- Interior Secretary Gale Norton has the final decision on the next flood.
