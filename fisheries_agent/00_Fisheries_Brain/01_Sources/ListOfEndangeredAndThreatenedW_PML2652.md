# Endangered and Threatened Wildlife and Plants: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service List (50 CFR 17.11 and 17.12)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a regulatory reproduction of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's List of Endangered and Threatened Wildlife and Plants, codified at 50 CFR 17.11 (wildlife) and 17.12 (plants). It is taken from the *Environment Reporter* (Bureau of National Affairs), with a publication stamp of 1-4-85. The list reflects amendments published in the *Federal Register* from 1983 to 1984 under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It is an administrative list, not a research study, so it contains no sampling data, population estimates or ecological measurements. Its main value to an ichthyologist is that it identifies which fishes, mussels and snails hold federal protected status, and where.

## Background & Description

The list is organised into two subparts. Subpart B (§17.11) covers wildlife. Subpart C (§17.12) covers plants, and §17.13 is reserved for future amendments. Each entry gives the common name, scientific name, vertebrate population where endangered or threatened, historic range, status, "When Listed" footnote, critical habitat reference and special-rules reference.

Key definitions and conventions in the text:

- **Status symbols:** "E" for Endangered, "T" for Threatened, and "E [or T] (S/A)" for species treated as listed because of similarity of appearance.
- **Experimental populations:** "XE" (essential) and "XN" (nonessential).
- **"NA"** in the special-rules or critical-habitat columns means no special rules or critical habitat apply, though other parts of Title 50 may still apply.
- **Geographic populations:** Populations of the same subspecies or species are identified by their geographic boundaries, so a single species can appear more than once.
- **Scientific names:** The most recently accepted name is used, and synonyms are given in parentheses where confusion may arise. Common names may vary in local use and are not reliable for identifying specimens.
- **Taxonomic scope:** Listing a genus includes all lower taxa. The text gives [[Gibbons]] (genus *Hylobates*) as an example, listed Endangered throughout its range in China, India and Southeast Asia.
- **Historic range:** This column does not limit the application of prohibitions, which apply wherever the species is found.

The entries cover mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fishes, mussels, snails, insects and plants.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The list covers a wide range of habitats and regions. Aquatic and freshwater taxa are well represented, including:

- **Pupfishes and other fishes**, such as the [[Ash Meadows Amargosa Pupfish]] (Ash Meadows, Nevada), and a catfish with Yaqui and Arizona-related entries.
- **Pearly mussels (Unionidae)**, a large group of freshwater bivalves. Entries include pigtoes, pocketbooks, pearly mussels and "clubshell"-type species, with many occurring in the eastern United States.
- **Freshwater snails** and springsnails from the western United States.

Marine and coastal listings include sea turtles and marine mammals, and several entries are listed with ranges in the Pacific, Atlantic and Indian Oceans. Protected plants are mainly western and southeastern North American taxa, with some Hawaiian and Caribbean species. Historic ranges range from single localities to continental extents, and the text notes that present distributions "may be greatly reduced" from them.

Notable terrestrial entries mentioned in the text include the [[Bald Eagle]], listed as Threatened in five states (WA, OR, MN, WI and MI) in 1978. The [[Red Wolf]] (*Canis rufus*) is listed in the southeastern United States, and the [[Maned Wolf]] (*Chrysocyon brachyurus*) is listed across South America.

## Key Findings

- The list is a compilation of amendments published in the *Federal Register* between 1983 and 1984. It is current to the 1-4-85 Environment Reporter printing.
- Each entry carries a status (E, T or E [or T] (S/A)). Experimental populations are flagged as XE or XN.
- Listing decisions are made at the level of the geographic vertebrate population, so a species can be partly endangered and partly threatened across its range.
- Fishes and freshwater invertebrates are a significant share of the aquatic entries. Pearly mussels, pupfishes and catfishes stand out for their restricted ranges.
- Taxonomic listings cover all subordinate taxa. The bald eagle example shows that a species can be listed for only part of its range.
- Non-regulatory information (spelling, historic range, footnotes, synonyms and names) may be updated in annual revisions without public notice. Changing the species definition or its status requires the procedures of 50 CFR Part 424.
- Footnote references in the "When Listed" column point to the *Federal Register* publications that established each listing, many from 1975 to 1984.
- Special rules and critical habitat cross-references direct users to other parts of Title 50 (Parts 17, 217–227 and 402) and to port-of-entry or import/export requirements.
