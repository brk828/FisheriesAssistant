# Species Relationships Among Fishes of the Genus Gila in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic study examines the [[Gila Robusta]] complex of chubs (family [[Cyprinidae]]) in the [[Colorado River]] basin. The authors argue that three species coexist as reproductively isolated populations in the main channels of the upper Colorado and [[Green River]]: [[Gila Robusta]] (roundtail), [[Gila Cypha]] ([[Humpback Chub]]) and [[Gila Elegans]] ([[Bonytail]]). Using multivariate morphometrics and a new nuchal hump measurement, the authors develop field-usable identification keys. The work is framed around the management of endangered and threatened fishes, and it notes that taxonomic confusion had hindered sound policy.

## Background & Description

The chubs were historically confused because earlier studies relied on subjective traits. A prior analysis by Holden and Stalnaker used subjective codes for nuchal hump form and coded continuous measurements into discrete categories, which obscured intermediate morphology and produced spurious "intergrades."

The study's objectives were to:
1. Test the validity and relationships of the roundtail, bonytail and humpback chubs in big-river habitat.
2. Determine their relationships to other [[Gila]] populations in the middle and upper Colorado basin.
3. Identify characters useful for identifying young and adult fish.

The authors did not treat the [[Gila Seminuda]] group of the [[Gila River Basin]] in full, except to note that [[Gila Seminuda]] may be a separate evolutionary line secondarily related to [[Gila Robusta]].

Specimens came from the U.S. National Museum, Arizona State University, the Bell Museum of Natural History, and the Museum of Northern Arizona. Acknowledged funding came from the National Science Foundation and the National Park Service.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** The three big-river species occupy the unmodified main channels of the Colorado and Green rivers. Differentiation is linked to ecological roles and habitat preferences within this complex environment.
- **Medium-sized tributaries:** Populations such as the [[Virgin River]] and [[San Juan River]] chubs show intermediate or mixed morphology. The authors interpret these as local adaptations rather than true intergrades, though limited introgression over thousands of years cannot be ruled out.
- **Reservoir disturbance:** Construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] destroyed main-channel habitat and created [[Lake Powell]]. The authors predict that the three species are breaking down locally there, and that a single generalized species may eventually result, though the reservoir appears to be silting too rapidly for stability.
- **Threats:** Dams destroy swift-water habitat above obstructions and alter temperature and turbidity downstream. Exotic competitors and predators are also threats, especially in these modified waters.
- **Feeding:** Members of the complex appear to be omnivores or carnivores, with specializations related to habitat and food-processing structures (jaws, teeth).

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- A [[Principal Components Analysis]] of 34 meristic and morphometric characters from 140 specimens separated the large-river fish into three distinct clusters.
- A separate population analysis used a nuchal hump ratio, measured to 0.1 mm, on 72 individuals. The ratio measures frontal depression relative to the distance between pelvic and dorsal fin insertions.
- The authors avoided circular logic by developing the population analysis and the key characters separately.

**Morphological Results**
- The first principal component reflected overall size. The second component was associated with vertebral counts, dorsal and anal fin rays, and [[Gill Rakers]]. The third component was uncorrelated with the second and related to fin-ray counts, gill rakers, pectoral length and eye diameter.
- The three species separated almost completely when the three components were combined.
- [[Gila Cypha]] and [[Gila Elegans]] separated completely, with only slight overlap between [[Gila Robusta]] and [[Gila Cypha]].
- One specimen (UMMZ 162818-related) had nine anal rays and grouped with [[Gila Robusta]], but it was distinct from all other specimens on nuchal hump development and was tentatively assigned to [[Gila Cypha]].

**Diagnostic Characters**
- [[Gill Rakers]] (Table 2) and [[Precaudal Vertebrae]] (Table 3) help distinguish the species. [[Gila Robusta]] has fewer gill rakers than the other species. Precaudal vertebrae range from 40 to 44, modally 42.
- Dorsal ray counts: [[Gila Robusta]] has nine; [[Gila Cypha]] usually has nine; [[Gila Elegans]] has ten dorsal and ten anal rays.
- [[Gila Seminuda]] tends to be intermediate on most counts but is closest to [[Gila Elegans]] in precaudal vertebrae, and separates from [[Gila Robusta]] on nuchal hump development.
- The nuchal hump ratio separates adults of the three species in the middle and upper Colorado basin.
- Characters such as caudal peduncle depth and snout position in relation to the mouth are also useful.

**Taxonomic Conclusions**
- The three main-channel populations are morphologically segregated and behave as reproductively isolated species.
- [[Gila Robusta]] is the most generalized of the three, and the other two species are extreme in several traits. This suggests that [[Gila Cypha]] and [[Gila Elegans]] may have evolved separately from [[Gila Robusta]].
- The authors refer to the entire complex as the [[Gila Robusta]] superspecies, including [[Gila Robusta]], [[Gila Cypha]], [[Gila Elegans]], and related forms such as [[Gila Seminuda]].

**Historical and Nomenclatural Notes**
- Earlier reports of [[Gila Elegans]] from the [[Little Colorado River]] above [[Grand Falls]] were based on a questionable specimen. The authors conclude that the type locality of [[Gila Elegans]] and [[Gila Robusta]] is most likely Grand Falls on the Little Colorado River, and that the original Zuni River attribution was an error.
- Erroneous claims about the classification, biology and distribution of Colorado River chubs spread after a 1962 poisoning event and led to misdirected management, including a claim that humpback chubs were available in Flaming Gorge Reservoir.

**Management Recommendations**
- Perpetuate the three species by protecting large unspoiled main-channel sections.
- Priority research is needed on habitat, food, reproduction, recruitment, and the population dynamics and genetics of the species.
- Use these species as models for understanding the ecology and evolution of big-river fishes.
