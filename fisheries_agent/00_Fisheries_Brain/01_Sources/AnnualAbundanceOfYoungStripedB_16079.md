# Annual Abundance of Young Striped Bass in the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game paper by [[Harold K. Chadwick]] reports annual tow-net surveys (1953–1962) of young-of-the-year [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*) in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]]. The study had two aims: to test whether an index of juvenile abundance could predict recruitment to the fishery three years later, and to identify environmental factors controlling spawning success. The author also tested whether [[Tow Net Sampling]] measured local abundance accurately. Juvenile abundance declined from a 1953–54 peak to a low in 1959, then rose to a ten-year high in 1962. Tow netting was judged reasonably accurate for 0.7–2.0 inch bass if all depths were sampled equally, but geographic and temporal biases limited the annual surveys. No environmental correlate of abundance was detected, though distribution appeared linked to salinity and runoff.

## Background & Description

Striped bass were the basis of California's largest estuarine sport fishery. Earlier methods, including [[Fyke Net]] sampling and plankton-net sampling of eggs and larvae, had important limitations. A 1947 exploratory survey found bass about one inch long throughout the lower river system, except in the Sacramento River above Isleton, which suggested that the abundance of one-inch bass, measured by tow net, was the best index of spawning success.

The Department ran more intensive surveys in 1948–1949 and 1951, then limited annual surveys from 1953. Three survey types were used:

- **Annual surveys:** a few fixed stations sampled over a short window timed to when bass reached a mean length of one inch at Antioch.
- **[[Delta-Wide Survey]]s:** about 30 stations sampled repeatedly across the nursery area.
- **Exploratory tow netting and [[Beach Seining]]:** used to define the species' range and the accuracy of local estimates.

Sampling used a skiff-mounted tow net (about 14 ft long, with a fine bobbinet cod end of roughly 0.1 in mesh) towed at about 2.7 ft/s. Vertical distribution was measured with three circular 3-ft nets fished simultaneously at 5, 15, and 25 ft.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area is an estuary dominated by tides, with flow reversal in summer. The Delta is essentially fresh, with salinities seldom above 1,000 ppm chlorides a few miles above the river confluence. Salinity rises sharply downstream and approaches 90% of seawater in [[San Pablo Bay]] in late summer. Waters are turbid, with Secchi readings seldom above one foot, except in the upper Sacramento River and the clearer bays west of Pittsburg.

Young bass were concentrated near shore and near the bottom in the western Delta, while eastern stations showed more even distribution. Bass tended to rise off the bottom on flood tides, most strongly at Station 12. Field data indicated that very young bass actively controlled their position, seeking shelter and low water velocity. Catches at the [[Tracy Fish Collection Facility]] were much higher at night than by day, supporting voluntary nocturnal movement.

Water temperature, salinity, runoff, and sky cover were measured against juvenile abundance. Peak spawning occurred at 60–67 °F. The author also noted that [[Pacific Sardine]] stock–recruitment work offered a possible model for bass.

## Key Findings

**Survey results and indices**
- Annual survey catches were highest in 1953–1954, lowest in 1957–1959, and reached a ten-year high in 1962. The annual survey index shows a fall from a 1953–54 peak to a 1959 low, then an increase through 1962.
- The 1959 annual survey was two weeks late because bass reached one inch a month earlier than usual that year.
- Delta-wide indices agreed in trend (1959–1962). Index B was judged superior. The author recommended Index D for future use because its stations cover the Delta better.
- The best estimate of relative abundance over the ten-year period combined the annual survey indices for 1953–1956 and 1957–1958 with Delta-wide Index B for 1959–1962.
- The annual survey showed about a 20-fold difference between 1959 and 1962, while the Delta-wide survey showed about a sixfold difference. The author attributed the gap mainly to timing biases.

**Accuracy of tow netting**
- Size selectivity: standard-net efficiency rose sharply from 0.5 to 0.7 inch and reached about 100% at roughly 0.8 inch. Vulnerability ended at about 3.5 inch. Bass over 2.0 inch were sampled inefficiently.
- Vertical distribution: diagonal (all-depth) tows caught up to ten times more bass than surface tows, so sampling all depths equally is essential.
- Horizontal distribution: shoreline concentrations of small bass biased estimates downward, by about 15% on average (range 6–37%).
- Tidal stage: no consistent bias was found.
- Water transparency: no correlation with catch was found at turbidities under 13 inches, but the author could not fully evaluate this effect.

**Distribution and environment**
- Relative distribution correlated with salinity and runoff. Bass were farther upstream in high-salinity, low-runoff years. The authors caution that this rests on few years.
- No significant correlation was found between juvenile abundance and water temperature, temperature fluctuation, runoff, salinity, sky cover, or spawning stock. The author considered the data inadequate for a firm conclusion.
- Angler success in the Delta showed a negative relationship with juvenile abundance (four years only, not judged significant).

**Recruitment and growth**
- Striped bass enter the fishery as 3-year-olds. Angler catch per hour for 3-year-old bass in April–May 1959–1962 (0.034, 0.033, 0.016, 0.016) paralleled juvenile abundance for most years, excluding 1958.
- Fall party-boat catches (0.24, 0.35, 0.23, 0.24, 0.20 per hour, 1957–1961) partly tracked juvenile trends three to four years earlier. The author regarded this as a tentative hypothesis.
- Two-week growth increments of 0.3–0.7 inch are slower than the 0.5–1.1 inch reported for the [[Patuxent River]]. Growth was greatest in 1959 and smallest in 1962, suggesting negative density dependence.
- Modes in length frequencies coincided on successive surveys, suggesting biweekly cycles in spawning or survival that may relate to tidal stage.

**Management implications**
- Tow-net indices of 0.7–2.0 inch bass were considered reasonably accurate. Surveys should sample all depths equally and use Delta-wide coverage, not a few annual stations.
- Water development in the Delta could affect recruitment, so the author suggested that these indices could guide modifications under the State Water Resources Development System.
