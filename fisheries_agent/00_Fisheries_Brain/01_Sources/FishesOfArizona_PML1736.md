# Fishes of Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This is the front matter, introduction and species-account section of *Fishes of Arizona* by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University), published 30 June 1973 by the Arizona Game and Fish Department. The book is a state-wide faunal treatment that combines an artificial family key, a glossary of morphological terms, field collection and preservation guidance, and detailed accounts of individual species with keys, distribution maps and natural-history notes. The author notes that Arizona's native fish fauna has been heavily altered: the original fauna was at best 9 or so families, while at least 22 families and more than four times as many species (compared with pre-European times) now occur in the state's waters, largely through introductions and the loss of native species.

## Background & Description

The book was written for two audiences: scientists and non-technical sportsmen, students and amateur naturalists. It is based on a decade-long survey (the "past 10-year survey of the fishes of Arizona") and on collections from the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, Arizona State University, Arizona Game and Fish Department, and federal and state agencies. Distribution maps (Maps 3-42) plot pre-1900, 1901-1940, 1941-1960 and 1961-1972 records, distinguishing extant populations from historical localities and introduced populations.

Major sections:

- **Introduction: Habitats of Arizona Fishes** - describes drainage basins ([[Colorado River]], [[Gila River]], [[Little Colorado River]], [[Bill Williams River]]), closed basins, springs, reservoirs and canals.
- **Identification and Practical Aspects** - discusses common vs. scientific names (following the American Fisheries Society list), the classification hierarchy, the species concept, and difficulties with hybrids and undescribed forms.
- **Collection and Preservation** - covers permits, gear (seines, electrofishing, gill nets, traps), formalin fixation at about 10 percent, and labelling and field-note requirements.
- **Glossary** - defines terms for counting and measuring fin rays, scales, lateral line, and skeletal structures.
- **Artificial Key to Families** of inland United States bony fishes.
- **Accounts of Species** - keys and detailed entries for each family.
- **Hybrid Fishes, References, Index, Addendum.**

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arizona is highly diverse climatically and topographically, from low desert to high conifer forest. Water development has transformed fish habitats: the lower [[Colorado River]] was converted from braided, marshy channels into a sluice-way or canal, with native fishes largely lost there and declining upstream. Dams stabilize flows, colder summer releases and clearer water change conditions for native fauna, and reservoirs such as [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Mohave]] now dominate standing-water habitat. Small reservoirs, stock tanks and artificial canals provide new habitat largely occupied by introduced species.

Overgrazing by livestock in the 1870s-1890s affected streams and riparian vegetation. High-elevation streams remain the least disturbed and support native trouts. Dry or intermittent streams are common, and native fishes persist in springs, cienegas and isolated pools.

The author documents introductions of many species, including [[Threadfin Shad]] (introduced to western United States from Tennessee in 1953 into Lake Mead), [[Carp]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Coho Salmon]], [[White Sturgeon]] (introduced into Lake Havasu in 1967), [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Tilapia]]. Introduced species frequently compete with or prey on native fishes, and hybridization threatens native trout and chub populations.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna size and change:** Indigenous peoples had "scarcely more than 25 kinds" of freshwater fishes available; at the time of writing more than four times that many species occurred in the state's waters, largely through introductions. Several native species had been destroyed.
- **Families:** At least 22 families occur in Arizona or contiguous waters; roughly 46 inland families are recorded from the United States. The original fauna included at best 9 families, three principally marine.
- **Species accounts:** The book treats more than 50 species in detail across families including Acipenseridae, Elopidae, Anguillidae, Clupeidae, Salmonidae, Esocidae, Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Percichthyidae, Centrarchidae, Percidae, Cichlidae, Cottidae and others.
- **Native fishes at risk:** The [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), once up to about 1.3 m and possibly 30 kg, is essentially extinct in Arizona, with the last adult in the Salt River reported in 1937 and the last Salt River specimens caught in 1958. The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was declining in the lower Colorado and extirpated from much of the Gila basin. The [[Spikedace]] and [[Woundfin]] have sharply reduced ranges, and the [[Little Colorado River Spinedace]] was "endangered" at the time of writing.
- **Trouts:** Two trouts are native to Arizona: the [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*) and the [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*, formerly *S. apache* / "Arizona trout" of the upper Little Colorado and Salt systems). Native trouts were genetically swamped by introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and were reduced to high-elevation refugia. Eight other salmonids are introduced; three are maintained only by stocking.
- **Taxonomic problems:** Chubs of the *Gila robusta* complex (roundtail chub subspecies *G. r. robusta*, *G. r. grahami*, *G. r. seminuda*), spinedaces, and suckers (*Catostomus*, *Pantosteus*) are difficult to delimit; hybridization is common. The author notes an estimated 10 percent of Western North American fishes were undescribed in 1959, and some Arizona forms appeared undescribed.
- **Collection guidance:** Specimens should be preserved in roughly 10 percent formalin (a 4 percent formaldehyde solution), not overcrowded, labelled with locality, habitat, date and collector, and eventually transferred to 40 percent isopropanol or 20 percent ethanol; color notes are stressed because preservation fades breeding colors.
- **Threadfin shad:** Introduced to provide forage for game fish; it spread through the lower Colorado and central Arizona impoundments, and population crashes occur when water temperatures drop below about 7-10 degrees C.
- **Golden shiners and red shiners:** These introduced minnows compete with and displace native species; golden shiners have caused management problems in Mogollon Rim trout waters, and red shiners have contributed substantially to declines of native minnows.
- **Scientific names:** The author follows the American Fisheries Society list (Bailey et al., 1970) for common names and emphasizes that scientific names give stability in communication.
