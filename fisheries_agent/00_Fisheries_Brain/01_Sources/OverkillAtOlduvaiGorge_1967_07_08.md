> **Note on scope:** This source is a paleontological/archaeological rebuttal letter concerning the "overkill hypothesis" of Pleistocene megafauna extinction in Africa. It contains **no fish, ichthyological, or aquatic ecosystem data**. The note below faithfully represents the actual content — a debate between P. S. Martin and L. S. B. Leakey over the cause of large mammal extinction at [[Olduvai Gorge]] — rather than forcing a fish-centric framing.

## Executive Summary

This 1967 *Nature* letter by P. S. Martin is a direct response to L. S. B. Leakey's critique of Martin's earlier "overkill" hypothesis, which proposes that the extinction of large-bodied mammals (>50 kg) in Late Pleistocene Africa was driven by prehistoric human hunting rather than climatic change. Martin argues that even after incorporating Leakey's proposed corrections to the extinct mammal list, the overall pattern remains essentially unchanged: a substantial spike in megafaunal extinction coincides with the appearance of Acheulean hand-axe hunters at [[Olduvai Gorge]]. Martin rejects Leakey's drought-based explanation, citing modern desert-dwelling megafauna near Lake Rudolph as evidence against climate as the primary extinction driver, and reasserts overkill by early humans as the "least improbable hypothesis."

## Background & Description

- **Core debate:** Whether the extinction of large African mammals at the end of the Middle Pleistocene was caused by:
  1. **Prehistoric human overkill** (Martin's hypothesis), or
  2. **Catastrophic drought/climatic change** (Leakey's position).
- **Key site:** [[Olduvai Gorge]], described as the richest single stratified fossil deposit representing this time interval in East Africa.
- **Stratigraphic framework:**
  - **Upper Villafrancian (Oldowan Culture)** — Bed I: 5 extinct genera last recorded here.
  - **Bed II (Lower)** — associated with Chellean tools: 6 extinct genera, including Deinotherium.
  - **Bed II (Upper, Chellean)**: taxa such as Mesochoerus, Orthostonyx, Pultiphagonides, Phenacotragus, Pelorovis, and an unidentified machairodont (saber-toothed cat).
  - **Bed IV (Acheulean)** — late Middle Pleistocene hand-axe culture: 10 extinct genera surviving to this level, including Simopithecus, Stylohipparion, Notochoerus, Tapinochoerus, Afrochoerus, Libytherium, Thaleroceros, Parmularius, Xenocephalus, and Bularchus.
- **Taxonomic groups represented** among the 21 extinct large-mammal genera (>50 kg body mass):
  - [[Chalicotheriidae]] (Ancylotherium)
  - [[Suidae]] (multiple genera: Ectopotamochoerus, Promesochoerus, Pronotochoerus, Mesochoerus, Notochoerus, Tapinochoerus, Afrochoerus)
  - [[Deinotheriidae]] (Deinotherium)
  - [[Cercopithecidae]] (Simopithecus)
  - [[Equidae]] (Stylohipparion)
  - [[Giraffidae]] (Libytherium)
  - [[Bovidae]] (Pelorovis, Phenacotragus, Thaleroceros, Parmularius, Xenocephalus, Bularchus, Pultiphagonides)
  - [[Machairodontinae]] (unidentified saber-toothed cat genus)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Martin disputes Leakey's drought hypothesis by pointing to **modern ecological analogues**: elephant, hippopotamus, black rhino, giraffe, and buffalo historically occupied the desertic shores of **Lake Rudolph** (<250 mm annual rainfall), one of the driest regions of equatorial Africa — arguing this undermines drought as a sufficient explanation for megafaunal die-off.
- Leakey's own prior writings (quoted in the letter) caution against inferring paleoclimate or habitat from fossil megafauna, since living large mammals (e.g., zebra, giraffe) occupy highly variable habitats from open savannah to tropical forest margins near **Lake Manyara**.
- Martin highlights the **absence of comparable extinction events elsewhere** (e.g., Madagascar) during the same drought period, arguing that if drought were the cause, its effects should not be uniquely confined to mainland Africa.
- The general argument situates megafaunal extinction within a broader **global pattern**: extinction waves are proposed to coincide with the arrival or cultural development of stone-age human hunters on a given landmass, rather than with regional climatic events.

## Key Findings

- Incorporating all of Leakey's proposed revisions to the African extinct mammal list produces only **trivial net changes**:
  - Living large genera: 40 → 41
  - Later Pleistocene extinct genera: >26 → >29
  - Earlier Pleistocene extinct genera: 19 → 18
  - Overall later Pleistocene megafaunal extinction rate: 39% → 41%
- At Olduvai Gorge specifically, **21 extinct large-mammal genera (>50 kg)** are documented:
  - 5 last recorded in the Upper Villafrancian (Bed I)
  - 6 in the Early Middle Pleistocene (Chellean-associated, Bed II)
  - 10 surviving into Bed IV (Acheulean, late Middle Pleistocene)
- At least **4 additional Bed II genera** (Mesochoerus, Orthostonyx, Phenacotragus, and an unnamed machairodont) may have persisted as contemporaries of the Bed IV hand-axe fauna based on Acheulean associations documented elsewhere — suggesting the true extinction signal coinciding with Acheulean hunters may be even larger than currently recorded.
- **Generic extinction during the time of Acheulean hand-axe hunters "far outstrips" extinction during any earlier interval** in the known African Pleistocene record.
- Martin concludes that the coincidence of major Late Pleistocene megafaunal extinction with the emergence of big-game-hunting cultures — at Olduvai and globally — supports **overkill by prehistoric humans** as the most parsimonious explanation, pending discovery of a counterexample (a landmass with major megafaunal extinction unrelated to human arrival).
