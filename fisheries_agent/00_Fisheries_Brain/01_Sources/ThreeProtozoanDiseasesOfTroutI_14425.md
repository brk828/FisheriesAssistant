# Three Protozoan Diseases of Trout in California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #parasitology #salmonidae #microsporidia #myxosporidia #protozoa #fish-pathology

## Executive Summary

This 1955 California Department of Fish and Game report by [[J. H. Wales]] and [[H. Wolf]] documents three distinct protozoan pathogens affecting trout and salmon populations in California hatcheries and wild waters, observed over a nine-year study period. The diseases—caused by [[Cryptobia Borreli]] (a hemoflagellate), an undescribed [[Plistophora]] sp. (a microsporidian), and [[Ceratomyxa Shasta]] (a myxosporidian)—represent significant threats to both hatchery-reared and wild [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) and related [[Salmonidae]] species. Notably, the *Plistophora* infection represents the first documented microsporidian parasite in the family Salmonidae, while *Ceratomyxa shasta* represents the first report of the genus *Ceratomyxa* as a freshwater tissue parasite (previously known only from marine fish gall/urinary bladders). All three diseases caused significant mortality events in hatchery settings, with variable but concerning presence in wild populations.

## Background & Description

### Cryptobia borreli
- Hemoflagellate protozoan (Class Mastigophora, Order Protomonadina)
- Found in blood, skin, ascitic fluid, myocomma, connective tissue of muscle bundles, and kidney parenchyma
- Notably absent from intestinal canal in California hosts (contrary to literature reports by [[Wenyon]] 1926)
- Vectored by leeches (*[[Piscicola Salmositica]]*), found harboring the parasite in the gut, though multiplicative phases not demonstrated

### Plistophora sp.
- Microsporidian (Class Sporozoa, Order Microsporidia)
- Developmental stages found exclusively in gill filament tissue
- **Vegetative stages (schizonts):** 5 μ in diameter (young) up to 15 μ × 40 μ (developing sporonts)
- **Spore cysts:** 120–180 μ in diameter
- **Spores:** 2.4 × 7.5 μ
- Spore cysts relatively rare in field samples; early schizont stages more commonly observed, complicating historical diagnosis

### Ceratomyxa shasta
- Myxosporidian (Class Sporozoa, Order Myxosporidia)
- First species of genus *Ceratomyxa* described as a tissue parasite (vs. lumen-dwelling in marine hosts)
- Both trophozoites and characteristically-shaped spores observed in fresh tissue preparations
- Infects an unusually broad range of tissues: alimentary tract, liver, gall bladder, spleen, gonads, kidney, heart, gills, and skin

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Geographic range:** Klamath and Sacramento River drainages, Northern California; multiple coastal river systems; several state hatcheries (Mt. Shasta, Darrah Springs, Crystal Lake)
- **Host range:**
  - *Cryptobia borreli*: Broad host range including [[Steelhead Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[King Salmon]], [[Silver Salmon]], [[Klamath Large-Scaled Sucker]], and sculpin (*Cottus* sp.)
  - *Plistophora* sp.: Domesticated and wild rainbow trout strains, [[Kokanee Salmon]], and sculpin
  - *Ceratomyxa shasta*: Restricted to rainbow trout (fall- and spring-spawning strains) at a single hatchery; notably absent in co-occurring brook trout and brown trout
- **Transmission vectors:** Leeches (*Piscicola salmositica*) implicated as vector for *Cryptobia*; water source strongly implicated in *Ceratomyxa shasta* transmission (Crystal Lake springs vs. Rock Creek water)
- **Water source dependency:** *C. shasta* epizootics tied to specific spring water source at Crystal Lake Hatchery; disease incidence dropped sharply after switching hatchery water supply to Rock Creek, despite both sources originating from springs only ~1 mile apart
- Attempts to eradicate wild fish reservoirs from Crystal Lake were unsuccessful, suggesting a persistent environmental/wild-fish reservoir for *C. shasta*

## Key Findings

### Cryptobia borreli
- First observed in 1949 in fall-spawning rainbow trout at Mt. Shasta Hatchery; also found same year in yearling king salmon
- King salmon held to yearling age proved exceptionally vulnerable, suffering high mortality with no secondary invaders involved
- Rainbow brood fish post-spawning losses likely due to combined effects of *Cryptobia* and secondary fungal invader **[[Saprolegnia Parasitica]]**
- **Blood smear infection rates (wild fish):**
  - Adult king salmon: 1.5% positive (n=127)
  - Adult steelhead: 3/7 positive
  - Steelhead yearlings/2-year-olds: 39% positive (n=129)
  - Silver salmon yearlings: 2/8 heavily infected (both behaviorally abnormal)
- **Clinical signs:** anemia, whitish gelatinous gill exudate, reduced blood clotting time, raised scales, excess mucus, exophthalmia, distended abdomen, lethargy, abnormal darkening

### Plistophora sp.
- First microsporidian ever recorded in family Salmonidae
- First identified 1954 in wild steelhead fingerlings at Darrah Springs Hatchery; soon after in B.C. rainbow stock
- **Two major epizootics documented:**
  - ~170,000 steelhead fingerlings — near-complete loss
  - B.C. rainbow fingerling group — >75% lost
- In one wild coastal stream sample, ~75% of yearling steelhead were infected; nearly half of infected fish so heavily parasitized that survival was doubtful
- Disease likely acts as predisposing factor for other disorders and may reduce survival of hatchery-planted fish
- Correlation between pale gills (anemia) and parasite abundance was strong in hatchery fish but weak in wild steelhead populations
- Diagnosis requires high-dry or oil-immersion microscopy of gill filaments; external symptoms nonspecific and easily confused with dietary anemia

### Ceratomyxa shasta
- First observed 1948 at Crystal Lake Hatchery; formally described as new species by [[Elmer R. Noble]] (1950)
- First report of genus *Ceratomyxa* in a freshwater fish and as a tissue parasite (previously only known as gall/urinary bladder parasites in marine fish)
- Geographically restricted to Crystal Lake Hatchery vicinity — not found in other hatcheries or wild fish elsewhere
- Disease incidence sharply declined after hatchery water source switched from Crystal Lake springs to Rock Creek, though mechanism remains unexplained
- Typically affects fish at 12–16 fish/pound size class (fall/winter onset), though smaller and larger fish occasionally infected
- All experimental transmission attempts (infected → uninfected fish) were unsuccessful
- Hind gut consistently shows characteristic swelling across all tissue layers in infected fish
- Clinical signs: sluggishness, seeking shallow water, coelomic distension, exophthalmia from ascitic fluid accumulation

### Cross-Cutting Observations
- All three diseases present significant challenges to [[California Department of Fish and Game]] hatchery operations
- Two of three diseases (*Cryptobia* and *Plistophora*) considered likely significant in wild salmonid populations
- Authors emphasize incomplete understanding of protozoan life histories and call for further study
- Related taxonomic work referenced: [[Katz 1951]] described two new *Cryptobia* hemoflagellates from western Washington teleosts, suggesting broader Pacific Coast distribution of related parasites
