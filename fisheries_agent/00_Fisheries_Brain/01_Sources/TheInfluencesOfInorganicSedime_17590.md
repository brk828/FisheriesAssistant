# The Influences of Inorganic Sediment on the Aquatic Life of Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game review, by [[Almo J. Cordone]] and [[Don W. Kelley]] (1960), synthesizes investigations on how [[Inorganic Sediment]] affects stream fisheries. The authors conclude that sediment is harmful to [[Salmonids]] streams, mainly through indirect pathways: smothering of [[Redd|redds]] and eggs, loss of [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]] and trout food, and filling of shelter. Direct lethal effects on adult fish require high turbidity and are poorly documented. The review argues that the gradual deposition of erosion silt is more damaging over time than spectacular discharges, and it calls for protective [[Sediment Standards]] and long-term research.

## Background & Description

The review is not a complete literature survey. It focuses on studies of inorganic sediment, excluding chemical pollutants such as heavy-metal tailings, and it treats the physics of erosion and sedimentation as a separate field. The authors frame sediment within California's rapid population growth, which threatens fisheries alongside dams, pollution, and erosion. Historical examples include:

- [[Iowa Stream Fauna Changes]] documented by Aitken (1936), where trout and smallmouth bass streams shifted to rough fish as erosion increased.
- [[Ohio Fish Fauna Decline]] summarized by Trautman (1957) for 1750 to 1950, shifting from clear-water species to turbidity-tolerant forms.
- [[Atlantic Salmon Extinction]] in Lake Ontario tributaries, attributed by Wolf (1950) to silting of spawning grounds.
- [[Hydraulic Mining]] in California's Sierra Nevada (1850 to 1900), which covered Sacramento Valley farmland and temporarily destroyed king salmon runs.

### Direct Effects on Fishes

[[Wallen]] (1951) ran controlled aquarium tests on 380 fishes of 16 warmwater species using montmorillonite clay. Behavioral reactions began near 20,000 ppm, lethal turbidities were 175,000 to 225,000 ppm, and death was caused by gill clogging. Other studies, including [[Griffin]] (1938), [[Campbell]] (1954), and [[Pautzke]] (1938), are less conclusive because of small samples or uncontrolled conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Eggs and alevins.** Eggs and alevins are the most sensitive life stages. [[Hobbs]] (1937) in New Zealand found that as little as 4 percent fine material passing a 0.03-inch screen was damaging. [[Shaw and Maga]] (1943) reported silver salmon fry yields of 79.9 percent in hatchery baskets, 25.4 percent (maximum) in clean gravel, and 2.4 percent maximum with mining silt added during incubation. [[Cooper]] (1956) on the [[Horsefly River]] showed that survival falls in proportion to reduced intragravel water flow, and that fine particles reduce flow more than coarse ones. [[Gangmark and Bakkala]] (1960) on [[Mill Creek]] correlated seepage velocity with egg mortality (for example, 24 to 40 percent at 0.5 to 1.5 feet per hour velocities).

**Dissolved oxygen.** [[Alderdice]] and colleagues showed that critical dissolved oxygen levels for chum salmon eggs range from about 1 ppm early in development to over 7 ppm before hatching. [[Dunham]] (1958) described [[Benthal Decomposition]], where silt covering organic deposits consumes oxygen at a critical time, especially in spring and summer.

**Bottom fauna.** Silt reduces invertebrate abundance, which lowers trout food. [[Tebo]] (1955) on Shope Creek, North Carolina, recorded 7.3 organisms per square foot in silted areas versus 25.5 at unsilted stations. [[Ziebell]] (1957) found 173 organisms per square foot above a gravel washing plant on the South Fork Chehalis River and only 4 per square foot 100 yards below it. [[Sumner and Smith]] (1939) reported that silted Yuba River tributaries produced 63 percent, and American River tributaries 41 percent, of the fauna of clean areas.

**Substrate.** [[Tarzwell]] (1937) rated bottom types for productivity, from sand (1) to rubble (29) to moss on gravel and rubble (140) and watercress (8,301). [[Smith and Moyle]] (1944) identified rubble as the most productive substrate.

**Shelter.** [[McCrimmon]] (1954) found that in Duffin Creek, Ontario, sedimentation destroyed the shelter that planted Atlantic salmon fry needed, and predation then increased. The authors suggest that in California, shelter limits catchable trout in many small streams.

## Key Findings

1. **Direct lethality is uncertain.** Adult fish tolerate high turbidity well. Lethal effects generally require extreme concentrations or abrasive, toxic, or organic material. The authors advise that the question "Is sediment directly harmful?" usually cannot be answered without more data.

2. **Indirect effects dominate.** Losses from smothered eggs, reduced food, and loss of shelter likely occur before direct harm to adults. Fisheries workers should base management on these indirect effects.

3. **Eggs are the most vulnerable stage.** Even moderate deposition is detrimental. The authors consider sedimentation "probably one of the most important factors limiting the natural reproduction of salmonids in streams."

4. **Timing matters.** Silt added during incubation, especially early, is more damaging than silt added near hatching, because alevins can move and escape later. Chronic small additions can be more harmful than sudden flushes.

5. **Bottom fauna reductions are measurable.** Above-below sampling showed reductions of 75 to over 90 percent below gravel washing and mining operations. Recovery occurs within about a year once freshets flush silt out (Powder River example).

6. **Sampling is statistically demanding.** [[Needham and Usinger]] (1956) estimated 194 samples for total wet weight and 73 for total numbers at the 95 percent confidence level. [[Gaufin, Harris, and Walter]] (1956) showed that five samples capture over 80 percent of species. The authors recommend paired transects above and below sediment sources in similar habitats.

7. **Standards are needed but difficult.** The [[Horsefly River]] recommendations (no placer mining in streambeds, settling basins, effluent turbidity below 25 ppm from July 1 to April 1, and sediment finer than 0.1 mm) are the most developed. [[Ellis]] (1944) proposed a 1,000-mesh particle limit and a millionth-intensity light depth of at least 5 meters. Central Valley waste discharge clauses prohibit increases in natural turbidity from May 1 to November 1 and prohibit silt or gravel deposits.

8. **Long-term research is limited.** Four long-term logging-sediment projects were underway: Idaho (four trout streams), Alaska (four salmon streams, 15-year program), Sagehen Creek in California, and Oregon trout streams.

9. **Core conclusion.** The authors conclude that "clean stream bottoms mean good trout populations," and that most damage in the Sierra Nevada is preventable with known land-use practices. They call for a land husbandry philosophy that prevents "untreated and running sores" on the land surface and warn that many trout streams will be destroyed by sediment deposition unless this changes.
