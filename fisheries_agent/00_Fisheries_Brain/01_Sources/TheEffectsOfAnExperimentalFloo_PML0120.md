# Effects of an Experimental Flood on the Aquatic Biota and Their Habitats in the Colorado River, Glen and Grand Canyons, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department report (December 1996) evaluates the ecological effects of the 1996 [[Experimental Flood]] released from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]] in Grand Canyon. The flood was a beach/habitat-building test: about 1,274 m³/s (45,000 cfs) for seven days (27 March to 1 April 1996), intended to rebuild sandbars, reform [[Backwater]] habitat for juvenile native fishes, and reduce non-native fish populations. The study examined fish distribution and habitat use, backwater sediments, backwater numbers, benthic invertebrates, zooplankton, and fish diet, with supporting chapters on the Glen Canyon tailwater (algae, macrophytes, macroinvertebrates, rainbow trout and flannelmouth suckers).

Overall, native fishes were largely unaffected, exotic species (notably [[Plains Killifish]] and [[Fathead Minnow]]) declined, backwater numbers and surface area rose immediately after the flood but later fell, sediment shifted toward sand, and benthic invertebrate density and biomass declined.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] through Grand Canyon was historically warm, seasonally turbid, and dominated by large spring floods (pre-dam mean annual peak about 2,439 m³/s; Stevens 1983). After closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963, flows were regulated, releases became cold because of hypolimnial discharge, and seasonal high flows shifted from spring to later in the year. Occasional large tributary floods from the [[Little Colorado River]] remain the main natural hydrological disturbance.

Four of eight original native species persist in Grand Canyon: [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Speckled Dace]]. About 25 exotic fish species have been reported from this reach. Table 1 of the source lists 19 fish species handled, including exotics such as [[Common Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Redside Shiner]], [[Plains Killifish]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]].

Backwaters are quiet, slow-water pockets connected to the mainchannel, formed in eddies and reattachment bars. They are warmer, have lower velocities and more fine sediment and organic matter, and support higher benthic invertebrate densities. They have become important rearing habitat for larval and juvenile native fishes because of cold mainstem releases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** Lee's Ferry (RK 0) to Diamond Creek (RK 363.16), divided into eight sampling reaches. The Little Colorado River confluence (RK 98.95) is key because all native species spawn there.
- **Pre-flood flows:** steady ~226 m³/s (8,000 cfs) before and after the flood; flood peak 1,274 m³/s (45,000 cfs).
- **Water quality:** backwater temperatures can be as much as 5 °C warmer than the mainstem in May and June. Backwater specific conductance and temperature after the flood were largely sub-optimal for larval and juvenile native fishes.
- **Sampling:** electrofishing, minnow traps, trammel nets and seines; CPUE compared with non-parametric tests (α = 0.05).
- **Gaps noted:** the study could not mark-recapture fish for dispersal estimates because too few fish were captured.

## Key Findings

**Fish distribution and habitat (Distribution and Dispersal chapter)**
- Ten fish species were captured in the pre/post comparison; native fishes were relatively common. Fathead minnow and Plains killifish were the most commonly captured exotics.
- Native fish catch rates were largely unchanged; only [[Bluehead Sucker]] backwater seine CPUE declined significantly (0.5 to 0.05 fish/100 m² seined).
- [[Plains Killifish]] were nearly eliminated: 43 were captured before the flood and none after.
- [[Fathead Minnow]] minnow-trap CPUE dropped about tenfold in Reaches 3 and 4.
- [[Rainbow Trout]] backwater seine and trammel-net CPUE increased after the flood, probably through displacement, while electrofishing CPUE in Reach 4 fell about fourfold.
- [[Speckled Dace]] minnow-trap CPUE rose after the flood (1.4 to 2.7 fish/24 h), probably because sand deposition made talus shorelines resemble debris fans.

**Habitat use by juvenile fishes (Habitat Use chapter)**
- Humpback chub electrofishing catch was greatest at low flows (2.5 fish/10 min) and higher along talus shorelines at low flow (4.6 fish/10 min versus 0.5 at high flow).
- Fathead minnow catch was higher at low flows; rainbow trout electrofishing was higher at low flow (3.4 fish/10 min).
- Turbidity during the flood (mean 58.7 NTU versus 8.6 before) reduced electrofishing efficiency, confounding results.
- The authors concluded that native fish did not disperse downstream, supporting the hypothesis of [[Mockley and Meffe]] (1987) that native fishes resist floods.

**Backwater sediments (Backwater Sediments chapter)**
- Sediments changed significantly. Before the flood, silt and sand each made up about 50% of backwater sediment; afterward sand dominated.
- Silt, coarse particulate organic matter and fine particulate organic matter were significantly reduced after the flood.
- One backwater near RK 104.99 retained high silt, suggesting a 7-day flood at 45,000 cfs did not scour compacted silt everywhere.

**Backwater numbers and surface area (Backwater Numbers chapter)**
- Aerial videography at 8,000 cfs found eight additional backwaters after the flood; backwater surface area rose from 172.5 m² to 285.1 m² (significantly greater, p = 0.0002).
- Ground census during AGFD trips found fewer backwaters after the flood (68 before versus 42 after), reflecting differing discharges (13,405 cfs versus 18,419 cfs).
- The authors concluded that post-flood operating flows and fluctuations were detrimental to backwater longevity.

**Benthic invertebrates (Benthos chapter)**
- Mean total invertebrate density declined from 11,426 to 3,581 individuals/m² (p = 0.038); biomass fell about 86%.
- Oligochaetes, nematodes and molluscs dominated before the flood; oligochaetes comprised 92% of the post-flood community.
- Detritus biomass rose significantly (131.1 to 259.6 g/m²; p = 0.0184).
- Recolonization by chironomids was expected to be rapid.

**Zooplankton (Zooplankton chapter)**
- Total zooplankton density was significantly greater after the flood (2,916.7 versus 1,916.7 /m³ in the source's reported comparison); mean backwater temperature rose from 9.9 °C to 12.0 °C.
- Eight littoral taxa present before the flood were absent afterward.
- Zooplankton density did not differ between backwater and mainchannel habitats, attributed to high water exchange.

**Humpback chub diet (Diet of Adult Humpback Chub chapter)**
- 43 adult humpback chub (>250 mm TL) were analyzed (pre-flood n = 9, flood n = 16, post-flood n = 18).
- [[Simuliidae]] (black flies) were the most frequent food, followed by chironomids and [[Gammarus lacustris]]; terrestrial insects increased during the flood.
- Diet was largely unchanged in total number and biomass, and the authors concluded flood effects on feeding were minimal.

**Glen Canyon tailwater biota (Glen Canyon Dam Tailwater chapter)**
- Epilithon biomass (AFDW) was unaffected, but chlorophyll a fell about 40%.
- Diatom epiphyte densities declined about 66% in the week after the flood, with large/upright taxa most affected.
- Submerged macrophyte standing stock was reduced; [[Potamogeton]] recolonized sand within about one month, while [[Chara]] recolonized by about seven months.
- Rainbow trout condition and abundance changed little through fall; strong young-of-year recruitment was evident in November.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Native fishes were largely unaffected; exotics were partly suppressed, though likely temporarily.
- Post-flood operations reduced the longevity of newly created backwaters; floods with a slower downramp may preserve them.
- Future floods of different magnitude or duration may be required to significantly reduce exotic populations.
- Long-term monitoring is needed on backwater longevity, recolonization, and effects on spawning and recruitment.
