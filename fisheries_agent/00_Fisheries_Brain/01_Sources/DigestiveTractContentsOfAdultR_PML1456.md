# Digestive Tract Contents of Adult Razorback Suckers in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 study by Paul C. Marsh (Center for Environmental Studies and Department of Zoology, Arizona State University) examines the diet of adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) from [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. Digestive tracts from 34 preserved adults were analysed. Contents were dominated by planktonic crustaceans, with diatoms, filamentous algae, detritus and inorganic sediment also present. Gill raker morphology and the dominance of zooplankton point to planktivory, but the presence of benthic material shows that bottom feeding also occurs. The authors conclude that the species is a facultative mix of planktivore and benthic feeder, and that the material found on the bottom may be either selected food or incidental to burrowing.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its populations and conservation status had drawn considerable attention by 1987 because of declining numbers and imperilment across its range (McAda and Wydoski 1980; Minckley 1983). Basic life-history information, including diet and feeding behaviour, was poorly known. Because sacrificing protected fish for stomach analysis was hard to justify, the authors reported diet data from 34 specimens that had become available as preserved voucher material.

Specimens were collected by trammel net from shoreline areas of Lake Mohave, on 11 May 1980 (N = 8) and in 1984 on several dates, including January (N = 10) and February, with one 1984 collection of N = 13. Fish were killed and preserved in formalin. Standard length (SL) was recorded to the nearest millimetre, and sex was determined by internal examination.

Methods included the following steps:
- Digestive tracts were excised. Ten-millilitre aliquots were taken from the anterior third (1980 fish) or from the anterior, middle and posterior thirds (1984 fish).
- Each aliquot was diluted in 50 mL of water, and 1.0 mL of the suspension was counted in a Sedgewick-Rafter chamber.
- Organisms were identified by light microscopy at 35 to 400x magnification.
- Two metrics were calculated: frequency of occurrence (percentage of non-empty tracts containing an item) and predominance (percentage of tracts in which that item made up the largest proportion of total contents).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish size.** Fish were considered representative of the Lake Mohave population (Minckley 1983; Bozek et al. 1984). Males averaged 431 mm SL, and females averaged 492 mm (range 411-530 mm). The male range is partly illegible in the source scan.

**Sampling.** Gut contents differed among fore-, mid- and hindgut samples within individuals, so these were pooled for analysis. Contents did not differ among collection dates or between sexes. Two of the 34 tracts (6%) were empty and were excluded, leaving 32 for analysis. Numbers of identifiable animals per millilitre were calculated for 24 specimens collected in 1984.

**Food items.** The main dietary categories were:
- **Planktonic crustaceans:** *Bosmina* sp. was the most abundant item and occurred in all analysed tracts. *Daphnia* sp. occurred in 72% of fish. Other cladocerans (*Chydorus*, *Leptodora*) were less frequent.
- **Rotifers, ostracods and copepods:** Rotifers and ostracods occurred in 53% of tracts each, and copepods in 34%. None of these was predominant in any tract.
- **Chironomid larvae (benthic insects):** Found in 3% of tracts.
- **Diatoms:** Mainly *Fragilaria crotonensis*, found in a large share of tracts.
- **Filamentous green algae:** Also present, though less common than diatoms.
- **Detritus and amorphous organic matter:** Found in 56% of tracts.
- **Inorganic material (e.g., sand):** Found in 16% of fish.

Items most often present were not always the ones that made up the largest share of contents. Rotifers, ostracods and copepods all occurred in about one-third or more of tracts yet predominated in none. The authors therefore argue that both frequency and predominance must be considered to judge the relative importance of food items.

**Behaviour.** Field observers have described razorback suckers "bouncing" along the bottom during forward movement (Minckley 1973). The fish take up large mouthfuls of sediment, manipulate it, and expel material through the opercula while apparently keeping food particles. Some individuals burrow head-first into the substrate, sometimes to a depth of more than a metre, and stir up sediment for several minutes, sometimes while moving forward over distances approaching one metre (original observations at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico).

## Key Findings

- **Dietary composition:** Digestive tracts of 34 adult razorback suckers from Lake Mohave were dominated by planktonic crustaceans (especially [[Bosmina]] and [[Daphnia]]). Diatoms, filamentous algae, detritus and inorganic sediment were also present.
- **Feeding mode:** The fish are morphologically adapted for planktivory, with long, fine [[Gill Rakers]] that resemble those of planktivorous lake suckers. The authors describe the species as primarily planktivorous, with a definite [[Benthic Feeding]] component.
- **Benthic evidence:** Benthic material in the gut, including chironomid larvae and sediment, shows that bottom feeding occurs. It is not clear whether the bottom material was chosen as food or was taken in during burrowing and sediment excavation.
- **Methodological point:** Frequency of occurrence and predominance give different pictures of diet, so both should be reported.
- **Comparison with earlier studies:** The results agree with earlier reports of zooplankton feeding in Lake Mohave (Minckley 1973; Allan and Roden 1978). Earlier reports from the Colorado River (Dill 1944) and the Green River (Banks 1964; Vanicek 1967) describe silt, detritus, plant debris and insect larvae in the gut, which is consistent with benthic feeding.
- **Conservation context:** Because the species is endangered and fish were scarce, the authors used preserved specimens rather than sacrificing additional fish. They note that little life-history information was available.
