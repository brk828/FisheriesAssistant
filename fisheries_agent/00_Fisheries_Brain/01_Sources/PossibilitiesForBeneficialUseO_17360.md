# Possibilities for Beneficial Uses of Heated Water Discharges into Cooling Reservoirs

#ichthyology #reservoir-ecology #Centrarchidae #thermal-pollution #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1970 conference paper presents research on the ecological effects of heated water discharges from fossil-fueled steam electric generating plants into cooling reservoirs in Missouri. Studies conducted at [[Montrose Lake]] (1500 acres) and [[Thomas Hill Reservoir]] (4500 acres) examined water quality, primary production, plankton, bottom fauna, and fish populations. The research identified several beneficial effects of thermal discharge including extended winter fish growth, establishment of [[Threadfin Shad]] populations beyond their natural range, and development of productive winter fisheries. The authors propose that controlled thermal discharges into purpose-built cooling reservoirs can provide fisheries benefits when properly managed, though they caution against discharge into natural lakes or multipurpose reservoirs.

## Background & Description

Most literature on heated water effects has focused on marine, estuarine, or river environments, with rivers showing the most pronounced impacts due to small receiving volumes. Reported effects include dissolved oxygen reduction, temperature peaks of 100-107°F, restriction of encrusting organisms, and reduction of bottom fauna. Some beneficial effects documented in the literature include increased fish harvest in late winter, attraction of fishes to warm water areas, increased filamentous algae growth, increased winter primary production, and increased zooplankton production.

The Missouri study sites were shallow reservoirs (mean depth <12 feet) built by private electric companies for cooling water. At [[Montrose Lake]], operating at capacity, the entire water mass circulated through the plant once weekly. Both reservoirs had turbid water with no municipal or industrial pollution except limited acid mine drainage. The [[Montrose Lake]] plant was built in 1956.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water Quality**: The most significant effect was temperature elevation of 9-14°F above ambient in the discharge canal vicinity. During ice cover periods, discharge water kept approximately 10% of total surface area ice-free. No important differences existed in dissolved oxygen, pH, or alkalinity between heated effluent and reservoir water.

Dissolved solids concentration was high compared to other Missouri reservoirs, resulting from excessive evaporation caused by elevated temperature (forced evaporation) and acid mine drainage. Acid drainage caused small decreases in bicarbonate alkalinity and significant increases in dissolved substances, especially sulfates. Sulfate became the most abundant anion rather than carbonate typical of natural hardwater lakes.

**Primary Production**: Photosynthesis of plankton algae measured by carbon-14 uptake showed positive response to temperature elevation. Water samples from different temperature areas were incubated at collection temperatures in thermostatically controlled baths.

**Plankton and Bottom Fauna**: Monthly collections from June 1965-April 1967 at [[Montrose Lake]] showed no major qualitative or quantitative differences in standing crops between heated and unheated regions. Standing crops of bottom fauna were low compared to other lakes and reservoirs.

Food habit analyses revealed: [[Gizzard Shad]] consumed primarily zooplankton; [[Bluegill Sunfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Carp]] ate invertebrates including mayfly [[Hexagenia]], fly larvae (Tendipedidae), and copepods (Chydoridae); [[Channel Catfish]] consumed [[Hexagenia]], Tendipedidae, and mayfly [[Stenonema]].

## Key Findings

**Fish Population Responses**:

1. **[[Threadfin Shad]] Establishment**: This southeastern forage fish, limited northward by low temperature, was successfully stocked at [[Montrose Lake]]. It reproduces throughout the lake annually, provides forage for carnivorous fishes, and adults survive winter in heated discharge to repeat the cycle. This range extension was possible only because of heated water effluent.

2. **Extended Winter Growth**: Continued growth of [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[White Crappie]] occurred during winter months in heated water. Best evidence came from young-of-the-year fishes. Large fishes showed annulus formation on scales in December (rather than May) coinciding with generator breakdown and temperature reduction. Growth resumed when generator was repaired and temperature increased. Observations on [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill Sunfish]], orange-spotted sunfish, [[Gizzard Shad]], and [[Channel Catfish]] were inconclusive.

3. **Winter Fishery Development**: At [[Thomas Hill Reservoir]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[White Crappie]], and bullheads were attracted to heated water and fed actively during winter, creating an open water winter fishery. During peak winter fishery 1967-68, an estimated 1000 fish per day were removed from less than 50 acres in the heated water arm.

**Management Conclusions**:

1. **Reservoir Volume**: Impact of heated water lessens as reservoir volume increases. Clark (1969) proposed 1000-2000 acres for a 1000 megawatt plant to avoid pollution effects. The 4500-acre [[Thomas Hill Reservoir]] should be adequate for the 475 megawatt plant.

2. **Cooling Canal Design**: Long cooling canal (0.9 mile with turbulence at [[Thomas Hill Reservoir]]) results in heat loss to atmosphere before discharge.

3. **No Lethal Effects**: Under conditions at [[Thomas Hill Reservoir]] (250,000 gpm discharge into 4500 acres), no lethal effects from heated water were observed. More serious effects would result from similar discharge into small lakes or streams.

4. **Beneficial Use Potential** in shallow, intermediate-size reservoirs:
   - Fish migration creates winter density buildup in heated water
   - Increased density provides extended winter harvest (may require harvest control)
   - Continued winter growth could extend growing season from 6 to 12 months at Columbia, Missouri latitude
   - Increased commercial fish production possible for [[Channel Catfish]], exotic warmwater fishes, and basket culture techniques
   - Strategic power plant location could optimize fish harvest and hatchery use of heated effluent
   - Discharge into major reservoir arm (versus main body) provides large elevated-temperature area
   - New plant construction should consider fish rearing ponds supplied with heated water

**Important Caveats**: Conclusions apply only to artificial cooling reservoirs built for waste heat exchange. Authors do not recommend heated water discharge into natural lakes or multipurpose reservoirs where biota alteration and eutrophication acceleration may be undesirable.

**Future Considerations**: By 1980, steam-electric utilities were estimated to require one-fifth to one-sixth of total freshwater runoff (1,200 billion gallons per day) for cooling water. Detailed ecological knowledge of heated water environments is imperative for sound biological management. The research suggests possibilities for reservoir management programs incorporating beneficial uses of thermal discharges.
