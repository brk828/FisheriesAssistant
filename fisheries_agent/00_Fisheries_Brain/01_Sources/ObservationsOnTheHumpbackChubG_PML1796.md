# Humpback Chub in the Colorado River and Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a study of the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), an endangered cyprinid fish of the [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries in [[Grand Canyon National Park]], Arizona. The authors (Bowell and Usher, with contributions from Minckley and associates) combine field collections from 1975 to 1979 with a review of historical records to address distribution, movement, reproduction, growth, diet, behavior, and causes of decline. The species was listed as endangered in 1973. The authors conclude that the population is closely tied to the [[Little Colorado River]] and that flow regulation by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has largely halted natural reproduction in the mainstem.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Gila cypha*, a large-bodied cyprinid of the Colorado River system. The largest individual reported in this study measured 358 mm total length.
- **Status:** Listed as endangered in 1973. Before the study, the population in Grand Canyon was considered critically endangered.
- **Historical records:** Early river explorers knew the fish. The [[Kolb Brothers]] reported catching one for supper near the mouth of the Little Colorado River in the early 1900s (Kolb and Kolb 1914). The species was formally described in 1946 from specimens collected at River Mile 61 in Grand Canyon (Miller 1946).
- **Range expansion records:** Specimens were later collected in the Yampa River and Upper Colorado River near Grand Junction (Kidd 1977). Archaeological remains at [[Stanton's Cave]] (River Mile 65.7) date to about 4000 B.P., and remains were also found at Catclaw Cave, which is now inundated. These finds show the species once occupied a wider range, including portions of the Colorado River well below Grand Canyon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and methods.** The study covered the Colorado River from Lees Ferry to Separation Rapids, and included the Little Colorado River and other tributaries. Methods included seines, trammel nets, hoop nets, fyke nets, electrofishing, and angling, supplemented by information from commercial river runners and other agency collections.

**Distribution and movement.** Adults occupy mainstem habitats with large boulders, little or no current, and areas below travertine dams. Young-of-the-year fish and yearlings are generally found in the Little Colorado River throughout the year. Adults concentrate heavily in the [[Confluence Zone]] during spring and summer and use it far less in fall and winter. Movement data are lacking because no tagged individuals had been recaptured.

**Reproduction and growth.** Spawning was not directly observed. Based on fish in reproductive condition and smaller fish in June and July, the reproductive season was estimated to run from March through June, possibly July. Reproduction in the mainstem is suspected but not confirmed. Mainstem fish were collected in April at water temperatures of about 9 °C, and the authors suggest spawning would require temperatures of roughly 16 to 18 °C. Aging with opercular bones showed rapid growth in the first years, with growth declining markedly after about age five.

**Diet and behavior.** Food habit data were limited. Stomachs and fish from the bottom contained aquatic insects, and the species was observed feeding in midwater and on the bottom. Aggregations of adults were observed in 1978, with groups of two to three and occasionally more than thirty fish recorded together.

**Threats.** Dam operations are identified as the most important cause of decline. Cold, clear, regulated water lowered temperatures and suppressed reproduction. Daily fluctuations of more than 1.5 m during high flows and sharp drops in water level strand larvae and juveniles and expose spawning areas, which exposes young fish to harsher mainstem conditions.

## Key Findings

- **Range:** Historic and recent records place the humpback chub from the Yampa and Upper Colorado rivers to the Grand Canyon and well downstream.
- **Nursery role:** The Little Colorado River is the main spawning site and nursery for the species in Grand Canyon. Estimated densities of young-of-the-year ranged from 167 to 6,364 fish per hectare, depending on locality and season, and were higher in the upper Little Colorado River.
- **Reproduction:** The reproductive season is estimated at March through June (possibly July). Natural mainstem reproduction is suspected but unconfirmed.
- **Growth:** The fish reach roughly 75 to 120 mm in their first year, and growth slows markedly after about five years. Young fish grew from about 27 to 72 mm between June and October 1978.
- **Decline:** Dam operations (cold water, altered temperature regime, daily flow fluctuations) are the main cause of decline.
- **Recommendations:** The authors recommend that the Little Colorado River from Blue Springs (13 miles / 20.8 km upstream) to its mouth be declared critical habitat. They also recommend that the Colorado River from Glen Canyon Dam to Separation Rapids be considered for listing, that further ecological studies be conducted, and that long-term monitoring of the Grand Canyon population be established.
