# Hatchery Fish, Hybridization and the Decline of Wild Angling

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This essay, written as a polemic by [[Ted Williams]] with reference to [[Robert H. Smith]], argues that US fish managers are "making tomorrow's fish today" by replacing wild, self-sustaining fish with hatchery-raised, hybridized and genetically manipulated stock. The author contends that stocked trout are physically degraded, that hybrids are engineered for convenience and for angler success rather than ecological resilience, and that these practices threaten wild populations and ecosystems. The piece is opinion-based and reports anecdotes and quoted statements from managers and biologists rather than controlled research data.

## Background & Description

- **Author and contributors:** Ted Williams wrote the article; John H. Uehlinger provided drawings. Robert H. Smith, a retired wildlife biologist living in Central Point, Oregon, is the author's fishing companion and author of *Native Trout of North America*.
- **Context:** The article appeared in the January 1986 issue of *Field & Stream*. Smith and Williams keep life-lists of wild fish species they have caught, and Smith has taken every native North American salmonid species and subspecies except three: Maine's blueback trout, the Sunapee golden trout and Ontario's aurora trout.
- **Hatchery trout:** The author describes stocked trout as selected for processed pellets rather than natural food, with worn fins, deformed gill covers, bent jaws and inbred deformities. Wild trout are described as shy, while hatchery trout beg for food.
- **Key species discussed:**
  - [[Brown Trout]], [[Walleye]], [[Muskellunge]] and [[Striped Bass]] are described as prized gamefish because they are wary and selective.
  - [[Tiger Trout]] (brook trout x brown trout), [[Saugeye]] (sauger x walleye), [[Tiger Muskie]] and the [[Wiper]] (white bass x striped bass) are presented as managers' "dumber" hybrid substitutes.
  - [[Golden Trout]] strains, the [[Albino Rainbow Trout]] ("Albino Rainbow") and the [[Triploid Chinook Salmon]] are also discussed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Genetic contamination:** Hatchery rainbows hybridize with cutthroat trout in western headwaters, reducing native pure stocks to extinction through introgression.
- **Hybrid vigor and pollution tolerance:** Hybrids are said to grow fast and fight hard, and managers claim they tolerate polluted or deteriorating habitat. The author rejects this as a rationale for ignoring pollution.
- **Escape and establishment risk:** Viper (wiper) reproduction in the wild, through backcrossing with white bass, has been documented in Texas. When wipers were backcrossed with striped bass in the laboratory, nearly fifty percent of the progeny were badly deformed. The author asks what would happen if hybrid genes spread into wild striped bass populations.
- **Triploid fish:** Triploid chinook salmon are stocked in Lake Michigan and Lake Superior waters by Wisconsin and Michigan. Diploid chinooks in these lakes compete with native trout and disrupt spawning, but triploids are sterile and grow larger.
- **Antifreeze gene transfer:** A winter flounder antifreeze gene has been isolated and is proposed for transfer to striped bass and trout, which could allow these fish to colonize colder habitats with unknown ecological effects.

## Key Findings

- **Hatchery trout are physically compromised:** Hatchery trout lose fins and scales in concrete raceways, become deformed through inbreeding and are described as "wriggling sausages" by the author.
- **Managers prefer hybrids that are easy to catch:** Managers are described as making "dumber fish" to give anglers a "good return," with the public quickly removing stocked hybrids.
- **Hybrid breeding techniques are described in detail:** Wipers are produced by stripping gametes from white bass and striped bass, injecting hormones (human chorionic gonadotropin) and then mixing gametes in a pan.
- **Polyploidy is used to produce sterile fish:** Triploid fish are created by shocking eggs with heat, hydrostatic pressure or chemicals, which keeps them sterile and allows them to grow to very large sizes.
- **Genetic manipulation is proceeding rapidly:** Gynogenesis via ultraviolet-irradiated sperm, growth hormone transgenic salmon and the antifreeze gene transfer are described as already in development.
- **Management decisions are criticized:** The author argues that hybrids are not controllable or easily shut off and that managers prioritize keeping staff employed over ecological protection.
- **Boneless trout are in production:** The Hagerman Springs Trout Company in Buhl, Idaho, is described as producing a boneless trout to match hatchery-processed fish, which the author treats as a satirical emblem of the trend.
