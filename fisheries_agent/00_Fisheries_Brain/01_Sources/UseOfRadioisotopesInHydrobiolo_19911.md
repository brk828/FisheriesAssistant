# Use of Radioisotopes in Hydrobiology and Fish Culture

#ichthyology #marine-biology #radioisotopes #hydrobiology

## Executive Summary

This 1961 review in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 90, No. 1, pp. 49-57) by F. F. Hooper, H. A. Popoviak, and S. F. Snieszko surveys how [[Radioisotopes]] have been applied in [[Hydrobiology]] and [[Fish Culture]]. The authors were prepared by the Committee on Hydrobiology and Fish Culture of the American Fisheries Society for 1959-1960. The paper aims to acquaint fishery workers with new research possibilities opened by the availability of radioactive materials. It covers six research areas: chemical cycles in lakes and ponds, productivity measurements, food relationships, fish diseases and nutrition, tracing movements of water and pollutants, and marking aquatic animals. It also reviews U.S. licensing rules and future prospects. Fallout and reactor-waste hazard studies are excluded and referred to a separate bibliography.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Recent research in hydrobiology and fish culture using radioactive tracers, written as a practical guide for fishery workers.
- **Authors' affiliations:** Michigan Department of Conservation, Ann Arbor (Hooper); U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Cortland, New York and Leetown, West Virginia (Popoviak and Snieszko).
- **Excluded material:** Biological effects of fallout and radioactive wastes, which were evaluated mainly as hazards. Readers are referred to [[Klement and Wallen Bibliography]] (1960).
- **Related reviews cited:** Zhadin, Kuznetsov, and Timofeev-Resovsky (1958) on Soviet hydrobiological isotope work; the 1957 Naples symposium on [[Marine Biological Applications of Radioisotope Research]]; and reviews by Griffin (1952) and Pendleton (1956) on marking animals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Chemical cycles.** [[Phosphorus-32]] is the most used tracer because its short half-life makes field work relatively safe and because it is rapidly taken up by bacteria and plants. Key findings include:
- Hutchinson and Bowen (1950) showed phosphorus moving from the epilimnion to the hypolimnion of stratified lakes through sedimentation of seston.
- Hayes and co-workers in Nova Scotian lakes showed that phosphorus is continually exchanged between lake water and mud, plankton, bacteria, and rooted plants. Fertilizer phosphorus behaves like the tracer and equilibrates with the solids.
- Rigler (1956) at Toussaint Lake, Ontario, confirmed these findings and highlighted planktonic bacteria in phosphate turnover.
- Zicker, Berger, and Hasler (1956) found that virtually no phosphorus was released from bottom mud without stirring from deeper than one-quarter inch below the mud surface.
- Likens and Hasler (1960) tracked [[Radiosodium]] in a meromictic lake. Sugawara, Koyama, and Noda (1954) found that ferric hydroxide floc sorbs sulphate and carries it to the bottom.
- Ivanov (reviewed by Zhadin et al.) reported sulphate reduction mainly in the surface layers of bottom deposits, with Solyonoye Lake producing H₂S at 20 mg per liter per day.

**Productivity.** [[Carbon-14]] is the most widely used isotope in hydrobiology. It measures phytoplankton carbon uptake and fixation, which estimates organic matter production in oceans, lakes, and ponds.

**Food relationships.** Davis and Foster (1958) found that animals obtain nearly all their radioactivity by ingesting food in the Columbia River system. Labeled invertebrates were fed to fish, including amphipods, ostracods, and mosquito larvae fed to the least chub. Nelson found that rainbow trout took up tracer phosphorus about twice as fast as brook trout.

**Movement of water and pollutants.** A Michigan trout stream dosed with 1.5 mL of P³² (23 millicuries) remained detectable 2.5 miles downstream for over 2 hours. Simpson et al. (1958) found that density differences from temperature controlled the path of sewage discharged into the Mohawk River.

## Key Findings

- **Isotope use is growing rapidly** in hydrobiology and fish culture, and the authors expect greater use as trained personnel enter the field.
- **Carbon-14 productivity technique:** The method is best used for relative comparisons rather than absolute production. A standard protocol uses light and dark bottles incubated for 2 to 8 hours, with ampoules of 2 to 4 µCi. The UNESCO-funded central agency at Charlottenlund Slot, Denmark (established 1958), provides standardized ampoules and counting services on a nonprofit basis.
- **Nutrient turnover:** Phosphorus and other tracers show rapid exchange between water and biological and mineral solids, which explains the quick drop in water phosphorus after fertilization.
- **Mineral nutrition:** Phillips et al. (1957-1959) at the Cortland Laboratory showed that trout absorb dissolved calcium directly, and that dissolved calcium contributes significantly to structural growth. Rosenthal (1958) found that affected lordotic guppies take up less Ca⁴⁵ but retain more in bone.
- **Tagging limits:** Seymour (1958) concluded that conventional fish marks are generally more practical than isotope tags. Four limitations were identified: poor radiation penetration through water (a few feet at most), possible harm to tagged animals, public health hazards from long-lived tags, and no established method to identify individuals within a tagged group. Boroughs et al. (1956) showed that strontium-89 in marine fish can be estimated by external probe monitoring, avoiding dissection.
- **Licensing:** Under 10 CFR Part 30, general licenses allow up to 50 µCi of C¹⁴ per quantity, with up to 10 quantities on hand (500 µCi total). Specific licenses for larger amounts are judged individually by the Atomic Energy Commission, weighing applicant training and radiation protection.
- **Future outlook:** Field use will stay restricted by health concerns. Expansion depends on better understanding of radiation hazards and on public acceptance, which the AEC's teacher-training program may help build.
