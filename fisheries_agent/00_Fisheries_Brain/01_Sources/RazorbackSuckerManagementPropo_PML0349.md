# Razorback Sucker Management Proposal to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (February 1985)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #razorback-sucker

## Executive Summary

This 1985 letter from the [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]] (CRWC) to the Regional Director of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) raises concern about the rapid decline of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The largest known population, in [[Lake Mohave]], is estimated at 58,000 individuals (±25,000) and appears to be old and reproducing poorly. The CRWC argues that a basin-wide management effort is needed regardless of whether the species is formally listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It proposes a work group to develop a management strategy and asks the USFWS to support a meeting in June 1985 at Laughlin, Nevada.

## Background & Description

The CRWC is an organization of the seven states in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its member states include Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming. Each state is statutorily mandated to manage fish and wildlife resources within its own boundaries. The council has worked on fishery and wildlife issues for more than two decades and favors interstate cooperation as a way to achieve basin-wide management.

The razorback sucker formerly ranged throughout the Colorado River system, reaching as far upstream as the Utah–Wyoming border. The letter identifies the following regional efforts:

- **Lower Colorado River Basin:** Management plans were agreed upon and are progressing under a cooperative agreement between USFWS Region 2 and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. The agreement was made in place of listing the species and gave states a mechanism for developing and implementing management strategies.
- **Upper Colorado River Basin:** Efforts are described as more recent and not yet fully effective.
- **Cooperating federal agencies** are listed in the letter but are not detailed in the text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The main concern is the failure of razorback suckers to recruit in lentic (still-water) habitats.

- **Lake Mohave:** Current population estimate is 58,000 (±25,000) individuals. Earlier estimates exceeded 100,000. Preliminary age and growth data suggest an old, slow-growing population with an average age of about 30 years. The population is considered at risk because of an apparent lack of recruitment.
- **Lake Mead:** Large numbers of razorbacks were observed after the closure of [[Hoover Dam]] and up to the early 1960s, roughly 30 years after closure. Observations are now infrequent and declining.
- **Cause:** The letter attributes the decline to an apparent inability of razorbacks to reproduce successfully in reservoirs. The author expects the Lake Mohave population to follow the same trajectory as Lake Mead.

Fry and fingerling introductions began in 1981 and are planned to continue through 1991. Their success is to be judged by monitoring juvenile recruitment and observing spawning adults.

## Key Findings

1. **Population decline:** The razorback sucker is declining rapidly across its range. The Lake Mohave population is estimated at 58,000 (±25,000), well below the earlier estimate of more than 100,000.
2. **Age structure:** Preliminary data point to an aging population (about 30 years average age) with slow growth.
3. **Recruitment failure:** The species is not reproducing successfully in reservoirs, as shown by the decline in Lake Mead after the closure of Hoover Dam.
4. **Management status:** Management plans exist in the Lower Colorado River Basin through a cooperative USFWS–Arizona Game and Fish Department agreement. Stocking began in 1981 and runs through 1991.
5. **Policy position:** The CRWC supports basin-wide management regardless of formal listing and says states can act effectively when they are not constrained by the complexities associated with listed species.
6. **Proposed actions:**
   - Establish a work group made up of the seven basin states, the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Upper and Lower Colorado regions), and USFWS Regions 2 and 6.
   - Hold a two-day meeting in June 1985 at Laughlin, Nevada, alongside a related committee meeting (the committee name is partly garbled in the scan). Attendees would include members of the [[Native Species Committee]], the CRWC, the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], USFWS, and biologists involved in razorback field and planning work.
   - Continue ongoing razorback research.
7. **Requested response:** The CRWC asks the USFWS Regional Director to consider the proposal and reply promptly to the CRWC Technical Committee Chairman. Copies were sent to the Technical Committee, CRWC council members, the Native Species Committee, and the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team.
