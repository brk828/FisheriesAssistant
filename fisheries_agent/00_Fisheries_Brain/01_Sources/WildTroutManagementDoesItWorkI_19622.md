# Wild Trout Management: Does It Work in the 1990s?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife report by R. Barry Nehring evaluates whether [[Quality Trout Management]] and wild-trout stocking strategies can re-establish self-sustaining wild [[Rainbow Trout]] populations in large streams once dominated by [[Brown Trout]]. Drawing on electrofishing surveys and stocking records from four study rivers (Animas, Fryingpan, Gunnison, and Rio Grande), the author concludes that establishing wild rainbow populations is feasible within about a decade, provided that hatchery support, restrictive angling regulations, and specific stocking practices are used. The study also documents that predation and competition by brown trout, and the thermal regime below reservoirs, strongly shape outcomes.

## Background & Description

Between 1945 and 1980, angling pressure on Colorado's stream fisheries rose steeply. License sales grew from 300,000 in 1950 to more than 700,000 in 1980. Combined with liberal bag limits, this pressure reduced the density of larger wild brown trout and largely eliminated self-sustaining rainbow trout stocks from most large trout streams by the 1970s. In major streams such as the Arkansas, Blue, Gunnison, and Rio Grande rivers, brown trout made up 95-99% of the wild trout population.

In 1979, following a [[Wild Trout Symposium]] at [[Yellowstone National Park]], the CDOW Director began a "quality trout" program using restrictive bag limits, size limits, and terminal tackle restrictions. Results were described as an overwhelming success: trout density, biomass, and numbers of quality fish (≥35 cm, or 14 inches) rose substantially. Wild rainbow stocks grew at near-exponential rates, and in several streams rainbows overtook brown trout in density, biomass, and large-fish numbers within 3-5 years. Catch rates rose 200-300%, with rainbows comprising most of the catch.

Motivated by these results, CDOW began stocking rainbow fingerlings in 1984 with the goal of creating self-sustaining wild populations. Three secondary questions guided the work: whether domesticated-strain rainbows survive in the wild as well as wild-strain fish, whether rainbows survive as well as brown trout, and whether long-term survival is adequate for self-sustaining populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** Before stocking, electrofishing showed no wild rainbow population in any study stream, so later increases were attributed to stocking and natural reproduction. Similar numbers and sizes of wild and domesticated fingerlings were stocked together and distinguished by fin clips. Strains compared were [[Hot Creek California Rainbow Trout]] (HCC), Bellaire (BELL), and Tasmanian (TAS) domesticated strains, and the wild [[Colorado River Rainbow Trout]] (CRR) from Middle Park. Starch gel electrophoresis (1993) indicated genetic differences between the CRR and the BELL and TAS strains.

**Animas River.** Two non-contiguous reaches near Durango were managed with an 8 trout/day bag limit from 1977 to 1991. Chronic sediment loading, heavy metal pollution, and organic loading from cattle grazing and development prevented self-sustaining wild trout, so the fishery relied on stocked salmonids.

**Fryingpan River.** Upstream of [[Ruedi Reservoir]], a 1967 impoundment flooded the upper 21 km. Releases from the reservoir's bottom were cold (April-May means 38-41°F), and water below 42°F is lethal to rainbow eggs, so wild rainbow recruitment failed in the 11.37 km reach nearest the dam. A temporary food boom from [[Mysis Shrimp]] entrained in hypolimnial releases (1985-1987) drove total trout biomass above 1000 kg/ha in September 1986; the boom ended in 1988, and brown trout came to dominate.

**Gunnison and Rio Grande rivers.** These served as further study sites with differing regulations and stocking durations, described below.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking history:** Between 1937 and 1941 the Fryingpan was stocked with over 300,000 trout (205,000 rainbow, 69,200 brown, 11,000 cutthroat, 29,000 brook) per Hunter and Parson (1943). From 1968 to 1978, about 10,000 catchable rainbows were stocked annually. Since 1986, 10,000 fingerlings of 7-8 cm have been stocked, which the author describes as a major gain in efficiency.
- **Strain survival:** One-year survival estimates (1985-1989) and multi-year records showed that only the wild-strain CRR and wild brown trout fingerlings survived to age three and beyond. CRR survivors from a 1987 plant were collected in the Rio Grande in October 1992.
- **Stocking size:** Survival advantage was negligible once fingerlings reached about 5 cm, and stocking above 10 cm produced survival rates that were nearly inversely proportional to size. "Bigger is better" was not supported.
- **Private reach test:** About 2,300 CRR trout reared to catchable size were stocked into a 10.8 km private Rio Grande reach in August 1986. An estimated 978 remained six weeks later, and only one marked fish was collected in April 1987.
- **Animas stocking practice:** Fingerlings stocked at under 5 cm during 1981-1986 produced low trout density and biomass, and few brown trout ≥40 cm. From 1986, fingerlings of 7-10 cm were planted in August or September, after runoff, with boat distribution from 1987-1989.
- **Fryingpan predation:** Rainbow density and biomass in the catch-and-release section declined from 1986 to 1992 as brown trout (240-450 kg/ha biomass) foraged heavily on stocked fingerlings. The author states that predation is the likely cause but was not directly demonstrated.
- **Fryingpan regulation effects:** Restrictive regulations (2 trout bag limit, 40.6 cm minimum, fly/lure only) from 1986 to 1992 raised all rainbow and brown parameters steadily. The 1993 regulation on the full 22.6 km reach aimed to cut brown trout while boosting rainbows; early Taylor Creek results were encouraging, but outcomes remained to be confirmed.
- **Angler response:** Despite a 3.7-fold rise in fishing pressure between 1942 and 1986, catch rates and fish size improved under the conservative stocking and restrictive regime.
- **Gunnison River:** Bag and size limits began in 1988, eight years into the study. Rainbow biomass and density ≥35 cm rose steadily, peaking in 1991-1992. Wild rainbow reproduction was first observed in 1989 and confirmed every year through 1992.
- **Rio Grande River:** Three sections (3.5, 5.6, and 10.8 km) were stocked for three, five, and six years. Brown trout showed no clear trend except at Coller Wildlife Area. Rainbow parameters rose during stocking (1984-1988) and peaked in 1989-1992. Wild rainbow fry were first collected in 1988 and reproduction expanded across a wider reach through 1992.
- **Conclusions:** Establishing wild rainbow populations in brown-trout-dominated streams is feasible in under a decade, and the hatchery system was critical for broodstock, egg hatching, and fingerling rearing.
- **Management guidelines (summary):** (1) Use only wild source stock for broodstock; (2) distribute fingerlings by boat; (3) stock 7-12 cm fingerlings; (4) stock July-September on the descending hydrograph; (5) use restrictive size and bag limits; (6) check that incubation temperatures stay above 6°C (42°F), since eggs below that threshold rarely develop beyond the eyed stage.
