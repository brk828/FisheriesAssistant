# Fishes of Cedar Creek and the South Fork of the Cottonwood River, Chase County, Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Percidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 survey by [[Frank B. Cross]] documents the fish fauna of two small streams in the [[Flint Hills Region]] of east-central Kansas: the South Fork of the Cottonwood River and its tributary, [[Cedar Creek]]. Based chiefly on collections from 12 stations (nine on the South Fork, three on Cedar Creek) made in August 1952, supplemented by museum records, the study annotates the species present and discusses their distributions. The South Fork supports a higher proportion of [[Ozarkian Fauna]] species than most Flint Hills streams, which the author attributes to its permanent flow, steep gradient, and rocky riffles. The paper also corrects several earlier identifications and documents apparent local extirpations.

## Background & Description

The study area drains northward through Chase County into the [[Cottonwood River]], a tributary of the [[Neosho River]]. Several Ozarkian species are thought to have dispersed westward onto the plains along this river system, and the extent of that penetration was poorly known.

- **South Fork:** Rises at about 1,500 ft in Butler County and flows about 35 miles north to the Cottonwood River near Cottonwood Falls. Headwater tributaries include Thurman, Mercer and Little Cedar creeks; Rock Creek is the main western tributary. Gradient is 7 ft/mile between Matfield Green and Bazaar and 5 ft/mile below Bazaar, with tributary gradients of 16 to 30 ft/mile.
- **Cedar Creek:** About 27 miles long, entirely within Chase County, with headwaters Cedar, Middle and Bill's creeks and a major tributary, Coon Creek. Gradient is 14 ft/mile above the upper confluence and 5.5 ft/mile below it.
- **Geology:** The South Fork cuts through Council Grove and Chase group rocks (Florence flint, Fort Riley and Cottonwood limestones, Neva limestone, shales). Cedar Creek lies mainly within the Chase group, with some Sumner group rock on the uplands.
- **Land use:** Much of the watershed is virgin bluestem pasture. Bottomlands are cultivated (corn, alfalfa, sorghums, small grains). Streams retain a well-developed fringe forest (elm, oak, hackberry, walnut, willow and others).
- **Substrate:** South Fork bottoms are mostly cherty limestone and shale gravel and rubble, with bedrock in swifter reaches and firm gray clay in pools. Cedar Creek is dominated by mud and has narrower, deeper channels.
- **Conditions at survey:** A recent drought meant the South Fork occupied only about half its channel width. Turbidity was slight, but plankton growth reduced clarity downstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Forage and predators:** The South Fork bass population is supported by abundant forage minnows and crayfish (*Orconectes nais*), with hundreds seined at some stations.
- **Faunal mix:** The South Fork hosts more Ozarkian species than most Flint Hills streams, likely because its permanent flow, gradient, and riffles resemble Ozark streams. Plains species such as the [[Red Shiner]] dominate most plains creeks but were common only at one South Fork station.
- **Habitat associations:** Species were tied to particular conditions. Stonecats were abundant in shale-strewn riffles; the orangethroat darter dominated riffles throughout both streams; the fantail darter was most common near headwater springs; the bluntface shiner preferred moderately fast, clear water; and the redfin shiner inhabited deeper pools.
- **Temperature:** Water temperatures recorded at stations ranged from about 62°F at spring outlets to 84°F in mainstream pools, with spring-fed tributaries markedly cooler.
- **Extirpation:** The [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Sauger]] appear to have been extirpated from the South Fork. The author links the sauger's loss to larger water-level fluctuations, higher temperatures and turbidity from agricultural development.
- **Fishing:** The South Fork is described as one of the best small fishing streams in Kansas for spotted bass, though local anglers reported declining success.

## Key Findings

- **Survey effort:** 12 collecting stations (nine South Fork, three Cedar Creek), sampled August 1952 by Frank B. Cross and Robert L. Carpenter, plus earlier museum material (including [[Jennings 1942]] records).
- **Species checklist:** The annotated list covers roughly 60 taxa across six families (Lepisosteidae, Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Ictaluridae, Centrarchidae, Percidae), including a hybrid. Notable taxa include:
  - [[Spotted Bass]] (*Micropterus punctulatus*): abundant at nearly all stations; young-of-the-year and juveniles common.
  - [[Striped Shiner]] (*Notropis zonata pilsbryi*): the most abundant species in both streams. Earlier Kansas records were reassessed, and the species is largely confined to the Ozark Upland.
  - [[Rosyface Shiner]] (*Notropis rubella*): abundant in the South Fork, and an Ozarkian species.
  - [[Golden Redhorse]] (*Moxostoma erythrurum*): young-of-the-year (32–56 mm standard length) abundant at most stations.
  - [[Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum pullum*): abundant at all stations.
  - Orangethroat darter (*Etheostoma spectabile pulchellum*): abundant on riffles throughout both streams.
- **Taxonomic corrections:**
  - *Notropis cornuta* was removed from the South Fork list; the Rock Creek specimens reported by Jennings are *N. zonata pilsbryi*.
  - A specimen of *Micropterus* from Rock Creek was identified as *M. dolomieui velox*, and the Jennings spotted bass record is partly reassigned to *M. punctulatus*.
  - An undescribed *Hybopsis* (X-marked) and an undescribed *Schilbeodes* madtom are under study by specialists.
- **Sand shiner intergrades:** South Fork and Cedar Creek sand shiners (*Notropis deliciosa*) showed intermediate scale counts (24–28 scale rows, average 25.2), and were tentatively treated as *deliciosa* × *missuriensis* intergrades.
- **Hybrid record:** One *Notropis rubella* × *N. zonata pilsbryi* hybrid was collected at South Fork station 4, intermediate in pigmentation and morphology.
- **Range extensions:** The paper extends known western limits of several Ozarkian species.
- **Conclusion:** The South Fork is a biologically important Ozarkian-influenced stream whose fauna shows signs of change from agricultural development, with some species possibly absent during the low-water survey period but likely to reappear at higher flows.
