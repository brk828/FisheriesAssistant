## Executive Summary
This 1988 proposal by the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) outlines a plan to renovate **Finley Tank**, an artificial spring-fed pond on the National Audubon Society's Research Ranch Sanctuary near Elgin, AZ. The tank currently harbors a population of [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*), introduced c. 1978, which are non-native to the Gila River basin. The proposal identifies two critical conservation issues: (1) the risk of hybridization between non-native Quitobaquito pupfish and the native [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*) planned for reintroduction into nearby Gila basin drainages (e.g., Babocomari Ranch), and (2) concerns that the Finley Tank stock itself may be genetically introgressed due to laboratory mixing practices under Dr. Boyd Kynard, compromising its value as a genetic reservoir for pure Quitobaquito pupfish. The proposed solution is complete piscicidal renovation using [[Rotenone]], followed by recolonization with genetically verified native desert pupfish supplied by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS).

## Background & Description
The source document is a conservation/management proposal and does not provide detailed morphological or meristic data on the pupfish. Key taxonomic distinctions noted are geographic and genetic rather than morphological:

- **Desert Pupfish (*C. m. macularius*)**: Native form historically distributed throughout the Colorado River and Gila River basins; originally described from the San Pedro River. Now extirpated from the vast majority of its historic range.
- **Quitobaquito Pupfish (*C. m. eremus*)**: A distinct subspecies native only to Quitobaquito Spring, a tributary of the Rio Sonoyta drainage (draining directly to the Gulf of California, with no hydrological connection to the Colorado River basin).
- **Genetic Integrity Concern**: The Finley Tank population is suspected to be genetically compromised (introgressed) due to co-housing of multiple pupfish species in shared aquaria at the University of Arizona under Dr. Boyd Kynard, raising doubt as to whether the stock represents "pure" *C. m. eremus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Type**: Finley Tank is a small, artificial impoundment created by a high-retention dam, capturing outflow from a small seepage spring of unclear (possibly anthropogenic) origin. It sits within a small, stable drainage basin in an arroyo system.
- **Sympatric Vertebrate Fauna**: The pond ecosystem supports non-fish vertebrates including the [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] (*Rana chiricahuensis*), [[Mexican Garter Snake]] (*Thamnophis eques*), and [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon sonoriense*). Of these, only the leopard frog is expected to be significantly harmed by rotenone treatment.
- **Invertebrate Community**: Supports an aquatic invertebrate community (including aquatic insects) that is expected to naturally recolonize post-treatment, with full ecosystem recovery anticipated by the end of the summer rainy season. Recolonization could be facilitated by inoculating the tank with water samples from Post and/or O'Donnell canyons.
- **Regional Connectivity/Threat Pathway**: Finley Tank sits upstream (1-2 miles) of the Babocomari Ranch, a planned desert pupfish reintroduction site, creating a direct hybridization risk pathway via downstream escape (natural flooding events or anthropogenic "bait-fish" translocation).
- **Comparable Site**: [[Bog Hole]] in the San Rafael Valley represents an analogous non-native pupfish population slated for similar renovation, underscoring a broader regional conservation strategy for the San Pedro and Santa Cruz river drainages.

## Key Findings
- **Non-native Status Confirmed**: Genetic/taxonomic review determined Finley Tank pupfish are Quitobaquito pupfish (*C. m. eremus*), not native to the Gila River basin, and thus inappropriate for the site.
- **Dual Justification for Removal**: Either (a) the risk of hybridization with reintroduced native desert pupfish, or (b) the potential genetic impurity of the Finley Tank stock itself, independently justifies eradication and replacement.
- **Renovation Method**: Complete eradication via [[Rotenone]] application is proposed, following water drawdown and vegetation removal (via seining or copper sulfate) to maximize toxicant efficacy and minimize refugia for pupfish eggs.
- **Treatment Protocol**: A two-stage rotenone application (separated by 2–4 weeks) is planned to eliminate survivors and newly hatched fish, ideally conducted in early spring (April–early May) to align with herpetofauna activity cycles and minimize impact on leopard frog breeding.
- **Non-target Species Management**: Leopard frogs, garter snakes, and mud turtles would be captured (via seining, live-trapping, hand capture) and held in captivity or relocated during treatment, then returned post-renovation.
- **Recolonization Plan**: Following rotenone breakdown, USFWS would supply native desert pupfish stock to recolonize Finley Tank, contingent on Audubon Society approval.
- **Research Value**: The renovation creates opportunities for baseline [[Conservation Genetics]] sampling (frozen tissue archive of restocked pupfish), and for mark-recapture studies of leopard frogs, garter snakes, and mud turtles, complementing ongoing work by Phil Rosen and addressing a data gap in Arizona leopard frog natural history.
- **Illegal Translocation Risk**: Documented incidents (e.g., a 1986 unauthorized draining event, admitted illegal fish removals/stocking) demonstrate real-world hybridization risk from well-meaning but uninformed actors, reinforcing urgency for renovation.
