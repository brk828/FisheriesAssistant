# Fish Eggs and Larvae of the Upper Chesapeake Bay

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae #engraulidae #ichthyoplankton

## Executive Summary

This Natural Resources Institute Special Report (No. 4), by [[William Dovel]], synthesizes eleven years (1960 to 1968) of plankton-net sampling of fish eggs and larvae in the upper [[Chesapeake Bay]] and its tributaries, including the [[Patuxent River]], [[Magothy River]], [[Susquehanna River]] and the [[Chesapeake and Delaware Canal]]. Nearly fifty fish species were recorded as eggs or larvae, from a total of 5,148,596 eggs and larvae. The report argues that the low-salinity portion of the estuary functions as a common nursery area for freshwater, estuarine and marine spawners, and that water quality there must be favorable, not merely tolerable, to protect these resources.

## Background & Description

The study was initiated in spring 1960 under [[Romeo Mansueti]], focusing on pelagic eggs and larvae in channel areas. Sampling progressed from a 1961 pilot survey, to weekly sampling of the Patuxent from January 1963 (reduced to biweekly in 1964 and 1965), to Magothy River work (March 1965 to May 1966), and upper bay sampling from November 1965. The work was supported by the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]], the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, and the Natural Resources Institute, University of Maryland. The foreword, by L. Eugene Cronin, Director, is dated October 1971.

Methods centred on [[Meter Net]] plankton sampling (0.4 x 0.6 mm mesh) at surface, mid-depth and bottom, with tows standardized to 10 minutes at 1-3 knots. Temperature and salinity were measured with an induction conductivity salinometer. Samples were preserved in 4% formalin, and aliquoting was used for large samples. Salinity was adopted as the principal ecological "yardstick" for the estuary, although the salinity gradient shifts yearly with freshwater input.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Salinity gradients:** The Patuxent is a two-layered estuary, with 56 miles of tidewater. The Magothy is short (7.0 miles), and its salinities at the uppermost station ranged from 5 to 14 ‰ over the 15-month study. Upper bay salinities range from about 12.0 ‰ off Tolchester to 0.0 ‰ off Turkey Point.
- **Susquehanna influence:** The Susquehanna River supplies slightly more than 50% of the freshwater input to Chesapeake Bay, and dams and industrial use have altered its biota.
- **Canal dynamics:** Salinity in the canal stayed between 0.0 and 4.0 ‰ in April to June 1966 and 1967, with maximum currents of 2.5 knots.
- **Oxygen and food:** Summer dissolved oxygen falls below about 50 feet, and food organisms are scarce in deeper water, which keeps brackish-spawner larvae near shallow spawning grounds.
- **Zooplankton:** Herman et al. (1968) describe a high copepod turnover in the low-salinity Patuxent in spring, from about one month in winter to about one day in midsummer.
- **Human pressure:** Low-salinity zones lie near densely populated areas where cities discharge wastes, a central concern of the foreword.

## Key Findings

**Overall community**
- 5,148,596 eggs and larvae representing 48 fish species were processed from January 1960 to November 22, 1968. Eggs numbered 3,879,477 and larvae 1,269,118. Fewer than 0.1% of fish could not be identified.
- Eggs were dominated by [[Bay Anchovy]] (3,310,258) and [[Hogchoker]] (411,954).
- Larvae were dominated by [[Naked Goby]] (about 55.2% of all larvae) and bay anchovy.
- 95% of fish larvae were found in the 0 to 11 ‰ salinity range, which the author terms a "[[Critical Zone]]" or common estuarine nursery.

**Freshwater spawners**
- [[Blueback Herring]] and [[Alewife]] eggs were abundant, but egg identification was often not possible. Blueback herring juveniles showed two age groups in the upper estuary.
- [[White Perch]] eggs totalled 59,736. Larvae were abundant in low salinity waters, and some juveniles stayed in the nursery area for about one year.
- [[Striped Bass]] eggs were most abundant from mid-April to late May, and no eggs were found above 3 ‰. A ratio of 1 striped bass to 23 white perch was found among 2,718 identifiable juveniles in 1966.
- [[Yellow Perch]] larvae were most abundant at very low salinity.

**Estuarine spawners**
- Bay anchovy eggs peaked in July. About 47.6% of eggs came from waters of 13 to 15 ‰. Anchovies probably mature before one year of age and have a short life span.
- The bay anchovy is a major forage fish for striped bass. The author notes its possible value as an untapped resource but cautions against exploitation without considering its forage role.
- [[Northern Pipefish]] showed two spawning peaks, in early May and late July.
- The hogchoker spawns in salinities above 9 ‰ in the lower river. Larvae move upstream to a nursery zone and then return downstream in spring, a cycle that continues to at least the fourth year.

**Marine spawners**
- [[Atlantic Menhaden]] eggs (9,942) were found in the Solomons area and in the upper bay. Young fish entered the upper estuary in late winter and spring.
- [[Atlantic Croaker]] post larvae and juveniles appear to be affected by striped bass predation during cold periods.
- [[Southern Kingfish]] eggs (2,393) were tentatively identified, and larvae survived only 7 days in captivity.

**Movement and survival**
- Freshwater-spawned larvae are carried downstream, while marine and estuarine fish use an upstream subsurface current. Coho salmon and Pacific salmon experiments show that salinity tolerance develops and drives downstream migration.
- Movements through the estuary are rapid, so weekly sampling is a minimum requirement.

**Growth and sampling**
- Growth is rapid from May to late summer, and a schematic (Fig. 12) was preferred over single line fits, which gave correlation coefficients of r = 0.8 but poorly represented growth.
- Each species has a "point of no return" in size beyond which plankton gear is ineffective, ranging from about 10 mm for gobies to over 100 mm for others.
- Eggs or larvae were present in the estuary at almost all times of year.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The upper bay low-salinity zone is a critical nursery area for fish from fresh water, the estuary and the sea. Protecting its water quality is essential for these resources.
- Further research is required for optimal management, including comparison of maturation under fresh and brackish conditions.
