# The Cui-ui

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

The cui-ui (*Chasmistes cujus*) is a large, long-lived, omnivorous sucker endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. Its survival depends on river flows in the [[Truckee River]] that were sharply reduced by [[Newlands Project]] diversions, upstream withdrawals, and drought. Listed as endangered by the federal government in 1967 and by Nevada, the species is maintained by [[Stampede Reservoir]] storage, fish passage at the [[Marble Bluff Fish Facility]], and hatchery augmentation. The authors (Buchanan and Coleman, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) conclude that restoration is not assured until a consistent, clean water supply is guaranteed, and that the population's near-term fate hinges on a small number of year-classes produced since 1980.

## Background & Description

- **Appearance:** Dorsal surface blackish-brown, ventral surface creamy-white; plump, coarsely scaled body.
- **Longevity:** Females live up to 42 years; males rarely reach their late twenties.
- **Growth:** Sexes grow at similar rates until maturity at about 6 to 12 years. Length is about 4 inches at age one and 12 inches at age five. Adult growth slows to about 0.25 inch/year (males) and 0.5 inch/year (females). Maximum length may reach 27 inches.
- **Fecundity:** About 20,000 eggs for a 16-inch female and 105,000 for a 26-inch female. Egg viability declines sharply at about 30 years of age.
- **Taxonomic significance:** The cui-ui is the last pure species of the genus *Chasmistes*. Related [[June Sucker]] (*C. liorus*) and [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*C. brevirostris*) have hybridized with other suckers.
- **Cultural significance:** Archaeological evidence indicates use of Pyramid Lake fishes for at least 4,000 years. The [[Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe]] (Kuyuidokado band; name means "cui-ui eaters") depended on the spawning run, which was a time of shared food and celebration. The fish was historically important alongside the [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (Pyramid Lake strain).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning and early life history**
- Adults hold near the mouth of the Truckee River and migrate upstream in April or May, depending on inflow and temperature. The run lasts four to eight weeks, with about 80 percent entering over 7 to 14 days.
- Spawners mostly remain in the lower 3 to 6 miles of river (a few reach nearly 12 miles). They spend up to 16 days in the river and spawn over gravel in 10 inches to 4 feet of water at 1 to 2 feet per second.
- Most adults probably spawn only once every three or four years.
- Eggs hatch in one to two weeks depending on temperature. Embryo mortality is high when daily maximum temperatures exceed 63°F. Yolk-sac larvae spend 5 to 10 days in gravel before emerging and are swept downstream to the lake.
- In the lake, larvae use the shallow littoral zone and later disperse to deeper water. Adults and larvae feed on zooplankton and benthic invertebrates and are commonly found near the bottom at 50 to 100 feet.

**Historical decline and hydrology**
- Pyramid Lake (about 180 square miles) is a remnant of ancient Lake Lahontan (about 8,700 square miles, roughly 9,000 to 40,000 years ago). The [[Truckee River]] is its only permanent water source.
- Pre-regulation average annual inflow was about 600,000 acre-feet; current flow is roughly halved.
- Pyramid Lake receded 85 feet in 60 years, to its lowest level in nearly 4,000 years. [[Winnemucca Lake]] dried by 1938.
- A delta formed across the river mouth; by 1926 fish were stranded, and reproduction was limited to high-water years.
- Historic spawning reached about 25 miles upstream in the 1800s; today runs go 6 to 12 miles.

**Water rights and diversions**
- The [[Orr Ditch Decree]] (1944) adjudicated rights with priority dates from 1859 to 1902 and allocated the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe an irrigation right for about 5 percent of historic normal flow, without regard for lake fish needs.
- The [[Newlands Project]] has diverted Truckee River water since 1907 through Derby Dam. Its delivery efficiency in 1985 was only 55 percent.

## Key Findings

1. **Population status:** Only two major year-classes were produced from 1950 to 1980 (1950 and 1969). The 1969 class (about 100,000 fish) dominates the adult population.
2. **Recent runs:** Six successful spawning runs occurred since 1980, averaging 13,600 fish (range 5,000 to 36,300). Hundreds of millions of larvae were produced, but population statistics are lacking to judge whether these year-classes can sustain the species.
3. **Water supply:** Stampede Reservoir has 226,000 acre-feet of capacity, but usable storage averages about 50,000 acre-feet per year, versus 400,000 acre-feet needed to maintain Pyramid Lake (minimum 200,000 acre-feet in spring).
4. **Court protection:** A 1982 federal court ruling required Stampede water to be used for the Pyramid Lake fishery until the cui-ui and Lahontan cutthroat trout are delisted or other sources are found. The Ninth Circuit affirmed it, and the Supreme Court declined review.
5. **Management:** The FWS recovery goal is an optimum self-sustaining population in the Truckee River/Pyramid Lake system. Hatchery releases were 7.7 to 11.7 million larvae per year from 1982 to 1986. The tribe took over hatchery operations in 1978.
6. **Research:** A large FWS research program ran from 1981 to 1983 but was later reduced by budget cuts.
7. **Projected threats:** Reno/Sparks may face drought-year shortages by 1992. Sierra Pacific Power has proposed obtaining about 50 percent of Stampede's storage, and Washoe County applied for 9,000 cubic feet per second.
8. **Water quality:** Nutrient accumulation in Pyramid Lake from urban growth could harm cui-ui survival.
9. **Management outlook:** Newlands Project efficiency targets of 63 percent (Bureau of Reclamation) are insufficient. Efficiencies of 80 to 85 percent would substantially benefit the fishery. The authors call for a basin-wide water-management plan.
