# Radiotelemetry of Razorback Suckers in the Gila River, Eastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study used internal radiotransmitters to track hatchery-reared and wild [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) released into the [[Gila River]] in May and October 1988. The work aimed to learn the fate, movements, and habitat use of reintroduced fish, information the authors considered essential for evaluating a long-running [[Reintroduction Program]]. Of 23 tagged fish, most moved, but significantly more moved downstream than upstream. One fish was confirmed dead, most tracked fish were lost from contact, and the ultimate fate of one fish remained unknown. Fish occupied wide, sandy, mid-channel runs of modest depth and current velocity. The authors concluded that radiotelemetry is feasible for this species and recommended its use to guide conventional monitoring.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid fish native to the Colorado River system. Its populations were largely extirpated from the Gila River, and a [[Reintroduction Program]] began in the 1980s. Early stocking relied on hatchery-reared fish. Survival of newly planted fish was low, and stocking of larvae and small juveniles was abandoned in favor of larger, more predator-resistant fish. Earlier work had shown that juvenile razorback suckers moved downstream after release, but the behavior of stocked adults was unknown.

The study was conducted by Arizona State University with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and the [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]] (partial funding and equipment), with fish supplied by the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Radiotransmitters were surgically implanted into fish, a procedure described in detail under [[Radio Transmitter Surgery]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study reach.** Fish were released at the [[Old Safford Bridge]], Greenlee County, Arizona. Tracking covered the river from the Arizona–New Mexico state line downstream about 55 km to the stocking site, then another 38 km to the [[San Jose Diversion]]. At low flow, most of the river is diverted into irrigation canals at this point and re-emerges from subsurface flow a few hundred metres to a kilometre or more downstream. Searches extended about 90 km downstream to the delta of [[San Carlos Reservoir]].

**Water conditions.** Low flow in September reduced riffle depths to a few centimetres in places. This increased the chance that fish would be stranded and exposed to predators. Water in the reservoir had conductance above 1000 µmhos/cm at 25 °C, which attenuates radio signals.

**Predation.** The authors found clear evidence of attacks by terrestrial or aerial predators or scavengers. Tooth marks were present on a recovered transmitter, and one transmitter was located in an apparent raptor nest on a cliff above the river. Whether fish were eaten after death or killed by predators could not be determined.

## Key Findings

### Study Design and Sample

| Group | Fish | Source and handling | Release |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group I | 14 fish: 10 hatchery-reared (4-, 6-, and 7-year-olds) plus 4 wild adults | Internal radiotags, released immediately after surgery | 24 May 1988, Old Safford Bridge |
| Group II | 9 hatchery-reared 4-year-olds | Surgery performed at the hatchery; fish recovered in an outdoor pond | 12 October 1988, Old Safford Bridge |

- Group II transmitters were implanted on 30 August 1988. Two transmitters were recovered and successfully re-implanted into healthy fish by hatchery staff. All nine implanted fish appeared to be in good condition at release.
- Habitat data were collected at each fish contact. Fish were located by triangulation, then visually confirmed in clear water or forced to move by a wading investigator in turbid water. Stream width, depth, substrate, and current velocity were estimated at each point. Where data were not recorded at contact, sites were revisited within days at similar discharge and habitat was reconstructed.

### Group I Movements (May 1988 release)

- All Group I fish stayed within 0.8 km of the release site during the first 12 hours. Movement occurred mainly at night.
- Over the following days, **3 of 14** fish moved upstream an average of **7.2 km** (range 0.2–23.7 km). These fish remained at large for an average of **10 days** (range 1–32).
- **11 fish** moved downstream an average of **11.9 km** (range 1.5–32.0 km). Contact was maintained for an average of **19 days**.
- Habitats used by upstream and downstream movers did not differ significantly (t-test, n = 39, p > 0.05).
- Occupied habitats averaged about **10 m** wide, **0.4 m** deep, and had a current velocity of **0.21 ± 0.17 m/s** (n = 80 contacts).

### Group I Mortality and Loss of Contact

- By late June (32 days after release), **1 of 14** fish was dead, and its transmitter was recovered with tooth marks.
- Contact was maintained with two other transmitters, but neither fish nor transmitter could be recovered.
- Contact was lost with two other fish. Only two fish with functional transmitters remained alive.
- Radiotracking by truck along about **128 km** of irrigation canals in Safford Valley (twice) and along the **90 km** river reach to the San Carlos delta (once) found no contacts. Straight-line detection distances in air ranged from about 50 m to more than 150 m. Surveys were considered reliable because water was shallow (rarely above 50 cm) and equipment was operated close to the water.

### Group II Movements (October 1988 release)

- **7 of 9** fish moved downstream an average of **8.1 km** (range 0.1–37.2 km) and were tracked for an average of **39 days**.
- Contact was lost with two fish shortly after release.
- Occupied habitats averaged about **10 m** wide, **0.5 m** deep, and had a current velocity of **0.30 ± 0.20 m/s** (n = 11 contacts). Velocity was higher than for Group I fish, but the difference was not significant (t-test, n = 39, p > 0.05).
- No contacts were recorded during later radiotracking of all major canals and the Gila River reach down to the San Carlos delta.

### San Carlos Reservoir Search

- Reservoir searches in April showed that signal range shrinks with depth. A transmitter suspended at **3.0 m** could not be detected, and range at **1.0 m** depth was about **5.0 m**.
- Shallow shorelines, deltaic areas of both arms, and coves near Coolidge Dam were searched thoroughly for **3 days** without any signals detected.
- Reduced range may also reflect water conductance, transmitter condition (including battery life), receiver condition, or some combination of these.
- Short contact periods for many Group II fish may have resulted from transmitter battery failure, since most batteries had been active for periods approaching their rated lifetimes.

### Habitat Use

- Radiotagged fish occupied mainly **wide, sandy, mid-channel runs** of modest depth and current velocity. This is the most common habitat type in the study reach of the Gila River.
- Eddies, quiet backwaters, and deep pools were available but used by only a few individuals.
- Similar habitat relationships were reported for radiotagged adult razorback suckers in the [[Green River]], Utah (Tyus 1987), even though the Green River is substantially larger than the Gila River.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- Radiotagged razorback suckers stocked into the Gila River showed **significantly more downstream than upstream movement** (chi-square = 13.50, df = 1, p < 0.01). Earlier studies of smaller juveniles in the Gila River reported similar results.
- The ultimate fate of **1 of 23** radiotagged fish remains unknown. The authors suspected that some fish were removed by predators, moved into canals and onto irrigated fields, or moved into San Carlos Reservoir. Burial of transmitters in substrate was considered unlikely because other buried transmitters (to depths of 0.3 m) were recovered.
- The study demonstrated the feasibility of radiotelemetry for this species in the Gila River and helped identify logistical problems.
- To reduce short-term mortality, the authors recommended implanting transmitters in the hatchery, allowing recovery time after surgery and handling, and stocking during relatively high-flow periods (e.g., late autumn–winter) to avoid predation by terrestrial and aerial animals.
- Evaluation of the 10-year reintroduction program requires knowledge of the fate of stocked fish. Recaptures had been inadequate for this purpose (Minckley et al. 1990). The authors recommended additional radiotelemetry to locate habitats and waters where tagged fish live, so they can be sampled by conventional methods. They also recommended tracking fish in backwaters to quantify apparent diel movement patterns, and intensive sampling of fish in major receiving reservoirs downstream of release sites.
