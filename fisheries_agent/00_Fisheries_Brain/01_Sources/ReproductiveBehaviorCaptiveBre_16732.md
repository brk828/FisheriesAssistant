# Captive Breeding and Restoration Ecology of Endangered Southeastern Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study reports captive propagation of rare southeastern U.S. stream fishes with contrasting spawning modes: the [[Blackside Dace]] (nest-associate spawner), the [[Spotfin Chub]] (fractional crevice spawner), and the [[Boulder Darter]] and its surrogate, the [[Bloodfin Darter]] (egg-clumping crevice spawners). The authors, working through [[Conservation Fisheries Inc]], report success with nine species overall. Captive production is presented as a complement to in situ habitat protection, providing life-history data and stock for [[Reintroduction]] into streams where populations were lost. Survival of deposited eggs was variable but generally high, at 50–90%. The authors argue that early life-history behavior (embryo drift, benthic settling, egg burial by siltation) constrains recovery, and they call for proactive conservation, regional gene-pool facilities, and integration of taxonomy and field biology.

## Background & Description

**Context of decline.** Darters (Percidae), madtoms (Ictaluridae) and minnows (Cyprinidae) dominate lists of rare southeastern fishes, yet conservation funding has been sparse. Two historical events are described:

- **Green River, 1962:** Rotenone poisoning of roughly 600 km of river for a trout stocking program ahead of the [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] eliminated several populations of now-endangered Colorado River fishes.
- **[[Abrams Creek]], 1957:** A reclamation project using ichthyocides for trout fishing permanently eliminated nearly half of the 63 fish species in a 19.4 km reach. Four species now federally listed as endangered or threatened were extirpated, largely because the [[Chilhowee Reservoir]] impoundment isolated the creek.

**Species profiles.**

- **Blackside dace (*Phoxinus cumberlandensis*):** A schooling minnow of small streams in the upper [[Cumberland River]] drainage (southeastern Kentucky, northeastern Tennessee). Listed as threatened in 1987 after [[Coal Strip Mining]] degraded many streams. Females can produce more than 1500 eggs. It spawns only with pebble-nest builders such as [[Stoneroller]] and [[River Chub]].
- **Spotfin chub (*Cyprinella monacha*):** Historically widespread in the [[Tennessee River]] drainage, now in four extant tributary systems. Listed as threatened in 1977.
- **Boulder darter (*Etheostoma wapiti*):** Restricted to about 96 river km of the mainstem [[Elk River]] in Tennessee and Alabama. Listed as endangered in 1988. Believed extirpated from [[Shoal Creek]] by water pollution, although water quality there has improved after wastewater upgrades.
- **Bloodfin darter (*Etheostoma sanguifluum*):** Member of the *E. maculatum* group, used as a surrogate because boulder darters are too rare to spare.

**Prior and parallel projects.** Captive propagation and reintroduction had been successful for the [[Smoky Madtom]], [[Duskytail Darter]] and [[Yellowfin Madtom]] (Rakes et al.). CFI was contracted by the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service and the Tennessee Wildlife Resources Agency to propagate fishes for Abrams Creek. Attempts to restock spotfin chubs from wild stock into Abrams Creek failed to establish a population, and by 1992 the program moved to captively produced fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Genetics and metapopulations.** Strange & Burr analyzed genetic variation in blackside dace and found that migration maintains populations. The species can recolonize degraded habitat when conditions improve, but only if protected refugia and dispersal corridors remain. Because of genetic differences, metapopulations should be managed separately, and reintroduction may be needed where natural reinvasion is impossible.

**Spawning modes.** The study covers three reproductive strategies:

- *Nest-associate spawning* (blackside dace): eggs are broadcast among gravel and pebbles of a minnow-built nest.
- *Fractional crevice spawning* (spotfin chub): eggs are deposited in rock crevices on the stream bottom.
- *Egg-clumping in crevices* (boulder and bloodfin darters): eggs are clumped and guarded by a male in wedge-shaped spaces.

**Threats identified.** Surface coal mining and siltation (blackside dace), impoundments, pollution and agricultural impacts (boulder darter), and fragmentation and loss of unsilted spawning substrate (spotfin chub) are all cited. The authors note that the spotfin chub's fragmented distribution may reflect limited clean spawning substrate.

## Key Findings

### Methods and Husbandry

- Fish were held at [[Conservation Fisheries Inc]] (Knoxville, TN) in centralized recirculating systems of 900 L or 2400 L, with 75 L, 380 L and other tanks.
- Water was deionized (reverse osmosis) and blended with dechlorinated tap water and buffers to match natural pH and hardness.
- Temperature and [[Photoperiod]] were gradually cycled from a winter low of 12°C to about 26°C.
- Eggs were incubated in plastic trays with flowing water. Larvae were fed copepods, ostracods, [[Artemia]] nauplii and rotifer-size dry food; juveniles received blackworms, bloodworms and Daphnia.

### Blackside Dace

- **Broodstock:** 24 adults (12 females, 12 males) were seined from Buck Creek, Whitley County, Kentucky, on 7 May 1993. The stock was being removed from a stream reach isolated above a coal settling pond, where it was aging and not reproducing.
- **Spawning trigger:** Males were moved in with females once spawning ceased, and milt from river chubs and/or stonerollers was introduced, consistent with a pheromone or chemical cue hypothesis.
- **Results:** About 300 eggs were recovered three days after introduction (approximately 230 fertile). Two weeks later, about 500 eggs were collected from a second episode, of which about 100 were fertile. All adult dace died within three days of spawning.
- **Thresholds:** The tank with eight males and four females spawned, while the tank with four males and eight females did not, suggesting a minimum number of reproductively active males is needed.
- **Release:** 260 individuals were released at two sites in the Buck Creek watershed in October 1993. Success was undetermined at time of writing.
- **Captive generations:** Juveniles reached 55–60 mm TL at one year. A second spawning in May 1995 (with a nuptial river chub) yielded about 125 eggs, fewer than half fertile, and about 40 F₁ fish were reared. The F₁ suffered senescence and mortality. As of February 1998, about 12 F₂ fish (about four years old) were maintained.

### Spotfin Chub

- **Broodstock:** Nine immature fish were collected in fall 1992 and summer 1993.
- **Maturity and spawning:** Fish matured in summer 1994 at about two years. Spawning occurred from 13 July to 13 September 1994, after four months at a 15 h photoperiod and 20–24°C. Feeding was increased to 2–3 times daily before spawning.
- **Behavior:** One dominant male controlled the spawning site. Mating lasted only a fraction of a second. Eggs were placed exclusively in the bottom crevice adjacent to the floor. Neither parent cared for eggs, and exposed eggs were cannibalized.
- **Substrates:** Stacks of 8–15 unglazed ceramic tiles (15.3 × 15.3 × 0.5 cm) with 2–3 mm crevices were the most useful substrate, modified from Snyder (1993).
- **Development:** Larvae remained benthic until about 30 days and 18–23 mm TL.
- **Release:** 700 young and the nine adults were released into Abrams Creek in November 1994, and most of the remaining 1500 young in April 1995. Twenty-five young and nine wild-caught fish (summer 1996) remained in captivity.

### Bloodfin Darter (Surrogate)

- **Broodstock:** Eleven fish were collected from the Big South Fork of the Cumberland River at Station Camp Creek in October 1993. Two large males (>60 mm TL) and two females (about 50 mm TL) were used.
- **Spawning:** In 1994, two females mated with one male in 30–40° crevices between tiles, producing about 20 eggs per mating event. Clutches were stacked, and embryos within one nest hatched synchronously within 24 h.
- **Survival:** Survivorship was low because larvae could not initially be fed minute prey. A commercial dry rotifer-size food proved effective.
- **Release:** Fish reached 55 mm TL at five months and were released into the Big South Fork.

### Boulder Darter

- **Broodstock:** Three fish (one female, two males) were collected from the Elk River, Lincoln County, on 20 September 1994.
- **First spawning (1995):** Fish became reproductive in April 1995. Sixteen eggs were produced from three matings, of which nine were viable.
- **Juveniles:** Five juveniles measuring 35–50 mm TL were held as of 31 December 1995.
- **Second season (May 1996):** The same pair produced four clutches over 30 days, with 20–30 eggs per mating. The largest guarded clutch contained about 120 eggs. The male also mated with a female offspring, producing about 150 further eggs in five clutches over 12 days. Of about 375 eggs deposited by the two females, about 265 were fertile.
- **Development:** Larvae were pelagic for 12–20 days. More than 50% required 30 days to become benthic, and a few remained pelagic for up to 45 days.

### Discussion and Implications

- **Infertile "dummy" eggs:** Infertile eggs were found in all blackside dace clutches. The authors speculate they dilute predation on viable eggs, as proposed for nest associations (Johnston 1991, 1994). No other species is known to use this strategy.
- **Siltation risk:** Blackside dace eggs are negatively buoyant and embryos remain in gravel crevices for up to two days, making them vulnerable to smothering by sediment in strip-mined streams.
- **Substrate limitation:** Spotfin chubs spawn only in lowermost crevices and their larvae are benthic, so availability of unsilted spawning substrate may limit distribution. Other, more abundant *Cyprinella* spawn in woody debris or off-bottom crevices.
- **Drift and downstream loss:** Boulder darter embryos may drift for many days, so the lack of clean benthic habitat downstream of spawning areas could contribute to decline. A broader riverine perspective is needed for management.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- A proactive strategy is required to protect southeastern fishes. Regional facilities for living and frozen gene pools are needed, and captive propagation should be developed for common representatives of as many taxa as possible to serve as surrogates for rare species.
- Life-history information is often incomplete, yet it is needed to select reintroduction sites, identify limiting life stages, and design spawning substrates.
- Successful recovery depends on integrating taxonomy (accurate phylogenies), field biology, and laboratory research. The authors warn that declining emphasis on systematics and field work at universities threatens this integration.
- Captive propagation is presented as useful for refining water quality standards and for watershed restoration, and for revealing behavioral constraints on reproduction in altered habitats.
