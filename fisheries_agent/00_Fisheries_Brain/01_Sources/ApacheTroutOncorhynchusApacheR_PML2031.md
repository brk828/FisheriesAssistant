# Apache Trout Recovery Plan (Second Revision)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This second revision of the [[Apache Trout Recovery Plan]], approved March 5, 1998 by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS), addresses *Oncorhynchus apache*, a [[Salmonidae|salmonid]] listed as threatened. The plan was prepared by the [[Apache Trout Recovery Team]] with members from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), the USFWS, the [[U.S. Forest Service]] (USFS), and the [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]] (WMAT).

- **Current status:** At least 14 unhybridized populations exist within historic range in Gila, Apache, and Greenlee Counties, Arizona, representing twelve discrete natural stocks of pure Apache trout. Eleven introduced populations await genetic testing, and two introduced populations established outside historic range in the late 1960s were confirmed unhybridized.
- **Threats:** Habitat alteration, competition and predation by non-indigenous fishes, and hybridization are the principal threats.
- **Recovery objective:** Delist Apache trout. Delisting should be initiated in 2000 if recovery criteria are met.
- **Recovery criteria:** Replicate all natural pure stocks, eliminate non-indigenous fishes from replicate streams, increase genetic sampling, monitor predominant streams, maintain a hatchery program, and address threats through combined fisheries and land management.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and listing history.** Before the turn of the twentieth century, Apache trout were the only salmonid resident to streams originating in the [[White Mountains]]. Introduced trout and habitat degradation eliminated or reduced most populations within about 50 years. The species was listed as endangered in 1969, protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1973, and downlisted to threatened in 1974 or 1975 (sources differ). It was historically known as *Salmo apache* and has been called *Salmo gilae*, *Salmo pleuriticus*, and *Salmo mykiss pleuriticus* in earlier literature. The nomenclature change to *Oncorhynchus apache* followed [[Robins et al. 1991]].

**Morphology.** Apache trout have a deep, compressed body, a large dorsal fin, and pronounced, roundish, medium-sized spots, often uniformly spaced. Ground colour is yellowish or yellow-olive, with purple and pink tints on live fish. Dorsal, pelvic, and anal fins have cream or yellowish tips. Meristic counts reported include 58–61 vertebrae, 21–41 pyloric caeca, 133–172 lateral line scales (range of means 146–158), and 32–40 scales above the lateral line (range of means 34–36). Some fish with a red lateral band from [[Hurricane Creek]] were later confirmed as Apache trout by biochemical genetics.

**Genetics.** Apache trout have 56 diploid chromosomes with 106 arms. Isozyme polymorphisms serve as diagnostic markers for distinguishing Apache trout from [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarkii*).

**Distribution.** Historically, Apache trout inhabited most streams of east-central Arizona's White Mountains, including the headwaters of the Little Colorado, Salt, and San Francisco rivers. Their native range overlaps with [[Gila Trout]], and some specimens from Oak Creek and Chitty Creek have been ambiguous. Current pure populations are confined largely to headwater reaches above natural barriers.

**Life history and diet.** Spawning occurs from March to mid-June, varying with elevation, and redd construction begins at water temperatures of 8°C. Fecundity is size-related; fish of 13.1–19.1 cm TL produced 72–238 eggs, while 29.84–34.92 cm fish produced 646–1,083 eggs. The smallest mature female was 13.0 cm TL and the smallest mature male 14.5 cm TL, corresponding to a spawning age of 3 years. Eggs hatch in about 60 days. Diet shifts with size: fish 6–9 cm mainly consume Ephemeroptera, while fish 15 cm and larger consume more Trichoptera; terrestrial insects are eaten by all sizes. In [[Ireland Valley Reservoir]], Apache trout were omnivorous, feeding on surface and bottom organisms, zooplankton, crustaceans, snails, leeches, nematodes, and fish.

**Controlled propagation.** Hatchery broodstock is reared at [[Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]] (WCNFH). Hatchery fish grow rapidly, with females often exceeding 600 mm and 2,200 g within four years, and fecundity 2–10 times that of wild fish. Hatchery production rose from 1,200 in 1986 to 550,000 in 1990. The 1993 world angling record was 2.71 kg (610 mm TL), taken from [[Hurricane Lake]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** Information on life stages is limited. Apache trout occupy coldwater headwater streams subject to extreme temperature and flow variation. Winter anchor ice and ice bridges are common. Occupied streams have low pool-to-riffle ratios, widths greatly exceeding depths, and habitats dominated by riffles and runs.

**Non-indigenous species.** Rainbow, cutthroat, brook (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), and brown (*Salmo trutta*) trout were stocked for over 50 years. Non-indigenous salmonids compete with and prey on Apache trout. Competition with brown and brook trout was identified as a cause of decline [[Rinne and Minckley 1985]]. Cutthroat trout stocking was recorded from at least 1920 to 1942, and rainbow trout stocking from 1934 to 1954. Some stocking continues under Section 7 review.

**Habitat degradation.** Forestry, mining, grazing, reservoir construction, agriculture, and associated road building reduced habitat, especially at lower elevations. The range is estimated to have fallen to about 48 stream km, compared with an estimated original range of about 965 km on the Reservation, and the present distribution of confirmed unhybridized populations is at least 140 km.

**Conservation history.** The WMAT closed streams to fishing from 1955 onward and built Christmas Tree Lake. The AGFD established hatchery broodstock at Sterling Springs State Fish Hatchery in 1963. Populations were re-established in several national forest streams. Recovery actions since the 1980s have included stream renovation with [[Antimycin A]] and reintroduction, and interagency habitat surveys on the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]] (ASNF). Two 1993 USFS Decision Notices and a third 1995 notice addressed habitat restoration on Apache trout streams.

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** Of 31 wild populations sampled (1987–89), 11 were unhybridized; rainbow trout introgression occurred in 19 populations, and cutthroat-related hybridization in four. Seven additional streams were later sampled, bringing pure populations to at least 16, representing 12 discrete natural stocks.
- **Hybridization:** Backcrossing toward Apache trout predominated in 18 of 19 hybridized populations. [[KP Creek]] was the exception, where backcrosses toward rainbow trout were more common. Hybrid analysis used 645 individuals genotyped at 55+ loci.
- **Source populations:** Boggy/Loafer, Coyote/Mamie, Crooked, Deep, East Fork White River, Firebox, Flash, and North Canyon creeks are identified as genetic sources for introductions and broodstock.
- **Replication plan:** Several pure populations are to be replicated into other streams, such as Coyote/Mamie into Ord Creek and Soldier Creek into Coleman and Snake creeks. Unless populations become critically small, no hatchery fish should be introduced into replicate streams.
- **Management targets:** 33 streams are to be managed to contain Apache trout free of competition and hybridization. Ten enhancement streams, totalling 317 km, will support sport fishing and non-self-sustaining populations. Forty recovery streams totalling 418 km are the goal.
- **Genetic diversity:** Three alleles found in the wild but absent from the East Fork White River hatchery broodstock should be incorporated into the hatchery to preserve variability and limit inbreeding depression.
- **Monitoring:** Seven partially hybridized streams require periodic resampling, and 13 streams need fish and habitat monitoring at permanent stations to evaluate restoration.
- **Delisting outlook:** The team believes delisting can occur as early as 2000 if the plan's steps are completed. Its feasibility rests on manageable threats from hybridization, competition, predation, and habitat degradation.
