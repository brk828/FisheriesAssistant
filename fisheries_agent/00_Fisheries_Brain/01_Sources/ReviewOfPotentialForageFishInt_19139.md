# Review of Potential Forage Fish Introductions for Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #forage-fish #species-introduction #striped-bass

## Executive Summary

This 1986 report by the Arizona Game and Fish Department evaluates 18 potential forage fish species for introduction into [[Lake Mead]] to support declining [[Striped Bass]] populations. [[Threadfin Shad]] populations had decreased during the late 1970s, resulting in poor growth and emaciation of striped bass. The Colorado River Wildlife Council's Striped Bass Committee commissioned this literature review to assess alternative forage species. After evaluation, the committee recommended against any forage fish introductions due to Lake Mead's low productivity and potential impacts to existing species, including endangered fishes in the [[Colorado River]] system.

## Background & Description

[[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) were introduced into Lake Mead in 1969 when [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) populations were abundant. As efficient predators, striped bass preyed extensively upon threadfin shad. However, shad numbers decreased during the late 1970s, causing striped bass to experience poor growth. Adult striped bass became emaciated and apparently declined in numbers during the early 1980s.

The report examines 18 candidate species across multiple families:

**Atherinidae (Silversides):**
- [[Mississippi Silverside]] (*Menidia audens*): 55-125 mm, surface-dwelling, spawns May-July at 17-34°C, feeds on insects and zooplankton
- [[Brook Silverside]] (*Labidesthes sicculus*): 63-112 mm, spawns June-August at 18-24°C, surface-dwelling

**Catostomidae (Suckers):**
- [[Lake Chubsucker]] (*Erimyzon sucetta*): 130-316 mm, spawns March-April, produces 3,000-20,000 eggs per female, feeds on zooplankton and benthic insects

**Clupeidae (Herrings and Shads):**
- [[Blueback Herring]] (*Alosa aestivalis*): 250-381 mm, anadromous, spawns April-September at 10.5-24°C, produces 45,800-349,700 eggs per female
- [[Alewife]] (*Alosa pseudoharengus*): 254-356 mm, spawns April-August at 15.6-22.2°C, produces 60,000-100,000 eggs per female, found at 50-100 m depth
- [[American Shad]] (*Alosa sapidissima*): 250-350 mm, anadromous, produces 277,000-659,000 eggs per female, die-offs occur after spawning
- [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*): 338-442 mm, spawns April-June at 10-21.1°C, produces 28,545-543,910 eggs per female, feeds on phytoplankton and zooplankton

**Salmonidae (Ciscoes and Smelts):**
- [[Longjaw Cisco]] (*Coregonus alpenae*): 270-340 mm, spawns in fall at 20-50 m depth, found at 100-300 m
- [[Lake Herring]] (*Coregonus artedii*): 279-558 mm, spawns in fall at 4-5°C, produces 6,000-29,000 eggs per female
- [[Deepwater Cisco]] (*Coregonus johannae*): 250-300 mm, spawns August-September, found at 32-200 m
- [[Kiyi]] (*Coregonus kiyi*): up to 254 mm, spawns October-January at 1.7-3.4°C at 100-183 m depth
- [[Bonneville Cisco]] (*Prosopium gemmiferum*): up to 216 mm, endemic to Bear Lake (Utah-Idaho), spawns January at 0.6-5.6°C, produces 3,600 eggs per female
- [[Pond Smelt]] (*Hypomesus olidus*): 75-150 mm, spawns April-June, produces 18,000-33,000 eggs per female
- [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*): 75-250 mm, spawns in spring at 8.9-18.3°C, produces 8,500-69,600 eggs per female, carnivorous

**Cyprinidae (Minnows and Chubs):**
- [[Lake Chub]] (*Couesius plumbeus*): 60-227 mm, spawns April-August at 14-19°C, produces 500 eggs per female
- [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*): 127-356 mm, spawns June-August at 10.5-20°C, produces 4,900-83,800 eggs per female, tends to overpopulate
- [[Silver Chub]] (*Hybopsis storeriana*): 102-231 mm, spawns May-June at 21°C in pelagic areas at 9-20 m depth
- [[Emerald Shiner]] (*Notropis atherinoides*): 51-120 mm, spawns July-August at 24°C, eggs hatch in 24 hours, populations fluctuate widely
- [[Spottail Shiner]] (*Notropis hudsonius*): 64-132 mm, spawns May-July, produces 100-2,600 eggs per female, found at 1-20 m depth
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*): 46-90 mm, spawns late May-early September, extremely tolerant of high temperatures (up to 39.5°C)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mead is characterized by low productivity, which limits forage fish production potential. The reservoir experiences significant seasonal temperature fluctuations and water level changes associated with hydroelectric operations on the [[Colorado River]] system. The presence of endangered fishes in the Colorado River system was a major concern regarding species introductions.

Most candidate species are native to cooler northern waters or require specific spawning conditions (flowing water, specific substrates, temperature regimes) not readily available in Lake Mead. Several species (alewife, American shad) are prone to massive die-offs from temperature stress or spawning mortality. Many species are primarily littoral or surface-dwelling, making them unavailable to pelagic striped bass.

Competition for zooplankton and other food resources between introduced forage species and existing fish communities (including game fish and native species) was identified as a significant concern. Several species (rainbow smelt, Utah chub) are known to prey on fish eggs and larvae, potentially impacting native and sport fish reproduction.

## Key Findings

**Primary Recommendation:** The Colorado River Wildlife Council's Striped Bass Committee recommended **no forage fish introductions** into Lake Mead based on this evaluation.

**Key Limiting Factors:**
1. **Low Productivity:** Lake Mead's oligotrophic conditions limit potential forage fish production
2. **Endangered Species Concerns:** Risk of impacts to endangered Colorado River fishes
3. **Habitat Mismatch:** Most candidate species require conditions not present in Lake Mead
4. **Availability Issues:** Many species are littoral or surface-dwelling, unavailable to pelagic striped bass

**Species-Specific Conclusions:**

*Unsuitable for Pelagic Stripers:*
- Mississippi and brook silversides: surface-dwelling, rarely below 2 feet, primarily littoral
- Spottail shiner: vertical distribution limits availability
- Red shiner: limited seasonal availability, primarily littoral

*Potential Concerns:*
- Alewife: excellent forage but prone to massive die-offs from temperature fluctuations
- American shad: die-offs after spawning, young grow too large quickly
- Rainbow smelt: carnivorous, may impact endangered fishes and native fish eggs/larvae
- Utah chub: tends to overpopulate
- Gizzard shad: rapid growth, soon too large except for large striped bass

*Competition Issues:*
All candidate species would compete with existing forage and game fish for zooplankton, insects, and other food resources.

**Alternative Management Strategy:**
The report suggests enhancing existing [[Red Shiner]] populations in Lake Mead by providing spawning and escape habitat in bays and coves, which could increase forage for [[Largemouth Bass]] and other centrarchids without introducing new species.
