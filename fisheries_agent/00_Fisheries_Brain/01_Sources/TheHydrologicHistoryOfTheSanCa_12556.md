# Hydrologic History of San Carlos Reservoir, Arizona, 1929–71

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrology

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Professional Paper (655-N), by Frank P. Kipple, reconstructs the hydrologic history of [[San Carlos Reservoir]] on the [[Gila River]] in Arizona for water years 1929–71. It focuses on [[Evapotranspiration]] (ET) from the exposed reservoir land, lake [[Evaporation]], [[Bank Storage]], and [[Reservoir Sedimentation]], using a [[Water Budget]] approach. The document is a hydrologic and sedimentation study. It contains no fish, invertebrate, or aquatic-species data, so the species fields are marked N/A.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Part of the [[Gila River Phreatophyte Project]], a U.S. Geological Survey study of the hydrologic effects of phreatophyte control. The aim was to evaluate reservoir ET and its change from 1929 to 1971, along with secondary objectives on sediment, lake evaporation, and bank storage.
- **Dam and reservoir:** [[Coolidge Dam]] was built from January 1927 and completed in October 1928. Impoundment began 15 November 1928. It is a multidomed structure, 850 ft (259 m) long including two spillways, rising 203 ft (62 m) to the spillway crest at elevation 2,511 ft (765 m). Maximum storage at 2,523 ft (769 m) is 1,267,000 acre-ft. The lowest outlet sill is at 2,382.63 ft (726 m).
- **Purpose of reservoir:** Irrigation storage for about 100,000 acres (50,000 acres of Indian lands and 50,000 acres of private land in the Florence–Casa Grande Valley). Power generation (two generators, 10,000 kVA combined), recreation (leased to the San Carlos Apache Indian Tribe), and incidental flood protection.
- **Legal framework:** A 1935 court decree (U.S. vs. Gila Valley Irrigation District) apportioned Gila River water between upstream users and the San Carlos Project based on priority and on the amount stored in the reservoir.
- **Survey history:** Five capacity surveys were used: 1914–15, 1935, 1937, 1947, and 1966.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Phreatophytes and channel plugging:** Shallow water tables and periodic inundation on the upstream flat favor [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix*), which encroaches on the inflow channel. During 1962–65 the Gila River inflow channel was blocked by encroaching saltcedar, reduced gradient, and deposition of floating debris. The plugging progressed 1.5 mi (2.4 km) in 1963, 1.8 mi (2.9 km) in 1964, and 0.8 mi (1.3 km) in 1965, mainly from summer flows. A dragline channel was excavated in summer 1965.
- **Summer floods:** [[Summer Monsoon Flooding]] from intense thunderstorms strips debris from small tributary watersheds and delivers it to the Gila River.
- **Climate:** Mean annual precipitation was 13.87 in. (352 mm) for 1931–71, with 42% falling in July–October. Mean annual pan evaporation was 97.3 in. (2,470 mm). Pan evaporation at San Carlos showed a downward trend, which was judged inconsistent with the precipitation trend and was tested against nearby stations (Mesa, Roosevelt Lake, Tucson).
- **Ground water:** Ground-water inflow came from flood-plain alluvium and basin-fill inflow. Total ground-water inflow is about 15.6 acre-ft per day in the Gila arm and 6.3 acre-ft per day in the San Carlos arm.

## Key Findings

**Water budget (1931–71)**
- Mean inflow shares: [[Gila River]] 78.2%, [[San Carlos River]] 14.5%, ground water 3.5%, precipitation 2.2%, tributary flow 1.6%.
- Outflow shares: Gila River release 78.2%, ET 11.3%, lake surface evaporation 10.5%.
- Mean annual ET from the reservoir water budget: 26,230 acre-ft (32.3 hm³) per year. Mean ET depth from the exposed area: 1.47 ft (0.448 m). Including precipitation on the exposed surface, total ET depth is 2.62 ft (0.80 m) per year.
- Mean annual evaporation from the water surface: 24,611 acre-ft (30.3 hm³) per year, or a depth of 69.95 in. (1,780 mm). The mean pan coefficient was 0.80 for 1964–71 energy-budget and mass-transfer computations.

**Inflow**
- Mean annual combined inflow for 1929–71 was 214,940 acre-ft (265 hm³), with a median of 159,000 acre-ft. The Gila River mean was 181,490 acre-ft and the San Carlos River mean 33,450 acre-ft.
- The San Carlos River drains 8.6% of the contributing area but supplies 15.6% of streamflow.

**Sedimentation**
- Surface-water capacity fell from 1,266,837 acre-ft (1,562 hm³) in November 1928 to 1,170,118 acre-ft (1,443 hm³) in 1966, a loss of 96,719 acre-ft (119 hm³), or 7.6%.
- Usable surface-water storage fell by 72,476 acre-ft (89 hm³). Dead storage fell 96% from 25,143 to 900 acre-ft.
- Mean annual sediment deposition was 2,553 acre-ft (3.1 hm³), equal to 0.20% of original capacity. Mean deposition over the 1929–66 water years was 2,853 acre-ft (3.15 hm³) per year, or 68% of the 1914 prediction.
- The 1914 U.S. Army Corps of Engineers prediction was 83% trap efficiency; trap efficiency is probably 96% or more (Gottschalk, in Chow, 1964).
- Mean sediment concentration was 13,684 ppm (13,808 mg/L) for 1928–66.
- Sediment deposition rates by period were 0.0198, 0.0059, 0.0084, and 0.0110 (volume deposited/volume of streamflow).

**Bank storage**
- Usable bank storage at the spillway elevation of 2,511 ft was about 152,800 acre-ft (188 hm³) for 1931–47 and about 159,200 acre-ft (196 hm³) for 1948–71. This is about 14% of total usable storage at maximum level.
- The shift in the rating reflects increased bank storage from sediment accumulation.

**Stage and storage**
- Highest stage of record: 2,501.62 ft (762.49 m) on 18 March 1942.
- Stage exceeded 2,439 ft 25% of the time, 2,416 ft 50% of the time, and 2,392 ft 75% of the time.
- Usable surface-water storage exceeded 176,000 acre-ft 25% of the time, 64,000 acre-ft 50% of the time, and 11,000 acre-ft 75% of the time.

**Simulation and ratings**
- A sediment-deposition simulation (inflow, distribution, and compaction phases) was used to interpolate storage changes between surveys. Compaction was dropped from the final model because its effect was small relative to simulation error.
- Optimum summer-to-winter concentration ratios ranged from 1.35 to 13.2. The fraction of coarse sediment averaged 0.39.
- Computed capacity ratings differed from curve-fitted ratings by a maximum of 763 acre-ft (0.063% of total capacity), with a mean difference of 0.23%.

**Conclusions**
- Existing reservoir records allow an inexpensive evaluation of reservoir performance, including bank storage, evaporation and transpiration losses, and sediment accumulation.
- Changes in ET caused by vegetation growth could not be conclusively detected in the data. Apparent increases between periods were within the range of annual variability.
- The simulation model needs further testing before it can reliably predict the location of sediment deposits or estimate compaction.
