# Grazing Minnows, Piscivorous Bass, and Stream Algae: Dynamics of a Strong Interaction

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how [[Campostoma]] (algae-grazing minnows), [[Largemouth Bass]] (piscivorous predators), and [[Filamentous Algae]] are distributed among pools of a small stream, [[Brier Creek]], in south-central [[Oklahoma]]. Seven censuses of 14 consecutive pools (November 1982 to September 1983) showed that bass and Campostoma were spatially segregated in most surveys, with complementarity significant in six of seven censuses. Campostoma and algae were also inversely related in late summer and autumn. Field manipulations, in which bass were added to a Campostoma pool and Campostoma were added to a bass-free pool, showed that these patterns arise from rapid within-pool processes: grazing predation, predation by bass, and predator avoidance by minnows. The authors conclude that these biotic interactions strongly shape pool-to-pool distributions during long periods of low discharge, and that floods and between-pool movements disrupt the pattern.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** The study covered a 1-km reach of Brier Creek in Marshall County, Oklahoma (approx. 34°1'N, 97°2'W). The stream is free-flowing except in very dry summers. Pools rarely exceed 2 m in depth at normal flow, most have maximum depths of 30–70 cm, and pools are separated by shallow riffles only 2–3 cm deep. Substrates include bedrock, cobble and gravel.

**Fish assemblage.** Minnows dominate pool fish numbers, including Campostoma anomalum, Notropis species, and a topminnow (Fundulus notatus). Other species include green sunfish (Lepomis cyanellus), longear sunfish (L. megalotis) and largemouth bass. Over seven snorkeling censuses, Campostoma made up 35% of the fish counted (617 of 1,745 individuals).

**Invertebrates.** The invertebrate fauna is described as relatively depauperate. Chironomid and ceratopogonid larvae occur in moderate numbers, while mayfly and odonate larvae are less common. Caddisflies and snails are sparse except in very shallow riffles, and crayfish are rare.

**Background literature.** The authors note that herbivorous fishes are well documented in marine systems but much less studied in freshwater streams. Of roughly 45 North American minnow, sucker, killifish and pupfish species that are at least partly algivorous, 21 are primarily herbivorous. Earlier work by [[Power and Matthews 1983]] found that bass and Campostoma segregate among these pools, but the mechanism (predation, predator avoidance, or both) was unknown.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Grazing and predation control.** Grazers and predators can strongly structure benthic communities. In the absence of predators, grazers can denude substrates of attached algae, removing food and cover for other aquatic organisms. Predators can also alter prey impacts by reducing prey numbers, constraining feeding in space or time, or causing prey to emigrate or shift habitat.

**Flow regime.** Stream stage data came from staff gauge readings and debris lines. Two major floods occurred during the study: a crest of 1.7 m on 17 March 1983 and 2.7 m on 14 June 1983 above base flow. All other stage fluctuations in the year were under 0.5 m. Floods scour algae from most substrates, and algae recover conspicuously only about 1–5 weeks later. Low discharge (approx. 0.01–0.02 m³/s) keeps riffles isolating pools for long periods.

**Algal community.** Algal assemblages were dominated by [[Spirogyra]] and [[Rhizoclonium]] (attached filamentous green algae), along with blue-green filaments (mainly Oscillatoria) and diatoms (Cyclotella, Fragilaria, Synedra, Achnanthes, Navicula, Gomphonema). Algal standing crop was estimated by measuring the height of algal mats. Height correlated strongly with ash-free dry mass per square centimetre (r = 0.91, n = 23, P < .001) and with density indices for all algae sampled (r = 0.16, n = 953, P < .001).

## Key Findings

**1. Pool-to-pool segregation of bass and Campostoma.** Bass and Campostoma were spatially segregated among the 14 pools for most of the year, despite eight instances in which pools switched between "bass" and "Campostoma" status. Complementarity was significant in six of seven censuses (.03 > P > .001, [[Fisher's Exact Test]]). Bass and Campostoma co-occurred in more than two pools on only two census dates, both following the year's largest floods. Where they coexisted, bass occupied deep areas and Campostoma occupied shallow areas.

**2. Campostoma and algae complementarity.** Algal standing crops were consistently low in Campostoma pools and high in pools lacking Campostoma that contained bass. Complementarity was significant on 8 November and 19 November 1982 and on 5 September 1983 (P = .025, .05, and .002, respectively). It did not hold on two June 1983 dates, when floods strongly affected algal distribution. On 13 June, four Campostoma pools had well-developed algal crops, with 27–93% of sampled sites bearing algae more than 2 cm high. Six days after the flood on 18 June, none of the 14 pools had conspicuous algae.

**3. Campostoma addition to a bass pool (autumn 1983).** Bass were removed from Pool 5 by electroshocking, and 12 Campostoma (approx. 1.4 individuals/m²) were added to one side, chosen by coin toss. Within 3 days, algal standing crop on the Campostoma side was significantly reduced (P = .025, Wilcoxon Matched Pairs Signed Ranks Test). Algae on the side without Campostoma increased by 3 October (P < .001, Wilcoxon Test). By 3 October, the Campostoma side was largely devoid of algae and contained large amounts of detritus. The Spirogyra bloom on the control side did not occur on the Campostoma side. Green Campostoma feces composed mainly of intact Spirogyra filaments appeared after 26 September.

**4. Control pool.** In an unmanipulated Campostoma pool (density 3.1 individuals/m²), algal standing crop did not increase over the two-month study, suggesting that grazing by minnows prevented autumn algal blooms. The higher Campostoma density in the control pool, compared with 1.4 individuals/m² on the experimental side of Pool 5 and 0.8 minnows/m² in Pool 9, may account for the lower algal crop there.

**5. Bass addition to a Campostoma pool (September 1983).** Three largemouth bass (18, 15, and 28 cm SL) were added to Pool 9, which had about 70 Campostoma (approx. 0.8 individuals/m²) and no bass prior to the experiment.
- Campostoma moved from the deepest part of the pool (60–70 cm) to shallow areas within 2 h 40 min of bass addition.
- By the next day, the school had left a 10–30 cm deep upstream pocket after a water snake (Nerodia sp., about 70 cm long) was seen nearby.
- Over the five-week experiment, the number of Campostoma sighted in Pool 9 dwindled, and minnows spent more time hiding under cobbles.
- Some Campostoma emigrated through a blocked upstream section, with two recovered on 24 September; this count likely underestimated emigration.
- Algal standing crop in shallow areas (5–20 cm) decreased significantly from 9 to 13 September (P = .05, Wilcoxon Paired-samples test). In deeper areas, algae increased; standing crop was significantly higher by 26 September (P < .025).

**6. Second bass-addition experiment (June 1984).** Before bass were added to Pool 9, snorkeling counted 74 adult Campostoma (4–8 cm SL) and 35 juveniles (2–3 cm SL).
- Within one hour, large Campostoma had moved to shallow areas and avoided deep water for the rest of the experiment (shifts to shallow areas were highly significant for large and small fish, P < .0005 and < .005, Kolmogorov–Smirnov Test).
- Large Campostoma feeding rates fell from 10.8 bites/min (SE = 2.3, n = 35) to 3.2 bites/min (SE = 1.3, n = 34) after bass addition (t = 3.22, P < .005).
- Juvenile feeding rates were not significantly different before (31.1 bites/min) and after (20.5 bites/min) bass addition.
- Emigration of large Campostoma through escape ports occurred over the following week, with 25–40 individuals moving between Pools 9 and 10.
- At the end, 10 large and 13 juvenile Campostoma were unaccounted for and presumed eaten by bass.
- Algal standing crop in shallow areas was not significantly different from pre-addition levels, but in deeper water it had become significantly higher (P < .01, Wilcoxon Test), due to the development of a 2–10 mm "fuzz" of diatoms, Rhizoclonium and Spirogyra on previously bare bedrock and cobbles.

**7. Rapid within-pool dynamics.** Campostoma reduced algae to low levels within 9 days of introduction to Pool 5. Bass displaced Campostoma from deep to shallow areas within hours, and changes in minnow distribution and activity led to increases in algae within one week. The authors argue that these within-pool processes are fast enough to account for the abrupt pool-to-pool changes seen in the natural stream.

**8. Role of flow and stream size.** The strong biotic structuring in Brier Creek appears linked to long periods of low, constant discharge. Shallow riffles limit fish movement and drift algae between pools, and small pools with low structural complexity can be rapidly restructured by biotic interactions when disturbed. Preliminary data from larger Oklahoma and Arkansas streams suggest that Campostoma and bass do co-occur in pools there. In the Baron Fork of the Illinois River (a fourth-order channel with deep riffles and larger, more complex pools), the dominant bass is the smallmouth bass (Micropterus dolomieu), which may prey on crayfish in preference to fish. The authors suggest that large, complex pools and movement through deep riffles may prevent the development of distinct "Campostoma" and "bass" pools.

**9. Theoretical implications.** Theory predicts that spatial segregation of predators and prey is favored when prey disperse faster than predators, or when predators migrate only during restricted periods (e.g., [[Huffaker 1958]]; [[Hastings 1977]]). The authors suggest that discharge-driven dispersal in streams may provide natural tests of these models.
