# Tag-Nachtrhythmus von Baetidenlarven in der „Organischen Drift"

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Baetidae #Ephemeroptera #invertebrate-drift

## Executive Summary
This 1963 study by K. Müller documents a pronounced **diel (day-night) rhythm** in the "organic drift" of mayfly larvae (**Baetis rhodani** and **Baetis vernus**) in the Breitenbach, a small tributary stream of the upper Fulda River in the Central German Uplands. Using systematic filtration of stream water at sunrise and sunset, researchers found that the overwhelming majority (~93–96%) of drifting Baetidae larvae were captured during nighttime hours, with drift intensity peaking around dusk and dawn. The pattern shows one or two nightly maxima depending on season, is not correlated with water temperature, and is not fully explained by light intensity alone — suggesting an **endogenous (internal) biological rhythm** underlies the behavior, though negative phototaxis (previously demonstrated by Scherer) may also contribute.

## Background & Description
- **Taxa studied**: Larvae of two mayfly (Ephemeroptera) species belonging to the family **[[Baetidae]]**: *Baetis rhodani* and *Baetis vernus* (referred to collectively as "Baetidenlarven").
- **Study site**: The **Breitenbach**, a small headwater stream feeding into the upper **[[Fulda River]]**, located in the Central German Uplands ("mitteldeutsches Bergland"). Sampling occurred approximately 500 m downstream from the stream's source.
- **Research institution**: Limnologische Flußstation Schlitz, a branch of the Hydrobiologische Anstalt der Max-Planck-Gesellschaft.
- **Phenomenon of interest**: "**Organic drift**" — the passive/active downstream transport of benthic invertebrates in flowing water, a well-known but incompletely understood behavior in lotic (running-water) ecosystems.
- **Methodology**:
  - Daily sampling at sunrise and sunset using specialized sieve/filter devices ("Siebvorrichtungen").
  - Each sample filtered approximately 35 m³ of stream water (~1/10 of total discharge).
  - Sampling period: 1 May – 30 September 1962.
  - Additional finer-resolution sampling (1–2 hour intervals) was conducted to characterize the shape of the diel drift curve, notably during a short intensive period (21–29 August 1962) and again in October 1962.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Total catch**: 28,240 organisms captured in the drift over the study period; of these, **3,064 were Baetidae larvae**.
- **Water temperature**: Found to be relatively stable due to the site's proximity to the stream source ("Quellnähe"), especially on days without solar input. Critically, **temperature fluctuations did not correlate with drift patterns** — temperature changes lagged behind both light changes and drift changes at dawn and dusk, ruling out temperature as the primary driver.
- **Light regime**: Drift increases began during evening twilight and decreased during morning twilight, implicating light as a contributing cue. However, light alone could not fully account for the drift curve's structure (including double maxima), pointing toward an **endogenous rhythm** rather than a purely exogenous (light-driven) response.
- **Phototaxis link**: Prior work by **Scherer (1962)** demonstrated pronounced **negative phototaxis** in *Baetis rhodani* larvae. The authors propose that this phototactic behavior may interact with or partially explain the elevated nocturnal drift, a hypothesis flagged for further investigation.
- **Seasonal variation**: The ratio of larvae drifting per daytime hour vs. per nighttime hour varied by month, always strongly favoring night:

| Month | Day:Night Drift Ratio (per hour) |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|
| May | 1:27 |
| June | 1:83 |
| July | 1:63 |
| August | 1:29 |
| September | 1:27 |

## Key Findings
- **Baetid mayfly larvae exhibit a strong, consistent nocturnal drift pattern**, with drift rates per hour dramatically higher at night than during the day across all months sampled (ratios ranging from 1:27 to 1:83, day:night).
- Across the full study period (1 May – 30 Sept 1962), **93.7%–96.1% of drifting Baetidae larvae were captured during nighttime hours**, based on the summary table of day/night drift counts and percentages.
- Fine-scale (1–2 hour interval) sampling revealed that nighttime drift is not uniform but follows **maximum curves ("Maximumkurven")**, with **one or two distinct peaks** depending on the time of year (observed in August and October 1962 datasets).
- Drift increases begin at **evening dusk** and drift decreases occur at **morning dawn**, tightly bracketing the dark period.
- **Water temperature is decoupled from drift timing** — it is comparatively stable near the spring source and lags behind both the light cycle and the drift response, effectively excluding temperature as a proximate cause of the rhythm.
- The authors conclude that light exposure alone cannot fully explain the double-peaked nocturnal maxima, and infer the likely presence of an **endogenous circadian-type rhythm** governing larval drift behavior.
- Future work (flagged in the paper) aims to determine whether the previously documented **negative phototaxis** in *Baetis rhodani* larvae ([[Scherer 1962]]) is mechanistically linked to the observed increase in nocturnal drift.
- This work builds on earlier drift methodology established by **[[Müller 1953]]** (Institute of Freshwater Research, Drottningholm).
