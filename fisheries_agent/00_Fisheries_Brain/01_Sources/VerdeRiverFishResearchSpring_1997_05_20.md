# Verde River Fish Research — Spring 1997 Survey

## Executive Summary
This report summarizes a fishery survey conducted by the USDA Forest Service (Tonto National Forest) in collaboration with [[John Rinne]] (Rocky Mountain Research Station) and Jerome A. Stefferud, examining fish assemblages and instream habitat conditions at seven sites along the upper [[Verde River]], Arizona, during spring 1997. The study documents the relative abundance of native and nonnative fish species and quantifies physical habitat parameters (depth, width, velocity, discharge) at each sampling location. The data reflect ongoing concern over the status of native Gila River basin fishes — several of which are imperiled — in the context of increasing nonnative fish presence.

## Site Locations
Seven sites were sampled along the upper Verde River:
- [[Burnt Ranch]]
- FR638
- [[Duff Spring]]
- [[Bear Siding]]
- [[Perkinsville]]
- [[Black Bridge]]
- [[Sycamore]]

## Background & Description
Species-specific morphological detail is not provided in this data summary; however, the survey distinguishes among the following taxa, each with well-documented Sonoran Desert fish morphotypes:

- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*) — small-bodied cyprinid, key native minnow of the Gila basin.
- **[[Desert Sucker]]** (*Catostomus clarkii*) — subterminal, cartilaginous-edged mouth adapted for algae scraping.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]** (*Catostomus insignis*) — larger-bodied catostomid, benthic invertivore/omnivore.
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*) — native cyprinid of conservation concern, robust body form suited to pool habitats.
- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*) — small, laterally compressed cyprinid adapted to swift riffle habitats; federally listed as threatened.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*) — small benthic cyprinid, habitat generalist.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) — nonnative cyprinid, deep-bodied minnow.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and **[[Flathead Catfish]]** (*Pylodictis olivaris*) — nonnative ictalurids, piscivorous/benthic predators.
- **[[Mosquitofish]]** (*Gambusia affinis*) — small nonnative poeciliid, live-bearer.
- **[[Green Sunfish]]** (*Lepomis cyanellus*) and **[[Smallmouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus dolomieu*) — nonnative centrarchid predators.
- **[[Fathead Minnow]]** (*Pimephales promelas*) — nonnative cyprinid, tolerant of degraded habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The upper Verde River supports a mixed assemblage of native Gila River basin fishes and introduced warmwater species, reflecting a broader pattern of native fish decline across the [[Gila River Basin]]:

- **Native species** ([[Longfin Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Spikedace]], [[Speckled Dace]]) dominate numerically at several sites, particularly at Bear Siding and Perkinsville, where sucker species (Desert and Sonora Sucker) comprised over 30% relative abundance.
- **Nonnative species** ([[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Fathead Minnow]]) were present at variable but sometimes substantial proportions, with notable nonnative dominance at Duff Spring and Black Bridge (>90% nonnative composition reported at some sites, largely driven by [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Red Shiner]]).
- Site-level habitat differences (velocity, depth, discharge) likely structure the differential distribution of riffle-adapted natives (e.g., [[Spikedace]], [[Speckled Dace]]) versus pool/run-associated nonnative predators (e.g., [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]]).
- The coexistence of federally sensitive native cyprinids with predatory/competitive nonnative species at several sites underscores the conservation significance of upper Verde River reaches as native fish refugia.

## Habitat Data Summary (Physical Parameters by Site)
Instream habitat measurements were collected concurrently with fish sampling at all seven sites (Table 3), including channel length, width, depth, cross-sectional area, water velocity, discharge, and volume.

| Site | Avg. Depth (m) | Avg. Velocity (m/s) | Avg. Discharge (m³/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burnt Ranch | 0.24 | 0.75 | 0.24 |
| FR638 | 0.24 | 0.98 | 0.40 |
| Duff Spring | 0.26 | 1.60 | 0.49 |
| Bear Siding | 0.43 | 2.67 | 0.47 |
| Perkinsville | 0.25 | 1.17 | 0.38 |
| Black Bridge | 0.34 | 1.74 | 0.54 |
| Sycamore | — | — | 0.31 |
| **All Sites (mean)** | **0.30** | **1.61** | **0.56** |

- Bear Siding exhibited the highest average water velocity (2.67 m/s) and greatest depth (0.43 m) among sampled sites, suggesting swifter, more turbulent riffle/run habitat.
- Burnt Ranch and FR638 had comparatively lower velocities and shallower depths, more indicative of low-gradient run/pool habitat.
- These physical gradients across the seven sites likely correspond with the observed shifts in native versus nonnative species composition.

## Key Findings
1. Seven sites on the upper Verde River were surveyed for fish community composition and physical habitat characteristics in spring 1997.
2. Thirteen fish species were documented, comprising six native Gila basin species ([[Longfin Dace]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Spikedace]], [[Speckled Dace]]) and seven nonnative species ([[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Fathead Minnow]]).
3. Relative abundance of native versus nonnative fishes varied considerably by site, with some locations (e.g., Bear Siding, Perkinsville) retaining strong native dominance while others (e.g., Duff Spring, Black Bridge) showed high nonnative presence.
4. Habitat data indicate substantial variation in channel velocity, depth, and discharge among sites, which may help explain differential distribution patterns of riffle- versus pool-adapted fish species.
5. The presence of [[Spikedace]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] — both species of significant conservation concern in the Gila River basin — alongside competitive/predatory nonnatives such as [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Green Sunfish]] highlights the upper Verde River's importance as both a refuge and a risk zone for native fish conservation.
