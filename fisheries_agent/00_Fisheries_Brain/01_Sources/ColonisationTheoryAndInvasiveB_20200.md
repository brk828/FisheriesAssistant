# Colonisation Theory and Invasive Biota: The Great Fish River, a Case History

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper presents a primer on colonisation theory and applies it to invasive fishes in the [[Great Fish River]], South Africa. It aims to (a) characterise successful invader species in aquatic systems and (b) identify habitat properties that make a system susceptible to invasion. The authors also standardise terminology for introduced biota. The case study centres on fish translocated into the river through the [[Orange-Fish Tunnel]], which opened in May 1975 as part of the [[Orange River Project]]. The authors conclude that theoretical predictors of invasion success are of limited practical value, and that the pattern of invasion in the Great Fish River is clear but its ecological consequences remain undetermined.

## Background & Description

**Terminology.** The authors define several key terms (Table I): [[Introduced Species]], [[Exotic Species]], [[Alien Species]], [[Foreign Species]], [[Indigenous Species]], [[Endemic Species]], [[Established Species]], [[Invasive Species]], [[Translocated Species]], [[Transplant]] and [[Colonisation]]. Terms were previously used interchangeably, so standardisation is a stated goal.

**Factors affecting invasion success.** Table II divides influences into intrinsic factors (fecundity, growth rate and size at maturity, r/K-strategy, reproductive guild, genetic plasticity, trophic level, rate and mode of dispersal) and extrinsic factors (physico-chemical features, competition, disease, parasitism, food availability, habitat). Table III lists habitat characteristics that favour invasion, including depauperate fauna, few predators or parasites, abundant food, structural redundancy of indigenous fauna, disturbance by man and post-stress conditions.

**Colonisation theory.** Drawing on [[Island Biogeography]] (MacArthur, 1972), small populations are more prone to extinction than large ones. Successful colonisers therefore need a rapid rate of population increase. Figure 1 shows that a population with a birth rate 1000 times its death rate escapes the vulnerable small-population stage far faster than one with birth and death rates in near balance. Immigration and extinction rates of isolated communities tend toward equilibrium, and isolated drainages are compared with islands, each with its own microhabitats (see [[River Continuum Concept]]).

**Niche theory.** Following [[Hutchinson]] (1957), a niche is a multidimensional hypervolume. Whittaker et al. (1973) describe functional and place niche concepts. The [[Competitive Exclusion Principle]] holds that sympatric species cannot coexist indefinitely in the same niche, so an invader must outcompete natives or partition resources. The authors argue that in practice species usually differ in at least one niche dimension.

**Population growth and life-history strategy.** Growth follows exponential (J-shaped) curves without environmental resistance and sigmoid (logistic) curves with it. [[R-Selection]] species show rapid development, early maturity, high fecundity, density-independent mortality and dominance in variable or recovering environments. [[K-Selection]] species show slow development, late maturity, density-dependent mortality and dominance in stable environments (Table IV, adapted from Pianka, 1970). The authors note that Caswell (1982) considered this dichotomy impractical, because the extremes are rarely realised.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regional ichthyofauna.** Southern African drainages fall into four regions: East Coast, South Coast, Orange and Limpopo. Southern African rivers are depauperate in indigenous fish and show high endemism. Species richness declines with increasing southerly latitude. Southern coastal rivers have small discharges and catchments, and their fish faunas are correspondingly small. Table V lists indigenous species counts and catchment areas. The Great Fish River has 11 indigenous species and a catchment of 18,793 km², compared with 62 species and 51,290 km² for the Limpopo.

**Natural instability.** Most South African drainages other than the Limpopo experience severe natural fluctuations in water quantity and quality. This has produced resilient faunas that are less susceptible to exotic invasion. The authors argue that exotics which are ecological equivalents or close relatives of indigenous species pose a greater threat than unrelated foreign species.

**Orange-Fish Tunnel.** The tunnel is 82.45 km long, with a diameter of 5.33 m and a gradient of 1:2000. It passes beneath the Suurberg Plateau, with its intake at Oviston on [[Verwoerd Dam]] (Hendrik Verwoerd Dam) in the Orange River drainage and its outlet in the Teebus River, within the Great Fish catchment. It was built to support irrigated farming in the Great Fish and Sundays drainages. Water flow converted the once-seasonal Fish River into a permanently flowing system. Orange River water is turbid: Secchi readings in [[Lake Le Roux]] ranged from 10 to 160 cm, and about 90% of suspended sediment settles in Lake Verwoerd. Fish passing through the tunnel face a grid at the intake, baffles, and "pepper-pot" valves under a 70 m static head. Adults are unlikely to survive transit, but eggs and larvae may survive.

**Catchment and climate.** The Great Fish River drains mostly arid country, and its upper tributaries flow only after regional storms. Most of the catchment receives 250–500 mm of rain per year, with some areas as low as 125 mm. The Koonap and Kat rivers, draining the Suurberg Mountains, receive 500–1000 mm.

**Habitat change.** Before the tunnel, the lower mainstem flowed intermittently and the upper river was a series of isolated pools with few species. Permanent flow opened new niches for colonisation. The authors suggest that habitat alteration may threaten native fish as much as invasive species do. Orange River effects are confined to the mainstem between the tunnel and the river mouth, and tributaries are unaffected.

## Key Findings

**Survey history and first records.** Fish surveys began in October 1973 and ran to February 1979, concentrated in [[Grassridge Dam]]. Before the tunnel opened, only [[Labeo umbratus]], [[Cyprinus carpio]] and [[Barbus anoplus]] were recorded there. The first translocated species were caught in February 1977: [[Barbus aeneus]] and [[Labeo capensis]]. [[Clarias gariepinus]] followed in February 1978. Sampling was suspended in 1979, and [[Gephyroglanis sclateri]] was recorded in May 1983, the fourth translocated species.

**Exotic component of the fauna.** Ten of the 26 recorded species (38%) are exotic. Five are present as a direct result of the tunnel: Barbus aeneus, Labeo capensis, Labeo umbratus, Clarias gariepinus and Gephyroglanis sclateri. Other exotics include [[Lepomis macrochirus]], [[Micropterus salmoides]] and [[Tilapia sparrmanii]]. Table VI also lists native, restricted-range species such as [[Sandelia bainsii]] and [[Barbus pallidus]]. Several species are marked as suspected but unrecorded, including [[Oreochromis mossambica]] and [[Salmo gairdneri]].

**Barbus aeneus.** This species prefers flowing water. It was recorded in Grassridge Dam in 1976 and has not been observed further downstream. Grassridge Dam appears to act as a faunal filter that limits downstream dispersal.

**Labeo capensis.** Downstream dispersal has occurred since 1977, but abundance remains low. The species prefers lentic conditions and is caught mainly in flowing water upstream of the impoundment. Cambray and Jubb (1977) suggested competition with L. umbratus could develop, but evidence is lacking.

**Labeo umbratus and Barbus anoplus.** Both are native to the Orange and Fish drainages, so it is uncertain whether specimens from the Teebus River are indigenous or tunnel-introduced. L. umbratus prefers rocky riffle habitat and is hard to sample in turbid water. Its apparent disappearance from Verwoerd Dam by June 1975 is probably a sampling artefact. The authors propose electrophoretic studies to resolve these questions.

**Threat to native species.** Sandelia bainsii is restricted to the Koonap and Kat rivers, and Barbus pallidus to the Cap River tributary. Both are likely threatened even without translocated species. The authors state that little can yet be determined from existing data.

**Overall conclusions.** The authors describe the invasion pattern as fairly clear and consistent, but say the study is at an early stage and long-term monitoring is needed. The idea that the Fish River community has been enriched by species additions is attractive but unconfirmed. They conclude that the theoretical factors for predicting invasion are useful mainly for describing events during and after invasion, not for forecasting outcomes. Each system must therefore be assessed on its own terms. The paper's main recommendation is long-term population monitoring and genetic research on the species of concern.
