# Seasonal Variations and Abundance of Cladocera and Copepoda in the Fall and Verdigris Rivers, Kansas

#entomostraca #cladocera #copepoda #limnology #zooplankton

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study from [[Kansas State Teachers College]] (Emporia) examines the seasonal abundance of [[Cladocera]] and [[Copepoda]] (collectively called entomostraca) and the physical-chemical conditions of the [[Fall River]] and [[Verdigris River]] in southeastern Kansas. Sampling ran from April 1954 to March 1955 at eight stations, four on each river, between Neodesha and Independence. The Fall River is impounded upstream by the [[Fall River Reservoir]], while the Verdigris River has no large impoundments. The work was financed by the Standard Oil Company (Indiana), and the author notes that little prior published data existed for Kansas rivers. The study is presented as preimpoundment baseline data for a proposed federal dam on the Verdigris River. The document is a journal article, not a fish study, although its companion paper in the same volume ([[Schelske 1957]]) covers the fishes of the same rivers.

## Background & Description

**Study rationale.** The author cites [[Pennak 1949]] on the need for year-round aquatic ecology studies and [[Schelske 1957]] on the lack of limnological data for Kansas rivers, where numerous federal reservoirs had been built or authorized. Earlier Kansas work was limited to a few publications, including [[Leonard and Ponder 1949]], [[Ratzlaff 1952]], and [[Andrews and Breukelman 1952]]. [[Brooks 1956]] later completed a dissertation on Kansas Cladocera.

**Study area.** Both rivers lie in the southeastern corner of Kansas within the [[Arkansas River Basin]]. The Verdigris River is about 351 miles long, relatively sluggish, and both overflows in heavy rain and becomes intermittent in dry periods. The Fall River is about 90 miles long and, because of the reservoir about 54 miles above its mouth, held a fairly constant water level in the study area. Dominant riparian flora included cottonwood, black willow, elm, sycamore, hackberry, poison ivy, ragweed, and smartweed.

**Stations.** Four stations lay on the lower ~10 miles of the Fall River (II, III, V, VI) and four on a ~20-mile middle section of the Verdigris River (I, VII, VIII, IX). Station IV, a storage lagoon of the [[Standard Oil Company Refinery]] at Neodesha (~13 acres, about 90 acre-feet), was excluded, as was Station X on the Elk River. The lagoon was noted for abundant plankton, and its releases into the Fall River were considered likely to introduce zooplankters at Station V. Substrates ranged from sand and gravel to rocky or silty bottoms, with riffles, limestone outcroppings, and abundant [[Cladophora]] in several reaches.

**Methods.** Each plankton sample was 4 liters of water collected with a [[Kemmerer Water Sampler]], strained through bolting silk (173 meshes per inch), and preserved in formalin (about 10 per cent). Organisms were counted with a Sedgwick-Rafter cell and identified using keys in [[Pennak 1953]] and [[Ward and Whipple 1945]]. Sampling was weekly during the first five months, when conditions changed rapidly because of drought, and roughly bimonthly afterward. Physical-chemical variables were measured as follows:

- [[Seston]] by centrifugation, drying, and oxidation (organic and inorganic fractions by weight).
- [[Turbidity]] with a U.S. Geological Survey turbidimeter.
- Water temperature with a Whitney underwater electrical resistance thermometer.
- Flow data from the Army Corps of Engineers, Tulsa District.
- Chemical analyses (free carbon dioxide, pH, methyl orange alkalinity, [[Dissolved Oxygen]], total hardness) by the Standard Oil Company laboratory at Neodesha, following Standard Methods (American Public Health Association).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and flow.** Surface temperatures ranged from 1.5°C (Fall River, Station II, January 1955) to 39.2°C (Station V, July 23, 1954). In the Verdigris River the minimum was 0.7°C (Station I, February 12, 1955) and the maximum 37.2°C (Station IX, July 17, 1954). Station III ran consistently warmer because the refinery released heated cooling water about 400 yards upstream. Thin ice covered most stations in January and February. Maximum flow occurred in April. Monthly mean discharge ranged from 3.5 to 306.5 cfs in the Fall River and from zero to 1221.8 cfs in the Verdigris, which had no flow above the confluence from late July through September and only slight flow in November and December.

**Turbidity and seston.** The Fall River was the clearer river, with monthly mean turbidity from under 10 to 97 ppm, versus under 10 to 538 ppm in the Verdigris. Both were lower than values reported for the Mississippi and Missouri rivers. Total seston was generally higher in the Verdigris (4.5 to 294.7 mg/L monthly means) than in the Fall (4.4 to 32.1 mg/L). Organic seston made up a larger share of total seston in the Fall River (22.4 to 72 per cent) than in the Verdigris (2.0 to 87.1 per cent).

**Chemistry.**

| Variable | Fall River | Verdigris River |
|---|---|---|
| pH | 6.9 to 8.4 | 7.0 to 8.4 |
| Free CO₂ (ppm) | zero to 6.6 | zero to 12.9 |
| Methyl orange alkalinity (ppm) | 106 to 184 | 58 to 202 |
| Dissolved oxygen (ppm) | 3.9 to 14.2 | 4.1 to 13.9 |
| Total hardness (ppm) | 109 to 336 | 98 to 438 |

Dissolved oxygen was inversely related to temperature, and oxygen was judged sufficient for aquatic life at nearly all stations most of the year. Free CO₂ rose in fall and winter, probably from decomposition of organic matter, but the author notes that decomposition gases are generally minimal in flowing water. Relatively constant pH is typical of [[Lotic Waters]].

**Comparison with other studies.** The author draws on [[Wallen 1954]] and others to show that Kansas and Oklahoma values fall within the range reported for other stream studies, while Oklahoma stream turbidity during rain could reach 18,000 ppm.

## Key Findings

**Overall entomostraca abundance**

- **Fall River:** 32 species, 4,532 individuals, from a 480-liter sample. Cladocera made up 88.7 per cent of entomostraca (24 species, 4,018 individuals). Copepods accounted for 11.8 per cent (8 species, 514 individuals).
- **Verdigris River:** 33 species, 1,939 individuals, from a 484-liter sample. Cladocera made up 67.0 per cent (22 species, 1,299 individuals). Copepods made up 33.0 per cent (11 species, 640 individuals).
- Station V had the greatest abundance (3,709 individuals), and Station III the least (78 individuals).
- Copepods outnumbered cladocerans only at Station I.

**Dominant cladocerans**

- [[Moina Brachiata]] was the most abundant entomostracan in either river, comprising 66.3 per cent of Fall River entomostraca (3,002 individuals). It appeared mainly in June and July and is thought to have been introduced from the lagoon at Station IV. Males were present at Station V in June, possibly from overcrowding. Brooks found it only once in Kansas.
- [[Simocephalus Serrulatus]] was second in the Fall River (680 individuals, 15.0 per cent), peaking at Station V in August, September, and December.
- [[Chydorus Sphaericus]] was the most abundant cladoceran in the Verdigris River (40.3 per cent of entomostraca) and was second overall. Its clearest pulse came in April at Stations VII, VIII, and IX.
- [[Simocephalus Vetulus]] was the second most abundant Verdigris cladoceran (about 7 per cent). It showed the only winter pulse, with over 85 per cent of its individuals collected in December.
- [[Bosmina Longirostris]] (about 5 per cent in the Verdigris) peaked in April and May.
- [[Diaphanosoma Brachyurum]] was found only in July and August in the Fall River, consistent with a summer form.

**Dominant copepods**

- [[Cyclops Vernalis]] was the most abundant copepod in both rivers: 6.3 per cent of Fall River entomostraca (286 individuals) and 23.7 per cent of Verdigris entomostraca (461 individuals). It appeared year-round, most abundantly in spring and summer.
- [[Eucyclops Agilis]] was the second most abundant copepod in both rivers (3.4 per cent in the Fall, 4.5 per cent in the Verdigris).
- [[Cyclops Bicuspidatus]] was never abundant and appeared mainly in spring and winter.

**Concentration of species.** Two species (Moina brachiata and Simocephalus serrulatus) made up more than 80 per cent of entomostraca in the Fall River. In the Verdigris, Chydorus sphaericus and Simocephalus vetulus made up about 47 per cent. The author notes that [[Brooks 1956]] found only 4 predominant species among 41 Kansas cladoceran species.

**Controlling factors**

- No clear relationship was found between entomostraca abundance and any measured chemical variable. Sampling intervals of one to two weeks may have hidden such relationships.
- Cladocerans and copepods were most abundant from April to July, when mean water temperatures ranged from 14° to 32.5°C and flow was continuous.
- Brooks reported an optimum temperature range of 22° to 32°C for Kansas cladocerans.
- Water flow was judged the major limiting factor. Entomostraca were scarce during low flow, and disappeared in the Verdigris when flow ceased.
- Low turbidity may have let organisms evade the sampler, a phenomenon suggested by [[Andrews 1949]].

**Data inconsistencies.** The Summary lists 82 and 83 species and certain temperature and dissolved oxygen maxima and minima that differ from the body text (for example, Verdigris maximum temperature given as 87.2°C in the summary and 37.2°C in the body). The body text figures (32 and 33 species; 37.2°C) are used in this note, since they agree with the tables and narrative.

**Recommendations and implications.** The author stresses that the data serve as preimpoundment baseline information for the proposed Verdigris River dam, and that year-round studies are needed. Seasonal patterns from one year should not be used to predict species occurrence elsewhere, because timing of peak populations varies between years.
