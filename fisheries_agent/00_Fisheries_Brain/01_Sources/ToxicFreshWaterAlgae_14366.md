# Toxic Fresh-water Algae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyanophyta #harmful-algal-blooms

## Executive Summary

This 1954 review by Ingram and Prescott synthesizes decades of field and laboratory evidence (1878–1952) on toxic freshwater blue-green algae (Cyanophyta) and their effects on humans, domestic animals, and fish. The authors conclude that several genera of blue-green algae — [[Microcystis]], [[Aphanizomenon]], [[Anabaena]], [[Nodularia]], [[Coelosphaerium]], and [[Gloeotrichia]] — are implicated in mass mortality events among livestock, birds, and fish, primarily through toxin release during algal bloom decay and wind-driven bloom concentration along shorelines. While human gastroenteritis outbreaks have been circumstantially linked to algal-laden water during drought conditions, no definitive causal mechanism was established. In contrast, fish and livestock deaths show clearer experimental and field linkage to decomposing algal blooms, implicating both **oxygen depletion** and **direct toxicity** (e.g., hydroxylamine, hydrogen sulfide, and unidentified heat-stable, water-soluble toxins) as co-occurring lethal mechanisms.

## Background & Description

The paper is a review compiling veterinary, epidemiological, and limnological literature on Cyanophyta (blue-green algae) toxicity. Cyanophyta are distinguished from other algal groups by lacking membrane-bound chromatophores; their pigments (chlorophyll-a, carotenes, xanthophylls, c-phycocyanin, c-phycoerythrin) are dispersed in the peripheral cytoplasm, and their primitive nucleus (central body) lacks a nuclear membrane and nucleolus — a bacteria-like trait.

Genera identified as producing toxic species include:
- [[Microcystis]] (M. aeruginosa, M. flos-aquae, M. toxica)
- [[Aphanizomenon]] (A. flos-aquae)
- [[Anabaena]] (A. flos-aquae)
- [[Nodularia]] (N. spumigena)
- [[Coelosphaerium]] (C. Kuetzingianum, C. Naegelianum)
- [[Gloeotrichia]] (G. echinulata)

Not all blue-green algal blooms are toxic (Wheeler et al., 1942), and toxicity appears to vary temporally and possibly by growth/decomposition stage within the same waterbody.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Bloom dynamics and wind concentration:** A recurring ecological pattern across nearly all documented mortality events (Australia 1878, Minnesota 1882–1934, Ontario 1933, South Africa 1927–1945, Bermuda 1948) is that wind blows algal blooms into dense shoreline scums (2–6 inches thick), concentrating toxins and creating localized "kill zones" for animals and fish drinking or inhabiting these lee-shore areas.

**Drought and water supply stress:** The 1930–31 drought in the Ohio and Potomac River basins created conditions (reduced dilution, elevated organic/algal load) coincident with human gastroenteritis outbreaks in Charleston, WV; Louisville, KY; Cincinnati, OH; and Washington, D.C. Algal/plankton counts reached "many thousands per milliliter" versus normal hundreds. However, bacteriological water quality remained within accepted safety standards, suggesting a non-bacterial (possibly algal-toxin or organic decomposition) etiology that was never conclusively proven.

**Fish kill mechanism — dual pathway:** Two distinct but often co-occurring mechanisms are described for fish mortality associated with decaying algal blooms:
1. **Hypoxia** — bacterial decomposition of algal biomass depletes dissolved oxygen (documented in the [[Yahara River]], Wisconsin fish kill; Mackenthum et al. 1948).
2. **Direct chemical toxicity** — Prescott (1948) demonstrated fish deaths under dissolved oxygen conditions adequate for fish survival (4–7.5 ppm), implicating waterborne toxins such as **hydroxylamine** (a proteinaceous decomposition product) and **hydrogen sulfide** (measured at 8.5 ppm, likely underestimated due to distillation losses) released during decay of [[Aphanizomenon flos-aquae]].

**Cross-taxa toxicity:** The same algal genera (particularly Microcystis) affect mammals (cattle, sheep, horses, pigs, dogs, rabbits, mice, guinea pigs, cats), birds (poultry, pigeons, water birds), and fish, indicating a broadly acting, non-species-specific toxin or toxin class.

## Key Findings

### Fish Mortality Events (Experimental & Field)
- **Prescott (1948) aquarium experiments** with decomposing *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*:
  - 20 gallons of decaying algae added to aquaria killed all fish except one crappie within 6 hours (D.O. 4–6 ppm).
  - Species killed: [[Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), imported [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), [[Orange-Spotted Sunfish]] (*Lepomis humilis*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and [[Buffalo Fish]] (*Megastomatobus cyprinella*).
  - A second trial with satisfactory D.O. (5 gallons decaying algae) killed [[Sheephead]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*), [[Bluntnose Minnow]] (*Hyborhynchus notatus*), [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), [[Pumpkinseed]] (*Lepomis gibbosus*), and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) within 5 hours.
  - Field ponds at Storm Lake, Iowa, with D.O. maintained at 7.5 ppm still produced fish deaths (crappie, carp, bullhead) within 2 hours — supporting a toxic (non-hypoxic) mechanism.
  - Chemical analysis of decomposed blue-green algae revealed **hydroxylamine** in lethal concentrations and **hydrogen sulfide** at 8.5 ppm (likely underestimated).

- **Mackenthum et al. (1948)** — Yahara River, Wisconsin: [[Yellow Perch]], [[Crappie]], and a common [[Sucker]] died in aquaria with river water during a natural fish kill event tied to decomposing *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*. Conclusion: primary cause was oxygen depletion from bloom decomposition; toxic byproducts were considered a secondary contributing factor.

- **Prescott (1939)** reiterated that decaying proteinaceous blue-green algae can generate sufficient hydroxylamine and related derivatives to poison fish trapped in shallow bay waters with decomposing bloom masses.

### Toxicity to Domestic Animals & Wildlife (Comparative Context)
- First documented case: Francis (1878), Lake Alexandrina, South Australia — *Nodularia spumigena* bloom killed sheep, horses, dogs, and pigs within hours of ingestion.
- Minnesota lakes (1882–1934): Repeated cattle, poultry, and hog deaths linked to *Gloeotrichia*, *Aphanizomenon flos-aquae*, *Coelosphaerium Kuetzingianum*, *Anabaena flos-aquae*, and *Microcystis flos-aquae*/*M. aeruginosa* blooms (Fitch et al., 1934).
- South Africa (1927–1945): *Microcystis toxica* (Stephens, 1948) identified as a novel species producing a potent hepatotoxin, responsible for thousands of livestock deaths (Steyn, 1945; Louw, 1950).
- Symptomatology in livestock: acute paralysis/convulsions (high-dose exposure) vs. constipation, reduced milk yield, weakness, photosensitization, and jaundice (low-dose chronic exposure).

### Toxin Characteristics (Chemistry)
Findings from Fitch et al. (1934), Wheeler et al. (1942), and Olson (1952) collectively describe the crude algal toxin(s) as:
- Non-volatile, heat-stable when dry (100°C/100 hrs), less stable when wet unless neutral pH
- Water/alcohol soluble; non-soluble in benzene, ether, chloroform
- Small molecular weight — passes through dialysis membranes/ultrafilters
- Not an alkaloid, toxalbumin, cyanide, or botulinus toxin (per most tests); Louw (1950), however, isolated an alkaloid-like liver poison (picrate form) from *M. toxica*
- Resistant to standard water treatment: survives alum coagulation, chlorination, filtration, and even activated carbon treatment (Norite A) at operational dosages
- Minimum lethal dose in mice: as low as 0.004 mg (dialysate residue, Wheeler et al. 1942)

### Human Health Relevance (Contextual, Non-Fish)
No definitive causal link was established between algal blooms and human gastroenteritis outbreaks (Charleston, WV, 1930; broader Ohio/Potomac drought-associated illness, 1930–31), despite strong circumstantial/epidemiological suspicion (Tisdale 1931, 1931a; Veldee 1931; Olson 1949, 1952).

### Taxonomic Note
Nomenclatural revisions are flagged in the source: *Microcystis* was reclassified to *Polycystis* by Smith (1950), though Prescott (1951) retained *Microcystis*.
