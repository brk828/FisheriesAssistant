# Standing Crops of Fishes of Bonita Creek, Graham County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report by Robert W. Clarkson (June 10, 1980) quantifies the standing crop (biomass and density) of fishes in [[Bonita Creek]], a small desert tributary of the [[Gila River]] in southeastern Arizona. Electrofishing was conducted at three sites from November 1977 to December 1978, spanning a major flood in March 1978. The study found very high standing biomass for a desert stream, exceeding most reported values from mesic (non-desert) streams, and attributes this to the dominance of autochthonous primary production in desert systems. Standing crops fell sharply after the flood, and the author suggests several non-exclusive causes, including flood scour, habitat simplification, and downstream displacement of fishes.

## Background & Description

Standing crop measurements are common for mesic, forested streams, especially trout streams, but few exist for low-elevation desert streams. Clarkson notes that standing biomass alone is of limited meaning; relative productivity can be inferred only when turnover rate is known. The study aimed to monitor community trends in a desert stream and to compare desert and mesic streams in terms of relative productivity.

**Study area.** Bonita Creek arises from multiple springs on the [[San Carlos Apache Reservation]], flowing southeasterly between the Gila Range and Turtle Mountain. Its upper drainage is of low relief and broad valley, while lower reaches are box canyons with walls up to 60 m or more. The basin is underlain by Tertiary volcanic material and Quaternary alluvium. Drainage area is 958 km². Flow is chiefly perennial but intermittent in many reaches. Groundwater is pumped through an [[Infiltration Gallery]] about 6 km above the Gila confluence; [[Retention Dams]] built upstream in 1956 have largely prevented introduced fishes from the Gila mainstream from invading the upper creek.

**Sampling sites.**
- Upper site: Midnight Canyon, elevation 1152 m, in a broad valley with sycamore-shaded undercut banks and Baccharis cover before the flood.
- Middle site: just upstream of Lee's Ranch, elevation 1052 m, in a narrow canyon.
- Lower site: elevation 951 m, about 3 km above the Gila confluence, with a stony riffle and a deep undercut pool; its velocity was lower and it was affected by road grading and intermittency.

**Methods.** Fish were collected by [[Electrofishing]] with a 115 V AC gasoline generator into a 4.5 × 1.2 m, 7.5 mm mesh blocking seine. Each site was shocked three consecutive times, and bottom materials were agitated to dislodge fish. Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin, transferred to water, sorted, counted, and weighed to 0.1 g, then stored at the [[Arizona State University Collection of Fishes]]. Clarkson assessed preservation effects on *Agosia chrysogaster* (115 specimens): live weight was 264.5 g, rising to 305.9 g after five days in formalin (reported as a 13.5% increase) and 296.2 g after six further days in water (reported as 10.8% above live weight). These results were consistent with a roughly 10% formalin-related gain reported for yellow perch.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish community.** Five native species and one hybrid combination were collected, along with six introduced forms. Native species included:
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), abundant throughout most of the study area
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), abundant throughout most of the study area
- [[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), abundant throughout most of the study area
- [[Gila Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), healthy populations mostly in upstream reaches
- [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), rare, adults only, restricted to backwaters and undercut banks in the mainstream

Hybrids between *C. insignis* and *P. clarki* occurred sporadically.

Introduced species occurred mainly below the retention dams, where they tended to displace natives during stable flows. [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Channel Catfish]] were most abundant numerically among introduced fishes, while [[Common Carp]] contributed the most biomass through few large individuals. [[Red Shiner]] and [[Yellow Bullhead]] were extremely rare, and [[Mosquitofish]] occurred sporadically in pools near the confluence; the last two appeared only in qualitative collections.

**Desert versus mesic productivity.** Clarkson argues that desert streams depend primarily on [[Autochthonous Primary Production]] rather than [[Allochthonous Litter Input]], because terrestrial litter is scarce in xeric settings and is quickly flushed out during flash floods. Canyon walls limit sunlight and thus in-stream primary production, which likely explains higher biomass at the open upper site than at the canyon sites. Comparative studies cited include [[Tecopa Bore]] (desert, California) and [[Aravaipa Creek]] (desert, Arizona).

**Flood effects.** The [[March 1978 Flood]] (March 22–23) scoured the channel, removed bank cover, straightened channels, and lowered water levels. Canyon constriction turns floodwaters into a sluiceway, giving fishes little protection.

## Key Findings

**Biomass and density**
- Bonita Creek biomass ranged from 0.4 to 137.7 gm/m² in Table 1, but the text reports a maximum of 149.85 gm/m² (upper site, December 1978, almost entirely *P. clarki*). This is more than three times the maximum for non-desert streams.
- Site averages (Table 2, all species, Nov 1977–Dec 1978): upper 78.99 gm/m² (density 8.91/m²), middle 34.74 gm/m² (6.59/m²), lower 26.32 gm/m² (11.78/m², dominated by carp and small fish in the lower reach).
- Upper-site biomass was highest before the flood (for example, 137.76 gm/m² on Feb 4, 1978) and fell to 17.87 gm/m² on March 31. It then recovered to 149.85 gm/m² by December 1978, partly attributed to migration from intermittent areas.
- Middle-site biomass showed no real reduction after the flood, possibly because of downstream displacement of *P. clarki*.
- Lower-site data were excluded from quantitative comparison after intermittency and road-grading disturbance; introduced species were essentially eliminated after the flood.

**Comparisons with other streams**
- Highest previously reported maxima: Tecopa Bore (~95.8 gm/m²) and Aravaipa Creek (desert, Arizona; value not legible in the source).
- Non-desert maxima: River Thames, U.K. (47.6 gm/m²); Convict Creek, California (48.7 gm/m²).
- Clarkson states that the two undisturbed upper-site averages (56.86 gm/m², as reported) are at or above non-desert maxima.
- Invertebrate standing crops in desert streams (Bruns, 1977; Gray, 1980) are similarly high, supporting a strong autochthonous energy base.

**Proposed causes of post-flood losses (not mutually exclusive)**
1. Direct physical mortality from flood scour (no dead fish were found).
2. Loss of habitat quality and diversity, and of food supplies.
3. Post-spawning mortality of adults, which is considered unlikely to be major (spawning was judged to occur mid-December 1977 to mid-February 1978).
4. Increased stream width and discharge after the flood, reducing fish per unit area; considered unlikely by the author, though not ruled out, because channel width was not recorded separately.

**Conclusions**
- Desert streams can sustain very high secondary production, supported by autochthonous primary production, even with periodic flood-driven decimation.
- Clarkson suggests the results contradict Hynes (1963), who argued that autotrophs cannot play a significant nutritional role in frequently flooded streams, and support Minshall (1978), who contended that in-stream autochthonous energy sources have been underemphasized.
- Long-term monitoring is needed to address whether reproduction in Bonita Creek is restricted to headwaters or has failed in recent years, as adult-only *Gila intermedia* collections suggest.
