# Additional observations concerning residual sockeye and kokanee (Oncorhynchus nerka)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #oncorhynchus-nerka #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This paper by W. E. Ricker (1959) presents two case studies expanding understanding of life-history plasticity in [[Oncorhynchus Nerka]]. First, it documents an inadvertent experiment in which anadromous [[Sockeye Salmon]] fry planted in barren, landlocked lakes (Crawford and Rainbow Lakes, tributary to Stuart Lake, BC) produced lake-maturing "**residual**" sockeye populations that never migrated to sea, reproduced successfully in freshwater, and in some cases survived spawning. Second, the paper corrects an earlier (Ricker, 1938, 1940) assumption about the origin of [[Kokanee]] found in Cultus Lake, BC, demonstrating via colour, size, age, spawn timing, and scale resorption evidence that these fish were not evolved from native Cultus sockeye stock but were instead unintentional escapees from Kootenay Lake (West End) kokanee eggs reared at the Smith Falls Hatchery in 1933.

## Background & Description

### Crawford/Rainbow Lake Residual Sockeye
- **Body colour at maturity**: Dull greyish-green on back and sides, mottled grey underneath — contrasts sharply with the bright red maturity colouration typical of anadromous sockeye from all three candidate donor stocks (Babine Lake, Birkenhead River, Lakelse Lake).
- **Size range**: Migrant fry/fingerlings ranged ~2 to 7 inches depending on year and age class (two distinct size classes noted in 1925: ~4½ in. and ~7 in., interpreted as yearlings vs. two-year-olds).
- **Sex ratio**: Strong excess of females among mature landlocked fish (only 36 of 307 fish sampled by September 4, 1929 were male) — this is the *reverse* of the male-biased sex ratio found in [[Cultus Lake]] residuals.
- **Egg quality**: Poor, attributed to scarcity of males and gillnet capture method.
- **Post-spawning condition**: Seven spawned fish held in pens for two weeks showed improved vigor, suggesting at least some individuals survive spawning (contrary to the assumption that all sockeye die after first spawning) — corroborated by Donaldson's independent observation of post-spawning survival in Chapman Lake "kokanee."

### Cultus Lake Kokanee (Kootenay-derived)
- **Fork length progression across sampled years**: 223 mm (1934, n=1), 346 mm (1935, n=13), 402 mm (1936, n=2).
- **Minimum ages (scale-derived)**: 1+ (1934), 2+ (1935), 3+ (1936) — consistent with a single cohort (1932 year-class) maturing progressively.
- **Colour**: Bright, matching parental West End Kootenay Lake kokanee stock rather than the dull colouration of local Cultus residual sockeye.
- **Scale resorption**: Marked resorption at maturity, consistent with West End Kootenay kokanee characteristics (per [[Vernon 1957]]) rather than being an artifact of an extra unaccounted year of growth.
- **Comparative growth**: Cultus-reared kokanee averaged ~100 mm longer than source West End Kootenay stock (e.g., 346–402 mm vs. 238–247 mm), possibly due to warmer temperatures or longer growing season at Cultus Lake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Residual sockeye formation mechanism**: Anadromous sockeye fry planted into barren nursery lakes with outlet streams too precipitous for salmon passage became permanently landlocked, producing self-sustaining lake-maturing populations under complete selective pressure against anadromy — a proposed model for the initial stages of [[Kokanee]] evolution.
- **Multiple candidate source stocks**: Crawford Lake fry originated variably from Babine Lake (Skeena tributary), Birkenhead River (Fraser/Harrison system), and Lakelse Lake (Skeena tributary) across different spawning years (1920–1928), making definitive parentage of the residual stock unresolvable.
- **Spawning phenology divergence**: Cultus kokanee spawned earliest (~early September), West End Kootenay kokanee spawned later (~end of September), and native Cultus sockeye/residuals spawned latest (October–December, peaking mid-November) — indicating strong reproductive isolation despite shared habitat.
- **Colour polymorphism is not a fixed marker of life history**: Dull colouration is not universal among residuals (Baker Lake residual sockeye were fairly bright red) nor exclusive to them (South End Kootenay kokanee and anadromous Alistair Lake sockeye are also dull) — cautioning against using body colour alone to infer residual vs. anadromous origin.
- **Reversibility of dull colouration under anadromy**: Progeny of dull Lake Wenatchee kokanee that migrated to sea returned as bright red, normally-sized anadromous sockeye, suggesting colour expression is environmentally/developmentally plastic and linked to migratory pathway rather than fixed genetically.
- **Selective pressure hypothesis**: Increasing anthropogenic hazards to smolt migration (irrigation diversions, inadequate fishways, fisheries) may have driven recent kokanee evolution from residual ancestors in systems like Lake Wenatchee.

## Key Findings

1. **Crawford/Rainbow Lake sockeye** became fully landlocked after fry plantings in the 1920s ceased normal seaward migration, producing a self-reproducing freshwater "residual" population — demonstrating that stringent physical barriers to anadromy can rapidly produce alternate life-history types.
2. Landlocked Crawford Lake sockeye showed **female-biased sex ratios**, opposite to the male-biased ratio observed in Cultus Lake residuals, indicating sex-ratio outcomes in residual formation are not uniform across populations.
3. Evidence (albeit limited) suggests **some sockeye can survive spawning** when landlocked/residual, challenging the general rule that all *Oncorhynchus nerka* die after their first spawning event.
4. The **Cultus Lake kokanee** captured in 1934–36 were re-identified, via convergent lines of evidence (single-cohort aging, colour, scale resorption pattern, growth rate, spawn timing), as descendants of Kootenay Lake West End kokanee eggs reared at the Smith Falls Hatchery in 1933, rather than as an independently evolved native Cultus Lake stock as previously proposed by Ricker (1938, 1940).
5. This reinterpretation **does not invalidate** the broader hypothesis that kokanee stocks in many lakes originated from residual sockeye populations; the Crawford Lake case provides direct supporting evidence for how quickly such transitions can occur under strong anadromy-limiting selection.
6. **Body colouration at maturity is an unreliable sole indicator** of residual vs. anadromous vs. kokanee status, as dull and bright phenotypes occur across all three life-history categories depending on stock (e.g., Baker Lake residuals bright red; South End Kootenay kokanee dull; Alistair Lake anadromous sockeye dull).
7. Findings reinforce the broader research program (Ricker 1938, 1940; [[Vernon 1957]]; Rounsefell 1958) establishing *Oncorhynchus nerka* as an exceptionally plastic species with regard to migratory life history, capable of repeated evolutionary transitions between anadromous and freshwater-resident forms.
