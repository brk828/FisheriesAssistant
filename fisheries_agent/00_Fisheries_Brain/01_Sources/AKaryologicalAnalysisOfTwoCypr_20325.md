# Karyological Analysis of Two Cyprinid Fishes: Notemigonus crysoleucas and Notropis lutrensis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 cytological study by [[Michael Lieppman]] and [[Clark Hubbs]] (University of Texas at Austin) examines two Texas minnows, [[Notemigonus Crysoleucas]] ([[Golden Shiner]]) and [[Notropis Lutrensis]] ([[Red Shiner]]). Both species have a diploid chromosome number of 2n = 50, but their karyotypes differ clearly in arm morphology. The paper also presents a modified [[Gill Arch Technique]] for preparing fish chromosomes, which gave reliable mitotic figures. The authors argue that cytotaxonomy could help resolve the long-running controversy over supergeneric classification in the [[Cyprinidae]], the largest fish family in the study's context.

## Background & Description

Karyological traits are widely used in taxonomy, but fish cytology has been limited because fish chromosomes are small and earlier techniques often gave doubtful counts and poor morphological detail. The intrafamilial classification of cyprinids is controversial. [[Greenwood et al. (1966)]] criticized the systems of Regan (1911), Brittan (1954), and Ramaswami (1955) without proposing an alternative, so the authors suggest chromosome data could help settle osteology-based disputes.

The two study species are:

- **[[Notemigonus Crysoleucas]]**: a monotypic genus found in eastern North America, related to the western Eurasian genus [[Abramis Brama]] according to earlier literature.
- **[[Notropis Lutrensis]]**: a North American minnow. Males are identifiable by blue bodies and red fins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Specimens were collected at three freshwater sites in Texas:

- The [[Colorado River]] below Tom Miller Dam at Austin
- [[Shoal Creek]] in Austin
- [[Braes Bayou]] in southwest Houston, a slightly brackish system

Hatchery-reared [[Notemigonus Crysoleucas]] obtained from a tackle shop in Austin were compared with wild fish. No chromosomal variation was found, so hatchery specimens were included in the final data (Table 1).

Water conditions mattered for the protocol. Fish were transported in their native water, which was aerated. The authors noted that failure to aerate would reduce mitotic activity in gill epithelial cells. Hypotonic treatment had to suit the salinity of the source water. Freshwater fish from areas with little saltwater intrusion were treated in double-distilled water, while fish from the brackish Braes Bayou required a 1% TM solution, since distilled water caused cells to burst.

## Key Findings

### Methods and Technique

The authors modified the [[McPhail and Jones (1966)]] gill arch method. The procedure included these steps:

1. **Colchicine injection:** Fish weighing 1 to 2 g received intramuscular injections of 0.03 to 0.04 ml of 0.025% [[Colchicine]] solution, scaled to body weight. Fish were held in native water for 3 hours at room temperature.
2. **Gill arch removal and hypotonic treatment:** The three forward right gill arches were removed and incubated for 30 minutes in double-distilled water, or for 20 minutes in 1% TM for brackish-source fish.
3. **Sexing and preservation:** Sex was determined by dissection. All specimens were sexually mature. Fish were preserved in 10% formalin for one week and then moved to 50% ethyl alcohol.
4. **Fixation and staining:** Arches were placed in 50% acetic acid for 20 minutes, air-dried for one minute, and stained with 1% acetic orcein for 10 minutes.
5. **Squash preparation:** Cells were shed onto a coverslip, squashed under filter paper with graded thumb pressure, and sealed with Kronig cement. Slides were examined by [[Phase Contrast Microscopy]].

The key factors for good metaphase spreads were colchicine concentration and treatment duration. The forward-most gill arch consistently had a higher [[Mitotic Index]] than the others. The authors suggest that one arch could be sampled without sacrificing the fish, which would be useful for hybrids or rare species, if this pattern holds in other fishes.

### Chromosome Number

Both species have a [[Diploid Chromosome Number]] of 50. No polymorphism was seen among gill epithelial cells. Table 1 summarizes modal counts (cells scored and specimens examined):

| Species | Sex | Cells | Specimens | Cells at 2n = 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *N. lutrensis* | Female | 142 | 44 | 126 |
| *N. lutrensis* | Male | 131 | 64 | 122 |
| *N. crysoleucas* | Female | 89 | 34 | 84 |
| *N. crysoleucas* | Male | 96 | 27 | 89 |

Most cells in each group fell at 50 chromosomes. The table also includes counts at 48, 49, 51, and 52, plus an extra column labeled "AT" whose meaning the source does not clarify.

### Karyotype Differences

Matching homologous pairs was subjective because many chromosomes looked alike. Still, the two species differ clearly:

- ***N. crysoleucas*:** 8 pairs of metacentric or near-metacentric chromosomes, 12 pairs of submetacentric or subtelocentric chromosomes, and 5 pairs of telocentric chromosomes.
- ***N. lutrensis*:** 5 pairs of metacentric or near-metacentric, 16 pairs of submetacentric or subtelocentric, and 4 pairs of telocentric chromosomes.
- ***N. crysoleucas* has one pair of very long telocentrics** that are absent in *N. lutrensis*.

No karyotypic sexual dimorphism was found in either species. The authors note that this makes sex chromosomes unlikely, in contrast to the [[Gambusia affinis]] case reported by [[Chen and Ebeling (1968)]].

### Comparative Cytology

- **Other cyprinids:** [[Rhinichthys evermanni]] also has 2n = 50, with mostly metacentric chromosomes. Cultured [[Pimephales promelas]] cells showed modal counts of 49 to 51, with karyotype variation in culture, so cultured-cell data must be used cautiously for taxonomy.
- **Post (1965) survey:** Of 15 cyprinids listed, 13 have a haploid number of 24. Some [[Barbus]] species have more chromosomes, such as *B. tetrazona* (n = 25) and *B. fasciatus* (n = 26).
- **Teleost baseline:** Most teleosts have n = 24, and reductions from this number are more common than increases. Most high counts occur in the primitive order Isospondyli.
- **North American cyprinids:** The three North American cyprinids with known counts all have n = 25. The authors discuss whether this reflects a fusion of two chromosome pairs from an ancestral n = 26 or a change from n = 24.

### Evolutionary and Biogeographic Implications

The authors caution that chromosome number alone can be misleading, since centromeric fusion, unequal translocations, and polyploidy can change number or arm count without equivalent changes in chromatin content. Because the *Notemigonus* and *Abramis* relationship spans the Atlantic, the authors question the usual [[Bering Strait]] dispersal model. They note that the absence of either form in the intervening area suggests trans-Atlantic migration.

### Conclusions

- Both species have 2n = 50, but their karyotypes differ in arm configuration and in the presence of one long telocentric pair.
- The modified gill arch technique gives reliable metaphase spreads for cyprinid fishes.
- Karyological data could help resolve cyprinid classification, but the Old World comparative data available at the time were minimal.
