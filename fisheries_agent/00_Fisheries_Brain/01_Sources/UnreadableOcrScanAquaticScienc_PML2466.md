# Changes in Native Fish Distribution in the Green River System, Utah-Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1973 paper from the *Utah Academy Proceedings* (Vol. 50, Part 1) reports on the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit]]'s 1963–1966 field studies and later collections on native fishes of the [[Green River]] system. The authors, [[Clair B. Stalnaker]] and [[Paul B. Holden]], examine how the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962 changed the river environment and the distribution of seven large-river endemic fishes. Their main conclusion is that [[Trout]] have replaced native species in the upper Green River down to the Yampa confluence, and that four native species are now rare and may be endangered.

The document also contains two other items. The first pages are a reference list from unrelated forest recreation and rangeland studies. The final section is a truncated paper on the plant *Mimulus guttatus* (Scrophulariaceae), which is not an aquatic taxon.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River System]] was historically a set of large, warm, turbid desert rivers with steep canyons, rapids, and sharp changes in flow. Its native fish fauna had one of the highest levels of [[Endemism]] among North American river systems. The endemics fall into two groups: small fishes with limited ranges in the lower basin, and large-river fishes of the mainstem rivers, especially the Green River system.

The paper focuses on seven indigenous large-river species:

- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), North America's largest minnow, reported above 50 pounds
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), a robust minnow of the upper basin
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), streamlined for swift water
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), with a humped back thought to be an adaptation to swift water
- [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), found in two forms (narrow and wide peduncle)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), the most common endemic fish

The humpback chub and Colorado squawfish were already listed as "rare and endangered" by the [[U.S. Department of the Interior]] (USDI, 1968).

The paper's purpose was set after the 1963 review of the [[Green River Fish Manipulation Program]] by the Secretary of the Interior, which asked for an assessment of rotenone impacts and a longer-term study of habitat and fish changes in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. The study objectives were to:

1. Describe changes in the river environment after closure of Flaming Gorge Dam.
2. Determine species composition, distribution, and abundance of fishes from Flaming Gorge Dam to Ouray, Utah (166 river miles).
3. Compare postimpoundment fish distribution with preimpoundment collections.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Impoundment effects.** Large reservoirs now fill much of the Colorado River canyons, and little free-flowing river remains, especially in the lower basin. The upper basin, including the Green River, is the last major stronghold of large-river endemics. Two major reservoirs built in the 1960s are [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] on the upper Green River and [[Lake Powell]] behind [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

**Downstream changes below Flaming Gorge Dam.** After the 1962 closure, the Green River changed significantly from the dam to the mouth of the [[Yampa River]], 65 miles downstream:

- Seasonal flow fluctuations were reduced and daily fluctuations increased.
- Summer water temperatures were lower and winter temperatures higher.
- Turbidity was greatly reduced.

The Yampa River kept its natural character and moderated these changes downstream. All species present before impoundment still occur in the Green River below the Yampa mouth.

**Introduced salmonids.** [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] have replaced the large native fishes in the cold, clear water below Flaming Gorge Dam and above the Yampa confluence.

**Habitat and reproduction.** Of the seven species, only flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and roundtail chub occurred above the Yampa mouth before impoundment. None of the native species is known to reproduce in that reach.

## Key Findings

**Field study and sampling.** Fieldwork ran from summer 1963 to fall 1966, with collections continuing from 1969. Collections covered the upper Green River basin and its major tributaries, including the [[White River]] and [[Little Snake River]]. Results are mapped in a site figure (Figure 1), which shows collection sites and years.

**Species status (present distribution):**

- **Flannelmouth sucker:** The most common native species. Found at all collecting sites and apparently reproducing throughout the upper basin, except in the cold water below Flaming Gorge Dam.
- **Bluehead sucker:** Found at all sampling sites and apparently reproducing throughout the upper basin. Relatively rare in the lower Green River near [[Canyonlands National Park]].
- **Colorado squawfish:** Still present in limited numbers. Young-of-the-year were collected only from the Yampa River and the Green River within Dinosaur National Monument, and also from the Green in the Canyonlands area and Desolation Canyon. Abundance is much lower than before the 1960s.
- **Roundtail chub:** Present and reproducing in the upper Green and Yampa rivers and their tributaries, including the White and Little Snake rivers.
- **Bonytail chub:** Restricted to mainstem rivers and declining to rarity. Found in the Yampa and Green within Dinosaur National Monument, where it appears to reproduce successfully, and in the lower Green in Desolation Canyon and Canyonlands, where only a few adults have been seen.
- **Humpback chub:** One of the rarest Colorado basin endemics, known only from limited canyon areas of the Yampa and Green rivers in Dinosaur National Monument and Desolation Canyon.
- **Humpback sucker:** Still present in limited numbers, but mostly large adults have been taken, suggesting minimal reproductive success. Abundance has declined considerably in the 20th century.

**Historical records cited.** Early collectors described the Colorado squawfish as common and the humpback sucker as "very common" and used for food. Preimpoundment surveys reported the bluehead sucker as common in the Green River. The flannelmouth sucker was described as the most common large-river native, making up the greatest share of fish biomass.

**Conclusions:**

1. Trout have replaced native species in the upper Green River down to the Yampa confluence.
2. Four species (humpback chub, Colorado squawfish, bonytail chub, and humpback sucker) are rare and may be endangered.
3. Only three areas in the upper Colorado system appear ecologically suitable for maintaining reproducing populations of these large-river endemics: the [[Yampa River]] (the Green's major tributary), the Green and Colorado rivers in the Canyonlands section of southeastern Utah, and Desolation Canyon on the middle Green River.
