# Apache National Forest Stream Survey Report (1937 Field Season)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report, based on a survey conducted in June and July of an unspecified year (field data dated 1937), assesses the physical, chemical and biological condition of the principal fishing streams on the Arizona Division of the [[Apache National Forest]]. The survey was prepared for the Arizona Sportsmen's Association in Phoenix and the State Game Warden in Phoenix. It covers the [[Black River]] system (West Fork, East Fork, Hayground Creek, Bone Creek, Soneyard Creek, Bear Creek), the [[Little Colorado River]] system (West Fork, East Fork), and several minor tributaries. Each stream was evaluated for habitat, food supply and fish populations, and management recommendations were made, chiefly stocking with [[Arizona Native Trout]] and stream improvement work.

## Background & Description

The survey measured stream length by pacing, width and depth by tape, and water temperature at every station. Chemical analyses (quantitative tests for dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, bicarbonates, carbonates and hydrogen-ion concentration) were performed. Biological work included quantitative stream-bottom food sampling using a square-foot sampler, fish collection by angling and seining, fish length and weight measurements to determine condition factor, and age determination by counting annual rings (winter checks) on scales under a microscope. Data tables are appended to the original report (Tables 1 to 4 and benthic tables for individual stations).

Survey partners and scope: the study covered approximately 12 streams or sections, with the Black River's West Fork section alone about twenty miles long.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water temperature:** Upper, high-elevation reaches were cold, while lower reaches of several streams (Soneyard Creek, Little Colorado West Fork lower section, East Fork lower section) were too warm for trout. Shade from trees and shrubs was identified as a means of lowering stream temperature.
- **Habitat:** Most streams had gravel-rubble riffles with poor food production in the upper reaches. Pools were often shallow, silted or lacking adequate cover. Bank undercutting, grazing and erosion were noted, and fencing and bottom-structure work were recommended.
- **Chemistry:** Chemical conditions were reported as generally satisfactory for fish and high water temperature was identified as the primary limiting factor on several streams.
- **Food base:** Benthic invertebrate samples were dominated by Diptera (true flies), Ephemeroptera (mayflies), Plecoptera (stoneflies), Trichoptera (caddisflies), Coleoptera (beetles), and Oligochaeta and Amphipoda in some samples. Food biomass was reported in pounds per acre in several stations (for example, 101.58 lbs per acre on East Fork of Black River near Buffalo crossing; 45.99 lbs per acre on Little Colorado East Fork above Voight Ranch).
- **Competition:** Suckers and minnows were abundant in several reaches. Their role as competitors with trout or as forage for larger trout was debated, and removal of undesirable species was considered.

## Key Findings

**Stream-by-stream assessment and recommendations:**

- **West Fork of Black River (about 20 miles):** Among the most productive trout streams on the Apache. Upper reaches cold with gravel riffles; rainbow trout and Arizona Native hybrids dominant. Stocking of Arizona Natives recommended once available, with a legal-size stocking ratio about equal to the expected harvest. Stream improvement needed in middle and lower sections.
- **Hayground Creek:** Small stream with about two miles of good trout water upstream. Only a few rainbow trout and Arizona Native x Rainbow hybrids present. Natural reproduction was fairly good in 1937, but stocking was judged necessary for maximum fish production.
- **Bone Creek:** Very small tributary with low flow and abundant food in riffles, but fish were very small. Rainbow x Arizona Native hybrids were the only fish found. Stream improvement had been ineffective, and light stocking of Arizona Natives was suggested.
- **East Fork of Black River:** Largest-volume section studied, with a rocky canyon in the middle reach creating high temperatures. Trout were concentrated in shaded sections; suckers and minnows were extremely abundant, with many large suckers. Fencing and rip-rap protection were recommended, and Rainbow and Brook trout stocking was discussed.
- **Soneyard Creek:** Warm, shallow and sluggish stream with poor food production. Fish were Yellowstone Cutthroat, sculpins and suckers; coarse fish were abundant. No fish stocking was recommended until habitat improved.
- **Bear Creek:** Very small, cold stream with a Brook trout-like fish population; the only stream where trout were the only fish species.
- **Little Colorado River, West Fork:** Rainbow trout the only trout; warm water temperatures limited production. Stocking of legal-size Arizona Natives recommended, with legal-size Rainbows as a substitute if needed.
- **Little Colorado River, East Fork:** Brown Trout and Rainbow Trout, with abundant minnows and suckers. Good balance between trout and coarse fish, with minnows and suckers thought to supply food for larger fish.
- **Little Colorado River main stem (near Springerville):** Mainly Rainbow trout and Arizona Natives, with coarse fish dominant. Plantings of Arizona Natives considered satisfactory.

**Overall management conclusions:**

- Stocking of Arizona Native trout was preferred over other species as the stock most suitable to the local environment, but availability limited implementation.
- Stream improvement (bank protection, bottom structures, shade planting, fencing against livestock) was recommended on most streams, and maintenance work was needed where earlier projects had been done.
- Benthic food sampling showed that food production was highly variable among streams, and aquatic plant growth was noted as a means of increasing food production.
- Numerous specific sample tables (benthic composition, biomass and food-volume percentages) are included in the original report; these were summarized rather than reproduced here.
