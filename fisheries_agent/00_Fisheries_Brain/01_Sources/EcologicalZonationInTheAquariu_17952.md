# Ecological Zonation in the Aquarium

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #gasterosteidae #ecological-zonation

## Executive Summary

This article by [[William R. Kenney]], Aquarium Manager at the Springfield Science Museum, argues that the distribution of organisms is non-random and governed by physical and biological limiting factors. Using a stream survey and examples from the intertidal zone, Moapa Dace and Threespine Stickleback, Kenney concludes that competition often restricts fish to particular habitats even where the physical environment would permit them to live there. Aquarists can therefore keep fish outside their natural habitat by removing competition and meeting their biological needs, without duplicating every physical condition exactly. The piece is the first of a series; a later installment was announced to focus on catfish.

## Background & Description

The article opens with the premise that organisms are found within the geographic range of their species and, within that range, within suitable habitat. Two classes of factors restrict them:

- **Physical limiting factors:** pressure (depth), flow rate, bottom type, water hardness, salinity, oxygen concentration and pH.
- **Biological limiting factors:** food availability, predation intensity, obligate symbionts, competition from other species, and subtler influences.

Kenney uses an orchid example to illustrate a limiting factor. Orchids grow only in shade in nature, yet flourish in direct sunlight in the laboratory if kept cool. Heat, not light intensity, is therefore the limiting factor.

Kenney also identifies four weaknesses in the common advice to match aquarium water to a fish's native water exactly. That approach ignores biological factors, assumes all physical properties can be measured, assumes they can be duplicated exactly (which Kenney says is not possible), and can mislead hobbyists into thinking exact duplication is required for success.

The article notes that fish behavior, especially territoriality, courtship and spawning, is complex and resists general description. A page heading lists the White Catfish (*Ictalurus catus*), but the text does not discuss this species beyond announcing the following article on catfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream survey (clinal variation).** Kenney netted a stream that was swift and deep at the center and slower toward the edges. Differences were slight and spanned only feet. Catches were:

- **Location 1 (channel center):** many [[Longnose Dace]] and no other species.
- **Location 2 (slightly slower and shallower):** some [[Blacknose Dace]] and one stray Longnose Dace.
- **Location 3 (slower still):** some Blacknose Dace, quite a few [[Common Shiner]] and some [[Tessellated Darter]].

The habitat changed gradually, but the fish fauna changed abruptly. Kenney concluded that current was not what kept Longnose Dace out of the slower water. Competition with the other species was more likely. Specimens taken to the museum were housed in tanks with negligible current, and all four species were reported as thriving.

**Intertidal zonation.** The seashore intertidal zone offers an easily measured gradient in tidal exposure. Organisms zone abruptly, and each gives way to the next when a competitor is slightly favored. When competitors were removed experimentally, other species colonized the whole gradient. Normal zonation returned only after competitors were reintroduced, sometimes after a long period.

**Moapa Dace (*Moapa coriacea*).** This cyprinid is confined to a few warm springs in the Moapa River, Clark County, Nevada. Cold downstream water is its present physical barrier. Kenney notes that *M. coriacea* is the only member of its genus and that no closely related forms exist elsewhere. He suggests that a population moving into colder water would face competition from temperate-water species better adapted to those conditions.

**Threespine Stickleback (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*).** This species, the most widespread and popular gasterosteid in aquaria, occurs in northern-hemisphere coastal temperate waters and, in some regions, in fresh water. Freshwater populations, mainly in Europe, are genetically distinct, as shown by a drastic reduction in body plates. A California freshwater population has received the subspecific name *G. a. williamsoni*. Kenney argues that competition limits freshwater colonization elsewhere. US freshwaters are rich in perciform fishes, which compete well in fresh water. Europe's freshwater perciform fauna is depauperate, consisting mainly of [[Eurasian Perch]], and its characteristic fish are soft-rayed cyprinids, which the stickleback can compete with successfully.

## Key Findings

- Species distribution is non-random and is set by physical factors, biological factors, or both. Either type can prevent survival outside an appropriate habitat.
- Physical factors can be the limiting factor. In the orchid example, temperature limited growth in sunlight, not light intensity.
- Biological factors, especially competition, can restrict species even where physical conditions appear suitable. Stream data showed abrupt faunal change across a gradual habitat gradient.
- Aquarists do not need to duplicate every natural physical condition. Removing competition, feeding fish, controlling population density and providing for reproduction allowed the four stream species to persist, and possibly coexist, in inappropriate habitat.
- Intertidal organisms zone abruptly and can colonize the whole gradient when competitors are removed. Normal zonation returns only when competitors are reintroduced.
- The Moapa Dace is restricted by cold water and probably by competition with temperate-water species, which would also limit any population that tried to colonize colder waters.
- Freshwater colonization by the Threespine Stickleback is probably limited by competition. Its success in Europe reflects the depauperate perciform fauna there and its ability to compete with soft-rayed cyprinids.
- Fish reproductive and other behaviors are too varied for simple generalization. The article was intended as an introduction and not a full treatment.
