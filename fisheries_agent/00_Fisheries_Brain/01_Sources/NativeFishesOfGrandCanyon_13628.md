# Native Fishes of Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This illustrated field guide, produced by [[Grand Canyon River Guides]] in 1993, profiles eight native fish species of the [[Grand Canyon]] section of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Of the eight, three are listed as federally endangered ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]]), [[Razorback Sucker]] is also federally endangered, and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] is a federal candidate species. Four species (Colorado Squawfish, Bonytail, Roundtail Chub, Razorback Sucker) are extirpated or extremely rare in the canyon, while [[Speckled Dace]] remains common. The guide identifies a common set of threats across nearly all species: flow depletion, altered water chemistry, reservoir-flooded habitat, introduced parasites and diseases, predation and competition from non-native fish, and hybridization.

## Background & Description

The guide covers species with overlapping life histories: most mature around 3 years of age, lay sticky eggs over gravel or cobble during spring or early summer runoff, and produce larvae about 1/3 inch long that hatch in roughly 5 days. Key morphological and size data are summarized below.

Notable identification features include the humpback chub's abrupt post-cranial hump, the razorback sucker's ridge-like keel, and the bluehead sucker's torpedo-shaped body with a rosy lateral band. The Colorado squawfish has a large upper jaw extending past the center of the eye (chubs' jaws reach only to the front of the eye), and its sharp throat teeth (pharyngeal teeth) rake and tear prey. The genus name *Ptychocheilus* means "folded lip" and the species name *lucius* means "pike." The speckled dace is identified by a black lateral face band running from snout through eye.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Feeding strategies differ across the group. Suckers use fleshy, protrusible mouths to gather insects and algae from the drift and river bottom, while the bluehead sucker scrapes diatoms directly off rocks. Colorado squawfish are primarily fish-eaters but also take insects, small mammals, and birds. Roundtail chub aggregate along eddy lines to feed on drifting insects. Speckled dace eat small insects and crustaceans and serve as prey for predatory fish such as large trout and channel catfish.

Reproduction depends on high spring flows. Razorback suckers broadcast up to 100,000 eggs on gravel bars during peak runoff, flannelmouth sucker larvae drift great distances downstream to nursery backwaters, and Colorado squawfish eggs (up to 75,000 per female, June-July) produce larvae that remain in backwater nurseries for most of their first year. Historically the squawfish was the top predator of the Colorado River, but large mainstem dams have blocked migration routes, inundated habitat, and altered flow regimes.

Distribution is fragmented. Humpback chub occupy six canyon locations (Grand Canyon, Westwater, Black Rocks, Cataract, Desolation, and Yampa canyons). Razorback suckers are found in upper and lower basin rivers, with greatest numbers in [[Lake Mohave]]. Bonytails persist in small numbers in Lake Mohave and as isolated individuals in upper-basin canyons. Speckled dace are widespread across the basin and western North America, though their population trends are unknown.

## Key Findings

- **Endangered status:** Humpback chub, bonytail, Colorado squawfish, and razorback sucker are federally listed as endangered.
- **Candidate status:** Flannelmouth sucker is a federal candidate species, possibly warranting future listing.
- **Extirpation:** Colorado squawfish, bonytail, and roundtail chub are extirpated from Grand Canyon; razorback sucker is extremely rare there.
- **Declining populations:** Flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and roundtail chub are decreasing in number; speckled dace is common but its trends are unknown.
- **Shared threats:** [[Flow Depletion]], altered water chemistry, reservoir-flooded habitat, introduced parasites and diseases, predation and competition from [[Non-Native Fish]], and [[Hybridization]] (with roundtail chub, bonytail, humpback chub, or introduced white suckers) affect the native community.
- **Hybridization risk:** Breakdown of reproductive isolating mechanisms threatens humpback chub and bonytail through hybridization with roundtail chub.
- **Dam impacts:** Large mainstem dams are identified as the primary cause of Colorado squawfish endangerment, through blocked migration, inundated habitat, and altered flows.
- **Protection:** Several species (flannelmouth and bluehead suckers in particular) are protected by Colorado, Utah, New Mexico, Arizona, and California.
- **Conservation context:** The guide's publisher, [[Grand Canyon River Guides]], is a non-profit organization focused on protecting Grand Canyon, setting river-profession standards, and providing river experiences.
