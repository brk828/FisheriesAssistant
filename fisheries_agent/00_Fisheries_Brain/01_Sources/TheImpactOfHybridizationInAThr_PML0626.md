# Hybridization and Threatened Trout of the Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 study, by [[Thomas E. Dowling]] and [[Michael R. Childs]] of Arizona State University, examines hybridization between the threatened [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*) and endangered [[Gila Trout]] (*O. gilae*) and introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*O. mykiss*) and [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*O. clarki*) in the upper Gila River drainage. The authors combined mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) restriction analysis with allozyme markers. They found that Apache and Gila trouts are very similar genetically and more closely related to rainbow trout than to cutthroat trout. Allozymes showed extensive hybridization of Apache trout with rainbow trout in four populations, with no evidence of reproductive isolation, yet mtDNA showed an absence of rainbow trout haplotypes in the hybrid populations. This pattern suggests partial reproductive isolation and a biased direction of introgression. The authors conclude that more study of population structure is needed before management decisions, and that extreme care must be taken before eliminating contaminated populations.

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** Apache trout and Gila trout are native to the Gila River drainage in Arizona and New Mexico. The Apache trout was listed as threatened and the Gila trout as endangered under US federal law. Declines were attributed mainly to habitat reduction and interactions with introduced trout.
- **Introduced species:** Rainbow trout and several cutthroat subspecies were stocked widely for sport and bait. Introductions have caused competitive exclusion, predation and hybridization.
- **Reference material:** Four hatchery strains of rainbow trout and five cutthroat subspecies (Snake River, Lahontan, Colorado River, greenback and Rio Grande) served as reference specimens. Apache trout were sourced from a 1983 hatchery stock (from 20 males and 30 females, supplemented in 1984) and Gila trout from a 1988 stock (10 males and 10 females from Main Diamond Creek, New Mexico).
- **Sampled populations (1989):** Four suspected hybrid populations were sampled: Boggy Creek (N = 14), KP Creek (N = 72), Paddy Creek (N = 50), and Chitty Creek (N = 63; mtDNA surveyed in 22 individuals).
- **Methods:** Three diagnostic allozyme loci (Adh-A, Ldh-C, Pep-LGG), starch gel electrophoresis, restriction endonuclease analysis of mtDNA with four- and six-base cutters, hybrid index scoring, Hardy-Weinberg and linkage disequilibrium tests, and UPGMA clustering.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Native western trout have suffered from dams, water diversion, grazing and other land management, along with competition, predation and hybridization with introduced trouts. The authors note that the native fauna of western North America is largely at risk of extinction.
- Apache trout's historic range has been reduced by roughly 95% (Rinne 1988). Its range was naturally fragmented into isolated headwater populations, which may have led to local adaptation.
- Populations of native trout are often small, making them vulnerable to genetic drift, although this did not appear to explain the mtDNA pattern.
- Chitty Creek was poisoned with piscicide in 1969 and restocked. Its original trout inhabitant remains uncertain. Analysis showed complete replacement of any native trout by rainbow trout.
- The study area's stocking history is poorly documented, complicating the identification of stocks and timing of introductions.

## Key Findings

1. **Genetic relationships:** Restriction analysis showed Apache and Gila trouts to be very similar (about 1.5% sequence divergence from rainbow trout); the cutthroat mtDNA was the most divergent (about 3.8%).
2. **Diagnostic markers:** Three allozyme loci (Adh-A, Ldh-C, Pep-LGG) and two restriction enzymes (NheI, AvaI) distinguished native from introduced trouts. Loci could not separate Apache from Gila trout, and the authors could not replicate a previously reported diagnostic peptidase.
3. **Allozyme hybridization:** Hybridization with rainbow trout was extensive in Boggy, KP and Paddy creeks. At Chitty Creek, only introduced alleles were found.
4. **No allozyme evidence of isolation:** Hardy-Weinberg tests showed no deviations. Linkage disequilibrium was detected in only 1 of 9 comparisons, with three approaching significance (mostly at Paddy Creek).
5. **Hybrid index:** The distribution of scores was skewed toward intermediate values at Boggy Creek, but was normally distributed at KP and Paddy creeks, consistent with random mating.
6. **mtDNA bias:** Nearly all individuals from the three hybrid populations carried Apache trout mtDNA. The five exceptions at Paddy Creek carried Colorado River cutthroat mtDNA. No rainbow trout mtDNA was found, and the number of introduced mtDNAs was significantly lower than expected.
7. **Interpretation:** Four explanations were considered for the mtDNA bias: stochastic loss, selection favoring Apache mtDNA, differential selection among reciprocal crosses, and differential assortative mating. The authors judged drift and nuclear-cytoplasmic selection unlikely, leaving biological or behavioral isolation as the most plausible.
8. **Management implications:** Eliminating hybrid populations could cost locally adaptive variation, which may be more harmful than introgression. Apache trout introgression was widespread, but rainbow trout alleles exceeded 15% in only 15% of populations surveyed (unpublished report). The authors recommend surveying "pure" populations throughout the range and monitoring hybrid populations.
9. **Conservation recommendation:** The authors call for "extreme care" before eliminating contaminated populations. They also recommend genetic markers linked to adaptive loci to assess the impact of hybridization.
