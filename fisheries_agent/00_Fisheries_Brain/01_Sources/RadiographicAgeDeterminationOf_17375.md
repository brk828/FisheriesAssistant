# Radiographic Age Determination of Pacific Salmon: Species and Seasonal Inferences (with Friable Pigments and Ceramic Surfaces Abstract)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Journal of Field Archaeology short contribution by Aubrey Cannon (University of Toronto) presents a method for estimating the age of archaeologically recovered [[Pacific Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus* sp.) vertebrae using portable [[Radiography]]. Growth annuli in the vertebrae were not visible by eye, so the author radiographed them and counted winter-growth bands. The method was validated against scale annuli, known weights of [[Chum Salmon]], and comparative specimens. Applied to material from the [[Namu Shell Midden]] on the central coast of British Columbia, it explained size variation in the salmon vertebrae and showed that the site's salmon fishery was consistently seasonal over more than 4000 years. The document also includes a short abstract by James A. Neely and Paul S. Storch on conservation of friable pigments on ceramics from sw Iran. That item is not aquatic and is summarized only briefly below.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and context.** Age and growth studies of fish are used in fisheries research and in archaeology, mainly to infer seasonality of prehistoric fishing or climatic effects on growth. In archaeological assemblages it is often hard to recover enough remains for reliable averages. Bone increments give imprecise seasonal readings (Monks 1981).

**Why salmon.** Each [[Pacific Salmon]] species returns to spawn at a characteristic age, and each has a narrow spawning season. Age profiles of salmon remains therefore allow inference of which species were caught and when. Most Oncorhynchus skeletal elements cannot be identified to species by morphology alone, so age is the practical route (D. Cannon 1987).

**Study site.** [[Namu Shell Midden]] (EISx-1) has more or less continuous occupation from as early as 10,000 b.p. (radiocarbon years). Early deposits lacked shell, and soil acidity prevented bone preservation. Excavations in 1978 exposed 4 m of midden (Carlson 1979). Salmon vertebrae were well preserved from 6500–5000 b.p. and 5000–2200 b.p., with tens of thousands recovered. One 2 m × 2 m unit was analyzed in detail.

**Species life histories (as summarized in the text):**

- **[[Pink Salmon]]** (*O. gorbuscha*): almost invariably spawn at two years of age; peak spawning on the central coast in late September.
- **[[Chum Salmon]]** (*O. keta*): spawn at two to seven years, with the greatest number at four years (Hunter 1959); substantial numbers at three years. Enter streams late September to early October, older fish first.
- **[[Coho Salmon]]** (*O. kisutch*): 95% spawn in their third year (after two winters' growth) in British Columbia; the latest-spawning species, October–November.
- **[[Sockeye Salmon]]** (*O. nerka*): spawn at four or five years; concentrated in a few large rivers. The Fraser system provides 67% of British Columbia and Washington production; Rivers and Smith Inlets provide 16%. Central coast peak spawning is in July.
- **[[Chinook Salmon]]** (*O. tshawytscha*): largest Pacific salmon; spawn at three to eight years, most at four or five. Now rare in small central coast streams; peak spawning in September in central and northern British Columbia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal fishing and migration.** Because native fishers mostly took salmon on their return to spawning streams, the age profile of recovered fish reveals both species and time of year. Today much of the central coast native population travels to Rivers Inlet in late June for the sockeye run, the first of the season's salmon runs (Rohner 1967).

**Regional salmon streams.** Pink and chum salmon spawn in substantial numbers in many central coast streams, including the Koeye River south of Namu and the Namu River. Sockeye and chinook are restricted to a few major systems or are now rare in small coastal streams.

**Environmental and climatic background.** The author notes that the Namu vertebrae showed no systematic size trend that would indicate temporal climate shifts in growth rates. Instead, there was considerable level-to-level fluctuation in mean vertebral width, and the pattern was stronger when converted to weight. Full salmon-stream productivity is thought to have been reached around 5000 b.p. (Fladmark 1975). The local Namu salmon fishery declined sharply after about 3500 b.p. (A. Cannon 1987).

**Archaeological significance.** Salmon were central to prehistoric subsistence around the North Pacific Rim. The author argues that rapid, inexpensive age-based methods for salmon remains have broad regional value. Potential applications include the Fraser and Columbia drainages, where age profiles could distinguish spring chinook sites from fall fishing sites. Among the Thompson Indians of the Fraser Plateau, chinook and sockeye were most preferred, with pink salmon used when other species were scarce (Teit 1900).

## Key Findings

**Method development and validation**

- Namu salmon vertebrae showed no visible growth annuli under the naked eye or microscope, and sectioning was judged impractical for several hundred vertebrae.
- Vertebrae were radiographed on dental x-ray plates (H. G. Fischer FP200 portable unit, Simon Fraser University; 70 kvp, 20 mA, 0.5 s, 64 mm distance). Alternating narrow bright (radiopaque, winter) and wider dark (radiolucent, summer) bands were clearly visible.
- Validation involved three checks: (1) comparative *Oncorhynchus* specimens, (2) comparison with scale annuli, and (3) comparison of first-annulus width with known average weights of chum salmon at the end of their first year (Ricker 1964). All checks confirmed the radiographic rings as true annuli.
- Weight of living fish can be estimated from annulus width using a generalized regression (Casteel 1974).
- A comparative chinook specimen showed five winter rings, indicating a six-year-old fish.
- The method failed for other families (Gadidae, Hexagrammidae, Pleuronectidae). The author suggests salmon bone's lower density lets x-rays pass through summer bands, while the entire surface of denser bone in other fish is radiopaque.

**Age profile at Namu**

- Overall, 88.9% of vertebrae showed two winters' growth (equivalent to three-year-old fish), 3.0% showed one winter, and 8.1% showed three winters.
- No vertebrae indicated fish older than four years (three winters' growth), and only a small percentage reached four years.
- Level-by-level variation in the proportion of two- and four-year-old fish explains the observed variation in vertebral size: levels with more two-year-olds or fewer four-year-olds had smaller vertebrae.
- The table of age classes by period (sampled across the 2 m × 2 m unit) is partly garbled in the source, so only the overall percentages above are reported with confidence.

**Species and seasonal inference**

- Absence of sockeye (four to five years) and most chinook indicates no summer salmon fishery at Namu. This supports evidence for a probable summer seasonal abandonment of the site (A. Cannon 1987).
- Pink salmon contributed very little. Namu was never an important pink salmon fishing site.
- The remaining catch was mostly coho and possibly later-spawning three-year-old chum salmon. A small but significant share of four-year-old chum indicates their presence. Chum were favored for winter preservation (Romanoff 1985).
- The rarity of four-year-old chum is explained by timing: older fish enter streams first, so a late arrival at Namu would miss most of them. The best explanation is a consistent arrival in early to mid-October, which allowed fishing in the late chum run and the coho run in October–November.
- The author considered and rejected the alternative that different species or four-year-old chum were differentially processed at the site.

**Long-term consistency**

- Seasonal fishing patterns were remarkably consistent over at least 4000 years of occupation. This was true both before full salmon-stream productivity around 5000 b.p. and after the decline of the local fishery after 3500 b.p.
- The author interprets this as evidence of an early and continuing coastal adaptation attuned to the availability of different salmon species.

**Recommendations for future work**

- Test age profiles at other North Pacific coast sites with substantial salmon vertebrae.
- Compare salmon assemblages across Namu-region sites, for example sockeye at Rivers Inlet and pink salmon at the Koeye River, to test long-term seasonal consistency.

**Second item: Friable Pigments and Ceramic Surfaces (Neely and Storch)**

- This short abstract covers the loss of friable pigments and ceramic surfaces on sherds recovered by survey in sw Iran (Deh Luran Plain).
- Pigments and salt efflorescences were identified by optical and instrumental analysis. A cleaning method removed calcareous encrustations without acids and also solved an x-ray diffraction problem caused by salt-encrusted pigments.
- Several consolidants were tested on polychrome painted sherds; an acrylic resin proved acceptable.
- This is a terrestrial conservation study with no aquatic content.
