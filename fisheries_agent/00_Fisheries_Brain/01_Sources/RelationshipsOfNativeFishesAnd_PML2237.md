# Relationships of Native Fishes and Aquatic Macrohabitat in the Verde River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports the first two years (1994–1995) of a planned ten-year study of how native fishes use [[Aquatic Macrohabitat]] types in the [[Upper Verde River]], one of the few remaining undammed free-flowing rivers in the Southwest. The authors had three aims: to define the physical characteristics of five qualitatively identified macrohabitats, to determine how each native species associates with and uses them, and to assess the management implications. Native fish use appears to be governed mainly by water velocity and, to a lesser extent, depth. Velocity and gradient emerged as the most consistent habitat criteria, while substrate and depth were less clearly defined. In spring 1996 the authors began quantitative habitat work at a microhabitat site using [[Laser Survey Technology]] and [[Pebble Count Method]] substrate sampling.

## Background & Description

Information on habitat use by native fishes in large southwestern desert rivers was limited. Earlier work had delimited habitat for a native fish community in the small [[Aravaipa Creek]] and provided limited data on two native species in larger southwestern rivers. The upper Verde River reach studied remains undammed and free-flowing, and six native species still occur there alongside an equal number of non-native, introduced species.

Base flows range from 0.57 m³/s at the Paulden stream gage (upper study area) to 1.42–1.70 m³/s at the Sycamore Creek site. The river was affected by a 50-year flood event in winter 1992–93.

**Study design and methods**

- Seven established sampling sites have been monitored since spring 1994.
- Reaches are sampled twice yearly: all seven in spring (April), and only the uppermost and lowermost sites in autumn (October), to limit disturbance to fish populations.
- Habitats were first classified qualitatively. Four major flowing-water habitats were identified: high-gradient riffles (HGR), low-gradient riffles (LGR), runs (RUN) and glides (GLD). Roughly a dozen pool types were grouped into one category, POOL.
- Each habitat's length was measured, and width, depth and velocity were recorded at three equidistantly spaced transects each spring.
- Stream substrates were initially only generally categorized. Fish were collected by backpack electrofishing using a small-mesh seine.
- In spring 1996 the authors began quantitative definition of macrohabitats, sampling 14 microhabitats at the [[Burnt Ranch]] site, with laser-generated gradients and pebble counts of four substrate classes (sand, gravel, pebble, cobble/boulder).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat**

- Pools and glides had the greatest mean depth and the lowest mean velocity among the habitat types. Glides had the greatest mean width.
- Velocity and depth varied among the four flowing-water habitats, and no clear trend was evident for some habitat variables. Mean velocity of lotic habitats (flowing water) is summarized in Table 1 of the source, though the OCR of the table values is partly illegible and is not reproduced here.
- Laser-estimated gradients at the 14 microhabitats clearly separated the habitat types, supporting the qualitative designations.
- Substrate composition was related to water velocity. Sand deposition increased in the downstream habitat sequence, while gravel and pebble were concentrated in faster-flowing areas. The authors describe substrate as a product of velocity and sediment supply, and possibly as the more informative indicator of habitat stability.

**Fish–habitat associations**

- Native fishes were collected in all habitat types, with the community's use of macrohabitats summarized in Figure 3 of the source (1994–95).
- [[Spikedace]] (Meda fulgida) and [[Longfin Dace]] (Agosia chrysogaster) were the most frequently collected native species in faster-water habitats, while [[Speckled Dace]] (Rhinichthys osculus) and [[Desert Sucker]] (Catostomus clarki) also appeared in high-gradient riffles.
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (Catostomus insignis) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (Gila robusta) were associated with low-gradient riffles and runs at the microhabitat sites.
- Most of the federal and state-listed species were collected in runs and glides; the exact proportion is partly illegible in the OCR text.
- Preliminary observations indicated that spikedace were strongly linked to a narrow range of gradient and velocity conditions, and that substrate was important to them.

**Conservation and management relevance**

Habitat quality for spawning and rearing depends on gravel and pebble substrate and on flow regime. Flood events can remove and reshape habitats, reducing or enhancing these features and creating new habitat assemblages. The authors suggest that riparian vegetation and streambank stability also influence substrate and gradient in the upper Verde and recommend that land-management practices, including grazing strategies, be evaluated against fish habitat needs.

## Key Findings

- Native fish use in the upper Verde River is governed largely by water velocity, and secondarily by depth. Gradient and velocity were the most consistent habitat criteria.
- Qualitative macrohabitat classes (HGR, LGR, RUN, GLD, POOL) are reasonably supported by the velocity and gradient data from the 14 laser-surveyed microhabitats at Burnt Ranch.
- Depth was a less definitive habitat characteristic than velocity or gradient for microhabitat designation.
- Substrate composition is strongly influenced by water velocity, and the authors suggest that substrate may be an important factor in fish distribution, though only limited data (spring 1996) are currently available.
- Spikedace and longfin dace were most often collected in faster-water habitat types; speckled dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker and roundtail chub showed distinct but overlapping habitat use.
- Most federal and state-listed species occurred in runs and glides.
- Flooding and sediment deposition are key processes that reshape habitat, and the authors plan to link long-term macrohabitat data to fish community responses over the ten-year study.
- The authors acknowledge that more quantitative microhabitat data and additional sampling periods are required before definitive habitat relationships and management criteria can be established.
