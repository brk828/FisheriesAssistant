# Bemerkungen zum Phänomen der intralakustrischen Speciation (Remarks on the Phenomenon of Intralacustrine Speciation)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a front-matter and contents section of the *Festgabe für Werner Ulrich* (Zoologische Beiträge, Neue Folge, Band 5, 1960), followed by the core research paper by [[Curt Kosswig]] on the origin of cichlid species swarms in the great East African lakes. Kosswig argues that fish endemism in these lakes takes two distinct forms: many non-cichlid families contain only a few endemic species, whereas [[Cichlidae]] alone have undergone explosive, radiative speciation within single lakes. He reviews competing explanations (geographic isolation versus in-lake divergence), revisits his 1947 hypothesis that homogamous mate choice in substrate-spawning cichlids could start speciation, and weighs evidence from [[Fryer]] and [[Lowe]] on [[Mbuna]] and [[Tilapia Squamipinnis Group]] ecology. He concludes that simple behavioural or reproductive mutations may initiate speciation and that [[Preadaptation]] explains much of the adaptive radiation better than selection alone.

## Background & Description

The volume contents list about 20 papers by colleagues and students of Ulrich, covering insects, molluscs, bees, a parasite larva, a ground-dwelling reptile and a fish-skull study. Only the Kosswig paper (pp. 497–512) concerns aquatic animals. The volume's editorial notes specify that manuscripts should be short and include an abstract.

The paper opens with a comparison of fish endemism in three great lakes, drawing on [[Brooks]] (1950) and [[Poll]] (1946, 1953, 1956). Its central taxon is the cichlid, which forms "species swarms" (Speciesschwärme) of closely related, distinct species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Endemism in Lake Nyassa and Lake Victoria (Table 1, after Brooks 1950)**
- [[Lake Nyassa]]: 45 non-cichlid species versus 178 cichlids. Of the non-cichlids, 20 are endemic. Of the cichlids, 174 are endemic, including 20 endemic genera.
- [[Lake Victoria]]: 39 non-cichlid species versus 77 cichlids. Of the non-cichlids, 21 are endemic. About 70 cichlid endemics are recorded, including 4 monotypic genera.

**Endemism in Lake Tanganyika (Table 2, after Poll 1953)**
- 13 non-cichlid families with 26 genera and 58 species, of which 39 are endemic (about 3 per family).
- 8 endemic genera, 6 of them monotypic and 2 with two species each.
- The remaining 31 endemic species include Barbus (3), Chrysichthys (6), Synodontis (5), Lates (3) and Mastacembelus (7). Whether these form in-lake species swarms requires further systematic analysis.
- Cichlids: about 122 endemic species in about 36 genera. Species-rich genera include [[Lamprologus]] (31 species), Limnochromis (11), Trematocara (8) and Bathybates (7).

Kosswig treats non-cichlid endemism as a product of old, isolated lakes. Cichlid radiation, by contrast, implies "einen viel größeren evolutorischen Elan" (much greater evolutionary drive).

**Speciation hypotheses**
- *Geographic isolation*: lake basins split into sub-basins and then rejoined. This fits Lake Victoria, which probably split into isolated basins in the last interglacial. It fits Lakes Tanganyika and Nyassa less well, since both were deep for long periods.
- *In-lake divergence*: Kosswig's 1947 hypothesis proposes homogamy (assortative mating) in substrate spawners. Pair formation in aquaria, with partners selected from groups of half-grown juveniles, suggests that a simple genetic basis for mate preference could start speciation.

**Reproductive modes**
- *Substrate spawning and biparental care*: eggs are attached to a surface and guarded by both parents. [[Lamprologus leleupi]] shows this typical pattern.
- *[[Mouthbrooding]]*: eggs are carried in the mouth of one parent, often well past the embryonic stage. Kosswig regards this as a secondary state derived from the typical care pattern. Mouthbrooding arose polyphyletically in African and South American cichlids and in [[Tilapia]]. Labyrinth fishes show a similar mouth-based brood care.
- Kosswig had earlier stated that mouthbrooders were unlikely to speciate through homogamy. Later work by Trewavas, Lowe, Marlier and Fryer showed that mouthbrooders do form species swarms, including the [[Mbuna]] (about 30 species, all mouthbrooders, on rocky shores of Lake Nyassa), [[Tropheus]] from Lake Tanganyika, and the Tilapia squamipinnis group.

**Ecological differentiation**
- Tanganyikan cichlids differ ecologically: some live only on sandy shores, others are bound to rocky shores.
- Mbuna are tied to shallow rocky shores. Their larvae are not pelagic, so sandy stretches act as dispersal barriers.
- Divergence in [[Tropheus]] is linked to depth. *T. moori* occupies shallow water down to about 5 m, and *T. duboisi* replaces it in slightly deeper water. The two are now distinguished by colour.

**Lowe's Tilapia data (Lake Nyassa)**
- The *T. squamipinnis* group comprises *T. saka*, *T. lidole*, *T. karongae* and *T. squamipinnis*. All four occur in the northern lake for most of the year. In the south, *T. karongae* is absent.
- *T. saka* spawns at 2–4 m from August to November. *T. squamipinnis* spawns at 16–20 m from December to April.
- Females release young at different sizes: *T. saka* at 24 mm, *T. squamipinnis* at 30 mm, and *T. lidole* at 52 mm.
- Males are coloured differently: most are glossy black, but *T. squamipinnis* males are blue at the spawning site.

**Feeding and niche**
- Mbuna feed mainly on algal growth on rocks. Fryer reports that predatory fish, not food shortage, limit their numbers.
- Some cichlids partition food by mouth structure, but related species with similar mouths may eat different foods, and unrelated species may share the same diet. Kosswig concludes that food competition has not been a decisive factor in Mbuna evolution.

## Key Findings

1. **Two types of endemism.** Non-cichlid families in the East African lakes have a few endemic species each. Cichlids alone show extensive in-lake speciation and radiation into species swarms (Summary, point 1).

2. **Homogamy as a starting mechanism.** Kosswig maintains that assortative mating in substrate spawners is a plausible first step. Lamprologus leleupi, a substrate spawner with biparental care, has been bred successfully in Hamburg. This gives the 1947 hypothesis renewed relevance (point 3).

3. **Minimal genetic change.** Kosswig argues that two mutations may suffice to split a panmictic breeding community while the rest of the gene pool is retained. Examples include a new male colour that females prefer, or a mate-recognition secretion. A variant in brood period, where 25% of young leave the mouth earlier, could also start divergence (pp. 503).

4. **Sympatric divergence through depth and habitat.** Shifts to slightly deeper water on the same shore, along with dispersal to other rocky shores, can generate local differentiation. Small local population sizes and few large yolky eggs favour genetic drift among micro-populations.

5. **Colour variation and colour change.** Colour differs among populations, sometimes only in frequency, and sometimes one colour type reaches 100% at a locality. Cichlids also change colour with age, sex and mood, which makes the genetic basis of colour hard to judge.

6. **Disagreement with Fryer.** Fryer favours allopatric speciation through colonisation of isolated rocky shores. Kosswig stresses that Mbuna are all mouthbrooders, so his substrate-spawner model does not apply to them.

7. **Disagreement with Lowe.** Lowe explains the Tilapia radiation through lake-level fluctuations, which may have isolated populations at depth, plus shifts in spawning time. Kosswig notes that the order in which traits evolved is still uncertain.

8. **Comparison with Darwin's finches.** Kosswig compares the cichlid pattern with [[Galapagos Finches]] (Geospizinae), where beak shape and diet do not map one-to-one. Instead, instinctive dietary shifts can precede beak change.

9. **Preadaptation favoured.** Kosswig gives preference to [[Preadaptation]] theory over purely selectionist explanations. Ancestors' reproductive biology, morphology and behaviour set the potential for later radiation. Lowe applies this idea to Tilapia returning to deep spawning sites (pp. 510).

10. **Open questions.** Kosswig states that genetic evidence for colour types is minimal, that the systematic status of the non-cichlid endemic swarms is unresolved, and that field observation of living species swarms is needed.
