# Declining Salmon and Steelhead Populations: New Endangered Species Concerns for the West

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This issue of the [[Endangered Species Update]] (February 1992, Vol. 9 No. 4, School of Natural Resources, University of Michigan) reprints a feature article by Jack E. Williams, James A. Lichatowich, and Willa Nehlsen on the decline of West Coast anadromous salmonids. The article traces a history of overfishing, habitat destruction, dam construction, and hatchery dependence that has reduced [[Pacific Salmon]] and [[Steelhead]] stocks across California, Oregon, Washington and Idaho. Drawing on the [[Nehlsen Et Al 1991 Stock Status Report]] of the [[American Fisheries Society]], it reports that 214 native stocks met risk criteria, with only two listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It argues that stock-level conservation and a shift toward riparian and river restoration, rather than hatchery substitution, are needed. The issue also contains a book review of the [[Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem]] environmental profile.

## Background & Description

The article frames salmon as a resource long central to the region's human history. Indigenous tribes sustained sustainable harvests for about 10,000 years. Commercial exploitation began with the first West Coast salmon cannery in 1864 (Sacramento), followed by a Columbia River cannery two years later. Overfishing led to sequential collapses in Columbia River basin stocks: chinook fished out by 1884, steelhead and sockeye crashing around 1900, chum and coho in the 1920s, and all Columbia species significantly declined by 1945. In the Sacramento-San Joaquin system, overfishing was blamed for a 90 percent decline in commercial harvest from more than 9 million pounds live weight annually in the early 1880s.

Habitat loss compounded harvest pressure. Hydraulic mining, agriculture, logging, and dam construction (major dams on the Columbia, San Joaquin and Sacramento by the 1940s) degraded spawning, rearing and migratory habitat. Hatcheries, beginning with [[Livingston Stone]]'s McCloud River station in 1872, were adopted as mitigation.

Salmon return to natal streams through strong homing, forming local populations termed "stocks." Hereditary differences among stocks increase with geographic separation through local adaptation. Reduced survival of transplanted coho (Reisenbichler 1988) supports this. The authors argue stocks are the basic evolutionary building blocks and therefore the appropriate conservation unit.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Status of the resource.** The [[Nehlsen Et Al 1991 Stock Status Report]] classified stocks as at high risk of extinction (escapements declining, fewer than one returning adult per spawner, or under 200 fish in one or more of the past five years), at moderate risk (stable but previously declined, above 200), or of special concern. The report is characterized as provisional because data are incomplete and some native stocks may be masked by hatchery returns (e.g., Washington's Nooksack River coho, Oregon's Sandy River spring chinook).

**Dams and habitat.** Dams without fish passage were the most obvious cause of extinction. Examples include Dworshak Dam (1974, Clearwater River), Iron Gate Dam (1916, Klamath River), and Friant Dam (1948, San Joaquin River). In the Columbia Basin, natural production is about 5 percent of historic levels, and juvenile mortality at each mainstem dam may reach 45 percent during low flows (Raymond 1988). The [[Elwha River]] dams, blocking runs for about 80 years, may be removed.

**Logging.** In Oregon's coastal streams, timber harvest had the greatest impact on salmon habitat. Coho production is about half historical levels (circa 1900), while chinook production appears similar but may be misleadingly high because early logging and splash damming had already depressed chinook before 1900.

**Hatcheries.** About 75 percent of Columbia River adult fish are artificially reared in roughly 100 hatcheries. Hatchery introgression affects about half (104) of the 214 stocks.

**Conflicting economies.** Table 2 (adapted from Lichatowich 1992) contrasts the industrial economy (fossil fuel energy, centralized production, external change) with the natural economy (solar energy, dispersed production, internal change, interdependence), which the authors use to explain persistent conflict in salmon management.

**Book review.** The reviewed [[Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem]] profile (1991, Greater Yellowstone Coalition, 131 pages, six parts) covers ecosystem delineation, functional processes, habitats, wildlife (elk, grizzly bear, wolverine, burrowing owl), human impacts, and alternative futures. The reviewer, David J. Zaber, praises its unbiased, accessible presentation and recommends similar profiles for other ecosystems.

## Key Findings

- **Stock status (Nehlsen et al. 1991):** 214 native, naturally spawning stocks or stock groups were identified in California, the Oregon Coast, the Columbia Basin, and Washington Coast/Puget Sound. Of these, 2 were listed under the ESA (Sacramento River winter chinook, threatened; Snake River sockeye, endangered), 100 were at high risk of extinction, 58 at moderate risk, and 54 of special concern.
- **Regional breakdown (Table 1):** The Columbia Basin had 71 stocks (rounded from the table total of 214 by region: California 39, Oregon Coast 58, Columbia Basin 71, Washington Coast/Puget Sound 41).
- **Extirpations:** At least 106 major populations have been extirpated: 50 chinook, 15 coho, 9 sockeye, 5 chum, 2 pink, 23 steelhead and 2 sea-run cutthroat stocks. A conservation group counted more than 200 extinct Columbia Basin stocks when smaller streams were included.
- **Columbia Basin proportions:** Of approximately 192 anadromous stocks, 67 (35%) are extinct, 36 (19%) high risk, 14 (7%) moderate risk, and 26 (13%) of special concern; 49 (26%) are considered secure.
- **Sacramento River winter chinook:** Listed as threatened on 4 August 1989 (emergency rule), the first salmon stock protected under the ESA. A 1989 run of 547 adults compared with an average of 86,509 adults (1967–1969). The 1991 run past Red Bluff totalled 191 fish. A petition to reclassify it as endangered is pending.
- **Snake River sockeye:** Declared endangered on 20 November 1991. Only 4 adults returned to Redfish Lake, Idaho, in 1991, from an emergency captive broodstock program.
- **ESU policy:** NMFS's 20 November 1991 policy defines a stock as an evolutionarily significant unit if it is substantially reproductively isolated and represents an important component of the evolutionary legacy of the species.
- **Snake River fall chinook:** NMFS found "compelling evidence" that this run constitutes an ESU.
- **Lower Columbia River coho:** Not considered a species under the policy and treated as extinct, a decision the authors argue may confuse management of remaining wild fish. Clackamas River wild coho have declined to about 300 adults.
- **Hatchery role:** Cobb (1930) warned hatcheries could threaten the fishing industry; McEvoy (1986) concluded hatcheries contributed to fishery decline. The Columbia Basin now supports about 2 million hatchery and 0.5 million wild salmon, against historic runs of 10–16 million.
- **Recommendations:** The authors call for shifting effort to riparian restoration, natural production and river restoration, arguing that species-by-species listing of the 214 stocks would be prohibitively expensive, and that hatcheries should not substitute for habitat integrity. Oregon's Wild Fish Policy (1978, strengthened 1991) and the Columbia Basin Fish and Wildlife Program are cited as steps toward this.
- **Federal forest actions:** Draft Northern Spotted Owl Recovery Team recommendations for stream riparian protection, and a Congressional panel ("Gang of Four Report," Johnson 1991), call for substantial watershed changes on federal lands.
