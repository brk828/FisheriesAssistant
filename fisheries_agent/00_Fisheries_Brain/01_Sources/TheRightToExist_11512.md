# The Right to Exist: A Report on Our Endangered Wildlife

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-wildlife

## Executive Summary

This U.S. government report, issued by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] (apparently late 1960s), argues that species are now disappearing faster than they evolve, largely because of human actions. It reviews historic extinctions in North America, describes endangered animals and their status, and outlines the federal response, including the [[Endangered Species Preservation Act]] of 1966, captive breeding, habitat acquisition, and regulation of hunting. It also makes a case for public concern, economic benefit, and the intrinsic value of wildlife. Fish are a minority of the species discussed; the document covers wildlife broadly.

## Background & Description

The report opens with a historical contrast: passenger pigeons once darkened the sky, and the last one died in a zoo roughly half a century before publication. The [[American Bison]] was reduced to a few hundred animals and recovered after conservation efforts. The authors use this to argue that imperiled species can be saved when public concern is strong enough.

Species have always gone extinct through climate change, glaciation, and competition. The report argues that human technology, introduced species, and direct destruction have accelerated this process. [[North America]] experienced the most drastic wildlife losses in historic times. Losses listed include the [[Passenger Pigeon]], [[Heath Hen]], Carolina parakeet, great auk, Labrador duck, and several fish: Michigan grayling, San Gorgonio trout, Pahranagat spinedace, thicktail chub, harelip sucker, and Ash Meadows killifish.

Island species are especially vulnerable. Twenty-two native birds of the [[Hawaiian Islands]] are gone, and Puerto Rico has lost two island-confined birds. Introduced rats, rabbits, goats, cats, mongooses, and humans are identified as major destroyers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report frames extinction through niche theory: species are adapted to specific niches, and when conditions change they must "adapt or perish." Threats it names include:

- **Habitat loss and alteration:** drainage, fire, deforestation, grazing, cultivation, and inundation by dams.
- **Pollution and water changes:** pollution, reduced stream flow, siltation, and altered water temperature.
- **Introduced species:** competition, predation, disease, and interbreeding (which can erase subspecies traits).
- **Direct exploitation:** market hunting, plume and fur trade, and mass slaughter of flocking birds.

Vulnerability traits include flightlessness, dense flocking, and restricted range. The [[Laysan Rail]] and the great auk are cited as examples.

The report stresses that a species is only "endangered" when its survival is in immediate jeopardy. It defines three additional categories: rare, peripheral, and status undetermined.

## Key Findings

**Status classification (Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife):**
- **Endangered:** about 90 mammals, birds, fish, reptiles, and amphibians.
- **Rare:** nearly 45 mammals, birds, fish, reptiles, and amphibians.
- **Peripheral (edge of U.S. range):** over 80 mammals, birds, fish, and reptiles.
- **Status undetermined:** over 100 mammals, birds, and fish.
- The first official list (1967) named 78 endangered species.

**Selected endangered species and status:**
- **[[Key Deer]]** (*Odocoileus virginianus clavium*): reduced to an estimated 30 by 1949. The [[National Key Deer Refuge]] was established in 1953. Numbers have increased slowly, with over 1,400 on the refuge.
- **[[Black-Footed Ferret]]** (*Mustela nigripes*): depends on [[Prairie Dog]] colonies for prey and dens. Near extinction.
- **[[Whooping Crane]]** (*Grus americana*): about 50 in the wild, wintering at [[Aransas National Wildlife Refuge]], Texas. Captive propagation and egg transfers from Canada added to the captive population. Of 15 eggs hatched in two years, 10 young were healthy in early 1969, bringing the world total to 18.
- **[[Ivory-Billed Woodpecker]]** (*Campephilus principalis*): dependent on large tracts of dead and dying trees. Forest removal reduced its habitat "to the vanishing point."
- **[[Florida Everglade Kite]]** (*Rostrhamus sociabilis plumbeus*): a few birds near Lake Okeechobee, dependent on the apple snail.
- **[[Attwater's Prairie Chicken]]** (*Tympanuchus cupido attwateri*): under 1,000 on the Texas coastal prairie.
- **[[California Condor]]** (*Gymnogyps californianus*): about 50, mostly in the Los Padres National Forest.
- **[[Nene]]** (*Branta sandvicensis*): fewer than 50 by 1950. Recovered to nearly 400 wild birds on Hawaii and Maui, plus about 300 in captivity.
- **[[Aleutian Canada Goose]]** (*Branta canadensis leucopareia*): rediscovered on Buldir Island in 1963. About 300 wild birds and 60 in captivity.
- **[[Apache Trout]]** (*Salmo sp.*): reduced to two small streams and two lakes on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation, Arizona, after competition and deforestation.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): occupies the Green and Colorado Rivers north of the Grand Canyon. Its upper range was inundated by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].
- **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon diabolis*): survives in one spring-fed pool in Devils Hole National Monument, Nevada. Requires warm water and algae, and is vulnerable to human disturbance.
- Other cases: the blind salamander, whose only known habitat (Ezell's Cave, Texas) was saved by [[The Nature Conservancy]]. The [[Guadalupe Fur Seal]] is slowly recovering from near extinction.

**Bald eagle:** declining steadily, with causes unclear. Pesticides, loss of nest trees, human encroachment, and illegal killing are suspected.

**Management and programs:**
- Congress passed the Endangered Species Preservation Act in 1966.
- Captive breeding and research are conducted at the [[Patuxent Wildlife Research Center]], Maryland.
- Federal agencies control 765 million acres of public land and 50 million acres of Indian land.
- The [[National Wildlife Refuge System]] covers more than 28 million acres and is home to about 16 endangered forms.
- A [[Trumpeter Swan]] recovery at Red Rock Lakes Refuge, Montana, is cited as a major success.
- Transplantation is proposed as a last resort, and [[Insurance Populations]] are recommended for species with very limited ranges. A 1916 fire on Martha's Vineyard destroyed most of the heath hen population.

**Cooperative partners:** the [[National Audubon Society]], [[World Wildlife Fund]], [[The Nature Conservancy]], and the Red Lake Band of the Chippewa Tribe, which protects eagle nest sites on 500,000 acres.

**Regulation:** protective measures include closed seasons, delayed hunting (for example, snow geese in Canada to protect Ross geese), restrictions on methods and bag limits, hunting by permit, and sanctuaries. The report notes that treaties, legislation, and enforcement have largely ended market hunting.

**Central argument:** the report concludes that every species has value and that its loss diminishes the whole. It also cites economic benefits, such as tourism at Aransas, and calls for public education and continued research, since "once lost, no species can be retrieved."
