# Predator Removal at the Confluence of the Little Colorado River and Grand Canyon: 1997 Proposal and Peer Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1997 study proposal from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Research Branch) to test how effective removal of large nonnative predatory and competitive fishes is at protecting native fishes near the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) in [[Grand Canyon]]. The LCR supports the largest remaining population of [[Humpback Chub]], an endangered species, and nonnative predators are suspected of reducing recruitment. The proposal uses several capture methods ([[Gill Netting]], [[Setlining]], [[Spear Gunning]], [[Bow Fishing]], and [[Angling]]) over a six-day removal period in June 1997, with [[Snorkel Survey]] counts before and after. The package also includes an external review letter, the project budget, schedule, personnel vitae, literature cited, and a draft statistical analysis plan. Reviewer comments from Arizona State University questioned the lack of a defined control area, the weak justification for snorkel counts, the absence of evidence that [[Common Carp]] are predatory, and the applicability of visual methods in turbid water.

## Background & Description

**Native fish decline.** The Grand Canyon historically held eight endemic fish species. Four are now extirpated from the region: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Humpback chub is endangered. Habitat loss, blockage of migration, cold hypolimnial releases, and discharge fluctuations from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] are cited as causes. Humpback chub, bluehead sucker, and flannelmouth sucker persist in reduced numbers, likely because they spawn in tributaries.

**Predation evidence.** The proposal summarizes prior evidence for predation on humpback chub:

- Crescent-shaped wounds on humpback chub were attributed to channel catfish predation (Kaeding and Zimmerman 1983; Karp and Tyus 1990).
- Douglas and Marsh (1996) reported that approximately 4% of channel catfish collected in the LCR contained between one and seven humpback chub (85 to 200 mm total length). About 1.7% of rainbow trout and 16% of black bullheads examined also contained humpback chub.
- Valdez and Ryel (1995) documented brown trout consuming humpback chub in the mainstem Colorado River.

**Study system.** The LCR drains 141,155 km² of eastern and northern Arizona and western New Mexico. It flows 412 km from headwaters to the [[Confluence]] with the Colorado River. Its lower 21 km is perennial, sustained by springs, chiefly [[Blue Spring]], which provides a nearly constant base flow of 6.3 m³/s. Blue Spring water is about 20 °C, and water near the LCR mouth averages 9 °C warmer than the regulated Colorado River.

**Review and authorship.** The proposal was written by William R. Persons, Thomas J. Dresser Jr., Timothy L. Hoffnagle, and Mark J. Brouder. Persons, the Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Studies Coordinator, submitted it to [[Paul C. Marsh]] at Arizona State University on March 27, 1997, with comments requested by April 30, 1997. Comments were returned on April 7, 1997.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predator and competitor species.** Target nonnatives include channel catfish, common carp, black bullhead, rainbow trout, and brown trout. Fathead minnow and red shiner are also monitored. The proposal presents these as predators or competitors of native fishes (humpback chub, flannelmouth sucker, speckled dace, and bluehead sucker), whose lower perennial reach is seasonally used by all life stages.

**Historical introductions.** Earlier work (Miller 1961; Minckley 1973, 1991) linked introduced predatory and competitive fishes, and their parasites, to native fish declines in the region.

**Water conditions.** The LCR is typically cloudy blue. Turbidity can limit visual methods such as snorkel counts and bow fishing, which the reviewer flagged as a major concern.

**Access and safety.** The study area is the lower 3 km of the LCR. Predator removal occurs in the lower 1.2 km, with a control reach upstream at about 1200 m. Field camp ("Boulder camp") sits about 2.2 km upstream of the confluence and is reached by helicopter provided by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. Spear gunning, bow fishing, and angling are restricted to early morning or late afternoon when visitors are absent.

## Key Findings

**Study objectives (1997 field season)**

1. Evaluate spear guns, archery, bow fishing, angling, setlines, and large-mesh gill nets for removing potential predators of native fishes.
2. Determine stomach contents of large predatory and competitive nonnatives (channel catfish, common carp, rainbow trout, brown trout) during early summer.
3. Determine incidence and infestation rates of parasites in large nonnative fishes.

**Field methods**

- **Dates:** Sampling from 2 to 10 June 1997, when base flow is reached and clear-water conditions favor capture and visual counts.
- **Gill nets:** Six to eight large-mesh (multifilament) nets fished at selected eddies, checked about hourly and reset, to avoid losing digested prey and to let small natives pass. Catch-per-unit-effort (fish per net-hour) is recorded.
- **Setlines:** Three to five night-set lines, each 30 m mainline with 10 hooks spaced 3 m apart, baited, and checked after about twelve hours.
- **Spear gunning and bow fishing:** Targeting channel catfish and carp. Two archers and two spear gunners document effort hours.
- **Angling:** Targeting rainbow and brown trout at the confluence, with effort recorded in hours.
- **Snorkel counts:** Three snorkelers float downstream along transects (two shoreline, one midchannel) across the 1.2 km and control reaches, counting large nonnatives by species. Surveys occur before removal, immediately after, and again in September 1997.
- **Water quality:** Temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, and conductivity recorded by two DataSondes (upstream and near the confluence). Turbidity is measured daily at standardized sites. Discharge is estimated from a USGS gauge near Cameron, Arizona.

**Diet and parasite sampling**

- All collected nonnatives are measured (total length, mm) and weighed (nearest gram).
- Rainbow trout stomachs are collected at dusk and dawn, with a target of 200 stomachs over the ten-day period.
- A total of 200 common carp are to be sacrificed for stomach analysis. Catfish, bullheads, and trout are also sacrificed for stomach analysis.
- Contents are identified to the lowest taxon and quantified by frequency of occurrence, percent composition by number, and weight. Gastrointestinal parasites are identified and enumerated.
- Pectoral spines of catfish and bullheads are collected for age estimation if possible.

**Statistical plan**

- Effectiveness is evaluated by comparing predator counts in control and treatment areas before and after removal, and recovery is assessed from immediately after removal to fall.
- Option 1: A single-classification G-test (goodness of fit) comparing before and after counts, with expected ratios of 0.5 under the null hypothesis of no effect. Example table in the draft: 45 pre-removal and 13 post-removal (n = 58), expected 29 each.
- Option 2: Two 1200 m blocks (above and below the railroad tie rock) analyzed with a 2 × 2 G-test of independence, which treats margins as unfixed (Sokal and Rohlf 1981). Example table in the draft: block 1 with 20 pre and 5 post; block 2 with 25 pre and 8 post (totals 45 and 13, n = 58). The table values are illustrative and are not survey results.

**Reviewer concerns (Paul C. Marsh, 7 April 1997)**

1. The proposal does not state how results will be applied. The reviewer asks whether this is research for its own sake or a practical study.
2. The "control" area is never defined. The reviewer doubts that upstream reaches above 1200 m are comparable to the confluence.
3. A single snorkel pass by three divers gives one datum, which cannot estimate sampling variance or support a significance test.
4. The case that carp prey on native fishes is not made, and the justification for bow fishing is weak. Carp consumption of eggs and fertilized ova is anecdotal. The technology for assessing carp diet is nontrivial.
5. Turbidity may limit visual assessment. The reviewer asks for a contingency plan and for integration with prey abundance or availability estimates.

**Logistics and budget**

- Helicopter transport is provided by the Bureau of Reclamation (Robert Clarkson). All other costs (labor, travel, field supplies) are covered by the Arizona Game and Fish Department (Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Studies).
- Six workers are scheduled for the field camp. Three additional Arizona Game and Fish volunteers are to be selected by archery and spear gunning skill, and Dresser is no longer participating.
- Flight dates set by email (11 June 1997 message): 2 June (in) and 10 June (out), each with six people and gear.
- National Park Service and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service permits were being secured at the time of writing.

**Schedule and deliverables**

- June 2, 1997: Transport crew to Boulder camp and begin first snorkel surveys.
- June 3 to 4: Complete snorkel surveys and begin removal.
- June 4 to 9: Removal efforts.
- June 10: Post-removal snorkel surveys, break camp, leave site.
- September 1, 1997: Follow-up snorkel survey to assess longer-term effects.
- Interim trip report: due after field work.
- Final report: due January 25, 1998, following lab work and data analysis.
