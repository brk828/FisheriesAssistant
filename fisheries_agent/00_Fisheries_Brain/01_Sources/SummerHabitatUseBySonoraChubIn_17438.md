# Summer Habitat Use by Sonora Chub in Sycamore Creek, Santa Cruz County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report, prepared by Jeanette Carpenter and O. Eugene Maughan for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] under the project "Habitat Use of the Sonora Chub, Gila ditaenia" (11/15/89 to 6/30/92), quantifies summer habitat use by the federally threatened [[Sonora Chub]] in [[Sycamore Creek]]. The study used a two-scale design: macrohabitat analysis of stream reaches classified by life stage present, and microhabitat analysis of random sampling points at eight pool sites. Persistence of habitat through the summer drought was the central index of habitat quality. Adult-occupied macrohabitats were deeper, larger and lost less area than other areas, and microhabitat selection favored slow or zero velocity, bedrock or fine substrates, and proximity to cover, with patterns varying among pools and life stages.

## Background & Description

The Sonora chub is a small, dark minnow endemic to the [[Rio de la Concepcion Basin]]. It is federally listed as threatened (USFWS 1986), with critical habitat designated in the Sycamore Creek watershed, and is state listed as endangered (AGFD 1988). In the United States it occurs only in Sycamore Canyon, which forms the headwaters of the Rio Altar. Adults rarely exceed 125 mm TL in Arizona, while Mexican fish may reach 190 mm. Breeding males show red coloration at fin axils and mouth corners.

Published habitat information was previously qualitative. Density was judged unsuitable as a habitat-quality measure because fish concentrate in shrinking, isolated pools, and depletion sampling was prohibited under handling regulations. Habitat quality was therefore defined as areas supporting a variety of life stages, following [[Van Horne]] (1983, 1986). Macrohabitat quality was indexed by the presence of adults with breeding colors, with the smallest breeding-colored fish observed at 5.8 cm TL.

The study area spans about 11 km of canyon between Yank's Spring (1220 m) and the Mexican border (1035 m), in the [[Pajarito Mountains]], about 40 km west of Nogales. Vegetation grades from oak woodland at the headwaters to Sonoran desert scrub at the border. The canyon lies within the [[Goodding Research Natural Area]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and climate.** Sycamore Creek is an interrupted stream in low-flow years, existing mainly as isolated pools. Limiting conditions are greatest in May and June. Water temperatures tracked Nogales air temperatures closely (r² = 0.817). Water temperature ranged from -2.4°C to 29.7°C in the recorded series, with a field maximum of 37°C at an occupied location, though most pools rarely exceeded 30°C. Rainfall in 1990 was nearly double the July average, while the 1991 monsoon was late and produced about half the long-term average.

**Drying and habitat loss.** Surface area and fish losses were tracked between May and July 1991. Pools dried rapidly, and channels between them evaporated within hours.

**Food and predation.** Surface flow loss eliminated invertebrate drift, so isolated pools may have limited food. Potential predators include [[Coati]] (Nasua narica), herons, garter snakes, and dytiscid and hydrophilid beetles. Coati predation was strongly suspected but not directly observed.

**Other species.** Sonora chub is the only native fish in Sycamore Canyon. Introduced [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Mosquitofish]] have been reported, and longfin dace records are considered erroneous. In Mexico, Sonora chub coexist with native and exotic species, and exotics were at very low densities (1.95% by number).

**Threats.** Sediment loading after the 1983 and 1990 floods filled many deep pools, livestock trespass occurred within the RNA, and uranium deposits and mining claims exist in the watershed. The cause of Tarahumara frog extirpation remains undetermined.

## Key Findings

**Macrohabitat (Methods and Results)**
- Macrohabitats were classified by life stage: Category A (adults), B (immature fish), C (juveniles only), D (no fish). Surveys ran 11 May to 30 June 1990 and 16 May to 7 July 1991.
- Of surface water area occupied by fish in May 1991, 65% dried by July. Category A lost 19.2% of its area, Category B lost 69.7%, Category C lost 74.2%, and Category D lost 93.8%.
- Of the estimated 30,573 fish in May 1991, about 6,290 (roughly 20%) were lost from drying habitats by July. Losses were 12% of fish in Category A, 31% in Category B, and 54% in Category C.
- Mean fish per macrohabitat rose from May to July in all categories. In July, Category A averaged 252 fish against 86 (B) and 71 (C). Density proved misleading because habitat volume declined.
- In May, adult-occupied habitats were deeper (0.49 m), wider (3.87 m) and had larger surface areas than unoccupied areas. By July, adult habitats were the deepest (0.37 m) and largest (29.89 m²), and their depth decrease (19.6%) was the smallest.
- Dominant substrate differed significantly in 1991 (G = 25.5, df = 3, P < 0.0001). Persistent habitats were more often bedrock/sand (0.46 vs 0.40), while rubble/gravel habitats were less frequently persistent.
- In 1990, floating cover was lower in persistent habitats (75% contained less than 50% cover), with G = 9.06, df = 3, 0.05 > P > 0.025.

**Microhabitat (Methods and Results)**
- Eight randomly selected pool sites were sampled with 50 random points each (100 pooled points for some sites), with two subsamples at least two days apart from 13 June to 27 July 1991.
- Velocity selection was significant in Site 5 (P = 0.003), where adults and juveniles selected zero velocity. Measurable velocity occurred at only two sites.
- Juveniles selected shallow water (under 20 cm; Site 66, P < 0.001), while subadults used 11 to 30 cm and adults preferred depths over 10 cm. Depth use scaled with fish size.
- Sunlit areas were selected more often than shaded ones, with no consistent pattern across age classes.
- Rocky cover was selected by all life stages in Site 73 (P = 0.004) and Site 46 (P = 0.040). Juveniles selected plant material in Site 24 and algae in Site 5.
- Bedrock was selected at several sites, including by all fish in Site 13 (P ≤ 0.044) and by adults in Site 22 (P = 0.0004). Adults selected fine to coarse gravel in Site 5 (P = 0.001).
- Subadults used coarser substrates than adults in Sites 5 and 66, and remained closer to cover than adults in Site 5 (P = 0.0001).

**Limiting Factors and Management**
- Low dissolved oxygen (as low as 1.0 ppm in occupied pools) is considered a likely limiting factor. About 14 dead fish (35 to 100 mm TL) were found on 29-30 June 1990 after cloudy, record-hot weather.
- Authors doubt high temperature is ever critically limiting in most pools.
- Post-1990 flood surveys recorded many deep pools filled with sand. Adult habitat is most suitable where pools are deep with impervious substrates.
- Grazing within and upstream of the RNA is a concern, with cattle observed in the RNA despite closure and riparian vegetation absent beyond the northern RNA boundary.
- The authors recommend routine monitoring in late June (dry season) and September-October (post-rain), and caution that the population may be at risk during sustained drought.
- Translocations into cattle tanks or tinajas with steep slopes would probably support adults, but recruitment requires shallow areas and cover for juveniles.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning was evident after the 1990 floods. Fry and juveniles (15-18 mm) were observed in April, May and September 1990 and 1991, suggesting spawning after spring and monsoon rains.
- Breeding colors have been recorded in August, October, and March. The species may breed year-round if conditions allow.
