# Nannochromis nudiceps: Aquarium Husbandry and Spawning Behavior of an African Dwarf Cichlid

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 *Tropical Fish Hobbyist* article by H. J. Richter describes the dwarf cichlid [[Nannochromis Nudiceps]], a member of the African genus [[Nannochromis]]. The genus includes three known species (N. nudiceps, N. dimidiatus and N. squamiceps), all from the lower [[Congo River]], including [[Stanley Pool]]. The author describes the fish's appearance, water requirements, temperament, and cave-spawning behavior, and gives a detailed account of a breeding program in a home aquarium. The piece is partly commercial: most of its pages are advertisements for [[Miracle Plastics]] spawning grass, heaters, [[Split Flake Food]] and a breeding book.

## Background & Description

- **Genus:** [[Nannochromis]] is a group of dwarf cichlids, listed alongside the better-known [[Pelmatochromis]]. Recent knowledge recognises three species.
- **Body form:** Unlike most fish from similar habitats, members of the genus have an upward-curved body, most pronounced in older specimens.
- **Size:** Adult male N. nudiceps reach about 2³⁄₄ inches (the OCR reads "23 inches", a likely scan error for 2¾ in). Females generally grow about ½ inch less than males.
- **Coloration:** The basic body colour is greenish-yellow, with shiny light-blue spots on the sides and head and an emerald-green belly. A delicate pink tint appears above the anal fin during mating. The dorsal fin has a white seam closed by near-black fin-ray tips. The upper tail is banded black and white; the lower tail has a dot pattern.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Females carry an extruded ovipositor when ready to spawn; males show a narrower, shorter genital papilla.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** The species is not demanding and tolerates both hard and soft water, though softer water brings out brighter colours. Water filtered through peat moss suits it best.
- **Temperament:** The fish are shy and retreat to hiding places, especially after introduction. Once a pair claims a cave, they defend the territory and rarely leave it. Frequent rearrangement of the tank reduces shyness.
- **Shelter:** Rock structures, coconut shells, inverted flower pots and driftwood are recommended. Without a cave-like structure, the species burrows under rocks to make its own.
- **Community tanks:** Fry in community tanks are heavily predated by other inhabitants, and only a few survive in densely planted tanks.
- **Breeding tank setup:** A 28 × 12 × 10 inch tank with a 1½-inch layer of fine gravel (about ¼ inch granulation, suited to dwarf cichlid mouths) and a fine-grained air stone set to minimal flow. Water was a 1:1 mix of tap water (about 18° dH) and rain water (about 4° dH), with peat extract added until the water was light brown and a liquid acidifier (Zoropin) to bring pH to about 6. Temperature was about 76 °F, with strong aeration for one or two days before introducing the pair.

## Key Findings

- **Parental care:** The male typically claims the overturned flower pot first and courts the female by trembling until she responds. Both sexes clear pebbles, building a cone-shaped gravel mound in front of the entrance. The author notes that the flower pot was preferred over all other caves.
- **Spawning:** Eggs are laid on the pot's roof, where they hang from threads. The yellowish-white eggs are cleaned and fanned by the female. The male is chased from the cave after spawning.
- **Brood size:** Brood size was about 80 fry at the first spawning in the author's tank and about 140 at later spawnings.
- **Brood care:** After 8 to 10 days the female leads the swarm out of the pot. The male rejoins the family after the fry become free-swimming and helps herd them. Both parents guard the brood, and the female shepherds stragglers back at nightfall. The male is excluded from the pot overnight.
- **Rearing diet:** Parents were fed flake food, and very small Cyclops nauplii were added for the fry. Fry reached about ½ inch after roughly three weeks and about 1 inch by five weeks. Some "belly-sliders" (non-viable or dead-on-arrival fry) occurred in each spawning.
- **Recommendation:** The author encourages aquarists to keep and breed the species, which he considers underrepresented in the hobby trade.
- **Advertising content:** Most pages promote commercial products, including the spawning grass, Robin heaters, flake foods (Split Flake Food: 46% protein, 4% fat per 1000 g) and the book [[Breeding Aquarium Fishes]] ($9.95, about 500 pages). These claims are the manufacturers' own and were not independently assessed.
