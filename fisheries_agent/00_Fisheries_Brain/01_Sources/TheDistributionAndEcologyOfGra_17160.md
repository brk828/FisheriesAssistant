# Gram-Negative Bacteria in English Lakes: Distribution and Ecology

#bacteria #limnology #microbiology #sulphur-bacteria

## Executive Summary

This 1960 review by Vera G. Collins, of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] in Ambleside, examines the distribution and ecology of Gram-negative bacteria other than Enterobacteriaceae in English lakes, with emphasis on [[Pseudomonas-Achromobacter]] groups. The author argues that lake bacteriology is primarily an ecological problem rather than a study of bacterial physiology alone. The work draws on weekly samples from [[Windermere]] and [[Esthwaite Water]] and monthly surface samples from every other lake in the District. Its central conclusions are that bacterial numbers fall through the stratified summer, rise at autumn overturn, peak in winter, and form dense layers at the [[Thermocline]] after heavy rain in calm weather. The paper also proposes a tentative microbial sequence linking the [[Hypolimnion]], mud surface and sulphur cycle.

## Background & Description

The paper is a contribution to a symposium on Pseudomonas and Achromobacter, published in the *Journal of Applied Bacteriology* (vol. 23, no. 3, December 1960). It is organised into five sections: introduction, lake-water bacteria (seasonal changes; stratification and overturn), lake-mud bacteria, a suggested sequence of microbiological events, and a summary.

Bacteria in lake water are grouped into three categories:
- Washed-in organisms from outside sources, most of which perish.
- Indigenous types able to grow in dilute nutrient solutions at low concentrations of organic matter.
- Surface-dependent types associated with soil particles, mud detritus and [[Plankton]]. This group includes stalked, hold-fast forms and forms that adhere by mucilage to container surfaces.

Sampling methods named in the text include a [[Jenkin Surface-Mud Sampler]] and enrichment culture techniques applied to Esthwaite Water mud.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal dynamics.** Growth is normally limited by available carbon and energy. During long rainless periods counts fall steadily. They rise in surface waters at maximum temperature and after heavy rain, partly from washed-in bacteria and partly from multiplication of types that use the imported organic matter. The water selects which soil bacteria survive, and some common soil bacteria are absent from lake water. Selection depends on dissolved substances (particularly [[Phosphates]]), the availability of organic nitrogen, and the presence of particulate matter for attachment.

**Stratification and overturn.** Bacterial numbers decreased at all depths from the onset of stratification in May until overturn in October or November. Heavy rainfall during a spell of very calm weather produced a concentration of bacteria at the thermocline, more pronounced in Windermere. The author offers two explanations: cooler, sediment-laden river water slid under the warm surface layers, or a buoyant plankton "sump" at the thermocline served as a nutrient store. At overturn, high plate counts at depth indicated release of fine particulate matter from the mud surface, and surface samples contained types previously found only in deep water.

**Mud microbiology.** Aerobic bacteria dominate the immediate surface of the Esthwaite Water mud and decline with depth in cores 20–30 cm long. Surface groups include ammonifiers, proteolytic organisms, denitrifiers, nitrite-producing organisms, weak nitrogen fixers, sugar and starch fermenters, cellulose and chitin decomposers, and a small fat-hydrolysing group. Sulphate-reducing bacteria are abundant below about 4 cm, where oxygen tension is low. The relative abundance of [[Desulphovibrio Desulphuricans]] and [[Thiobacillus Thioparus]] depends entirely on oxygen in the overlying water.

**Sulphur bacteria.** Seasonal groups track hydrogen sulphide over the mud: coloured sulphur bacteria ([[Chromatium]]), the colourless [[Beggiatoa]], coloured non-sulphur bacteria ([[Rhodopseudomonas]]) and the [[Achromatium]] lime-sulphur organism.

## Key Findings

- **Bacterial counts decline through summer stratification** (May to October/November) at all depths, then rise in deep samples at overturn.
- **Thermocline concentration after rain:** plate counts were 2,400 bacteria/ml above the thermocline, 47,700/ml at its top, 1,460/ml within it, and 60,240/ml at its base. Counts fell again below to 60 m, the deepest sampled level.
- **Winter maximum:** bacterial numbers are highest in winter, when lake temperature is uniform and seasonal rainfall peaks.
- **Overturn dispersal:** circulation spreads mud-surface and deep-water bacteria through the whole water column, changing surface colony types without raising surface counts.
- **Mud zonation:** aerobic bacteria dominate the surface and sulphate reducers dominate below about 4 cm in Esthwaite Water mud.
- **Sulphur cycle:** during stratification, coloured sulphur bacteria oxidise sulphide back to sulphate; at overturn, thiosulphate-oxidising bacteria and Beggiatoa and Achromatium complete the cycle on the mud surface.
- **Light-limited photosynthesis:** in Esthwaite Water (maximum depth 15 m), mass growths of Chromatium occurred at 9–13 m, showing that light reaches these depths in sufficient amounts.
- **Slow nitrification:** nitrification tests required 30 days for a slightly positive reaction.
- **Proposed model:** stratification produces a hypolimnetic anaerobic zone, enabling a layered microbial system that disperses at overturn. The author describes this as a speculative sequence requiring further testing.
