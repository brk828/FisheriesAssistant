# Some Observations on Limnology and Fish Distribution in the Illinois River Below Tenkiller Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #limnology #Salmonidae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 field study by Phillip B. Summers (Oklahoma Game and Fish Department) investigated whether the cold tailwater section of the [[Illinois River]] below [[Tenkiller Reservoir]] could support a "put-and-take" style [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery following the onset of hydroelectric plant operations. Building on prior work by Finnell (1953), which identified low dissolved oxygen (D.O.), rather than temperature or flow, as the limiting factor for trout survival, this study measured temperature and D.O. at four stations across an 8.5-mile reach during hydropower discharge, pre-discharge, and no-discharge periods in August 1954. Results confirmed that severe [[Hypolimnetic Oxygen Depletion]] released from the reservoir's thermocline dramatically depressed D.O. levels in the river during turbine operation, creating conditions lethal or near-lethal to trout and stressful to warm-water species. No rainbow trout were captured, and fish community composition and abundance were poor, supporting the conclusion that a sustained summer trout fishery was not viable under existing dam operation schedules.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study system**: An 8.5-mile tailwater reach of the Illinois River immediately below Tenkiller Dam, sampled at four stations:
- **Station I** — penstock tunnel outlet, directly below the power plant
- **Station II** — deep pool ~2 miles below dam (Corps of Engineers gaging station)
- **Station III** — above a riffle at Oak Grove Recreation Area, 5 miles below dam
- **Station IV** — under the U.S. Highway 64 bridge near Gore, ~8.5 miles below dam

**Driving environmental mechanism**: [[Tenkiller Reservoir]] develops a stable summer [[Thermocline]] with a hypolimnion severely depleted in dissolved oxygen (0.0–1.5 ppm). Hydroelectric discharge through the penstock releases this hypolimnetic water downstream, producing sharp drops in both temperature and D.O. in the river, especially during active turbine operation (0700–2100 on weekdays, ~2,020–4,040 cfs, avg. 3,365 cfs).

**Oxygen gradient and spatial pattern**:
- During discharge, D.O. averaged below 1.5 ppm for at least 2 miles below the dam and below 2.0 ppm out to 5 miles.
- On August 10, D.O. dropped to 1.3 ppm at Station III (5 miles downstream) during a 4,000 cfs release — near the documented [[Median Lethal Oxygen Concentration]] for rainbow trout (1.34 ppm at 60°F per Burdick et al. 1954).
- D.O. was significantly higher during the pre-discharge period than during discharge, and highest on days of no discharge (weekends).

**Temperature pattern**: Temperatures during discharge and pre-discharge were similar and within acceptable trout tolerance ranges; however, on no-discharge days, temperatures rose significantly, approaching conditions that could reach the lethal limit (85°F) for trout if no-flow periods extended beyond 48 hours.

**Refugia**: A backwater slough near Station III (~0.5 mile long) showed improved conditions moving from mouth to head (D.O. 2.4→3.2 ppm; temp 62.4→67.1°F), suggesting such [[Backwater Slough|sloughs]] may serve as thermal/oxygen refugia for fish during high-flow, low-oxygen events in the main channel.

**Observed distress behavior**: Mid-morning observations in a backwater 100 yards below the penstock outlet revealed shad and bluegill schooling just beneath the surface and carp and buffalo gasping at the surface — classic [[Hypoxia Avoidance Behavior]]. Gar surfacing (likely aerial respiration via modified swim bladder) was observed regularly ~0.4 mile downstream of the dam.

**Angler impact**: Fishing success was very limited during high-flow periods; catches were only feasible in the brief window before turbine operation began each morning.

## Key Findings

- **No rainbow trout were captured or reported caught** during the study period, and the persistently low D.O. throughout summer render the river largely unsuitable for sustained trout growth and survival.
- **Nineteen fish species** were collected under D.O. conditions below 3.0 ppm; 15 species were taken below 2.0 ppm; 13 species below 1.5 ppm — demonstrating substantial hypoxia tolerance among the resident warm-water community (Table II).
- **Species surviving at very low D.O. (0.5–0.9 ppm)** in swift current at 54–64°F included: [[Black Bullhead]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Longear Sunfish]], [[Rock Bass]], [[Longnose Gar]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]].
- **Only bullheads** were found alive at D.O. concentrations below 0.5 ppm, marking them as the most hypoxia-tolerant species recorded.
- Comparative literature values cited for context:
  - [[Bluegill]]: 50% mortality after 48 hrs at 1.3 ppm D.O./73°F (Sprugel 1951); 100% mortality after 24 hrs at 0.3 ppm/54°F.
  - [[Smallmouth Bass]]: median lethal D.O. of 0.87 ppm at 60°F (Burdick et al. 1954).
  - [[Rainbow Trout]]: median lethal D.O. of 1.34 ppm at 60°F after 48 hrs (Burdick et al. 1954).
  - Winter D.O. thresholds under ice (Moore 1942): [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Northern Pike]] 2.3 ppm; [[Yellow Perch]] 1.5 ppm; [[Black Crappie]] 1.4 ppm; [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]] 0.9 ppm; [[Bluegill]] 0.8 ppm; [[Black Bullhead]] 0.3 ppm; [[Golden Shiner]] 0.0 ppm.
  - Cooper and Washburn (1949) found generally lower winter D.O. thresholds in Michigan lakes for the same species, notably for largemouth bass and black crappie.
- **Total catch was low overall** (3,873 individuals in 50 trap lifts), with few fish of catchable/legal size, indicating a depauperate, stress-limited fishery rather than a robust community.
- **Conclusion**: The primary limiting factor for a viable trout fishery below Tenkiller Dam is [[Dissolved Oxygen Depletion]] driven by hypolimnetic reservoir releases, not temperature or streamflow — corroborating Finnell's (1953) earlier hypothesis. Management implications suggest that maintaining a trout fishery would require operational changes (e.g., reservoir destratification, oxygenation, or altered release schedules) rather than temperature control alone.
