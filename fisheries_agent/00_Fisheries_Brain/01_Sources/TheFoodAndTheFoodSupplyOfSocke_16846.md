# Food and Feeding of Sockeye Salmon (*Oncorhynchus nerka*) in Cultus Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1937 study by [[William E. Ricker]] (Pacific Biological Station) analyses the stomach contents of several hundred [[Sockeye Salmon]] from [[Cultus Lake]], British Columbia, to determine how young fish relate to the lake's plankton supply. Fingerlings feed almost exclusively on pelagic crustacean [[Zooplankton]] (Entomostraca), which are their main food as older fish. Feeding intensity peaks in July and August and is lowest in winter. Summer feeding is concentrated between about 5 and 15 m depth, bounded by food scarcity above and poor light or low temperature below. Interspecific competition with other fish is judged minor in summer, whereas intraspecific competition in years of large sockeye populations reduces growth and depletes late-summer plankton.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Sockeye in Cultus Lake comprise fingerlings (first year), yearlings (second year), and a smaller number of third- and fourth-year fish. Most migrate seaward in April and May as year-old fish; a variable minority are two-year-olds.
- **Series context:** The paper is No. 2 of a series on factors affecting the behaviour and survival of sockeye during their lacustrine existence. Migration counts since 1926 are drawn from [[Foerster]]'s publications.
- **Sampling methods:** Bottom-set gill nets (mesh 16–38 mm stretched) caught a few yearlings and older fish. Most fingerlings were obtained from the stomachs of predaceous fish. Only stomach contents were analysed, weighed wet and counted as plankton subsamples.
- **Food weighting ("units"):** Epischura = 10, Cyclops = 1, Daphnia = 5 (May–September) or 3 (October–April), Bosmina = 1, midge pupae = 50. One unit is approximately 0.01 mg wet weight.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dominant prey:** [[Epischura Nevadensis]], [[Cyclops Bicuspidatus]], [[Daphnia Pulex]] and [[Bosmina Obtusirostris]]. Midge larvae (mainly Chironominae) were of some importance. Minor items included Melosira diatoms, Ostracoda and terrestrial insects.
- **Thermal and light structure:** Feeding is centred on the [[Thermocline]] and adjacent strips of epilimnion and hypolimnion. Epischura occupies mainly the epilimnion, where other crustaceans are scarce.
- **Temperature and digestion:** Digestion trials were run at 11.5–13.5 °C.
- **Competitors:** At least eight other fish species consume plankton, including [[Coho Salmon]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Squawfish]], shiners, sticklebacks, sculpins and suckers. Most are littoral in summer, so pelagic competition with sockeye is judged negligible at that time.
- **Lake morphometry:** Lake area is 6.26 million m², volume 201 million m³. The summer pelagic feeding stratum (5–15 m) covers 5.9 million m² and holds 57 million m³.

## Key Findings

**1. Seasonal feeding and consumption**
- Average stomach contents rise from a small May value to about 31 mg in summer, fall to 5–10 mg in January, and reach about 35 mg in April–May.
- Corrected for fish size (mean of cubed length), feeding peaks in July and August, coinciding with the fastest growth.
- Consumption falls sharply toward late summer as the Daphnia pulse disappears in September.

**2. Prey selection**
- Daphnia is most readily taken, except when sockeye are very small (May–June).
- Bosmina is the main prey of the smallest fingerlings.
- Cyclops is abundant in winter and early spring (up to 90% of prey) but only about 5% in summer.
- Epischura is much more heavily used in winter than in summer relative to its abundance.
- Four factors are proposed to explain predation: abundance, size (larger prey taken more often), habitat selection (Epischura avoided in summer), and an unexplained factor separating Bosmina from Cyclops.

**3. Individual preferences**
- In a confined space (under 0.2 m³), ten fish held at 10 m differed strongly in what they ate. One fish took mainly Cyclops, while most took mainly Daphnia.
- Individual preference is therefore inferred, and averages from small samples are unreliable.

**4. Feeding depth experiment (July 1935)**
- Fish held at 10 m (15.0 °C) averaged 370 food units; fish held at 20 m (6.8 °C) averaged 60 units.
- The difference is statistically significant (Fisher's t). Cyclops were absent at 20 m, suggesting reduced illumination as well as temperature limited feeding.
- Digestion of a meal was essentially complete by about eight hours at 11.5–13.5 °C.

**5. Intraspecific competition**
- Estimated populations of about 36 million fingerlings in early summer 1932, falling to about 16 million by mid-August.
- The August Daphnia supply was estimated at 60 × 10¹⁰ individuals in the 5 m-bounded area, roughly 37,000 per sockeye. Daphnia consumption by large sockeye was on the order of 500 per day.
- August Entomostraca abundance is inversely correlated with fingerling numbers (r = −0.975, roughly 1 chance in 50 of arising by chance).
- Yearling migrants were smaller in years of very large runs (1.4–2.5 million migrants gave 66–72 mm, 3.1–3.7 g) than in small-run years, indicating growth is limited at high density.
- Small migrants showed no extra mortality in the lake or handicap at sea, so the lake's plankton is not fully used in years of small populations.

**6. Plankton supply**
- Food available is greatest May–July and least December–February, roughly six-fold higher in summer.
- Late-summer abundance was much lower in 1928 and 1932 than in 1933 and 1934.
- Year-to-year variation in plankton is much smaller than variation in sockeye numbers.

**7. Downstream migration**
- Seaward migration in April–May occurs when food organisms are increasing in number and moving to deeper water. The author therefore concludes that food scarcity does not drive migration.

**Summary conclusions:** The principal foraging zone of fingerlings in summer is 5–15 m. Interspecific competition is minor in summer but intraspecific competition is significant in years of large populations. Migration is not triggered by malnutrition.
