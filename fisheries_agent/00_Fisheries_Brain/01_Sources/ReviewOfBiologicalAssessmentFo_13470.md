# Arizona Game and Fish Department Review of Hoover Dam Modifications Biological Assessment

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This letter from the Arizona Game and Fish Department to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service critiques a Biological Assessment prepared by the Bureau of Reclamation regarding proposed modifications to the [[Hoover Dam]] Power Plant. The Department identifies significant deficiencies in the assessment's treatment of two endangered native fish species: [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The primary concern is that the assessment fails to adequately evaluate physical, chemical, and biological habitat changes resulting from modified dam operations, instead relying primarily on species rarity and nonnative fish interactions to justify "no effect" determinations. [[Lake Mohave]] represents the largest known remaining population of both species and is designated as a high-priority recovery area.

## Background & Description

The document is formal correspondence dated August 26, 1991, from Bruce D. Taubert, Chief of Wildlife Management Division, Arizona Game and Fish Department, to Sam Spiller of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Ecological Services. It follows a July 22, 1991 meeting attended by multiple agencies including the Bureau of Reclamation Lower Colorado Region, Lake Mead National Recreation Area, Pinetop Fisheries Assistance Office, Arizona Flycasters, and researchers from [[Arizona State University]] (Dr. W.L. Minckley and Dr. Paul Marsh).

[[Bonytail Chub]] is described in the assessment context as "very rare at Lake Mohave" and "near the point of extinction." The species' recovery plan designates [[Lake Mohave]] as a high-priority recovery and reintroduction site, with the primary purpose of serving as "a wild refugium and study area for bonytail chub." The lake has supported an important population of adult bonytail chub used as broodstock in recovery efforts.

[[Razorback Sucker]] spawning behavior is described as utilizing "relatively shallow, wave washed cobbles for spawning activities in Lake Mohave." The species is characterized as planktivorous. Current populations show lack of documented recruitment, though the Department notes that absence of documentation does not necessarily imply complete recruitment failure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Lake Mohave]], located downstream of [[Hoover Dam]], represents critical habitat for both endangered species. Dr. Paul Marsh stated in a July 10, 1991 letter that "Lake Mohave represents the largest known remaining population of both species."

The ecological concerns center on multiple habitat components:

**Physical Habitat**: The [[Black Canyon]] reach immediately below Hoover Dam and extending into [[Lake Mohave]] faces potential modifications from changed dam operations. Lake level fluctuations induced by modified operations could affect shallow spawning areas. The Final EIS (Reclamation 1984) indicated that under extreme operational scenarios, [[Razorback Sucker]] could be displaced from areas immediately below Hoover Dam to areas of greater habitat suitability.

**Chemical and Biological Features**: The assessment must address limnological changes in [[Black Canyon]] and [[Lake Mohave]]. The Final EIS discussed potential scour and reestablishment of periphyton in Black Canyon. Modified operations could result in changes to nutrient cycling, primary productivity, and secondary productivity, which would influence planktivorous species like [[Razorback Sucker]].

**Nonnative Species Interactions**: The original Biological Assessment emphasized interactions between [[Razorback Sucker]] and nonnative fishes in [[Lake Mohave]], particularly regarding predation upon and competition with larval razorback sucker. However, the Department argues this should be treated as a mitigating factor rather than the primary basis for effect determination.

**Spawning and Recruitment**: The Final EIS indicated possible reduction in [[Razorback Sucker]] spawning success due to changed dam operations, with lake level fluctuations potentially decreasing egg and fry survival. Increased dewatering and desiccation effects were not adequately considered in the assessment.

## Key Findings

**Assessment Deficiencies for [[Bonytail Chub]]**:
- The Biological Assessment's discussion relied solely on the absence of bonytail chub from the [[Black Canyon]] reach immediately below Hoover Dam
- Failed to address possible modifications of physical habitat in that reach extending into [[Lake Mohave]]
- Did not evaluate reasonably expected changes to physical, chemical, and biological features of habitat available to the species
- Determination of effect must be consistent with conservation and recovery of the species per Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act

**Assessment Deficiencies for [[Razorback Sucker]]**:
- Inappropriate to justify "no effect" finding singularly upon interactions with nonnative fishes
- Must relate consequences to potential changes in physical, chemical, and biological habitat features
- Current lack of documented recruitment does not necessarily imply recruitment is entirely lacking
- Predation and competition with nonnative fishes may not be the singular cause of recruitment failure
- Failed to discuss potentially increased fluctuations at [[Lake Mohave]] beyond current fluctuation levels
- Did not extend ramifications of modified operations scenario into [[Lake Mohave]]
- Insufficient evidence provided for "no effect" determination
- Effects of increased dewatering and desiccation not considered

**Limnological Concerns**:
- Evidence for "no effect" on periphyton scour and reestablishment in [[Black Canyon]] deemed insufficient
- Assessment did not address potential chemical or biological changes in nutrient cycling
- Changes to primary and secondary productivity could influence planktivorous species

**Procedural and Consultation Issues**:
- Department's direct participation began recently, with only informal contacts prior to July 22, 1991 meeting
- Burden inappropriately placed on resource agencies to make case for revision rather than on action agency to prepare adequate assessment
- Interagency coordination should occur at earliest stages of consultation
- Department willing to participate with Service and Reclamation in improving the Assessment
- Section 6 of the Endangered Species Act establishes cooperative agreements with states maintaining adequate conservation programs
- Arizona Game and Fish Commission and Department have authority to conserve resident species determined to be endangered or threatened

**Recommendations**:
- Biological Assessment requires revision based on assessment of physical, chemical, and biological changes traceable to proposed action
- Native and nonnative fish interactions should be discussed as mitigating factors, not to exclusion of direct effects
- Bureau of Reclamation should be requested to revise assessment to fully evaluate effects of proposed dam operation changes
- Only with substantive assessment can Service objectively make determination of effect
- Department should play integral and useful role in extended or formalized consultation
