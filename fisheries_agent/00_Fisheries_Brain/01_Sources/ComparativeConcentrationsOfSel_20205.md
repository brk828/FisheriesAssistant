# Comparative Concentrations of Nutrients and Metals in Water, Suspended Matter, Hydrosol, Plants, and Fish of Chattahoochee River Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-reservoir

## Executive Summary

This Auburn University Agricultural Experiment Station study, led by J. M. Lawrence (Professor, Fisheries), compares concentrations of six elements (calcium, magnesium, iron, manganese, copper, and zinc) across the compartments of [[Chattahoochee River]] reservoir ecosystems. Samples were taken from filtered water and suspended matter in three lower reservoirs, and from water, suspended matter, bottom hydrosol, rooted aquatic plants, and fish in [[Lake Eufaula]]. The work ran March through October 1967 and 1968. The authors argue that conventional water analysis, which discards particulate matter after filtration, understates the movement of heavy metals through a reservoir. Fish are a minor focus; the main result is a comparative chemical profile of the whole aquatic environment.

## Background & Description

The [[Chattahoochee River]] rises in the Blue Ridge foothills of North Georgia and is controlled by a series of impoundments. The reservoirs relevant to this study are:

- **[[Lake Sidney Lanier]]**: uppermost impoundment, 38,000 acres, drainage area 1,040 square miles, entirely in the Blue Ridge foothills.
- **[[Bartlett's Ferry Reservoir]]**: about 150 miles downstream, 5,850 acres, drainage area 4,260 square miles.
- **[[Lake Eufaula]]**: 10th downstream impoundment, 45,000 acres, average depth 20.4 feet, drainage area 7,460 square miles. Average flow since impoundment in 1963 was 10,800 cfs.
- **[[Lake Seminole]]**: 12th impoundment, formed at the junction of the Chattahoochee and [[Flint River]], 37,500 acres, average depth 9 feet, total drainage area 17,150 square miles (8,650 on the Chattahoochee and 8,500 on the Flint).

Physiography changes downstream. The Lake Sidney Lanier drainage lies in the Blue Ridge foothills, the reach to Columbus, Georgia is Piedmont plateau, Lake Eufaula's drainage is mainly Coastal Plain (with some Black Belt soil on its western side), and Lake Seminole's drainage is lower Coastal Plain. Numerous towns and cities discharge domestic and industrial wastes into the river, so by the upper end of Lake Eufaula the river has received effluent from over 3/4 million people.

### Sampling Methods

- **Raw water and suspended matter**: collected at the surface and at 10- or 20-foot depth intervals from several stations. Particulates were retained on an 0.8 µ Millipore filter, ashed, dissolved in aqua regia, and redissolved in dilute HCl. Results were expressed as ppm of the water volume filtered.
- **Hydrosol (bottom interface or "bottom ooze")**: collected with an all-plastic sampler covering 0.5 or 1 square foot. Samples were dried, ground, and extracted with neutral ammonium acetate. Results were expressed as ppm dry weight of soil. The sampler took two years to develop.
- **Rooted plants**: freeze-dried, ground, digested as above, and expressed as ppm dry weight.
- **Fish**: collected by seining, electrofishing, rotenone, and dynamite, then stored on ice, identified by species, measured, weighed, freeze-dried, and digested. Results were expressed as ppm dry weight.
- **Analysis**: all six elements were measured by [[Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** The upper watershed is poor in calcium salts. Concentrations rise in the lower Coastal Plain, where [[Limestone]] springs add to runoff. Magnesium is scarce in the watershed's soils, and the limestone beds on the Flint River and Spring Creek arms of Lake Seminole are described as even poorer sources. Calcium and magnesium in the Chattahoochee total less than 7 ppm, placing the river among the lowest 5 percent of surface waters in the world.

**Iron and manganese.** Iron is abundant in upper-watershed soils and enters the river mainly on suspended soil particles. Both metals are largely insoluble under aerobic conditions but become reduced and soluble under anaerobic conditions, which occur in deeper Lake Eufaula waters during hot weather. Material may therefore be recycled from the hydrosol back into the water column.

**Copper and zinc.** Copper is assumed to come from biological sources such as plankton. Zinc is thought to derive mainly from watershed soils, with only a minor contribution from sewage and industrial pollution. Fish-pond runoff without an external zinc source also showed high zinc, supporting the watershed origin.

**Plants and fishes.** Rooted vegetation has never become abundant in Lake Eufaula. The authors attribute this partly to the [[Winter Drawdown]] management practice, which likely removes overwintering propagules of nuisance plants. Only three species produced enough material for analysis: [[Alligatorweed]], [[Smartweeds]], and [[Paspalum Fluitans]]. Lake Eufaula is known for [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Crappie]] fishing. Population estimates give roughly 145 pounds of fish per acre, from at least 30 species.

## Key Findings

### 1. Upstream and downstream water chemistry (Table 1)

- Calcium in filtered water ranged from 2.525 ppm at Bartlett's Ferry Reservoir to 22.107 ppm in the Spring Creek arm of Lake Seminole. Concentrations increase toward the lower Coastal Plain.
- Iron in suspended matter was several times higher than in filtered water. Iron in filtered water probably includes colloidal forms that passed the 0.8 µ filter.
- Manganese did not follow a simple pattern. Colloidal or soluble inflow dominated in the red-land area, and particulate export dominated in the sandy-land downstream reaches.
- Most zinc in the filtered fraction seemed to be soluble or colloidal. Zinc in suspended matter was often higher than the averages suggest, which the authors read as soluble zinc available for uptake by phytoplankton.
- Ignoring suspended matter has little effect on calcium and magnesium totals but a marked effect on iron, manganese, copper, and zinc totals.

### 2. Hydrosol (bottom muck) in Lake Eufaula

- Recovered layers were 1/8 to 3/8 inch thick. Dry weight averaged 265 grams per square foot, with a range of 5 to 500+ grams.
- Average extractable calcium was 1,640 ppm (range 775–3,250 ppm), equal to 434.6 mg per square foot. Magnesium averaged 380 ppm (range 170–2,150 ppm), or 100.7 mg per square foot.
- Average extractable iron was 320 ppm (range 11–3,950 ppm), or 84.8 mg per square foot. Manganese averaged 540 ppm (range 80–1,330 ppm), or 143.1 mg per square foot. Manganese was more extractable than iron, which the authors say is the reverse of the other compartments and needs further study.
- Average extractable copper was 8 ppm (range 0.2–30 ppm), or 2.1 mg per square foot. Zinc averaged 28 ppm (range 6.5–90 ppm), or 7.4 mg per square foot.
- The authors note that the extraction method was adapted from terrestrial soil science and may not be optimal for aquatic muck.

| Copper (ppm) | 80 (32–230) | 65 (10–250) | 33 (16–46) |

- The grass *P. fluitans* accumulated more manganese and zinc than the broad-leaved species. The broad-leaved species accumulated about twice as much copper.
- Iron-to-manganese ratios were about 4:1 in broad-leaved plants, similar to raw water, and about 0.8:1 in the grass, similar to hydrosol.

### 4. Resident fish (average of 10 species, whole-body dry weight)

- Calcium averaged 37,753 ppm (range 20,540–50,436 ppm). This is the largest pool of any element in the study.
- Magnesium averaged 1,939 ppm (range 1,401–2,569 ppm).
- Iron averaged 290 ppm (range 23–2,500 ppm). Manganese averaged 57.5 ppm (range 6–138 ppm).
- Copper averaged 59.7 ppm (range 11–330 ppm). Zinc averaged 211.5 ppm (range 40–940 ppm).
- Fish calcium and magnesium levels do not differ materially from those of the same species in calcium-rich waters. The authors suggest these fishes absorb and assimilate both elements even under physiological stress.
- Fish iron and manganese ratios roughly matched raw reservoir waters, and fish copper and zinc ratios roughly matched raw river waters.

### 5. Summary element abundance orders

- **Raw waters**: Ca > Mg > Fe > Zn > Mn > Cu
- **Broad-leaved plants**: Ca > Mg > Fe > Mn > Zn > Cu
- **Grass (*P. fluitans*)**: Mg > Ca > Mn > Fe > Zn > Cu
- **Fish**: Ca > Mg > Fe > Zn > Cu > Mn

### 6. Conclusions and recommendations

- Conventional analyses that discard suspended matter underestimate heavy-metal movement in flowing water. Including particulates gives a more realistic picture.
- The authors conclude that similar comparative studies are needed on many other watersheds before the chemical composition of an aquatic environment can be adequately assessed.
- Further research is needed on hydrosol extraction methods and on the iron-manganese ratio in lake bottom muck.
