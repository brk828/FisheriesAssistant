# Investigation of Techniques to Establish and Maintain Arctic Grayling and Apache Trout Lake Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Technical Report (No. 12, Federal Aid Project F-14-R; Clarkson and Dreyer, 1992, revised 1996) evaluates a "featured species" management concept using [[Arctic Grayling]] and [[Apache Trout]] in two high-elevation lakes, [[Lee Valley Reservoir]] and [[Bear Canyon Lake]], from 1986 to 1990. The concept was proposed in the [[Arizona Coldwater Fisheries Strategic Plan]] to supply uncommon, unique angling opportunities. Lee Valley Reservoir supported a successful fishery with high catch rates, and anglers strongly supported it. Bear Canyon Lake performed poorly. The authors identify physical-chemical conditions (algal-driven pH swings and winter oxygen depletion) as the greatest long-term threat to the Lee Valley fishery, and they recommend stocking and regulatory measures for continued management.

## Background & Description

**Management context.** The 1985 Coldwater Plan defined standards for lake featured-species fisheries: 1.7 angler days per surface acre per week; a sustainable harvest rate of 0.1–0.3 fish per hour; and harvestable fish of 9–12 inches (Arctic grayling) or 8–14 inches (Apache trout). Earlier grayling and Apache trout fisheries in Arizona (1960s–1980s) had been fished out after culturing ended.

**Species.**
- [[Arctic Grayling]] (*Thymallus arcticus*): stocked from Wyoming (Meadow Creek source) and Alaska egg sources.
- [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*): Arizona's endemic trout, supplied from the [[Alchesay National Fish Hatchery]] from 1988.
- [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*): the prior put-grow-and-take mainstay, discontinued at both lakes in 1986.

**Study sites.**
- *Lee Valley Reservoir*: about 2,877 m elevation, the highest reservoir in Arizona; mean surface area about 14 ha (18.1 ha at spillway); maximum depth near 6 m, mean 2.9 m; built 1899 on Lee Valley Creek.
- *Bear Canyon Lake*: about 2,304 m elevation on the Mogollon Rim; 26 surface ha; maximum depth 17 m; impounded by AGFD in 1964.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lee Valley Reservoir.** The watershed (about 2.7 km²) lies in Petran subalpine conifer forest. Mean annual precipitation is 76 cm and mean evaporation 112 cm. Historically the basin held Apache trout and bluehead mountain sucker. Fathead minnows were introduced before 1978. A hybrid of Apache and rainbow trout was collected in 1947. Study-period water quality showed a 6.0–9.6 pH range, with pH above 9.0 for three months in 1988 and algal blooms in August 1989. Winter dissolved oxygen fell to marginal levels (under 4 mg/L) in March. Conductivity was 40–67 µS/cm.

**Bear Canyon Lake.** The lake is fed by snowmelt from ephemeral tributaries. Surface pH was 5.7–6.1, conductivity 28–33 µS/cm, and epilimnetic oxygen always exceeded 6 mg/L. Native fishes in adjacent perennial streams included speckled dace, Little Colorado spinedace, and roundtail chub.

**Trophic relationships.** Zooplankton in Lee Valley was dominated by *Daphnia*; Bear Canyon zooplankton densities were roughly an order of magnitude lower. Benthic samples in Lee Valley were dominated by oligochaetes (up to 11,644/m²), with fingernail clams (*Pisidium*) and [[Hyalella Azteca]] also abundant. Bear Canyon benthos was not sampled.

## Key Findings

**Angler use and catch**
- Lee Valley angler effort was roughly half the 1986 estimate of 20,963 hours. Annual pressure was about 2.4 (1987) and 2.7 (1989) angler days per acre per week, within management limits.
- Bear Canyon effort was 3,200 hours in 1987 and 7,000 in 1988; pressure was 1.0 and 0.5 angler days per acre per week.
- Lee Valley mean catch rate rose from 0.81 fish/hr (1987) to a peak of 2.20 fish/hr (1989), then 1.66 fish/hr in 1990. Mean annual Arctic grayling catch was about 1.58 fish/hr in 1988–90 (with a 1989 peak of 4.39 fish/hr in May).
- Apache trout catch was 0.32 fish/hr (1988) and 0.62 fish/hr (1989).
- Bear Canyon catch rates stayed below 0.2 fish/hr for most months, and harvest was near zero.

**Harvest**
- Lee Valley harvest was 0.22 fish/hr in 1987 (all brook trout, about 2,400 fish), 0.06 in 1988, 0.11 in 1989 and 0.12 in 1990 (all Arctic grayling; estimated 1,332 and 1,164 fish). Harvest was near or below the 0.1 fish/hr minimum standard.
- Apache trout never reached legal size before over-winter losses.

**Angler attitudes**
- A large majority of Lee Valley anglers supported the gear restrictions, bag limits, and species choice. About 90% of anglers favored the 12-inch minimum size limit in 1989.
- Bear Canyon anglers expressed only mild support or dissatisfaction.

**Survival and population**
- Lee Valley Arctic grayling Peterson estimates: 11,969 (1988, 95% CI 9,744–14,682); 8,189 (spring 1989); 5,355 (1990). Survival was 68% (1988–89) and 65% (1989–90), with about 47% of 1990 mortality attributed to angling.
- Over-winter mortality of the 1988 cohort was near total.
- Apache trout estimate: 1,865 (1,646–2,713) survived to October 1988 from 5,071 stocked; all deaths were attributed to natural or hooking mortality.

**Growth**
- The 1986 Arctic grayling cohort grew from 76 mm (May 1987) to 236 mm by November 1987 (26.6 mm/month). Mean length reached 301 mm by November 1988, just below the 305 mm legal limit.
- Maximum recorded grayling length in Lee Valley was 365 mm.
- Apache trout stocked in 1988 reached 234 mm and 127 g by November.
- Bear Canyon grayling grew roughly 10% slower than Lee Valley fish.

**Trophic effects**
- Introduction of Arctic grayling is hypothesized to have reduced *Daphnia* and increased rotifer and *Bosmina* densities, consistent with a trophic cascade. Primary production was not measured.
- Diet overlap (Schoener index) between age-2 grayling and age-1 Apache trout was 0.61 (May), 0.47 (August), and 0.69 (November) in 1988.
- Apache trout were omnivorous and recorded as piscivorous, reportedly the first records for the species.

**Stocking and egg take**
- Lee Valley received about 43,672 fingerling grayling in November 1986, roughly 1,200 fish/ha.
- Egg losses from shipments were heavy: about 100,000 Alaska eggs died in 1986, and all 199,000 Alaska eggs died in 1988.
- An egg take of about 880,000 eggs in 1989 was lost to a hatchery headbox collapse. About 900,000 eggs were collected in 1990.

**Conclusions and management options**
1. Control nutrient eutrophication. Late-autumn algal blooms threaten winterkill and pH swings. Buffering (calcium carbonate), phosphorus inactivation, and watershed nutrient control (e.g., fencing cattle) were suggested.
2. Maintain current bag and gear restrictions at Lee Valley.
3. Stock about 5,000 age-1 grayling in spring; spring stocking of age-1 fish could roughly double the expected annual yield compared with autumn fingerlings.
4. Expect poor over-winter survival of Apache trout; stock catchable-size fish if harvest is expected.
5. Manage Bear Canyon Lake as a put-and-take fishery with relaxed regulations, or drop it from featured-species management; the authors removed it as a research site in 1989.
6. Continue monitoring and creel surveys; use the equilibrium yield model to test regulation changes.
