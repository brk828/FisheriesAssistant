# Small Nonnative Fishes as Predators of Larval Razorback Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

The endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a big-river fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]], has shown no sustainable recruitment in four decades, even though spawning adults and larvae are present. Predation by [[Nonnative Fishes]] has been proposed as a major cause. Control efforts have removed large nonnative game predators, which has reduced their abundance, but small nonnative fishes have increased in some areas.

The study used laboratory predation trials to test whether nine nonnative species consume larval razorback suckers. All nine species fed on larvae (total length 9–16 mm). The authors conclude that small nonnative fishes may be limiting recovery of the species.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was abundant throughout the Colorado River basin historically. Populations declined in the lower basin by 1935 and in the upper basin by the 1960s. Causes of decline include [[Flow Regulation]], habitat modification, pesticides and pollutants, and interactions with nonnative fishes.

[[Minckley]] (1983) concluded that predation by nonnative fishes on early life stages was the primary cause of the precipitous decline. Stocking of nonnative fishes in the basin began in the 1880s, and numerous introductions followed by the 1900s.

Study species were chosen because they span a broad ecological range and are abundant within the historical range of the razorback sucker:

- **Small adults (three species):** [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*).
- **Juveniles of larger species (six species):** [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental design.** Trials were run in aquarium tanks with opaque film and paper on the sides and back. A fine-mesh opaque screen separated predators from prey at the start. Each treatment tank held four predators of one species on one side and 20 larval razorback suckers on the other. After a 10-minute acclimation period, the screen was removed. Predators were also offered alternative food (bloodworms, thawed adult [[Artemia]], and larval flake food). Control tanks held 20 larvae and no predators.

**Trial conditions.** Trials ran for 24 hours or until more than 50% of larvae were eaten. Water temperatures were 20–25 °C. Trials took place from 21–28 March 2003 and 15–30 March 2004.

**Larval prey.** Larvae were 10–16 mm total length, with body depths of 1.8–2.4 mm. Subsamples were measured before and after trials to avoid stressing larvae used in the experiments.

**Predator gape.** Mean gape ranged from 2.6 mm in fathead minnows to 7.8 mm in threadfin shad. Some fathead minnows had gapes smaller than the body depth of available larvae, so gape limitation may restrict their feeding on some prey.

**Analysis.** Two-sample, separate-variance t-tests compared mortality in treatment and control tanks. Where control mortality was zero, a one-sample t-test checked whether treatment mortality was significantly above zero.

**Gut content limitations.** Gut contents of the tested species and sizes rarely contain larval fishes, and [[Gut Content Analysis]] of field-collected fish is unreliable. Earlier studies found that larvae digest quickly and may be missed. Absence of larvae in field samples therefore does not show that nonnative fishes are not preying on native larvae.

**Size-selective and habitat overlap.** For many nonnative fishes, prey size increases with predator size, so large fishes are unlikely to eat small larvae. Small nonnative fishes and juveniles of larger species are more numerous and use the same shallow shorelines, backwaters, and vegetated nursery habitats as razorback suckers and other native fishes.

## Key Findings

**1. All nine nonnative species consumed larval razorback suckers.** This includes six species not previously tested in this role: juvenile common carp, yellow bullhead, channel catfish, rainbow trout, bluegill, and green sunfish.

**2. Predation rates differed among species.**

- Rainbow trout and green sunfish were the most voracious.
- Rainbow trout had 0.3 mean mortalities per tank in three control tanks. Green sunfish control tanks had zero mortality.
- Trials for the moderately predaceous species lasted more than 1 hour, ranging from 5 to 24 hours.

**3. Fathead minnows were gape-limited but still preyed on larvae.** Some individuals had gapes smaller than larval body depth. The authors observed larval predation by fathead minnows, though they did not see other attacks. The authors suggest differences from earlier studies may reflect the availability of smaller alternative food, different research goals, and shorter observation periods. [[Karp and Tyus]] (1990) and [[Markle and Dunsmoor]] (2007) reported fathead minnow attacks on larvae in other settings.

**4. Gut content analysis underestimates predation.** Field stomach analyses can miss larval prey because of rapid digestion, so the absence of larvae in gut contents is not evidence against predation.

**5. Conclusion.** Given prey-size selection, habitat overlap, and high relative abundance, small nonnative fishes pose a major threat to early life stages of razorback suckers. Without management of these predatory impacts, recovery of the species will be limited.
