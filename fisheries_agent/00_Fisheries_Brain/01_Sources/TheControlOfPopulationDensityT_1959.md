## Executive Summary
This 1959 theoretical paper by V. C. Wynne-Edwards is a foundational text in population ecology, not a fish-specific taxonomic study. It proposes that animal population densities are not merely limited by extrinsic factors (food, predation) but are **actively self-regulated** through evolved social conventions (territoriality, social hierarchies, communal displays) that function as a "proxy" for direct competition over resources. Although the paper's primary case studies are avian, it draws heavily on **marine ecological data** — most notably a strong correlation between pelagic bird density and plankton biomass, and an analogous correlation between whale density and krill (*Euphausia superba*) biomass — to support the broader thesis that population density tracks food-resource productivity. The paper also discusses fisheries and whaling management as human analogues of "conventional" resource-limiting behavior, coining the generalized concept of "overfishing" as any unrestricted exploitation of a limiting resource.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The paper frames marine and avian systems as parallel case studies of density-dependent resource tracking:

- **Pelagic seabirds and plankton (North Atlantic):** Jespersen (1930), analyzing data from the research vessel *Dana* (1920–22), found bird densities correlated with plankton density across a 10° latitude/longitude grid at **r = 0.85 (P < 0.001)**, despite plankton abundance varying over 100-fold across sampled regions. This is cited as strong evidence that pelagic birds actively disperse themselves to match local food-resource productivity — a marine analogue to territorial food-tracking in land birds.

- **[[Baleen whales]] and [[Antarctic krill]] (South Georgia):** Hardy & Gunther (1935) demonstrated an analogous density correlation between whalebone whales and their staple prey, the krill *Euphausia superba*, in waters adjacent to the South Georgia whaling grounds (1926–27 Discovery Reports survey). This is used as independent confirmation, in a marine mammal/invertebrate predator-prey system, of the general principle that consumer density tracks resource density.

- **Terrestrial comparison (tits):** Kluyver & Tinbergen (1953) showed *Parus major*, *P. caeruleus*, and *P. ater* breed at higher densities and hold smaller territories in resource-rich mixed woods versus pinewoods in the Netherlands, illustrating territory-size plasticity as a density-regulating mechanism dependent on habitat productivity — conceptually parallel to the marine correlations.

- **Human fisheries and whaling as ecological analogues:** The paper explicitly frames commercial fishing and whaling as cases where humans act as "predators" exploiting natural stocks, and highlights the risk of **overfishing** — depleting a resource beyond its sustainable yield — as a universal ecological hazard, not unique to human exploitation. The International Whaling Convention's system of capping catches at an agreed number of "blue-whale units" per season is cited as a real-world example of an artificial, convention-based limit on exploitation, analogous to the "conventional" social behaviors (territoriality, hierarchy) the author proposes evolved in wild animal populations to prevent self-imposed overfishing of their own food resources.

## Key Findings
- **Density–food correlation:** Across widely disparate taxa (pelagic birds/plankton; baleen whales/krill), population density tracks local food abundance with striking statistical strength (r = 0.85, P < 0.001 in the *Dana* seabird/plankton dataset), supporting the idea that animals actively self-distribute according to resource productivity rather than being passively limited by starvation alone.
- **"Overfishing" as a general ecological hazard:** The author generalizes the fisheries/whaling concept of overfishing (unsustainable exploitation leading to stock depletion and diminishing returns) as an inherent risk faced by *any* consumer–resource (predator–prey) relationship in nature, not just human harvest systems.
- **Conventional (social) behaviors as regulatory mechanisms:** Territoriality, communal nest-site tenure (e.g., in [[Corvus frugilegus]]), social hierarchies/peck-orders, and communal roosting (e.g., [[Sturnus vulgaris]]) are proposed as evolved "conventions" that substitute for direct competition over food, thereby capping population density before resource depletion occurs — a mechanism the author terms **epideictic behavior/demonstration**.
- **Human whaling/fishery management as a real-world parallel:** International catch-limit systems (e.g., blue-whale-unit quotas) are presented as a human institutional analogue to the naturally-evolved density-regulating conventions hypothesized to occur in wild populations.
- **Implication for marine resource management:** Though not the paper's central focus, the strong empirical correlations from marine systems (bird–plankton, whale–krill) are used as the most quantitatively robust evidence in the entire paper for the density-dependent, resource-tracking behavior underlying the author's broader self-regulation hypothesis — making this a frequently cited early theoretical touchstone in marine population ecology and fisheries science discussions of density-dependence.
