# Impact of Flooding in a Sonoran Desert Stream, Including Elimination of an Endangered Fish Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents the effects of severe winter flooding (winter 1977–78) on [[Tule Creek]], a small perennial desert stream in Yavapai County, Arizona, about 58 km NW of Phoenix. The flood, most likely caused by an 8-day storm beginning in late February 1978, re-channelized the stream, scoured sediments from an organic [[Ciénega]], and eliminated the only persisting population of the [[Sonoran Topminnow]], an endangered fish. Other vertebrates, including [[Leopard Frog]] larvae, were also affected, while [[Canyon Treefrog]] and [[Saltcedar]] appeared for the first time. The authors conclude that effects were extensive and immediate, but that most biota other than the topminnow recovered quickly, and that only "100-year" rains of this magnitude could produce such change in an established ciénega.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Tule Creek is a 800 m long perennial reach, part of a watershed of about 29 km² (OCR-uncertain value). Elevations range from roughly 640 m to 1,186 m. The creek is in the [[Sonoran Desert]], where intense summer thunderstorms and sparse winter frontal storms produce contrasting flood regimes.
- **Pre-flood condition:** Surface flow was rarely more than 0.5 m wide and contained 37 discrete pools. The channel substrate was highly organic, with some deposits about 2.0 m thick. Dominant plants included three-square (*Scirpus olneyi*) and lizardtail (*Anemopsis californica*), with willows and horsetail on saturated soils.
- **Vertebrate fauna before flooding:** Four species were present: [[Leopard Frog]] (*Rana pipiens*, lowland form), [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon sonoriense*), [[Black-Necked Garter Snake]] (*Thamnophis cyrtopsis*), and the Sonoran Topminnow. Populations were dense, with the topminnow occurring throughout the lower 730 m and numbering in the thousands.
- **Topminnow history:** The population was introduced to extend the range of this endangered species and was one of the few transplanted stocks to persist for more than a few months, reaching about 10 years. The species is classed as endangered in the United States, though still widespread in Mexico.
- **Authors and publication:** Collins et al., *The Southwestern Naturalist*, vol. 26, no. 4, November 1981. The authors are from the Department of Zoology, [[Arizona State University]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flood regime and rainfall.** Relatively high rainfall occurred from January to early March 1978, with the greatest totals in late February and early March. The authors attribute most channel alteration to the 8-day storm rather than cumulative rainfall. If a flow speed of 1.0 m/s is assumed, the post-flood channel cross-section could have carried about 570 m³/s at peak, compared with a mean modal discharge of about 0.02 m³/s.

**Geomorphic change.** The channel became more linear through incision and partial removal of ciénega deposits. Pools fell from 37 to 29, and sediments were removed to a maximum depth of 2.0 m. A minimum of 2,726 m³ of sediment was displaced downstream toward the [[Agua Fria River]], probably an underestimate because material was also deposited from upstream. Water flowed as much as 2.0 m below the stranded ciénega surface. The authors note that the stream's form had been shaped by floods of lesser magnitude (1968–77), which had deposited thin inorganic layers later incorporated into the substrate.

**Habitat role of the ciénega.** Dense, matted sedge and lizardtail roots provided habitat for topminnows and tadpoles. When these were removed by scour, the associated fish and tadpoles were also decimated. Although plant numbers were drastically reduced, no major plant species was exterminated.

**Invertebrates.** Macroinvertebrate populations were greatly reduced but recovered quickly, through both immigration (e.g., large Belostomatidae) and reproduction by survivors (e.g., baetid mayflies). Gray (1980), cited for comparison, reported recovery in 2 or 3 weeks in other desert streams, whereas Siegfried and Knight (1977) found several months.

## Key Findings

1. **Elimination of the topminnow.** The Sonoran Topminnow population in Tule Creek was eliminated. Because no populations exist in the Agua Fria drainage to supply immigrants, the authors conclude it "should remain exterminated unless reintroduced." Habitat destruction and predation by introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and other piscivores had already reduced the species to a few isolated enclaves.
2. **Leopard frogs.** No larval [[Leopard Frog]]s were present immediately after flooding (19 March), when overwintering adpoles should have been present. Adults nonetheless bred successfully in early April.
3. **Reptiles.** [[Black-Necked Garter Snake]]s and [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]]s were present in relatively large numbers after flooding, indicating resilience.
4. **New arrivals.** Juvenile [[Canyon Treefrog]]s (*Hyla arenicolor*) were recorded for the first time from the reach, presumably transported by floodwaters from upstream. The authors suggest habitat change from ciénega to a higher-gradient, rocky brook may have aided establishment.
5. **Saltcedar invasion.** Several dozen [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) seedlings were found on open sandbars on 25 August 1978, the first records for the study area. The nearest known seed source was the Agua Fria River. The authors suggest scouring created moist germination microsites, and that the species may eventually dominate the altered channel.
6. **Successional reset.** Removal of the ciénega set the ecosystem back to an earlier successional state. Narrower, more steeply graded channels are likely more vulnerable to future flooding and erosion.
7. **Recovery expectations.** Most biota other than the topminnow showed relatively minor long-term impacts. The resident population was reestablished within 6 weeks, and natural selection is expected to favor rapid recolonization in such flood-prone systems.
8. **Flood magnitude.** In an established ciénega, only "100-year" rains, such as those in winter 1978, are powerful enough to produce the massive change seen in Tule Creek. Dams, water developments, and introduced competitors and predators now prevent natural re-immigration of the topminnow from larger historical streams.

**Methods note.** Flood impacts were quantified by mapping pools and riffles before (May 1977) and after (June 1978) flooding, and by estimating sediment volume from 20 m transects. Surface flow was repeatedly surveyed for vertebrates and vegetation changes.
