# Decline of Freshwater Fishes and Fisheries in West-Central Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #atherinidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two items from *Fisheries* (April 1998). The first is a fragment of the NMFS **Angler Conservation Education Plan**, which sets out goals for angler stewardship and ethics. The second, and main, item is a research article by Lyons, González-Hernández, Soto-Galera and Guzmán-Arroyo on the decline of freshwater fishes and fisheries in west-central Mexico. The article reports that fish communities in the [[Lerma River Basin]], [[Lake Chapala]] and the [[Ayuquila River]] are declining sharply because of environmental degradation. Three of 44 native fishes in the Lerma basin are extinct and 23 more are greatly reduced. Lake Chapala's total catch has risen, but valuable native species have collapsed and exotics now make up a large share of the catch. The authors call for protecting remaining native fish localities, reducing water withdrawals and pollution, controlling fishing effort, and increasing collaboration with U.S. and Canadian fisheries professionals.

## Background & Description

**Angler education (NMFS plan fragment).** The NMFS produced educational materials to promote safer boat operation, respect for property and other people, and universally responsible fishing. A task force of recreational fishing interests developed the [[Angler Conservation Education Plan]] to build on earlier efforts. It was organized around seven goals, including educating anglers about their impacts, increasing regulatory compliance, expanding angler involvement in management, promoting careful handling of bycatch, and boosting recognition of [[Catch-and-Release]] skills. The plan cites Vander Steop and Gramann (1987), who reported an 88% reduction in damaging behavior among park visitors after verbal appeals. The fragment's authors argue that indirect, voluntary approaches can be less costly and less controversial than regulation and enforcement.

**Study region.** West-central Mexico is a seasonally arid Pacific-slope region between Mazatlán and Acapulco. It has about 100 native fish species. Endemism is high, with about 70% of fishes found nowhere else. Almost 45 species are viviparous, and the endemic subfamily [[Goodeidae]] (Goodeinae) has been important for studies of reproduction and evolution. The egg-laying endemic genus [[Chirostoma]] (Atherinidae) forms a species flock that supports commercial and subsistence fisheries that have lasted for thousands of years.

**Study scope.** The authors reviewed published literature and unpublished data on three areas: the Lerma River basin (about 53,500 km², with the 700-km Lerma River), Lake Chapala (Mexico's largest natural lake, maximum area 1,250 km² and maximum depth 7 m), and the Ayuquila River watershed (about 1,200 km², part of the Armeria River basin).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate:** Annual precipitation ranges from more than 1,250 mm near the coast to under 500 mm inland. A distinct dry season runs from roughly October to May, and water demand peaks then.
- **Lerma basin:** Among Mexico's most densely populated and developed areas. Dams on the mainstem and major tributaries store water for the dry season. Untreated municipal, industrial and agricultural wastes enter rivers throughout the basin.
- **Lake Chapala:** Receives large inputs of waste from the Lerma River. When dry-season withdrawals reduce river flow, lake area drops by up to 21% and volume by up to 69%.
- **Ayuquila River:** Near the head of the Autlán valley, more than 99% of the river is diverted during the dry season to irrigate sugar cane. Less than half of the diverted water returns to the channel. Downstream, the river receives untreated sugar mill wastes and untreated sewage from Autlán and El Grullo (combined 1990 population about 80,000).
- **Protected area:** The [[Sierra de Manantlán Biosphere Reserve]] protects part of the Ayuquila drainage below the Autlán valley. Local residents and institutions help manage it.

## Key Findings

### Lerma River Basin
- Of 116 localities with pre-1985 fish data, 29 (25%) no longer had water and 30 (26%) were too polluted to hold fish by 1985–1993. More than half of the localities that once supported fish no longer do.
- Only 17 sites (15%), mostly lakes, springs and small streams far from population centers, still held fish species sensitive to degradation.
- Of 44 native species (31 endemic), **3 (7%) are likely extinct**: *Algansea barbata*, *Chirostoma charari* and *Chirostoma compressum*.
- Another **14 species (32%)** have major range contractions. **9 species (20%)** have major abundance declines.
- At least **26 of 44 native species (59%)** are markedly less common than earlier in the century. No native species has expanded its range or abundance.
- At least **11 exotic species** have become established in the basin.
- The authors compare the degree of degradation to the worst documented cases in similarly sized basins worldwide.

### Lake Chapala
- No fish species are known to have disappeared from the lake, but several commercially important species are now rare.
- Total commercial landings rose from **600–2,500 mt (1938–1970)** to **300–8,900 mt (1980–1995)**, with much greater variability in the later period. The increase came mainly from charales, the exotic carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) and the exotic tilapia (*Oreochromis aureus*, established since about 1980). Greater catches reflected more fishers and better gear, including motorized boats and synthetic nets.
- **Charales** (five small *Chirostoma* species) are the main native component of the catch by weight.
- **Pescado blanco** (three large piscivorous *Chirostoma* species) has followed a boom-and-bust pattern, ranging from 2 to 112 mt per year. Catches declined severely in the 1990s, and fishers believe the species is now rare.
- **Bagre** (native *Ictalurus dugesi* and *I. ochoterenai*, plus exotic *I. punctatus*) peaked at a record 465 mt in 1982. Catches then collapsed to 4 mt by 1990, probably from overfishing combined with habitat loss when lake levels fell during diversions. Catches remained low (3–10 mt per year) after lake levels recovered.
- **Popoche** (*Algansea popoche*) declined steadily from 1938 to 1970. It is now nearly extinct. Monthly sampling in 1988–1991 yielded only a single individual.
- Catches fell after 1990, largely because of an infestation of the exotic water hyacinth (*Eichhornia crassipes*), and possibly because fish abundance also declined.

### Ayuquila River
- Seven native species have been extirpated from two river sections.
- Above the Autlán valley, 3 of 10 native species have disappeared.
- Within the Autlán valley, 4 of 10 species have been eliminated, including 3 of high fishery value. The segment has no fishery or recreational use, and its invertebrate community has been severely reduced.
- Below the valley, an intact fish community persists and supports an important subsistence fishery. Fish species richness and dissolved oxygen are sharply reduced for about 25 km below the sugar mill in the dry season, and recovery is partial in the wet season.
- Local reports describe illness in cattle and residents exposed to the river, including children with skin, intestinal and respiratory disease. The authors treat these as observations reported by Santana-C. et al. (1993), not as tested health findings.

### Regional Comparisons and Conservation Priorities
- In the Valley of Mexico, three of four endemic fishes are extinct and one is officially threatened.
- In the Chihuahuan Desert region, at least 4 of 55 native fish species and subspecies are extinct, and 25 have been locally extirpated.
- **Priorities:** protect remaining native-fish localities (especially headwaters, springs and small lakes); improve treatment of municipal and industrial discharges; use water more efficiently for irrigation; and control fishing effort in Lake Chapala, possibly by limiting the number of commercial fishers.
- **Biosphere reserve model:** The authors consider this approach the most realistic for much of west-central Mexico, where population densities are too high for large national parks or refuges without residents. For the Ayuquila, they say the most effective immediate step is reducing sugar mill pollution, and they call for low-cost treatment technologies.
- **International collaboration:** Mexico has few ichthyologists and fisheries biologists, and they face tight budgets. The authors urge U.S. and Canadian professionals to join collaborative projects through the American Fisheries Society's "Bridge to Mexico" program.
