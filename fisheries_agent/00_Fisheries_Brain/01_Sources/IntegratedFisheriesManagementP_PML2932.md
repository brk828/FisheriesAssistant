# Integrated Fisheries Management Plan for the Little Colorado River Watershed

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Technical Report 146 (July 2001, edited by Kirk L. Young, E. Patricia Lopez and David B. Dorum) presents a reach-level management plan for the [[Little Colorado River]] watershed. It integrates management of threatened native fishes and recreational sportfish, a departure from earlier practice in which the two were managed separately and often in conflict. The plan rests on a [[Watershed-Based Fisheries Management]] approach, a [[Cell-Based GIS Habitat Modeling]] analysis of 804 sample sites, and 154 stream and 98 lake management units, each with recommendations. The watershed supports about 850,000 angler days per year, with an estimated economic benefit above $80 million, and contains nine native fish species, four of them federally listed.

## Background & Description

The Little Colorado River (LCR) watershed covers roughly 70,160 km² (27,089 mi²) on the [[Colorado Plateau]]. Elevation ranges from 3,850 m (12,600 ft) on Mt. Humphreys to 830 m (2,725 ft) at the confluence with the Colorado River in Grand Canyon. Mean annual precipitation exceeds 75 cm (30 in) at high elevations and falls to 15 cm (6 in) or less on the plateau.

Management authority derives from Arizona Revised Statute Title 17 and the AGFD [[Wildlife 2000 Strategic Plan]], which directs staff to "develop watershed-based management plans" that minimize conflict between sportfish and native fish. Federal funding (Dingell-Johnson and Wallop-Breaux) and the [[Endangered Species Act]] shape compliance requirements.

Two parallel approaches were developed from 1995: the Integrated Plan (this report, NGTR 146) and a companion Watershed Plan (NGTR 169, Allison and Kubly 2001). The Foreword notes that both were reviewed internally and externally, and that AGFD still needs a unified long-term vision.

Historical context is significant. Early Spanish and 19th-century explorers described a river lined with cottonwoods and willows and rich grasslands. Overgrazing under the Aztec Land and Cattle Company, drought in the 1890s, and dam building by Mormon settlers degraded riparian and stream habitats. Stocking of non-native trout began in the 1917 to 1930s era, and more than 80 introduced fish species are now recorded in Arizona, against 32 native species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species of concern.** Four federally listed species (Little Colorado spinedace, Apache trout, razorback sucker, humpback chub) and the [[Roundtail Chub]] appear in the watershed. Of the nine native species, the plan treats Little Colorado sucker, speckled dace, bluehead sucker and Little Colorado spinedace as target native fishes for habitat modeling.

**Predation and competition.** Section 7 consultation under the ESA found that predation by stocked trout and their progeny threatens the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]]. Three reservoirs were identified as potential problem areas (Blinn and Runck; Marsh and Douglas 1997).

**Hydrology.** Median gaged flow in the upper LCR basins totals 195,224 acre-feet per year; cultural depletions average 91,480 acre-feet annually, and natural losses add 50,720 acre-feet. Reservoir evaporation often exceeds 127 cm (50 in) per year.

**Land ownership.** Private land (36.2%), federal land (36.7%) and state land (19.2%) divide the most water-generating part of the watershed. AGFD manages about 0.12% of the land base.

**Habitat condition.** Lower reaches, once perennial with narrow banks, are now wide, sandy and intermittent. Salt cedar has replaced broadleaf riparian stands in many areas.

## Key Findings

- **Integrated management framework:** Native and sportfish are to be managed together in mixed assemblages according to habitat preference, with management units as the planning building blocks.
- **Native fish needs:** A native fish expert group (November 1998) recommended no net loss of any population for speckled dace, bluehead sucker and Little Colorado sucker, repatriation into drainages where they were likely poisoned, and refugia for spinedace in drainages such as East Clear Creek, Chevelon Creek, Nutrioso and Rudd creeks, and Silver Creek.
- **Roundtail chub:** Two existing populations (Clear Creek and Chevelon Canyon) are to be maintained and enhanced, and repatriation is planned to create two populations in each of four sub-basins.
- **Apache trout:** Management follows the Apache Trout Implementation Plan, with repatriation into LCR headwaters within former range.
- **Management units:** 98 lake units and 154 stream units (1,534.8 mi / 2,468.2 km of stream) were delineated and evaluated with native and sportfish flowcharts.
- **Lake emphasis:** 52 lake units (6,432 surface acres) are dedicated to sportfish, 3 units (5 acres) to native fish, and 14 units (813 acres) remain unknown.
- **Stream emphasis:** About 53.6% of stream miles are unknown, 42.0% native fish emphasis and 4.4% sportfish emphasis. Of perennial waters, 58.6% carry native emphasis and 29.6% sportfish emphasis.
- **Species incompatibility:** Database search flagged potentially antagonistic pairs in 18 units (Table 5.1), most often Roundtail Chub with Little Colorado Spinedace, and Apache Trout with Rainbow Trout or Brown Trout; 14 mitigating factors are listed.
- **Habitat models:** [[Cell-Based GIS Habitat Modeling]] based on 804 sample sites (27,949 records) produced suitability profiles for 6 native and 19 non-native species. Little Colorado Spinedace was observed at 85 sites, Little Colorado Sucker at 90, Bluehead Sucker at 184, Speckled Dace at 330. Roundtail Chub (8 sites) and Apache Trout (26 sites) were too rare for models.
- **Model limitations:** Sampling was clumped and non-random, the stream-type cover has a 0.8 km minimum mapping unit, DEM vertical errors range from 1 to 3 m, and spatial autocorrelation was not accounted for. The authors describe the models as "liberal internally, but conservative externally."
- **Habitat-use analysis:** Logistic regression on 339 lotic sites showed that spinedace and suckers increase with stream order and decline with link slope; speckled dace and fathead minnow favour runs over pools.
- **Data management:** Georeferenced databases (fish collection, stocking, museum, creel, management actions, notes) and GIS covers were built to support future prioritisation.
- **Recommendations:** Standardise data collection, record UTM coordinates, improve hydrology covers (add an interrupted-perennial class), support five-year review cycles, and pursue participation in watershed associations such as the Verde Watershed Association.
- **Schedule:** Watershed-based plans for at least two watersheds were targeted by 2006 (AGFD 2001).
