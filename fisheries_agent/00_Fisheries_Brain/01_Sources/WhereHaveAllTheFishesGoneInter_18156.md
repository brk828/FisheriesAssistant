# Where Have All the Fishes Gone? Interactive Factors Producing Fish Declines in the Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #Osmeridae #Cyprinidae #Gobiidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[William A. Bennett]] and [[Peter B. Moyle]] (in [[San Francisco Bay: The Ecosystem]], 1996) examines the sharp decline of fish populations in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary]]. The authors use a conceptual model in which alteration of freshwater outflow affects the survival of larval and juvenile fishes (recruitment success) through six interacting pathways. They argue that recruitment is regulated by several pathways acting together or synergistically, so single-factor explanations are inadequate. Reduced outflow, driven by [[Water Diversion]] and drought, is identified as the single most important factor, and the authors conclude that ensuring adequate outflow is the most pragmatic management action.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Review of factors behind declines in estuarine fish populations, framed around recruitment (survival through the first year of life), which the authors define operationally as poor first-year survival.
- **Status of species:** Winter-run [[Chinook Salmon]] is listed as endangered, [[Delta Smelt]] as threatened, and Sacramento Splittail has been proposed for listing. Fisheries for [[Striped Bass]], Chinook Salmon, and others have been reduced to low levels.
- **Study area:** The estuary is divided into four sub-embayments. The [[Delta]], the uppermost part of the estuary, is the focal region (Figure 1).
- **Life histories:** Most declining species are short-lived (about 1 to 3 years, with splittail at 5 to 7). Table 1 compares life span, spawning season, egg type, size at hatch, starvation sensitivity, pelagic or benthic habitat, and Delta dependence for 14 species.
- **Early life knowledge:** Early life stages are poorly known for many of the most severely affected species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Freshwater outflow and climate.** The estuary drains about 40% of the state's surface area. About 70% of California's rainfall falls north of the estuary, while about 80% of water consumption occurs south of it. Precipitation variability increased in 1974 to 1994 relative to the previous 50 to 100 years. The 1976 and 1977 droughts, the extreme wet year of 1983, and the wettest month on record (February 1986) are noted. The 1976 to 1977 [[El Niño]] produced a persistent Pacific climate shift affecting about 40 variables.

**Diversion.** Upstream diversions remove about 30% of mean annual unimpaired flow. Within-Delta agricultural diversions total about 1 million acre-feet per year across about 2,000 small diversions. South Delta pumps of the [[State Water Project]] and [[Central Valley Project]] have taken up to 60% of inflow in some years, producing reverse flows in the San Joaquin River.

**Habitat loss.** Since 1852, Delta islands have been diked for farmland. About 90% of the former wetland became dry land, and only about 3% of the Delta still resembles its original shallow-water habitat. Shallow-water habitat now forms mainly in very wet springs. Native [[Sacramento Splittail]] recruitment is strongly tied to this habitat.

**Pollution.** Pollution began in the 1860s with hydraulic mining sediment and mercury. Later sources included sewage, industry, and agriculture. The [[Clean Water Act]] (1972) and the Porter-Cologne Act followed. Contaminants found include pesticides, heavy metals, petroleum compounds, and organochlorines. Rice-field pesticide runoff in the Sacramento River, which can contribute up to 30% of river volume, is a key concern for striped bass.

**Species invasions.** Introductions in the late 1800s included American shad (1871), white catfish (1874), and striped bass (1879). Invasions accelerated during 1985 to 1993, and the [[Asiatic Clam]] (Potamocorbula amurensis) became the dominant filter feeder after 1986. The shimofuri goby arrived around 1986 and is now among the most abundant fishes. Inland silversides, introduced to Clear Lake in 1966, spread to the Delta.

## Key Findings

**1. Six interacting pathways link outflow to recruitment:**
- **Path 1, Transport and entrainment:** Cross-Delta flow carries larvae and juveniles into the south Delta pumps, where they are entrained and preyed upon near [[Clifton Court Forebay]].
- **Path 2, Retention and advection:** Under low outflow, tidal mixing replaces gravitational circulation, reducing the retention of larvae in the upper estuary. Larval striped bass and juvenile longfin smelt migrate vertically to maintain position.
- **Path 3, Riverine input and food limitation:** Low outflow was expected to reduce food, but field evidence was mixed. Of 980 field-caught larvae (1988 to 1991), all had been feeding. Otolith data show larval growth of 0.15 to 0.22 mm/day in drought years versus 0.25 mm/day in the wet year 1986, and well below the roughly 0.30 mm/day reported for [[Chesapeake Bay]] striped bass. Slow growth lengthens the vulnerable larval period.
- **Path 4, Species introductions:** Exotic species establish more easily when assemblages are stressed by habitat change or reduced outflow. An intraguild predation relationship may exist between inland silversides and delta smelt, with diet overlap and negative abundance association in dry years (P < 0.05, R² = 0.38).
- **Path 5, Dilution and flushing of pollutants:** In 1988 to 1990, 26 to 30% of field-caught striped bass larvae showed liver alterations consistent with rice pesticide exposure. Toxicity declined about 50% in 1991, but year-class success remained poor.
- **Path 6, Habitat alteration:** Loss of shallow, wetland, and spawning habitat reduces productivity and residence time for larval and juvenile fishes.

**2. Recruitment failure is multi-causal.** The authors argue that food limitation, toxic exposure, and entrainment act stochastically and synergistically, especially in low outflow years. In 1991, toxicity fell about 50%, but recruitment stayed very low, indicating that other factors such as slow growth and entrainment limited survivors. Under these conditions, "if one pathway doesn't kill you, odds are the other ones will."

**3. Striped bass is an atypical model.** Striped bass live 20 to 40 years and mature at about age 3, whereas most native species live under 5 years. Larval striped bass survive about 30 days without food, roughly twice as long as similarly sized larvae. [[Delta Smelt]] larvae starve within 10 to 14 days post-hatch, and rotifers, their preferred food, have declined sharply.

**4. Benthic and invasive species fare better.** Most declining species are pelagic Delta residents needing suitable spawning substrate, making them vulnerable to all six pathways. Benthic species such as [[Yellowfin Goby]] and white sturgeon, and the two goby species, have done better, partly because they use abundant benthic prey and riprap habitat. The inland silverside persists because it is inshore, a fractional spawner, and abundant upstream.

**5. Harvest and extinction risk.** Overharvest contributed to 19th-century declines of chinook salmon and white sturgeon. Removal of large female striped bass, which contribute disproportionately to recruitment, may have contributed to their decline. Sampling of depressed populations substantially increases extinction risk.

**6. Management conclusions.**
- Outflow is the single most important factor. Solving diversion problems may not produce immediate recovery, because the food web may reorganize under a different hydrodynamic regime and reveal new limits.
- Recommended actions include improving estuarine hydrodynamics, reducing entrainment, decreasing pesticide runoff, and expanding shallow-water spawning habitat.
- Long-term monitoring paired with process-oriented experiments is needed to understand early life stages and to target management.
- The authors judge that ensuring adequate outflow is the most pragmatic and promising solution, given the need to prevent extinctions and the poor life history data for many severely affected species.
