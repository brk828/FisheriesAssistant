## Executive Summary

This 1950 progress report from the New Hampshire Fish and Game Department details a multi-year (1947–1949) research program investigating pond and lake fertilization as a method to increase natural food availability and carrying capacity for both warm-water and cold-water fisheries. Laboratory aquarium trials compared four fertilizer types — commercial 5-10-10, horse dressing, hay/superphosphate combination, and hen dressing — finding **hen dressing** and **commercial 5-10-10** to be the most effective at stimulating plankton blooms and improving water chemistry. Field trials in controlled hatchery pools using [[Smallmouth Bass]] fry demonstrated that hen dressing produced greater plankton biomass and higher bass growth rates, though at the cost of increased predation-related mortality from aquatic insects attracted to the organic fertilizer. A landmark case study converted a 10-acre acidic, unproductive "quaking bog" pond into a productive [[Brook Trout]] fishery through reclamation, liming, and hen dressing fertilization, yielding excellent angler returns and trout growth rates comparable to hatchery-reared fish.

## Background & Description

- **Objective:** Develop and validate fertilization protocols to increase natural food production (plankton) in New Hampshire ponds and lakes, thereby improving fish carrying capacity for both [[Salmonidae]] (cold-water) and [[Centrarchidae]] (warm-water) species.
- **Study Period:** Laboratory work began December 5, 1947, continuing through field trials in 1948–1949.
- **Institutional Support:** New Hampshire Fish and Game Department, in cooperation with the [[University of New Hampshire]] (thesis component under Professor C. F. Jackson).
- **Species of Focus:**
  - [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu dolomieu*) — warm-water rearing trials.
  - [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) — cold-water pond reclamation and fertilization case study.

### Laboratory Methodology
- Four aquariums (6 gallons pond water each) tested:
  1. Commercial 5-10-10 fertilizer
  2. Horse dressing
  3. Hay + 20% superphosphate (10:1 ratio)
  4. Hen dressing
- Daily monitoring: temperature, plankton counts, water color, pH (colorimetric), dissolved oxygen (Winkler & Permanganate methods), and free CO2 (ppm).
- Experiment repeated in winter 1948–49 with consistent results.

### Field Methodology
- Three identical 18 ft × 100 ft × 2 ft deep test pools at the **New Hampshire State Fish Hatchery, Warren, NH**, fed by the Baker River (60–66°F in summer).
- Brush pile cover (alder/birch base, hemlock/pine top) added to reduce cannibalism among bass fry.
- Fertilization rate: 1 part per thousand by weight (225 lbs per pool per application).
- Weekly surveys: temperature, plankton volume (cc/cu meter via Wisconsin plankton net + settling method), pH, dissolved oxygen, free CO2.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water Chemistry Effects:**
  - Hen dressing acted as a natural buffering/neutralizing agent, raising pH from 6.0 to 7.0 in acidic waters — recommended specifically for ponds with acidic conditions.
  - Commercial 5-10-10 maintained a narrower, slightly acidic pH range (6.0–6.2).
  - Both hay/superphosphate and hen dressing treatments caused a temporary total depletion of dissolved oxygen (~10 days post-application) due to peak decomposition, followed by oxygen recovery via photosynthetic rebound.

- **Plankton Community Composition:**
  - Hay + superphosphate tank: brown scum dominated by **Diatoms** and **Brown Protozoa** intermixed with **Blue-Green Algae**.
  - Hen dressing tank: evenly distributed bloom of **Diatoms, Mastigophora, Infusoria, Rotifers, and Crustaceans** in high abundance.
  - Commercial 5-10-10 promoted shoreline growth of green algae (*Zygnema*), while control pools showed only scant algae (*Mougeotia*).

- **Predator-Prey Dynamics:**
  - Hen-dressing-fertilized pools attracted more predacious aquatic insects, including **Giant Water Bugs** (*Lethocerus americanus*, order Hemiptera) and **water tigers** (family [[Dytiscidae]]), which preyed heavily on bass fry, elevating mortality despite superior growth rates.
  - Control (unfertilized) pools also suffered water bug predation and showed the highest degree of cannibalism among surviving bass, evidenced by non-uniform size distribution.
  - 5-10-10 fertilized pools were notably free of predacious insect colonization, contributing to higher survival despite lower growth rate.

- **Case Study – Acidic Bog Pond Reclamation:**
  - 10-acre pond (138 acre-feet water volume) originally used for log storage after the **1938 hurricane**, resulting in highly acidic (pH ~4.4), tea-colored, unproductive water surrounded by a floating "quaking" bog.
  - Reclamation performed first to eliminate competing wild fish (e.g., pickerel) before trout introduction.
  - Treated with 1,800 lbs crushed lime followed by 1 ton of hen dressing (10 ppm fertilization ratio); repeated three times over the 1949 season.
  - Water color shifted from tea-colored to greenish, pH rose from 4.4 to 6.0, and plankton volume increased from mere traces to >5 cc/cu meter between July 1947 and July 1949.

## Key Findings

1. **Most Effective Fertilizers:** Hen dressing and commercial 5-10-10 outperformed horse dressing and hay/superphosphate combinations in both plankton production and water chemistry improvement.
2. **Bloom Duration:** Hen dressing produced a plankton bloom beginning just 3 days post-application, lasting 31 days; 5-10-10 bloomed later (14 days) but persisted for ~5 months.
3. **Cost-Benefit Tradeoff:** Hen dressing is nearly free but labor-intensive to apply; commercial 5-10-10 (~$2.35/100 lbs) is more expensive but logistically superior (boat/aerial application), useful for remote or reclaimed waters.

### Smallmouth Bass Pool Trial Results (Summary Table)

| Pool | Fertilizer | Bass Stocked | Bass Recovered | % Survival | Avg Weight/Bass (g) | Avg Length (in) |
|------|-----------|--------------|-----------------|------------|----------------------|------------------|
| #1 | Commercial 5-10-10 | 2,000 | 633 | 31.65% | 0.716 | 1.375" |
| #2 | Control (none) | 2,000 | 76 | 3.8% | 2.98 | 2.250" |
| #3 | Hen Dressing | 2,000 | 80 | 4% | 7.78 | 3.000" |

*Note: Pool #3 (hen dressing) had the greatest individual growth rate despite the highest predation-driven mortality — a clear productivity-vs-survival tradeoff.*

4. **Auxiliary Field Trial:** A 1/3-acre pond fertilized twice with hen dressing, stocked with 10,000 bass fry, yielded 2,120 fingerlings (21% return), averaging 3"–6" long, totaling 30.25 lbs.
5. **Sportsmen's Club Case Study:** A club-managed 1/3-acre pond, reclaimed and fertilized with lime and a custom 4-1-1 blend (cottonseed meal, 7-7-7, nitrate of soda), stocked with 15,000 [[Smallmouth Bass]] fry, returned 1,130 bass (~10%) averaging 3"–4", with the largest individual reaching 6¾" (likely cannibalistic).
6. **Brook Trout Success Case:** 800 brook trout fingerlings (3¾" avg) stocked in the reclaimed acidic bog pond; the following season, 35 anglers caught 193 trout averaging just under 5 fish/lb, ranging 7"–9.5", with some individuals reaching 11" — growth rates comparable to hatchery-reared fish.
7. **Management Recommendation:** Hen dressing is particularly suited for acidic ponds due to its neutralizing/buffering capacity; commercial fertilizers are better suited for large-scale, logistically difficult, or aerial-application scenarios and for post-reclamation ponds lacking a natural fish population to sustain fertility cycling.
8. **Broader Implication:** Fertilization-driven improvements in pond chemistry and plankton productivity can transform historically unproductive or acidic waters into viable [[Brook Trout]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] fisheries within one to two growing seasons.
