# Gila Topminnow Extirpation Sites in Arizona: 1994-95 Field Season Results

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) technical report evaluates [[Gila Topminnow]] reintroduction sites in Arizona during the 1994-95 field season. Many reintroduced populations have failed, yet sites that are not formally declared "extirpated" still trigger [[Section 7 Consultation]] obligations. The report applies draft extirpation criteria from the proposed revised recovery plan (Abarca et al. 1994) to reduce this administrative backlog. Of 45 sites evaluated, 36 were recommended for extirpation designation (12 in 1994, 24 before 1994). Nine sites did not meet the criteria or were recommended for additional management, including augmentation stocking.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) is a small live-bearing fish in the family [[Poeciliidae]]. Since 1981, AGFD, the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM), the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS), and the [[U.S. Forest Service]] (USFS) have cooperated on its recovery. Topminnows have been reintroduced at more than 200 sites with variable success. Many failures were attributed to poor site selection, and current criteria, adapted from Abarca et al. (1994), consider:

- Drainage area, elevation, stream flow, and gradient
- Stream geomorphology (Rosgen C-2 through C-5 channel types; see [[Rosgen Stream Classification]])
- Pond surface area and depth
- Habitat complexity and protection from flash floods
- Moderate to abundant aquatic vegetation
- Only native, nonpredatory companion fishes
- Water quality under state standards
- Location within the historical Gila River drainage

Extirpation criteria (Abarca et al. 1994) provisionally declare a population extirpated after:

1. Qualified biologists fail to collect topminnows in at least five consecutive surveys for natural populations, or three for reintroduced populations.
2. Surveys cover the majority of available habitat.
3. Surveys occur when detection is most likely (usually June through November).
4. Mosquitofish are preserved at each survey and voucher specimens are placed in museums.
5. Dry sites are checked for surface water above and below, per steps 1-4.

After a provisional determination, a report is prepared, reviewed by AGFD and other biologists, and a final decision is made by AGFD and USFWS with the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]]. The draft plan has not been finalized by USFWS, but AGFD applies its criteria in this report.

The report also covers [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), which was stocked at several sites alongside topminnows. Extirpation criteria for pupfish are not defined in the 1993 Desert Pupfish Recovery Plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most sites are small, man-made or modified features such as stock tanks, troughs, windmill-fed pools, artesian wells, and spring boxes. Others are steep, narrow drainages prone to flash floods. Recurring threats include:

- **[[Desiccation]]:** Failed wells, broken pipes, drought, and seasonal drying of tanks and washes. Many sites were eliminated this way.
- **[[Flooding]]:** Scouring floods in steep drainages (e.g., [[Buehman Canyon]], [[Seven Springs]], [[Humbug Creek]]). Floods were documented in 1989, 1993, and other years.
- **Temperature extremes:** Cold water at high elevations (e.g., [[El Pilar Tank]]) and high water temperatures and overgrowth at low-flow sites.
- **Vegetation and algae:** Dense cattails, [[Chara]], and algae in small tanks, sometimes requiring dredging and grazing control.
- **[[Nonnative Fishes]] and predators:** [[Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Goldfish]], and [[Fathead Minnow]] occur at several sites, and [[Longfin Dace]] was observed preying on juvenile topminnows under aquarium conditions.
- **Unauthorized introductions:** Local residents may have moved fish or stocked nonnatives.

Survey thoroughness and habitat coverage were inconsistently recorded in historical monitoring. Estimates rely on field notes and personal communications. Monitoring by [[James Brooks]], [[Lee Simons]], [[Brian Bagley]], [[Francisco Abarca]], and [[Matt Brown]] is considered thorough unless otherwise noted.

## Key Findings

**Scope and status of sites**

- 45 sites were evaluated: 12 met criteria during 1994, 24 met criteria before 1994, and 9 did not meet criteria or were recommended for additional actions.
- Reintroduction and monitoring activities had continued for at least 13 years, involving many organizations and at least 50 individuals listed in the affiliations table.
- Stocking sources were mainly [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]] populations originating from Monkey, Cocio, and Bylas springs. Stocking sizes ranged from 100 to 2,000 fish per site.

**Sites recommended as extirpated (1994 group, 12 sites)**

- [[Buckhorn Spring]] #1: too steep (drop of about 210 m over 3.2 km); not restocked.
- [[Cedar Spring]]: 100 m of surface flow in 1982; heavy cattle use and highway runoff.
- [[Chalky Butte Well]]: desiccation of the pond and algae/Chara overgrowth; 1,000 fish stocked in 1982.
- [[Corner Artesian Site]] #41: persisted at least eight years, then lost to well failure and desiccation (1989-1991).
- [[Holly Spring]]: limited habitat; dry below a 10 × 8 × 0.9 m source pool in 1994.
- [[Little Nogales Spring]]: steep drainage with a 3 m waterfall above the stocking site; only 30 adults collected in 1989.
- [[Little Outfit Tank]]: unclear stocking location; no suitable habitat found.
- [[Martin Well]]: status based on a single topminnow found in lab identification; nonnative fish a concern.
- [[Mesquite Flat Trough]]: persisted until 1989 but limited habitat.
- [[Mesquite Spring Tank]]: desiccation after 1987; population under 200 in 1986.
- [[Nogales Spring]]: 258 stocked in 1988; none collected in 1991, 1992, or 1994.
- [[Shute Springs]]: troughs and overflow puddles sampled 1992-1994; no topminnows.

**Sites recommended as extirpated (pre-1994 group, 24 sites)**

- Major causes: desiccation (e.g., [[Alambre Tank]], [[Artesian Well #3]], [[Artesian Well #4]], [[Pilot Tank]]), flooding (e.g., [[Castle Creek]], [[Bench Well]], [[Happy Camp Spring]]), cold temperatures (e.g., [[El Pilar Tank]]), and unknown causes (e.g., [[Indian Spring #2]], [[Little Nob Well]], [[Packard Spring]]).
- [[Alambre Tank]] (1993): 100 × 70 m, 29 °C, DO 6.1 ppm, pH 8.8; three seine efforts captured 34 largemouth bass (150 mm) and no topminnows.
- [[Bear Canyon]]: 2,000 fish stocked in 1982, but no topminnows collected afterward; site above accepted elevation range.
- [[Tres Alamos]]: outside historical range; die-off in 1989 of unknown cause. Desert pupfish also considered extirpated there.
- [[Upper Horrell Spring]]: topminnows persisted in adjacent [[Campaign Creek]], but the spring habitat is unsuitable.
- [[Zig Zag Spring]]: only longfin dace and green sunfish found.

**Sites not meeting extirpation criteria (9 sites)**

- [[Buehman Canyon]]: water year-round, but no topminnows or pupfish in 1989, 1991, or 1993. Upstream reaches should be surveyed and augmentation considered.
- [[Green Tanks]]: qualifies for extirpation, but site enhancement could make it suitable. Discussions with BLM and the rancher should resume.
- [[Horse Creek]]: a former Category 1 site; occasional floods and desiccation. Recommended for augmentation stocking.
- [[Hull Spring]]: occasionally dries; restock if water proves perennial.
- [[Humbug Creek]]: topminnows persisted from after 1982 until at least 1989; augmentation recommended from a natural population.
- [[Indian Spring #1]]: a Category 1 site that supported a small population for at least 7 years; augmentation recommended.
- [[Peoples Canyon]]: a 1989 flood removed fish; the 1989 survey does not count as monitoring, so the drainage should be resurveyed.
- [[Rincon]] (Saguaro National Monument): lower tinajas not permanent; upper tinajas to be re-evaluated for introduction.
- [[Seven Springs]]: flood-prone, heavily used for recreation, with nonnatives present; augmentation recommended with management options.

**Conclusions**

- Most failures were attributed to desiccation or flooding, often linked to small, ephemeral habitats, failed wells, or steep drainages that do not meet current site-selection criteria.
- Sites recommended for extirpation should not be restocked. Those with potential for enhancement or reliable perennial flow should be reconsidered.
- Continued monitoring, habitat management, and control of nonnative fishes are needed to improve long-term recovery success.
