# Trout Stream Management Investigations in Swift River, Albany, New Hampshire (1955 Progress Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This New Hampshire Fish and Game Department progress report, prepared by Arthur E. Newell under the [[Dingell-Johnson Project]] (F-5-R-4), evaluates trout management in the [[Swift River]] watershed during the 1955 season. It combines a [[Creel Census]], tagged-fish return studies of [[Hatchery Stocking]], [[Rotenone Sampling]] of wild populations, movement studies, a weir study on [[Oliverian Brook]], and a post-season reclamation. The author concludes that fishing pressure is light across the watershed but moderate in the stocked roadside sections, that hatchery returns are low (about 39% for brook trout and 50% for rainbow trout), and that a large share of stocked fish disappear during the season. The report recommends changes to stocking policy and the abolition of legal length limits on trout.

## Background & Description

Swift River is a scoured, cold mountain stream system. The main river within the study area is about 18 miles long with approximately 88 surface acres. Sections II and III form a roadside stocked portion of about 3 miles and roughly 22 acres, which receives most of the angling. Upper sections and tributaries are less accessible and lightly fished.

The project used several methods:

- **Creel census:** A voluntary census of anglers at checking stations, plus a roving census below the study area. Weekend and holiday data from 1953 were used to expand 1955 figures. The author notes that at least 90 percent of anglers cooperated.
- **Tagging:** Stocked fish were marked with numbered [[Jaw Tag]]s, and some were adipose fin-clipped to test tag effects.
- **Population sampling:** Sample reaches were treated with [[Rotenone Sampling]], and fish were weighed, measured and scale-sampled.
- **Movement and weir work:** A [[Wolfe Trap]] on Oliverian Brook recorded upstream and downstream movement.

The report is organized as Job Completion Reports I through VI. Job I covers fishing statistics, Job II population studies, Job III movements, Job IV age and survival, and related work on Sabbaday and Deer brooks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Pressure and success were tied to weather and water conditions. In May, low water encouraged early angling, but water below 50°F coincided with poor success and slow catches. Sharp rises in water level reduced angling success. A long hot, dry spell in July and August discouraged anglers. Stocked fish tended to move little, especially when released at temperatures above 50°F. Fish stocked in May, below 50°F, moved more.

Wild brook trout were dominated by small fish. Native fish were most abundant in the upper watershed and tributaries, which were little fished. The stocked roadside sections had few native fish. Hatchery fish lost condition after stocking, becoming more slender as they adapted to stream life. Summer mortality of stocked fish was high, which the author attributes to unidentified causes rather than carry-over or winter losses. A 1955 fire ban in 1953 (June 27 to July 6) also affected pressure comparisons between years.

## Key Findings

**Fishing pressure and success**
- The 1955 expanded census estimated 1,489 anglers, 4,028 angling hours (2.71 hours per angler), and 46 man-hours per acre per year across the watershed. Roadside Sections II and III had at least 135 man-hours per acre per year.
- 73.66 percent of anglers fished only the stocked roadside sections.
- The author judged pressure light overall but moderate in the stocked sections, and sustained through the season rather than concentrated early.
- Success was 0.63 fish per man-hour (expanded data), and 46.32 percent of anglers caught at least one trout. The statewide average cited was 0.75 fish per man-hour. Swift River ranked fairly well against other northeastern streams.

**Total yield**
- Estimated 2,524 trout (603.76 lb) were taken in 1955. Brook trout made up 67.99 percent of the catch by number and 64.67 percent by weight.
- Yield in Sections II and III was about 24 lb per acre per year, which the author considered low for an intensively stocked stream but indicative of what stocking can produce.

**Returns of stocked trout**
- Brook trout: 1,083 of 2,771 stocked (39.08%) were returned. Adjusted for possible out-of-area catch, the figure rises to a maximum of 42.04%.
- Rainbow trout: 757 of 1,500 stocked (50.47%) were returned. Adjusted, the maximum is 52.54%.
- Fin-clipped fish were returned at higher rates than jaw-tagged fish, especially for rainbow trout (42.00% versus 27.60% in the May stocking). The author recommends fin-clipping for future return studies.
- Mid-season stockings returned best, May was intermediate, and August stockings returned worst.
- Larger fish (8+ inches) returned better than smaller fish (6–8 inches).
- Most fish caught from any one stocking were taken within about 30 days. Returns from May and June stockings dropped after July 4, suggesting the river was fished out.

**Movement**
- Average movement was under 1.5 miles in either direction. Brook trout averaged 0.53 miles upstream and 1.23 miles downstream. Rainbow trout averaged 0.49 miles upstream and 1.29 miles downstream.
- Most fish showed no movement, and most movers stayed within three-quarters of a mile of the stocking point.
- Estimated downstream loss out of the study area was about 62 rainbow trout and 210 brook trout, which the author considered minor.

**Mortality and post-season reclamation**
- A reclamation of a sample reach on October 6, 1955 recovered 51 marked rainbow trout and 32 marked brook trout.
- Accounting for returns, out-migration and fish still present, about 477 rainbow trout (31%) and 1,350 brook trout (48%) were unaccounted for, attributed to summer mortality.

**Condition, growth and age**
- Stocked fish lost condition after release. Last reliable condition factors were in the C = 36 to 38 range. Native brook trout averaged 36.39 (1953) and 36.64 (1955). The author suggests C ≈ 36 as a desirable target.
- Recaptured brook trout averaged a length gain of 0.30 inches and a weight loss of 0.17 ounces. Rainbow trout averaged a gain of 0.49 inches and 0.07 ounces.
- Of 3,125 wild brook trout sampled, only 122 (3.9%) were legal size (6 inches or more). Age analysis of 358 fish found four age groups. Males grew slightly faster than females. Most fish reached legal size during their third summer.
- Survival from age group I to older groups was 8.69 percent. Sex ratio in 826 examined fish was 47.58% male and 52.42% female.

**Tributary populations**
- Sabbaday Brook: 3,978 brook trout after total reclamation, with 133 (3.34%) legal.
- Six tributaries sampled: estimated 22,884 brook trout in 18.2 miles, with 586 (2.56%) legal. Sub-tributaries were mostly fishless.
- Population estimates from partial sampling were within 15.32 percent of the total count on Sabbaday Brook.

**Oliverian Brook weir**
- The Wolfe-type weir (July 1 to October 31) captured 403 wild brook trout (mean length 4.16 inches) and 2 common suckers.
- Movement showed no clear correlation with temperature or flow. Upstream movement increased in mid-summer and during spawning, but was partly balanced by downstream movement. Trout spawned wherever suitable grounds existed.
- The weir cost about $2,800 and was difficult to screen for variable flows.

**Recommendations**
1. Expand efforts to draw anglers into the upper watershed and tributaries.
2. Replace brook trout with rainbow trout in the stocking schedule (about 4,400 rainbow trout per year proposed).
3. Begin stocking after water temperatures exceed 50°F, stock only 8+ inch fish, and do not stock above Rocky Gorge.
4. Use a stocking schedule of about 1,000 fish in May, 1,200 in mid-June, 1,200 after July 4, and 1,000 in the first week of August. No stocking after the first week of August, and none specifically for the September or October fly season.
5. Abolish legal length limits on brook trout, and likely on rainbow and brown trout, to allow harvest of wild fish.
6. Investigate the causes of high in-season mortality, and coordinate with hatcheries to rear fish better suited to stream life.
7. Conduct further studies on the optimum number of fish to stock, and on the effects of tags.
