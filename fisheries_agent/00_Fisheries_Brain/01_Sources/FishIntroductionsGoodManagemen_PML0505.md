# Fish Introductions: Good Management or No Management?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-introductions

## Executive Summary

This critical review by [[Walter R. Courtenay Jr.]] and [[C. Richard Robins]] (published in *CRC Critical Reviews in Aquatic Sciences*, 1989) argues that many deliberate introductions of nonnative fishes in North America reflect mismanagement or no management. The authors trace the history of [[Introduction Policy Procedures]] from Courtenay and Robins' eight-step procedure (1973) through the [[American Fisheries Society]] position statements of 1972 and 1986, and show how the 1986 revision deleted key safeguards, notably the requirement that agencies not approve their own introductions. Through case studies (the [[Salton Sea]], [[Colorado River Reservoirs]], [[Lake Powell]], [[Lake Sakakawea]], Florida and Texas [[Cichla]] and Nile perch efforts, Pennsylvania's Amur pike, and Pacific and Hawaiian marine introductions), the authors conclude that peer review, interjurisdictional councils and genetic safeguards are essential.

## Background & Description

The review opens by noting that the literature on introduced animals has grown rapidly, often written by authors who were not consulted on the introductions. Courtenay and Robins (1973) proposed an eight-step procedure, derived from [[Lachner et al. 1970]], comprising rationale, search and selection, preliminary assessment of impact, publicity and review, experimental research, publicity and independent review, and implementation. The authors emphasize that "publicity and independent review" is the cornerstone of the procedure.

The authors distinguish *introductions* (establishing a species where it is not native) from *stocking* (supplementation of a native species that requires periodic restocking). The 1972 [[American Fisheries Society Position Statement]] was approved at a meeting in Hot Springs, Arkansas, and was revised in 1986 by [[Kohler and Courtenay]] with deletions marked by lined-through text. The 1986 version removed the call for fish disease facilities and research, removed the call for legislation regulating fish farm construction, and altered the rule that agencies should not approve their own results.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Salton Sea (California):** A saline lake formed by the 1905–1907 Colorado River flood. Salinity rose from about 6‰ (1907) to 33‰ (1956) and about 37‰ by the early 1980s, eliminating freshwater fauna. Early fishes were carp, bonytail, razorback sucker, cutthroat trout, striped mullet and desert pupfish. A "shotgun" stocking program (1929–1956) released 33 species, of which only three established: sargo (*Anisotremus davidsoni*), bairdiella (*Bairdiella icistia*) and orangemouth corvina (*Cynoscion xanthulus*). Mozambique tilapia (*Oreochromis mossambicus*) became established by 1979–1980, with desert pupfish populations largely collapsing and the pupfish now federally listed as endangered.
- **Colorado River reservoirs:** Closure of [[Hoover Dam]] in 1935 altered flow regimes. Introductions included [[Redshiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) to Lake Mead (1953), striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*) from 1959 onward, and rainbow trout stocking from 1969. Striped bass were introduced into Lake Mead in 1969, and the threadfin shad forage base subsequently declined.
- **Lake Powell and Utah:** The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources proposed introducing rainbow smelt (*Osmerus mordax*) as forage. The authors argue that smelt would enter the Colorado River below Glen Canyon Dam and downstream systems, including the Central Arizona Project Canal.
- **Lake Sakakawea (North Dakota):** The walleye forage base was rainbow smelt, introduced in 1971. North Dakota proposed introducing zander (*Stizostedion lucioperca*), which the authors judged likely to compete with and hybridize with native walleye (*S. vitreum*).
- **Florida and Texas peacock bass:** Florida introduced *Cichla ocellaris* and *C. temensis* into canals of southeastern Florida in 1984 in its interest in peacock bass introductions; the authors note that a cold tolerance limit of 15°C was observed in lab tests. Texas received *Cichla* in 1975 and found reservoirs too hot or cold for overwintering.
- **Gatun Lake (Panama):** A 1965 *Cichla* introduction reportedly eliminated six of eight common fish species and reduced a seventh.
- **Pennsylvania (Amur pike):** *Esox reichertii* was introduced to Glendale Lake, a reservoir, and escaped during 1972 flooding after Hurricane Agnes. The authors note the risk of hybridization with northern pike (*E. lucius*).
- **Hawaii and marine introductions:** Of 21 marine fish species deliberately introduced to Hawaii, 7 became established; 4 are rated as mistakes. Marine aquarium fish releases and marine cage culture (e.g., Atlantic salmon in Puget Sound) are also discussed as escape risks.

## Key Findings

- **Procedural failure:** The authors conclude that the 1986 American Fisheries Society policy was "eviscerated" relative to the 1972 and 1973 procedures, and that state agencies were reluctant to adopt any procedures limiting their freedom of choice.
- **Salton Sea as a success case:** The Salton Sea sport fishery, based on the sargo and sciaenid food chain, was described as a case of serendipitous success in a virgin, essentially marine environment. Tilapia introductions were largely unplanned, and tilapia were recorded as "massive" in warm weather, though their impact on other fishes had not been severe.
- **Copycat management:** Other western states attempted to replicate the Salton Sea success in brackish lakes without chemical and physical analyses, and the authors state that the number of failures exceeded the successes.
- **Texas "try it and see" approach:** Texas made many introductions, including an orangemouth corvina × spotted seatrout hybrid, intergeneric hybrids of red drum and black drum, and attempted introduction of totoaba. The authors describe this as a no-management approach, later shifting toward a no-introduction policy.
- **Cascading effects:** Striped bass introduced to Lake Mead reduced the threadfin shad forage base, causing a collapse of the trout fishery by 1976. Striped bass were also later found in Lake Mohave, with young-of-the-year first recorded in 1981.
- **Nile perch rejection:** An AFS committee (1971) recommended against Nile perch introduction; Texas's 1979 Nile perch introduction failed, and the authors cite disastrous effects in Lake Victoria.
- **Genetic risk:** The authors emphasize introgression and hybridization risks (zander × walleye, Amur pike × northern pike, Florida × northern largemouth bass, and the unplanned blue bass hybrid), recommending genetic compatibility studies in the Rationale stage.
- **Peer review as safeguard:** The authors argue that agency personnel must not have sole authority to approve their own introductions, and that interjurisdictional councils, such as the [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]], are the model to follow.
- **Recommendations:** Federal agencies (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Canada's Department of Fisheries and Oceans) should adopt the procedure, with federal funding (e.g., Dingell-Johnson funds) potentially withheld from noncooperating states as the only enforcement mechanism.
