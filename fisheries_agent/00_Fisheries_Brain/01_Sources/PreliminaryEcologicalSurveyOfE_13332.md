# Preliminary Ecological Survey of Estero Soldado, Sonora, Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #estuarine-ecology

## Executive Summary

This technical report presents a preliminary "before" ecological survey of [[Estero Soldado]], a shallow negative estuary (estero) on the Gulf of California coast near [[Guaymas]], Sonora. The survey was commissioned as an impact study ahead of a proposed marina and tourist resort. Field work took place in October and November 1972. The estero supported 354 identified species of marine plants and animals, a high diversity for so small an estuary, which the author attributes to its varied habitats and the [[Mangrove Scrub Community]] that underpins its food web. The estero is described as an important refuge and nursery for juvenile and adult marine life, including commercial and sport species. The author concludes that the marina can proceed without upsetting the ecological balance if precautions are taken, and offers 12 recommendations focused on protecting the mangroves, the shoreline, and water exchange with the planned marina. The survey is explicitly preliminary: seasonal dynamics were not studied, so the species counts and abundance estimates are likely conservative.

## Background & Description

**Study setting and purpose.** Estero Soldado opens into Ensenada San Francisco, about five miles east of Bahía San Carlos and two miles north of Bahía Bacochibampo. A preliminary physical survey by [[Christopher C. Mathewson]] (May 1971) had recommended a complete biological inventory before construction began. This survey had four objectives:
1. Inventory all plant and animal species resident or transient in the estero.
2. Determine the relative abundance and importance of dominant species.
3. Designate habitat types and their importance to commercially significant species.
4. Estimate productivity and the estero's value as a refuge and nursery.

**Field methods.** Field parties visited on October 13–14 and November 10–13, 1972. Samples were collected with beach seines, otter trawls, and ichthyocide, and skin diving was used for direct observation. Specimens were preserved in formalin, identified to species where possible, and catalogued in the University of Arizona marine collections. Temperature was measured with mercury thermometers and thermistors, dissolved oxygen with a YSI probe, and salinity with an optical refractometer (accurate to 0.5 ‰). Taxonomic identifications were made by specialists, including Dr. Arthur Dennis (algae), Roy Houston (mollusks), and others for birds and plankton.

**Physical character.** The estero covers about 0.5 square miles and is shallow (average depth about two feet). It has a narrow mouth and a shallow main channel. It is a negative estuary, meaning evaporation exceeds precipitation, which produces a reverse salinity gradient: hypersaline water at the head (value illegible in the scan) and near-normal ocean salinity (35 ‰) at the mouth. The estero is shrinking through sediment and organic deposition, but it is not considered senescent.

**Habitat types.** The author identifies five major habitats:
1. Rocky shoreline along the eastern and southern margins.
2. Mangrove scrub in the central and far parts of the estero, with extensive mangrove stands near the mouth.
3. Muddy bottoms and shorelines at the head of the estero.
4. Inner and outer sandy shores in the delta and along the main channels.
5. The supralittoral zone above the high tide line, used by some marine species.

The estero was divided into five regions (I–V) for reference.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Primary producers and detritus.** Mangroves (black mangrove *Avicennia germinans*, dominant in the interior; red mangrove *Rhizophora mangle*, along the perimeter; and a rare white mangrove), seaweeds (*Spyridia*, *Caulerpa*, *Enteromorpha*), and pickleweed (*Salicornia*) form the base of the food web. Their decomposition produces detritus that feeds mullets, worms, shrimp, and crabs. Phytoplankton contributes less than it does in outer coastal waters. The author describes the estero as a nutrient trap where productivity is largely indirect, through [[Detritus]] decomposition.

**Rocky shores.** Rocky substrates support barnacles, at least nine benthic algae species, rock oysters (*Ostrea* spp.), the common stone crab, and the isopod *Ligia occidentalis*. Dense algal growth provides shelter and food for juvenile fishes.

**Mangroves.** Mangroves support sessile invertebrates (sponges, sea squirts, barnacles, oysters) and burrowing organisms (shipworms, mussels, polychaetes, including a possibly new fireworm-related amphinomid). They also shelter juvenile snappers, groupers, and jewfish. The author notes that the mangroves cool the water and limit evaporation.

**Muddy bottoms.** Fine silty mud covers the basins in Area III, where tidal currents are minimal. The bottoms host about 30 clam species, dominated by the [[California Venus Clam]] (*Chione californiensis*). Muddy areas also yield the [[Brown Shrimp]] and [[Blue Shrimp]] (*Penaeus californiensis* and *P. stylirostris*) in abundance. Anaerobic conditions with hydrogen sulfide odors occur below the surface in some places, but no oxygen-free waters were found.

**Sandy shores.** Ghost crabs, grapsoid crabs, and fiddler crabs are common. Sandy shores also host rays, mojarras, mullets, grunts, croakers, and a possibly new flounder species. Young-of-the-year [[Grunion]] (*Leuresthes sardina*) were seined just inside the mouth. The outer beach is a spawning ground for grunion, and the estero may be the most important nursery for this species in the Guaymas area, which marks the southern edge of its range.

**Surface (pelagic) waters.** Plankton nets collected diatoms, copepods, and larval forms of mollusks, barnacles, crabs, and fishes. Three anchovy species, one herring, two halfbeaks, and one needlefish were seined. Two of the anchovy species are new records for the Gulf of California. White mullet and mojarra schools are conspicuous at the surface.

**Birds.** The estero is a refuge and feeding ground for resident and migratory birds, partly because it lies on the [[Pacific Flyway]]. Spring is the best time for bird watching.

**Human use.** Local Mexicans and Yaqui Indians fish the estero, and Yaqui fishers were observed harvesting clams. No fishery statistics were available. [[Shell Mounds]] found along the shore indicate heavy shellfish use in the past and possible human occupation thousands of years ago.

## Key Findings

**Biodiversity.** The inventory recorded **354 species**, a conservative estimate that excludes microorganisms, insects, reptiles, terrestrial birds, and mammals. The author expects the count to rise to about 500 with more seasonal sampling. The table subtotals are:

| Group | Species count |
|---|---|
| Algae (benthic) | 19 |
| Higher shore plants | 25 |
| Invertebrates | 153 |
| Fishes | 75 (text) |
| Birds (table subtotal, expected rather than sighted) | 82 |

The text gives 62 for birds, which conflicts with the table's subtotal of 82. The report also contains a subtotal of 25 for fishes, which conflicts with the text figure of 75. The totals of 354 match the text's figures for fishes and invertebrates. Notable fish species include [[White Mullet]] (the most abundant fish), several mojarras, juvenile snappers (three species), [[Jewfish]], and the [[Baya Grouper]], plus gobies and blennies typical of Guaymas shores. The author describes the estero as a focal point for emigrating juveniles from the sandy bays and rocky coasts of the Bahía San Carlos–Ensenada San Francisco region.

**Notable findings.**
- Several species represent possible new species or range records, including a flounder, a fireworm-related amphinomid worm, two sea anemones, and two anchovy species that are new Gulf of California records.
- Seaweed beds (*Caulerpa*) harbor an invertebrate assemblage characteristic of more southern waters.
- Shrimp abundance in Area III was described as unusually high, and the author had not seen as many shrimp in any other upper Gulf estero.

**Estero as refuge and nursery.** The author concludes the estero is a major nursery for grunion, commercial shrimp, snappers, groupers, and mullet, and a refuge for migratory birds. The author argues that its scientific, educational, and recreational value (including [[Shell Mounds]] archaeology and marine ecology) is significant. Its productivity appears higher than that of other Gulf esteros, although its species diversity is similar.

**Trophic structure.** A generalized food web (Figure 2) was constructed from relative abundance estimates and feeding habits from other estuaries. It is hypothetical and not based on food-habit data from this estero. Removing the mangroves would eliminate the base of the food web, and removing shoreline vegetation would reduce productivity, simplify the food web, and raise water temperature and salinity.

**Recommendations (12 in total).** The recommendations are ecologically driven and summarized below:
1. Excavate the "fossil" estero northeast of the estero as the marina site instead of Area II, since it is larger, nearer the highway, and would create new habitat.
2. If Area II is used, build the dike without culverts so that water exchange occurs only by seepage, and flush Area II through the new channel only.
3. Place the earth fill dike before the southern widening of Area II to minimize mangrove removal.
4. Protect the beach dunes near the mouth, since they keep the main channel open.
5. Do not develop Area III as a recreational boating area, because dredging would release hydrogen sulfide and damage the most productive shrimp grounds.
6. Avoid dredging new channels, since circulation is adequate.
7. Plan any jetty carefully with a qualified consultant to avoid disturbing sediment transport and grunion spawning beaches.
8. Protect the mangrove scrub, regulate fishing and boating, and ban dune buggies and motorbikes.
9. Regulate vacation home construction, limit sediment inputs, and prohibit raw sewage discharge.
10. Dredge the main channel only when absolutely necessary.
11. Implement sewage treatment facilities at the marina, as recommended by Mathewson.
12. Conduct an "after" survey about one year following construction to monitor environmental change.

**Overall conclusion.** The author judges the proposed marina feasible if the mangroves, shoreline, and water exchange are protected. The estero is characterized as a beautiful, pristine lagoon that can support recreation compatible with its ecology if a major portion remains in natural condition.
