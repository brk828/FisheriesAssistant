# Movement of Channel Catfish in the Savannah River and Upper Three Runs, 1953–1954

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 report by Robert L. Humphries (Institute of Radiation Ecology, University of Georgia; AEC Research Contract AT(07-2)-10) documents a two-season mark-recapture study of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) movements in the [[Savannah River]] and its tributary [[Upper Three Runs]] within the [[Savannah River Plant]] area. The work was motivated by concern that fish moving between contaminated waters and the river could carry [[Radionuclide]] contaminants back to the human population through angling. The study found that channel catfish moved seasonally between the tributary and the river, that upstream movement into Upper Three Runs occurred in spring and downstream movement in June–July, and that river level strongly influenced trap catches. Tag returns by anglers were unusually high (12.2%).

## Background & Description

- **Study focus:** Channel catfish were chosen because they are abundant, easily trapped alive, and economically the most important fish in this river section; other species were marked incidentally.
- **Context:** Several small tributaries had been closed to fishing when the plant began operations in 1951, raising the possibility that fish could be exposed to radioactive effluents and later be caught by fishermen. The study was a follow-up to a [[U.S. Public Health Service]] survey and complemented a fish survey by Harry W. Freeman (University of South Carolina) and biological surveys by the Philadelphia Academy of Natural Sciences.
- **Study area:** The Savannah River is a large, meandering Southeastern Coastal Plain river with a well-developed floodplain. Upper Three Runs is a blackwater creek, the one tributary that retained near-natural conditions and was the most accessible and largest stream in the area.
- **Hydrology:** Recorded river discharge ranged from a maximum of 22,000+ c.f.s. (figure garbled in source) to a minimum of 5,260 c.f.s., with an average of mean monthly flows of 6,775 c.f.s. Upper Three Runs flows usually ranged between 130 and 300 c.f.s.
- **Study sites:** Seven trapping stations were numbered 1, 2, 3, 4 (in Upper Three Runs area and river), and 1-2, 1-3, 2-3 (in Upper Three Runs). Stations were located by river mile, with Station 3 at the creek mouth (mile 171.6).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Trap design:** Wire-mesh cylinder traps (4 ft long, 2 ft diameter) with funnel entrances, baited with cottonseed meal cake and set on the bottom, mimicking commercial catfish fishing in the Savannah River.
- **Marking:** In 1953, fish were fin-clipped using a 10-code system based on paired fins (e.g., left pectoral = 1, right pectoral = 2, left pelvic = 3, right pelvic = 4). A tattoo trial was tried briefly and discontinued. In 1954, serially numbered [[Petersen Tag|Petersen-Type Tags]] (two 0.5-inch red plastic discs joined by a nickel pin) were applied below the dorsal fin.
- **Environmental drivers:** Trap catches were related to river-level fluctuations. Fluctuating water levels increased catch until the river overflowed its banks, after which catch decreased; stable water levels also corresponded with low catch. The authors assumed rising water increased fish activity and that fish moved onto the inundated floodplain during overflow, out of trap range.
- **Associated species:** Flat bullheads (*Ameiurus natalis*) were more abundant in Upper Three Runs and showed strong site fidelity. Incidental species included redhorse suckers, largemouth bass, bluegill, and eels, among others.

## Key Findings

**Trapping results**
- 1953 (June 5–December 12): 2,176 fish handled, 464 recaptures, and 1,712 individuals marked. Channel catfish: 1,921 handled, 446 recaptures, 1,475 marked.
- 1954 (April 3–September 19): 526 fish handled, 54 recaptures, 472 tagged. Channel catfish: 413 handled, 44 recaptures (as reported in the text), 369 tagged.
- Trapping success differed sharply between years. The 1953 season was more evenly distributed through summer, while the 1954 catch concentrated in May and June.

**Movement patterns**
- **River fish** were relatively sedentary; the highest proportion of recaptures remained at the marking station (up to 22% at Station 1, with 5% of Station 1 fish recaptured elsewhere).
- **Upper Three Runs fish** showed mostly downstream movement in June–July 1953 and were rarely recaptured at their marking station.
- **Spring upstream movement:** In 1954, tagged fish moved upstream from the river into Upper Three Runs between mid-April and mid-July.
- **Seasonal absence:** No channel catfish were caught above Station 4 in Upper Three Runs after August 5, 1953 or after July 31, 1954, despite additional gill net sets.
- **Maximum distances:** Two fish moved 16.1 miles (the note records a maximum of 67.9 miles downstream and 32.1 miles upstream, per the summary), with the largest single distances recorded by anglers.

**Angler returns**
- Of 369 channel catfish tagged in 1954, at least 45 (12.2%) were caught by fishermen by July 1, 1955. The authors considered this very high and suggested low population density, high harvest rate, or both.
- Angler returns from river stations suggested downstream movement from May to late July, and upstream movement from mid-July through October 1954.
- Three returned tags from Upper Three Runs fish showed no movement despite 332–352 days at large.

**Comparison with other studies**
- Stream studies of [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Rock Bass]], and centrarchids showed sedentary behavior; [[Channel Catfish]] studies in the Des Moines River and the [[Upper Mississippi River]] reported limited but sometimes long-distance movements.
- The Savannah River movements were judged more intensive and localized than those in other studies, particularly the Des Moines River.

**Conclusions**
- Channel catfish move between the Savannah River and Upper Three Runs seasonally, supporting the possibility of a route for radionuclides to reach human consumers.
- River level is a key driver of trap catch and probably of fish activity and distribution.
- Movement types were grouped into (1) home-range movement, (2) spring upstream and early-summer downstream movement into and out of the tributary, and (3) long-distance random movement in the river.
- The author suggested school movement may occur but data are insufficient to confirm it.
