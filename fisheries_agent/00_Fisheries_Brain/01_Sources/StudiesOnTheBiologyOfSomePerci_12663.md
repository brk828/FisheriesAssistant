# Life History of Three Riffle-Dwelling Darters (Percidae) from Western Pennsylvania

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1950 study by [[Lachner]], [[Westlake]] and [[Handwerk]] describes the life history of three darters of the Percidae in French Creek, part of the Allegheny River system (Ohio River basin), western Pennsylvania: the [[Eastern Banded Darter]] (*Poecilichthys zonalis zonalis*), the [[Variegated Darter]] (*Poecilichthys variatus*) and the [[Northern Greenside Darter]] (*Etheostoma blennoides blennoides*). Data were gathered mainly by seining, with [[Scale Analysis]] used for age determination. The authors report riffle habitat, abundance and density, sexual dimorphism, sex ratio, age structure, growth, longevity and spawning timing. Most of the population was removed from one riffle, allowing a density estimate. The darters are short-lived (typically four years), mature at two years, and spawn in May to early June.

## Background & Description

The darters were previously known mainly from descriptive accounts, and their life histories were poorly documented, while larger percids had received much more study. The three species were chosen because they are common in the upper Allegheny River system. The authors aimed to describe habitat, associates, abundance, density, sexual dimorphism, sex ratio, longevity and growth rates.

Sexual dimorphism is strongly developed. Males have brighter, more pigmented colors that persist through the year, grow faster and reach larger sizes, and usually have larger anal, first and second dorsal fins and smaller pectoral fins. Lateral green markings in males form broad vertical bands in blennoides, while females show pale, Y-shaped markings. Males also have smaller anal papillae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** These darters are riffle-inhabiting fishes, most common in clean, moderate-sized rivers with fast flow and rubble, gravel and some sand bottoms with aquatic plants. At the main study site, French Creek at Carlton, Mercer County, the riffle was about 150-200 ft wide and about 75 ft long, with water about 2 ft deep at the crest and 61°F on 5 and 8 October 1947. Common plants included water moss (*Fontinalis*), pondweeds (*Potamogeton*) and wild celery (*Vallisneria*). Deep slack water (4-8 ft) above and below the riffle acts as an ecological barrier that limits the species' distribution.

**Associates.** Riffle associates at Carlton included [[Johnny Darter]], [[Fantail Darter]], [[Log Perch]], [[Blacksided Darter]], [[Spotted Darter]], [[Logperch Shiner]] and [[Rock Bass]], among about 30 other fish species. At Venango, [[Sand Darter]] (*Ammocrypta pellucida*) and lampreys were also recorded. The darters partition microhabitats: they avoid the crest and the deep base of the riffle, and sandy or heavily vegetated shores are occupied by other species.

**Seasonality.** Riffle-darter occurrence was constant across seasons, while cyprinids varied with feeding and spawning. The authors noted that the darters are important consumers of aquatic insects, which matters for assessing water suitability for game fish.

## Key Findings

**Material and abundance**
- More than 2,000 specimens were collected; 873 *zonalis*, 416 *variatus* and 176 *blennoides* were aged.
- 1,336 specimens (809 *zonalis*, 372 *variatus*, 155 *blennoides*) were taken from the Carlton riffle on 5 and 8 October 1947.
- Intensive seining (a 20 ft net, 1/4 inch mesh) took about 8-9 hours and removed an estimated 80-85% of the riffle population. Electrofishing and poisoning were rejected because the fish hide under stones and lack a swim bladder.
- *Zonalis* made up 56% of the darters taken; *variatus* 26%; *blennoides* 10%.
- The riffle covered about 15,000 sq ft. The estimated population was about 1,725 darters, roughly one per 9 sq ft, with an estimated spacing of about 3 ft.

**Sex ratio**
- Males outnumbered females in all three species. The proportion of males increased with age.
- Zonalis and variatus showed no significant departure from 1:1 in the first-year group; other age groups did depart significantly. For *blennoides*, the 2-year group was 69:19 (3.5:1), with chi-square 28.40.

**Age and growth**
- Scale annuli formed in late May to early June, coinciding with spawning in adults. Growth was retarded after the second summer in *blennoides*.
- Males grew faster than females, and the difference was statistically significant at the end of the first summer for all three species.
- Mean standard lengths at the end of each summer (males, mm):
  - *Zonalis*: 30.5, 40.2, 48.7, 53.7
  - *Variatus*: 35.0, 61.3, 66.7, 73.1
  - *Blennoides*: 40.3, 58.3, 68.4, 76.5
- Mean standard lengths at the end of each summer (females, mm):
  - *Zonalis*: 29.1, 38.1, 43.4, 45.7
  - *Variatus*: 33.1, 55.0, 61.6, 71.2
  - *Blennoides*: 38.4, 54.2, 59.5, 60.0

**Maturity and reproduction**
- All three species mature and spawn at two years of age. Some *zonalis* mature at one year.
- Reproduction is probably limited to May and early June in Pennsylvania. *Variatus* spawns earliest and *zonalis* latest.
- Ripe and spent *blennoides* were collected on 27 May 1948.

**Longevity**
- The typical lifespan is four years. Only one *blennoides* (a female) survived five summers. Only 13 males and 8 females of *zonalis* survived the fourth summer, about 2.3-2.5% of the sampled population.
- Largest recorded specimens: *blennoides* 82 mm (male) and 75 mm (female); *variatus* 76 mm (male) and 74 mm (female); *zonalis* 62 mm (male) and 48 mm (female).

**Conclusions**
- The northern greenside darter grows fastest, reaches the largest size and lives longest of the three species.
- Growth variability and post-maturity growth retardation cause extensive overlap in length-frequency distributions, so age cannot reliably be estimated from length alone.
- The authors note that the breeding behaviour of these darters is largely unknown. Males in related percids often guard nests or eggs, but this is not consistent across the group.
