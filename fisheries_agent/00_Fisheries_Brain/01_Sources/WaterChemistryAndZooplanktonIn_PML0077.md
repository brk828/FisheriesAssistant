# Water Chemistry and Zooplankton in the Lake Powell Forebay and Glen Canyon Dam Tailwater

#zooplankton #limnology #freshwater-ecology #tailwater

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department draft final report (March 1995) describes physical-chemical conditions and zooplankton density and composition in the [[Lake Powell]] forebay and the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] tailwater to [[Lee's Ferry]] (about 25 km downstream). Sampling covered the [[Interim Flow Criteria]] period from August 1991 to December 1994. The work was funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program. The authors examine how dam-driven flow variables relate to lower trophic levels that provide food for managed fishes. Key results: strong seasonal and interannual shifts in conductivity, temperature, dissolved oxygen (DO) and pH in the reservoir; zooplankton densities that were much higher in 1990–1992 than in 1993–1994; and evidence that tailwater zooplankton derive mainly from Lake Powell, with possible copepod reproduction within the tailwater.

## Background & Description

The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) began in 1982 in response to concerns about how Bureau of Reclamation management of Colorado River flows through Glen Canyon Dam affects the environment. Phase I ran from 1982 to 1988, and Phase II began in 1988 with testable hypotheses intended to support evaluation of dam operations. A draft Environmental Impact Statement on dam operations was filed with the Environmental Protection Agency on January 4, 1994.

Since August 1, 1991, releases have followed [[Interim Flow Criteria]] set by the Secretary of the Interior:

- Minimum release of 5,000 cfs and maximum of 20,000 cfs.
- Daily range limited to 8,000 cfs, depending on total monthly flow.
- Hourly increases limited to 2,500 cfs and decreases to 1,500 cfs.
- Release errors up to 10% permitted.

The stated purpose was to document conditions during the interim flow regime (August 1991 to December 1994) and to provide a baseline for long-term monitoring. The authors specifically studied:

1. Physicochemical parameters (temperature, DO, pH, specific conductance).
2. Zooplankton density and composition.

Macroinvertebrates and particulate organic matter were reported separately (Ayers and McKinney 1995).

Zooplankton in Lake Powell and the tailwater had been studied before interim flows (reviewed by Blinn and Cole 1991 and Stanford and Ward 1991), but seasonal density and community composition during interim flows were poorly known.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dam influence on the food web.** Daily fluctuation in tailwater flow, release withdrawal regimes and possibly selective depth withdrawal may affect tailwater [[Epilithic Periphyton]], zooplankton and transport of particulate organic matter. Lake plankton passing through tailwater systems may be altered quantitatively and qualitatively. Zooplankton support microfauna and benthic invertebrates, so they are a link between reservoir processes and fish food supply.

**Origin of tailwater zooplankton.** Earlier work (Angradi 1994; Haury 1986) indicated that tailwater phytoplankton and zooplankton derive mainly from lentic populations in Lake Powell. Haury (1986) also suggested that zooplankton may reproduce in the river.

**Reservoir circulation.** Lake Powell circulation follows a seasonal pattern (Merritt and Johnson 1977):

- Strong [[Advective Overflow]] in late spring and early summer.
- Interflow in late summer and early fall.
- Underflow in late fall and winter.
- Convective mixing in winter to about 60 m.

The southern hypolimnion is nearly isothermal and isohaline. In summer the upper water becomes strongly stratified, with a well-defined epilimnion 30 to 50 feet thick.

**Hydrologic context.** Lake elevation rose from 3612 ft to 3667 ft between March 1 and August 1, 1993, a rise of 55 ft (16.8 m) driven by large spring runoff. The 1994 spring runoff was much smaller.

## Key Findings

### Methods overview

- **Physicochemical sampling:** Temperature, DO, pH and [[Specific Conductance]] were measured monthly from April 1993 at a point 200 m in front of the dam, using a Hydrolab Datasonde and Surveyor logging system. Profiles were taken every 1.5 m to 60 m, then every 3 m to 91 m (from August 1993). Dam draft tube and Colorado River (-14 mile and Lee's Ferry) surface measurements were also taken.
- **Zooplankton sampling:** Samples were collected by pumping water through an 80 µm plankton net. Pre-April 1993 volumes were 10–200 L, and 100 L thereafter. Subsample counts were averaged. Sampling was at the forebay surface and penstock depth (about 55–60 m), at dam draft tubes and at Lee's Ferry.

### Physical and chemical results

- **Conductivity:** Upper-water conductivity fell from an average of 0.876 µS/cm (July 1993) to 0.761 µS/cm (August 1993) as low-conductivity runoff arrived. By November 1993 it was about 0.755 µS/cm to 50 m and 1.022 µS/cm at 91 m. A [[Chemocline]] formed at 55–65 m by January 1994 and had dissipated by May 1994.
- **Temperature:** The maximum surface temperature was 24.72 °C in summer 1993 and 27.87 °C in summer 1994. By February 1994 the water column was nearly uniform at 9.2 ± 0.5 °C from the surface to 60 m. Summer 1994 warming was shallower than in 1993, probably because of lower inflow volume. A strong [[Thermocline]] formed at 10–20 m in July and August 1994.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** A low-DO pocket of 4.4 mg/L at 17 m (September 1993) dropped to 27 m and fell to 3.3 mg/L by October. A similar low-DO zone appeared at 37 m in October 1994. DO was about 10 mg/L from the surface to 55 m in January 1994. The maximum, 12.23 mg/L, occurred at 5 m in April 1994.
- **pH:** A low-pH pocket (6.5 in August 1993, rising to 7.5 by September) occurred near 30 m. Winter mixing gave uniform pH 8.1 from the surface to 55 m (November 1993–January 1994). The highest values (above 8.2) occurred from the surface to about 15 m in June–September.
- **Draft tubes and tailwater:** Draft tube conductivity, temperature and pH tracked those at Lee's Ferry, indicating that outflow from Lake Powell controls tailwater limnology. Draft tube DO was slightly higher than penstock-level values, probably from air injection during passage. Lee's Ferry DO was often higher, likely from aeration and photosynthesis within Glen Canyon.

### Zooplankton results

**1990–1992 (forebay and draft tubes):**
- Forebay surface densities were very high. March and June–July 1990 ranged from about 140,900 to 699,900 per m³. December 1990, April and August 1991 averaged 53,153 to 522,779 per m³. November 1991 and January and March 1992 averaged 56,875 to 118,291 per m³.
- Copepods, cladocerans, nauplii and rotifers each varied with month and depth, and rotifers stayed uniformly low.
- Glen Canyon draft tube collections (October 1989–December 1991) had a mean of 22,508 per m³: copepods 55%, nauplii 27%, cladocerans 17%, rotifers 0.5%. The range was 1,000 per m³ (March 1990) to 50,652 per m³ (June 1991).
- Lee's Ferry collections over the same period had a mean of 23,151 per m³.

**1993–1995 (surface, penstock, draft tubes, Lee's Ferry):**
- Lake surface densities peaked at 39,240 per m³ in January 1994. Rotifers reached 29,100 per m³ in March 1994. Lake surface composition was nauplii 49%, copepods 29%, rotifers 17%, cladocerans 5%.
- Penstock-depth densities were much lower than surface values. The mean was 4,776 per m³, with nauplii 73%, copepods 18%, rotifers 5% and cladocerans 4%. The high was 13,200 per m³ (December 1993) and the low was 1,400 per m³ (February 1994).
- Draft tube collections from December 1993 to January 1995 averaged 11,700 per m³: nauplii 74%, copepods 24%, rotifers about 1%, cladocerans about 1%. The low was 2,200 per m³ in April 1994.
- Lee's Ferry collections from June 1993 to January 1995 averaged about 4,200 per m³: nauplii 73%, copepods 20%, rotifers 5%, cladocerans 3%. The high was 9,200 per m³ (December 1993) and the low was 200 per m³ (March 1994).

### Principal conclusions

1. **Interannual decline:** Zooplankton densities in 1990–1992 far exceeded those in 1993–1994 at the forebay surface, penstock depth, draft tubes and Lee's Ferry. Cladocerans were more prevalent in 1990–1992 than in 1993–1994.
2. **Lake-to-river linkage:** Composition similarity between Lee's Ferry and the draft tubes, and the high similarity of draft tube and reservoir communities, supports Haury's (1986) view that tailwater zooplankton derive mainly from Lake Powell.
3. **Possible tailwater reproduction:** Copepods and nauplii in the draft tubes and Lee's Ferry suggest possible copepod reproduction within the tailwater. Net recruitment may occur in some months and net loss in others.
4. **Downstream change:** In 1993–1994, draft tube densities exceeded Lee's Ferry in eight of fourteen months, pointing to a downstream decrease in density. This contrasts with Haury (1986).
5. **Depth distribution:** Nauplii exceeded copepod density at penstock depth in all but two months, which suggests they may contribute strongly to water withdrawn into the tailwater.
6. **Sampling caveat:** Cladocerans comprised only about 1–6% of samples in 1993–1995. Authors note that the diaphragm pump and passage through draft tubes may have damaged cladocerans, so they may be underestimated, though damage was judged slight.
7. **Drivers of change:** Lower reservoir elevations in 1990–1991 may have contributed to higher densities then. Sampling protocol changed in April 1991, but densities did not drop to 1993–1994 levels until later. No clear relationship was found between penstock depth and zooplankton density, and warmer surface water or colder penstock water did not clearly affect density.
8. **Management implications:** The authors recommend that multilevel intake design account for both annual runoff and convective summer heating, since reservoir warming depends on spring runoff volume as well as air temperature.
