# Status of the Animal Food Fishery in Northern California, 1956 and 1957

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bottom-fishes

## Executive Summary

This paper by E. A. Best of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] reviews the [[Animal Food Fishery]] of Northern California, in which unmarketable trawl-caught "trash" fish are ground, frozen or canned for fur-animal and pet food. Landings grew from about 528,000 pounds of whole fish in 1953 to 3,475,000 pounds in 1956 and 2,980,000 pounds in 1957. A shoreside sampling program showed that [[Arrowtooth Halibut]], [[Pacific Hake]] and [[Sablefish]] made up nearly three-fourths of the whole-fish weight. Landing receipts badly misreported species composition, so the author used sampling data to correct the estimates. The author concludes that the fishery is not believed to be harming California's bottom fish resources.

## Background & Description

The fishery began in October 1952. Three processing plants, at [[Fields Landing]], [[Fort Bragg]] and Oakland, use most of the trawl-caught whole fish sold for animal food. The Fields Landing and Fort Bragg plants grind and freeze whole fish and frames for the fur industry. The Oakland plant cans its raw material to make pet food. A fourth plant, to reduce fish scraps to liquid concentrate, was established at Eureka after the paper was prepared.

Frames and viscera (fish scraps) are the waste from fillet plants and are the more dependable raw material. The plants processed 7,845,000 pounds of frames and viscera in 1956 and 8,505,000 pounds in 1957. Under [[Section 8011 of the California Fish and Game Code]], landing receipts list species and weights, so scraps are not reported again unless reduced.

Four northern California [[Otter Trawl]] vessels deliver all the trash fish they catch. Two make daily trips, and two make extended trips and ice their whole catch. About ten more boats bring in trash fish that does not need icing, caught on the last day of a trip. No fishermen fish solely for this market; they use it to supplement income from market fish.

The sampling program ran from April 1956. It was limited by workload and weather, so it covered only Fields Landing in full species detail. Samples weighed from 80 pounds to over 20,000 pounds. They amounted to 0.3 and 0.4 percent of the total trash fish weight landed at Fields Landing in 1956 and 1957. Samples came from vessels fishing between Cape Mendocino and Cape Blanco. Common names follow [[Phillips (1957)]] and [[Roedel (1953)]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Otter Trawl]] is largely non-selective on mixed-species grounds, but seafood markets buy only certain species. The unsalable remainder, called "trash" fish, was historically discarded at sea, and in many places still is. The animal food industry therefore uses species that were previously wasted. Sharks and rays are the notable exception to the otherwise usable catch.

Fish must be fresh on arrival, so vessels on trips longer than one day must ice their catch. Availability of bottom fish varies through the year. Market demand also changes fishing effort. Weather in winter keeps boats in shallower water near port, which increases the take of [[Curlfin Turbot]] and [[Pacific Sanddab]] from January to March. Small [[Sablefish]] were extremely abundant in 1957, and at times heavy concentrations led fishermen to move to other grounds.

Size is a key factor. Many species used for animal food are too small for market: sablefish averaged about 18.5 inches and under two pounds round weight, and most [[Dover Sole]] used were under 14 inches total length. Many markets set minimum sizes, although California commercial regulations set none for bottom fish.

## Key Findings

**Landings and production**
- Whole-fish production: about 528,000 lb (1953), 3,475,000 lb (1956), 2,980,000 lb (1957).
- Frames and viscera: 7,845,000 lb (1956), 8,505,000 lb (1957).
- Author's summary: utilization has increased sixfold since 1952 and exceeds three million pounds of whole fish a year, plus about eight million pounds of carcasses.

**Principal species (sampling data)**
- [[Arrowtooth Halibut]], hake and sablefish together made up nearly three-quarters of the whole-fish weight in 1956 and 1957.
- [[Sablefish]]: 23% (1956) and 38% (1957) of animal food poundage. Fish averaged about 18.5 in and under two pounds.
- [[Rex Sole]]: 6% (1956) and 7% (1957).
- [[Dover Sole]]: 6% (1956) and 2% (1957), almost all undersized.
- Other marketable soles (including [[Curlfin Turbot]] and [[Pacific Sanddab]]): 2% (1956) and 6% (1957).
- [[Rockfish]]: rose from 8% (1956) to 14% (1957) after increased market demand. Five species, including the [[Shortspine Channel Rockfish]], made up about 85% of rockfish weight. Only dark-blotched and splitnose rockfish are used by fresh markets, and only to a limited extent.
- Sharks and rays are not used.

**Reporting problems**
- Landing receipts often recorded the total trash weight under one or two species, mostly arrowtooth halibut and hake.
- Receipts listed sablefish as only 0.67% (1956) and 0.38% (1957) of poundage, against sampled values of 23% and 38%.
- Reported hake sometimes appeared when none was present.
- Total poundage is probably correct, but species figures are distorted. The author says the animal food and human consumption fish cannot easily be separated, and that price per pound is the only practical indicator.

**Recommendations and conclusions**
- A separate reporting category is needed for animal food and any future industrial use of trawl-caught fish.
- The species composition matched earlier sampling at sea by [[Gates (1955)]] for 1953 and 1954.
- The fishery does no apparent harm to established fisheries. It uses fish formerly discarded, and no fishery was developed solely to supply it.
- The author does not believe the fishery is harming California's bottom fish resources.
