# Proceedings of the Creel Census Symposium (Iowa State College, c. 1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #creel-census

## Executive Summary

This document is the proceedings of a symposium on [[Creel Census]] methods held at Iowa State College, organized by Dr. Kenneth D. Carlander. Papers and discussion from fisheries agencies in the Midwest, Rocky Mountain region, Canada, and Tennessee address how to estimate angler harvest, fishing effort, and catch rates. The central theme is that creel census data are among the most important tools for fishery management, yet many existing programs rely on uncontrolled or voluntary sampling that can be biased. Contributors argue for controlled, probability-based sampling designs tailored to each water body.

## Background & Description

The symposium was convened after Charles Di Costanzo's three summers of creel work on [[Clear Lake]] prompted interest among [[Iowa Conservation Commission]] biologists. Attendance included roughly 50 registered biologists, statisticians, and agency staff from Iowa, Minnesota, Missouri, Illinois, Wisconsin, Nebraska, South Dakota, Tennessee, Michigan, and Canada, plus federal representatives from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]]. Proceedings were transcribed, with funding from the [[Sport Fishing Institute]].

Carlander proposed a classification of creel methods:
- **Complete coverage**: no real sampling problems, outside the symposium's scope.
- **Estimate from samples**: voluntary reporting (indiscriminate or selected group) and contact sampling (indiscriminate, systematic, random, or stratified).
- A further distinction between **immediate** censuses (during or at end of trip) and **delayed** censuses (relying on fisherman memory).

Five information types were identified: species composition, angler type, catch success, fishing pressure, and harvest (the product of success and pressure).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The papers cover a range of waters:
- **Iowa natural lakes**: shallow, glacial, eutrophic lakes in the northwest quarter of the state. Nine lakes totalling about 20,000 acres were sampled, roughly 70 percent of Iowa's natural-lake fishing water. Dominant catches include [[Bullhead]], [[Crappie]], [[Walleye]] and [[Yellow Perch]].
- **Spirit and Okoboji Lakes**: deep enough to avoid heavy winterkill, supporting high populations of game and pan fish. Spirit Lake is about 5,680 acres.
- **Clear Lake, Iowa**: 3,642 acres, with ten liveries and about 375 boats for hire. Principal open-water species were yellow bass, bullhead, bluegill, yellow perch, and crappies.
- **Missouri reservoirs and streams**: [[Clearwater Lake]], [[Lake Taneycomo]], [[Lake Wappapello]], [[Norfork Lake]], the Big Piney, and Niangua rivers.
- **Northeast Iowa streams**: trout, warmwater, catfish, and crappie fishing.
- **Des Moines River**: channel catfish, carp, walleye, and bullheads were the main targets.
- **Yellowstone Lake, Wyoming**: cutthroat trout fishery studied by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], with about 2,000 small private boats each summer.
- **Lake Mendota, Wisconsin**: winter perch and panfish fishery with heavy ice-fishing pressure.
- **Kentucky Lake (Tennessee)**: large reservoir censused partly by aerial boat counts.

## Key Findings

**Iowa 5-day census (Tom Moen)**
- The 5-day census (May 15 to June 30) was adopted to reduce costs and sample the better fishing period. Aggregated 10-year data covered 0.5 million fishermen, 1.5 million hours, and 2.5 million fish.
- Bullheads consistently made up over 50 percent of the catch. Catch per hour across all lakes stayed at or above 1.0 except in 1955 (mean 0.97).
- Dickinson County data did not support the assumption that May 15 to July 1 was the best fishing period.

**Year-round census, Spirit and Okoboji Lakes (Earl Rose)**
- Interviews are made from boats, with separate contact types (bank, dock, wader, boat, and winter).
- In West Okoboji, 38.2 percent of 1955 fish were taken by winter anglers.
- Total harvest could not be calculated under current methods because of many access points, night fishing, and boat traffic.
- A catch of 1.0 fish per hour over a season was adopted as the benchmark for "good" fishing.

**Completed vs. uncompleted trips (Earl Rose)**
- On Spirit Lake, completed trips averaged 1.2 fish per hour, about 70 percent above the 0.9 for uncompleted trips. Average trip length was 3.5 hours versus 2.9 hours.
- West Okoboji showed less difference; completed-trip average was 3.2 hours.

**Clear Lake sampling evaluation (Charles Di Costanzo)**
- Incomplete-trip (spot) contacts may be biased toward early-trip success; the bias may be positive or negative.
- Statistical tests indicated no significant overall bias. In 1953, chi-square tests on species composition were non-significant (chi-square = 6.31, 6 df for 1953; 9.01, 8 df for 1955).
- Angler counts with a ratio-based livery check gave 129,000 versus 171,000 man-hours; the difference is attributed to time spent at the lake rather than actively fishing.

**Missouri creel censuses (John Funk)**
- The general (uncontrolled) census sampled only 0.68 percent of trips on average, compared with 14.26 percent for intensive lake censuses and 1.11 percent for stream censuses.
- General census catch rates averaged about 55 percent higher than intensive censuses.
- The one dominant species matched only about half the time, and the four most abundant species matched in only 9 of 21 comparisons.

**Northeast Iowa voluntary census (R. E. Cleary)**
- Five "better-than-average" anglers per county were recruited. Contact turnover averaged over 30 percent annually.
- Catfish catch per hour was 57 to 70 across years, considered the most stable index.

**Des Moines River (Harry Harrison)**
- About 2,000 anglers were contacted per year, with catch rates of 0.6, 0.7, and 0.4 fish per hour in 1953–1955.
- Airplane counts showed up to 200 anglers on a 200-mile reach on Sundays and holidays.

**Mayhew artificial lakes design**
- Six southern Iowa lakes were sampled by boat livery operators; the design divided the fishing day into six two-hour intervals, excluding 6–8 a.m.
- Census was run from April 15 to June 21, with peak days (Thursday to Sunday) sampled more heavily.
- Cost per contact ranged from $0.07 to $1.32 in 1955.

**Yellowstone Lake (Oliver Cope)**
- Multiple methods were used by unit: boat docks (questionnaire cards tested against interviews), guide boats (guide books), Fishing Bridge (rod counts), private boats (permit cards), large private boats (least reliable), and shoreline (Beckwith's complete and incomplete design).
- In 1955, 31,651 rowboat anglers used the docks, 15,000 used guide boats, and 9,280 used small private boats. Total catch was 286,056 fish; shore effort was 175,190 hours.

**Jessen's statistical comments**
- Total catch is F = N × H × f (trips × hours per trip × catch per hour). Stratified two-way (week and day-of-week) sampling is generally most efficient.
- Sampling only during peak periods introduces bias; the incomplete-trip design requires that catch rate be the same in early and late trip portions, and across trip lengths.

**Discussion highlights**
- Minnesota reported that the fish yield from its 12 lakes was roughly constant over four years, and that the average size of catch is used as an index of overfishing.
- Lake Mendota (Wisconsin) estimates: about 2,500 fishermen on an average Sunday, roughly 5 hours each, and about 5 fish per hour.
- Aerial and parking-lot counts were used as independent checks; Lake Mendota averaged about 2.25 to 2.32 fishermen per car.

**Closing recommendations (Carlander)**
- Voluntary reports can yield useful data for specific purposes but rarely support accurate harvest estimates.
- Controlled sampling with probability measures is needed; it need not be more expensive than indiscriminate sampling.
- Fishery biologists need statistical planning support, and sampling designs should be "tailor-made" for each fishery.
- Catch per hour data should not be averaged as ratios without accounting for the skewed distribution and zero catches.
