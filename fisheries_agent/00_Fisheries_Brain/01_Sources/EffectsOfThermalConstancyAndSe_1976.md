## Executive Summary

This 1976 study by James V. Ward examines how an altered thermal regime below **Cheesman Dam** (a deep-release reservoir on the South Platte River, Colorado) restructures **stream macroinvertebrate communities**. Although the receiving stream was otherwise physicochemically favorable (no flow fluctuation, siltation, or oxygen depletion issues typical of other dammed rivers), the dam produced a distinctive thermal signature: strong **diurnal and seasonal temperature constancy**, a **delayed seasonal temperature maximum** (occurring in late October rather than midsummer), and **summer-cool/winter-warm** conditions relative to unregulated streams. These thermal anomalies — rather than conventional pollution-type stressors — are hypothesized to drive markedly reduced species diversity and equitability, altered taxonomic composition, and increased dominance by a few species. The paper is most directly relevant to fish biology insofar as it discusses **reduced fish diversity below hypolimnial-release dams**, its cascading effect on invertebrate predation pressure, and management implications for tailwater trout fisheries.

## Background & Description

*(Not a morphology-focused paper; taxonomic/functional notes below substitute for morphological description, as is appropriate for a macroinvertebrate community study.)*

- **Dominant taxa** (87% of total organisms in the study section): two mayflies — *Baetis* sp. and *[[Ephemerella inermis]]* — and two dipterans — *Cardiocladius* sp. and *[[Simulium arcticum]]*.
- *Baetis* sp. and *Cardiocladius* sp. alone comprised 69% of total density.
- *Baetis* sp. reached densities >12,000 organisms/m², indicating extreme dominance and space monopolization.
- *[[Gammarus lacustris]]* (Amphipoda) — a cold stenotherm normally restricted to high elevations — was anomalously favored below the dam and is physiologically capable of completing its life cycle under constant-temperature conditions.
- Gastropoda and Amphipoda, not normally present in Colorado granitic foothill streams, appeared below the dam, apparently favored by the modified thermal regime.
- Only a single stonefly (Plecoptera) species was recorded in the entire study section (absent at Site 1), versus a diverse mayfly/stonefly fauna in unregulated reaches downstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study system:** South Platte River below Cheesman Lake (elev. 2087 m), a deep-release storage reservoir (built 1905), central Colorado mountains. Four rubble-riffle sites sampled at 0.25, 2.4, 5, and 8.5 km below the dam, approximately every 28 days for one year.
- **Thermal gradient:** Mean annual temperature increased downstream (5.8 → 8.8°C, Sites 1–4); annual range 3–13°C (Sites 1–3) to 3–14°C (Site 4). Seasonal maximum delayed to late October at upper sites (coinciding with reservoir overturn) vs. early August at Site 4. Diurnal fluctuation increased sharply downstream (up to 6°C at Site 4 vs. near-constant at Site 1).
- **Diversity gradient:** Species richness, [[Shannon-Weaver Diversity Index]], and equitability all increased with distance from the dam, resembling the recovery zone of a polluted stream — despite the absence of classic pollution stressors. Maximum Shannon-Weaver value recorded (2.7) remained below the 3.0–4.0 range typical of unpolluted streams (Wilhm, 1970). Equitability (0.27–0.35) was well below the 0.6–0.8 unpolluted benchmark and actually *decreased* downstream even as species number rose, attributed to increasing space limitation and dominance by *Baetis*.
- **Taxon-specific responses:**
  - Ephemeroptera and Plecoptera: exceptionally low diversity near the dam.
  - Trichoptera, Coleoptera, Diptera: moderately reduced relative to unregulated streams; Diptera considered most tolerant of dam-altered conditions.
  - Amphipoda, Gastropoda: favored/increased — atypical for the region.
  - "Fugitive species" (e.g., *[[Ephemerella grandis]]*, *[[Rhithrogena doddsi]]*) occurred only rarely at the most downstream site, suggesting Site 4 functions as an **ecotone** between the dam-altered upstream reach and the natural thermal regime downstream.
- **Predator-prey context:** Reduced Plecoptera (major invertebrate predators) and reduced **fish diversity** below hypolimnial-release dams (citing Spence and Hynes 1971b) are hypothesized to relax predation pressure, permitting space monopolization by dominant prey such as *Baetis*. The author draws an explicit analogy to Paine's (1966) rocky intertidal removal experiments, where predator loss reduced overall community diversity via space competition rather than direct consumption effects.
- **Fishery implications:** Despite depressed invertebrate diversity, the high standing crop of macroinvertebrates and ice-free winter conditions support a productive year-round trout fishery in the South Platte below Cheesman Dam — though the author cautions that low-diversity systems are inherently more vulnerable to perturbation. Poor tailwater fisheries elsewhere in the U.S. are attributed partly to penstock/outlet design releasing water at depths unsuitable for either cold- or warm-water fish communities (citing Pfitzer 1967; Hubbs 1972).

## Key Findings

1. **Thermal constancy over conventional pollution** appears to be the primary driver of reduced macroinvertebrate diversity below Cheesman Dam, since flow, oxygen, and sedimentation conditions remained favorable throughout the study section.
2. **Four thermal anomalies** are proposed as mechanistically linked to reduced community diversity: (a) diurnal/seasonal thermal constancy promoting a shift toward competitive-exclusion "equilibrium communities"; (b) delayed seasonal temperature maximum (late October vs. normal midsummer); (c) summer-cold conditions limiting day-degree accumulation needed for larval maturation/emergence; and (d) winter-warm conditions disrupting diapause, hatching cues, and synchronous emergence.
3. **Downstream recovery gradient**: diversity and equitability increase with distance from the dam, but even at 8.5 km (Site 4) the regime remains abnormal (e.g., absence of surface ice while the river froze solid just downstream), and full recovery to "normal" unregulated conditions had not occurred by Site 4.
4. **Competitive and productivity effects**: Warmer winters and ice-free conditions may allow extended/year-round emergence and continuous aufwuchs (algal) production, increasing overall productivity but intensifying competition — potentially eliminating rarer species (per Hohn 1961; Patrick 1970; Yount 1956's inverse diversity–productivity relationship).
5. **Reduced predation hypothesis**: Elimination or reduction of major invertebrate predators (Plecoptera) and reduced fish diversity below the dam may permit dominant prey taxa (*Baetis*) to monopolize space, further depressing community diversity — paralleling Paine's (1966) intertidal predator-removal findings.
6. **Management implication**: The findings argue that dam design and operation (e.g., variable-depth water intake systems per Hubbs 1972) should account for thermal-regime effects on aquatic community structure and productivity, not just flow and water-quality parameters, to preserve more natural [[Species Diversity]] and reduce ecosystem vulnerability to perturbation.
