# Overview of Telemetry Equipment and Techniques Used with Native Fish in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at the 15th International Symposium on Biotelemetry (Juneau, Alaska, May 1999; published 2000), reviews the radiotelemetry equipment and surgical techniques used by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] on large-river native fishes of the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. Radiotelemetry has filled major information gaps on distribution, migration, seasonal habitat use and spawning-area delineation for five listed or declining species. The author, Bob D. Burdick, describes surgical protocols, transmitter and receiver types, antenna designs, and retention and signal-range results, concluding that external-antenna, high-band encoded tags with high-voltage lithium batteries give the best reception in the saline, highly conductive waters of the region.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Roundtail Chub]]. Four of these are federally listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- **Status:** Of 55 native and non-native fish species in the upper basin, 42 (76%) are native (Tyus et al. 1982). The Colorado Pikeminnow, the largest North American minnow, now occupies roughly 20% of its historic range. Bonytail were considered the rarest native fish in the basin, with occasional wild individuals and most fish being hatchery-reared.
- **Threats cited:** Altered hydrologic regime, degraded water quality, competition and predation from introduced non-native fish, reduced recruitment from loss of spawning and nursery habitat, reduced food base, and angler-related mortality of adults. Dams built from the 1920s onward, and the 1956 Colorado River Storage Project Act, transformed the river into a series of reservoirs with cold-water releases.
- **Study rationale:** Historic quantitative data on fish distribution and abundance are scarce, and native fish declined before causal factors were identified, so telemetry was adopted to test hypotheses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water conditions:** Water conductivity (up to 1,200 µS/cm) in southwestern rivers strongly attenuates radio signals. Visual observation is often impossible because of turbidity, and ultrasonic tags performed poorly in riverine settings (limited range, signal loss from turbulence and boulders) and in large lakes such as [[Lake Powell]] (wave action).
- **Depth limit:** Controlled experiments showed no radio contact with transmitters deeper than about 5 m. Field tests showed external-antenna high-band tags could be detected at 4–5 m depth in highly conductive water.
- **Signal range:** Maximum reception for an ATS Model 6 external-antenna tag (40–41 MHz) was about 100 m (825 µS/cm, 25 °C). A Lotek MCFT-7A high-band tag reached about 500 m (840 µS/cm, 25 °C).
- **Monitoring sites:** Key study areas include the [[Green River]] (Utah), [[Colorado River]] near Black Rocks (Colorado), [[Gunnison River]] and [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] fishway (Colorado), [[San Juan River]], [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Grand Canyon]].

## Key Findings

**Surgical protocol**
- Based on protocols from Tyus (1982), Valdez and Nilson (1982) and Archer et al. (1985).
- Wild fish were anesthetized with MS-222 (tricaine methanesulfonate, about 100–200 mg/L for about 3–4 min), operated on ventral-side up with aerated water passed over the gills, and typically took 7–10 minutes.
- Females of Colorado Pikeminnow were not implanted during late spring and early summer, to avoid spawning-period stress and transmitter expulsion from enlarging egg masses.
- External antennas were threaded through a hollow avian feeding tube to expedite placement. Incisions were closed with absorbable or non-absorbable sutures.
- From the 1990s, fish received intraperitoneal gentamicin sulfate (100 mg/ml, 0.5 ml per kg body weight).

**Transmitters**
- Tags varied in size, frequency, pulse rate and battery life. Examples include AVM SM-1 (40.600–40.699 MHz; 9 g air weight, 90-day life), Smith-Root tags (28–30 g air weight, 360-day life, internal antenna) and Lotek MCFT-7A (149.760 MHz).
- Transmitter-to-fish weight ratios were no more than 2.4% for Colorado Pikeminnow and razorback sucker and 2.5% for humpback chub.
- Individual 40 MHz fish were identified by frequency (separated by at least 10 kHz) and pulse rate (30–60 pulses/min). Lotek high-band tags used unique identity codes.

**Receivers and antennas**
- Smith-Root RF40, SR40 and ATS R2000 receivers (40–41 MHz) were used from 1980 onward. Lotek SRX400 data-logging receivers supported automated monitoring at remote stations.
- A fishway station at Redlands Diversion Dam (installed June 1996) had seven antenna sites, four aerial and three underwater.
- Large Cushcraft and Telex Yagi antennas (boom 3.7 m, element 3.2 m) gave greater range but were cumbersome.

**Retention, survival and effects**
- Tyus (1988) found that growth and mortality of adult Colorado Pikeminnow with radio transmitters did not differ from non-implanted fish. Beeswax-coated implants gave high retention and low expulsion rates, and acrylic-coated tags (1990s) gave similar retention.
- Mortality followed implantation and release in some Colorado Pikeminnow and Roundtail Chub, though the cause is uncertain.
- Mortality of domestic-reared bonytail in the Green River exceeded 90%, which Modde and Meyer (1991) attributed to failure to adapt to natural conditions.
- External-antenna tags can persist as open wounds after battery expiry and must be removed by cutting the cable near the body wall.

**Future plans (1999)**
- Continue implanting Lotek MCFT-7A tags in adult Colorado Pikeminnow.
- Test a lighter (10–13 g), smaller tag with external antenna in sub-adult Colorado Pikeminnow (400–550 mm). Field tests in the Gunnison River (1999) detected this tag at 4 m depth and about 250 m horizontal distance, with reception 25% lower than a comparable Lotek model with a 2-V lithium cell.

**Conclusion:** High-band frequency, external-antenna transmitters and high-voltage lithium batteries have improved the detection of tagged fish in deep, highly conductive waters of the upper Colorado River basin.
