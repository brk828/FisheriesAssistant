# Triploid Grass Carp for Aquatic Plant Control

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service leaflet (Fish and Wildlife Leaflet 8, 1987), prepared by [[James P. Clugston]] of the National Fishery Research Laboratory and [[Jerome V. Shireman]] of the University of Florida, summarizes the use of sterile triploid [[Grass Carp]] as a [[Biological Control]] agent for unwanted aquatic vegetation. The authors present triploid fish as a relatively low-cost, long-term control option with few side effects when properly stocked, while noting persistent uncertainty about the long-term ecological effects of open-system introductions. The leaflet covers production and verification of triploids, feeding biology, temperature tolerance, stocking rates, comparisons with other herbivorous fish, and availability.

## Background & Description

Aquatic plants are needed for bottom stabilization and cover in ponds, lakes, and streams, but become a costly problem when they interfere with human uses. Control methods include water level manipulation, mechanical removal, [[Herbicide]] application, and biological control by plant-eating animals.

The grass carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), native to eastern Asia, was brought into the United States in 1963 for research. It now occurs in the wild or under research conditions in at least 35 States. Eight States (Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Kentucky, Nebraska, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Virginia) issue special permits for triploid use, while many others prohibit or restrict the species.

**Triploid characteristics:**
- Triploids have 72 chromosomes; normal diploid (fertile) grass carp have 48.
- Triploids are presumed to be functionally sterile based on preliminary studies.
- Triploidy is induced by physically shocking fertilized eggs (warm or cold immersion, or hydrostatic pressure) to retain a chromosome set normally expelled during cell division.
- Because treatment does not yield 100% triploids, ploidy is verified, usually by electronically measuring red blood cell nucleus volume. Most agencies require this testing before stocking.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regulatory status:** A U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service "Biological Opinion" issued on 2 December 1985, prompted by a South Carolina request, found no reason to prohibit stocking of triploid grass carp in closed or open systems. It relied on [[University of California at Davis]] research indicating that female triploids are functionally sterile and male triploid sperm is probably nonfunctional. The opinion is a legal document for agencies seeking Federal aid (including [[Wallop-Breaux Funds]]) and does not promote the species.

**Diet:** Grass carp are nearly wholly herbivorous after reaching about 4 in. (100 mm). Fish under about 2 in. (50 mm) eat mainly zooplankton. Diet then shifts to filamentous algae, duckweed, and soft pondweeds (preferred throughout life), with large fish also eating emergent reeds, rushes, and sedges. Without aquatic vegetation, they feed on overhanging terrestrial plants or supplemental cut vegetation. Table 1 lists common eaten species, including [[Filamentous Algae]] (*Cladophora*, *Pithophora*), [[Duckweed]] (*Lemna*, *Wolffia*), [[Water Hyacinth]], [[Pondweeds]] (*Potamogeton*), [[Watermilfoil]] (*Myriophyllum*), and [[Eelgrass]] (*Vallisneria americana*).

**Temperature:** Grass carp tolerate a wider temperature range than most herbivorous fish, making them potentially useful across the United States. Feeding is irregular at 3-6 °C (37-43 °F), becomes steady at about 14 °C (57 °F), peaks at 20-26 °C (68-79 °F), and decreases near 33 °C (91 °F).

## Key Findings

- **Stocking rates** vary among States from about 3 to 200 fish per acre, depending on fish size. Fish are initially stocked at the low end of the range and supplemented as needed. Adequate numbers should remove weeds within 2 years; overstocking risks poor water quality and starvation.
- **Fish under 200-250 mm** should not be stocked where predaceous fish are present.
- **Consumption estimates** (percent of body weight eaten per day): under 6 lb (2.7 kg), 100%; 6-13 lb (2.7-5.9 kg), 75%; over 13 lb (5.9 kg), 25%.
- **Stocking rates** can be estimated from plant biomass, the growing season within 20-26 °C, consumption rates, and the desired control period.
- **Herbicide-plus-fish alternative:** initial herbicide treatment followed by triploid stocking to maintain control may cost more upfront but controls weeds faster with fewer fish.
- **Comparison with other herbivores:** [[Tilapia]] and [[Silver Dollar Fish]] eat less vegetation per fish, are restricted to warm climates, and are prolific, making population control difficult. Triploid grass carp are presented as meeting the key criteria for biocontrol: high vegetation consumption and broad environmental tolerance, plus reduced risk of wild establishment if sterile.
- **Availability and cost:** At least six U.S. hatcheries commercially produce triploid grass carp. Guaranteed triploids cost $4-$5 per 8-in. (200-mm) fish, with costs expected to fall as supply grows.
- **Remaining uncertainty:** No significant negative environmental impacts from the widespread distribution had been observed, but long-term ecological effects in open systems remain uncertain, and state regulations still apply.
