# Movements of Sub-Adult Razorback Suckers in a Colorado River Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This interim progress report, prepared by Paul C. Marsh and Brian R. Kesner of the [[Arizona State University]] Department of Biology (15 October 1999), documents a radio-telemetry study of sub-adult [[Razorback Sucker]]s released into [[Lake Havasu]] on the lower [[Colorado River]]. The study tested whether sub-adult razorback suckers released near water-diversion intakes would be [[Entrainment|entrained]] into the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) canal, the [[Metropolitan Water District of Southern California]] (MWD) intake, or [[Parker Dam]].

Twenty fish were tagged with external radio transmitters and released on 08 December 1998. No entrainment was confirmed at any structure. Most fish that were located remained in the shallow, turbid [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]] (BWRNWR) arm of the lake. The report is an interim document, and its conclusions are limited by the tag life (expiry in March 1999) and by detection range in deep water.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The razorback sucker is an imperiled native fish of the Colorado River system. Its entrainment into canals and water-control structures is a conservation concern. The report notes records of this species in southwestern canals dating to the late 1800s, including fish taken from [[Salt River Project]] canals near Phoenix. More recent records include three adult fish in the newly operated CAP canal (1996–1997) and two larvae captured in drift nets there in 1997.

**Entrainment context.** The authors state that entrainment potential clearly exists, but that the circumstances under which it occurs are unclear. They propose that it may be a random event, or may result from fish proximity to a structure, directed movement, or behaviour linked to spawning, river or reservoir stage, and facility operations. Fish may also remain in the quiet shelter of penstocks or pump intake tubes during shutdowns and then be drawn into the system at start-up. Some individuals apparently survive passage and can colonise receiving waters.

**Study objective.** The purpose was to examine short-term entrainment of sub-adult razorback suckers into water-control structures on Lake Havasu. Wild adult and larval razorback suckers, along with repatriated fish, occupy the lake.

**Intake locations.** The MWD withdraws water at the [[Whitsett Pumping Plant]] on the California shore, about 3.5 km upstream of Parker Dam. The CAP intake ([[Havasu Pumping Plant]]) sits inside a short (0.5 km) intake channel on the Arizona side, about 3 km upstream of the dam.

**Pumping capacities.** The report lists nominal rated capacity as 2,025 CFS for the nine MWD pumps and 3,000 CFS for the six CAP pumps. Average pumping during the study period (09 December 1998 to 24 March 1999) was 1,439 CFS for MWD and 2,215 CFS for CAP.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Havasu.** The reservoir formed in 1938 when Parker Dam closed. Arizona and California share its shoreline. The main body is about 42 km long, with a maximum depth of about 22 m near the dam.

**Bill Williams arm.** Upstream, within BWRNWR, the lake becomes shallow, with a silt and sand bottom. Reed grass (*Phragmites australis*) lines the banks, and shallows are choked in places by dense cattail (*Typha domingensis*). Water is much more turbid here. Visibility falls below 0.5 m upstream of the U.S. Highway 95 bridge over the Bill Williams River. The river was dry for several km upstream of the lake throughout the study, so fish could not move into the stream.

**Hydrology.** Releases from Parker Dam averaged 11,586 CFS over the study period, with highest discharge usually during the day. Lake surface elevation ranged from 450.5 to 441.5 feet MSL. At the lower elevation, much of the study area was inaccessible by boat due to shallow water.

**Fish origin and handling.** The fish were wild [[Lake Mohave]] larvae captured in winter–spring 1997 and reared by the [[Native Fish Work Group]] at Boulder City Wetlands Ponds, Nevada. They were trammel-netted, collected on 07 December 1998, and transported in water near 16 °C containing MS-222 (5 mg/L) and 5% sodium chloride to reduce stress.

## Key Findings

**Methods summary**
- Twenty sub-adult razorback suckers were fitted with external radio transmitters (ATS Inc.) and injected with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT, 400 kHz). Nine additional fish were PIT-tagged without transmitters and released as backups.
- Transmitters were 13 × 45 mm cylinders with a 21 cm trailing antenna, weighed 8 g, operated at 40–42 MHz, and had a nominal battery life of 90 days. Signal range was generally greater than 50 m.
- Ten fish were released at the mouth of the CAP intake channel, and ten in a quiet cove about 3.5 km up-lake from the MWD pumping facility.
- Three fixed [[Radio Telemetry|radio receivers]] with data loggers monitored Parker Dam, the CAP intake and the MWD intake. A mobile receiver with a hand-held loop antenna was used for weekly boat surveys, day and night, until tags expired in March 1999.

**Entrainment results**
- **No entrainment was confirmed.** No fish was detected by the CAP intake monitor or the Parker Dam monitor.
- The MWD monitor recorded one transmitter signal. A search of the receiving reservoir found no evidence that this fish entered the intake system.
- Fish MWD-181 was "possibly entrained" by the MWD intake according to the individual histories, but this was not confirmed. Its signal was weak and was last recorded near Question Mark Cove in 3 to 4 m of water.
- Data loggers ran continuously except for an 8-hour power loss at Parker Dam (approximately 1900 on 26 January to 0200 on 27 January 1999).

**Dispersal from the CAP release site (10 fish)**
- Six fish moved immediately into shallow, near-shore water of the BWRNWR. A seventh moved there after a brief downstream excursion. The summary states that all seven remained within BWRNWR through March 1999.
- Two fish that initially entered the BWRNWR left the area and were not encountered again (CAP-030 and CAP-040).
- One fish (CAP-050) was never contacted after release. It was probably in deep water near the peninsula (about 12 m).

**Dispersal from the MWD release site (10 fish)**
- Four fish moved 4 to 6 km across the lake into the BWRNWR. Two remained there until March, and two left the area and were not encountered again.
- Six fish were never contacted after release (MWD-110, -150, -160, -171, -191, -201).

**Overall contact statistics**
- Nine of 10 CAP-released fish and four of 10 MWD-released fish were contacted at least once after release.
- Ten of 13 contacted fish were never detected outside the BWRNWR boundary. Eight of 10 fish contacted within the refuge never left it.
- Seven fish were never contacted. The authors suggest transmitter failure, movement into deep water beyond signal range, predation or scavenging, or upstream movement out of the survey area as possible causes.

**Habitat use and behaviour**
- Individual fish showed site fidelity to particular areas. Examples include fish residing near the [[Bill Williams River]] bridge, in Question Mark Cove, at Havasu Springs docking area and at Office Cove.
- One fish (MWD-120) moved in January 1999 from a reedy cove to a location under a man-made fish habitat structure, coinciding with a drop in lake level that blocked access to the reed grass.
- The authors note that razorback suckers typically associate with shallow, littoral habitat and do not spend much time in deep water, citing [[Mueller and Marsh]] (1998) and [[Mueller et al.]] (1998). Signal loss in deep water therefore limits the interpretation of the "never contacted" fish.

**Study limitations**
- The report is an interim document. The authors acknowledge that the probability of contacting fish outside the Bill Williams arm was low, because the shallow, clear-signal area received most survey effort after the first two weeks.
- Tag life ended in March 1999, so long-term fate could not be determined.
