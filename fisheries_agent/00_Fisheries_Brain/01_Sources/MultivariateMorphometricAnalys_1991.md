## Executive Summary
This study by Muoneke, Maughan, and Douglas (1991) addresses the taxonomic ambiguity created by widespread introductions of [[Striped Bass]], [[White Bass]], and their fertile hybrid ([[Striped Bass X White Bass Hybrid]]) throughout southeastern U.S. reservoirs. Because these three *Morone* forms co-occur but are subject to different management and fishing regulations, reliable field identification is critical. Traditional single-character diagnostics (e.g., tongue tooth-patch counts) proved unreliable due to high variability, especially in white bass and hybrids. The authors applied **sheared principal components analysis (SPCA)** and **discriminant analysis (DA)** to 52 morphometric characters (a truss-network design) measured on 147 specimens. Striped bass were cleanly separated from the other two forms with 100% classification accuracy, while white bass and hybrids showed substantial morphological overlap, yielding an overall 85% correct classification rate. The authors recommend managing white bass and hybrids as a single unit stock for regulatory and law-enforcement purposes, given the practical difficulty of reliably telling them apart.

## Background & Description
- **Sampling design**: 51 morphometric characters plus standard length (Table 1), chosen to form a **truss network** (Strauss and Bookstein 1982) spanning longitudinal, transverse, and oblique body axes — improving on traditional cephalic/caudal-biased character sets (Hubbs and Lagler 1947).
- **Size ranges (standard length)**:
  - White bass: 76–295 mm (mean 170 mm)
  - Hybrids: 78–490 mm (mean 236 mm)
  - Striped bass: 169–398 mm (mean 264 mm)
- **Key discriminating traits — Striped Bass vs. others** (via SPCA, component H₁):
  - Longer **snout length** (character 8)
  - Longer **second dorsal spine length** (character 14)
  - Shorter **postpectoral length** (character 2)
  - Shorter **base length of pelvic fin** (character 30)
  - Shorter **third anal spine length** (character 25)
- **Key discriminating traits — White Bass vs. Hybrids** (via SPCA, component H₂):
  - Longer **second anal spine length** (character 15)
  - Longer **third anal spine length** (character 25)
  - Longer **orbital length** (character 28)
  - Longer **base length of pelvic fin** (character 30)
  - Larger **eye diameter** (character 49)
  - All above measures tend to be *greater* in white bass than in hybrids.
- **Hybrid intermediacy**: Hybrids show morphology intermediate between parental species for several traits, notably:
  - Greater **distance between posterior of anal fin and posterior of second dorsal fin** (character 41)
  - Greater **distance between orbit and preopercle** (character 47)
  - Greater **first dorsal fin height** (character 27)
- **Discriminant Function 1** (accounting for 87.59% of between-group variance) separated all three forms using 10 characters: postpectoral length (2), snout length (8), second (15) and third (25) anal spine lengths, first dorsal fin height (27), pelvic fin base length (30), anal-to-second-dorsal fin distances (40, 41), anal fin height (43), and orbit-to-preopercle distance (47).
- **Discriminant Function 2** (12.41% of variance) separated hybrids from both parental species but not the parental species from each other.
- **Mahalanobis distances (D²)**: White bass–hybrids = 9.8; hybrids–striped bass = 59; striped bass–white bass = 83 — quantitatively confirming striped bass as the most morphologically distinct group.
- **Canonical correlation**: 0.96 along canonical axis 1; Wilks' lambda = 0.03 (P < 0.01).
- Only 4 of the 13 diagnostic characters identified in this study had been used by prior researchers (Bayless, Williams, Kerby, Harrell, Bishop), underscoring the value of the truss-based multivariate approach over earlier univariate/ratio-based methods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- All three *Morone* forms are managed as **recreational sport fish** in reservoir and stream ecosystems across the southeastern USA, well outside their ancestral estuarine/anadromous native range.
- The [[Striped Bass X White Bass Hybrid]] was deliberately propagated and stocked (beginning with Stevens 1967) due to desirable traits: high survival, fast early growth, general hardiness, strong sport qualities, and broad adaptability to reservoir conditions (Bayless 1968; Bishop 1968; Ware 1975).
- By the early 1980s, striped bass and their hybrids occupied **57% of total U.S. reservoir surface area** (Stevens 1984), reflecting the scale of anthropogenic introduction and range overlap with native/introduced white bass populations.
- **Hybridization and backcrossing concerns**: Because hybrids are fertile, there is documented and suspected natural reproduction/backcrossing in the wild:
  - Natural [[Morone]] hybridization (white bass × white perch) reported in Lake Erie (Todd 1986).
  - Suspected natural striped bass × white bass hybridization in Lake Maumelle and Beaver Lake, Arkansas (Crawford et al. 1987).
  - Confirmed hybrid reproduction in the Savannah River, Georgia (Avise and Van Den Avyle 1984) and Palestine Reservoir, Texas (Forshage et al. 1988).
- This reproductive overlap complicates fisheries management because unregulated interbreeding blurs the boundaries between managed "species" units within shared reservoir habitats, motivating the need for robust identification tools.
- Specimens for this study were drawn from multiple Oklahoma reservoirs (Fort Supply, Canton, Optima, Carl Blackwell, Great Salt Plains) and Texoma Reservoir (Oklahoma-Texas), plus hatchery-reared hybrids from the Southeastern Fish Culture Laboratory, Alabama — illustrating the geographic breadth of co-occurring *Morone* stocks.

## Key Findings
- **No single morphometric or meristic character** reliably distinguishes striped bass, white bass, and their hybrids — consistent with prior univariate studies (Bayless 1972; Williams 1976; Kerby 1979, 1980).
- **Sheared Principal Components Analysis (SPCA)** effectively removed size-related allometric effects, revealing shape-based separation: striped bass were clearly distinguishable, but white bass–hybrid separation remained weak.
- **Discriminant Analysis (DA)** using 10 characters achieved:
  - **100% correct classification of striped bass**
  - **80% correct classification of both white bass and hybrids** (independent validation sample)
  - **85% overall correct classification rate**
- Findings corroborate Kerby (1980) regarding second anal spine length being longer in white bass than in striped bass/hybrids, but **contradict Bayless (1968)**, who reported the opposite pattern between hybrids and white bass.
- Compared to prior morphometric discrimination efforts — Waldman et al. (1988): 70–80% precision for striped bass stock delineation; Harrell and Dean (1988): 83% resolution for juvenile *Morone* crosses — this study's truss-network, multivariate approach achieved **higher resolution (85%)**, likely due to the larger character set and wider specimen size range.
- **Management recommendation**: Given persistent overlap between white bass and hybrids even under the best multivariate models, the authors recommend that fishing regulations and management strategies be established for *Morone* fisheries as a whole rather than for individual species/forms, particularly in waters containing both white bass and hybrids.
- **Methodological note**: Ratios of specimens to variables were adequate for white bass (4.4:1) and hybrids (4.2:1) but low for striped bass (1.5:1), falling short of the commonly recommended 3–4:1 minimum, which may limit confidence in striped bass-specific coefficients despite their perfect classification rate.
