# Palm Lake Fish Kill and Salvage Summary (April 1992)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This memorandum, dated May 5, 1992, from [[Francisco J. Abarca]] (Native Fish Program Manager) to file, summarizes a response to reports of fish dying at [[Palm Lake]] in the [[Hassayampa River Preserve]], April 21–24, 1992. The team of [[Rob Bettaso]], [[Bruce Denova]] and [[David A. Weedman]] (Assistant Native Ichthyologist) investigated the cause and attempted to salvage endangered fish. Field data pointed to severe, low [[Dissolved Oxygen]] as the main cause, consistent with a "traditional summer fish kill." The team captured a small number of live [[Bonytail Chub]] and moved them to the [[Page Springs Hatchery]], while many fish died. The memo records repeated, mostly unsuccessful salvage efforts.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Determine the cause of a fish kill and rescue as many endangered fish as possible.
- **Setting:** Palm Lake lies within the Hassayampa River Preserve. Cattails are thick along most of the shoreline, and the maximum depth is 9.5 feet.
- **Species involved:**
  - [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), the main target for salvage, including live, dead, and moribund individuals.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), one individual observed near the surface.
  - [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), stunned by electroshocking.
  - Topminnow and/or pupfish were the targets of minnow traps in the upper spring pool; the traps contained only crayfish.
- **Personnel:** [[Val Little]] (Preserve Manager) first reported the surface gaping. [[Dahlberg]] visited on April 22 to assess water quality.

The team also noted that the DO meter was checked against a bucket of lake water and found to give fairly accurate readings. A dense, unidentified [[Zooplankton]] bloom was observed in a bucket of lake water and was suspected to contribute to the oxygen deficit.

**Habitat and fish behavior:** Fish were seen gaping (piping) at the surface in early morning, which the team interpreted as a sign of low oxygen. Surface activity ceased by late morning, which was attributed to sunlight, surface water temperature and activity. Fish reappeared only briefly, and the team noted that the shallow depth and dense [[Cattails]] allowed fish to escape netting.

## Key Findings

- **Cause of kill:** The team hypothesized, and Dahlberg confirmed, that the kill resulted from low dissolved oxygen, described as a "traditional summer fish kill." Readings were as low as 0.1 ppm.
- **Salvage results (count given in the document):**
  - April 21: 1 live [[Bonytail Chub]] and 2 dead from the bottom during the first seine. Later, the trammel net caught 2 live and 3 dead bonytail (one was entangled). A dead fish was also recovered by hand.
  - April 22 morning: about 9 bonytail were netted from the canoe and 2 from shore; 5 captured on the first seine pull, and 2 more dead from the bottom later.
  - April 22: 20 bonytail were loaded onto the hatchery truck and transported to Page Springs. Three additional mortalities were found floating at 1400.
  - April 23–24: 1 live bonytail was captured by electroshocking, and 1 more was seen floating alive and later revived. Numerous [[Mosquitofish]] were stunned.
- **Ineffective methods:** Trammel net, [[Cast Net]], [[Seine Net]], bag seine, and the backpack electroshocker were all tried, with limited results. Escape cover (dense cattails) and shallow depth reduced capture success. The [[Electroshocking]] settings matched those used for [[Bonytail Chub]] collection in the [[Little Colorado River]].
- **Outcome:** Surviving bonytail were held in an aquarium at the Preserve and appeared to be doing well by 1200 on April 24. The memo leaves their final disposition unstated in the visible text.
- **Conclusion:** The event was an oxygen-driven summer die-off with limited salvage success. Surface-piping fish and low DO together indicated the lake's oxygen budget had collapsed, and the dense zooplankton bloom and thick cattails appear to have contributed.
