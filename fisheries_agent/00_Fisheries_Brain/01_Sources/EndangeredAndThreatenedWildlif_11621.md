# Endangered and threatened wildlife and plants; Determination of endangered status for the Virgin River chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #Gila

## Executive Summary

This is a **1989 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Final Rule** listing the **Virgin River Chub** (*Gila robusta seminuda*) as an **endangered species** under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. The subspecies is endemic to a historically ~184-mile reach of the Virgin River in southwestern Utah, northwestern Arizona, and southeastern Nevada, but by the time of listing occupied only about 50 miles of habitat between Mesquite, Nevada and La Verkin Creek near Hurricane, Utah. Primary threats include habitat destruction from diversions and impoundments, disease (Asian fish tapeworm), competition/predation from exotic fishes (notably red shiner), catastrophic flood events, and toxic spills. Critical habitat designation was postponed pending economic analysis under §4(b)(6)(C) of the Act.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic history**: First collected from the Virgin River near Washington, Utah, by the Wheeler Survey and originally described as intermediate between [[Gila Robusta]] and [[Gila Elegans]] (Cope and Yarrow 1875). Later treated as a subspecies of *G. robusta* (Ellis 1914; Miller 1946; LaRivers and Trelease 1952). Holden and Stalnaker (1970) restricted the subspecific name *seminuda* to the Virgin River population specifically; this designation was supported by Minckley (1973) and extensive taxonomic work by Smith et al. (1977).
- **Morphology**: A very silvery, medium-sized minnow.
  - Average total length: ~20 cm (8 in)
  - Maximum total length: up to 45 cm (18 in)
  - Dorsal, anal, and pelvic fin rays: 9–10
  - Gill rakers: 24–31
  - Diagnostic feature: small, deeply embedded scales on back, breast, and part of belly (difficult to see, sometimes absent) — basis for the subspecific epithet "seminuda" (meaning "half-naked").
- **Related taxon**: An undescribed subspecies of *Gila robusta* occurs in the [[Moapa River]], Nevada (historically a Virgin River tributary, now both flow into Lake Mead). This Moapa form has also declined (Cross 1976; Deacon and Bradley 1972) but was **not** covered by this listing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical range**: Believed to have occupied the Virgin River from its original confluence with the mainstem [[Colorado River]] upstream to La Verkin Creek near Hurricane, Utah.
- **Habitat preference**: Deeper, swift (but non-turbulent) water associated with boulders or other cover; sand and gravel substrates; water temperatures below 90°F (30°C). Highly tolerant of high salinity and turbidity (Deacon and Holden 1977).
- **Diet**: Omnivorous — algae, aquatic and terrestrial insects, organic detritus, and crustaceans (Cross 1975).
- **Habitat loss**:
  - ~35 miles dewatered/reduced by irrigation diversions (primarily 1860–1900).
  - ~40 miles inundated by Hoover Dam/Lake Mead impoundment.
  - Combined losses represent ~56% of the species' original habitat.
- **Co-occurring endangered species**: The federally endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) inhabits overlapping reaches; recovery coordinated originally by the Woundfin Recovery Team, later renamed the **Virgin River Fishes Recovery Team**.
- **Exotic species interactions**:
  - Historically the Virgin River resisted invasion by exotic predatory fish (e.g., [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], largemouth bass) due to extreme natural conditions (high salinity, temperature, turbidity, fluctuating flow).
  - The exotic **red shiner** (*Notropis lutrensis*) invaded the upper Virgin River, becoming dominant in collections within one year of establishment (1985) in the St. George area; implicated in decline of native fishes including chub and woundfin.
  - In 1988, a 21-mile reach (Washington Fields Diversion to Virgin River Gorge) was chemically treated to eradicate all fish (including red shiner) after salvaging ~1,200 Virgin River Chub; a barrier dam was constructed at the head of the Virgin River Gorge to prevent reinvasion.
- **Disease**: The **Asian fish tapeworm** (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) is a major threat (Deacon 1986; Heckmann et al. 1986), first documented in Virgin River Chub near St. George, likely present since 1979; possibly introduced via red shiner. Infected fish are less able to cope with environmental stress and compete with exotic species. Parasite loads correlated with poor water quality, flow, and habitat disturbance.
- **Catastrophic events**: Shortly after the 1988 eradication project, a dike failure at [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] released ~25,000 acre-feet of water, causing a devastating flood impacting fish populations across 85 miles of the Virgin River.

## Key Findings

### Population & Abundance Data
- Cross (1975, 1973–1975 study): Collected only **154 individual chubs**, just **1%** of 10,822 total native fish specimens collected. Very few young-of-the-year or adults over 17.5 cm (7 in) standard length were found.
- Reproductive success (Woundfin Recovery Team & Hickman 1985, 1988): Good chub reproduction documented only in **1978, 1983, and 1986** — i.e., only 3 of 12 years surveyed (1977–1988). Poor/minimal reproduction in 1984, 1985, 1987, 1988.
- Hickman's higher catch numbers (1985, 1988) were **not directly comparable** to Cross (1975) due to more efficient gear and different sampling sites/effort — not indicative of a true population increase.

### Distribution Data
- Historic range: ~184 miles of the Virgin River.
- Current (as of 1989) occupied range: ~50 miles of mainstem river between Mesquite, NV and La Verkin Creek near Hurricane, UT.
- Documented in a tributary only **twice** (Cross 1975; Hickman 1985) — essentially a mainstem-restricted species.

### Regulatory/Conservation Status at Time of Listing
- **Arizona**: Group 2, Threatened Native Wildlife (Arizona Game and Fish Commission 1982) — species whose continued presence in the state is in jeopardy.
- **Nevada**: Listed as "sensitive" (Nevada Board of Wildlife Commissioners 1981) — candidate for more restrictive status.
- **Utah**: Listed as "threatened" — likely to become endangered in the foreseeable future.
- None of these state-level listings provided **habitat protection**, only protection from unregulated taking.
- **Federal action**: Endangered status became effective **September 25, 1989**; critical habitat designation postponed under §4(b)(6)(C) pending economic analysis.

### Threats Summary (ESA Five-Factor Analysis)
| Factor | Threat Detail |
|---|---|
| A. Habitat destruction/modification | Impoundments, diversions, altered flow regimes, temperature/salinity changes, loss of marshes/backwaters, channel simplification |
| B. Overutilization | No evidence of overuse for commercial/recreational/scientific purposes |
| C. Disease/predation | Asian fish tapeworm; limited but increasing exotic fish invasion (notably red shiner) |
| D. Inadequate regulatory mechanisms | State listings lack habitat protection provisions; Utah's 1986 in-stream flow law offers some potential protection; Nevada water law lacks in-stream flow provisions |
| E. Other factors | Small population size and restricted range amplify vulnerability to single catastrophic events (e.g., flood, toxic spill) |

### Proposed Recovery Actions
1. Studies on larval drift and impacts of parasites/red shiners.
2. Chemical removal of all fish below Washington Fields Diversion with restocking of native species (including chub).
3. Construction of a fish passage barrier below Riverside, Nevada.
4. Development of water management policy recommendations.
5. Securing legally protected in-stream flow rights.
