# Mitochondrial DNA Variability in the Razorback Sucker and Genetic Structure of an Endangered Sonoran Topminnow Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two conservation-genetics papers in *Conservation Biology* (Volume 10, No. 1, February 1996). The main paper (Dowling et al.) examines mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) diversity in the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) from a source population in [[Lake Mohave]] and from three hatchery year classes produced at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH). The authors find high genetic diversity in the wild source population but significantly lower diversity in the 1990 hatchery year class. They recommend that wild-spawned larvae be collected and reared in predator-free bays as the most cost-effective way to preserve genetic diversity. A second paper (Quattro et al.) appended at the end compares mtDNA variation in the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) across Arizona and Sonora, and finds a highly divergent lineage in the Rio Yaqui drainage of Arizona.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large, long-lived catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Historically widespread and abundant, it declined sharply after the construction of reservoirs. Populations briefly increased in new impoundments, but recruitment failed, and populations disappeared roughly 40 years after impoundment. The species was federally listed as endangered in 1991 (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1991). The largest remaining populations are in the upper Green River (fewer than 1,000 adults, per Tyus and Karp 1989) and in Lake Mohave (fewer than 23,300 adults in 1993, per Marsh 1994).

Hatchery culture of the species began at DNFH in 1981. The authors contrast the goals of production hatcheries, which select for rapid growth and high survivorship, with those of [[Captive Breeding]] for imperiled taxa, which must maintain [[Genetic Diversity]] and avoid adaptation to captivity. Mitochondrial DNA was chosen because it is maternally inherited and evolves rapidly in many vertebrates, making it a highly polymorphic marker for tracing maternal lineages.

**Methods (Dowling et al.):**
- Source: gamete samples (ova or milt) from 25 adults captured at Lake Mohave, 1987-1989. Lake Mohave is a mainstem Colorado River impoundment created by closure of [[Davis Dam]] in the early 1950s.
- Hatchery: tissue from 31 juveniles representing three year classes from DNFH (1987, n = 10; 1989, n = 11; 1990, n = 10), sampled in 1990.
- mtDNA was isolated and digested with eight restriction enzymes (see [[Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism]]). Haplotypes were identified with composite eight-letter codes.
- Diversity was measured as haplotype diversity (h) and number of haplotypes, with significance tested by random resampling. Effects of broodstock size were examined by computer simulation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors attribute the species' decline primarily to [[Recruitment Failure]]. Among almost 12,500 fish handled in Lake Mohave since 1974, no verified natural recruits were found. Large numbers of larvae hatch each year but soon fall to predation, possibly linked to nutritional constraints after yolk absorption. All adults in Lake Mohave are thought to have hatched in the early 1950s, before the spread of nonnative predators. Adults average 25 to more than 40 years of age, and juveniles are virtually unknown.

Lake Mohave consists of an upstream riverine reach of about 60 km that grades into a wider lower basin of about 48 km. There are no direct tributaries, and the population is concentrated in the upper parts of the lower basin. Fish are most easily captured near shore during spawning from late autumn through spring. The species made up 10-30% of net catches in spawning areas over the past 20 years.

The Sonoran Topminnow study (Quattro et al.) addresses a related concern: restoring endangered fishes requires an understanding of evolutionary relationships among remaining natural populations. The species remains locally abundant in Sonora, Mexico, but has declined in its former range in Arizona.

## Key Findings

### Razorback Sucker (Dowling et al.)

- **Source population diversity is high.** Lake Mohave showed a haplotype diversity of h = 0.97 and a large number of unique haplotypes (17 in 25 individuals). The authors describe this as remarkably high and suggest the population descends from an exceedingly large, diverse, panmictic population in the lower Colorado River basin.
- **Hatchery diversity varied among year classes:**
  - 1987: h = 0.89, 6 haplotypes in 10 individuals
  - 1989: h = 0.91, 7 haplotypes in 11 individuals
  - 1990: h = 0.71, 4 haplotypes in 10 individuals. This value was significantly lower than the source population.
- **Effective number of females (Ne) was low in the 1990 class.** Estimates were 23.8 (1987), 32.2 (1989), and 6.9 (1990). The 1987 and 1990 values were appreciably smaller than the actual number of females used.
- **Simulations show limited sensitivity to broodstock size for diversity.** Broodstocks of about 20 individuals produced progeny with mean h approaching 0.95. The number of alleles, however, rose directly with broodstock size, so it is a poor indicator of genetic diversity in high-diversity organisms.
- **Possible causes of the 1990 decline:** Differences among females in fecundity or progeny viability, or both. Survival from fertilized ova to swim-up in 1990 (n = 10) was below the minimum of 22.5% recorded in DNFH reports for the seven preceding year classes (mean 40.1%, maximum 71.4%). Pooling of ova from several females prevents identification of individual contributions. Record-keeping problems and mixing of wild and hatchery fish further complicated the genealogy. The specific origin of the 1990 class could not be determined, and close inbreeding is a possibility.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Pursue protective custody and rearing of wild-spawned larvae collected from the lake, followed by release after they reach a size with high survival. The authors call this the most cost-effective option for preserving genetic diversity.
  - Continue naturally spawning adults in protected, predator-free bays, using different adults each year.
  - Do not release current hatchery progeny to replace the Lake Mohave population.
  - Replace the DNFH broodstock with wild-caught individuals and document genetic diversity in offspring.
  - Keep all three options open, because hatchery production remains necessary as a backup.

### Sonoran Topminnow (Quattro et al.)

- No mtDNA variation was detected within or among samples from the Gila River basin in Arizona.
- Considerable mtDNA variation was found within and among populations from several river systems in Sonora.
- A population from the upper Rio Yaqui in southeastern Arizona showed substantial divergence from all other populations examined. The authors argue this lineage is relevant to management in Arizona and call for more detailed genetic and morphological studies to define its distribution and taxonomic status.
