# The Most Dreaded Fish in the Amazon River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #candiru-vampire-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1968 newsletter issue from [[Carolina Biological Supply Company]] (Burlington, North Carolina) contains three items. The lead article, by [[Charles O. Masters]], profiles the [[Candiru]] or vampire fish, a small South American catfish-like fish described as the most dangerous fish encountered by collectors in the [[Amazon River]]. The issue also includes a reader letter from Dr. Leonard G. Katz on maintaining closed [[Marine Aquaria]], a company telephone number change, and a catalogue offering [[Preserved Rabbits]] as a dissection specimen alternative to cats.

## Background & Description

The article opens by contrasting the candiru with other feared Amazonian animals: the [[Piranha]], which can skeletonize prey, and the [[Electric Eel]]. The candiru is described as hardly larger than a man's little finger, or "about the size of a short pencil stub." Its body is soft and scaleless, and it resembles salamanders found in local ponds. Erectile, rear-pointing spines around the head are its distinguishing external feature.

The article cites [[George S. Myers]] of Stanford University (1947) on the diversity of [[Catfish]]: fewer than 20 catfish species are known from the entire Mississippi Basin, whereas over 450 are recorded from the less-studied Amazon Basin, with more discovered almost every year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and behavior:** Candiru habitually live inside the gill cavities of much larger fishes such as catfishes. Their slender bodies allow them to penetrate far between the gill openings, where sharp teeth and spines start a flow of blood. The article states that the fish swell to an enormous size as they feed.

**Human encounters:** The author notes that the fish has an affinity for human and animal urine, though this is not confirmed as the sole cause of its behavior. Candiru are reported to enter the urogenital openings of people wading or bathing. Spines on the head prevent withdrawal once the fish has entered. Forest hospital records and case histories document the danger, and the article notes that tales of the fish date back over a hundred years, initially dismissed as fantastic before being accepted as true.

**Collecting conditions:** Seine collecting is described as productive in shallow tributaries and along Amazon margins, where plant life is lush and fishes are colorful and plentiful. Wading is considered risky because of predatory fish.

**Aquarium trade:** Some collected fishes would fetch high prices in large-city pet stores, but transport is described as difficult and costly.

## Key Findings

**Candiru (vampire fish) hazard**
- The article describes the candiru as the most treacherous fish collectors may encounter in any waters of the world, and the only known vertebrate parasitic on man (as stated by the author).
- Reported attacks mainly involve the urinary tract. Reports of entry into the mouth, ears, nostrils, and anus exist but are not confirmed.
- Females are described as especially vulnerable, while males suffer more, possibly due to differences in genitourinary structure. The author attributes this to anatomy, not a confirmed finding.
- Blood loss is often heavy enough to make victims prostrate. Surgery performed promptly is necessary, as untreated attacks can be fatal; infection is an ongoing risk.
- Folk remedies are mentioned: swallowing the acrid juice of a green fruit from a local tree sometimes expels the fish within two hours, but the author calls this unreliable.
- Woven palm-fiber sheaths are worn by bathers for protection but are "not 100% safe."
- Candiru die shortly after removal from water, and no expedition has successfully brought one home for public display.

**Amazon biodiversity**
- Myers (1947): fewer than 20 catfish species in the Mississippi Basin versus over 450 recorded in the Amazon Basin.

**Marine aquaria guidance (Dr. Leonard G. Katz, responding to C. O. Masters' November 1967 article)**
- Container: an all-glass aquarium bonded with Silastic is best; Plexiglass is satisfactory but scratches easily; stainless steel is unsuitable because the cement can be toxic and salt water reacts with the metal.
- Minimum volume for marine fish: 20 gallons, with 30 gallons preferred. Minor toxic problems are far more serious in a 10-gallon tank.
- Specific gravity: 1.025 need not be held constant. Values from 1.020 to 1.029 are acceptable as long as changes are gradual.
- Plants and fish do not mix in closed systems.
- New fish should be added gradually and quarantined for one to two weeks in a "hospital aquarium" to prevent introducing disease.
