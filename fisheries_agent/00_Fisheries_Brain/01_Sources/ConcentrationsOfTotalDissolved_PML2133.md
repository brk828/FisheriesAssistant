# Tolerance of Total Dissolved Solids by Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by [[R. Pimentel]] and R. V. Bulkley, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, tested the salinity preferences and avoidances of young-of-the-year or yearling [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]] in a [[Salinity Gradient Device]] that spanned a range of [[Total Dissolved Solids]] (TDS) concentrations. Each species showed a distinct tolerance band. The study concludes that future TDS increases in the [[Colorado River]] would harm municipal, industrial, and agricultural water uses before these endangered fishes were affected. Development of [[Oil Shale]] resources is identified as a potential source of increased salinity.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River is heavily regulated and serves as the main water supply for the arid southwestern United States. Reservoirs and agricultural diversions raise TDS concentrations through evaporation and soil leaching. Desalination and reservoir management have been used to control salinity, and the basin faces new pressures from energy and mineral extraction, including oil-shale processing, saline groundwater, and new dams.

The three study species are native to the upper basin:

- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): The largest minnow endemic to North America. Its original range was the entire river system, but it now persists only in the main stems of the Green and upper Colorado rivers.
- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): Not described until 1946 and never common. It lives in deep, swift water in steep canyons, with current distribution in the Green, Little Colorado, and upper Colorado rivers.
- **Bonytail** (*Gila elegans*): Once the most abundant fish in the main channels, now the rarest. In the five years before publication it had been found only in [[Lake Mohave]] and Desolation Canyon of the Green River. Reservoir spawning has been reported, but no survival of young fish had been documented. The authors consider it the species closest to extinction.

Before this study, almost nothing was known about how elevated TDS affected these fishes. The stated objective was to determine the TDS concentrations preferred or avoided by juveniles, to help developers reduce project impacts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental water chemistry.** Fish were held in dechlorinated Logan City water (the control compartment). Compartment 2 held major ions at approximate mean Colorado River composition near Moab, Utah (USGS 1978 water year, April–July excluded). Compartments 3–8 had increasing concentrations of Mg²⁺, Na⁺, and SO₄²⁻, raised by a combined total of 1 g/L per compartment, which produced a TDS gradient from about 210 mg/L (compartment 1) to about 7,400 mg/L (compartment 8). These three ions are the main constituents of saline water from oil-shale processing.

**Conditions.** Dissolved oxygen stayed at 90–100% of saturation, pH at 7.6–8.2, and total ammonia below 0.2 mg/L. Fish were acclimated to 14 °C (±1 °C). Test temperatures differed by species: 16–18 °C for bonytails, 14–16 °C for Colorado squawfish, and 12 °C for humpback chubs. The authors note that temperature affects osmoregulation and therefore TDS tolerance, which limits direct comparison among species.

**Broader threats.** Oil-shale process waters in the drainage were reported to have TDS concentrations of up to about 24,500 mg/L (OCR-garbled range). Tributaries polluted by such waters may be avoided by fish, causing habitat loss. The authors also suggest that the bonytail's high-TDS preference could have isolated it from Colorado Squawfish, which historically may have occupied saltier water.

## Key Findings

### Preferred and Avoided TDS Concentrations

| Species | Preferred TDS (mg/L) | Avoided TDS (mg/L) | Test temperature (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colorado Squawfish | 560–1,150 | >4,400 | 14–16 |
| Humpback Chub | 1,000–2,500 | >5,100 | 12 |
| Bonytail | 4,100–4,700 | 5,600–6,600 | 16–18 |

- **Colorado Squawfish** had the lowest preferred and avoided concentrations. Its preference was similar to present TDS in the Green and Colorado rivers, and it avoided the higher-salinity zones.
- **Humpback Chub** was intermediate for both preference and avoidance. Its preference matched TDS in the Green, Colorado, and Little Colorado rivers.
- **Bonytail** preferred TDS about four times higher than present river concentrations. It also avoided the lowest concentrations most strongly.

### Experimental Design and Sample Sizes

- Trials used 30 Colorado Squawfish, 40 Humpback Chubs, or 40 Bonytails per run, released in the first compartment.
- Each species had three replicate runs and three controls (all compartments filled with city water). Control runs were pooled to give one control distribution per species.
- Fish positions were recorded every 0.5 hours for the first 2 hours and again at 24 hours. Only the 24-hour distribution was used to define preference and avoidance.
- Preferred concentration was defined as the mode of the pooled treatment distribution. Avoided concentration was the TDS level avoided by 95% of pooled fish.
- Test results were analysed by chi-square goodness-of-fit tests against a uniform distribution. A result was accepted only when p ≤ 0.05.

### Statistical Observations

- Schooling behaviour reduced the effective sample size. The authors therefore relied on the chi-square results only when p was ≤ 0.05.
- Independence tests were significant in two of three replicates for Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub, and in all three for Bonytail.
- The strength of schooling appeared inversely related to control uniformity. Colorado Squawfish were the weakest schoolers, and Humpback Chub were the strongest.
- All treatment distributions diverged significantly from uniform, indicating a real concentration preference in each species.

### Management and Economic Implications

- The authors suggest that ecological impacts would be felt after economic ones. Other water uses would be severely affected before salinity increases reduced the survival of these fishes.
- A legal flow-weighted TDS limit for Imperial Dam is set at a value within the preferred range of the study fishes (OCR-garbled number, apparently 829 or 879 mg/L).
- The U.S. Department of the Interior (1981) estimated that each 1 mg/L increase in average TDS in the annual lower basin discharge would cost downstream users $450,000 (1980 dollars). About one-third of users are farms and the remainder are municipal or industrial. The lower basin has about 12 million water users.
- The authors caution that the experiment was short-term and limited to juveniles. Eggs and larvae may be more sensitive, and temperature effects on TDS avoidance were not fully tested. Results should be extended to other life stages and temperatures before being applied to management decisions.
