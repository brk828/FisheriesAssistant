# Cibola High Levee Pond Annual Report 2004: Early Life Ecology of Bonytail and Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is the fourth and final annual report of a five-year U.S. Geological Survey study of the early life ecology of [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] at the [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] (CHLP), a roughly 5-acre backwater on the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Juveniles of both species were collected, confirming natural recruitment, with year classes from 2002 through 2004 represented. However, [[Largemouth Bass]] also spawned, and the authors judge that native recruitment will eventually cease as predator numbers grow. Predator/prey tank tests showed that many non-native species, including young-of-year game fish, [[Common Carp]], and invertebrate and amphibian predators, consume razorback larvae and eggs. Telemetry showed adult bonytail sheltering in riprap by day and feeding in open water at night. Bonytail also died after spawning-season tagging and handling, and the report recommends prompt renovation and a formal management plan.

## Background & Description

The study aims to describe the early life history and habitat requirements of bonytail (*Gila elegans*) and razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*). CHLP is described as the only location where both species were successfully producing young, and the findings are intended to guide replication on a larger scale. The report is organized into six sections: physical water quality, preliminary recapture analysis, bonytail cover utilization, predator/prey tank tests, miscellaneous studies, and management recommendations. Two appendices are included: a telemetry study (Appendix A, by [[Paul C. Marsh]] of Arizona State University) and a diet analysis (Appendix B, by Marsh and Jason D. Schooley).

The pond was reclaimed to eliminate non-native fishes and first stocked with native species in 1993. Bonytail are described as extremely prolific, producing tens of thousands of young annually, while razorback suckers are relatively long-lived and their young are hard to sample.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality**
- Conductance peaked at 1106 µS/cm in January and fell to 980 µS/cm in June, linked to river exchange and evaporation.
- Surface temperatures reached 18 °C nearly three weeks earlier than in the two previous years. Bonytail spawned about a month early.
- Surface temperatures ranged from 12 to 34 °C. Minimum water temperatures exceeded 30 °C at all depths by late July.
- More than 95% of the pond volume held over 6 mg/L dissolved oxygen, with no evidence of anoxia. Groundwater upwelling and a 2–3 °C diel cycle appear to limit stratification.
- Water depth fluctuates with river elevation, from about 2 m in winter to 3.4 m in summer.

**Habitat and cover**
- Cover includes large riprap along the high levee, natural bank cavities, beaver dens, cattail stands, and submergent vegetation. Vegetation covered 10–20% of the surface and an estimated 40–60% of the volume at peak growth.
- Riprap substitutes for historic flood debris and root wads lost when lower Colorado River banks were deforested.
- Fish-eating birds such as herons, egrets, osprey, pelicans, and cormorants are common, and talon scars suggest bonytail are targeted.

**Predation and non-native species**
- Native fish eggs, larvae, and fry are taken by crayfish, predatory insects, tadpoles, bullfrogs, and birds. Herons and osprey also prey on natives larger than 40 cm.
- Non-natives found at CHLP included [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] and [[Bullfrog]].

## Key Findings

**1. Natural recruitment and recolonization**
- Juvenile bonytail and razorback suckers were collected in 2004, with young from 2002, 2003, and 2004 represented.
- About 100 young largemouth bass (5–50 cm) were seen during late-July snorkeling, distributed across the pond.
- Bonytail recruitment is estimated to have occurred in 11 of the past 12 years; razorback recruitment in at least 5 of 12 years.
- Razorback recruitment during 2003–2004 was estimated at 11%, considered conservative. The authors believe the true figure may be 15–20%.

**2. Size structure and growth**
- Small bonytail (<200 mm) were far more abundant than larger fish in trammel-net samples from 2001–2002. Sampling changed to minnow traps and hoop nets in 2003–2004, which caught very few small fish.
- Bonytail growth was measured for 31 recaptured individuals (9–24 months after release). Small bonytail (<200 mm) grew about 3.5 mm/month. Growth fell with size and declined sharply beyond 400 mm.
- The largest bonytail (over 500 mm) were all female; all males were under 490 mm. Females exceeded 99% of razorback suckers over 500 mm.
- Razorback growth was over 6 mm/month for small fish, fell to 2.5 mm/month or less above 350 mm, and reached about 0.5 mm/month for the largest fish (under 500 mm).
- The oldest razorback suckers were under 14 years old.

**3. Spawning stress and tagging mortality**
- Bonytail spawned a month early due to a warm spring. Combined with spawning exertion, this was linked to stress and post-release mortality of study fish, including controls in hatchery tests.
- Small telemetry-tagged bonytail (2004, n=9) were presumed dead by early May, with mortality within 14 days in hatchery tests of tagging.

**4. Cover use by bonytail (telemetry, Appendix A)**
- Adult bonytail (2003, n=10, mean TL 453 mm) used riprap interstices by day and moved into open water at night, presumably to feed.
- Daytime use showed significant site fidelity (χ² = 697.93, df = 25, P < 0.001). Signals were never contacted in seven zones, and six zones had 19 or more contacts each.
- Subadult bonytail (2004) occupied open water day and night and did not seek specific cover, though their behavior may be atypical given their mortality.
- Trammel nets set at dusk and dawn caught over 90% of bonytail as they entered or exited riprap. Hoop nets caught only three bonytail from four overnight sets.
- A 30-gallon aquarium test showed that 7-cm bonytail moved to vegetative cover over rock and gravel.
- Snorkeling in late July observed over 100 razorback suckers and 100 bonytail near dense spiny naiad, with bonytail feeding on new plant growth.

**5. Predator/prey tank tests**
- In 2004, all species tested consumed razorback larvae. Mean larval mortality was:
  - Over 77%: common carp, green sunfish, and channel catfish
  - Under 10%: bullfrog tadpoles, threadfin shad, and fathead minnows
- Crayfish consumed razorback eggs at an instantaneous rate of 1.1 eggs per crayfish per hour. Tadpoles consumed 0.03 eggs per tadpole per hour.
- Green sunfish consumed about 0.21 larvae per predator per hour, and channel catfish about 0.15.
- Young-of-year game fish showed the highest predation rates. The authors argue that non-game fish such as common carp are significant potential predators too.
- The authors caution that stomach-content analyses can miss larval prey, since larvae digest quickly.

**6. Diet of bonytail (Appendix B)**
- Gut contents were sampled non-lethally from 72 bonytail (28 from 2003 at TL >375 mm; 44 from 2004 at smaller sizes).
- Of the 72 fish, 13 (18%) had empty guts: 33% of evening samples versus 7% of night samples.
- Plant matter decreased, and invertebrate matter increased, with fish size. Fish remains occurred only in fish longer than 425 mm.
- Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) was found in one specimen and in tapeworm proglottids in 8 of 72 (11%) samples. The authors describe this as the first record of the parasite in wild bonytail on the lower Colorado River.

**7. Management recommendations**
- The authors judge that the presence of largemouth bass signals the "eventual failure" of native recruitment, expected within 1 to 3 years.
- They advise against mechanical predator suppression for now and recommend renovation "in the very near future."
- Future refuges should include mature fish in initial stocking, because bonytail mature in one or two years while razorback females need 3–4 years.
- A threshold of non-native fish exceeding 5% of the annual sample should trigger salvage and renovation, timed for the spring spawn. Renovation may be needed only every 5–10 years.
- Management should initially maintain the native community, with a low-maintenance plan that includes annual fall sampling.

**8. Planned work for 2005**
- Monitor size structure, bass expansion, and bonytail spawning success; complete scale aging, telemetry, predator/prey, and diet analyses; analyze zooplankton; field-test a DIDSON camera; and produce a final report, brochure, and video.
- Funding ends in September 2005.
