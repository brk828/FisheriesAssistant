## Executive Summary

This study (Korschgen & Baskett, 1963) examines the food habits of [[Bullfrog|Bullfrogs]] (*Rana catesbeiana*) in two contrasting Missouri freshwater habitats: a large shallow impoundment (Duck Creek Wildlife Management Area, 1775 acres) and three eastern Missouri streams (Meramec, Bourbeuse, and Salt rivers). Although the paper is centered on amphibian trophic ecology rather than fish biology per se, it provides quantifiable data on **fish as prey items** within an omnivorous predator's diet, alongside crustaceans, insects, mammals, other amphibians/reptiles, and incidental plant material. Findings indicate fish comprised a minor but consistent dietary component only in the impoundment habitat, while [[Crayfish]] and insects dominated across all habitat types. The study emphasizes that bullfrog feeding patterns are broadly similar across impoundments, streams, and farm ponds, with crayfish being a near-universal midsummer staple.

No fish were identified in stream (river) bullfrog samples; all fish prey occurrences were restricted to the Duck Creek impoundment collections.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predator-prey context:** Fish occupy a minor trophic role as prey within the diet of a generalist amphibian predator ([[Bullfrog]]) in a vegetated, shallow, flooded bottomland-hardwood impoundment habitat (dominant submerged vegetation: [[Myriophyllum|Water Milfoil]] and [[Ceratophyllum|Coontail]]).

**Habitat context for fish prey:**
- The impoundment (Duck Creek WMA) featured full pool depths of 2–6 feet, shallow margins, and dense aquatic macrophyte cover — conditions supporting small-bodied fish such as [[Bluegill]], bullhead catfish, and [[Blackhead Minnow|Blackhead Minnows]].
- No fish predation was recorded in the three eastern Missouri streams (Meramec, Bourbeuse, Salt rivers), despite these systems supporting typical Ozark stream fish assemblages — possibly reflecting bullfrog foraging behavior, prey availability, or stream hydrology (faster current, rockier substrate) reducing capture opportunity relative to still impoundment waters.
- Fish contributed only 3.1% of total food volume in impoundment bullfrogs and 0% in stream bullfrogs, ranking well below crayfish (31.6%/39.2%), insects (26.4%/41.9%), mammals (15.3%/4.5%), and amphibians/reptiles (13.9%/10.8%).

## Key Findings

- **Fish as prey are a minor, habitat-specific dietary component**: Fish occurred exclusively in impoundment bullfrog stomachs (10 occurrences total among 278 stomachs), comprising only 3.1% of total food volume; no fish remains were found in any of the 130 stream-dwelling bullfrog stomachs.
- **Fish species identified**: [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), bullhead catfish (*Ameiurus*/*Ictalurus* sp.), and [[Blackhead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) — all small-bodied species typical of quiet, vegetated impoundment waters.
- **Crayfish dominate over fish as aquatic prey**: Across all three habitat types (impoundment, streams, farm ponds), [[Crayfish]] far outweighed fish as a food resource, comprising 29.8–55.2% of monthly diet volume at Duck Creek and 39.2% overall in streams; crayfish genera differed by habitat (*Procambarus* sp. dominant in quiet impoundment waters vs. *Orconectes* sp. dominant in flowing Ozark streams).
- **Opportunistic/gorging behavior overshadows fish predation**: Bullfrogs exhibited pronounced dietary shifts toward locally superabundant prey (e.g., [[Meadow Mouse|Meadow Mice]] in July 1959, comprising 43.9% of monthly diet; [[Cicada|Cicadas]] during a 1959 emergence comprising up to 42% of monthly volume on the Bourbeuse River), which diluted the relative importance of fish and other steady-state prey items.
- **General dietary architecture is consistent across habitats**: Despite habitat differences (still impoundment vs. flowing streams vs. small farm ponds), the six major prey categories (crustaceans, insects, mammals, amphibians/reptiles, miscellaneous invertebrates, fish) remained broadly consistent in rank importance between the Duck Creek impoundment and previously studied central Missouri [[Farm Pond|farm ponds]] (Korschgen & Moyle, 1955), supporting the characterization of *Rana catesbeiana* as a highly opportunistic, generalist predator (per [[Dickerson 1931]]: "will eat any moving object that he can swallow or partially swallow").
- **Plant material was incidental**: Vegetation (leaves, stems, duckweed, acorns) was recorded frequently but interpreted as incidentally ingested rather than a deliberate dietary target, and thus excluded from ecological interpretation of prey selection.
