# Sound Discrimination by Males of Two Cyprinid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #bioacoustics #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1960 study by Exalton A. Delco, Jr. investigates whether species-specific sounds produced by female [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid fishes]] function as a premating (sexual) isolating mechanism, analogous to anuran mating calls. Using two sympatric minnow species—the [[Blacktail Shiner]] (*Notropis venustus*) and the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*)—the author recorded sub-audible sounds produced exclusively by ripe females and tested male behavioral responses via simultaneous playback of conspecific and heterospecific calls. Results demonstrated that males of both species exhibited a significant positive approach response toward the calls of their own species, supporting the hypothesis that female-produced sounds serve as a species-recognition device and contribute to reproductive isolation between these two closely related, co-occurring species.

## Background & Description
This paper does not focus on external/skeletal morphology but rather on **acoustic morphology** (call structure), analyzed via sound spectrograph (Kay Electric Company Sonograph). Key distinctions between species' calls (Table III):

| *Notropis lutrensis* | Sandy Creek, 18.5 mi E of Llano, TX | 0.84 | 200–275 | 8 trills/sec |

- **Sound source**: Sounds were produced *only* by ripe females of both species.
- **Audibility**: Calls were inaudible to the unaided human ear; detection required a waterproofed microphone and tape recorder system.
- **Context of production**: Sounds occurred when males were visibly present but not in physical contact with females (suggesting a pre-contact signaling function).
- No investigation was made into the specific sound-producing anatomical structure (e.g., pharyngeal teeth, swim bladder mechanisms common in other [[Cyprinidae]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Both species are found in Texas river/stream systems within the same general drainage (Colorado River system tributaries), representing sympatric or parapatric freshwater cyprinids.
  - *N. venustus* sampled from the San Saba River at Fort McKavett, TX.
  - *N. lutrensis* sampled from Sandy Creek at the base of Enchanted Rock, near Llano, TX.
- **Reproductive behavior**: Sound production is tied to courtship/pre-spawning condition, occurring specifically in "ripe" (reproductively mature) females.
- **Sexual isolation mechanism**: The study frames female acoustic signals as a potential premating [[Reproductive Isolation|isolating mechanism]] preventing hybridization between two congeneric, co-occurring *Notropis* species—paralleling mechanisms described in anurans (see [[Blair 1958]] on frog/toad mating calls) and other soniferous fishes like [[Haemulidae|grunts]], [[Sciaenidae|drums and croakers]], and gouramis (*Trichopsis vittatus*).
- **Behavioral ecology**: Males displayed increased locomotor activity and localization behavior (swimming toward and "nipping" at speakers) during experimental (sound) trials compared to controls (silent), indicating active sound-source-seeking behavior tied to the acoustic stimulus rather than random movement.

## Key Findings
1. **Sex-specific sound production**: Only ripe females of *N. venustus* and *N. lutrensis* produced detectable sounds; males were silent.
2. **Male discrimination ability**: Males of both species spent significantly more time in the observation chamber broadcasting conspecific female calls versus heterospecific calls, indicating active sound discrimination (see [[Table I]] and [[Table II]] data).
3. **Species asymmetry in response strength**:
   - *N. lutrensis* (both sexes) showed a strong, clear preference for conspecific *N. lutrensis* female sounds.
   - Male *N. venustus* responded strongly to conspecific sounds; however, **female *N. venustus* response was minimal**, suggesting sex- or species-specific differences in sound sensitivity, motivation, or experimental recording quality.
   - The stronger response in *N. lutrensis* females (vs. *N. venustus* females) is hypothesized to relate to differences in sexual readiness, sound localization ability, site fidelity/affinity behavior, or asymmetric recording quality between species.
4. **Localization behavior in males**: Males approached and physically contacted ("nipped") the speaker transmitting conspecific sound, indicating well-developed sound localization capacity.
5. **Confounding variable**: Apparent food-associated site fidelity was noted as a factor that could obscure or bias measures of pure sound discrimination in some trials.
6. **Acoustic divergence between species**: The two species' calls differ measurably in **duration**, **dominant frequency**, and **trill structure** (*N. lutrensis* calls are trilled at ~8 trills/sec; *N. venustus* calls are not trilled), providing a plausible physical basis for discrimination, though the specific acoustic parameter driving recognition was not experimentally isolated.
7. **Conclusion**: Female-produced sounds in these two [[Notropis]] species likely function as a species-recognition signal and an important premating (sexual) isolating mechanism, supporting the broader hypothesis (per [[Moulton 1956]], [[Tavolga 1956|Tavolga]], and others) that fish sounds can serve reproductive isolation functions similar to anuran vocalizations.
