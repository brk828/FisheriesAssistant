# A Preliminary Key to the Identification of Larval Fishes of Oklahoma, with Particular Reference to Canton Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #larval-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1967 bulletin from the [[Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory]] by Edwin B. May and Charles R. Gasaway presents a preliminary, dichotomous, illustrated key for identifying larval and early juvenile freshwater fishes of Oklahoma, built mainly from meter-net collections in [[Canton Reservoir]]. The key also covers other Oklahoma species, defines a refined terminology of early life stages based on yolk and fin development, summarizes larval development, and includes a partial bibliography organized by family. The authors state the work is intended primarily as an ecological tool rather than a detailed account of early life histories.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The authors note that comprehensive sources for identifying young fishes were scarce, and that rearing or collecting reference series was often impractical. The key describes only the characters needed for rapid identification. Meristic and allometric detail was omitted, and the authors state it should be drawn from the literature where available. Prolarvae (yolk-bearing stages) were largely excluded, except for species with large, long-retained yolk sacs.

**Study area and sampling.** Periodic meter-net hauls were made from early March through August in 1965 and 1966, mostly with a 1/32-inch square-mesh meter net towed by a 24-foot outboard boat. Canton Reservoir was described as having approximately 28 species in 12 families, including 2 catostomids, 7 centrarchids and 7 cyprinids (carp excluded), of which only 5 were common. Specimens were also drawn from [[Lake Texoma]], [[Tenkiller Ferry Reservoir]] and several hatcheries and museum collections; Table I (not reproduced) lists sources for each species.

**Terminology.** The authors refined life-history stage definitions:
- **Young (Y):** from hatching to sexual maturity.
- **Larva (L):** from hatching until a full complement of juvenile characters is acquired. Subdivided into **Prolarva (P)**, still bearing yolk; and **Postlarva (PL)**, comprising **Early Postlarva (EP)**, after yolk absorption until all soft vertical fin rays have developed, and **Late Postlarva (LP)**, when soft vertical fin ray counts match the adult.
- **Juvenile (J):** all fins developed, spines and rays ossified and counts identical to the adult, ending at sexual maturity.

**Identification approach.** Fishes were identified by following developmental sequences from reared laboratory young, by elimination, by adult characters and by comparison with published illustrations. Confidence came from the number of reared fishes, series completeness (1/2-mm intervals for some species) and knowledge of local relative abundances.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Environmental variation:** The authors acknowledge that light, temperature and dissolved substances influence meristic characters and pigmentation. Specimens from natural waters differed markedly from laboratory-reared fish, and living larvae often look very different from opaque preserved specimens.
- **Preservation:** Specimens should be fixed and stored in buffered formalin (2-5%). Pigment fades in preservation, more slowly if kept in the dark, so pigment-based keys are flagged as unreliable for turbid, very clear or long-preserved material.
- **Habitat-dependent appearance:** Light availability affects pigment density and form, so fishes from different habitats may look very different.
- **Abundance notes:** Gizzard shad and certain silversides and drum were described as very abundant in reservoir meter-net samples, while some species (e.g., Stoneroller in open-water samples, sunfish in some cases) were seldom taken.
- **Introduced and non-spawning species:** White bass, striped bass, northern pike and rainbow trout are noted as introduced or stocked, with no known spawning in Oklahoma at the time.

## Key Findings

**Key structure and diagnostic characters**
- The key is dichotomous, with 58 numbered couplets (plus alternatives), and relies on:
  - Preanal versus postanal distance.
  - Gut length, shape and thickness.
  - Pigment density (rated lightly to very heavily pigmented; see Figure 2).
  - Fin ray counts (dorsal, anal) and spine presence.
  - Adhesive organ presence on the snout.
  - Head length relative to total length.
- Pigment is described as an unreliable character and should be checked both ways for clear, turbid or preserved specimens.
- Gut characters are described as among the best for identifying larval fishes.

**Developmental findings (from the Anatomy section)**
- Body shape of larvae is often very different from the adult before vertical fins develop; catfishes resemble adults even before the yolk is reduced, while shad may take longer to attain adult shape.
- Myomere counts change with age and can be useful for defining stages.
- The gut is usually conspicuous and straight after yolk absorption, and approaches adult form by the time all fins have developed.
- Most fishes have little or no pigment at hatching; pigment takes several days to develop.
- Fin development was the most consistent external feature examined. Pectoral and caudal fins develop first, pelvics last. Anal and dorsal rays develop at about the same rate, and the spinous dorsal appears last.

**Family-level diagnostics noted in "Brief Descriptions"**
- **Lepisosteidae (gars):** very large prolarvae, a conspicuous adhesive organ, and heavily pigmented rudimentary fins.
- **Clupeidae ([[Gizzard Shad]]):** filiform body and a very long, vertically banded gut that reaches nearly to the caudal fin base.
- **Cyprinidae (minnows; carp distinguished by a dark caudal streak that branches at the operculum):** slender bodies with long, straight, thin guts.
- **Catostomidae (suckers):** very difficult to separate from cyprinids; larvae probably uncommon in reservoirs.
- **Ictaluridae (catfishes):** very large prolarvae with conspicuous barbels and fin rays.
- **Centrarchidae:** Micropterus are usually heavily pigmented and deep-bodied; Lepomis guts are slightly longer than Pomoxis; Pomoxis has the shortest gut of any centrarchid examined.
- **Percidae:** Percina caprodes has a long, straight, thick gut; Stizostedion has canine teeth evident as postlarvae.
- **Atherinidae ([[Brook Silverside]] and [[Mississippi Silverside]]):** very short gut and an anus that migrates posteriorly with age.
- **Sciaenidae ([[Freshwater Drum]]):** large head and abdominal region, an extremely long dorsal fin, and often abundant in reservoir samples.

**Limitations and caveats**
- The key is preliminary and expected to be revised as more information becomes available.
- Variability is common, so the key cannot be expected to work with absolute accuracy. Misidentifications may arise from unexamined stages, aberrant specimens or species not included.
- Identifications should remain tentative until large series of the same species have been examined.
- Some species groups (certain minnows, sunfishes and suckers) are expected to remain difficult to separate even with better material.

**Bibliography**
- The authors compiled a partial bibliography of larval freshwater fish literature, organized by family and limited to publications relevant to Oklahoma families. It is not a complete list, and the authors acknowledge other useful references may be missing.
- Families covered include Acipenseridae, Polyodontidae, Lepisosteidae, Amiidae, Clupeidae, Salmonidae, Hiodontidae, Umbridae, Esocidae, Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae, Centrarchidae, Percidae, Sciaenidae and others.
