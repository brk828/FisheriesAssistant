# Factors Influencing Poststocking Dispersal of Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) disperse after release into the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species, once common in the basin, has declined sharply and survives mainly as old adults, with poor juvenile survival. Researchers used radio telemetry to track fish released in three habitat types: [[Lake Powell]] (reservoir), a small backwater on the [[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]], and a large backwater on the lower Colorado River. They compared fish that were given a period of [[Site Acclimation]] with reference fish released directly. Dispersal distance was largely unaffected by acclimation in the reservoir and large backwater, but fish in the small backwater dispersed far downstream, and the study concludes that release site and [[Flow Conditioning]] shape post-release outcomes more than acclimation alone.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is an endangered catostomid fish that was listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1991 (USFWS 1991). It was historically abundant across the upper and lower Colorado River system. Recovery depends on [[Reintroduction Programs]] that stock hatchery-reared fish, but stocked fish often show poor survival, which the authors link to physical and physiological stress from [[Hatchery Rearing]], handling, and transport.

Fish were sourced from three facilities:
- [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] (pond and raceway rearing)
- [[Boulder City Municipal Ponds]] (pond rearing)
- [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (raceway rearing)

Fish were held in ponds, raceways, or flow-conditioned tanks before release. Release groups typically contained 15 fish, and total stocked numbers were about 30 fish per test site. Test fish were tagged with radio transmitters for tracking.

Table 1 of the source lists rearing type, test site, length, and weight for each group. Lengths were roughly 290 to 1,018 mm and weights were roughly 304 to 925 g, varying by source and group. Exact values are reported in the original table and are not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study sites differed in flow, cover, and geography:

- **[[Lake Powell]]:** A large reservoir of about 95 km, with the release site near the reservoir. Fish dispersed toward shore and into vegetative or rocky cover. The reservoir also includes the lower [[San Juan River]] reach, which was surveyed for about 15 km upstream.
- **[[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]] confluence:** A steep-walled canyon system with rapid flow (about 0.1 to 1.0 m/s) and sparse shoreline cover. Fish released in the small backwater moved downstream toward the reservoir (about 350 km of survey distance) and frequently drifted with flow.
- **Lower [[Colorado River]] ([[Davis Dam]] to [[Lake Havasu]]):** A 90 km reach with a large, shallow backwater containing submergent and emergent vegetation. Flow-conditioned fish stayed in backwaters more often than non-conditioned fish.

The authors note that flow-dominated, canyon-bound reaches lack cover, which likely contributed to downstream drift and poor survival. They also observe that handling and transport stress can accumulate over days or weeks, potentially causing chronic fatigue or death.

## Key Findings

**Methods and tracking**
- Fish were tracked for 28 days. Only fish tracked for at least 14 days were analyzed because contact was lost for some individuals.
- Surveys were conducted weekly by motorized boat, raft, or jet boat. Locations were determined using [[Global Positioning System]] (GPS) coordinates and navigational maps.

**Reservoir ([[Lake Powell]])**
- Fish were detected 145 times in the first month. All fish had left the release site within 5 days.
- By days 3 to 4, most fish (72%) were found within 10 m of shore in vegetative or rocky cover.
- Movement became minimal (<0.1 km/week) by week 3.
- Acclimated fish dispersed an average of 3.3 km, and reference fish dispersed 4.3 km. Maximum distances were 11.1 km (acclimated) and 12.4 km (reference).

**Small backwater ([[Green River]] to [[Lake Powell]])**
- All fish left the release site within 5 days, and both groups averaged about 5 km/day downstream during the first week.
- Twenty fish (8 acclimated, 12 control) were detected two weeks after release, accounting for 70 contacts.
- At the Green River and Colorado River confluence (54 km), six fish (4 acclimated, 2 control) swam upstream into the Colorado River.
- Fish moved downstream at averages of 66 km (acclimated) and 71 km (control).
- Mean drift distance was 69.5 km for small-backwater fish. The authors describe this prolonged drift as evidence that pond-reared fish perform poorly in flowing water.

**Large backwater (lower [[Colorado River]])**
- 58 fish were followed for one month, yielding 1,121 contacts.
- 21 fish remained in the backwater for at least one month.
- Six fish moved into the mainstream river. Most (95%) fish remaining in the river were found in small backwaters.
- Fish that moved downstream averaged 6.6 km (maximum 49 km downstream and 9 km upstream by the end of week 4).
- Mean distance was 7.6 km for large-backwater fish.

**Statistical results**
- A [[Kruskal-Wallis Test]] across the three release groups showed significant differences in dispersal (df = 2, p < 0.01). Dispersal was greatest for fish released in the small backwater.
- Among four site-by-treatment comparisons, acclimated and control fish did not differ significantly (P-values from 0.14 to 0.99).
- A [[Wilcoxon Test]] found that flow-conditioned fish differed significantly from pond-reared fish in dispersal rate (z = −2.298, P = 0.0216, N = 30).
- Acclimation periods of less than 7 days had no detectable effect on short-term dispersal.

**Interpretation and conclusions**
- Site acclimation results were inconclusive, partly because of small sample sizes and variable acclimation periods.
- The authors propose that [[Flow Conditioning]] improves swimming performance and reduces stress, which may increase post-release survival. Their discussion draws on salmonid studies showing that exercise and flow conditioning improve growth, stamina, and predator evasion.
- Stress from handling, transport, and hatchery rearing may have cumulative effects on fish performance. Reducing these stresses may improve reintroduction outcomes.
- The study recommends further research on winter releases and longer-term survival, and emphasizes that release-site selection and cover availability strongly influence dispersal.
