# Captive Broodstock Recommendations for Bonytail Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, submitted by Philip W. Hedrick (Arizona State University) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Parker Fishery Resources Office), evaluates the genetic status of the roughly 350 remaining F1 [[Bonytail Chub]] progeny from wild parents spawned in 1981, and sets out a protocol for integrating them, and any wild fish captured later, into a new captive broodstock. The central conclusion is that the F1 group retains relatively little genetic variation. The F1 stock descends from at most ten parents, and the contributions of the original matings were unequal. The effective number of founders is estimated at 8.48 under a "high" estimate and as low as 3.48 under a "low" estimate derived from allozyme and mitochondrial DNA data.

## Background & Description

The bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*) is a federally listed endangered cyprinid and one of the most critically endangered North American freshwater fishes. Its decline is attributed mainly to habitat destruction and alteration from water development, and to introduced non-native fishes. The only known wild bonytail are an unknown number of large, old adults persisting in [[Lake Mohave]]. Little or no recruitment appears to have occurred since the 1954 closure of [[Davis Dam]] and the subsequent establishment of non-native sport fishes.

In 1981, the USFWS produced F1 progeny at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] from wild adults captured in Lake Mohave. The original broodstock was built from five adult males and six wild-caught females captured in the 1970s. The stated aims of the report are:

- To assess the approximately 350 surviving F1 fish held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFHTC).
- To establish a protocol for integrating these fish and any newly captured wild fish into a future captive broodstock.
- To outline maintenance guidelines and recommend genetic research to evaluate the program.

Because wild parents and their tissues were not retained, and F1 fish were not individually marked by mating, genetic data from random F1 and F2 samples are the only means of inferring the actual parental contributions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report addresses the fish's population genetics, not its ecosystem. Relevant context includes:

- **Reproductive history:** Of the six wild females, one (female 1) produced no eggs. The other five yielded 5,850 to 37,700 eggs (mean 25,090). Fertilization ranged from 0.91 to 0.98 (mean 0.95). The resulting proportions of fertilized eggs per female (pi) ranged from 0.049 to 0.290 (mean 0.2). Each female was fertilized by one male, and one male was used with two females, though which two was not recorded.
- **Captive selection:** The author cites evidence that hatchery conditions can select for traits that reduce wild fitness (Frankham et al., 1986; Ryman and Utter, 1987). Selection is more likely with multiple consecutive hatchery generations and unequal parental contributions. Hatchery-reared fish released to the wild may therefore be maladapted.
- **Wild origin uncertainty:** Marsh (1997) examined 17 fish with varying capture histories and estimated that the wild proportion of recently collected bonytail ranged from a low figure (OCR-garbled; reads as roughly 11%) to perhaps two-thirds.

## Key Findings

### Parental contributions and allozyme data
- Allozyme data came from 30 F1 fish (DeMarais, unpublished; three loci) and 24 F2 juveniles from the 1985 year class (Minckley et al., 1989; six loci). Pgm-A in the F1 sample showed a significant excess of heterozygotes over Hardy-Weinberg expectations.
- Six three-locus genotypes were found among the 30 F1 fish. Genotype 1 occurred in 22 of 30 individuals.
- Binomial analysis of five matings gave: zero such matings, probability 0.513; one mating, 0.366; two or more, 0.121. A single contributing mating was therefore about 3 times more likely than multiple matings, suggesting that all or most of the 22 genotype-1 fish came from one mating.
- The remaining eight F1 fish were consistent with a second mating, and the Aat-A ratios suggested a third. The resulting "low" estimate assigns parental contributions of p1 = 0.733, p2 = 0.1335 and p3 = 0.1335 across three matings.

### Mitochondrial DNA
- All 30 F1 fish shared an identical mtDNA haplotype (a).
- Among 16 additional carcasses, 10 (0.625) carried haplotype a and six carried a different haplotype (b). This indicates that wild genetic variation not present in the original captured fish or their descendants may exist.
- Simulations (Table 4) showed that a monomorphic sample of 30 F1 fish was more likely under the "low" estimate. For a population frequency of a = 0.9, the text reports 0.738 (low) versus 0.619 (high). Table 4 lists 0.691 for the high estimate, a discrepancy in the source. The simulation count is also inconsistent: the text cites 1,000 samples, while the Table 4 caption cites over 10,000. The overall conclusion is that the F1 ancestry is better represented by the "low" estimate.

### Effective number of founders
- Under the "high" estimate, the effective number of founders for the original broodstock is Ne,f = 8.48. This is 84.8% of the maximum of ten, reached only when all five matings contribute equally. The shortfall is attributed to the relative evenness of contributions.
- Under the "low" estimate, Ne,f = 3.48, reflecting three effective matings with most progeny from one pair.
- If two reproducing females were fertilized by the same male, the high estimate would be somewhat lower.

### Future broodstock size
- With a finite number of F1 parents, retained variation declines. With 30 pairs (60 fish), the ratios of effective founders to the maximum are 89.3% (high) and 97.1% (low). With 150 pairs, they are 97.5% and 99.4%.
- Gains beyond 30 pairs are small. The author therefore suggests that many more than 30 pairs are unnecessary unless progeny reproducing in a given year are less representative than the Table 1 proportions.

### Inclusion of wild fish
- Additional wild fish would be highly valuable, since the original broodstock is already at its maximum effective founder number under the high estimate.
- One added wild fish would raise the high estimate from 8.48 to 9.58 (11.8%) and the low estimate from 3.48 to 4.48.
- Representation guideline: each wild fish should contribute between 1/(3.48 + Nw) and 1/(8.48 + Nw) of the progeny, where Nw is the number of wild fish captured.
- Using the high estimate, about nine F1 × wild matings would be needed to give one wild fish the contribution of a typical 1981 wild parent. Under the low estimate, four wild fish would contribute more than the present F1 fish.
- If there are fewer than about five wild fish, individual captured F1 and F2 fish could be incorporated at roughly one F1-parent equivalent and half an F1-parent equivalent, respectively, because they survived natural predation and, for F2 fish, cannibalism in hatchery ponds.

### Maintenance guidelines
- Use long generation intervals (ten or more years) to slow loss of genetic variation per year.
- Keep mean inbreeding low, avoiding full-sib and half-sib matings where possible.
- Uniquely mark individuals from each mating so that pedigrees are known.
- Choose broodstock parents at random, independent of phenotype, unless a genetic or congenital problem is identified.

### General recommendations
1. Use 30 to 60 pairs of F1 parents spread over several years to retain most of the original variation.
2. Include as many wild fish as possible. If ten or more wild fish are captured, base the broodstock on them and cap the F1 contribution at 25%.
3. Maintain a complete pedigree and detailed records, and manage the broodstock to minimise inbreeding.
4. Maintain the broodstock at no fewer than two separate sites, for protection against disease, accidents and natural disasters.
5. Develop highly variable genetic markers (microsatellite and major histocompatibility complex loci) to verify wild origin and identify close relatives.
6. Conduct experimental crosses between known full siblings to measure inbreeding depression in growth, fecundity and survival, so that effects on fitness can be predicted.
