# Resource Use by Larval and Early Juvenile Native Fishes in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how early life stages (under 30 mm total length) of four [[Native Fishes]] of the [[Colorado River Basin]] use nearshore habitat, allocate behavior in the water column, and feed in the [[Little Colorado River]], a relatively unaltered tributary in [[Grand Canyon]]. The authors compare microhabitat use, behavioral time budgets, and diet among [[Humpback Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. The results indicate that some [[Resource Partitioning]] occurs among larval fishes, with the strongest differences between catostomids and cyprinids. The authors frame the work as a basis for managing larval habitat in the regulated mainstem below [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

## Background & Description

Native fishes of the Colorado River basin have declined since the late 1800s, and many populations have substantial adults but rare or absent recruitment of early life stages. Altered temperature and flow regimes below mainstem dams are implicated. Cold hypolimnetic releases can retard embryonic development and reduce swimming performance, and fluctuating discharge most affects the shallow nearshore areas used by larval stream fishes.

The Little Colorado River is the largest tributary entering the Colorado River in Grand Canyon. It is a spawning and rearing site for four native species: flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, humpback chub, and speckled dace. Mainstem temperatures below Glen Canyon Dam range from 7 °C to 14 °C, and native reproduction there is limited. Apart from [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the upper basin, the ecology of larval and early juvenile native fishes was poorly known, and few dietary studies existed.

The study area was the terminal 14 km of the canyon-bound Little Colorado River. Baseflow is sustained by spring inflow, and the field work was conducted during clear-water, low-flow periods (April to August 1993 for habitat, 1993 and 1994 for behavior and diet).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat definition:** Nearshore habitats were defined as areas with current velocities below 0.2 m/s and bounded by shoreline. Fish typically cannot hold position in velocities above three to seven body lengths per second (Webb 1975).
- **Habitat variables:** Depth, distance from shore, current velocity, substrate, and cover were measured at 10 cm intervals along transects perpendicular to flow. Substrate and cover categories followed [[Lane Sediment Classification]] and study-specific definitions.
- **Life stages:** Fish were classified as protolarval, mesolarval, or metalarval based on length, following [[Snyder Larval Classification]] (Snyder 1981; Snyder and Muth 1990).
- **Behavior:** Focal-animal observations of 5 min per individual were used to score feeding and nonfeeding behavior and vertical position in five water-column zones.
- **Diet:** Gut contents were examined and estimated by relative volume using a point system. Diet overlap was calculated with [[Schoener Overlap Index]] (0 = no overlap, 1 = complete overlap). Fish carrying [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) were excluded from diet analysis.

## Key Findings

**Habitat use**
- 1,199 sites were measured, representing 1,341 fish: 444 bluehead suckers, 124 flannelmouth suckers, 251 humpback chub, and 522 speckled dace.
- Logistic models correctly classified species 54.4–88.9% of the time and life stages within species 59.7–94.7% of the time.
- Bluehead suckers used shallower water than any other species and were the most distinctive in habitat use, with more interspecific differences than any other species.
- Flannelmouth suckers appeared to be habitat generalists; their habitat use could not be separated from either cyprinid.
- Older larval stages generally occupied deeper water and areas farther from shore (except humpback chub), a pattern the authors link to a trade-off between foraging efficiency and predation risk, though morphological and dietary change may also explain it.

**Behavior**
- Time budgets differed significantly among species (G-test, P < 0.005).
- Bluehead suckers spent less time feeding than flannelmouth suckers, and speckled dace spent less time feeding than both flannelmouth suckers and humpback chub.
- Bluehead suckers used the upper third of the water column less than the other three species and the surface less than flannelmouth suckers or humpback chub.
- All four species used the benthic zone more than any other water-column category.

**Diet**
- Immature [[Chironomidae]] (midges) were the major identifiable diet component for all fish, comprising more than 60% occurrence and approximately one-third to one-half of total gut volume.
- Humpback chub consumed more taxa than any other species; cyprinids ingested more food types than catostomids.
- Diet overlap was highest within families: catostomids 0.91, cyprinids 0.82. Humpback chub overlapped little with bluehead suckers (0.58) or flannelmouth suckers (0.56). Speckled dace overlapped 0.60 with bluehead suckers and 0.61 with flannelmouth suckers.
- Diatoms occurred only in catostomid stomachs.
- Diel sampling in 1994 showed diet variation only in bluehead suckers, which had higher immature chironomid volume in daytime samples than at 2300 hours. Bluehead sucker gut fullness was greater by day than at night (P < 0.001).

**Conclusions**
- Some resource partitioning exists among larval fishes, and foraging strategies appear to differ at the family level (benthic/inorganic feeding in catostomids versus surface/water-column feeding in cyprinids).
- Shallow water, fine substrates, varied cover, and slow currents in nearshore areas should be conserved in both the Little Colorado River and the mainstem Colorado River. Future operations of Glen Canyon Dam should consider impacts on these areas.
- Laboratory studies are recommended to determine whether observed differences reflect competition, predator avoidance, morphology, or a combination of these factors.
