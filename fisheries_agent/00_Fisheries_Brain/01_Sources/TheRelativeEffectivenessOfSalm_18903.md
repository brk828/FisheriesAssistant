# The Relative Effectiveness of Salmon Eggs and Earthworms as Bait for Trout in the Sturgeon River, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study by David S. Shetter, of the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]] (Michigan Department of Conservation), tested whether [[Salmon Eggs]] are more effective than [[Earthworms]] as bait for trout, and whether [[Chumming]] increases catch. Experimental fishing took place on 35 days in 1957 and 1958 at 12 sites on the [[Sturgeon River]] and in the "walleye hole" off the river mouth in [[Burt Lake]]. The study was prompted by claims that salmon eggs threatened the [[Rainbow Trout]] population, which led to a [[Lure Restriction]] on the river in 1954.

Salmon eggs caught lake-run rainbow trout about 3.8 times as fast as worms. Chumming did not improve either bait. The restriction's effect on natural reproduction could not be assessed.

## Background & Description

The study compared two popular natural baits for trout in the Sturgeon River drainage: salmon eggs and large earthworms (*Lumbricus terrestris*). The Conservation Commission banned salmon eggs on the Sturgeon River after April 20, 1954, and on Burt Lake and the West Branch of the Sturgeon after April 20, 1956.

The trout studied were:
- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*): the main target species. Fish of 10.0 inches or more that were silvery were classed as [[Lake-Run Rainbow Trout]]. Planted legal-size fish made length alone an unreliable criterion.
- **[[Brown Trout]]** (*Salmo trutta*) and **[[Brook Trout]]** (*Salvelinus fontinalis*): caught in smaller numbers.

**Methods:** Four anglers fished in two teams, alternating bait and chumming in a systematic design. Each angler used standardized tackle: a fly or spinning rod with 6- or 8-lb monofilament, No. 8 or 10 gold hooks for eggs, No. 6 Mustad hooks for worms, and split shot about 14 inches above the hook. Chumming meant tossing 5 to 30 pieces of bait into the water. Ten Fish Division employees took part, most experienced with worms but few with eggs, which the author notes would bias results toward worms. Records were kept hourly, and fish were measured in inches. Most fish over 10 inches were scale-sampled for age. Roughly 20 hooking mortalities occurred, and all other fish were released.

**Statistics:** Catch-per-hour data were log-transformed and analysed by a factorial [[Analysis Of Variance]] with bait, method and 14-day time block as fixed factors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Seasonal lake-run presence:** Lake-run rainbows were scarce in fall 1957 and spring 1958. The author suggests that warm weather in early March 1958 triggered an early spawning run, so many fish had returned to Burt Lake before the trout season opened. Most fall lake-run fish were taken in the walleye hole at Burt Lake. The summer 1958 fishing produced the only large sample of lake-run fish in the river.
- **Hooking and loss:** Of 83 lake-run rainbows hooked across all series, 63 (75.9%) were landed. Most of the 20 lost fish escaped by throwing the hook or tearing free. Fewer than five broke the tackle. Most egg-caught fish were lightly hooked in the jaw edge.
- **Natural reproduction:** Direct-current shocking in 1955, 1957 and 1958 showed natural reproduction in the West Branch. Young-of-the-year rainbows were found at 17 of 23 sites sampled in the main river in August 1958 (alternating-current [[Electrofishing]]). The author concludes that reproduction continues in the drainage, but its level relative to pre-1954 cannot be determined.
- **Non-trout catch (excluded from tabulations):** 20 white suckers, 1 walleye, 1 creek chub and 1 sculpin.

## Key Findings

**Bait efficiency (primary result)**
- Lake-run rainbows, summer 1958 (47 fish): salmon eggs were **3.8 times** more effective than worms (95% CI 2.2–7.0). The bait effect was highly significant (p at the 1% level), with no other significant effects or interactions.
- Eggs were also more effective for other trout in all three seasons: fall 1957 2.6× (1.6–4.2), spring 1958 6.4× (3.2–13.0), and summer 1958 9.3× (5.6–15.2). The egg advantage grew across the series, which the author attributes to angler learning or a seasonal effect.
- Across all trout, eggs were about 3 times as effective as worms.
- Eggs took significantly more small rainbows, but both baits took about the same proportion of large rainbows.

**Chumming**
- Chumming did not increase catch for any bait or size class. For small fish under 7.0 inches, chumming took fewer than half as many as non-chummed fishing (chi-square = 12.64, p < 0.1%, 1 df).

**Size of fish**
- Mean total length was 8.6 inches for eggs and 9.0 inches for worms. This difference was not significant.
- About 24% of egg-caught and 15% of worm-caught rainbows were under 7.0 inches. The size-frequency heterogeneity test was significant (chi-square = 15.75, 6 df).

**Catch totals (778 hours, 10 anglers)**
- 506 rainbow, 73 brown and 12 brook trout: fall 1957 (330 h; 122 rainbow, 25 brown, 3 brook), spring 1958 (192 h; 138 rainbow, 40 brown, 9 brook) and summer 1958 (256 h; 246 rainbow, 8 brown).

**Age structure**
- Scale samples came from 242 rainbows, 71 browns and 13 brooks. Rainbow ages ranged from O to VII, brown trout I to VIII, and brook trout I to III.
- Yearlings (age I) dominated the fall and summer catches. Ages II and III were more common in spring. The three youngest age groups made up about 90% of hooked rainbows across seasons and baits.

**Management conclusions**
- Salmon eggs are several times more effective than worms, but chumming with either bait does not increase catch.
- The 1954 egg restriction's effect on the rainbow population cannot be measured with available data. Natural reproduction is confirmed, but its level relative to the pre-restriction period is unknown.
