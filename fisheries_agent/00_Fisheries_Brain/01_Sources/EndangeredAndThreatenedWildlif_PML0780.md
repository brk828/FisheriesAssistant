# Critical Habitat Designation for the Rio Grande Silvery Minnow (Federal Register Proposed Rule, June 6, 2002)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) proposed rule, published in the *Federal Register* on June 6, 2002, proposes to designate [[Critical Habitat]] for the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]], a species federally listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973). The proposal covers the middle Rio Grande from [[Cochiti Dam]] to [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]] in New Mexico, with a lateral extent of existing levees or 91.4 m (300 ft) of riparian zone on each side of the channel. The document also gives notice of a draft economic analysis, a draft [[Environmental Impact Statement]], and two public hearings. The Service excludes the lower Rio Grande and middle Pecos River, which it considers essential, in favor of a [[Section 10(j) Experimental Population]] strategy for reestablishment. The proposal was issued under a federal court order following the 1999 designation being set aside.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** One of seven species in the genus *Hybognathus* in the United States; first described by Girard (1856) from specimens taken near Fort Brown, Cameron County, Texas.
- **Morphology:** A stout, silvery minnow with moderately small eyes, a small slightly oblique mouth, and no barbels. Adults reach up to 90 mm (3.5 in) total length. The dorsal fin is distinctly pointed.
- **Reproduction:** A [[Pelagic Spawner]]; each female produces an average of about 3,000 semi-buoyant, non-adhesive eggs per spawning event. Eggs hatch in roughly 24 hours at 30°C and about 50 hours at 20°C.
- **Lifespan:** Most individuals live only one year, so a successful annual spawn is key to survival.
- **Diet:** A generalized forager consuming suspended plankton, algae, diatoms and detritus.
- **Habitat preferences:** Adults are common in shallow, braided runs over sand; young-of-year occupy shallow, low-velocity backwaters with sand-silt substrates. Depths of under 20 cm to about 40 cm and velocities of 10 to 30 cm/s are typical.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical range and decline.** The species was historically one of the most abundant and widespread fishes in the [[Rio Grande Basin]], from Espanola, NM, to the Gulf of Mexico, and also occurred in the [[Pecos River]]. It is now limited to roughly 5 percent (the document states "only 5 percent" in one place and "less than five percent" in another) of its historic range. Decline is linked to:
- Dam construction beginning with the Elephant Butte Dam closure in 1916 and five major mainstem dams.
- Flow regime alteration, water diversion for agriculture, channelization, and reduced water quality.
- Competition, hybridization concerns, and predation by non-native fishes, notably the [[Plains Minnow]].

**Drying and flow.** Prolonged low or no flow is the most serious threat. Isolated pools receiving no surface inflow can become unsuitable within hours; pools persisting beyond about 48 hours showed large losses. The [[Low Flow Conveyance Channel]] (LFCC) drains river water toward Elephant Butte during periods of low flow, and the San Acacia reach can experience prolonged low or no flow. Irrigation season (about March 1 to October 1) strands fish in diversion channels.

**Habitat modification.** Channelization, levees, and bank stabilization have straightened reaches, increased current velocity and reduced habitat complexity. Diversion dams (Angostura, Isleta, San Acacia) block upstream recolonization.

**Hydrologic reliance.** Spring runoff triggers spawning, and downstream drift of eggs and larvae (216 to 359 km, or 134 to 223 mi, per the text) matters for recruitment.

**Tolerance.** The species evolved in a highly variable ecosystem and is thought more tolerant of elevated temperatures and low dissolved oxygen for short periods than many non-native species.

## Key Findings

- **Current range:** The silvery minnow presently occurs only in the middle Rio Grande between Cochiti Dam, Sandoval County, and the headwaters of Elephant Butte Reservoir, Sierra County, NM.
- **Proposed critical habitat:** Designation covers the middle Rio Grande (Cochiti Dam to Elephant Butte Dam), bounded by levees or 91.4 m (300 ft) of riparian zone on each side of the bankfull width.
- **Reach-by-reach analysis:** The Service identified 12 reaches in the middle Rio Grande as essential and proposed them for designation. Reaches in the middle Pecos River and lower Rio Grande (from Big Bend National Park to the Terrell/Val Verde County line) were found essential but **not** proposed, because of the 4(b)(2) exclusion analysis and the planned experimental population. Other reaches (e.g., below Amistad Dam, below Falcon Dam, the Isleta–Angostura area) were judged not essential.
- **Conservation strategy:** Reestablishing the species in historic range outside the middle Rio Grande, under Section 10(j), is the preferred approach. The Service argues that critical habitat would reduce management flexibility for an experimental population.
- **Propagation and augmentation:** The Recovery Plan led to a captive propagation program housed at five facilities. Roughly 220,000 larvae and adults were released into the middle Rio Grande from May 1996 onward.
- **Economic and regulatory effects:** The draft economic analysis examined small-business impacts, with municipal wastewater discharges identified as the main activity likely affected by consultation. The rule would mainly affect actions with a federal nexus, and the Service certified it would not have a significant effect on a substantial number of small entities.
- **Public process:** Public hearings were scheduled for June 25, 2002, in Socorro, NM, and June 26, 2002, in Albuquerque, NM. Comments were due September 4, 2002, and the comment period was later extended to October 2, 2002.
- **Tribal consultation:** Several Indian pueblos (Cochiti, Santo Domingo, San Felipe, Santa Ana, Sandia, and Isleta) hold land within the proposed unit; the Service committed to government-to-government consultation.
- **Related correspondence (not part of the rule):** Appended items include a September 2002 FWS email on razorback sucker larvae captured in the Gunnison River, and a 2002 cienega video script describing [[Gila Chub]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Desert Pupfish]] in southwestern cienega habitats and the threat posed by non-native species.
