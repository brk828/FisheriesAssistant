# Zebra Cichlid Invasion of Morelos Rivers and Rock Replicas for Dry Terraria

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This German-language magazine issue, translated from English, contains two articles. The first is a conservation report by Topiltzin Contreras-MacBeath, of the Ichthyological Institute at the Biological Research Centre of the Universidad de Morelos, Mexico. It documents the displacement of native cichlids by the introduced [[Zebra Cichlid]] (*Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*) in the [[Rio Amacuzac]] of [[Morelos]]. The second is a practical guide by Ernst Paulduro on building natural-looking rock formations from [[Polyurethane Hard Foam]] for [[Dry Terrarium]] use. The aquatic content is the focus of this note.

## Background & Description

The author notes that introductions of fish outside their native range often have severe effects on local fauna. The FAO-supported spread of [[Tilapia]] in the tropics is cited as a major example that many ichthyologists regard as an ecological catastrophe. The Zebra Cichlid's natural range is Central America. Its appearance in Morelos was most likely due to release by an aquarist, although this was not confirmed.

Morelos lies on the boundary between the Nearctic and Neotropical zoogeographic regions. The [[Rio Balsas Basin]] is the largest river system draining into the Pacific from southern Mexico, covering 112,320 km². The state's waters range from cold, clear mountain lakes and streams in the north, which hold species such as *Girardinichthys multiradiatus* and *Hybopsis boucardi*, to warmer, often turbid tropical rivers in the centre and south. The latter hold [[Rio Balsas Catfish]] (*Ictalurus balsanus*), [[Cichlasoma Istlanum]] (*C. istlanum*, local name Majorra criolla) and [[Platilla]] (*Astyanax fasciatus*).

Non-native species already present include [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), carp (*Cyprinus carpio*, *Ctenopharyngodon idellus*), [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (*Oreochromis mossambicus*), *C. cyanoguttatum*, and more recently *C. nigrofasciatum*. Water resources throughout Morelos are affected by aquaculture, over-extraction, industrial pollution and urban demand, pressures that intensify with population growth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Ichthyological Institute began monthly surveys of the Rio Amacuzac to study the morphology, bio-ecology and behaviour of *I. balsanus*, *C. istlanum* and *A. fasciatus*. The river section was chosen because it held the largest remaining population of *C. istlanum*.

On 8 April 1987, a single *C. nigrofasciatum* was caught while the team searched for brooding catfish. It was preserved in 10% formalin and deposited in the institute's collection. Later visits found increasing numbers of Zebra Cichlids, first as occasional catches and then as guarding and brood-caring pairs along the entire shoreline.

In April 1989, a 1000 m stretch was sampled with [[Cast Net Sampling]] over three hours. The catch was about 50 adult *C. nigrofasciatum* and only one pair of *C. istlanum*. Cast-net sampling and underwater observation at other sites found many brood-caring pairs of *C. nigrofasciatum* and very few *C. istlanum*. Large schools of tilapia, normally conspicuous there, were absent.

The authors attribute the displacement mainly to reproduction. Figure 2 in the original shows that *C. nigrofasciatum* reaches sexual maturity much earlier than *C. istlanum*. A pair in a 20-litre aquarium bred every 26 to 28 days over several months, indicating a high reproductive rate and a small space requirement. *C. istlanum* follows a typical annual breeding cycle. The authors also note that *C. istlanum* is subject to fishing pressure, which the Zebra Cichlid does not face. The local population, which fishes the rivers and nearby ponds for subsistence, is therefore losing a food source as well as native biodiversity.

Support came from the state agency SEDUE (Amt für Stadtentwicklung und Ökologie, Morelos). The team is studying the species' diet, life cycle, reproduction and environmental requirements, and is surveying waters of the Federal District to define its range. The stated goal is to predict the rate and future spread of displacement and to provide a scientific basis for control.

## Key Findings

- **Invasion of native cichlids:** *C. nigrofasciatum* has largely replaced *C. istlanum* in the Rio Amacuzac, with about 50 adults versus one pair in a 1000 m sample (April 1989).
- **Pattern is widespread:** Brood-caring Zebra Cichlid pairs were found at every site surveyed, with few *C. istlanum* observed.
- **Likely mechanism:** Earlier sexual maturity and repeated breeding (every 26 to 28 days in captivity) give *C. nigrofasciatum* a reproductive advantage over *C. istlanum*'s annual cycle.
- **Initial introduction:** The first specimen was taken in 1987, and the authors suspect release by an aquarist.
- **Fishing asymmetry:** Subsistence fishing targets *C. istlanum* but not the Zebra Cichlid, which increases the native species' disadvantage and reduces food for rural families.
- **Management gap:** The authors say the state agency has not yet recognised the true extent of the threat. They call for funding to intensify research on diet, reproduction and range.
- **Broader risk:** If the species establishes itself permanently in the Rio Balsas system, Mexico's largest Pacific drainage, the consequences could extend well beyond a local problem.

**Second article (terraria):** Paulduro recommends PUR hard foam, preferably pre-foamed and CFC-free, as the lightest and most workable base for artificial rock formations. The article covers shaping, surface texturing and the need for a dust mask and extraction when working with the material.
