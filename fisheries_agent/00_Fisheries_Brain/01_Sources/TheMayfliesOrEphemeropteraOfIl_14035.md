# The Mayflies (Ephemeroptera) of Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ephemeroptera

## Executive Summary

This 1953 Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Volume 26, Article 1) by B. D. Burks is a taxonomic monograph and practical guide to the mayflies of Illinois. It covers the biology of the order, its value as [[Fish Food]], habitat associations of Illinois species, life history and emergence patterns, dispersal, rearing and collecting methods, and a family-level key with species descriptions. The study reports 126 Illinois species, 15 of them new, within 48 genera and 222 species treated for North America north of Mexico. The bulletin is primarily an invertebrate taxonomy work; fish are discussed only as predators of mayflies.

## Background & Description

Mayflies (order Ephemeroptera) are aquatic as nymphs and aerial as adults. Nymphs have a distinct head, thorax and abdomen, three pairs of legs, paired gills on the middle abdominal segments, and two or three caudal filaments. Mayflies are unusual among insects in molting after acquiring functional wings: the first winged stage, the subimago, molts into the true adult (imago) in most species. Adults have vestigial mouthparts and digestive systems and do not feed, living only hours to a few days.

The author notes that more than 550 mayfly species are known from North America north of Mexico. Benjamin D. Walsh, Illinois's first State Entomologist, studied mayflies around Rock Island in the 1860s and recorded 31 species there. Burks, rediscovering that fauna in the 1940s, recovered only 8 of those 31 species at Rock Island, a figure the foreword puts at about 26 per cent of the original fauna, reflecting dredging, damming, siltation and pollution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food web role.** Mayflies convert microscopic food and vegetable detritus into larger prey. Mayflies appear in the stomachs of most large Illinois fishes, including all sport fishes such as [[Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and sunfish. During heavy emergence, fish of many species may feed chiefly on mayflies. Adults and nymphs are also eaten by birds, amphibians, spiders and predaceous insects. A New Zealand case is cited: introduced [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] reduced once-abundant mayfly faunas to near extinction within about 20 years of introduction.

**Habitat associations.** Burks lists twelve habitat types with characteristic species:
- Large, slow rivers (Mississippi, Ohio, Illinois): *Hexagenia bilineata*, *H. limbata*, *Pentagenia vittigera*, *Potamanthus* spp., *Ephoron* spp.
- Moderate, fairly rapid rivers (Kankakee, Rock): *Hexagenia rigida*, *Ephemerella* spp., *Baetisca bajkovi*, *Stenonema* spp.
- Small, fast streams (Salt Fork River, Lusk Creek): *Isonychia* spp., *Baetis* spp., *Oligoneuria ammophila*.
- Silty, sluggish streams of central Illinois: *Stenonema tripunctatum*.
- Spring-fed brooks: *Paraleptophlebia* spp., *Baetis vagans*.
- Ponds in woods (Lake, Cook, Du Page counties): *Callibaetis* spp.
- Temporary ponds, pools and backwaters: *Leptophlebia* spp., *Caenis* spp., *Tricorythodes* spp.
- Large lakes: *Ephemera simulans* (Lake Michigan).
- Small glacial lakes (Lake County): *Ephemerella lutulenta* and *E. temporalis*.

Mayfly presence can indicate water condition and suitability for domestic or industrial use. Impoundments support mayfly flights that reflect their character: some produce only *Caenis simulans*, others *Hexagenia* species.

**Life history.** Nymphal development ranges from 5–6 weeks in *Callibaetis* to about 2 years in *Hexagenia*. Many species show overlapping broods, and some Baetis have two or three emergence peaks per season. Subimago stages last 1–1½ days, and relative humidity is the most critical factor for successful subimago-to-adult transition. Eggs of some genera (*Ameletus*, *Siphlonurus*) survive summer desiccation and hatch the following spring.

**Dispersal.** Mating and oviposition usually occur in the natal water body, but fertilized females can fly several miles. Burks documents *Hexagenia bilineata* colonizing Lake Glendale, an impoundment in Pope County, from the Ohio River about 12 air-line miles away.

**Nuisance events.** Mass emergences can clog radiators, sewers and traffic, as in a 1940 Sterling, Illinois, event where insects piled up to four feet on a bridge.

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** 126 Illinois species recorded, 15 described as new; 48 genera and 222 species considered across North America north of Mexico.
- **Walsh comparison:** Only 8 of the 31 species Walsh recorded at Rock Island were recovered there, though 27 of 31 were found elsewhere by searching other localities.
- **Habitat types:** Twelve Illinois habitat categories, each with characteristic mayfly species.
- **Largest Illinois mayflies:** The family Ephemeridae (*Hexagenia*, *Pentagenia*, *Ephemera*, *Potamanthus*, *Ephoron*) includes the most abundant large species, which emerge in enormous numbers from larger rivers and lakes.
- **Commonest Illinois species:** *Hexagenia* is cited as the most common Illinois genus and the most important mayfly food for fishes in large lakes and streams.
- **Emergence timing:** *Hexagenia bilineata* peaks mid-July; *H. limbata* peaks late June–July; *H. rigida* peaks in June; *Callibaetis* emerges April–October with a late May/early June peak.
- **Life history:** Callibaetis nymphs mature in 5–6 weeks; *Baetis vagans* has 27 instars and a 6–9 month nymphal life; *Hexagenia* requires 2 years.
- **Subimago water loss:** Subimagoes lose about 25 per cent of body weight during the subimaginal stage.
- **Rearing:** Recommended rearing pans are about 10 inches in diameter and 4 inches deep; reared adults are kept with a green leaf in a loosely capped vial to regulate humidity.
- **Preservation:** Adults are best preserved dry on pins; nymphs and exuviae in 70 per cent ethyl alcohol. Formalin, acetic acid and glycerin are discouraged. Walsh specimens preserved dry for more than 80 years still retained most color characters.
- **Taxonomic framework:** The author adopts a 10-family classification, placing Neoephemeridae as a new family; he identifies at least 11 distinct evolutionary lines within the order.
- **Specific taxonomic actions:** *Potamanthus flaveola* is treated as a synonym of *P. verticis*; *Ephemerella semiflava* as a synonym of *E. excrucians*; *Ephemerella concinnata* as a synonym of *E. aurivillii*.
- **Key references cited:** Traver (1935a) as the standard North American keys; Needham et al. (1935) *The Biology of Mayflies*; McDunnough's Caenis revision (1931); Eaton's 1883–1888 monograph.
