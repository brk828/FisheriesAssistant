# Razorback Sucker Reintroduction Monitoring: Lower Colorado River, February to October 1987

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of monthly progress reports (February through November 1987) from a [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) reintroduction and monitoring study on the lower [[Colorado River]], prepared by the California Department of Fish and Game and written by Razorback Sucker consultant Dan Langhorst. The project stocked hatchery-reared juveniles and fry into backwaters and ponds, then used trammel nets, minnow traps, electrofishing, spotlight-based larval surveys, and stomach analyses to assess predation, dispersal, and survival. Stocking success was poor in several releases, and the main threats identified were non-native predators and habitat conditions in backwaters.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid endemic to the Colorado River basin. In this study, the target fish were juveniles reared at the Mitchell/Wiland Native Fish Ponds and the Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond, and fry supplied by [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico. Juveniles were typically 4 to 12 inches in total length and in good physical condition at stocking. Fry were described as photo-tactic for roughly the first three weeks of life, which allowed them to be monitored with a hand-held spotlight ([[Spotlight Survey]] or Q-beam survey).

Field crews were coordinated under [[California Department of Fish and Game]] and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Parker, Arizona) staff, with work hours logged at 40 per week.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predators:** Non-native fish predation was identified as a major cause of native southwestern fish declines. Stomach analyses found predators such as [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Redear Sunfish]] in backwaters, and several contained razorback suckers or larval fish.
- **Water levels:** Backwater habitats fluctuated daily (about 1 to 2 meters at Goose Flats), and low river flows in March reduced some Parker Division sites to shallow (under 10 cm) depressions or dry flats.
- **Habitat:** Sites were evaluated for size, cover, depth, substrate, and predator presence. Suitable backwaters were generally sandy with bulrush, cattail, or phragmites margins, and some held dense aquatic vegetation (Myriophyllum, Najas, Potamogeton) that provided cover but could limit electrofishing.
- **Disease and mortality:** Juveniles reared at the Mitchell/Wiland ponds suffered a heavy outbreak of anchorworm (*Lernaea* sp.) in October and November 1987, and mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) were suspected as a vector. Herbicide (Roundup) applied to a golf course pond in July 1987 also caused a fish kill of more than 400 razorback suckers.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and release operations**
- Approximately 60 juveniles (4 to 12 inches) were removed from two ponds in February 1987 and stocked at Senator Wash Reservoir; about 5,000 fry were stocked in March 1986 (prior year).
- Fry from Dexter National Fish Hatchery were stocked in backwater sites beginning March 31, 1987.
- Juveniles were stocked in May, June, August, September, October, and November 1987 at sites including Goose Flats Backwater, Colorado River backwaters near River Mile 167 (Arizona and California sides), and Oxbow Pond (River Mile 102).
- Stocking totals were recorded on several dates, including 5,088, 5,098, 90,736, 24,064, 26,928, and 151,904 fry (per the June table, which lists total numbers stocked by location, plus a separate 9,200 stocked in August and 4,015 juveniles stocked November 17 at Goose Flats).

**Predator control and site selection**
- Lake La Paz (about 1 acre, CRIT land near Ehrenberg, Arizona) was treated with rotenone on March 15, 1987, to remove predators before use as a rearing facility; it was fertilized with alfalfa pellets and phosphorus in March.
- Netting in February and March found predators at multiple sites; Goose Flats and Parker backwaters were recommended as suitable, with depths exceeding 2 meters in some Parker sites.
- The Blythe Municipal Golf Course Pond held roughly 1,000 juvenile razorbacks in late 1987 but produced low capture numbers with minnow traps; an Oneida net proved more effective.

**Larval and juvenile movement**
- Q-beam spotlight surveys showed larval counts decreasing with distance from stocking sites and declining sharply within about two weeks after stocking. Possible causes were predation, transport out of the stocking site, or fish outgrowing their photo-tactic behavior.
- Trammel net data at Goose Flats (May and June) showed nocturnal movement, with most razorback suckers captured between 0400 and 0600 hours and near the shoreline.
- In a stomach analysis of 428 larval predators (channel catfish, largemouth bass, and others), larval fish occurred in 10 stomachs; bluegill contained the most larval fish (8 of 157, 51%), with an average of 4.75 larvae per stomach.

**Survival and recapture**
- Electrofishing at Goose Flats on November 18, 24, and December 1, 1987 (two weeks after stocking 4,015 fish) recovered decreasing numbers of razorbacks. Capture success fell from 3% of stocked fish one day after stocking, indicating dispersal or predation as likely causes.
- Razorback suckers were not captured at multiple other sites, including Oxbow Pond, Parker backwaters, and Colorado River main channel sections surveyed in fall 1987.
- Fall electrofishing of the main Colorado River (sections between River Miles 103 and 174) found no razorbacks.

**Conclusion**
Results point to non-native predators, poor retention in backwaters, and disease as the main obstacles to establishing reintroduced razorback suckers. Recommended next steps included continued predator netting, use of predator-exclusion netting, and further stomach analysis for confirmation of larval identification.
