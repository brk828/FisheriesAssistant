# Aravaipa Canyon: A Desert Stream's Hidden Sandstone Corridor

#ichthyology #marine-biology #minnows

## Executive Summary

This 1965 *Arizona Highways* travel narrative describes a multi-day horseback trip through the middle section of [[Aravaipa Creek]] in [[Aravaipa Canyon]], a remote sandstone canyon in the [[Galiuro Mountains]] of southern Arizona. The author, a scenery photographer, and companion Ray Inglish follow the perennial stream through a secluded "inner canyon" (the "box") between ranch country at either end. The piece is primarily descriptive and historical rather than scientific. Its aquatic content is limited to a brief note on small fish, but it documents the stream's riparian vegetation, its role in supporting ranching, the threat of flash floods, and the history of the 1871 [[Camp Grant Massacre]].

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Aravaipa Creek runs nearly 60 miles northwest from the [[Pinaleno Mountains]] to the [[San Pedro River]]. The author focuses on the middle 25-mile stretch, the only section where the water is above ground. Elsewhere the creek is described as preferring to begin and end "in hidden sanctuaries in the sand."
- **Three zones:** The course consists of the upper canyon (open ranch country), the lower canyon (open ranch country, reached about ten miles north of Mammoth via Arizona 77), and the inner canyon between them, described as secluded, spectacular, and rugged.
- **Route and access:** The lower canyon is reached by a gravel road from the highway, about 15 miles from the highway to the forks at the [[Wood Brothers' Panorama Ranch]] and the [[Painted Cave Ranch]]. Access to the inner canyon requires passing through one of these privately owned properties, and the author stresses that permission must be requested.
- **Trip length:** The horseback round trip through the box took two days. The distance from the Painted Cave Ranch to the upper canyon is given as about twelve rugged miles.
- **Name origin:** "Aravaipa" is said to derive from a Nevome Pima word, *arrivapa*, meaning "girls," later applied to a local tribe.
- **Ranching history:** Ranches in the lower and upper canyon, including those of the Woods, Joe and Gussie Fleiger, and [[Lupe Salazar]], are described as families who have lived in the canyon for generations, with Lupe having lived on the upper Aravaipa all his life.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish and aquatic life**
- The narrative notes "myriads of small fish" in the clear water, ranging from minnow size to four or five inches in length.
- A rancher told the author that a government survey several years earlier had found at least seven varieties in the canyon. This is secondhand and no species names, counts, or survey details are provided.
- The author attributes the small adult size to shallow water and high summer temperatures. This is presented as a probable explanation, not a tested finding.
- The stream is described as shallow and pebble-bottomed in places, with deeper pools and faster stretches inside the box. Water is continually crossed on horseback.

**Riparian and desert vegetation**
- Cottonwoods, sycamores, and occasional walnut trees line the creek, reaching "giant proportions" because of the unfailing water supply.
- Upland desert vegetation includes saguaros, prickly pears, barrel cacti, chollas, agaves, palo verdes, mesquites, and ocotillos. Cacti cling to cliff faces and add color to the canyon walls in spring.

**Wildlife**
- Mentioned fauna include horses, cattle, coyotes, javelinas (wild hogs), and an unspecified canyon creature visiting the stream at dusk. A hawk is observed high above the canyon.

**Hydrology and hazards**
- Walls of the canyon rise 700 feet or more above the floor at the junction with [[Virgus Canyon]].
- Flash floods are a recurring threat. Driftwood from past floods was lodged in crevices nearly thirty feet above the trail, and a house trailer was swept away and smashed about two years before the trip.
- Intermittent falls, dry in normal conditions, occur at [[Horse Camp Canyon]] and are carved by periodic flows.
- A small spring at the base of a cliff sustains a pool in Horse Camp Canyon.

**Geology**
- Sandstone bluffs, pinnacles, and colorful cliffs (browns, beige, pink, rust, and reddish-brown) define the inner canyon. Walls near the upper canyon are described as softer sandstone, pitted with water-erosion holes.

## Key Findings

1. **The inner canyon is a remote, scenic perennial stream corridor.** The author presents it as a little-known scenic resource, contrasted with better-known Arizona attractions like the [[Grand Canyon]] and [[Monument Valley]].
2. **The stream supports a small fish community that is not identified.** The only figure is a rancher's report of at least seven varieties from an earlier government survey. The author's explanation for the small fish size is shallow water and high summer temperatures.
3. **Water availability sustains extensive riparian woodland and ranching.** Cottonwoods, sycamores, and walnuts thrive, and several ranches depend on the creek.
4. **Flash flooding is a significant hazard.** Historical flood debris and a recent trailer loss illustrate the risk to people and property.
5. **The canyon carries significant human history.** Lupe Salazar's family, the Fleigers' repurposed Painted Cave, and the Apache history of the lower canyon are all described.
6. **Camp Grant Massacre (1871).** In April 1871, a group of forty citizens with about 100 Papagos attacked the sleeping Aravaipa Apache at the Camp Grant Reservation, killing 85 men, women, and children and taking thirty captive. The men responsible were later acquitted.
7. **Access requires permission and passage through private land.** The author emphasizes that the inner canyon is reachable only through the Panorama Ranch or the Painted Cave Ranch and that landowner permission is required.

**Note on scope:** The article is a 1965 travel and photography piece. It provides no quantitative fish survey data, no species identifications, no water chemistry measurements, and no formal ecological study. Statements about fish variety and size are anecdotal or speculative and should be treated accordingly.
