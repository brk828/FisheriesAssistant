## Executive Summary
This document is a USFWS (Pinetop Fishery Assistance Office) research and sampling plan, part of the **Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) Phase II** program, designed to characterize habitat use by the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and other native fishes in the Little Colorado River (LCR), a major tributary of the Colorado River in Grand Canyon. Rather than presenting new morphological or population data, the paper outlines a coordinated, multi-agency (USFWS, Arizona State University [ASU], Arizona Game & Fish [AGF], BioWest) field methodology to map microhabitat and macrohabitat availability, assess seasonal habitat use, identify spawning habitat, and model the effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] discharge regimes on habitat availability in the LCR. The plan divides the LCR into distinct study reaches (Confluence, Lower Base Camp, Upper Base Camp, Atomizer Falls, and Atomizer Falls–Blue Springs) and specifies standardized, low-impact sampling protocols (mini-hoopnets, minnow traps, trammel nets, seining) intended to minimize disturbance to this endangered species while generating quantitative habitat-use and habitat-selection data.

## Background & Description
No morphological data on *Gila cypha* or associated native fishes are presented in this source; the document is a methodological/study-design paper focused on habitat assessment protocols rather than anatomical description. For morphological context, see [[Gila Cypha]] species accounts and comparative notes on [[Gila Chub]] and other [[Cyprinidae]] of the Colorado River Basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **System**: [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), a tributary of the mainstem [[Colorado River]] within Grand Canyon, downstream of [[Glen Canyon Dam]].
- **Key habitat types identified**: pools, raceways, tailwaters, riffles, travertine reefs/dams, shallow edge habitats, and backwaters — collectively forming a mosaic of microhabitats used differentially by native fish life stages.
- **Confluence Zone (LCR-Colorado River, km -0.5 to 1)**: A critical seasonal congregation area; large numbers of [[Humpback Chub]] aggregate here in spring before ascending the LCR, likely for [[Spawning Migration|spawning migration]] purposes. Physicochemical conditions here are directly influenced by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] release regimes.
- **Lower Base Camp reach (0–7 km)**: Contains representative macrohabitats (pools, raceways, tailwaters, riffles, travertine reefs/dams) used for baseline microhabitat-use assessment.
- **Upper Base Camp reach (km 8–21)**: Subdivided into three ecologically distinct zones:
  - **Sipapu to Atomizer Falls (km 8–14.5)** — primary upper-river sampling and hoopnet focus.
  - **Atomizer Falls Reach (km 14–15.5)** — treated as the upstream terminus of Humpback Chub distribution in the LCR; falls likely act as a natural upstream barrier.
  - **Atomizer Falls to Blue Springs (km 15.5–21)** — perennially flowing reach above Atomizer Falls, sampled for comparative fish community/habitat data.
- **Discharge dependency**: Habitat availability and physicochemical conditions in the LCR and confluence area are hypothesized to be strongly modulated by both LCR discharge (e.g., spring snowmelt pulses) and Glen Canyon Dam-regulated flows on the mainstem Colorado River, motivating the river-modeling component of the study (led by co-investigator Dr. Vicente Lopes, University of Arizona).
- **Species interactions/context**: Study explicitly considers "other native fishes" alongside Humpback Chub, implying a shared habitat template among LCR native fish assemblages, though specific taxa are not enumerated in this excerpt. See [[Colorado River Native Fish Assemblage]].
- **Conservation status driver**: *Gila cypha* is identified as an **endangered species** with strict incidental mortality limits, directly shaping the choice of low-impact, low-mortality sampling gear (e.g., avoidance of electroshocking due to high water conductivity; caution with trammel nets due to entanglement-related mortality risk).

## Key Findings

### Research Objectives
1. Quantify habitat availability via systematic mapping of the LCR.
2. Determine seasonal patterns of habitat use by juvenile and adult native fishes.
3. Identify [[Humpback Chub]] spawning habitat within the LCR.
4. Model predicted effects of seasonal/intermittent high discharge events on habitat availability (in collaboration with river-modeling co-investigator).
5. Evaluate feasibility of establishing a **second spawning aggregation** of Humpback Chub as a conservation/redundancy measure.

### Study Design and Spatial Structure
- The LCR (km -0.5 to 21) was fully mapped at a **1 × 100 m grid resolution** by end of August 1991.
- Finer-scale **1 × 20 m grid mapping** completed for the 0–4 km (Lower Base Camp) and 9.75–12 km (Upper Base Camp) reaches.
- Exclusive 500 m study areas (with 100 m buffer zones) established at Lower and Upper Base Camps to avoid duplicated/interfering sampling among research teams (USFWS, ASU, AGF).
- Smaller 200–300 m study areas designated at Atomizer Falls due to the site's function as a distributional boundary for Humpback Chub.
- A planned **geographic survey at 5–10 m resolution** (GCES + National Park Service, February 1992) was intended to support overlay of microhabitat transects, fish sampling gear locations, and radio-tracking data.

### Sampling Methodology
- **Primary gear**: 20″ × 4′ mini-hoopnets (1/4″ mesh), deployed in regular grids (10–20 m spacing) within study reaches; set/run every 12 hours, moved upstream every 24 hours to avoid repeated disturbance at a single site (no site sampled >1 day/month).
- **Secondary gear**: Gee's-style 1/4″ mesh minnow traps (grids of ≥8 traps, 1–2 m spacing) targeting shallow edge habitats, run simultaneously with hoopnet grids.
- **Trammel nets** (deployed by ASU): used for larger fish and point-of-capture habitat data, but noted as size-selective, physically damaging (entanglement trauma), and posing incidental mortality risk to Humpback Chub — an endangered-species conservation concern.
- **Seining**: limited utility in LCR due to travertine/boulder substrate; effective mainly for small and young-of-year fish in shallow/edge habitats; conducted quarterly with AGF.
- **Electroshocking**: explicitly ruled out as ineffective due to high water conductivity in the LCR.
- **Radio telemetry**: BioWest personnel tracked radio-tagged chub into the confluence zone, with USFWS providing concurrent microhabitat-use data collection.
- Habitat variables were measured *before* net deployment (pre-set conditions) to allow comparison of used vs. available habitat and infer habitat selection/preference.

### Temporal Sampling Schedule
- Confluence zone: quarterly permanent-transect measurements (winter, spring, summer, fall), plus opportunistic sampling during high-flow/snowmelt events; baseline data collected at summer low flow (4,000 cfs Colorado River; 200 cfs LCR base flow) with trammel net fish-habitat data from 21 July–24 August 1991.
- Base Camp reaches: monthly sampling (April–October), bimonthly in winter (November–March); seining/observational surveys conducted quarterly.
- Atomizer Falls and Atomizer Falls–Blue Springs reaches: one week per quarter, with supplemental sampling during peak spring chub migration/spawning.

### Interagency Coordination Framework
- "Exclusive" study areas concept: only one agency samples a given area per month, reducing cumulative disturbance to fish populations while ensuring full spatial coverage of the LCR across USFWS, ASU, and AGF efforts.
- Data-sharing agreement: mark/recapture data from USFWS exclusive study areas shared with ASU and AGF.
- Reduced-frequency habitat measurement was also planned around **ASU hoopnet sets** outside USFWS exclusive areas, using standardized idealized conditions (1/4″ mesh preferred, paired net sets sampling different habitat types, 12-hr soak intervals, ≤1 week/month sampling frequency at a given site) to support macrohabitat-use inference, albeit without paired habitat-availability data for selection/preference analysis.

### Conservation/Management Rationale
- Sampling protocols were explicitly designed to minimize behavioral and demographic impacts on the fish population (short net-retention times, low sampling frequency/intensity, low-impact gear selection) to preserve natural ecology and reduce sampling bias — directly relevant to management of *Gila cypha* under endangered species protections and to broader [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations impact assessments on native [[Colorado River]] fish fauna.
