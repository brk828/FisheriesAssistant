# Sodium Amytal as an Aid in Fish Transportation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented by Alex Calhoun of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] at the 83rd Annual Meeting of the [[American Fisheries Society]] (Milwaukee, Wisconsin, September 1953), reports that the sedative [[Sodium Amytal]] (a barbiturate) allows fish to be held and transported at far higher densities. The drug was discovered by a pilot who was stocking roughly 600 high mountain lakes each year by airplane. Used at about 1/2 grain per gallon, it allowed the planting load to roughly double, with no observed losses or lasting ill effects in tests. The author notes that sodium amytal is not necessarily the best drug for the purpose and recommends wider screening of other agents.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Stocking high mountain lakes by [[Aerial Fish Planting]] was limited by the short ice-free season and by the small number of lakes that could be served per trip. Fingerlings were trucked to the plane in milk cans and concentrated into four-gallon loads, and fish began showing distress within about 30 minutes of being held in these containers.
- **Origin of the method:** The pilot consulted a druggist friend, who suggested a hypnotic barbiturate. Preliminary tests with sodium amytal were promising, and fish were held for long periods with no apparent ill effects.
- **Species tested:** [[Catchable Rainbow Trout]] and [[Fingerling Rainbow Trout]], [[Fingerling Eastern Brook Trout]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Golden Shiner]]. The drug was reported successful with all species tried so far.
- **Operational procedure:** Four gallons of water containing about three grains of sodium amytal per gallon were placed in an aluminum milk can on the plane. Roughly 120 ounces of fingerling trout were then added, about five times the maximum load used without the drug. Fish could be held much longer without distress than at lower concentrations without the drug. Container space, rather than water weight, became the limiting factor, and reducing container size was planned for the following year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dose and holding tests**

| Test (as reported) | Fish | Water volume | Drug concentration | Duration | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extreme experiment, strong aeration (museum jar) | 8 lb catchable rainbow trout | 1 gallon | 1/2 grain/gal | 8 hours | No loss |
| Milk can, strong aeration | 10 lb catchable rainbow trout | 3 gallons | 1/2 grain/gal | 4 hours | No loss; no apparent ill effects in two weeks of observation |
| General small-container tests | Catchable trout | Small containers | Not stated | 10 hours | 5 lb per gallon held without trouble |

- **Low concentrations (about 1/2 grain per gallon):** Considered the most satisfactory dose. Small trout behaved in an aquarium much like untreated fish and swam actively when released into a pond, with no ill effects.
- **High concentrations (about 5 grains per gallon):** Tended to immobilize trout and eventually kill them. In one test, 8 lb per gallon in a 5-grain solution for seven hours caused considerable distress, though the fish recovered after release into a pond.
- **Long-term observation:** Test fish were held for several months with no apparent ill effects.
- **Temperature:** Early fingerling tests ran at about 47 to 53°F. The author concludes that water temperatures of 50°F or less are best for trout. The drug's effectiveness drops sharply as temperatures rise above 50°F.
- **Field results:** Observers on the ground saw normal numbers of fry swimming near the shore after each drugged plant.

**Proposed mechanisms**

- Thiouracil, a thyroid depressant, reduced oxygen consumption in stoneroller minnows by roughly 20% (value partly illegible in the scan). The author suspects sodium amytal does more than this, and proposes that lowered metabolism and reduced excretion may explain the effect.
- Ammonia and other excretory products accumulate in the water and impair trout. At 1 part per million ammonia, blood oxygen falls to about one-seventh of normal and blood carbon dioxide rises, causing suffocation.
- A 13°F rise in water temperature (47°F to 60°F) may increase excretion tenfold, which fits the observed loss of drug effectiveness at higher temperatures.

## Key Findings

1. **Higher carrying capacity:** Sodium amytal roughly doubled the normal carrying capacity of planting equipment in the field, and the plane's planting load was reportedly about five times the maximum without the drug.
2. **Dose:** About 1/2 grain per gallon was the most satisfactory concentration. Doses around 5 grains per gallon tended to stun and eventually kill trout.
3. **Holding performance:** Five pounds of fish per gallon could be held for 10 hours in small containers without trouble. Specific test results showed no losses at 8 lb per gallon for 8 hours and at 10 lb in 3 gallons for 4 hours, both with strong aeration.
4. **Survival and recovery:** No losses were recorded in the reported tests, and held fish showed no apparent ill effects over several months or two weeks of observation.
5. **Temperature limit:** The drug works best at about 50°F or below, with reduced effectiveness above that.
6. **Economics:** Planting costs per 1,000 fish were reduced, with a savings reported for that year (exact figures are illegible in the scan). The method also allowed the crew to take Sundays off for the first time.
7. **Caveats:** The author acknowledges that sodium amytal is not known to be the best option and that undesirable effects may have been overlooked during early enthusiasm. Further testing was recommended, including screening of other drugs. A preliminary test with [[Urethane]] at low concentrations also looked promising.
8. **Proposed mechanism:** Reduced metabolism and excretion, with lower ammonia build-up, are considered the likely explanation, though the author states more is involved.
