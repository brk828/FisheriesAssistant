# Arizona Wildlife Views, Mar.-Apr. 1968: Salt River Fishes, Prescott Lakes, Gila Monster, and Trout Tagging

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This bi-monthly issue of *Wildlife Views*, published by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Vol. 15, No. 2), is a popular-science magazine rather than a single research report. Its main feature is an illustrated identification key to the common fishes of the [[Salt River Reservoirs]] in central Arizona, written by [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[James E. Johnson]] of Arizona State University. Other articles cover recreational lakes near Prescott, the [[Gila Monster]] (a protected venomous lizard), a [[Lake Mohave]] trout tagging trial using a new nylon-streamer tag, and a set of shorter department notes and reader letters.

## Background & Description

**Salt River fish fauna.** Before man-made lakes were built and eastern fishes were introduced, no more than 14 native species occurred in the Salt River area, several probably rare. About 40 species have since been recorded in the reservoirs. Studies from 1963 onward documented 27 of these, and at least 24 are common enough to be seen by anglers. The reservoirs are Roosevelt, Apache, Canyon, and Saguaro lakes, which hold fish populations numbering in the tens of thousands.

**Identification key.** The key uses opposing pairs of characters, a minimum of technical terms, and an adjustable one-inch scale line beside each drawing to indicate approximate size. It proceeds through 23 numbered couplets covering catfish, shad, minnows, suckers, and centrarchids (sunfish and bass). Examples include:
- Channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*): deeply forked tail fin.
- Threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*): saw-like keel on the belly.
- Walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum*): two anal spines and long, recurved canine teeth.
- Largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*): upper jaw extends past the rear margin of the eye.
- Bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*) versus redear sunfish (*L. microlophus*): presence of a dark spot on the dorsal fin rear margin and ear-flap colour.

Readers who cannot identify a specimen are asked to bring it to the Department or a state university.

**Prescott lakes.** Prescott (founded 1864) sits at the northern edge of a large Ponderosa pine forest. Four fishing lakes lie within a few miles of the city:
- **[[Lynx Lake]]**: the most popular and most developed; a regularly stocked trout fishery, built by the Game and Fish Department in 1962. Paving of its access road was expected by June 1 of that year.
- **[[Granite Basin Lake]]**: a small impoundment with picnic, camping, and horse trails, open seasonally from about April 15.
- **[[Watson Lake]]**: warm-water species, lightly fished, with a private fee camping area.
- **[[Willow Lake]]**: part of a large city park, with boat access on Granite Dells Road.

Other sites mentioned include the Goldwater Lakes (municipal water supply, built 1933, about 35 acres, closed to fishing) and Hassayampa Lake, a former trout lake drained in 1963 whose feeder stream had become polluted through land-use changes upstream.

**Gila monster (*Heloderma suspectum*).** Written by non-game biologist [[Dick Todd]], this account describes a stocky, blunt-headed lizard with bead-like scales and a yellow, orange, or salmon-pink and black pattern. It is one of only two lizards confirmed as venomous; the other, the Mexican beaded lizard (*H. horridum*), occurs mainly in western Mexico. The largest recorded total length is 24 inches, and the fat-storing tail is one-third to one-half of body length.

**Trout tagging.** [[Wes Martin]], Regional Supervisor in Kingman, describes a new plastic tagging method (see Key Findings).

**Other notes.** Briefer items cover Region VI supervisor [[Dick Endress]] (a Purdue-trained wildlife conservationist who joined the Department in 1964), a [[Northern Pike]] state record from Lower Lake Mary, the [[Arizona Firearm Safety Course]], a bighorn sheep hunt in the Cabeza Prieta National Game Range, and reader letters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Introduced fishes and reservoir change.** Reservoir creation and stocking of eastern species raised the Salt River fauna from at most 14 species to about 40. The authors note that some introduced species are undesirable if moved to other waters, and that useful sport or food fish may be discarded when their value is unknown to anglers.
- **Drought and storms.** A severe winter storm in the Arizona high country caused problems for wildlife. Pronghorn antelope were widely affected, and javelina, quail, turkeys, deer, and elk were affected to a lesser degree. The storm was expected to fill reservoirs and produce green feed that would benefit small game, especially quail, the following fall.
- **Desert and riparian habitat.** The Gila monster inhabits desert valleys and canyons below about 4,500 feet, using mammal burrows as retreats. It depends on water and dies if deprived of it.
- **Watershed condition.** The Hassayampa Lake feeder stream had been degraded by improper upstream land use.
- **Habitat and grazing.** A reader letter argues that the open-range system is the greatest obstacle to habitat improvement in Arizona and that uncontrolled cattle reduce game habitat.

## Key Findings

**Fish identification (Minckley and Johnson).**
- The key covers about 24 commonly encountered species from the Salt River reservoirs, drawn from 27 species confirmed since 1963 out of about 40 recorded.
- It uses opposing pairs of characteristics, with selection of the best-fitting alternative leading to the next couplet.

**Prescott lakes (Schimmel).**
- Lynx Lake is the most developed and popular of the four Prescott waters.
- Lynx Lake was named after an 1863 incident in which pioneer Sam Miller was attacked by a lynx; a mining district had been formed on the creek earlier that year.

**Gila monster (Todd).**
- The species is fully protected by Arizona law and may not be possessed, purchased, or molested.
- Diet is poorly known but thought to include reptile and bird eggs, nestling birds, small rodents, rabbits, and other lizards.
- Females lay 5 to 13 eggs in moist sand; four-inch young emerge after about one month.
- One captive individual lived more than 19 years in a zoo; wild lifespan is unknown.
- Historical collecting for the live and stuffed animal trade threatened the species with extinction.

**Trout tagging (Martin).**
- Previous methods (fin clipping and metal jaw tags) were time-consuming, expensive, possibly harmful to fish, and often unreported by anglers.
- The new nylon-streamer tag costs about $2.50 per thousand, compared with $8 to $15 per thousand for other tags. It is inserted with a small gun, and cartridges hold 35 tags.
- Three Region III staff tagged 18,000 trout in two days, which the article describes as at least twice as fast as previous Department methods.
- Since the new tags were introduced in June 1967, 41,000 tagged fish have been released in Lake Mohave on the Upper Colorado River. This represents 8.3 percent of total trout stocked in 1967 and so far in 1968.
- About 410,000 fish were stocked in 1967, all from Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery, 15 miles below Hoover Dam.
- Anglers spend approximately one-quarter million dollars per year on fishing in the lake. The authors expect higher tag return rates than with earlier tags, though this was not yet measured.

**Other notable items.**
- A Lower Lake Mary northern pike record was set by five-year-old Teddy Wong (8 lb 5 oz), then broken six days later by his brother Fred Wong (8 lb 14 oz).
- More than 30,000 students have completed the Arizona Firearm Safety Course in its 12-plus years, taught by about 600 volunteer instructors.
- The Cabeza Prieta National Game Range was opened to bighorn sheep hunting for the first time, with two trophy rams taken. Unit 43B, the long-hunted Parker-to-Yuma area, produced the highest success ratio.
- Under a law effective the previous summer, eagles, hawks, and owls are protected; only starlings, ravens, crows, and English sparrows may be hunted among non-game birds.
