# Aztec Shiner (*Notropis sallei*): Systematics and Variation in Central Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Chernoff and Miller (1981, *Proceedings of the Biological Society of Washington* 94(1): 18–36) revise the taxonomy of the [[Aztec Shiner]], *Notropis sallei* (Günther), a cyprinid fish of the Mesa Central of Mexico. The species has a long and confused naming history, having been placed in no fewer than eight genera and 16 nominal taxa. Using meristic and morphometric data from multiple [[Drainage Basins]], the authors conclude that the populations represent a single, highly variable taxon, *Notropis sallei*. They also provide a redescription and discuss its provisional generic placement within *Notropis*.

## Background & Description

*Notropis sallei* is one of the southernmost representatives of American Cyprinidae. It is confined to the [[Mesa Central]] and occurs in:

- Upper tributaries of the [[Rio Lerma]], [[Rio Balsas]], and [[Rio Panuco]] drainages
- The [[Valley of Mexico]]
- The small endorheic basin of the [[Rio Grande de Morelia]]

All of these drainages drain to the Pacific except the Rio Panuco, which flows to the Gulf of Mexico. Headwater erosion by the Rio Panuco transferred Lerma basin fishes, including the Aztec shiner, to the Atlantic Slope.

Taxonomic confusion arose from inadequate study material and differing concepts of species and genera. Names proposed by [[Meek]] (1904), [[Regan]] (1906–08), [[de Buen]] (1940), and [[Alvarez]] (1970) did not resolve the number of taxa or their distributions. Several synonyms were proposed, including *Ceratichthys sallaei*, *Notropis aztecus*, *Aztecula mexicana*, *Notropis lermae*, and *Aztecula vittata*. The epithet was corrected from *sallaei* to *sallei* by [[Gilbert]] (1978), because the species honors Mr. A. Sallé.

**Morphological description (redescription, n = 212 unless noted):**

- Standard length to 80 mm
- Dorsal rays mostly 8 (205 of 212); anal rays mostly 7 (195)
- Lateral-line scales 39–64 (a range of 26 counts)
- Body-circumference scales 30–56
- Total vertebrae 34–38; precaudal vertebrae 18–21; caudal vertebrae 15–19
- Eye diameter 37–77 (mean 56) and snout length 49–84 (mean 64), in thousandths of SL
- Breeding males develop small tubercles on the head and body and pinkish red coloration on the mandibles and throat

**Diagnostic features:** small scales with apical radii only, reduced pharyngeal dentition (0,4–4,0), a short head with a blunt snout, a simple intestine with one loop, a maxilla without barbels, an ovoid urohyal ventral surface, and a tubular dermosphenotic. The last feature separates it from *Algansea*, *Gila crassicauda*, and *Temeculina*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors link the species' morphological plasticity to its environments. Populations occupy lakes, pools, and high- and low-gradient streams, and these habitats differ in water velocity. Lake populations, such as those of the [[Presa de Guadalupe]] in the Valley of Mexico, have larger eyes and are more laterally compressed. Fish from high-gradient streams of the Balsas, Lerma, and Panuco drainages are more terete and have smaller eyes. The authors cite earlier work showing that populations from fast water are more attenuate than quiet-water conspecifics, and they suggest that body attenuation may relate to temperature effects on vertebral number, as proposed by [[Stewart]] (1977).

The species is among the most variable cyprinids studied, with morphological variation comparable to or greater than other widespread minnows such as the [[Emerald Shiner]], [[Common Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Mexican Stoneroller]]. Unlike those species, *N. sallei* has a very restricted geographic range.

## Key Findings

**1. Single taxon.** The authors conclude that the Aztec shiner comprises one highly variable taxon. Diagnostic characters previously used to separate former taxa overlap broadly among populations, and no discrete clusters emerged from the analyses.

**2. Lateral-line scales.** Rio Balsas and Rio Panuco fish usually have more than 50 scales, while Valley of Mexico and Rio Lerma fish usually have fewer than 50. However, 26% of Valley of Mexico fish have more than 50 scales, so this character does not separate taxa.

**3. Body-circumference scales.** Means were 43.7 (Rio Balsas) and 44.8 (Rio Panuco) versus 38.0 (Valley of Mexico) and 38.8 (Rio Lerma). The pattern parallels the lateral-line results, and scale variation is high, with CVs of 6.4–8.7 for lateral-line scales and 7.5–10.0 for body-circumference scales.

**4. Vertebrae.** Vertebral counts are less variable than scale counts (CV 1.5–2.7 for total vertebrae). Ranges and means did not differ significantly among populations (P > .05). Total and caudal vertebrae correlate with lateral-line and body-circumference scales (r > .185, 114 df, P < .05), and caudal vertebrae correlate with caudal peduncle scales (r = .259, 114 df, P < .01).

**5. Dorsal ray count.** [[Meek]] (1904) reported nine dorsal rays for *Aztecula lermae*, but the authors consider this a misprint. They found no nine-rayed specimens among the types or Rio Lerma material. Nine rays appeared infrequently only in Valley of Mexico populations.

**6. Eye diameter and head shape.** Rio Balsas fish have smaller eye diameters, with means and 95% confidence limits that do not overlap other drainages. Their ranges, however, are almost entirely contained within those of other drainages. Eye diameter can vary by as much as 4% of SL within and among drainages. Rio Balsas fish also have longer snouts, which offsets their smaller eyes and produces relatively uniform head lengths across basins.

**7. Principal components analysis (PCA).** PC I explains 88.4% of variance (eigenvalue 13.3) and reflects size. Axis II (3.4% of variance) is positively correlated with eye diameter and negatively correlated with body width and caudal peduncle width. Together these axes form a morphocline from small-eyed, deep-bodied fish to laterally compressed, large-eyed fish, rather than discrete groups. Axis III (2.7%) relates to snout length, caudal peduncle length, and postdorsal length.

**8. Precaudal vertebrae and body width.** As body width increases, circumferential scales increase and precaudal vertebrae decrease. The authors cannot explain the vertebral decrease.

**9. Generic status (provisional).** *Notropis sallei* is provisionally kept in *Notropis* to maintain nomenclatural stability. The authors note that *N. sallei* lacks the low scale counts (under 55) once considered diagnostic of *Notropis*, along with *N. nazas* (44–57 scales). The urohyal shape of *N. sallei*, *N. amnis*, and *N. ornatus* is ovoid, which separates them from most other *Notropis*. The authors decline to infer relationships from this feature because the ovoid state may be primitive.

**10. Resolution of the epithet.** Examination of the syntypes of *Ceratichthys sallaei* showed that this name is not a synonym of *Algansea tincella* and is not part of *Algansea*. This allowed the epithet *sallei* to be retained for the species.

**Data sources.** The study examined specimens from six institutions: the British Museum of Natural History (BMNH), the Field Museum of Natural History (FMNH), Texas Cooperative Wildlife Collection (TCWC), Tulane University (TU), the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (UMMZ), and the United States National Museum (USNM). Most data come from UMMZ lots, several of which hold hundreds of specimens (for example, UMMZ 191695 with 383 and UMMZ 192373 with 369). Osteological comparisons used cleared-and-stained and skeletal material from about 30 *Notropis* and related species.
