# Courtship, Locomotion, Feeding, and Miscellaneous Behaviour of Pacific Bonito (Sarda chiliensis)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #scombridae

## Executive Summary

This document bundles three separately paginated research papers. The main, fish-focused study (Magnuson & Prescott, *Animal Behaviour* 14, 1966, pp. 54–67) describes the swimming, feeding, courtship, and miscellaneous behaviour of captive [[Pacific Bonito]] ([[Sarda Chiliensis]]), a pelagic schooling [[Scombridae|scombrid]], held at [[Marineland Of The Pacific]] in [[Palos Verdes]], California. The authors report the first documented courtship sequence for any scombrid, a multi-step pairing and gamete-release sequence, and nine miscellaneous behaviour patterns they interpret as possibly linked to olfaction, gill ventilation, drinking, social signalling, and digestion rather than "comfort."

The other two items are not fish studies: a short summary and reference list from a reversal-learning experiment on six greater hill myna birds (Gossette et al., page 2 running header), and a study of turn alternation in the pill bug *Armadillidium vulgare* (Kupfermann, *Animal Behaviour* 14, 1966, pp. 68–72). Both are summarized below for completeness.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** *Sarda chiliensis* (Cuvier) is a pelagic schooling scombrid. The authors note that courtship and pairing had never been described for scombrids, and that pelagic schooling fishes were often assumed to lack discrete courtship and pairing (Norman, 1948; Shaw, 1962).

**Study setting.** The fish were observed in the oval tank at Marineland of the Pacific in July 1962, May 1963, and March 1964. The tank is 33 m long and 16.3 m wide, with a depth of about 11 m at the deepest point (per the Summary). Fish were viewed through 170 windows at 1, 4, and 6.5 m depths. Seawater was pumped from a beach well at 1500–1800 l/min, with 9800 l/min recycled through filters. Temperature was 22 °C ± 1 °C. Copper ion at 0.17 ppm was used to control algae, parasites, and disease. Upper-level water circulated counterclockwise at a mean speed of 10 cm/s, with a clockwise eddy at the north-west end. Horizontal visibility exceeded 15 m.

**Specimens.** About 60 bonito were caught near Marineland in August 1961. Roughly 25 survived and became established. They fed on frozen smelt (Osmeridae). Ten were still alive in October 1964, after 38 months in captivity. Fish grew from 45–50 cm to 57 cm fork length during the first nine months. Three fish sampled in July 1962 averaged 56.8 cm (range 55.7–58.8 cm) fork length and 2.53 kg.

**Constraints.** The tank was part of a public display with more than 800 fish of over forty species. Five 15-minute diver-feeding shows were held daily. Bonito sometimes took food from divers, which interfered with controlled feeding. Observations were therefore made between shows and before the 10:00 opening.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Locomotion and schooling.** Fish swam continuously against the current at a mean speed of 88.2 cm/s and a tail-beat frequency of 1.42 beats/s when not feeding or courting. Among feeding fish, speed (body lengths/s, y) was related to tail-beat frequency (x) by y = 0.4905 + 0.6394x. During feeding, schooling broke down and individuals took on a vertically barred coloration and a yellowish middorsal stripe.

**Social context.** Individuals were identified by variable flank stripes. Courtship occurred within a school, and males often tail-nosed other schooling fish. These interactions, along with colour change, are central to the authors' interpretation of signalling.

**Reproductive state.** Gonad examination of three speared fish (two males, one female) suggested that the main spawning period had passed before the July 1962 observations. Male testes were 1.5% and 3.8% of body weight, with some free milt present. Female ovaries were 1.6% of body weight, with a modal ova diameter of 0.3 mm. A female caught at sea that week was similar, but also held forty free ova (modal diameter 1.5 mm), indicating spawning was largely complete.

## Key Findings

### 1. Courtship sequence (first description for a scombrid)

The authors describe the following patterns, with the main sequence being tail nosing, wobbling by the female, following by males, circle swimming, and gamete release:

- **[[Wobbling]]**: The female's path forms waves, with the body tilted 5–15° inward and a rapid, low-amplitude tail beat. One complete wobble lasts about 1.0 s.
- **[[Circle Swimming]]**: The pair swims in a tight circle about three body lengths in diameter, usually just below the surface and to a depth of 4 m. Gametes are released during this phase, which lasted up to 20 s.
- **Following**: A male swims in tandem behind a wobbling female. Males may show vertical barring, and tail nosing sometimes occurs.
- **[[Lateral Threat Display]]**: A male intensifies its barred coloration, extends its dorsal and pectoral fins, and banks toward a rival in a parallel position. Physical contact does not occur.
- **Fleeing and positioning**: Females flee from groups of males. Competing males swim in a dodging path to take up the following position.

The authors interpret courtship in three parts: (1) temporary pairing, (2) defence of a female or of the right to fertilize ova, and (3) a behaviour sequence leading to simultaneous, adjacent gamete release. Swimming in tandem in a small circle is suggested to increase the chance of fertilization.

### 2. Quantitative courtship frequencies (July 1962)

Summed over 520 min of 10-minute observations (12:00–18:00):

| Behaviour | Total frequency |
|---|---|
| Following (all) | 392 |
| Following by barred males | 255 |
| Following by unbarred males | 137 |
| Wobbling | 318 |
| Lateral threat plus positioning | 42 |
| Circle swimming | 31 |

Forty of the 111 ten-minute observation periods contained no courtship. Spearman rank correlations between courtship elements were positive and significant (P < 0.05) for most pairs, with the strongest association between wobbles and follows (r_s = 0.879 for all periods; 0.868 when zero-zero periods were excluded). Circle swimming and aggression were not significantly correlated (r_s = −0.016, n = 25, not significant). The authors interpret this as evidence that aggression interrupts the sequence before circle swimming can occur. Courtship often ended before circle swimming and seldom resumed without regressing.

### 3. Miscellaneous behaviour patterns

Nine additional patterns were recorded: **[[Mouth Closure]] (long)**, mouth closure (short), snap, yawn, lean, bend, jerk, quick swim, and defecation. Each is described briefly in the paper. Some were very rare (quick swim 0.2 per fish per 10 min; jerk 0.1). Mouth closure (long) was among the most common (7.6 per fish per 10 min). Patterns often occurred in rapid sequences, and 25% of July 1962 sequences contained three or more movements.

Fed versus not-fed comparisons (May 1963, n = 11 fish, Wilcoxon matched-pairs test) showed significant differences for:
- Mouth closure (long): 3.5 (fed, 0–4 h after meal) versus 11.6 (not fed, at least 12 h after meal), P < 0.05
- Bend: 2.6 versus 1.1, P < 0.05
- Yawn: 0.9 versus 0.4, P < 0.05

Other patterns did not differ significantly.

### 4. Suggested functions (hypotheses, not tested)

The authors argue that these movements should not be dismissed as "comfort movements" and propose the following possible functions:

- **Mouth closure (long)**: [[Olfaction]] (an "intermittent sniff" for food odours), [[Gill Ventilation]] (linked to oxygen demand after feeding), or both. Scombrids have accessory olfactory sacs (Gooding, 1963; Iwai & Nakamura, 1964). Less mouth closure after a meal is consistent with higher post-meal oxygen demand.
- **Yawn and snap, quick swim plus snap**: possibly water swallowing to replace osmotic loss, a drinking behaviour in marine fishes. A yawn was almost always followed by a snap.
- **Leans**: possibly a social signal that helps the school stay together by making fish more visible to each other. Leans were most common at dawn and dusk in July 1962, and also occurred at night.
- **Bends and jerks**: possibly linked to moving material through the gut. Bends were more common after a meal. The highest jerk and defecation counts (four each per 30 min) occurred 2–3 h after the morning feed. The authors call for more evidence.

### 5. Feeding and colour

When frozen smelt were tossed onto the surface, *S. chiliensis* was the first species to reach and eat them. The vertically barred colouration seen in feeding fish closely resembled the colouration of aggressive males in courtship. The authors suggest it may have a secondary signalling role, indicating food to other school members.

### 6. Other items in the document

**Myna reversal learning (Gossette et al.; running header only).** Six greater hill mynas completed nineteen successive reversals of a spatial discrimination task, with one session of twenty trials per day until ≤2 errors per session. Errors and sessions fell significantly after an initial peak on the first reversal. The mean number of consecutive errors after reversal fell from 8.5 in the first block of five reversals to 1.3 in the last. Final-reversal performance was lower than on the original discrimination. The authors suggest asymptotic myna performance might equal that of mammals, but a direct comparison was not possible because tasks differed among studies. Reference list only; the paper's opening was not included.

**[[Turn Alternation]] in the pill bug (Kupfermann).** Forty pill bugs (*Armadillidium vulgare*), collected in Ocala, Florida, were each tested with five trials at six forced-turn angles (30°, 50°, 70°, 90°, 110°). The free turn angle was a linear function of the forced angle (y = 0.62x, least squares). The effect was significant (P < 0.001), with slight deviation only at the extremes. This was a free-turn angle analysis, not a fish study. The author refers to earlier turn-alternation work in rats, paramecia, and insects.
