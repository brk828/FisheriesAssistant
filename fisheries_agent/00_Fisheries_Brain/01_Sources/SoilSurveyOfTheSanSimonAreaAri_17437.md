# Soil Survey of the San Simon Area, Arizona (1921)

#soil-science #pedology #arid-lands #non-aquatic-source

## Executive Summary

This 1921 [[Soil Survey]] by the [[Bureau Of Soils]], U.S. Department of Agriculture, in cooperation with the University of Arizona Department of Irrigation, maps the agricultural soils of the [[San Simon Valley]] in northeastern [[Cochise County]], Arizona. The surveyed area covers about 448 square miles (286,720 acres). The source addresses soils, climate, agriculture, land values, and [[Alkali Salts]] affecting irrigation. It contains no fish, invertebrate, plankton, or formal aquatic-ecosystem data. Its only water-related content concerns intermittent surface flow, groundwater, artesian wells, and a marsh, so the fish-oriented template fields are recorded as N/A.

## Background & Description

- **Location and extent:** The area lies between the Chiricahua and Dos Cabezas Mountains. Its eastern edge follows the Arizona-New Mexico line. The survey boundary was drawn to include all agricultural land that could be irrigated from underground sources or from a proposed run-off project on the eastern slopes of the Chiricahua and Dos Cabezas Mountains.
- **Topography:** The valley is described as a shallow trough formed by alluvial fans. Coarse sediment lies on steeper slopes and fine sediment spreads toward the valley axis. Agricultural land ranges from 3,500 to 4,800 feet above sea level. The mountains reach nearly 6,000 feet.
- **Drainage:** [[San Simon Creek]] is the main drainage. It rises in the [[San Simon Cienega]], a low marsh with groundwater at the surface, and flows about 35 miles to the [[Gila River]]. The creek is normally intermittent, with a small perennial flow maintained by seepage, marsh overflow, or waste water from flowing wells. Severe storms can raise it within minutes and flood adjacent bottomlands for a quarter mile or more.
- **History and settlement:** Open range was dominated by large cattle companies until [[Artesian Water]] was discovered in late 1910. Homesteaders then arrived, but many left after gaining title because dry farming was unreliable. Bowie and San Simon are the only towns. The 1920 census gave 885 inhabitants for the Bowie precinct and 526 for the San Simon precinct.
- **Infrastructure:** The [[Southern Pacific Railroad]] crosses the northern part of the area, and the Arizona Eastern Railroad branches from Bowie. Roads are poor, and telephone and electric service are limited.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate**
- San Simon station (3,609 ft) has a mean annual temperature of 61.8°F. Its highest recorded temperature is 111°F.
- Paradise station (5,436 ft, at the foot of the Chiricahua Mountains) has a mean annual temperature of 55.2°F and a mean summer temperature of 70.1°F.
- Annual precipitation is highly variable. San Simon ranges from 0.99 to 18.87 inches, and Paradise ranges from 8.29 to 26.63 inches. The text also gives a San Simon average of 6.18 inches and a Paradise average of 19.58 inches.
- The growing season is 277 days at San Simon and 172 days at Paradise. Average last spring frost at San Simon is February 22, and first fall frost is November 26.
- Summer rain falls as local thunderstorms with rapid run-off. Winter rain is gentler. Late winter and spring bring high winds and dust.

**Hydrology and groundwater**
- Artesian water is found at 400 to 600 feet in the San Simon belt. Wells are cased only at the surface and have silted, so flows in most old wells are 20 to 80 percent lower than before. New wells show larger flows than many old ones.
- Water is the main limit on crop production, and farming is concentrated where irrigation is possible.

**Soils and salinity**
- Soils are unleached and high in lime. The survey classes them as arid southwest soils.
- Soil groups are old valley-filling soils (about 85 percent of the area), recent-alluvial soils, and miscellaneous materials such as [[Rough Stony Land]] (about 1 percent) and [[Riverwash]].
- Alkali-affected land covers about 30 square miles, mainly on flat, poorly drained soils along San Simon Creek. Salt concentrations were measured with the [[Electrolytic-Bridge Method]]. Soils with less than 0.20 percent salts in the 6-foot section were considered free of injurious concentrations.
- Black alkali (sodium carbonate) is the most harmful salt. Crop growth is reduced when it exceeds about 0.08 percent with other salts. Decreased yields are generally expected above 0.20 to 0.40 percent total alkali in the 6-foot section.

## Key Findings

**Soil series and extent**
- Old valley-filling series include [[Mohave Gravelly Sandy Loam]], [[Cavot Gravelly Sandy Loam]] (the most extensive type in the area), [[Cave Gravelly Fine Sandy Loam]], [[Cochise Gravelly Sandy Loam]], [[Dos Cabezas Loam]], [[Imperial Clay]], and [[Karro Clay Loam]].
- Recent-alluvial series are the [[Gila Loam]], [[Pima Fine Sandy Loam]], and [[San Pedro Clay Loam]]. The San Pedro soils occupy the San Simon Cienega.
- The survey maps about 18 soil types, many with phases, plus Rough Stony Land and Riverwash. Mechanical analyses (particle-size distribution) are given for representative types.

**Crops and yields (as reported)**
- Sweet potatoes on Cavot soils yield 200 to 300 bushels per acre. Potatoes yield 100 to 200 bushels.
- Alfalfa on Dos Cabezas loam yields 4 to 6 tons per acre over five or six cuttings. Cavot alfalfa averages about 1 ton per cutting.
- Wheat yields 20 to 35 bushels per acre on Dos Cabezas loam and 10 to 20 bushels on Mohave soils with supplemental water.
- Milo yields 60 to 75 bushels per acre on Imperial clay. Cotton was grown for the first time on Imperial clay in 1920 and yielded one-half to three-fourths bale per acre.

**Land values (as reported)**
- Improved land ranges from $40 to $125 per acre, with the highest values on Dos Cabezas loam and Imperial clay.
- Unimproved land ranges from $8 to $30 per acre.

**Management recommendations**
- Add organic matter (barnyard manure or green-manure crops such as rye and oats) to improve water-holding capacity, which is low in most soils.
- Drain alkali-affected land. Natural deepening of San Simon Creek has helped, and tiling or deep ditches may be needed elsewhere.
- Enforce rules against the continuous flow of artesian wells, which the survey calls wasteful.
- Keep a loose surface mulch to reduce evaporation and salt accumulation.

**Limitations of the source**
- No statistics specific to the area were available for agriculture.
- The survey's own alkali classification was simplified because much land was still virgin desert.
