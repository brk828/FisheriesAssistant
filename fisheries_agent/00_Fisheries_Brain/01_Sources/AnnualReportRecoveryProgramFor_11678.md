# Recovery Program for Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin 1996 Annual Report

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

The 1996 Annual Report documents progress of the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]], a multi-agency effort to recover four endangered fish species—[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)—while allowing continued water development. Major accomplishments include completion of the [[Redlands Fish Ladder]], documentation of spawning events, discovery of newborn razorbacks, habitat acquisition, establishment of five hatchery facilities, and water flow agreements totaling over 65,000 acre-feet annually. The program enabled passage of over 7,700 native fish through the new ladder and stocked thousands of endangered fish while approving approximately 300 water projects providing 200,000+ acre-feet for human use.

## Background & Description

The [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] targets four endangered fish species endemic to the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, [[Cyprinidae]]): Large-bodied native cyprinid
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*, [[Catostomidae]]): Distinctive sucker with prominent dorsal ridge
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*, [[Cyprinidae]]): Rarest of the four endangered species
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*, [[Cyprinidae]]): Characterized by pronounced nuchal hump

The program operates through partnerships among federal agencies ([[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Western Area Power Administration]]), state wildlife agencies (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming), and stakeholder organizations including environmental groups, water users associations, and wildlife federations.

Five hatchery and grow-out facilities were established across Colorado and Utah:

1. **[[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]]**: 26 ponds (10 more planned), raises [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]
2. **[[Wahweap Endangered Fish Facility]]**: Five ponds (six more planned in 1997), maintains [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]]
3. **[[Horsethief Canyon Ponds]]**: Six ponds raising razorbacks, squawfish, bonytail, and humpback chubs
4. **[[24 Road Hatchery]]**: 36 tanks for newly hatched [[Razorback Sucker]]
5. **[[Clymer's Pond]]**: Raises razorbacks to approximately 8 inches for stocking

Fish are raised to 8-12 inches before stocking to improve survival rates. Hatchery protocols emphasize genetic diversity through breeding large numbers of parent fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The program emphasizes habitat preservation and restoration in the [[Colorado River]], [[Green River]], [[Gunnison River]], and [[Yampa River]] systems. Endangered fish utilize diverse habitat types including riffles, backwaters, pools, side channels, and flood plain wetlands.

**Habitat Acquisition and Protection:**
- 153-acre wetland parcel purchased near [[Grand Junction]], Colorado, providing diverse habitat types along the [[Colorado River]]
- 132-acre wetland at [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]], Utah, made available downstream of key [[Razorback Sucker]] spawning area

**Water Flow Agreements:**
- Up to 30,000 acre-feet annually from [[Grand Valley Irrigation Company]], [[Grand Valley Power Plant]], [[Orchard Mesa Irrigation District]], [[Grand Valley Water Users Association]], and [[Bureau of Reclamation]]
- 31,650 acre-feet from [[Ruedi Reservoir]] for [[Colorado River]] flows
- Up to 3,300 acre-feet annually from [[Steamboat Lake]] to [[Yampa River]] (which can drop to 20 cubic feet per second otherwise)

**Non-native Fish Management:**
Federal and state agencies finalized guidelines confining non-native fish stocking to areas with minimum conflict with endangered species. At [[Old Charlie Wash]] near Ouray, Utah, 45 newborn [[Razorback Sucker]] survived despite 6 tons of non-native fish (carp, fathead minnows, green sunfish, red shiners), demonstrating the protective value of vegetated flood plain habitat.

The [[Redlands Fish Ladder]], a 350-foot narrow U-shaped channel wrapping around [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] on the [[Gunnison River]] (two miles upstream of [[Colorado River]] confluence), opened 50 miles of historic range to endangered and native fish.

## Key Findings

**Spawning Documentation:**
- First documented spawning of [[Colorado Squawfish]] in upper [[Gunnison River]]
- First documented spawning of [[Colorado Squawfish]] in [[Grand Valley]] stretch of [[Colorado River]] since 1982

**Population Discoveries:**
- Newborn [[Razorback Sucker]] discovered near confluence of [[Green River]] and [[San Rafael River]], Utah, suggesting new or previously unidentified population

**Fish Passage Success:**
Between June and October 1996, the [[Redlands Fish Ladder]] enabled passage of:
- 1 endangered fish
- More than 7,700 other native fish migrating upstream in [[Gunnison River]]

**Stocking Efforts:**
- [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]]: 2,000 [[Bonytail]] stocked in [[Colorado River]]
- [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]: Over 1,000 [[Razorback Sucker]] stocked in [[Green River]], nearly 300 in [[Gunnison River]]

**Habitat Restoration Success:**
At [[Old Charlie Wash]] flood plain, 45 newborn [[Razorback Sucker]] survived alongside 6 tons of non-native fish. Program biologist Pat Nelson stated: "The fact that these razorbacks survived alongside these other fishes is, in my opinion, the most significant finding in the history of the Recovery Program." Vegetation in flood plains provided critical cover for survival.

**Water Development Balance:**
The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] approved approximately 300 water projects in Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming, providing 200,000+ acre-feet of water for human use and local economic development while advancing endangered fish recovery.

**Non-native Fish Policy:**
Agreement allows widespread stocking of trout and other non-native species in [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] while minimizing conflict with endangered fish. [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] committed to providing 40,000 catchable-size trout annually for stocking in select public ponds.

**Program Funding (1996):**
- Total budget: $10,068,800
- Largest allocation: Habitat management (58.7%, $5,914,200)
- Endangered fish stocking: 20.6% ($2,071,800)
- Research, monitoring, data management: 12.6% ($1,266,300)
- Non-native fish management: 2.0% ($204,300)
- Information and education: 1.9% ($189,500)

Primary funding sources: [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] capital funds (19.4%), [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (11.2%), State of Colorado (8.5%).
