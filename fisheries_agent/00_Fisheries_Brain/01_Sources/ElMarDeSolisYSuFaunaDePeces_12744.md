# The Mar de Solís: Paleogeography, Morphology and Oceanographic Conditions of the Río de la Plata

#ichthyology #marine-biology #oceanography

## Executive Summary

This Spanish-language monograph, the first part of a series by [[Fernando de Buen]] on the [[Mar de Solís]] and its fish fauna, treats the [[Río de la Plata]] as a declining intracontinental sea. It reconstructs the basin's geological history from the Cenozoic onward, describes its present morphology and sediments, and reports oceanographic observations from the 1937–38 cruise of the R.O.U. "Aspirante" together with 1938 tide, wind, temperature and salinity records from [[Montevideo]], [[Punta del Este]] and [[La Paloma]]. Fish are mentioned only as fossil taxa (sharks and cetacean remains) and as plankton and bottom-net sampling methods. The author states that the fish-species checklist is reserved for later contributions. The work is therefore best read as a physical and paleoenvironmental baseline for the fish habitat.

## Background & Description

The author, a natural scientist and head of the oceanographic department of the [[Dirección General de Pesca]] in Mexico, frames the work around two disciplines: [[Paleotalasografía]] (paleoceanography) and [[Paleolimnología]] (paleolimnology). He argues that these fields explain the present-day [[Talasografía]] (oceanography) and [[Limnología]] (limnology) of the estuary.

The author rejects both the name "Río de la Plata" and the term "Mar Dulce." He proposes calling the system the "Mar de Solís," a brackish, intracontinental sea. He describes it as having the biological, physical and chemical features of a large coastal lake, receiving major river inputs at the head of its continental embayment, and facing the open Atlantic. Its boundaries are geographic on the Argentine and Uruguayan coasts but purely oceanographic at the Atlantic mouth, where the balance between saline and brackish masses shifts.

Acknowledged sources include the Servicio Oceanográfico y de Pesca, the [[Museo de Historia Natural]] library, and the geologists [[Rodolfo Méndez Alzola]] and [[Joaquín Frenguelli]]. The oceanographic data came from [[Hispano V. Pérez Fontana]], who directed the meteorological and oceanographic work of the [[Aspirante Campaign]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geological framework.** The author attributes the basin to the Cenozoic rise of the [[Andes]] and to the slow subsidence of the [[Brasilia Shield]] and the [[Chaco-Pampean Plain]]. This subsidence let Atlantic waters push progressively inland during the Miocene and Pliocene. He cites fossil shark remains as evidence that the sea was once deep and highly saline. Borings in several Argentine provinces reached Tertiary strata down to more than 350 m relative to present sea level.

**Fossil fauna.** Sediments of the [[Entrerriense Transgression]] contain oysters (*Ostrea*) forming dense banks, along with *Myochlamys*, *Pododesmus*, *Amusium*, *Chione*, *Arca*, *Corbula*, *Balanus* and bryozoans. Fish-related fossils include the sharks *Odontaspis* and *Carcharias* and the whale genus *Balaenoptera*. Other remains include crustaceans, reptiles and mammals. The author notes that the warm-water fauna and dense oyster banks suggest waters warmer than today's.

**Later transgressions and regressions.** The [[Querandina Transgression]] of the Pleistocene was a modest marine advance. Regression followed, and the Mar de Solís has since been filled by sediment from the [[Río Paraná]] delta and the [[Río Uruguay]]. The author describes the basin as being in "franca senectud" (advanced senescence).

**Morphology and sediments.** The seafloor is heterogeneous. Muds accumulate in calm zones, and sands mark areas swept by waves and currents. Sediment classes follow Thoulet's scheme: sands contain less than 5% mud, sandy muds 5–50%, and muddy sands 50–90%. Muds dominate the [[Banco Ortiz]], the [[Plana del Indio]] and the [[Valle Oceánico del Este]]. Sands occur along the Uruguayan littoral between Montevideo and Punta San Gregorio.

**Hydrographic features.** The author identifies a submerged "[[Playa Honda]]" deposit, formed by sediment accumulation in the old western branch of the Uruguay–Paraná system. Other features include the [[Canal Martín García]], the Pozo de Barca Grande (about 9.5 miles long, at least 600 m wide, and at most 1,200 m wide, with depths of 6.6–8.6 m), and a delta bar at Martín García Island.

**Coastal features.** Along the Uruguayan coast, the author describes barrier and coastal lagoons such as José Ignacio, Castillo and Negra. He also describes a tombolo and enclosed beach at La Paloma, which the author compares to San Sebastián in Spain.

## Key Findings

- **Naming and status.** The author proposes "Mar de Solís" over "Río de la Plata" and calls it a brackish, intracontinental sea in decline, not a river or simple estuary.
- **Geological origin.** The basin formed through Cenozoic subsidence of the Brasilia block, with Andean uplift and Atlantic incursion from the Miocene–Pliocene. The author reports Tertiary strata at depths exceeding 350 m in Argentine boreholes.
- **Fossil evidence.** Shark (*Odontaspis*, *Carcharias*) and cetacean remains indicate deep, saline, Atlantic-influenced conditions during the Entrerriense transgression. Oyster banks suggest warmer waters than today.
- **Morphology.** The Plana del Indio occupies the basin centre, with the 10 m isobath marking its edge. The Banco Inglés and Banco Rouen separate the Seno Oceánico de Samborombón from the Valle Oceánico del Este. Sediment distribution is set by the balance of fluvial and marine energy.
- **Oceanographic campaign.** The R.O.U. "Aspirante" cruise (December 1937–February 1938) occupied 14 stations (St. I–XIV) with 186 operations. Its methods included water sampling with reversing bottles, Secchi disc readings, drifter current measurements, and plankton and bottom nets.
- **Transparency.** One Secchi reading was recorded: 3.50 m at St. I, Op. 7, on 13 December 1937 under clear skies.
- **Sea-level variation.** Wind, not astronomical tide, drives the sea-level changes of the Mar de Solís. In 1938 the amplitude was 2.76 m at Montevideo (range −0.29 to 2.47 m; mean 0.965 m) and 1.84 m at Punta del Este (range −0.25 to 1.59 m; mean 0.827 m). Sustained southerly winds raised sea level, and northerly winds lowered it.
- **Thermal anomaly.** In 1938, mean surface water at Montevideo was colder than the air (17.1 °C vs. 18.6 °C), the reverse of the pattern at Punta del Este (water 16.1 °C vs. air 15.4 °C). The author identifies a cold zone north of the Banco Inglés, which he considers a probable upwelling area linked to bottom currents.
- **Salinity.** Salinity is highly variable. Mean 1938 salinity was 8.90 at Montevideo and 24.91 at Punta del Este. The lowest monthly means at Montevideo occurred in April–May and July, and the highest in October–November. At La Paloma, a 26-hour series showed surface and 5 m salinities of 32.14–32.56, with a density inversion and a pulse of saltier, colder surface water on the morning of 23 February 1938.
- **Currents.** Currents are shaped by river discharge, winds and coastal embayments, which generate compensation circuits. The author argues that the boundary with the Atlantic is set by the balance between the two water masses, and that denser saline water can flow along the bottom beneath brackish surface layers.
- **Ecological implication.** The author argues that the sea's tidal regime has little bearing on species distribution. Instead, wind-driven level changes, salinity gradients and bottom sediment types control habitat conditions for benthic and pelagic life, including the mussel (*Mejillón*), which colonises intertidal zones exposed for long periods.
- **Stated plan.** The author intends to publish a fish-species checklist drawn from the literature in later parts of the series. This document does not include that list.
