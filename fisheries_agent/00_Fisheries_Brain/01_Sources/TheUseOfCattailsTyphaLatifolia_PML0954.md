# Largemouth Bass Habitat Use in Typha Latifolia Beds, Imperial Reservoir, Arizona, Determined by Underwater Telemetry

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Cooperative Fishery Research Unit report (Research Paper Series No. 73-2) uses [[Underwater Telemetry]] to examine how adult [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) use [[Cattails]] (*Typha latifolia*) in [[Imperial Reservoir]], Arizona. Four ultrasonically tagged bass were tracked for about four months in 1971. Bass were found in cover 98% of the time they were located. [[Typha Latifolia]] was the most used of the four cover types, accounting for 52% of tracked time. The authors argue that this figure is an underestimate, because signals were lost inside Typha beds. They conclude that Typha may be the single most important species for the success of this sport fish where it is a major shoreline constituent.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Largemouth Bass]], a [[Centrarchidae]] sport fish. Four adults (>40.5 cm) were used.
- **Study system:** [[Imperial Reservoir]], a contiguous flooded marsh with a small constant flow of water. Four study lakes (Lakes I–IV) were each less than 3 m deep.
- **Key plants:** [[Typha Latifolia]] (cattail), [[Phragmites Communis]] (reedgrass), [[Scirpus Acutus]] (three-sided bulrush), and [[Najas Marina]] (spiny naiad).
- **Prior work:** Earlier studies had established that cover and rooted aquatic plants are necessary for bass survival. [[Weaver and Zlebell 1976]] was the only prior study to stress the importance of Typha beds, and it addressed juvenile bass. The present study addresses adults.
- **Organization:** Research by the [[Arizona Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]], a partnership of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the University of Arizona, and the Arizona Department of Game and Fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study lakes**

| Lake | Approx. area | Shoreline / vegetation notes |
|------|--------------|------------------------------|
| I and II | about 3 ha each | Typha along about 40% of shoreline; mostly reedgrass with some bulrush; about 90% of lake bottom covered by spiny naiad in summer and fall; a few submerged dead trees |
| III | about 3 ha | Typha along about 30%; about 60% reedgrass, 5% bulrush, 15% bare shore; large naiad clumps |
| IV | about 1 ha, about 1 m deep | Typha about 20%; about 60% reedgrass, 20% bare; highest water flow rate of the four lakes |

**Habitat context:** Typha covers about 60% of the vegetated shoreline of Imperial Reservoir (Hallock 1973). Typha beds are the only major vegetative cover for almost two months in late winter and early spring, when naiad is mostly absent. During that period they provide cover for juveniles and spawning areas for adults.

**Telemetry limits:** Air spaces in Typha reduce ultrasonic signals, so bass were often lost when they moved deep into the beds. Transmitters were detectable at about 300 m and emitted for about six months.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Ultrasonic transmitters (15 × 44 mm), each on a different frequency, were surgically implanted in the abdominal cavities of four adult bass.
- Two bass were released into Lake I (bass C and B) and two into Lake III (bass J and S).
- Searches covered about 200 hours from early July through mid-October 1971. Bass were located with a non-directional hydrophone (general location) and a directional hydrophone (specific location).
- Only initial contacts were used to assess habitat use. A site was recorded when a fish stayed more than 10 minutes.

**Results**
- Bass were located 64 times and tracked for 67.6 hours.
- Cover habitats made up 98% of contacts.
- Initial contacts were in the Typha-Najas zone for 80% of contacts. Bass stayed in this zone for 87% of tracking time.
- Rocks and submerged trees accounted for 18% of contacts and 13% of tracking time.
- When bass were in the river channel or open water, they were always close to shore and moved quickly into cover.
- Bass were located in Typha for 52% of tracked time. Hallock's habitat-use data had Typha at 35.3% of contacts (table values are partly garbled in the source).
- Approximately 136–137 hours were spent searching without locating bass. The authors attribute most of these hours to bass in Typha, so the true Typha use is likely well above 52%.
- Bass were rarely found in adjacent lakes, although they could reach them through interconnecting channels.

**Individual fish**
- Bass B and J were located throughout all months of the study.
- Bass C was located regularly in July and August, then disappeared.
- Bass S was located only during the first few days after release.
- All bass were found in two or more areas. C, B and J remained at one area for periods up to several weeks.

**Conclusions**
- Typha is used extensively by adult largemouth bass, as earlier work found for juveniles. The authors conclude that Typha is used by all age classes and may be the most important species for this sport fish in marshy areas where Typha is a major constituent.
- The study is one of the few to use underwater telemetry to show habitat use by largemouth bass. Earlier telemetry studies (Warden and Lorio 1975; Winter 1977) did not consider Typha.
