# Development of Color Pattern in Pond-Reared Young of Five Bass Species (*Micropterus*)

## Executive Summary
This study by [[Ramsey and Smitherman 1972]] examined ontogenetic development of juvenile color patterning across six stocks representing five species/subspecies of southeastern black bass (*Micropterus*), including two geographic races of [[Micropterus coosae]] (Alabama and Apalachicola). Wild-caught adults were spawned in earthen ponds under standardized conditions, and young were reared with live minnow forage. Periodic sampling allowed tracking of pigmentation development relative to age and body size. A key outcome was the construction of an illustrated identification key based on juvenile pigmentation, and the demonstration that phenotypic color-pattern differences among parental stocks are heritable and persist even when offspring are reared under identical pond environments — supporting a genetic (rather than purely environmental) basis for juvenile coloration differences among [[Micropterus]] taxa.

## Background & Description
- **Focal trait:** Juvenile melanic (dark) pigmentation pattern development.
- **Operational definition of "juvenal" stage:** Specimens lacking complete melanic pigmentation at the posterior margin of the [[caudal fin]]. This serves as the taxonomic/ontogenetic cutoff point for classifying young bass as juveniles versus later developmental stages.
- **Species-specific pattern groupings** identified based on similarity of pigmentation ontogeny:
  - **Group 1 (most similar to each other at all life stages):** [[Micropterus salmoides]] and [[Micropterus punctulatus]] (*M. p. punctulatus*)
  - **Group 2 (most similar to each other at all life stages):** [[Micropterus coosae]] and [[Micropterus dolomieui]]
  - **Intermediate species:** [[Micropterus notius]] — pattern development positioned developmentally between the two groups above, sharing characteristics with both.
- An **illustrated dichotomous/identification key** to juvenile *Micropterus* species was constructed from these color pattern differences, intended as a practical taxonomic tool.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context:** All specimens were reared in **earthen aquaculture ponds**, a controlled semi-natural freshwater environment, rather than wild stream/river habitats.
- **Trophic conditions:** Juveniles were provided **minnow forage**, standardizing diet across all stocks to isolate genetic vs. environmental contributions to phenotype.
- **Geographic/taxonomic scope:** Encompasses multiple southeastern U.S. river drainage endemics/races, reflecting the regional diversification of *Micropterus* — notably distinguishing the Alabama and Apalachicola races of [[Micropterus coosae]], suggesting population-level (intraspecific) phenotypic divergence tied to distinct drainage basins.
- **Comparative/outgroup taxon:** Wild-caught [[Micropterus salmoides]] young were included only as a pigmentation-pattern comparison standard, not reared under the same experimental pond protocol as the other stocks.

## Key Findings
1. **Heritability of pigmentation pattern:** Phenotypic (color pattern) differences observed among wild parental stocks **persisted in offspring** even when spawned and reared under identical pond conditions — strong evidence that juvenile color pattern is under substantial genetic control rather than driven solely by environmental/rearing conditions.
2. **Two natural developmental groupings emerged:**
   - [[Micropterus salmoides]] & [[Micropterus punctulatus]] pattern developmentally allied.
   - [[Micropterus coosae]] & [[Micropterus dolomieui]] pattern developmentally allied.
3. **[[Micropterus notius]] shows an intermediate developmental trajectory**, bridging the two major pattern groups — potentially informative for phylogenetic relationships within *Micropterus*.
4. **Taxonomic utility:** The juvenile pigmentation-based identification key represents a novel practical tool for distinguishing young-of-year *Micropterus* species in the field/hatchery setting, where adult diagnostic characters are not yet developed.
5. **Intraspecific geographic variation:** Distinct Alabama and Apalachicola races of *M. coosae* were both included, implying that geographic population structure within a single species may also correspond to detectable pigmentation-pattern variation.
