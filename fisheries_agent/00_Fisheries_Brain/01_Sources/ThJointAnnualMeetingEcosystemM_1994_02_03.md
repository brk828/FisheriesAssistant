> **Note:** This source (27th Joint Annual Meeting: "Ecosystem Management: Shifting Priorities," AZ/NM TWS & AFS Chapters, 1994) does not contain fish science data. The extracted abstracts focus entirely on **amphibian and riparian herpetofauna ecology** in Arizona, primarily the [[Lowland Leopard Frog]] (*Rana yavapaiensis*) and co-occurring ranid frogs. The note below has been structured to faithfully reflect this herpetofaunal content rather than force a fish-centric framing.

## Executive Summary

Three related abstracts from Arizona Game and Fish Department's Nongame Branch (Seim, Hill, Sredl, Wallace) address the population dynamics, micro-habitat use, and statewide decline patterns of native ranid frogs, particularly the [[Lowland Leopard Frog]] (*Rana yavapaiensis*), alongside comparative data on *Rana chiricahuensis* (Chiricahua Leopard Frog), *Rana pipiens* (Northern Leopard Frog), *Rana blairi* (Plains Leopard Frog), introduced *Rana catesbeiana* (American Bullfrog), *Bufo microscaphus* (Arizona Toad), and two garter snake species (*Thamnophis eques*, *T. rufipunctatus*). Studies (1990–1993) used mark-recapture, age-stratified Jolly-Seber population modeling, and systematic riparian surveys (911 surveys, 763 sites) to characterize population instability, micro-habitat preferences, and elevation/hydrology associations, framing these within the broader context of unexplained Arizona ranid frog declines.

## Background & Description

- Metrics recorded per individual: **snout-vent length (SVL)** and **mass**, used as proxies for age-structure and body condition.
- Mean SVL and mass differed significantly by micro-habitat type at most sites (exception: Alamo Canyon), and differed between sites and between years — attributed to shifting **age-structure** in populations rather than distinct morphotypes.
- No significant difference in micro-habitat use was detected between sexes at any site.
- Notable condition finding: at [[Big Springs]], 1993 frogs had nearly identical mean SVL to 1992 but weighed **23% less**, indicating a body-condition decline independent of body-size trends — a possible early indicator of population stress.
- Sex ratio was generally 1:1, with one exception (Big Springs, 1992: female-skewed).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Species–habitat/elevation associations** (from statewide riparian herpetofauna surveys):
  - *R. yavapaiensis* ([[Lowland Leopard Frog]]): lowest elevations (mean 3,312 ft; range 1,785–5,360 ft); strong preference for **lotic** (flowing water) systems (P < 0.001).
  - *R. pipiens* (Northern Leopard Frog): highest elevations (mean 6,954 ft; range 6,220–7,475 ft); strong preference for **lentic** (still water) systems (P = 0.004).
  - *R. chiricahuensis* (Chiricahua Leopard Frog) and *R. catesbeiana* (Bullfrog): intermediate elevations (mean 5,262 ft and 4,978 ft respectively); little preference for lotic vs. lentic systems (P = 0.467 and P = 0.033).
  - *Bufo microscaphus* (Arizona Toad): intermediate elevations (mean 4,708 ft; range 2,200–8,400 ft); strong lotic preference (P < 0.001).
- **Micro-habitat partitioning** within sites (5 categories: large pool, small pool, riffle, marsh, debris):
  - Adults concentrated in **large pools** at [[Big Springs]], [[Reed Spring]], and [[Tule Creek]] — linked to reproductive activity and adult survival.
  - Juveniles concentrated in **small pools** (Big Springs, Reed Springs) and **marsh habitat** (Tule Creek) — linked to juvenile survival.
  - Elevated **debris habitat** use by both adults and juveniles at Big Springs, possibly reflecting habitat degradation from sediment deposition and water extraction reducing availability of preferred habitat types.
- No significant association found between detection of *R. chiricahuensis*, *R. pipiens*, or *R. yavapaiensis* and any of 5 land-use categories or 14 predator types — suggesting decline drivers may act at scales or via mechanisms not captured by simple co-occurrence surveys.

## Key Findings

- **Population variability:** Using age-stratified Jolly-Seber models (program JOLLYAGE), [[Lowland Leopard Frog]] population estimates fluctuated dramatically:
  - [[Big Springs]] (Graham County): 55–351 individuals (mean 220); mean capture probability 0.27 (range 0.15–0.44).
  - [[Tule Creek]] (Yavapai County): 12–1,369 individuals (mean 592); mean capture probability 0.14 (range 0.04–0.47).
  - Juvenile survival: Big Springs 0.03–1.19 (mean 0.56); Tule Creek 0–1.59 (mean 0.44).
  - Adult survival: Big Springs 0.13–1.14 (mean 0.79); Tule Creek 0.03–4.13 (mean 1.43) — implausibly high values suggest model/estimation artifacts from low capture probabilities.
  - Conclusion: populations are **highly variable and locally unstable**, likely driven by catastrophic climatic and epizootic (disease) events.
- **Micro-habitat management implications:** Large pools are essential for adult survival/reproduction; small pools and marsh habitats support juvenile survival — underscoring need for habitat heterogeneity in conservation planning for [[Ranid Frogs]].
- **Statewide status:** Of 911 surveys at 763 riparian sites (mostly National Forest land, 1990–1993), extant populations detected:
  - *Bufo microscaphus*: 15 sites
  - *Rana blairi*: 2 sites
  - *Rana catesbeiana* (non-native): 31 sites
  - *Rana chiricahuensis*: 25 sites
  - *Rana pipiens*: 14 sites
  - *Rana subaquavocalis*: 2 sites
  - *Rana yavapaiensis*: 97 sites
  - *Thamnophis eques*: 2 sites
  - *Thamnophis rufipunctatus*: 2 sites
- **Conservation priorities identified:** Expand surveys beyond National Forest lands; improve detection methods for rare species (*T. eques*, *T. rufipunctatus*); prioritize management for species with few extant populations — *R. blairi*, *R. chiricahuensis*, *R. pipiens*.
- **Conceptual framework:** [[Michael J. Sredl]]'s companion talk on "Unravelling Arizona Amphibian Declines" emphasizes that identifying **pattern and scale** (spatial, temporal, organizational) is a prerequisite to diagnosing causative factors behind [[Ranid Frog Decline]] and to prioritizing future inventory, monitoring, and research efforts.
