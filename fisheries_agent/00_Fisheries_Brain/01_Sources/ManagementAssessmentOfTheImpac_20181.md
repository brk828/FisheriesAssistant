# Management Assessment of Illegally Introduced Northern Pike at Parker Canyon Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esox-lucius

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) draft assessment, prepared by Don Mitchell and Kirk Young, evaluates the impacts of [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), which were illegally introduced into [[Parker Canyon Lake]] around late 1996 to early 1997. Evidence from gill netting, electrofishing and angler catches shows a reproducing pike population of up to four year classes. The pike are already preying on stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] and warm-water young fish, and the authors judge that escape during floods or illegal transfer could threaten other regional waters. Four management options are assessed. The authors recommend a combined approach: draw the lake down, remove aquatic vegetation, and treat the lake with [[Rotenone]] at least three times.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Parker Canyon Lake is a man-made reservoir at about 5,000 feet in oak woodland, about 80 miles southeast of Tucson and 25 miles southwest of Sierra Vista. Its watershed covers nine square miles. Water is supplied only by precipitation runoff, and there are no downstream water rights. At full pool the lake covers about 124 surface acres (just over 3,000 acre-feet). At the time of writing it covered about 82 acres and held just under 1,600 acre-feet.

**Management history.** The reservoir was built in 1962 with [[Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration]] funds and opened in 1964 as a year-round cold-water fishery. Illegal introductions of [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill Sunfish]], redear sunfish, green sunfish and black bullhead in the late 1960s forced a shift in the early 1970s to a self-sustaining warm-water fishery with a winter put-and-take trout fishery. AGFD has since stocked about 61,000 fingerling [[Channel Catfish]], nearly one million catchable trout, and more than 20,000 trout annually. Other legally stocked species include coho salmon, red shiner, fathead minnow, threadfin shad and freshwater shrimp.

**Angler use.** Angler surveys estimated 14,038 angler-days (November 1984 to April 1985) and 13,937 angler-days in 1993, of which 4,692 occurred from October through March. Of anglers surveyed, 42.1 percent sought rainbow trout. Trout anglers at the lake were estimated to spend $328,252 directly and generate $592,740 in indirect benefits annually. The typical angler travels 64.4 miles one way and fishes just over three hours. Road paving and urban growth are expected to increase use over the next five to ten years.

**Pike history.** Anglers caught the first pike in summer 1997. Sampling in 1998 and 1999 found the following:
- September 1998: 154 net hours yielded one pike (730 mm, 3,030 g).
- October 1998: 174 net hours yielded two adults (mean 648 mm).
- Scale ageing placed the three 1998 fish in the 3 to 4 year classes. The authors caution that scale ageing for pike can be unreliable.
- September 1999: 51.8 minutes of electrofishing produced three juveniles (283 to 340 mm, mean 304 mm), which confirmed reproduction. Sixty-four gill net hours produced three adults (664 to 770 mm, mean 711 mm).
- A gravid female was collected in December 1999.
- Based on 45 specimens, the authors describe a healthy reproducing population of up to four year classes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predation within the lake.** Stomach analysis of pike from the January survey found largemouth bass fingerlings in four fish and sunfish in two. The authors note that these were small pike. Larger pike shift to a diet of stocked rainbow trout. A September 1999 adult contained a 200-mm rainbow trout. The authors state that the severity of trout predation is not yet known.

**Literature on pike impacts.** The report reviews studies that generally found negative effects of pike on other fish:
- [[Horseshoe Lake]], Minnesota: significant, mostly negative growth changes in several sport species over 14 years (Anderson and Schupp 1986).
- Escanaba Lake, Wisconsin: smallmouth bass and bluegill became virtually extinct when pike became abundant (Kempinger and Carline 1977).
- Some studies found weaker effects, including Beyerle (1971), Hooper (1964) and McCarraher (1959).
- Cutthroat and bull trout declines have been linked to pike in Montana, and pike consumed 2,500 to 4,200 cutthroat trout per year in Cougar Bay, Idaho (Rich 1992).

**Thermal and oxygen tolerance.** Pike tolerance is a central issue. [[Goddard and Redmond]] (1978) found die-off began only at 32°C. Survival at 0.1 mg/L dissolved oxygen for several days in winter was also reported. [[Headrick and Carline]] (1993) identified suitable summer habitat as water below 26°C with dissolved oxygen above 3.0 mg/L. Recent studies at [[Pena Blanca Lake]] and [[Patagonia Lake]] found such zones, which the authors say makes pike survival and reproduction there plausible.

**Dispersal pathways.**
- *Human dispersal:* Two other regional cold-water lakes, [[Riggs Flat Lake]] and [[Rose Canyon Lake]], have classic pike habitat. Illegal stocking into these or the desert lakes is a concern.
- *Natural dispersal:* Flood events fill and spill the lake only about three years in ten. The creek below the dam stays cool enough for pike survival. Downstream habitat is small and intermittent, so pike would likely persist only briefly, but could devastate fish in isolated pools. The [[Gila Topminnow]] occurs in the upper Santa Cruz River in Mexico.
- Native fishes in the Santa Cruz and San Pedro drainages are already imperiled by other non-native species.

## Key Findings

1. **Establishment confirmed.** Pike are reproducing in Parker Canyon Lake, with up to four year classes documented from 45 specimens.
2. **Trout predation confirmed.** Predation on stocked rainbow trout is documented. The authors estimate annual losses of $5,625 to $9,450, using the Rich (1992) loss rate applied to 24,000 catchable trout stocked at $2.25 each ($54,000 per year). They expect actual losses to be higher because of the mild climate and longer growing season.
3. **Economic stakes.** Trout anglers contribute $592,740 annually in indirect benefits. The authors warn that a decline in trout fishing could reduce angler use and license revenue.
4. **Regional threat.** The authors consider the threat of spread to other regional waters "currently not acceptable."

**Management options assessed:**

- **Option 1: Adapt management.** No upfront cost, but the authors say it does nothing about escape or expansion and that impacts may be irreversible before they are documented.
- **Option 2: Selective removal and liberalized bag limits.** The authors judge this labor-intensive and unlikely to succeed. Only 9 pike were caught in over 300 net hours (0.03 fish per hour), so about 3,000 net hours would be needed to catch 100 fish.
- **Option 3: Vegetation removal.** Pike need aquatic vegetation to spawn, but [[Centrarchid]] populations depend on it too. Mechanical harvesting of 10 days costs about $3,400 and reaches only the top five or six feet. Chemical treatment costs $50 to $100 per gallon. Man-made habitat could add up to $50,000. The authors call this suppression, not removal.
- **Option 4: Total renovation.** Rotenone or [[Antimycin A]] treatment, or complete drying, would remove the population and end the threat of expansion. Rotenone costs about $75 per gallon. One gallon of Noxfish 5% treats 1 to 3 acre-feet at 2.5 PPM. Antimycin A costs about $350 per unit (3.8 acre-feet at 10 ppb). Treating the roughly 1,500 acre-feet in the lake would need 400 units, or $140,000. The authors note public opposition is likely and that refilling after drying would take two to four years of average precipitation.

**Recommendation.** The authors recommend a combined approach:
- Draw the lake down to a manageable level.
- Remove as much aquatic vegetation as possible.
- Treat with rotenone at least three times over six to eight months.
- Restock with catchable rainbow trout between treatments if timing allows, and screen the outlet and time releases to protect downstream native fish.
- Hold public meetings and involve stakeholders, including the store owner, campground concessionaire, U.S. Forest Service and homeowners association.

The authors note that full drainage would cost less in treatment but may cost more in lost license revenue. The warm-water fishery could take up to five years to restore, while the rainbow trout fishery could return sooner.
