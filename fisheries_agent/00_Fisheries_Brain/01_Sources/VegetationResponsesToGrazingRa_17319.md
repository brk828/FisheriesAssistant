# Vegetation Responses to Grazing, Rainfall, Site Condition, and Mesquite Control on Semidesert Range

#range-science #botany #poaceae #mesquite-control

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service Research Paper (RM-149, July 1975) by Dwight R. Cable and S. Clark Martin reports a 10-year study (1957–1966) of vegetation on four cattle pastures at the upper edge of the semidesert grass-shrub type on the [[Santa Rita Experimental Range]], near Tucson, Arizona. The study tested how [[Velvet Mesquite]] control, summer rainfall, [[Cattle Grazing]] intensity, slope, distance to water, salt placement, and [[Soil Series]] affect [[Perennial Grasses]] and [[Annual Grasses]].

The main conclusions are that mesquite control raised perennial grass production by 52 percent relative to untreated pastures, that grass output depends on rainfall from two successive summers, and that heavy use slows recovery after drought. Stocking rates can be estimated from rainfall records about as accurately as from forage measurements. Mesquite control was judged economically justified, with the investment recovered in about 6 years.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The four pastures (1, 7, 8, and 10) lie on a narrow band of foothills along the base of the Santa Rita Mountains, between 3,700 and 4,800 ft elevation. Two pastures (1 and 7) had mesquite killed between 1954 and 1957 (mesquite-killed, M-K). The other two (8 and 10) kept their mesquite (mesquite-alive, M-A). Average annual (October–September) precipitation ranged from 14.7 to 18.6 inches (1954–1966). Summer maximums were usually in the nineties °F.

**Vegetation history.** Grassland was dominant in the early 1900s. Velvet mesquite then invaded over about 40 years. Before the study, mesquite densities averaged about 80–180 trees per acre. Pastures were grazed yearlong by cow-calf herds from 1915. Stocking was reduced from 32–34 head per section (1915–1925) after productivity declined.

**Methods.** Measurements were taken at 20 randomly selected locations per pasture (80 transects in total).
- Basal intercept was measured on 100-ft line transects by the line-intercept method (Canfield 1942).
- Herbage production was estimated by double sampling (Wilm et al. 1944).
- Utilization was measured by the ungrazed-plant method (Roach 1950).
- Stocking rates were set each October by multiple regression on annual and perennial grass yields, targeting 40 percent use.

**Management changes (fall 1957).** The utilization objective was lowered to 40 percent, and alternate-year summer deferment (July–September) was started.

**Mesquite control costs.** Original treatment used diesel oil at about 5 cents per tree (1.5 pints/tree). A 1960 followup used 0.75 pint/tree.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and rainfall.** Southern Arizona has two rainy seasons, June–September and December–April. Over 1953–54 to 1965–66, annual precipitation averaged 17.07 inches. About 62 percent fell in June–September, 27 percent in December–April, 10 percent in October–November, and less than 1 percent in May. June–August rainfall showed a significant downward trend of 0.38 inch per year.

**Plant-water relations.** Perennial grasses depend on late-summer rainfall to enlarge basal buds, and on the current and previous summer's rain for foliage. Winter precipitation had little effect on the following summer's grass production. The grasses are warm-season species, and little soil moisture carries over from spring to summer. Perennial grasses have established root systems that give them a competitive advantage over annuals for soil moisture.

**Competition.** Annual grass output was highest on favorable sites and dropped as perennial grass competition increased. Among 8 transects on near-optimum sites in pasture 1, the perennial grass and annual grass output were strongly negatively related (88 percent of variation explained). Annual grass production was essentially zero where perennial grass production reached 940 lb/acre.

**Soils.** Three soils predominated on the study transects, with 69 of 80 transects on these soils:
- [[Whitehouse Soil]]: clayey subsoils (31 transects)
- [[Comora Soil]]: sandy or gravelly subsoils (23 transects, including Tumacacori)
- [[Coronado Soil]]: shallow, stony, steep-slope soils (15 transects)

**Shrub cover.** Other shrubs included false-mesquite (23–41 percent of total shrub intercept) and velvetpod mimosa. Mesquite made up 32 and 38 percent of total tree-shrub crown intercept on the M-A pastures, but less than 2 percent on the M-K pastures.

## Key Findings

1. **Mesquite control increased grass output.** Perennial grass production on M-K pastures rose 168 lb/acre (52 percent) relative to M-A pastures by about 1963. Three-year moving averages showed a 52.3 percent gain between 1957–59 and 1964–66.

2. **Two successive summers drive grass production.** The interaction product (previous June–September rainfall × current August rainfall) explained 63.5 to 91.0 percent of year-to-year variation in perennial grass production. Management decisions should account for the fact that it takes at least 2 years to recover from a 1-year drought.

3. **Heavy use slows recovery.** Transects with 52–59 percent mean use gained less in wet years following dry years. Gains were highest at the lowest use levels (21–28 percent). Two or three favorable years were needed for heavily used transects to recover.

4. **Arizona cottontop is the exception.** This species produced more in wet years under heavier use, which suggests it tolerates grazing (linked to its many basal and culm buds).

5. **Alternate-year summer deferment had no benefit.** It did not improve perennial grass production in the M-A pastures. The authors state that other Santa Rita studies found other rest schedules beneficial.

6. **Slope and soil preferences vary by species.** Of the 14 most productive species, 6 preferred slopes under 10 percent, 4 preferred slopes over 30 percent, and 4 showed no preference. Arizona cottontop favored flat slopes and grew poorly on [[Coronado Soil]]; side-oats grama did best on Coronado soil.

7. **Annual grass output is undependable.** It varied from essentially none (1962, the driest year) to 425 lb/acre on M-K pastures (1959). Annual grass production was three times as high on M-K as on M-A range. It decreased by about 50 lb/acre per 10 percent increase in mesquite crown cover and reached zero at about 40 percent cover.

8. **Mesquite cover stayed stable.** On M-K pastures, crown cover averaged under 0.5 percent at the end of the period (later intercept measures showed 1.59 and 1.43 percent in year 10). On M-A pastures, crown cover increased from 11.04 to 13.31 percent (pasture 8) and from 7.17 to 12.67 percent (pasture 10).

9. **Utilization was near target.** Ten-year perennial grass utilization on the four pastures ranged from about 30 to 50 percent. Transect use varied from 19 to 72 percent. Stocking rates were adjusted each fall to target about 40 percent use.

10. **Cattle diet favors some species.** Arizona cottontop made up 22.4 percent of the diet but only 13.6 percent of production. Black grama made up 7.1 percent of the diet but 11.8 percent of production. Slender grama, side-oats grama, and tall threeawns grazed roughly in proportion to their production.

11. **Palatability is the strongest factor in use.** Palatability ratings ranged from 0 to 73 percent across species and pastures. Among the major species, the four-pasture mean ranged from 12 percent (black grama) to 57 percent (Arizona cottontop).

12. **Distance and slope reduce use.** Use declined about 0.5 percent per 0.1 mile from water, and about 1.7 percent per 10 percent increase in slope. The overall regression was Percent use = 27.40 + 64.84 (P-C index) − 0.17 (Slope) − 4.94 (Distance), with F = 90.92**. The P-C index is a palatability-composition index that explained 61 to 94 percent of use variation among transects within a pasture.

13. **Salt placement shifts grazing.** A new salt ground placed 1.5 miles from water in 1961 raised use within 0.5 mile of it by 16.5 percent (P<0.01). Use in distant transects fell by 5.5 percent.

14. **Rainfall can estimate stocking.** Stocking rates predicted from the interaction rainfall product explained 73 to 94 percent of year-to-year variation. Correlations were higher (r = 0.85 to 0.97) than estimates based on grass production (r = 0.79 to 0.93). Rainfall is easier and cheaper to measure than grass production.

15. **Mesquite control is economically justified.** Stocking on M-K pastures rose 0.15 animal-unit month/acre relative to M-A pastures. Using O'Connell and Boster's 1972 net return of $5.82 per AUM, this equals $0.87/acre/year. Two aerial 2,4,5-T treatments (about $5.00/acre, roughly 90 percent release) would be recovered in about 6 years. Control treatments often last 15–20 years.

16. **Other benefits.** Mesquite control improves perennial grass cover, reduces runoff and sheet erosion, helps gullies heal, improves visibility for livestock handling, and creates more aesthetically pleasing open grassland.
