# Invertebrate Resistance and Resilience to Intermittency in a Desert Stream

#freshwater-ecology #macroinvertebrates #physidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how benthic macroinvertebrates in [[Sycamore Creek]], an intermittent [[Sonoran Desert]] stream in Arizona, cope with drying and rewetting. The authors tested two mechanisms of persistence: resistance (tolerance or avoidance during drying and water loss) and resilience (recovery after rewetting). Community shifts during drying occurred only at a site that became isolated from upstream reaches. Survival after water loss was very low, with few individuals persisting beyond 10 days. The authors conclude that persistence in desert streams depends mainly on rapid recolonization after flooding rewets dry channels, rather than on resistance.

## Background & Description

Drying is a central environmental feature shaping the distribution, abundance and life histories of temporary-stream fauna. Known responses include physiological tolerance, behavioral avoidance (such as burrowing), life-history modification, emigration and recolonization after disturbance. Desert streams experience both [[Flash Floods]] and drying. Earlier work focused on invertebrate responses to flooding, while the effects of drying on macroinvertebrates were poorly known.

The authors framed persistence in terms of three components:

- **Resistance I:** change during the period when the stream is shrinking but still wet.
- **Resistance II:** change during surface-water absence.
- **Resilience:** recovery after rewetting, measured as the slope of density against time since flooding.

The study addressed two questions: (1) what general community changes occur during drying, and (2) what mechanisms allow invertebrate populations to persist in intermittent desert streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Sycamore Creek flows south from the Mazatzal Mountains to the [[Verde River]]. The mid-basin study area lies at 610 m elevation and receives 300–400 mm of precipitation per year, mostly from winter storms (December–March) and summer monsoons (late July–September). Flow is often continuous in winter but becomes intermittent in summer; over 50% of the channel can lose surface flow in dry years. Within a single kilometer, some sites are perennial while others flow for as little as three months per year. The mid-basin reach is low gradient, unshaded, with gravel runs, pools and rocky riffles. Sediments are coarse sand and pebbles (5–50 mm), typically 50–100 cm deep. Summer water temperatures range from 20 to 30 °C.

**Study reaches.**
- **Site I** (140 × 20 m) held flow from February to June in 1989 and from late March to June in 1990. Drying began downstream, followed by loss of upstream connection, which isolated a pool before complete water loss.
- **Site II** (122 × 15 m) usually flowed for at least seven months and often dried only partially. In 1990 flow persisted in the upper third of the reach through summer; in 1989 the reach dried completely.

**Physicochemical stress.** Sediments and surface water became anoxic during drying. Exposed sediments reached temperatures above 60 °C, and moisture was lost rapidly. Moist benthic refuges (algal mats, crayfish burrows, deep sediments) are absent in these channels.

## Key Findings

### Methods

- **Drying-phase sampling:** five 80 cm² core samples (10 cm deep) per date at sites I and II, collected biweekly before drying and weekly afterward.
- **Avoidance traps:** three pairs of funnel traps (20 cm tapering to 5 cm, 50 µm mesh) at site II, deployed 1 m apart for 28 h on 5–6 August 1990, with bags emptied every 4 h.
- **Post-drying survival:** cores taken at 1, 3, 6, 10 and 15 days after water loss from five reaches, flooded with distilled water for 24 h, then sieved through a 50 µm net.
- **Rewetting:** cores and water-column samples taken within two days of rewetting on 30 March, 17 August and 5 September 1990.
- **Resilience:** slopes of log(x+1)-transformed density against time, compared with a multiple comparison of slopes and [[Tukey's Hsd Test]] at α = 0.05.

### Resistance During Drying

- No consistent density changes occurred during drying at either site. A flood on 23 July 1989 significantly reduced densities at site II (Student's t = 4.60, P < 0.001). Recovery was negligible: densities 2 days and 6 weeks after the flood did not differ (t = 0.65, P > 0.50). Days since flood were only weakly correlated with density (y = 0.4x + 22.4; r = 0.345, P < 0.062).
- At site I, the week before complete water loss produced clear taxonomic shifts. Communities became dominated by beetles, hemipterans and *Physella virgata*. On the final day in 1989, snails covered about 0.3 m² to a depth of 30 mm. *Chironomus*, with hemoglobin, was also abundant. Mayflies were rare or absent. The number of taxa did not change.
- Table 1 shows snails rising from 0.5% to 68.2% of benthos at site I in 1989 (5 June: 11.9%; 9 June: 68.2%). In 1990, snails reached 51.8% by 18 June.
- Site II showed no comparable shifts, probably because it dried faster and stayed connected to the upstream riffle.
- **Avoidance:** 2,397 individuals from 27 taxa were captured moving during drying at site II (1,727 individuals from 23 taxa upstream; 670 from 19 taxa downstream). No single taxon moved significantly upstream, but total upstream movement was significant (P = 0.0003). Net movement was upstream in all periods except one. Movement peaked between 24:00 and 04:00 and was lowest between 16:00 and 20:00.
- For comparison, a perennial reach in 1979 showed strong downstream drift (10,205 downstream vs. 594 upstream; P < 0.001).

### Survival After Water Loss

- Only 12 taxa were collected from dry reaches (Table 2). Slowly drying sites (3–4 days) supported more taxa and individuals than rapidly drying sites (complete loss within 2 h).
- Few individuals survived beyond 10 days, and only five were found two weeks after drying. Taxon counts fell from 10 (day 1) to 11 (day 3), 9 (day 6), 7 (day 10) and 5 (day 15).
- Individual numbers were significantly related to sediment moisture (r² = 0.46, P < 0.05).
- *Physella virgata* and tipulid larvae and pupae were the most abundant survivors. Tipulids appeared to complete larval development: day 1 and day 3 samples were dominated by larvae, while pupae dominated by day 6.

### Recovery After Rewetting

- Taxon numbers soon after rewetting ranged from two (winter flood, 30 March) to seven (summer flood, 17 August) and six (flood, 5 September).
- Early assemblages in March were dominated by chironomids and oligochaetes. After the summer floods, hemipterans and coleopterans were most common. Over 70 *Probezzia* larvae, many final-instar, were counted in one August core two days after flooding.
- Maximum densities occurred within a month of rewetting at both sites in March 1990 (Figure 4).
- Recovery rates differed significantly among sequences (F = 12.84, P < 0.0005). Resilience was highest at site II after rewetting in 1990 and lowest after the autumn 1989 flood at the same site.

### Discussion and Interpretation

- **Why densities fall during drying.** Density declines are attributed to deteriorating water quality, which favors taxa using atmospheric oxygen or hemoglobin, and to predation. Insect fragments and empty snail shells in site I samples suggest predation during the final days. Predator numbers rise during drying, as is also reported for other temporary waters.
- **Limited avoidance.** Upstream movement showed that some invertebrates could keep pace with receding margins. Often, however, drying outran movement, stranding individuals on the bed. Some beetles and bugs appear to seek drying pools for food. One study cited observed eight [[Giant Water Bug]] adults (*Abedus herberti*) consume 20 fish within a few hours.
- **Hyporheic zone.** Although cooler and moister, the hyporheic zone at Sycamore Creek is not an important refuge for benthic species. The exception is *Probezzia*, which was abundant there after one summer flood.
- **Recolonization pool.** The authors propose that slower recovery at dry sites reflects three changes: a shift toward snail-dominated assemblages lacking aerial-adult colonists, reduced habitat area and fewer pre-flood invertebrates, and greater distance from perennial sources. Before the 1990 rewetting, more than 2.5 km of dry channel lay upstream and 1.2 km downstream of site I. Perennial reaches were within 800 m of site II. Summer heat and low humidity can cut adult longevity by up to 50%.

### Conclusions

- Few taxa resist drying. Resistance during drying relies on tolerance of physicochemical extremes and on avoidance by upstream movement or flight.
- Survival through water loss is virtually absent. Persistence depends on recolonization after rewetting, which is why these benthos are considered resilience-oriented.
- Floods during drought periods may produce different recolonization trajectories because non-resistant species become scarce beforehand.
- The authors state that more data are needed from sites differing in flow permanence and distance from perennial sources to test these hypotheses.
