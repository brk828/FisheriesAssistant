# Observations on the Nutrition of Trout

#ichthyology #salmonidae #aquaculture #hatchery-management #fish-nutrition

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive report by Dr. C. M. McCay documents experimental work on trout nutrition conducted at the [[Connecticut State Hatchery]] at Burlington and the [[Cortland Experimental Hatchery]] in New York from 1927-1934. The research, representing cooperation between the New York State Conservation Department, the federal Bureau of Fisheries, and [[Cornell University]], aimed to improve feeding methods in fish hatcheries. Key findings demonstrate that trout require only 14-15% protein in their diet (approximately one-third of what they typically receive in standard hatchery feeds), can efficiently utilize [[Dry Skim Milk]] and [[Cottonseed Meal]] as economical feed alternatives, absorb calcium directly from water, and can be pigmented through dietary lipochromes. The work established fundamental principles for cost-effective trout production while maintaining fish health and quality.

## Background & Description

The [[Cortland Experimental Hatchery]] was established in summer 1932 as a purely experimental station designed to improve feeding methods across American hatcheries. The facility was fully equipped to conduct experiments with varying scales of trout populations, from very few individuals to thousands, allowing investigators to work unhampered by the restrictions of production hatcheries.

Standard hatchery meats used during this period included beef heart, spleen, liver, and lung. Chemical analysis revealed these meats on a dry basis contained 56-79% protein, with varying fat content (2-18%). Hatchery trout typically received diets where protein comprised approximately 50% or more of total calories—about three times the requirements of any animal species studied by nutrition experts at that time.

The research employed multiple experimental approaches: synthetic diets made from known chemical elements, nitrogen balance studies measuring feed nitrogen versus urinary/fecal nitrogen excretion, growth rate comparisons, mortality assessments, and feed conversion calculations. Experiments typically ran 12-44 weeks with groups ranging from 50 to over 1,000 fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The research recognized that trout growth rates are considerably slower than terrestrial animals like rats, chickens, and swine, suggesting protein requirements should not exceed and might be lower than those of rats. Water temperature and oxygen content significantly influenced feed conversion efficiency, with temperatures of 44-52°F permitting optimal conversion.

A critical ecological finding concerned calcium metabolism: trout can absorb calcium directly from water. In 12-week experiments, trout stored 5.4-6.2 milligrams of calcium per fish, with four-fifths obtained directly from water and only one-fifth from food. This adaptation means hatcheries with hard water need not supplement dietary calcium for bone development, though soft-water facilities may require dietary adjustments.

The research also addressed fat digestion challenges. Trout body temperature (approximately 50°F) remains below the melting points of common animal fats used in hatchery feeds (butter 82-95°F, lard 84-109°F, beef tallow 108-118°F). Trout body fat is a yellow oil that flows at 50°F with relatively high iodine numbers (90-119), indicating a "soft" fat composition. This temperature mismatch suggests digestive inefficiency with solid animal fats.

## Key Findings

**Protein Requirements:**
- Trout require between 11-25% protein, with 14% identified as the minimum effective level
- Diets exceeding 15% protein are adequate for growth
- Standard hatchery feeds provide approximately three times required protein levels
- Two-thirds of protein fed is burned for energy rather than tissue building
- Casein from milk and beef liver both proved effective protein sources
- Mortality was heavy on diets containing 10% or less protein; above this level losses were insignificant

**Dry Skim Milk as Feed:**
- Composition: 35.4% protein, 1.7% fat, 48.7% lactose, 8.1% ash, 3.9% water
- Cannot be fed alone indefinitely but highly effective when combined with fresh meat (especially liver)
- Swine digested 96.5% of whole milk proteins and retained 67.6% for tissue building
- For fry: mixture of one-third dry skim milk and two-thirds raw liver with yeast and cod liver oil produced 10% better growth than straight liver
- Fresh liver must be fed more frequently during earliest weeks; once every two weeks sufficient for large fingerlings
- Chief challenge is physical composition—very soluble unless bound with meat juices or produced in special granular forms

**Cottonseed Meal as Feed:**
- Composition: approximately 41% crude protein, 8.3% fat, 10.1% crude fiber
- Successfully incorporated at levels up to 70% without toxicity (contrasts with toxic linseed meal)
- Best results with diet #22: 25% preserved spleen, 25% fish meat, 20% cottonseed meal, 25% dry skim milk, 5% cod liver oil
- Beef liver proved best meat supplement, followed by kidney, lungs, heart, and spleen
- Diet combining cottonseed meal, dry skim milk, and salmon egg meal (28:29:28) with only 15% fresh meat showed excellent results
- All three trout species ([[Brook Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]]) made moderate growth on cottonseed meal diets
- Caution advised for breeding stock due to potential egg quality effects (analogous to poultry research findings)

**Feed Conversion Efficiency:**
- Under favorable conditions, approximately one-sixth (16-18%) of dry feed converts to trout body tissue
- Five-sixths used for metabolism (heart beating, intestinal movement, swimming energy)
- Trout bodies are one-fifth dry matter, four-fifths water
- Fresh beef liver: 1.2-2.2 pounds dry feed per pound live trout gain
- Diet #72 (25% dry skim milk, 25% cottonseed meal, 50% fresh beef liver): 2.6 average feed coefficient (range 2.0-3.6)
- Diet #76 (29% dry skim milk, 28% cottonseed meal, 15% raw beef liver, 28% salmon egg meal): 3.3 average feed coefficient
- Feed costs ranged $0.29-0.32 per pound trout produced (1930s dollars)
- Sack fry showed very efficient conversion: 76% of yolk converted to body tissue during first half of yolk-dependent period

**Calcium Metabolism:**
- Trout absorb four-fifths of required calcium directly from water
- Only one-fifth needs to come from food
- Bone growth continues even with limited dietary calcium in calcium-rich water
- Hard water hatcheries need not supplement calcium; soft water facilities may require dietary adjustments
- No evidence found that yeast or cod liver oil supplements provide benefits when fresh meat is included in diet

**Pigmentation:**
- Fat-soluble lipochromes (carotinoids) responsible for trout coloring
- Alcoholic extract from salmon eggs successfully colored trout starting at week 8
- Residue after extraction produced little coloring
- Pigmentation likely important for fish health (vitamin A precursors) as well as appearance
- Coloring materials are vital to life processes, not merely decorative excreta

**Vitamin Requirements:**
- No experimental evidence justified feeding yeast or cod liver oil when diets included fresh meat
- Trout do require vitamins, but fresh meat (especially liver) provides adequate amounts
- Failures with dry skim milk alone not due to zinc, copper, iodine, iron, or recognized vitamins (except possibly vitamin C)
- Fresh liver supplementation essential for complete nutrition

**Species Comparisons:**
- No evidence found that [[Brook Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Brown Trout]] differ in nutritional requirements
- Inherent species differences in growth rates exist independent of feedstuffs
- All three species made moderate growth on cottonseed meal-based diets

**Practical Recommendations:**
- Calculate protein content using dry basis to account for moisture differences
- Keep diets relatively low in fat until better understanding of fat utilization achieved
- Use fresh liver as primary meat supplement, especially for young fish
- Grind dry milk with liver and allow mixture to stand before feeding to bind milk
- Consider granular forms of dry skim milk that dissolve slowly
- Avoid linseed meal (toxic to trout)
- For breeding stock, exercise caution with cottonseed meal until more research completed
- Maintain water temperature 44-52°F and abundant oxygen for optimal feed conversion
