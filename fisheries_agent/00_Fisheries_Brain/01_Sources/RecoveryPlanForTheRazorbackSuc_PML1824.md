# Razorback Sucker Recovery Plan (First Draft)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This first-draft recovery plan, prepared by Dr. Wendell Minckley of [[Arizona State University]] and Dr. Gail Kobetich of the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], addresses the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species has declined sharply across its range. Apart from six adults held for five months in a small raceway at Arizona State University in 1965, no attempts had been made to maintain it or assess its status in nature. The plan states that restoring the species throughout its native range is impossible, and it proposes a staged program: survey the river, study the life history, develop [[Artificial Propagation]] methods, identify and protect habitat, and build public support, with the aim of sustained, multiple-use management.

## Background & Description

**Morphology.** The razorback sucker has an elongated head and a body that is laterally compressed and somewhat triangular in cross-section forward of the anal fin. A sharp-edged dorsal keel develops behind the head, supported by greatly enlarged neural spines. Key diagnostic traits include:

- Lateral-line scales: 66 to 87
- Dorsal fin rays: 13 to 16, usually 14 or 15
- Gill rakers: 44 to 50 on the first arch
- Mouth: inferior, may be protruded forward to a terminal position, and bordered by papillae-like lips
- Scales: often absent or obscured by thickened epidermis

**Coloration.** Adults are olive to brownish-black dorsally, lighter on the sides, and often yellowish to cream ventrally. A brown or blackish lateral stripe is typical; it may become pink on breeding males. Sexual dimorphism during breeding includes nuptial tubercles on the anal fin and caudal peduncle of males, and a thickened, fleshy, and papillose ovipositor-like region in females. Adults may reach lengths near one meter and weights above five kilograms.

**Identification.** The species is difficult to separate from other catostomids, including the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and the [[Humpback Sucker]]. Identification relies on keel structure, dorsal fin-ray count, and distribution of pharyngeal teeth. The size at which the pre-dorsal keel develops is not known.

**Nomenclature.** The common name "razorback sucker" describes the dorsal keel. Older fishermen used the name, which is a literal translation of the genus-level name.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Historically the species occupied mainstem rivers and their quiet backwaters, sloughs, and pools. In the lower basin, along the Colorado and the [[Gila River]] and its major tributaries, pool-backwater habitats were almost certainly used. The best evidence suggests these environments supported the largest adult populations, at least for much of the year.

**Historical use.** Razorback suckers were widely used as food by Native peoples. In the 1880s the species supported a commercial fishery on the [[San Pedro River]] in southern Arizona, with sales in Tombstone. Some populations sustained commercial fishing until the late 1900s in the lower basin, including at [[Saguaro Lake]].

**Causes of decline.** The decline coincides with major modifications to the Colorado River and its tributaries starting in the early 1900s. The plan identifies several interacting factors:

- Reduced flows and altered temperature and turbidity regimes
- Drainage of backwaters and loss of quiet habitat
- Mining and channelization, including late-1800s "arroyo cutting" that changed many tributaries
- Progressive impoundment of tributaries and the mainstem, which created physical barriers
- Increase in non-native fish species, mainly from the Mississippi River valley, rising almost exponentially after about 1930
- Hybridization with the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], which appears to be increasing at the last remaining locations and below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]

**Distribution.** The species was historically widespread throughout the Colorado Basin. The plan's distribution maps (Table 1 and Table 2 in the original) show its range and areas of uncertainty. Key current status points include:

- **Lower basin:** Glen Canyon Dam to the Sea of Cortez. The species has become increasingly scarce in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]] and is essentially absent from [[Lake Havasu]] below Davis Dam. No records exist for the past 20 to 25 years from the Mexican segment. The last collection of a young-of-the-year fish from the lower basin was in 1950 near Willow Beach.
- **Verde system and Salt River:** The species persisted at least until 1954 in the Verde system, and possibly in Salt River reservoirs, but is now extirpated from that portion of its range.
- **Upper basin:** Small, apparently reproducing populations persist near the confluence of the Yampa and Green rivers in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], Utah.
- **Other historical sites:** Records from the [[San Juan River]], the [[Virgin River]], and the upper Gila River suggest the species once occurred in these tributaries, though the evidence is sparse.

**Protected status.** The species is protected by state law in California, and its status is listed as threatened in the broader assessment of threatened freshwater fishes of the Colorado Basin states.

**Diet.** Juveniles (15 individuals examined) fed on zooplankton and some planktonic algae (diatoms). Adults have been reported to eat algae and dipteran larvae in the lower basin. Specimens from Lake Mohave had intestines filled with planktonic crustaceans in May. Feeding has been observed under floodlights at night. The gill rakers and small mouth suggest a filter-feeding habit.

**Life history.** Little is known about the life history. Most knowledge comes from artificial habitats, especially mainstem reservoirs. Adults move upstream to spawn. Spawning occurs in backwaters and bays from late winter (Lake Mohave, February) to early summer (below Glen Canyon Dam, May and early June). A single female may be attended by several males. Males align against the female using the head and dorsal keel, the pair vibrates laterally, and eggs are released and scattered. The eggs are adhesive and attach to the substrate. Growth rates, sex ratios, survivorship, and longevity are unknown.

## Key Findings

**1. Status and captive history.** The species has been in decline for decades. Only six adults have ever been held in captivity (one group at Arizona State University in 1965). These fish were easy to handle, fed readily, and showed no parasite or disease problems. Within a few days they responded to tapping on the raceway, and they tolerated blood sampling for hemoglobin and serum protein studies.

**2. Recovery objectives.** The plan's central principles are:

- Restoring the species to its full native range is not feasible, because the Colorado River has been modified too extensively.
- Pristine conditions should be reconstructed from historical records, so the physical and chemical parameters required by the species can be defined.
- Suitable areas identified as potential reestablishment sites should be tested and surveyed intensively.
- Captive-propagated fish should be released into the wild, and the life cycle should be documented under both artificial and natural conditions.
- Public outreach, including media coverage, should build support, using the approach taken with other charismatic species.

**3. Management philosophy.** The plan favors a multiple-use concept that combines species preservation with sport use (hunters and divers with spearfishing permits), possible commercial value as populations are re-established, and coordination with other fisheries programs. The aim is to manage the species at a level of sustained, multiple-use yield.

**4. Five-year agenda (FY 1974 to FY 1979).** The agenda assigns the following tasks, each with clear milestones:

- **Survey:** Begin a survey of the Lower Colorado River, standardize methods and forms, divide the river into segments, and assign lead agencies.
- **Life history:** Contract with universities to study the species' life history.
- **Artificial propagation:** Determine hatchery facilities and locate sites with suitable rearing facilities; then establish captive stocks.
- **Habitat:** Begin studies of unaltered habitat and identify the best remaining habitat.
- **Water development:** Monitor water-development projects that could affect the species.
- **Genetics:** Collect data on morphological variability.
- **Public relations:** Use press releases, magazines, television, and radio, and build a display center at the [[Phoenix Zoo]] (construction began in 1974 and continued into 1976).
- **Literature review:** Complete a literature review.

Later years emphasize compiling survey data, completing the life history studies, continuing artificial propagation and release of young fish, monitoring survival, and maintaining public relations. The 1979 to 1980 agenda extends these efforts "if necessary."
