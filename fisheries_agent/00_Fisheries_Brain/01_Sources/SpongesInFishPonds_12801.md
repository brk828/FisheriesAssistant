# Sponges in Fish Ponds

#invertebrate-ecology #freshwater-biology #Spongillidae #aquatic-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This document reports the discovery and identification of freshwater sponges in experimental fish ponds at [[Auburn University]] Agricultural Experiment Station and the U.S. Fish Cultural Station in [[Marion, Alabama]]. Three species were identified: [[Meyenia crateriformis]], [[Heteromeyenia argyrosperma]], and [[Asteromeyenia radiospiculata]]. The article describes the morphology, reproduction, and ecological role of these organisms in pond ecosystems, noting their potential value as indirect food sources for fish through associated aquatic insects.

## Background & Description

At least 32 species of freshwater sponges are known from North American lakes, ponds, and streams. Unlike marine bath sponges, freshwater sponges possess skeletons composed of siliceous spicules—tiny needle-like or variously shaped glass structures. Species identification requires dissolving the flesh with strong nitric acid and microscopic examination of both body spicules and gemmule spicules.

Morphologically, freshwater sponges exhibit highly variable forms. Specimens from Auburn ponds ranged from flat irregular growths less than one-fourth inch to more than six inches long. Some formed flat incrustations while others developed irregular thickenings and finger-like outgrowths. Sponges typically attach to solid substrates including sticks, logs, and stumps. Coloration varies: specimens containing symbiotic algae appear green, while those growing under bark in low-light conditions are yellowish brown. Surfaces appear velvety due to protruding needle-like spicules.

One specimen from Marion was biscuit-shaped, measuring 6 inches across and 3 inches thick, and was found floating after detachment. Another Marion specimen remained attached and branched without definite shape.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Freshwater sponges colonize new ponds slowly compared to other aquatic organisms. [[Aquatic Insects]] can fly to new water bodies rapidly, and fish migrate via tributary streams or overflow periods. Sponges lack mobility—they cannot swim, fly, or crawl—making them slow colonizers. [[Gemmules]] (asexual reproductive bodies) may be dispersed by birds and other animals.

Sponges reproduce both sexually and asexually through gemmules, which resist unfavorable conditions including freezing and drying. When favorable conditions return, new sponges develop from gemmules. Gemmule spicules differ among species and are essential for taxonomic identification.

Few predators consume freshwater sponges. Immature stages of certain aquatic insects—including [[Spongilla Fly]] and some [[Caddis Flies]]—consume sponges including their glassy spicules, which pass through digestive systems intact. These insects emerge as adults at the water surface, where they become available as fish food.

## Key Findings

- Three freshwater sponge species identified in Alabama fish ponds: [[Meyenia crateriformis]] and [[Heteromeyenia argyrosperma]] at Auburn; [[Asteromeyenia radiospiculata]] at Marion
- Sponges attach to submerged wood substrates (sticks, logs, stumps) and exhibit highly variable morphology
- Size range: less than 0.25 inches to more than 6 inches in length
- Coloration depends on algal symbiosis: green with algae present, yellowish brown in low-light conditions
- Siliceous spicules form skeleton; handlers should avoid eye contact and wash hands after handling
- Gemmules enable survival through freezing and drying, facilitating dispersal and colonization
- Sponges provide indirect value to fish ponds: aquatic insects (spongilla flies, caddis flies) consume sponges and subsequently serve as fish food during adult emergence
- Author requests specimen submissions for species identification to expand knowledge of freshwater sponge distribution in regional waters
