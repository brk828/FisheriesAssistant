# Predation by Salmo trutta in a Moorland Fishpond

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

T. T. Macan reports a long-term field experiment in [[Hodson's Tarn]], a small moorland fishpond of about half a hectare and 3 m maximum depth. After six years of study without fish, 500 [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) were introduced in autumn 1960, followed by a further 500 a year later. The paper uses three species as representative examples of different predation effects, while stressing that they lie along a continuous gradient rather than in distinct categories. The trout population declined sharply, and the commoner snail *Limnaea pereger* persisted, while scarce species such as the beetle *Rhantus exsoletus* disappeared. The paper is a preliminary summary presented at the 1966 congress in Stuttgart; the full account was published in earlier papers (Macan 1964, 1965, 1966).

## Background & Description

The study site, [[Hodson's Tarn]], has deep areas dominated by *Myriophyllum* beds, with *Potamogeton natans* near the inflow, *Carex rostrata* in shallow water near the inflow, and a thick *Littorella* sward elsewhere. Animals were sampled with a pond-net pushed three times along a 2 m line. By 1965 there were 14 collecting stations, increased from the original set after experience showed more were needed. Fish were caught with gill nets, and the 1961 year-class was marked by removing the adipose fin.

The three focal taxa were:
- [[Limnaea Pereger]] (great pond snail), an abundant mollusc with adults dying after summer egg-laying.
- [[Lestes Sponsa]] (damselfly), which overwinters as an egg, appears as a nymph in April, and emerges as an adult in July in warm summers.
- [[Rhantus Exsoletus]] (predaceous diving beetle), a scarce species recorded every year until fish were introduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The central question is how prey species persist under heavy predation by [[Brown Trout]]. The author notes that the fish-free years (1955 to 1960) preceded the introduction of trout, and that the trout population itself was recorded for only a few seasons. Predation appears to have acted most strongly on animals visible to the observer in open or sparsely vegetated water, which are also the ones a fish can see. Vegetation cover provided refuge: *Lestes* nymphs were absent from the *Myriophyllum* beds after predation, which covered about half the tarn. The shallow-water barrier created by thick vegetation also limited where larger trout could forage.

The paper suggests that *Limnaea pereger* may regulate its own growth through a water-borne growth-inhibiting substance, a hypothesis supported by work by Rose (1965) on tadpoles and earlier reports on molluscs. This mechanism would maintain a reserve of small snails that can replace losses among large individuals.

## Key Findings

**Trout population dynamics**
- The 1960 year-class: 428 fish caught, described as "a surprisingly good survival" of the original 500.
- The 1961 year-class: only 46 caught, with a considerable reduction once netting began after the winter of 1961/62.
- Netting was not carried out earlier, so it cannot be determined whether larger trout ate smaller ones of the later year-class.
- Results obtained just before the congress indicated very few fish, possibly none, remained in the tarn.
- No evidence of reproduction was found (Fig. 1). Trout weight rose sharply as numbers fell.
- Growing fish gain access to more of the pond, because larger fish are barred from shallow or densely vegetated areas.

**Limnaea pereger (snail)**
- Numbers declined steadily through each generation, except in 1957 when numbers were unusually low, suggesting density-dependent mortality.
- The 1960 year-class was exceptionally numerous and ended with greater numbers than any previous year-class, despite predation over winter.
- The 1961, 1962 and 1963 (noted as "1968" in the OCR text, likely a scan error) year-classes were strong. The 1963 year-class produced very few young, and numbers have since been lower than at any previous time.
- The rise in numbers began before trout were introduced, so predation does not explain the initial increase or subsequent decline.
- Snails were an important trout food throughout winter, with consumption increasing as snails grew larger. Consumption fell in early summer and again in 1964, when surface food became more available.
- Spring size distributions (Fig. 4) show many very small snails when numbers are high. One 2 mm snail in 1964 suggested very little growth in nearly a year, supporting the growth-inhibition hypothesis.

**Lestes sponsa (damselfly)**
- Large catches occurred at most stations but not in *Littorella* beds, and nymphs appeared in every sample opened in the season in which predation was intense.
- The 1961 nymph catch was high, probably because nymphs had only just reached a size that attracted fish, and then predation sharply reduced the population.
- Only a small catch was recorded in 1963; a single nymph was found in fish stomachs in 1964, the first such record.
- In 1964, *Lestes* was absent from the *Myriophyllum* beds, where it had previously been numerous. The author concludes that predation restricted nymphs to places with cover.
- Of 13 stations shown in Fig. 5 (14 stations in total), data were available for 13.

**Rhantus exsoletus (beetle) and dytiscid larvae (Table 1)**
- Adult catches were 1 to 7 per year from 1955 to 1960, and 0 to 1 per year from 1961 to 1965.
- Dytiscid larval catches were 13 to 92 per year from 1955 to 1960, with 21 in 1961, then 0 in 1962 and 1963, and 9 in each of 1964 and 1965.
- The author attributes the loss to predation on larvae, which swim in open water and are visible to fish.

**Community-level conclusions**
- Trout introduction shortened the species list, and it was the scarce species that disappeared.
- Standing crop declined slightly, but production probably increased because common species such as *L. pereger* sustained stable breeding populations while providing fish with food.

**Limitations**
- Netting data are incomplete for the early years, and the trout population was not monitored continuously.
- The three species are presented as examples along a continuum, and causal links are partly inferred.
