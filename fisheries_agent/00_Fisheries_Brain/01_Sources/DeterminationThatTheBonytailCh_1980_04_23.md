## Executive Summary

This 1980 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final rule formally designates the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) as an **Endangered Species** under the [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]]. Historically distributed throughout the large turbid mainstream rivers of the [[Colorado River Basin]] (Wyoming, Utah, Colorado, Arizona, Nevada, California), the species had been extirpated from nearly its entire range by the time of listing. The only known extant population existed in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada border), consisting entirely of senescent adults with no documented successful reproduction. No [[Critical Habitat]] was designated because no location was found to meet the physical/chemical requirements for successful spawning and recruitment. The determination followed a multi-year public comment process (1978–1980) involving federal agencies, state fish and game departments, water development interests, and industry stakeholders.

## Background & Description

The source document is a regulatory/legal text and does not provide detailed morphometric or anatomical descriptions. However, it references key taxonomic clarifications:

- Taxonomic confusion historically existed regarding whether the bonytail chub was a full species or a subspecies of the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*).
- A 1979 study by Smith, Miller, and Sable ("Species relationships among fishes of the genus *Gila* in the Upper Colorado River Drainage") established that the **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*), **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*), and **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) are three taxonomically distinct species within the *Gila robusta* species complex.
- Remaining wild specimens observed/collected were uniformly classified as "large size class individuals," inferring an aging, senile population structure with no juvenile recruitment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic habitat**: Large, turbid mainstream rivers of the Colorado River Basin, characterized by alternating swift-water canyons with torrential rapids and slow, meandering, sand-bottomed stretches.
- **Microhabitat preference**: Eddies adjacent to fairly swift current within large turbid rivers.
- **Thermal requirement for spawning**: Approximately 65°F (~18°C); cold tailwaters below dams (e.g., below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]) do not provide adequate spawning temperatures.
- **Historic range**: [[Green River]] (Wyoming, Utah), [[Yampa River]] and [[Gunnison River]] (Colorado), [[Colorado River]] (Arizona, Nevada, California), [[Gila River]] and [[Salt River]] (Arizona).
- **Current range at time of listing**: Restricted to [[Lake Mohave]], with historic presence noted (but no recent evidence) in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Havasu]].
- **Trophic/predation interactions**: Larval bonytail chubs are likely predated upon by introduced species including the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) and [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus*); introduced [[Bass]], [[Sunfish]], and [[Catfish]] are also implicated as predators of juveniles, though less abundant in the upper basin.
- **Interspecific competition**: Exotic fish species now numerically dominate native species throughout the Colorado River Basin, contributing to habitat and resource competition pressures.

## Key Findings

1. **Population status**: No viable, reproducing populations remained anywhere in the Colorado River Basin as of 1980. The sole confirmed population (Lake Mohave) comprised only elderly adults; spawning behavior was observed but produced no surviving young.
2. **Primary threat — habitat alteration**: Damming and impoundment (notably [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], closed 1962, and [[Davis Dam]] forming Lake Mohave) converted free-flowing turbid river habitat into a series of reservoirs and cold tailwaters unsuitable for spawning. Reservoir stabilization near planned capacity (post-1966) correlated with the onset of the sharpest population decline in the [[Green River]] within [[Dinosaur National Monument]].
3. **Dewatering**: Portions of the lower basin, especially the [[Gila River]] system, were dewatered by irrigation diversions, directly eliminating in-stream flow habitat and extirpating local populations.
4. **Predation and competition**: Introduced/exotic fish species were identified as probable contributors to recruitment failure via predation on larval/juvenile bonytail chubs and general competitive exclusion.
5. **Last confirmed wild adult collection**: 1974, from [[Desolation Canyon]] on the [[Green River]], Utah; subsequent surveys (including a 1979 Arizona Cooperative Fishery Unit study) found no evidence of viable populations elsewhere, including confirming absence from [[Lake Havasu]].
6. **No Critical Habitat designated**: The Service found no location meeting the biological requirements for successful breeding, reproduction, and rearing of offspring, and thus designated no Critical Habitat under 50 CFR 402.02.
7. **Regulatory outcome**: Listing as Endangered (effective May 23, 1980) triggered protections under Section 9(a)(1) of the ESA (prohibitions on take, import/export, interstate commercial transport) and Section 7 interagency consultation requirements; no known federal actions were identified as being immediately affected.
8. **Stakeholder conflict**: Comments (16 total responses) were split — support was grounded in recognition of habitat degradation impacts; opposition centered on potential restrictions to water development (hydroelectric, shale oil industrial processing, agriculture, interbasin export) and disputes over data adequacy and the Service's historical role in exotic species introduction and native "rough fish" eradication programs (e.g., prior to Flaming Gorge Reservoir closure).
9. **Not applicable threat factors**: Overutilization (commercial/sport/scientific/educational), inadequacy of existing regulatory mechanisms, and other natural/manmade factors were all assessed as **not applicable** contributing causes — the dominant drivers were habitat modification and predation/competition from introduced species.
