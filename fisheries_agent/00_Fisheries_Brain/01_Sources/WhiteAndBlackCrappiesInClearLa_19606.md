# White and Black Crappies in Clear Lake, Iowa, Summer 1960

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pomoxis

## Executive Summary

This study compared [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*) and [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) in [[Clear Lake]], [[Iowa]], during summer 1960. It reports a population shift from black to white crappies over roughly a decade, a decline in black crappie growth rate while white crappie growth stayed stable, very similar diets in both species, and a rise in turbidity proposed as a likely driver of these changes. The work was intended to improve fisheries management of these important sport fish.

## Background & Description

The two species are closely related sport fish of the genus *Pomoxis*. The study aimed to find better management methods by examining food habits and growth rates. Fish were sampled from all parts of the lake, day and night, and compared against earlier data from [[Erickson 1952]] covering 1941 to 1950.

**Sampling and sample size**
- 347 white crappies and 58 black crappies were examined in summer 1960.
- About 90% of fish were taken with [[Trawling]] or [[Gill Netting]] (24-hour sets, once a week). The rest came from bag seining or electric shocking.
- White crappies were caught in roughly equal numbers by gill nets and trawl. About 70% of black crappies came from the trawl.
- The trawl was used only in deeper water, and gill nets were usually set near shore. No size difference was noted between the two gear types.
- Lengths, weights and scale samples were recorded for all fish. Stomachs were taken from about 35% of fish and preserved in formalin for examination under a binocular microscope.

**Age determination**
- Scales were read from plastic impressions using a microprojector. Growth at each [[Annulus Formation]] was back-calculated from a [[Body-Scale Relationship]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population shift.** The 1960 catch was 86% white and 14% black crappie. Erickson's 1941 to 1950 Clear Lake data showed 84% black and 16% white. The authors read this as a clear shift from black to white crappies between 1950 and 1960.

**Annulus timing.** White crappies taken on June 7, 15 and 23 had not yet formed an annulus. Most white crappies taken from June 9 onward had formed one. Only one black crappie, taken June 6, was still forming an annulus. All other black crappies taken after June 24 had completed theirs.

**Turbidity.** Secchi disk readings averaged 58 inches in summer 1951 ([[Pearcy 1953]]). Readings in 1958 to 1960 were 22 to 24 inches. The authors describe this as a significant increase in turbidity. Harlan and Speaker (1956) note that white crappies tolerate turbid water better than black crappies, which the authors offer as a possible explanation for the population shift.

**Related species.** [[Buchholz 1960]] documented a decline in growth of [[Yellow Bass]] in Clear Lake from 1951 to 1958. Yellow bass and black crappie both slowed during this period, but white crappie did not.

## Key Findings

### 1. Population shift from black to white crappie
- Ratio in 1960: 86 white to 14 black. Ratio in 1941 to 1950: 84 black to 16 white.
- Young-of-the-year seine hauls at weekly intervals yielded over 120 white fingerlings for every 12 black fingerlings. The authors conclude the shift is continuing.

### 2. Growth patterns
- **White crappie:** Ages 0 to 7 were found, and most fish beyond their first winter belonged to age group III (1957 year class). Growth rate appeared unchanged since 1950, though 1950 data covered only the first three age groups. Growth is about the same as other Iowa lakes but below the average for most lakes in other states ([[Carlander 1953]]).
- **Black crappie:** Ages 0 to 8 were found. Older year classes were better represented than younger ones, which the authors link to the species' relative decline. After reaching 7 to 8 inches, black crappies grow slowly in Clear Lake now, though growth before 1950 was much better. First- and second-year growth is good, but later growth lags behind other lakes.

### 3. Body-scale relationships
- **White crappie:** Slope 1.238, intercept 1.627 inches. Erickson (1952) reported slope 0.960 and intercept 1.51 inches. The back-calculation used an intercept of 1.6 inches via a nomograph ([[Carlander and Smith 1944]]).
- **Black crappie:** Slope 1.059, intercept 1.689 inches. Erickson (1952) reported slope 0.957 and intercept 0.44 inches.

### 4. Food habits
Diets of the two species were very similar, with the same three most important items in the same order: [[Cladocera]], fish, and insect larvae.

| Item | White crappie (n = 117 stomachs) | Black crappie (n = 30 stomachs) |
|---|---|---|
| Cladocera | 79% | 77% |
| Insect larvae or pupae | 47% | 57% |
| Fish or fish scales | 37% | 50% |
| Insect adults | 13% | 27% |
| Plant material | 19% | 7% |

- **White crappie:** One of 117 stomachs was completely empty. Cladocera made up half or more of contents in 49% of stomachs (mainly *Bosmina*, *Daphnia* and *Leptodora*, most abundant in June and July). Fish or fish scales made up half or more of contents in 22% of stomachs. The smallest crappie found eating fish was 4.7 inches long.
- **Black crappie:** Four of 30 stomachs were empty. Fish consumption rose from 6% in 1950 to 50% in 1960.
- Both species ate less Cladocera and more fish as summer progressed. Fish became abundant in stomachs after about July 20, when young-of-the-year of usable size were common.
- Plant material and insect adults never exceeded 5% of stomach contents.

### 5. Proposed drivers and conclusions
- The authors suggest [[Turbidity]] as a likely reason for the population shift, since white crappies tolerate turbid water better.
- The slowing growth of black crappie is linked to the same period of declining water clarity, alongside a similar decline in yellow bass growth.
- The stable white crappie growth rate and continued dominance of young white crappies point to a long-term shift in the fish community rather than a short-term fluctuation.
