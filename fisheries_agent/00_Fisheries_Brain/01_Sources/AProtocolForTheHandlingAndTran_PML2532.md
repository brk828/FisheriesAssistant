# Bonytail Handling and Transport Protocol for the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This protocol, recommended by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], sets out how field personnel should handle, hold, and transport wild [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) captured in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The default goal is to keep every captured bonytail alive and deliver it to a [[Refugia]] (holding facility) for recovery. The protocol covers required equipment, strict field identification criteria, holding and transport procedures, notification chains, and temporary and permanent holding facilities. It is administered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] through its [[Colorado River Fishery Project]].

## Background & Description

The bonytail is a native cyprinid of the Colorado River system and a federally listed endangered fish. Because wild individuals are rare, every capture is treated as a recovery opportunity. The protocol applies to all personnel, not only researchers. Anyone who encounters a bonytail is advised that they may need to keep the fish alive for two or more days before the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] can take possession. If the researcher judges the risks of holding the fish too great, the fish should be returned to the river.

The protocol is written around the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] and its main rivers: the [[Green River]], the [[Colorado River]], and the [[Yampa River]].

### Required Equipment

Cooperators should obtain the following from the [[Vernal CRFP Field Station]] or the [[Grand Junction CRFP Field Station]]:

- One 100 to 150-quart cooler
- One or two 23 cubic foot oxygen cylinders with regulator, rubber tubing, and carbon diffuser
- One live net
- One vernier caliper
- One identification key for *Gila* (attached to the original document)
- One container of anesthetic, either [[MS-222 Anesthetic]] or [[Quinaldine]]

### Field Identification Criteria

Field identification must strictly follow these criteria:

1. Minimum total length of 300 mm.
2. Dorsal and anal fin-ray counts of 10/10 or greater.
3. Either of two morphometric ratios:
   - (a) Caudal peduncle length divided by head length greater than 1.0 (after Minckley 1973), or
   - (b) Head length divided by caudal peduncle depth greater than 5.0 (after Minckley 1973).

Measurement methods follow [[Lagler Bardach and Miller 1977]] (*Ichthyology*, p. 421). To reduce stress, fish should be anesthetized with MS-222 or Quinaldine before measurements are taken.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Capture Zones

Field teams that plan to sample intensively with electrofishing, entanglement nets, seines, or airboats in river reaches where [[Humpback Chub]] are present, or where bonytail have been collected, must be ready to capture a bonytail and carry the full equipment set. Designated reaches are those described in the [[USFWS Razorback Sucker Sensitive Reach Document]] for humpback chub, sampled within the last five years. The listed reaches are:

- [[Westwater Canyon]] (Colorado River)
- [[Cataract Canyon]] (Colorado River)
- [[Moab Canyon]] (Colorado River)
- [[Black Rocks]] (Colorado River)
- [[Gray Canyon]] (Green River)
- [[Whirlpool Canyon]] (Green River)
- [[Yampa Canyon]] (Yampa River, to Cross Mountain)

River-mile ranges for these reaches are given in the source table, which is partly illegible in the scanned copy.

In reaches where bonytail have not been captured recently, teams are not required to carry the full equipment set. If a bonytail is captured without the proper equipment, the field leader decides whether recovery is feasible. If recovery is not feasible, the fish's welfare comes first. If the fish is uninjured, its measurements and photographs should be recorded and it should be returned alive as soon as possible.

If the capture site is remote, the fish should not be left for later retrieval. Transporting it becomes the top priority, regardless of inconvenience.

### Holding and Transport Procedures

- Anesthetized fish are held in a cooler or container filled with river water, preferably from the capture area.
- Handling is kept to a minimum. Anesthetic should be used only when the fish meets the minimum criteria.
- Water temperature is controlled with crystal ice, frozen bottles in leak-proof containers, or cooler insulation. River water from the capture area can be added.
- If holding will last more than about 12 hours, or if conditions require it, the holding container can be partially submerged in the surrounding water.
- Tarred or knotted holding nets should not be used, because they abrade the fish.
- The container stays closed except when water is added or the fish is inspected. Water should be partly exchanged every one to several hours, at the discretion of the individual, to provide agitation and aeration. Water replenishment is not recommended unless the fish shows respiratory stress, such as rapid ventilation or gasping at the surface.
- Capture-site information (date, time, location, and notes) must be recorded and sent with the fish or to the final recipient.

### Notification and Logistics

The [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] coordinates the transport of fish from the river to temporary and permanent facilities. Sampling teams must notify the appropriate Service facility before each trip. Trip notes should include departure and return dates, trip length, and the person in charge.

- Teams sampling the Green and Yampa rivers notify the project leader at the Vernal office (Harold Tyus, or acting leader).
- Teams sampling the Colorado River subbasin notify the project leader at the Grand Junction office (Lynn Kaeding, or acting leader).

Field workers should understand that Service pickup may take one or more days, depending on location, and must be prepared to maintain the fish until then.

### Transport Equipment

A 250-gallon fish distribution tank mounted in a pickup truck moves bonytail from the river takeout point to the temporary holding facility. Vernal CRFP handles transport from the Green and Yampa rivers, and Grand Junction CRFP handles transport from the Colorado River.

### Temporary Holding Facilities

The designated temporary facilities are:

- [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] (Green River subbasin), Vernal, Utah
- [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] (Colorado River subbasin), Hotchkiss, Colorado

At Hotchkiss, fish are kept in tanks with a continuous supply of groundwater. At Ouray, they are held in ponds isolated from the mainstem Green River or in circular tanks. Staff at both facilities should be ready to treat fish for infections, fungal infections, and bacterial invasion of abraded areas.

If [[Lernaea]] (parasitic copepods) are present, fish are anesthetized and the parasites are removed. Immature Lernaea may be present but not visible. Any parasite removal must be recorded and reported when fish reach the permanent facility, so that proper treatment can follow.

### Permanent Holding Facility

Fish are moved from the temporary facility to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in Dexter, New Mexico, by airplane or by fish distribution tank and truck. The transfer should happen within two weeks of capture, or sooner if possible. At Dexter, fish should be permanently marked for identification.

## Key Findings

- **Protocol purpose:** Keep all wild bonytail alive and deliver them to refugia for recovery.
- **Holding period:** Field personnel may need to hold a bonytail for two or more days before pickup.
- **Identification standards:** A bonytail must be at least 300 mm total length, have dorsal and anal fin-ray counts of 10/10 or greater, and meet one of two morphometric ratios (caudal peduncle/head length > 1.0, or head length/caudal peduncle depth > 5.0).
- **Equipment:** Teams working in likely bonytail reaches must carry a 100 to 150-quart cooler, oxygen cylinder, live net, vernier caliper, identification key, and anesthetic.
- **Priority reaches:** Seven reach groups on the Colorado, Green, and Yampa rivers require full equipment.
- **Handling rules:** Minimal handling, anesthesia only when criteria are met, controlled water temperature, avoidance of abrasive nets, and aeration by periodic water exchange.
- **Transfer timeline:** Fish should reach the permanent facility within two weeks.
- **Responsibility:** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Colorado River Fishery Project coordinates the operation, and the Vernal and Grand Junction field stations handle transport from their respective basins.
- **Facilities:** Temporary holding is at Ouray NWR and Hotchkiss NFH, and permanent holding is at Dexter NFH, New Mexico.
