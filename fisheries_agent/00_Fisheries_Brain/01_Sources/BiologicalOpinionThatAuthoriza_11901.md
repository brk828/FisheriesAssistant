# Biological Opinion: Gila Topminnow and Dos S Unit Allotment Management Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #endangered-species #consultation

## Executive Summary

This biological opinion, issued February 14, 1994, by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Arizona Ecological Services State Office]], addresses formal consultation under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] regarding the proposed [[Allotment Management Plan]] for the Dos S Unit of the [[Sunflower Allotment]] on the [[Tonto National Forest]], [[Maricopa County]], Arizona. The consultation focuses on effects to the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), a small livebearing fish that persists at [[Mud Springs]] within the unit. The Service concludes that the proposed grazing management plan is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of the species, but anticipates incidental take through livestock trampling, water consumption, and habitat modification activities. The opinion includes mandatory terms and conditions to minimize take and improve topminnow habitat through construction of exclosed potholes and replacement of water delivery infrastructure.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] is a small (one to two inches long) livebearing fish of the family [[Poeciliidae]] (Minckley 1973). Listed as endangered on March 11, 1967, the species historically occurred in the Gila, Sonora, and de la Concepcion River drainages in Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico. Once among the most common fishes in the [[Gila River]] and its tributaries (Hubbs and Miller 1941), the species has been extirpated throughout most of its range due to habitat destruction (water diversion, stream downcutting, channelization, impoundment) and competition/predation by nonnative fish species, particularly [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) (Meffe et al. 1983).

At present, only 9 naturally occurring populations persist in the United States, with approximately 20 reintroduced populations. Eight of the nine natural populations are small and isolated; only three are free of mosquitofish. Reintroduction success has been low, with only about 9% survival of over 300 sites stocked since the 1930s.

At [[Mud Springs]] (SE1/4 Section 26, T.5N., R.8E), 200 Gila topminnow from [[Boyce-Thompson Arboretum]] (mixed stock, primarily [[Monkey Springs]] derivation) were stocked on June 9, 1982. The population has persisted for 12 years in a concrete stock watering trough (approximately 21 feet long, 3 feet wide, 18 inches deep, double compartment) and surrounding marshy area. Water is supplied via buried pipeline from a springbox in the seep area at an estimated rate of 700 gallons per day (possibly less currently). The trough is located within cattle corrals and has been continuously used by livestock throughout the topminnow occupation period. Population levels fluctuate from few to abundant. During hot dry periods, topminnow may be restricted to the trough when the marshy area desiccates.

The 1982 stocking was conducted under a 1981 Memorandum of Understanding between USFS, USFWS, and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] regarding Gila topminnow reintroductions. However, Mud Springs was not formally listed in the biological opinion covering that MOU (an error listed "Mud Spring," a different, unsuitable site). This created confusion about Section 7 coverage and the regulatory status of the population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Dos S Unit]] encompasses approximately 80,000 acres straddling [[Sycamore Creek]] northeast of Phoenix, with elevations ranging from 1,540 to 6,240 feet. Vegetation types include Sonoran desertscrub, mesquite bosque, mixed broadleaf deciduous riparian, interior chaparral, and desert grassland.

Current grazing authorization is 650 head plus natural increase under year-round grazing with two roundups (spring and fall). The proposed action would implement a rest-rotation system using seven pastures with an initial stocking rate of 450 cow/bulls. Two separate [[Santa Rita Grazing System]]s would manage three north pastures ([[Maverick Pasture]], [[Log Corral Pasture]], [[Pine Creek Pasture]]) and three south pastures ([[Otero Pasture]], [[Picadilla Pasture]], [[Adams Pasture]]), providing six months of use followed by twelve months of rest. [[Sycamore Pasture]] along Sycamore Creek would be closed to grazing for the first 10 years except for livestock movement between upland pastures (twice yearly, less than one day per crossing, no holding in riparian pasture).

The proposal includes extensive infrastructure development: 3 water storage tanks filled from State Route 87, 3 wells with submersible pumps and distribution systems, 10 trick tanks or saddle tanks, 2 miles of pipeline extensions, 5 spring developments, 5 corrals, and 20 miles of fencing (including 12 cattleguards). Construction would be phased over six years. Water storage tanks, wells, and developed springs would be fenced to exclude livestock. Spring development troughs would include wildlife ramps and float valves or overflow piping.

Monitoring would include repeat surveys of riparian vegetation on Sycamore Creek (baseline plus years 5 and 10), annual rephotography of 25 photopoints along Sycamore Creek, and production-utilization studies on upland vegetation beginning year 7.

The environmental baseline indicates heavy recreational use along much of Sycamore Creek with serious resource damage from off-highway vehicles, though this does not currently appear to impact Mud Springs. A proposed gravel pit, aquaculture facility, and resort on nearby private land (approximately 0.5 miles away on Sycamore Creek) could increase recreational use and introduce nonnative fish sources.

## Key Findings

**Biological Opinion Conclusion**: Authorization and implementation of the proposed Allotment Management Plan for the Dos S Unit is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of the Gila topminnow. No critical habitat has been designated for this species.

**Species Status Assessment**: The status of Gila topminnow is poor. The Mud Springs population is considered important to species survival and recovery despite occupying artificial habitat. The 12-year persistence demonstrates the site can support topminnow beyond short-term, particularly given failures at sites with apparently higher quality habitat.

**Mixed Effects Anticipated**:

*Adverse Effects*:
- Continued livestock use of trough and marshy area would maintain disturbance and remove water through consumption
- Increased cattle numbers under proposed management may increase adverse impacts
- Short-term mortality possible during pothole construction depending on location
- Replacement of springbox/pipeline carries risk of reduced water delivery or system failure
- New trough may not immediately provide suitable habitat; removal of old trough before new trough proves suitable could cause extirpation
- Development of five additional springs may adversely affect their potential as topminnow recovery sites

*Beneficial Effects*:
- Construction and maintenance of exclosed pothole(s) would increase habitat size and diversity, reducing extirpation risk from water system failure
- Replacement of springbox, pipeline, and drinker would make water supply more secure
- Exclusion of livestock from Sycamore Creek and lower reaches of [[Rock Creek]] and Picadilla Creek may improve habitat for native fishes through increased channel stability and habitat diversity
- Fencing of developed springs may improve recovery potential at certain sites

**Incidental Take Statement**: Three categories of anticipated take:

1. **Livestock trampling and water consumption**: Take considered exceeded if water level drops more than 4 inches below lowest point of trough lip (quantification by fish numbers not feasible due to sampling limitations and rapid population fluctuations)

2. **Pothole construction**: Anticipated take of all Gila topminnow within 20-foot radius of each pothole

3. **Springbox/pipeline/trough replacement**: Worst-case anticipated take includes all Gila topminnow at Mud Springs

**Mandatory Terms and Conditions** (11 specific requirements implementing 3 reasonable and prudent measures):

- Pothole and water system replacement must be completed **before** implementing grazing management in Picadilla and Pine Creek pastures
- Service concurrence required for pothole construction details
- Qualified fishery biologist must be present during pothole layout and construction
- Minimize heavy machinery surface area during construction
- Existing water system must remain unaltered until topminnow establish in replacement facilities (Service concurrence required before removal)
- Replacement trough must be concrete, sized for adequate depth relative to inflow
- New trough must be inoculated with plants, silt, and organisms from old trough
- Supplemental stocking from Service/AGFD-identified sources required
- Annual monitoring of topminnow populations (presence/absence, relative abundance in trough, marshy area, potholes)
- Daily monitoring during construction, monthly for three months post-construction
- Periodic water level monitoring when livestock use Picadilla, Pine Creek, or holding pastures
- Regular inspection and maintenance of springboxes, pipelines, troughs, and exclosure fencing
- Annual reporting to Service of water levels, inspection/maintenance records, livestock use timing and amounts
- Written construction records with before/after photos, maps, and project descriptions due within 60 days of completion

**Conservation Recommendations** (discretionary):
1. Pursue construction of barriers to upstream fish movement on Rock and Picadilla Creeks
2. Evaluate all springs on Dos S Unit for potential as topminnow recovery habitat

**Non-Concurrence on Mexican Spotted Owl**: The Service does not concur with USFS finding of "no effect" for [[Mexican Spotted Owl]] (*Strix occidentalis lucida*). [[Pine Creek Pasture]] contains suitable MSO habitat overlapping a management territory at its edge, plus additional suitable habitat north of [[Pine Mountain]]. At least two water improvements are planned within one mile of suitable habitat. Effects of livestock grazing on MSO and habitat remain largely unknown but could include vegetative structure alteration through browsing/trampling and changes in small mammal prey populations (particularly *Neotoma* spp., *Peromyscus* spp., *Microtus* spp.). Ganey (1992) concluded that management activities reducing small mammal populations in MSO-inhabited areas should be avoided and that effects of livestock grazing on herbaceous vegetation and *Microtus* abundance should be evaluated.

**Reinitiation Required If**: (1) incidental take exceeded; (2) new information reveals effects not considered; (3) action modified causing unconsidered effects; or (4) new species listed or critical habitat designated that may be affected.

**Literature Cited**: 11 references spanning 1941-1993, including key works on Gila topminnow status (Brooks 1985, 1986; Simons 1987; Bagley et al. 1991; Brown and Abarca 1992), ecology and decline (Hubbs and Miller 1941; Minckley 1973; Meffe et al. 1983; Vrijenhoek et al. 1985), recovery planning (USFWS 1984), and Mexican spotted owl food habits (Ganey 1992).
