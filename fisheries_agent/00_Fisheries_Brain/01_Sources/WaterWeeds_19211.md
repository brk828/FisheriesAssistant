# Water Weeds

#aquatic-ecology #freshwater-biology #aquatic-vegetation #lake-management

## Executive Summary

This article from the New York State Conservation Department discusses the challenges of aquatic weed control in freshwater systems. Written by Chief Aquatic Biologist John R. Greeley, it addresses the subjective nature of what constitutes a "weed," the complexity of control methods, and the ecological necessity of aquatic plants. The piece emphasizes that complete eradication is neither practical nor desirable, as aquatic vegetation forms the foundation of freshwater ecosystems. The article responds to widespread public inquiries about weed control while acknowledging the lack of simple, universal solutions.

## Background & Description

Aquatic weeds are defined as plants growing where they interfere with human activities or beliefs. The article notes that many aquatic plants serve dual roles—[[Potamogeton]] (pondweeds such as [[Sago Pondweed]]) are purchased by duck hunters to attract waterfowl, yet these same plants create problems for swimmers and beach owners. The document distinguishes between different types of aquatic vegetation: microscopic plankton flora (diatoms and algae), emergent flowering plants ([[Water Lilies]], [[Lotus]], [[Pickerel Weed]]), and submerged vegetation. The author emphasizes that plant life is a biological necessity in waters, with tons of invisible plants making up the plankton flora even in waters without visible weed beds. [[Sacandaga Reservoir]] is cited as producing fish abundantly in early years before weed beds developed, supported initially by diatoms, algae and other small plants.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Aquatic plants respond to fertilizers like terrestrial plants, and many waters are receiving increased nutrients from agricultural runoff, cottage septic tanks, and other human activities. Where plant nutrients and light penetration exist, aquatic plants will grow. The article stresses the ecological importance of aquatic vegetation: plants liberate oxygen essential for aquatic life, diatoms and algae coating stones provide food for mayfly nymphs which feed trout, and the entire aquatic food web depends on plant productivity. The document warns against complete plant removal, noting that waters without plants become biological deserts. Plant life carries on competition among species, similar to terrestrial ecosystems, where removing one species allows others to fill the space.

## Key Findings

**Control Methods and Limitations:**
- No "sure-fire experts" exist for aquatic weed control; practical solutions are limited despite extensive public inquiries to the Conservation Department
- Physical modifications include: bottom changes (sand drawing for beaches), light penetration reduction (experimental use of black dye nigracine), and fertility manipulation (heavy fertilization to stimulate plankton that blocks light to bottom weeds)
- Direct control methods: manual pulling, cutting with specialized equipment (weed saws, wire cables, scythes), and chemical treatment with 2-4-D preparations (safe for fish)
- Copper sulphate (blue vitreol) is effective only against small plants (algae and diatoms), not heavy weeds like pondweeds and water lilies; improper use can kill fish through direct toxicity or oxygen depletion from decomposing organic matter
- All methods are subject to local application only; rapid recolonization occurs from fragments, seeds, tubers, or rootstocks

**Management Philosophy:**
- Complete removal of aquatic weeds in large water bodies is "too fantastic to be a practical goal"
- Successful weed control must aim for partial change rather than complete removal
- Future management should work like forestry—removing certain species while favoring others
- First step is to identify which plant types are problematic and which should be encouraged
- Requirements for successful methods include permanence of results, safety to humans and fish, and reasonable application costs

**Practical Applications:**
- Copper sulphate has limited use early in season for reservoirs with microscopic plants causing taste or odor problems
- Heavy fertilization can create plankton blooms that suppress bottom weeds (field check: if you can see your hand underwater, water is too clear)
- Local weed control creates temporary clearings that rapidly refill, similar to vacant garden spaces
