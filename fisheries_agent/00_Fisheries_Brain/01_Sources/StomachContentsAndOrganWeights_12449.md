# Stomach Contents and Organ Weights of Bluefin Tuna, *Thunnus thynnus*, near Bimini, Bahamas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Scombridae

## Executive Summary
This 1959 study by Louis A. Krumholz examines the stomach contents and relative organ (viscera) weights of seven [[Bluefin Tuna]] (*Thunnus thynnus*) captured during the 1956 Bimini Tuna Tournament in the Bahamas. The research documents an unusually varied diet dominated by juvenile [[Porcupine Fish]] (*Diodon hystrix*) and salps, contrasting with prior records emphasizing finned schooling fishes like herring and mackerel. Additionally, the paper provides comparative visceral weight data, revealing that bluefin tuna possess a proportionally larger heart than related pelagic billfishes (marlins, sailfish) and freshwater species, potentially linked to thermoregulatory physiology, despite having the lowest overall relative visceral mass among all fishes compared.

## Background & Description
### Specimen Data (Table 1)
Seven bluefin tuna were examined, ranging from 88 to 100 inches in length and 361 to 565 lbs in weight (Tuna No. 1 excluded from weight analysis due to shark mutilation).

| Tuna No. | Length (in.) | Weight (lbs) | Sex |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 92 | ca. 470 | Male |
| 2 | 93 | 470 | Female |
| 3 | 100 | 565 | Male |
| 4 | 92 | 450 | Male |
| 5 | 88 | 361 | Male |
| 6 | 95 | 370 | Male |
| 7 | 97 | 562 | Female |

- **Sex ratio:** 5 males, 2 females
- **Gonad condition:** All specimens (both sexes) showed evidence of near-spawning or recently post-spawning condition — females' ovaries indicated recent spawning; males' testes contained mature sperm.

| Caecal mass | 0.663 |
| Intestine | 0.105 |
| Liver | 0.582 |

| Spleen | 0.136 |
| **Subtotal (non-gonadal viscera)** | **2.569** |
| Testes | 1.003 |
| Ovaries | 1.079 |
| **Grand Total (all viscera + gonads)** | **3.571** |

- Entire gut (stomach + caecal mass + intestine) = **1.50%** of total body weight.
- All viscera combined (excluding kidneys) = **3.57%** of total body weight.
- Gonads contributed more to body weight than any single other organ.
- **Heart size is notably large** relative to body weight compared to other pelagic and freshwater fishes — nearly **twice** the relative heart weight of [[Atlantic Marlins]] or the [[Sailfish]] (*Istiophorus americanus*).
  - Example: A 361-lb bluefin tuna heart weighed 524 g vs. a 360-lb [[Blue Marlin]] (*Makaira ampla*) heart at only 265 g.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Trophic role:** Opportunistic, wide-ranging pelagic predator capable of foraging across multiple depth strata — from surface (salps, juvenile porcupine fish) to bottom-associated organisms (crabs, octopus, snail radulae per deSylva 1956).
- **Habitat:** Open ocean pelagic zone near Bimini/Cat Cay, Bahamas; also documented in Gulf of Maine (Bigelow & Schroeder 1953) and Bermuda (Beebe 1936) with markedly different regional diets.
- **Regional diet variation:**
  - **Bimini, Bahamas (this study):** Dominated by juvenile [[Porcupine Fish]] (*Diodon hystrix*) and salps (*Pyrosoma atlantica gigantea*); notable absence of finned schooling fish (herring, mackerel, mullet) despite tuna being caught using mullet bait.
  - **Portland, Maine (Crane 1936):** Principal food was hake (*Merluccius bilineatus*), 8–13 inches long; also squid, krill, rosefish, clupeids.
  - **Gulf of Maine (Bigelow & Schroeder 1953):** Herring and mackerel as dominant prey.
  - **Cat Cay, Bahamas (deSylva 1956):** Squid remains and bottom-dwelling snail radulae; noted squid, flying fish, sardines, herring, krill as preferred items.
  - **Bermuda ([[Blackfin Tuna]], *Parathunnus atlanticus*; Beebe 1936):** Surprising dominance of bottom-associated squilla larvae despite pelagic swimming habits — parallels the bottom/surface dietary versatility seen in this bluefin study.
- **Thermal physiology:** Bluefin tuna maintain body temperatures 6–12°C above ambient water temperature (per Brown 1957); the paper speculates the disproportionately large heart may support this thermoregulatory capacity.
- **Prey aggregation behavior:** Juvenile porcupine fish (~walnut-sized, ~5g each) are known to aggregate near the surface, making them vulnerable to mass predation — consistent with the tuna having consumed up to 275 individuals in a single stomach.

## Key Findings
1. **Diet composition:** A total of 661 food organisms across 7 species were recovered from 7 tuna stomachs. No single stomach contained fewer than 2 or more than 4 food types.
   - *Diodon hystrix* (porcupine fish): 560 individuals — **85% of total count, >90% of total food weight**.
   - *Pyrosoma atlantica gigantea* (salp): 87 individuals in 4 stomachs.
   - Eel-like fish vertebral columns (unidentified species): 5 specimens across 3 stomachs (6–8.5 in. long).
   - Unidentified salp species: 3 specimens in 1 stomach.
   - *Portunus* sp. (portunid crabs): 4 specimens in 1 stomach.
   - Unidentified octopus beak: 1 specimen.
   - Unidentified plant leaf: 1 specimen (~5 in. long, crescent-shaped).
2. **No finned schooling fish** (herring, mackerel, mullet) were found in stomach contents, contradicting prior regional accounts (Crane 1936; Bigelow & Schroeder 1953) that emphasized such prey — despite tuna being caught using mullet bait, indicating opportunistic rather than bait-driven natural foraging.
3. Findings support conclusion that bluefin tuna diet is **highly variable geographically and temporally**, reflecting local prey abundance rather than fixed dietary preference.
4. **Comparative organ weight analysis (Table 3)** across bluefin tuna, [[Atlantic Marlins]], [[Sailfish]], and seven freshwater species (yellow bullhead, carp, largemouth bass, bluegill, redhorse, white crappie, black crappie) showed:
   - Bluefin tuna had the **lowest relative combined visceral weight** (excluding gonads) of all species compared.
   - Bluefin tuna had the **largest relative heart size** of all species compared — nearly double that of carp or sailfish, the next largest.
   - Digestive organs of white marlin and sailfish weighed **more than twice** as much (relatively) as those of bluefin tuna; blue marlin's digestive organs were **~75% heavier** relatively.
   - Marine vs. freshwater fish showed marked differences in relative liver and gall bladder size, likely linked to differing digestive processes.
5. **Reproductive status:** All seven tuna showed gonadal conditions indicating recent or imminent spawning, suggesting the Bimini tournament period (May) coincides with the bluefin tuna spawning season in this region.
6. Findings align with the general ecological principle (Bigelow & Schroeder 1953) that tuna prey preferentially on **locally abundant** organisms rather than a fixed set of preferred taxa, as also demonstrated in comparative studies of [[Blackfin Tuna]] and [[Yellowfin Tuna]] (*Neothunnus argentivittata*) diets near Bermuda and St. Lucia (Beebe 1936).
