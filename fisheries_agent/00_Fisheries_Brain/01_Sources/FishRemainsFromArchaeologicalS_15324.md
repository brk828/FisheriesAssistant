# Fish Remains from Archaeological Sites in the Lower Colorado River Basin, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 paper by [[Robert Rush Miller]] identifies fish remains recovered from three Arizona archaeological sites: [[Catclaw Cave]] on the lower [[Colorado River]], the Hohokam settlement of [[Snaketown]], and the Sobaipuri village of [[Quiburi]]. Using skeletal elements, Miller confirms that prehistoric peoples ate at least three native species, the [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]], and documents how these fishes were used as food before the river was transformed by dams and introduced species. The Snaketown sturgeon record is rejected as a misidentification.

## Background & Description

The lower Colorado River was historically a swift, silt-laden stream with large fluctuations in flow and turbidity. Construction of [[Hoover Dam]] and [[Davis Dam]], together with a large bait fishery established in 1950, converted it into a series of deep reservoirs with controlled flow. Competition and predation by introduced game and forage fishes further reduced the native fauna. Only three native species (one sucker and two minnows) were considered adapted to the extreme conditions of the lower river; a third minnow, evidently once present, is documented by the remains reported here.

The sites:
- **Catclaw Cave:** excavated in 1949 by the University of Arizona Department of Anthropology, on the east bank 15 miles below Hoover Dam and now inundated by [[Lake Mohave]]. The fish-bearing level was 2 feet thick, overlay at least 18 feet of sterile sand, and was dated by comparison with [[Willow Beach]] to approximately A.D. 750–1100.
- **Snaketown:** a Hohokam site on the Gila River, occupied from about 300 B.C. to A.D. 1400.
- **Quiburi:** a Sobaipuri site (c. A.D. 1150–1760) on the San Pedro River, Cochise County.

Catclaw Cave yielded over 375 bones and fragments; about 100 were identified to species level.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Historically, the lower Colorado and Gila rivers held large, abundant native fishes. Before dam construction the river retained a powerful current for roughly 20 miles below Boulder Canyon, but below that point the river broadened and likely could not sustain a permanent [[Humpback Chub]] population. The San Pedro River was a large perennial stream in the 1840s but became intermittent after erosion began around 1880 (Bryan, 1925). Miller notes that in much of the Colorado system the absence of other food fishes meant the suckers and squawfish were of disproportionate dietary importance.

Hunting and fishing methods recorded include dipnets used in receding waters after floods, bow and arrow, and spearing with pitchforks. Squawfish lie in deep holes below riffles and move close to shore to feed on drift material when the river rises.

## Key Findings

- **Humpback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** the most important food fish, attaining about 3 feet and 10–14 pounds (up to 16 pounds). The neural complex, a diagnostic bone, indicates at least seven individuals at Catclaw Cave, with the largest about 2–3 feet long and 10–12 pounds. Charred bones confirm consumption by Indigenous people. A commercial fisherman reported specimens up to 14 pounds and over 30 inches from Sahuaro Lake in 1949.
- **Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):** described as "one of the largest minnows in the world," with reported lengths of 4–6 feet. Remains at Catclaw Cave represent at least six individuals. The largest pharyngeal arch fragments imply a fish at least 5 feet 6 inches (516 feet as printed in the OCR text; likely 5½ feet) long. Thirty-one vertebrae at Quiburi, the largest 34.5 mm, suggest a total length of about 5 feet.
- **Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*):** identified from two pharyngeal arches, each from a different individual, measuring 24.0 mm and 26.3 mm, implying a total length of 17–18 inches. Miller notes that recognition as a distinct species is open to question and may reflect an extreme form within the *Gila robusta* subspecies series.
- **Flannelmouth Sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*):** probably occurred in the lower Colorado, based on modern distribution in the Gila basin and tributaries above Lake Mead, but was not positively identified in the cave material; specimens were used only for comparison.
- **Bonytail (*Gila robusta elegans*):** used for comparison only. Its pharyngeal arch differs from that of *G. cypha*, and the species is not known to exceed about 1¼ feet at Quiburi.
- **Snaketown sturgeon record rejected:** earlier identification by [[Glover M. Allen]] as sturgeon (*Acipenser*) is considered erroneous on zoogeographic grounds, since sturgeon are rare south of San Francisco Bay and do not enter freshwater south of the Sacramento system. Miller argues the bones were Colorado squawfish and humpback sucker vertebrae. These bones were subsequently lost.
- **Quiburi:** charred and marked vertebrae support the view that fish were eaten by the Sobaipuri. A 1846 report of "salmon trout" about 3 feet long from the San Pedro suggests squawfish occurred there at that time.
- **Conclusions:** the paper establishes that at least three native fishes were important prehistoric food sources in the lower Colorado and Gila basins, and that these species were probably eliminated or reduced by later environmental change and introduced fishes. Miller calls for more ichthyological surveys of major western rivers.
