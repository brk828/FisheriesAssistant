# Roosevelt Diversion Dam Removal: Environmental Assessment and Agency Correspondence

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1987 environmental assessment (EA) by SWCA, Inc. for the [[Salt River Project]] (SRP) on public-safety modifications to the [[Roosevelt Diversion Dam]] on the Salt River, Gila County, Arizona. The dam had caused five fatalities and five injuries since 1979, all recreation-related. Four alternatives were evaluated: the Agency Proposed Action (full demolition, Alternative 1), dam modification with fencing (Alternative 2), fencing alone (Alternative 3), and No Action (Alternative 4). Alternative 1 was selected as the proposed action because it removes the hazard. The EA itself acknowledges that it may cause unavoidable adverse impacts on native fishes, USGS stream gaging, and the Flathead Catfish fishery.

The appended correspondence contains a sharp critique from a fisheries scientist (a co-author of the EA's fish sections), who argued that the fish assessment was superficial and that removal would open the upper Salt River to exotic fishes. That critique is backed by Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) letters expressing similar concerns.

## Background & Description

- **Historical context:** The Roosevelt Power Canal and Diversion Dam, built in 1906 under the [[National Reclamation Act]] of 1902, was the first hydroelectric project of the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. The 400-ft dam (7 ft high) was built on alluvium, was damaged by floods (230 ft washed out in 1916), was repaired in 1937, and was permanently withdrawn from service in 1956.
- **Recreation:** Roosevelt Lake recorded 688,000 annual recreation days in 1980. Warning measures (signs in 1981, an aerial cable sign in 1982, and four flotation buoys in 1986) did not prevent accidents.
- **Fatal incidents:** On 13 April (two fatalities, one injury) and 20 April (two fatalities, four injuries, an inflatable raft party swept over the dam), plus one further fatality on 2 July, reportedly near the north abutment. The EA states that turbulence below the dam was the primary factor.
- **Legal context:** A letter from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (20 December 1985) noted that the 9th Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed that public-safety responsibility for the dam does not rest with the United States.
- **Study area:** Channel width about 100 ft at the bridge, widening to about 600 ft at the dam; about 45 acres of vegetation and 25 acres of nonvegetated channel.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation and wildlife:** Vegetation is dominated by [[Salt Cedar]]-mixed scrub (about 28 acres), [[Honey Mesquite]] association (about 6 acres), and [[Paloverde-Mixed Cactus]] series (about 11 acres). Alternative 1 would disturb less than 10 acres. About 40 vertebrate species are listed (mammals, birds, amphibians, reptiles, fishes). Special-status species include the federally endangered [[Bald Eagle]] and [[Peregrine Falcon]] (no adverse impacts expected), Desert Tortoise and Gila Monster (candidate or state-listed), and several native plants protected under Arizona native plant law.

**Fish community:** Riverine fishes above the dam are dominated by introduced species. Sampling by AGFD in May 1986 found that 59% of individuals were introduced (figures as printed in the source). Channel Catfish is the most prevalent introduced species. Flathead Catfish, including fish over nine pounds, made up 88% of the sampled fishes (9 of 12 individuals). Native fishes include [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Desert Mountain Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Longfin Dace]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. [[Razorback Sucker]] and Colorado Squawfish are experimentally stocked; the Colorado Squawfish is federally listed as endangered, but experimental-population status means it is not fully protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Hydrology and sediment:** The [[Salt River]] has a watershed of 4,305 square miles. The USGS gage 1 mile upstream of the dam is the only discharge instrument for the Salt River above Roosevelt Lake. Mean discharge over a 70-year period is 886 cfs (median of yearly means 680 cfs). Extreme values are 117,000 cfs (1941) and 59 cfs (1955). Removing the dam would release more than 200,000 cubic yards of sediment, degrading the channel by over seven feet and threatening gage accuracy.

**Water quality:** Table 5 gives 1982 water-year data for the Salt River, but the content is largely numeric and its figures are not reproduced here.

## Key Findings

1. **Preferred action (Alternative 1, demolition):** Removes the hazard but may cause unavoidable adverse impacts on native fishes, USGS gaging, and the Flathead Catfish fishery. Demolition would take place in July-September 2 of the low-flow period, with about 60 days of noise and blasting.
2. **Alternatives 2 and 3 (fencing):** Considered inadequate. Barriers may not restrict access, and they would reduce visual quality.
3. **No Action (Alternative 4):** Rejected because it does not address the hazard; additional deaths or injuries were called "a strong possibility."
4. **Debris disposal:** Removal would generate about 18,000 cubic yards of debris. Of eight disposal options, seven were dropped (leaving debris in place, burying it at the wall, building an access road or parking lot, filling sinkholes, a fishing jetty, an underwater reef or lake disposal, and an on-forest site). The preferred option is off-Forest disposal, including private land near Rockhouse Store about one-half mile northwest of the dam.
5. **Task Force process:** In May 1986 SRP formed a Task Force with AGFD, USFWS, USBR, and USFS. Seventeen alternatives were initially considered. The primary criterion was public-safety mitigation.
6. **Fisheries critique:** The reviewing scientist argued that the EA's fish analysis was cursory and internally inconsistent, that the Alternative 1 "adverse impacts" statement contradicted the "no major impacts" conclusion, and that no mitigation concepts had been developed for native fishes. The critique recommended re-examining alternatives to keep the dam in place and noted that free upstream access for exotic fishes could be "potentially disastrous" to native fish populations and to reintroduction programs.
7. **AGFD position (19 September 1986):** Catfish and buffalo fish moving upstream could jeopardize native fish reintroduction. AGFD preferred fencing alternatives with a log boom and suggested modifying the dam (removing the cement blocks and extending the apron) as an alternative.
8. **USFS position (25 September 1986 and 8 June 1987):** Fencing alternatives would have no adverse effect on the National Forest if access and visual provisions were included. Removal could harm endangered Colorado Squawfish and candidate Razorback Sucker, and angler use could decline sharply because Channel Catfish would no longer concentrate below the dam.
9. **USFWS position (12 June 1987):** Alternatives 2 and 3 should have no impact on the nesting bald eagle pair, provided work is completed in July-August. Alternative 1 could alter prey distribution and size classes for the eagles, and exotic fishes could compete with native fishes.
10. **USGS position (8 July 1986):** Removing the dam would seriously affect gage accuracy for several years. USGS "strongly recommend[s]" leaving the dam undisturbed.
11. **Regulatory steps:** A Clean Water Act Section 404 permit application was submitted to the Army Corps of Engineers (Los Angeles District) for a Nationwide Permit. Historic American Engineering Record documentation was accepted by the National Park Service in December 1984. The SHPO concurred in November 1986 that BuRec's Section 106 obligations had been met for the Diversion Dam.
12. **Water rights:** The diversion serving two farming operations (Gila County parcels 204-7-1A and 204-7-1B) has unclear water rights that will not be settled until the Gila River Adjudication. SRP plans to negotiate with the landowner before removal.
