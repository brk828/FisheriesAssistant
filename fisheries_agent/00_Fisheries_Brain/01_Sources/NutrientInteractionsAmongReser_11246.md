## Executive Summary

This paper (Paulson & Baker) is a **limnological/nutrient-budget study**, not a species-specific fish paper. It examines how the impoundment of [[Lake Powell]] (1963, Glen Canyon Dam) altered downstream water quality, sediment, and nutrient loading in the Colorado River, and how these changes propagated to [[Lake Mead]] (impounded 1935, Hoover Dam), located 450 km downstream. The study demonstrates that **reservoirs interact serially in multi-reservoir river systems**: nutrient retention in an upstream reservoir directly reduces nutrient inputs (and thus productivity) in a downstream reservoir. Lake Mead's two basins (Upper/Virgin Basin and Lower/Boulder Basin) behave almost as separate lake ecosystems due to disproportionate nitrogen vs. phosphorus loading, with implications for [[Reservoir Fisheries Management]] and downstream sport fisheries productivity.

- Lake Mead is morphometrically divided by **Boulder Canyon** into an **Upper Basin** (Virgin Basin, Overton Arm, Upper Arm) and a **Lower Basin** (Boulder Basin, Las Vegas Bay).
- Lake Powell is highly sinuous; Lake Mead is basin-segmented — this structural difference strongly affects how nutrients and turbid inflows distribute within each reservoir.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology & Thermal Regime**
- Pre-1963 Colorado River discharge showed strong spring runoff peaks (May–June); post-Powell regulated releases eliminated this peak, stabilizing flow year-round.
- River temperatures below Glen Canyon Dam now run ~5°C warmer in late fall/winter but ~10°C colder in summer than pre-dam conditions (average discharge ~8°C at the dam, warming to 15–18°C at Pierce's Ferry entering Lake Mead).

**Sediment Dynamics**
- Historically up to 140 million metric tons/year of suspended sediment entered Lake Mead, mostly during spring; ~47% deposited near Pierce's Ferry (Lara & Sanders 1970).
- Post-Powell (after 1966 fill), suspended sediment loads to Lake Mead dropped 70–80%; most sediment now derives from Grand Canyon tributaries rather than the upper watershed.

**Nutrient Loading & Trophic Structure**
- Colorado River supplies ~85% of Lake Mead's inorganic nitrogen; **Las Vegas Wash** supplies ~60% of phosphorus input, despite being a minor flow contributor.
- This spatial decoupling of N and P sources, combined with basin morphometry, causes the Lower (Boulder) Basin to become **nitrogen-limited** in summer while the Upper (Virgin) Basin remains chronically **phosphorus-limited**.
- Thermocline dynamics: erosion of the thermocline (dropping 10–15 m, July–September) mixes metalimnetic nitrate into the epilimnion, boosting late-summer productivity in Boulder Basin (where P is more available) but only modestly in Virgin Basin (P-limited).
- A "turbid overflow" plume that historically extended through Lake Mead's Upper Basin during spring floods has now shifted upstream and occurs instead in [[Lake Powell]], due to reduced sediment/discharge and cooler, denser underflow.

**Cross-Reservoir Nutrient Trapping**
- Lake Powell retains ~70% of dissolved phosphorus inputs via phytoplankton uptake and possible calcite coprecipitation, sharply reducing P delivery to Lake Mead's Upper Basin.
- Nitrate loading to Lake Mead decreased sharply post-1963, partially recovered 1966–1969 (possibly due to reduced retention or transient sediment N release in Lake Powell), then declined again — tracking a pattern attributed to biogeochemical processes within Lake Powell rather than hydrologic changes (conservative ions like Na⁺/K⁺ showed no comparable trend).

## Key Findings

1. **Serial reservoir nutrient interception**: Formation of an upstream reservoir (Lake Powell) reduces nutrient (especially N) and sediment loading to a downstream reservoir (Lake Mead) 450 km away, altering its trophic status.
2. **Basin-specific nutrient limitation** in Lake Mead: Lower Basin (Boulder Basin) → nitrogen-limited in summer (driven by Las Vegas Wash phosphorus dominance + reduced Colorado River nitrate); Upper Basin (Virgin Basin) → phosphorus-limited (driven by Lake Powell's phosphorus retention).
3. **Productivity gradient**: Areal phytoplankton productivity and phosphorus concentrations in Boulder Basin are roughly twice those in Virgin Basin; productivity decreases from the Upper Arm to Virgin Basin, then increases toward Hoover Dam.
4. **Historical inference**: Prior to Lake Powell, the Colorado River likely delivered enough phosphorus to sustain higher, farther-reaching productivity in Lake Mead's Upper Arm; the Lower Basin was likely most productive in the late 1960s (elevated Las Vegas Wash phosphorus + still-elevated Colorado River nitrate), with subsequent nitrogen limitation suppressing summer productivity since the early 1970s.
5. **Sediment trapping**: Lake Powell intercepts the majority of the Colorado River's sediment load, causing the classic "turbid overflow" phenomenon (previously documented in Lake Mead by Anderson & Pritchard 1951) to now occur in Lake Powell instead.
6. **Management implication**: Multi-reservoir systems on a single river (e.g., the [[Colorado River]] mainstem) must be managed collectively — upstream nutrient retention can be beneficial (reducing eutrophication) or detrimental (undermining sport fisheries productivity) downstream, directly relevant to [[Reservoir Fisheries Management]] and water quality regulation for systems like [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]].
