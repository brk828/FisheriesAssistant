# Fishery in Lake Wappapello, Missouri (1948–1951)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study assesses the fish community of [[Lake Wappapello]], a flood-control impoundment on the [[St. Francis River]] in southeastern Missouri, created in 1941 by the [[Corps Of Engineers]]. The [[Missouri Conservation Commission]] began investigations in 1948 after anglers reported declining success, particularly for black bass. Four years of net, seine and [[Rotenone Census]] sampling, plus creel census data from 1946–1951, were analysed to evaluate population structure, growth, and harvest.

The fish community was dominated by [[White Crappie]] and [[Carp]] in nets, and by non-game species (especially buffalo) in backwaters. Angler harvest was almost entirely crappie, and few anglers targeted black bass. The authors concluded that a substantial non-game resource was underharvested and recommended that a commercial fishery be considered.

## Background & Description

**Reservoir characteristics**
- Created in 1941 as part of the lower Mississippi River flood-control program.
- Conservation-pool level (355 ft Mean Gulf Level): surface area given as approximately 6,000 acres in the abstract and 5,700 acres in the introduction. Expands to 23,100 acres at flood-pool elevation (395 ft).
- Relatively shallow: maximum depth about 15 ft outside former river and tributary channels; at least half the lake is 5 ft deep or less.
- Drainage area of 1,310 square miles in the Ozark Highlands, with about 65% of the upstream river corridor forested.
- Aquatic vegetation is scarce, limited by water-level fluctuation and bottom-feeding fishes that disturb mud.
- Water levels were managed for malaria control from 1943 and for waterfowl hunting (stabilised at 358.5 ft during the season). Extensive plantings of cultivated wild millet were made to attract waterfowl.

**Study rationale**
- Fishing was reported as excellent during the first four years, with good black bass catches. Success for black bass then declined.
- Sampling was motivated by the need for better population estimates, which are difficult to obtain for large waters.

**Sampling methods**
- *Gill nets*: single and graded mesh, 1–2 in bar measure, 6 ft deep, 150–300 ft long.
- *Hoop nets*: 1¼- or 2¼-in mesh, 5 ft front hoop, baited with cottonseed cake.
- *Trammel nets*: used in 1948 only.
- *Trap nets*: 3 ft and 4 ft, following [[Crowe Trap Net]] design, used in 1950–1951.
- *Seines*: 15, 25 and 100 ft; a 25-ft, ¼-in mesh bag seine was used extensively in 1950–1951.
- *Rotenone*: applied to 1 acre (1949), 0.79 acre (1951) and 1.3 acres (1951) of coves. Tagged fish were used to test recovery efficiency.
- Test netting lasted 10 days in late summer or fall each year (1948–1951).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Forage and community structure**
- The minnow fauna comprised few species. The most abundant were the Mississippi blacktail shiner, bullhead minnow and common emerald shiner.
- Forage fishes were mainly brook silversides, young [[Gizzard Shad]] and small sunfishes. The authors suggested that their midsummer abundance may explain poor hook-and-line fishing in summer.

**Reproduction and water-level stability**
- Reproduction was judged satisfactory for largemouth bass, and excellent for white crappie in 1951. Spotted bass reproduction was very limited.
- In April–May 1950, two high-water periods were followed by 10-ft drops over 24 and 10 days. The authors suggested that spawning success for carp, buffalo, black bass and crappie may have been curtailed.

**Backwater habitat**
- Backwater coves were shallow (maximum 7–10 ft at 357.95 ft elevation) with water temperatures of 71–76 °F during the September census.
- Vegetation and water-level instability were thought to limit insect and plant food for [[Bluegill]].

## Key Findings

### 1. Net catch composition (1948–1951)
- A total of 2,993 fish weighing 2,865.0 lb were taken in nets (Table 1, summarised).
- [[White Crappie]] comprised about 52% by number (1,554 fish) and [[Carp]] about 20% (612 fish).
- Carp were nearly 50% of net-catch weight in each year. Non-game fish made up over 60% of weight in each year and about 70% overall.
- The carp total was strongly influenced by baited hoop nets.

### 2. Hoop-net trends
- Carp catch rates were identical in 1948 and 1949, roughly doubled in 1950, and fell in 1951. Mean fish weight rose in 1951, suggesting a dominant year class in both 1950 and 1951.
- White crappie catch rates were about three times higher in 1950 than in 1948–1949. The lower average weight in 1950 indicated a strong recruitment pulse, repeated in 1951.

### 3. Rotenone census (backwaters, 1951; 2.09 acres total)
- Standing crop was 273 lb per acre, with about 180.1 lb per acre of large non-game fish.
- [[Smallmouth Buffalo]], [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] and [[Black Buffalo]] together were the largest component; buffalo made up 67% of non-game weight (122.4 lb/acre). Carp comprised 14% (26.8 lb/acre).
- Game fish of catchable size were 7.6 lb/acre for [[Largemouth Bass]] (black bass), 7.1 lb/acre for panfish (white crappie and bluegill) and 12.1 lb/acre for [[Channel Catfish]].
- The abstract and text give "9% of the population" as game fish of usable size. This figure is inconsistent in the source, since the text associates 26.8 lb/acre with it, a value also given for carp.
- Excluding gizzard shad, the coves supported 114 catchable-size fish per acre, 32 of them game fish.
- Recovery efficiency was poor: only 35% of 53 tagged fish were recovered. Since 46 of the 53 were crappies, no correction was applied. The authors noted that the population estimates are therefore probably underestimates by an unknown amount.

### 4. Juvenile and intermediate-size fish
- Largemouth bass of intermediate size were present at about 14 fish per acre, which the authors judged would have been available to creel anglers in 1952.
- Fingerlings were dominated by [[Bluegill]] (about 1,427 per acre) and [[Gizzard Shad]] (about 1,221 per acre). Largemouth bass fingerlings numbered 85 per acre, against 3 spotted bass.
- Largemouth bass fingerlings outnumbered spotted bass 4:1 in 1950 and 10:1 in 1951.

### 5. Growth and age
- Growth rates were assessed for 8 species from body-scale relationships (Table 5).
- Overall, growth was average compared with other Missouri impoundments, except for [[Bluegill]] and [[Gizzard Shad]], which were slow to grow.
- [[Bluegill]]: average length of 4.4 in after 3 summers; most needed 4 growing seasons to reach 5.5 in.
- [[Gizzard Shad]]: fast first-year growth, slow thereafter. Ten-year-old fish were captured, with the 1943 year class dominant. Shad under 6 years old made up only 20% of the collection.
- [[Carp]]: the 1946 year class was prominent. Fish 18–21 in long (presumed 5 years old) made up 34% of 135 carp in 1951.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] grew faster than [[Spotted Bass]]. In 1951, age-group III (1948 year class) supplied most of the harvest (48% of largemouth and 60% of spotted bass in the creel).
- [[White Crappie]] grew slightly below the Missouri impoundment average but reached 10.2 in by the end of year 3.

### 6. Creel census
- *State-wide census (1946–1950)*: 10,854 anglers interviewed, catch rate 0.45 fish per hour, 71% of anglers successful. Boat anglers and still-fishers each exceeded 80%. Live bait users made up at least 95%.
- *Intensive census (May–December 1951)*: 5,367 anglers contacted, 66% successful, catch rate 0.53 fish per hour. 91% fished from boats and 75% used natural baits only. Artificial lures accounted for 1% of effort.
- [[White Crappie]] made up over 80% of the catch every year (88.1% in 1951). Largemouth bass fell from 9.6% (1946) to 1.4% (1951).
- Fishing pressure was 21.2 hours per acre (3.6 fisherman-days per acre), described as low. Harvest was 6.3 lb per acre.
- An estimated 50,575 fish were harvested from 8 May to 31 December 1951, including 44,575 crappie, 1,675 channel catfish, 1,375 carp, and about 700 black bass and an equal number of white bass.
- Trotlines were used by only 4% of anglers, yet they took 90% of the carp harvested, 42% of channel catfish and 85% of flathead catfish.
- The low yield was attributed partly to omitting early-spring fishing and partly to small fish size (crappie averaging about 7 oz).

### 7. Management conclusions
- **Largemouth bass**: a good population, with adults recovered in greater numbers than in four other Missouri impoundments censused similarly. The population is underutilised because few anglers target it.
- **Spotted bass**: outnumbered by largemouth bass, though believed to have been the dominant black bass in the [[St. Francis River]] before impoundment.
- **White crappie**: no evidence of decline. Excellent spawning was seen in 1951. Because of good reproduction, adequate growth and light fishing pressure, the authors suggested the daily limit of 12 could be increased.
- **Black crappie**: stocking of 10,000 in 1941 was judged unsuccessful.
- **Non-game fish**: abundant but largely unharvested. The authors recommended considering a commercial fishery, and permitting year-round gigging. Spring gigging for [[Carp]] during spawning was suggested as a way to add to the harvest. At the time, gigging was permitted only from 1 October to 31 December, between noon and midnight, and a commercial fishery was contrary to Commission policy for impounded waters.

### 8. Methodological caveats
- Nets are selective by species and size. Net data were used only as trend indicators under uniform gear and conditions.
- Rotenone recovery was incomplete (35%) and no correction was applied, so backwater standing-crop estimates are probably conservative.
