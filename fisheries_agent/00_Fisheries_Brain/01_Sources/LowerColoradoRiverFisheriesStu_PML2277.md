# Declining Sport Fishing in the Colorado River Backwaters Above Imperial Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bass-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This Arizona fisheries article, written for a general wildlife audience, describes how the creation of [[Imperial Dam]] in 1938 flooded the lower [[Colorado River]] and formed productive [[Backwater Lakes]]. Those lakes first supported a fishery with large fish, but the fishing has since declined: fish are still caught, but they are smaller. Fisheries biologists used creel data, age and growth studies, and water-quality sampling to identify the weakened link in the food chain. The article does not name the fish species, and it presents no formal statistical analysis. Its main message is that the fishery depends on a chain of biological, chemical and physical conditions, and that stocking or other quick fixes may not repair a damaged system.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Following the construction of Imperial Dam in 1938, the water level rose, overflowed the banks, and created many small backwater lakes in the sheltered shoreline coves. The change from stream to lake brought both obvious physical changes and less visible biological changes.
- **Food pyramid concept:** The article frames the fishery as a [[Food Pyramid]], in which small forage fish support larger predator fish, and anglers ultimately harvest the largest ones (the "lunker"). The system fails when one link weakens or breaks.
- **Fish response to flooding:** Fish were no longer confined to a small stream channel and could move across large areas. Flooded lands released valuable minerals that supported productive water.
- **Aquatic plants:** Increased aquatic plant growth followed. The article notes that this supported microorganisms, oxygen supply, and new cover for young fish.
- **Early success:** Anglers were initially pleased with the number and size of fish on their stringers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Old-timer accounts:** Long-time river residents described large fish caught years earlier. The article notes that memory may exaggerate, so it relies on records.
- **Decline:** Fish were still being caught, but they were smaller than before, and the [[Fishery Decline]] became the focus of investigation.
- **Lake succession:** The article states that a body of water tends to deteriorate as it ages, a natural process that affects fish production.
- **River position:** The water above Imperial Dam is close to the lower reaches of the river and has been used and reused along its course. Nutrients and chemicals may therefore have been depleted by the time they reach this section. Downstream reservoirs named include [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]].
- **Siltation and turbidity:** Silt can smother fish eggs during spawning season, so turbidity is monitored periodically.
- **Water fluctuation:** Changes in water level are considered important because they affect eggs, young fish and adults, and no single variable can be studied in isolation.

## Key Findings

**1. Fishery records show a decline in fish size and yield, not necessarily in catch rate.**
- A [[Creel Survey]] was taken to record what fish were caught, along with their size and weight.
- Catch in 1960–61 was reported as 0.32 fish, compared with 0.31 in an earlier year (1951). The article describes this as only an insignificant difference. The unit of measure is garbled in the source scan.
- Average size of bass creeled in 1960–61 was smaller than in an earlier period, but the exact figures are not reliably legible in the source.

**2. Age and growth studies show slower growth.**
- Ages were determined by counting annual rings on fish scales, and [[Age and Growth Analysis]] was compared with earlier studies.
- Bass taken in 1960–61 were younger for a given size than bass taken years earlier, meaning they did not show the expected growth.
- Fewer pounds of fish per acre were produced than in earlier years.

**3. Fishing pressure is light.**
- In 1954, fishing pressure on the Colorado River was 1.2 fisherman visits per acre per year, which the article calls extremely light for a body of water of this type.
- Anglers were said to release many fish because they considered them too small to keep.

**4. Monitoring requires several lines of evidence.**
- Fish sampling includes [[Scale Sampling]] along with length and weight measurements, plus spine samples for aging.
- [[Water Chemistry]] sampling is carried out periodically in each lake. Key variables include [[Nutrient Cycling]] (nitrates, phosphates), sulfates, total alkalinity, total dissolved salts, and dissolved oxygen.
- Lake-bottom samples are analyzed for soil fertility, and silt and turbidity are also measured.

**5. Research goals are defined in the study.**
The article lists four goals:
1. Determine present fish conditions.
2. Identify the type of lake suitable for benefit-oriented fish production.
3. Determine what makes the food pyramid in one lake better than another.
4. Identify the most favorable inlet and outlet system for any lake where development is attempted.

**6. Management principles.**
- The fishery cannot be improved without understanding the basic ecological relationships among fish species and the environment.
- Stocking can have the opposite effect in some cases, and may disrupt a delicate balance and weaken the chain further.
- Working with existing conditions is considered more important than introducing fish. Quick fixes are not always the answer.

**7. The research is ongoing.**
A long-term study of the backwater lakes has gathered physical, biological and chemical data. The article states that research has not reached its limit and will continue.
