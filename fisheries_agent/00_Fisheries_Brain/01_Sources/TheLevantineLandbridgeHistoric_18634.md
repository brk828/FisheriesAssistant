# Biogeography of the Levantine Landbridge and Zoological Survey of Saudi Arabia

#biogeography #zoogeography #conservation #Levantine-Landbridge #faunal-exchange #Middle-East

## Executive Summary

This document presents two distinct contributions to Middle Eastern biogeography. The first section by [[F.D. Por]] outlines ten theses on the biogeographic significance of the [[Levantine Landbridge]], describing it as the most important crossroad of biotic exchange on Earth—a three-way filter connecting the [[Ethiopian Realm]], [[Palaearctic Realm]], and [[Oriental Realm]]. The second section by [[W. Büttiker]] describes the [[Zoological Survey of Saudi Arabia]], a systematic inventory project initiated in 1975 focusing on terrestrial, limnetic, and marine taxa with applications to medical, veterinary, and agricultural zoology, as well as nature conservation.

## Background & Description

The [[Levantine Landbridge]] is defined as a sliver of Middle Eastern fertile to semi-arid land wedged between the [[Mediterranean Sea]] and the [[Arabian Desert]]. It functions as a "Biogeographic Filter" that selectively limits intergradation between adjacent biogeographic realms. The landbridge came into existence approximately 15 million years ago (Ma BP) during the Burdigalian age of the Miocene epoch when the [[Tethys Sea]] was bridged over.

The [[Syrian Rift Valley]] represents a critical longitudinal feature that opened during the Pliocene, establishing a major north-south axis for biotic exchange. This rift system contains a sequence of waterways including the [[Nile Valley]], [[Jordan River]], [[Litani River]], [[Orontes River]], and [[Euphrates River]], though these segments were never continuously connected. The rift is bordered by cis- and trans-rift mountain ranges that create stepping stones for species movement.

The [[Zoological Survey of Saudi Arabia]] began systematic animal sampling in 1975, with taxonomic studies conducted by 160 specialists. The survey encompasses terrestrial, limnetic, and marine taxa across the Kingdom's diverse biotopes including coasts, lowland plains, hills, mountains, and deserts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Levantine Landbridge]] crosses the continent-sized [[Palaeoeremic Desert]] (the "Saharo-Sindian" belt), which extends from the African Atlantic coast to the Indian [[Thar Desert]]. This extremely arid desert, established during the Miocene, represents the most important barrier to biotic interchange. The desert belt is latitudinal in extension, perpendicular to intercontinental biotic exchange routes.

The landbridge contains five distinct environments: (1) penetrating desert, (2) savannah, (3) hilly Mediterranean forest and garrigue, (4) riverine wetlands and forests, and (5) oasis environments. This diversity is principally a product of the [[Syrian Rift Valley]] physiography. Ethiopian wetland biota preferentially advance northward along the rift valley bottom, while Palaearctic biota advance southward along the mountain ranges.

The [[Taurus-Zagros Mountains]] form a latitudinal barrier guarding the northern approaches of the landbridge, extremely effective during glacial maxima. The Pleistocene climatic evolution, superposed on the progression of rifting, involved at least 10-20 glacial cycles of approximately 100,000 years each. The deepening rift valley and rising mountain ranges during the later Pleistocene established an evident predominance of southward-advancing Palaearctic biota.

In [[Saudi Arabia]], 40 terrestrial areas have been proposed by the [[Meteorology and Environmental Protection Administration]] (MEPA) for establishment as nature reserves based on preliminary survey findings identifying rare, endangered, and endemic species.

## Key Findings

**Levantine Landbridge Biogeographic Theses:**

1. The [[Levantine Landbridge]] is one of five major biogeographic filters separating terrestrial biogeographic realms, functioning within the Pleistocene time frame with filtering action modified by approximately 100,000-year glacial cycles.

2. The landbridge selectively separates the [[Ethiopian Realm]], [[Palaearctic Realm]], and [[Oriental Realm]], originating approximately 15 Ma BP. The filter episodically operated on a broader front including the [[Mediterranean Sea]] (especially at [[Gibraltar Straits]]) and across Southern Arabia and the [[Gulf of Oman]].

3. The landbridge represents the most important nodal point in distributional history of modern terrestrial biota globally—a three-way, Y-shaped exchange route with more complicated structure than other major filters. It is one of the oldest filters presenting the most complex picture of past and present biotic interchanges.

4. The extremely arid desert barrier, established around the time of landbridge formation, has never disappeared since Miocene origins. The [[Syrian Rift Valley]], starting in the Pliocene, established a longitudinal morphological feature opening northward, creating a major N-S axis for biotic exchange without which existing transversal barriers would have been hardly conquered.

5. The [[Palaeoeremic Desert]] belt is both a barrier and a center of biotic evolution, having recruited and evolved specific fauna from Ethiopian, Palaearctic, and old Cenozoic Oriental stocks. This fauna represents an independent "third" factor beyond Ethiopian or Palaearctic classifications.

6. The landbridge contains diverse environments including savannah, Mediterranean forest, garrigue, and riverine/oasis wetlands. The rift established a "steeplechase" continuation of waterways with shifting headwater connections, though never a continuous river system. The gap separating the [[Nile River]] from the [[Jordan River]] system was probably the most difficult to cross.

7. Physiographic features from rifting became more evident during the Pleistocene, with the last major tectonic events occurring 18,000 BP. The deepening rift valley and rising mountain ranges became detrimental to Oriental biota exchange and increasingly limited northward Ethiopian advance, while establishing prevalence of southward-advancing Palaearctic biota during later Pleistocene stages.

8. Pleistocene climatic evolution superposed on rifting progression may show increased amplitude of glacial peaks, further explaining recent Palaearctic expansion prevalence. Wet-warm conditions characterize interstadial transition periods, while interglacial peaks are arid or "hyper-arid." The landbridge presents "microdimensional" short-term possibilities for biotic adjustments compared to broad-front Sahara dynamics, evident along isotherms and isohyets accompanying rift valley mountain ranges (e.g., four phyto-climatic belts along the 40-kilometer, 1200-meter descent from [[Jerusalem]] to the [[Dead Sea]]).

9. Circum-Mediterranean distributional patterns represent an odd mixture of Palaearctic and Ethiopian stocks, evidencing Mediterranean crossing possibilities and passive/human transport. A similar but less evident circum-Erythrean pattern exists around the [[Red Sea]], composed mainly of Ethiopian biota.

10. Nature conservancy requires specific guidelines: preserve continuous distributional areas rather than endemic taxa; view the entire landbridge as a "Biospheric Reserve" with large areas of loose environmental management containing core areas of rigorous conservation. Priority core areas include rift valley rivers and wetlands, unpolluted coastal streams, mountain slopes with phyto-climatic sequences, and specific tree species (e.g., [[Acacia]] with Ethiopian associates, [[Juniperus]] with Palaearctic fauna). Maintaining open migration routes for European birds using the landbridge en route to/from Africa is overwhelmingly important.

**Saudi Arabian Zoological Survey:**

The systematic survey commenced in 1975 with emphasis on medical, veterinary, and agricultural zoology. Taxonomic studies by 160 specialists represent the beginning of a comprehensive animal inventory. Investigations include terrestrial, limnetic, and marine taxa, with scope extended to faunistics, zoogeography, ecology, and nature conservation.

Based on preliminary findings and biotope appraisal, 40 terrestrial areas have been proposed for different types of nature reserves where rare, endangered, and endemic species have been recorded. The survey is a long-term project jointly conducted by Pro Entomologia (Basle) and MEPA (Jeddah), with results published in "Fauna of Saudi Arabia" (eight volumes published as of document date).

The survey serves multiple purposes: (1) assessing and inventorizing terrestrial and marine species across different regions and biotopes, (2) understanding the Kingdom's fascinating geological and climatological past, and (3) supporting environmental protection essential for sustaining agricultural development and renewable natural resources including meat, fish, cereals, vegetables, and fruit production.
