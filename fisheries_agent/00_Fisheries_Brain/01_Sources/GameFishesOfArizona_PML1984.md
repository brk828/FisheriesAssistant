# Sport Fishing of Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department pamphlet, co-authored by Ernest E. Mulch and William C. Gamble, is a public angling guide. It introduces the state's sport fisheries and gives practical advice on tackle, bait, and fish behavior for trout, [[Largemouth Bass]], catfish, and panfish. The pamphlet presents its methods as the authors' experience-based opinion rather than controlled research. The extracted text is undated, and its printing codes are partly illegible. Maps, plates, and fish illustrations in the source contain no usable data and are omitted.

## Background & Description

- **Publisher:** [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], directed by John M. Hall. The Commission members listed include J. J. Mantle (Chairman, Tucson), Gordon Evans (Flagstaff), and others from Yuma and Superior.
- **Purpose:** The Foreword describes it as the first publication of its kind from the Department, written for anglers and the general public. It is offered free to license buyers, with printing costs as the only charge.
- **Structure:** Separate chapters cover [[Trout Fishing]], [[Bass Fishing]], [[Catfish Fishing]], [[Panfish Fishing]], and a warm-water and trout-water map section.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Trout (salmonids)**
- [[Rainbow Trout]] is the dominant stocked trout in Arizona. It is easy to catch and cheaper to hatch than other species. It does not become cannibalistic at large sizes as often as [[Brown Trout]]. A 24-inch brown trout may eat five to eight seven-inch trout in 24 hours.
- Rainbow trout are stocked as fry and fingerlings in mountain lakes, where they grew about two inches per month in summer. The text states that this rate far exceeds growth in the Department's rearing ponds.
- [[Cutthroat Trout]] are mainly found in lakes. They tend to stay in deeper water and are less acrobatic than rainbow trout.
- Trout favor fast riffles, pool heads, and undercut banks. Sterile water (long, slow, featureless runs) offers little cover or food.

**Bass**
- [[Largemouth Bass]] tolerate warmer water than trout and can be fished year-round in Arizona.
- The pamphlet classifies lakes as bowl-shaped or saucer-shaped. The most productive water in bowl-shaped lakes is the "littoral zone," where weed beds, rocks, logs, and brush piles provide structure.

**Catfish**
- Catfish fishing in Arizona is concentrated in the lower valley areas. Most catfish feed at night and in the evening. During the day they rest in deep pools, and they move into shallow water when river flows fluctuate.
- The stated minimum water temperature for catfish activity is 40 °F. The optimum is 70 °F or higher.

**Panfish**
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) and [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) are the two main panfish. They are found in most warm-water waters at lower elevations.
- Crappie school, and once a school is located, it usually stays in the area.
- Both species hold near obstructions, docks, and shorelines.

## Key Findings

**Trout**
- Rainbow trout are preferred for stocking because they are cheap to raise and easy to catch.
- Small hooks (the text cites size 16 or smaller as "most effective") and imitations of small aquatic insects are recommended, although the authors acknowledge that anglers disagree.
- Natural bait such as grubs, perch bugs, and hellgrammites often outperforms worms.
- Fish nymphs and wet flies upstream or diagonally across the current, approaching from behind because trout face upstream.
- In lakes, fish the shallow shoreline, points, and islands in weeds no deeper than 12 to 15 feet. Early morning and evening are generally best, though the text notes exceptions.

**Bass**
- Bass are considered among the most prized sport fish in the state.
- The best fishing is generally at dawn, dusk, and night. The text says this is "not always" true.
- Surface lures are popular, but the authors advise a very slow retrieve with a two-minute pause after each cast during extreme seasons.
- Light lines (eight to ten pounds tensile strength) are recommended. The authors argue that they increase accuracy and strike response, and that skilled anglers can handle large bass on them.
- Live bait such as worms, grubs, and goldfish is effective when the bait is vigorous.

**Catfish**
- Catfish anglers mostly fish at night and in the evening.
- Strong-smelling baits are the norm, including chicken entrails, coagulated chicken blood, carp entrails, and prepared stink baits. The authors note that the sense of smell in catfish makes stink baits effective even in murky water.
- Once a catfish is hooked, the angler should let it run with the line and wait several minutes before setting the hook.

**Panfish**
- [[Panfish Fishing]] needs minimal tackle, and it is recommended for teaching children.
- Bluegill and crappie are fished near structure and along shorelines and docks.
- Their soft mouths make hooks pull free if too much pressure is applied.

**General principles**
- The authors stress reading the water. Good trout water, bass structure, and catfish holding areas are all defined by specific features.
- Fishing regulations and live-bait rules are referred to current Arizona Game and Fish Department regulations, which are not reproduced in the pamphlet.
