# Methods of Sampling Scorpion Populations

#arachnology #sampling-methods #vejovidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 methods paper by [[Stanley C Williams]] evaluates [[Pitfall Trapping]] as a tool for sampling scorpion populations in the Sonoran Desert near [[Phoenix South Mountain]], Arizona. Over the study, 100 unbaited traps captured 607 scorpions of four species in the family Vejovidae. Covered traps caught significantly more scorpions than uncovered traps. Permanent removal of captured scorpions did not significantly change later catches over the study period. The author concludes that pitfall traps outperform [[Rock Rolling]] and [[Burrow Excavation]] for sampling, but lack some advantages of [[Ultraviolet Detection]].

## Background & Description

Scorpions were traditionally studied by turning surface objects such as rocks, boards, bark and trash. These methods usually yield few specimens, produce data that are hard to interpret ecologically, and miss species with specialized habits such as burrowing. The paper reports on pitfall trapping and compares it with the other methods then in use.

The four species captured were:

- [[Hadrurus Arizonensis]]: 156 specimens, one of two species analysed statistically.
- [[Vejovis Confusus]]: 445 specimens, the most abundant species captured.
- *Vejovis spinigerus* (Wood)
- *Vejovis stahnkei* Gertsch & Soleglad

Only *H. arizonensis* and *V. confusus* were numerous enough for statistical analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The traps were set at the eastern end of Phoenix South Mountain (30°26′ N, 112°01′ W), about 1 mile south of Guadalupe, Arizona. The site was chosen because it was relatively undisturbed, accessible, and had an abundant, diverse scorpion fauna. The mountain is a rugged, rocky range isolated by flat desert basins. Elevations range from 381 m at the eastern foot to 759 m at the highest peak.

**Vegetation.** The trap sites lay in the [[Larrea Franseria Association]] of the [[Sonoran Desert]]. The vegetation was dominated by [[Creosote Bush]] (*Larrea tridentata*) and bur-sage (*Franseria deltoidea*). Less abundant species included paloverde (*Cercidium microphyllum*), ironwood (*Olneya tesota*), white bur-sage (*Franseria dumosa*), Mormon tea (*Ephedra* sp.) and saguaro (*Carnegiea gigantea*).

**Soils and terrain.** The terrain was flat and smooth, with occasional shallow washes. The sandy loam soils were well compacted and often stony, and a hard, mineralized subsurface layer was common.

**Climate (28-year record).**
- Air temperatures ranged from −7 °C to 47 °C.
- Lowest temperatures occurred in December and January; highest in June to August.
- August rainfall ranged from 0.18 to 14.1 cm, averaging 2.85 cm.
- May averaged 0.33 cm and June 0.23 cm, the driest months.
- Weather data came from Phoenix Sky Harbor Airport, about 5 miles away.

## Key Findings

**1. Pitfall trapping was effective.** Traps used were number-10 food cans, buried flush with the soil surface and covered by a rock or masonite square. The 100 traps collected 607 scorpions of four species in about 5 months. The author judged that such a sample would have been nearly impossible to obtain by turning surface objects in this habitat.

**2. Covers increased catches.** Traps were tested in three cover treatments (rock, masonite square, no cover), with 33 traps per treatment and trap 100 excluded.
- *H. arizonensis* (28 specimens analysed): captures were not random among trap types (P < 0.05). Rock and masonite traps caught similar numbers, while uncovered traps caught distinctly fewer.
- *V. confusus* (44 specimens analysed): captures were not random among trap types (P < 0.01). Uncovered traps caught "strikingly fewer" specimens.
- Heterogeneity between species was not significant (0.70 > P > 0.50), so both species responded similarly.
- Data were collected from 1 August to 4 September 1966. The author could not determine whether scorpions sought shelter under covers or avoided uncovered traps.

**3. Removal did not reduce later catches.** To test the effect of [[Scorpion Removal]], one 50-trap area had all captured scorpions removed while another 50-trap area had them recorded and released. The study ran from 24 April to 9 October 1966.
- *H. arizonensis*: removal area 73, release area 83 (expected 78 each), 0.50 > P > 0.30.
- *V. confusus*: 222.5 in each area, 0.70 > P > 0.50.
- The author concluded that removal did not significantly alter the probability of future captures under the removal pressure used.
- Two explanations were proposed: other individuals moved into vacated space, and populations were large enough that removals were not detectable.

**4. Sample composition matched the community.** More species were caught by traps than were found by preliminary searches under rocks. The relative abundances of species in the traps resembled those seen with the ultraviolet method.

**5. Method comparison.** The paper's comparison of sampling methods is summarised below.

| Method | Main advantage | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| [[Pitfall Trapping]] | Large samples; continuous sampling; data divisible for statistics | Labour-intensive installation and checking; patchy distribution; bycatch and predators |
| [[Rock Rolling]] | Simple, no equipment | Small samples; misses rare species; unusable in sand dunes; lethal surface temperatures |
| [[Burrow Excavation]] | Identifies burrow occupants | Labour-intensive; habitat disturbance; few species per area |
| [[Bark Peeling]] | Can yield many specimens quickly | Detects only species with bark-shelter habits (e.g., [[Centruroides]]) |
| [[Ultraviolet Detection]] | Large samples; minimal disturbance; standardised counts | Requires night work and nocturnal activity; lamp hazards |

**6. Ultraviolet detection shows promise.** Scorpion cuticle fluoresces bright greenish-yellow under UV light. The author and a colleague collected about 2000 scorpions from a California sand-dune habitat in under 4 hours, and up to 17 were visible at once in one lamp's field. Lamp range was from a few feet to about 50 feet. Safety warnings included not looking into the lamp because of retinal damage, and watching for venomous animals such as rattlesnakes.

**7. Pitfall trap limitations.** Installation can be impossible in very rocky or thin soils. Scorpion dispersion is patchy, so adjacent traps can differ greatly in catch. Traps need rigid checking schedules and frequent maintenance. Bycatch must be removed to avoid attracting ants, necrophagous animals, and predators such as foxes and coyotes. The author warned that coyotes may learn to check trap lines nightly for scorpions.

**Conclusion.** The author recommends combining several methods, with emphasis on the most suitable method for each study's objectives. Pitfall traps with covers are a practical, standardised way to sample scorpions, though they are less versatile than ultraviolet detection for some purposes.
