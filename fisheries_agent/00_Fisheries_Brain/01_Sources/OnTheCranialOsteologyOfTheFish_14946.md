# Gill-Rakers of *Polyodon* and the Cranial Osteology of the Elopidae and Albulidae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #polyodontidae #elopidae #albulidae

## Executive Summary

The document is a scanned 1904 Proceedings of the Zoological Society article in two parts. Part one describes the gill-rakers of the [[Spoonbill Sturgeon]] (*Polyodon spathula*), a filter-feeding structure, and gives six numbered conclusions on their structure and function. Part two is a detailed study by W. G. Ridewood of the cranial skeleton of the [[Elopidae]] (tarpons and ladyfishes) and [[Albulidae]] (bonefishes), with comparisons across about 20 lower teleosts and extended discussion of the morphology of cranial bones. The author concludes that the Elopidae and Albulidae share few specialised characters and show no direct affinity, and that both retain many primitive features.

## Background & Description

**Gill-rakers of *Polyodon*.** The gill-rakers are setiform (bristle-like) structures. Each has a basal portion attached to the branchial arch by elastic fibres and a long free shaft. The matrix contains numerous lacunae linked by canaliculi, and the shaft contains anastomosing vascular channels with blood capillaries and loose connective tissue. Histologically the gill-raker closely resembles osteo-dentine. A prolongation of the branchial mucous membrane covers each raker. Movement is produced by muscle fibres inserted on each raker. The fish feeds on microscopic organisms, which the rakers filter out before they reach the gills. Nine plate figures illustrate sections and mounts.

**Skull preparation method.** Ridewood recommends a method of disarticulating the teleost skull into four parts. This allows close study of every bone, is space-efficient when stored, and avoids the errors of re-articulation. Material was supplied by museum curators, including Dr. A. Smith Woodward and Mr. G. A. Boulenger, and by G. Tate Regan at the British Museum (Natural History).

**Elopidae.** The skull of [[Elops Saurus]] was described from several specimens (labelled A to D). Ridewood notes features including large, thin supratemporals meeting in the midline, a dentigerous parasphenoid and vomer, and branchiostegal rays numbering 30 to 35 per side. A median [[Jugular Plate]] is present. Alongside it, [[Megalops Cyprinoides]] was examined in three specimens. Its skull differs in having a bullate opisthotic, a lateral temporal groove that is not roofed, and 23 to 25 branchiostegal rays.

**Albulidae.** [[Albula Conorhynchus]] was described from two skulls. Its ethmoid region is elongated, the interorbital septum is fully bony, the palatine has two articular heads, and only 15 branchiostegal rays are present. [[Bathythrissa Dorsalis]] (also called *Pterothrissus gisu*) was described from one specimen, and it has only six branchiostegal rays. Ridewood confirms the suggestion of Boulenger that Bathythrissa is not closely related to the Salmonidae or Clupeidae, and that it is nearly allied to Albula.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not give habitat, depth, or geographic distribution for any species, and it does not report field observations. Its ecological content is limited to functional morphology:

- The gill-rakers of *Polyodon* act as a sieve for microscopic food.
- Ridewood links differences in rakers and branchial structures to the diet of each genus, and notes that food-related specialisations, such as mouth size and suspensorium angle, vary with feeding mode.
- Mouth size is related to the orientation of the [[Hyomandibular Bone]] and the [[Symplectic Bone]]. Small-mouthed forms (*Chanos*, *Gonorhynchus*) show less forward slope of the hyomandibular than large-mouthed forms.
- Ridewood observes that the vomer-parasphenoid tooth patches, the posterior temporal fossa and the [[Subtemporal Fossa]] reflect the muscularity of the anterior trunk.

## Key Findings

**Gill-rakers of *Polyodon spathula***
1. The gill-rakers are setiform, with a basal portion attached by elastic fibres and a long free shaft.
2. The matrix is lacunar and canalicular, and the shaft has anastomosing vascular channels.
3. The raker structure closely resembles [[Osteo-Dentine]].
4. The branchial mucous membrane covers each raker as a complete investing coat.
5. Movement is produced by muscle fibres inserted on each raker.
6. The rakers filter microscopic food from the water and prevent it from entering the gills.

**Cranial osteology of Elopidae and Albulidae**
- Elopidae and Albulidae show no specialised character in common and no direct affinity. Their resemblances are explained by both families retaining many ancestral features from the stock that gave rise to the [[Teleostei]].
- The [[Elopidae]] are described as the most archaic living teleosts, though some extinct elopids (*Thrissopater*, *Spaniodon*) appear more specialised.
- Shared primitive features include small parietals meeting in the midline, a roofed posterior temporal fossa, subtemporal fossae, an [[Opisthotic Bone]], basisphenoid, and orbitosphenoid, an upper and lower hypohyal, an ossified first pharyngobranchial, and dentigerous dentary, premaxilla, entopterygoid and parasphenoid bones.
- Differences include the number of surmaxillae (two in Elopidae, one in Albulidae), the tooth-bearing maxilla (Elopidae) versus toothless maxilla (Albulidae), the [[Spicular Bone]] (present in Elopidae, absent in Albulidae), and the elongated ethmoid region of Albulidae.
- The [[Sesamoid Articular]] is unusually large in *Albula*.
- Branchiostegal ray counts are: *Elops* 30 to 35 (some passages give 32 to 35), *Megalops* 23 to 25, *Albula* 15, *Bathythrissa* 6.
- The [[Parietal Bones]] meet in the midline in both families. Ridewood argues that this is a primitive condition, though it may be secondary in some groups, as in *Chanos*.
- The author corrects several earlier taxonomic statements, including Boulenger's claim that the supraoccipital is separated from the frontals by the parietals in *Elops* and *Albula*.

**General remarks on lower teleosts**
- Ridewood compares 20 species of lower teleosts, including *Arapaima gigas*, *Heterotis niloticus*, *Clupea finta*, *Engraulis encrasicholus*, *Coilia nasus*, *Osteoglossum leichardti* and *Hyodon alosoides*.
- He argues for careful use of the ectosteal/endosteal distinction and recommends naming superficial ossifications (e.g., [[Squamosal Bone]], [[Prefrontal Bone]]) according to their dermal origin, even when they lie deep or are cartilage-bone.
- The [[Urohyal]] is an ossified tendon, not a basibranchiostegal bone. The term "basibranchiostegal" is therefore misleading.
- The [[Spicular Bone]] is an ossified ligament, not a modified first pharyngobranchial.
- Cranial and mouth characters are weighed as evidence of relationship, with the caveat that adaptive features such as swim-bladder diverticula and mouth form are unreliable indicators of close relationship.
