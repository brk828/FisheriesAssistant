# Effects of Organic Matter and Loading Rates of Fish on the Toxicity of Chloramine-T

#ichthyology #aquaculture #cyprinidae #toxicology #fish-health

## Executive Summary

This toxicological study examined how fish loading rates, feed levels, and fecal material affect the availability and toxicity of [[Chloramine-T]] (n-sodium-n-chloro-para-toluenesulfonamide), an antibacterial agent used to control [[Bacterial Gill Disease]] in cultured fishes. Using [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) as the test organism, researchers at the [[National Fisheries Research Center]] demonstrated that chloramine-T toxicity increased with higher fish loading rates but decreased significantly in the presence of organic matter (fish food and fecal material). The chemical's concentration in water declined more rapidly when fish were present, with the greatest losses occurring when fish were combined with feed or fecal material. These findings have important implications for the effective therapeutic use of chloramine-T in aquaculture settings.

## Background & Description

[[Chloramine-T]] is an antibacterial agent used as a disinfectant and preservative in the food industry and has proven effective for controlling [[Bacterial Gill Disease]] (BGD) in fish. BGD is caused by gram-negative bacteria including myxobacteria, aeromonads, and pseudomonads, and is highly contagious among cultured salmonids, particularly under crowded rearing conditions and inadequate nutrition. The disease can lead to substantial fish losses in aquaculture operations.

Previous research indicated that chloramine-T activity is affected by environmental factors. Van Duijn (1967) reported partial or total inactivation by organic detritus, while Cross and Hursey (1973) demonstrated activity loss in the presence of excessive organic matter and greater toxicity in soft, acid waters than in hard, alkaline waters. Bills et al. (1988) reported greater acute toxicity in warm versus cold water and in acid versus alkaline water.

The test organism, [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), is a small cyprinid fish commonly used in toxicological bioassays. Fish averaged 1.11 g and were cultured at the [[National Fisheries Research Center]] in [[LaCrosse, Wisconsin]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

All experiments were conducted in reconstituted soft water (total hardness 40-44 mg/L as CaCO₃) at 12°C using static test procedures in 15-L glass jars with oxygen-saturated water. The study examined three primary environmental variables relevant to aquaculture conditions:

**Fish Loading Rates**: Three loading densities were tested (0.52, 1.04, and 2.07 g/L) using 7, 14, or 28 fish per 15-L container. These densities represent typical to crowded aquaculture conditions.

**Feed Levels**: Fish food (Nelsons' Sterling Silver Cup No. 1) was added at concentrations of 0.07, 0.13, and 0.20 g/L, representing 1×, 2×, and 3× the recommended feeding rate for fish at 12°C (approximately 5% body weight per day). The feed contained crude protein 48%, crude fat 14%, crude fiber 3.6%, and ash 12.0%.

**Fecal Material**: Slurry of feces and bottom materials collected from a fish production raceway was added at dry weight equivalent concentrations of 0.017, 0.033, and 0.067 g/L.

Chloramine-T concentrations in test waters were determined by [[High Performance Liquid Chromatography]] (HPLC) at 0, 6, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours. Commercial grade chloramine-T (lot 12605, Badger Pharmacal, Inc.) yielded 98.4% available chlorine when analyzed.

## Key Findings

**Fish Loading Rate Effects**:
- Toxicity of chloramine-T increased significantly as fish loading rate increased from 0.52 to 2.07 g/L
- 24-h LC₅₀ values: 36.0 mg/L at 0.52 g/L loading vs. 14.0 mg/L at 2.07 g/L loading
- 96-h LC₅₀ values showed no significant differences among the three loading rates (8.50, 7.00, and 8.00 mg/L respectively)
- Higher loading rates may have stressed fish and increased toxicity, though oxygen concentrations remained above 50% saturation
- Chloramine-T concentrations decreased more rapidly at higher fish loading rates, with fish apparently absorbing more chemical from solution
- By 96 hours, decreased chloramine-T concentrations offset the increased toxicity associated with crowding

**Fish Feed Effects**:
- Presence of fish feed significantly decreased chloramine-T toxicity
- 96-h LC₅₀ values: 7.30 mg/L without feed, 14.2 mg/L with 0.07 g/L feed, 17.2 mg/L with 0.13 g/L feed, and 20.8 mg/L with 0.20 g/L feed
- At 6 mg/L chloramine-T concentration, addition of 0.07 g/L feed decreased chemical concentration by 80% within 96 hours
- Addition of 0.20 g/L feed decreased concentrations to below detection limit within 48 hours
- Loss of chloramine-T attributed to both adsorption on feed particles and depletion of chlorine due to oxidation of organic material

**Fecal Material Effects**:
- Presence of fecal material decreased chloramine-T toxicity in 96-h exposures
- 96-h LC₅₀ values ranged from 12.0 to 17.4 mg/L with fecal material present, compared to 7.40 mg/L without fecal material
- No significant differences among LC₅₀ values at 24-h exposures
- Chloramine-T concentration decrease was independent of initial concentration and directly correlated with amount of fecal material added

**Combined Effects (Fish Plus Feed)**:
- Combination of fish and feed caused the largest loss of chloramine-T observed in any treatment
- 100% loss over 72 hours at 6 mg/L with 0.13 g/L feed plus fish
- 100% loss over 48 hours at 6 mg/L with 0.20 g/L feed plus fish
- Most rapid reductions occurred between 0 and 24 hours
- Higher feed levels resulted in more rapid reduction in chloramine-T concentration

**Combined Effects (Fish Plus Fecal Material)**:
- Fish combined with fecal material decreased chloramine-T concentrations to levels similar to fish plus feed, though reductions were less significant
- At 6 mg/L chloramine-T, concentration decreased by approximately 67% over 96 hours
- At 10 mg/L chloramine-T, concentration reduced by approximately 50% over 96 hours

**Practical Implications**:
- Chloramine-T would be more effective if fish were not fed before treatment
- Fish culturists must be aware that treatments may not be effective if the system contains high levels of oxidizable material or material that can absorb the chemical
- Effective concentration of chloramine-T is significantly reduced by organic matter commonly present in aquaculture systems

**Analytical Methods**:
- All statistical analyses used Litchfield and Wilcoxon (1949) methods to compute LC₅₀ values and 95% confidence intervals
- Mortalities were recorded at 1, 3, 6, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours
- All tests were conducted in duplicate with 20 fish per concentration (except loading rate study)
