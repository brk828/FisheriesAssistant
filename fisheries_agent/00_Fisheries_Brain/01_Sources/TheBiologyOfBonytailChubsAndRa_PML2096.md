# The Biology of Bonytail Chubs and Razorback Suckers in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This technical research proposal, submitted in March 1981 to the [[Water and Power Resources Service]] (Lower Colorado River Region) by Larry J. Paulson, James E. Deacon and John P. Baker of the [[Lake Mead Limnological Research Center]] and the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, describes a multi-year study of two endangered native fishes, the [[Bonytail Chub]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]], in [[Lake Mohave]]. The authors argue that the species have declined sharply since the 1930s because of river impoundment, altered flows and temperatures, loss of food resources, and predation by introduced fishes. They also argue that the reproductive success of the remaining populations is severely limited. Proposed power projects at [[Hoover Dam]] could further harm these populations, so the authors propose sampling habitat use, distribution, spawning, food habits and periphyton response to discharge changes.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Historically the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) occurred throughout the [[Colorado River]] system. Bonytails are described as apparently extinct except for a small population persisting in Lake Mohave (Minckley 1979). The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has recently listed the bonytail as endangered, and the razorback may soon receive similar status.
- **Current status:** Razorbacks still occur in most parts of the river and are fairly common in [[Black Canyon]] below Hoover Dam (Bryant 1977; Paulson et al. 1980). Minckley (1980) contends that this population consists solely of adults and is heading toward extinction because of low reproductive success. Bonytails have not been captured or sighted in Black Canyon for several years.
- **Historical decline:** Declines began in the 1930s. A 1941 survey reported that four native species still occurred in the river below Hoover Dam (Moffett 1942). In a spring of the early 1950s, Jones and Sumner (1954) recorded about 500 bonytails and numerous razorbacks in the [[Eldorado Canyon]] area of Lake Mohave. Periodic netting in Cottonwood Basin since then has captured only a small number of each species.
- **Causes cited:** Minckley and Deacon (1968) and Minckley (1979) attribute the decline mainly to impoundments and the introduction of exotic fishes.
- **Introduced species:** [[Rainbow Trout]] were stocked in the river in 1935 and became an excellent fishery from Hoover Dam to Eldorado Canyon. [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]] were also collected or sighted below Hoover Dam in the early 1940s, though the timing of their introduction is unknown.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**River changes after Hoover Dam (1935).** The dam stabilized flows and sharply reduced suspended sediment. Water clarity increased, and clear, swift currents scoured the river bottom and exposed armored substrates in the first 4 miles below the dam by 1937, the next 18 miles by 1941, and down to river mile 52 by 1951. These changes allowed extensive growth of attached algae ([[Cladophora]]) on shoals and riffles. Mayflies (*Callibaetis*), midge larvae, dragonflies, damselflies and microcrustaceans were common. [[Gammarus]] introduced in 1941 were abundant from Willow Beach to Searchlight by 1950.

**Formation of Lake Mohave (Davis Dam).** The reservoir flooded desert land and inundated about 200 miles of new shoreline. Lentic habitats formed in the 15-mile stretch from Eldorado Canyon to Davis Dam. Periphyton and macrophytes were reduced in the lower reservoir but persisted in Black Canyon. Aquatic insects declined, possibly because of the loss of breeding habitat downstream. Centrarchids and channel catfish became very abundant in the reservoir.

**Discharge and temperature regime.** Hoover Dam was operated from the upper intake gates until the early 1950s, and then from the lower intake gates, after which discharge temperatures became nearly constant year-round near 12–13 °C. Seasonal temperature cycles, which trigger reproduction in most aquatic insects, were lost. Aquatic insects have since disappeared from the river. Oligochaetes, midges and amphipods now dominate the invertebrate fauna of Black Canyon.

**Substrate and periphyton.** Reduced currents in Black Canyon since reservoir formation have caused extensive bottom silting. Currents are still strong enough to scour the bottom in the first 3 river miles, mainly in summer when Hoover Dam discharges are high and Lake Mohave levels are low. Periphyton is now most productive in the first 3 miles, and its growth varies with seasonal water-level changes.

**Reproduction, predation and diet.** Spawning has been observed in shallow areas of upper Lake Mohave in February–May. Eggs from stripped ripe fish fertilized artificially produced offspring in hatcheries, but no bonytail juveniles have been seen in the lake. Jones and Sumner (1954) attributed this to predation on eggs by carp. A juvenile razorback was captured in Black Canyon in March 1979, the first evidence of successful reproduction in that area. Juvenile razorbacks were found where predatory centrarchids were abundant, so predation by exotic fishes is thought to limit survival. Filamentous algae (*Cladophora*) were historically an important part of both species' diets, and the authors suggest that reduced periphyton may be a significant factor in their decline.

**Parasites and disease.** Jones and Sumner (1954) found up to 15 organisms of *Lernaea* on adult bonytails in summer. Many razorbacks show lesions and deformities linked to poor condition. Most razorback brood stock collected from Lake Mohave in 1975 died at the Willow Beach Hatchery from bacterial infections (*Pseudomonas* and *Aeromonas*) carried from the reservoir.

**Proposed power projects.** The Water and Power Resources Service plans power projects at Hoover Dam that could further affect these populations. Higher peak discharges (119,000 cfs for upgraded generators, or 62,000 cfs for added generators) could increase scouring and substrate instability below the dam. Reregulation of Lake Mohave could lower reservoir levels, possibly as low as 570 ft elevation, and suppress spawning or destroy eggs in shallow areas.

**Study areas.** Lake Mohave is divided into three areas:
- **Black Canyon** (Hoover Dam to Eldorado Canyon): cold water (12–13 °C), swift currents, and high productivity.
- **Transition zone** (Eldorado Canyon to Little Basin): between river and reservoir conditions, with relatively high productivity.
- **Reservoir** (below Little Basin): true reservoir conditions, with thermal stratification at 10–15 m and moderate productivity.

## Key Findings

### Study Design and Planned Methods

**1. Habitat selection and population density**
- Six permanent sampling stations were proposed across the three areas, because past sampling was concentrated in [[Cottonwood Basin]] and Black Canyon and the Eldorado Canyon area historically held large populations.
- Monthly [[Scuba Surveys]] along standardized transects for two years, recording fish sightings, approximate sizes, vegetation and substrate, and limnological variables (temperature, oxygen, pH, conductivity, transparency). Night surveys were to be explored.
- [[Trammel Netting]] at each station (minimum two days of effort per station) four times a year (spring, summer, fall and winter) over two years, with five trammel nets. Captured fish were to be weighed, measured and color-tagged, and bonytails transported to the Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery.
- Population size to be estimated with the [[Schnabel Index]] (Ricker 1971). Scales would be collected for age and growth, and otoliths taken from any fish that died. Age work might be subcontracted to Dr. W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University).

**2. Distribution and movement**
- Radio telemetry was planned to track movements, since [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] would likely be useless in turbulent, noisy Black Canyon and high conductivity could reduce radio range in the reservoir. Consultant Dr. Rudy Ringe (University of Idaho) was to test background noise and conductivity effects first. Unless tests show otherwise, [[Radio Telemetry]] equipment would be used.
- Tags with three-month, six-month and one-year durations were to be surgically implanted, following procedures developed by Bill Loudermilk (California Department of Fish and Game). Tags would transmit at different frequencies or pulse rates to identify individuals.
- Fish were to be tracked for 24 hours twice a month, timed to high discharges (usually midweek) and low discharges (usually weekends) from Hoover Dam, and the work repeated in summer 1982 if needed.
- In November, at least 10 razorbacks and some bonytails (count illegible in scan) were to be radio-tagged, taken from the El Dorado area or another high-use area.

**3. Spawning and reproduction**
- Spawning (December–March) would be studied intensively, focusing on Eldorado Canyon and Little Basin, where spawning was previously observed.
- Scuba divers would monitor spawning sites weekly to count spawning fish and redds, record behavior with underwater television and still photography, measure redd depth relative to water-level fluctuations, and estimate egg deposition, hatching and emergence.
- An enclosed cove (possibly Arizona Cove) would be block-netted to exclude predators and assess predation impacts. Larval fish would be counted weekly.
- Adults would be sampled monthly during spawning. Eggs would be hand-stripped and artificially fertilized at the UNLV Fish Research Facility, and hatching times and growth monitored.
- Fish would be color-tagged before or after spawning to minimize effects on spawning behavior.

**4. Food habits**
- Stomach samples would be taken from live fish with a [[Stomach Sampler]] (Giles 1980), a syringe-type device that does not injure fish. Feeding behavior would also be observed during underwater surveys.
- Stomachs of museum specimens would be examined to define historic diets.

**5. Periphyton and invertebrate production**
- Periphyton would be surveyed by divers four times a year (February, May, August and November). Cover and substrate size would be recorded in the first 2 miles and in 100 m segments at each river mile down to Willow Beach.
- [[Artificial Substrate Samplers]] (boulder substrate samplers) would be installed at six river-mile sites between miles 1 and 12, at depths of 1 m, 3.5 m and the river bottom, and sampled in February, May, August and November.
- Drift samples and pan-type sediment traps would measure scouring and siltation, with sediment depth recorded monthly.
- Biomass would be estimated for periphyton and invertebrates. Results would be combined with Water and Power Resources Service substrate maps and velocity profiles to extrapolate to other discharge conditions.

**6. Impact analysis**
- The research would be combined with analysis of historical data to define current abundance, distribution, habitat, food, reproduction and principal predators of both species.
- The study would evaluate how proposed dam and reservoir changes could alter habitat and food resources and what effects they would have on the species' future status.
- If suitable habitat is found to be essential, options such as altering dam operations (discharge depth, seasonal patterns, water levels) or introducing suitable bottom substrates in Black Canyon could be explored. Hatchery culture and stocking would be considered if other measures fail.

### Summary of Anticipated Conclusions

The authors conclude that the main ecological impacts of the power projects would occur in Black Canyon and in upper Lake Mohave. Their preliminary findings are that higher peak discharges could increase scour and instability below the dam, and could either suppress or enhance periphyton, a link that has not been quantified. Higher discharges might also push razorbacks out of productive areas. The authors recognize that rare species may need more sampling areas, and they will adjust the sampling program if the first year's data show that the six areas are not used by the species.
