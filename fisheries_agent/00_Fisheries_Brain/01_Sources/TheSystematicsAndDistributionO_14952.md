# Systematics and Distribution of Crayfishes in California

#carcinology #freshwater-ecology #astacidae

## Executive Summary

This revision by J. A. Riegel (University of California, Davis) presents the first comprehensive account of California's crayfishes. It recognizes five valid species in three genera and two subfamilies, and it synonymizes [[Pacifastacus Trowbridgi]] with [[Pacifastacus Leniusculus]]. Only [[Pacifastacus Klamathensis]] and [[Pacifastacus Nigrescens]] are considered native. [[Pacifastacus Leniusculus]], [[Procambarus Clarki]], and [[Orconectes Virilis]] were introduced and are now widespread, mostly through deliberate transplantation for food, bait, or fishing. The paper includes keys, descriptions, locality records, and a distribution map (Figure 10), and it discusses ecology and the economic impact of burrowing crayfish.

## Background & Description

**Economic and ecological importance.** Crayfishes are an excellent food, valued as game fish forage, and used as bait by anglers. Their burrowing can damage irrigation structures in agricultural areas. Before this paper, the only general account was a popular report by Bonnot (1930).

**Historical taxonomy.** Early species were described during 19th-century expeditions, including Girard (1852), Dana (1852), and Stimpson (1859). Faxon (1885, 1889, 1898, 1914) and Hagen (1871) contributed major monographs. [[Bott 1950]] revised the astacines of western North America and erected the genus *Pacifastacus*, which this paper accepts.

**Morphological basis of classification.** Cambarine species are separated mainly by the morphology of the male first pleopod (see [[Gonopod]]). In *Pacifastacus*, the gonopods are conservative and show no sexual dimorphism. Other diagnostic characters include:
- Postorbital ridge spines
- Rostral acumen length
- Chela armature and setation
- Epistome shape

**Genera and subfamilies.** The Astacinae are represented by *Pacifastacus*, the only native genus of western North America. The Cambarinae are represented by *Procambarus* and *Orconectes*, which are native east of the Continental Divide. [[Hobbs 1942]] split *Cambarus* into six genera; only *Procambarus* and *Orconectes* occur in California.

**Methods.** Material came from collections throughout California, from circularized institutions, and from the literature. Specimen numbers are not given in the text. The author reports examining more than 200 *O. virilis* specimens from California.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat preferences.**
- *P. clarki* prefers warm, vegetated sloughs but also occurs in large streams, rice fields, and irrigation ditches. In southern California, where streams dry in summer, it survives by burrowing to the water table.
- *P. leniusculus* occupies a wide range of habitats, including large rivers, swift or sluggish streams, lakes, and muddy sloughs. It was once collected in dilute brackish water and shelters under large rocks and vegetation.
- *P. klamathensis* lives in cold, swift rivers and streams of northwestern and north-central California. It forages among rocks in very fast water and is unusual in this regard within its genus.
- *O. virilis* resembles *P. clarki* in habitat and is also a rice field pest. Four specimens were dug from burrows in a drained pond near Davis, but it could not be determined whether these burrows were escape structures or normal dens.

**Burrowing and seasonal behavior.** Burrowing is a key survival strategy for these crayfishes during dry periods and a source of their pest status.

**Reproduction (preliminary observations).**
- *P. clarki* breeds in late summer and early fall, and young hatch early in fall. Males molt from second to first form shortly before breeding. Second-form males predominate outside the breeding season.
- *P. leniusculus* breeds in late summer or fall. Females carry the eggs on the pleopods over winter, and the eggs hatch in spring. Females carrying eggs appear to remain secluded in their dens over winter.

**Introduction pathways.** Documented or reported introductions include:
- Frog-farm importation of *P. clarki* (1932), though *P. clarki* was already present in small numbers before that date.
- Introduction of *O. virilis* at Chico, Butte County, from laboratory ponds, probably 1939–1941.
- Shipments of *P. leniusculus* from the Columbia River to [[Brookdale Hatchery]] (1912), with later releases into the San Lorenzo River and a Nevada County pond.
- Transfer of *P. klamathensis* from the Klamath River to [[Lake Tahoe]] (1916).

## Key Findings

1. **Five valid species** are recognized: *P. leniusculus*, *P. klamathensis*, *P. nigrescens*, *P. gambeli*, *P. clarki*, and *O. virilis*. The author states that *P. trowbridgi* is a synonym of *P. leniusculus*. The text lists *P. gambeli* as a valid species while also noting it has not been found in California since its 1852 description, which makes it a doubtful record.

2. **Synonymy of *P. trowbridgi*:** The gonopods of *P. trowbridgi* and *P. leniusculus* are indistinguishable. Postorbital spine presence and rostral acumen length vary continuously in *P. leniusculus*, so the two "typical" forms represent extremes of one variable species.

3. **Native versus introduced status:** Only *P. klamathensis* and *P. nigrescens* are considered native. *P. leniusculus*, *P. clarki*, and *O. virilis* were introduced and are thriving.

4. **Distribution of *P. clarki*:** It is the only crayfish known south of the [[Tehachapi Mountains]] and is widespread in the Central Valley and southern California.

5. **Distribution of *P. leniusculus*:** It is widely distributed through central California.

6. **Distribution of *O. virilis*:** Its range is limited but widening in the [[Central Valley]], from Butte County in the north to San Joaquin County in the south.

7. **Distribution of *P. klamathensis*:** It is confined to northwestern California and the Lake Tahoe area, where it was introduced.

8. **Decline of *P. nigrescens*:** It appears to have been eliminated from the San Francisco Bay area, where the author's extensive collections found only *P. leniusculus*. It probably survives only in the upper reaches of the Pit River in northeastern California.

9. **Gaps in range:** Crayfishes have not been reported along the coast from the Big Sur River (Monterey County) to Santa Barbara County, or in streams draining the eastern Sierra Nevada from southern Mono County to northern San Bernardino County.

10. **Status is not fixed:** Continued transplantation and new importations could change the distribution of these species quickly. The author notes that the distribution data are generalized and based on limited collections.

11. **Management implications:** The author notes that Los Angeles-area reservoirs were closed to fishing to prevent crayfish introduction. Crayfish damage to irrigation structures and rice fields is also a concern.
