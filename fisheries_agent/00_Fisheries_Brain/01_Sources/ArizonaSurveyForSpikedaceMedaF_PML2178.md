# Arizona Survey for Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) and Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*) on the Fort Apache and San Carlos Apache Indian Reservations and Eagle Creek, 1985

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, submitted to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Office of Endangered Species in July 1995, documents a fish survey of the upper [[Salt River Basin]] and [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona, conducted 19 to 24 May 1985. The survey tested suspected declines and widespread extirpation of two federally listed minnows, [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. It confirmed that Loach Minnow is rare in the region and that Spikedace is absent or extremely rare in the upper Salt and Gila watersheds. Non-native fishes now dominate many of the systems surveyed. The authors attribute the decline to habitat degradation, competition and predation from introduced species, drought-related desiccation, and altered flow regimes.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Sampling over previous decades had shown marked declines in native fish across the region. This survey focused on sites where either target species had been recorded historically, or where a high probability of historic occurrence was suspected.
- **Authors and affiliations:** David L. Propst (New Mexico Department of Game and Fish), Paul C. Marsh ([[Arizona State University]] Center for Environmental Studies), and W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University, with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Dexter National Fish Hatchery).
- **Target species:**
  - *Spikedace* is associated with lower-gradient, lower-velocity streams with eddying pools and gravel-sand bottoms.
  - *Loach Minnow* is associated with turbulent riffles of intermediate-sized streams with moderate gradient, medium-to-high velocity currents, and cobble-boulder substrates often covered by filamentous algae.
- **Site selection:** Habitat suitability for the target species was the primary criterion, with previous collection records secondary. All historic localities in the upper Salt River were sampled, along with other apparently suitable habitats in the upper Gila basin.
- **Methods:** [[Electrofishing]] was the primary sampling method. Seines and gill nets were used where appropriate. All habitat types within each site were sampled, with the greatest effort on target-species microhabitats. Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin and deposited in the [[Arizona State University]] Collection of Fishes. Larval fishes were abundant but had not been identified at the time of writing; those data were to be issued as an addendum.
- **Sampling effort:** 16 localities in the upper Salt River drainage and 17 in the upper Gila River drainage (Table 1). Sites ranged from small warmwater tributaries to several major rivers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Upper Salt River basin.** Five native and three non-native fishes were collected.
- [[Speckled Dace]] was the most common and widespread native, found at 14 of 16 localities.
- [[Desert Mountain Sucker]] was found at 12 of 16 sites.
- [[Sonoran Sucker]] was found at 8 of 16 sites.
- [[Roundtail Chub]] was collected only from the Salt River mainstream and three stations on Carrizo Creek.
- Loach Minnow occurred in only two streams: the East Fork White River and a short reach of the White River just below the North and East Fork confluence.
- Non-native fishes were [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Fathead Minnow]]. All were relatively restricted in distribution.

**Upper Gila River basin.** Collections were mostly from [[Eagle Creek]], which yielded:
- [[Longfin Dace]], Sonoran Sucker, Desert Mountain Sucker, Spikedace, a problematic chub, and Roundtail Chub.
- Non-native Rainbow Trout and [[Channel Catfish]], plus an observed (not collected) [[Red Shiner]].

Other Gila basin sites:
- The [[San Carlos River]] supported only non-native Fathead Minnow and [[Green Sunfish]].
- Its major tributary, the [[Blue River]], had only native [[Gila Chub]] and Desert Mountain Sucker.
- [[Aravaipa Creek]], a reference stream known to support the target species, yielded Longfin Dace, Loach Minnow, Spikedace, Desert Mountain Sucker, and Sonoran Sucker.

**Extirpated or scarce natives.** Several species that formerly occupied the area are now extirpated or scarce: [[Apache Trout]] (headwaters), [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (larger streams).

**Expected but not collected non-natives.** These include [[Common Carp]], Red Shiner, bullhead catfishes, [[Flathead Catfish]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (the last recorded only in the Salt River system). Channel catfish (Eagle Creek only) and green sunfish (San Carlos River only) were unusual, though both are widespread in the region.

**Flood and habitat effects.**
- Flooding favors native fishes and suppresses non-natives. Non-native fishes had largely been removed from the system, probably displaced downstream by floods that reduced their abundance below sampling detection.
- Repeated observations show non-native populations re-establish under normal or drought flows.
- The authors note that flooding and the selection of suitable habitat strongly biased the data toward the target species.

**Threats to the target species.**
- Spikedace habitat was scarce in the upper Salt basin, and the Salt River mainstream was inadequately sampled due to high discharge.
- Loach Minnow rarity is attributed to habitat deterioration from [[Overgrazing]] and [[Stream Impoundment]], as well as competition and predation from introduced species.
- [[Drought and Desiccation]] is a further factor, especially in the upper Gila basin of Arizona.

## Key Findings

1. **Loach Minnow is rare.** It was found at only two streams in the upper Salt basin, while it was common in Aravaipa Creek, Eagle Creek, and the San Carlos River according to the authors' prior observations.
2. **Spikedace is absent or extremely rare** in the upper Salt and Gila watersheds, although it was present in the reference stream Aravaipa Creek. The authors consider it unlikely that viable populations occupied areas known only from a few historic records.
3. **Four species were widespread:** Speckled Dace, Longfin Dace, Sonoran Sucker, and Desert Mountain Sucker. Roundtail Chub and the problematic chub were restricted and uncommon.
4. **Non-native fishes are established** across much of the region, and their abundance fluctuates with flow regime.
5. **Several native fishes have been extirpated** or reduced to remnant populations, including Apache Trout, Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, and Flannelmouth Sucker.
6. **Taxonomic note:** The chub recorded in Eagle Creek was previously referred to *Gila nigrescens*, a name later shown to be invalid. The population is more closely related to *Gila intermedia* than to *Gila robusta* and may be of hybrid origin.
7. **Conservation status:** Spikedace survives mainly in the upper Verde River, Aravaipa Creek, and the Gila River mainstem in the Cliff-Gila Valley, New Mexico. Loach Minnow is concentrated in Aravaipa Creek, the Blue River (a San Francisco River tributary), the Tularosa River, and limited Gila mainstem segments in New Mexico. Elsewhere, residual populations are small and vulnerable to channel changes, habitat decline, and interactions with non-native fishes.
