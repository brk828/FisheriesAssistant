# Gila and Salt River Clearing and Channelization Project Report

#wildlife-management #riparian-ecology #Columbidae #habitat-conservation

## Executive Summary

This completion report documents wildlife habitat monitoring along the [[Gila River]] from Phoenix to [[Gillespie Dam]] during August 1965 to July 1966, in response to a proposed Army Corps of Engineers flood control project. The project would have cleared a 2,000-foot-wide channel through critical [[Mourning Dove]] and [[White-winged Dove]] nesting habitat. Hunter questionnaire data showed increasing dove harvest and hunter pressure in [[Maricopa County]] from 1962-1965. An unseasonal flood event in December 1965-January 1966 provided natural evidence that riparian vegetation had minimal effect on water flow. A $22.7 million flood control bond issue including the clearing project was defeated 3-to-1 by voters in March 1966, placing the project on inactive status.

## Background & Description

The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] conducted this study under Project FW-16-R-5, Work Plan 2, Job 2, to assess potential impacts of proposed channel clearing on wildlife habitat. The Army Corps of Engineers proposed clearing a 2,000-foot-wide channel through the [[Gila River]] corridor from Phoenix city limits (91st Avenue) west and south to [[Gillespie Dam]]. This area contains extensive mesquite and salt cedar vegetation providing critical nesting habitat for [[Mourning Dove]] (*Zenaida macroura*) and [[White-winged Dove]] (*Zenaida asiatica*). The stretch represents one of the primary dove-producing areas in Arizona and one of the most important [[White-winged Dove]] producing areas nationally. The project area includes [[Gila River]] waterfowl areas and associated federal withdrawal lands.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The riparian corridor along this stretch of the [[Gila River]] supports dense thickets of mesquite and salt cedar that provide essential nesting habitat for doves. The area's proximity to Phoenix makes it the primary dove hunting destination for metropolitan hunters. The [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] upstream are regulated by irrigation reservoirs designed primarily for water storage rather than flood control.

In December 1965-January 1966, heavy unseasonal rainfall and snowmelt filled these reservoirs, necessitating water releases. Flow at [[Granite Reef Dam]] began at 7,000 cubic feet per second (cfs) on December 22, peaked at 78,000 cfs on December 31, and ceased January 13. Aerial surveys during and after this flood event revealed that mesquite and salt cedar vegetation in the riverbed had very little effect on inhibiting natural water flow. Follow-up flights in March 1966 showed minimal damage to dove nesting habitat from high water. Flood damage to buildings and structures occurred almost exclusively to developments built within the normally dry riverbed, with very little damage outside the flood plain limits. Damage could not be attributed to water-spreading effects from riparian vegetation.

## Key Findings

**Dove Harvest and Hunter Pressure Trends (1962-1965):**
- 1962: 17,431 hunters, 51,300 trips, 297,974 birds harvested
- 1963: 20,448 hunters, 81,222 trips, 746,800 birds harvested
- 1964: 22,385 hunters, 114,995 trips, 974,985 birds harvested
- 1965: 24,588 hunters, 102,877 trips, 995,658 birds harvested

Consistent increases in both hunter numbers and harvest occurred across all four years in [[Maricopa County]]. The report notes that dove hunting pressure was expected to increase with anticipated human population growth, placing greater demands on dove populations dependent on salt cedar and mesquite thickets along the [[Gila River]].

**Flood Event Observations:**
Natural flood conditions provided empirical evidence regarding vegetation effects on water flow. The mesquite and salt cedar vegetation appeared to have very little effect in inhibiting natural water flow during peak discharge of 78,000 cfs. High water caused minimal damage to dove nesting habitat. Structural damage occurred primarily to buildings constructed in the normally dry riverbed rather than outside flood plain limits.

**Project Status Changes:**
On June 7, 1965, the [[Maricopa County Flood Control District]] Board of Directors recommended the Army Corps of Engineers restudy the need for clearing from 91st Avenue to [[Gillespie Dam]], placing the project under deferred status on September 1, 1965. At a December 15, 1965 public meeting, the Army Corps of Engineers maintained specifications for the 2,000-foot-wide clearing and rejected the narrower channel requested by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

On January 7, 1966, the [[Maricopa County]] Board of Supervisors recommended reducing the clearing width from 2,000 feet to a 500-foot pilot channel. On March 8, 1966, voters defeated the $22,679,000 flood control bond issue by a 3-to-1 margin, placing the entire program including the 91st Avenue-[[Gillespie Dam]] clearing on inactive status.

**Recommendations:**
The report recommends continuing the project until the vegetation clearing becomes a dead issue or is completed, given the area's importance and likelihood that channel clearing would be included in any revised flood control program.
