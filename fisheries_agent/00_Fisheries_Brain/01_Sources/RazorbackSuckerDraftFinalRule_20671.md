# Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) Final Rule: Listing as Endangered

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) document is the draft final rule, with accompanying summary and background, to list the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, without designating [[Critical Habitat]]. The species now occupies about 25 percent of its historic range in the [[Colorado River Basin]], with extremely low numbers and no significant adult recruitment for roughly 30 years. The rule concludes that threatened status is inadequate because the species is in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of its range.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large, monotypic sucker identified by a sharp dorsal keel behind the head and a large, fleshy subterminal mouth. Adults often exceed 3 kg (6 lb) and 600 mm (24 in). The species was first described by Abbott (1861) and placed in its own genus, *Xyrauchen*, by Kirsch (1889). Juveniles lack an obvious keel, making them hard to distinguish from other suckers.

Historically the species was abundant across roughly 5,635 km (3,500 mi) of the basin, including mainstem and tributary habitats in seven U.S. states and northern Mexico. Its range has since contracted to about 1,208 km (750 mi) in the upper basin, with a substantial lower-basin population persisting only in [[Lake Mohave]]. The [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922 divides the upper and lower basins at Lee Ferry, Arizona.

Life-history information is limited. Spawning occurs January through April in the lower basin and May through June in the upper Green River, over coarse gravel or cobble bars swept clean by currents or wave action, at water temperatures of roughly 10.5–18 °C. Larvae stay in gravel until swim-up. Adults show long migrations to spawning sites but are otherwise relatively sedentary, and radio-tracked fish in the Green River used depths of 0.6–3.4 m and velocities of 0.1–0.6 m/s. Diet includes planktonic crustaceans, diatoms, algae, midge larvae and detritus.

Age estimates from [[Otolith and Scale Ageing]] studies showed scales underestimate age: Lake Mohave fish were aged 24 to 44 years, and 89 percent of 70 sampled fish hatched before or at impoundment.

Regulatory history: the species was proposed as threatened in 1978 (withdrawn in 1980 after the ESA deadline lapsed), petitioned for endangered listing in March 1989, and proposed as endangered on May 22, 1990 (46 FR 21154). A public hearing was held in Farmington, New Mexico, on August 14, 1990.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Primary threats (ESA Section 4(a)(1) factors):**

- **Habitat modification (Factor A):** Since 1910, 15 dams have been built on the lower Colorado and its major tributaries. [[Glen Canyon Dam]] cooled 384 km (238 mi) of river, and [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] impounded about 500 km (310 mi) of habitat while lowering temperatures over 105 km (65 mi). Storage reduced spring runoff by 28 percent on the Green River and 37 percent on the Colorado River during May–June, disrupting flooding that maintains off-channel habitat. Peaking power operations at Flaming Gorge produced a 400 percent increase in daily flow fluctuation.
- **Temperature effects:** Adult preference was 22–25 °C, with avoidance below 14.7 °C and above 27.4 °C. Hatching success was poor below 15 °C, and total egg mortality occurred below 10 °C.
- **Predation and competition (Factors C and E):** Nonnative species including [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], and [[Northern Pike]] prey on eggs and larvae or compete for food and space. Researchers hypothesize predation is a major cause of the recruitment failure. Tyus et al. (1982) recorded 42 nonnative species established in the upper basin, and Minckley (1979) listed 37 in the lower basin.
- **Hybridization:** Hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] has been reported in the upper basin, but genetic analysis of Lake Mohave fish found less than 5 percent introgression, considered insignificant.
- **Regulatory gaps (Factor D):** State laws protect the species from take but do not address habitat destruction or nonnative stocking. The [[Recovery Program]] is cooperative and non-regulatory, and it does not apply to the San Juan River.

**Federal involvement:** The [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Western Area Power Administration]], and [[National Park Service]] are directly affected. The same Section 7 consultation already applies to the three other listed Colorado River fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]].

**State positions:** Six of seven state agencies commented, and most supported endangered listing. Nevada recommended threatened status in the lower basin and endangered status in the upper basin. California did not comment.

## Key Findings

- **Listing decision:** The razorback sucker is listed as endangered, without critical habitat. Threatened status was rejected because the species is "clearly in danger of extinction" due to insufficient recruitment.
- **Population status:** The species occupies about 25 percent of its historic range. Lake Mohave held an estimated 60,000 adults (Marsh and Minckley, in press). The upper Green River held 758 to 1,138 individuals (Lanigan and Tyus 1989). Only four juveniles (33–54 mm) were collected from Lake Mohave since the 1950s.
- **Recruitment failure:** No significant recruitment has been evident for about 30 years. Existing populations are mostly old fish that are dying off. McCarthy and Minckley (1987) predicted Lake Mohave could be extirpated before 2000.
- **Stocking results:** About 9.5 million larvae and juveniles were stocked in Arizona from 1981 to 1985, and about 2,000 young-of-the-year were released into the Green River from 1987 onward. These releases have not re-established the species.
- **Critical habitat not designated:** Habitat needs are poorly understood, and designation would not protect against nonnative fish. Section 7 consultation already covers habitat effects.
- **Comment summary:** 62 written and 18 oral comments were received from 11 federal agencies, 7 state agencies, 10 local governments, and 47 private groups or individuals. Issues addressed included threatened status, predation by river otters (undocumented), critical habitat, and the status of stocked fish. Stocked fish receive full ESA protection, and reintroductions require permits or a special experimental-population rule.
- **Recovery goal:** The Recovery Program aims to establish self-sustaining populations and protect natural habitat so that listing becomes unnecessary.
