# The Effect of DDT on the Fauna of a Central African Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #simuliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by [[H. B. N. Hynes]] and [[T. R. Williams]] examines how a single application of [[DDT]] affected the entire invertebrate community of the [[River Manafwa]], a rapid stream on the slopes of [[Mount Elgon]] in eastern Uganda. Samples were taken 29–31 days after dosing at eight stations from the forest edge to the mouth of the [[River Sala]]. The insecticide eliminated most insect species for varying distances downstream, but most populations recovered from surviving eggs. The principal predators ([[Neoperla spio]] and [[Hydropsychidae]]) were the most severely affected, which allowed prey mayflies and [[Simulium]] black flies to increase below the dosing point. The authors conclude that insecticide use against black flies can produce outbreaks rather than control.

## Background & Description

The authors situate the work in a context of concern that insecticides applied to streams for [[Simulium]] control could disrupt stream communities. Previous studies cited include [[Davies]] (1950), who reported a large increase in black flies after DDT treatment in Ontario, and [[Corbet]] (1958) on the [[Victoria Nile]]. Ecological work on African streams was described as very limited; the only comparable Central African studies were van Someren (1952) on Kenyan trout streams and Marlier (1954) on eastern Congo streams.

The [[River Manafwa]] rises at 13,900 feet on Mount Elgon and flows southwest toward Lake Kioga. The study section ran from about 6,000 ft at the forest edge to 3,950 ft at the mouth of the Sala, about 7.5 miles below Bulucheke. The substrate was stones and gravel with no fine silt, the current was swift, and algal growth was minimal. Fishes were scarce; the siluriid [[Amphilius jacksoni]] and a single [[Clarias]] were recorded, and fish were judged an unimportant part of the fauna.

The [[DDT]] application was made on 2 December 1960 by M. A. Prentice of the Department of Medical Entomology, Kampala. It maintained 1 part per million for 30 minutes at Bulucheke, just below the collecting station at 4,400 ft. It was a test of a new preparation, not a control programme. The [[River Sume]], a tributary, was treated the same day with a soluble vermiculite-plaster block and showed no detectable effect on its fauna.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling:** Fine-meshed nets (60 meshes per inch) were used, samples were preserved unsorted, and animals were later floated out in calcium chloride solution and hand-sorted. Crabs and fishes were collected with a coarser net. Total invertebrates numbered about 56,000.

**Altitudinal zonation:** Tricorythus, Ephemerythus and Centroptiloides were absent above 4,600 ft. Chimarrha occurred only at high altitudes, while Polymitarcys and Diceromyzon occurred only at low altitudes. Acentrella species showed clear zonation. The authors attribute this to temperature: water ranged from near 0 °C at the source to 20–30 °C in the lower reaches. [[Potamon niloticus]] was limited to waters above about 13 °C at midday.

**Food webs:** Most of the fauna consisted of mayflies and Simulium, which are herbivores or detritus feeders. The main carnivores were [[Neoperla spio]] (entirely carnivorous), [[Hydropsychidae]] (omnivorous), and crabs, with over 60 per cent of crabs containing animal remains.

**Biogeography:** Relict distribution was proposed for cold-water species such as Chimarrha and Acentrella 'C', linking them to a colder past climate and to Mount Kenya.

**Insect seasonality:** No marked seasonal cycle was apparent in the tropics, since specimens of all sizes were present for most species.

## Key Findings

- **Invertebrate loss:** A single DDT dose almost certainly eliminated most insect species for varying distances below the dosing point. Almost all species survived as eggs, consistent with widespread egg diapause in stream insects.
- **Predator decline:** [[Neoperla spio]], Hydropsychidae and [[Cheumatopsyche]] were among the most severely affected. Neoperla were reduced in number and size, and below the dosing point only at 3,950 ft did the normal size range reappear.
- **Prey increase:** Baetidae (including [[Baetis]] and [[Centroptilum]]) and Simulium became more abundant below the dosing point. Their predators were rare and small, so more eggs developed into larvae.
- **Unaffected taxa:** Crabs ([[Potamon]] spp.), fishes, Dugesia and some Chironomidae and Ceratopogonidae showed no detectable effects. Caenis, Acentrella 'B', Baetis and Centroptilum showed no change in maximum size.
- **Black fly species:** [[Simulium neavei]], vector-associated and phoretic on crabs, was eliminated. Its distribution was restricted to roughly 3,800–4,900 ft and may be temperature-determined. S. copleyi and S. lumbwanus were also phoretic on mayflies.
- **Sume results:** The vermiculite-block treatment had no measurable effect on Neoperla or Cheumatopsyche populations. Afronurus nymphs were smaller below the dosing point, with 55–62 per cent under 2 mm compared with 34 per cent above.
- **Management implications:** The authors argue that a single dose is only transient, but continued treatment could eliminate natural predators of S. neavei and cause severe outbreaks. Pest control must be planned using knowledge of the whole community, especially for [[Simulium damnosum]], which carries [[Onchocerca volvulus]].
- **Methodological value:** The study suggests DDT can serve as an ecological tool to measure growth rates. Caenis, Acentrella 'B', and some Baetis and Simulium may reach maturity within 30 days.
