# Arizona Riparian Council Newsletter, Volume 8, Number 3 (September 1995)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This September 1995 issue of the [[Arizona Riparian Council]] newsletter covers the [[Lower Colorado River Ecoregion]] (LCRE) and its conservation planning, state land preservation and water funding in Arizona, a comparative environmental risk assessment for riparian systems, a species profile of the endemic [[Razorback Sucker]], legislative and legal developments on rangeland grazing, a list of publications, and upcoming events. The newsletter is largely a mix of agency reports, news items and short articles rather than a single research study. Its most detailed biological content concerns the decline of native Colorado River fishes and the recovery effort for the razorback sucker.

## Background & Description

The LCRE is one of nine ecoregions identified by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in Region 2. The ecoregion covers 650,000 km² of western North America, drains portions of seven states and Mexico, and includes the Colorado River below Lee's Ferry in northern Arizona, the Grand Canyon, and the Colorado River Delta to the Gulf of California. The Lower Colorado River Ecoregion Team (LCRET) was formed in May 1994 as a self-management team with a single point of contact, coordinated with a Regional Facilitation Team.

The newsletter is published by the Arizona Riparian Council (ARC), formed in 1986 because of concern over riparian loss. The Council estimates that less than 10% of Arizona's original riparian acreage remains in natural form. Annual dues are $15. The issue also includes a farewell message from Kris Randall to Ed Fox, outgoing Director of the [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]], and an introduction from new co-editors Jeff Inwood and Cindy Zisner.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish and bird declines.** The LCRE has lost much of its historic diversity. Native fish declined with dam construction, especially the big river fishes: [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Bird declines were most severe in the elf owl, yellow-billed cuckoo, vermilion flycatcher, summer tanager and Bell's vireo, which the article links partly to the loss of tall trees.

**Riparian stressors in Arizona.** The [[Arizona Comparative Environmental Risk Project]] (ACERP) found that riparian systems occupy less than 0.5% of Arizona's landscape. The Arizona Game and Fish Department identified 266,786 acres of floodplain or riparian vegetation associated with 5,032 miles of perennial streams. The report identifies water management and land use as the major stressors. Groundwater pumping and surface diversions have lowered water tables, eliminating or greatly reducing riparian systems, especially extensive mesquite bosques. Grazing, agriculture, urbanization and exotic species introduction also degrade these systems.

**State land and water policy.** Governor Symington announced on 15 June 1995 an initiative to preserve approximately 700,000 acres of Arizona State Trust land as open space, with initial acquisition or lease costs estimated at $500 to $20,000 per acre. High-priority sites include Granite Mountain and Tumamoc Hills. The [[Arizona Water Protection Fund]] received 82 grant applications totaling $27,610,523 for the 1995 cycle, against roughly $49 million in total project costs.

**Rangeland policy.** The proposed Livestock Grazing Act, approved by the Senate Energy and Natural Resources Committee on 19 July, would limit grazing decisions to ranchers and affected permittees. Environmentalists argue that this would exclude sportsmen, conservationists, miners, loggers and wildlife groups. The Act would affect more than 270 million acres of federal land used by 29,000 ranchers.

## Key Findings

- **LCRET action items:** Projects include rehabilitating Topock Marsh (Havasu NWR), Martinez Lake (Imperial NWR) and Three-Finger Lake (Cibola NWR); acquiring Cibola Valley Irrigation and Drainage District land for migratory birds and greater sandhill cranes; protecting native vegetation on public land; and building endangered fish growout facilities.
- **ACERP:** Water management and land use are the main riparian stressors. Coordination between surface and groundwater management is described as nonexistent. Exotic species introduction is considered one of the few stressors with no reasonable elimination procedure. An Action Agenda will be submitted to the Governor.
- **Razorback sucker status:** The species is endemic to the Colorado River drainage. The last wild population of any size in the basin is in Lake Mohave. Larvae hatch in large numbers each year, yet none of the Lake Mohave larvae are known to reach maturity. The article tested three theories: turbine mortality (no evidence), food limitation (some support) and non-native predation (considered most important). Wild larvae are now captured by light, reared in aquaria, and released as electronically tagged juveniles into predator-free backwaters.
- **Publications:** The newsletter lists books on the Clean Water Act, wetlands and water resources, and articles on fish extinction in Virginia (Angermeier 1995), microtox testing for phorate in wetlands, and avian richness in revegetated riparian forest on the Rio Grande (Farley et al. 1994). The Rio Grande study found that a 5-year-old revegetated site hosted as many neotropical migrant species as a 30-year-old mature forest.
- **Events:** The Fall Get-Together was held 14-15 October 1995 at the [[Sierra Ancha Research Station]]. Other meetings included the Third Biennial Conference on Research on the Colorado Plateau (16-20 October 1995) and the Third International Conference on the Ecology and Management of Invasive Riparian and Wetland Plants (25-28 October 1995).
