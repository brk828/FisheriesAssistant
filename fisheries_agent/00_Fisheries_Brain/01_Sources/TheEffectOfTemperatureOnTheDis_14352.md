# The Effect of Temperature on the Distribution of the Mayfly Fauna of a Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ephemeroptera

## Executive Summary

This 1935 monograph by F. P. Ide (University of Toronto, published as *Publications of the Ontario Fisheries Research Laboratory No. 50*) examines how water temperature structures the distribution of mayfly nymphs along two trout streams of the [[Nottawasaga River]] system in central Ontario. Ten stations were chosen so that water temperature varied widely while depth, flow, bottom type, vegetation and sunlight exposure stayed similar. Species richness rises downstream, correlated with greater seasonal temperature fluctuation, and emergence seasons shorten and narrow to early summer at warmer sites. The author concludes that temperature sets the primary limits on distribution, with flow and substrate imposing secondary limits, and that distribution patterns reflect evolved differences in growth rate and thermal tolerance.

## Background & Description

The study covered the period from late May 1930 to May 1932. Mayflies were chosen because about fifty-five species occupy every part of the stream, and their gradual development avoids the long pupal resting stages of holometabolous insects. Observations on Plecoptera, Trichoptera and Amphipoda suggested the results may apply to other aquatic organisms.

**Study streams**
- **[[Pine River]]:** rises from permanent cold springs (about 7.5–8.0 °C) near Horning's Mills; stations 1, 2 and 3.
- **[[Mad River]]:** lower reaches (stations 4, 5, 6) are rapid and torrential through a gorge; upper reaches (stations A–D) are slow, silty and vegetated.
- **Nottawasaga system:** drops more than 1,000 feet from source to mouth at Georgian Bay, with about 500 feet of that drop in roughly six miles near the [[Niagara Escarpment]].

**Stations**
- Rapid-water stations 1–6 showed wide temperature differences with similar physical conditions.
- Slow-water stations A–D (upper Mad River) had greater depth, silt, and aquatic plants such as *Nymphaea* and *Potamogeton*.

**Methods**
- Nymphs were collected about every two weeks in summer, less often in winter, by turning stones; adults were sampled from tree leaves.
- Developmental stages (A–O) were assigned by frequency analysis of a ratio X (mesothoracic wing-pad length divided by seventh abdominal tergite length), with antennal segment counts used for younger instars.
- Temperatures came from hourly readings, a continuous recording thermometer at station D, and oxygen and pH readings (La Motte method).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature gradient:** the source stayed near 8 °C year-round. Station 1 ranged from about 6 to 11 °C; station 6 reached a summer maximum of about 30 °C and fell to 0 °C in winter. Estimated maxima on a common warm day (air 25 °C) were 9.0, 16.5, 19.5, 20.5, 21.5 and 24.0 °C for stations 1, 2, 3, 5, 4 and 6.
- **Oxygen and pH:** oxygen usually ran 5–7 cc per litre and was never below 4.5 cc per litre. Photosynthesis raised oxygen in the afternoon, with supersaturation downstream. Water was alkaline (pH 8.0 at the source), buffered by dolomite and marl.
- **Growth:** individuals near the source grew at roughly one-third the rate of conspecifics farther downstream.
- **Habitat use:** *Epeorus humeralis* oviposited in rapids and its young nymphs drifted to quieter water below the rapids as they grew. Temporary snowmelt pools warmed quickly and triggered very early emergence of *Blasturus nebulosus*.

## Key Findings

1. **Species richness increases downstream.** Station 1 had 7 species; station 2 had 15; station 3 had 16; station 5 had 22; station 4 had 21; and station 6 had 29. Between stations 1 and 6 about twenty-two species were added and only two (*Iron pleuralis* and *Ephemerella aurivilli*) dropped out. Richness at stations A, B and C+D was 11, 15 and 21.
2. **Emergence seasons shorten downstream.** Warmer sites restrict emergence to early summer, and winter splits some populations into two generations.
3. **Temperature sets the limits.** Absence of cold-tolerant or warm-tolerant species near the source or at station 6 reflects too-low growth rates or lethal high summer temperatures, respectively.
4. **Closely related species are seasonally isolated.** *Leptophlebia adoptiva* emerges before *L. mollis*. *Iron pleuralis* emerges before *Epeorus humeralis*. Within *Ephemerella*, *E. subvaria*, *E. invaria* and *E. excrucians* emerge in sequence and differ in size, pigmentation and male clasper form.
5. **Thermal selection.** Early and late emergers are removed by summer heat or winter cold. *Iron pleuralis* shows a probable egg dormancy of at least four months, and *Stenonema canadense* eggs hatched over about six weeks.
6. **Geographic pattern.** Source-zone forms have northern distributions, some circumpolar (e.g., *E. aurivilli*, reported from Alaska, Canada and Europe). Downstream forms are more southerly.
7. **Evolutionary interpretation.** The author proposes that distribution reflects evolved differences in metabolic rate and thermal tolerance rather than competition for food. Headwater and alpine torrential faunas are interpreted as cold-water relic faunas, with adaptation to swift water being secondary. The author cites [[Kennedy's Metabolic Rate Theory]] as consistent with these patterns.
8. **Non-mayfly observations.** The author notes that the same temperature-driven principles may apply to Plecoptera, Trichoptera and Amphipoda, and recognizes the stream's dependence on [[Trout Stream Habitat]] for these insect communities.
