# Biology of the Crayfish Orconectes causeyi and Its Use for Control of Aquatic Weeds in Trout Lakes

#carcinology #astacidae #orconectes

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife technical paper (Jack L. Dean, January 1969) evaluates the introduced crayfish [[Orconectes Causeyi]] as a biological control agent for submerged aquatic weeds in shallow [[Trout Lakes]] of the arid [[American Southwest]]. Weed-choked lakes limit trout fishing, cause winterkill, and produce lethal pH levels, while [[Herbicide Treatment]] and [[Fertilization for Algal Shading]] are costly, temporary, or unpredictable. Observations at Jackson and Bluewater lakes, and at Nutria Lake, suggested that dense crayfish populations suppressed weeds. Field work from May 1963 to March 1967 at [[Nutria Lake]], [[Ramah Lake]], and [[Mesa Hill Lake]] supports this: crayfish-occupied lakes became largely weed-free, while five control lakes without crayfish kept their weeds. The author concludes that O. causeyi is an effective and low-cost weed controller, with a few life-history drawbacks, and suggests that other crayfish species may be better suited to trout lakes.

## Background & Description

The paper begins by framing the problem. Submerged vegetation in the Southwest interferes with fishing, ties up water, imparts odors and tastes, contributes to winterkill, and drives pH up through photosynthesis. The author notes that pH above 10.0 is usually harmful to [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), the species favored for cooler lakes. Chemical control is limited: herbicides must be repeated annually, costs are high, weeds often replace one another, and treatments conflict with irrigation use and water-pollution concerns. Fertilizer-induced algal blooms are inexpensive but unpredictable in alkaline lakes and can produce nuisance growths.

Orconectes causeyi was recently described by Jester (1967). It belongs to the order Decapoda, family Astacidae, subfamily Cambarinae, and the Virilis section of the genus *Orconectes*. It is a large crayfish: the largest male measured 63 mm carapace length and 100 g, and the largest female 73 g at a body length of 4.9 inches.

**Methods.** Collection used seines (30-foot, 1/4-inch mesh), wire and burlap traps baited with dog food or liver, hand and dip nets, gill nets, burrow excavation, and lake draining. Monthly collections were made except in January. Body length was recorded for 4,780 crayfish and carapace length for 716; weights were taken for 460 individuals with complete appendages. Reproductive form of males was used to determine age groups alongside length-frequency distributions. Ovarian egg counts were made from 22 females, and stomach contents were examined from 110 crayfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** O. causeyi is not native to the Colorado River or upper Rio Grande watersheds. The earliest recorded introduction was in 1931 at Atrisco Drain, which empties into the Rio Grande south of Albuquerque, using crayfish from the Cimarron River. By 1966 it had spread more than 150 miles upstream and 200 miles downstream of Albuquerque. It was later stocked in the Colorado River watershed of western New Mexico and northeastern Arizona. It occurs at elevations from 4,000 feet (Caballo Lake, New Mexico) to 8,400 feet (Horseshoe Lake, Arizona), and it has established at Morgan Lake, a cooling reservoir for a thermoelectric plant.

**Habitat and tolerance.** Populations are most abundant where predaceous warm-water fishes such as [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*) are absent or scarce. Rocky bottoms are preferred. Adult temperature tolerance ranges from 33°F to 90°F, and immatures withstand the low 90s°F. One individual was taken in a gill net set in 70 feet of water at Conchas Lake, suggesting it is not restricted to shallow water. Populations have not become established in lakes subject to severe winterkill. At Nutria Lake in September 1965, seining indicated a density of 9,000 crayfish (140 lbs per acre) in the littoral zone.

**Water chemistry.** Across eight lakes with O. causeyi, pH ranged from 7.6 to 10.3, total dissolved solids from 122 to 1,440 ppm, calcium 5–230 ppm, magnesium 2–73 ppm, sodium 2–239 ppm, bicarbonates 26–308 ppm, chlorides 5–230 ppm, and sulphates 3–514 ppm. Upper tolerance limits for these variables are unknown.

**Feeding and weed control.** Historical reports support crayfish as weed controllers: Embody (1934) described a Canadian crayfish that eliminated pond vegetation in New York, and Goellner (1943) found that *O. immunis* cut off large amounts of *Najas flexilis*. In Ohio, Langlois (1935) reported weed control by *O. rusticus* in hatchery ponds. O. causeyi is omnivorous. In a stomach analysis of 110 crayfish from five lakes and one stream, 54% were empty; the contents of those with food were 51% algae, 27% vascular plant fragments, 18% detritus, and 4% animal matter (snails, young crayfish, and fish). Aquatic vascular plants were scarce in all but one lake. When abundant, the species controlled native submerged plants including slender pondweed, floating pondweed, leafy pondweed, coontail, white water buttercup, elodea, watermilfoil, and muskgrass, as well as filamentous algae. It does not control smartweed (*Polygonum*), bulrush (*Scirpus*), or cattails (*Typha*), which grow mainly in deep water.

**Burrowing.** Hobbs (1942) classed burrowers as primary, secondary, or tertiary; O. causeyi is a tertiary burrower that digs mainly in fall, winter, and during spring egg-laying. Its burrows fall into three types: tunnels under rocks (most common, used by all sizes and sexes), shallow simple holes, and complex above-waterline burrows dug by young of the year in moist soil for hibernation. It has not damaged dams or irrigation ditches and has been compatible with local agriculture.

**Turbidity.** O. causeyi does not roil sediments, but most lakes where it is present became less clear. The author attributes this to plankton algae blooms that follow nutrient release after weed removal, and possibly to crayfish stirring soft bottoms in large shallow, wind-swept lakes once held in place by plants.

## Key Findings

**Life history**
- Egg laying runs from late March to early May, depending on water temperature; eggs hatch in 6 to 9 weeks, mostly late May through June.
- Young remain attached to the female for 7 to 12 days and become free-living by late June.
- Sexual maturity is reached from mid-August to mid-September at 14 or 15 months. Many females die at 24 to 25 months after their first brood, but about 8% of 2-year-old females survive to produce a second brood.
- Females may not live beyond about 37 months.
- Mean ovarian egg production is 588, higher than any North American crayfish reported in the literature at the time. Fertile eggs on the pleopods of 77 females indicated a 29% loss of ovarian eggs.
- Immature sex ratio is 1:1; adult males outnumber females 1.5:1. About 12% of adult males survive to the next summer.

**Weed control results**
- *Nutria Lake*: crayfish were scarce in 1961 and the lake was nearly choked with slender pondweed. Crayfish became abundant by 1963, and by 1966 the lake was essentially weed free except for bulrushes and isolated deep-water beds.
- *Ramah Lake*: crayfish were scarce in 1963; by 1965 moderate numbers kept the shallow south end weed free, though smartweed persisted.
- *Mesa Hill Lake*: weeds persisted through 1965. Attempts to control them with algal blooms failed. Large crayfish were common in 1966, and weeds receded from deep areas.
- *Controls*: five lakes without crayfish kept submerged weeds abundant through every summer and fall, except where heavy early-season algal blooms shaded them out.
- Weed control was closely tied to crayfish density and to the proximity of rocky substrate, which crayfish preferred.

**Growth and size**
- Males grew from 3.5 to 22 mm carapace length (range 8–30 mm) in the first year and reached an average of 44 mm in the second season and 52 mm at maturity.
- Females averaged 21 mm at the end of the first growing season and 47 mm after maturity; the sexes did not differ significantly at that stage (t test, p = 0.526).
- The length-weight relation is the same for both sexes up to 40 mm carapace length; larger males are heavier.

**Recommendations and comparisons**
- Life-history comparison with *O. virilis* from Michigan (Momot 1964) shows nearly identical traits, except for maximum size and egg counts.
- O. causeyi's main drawbacks are its large size, which makes young too large for trout within 3 to 4 months, and its burrowing habit, which leaves fewer young available to trout in cooler months.
- The author proposes that the Great Lakes crayfish (*Orconectes propinquus*) may suit trout lakes better: it is primarily vegetarian, reaches a carapace length of no more than 40 mm, and is mostly non-burrowing, but it is absent from small ponds.
- Using two or more crayfish species together, one for weed control and another for trout forage, may give the best results.
