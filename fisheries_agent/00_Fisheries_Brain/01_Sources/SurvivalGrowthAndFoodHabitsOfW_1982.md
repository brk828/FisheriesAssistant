#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This study (Lynch, Johnson & Schell, 1982) evaluated the survival, growth, and food habits of **saugeye** — a hybrid of female [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*) and male [[Sauger]] (*Stizostedion canadense*) — stocked in four experimental Ohio ponds over three years (1979-1981). Saugeye were stocked as fingerlings (~34 mm) and tracked through their third summer. Results showed highly variable first-summer survival (0-81%), primarily driven by **predation pressure** from resident [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Green Sunfish]] rather than prey availability or stocking density. Where saugeye survived, growth was robust despite dense aquatic macrophyte cover, reaching 351-400 mm by the third year. Saugeye were found to be **exclusively piscivorous**, feeding opportunistically on the dominant available prey fish. The authors conclude saugeye show strong promise as an alternative large percid for small impoundment management, outperforming walleye (which historically show poor survival in ponds) and sauger (which rarely mature in pond environments).

## Background & Description

- **Origin**: F1 hybrid cross — female [[Walleye]] × male [[Sauger]]; identifying features first described by Stroud (1948).
- **Stocking size**: Mean total length = 34 mm (30 May 1979).
- **Growth trajectory** (Total Length):
  - End of Year 1 (Pond A): 195 mm
  - End of Year 1 (Pond C): 214 mm
  - End of Year 2 (all ponds): ~284-285 mm
  - End of Year 3 (Pond D): 351 mm
  - End of Year 3 (Pond B): 400 mm (significantly greater growth than Pond D, Kruskal-Wallis, *P* < 0.005)
- **Length-Weight Relationship** (pond saugeye):
  $$Log_{10}W = 3.01\ Log_{10}L - 5.10$$
  (P = 0.998; N = 73) — statistically indistinguishable from saugeye in Pleasant Hill Reservoir, indicating similar body condition between pond- and reservoir-raised fish.
- Comparative growth was slightly slower than reservoir-raised saugeye (e.g., Deer Creek, Pleasant Hill) and Lake Erie [[Sauger]]/[[Walleye]] populations, attributed to periods of prey scarcity rather than intrinsic growth limitation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Small (0.29-0.37 ha), shallow (max depth 3.0 m) warm-water ponds with dense [[Aquatic Macrophytes]] (*Najas guadalupensis*, *Myriophyllum sp.*, *Ceratophyllum demersum*, *Elodea canadense*).
- **Water Chemistry**: Dissolved oxygen 10-12 mg/L (June-July) declining to 5-7 mg/L (late Aug-Sept); pH 8.7-9.0 declining to 7.0-7.5, coinciding with macrophyte die-off/decay; hardness 69-86 mg/L; surface temps 24.5-32.0°C in summer, declining to 14.5-15.5°C by late September.
- **Trophic role**: Exclusively piscivorous throughout life stages sampled; behaves as an opportunistic predator utilizing whichever prey fish species dominates the local community — consistent with feeding ecology described for [[Walleye]] in eutrophic waters (Ryder and Kerr 1978).
- **Prey items recorded**: [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) young-of-year, and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*).
- **Predator interactions**: Survival strongly limited by presence of [[Largemouth Bass]] and adult [[Green Sunfish]], which preyed on stocked saugeye fingerlings — mirroring predation dynamics seen in stocked [[Tiger Muskellunge]] (Stein et al. 1981) and pond-reared [[Walleye]] (Smith and Moyle 1943).
- **Structural complexity**: Despite theoretical prey-refuge benefits of dense macrophytes (Glass 1971; Crowder and Cooper 1979), high macrophyte density in Ponds B and D coincided with *lowest* saugeye survival, suggesting predator density effects outweighed vegetative cover benefits; conversely, saugeye demonstrated efficient foraging within macrophyte beds once established (robust growth to 400 mm despite dense vegetation).

## Key Findings

- **Survival is Predation-Limited, Not Density-Limited**: Contrary to expectations from prior stocking studies (e.g., Beyerle 1978 on [[Northern Pike]]/[[Walleye]]), saugeye survival in this study was governed primarily by existing predator populations (bass, sunfish) rather than prey density or saugeye stocking density.
  - Pond A (1,250 saugeye/ha, low predators): 81% survival (95% CI: 51-100%)
  - Pond C (833 saugeye/ha, small green sunfish population): 31% minimum survival
  - Ponds B & D (large green sunfish / largemouth bass present): 0% first-summer survival
- **Long-term survival** (Age-II+, July 1980-Nov 1981): 24% (Pond B), 57% (Pond D).
- **Growth-Prey Coupling**: Weekly growth increment in Pond A dropped from 16 mm (prey-abundant period, ~86 fathead minnows/seine haul) to 1 mm (prey-scarce period, ~7 fathead minnows/seine haul), demonstrating strong density-dependent growth suppression once the saugeye population depleted its prey base.
- **Pond Suitability**: Saugeye successfully adapted to warm, macrophyte-dense pond conditions atypical of native walleye/sauger habitat, reaching desirable sizes (up to 400 mm) by age III.
- **Management Recommendation**: Saugeye represent a viable alternative to walleye (poor pond survival) and sauger (fails to mature in ponds) as a stockable large percid for small impoundments, provided low resident predator density or use of larger stocker fish to avoid early predation.
- **Reproductive Note**: No evidence of self-sustaining saugeye populations; some backcrossing to parent species (walleye/sauger) has been informally observed elsewhere, but periodic restocking is likely necessary for pond management programs.
- **Diet Summary Table** (Percentage Occurrence in Stomachs, 1979-1980): Fathead minnows and green sunfish dominated in Ponds A/C (1979); bluegill young-of-year dominated in Ponds B/C (1980); golden shiners dominated in Pond D (1980).
