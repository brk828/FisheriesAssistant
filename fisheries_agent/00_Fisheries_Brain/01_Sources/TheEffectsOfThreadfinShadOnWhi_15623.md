# Effects of Threadfin Shad on White Crappie in Isabella Reservoir, Kern County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game administrative report (Inland Fisheries Report No. 66-6, submitted March 1966) evaluates whether [[Threadfin Shad]] stocked as forage improved the growth of a stunted [[White Crappie]] population in [[Isabella Reservoir]]. Scales from 444 white crappie, collected by gill net between 1962 and 1965, were analyzed for growth. Growth increased after shad establishment, most strongly in the second and third years of life. Stomach records confirm shad use as forage across the year, including spring, which suggests winter carryover of shad. The author concludes that crappie have used shad as forage and grown significantly larger since shad introduction, though a direct causal link is not conclusively proven.

## Background & Description

[[Isabella Reservoir]] is the southernmost major reservoir in the Sierra Nevada, about 40 miles northeast of Bakersfield. It was built mainly for flood protection of Bakersfield and Kern County agricultural land. It has a gross pool of 570,000 acre-feet, a maximum surface area of 11,200 acres, and an average depth of 50 feet at gross pool.

White crappie were planted in 1955 (100 adults) and 1956 (390 adults). Reproduction was successful and no further plantings were needed. Anglers reported good early catches, with many one-pound fish, but in 1960 and 1961 reports of declining size prompted an investigation. A March 1961 creel census and scale sample (125 fish) showed that white crappie were the dominant predator and that growth had been very slow in 1960. The population was judged stunted, probably from overpopulation relative to available forage.

The reservoir holds many standing and submerged trees that provide crappie habitat and attract anglers. The fish community includes [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[White Catfish]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Sacramento Squawfish]], among others (about 15 species listed across planted, gill-net-captured, and uncaptured categories).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Forage introduction:** 3,000 threadfin shad were released in May 1961 and 2,500 more in May 1962. An August 1962 survey found that the shad had reproduced and were abundant.
- **Water volume:** Crappie growth did not track reservoir volume changes. Growth held steady or rose during the 1959–1962 volume decline, accelerated as storage rose from 1962 to early 1964, and kept increasing in most year classes through 1965 despite falling volume. The author attributes this to large, shallow, productive littoral areas that persist at all water levels.
- **Overwintering forage:** Early spring gill net catches yielded few shad. The author suggests this reflects either reduced activity at low water temperatures or winter die-offs. Shad remains nonetheless appeared in crappie stomachs in spring and summer.
- **Sampling methods:** Gill nets 150 feet long with 3/4- to 4-inch stretch mesh were set in fall 1962, spring/summer/fall 1963, and spring and fall 1964 and 1965.

## Key Findings

- **Growth gains after shad establishment:** Average increments increased 65% in second-year growth and 133% in third-year growth. First- and fourth-year growth rose 23% and 16%. Small samples (six fish or fewer) were excluded from the comparison.
- **Largest annual growth:** The greatest increment for all year classes occurred in the second year, averaging 2.8 inches. The smallest occurred in the fifth year.
- **Year-class advantage:** The 1958 year class had no shad available. The 1959 class had one year of shad use, and the 1960 class had two. By 1965 all year classes had access to shad. Growth of 1962 and later classes exceeded earlier classes, especially in years two and three.
- **Stomach evidence:** Shad remains occurred in crappie stomachs on every sampling trip in 1964 and 1965, including spring samples, indicating a substantial winter carryover of shad.
- **Overall conclusion:** The report states that the growth increase is not conclusive proof of a direct causal link. Four supporting points were given: shad are used as forage most of the year; crappie are larger on average; they grow faster; and water fluctuations do not control growth.
- **Recommendations for further study:**
  - Collect weight-length data to assess crappie condition.
  - Determine fork length at scale formation in young-of-the-year crappie.
  - Determine the size at which young crappie begin eating shad.
