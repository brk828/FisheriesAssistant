# Food of the Bigmouth Buffalo, *Ictiobus cyprinellus*, in Northwest Iowa Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 study of the [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*), the largest member of the sucker family in Iowa, tested the long-held view that the species is a bottom feeder similar to [[Common Carp]]. Analysis of 259 stomachs (184 containing food) from seven shallow, glacial, eutrophic [[Northwest Iowa Lakes]] collected 1946–1953 indicated that buffalo are predominantly plankton feeders, consuming mainly small crustaceans (Entomostraca). The author's method followed the [[Carp Food Habits Study]] (Moen, 1953).

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Three *Ictiobus* species occur in Iowa: the bigmouth buffalo (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*), the black buffalo (*Ictiobus niger*), and the smallmouth buffalo (*Ictiobus bubalus*). The bigmouth is the most common in northwest Iowa lakes; the other two are comparatively rare.
- **Management history:** Buffalo have been removed from Iowa lakes as undesirable fish since the early 1900s. Harlan and Speaker (1951) reported that nearly half a million pounds are removed annually from state inland waters by the State Conservation Commission.
- **Prior knowledge:** Earlier accounts (Forbes and Richardson, 1920; Everman and Clark, 1920; Eddy and Surber, 1947) were based on very few fish and described diets of algae, seeds, distillery slops, insect larvae, molluscs, and vegetation. These led to the assumption that buffalo are primarily bottom feeders.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study lakes:** Fish were collected from East Okoboji, Upper and Lower Gull area lakes (Gull/Little Spirit and Silver Lakes in Dickinson County), Spirit and Tuttle Lakes in Emmet County, and Storm Lake in Buena Vista County. All were shallow (maximum depth about 23 feet), glacial, and eutrophic, ranging from 1,000 to just under 6,000 acres.
- **Collection methods:** Seines, gillnets, and traps; drag seines operated by rough-fish removal crews supplied most specimens.
- **Dietary analysis:** The entire digestive tract was treated as stomach, since the buffalo gut is a continuous tube. Contents were volumetrically measured, mixed in water, and examined under a binocular microscope, with a 10% subsample taken from collections exceeding 10 cc. Food volumes were estimated as percentages and converted to cc; amounts under 0.1 cc were recorded as trace.
- **Plankton comparison:** Storm Lake plankton samples were taken on July 27, 1951 and February 4, 1952 using 10-liter water samples.

## Key Findings

- **Predominant diet:** Buffalo are mainly plankton feeders, eating principally small crustaceans (Entomostraca), which made up the bulk of food volume in most collections.
- **Insects:** Insect larvae were frequent but rarely exceeded 1% of total volume in any collection. Midge larvae (Tendipedidae) were the most common insects. Rotifers and hydrachnids were unimportant.
- **Plant material:** Mostly blue-green algae, chiefly *Microcystis* (with *Aphanizomenon* possibly under-recorded). Algae reached 51% of volume in two Spirit Lake buffalo (June) but seldom exceeded 6%. Diatoms were negligible; plant debris seldom exceeded 10% of volume.
- **Seasonal contrast:** Winter stomachs (56 examined from three lakes) were 43% occupied versus 80% in open water. Winter food was 100% entomostracans, with one trace of blue-green algae; stomach volumes were smaller in winter.
- **Supplementary data:** In 48 East Okoboji buffalo (1940, not part of the 259), 73% contained food, and entomostracans made up 90–95% of volume.
- **Organism counts (16 stomachs):** Young fish (6.7–7.8 g) contained 1,330 and 2,000 entomostracans. Adult summer stomachs ranged from 4,000 to 1,250,000 individuals (average 200,000). Winter stomachs averaged 8,600 (range 5,000–18,000).
- **Selectivity:** In Storm Lake, July plankton samples averaged 7.0 cladocerans and 12.2 copepods per liter, while four buffalo stomachs averaged 7,131 cladocerans and 6,492 copepods and contained no algae, despite blue-green algae being an estimated 10–20 times more numerous than entomostracans in the water. The author concluded buffalo are selective feeders, either choosing entomostracans or locating swarms free of algae. The counts carry considerable error.
- **Conclusion:** The assumption that bigmouth buffalo are primarily bottom feeders, similar to carp, is not supported by these data.
