# Ecology and Conservation Biology of Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in the Little Colorado River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1990 technical proposal submitted by [[Arizona State University]] to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] under the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program. It requests a five-year study (1990-1995) of the reproduction, movements, distribution, abundance, and early-life survivorship of the federally endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in the lower [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), Arizona. The proposal addresses data gaps in a species whose largest known population depends on the LCR for spawning and congregation. It describes base camps, field crew structure, sampling protocols, laboratory experiments on thermal shock and predation, coordination with other agencies, personnel and deliverables.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is a cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system of western North America. It was described by Miller (1946).
- **Conservation status:** Its distribution and numbers have declined sharply due to anthropogenic modification of its physical and biological environment. It was federally listed as endangered (USFWS 1983).
- **Population stronghold:** The largest known population lives in the Colorado River and tributaries in [[Grand Canyon]]. Substantial congregation and reproduction occur only in one major tributary, the LCR, and mainly near the [[Confluence Area]] of the two rivers.
- **Prior research gaps:** Earlier studies (e.g., Kaeding and Zimmerman 1983; Minckley 1977, 1979, 1989) concentrated on the spawning season and the confluence area. Other seasons received only sporadic attention. Key life-history questions, including juvenile and adult movement and residency in the LCR and basic reproductive biology, remained unresolved.
- **Coordinated programs:** The proposal fits within a set of related studies: [[AZGFD]] spawning-season monitoring and larval fish work, [[USFWS]] habitat studies, USBR/contractor research on the mainstem, and AZGFD native fish studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** The study covers 21 km of the lower LCR, from Blue Spring downstream to the mainstem confluence. It is divided into a confluence area and three sequential 7-km reaches (LCR1 to LCR3). LCR2 and LCR3 are further split into 1-km sections for stratified random sampling. The project may extend upstream into LCR1 if chub are found there.
- **Atomizer Falls:** A barrier that separates the sampled reaches from the upper reach to Blue Spring. Humpback chub have not been collected in the upper reach, which is generally thought to be unoccupied.
- **Flow regime:** Discharge at Cameron, Arizona, peaks during August-October (convective storms) and March-April (snowmelt). It is lowest from May to July. Extended no-flow periods occur at Cameron, while flow from Blue Spring stays constant.
- **Seasons defined:** Spring (March-May), summer (June-August), autumn (September-November), and winter (December-February). The proposal notes that hydrologic and biotic "seasons" may differ by year and species.
- **Habitat types targeted:** Quiet near-shore pools (including those above and below [[Travertine Dams]]), chutes and breaches through travertine dams, runs, and riffles.
- **Water chemistry:** LCR water chemistry is expected to limit radio-signal detection under base flow and depths greater than about 1 m (R. Williams, personal communication).
- **Non-native species:** Rainbow trout, common carp, and channel catfish are known or expected to co-occur with young humpback chub. Other non-natives mentioned include red shiner, fathead minnow, plains killifish, and striped bass.
- **Other native fishes:** Speckled dace, bluehead sucker, and flannelmouth sucker are also present. Their LCR data are described as essentially nonexistent.

## Key Findings

The document is a proposal, so its main points are the planned objectives, methods, and expected outputs.

**Logistics**
- Two semi-permanent base camps (four-year installations) are planned, at the Salt Canyon/Salt Trail site and about 3 km upstream from the confluence. Each has a wooden platform, three canvas wall tents, a kitchen area, and a gasoline generator.
- Radio communication links the camps, [[Grand Canyon National Park]] (NPS), [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] in Flagstaff, and the Navajo Natural Heritage Program in Window Rock. The plan includes ten hand-held radios (seven in use, three backup).
- Helicopters supplied by USBR move personnel and materials.

**Field crews**
- Crew sizes vary by season (Table 1). Peak spring-summer activity has the largest crews, with trap, net, and reproductive crews at each camp.
- Autumn and winter visits (15-30 days) use smaller 3-person crews, and cold-water outfitting is required.

**Distribution, abundance, and movement**
- Sampling uses hoop nets, seines, angling, and entanglement nets (as a backup), and records species, total length, weight, sex, health, and reproductive condition.
- Humpback chub are anesthetized in MS-222 and then PIT-tagged. Native fish over 100 mm TL are PIT-tagged. Smaller fish are marked by caudal fin clip.
- Absolute abundance will be estimated from mark-recapture data, using Peterson-type or multiple-census methods (e.g., Schumacher and Eschmeyer; Ricker 1975).
- Movement will be studied with bi-directional two-way fish traps (1.52 cm mesh) at three sites: about 1 km above the confluence, near the Salt Canyon camp, and above Atomizer Falls. The traps are expected to capture most fish longer than about 125 mm.
- A possible transplant of chub above Atomizer Falls is proposed to test establishment and residency.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning of humpback chub has never been observed. The study aims to identify spawning sites and behavior through a dedicated reproductive crew, using radio alerts, artificial substrate, and diving observations. Collecting fertilized ova is planned as confirmation.

**Early-life survivorship**
- Survival will be estimated from length-frequency analyses, mark-recapture data, and tetracycline marking, using [[Growth and Survival Analysis]] methods (Burnham et al. 1987).
- Laboratory tests will expose embryos reared at 10-25°C to instantaneous thermal shocks of 5, 10, and 15°C.

**Biotic interactions**
- The proposal states that predation by non-native fishes is the most important factor affecting native recruitment, citing Minckley and others. Natives survive to maturity in the absence of non-natives but die in large numbers at small size when non-natives are present.
- Planned work includes laboratory digestion-rate experiments (fish starved for 48 hours, then sacrificed at 10-240 minutes after feeding, at 10, 15, 20, and 25°C), laboratory predation trials, stomach examination of field-caught predators, and possible immunoassay methods (separate ASU study).

**Coordination, personnel, and deliverables**
- Coordination runs through the GCES/Conservation Measures Aquatic Coordination Team. USFWS habitat work is recommended for its first year to focus on transects and habitat data, with fish habitat use studies in year two.
- Co-investigators are Drs. Michael E. Douglas and Paul C. Marsh of ASU. The Navajo Natural Heritage Program (Michael C. Tremble, Director) is the subcontractor, providing up to four biologists for six weeks each per year.
- Deliverables: quarterly reports, annual reports due by 30 September each year, a draft final report by 28 February of year five, and a final report within 60 days of review comments. Raw data go to AZGFD.
- Permits held: AZGFD permits MRSH00000-15 and -23 and USFWS permit PRT-676811, with annual renewals.

**Schedule**
- Reconnaissance visits start in autumn 1990. Intensive field work runs from spring-summer 1991 through summer 1994. Laboratory work is completed in years 1-2 or 2-3, depending on larval availability.
