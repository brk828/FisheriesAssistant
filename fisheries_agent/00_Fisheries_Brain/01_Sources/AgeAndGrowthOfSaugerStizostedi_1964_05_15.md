# Age and Growth of Sauger, *Stizostedion canadense*, in Lewis and Clark Lake

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Percidae #Stizostedion #Sauger #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1964 study by C. David Vanicek examines the age, growth, condition, food habits, and sex ratio of the [[Sauger]] (*Stizostedion canadense*) population in [[Lewis And Clark Lake]] (Gavins Point Reservoir) and the adjacent Missouri River above the reservoir, based on 953 fish collected from May–October 1962. Using scale-based age determination (531 fish aged), the study found that sauger exhibit their greatest growth increment in the first year of life, with growth increments declining in each subsequent year. Multiple regression analysis revealed significant effects of age, location (river vs. reservoir), sex, and year class on growth, with reservoir fish ultimately outgrowing river fish in later years. No fish older than age VI were captured. The [[1956 Year Class]], produced in the reservoir's first year of impoundment, was notably abundant, and 1962 was a better-than-average growth year for young-of-year sauger. [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) were identified as the predominant food source. Female sauger became significantly more abundant than males in older age groups, consistent with findings in other studied populations.

## Background & Description

- **Total length measurement**: Snout tip to tip of caudal fin (compressed), measured to nearest millimeter.
- **Weight**: Recorded in ounces; length-weight relationships calculated using hundredths of pounds.
- **Condition factor (C)**: C = (W × 10^5)/L³ (W in lbs, L in inches)
  - Reservoir sauger average: **29.3**
  - River sauger average: **27.9**
  - Condition increased with length; no seasonal or sex-based differences detected.
  - Historical range for Lewis and Clark Lake: 27.2 (1948) to 35.4 (1956); unweighted 7-year mean = 30.7.
  - Fort Randall Reservoir range: 28.7 (1954) to 31.5 (1956).

- **Length-weight regression equations**:
  - Reservoir (n=166): Log W = -1.921 + 3.318 Log L
  - River (n=136): Log W = -2.052 + 3.418 Log L
  - Both regression coefficients (>3.0) indicate **positive allometric growth** — sauger become more robust (heavier per unit length) as they grow. Reservoir coefficient significantly >3.0 (p<0.01).
  - Analysis of covariance showed a **highly significant difference (p<0.01)** between adjusted means of river vs. reservoir regression lines, suggesting either separate populations or differing environmental influences. Reservoir fish were heavier-for-length up to 16 inches; this reversed for larger fish.

- **Body-scale relationship**: L = 1.428 + 2.550(R), where L = total length (inches), R = anterior scale radius ×44 (based on 80 fish; scale impressions on plastic strips, read at 44X magnification).

- **Annulus formation**: Early June identified as primary period of annulus formation. "Crossing over" of circuli in the lateral scale field was the most reliable annulus criterion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat**: [[Lewis And Clark Lake]] (Gavins Point Reservoir) is the smallest/southernmost of six Missouri River mainstem reservoirs (33,000 acres, 540,000 acre-ft capacity, max depth ~45 ft, 100-mi shoreline). Constructed for flow regulation, hydropower, flood control, navigation, and recreation; closed July 1955, reached full pool June 1956. Shoreline mostly steep/rocky with some flat, intermittently flooded areas; aquatic vegetation scarce. Niobrara River inflow deposits significant silt at the reservoir's upper end.

**Water conditions during study**: Max surface temps: 84°F (reservoir), 80°F (river), 72°F (Fort Randall tailwaters). Secchi depths ranged 1–60 inches, with turbidity highest near Niobrara mouth.

**Benthic fauna** (per Schmulbach and Sandholm 1962): chironomids, *Hexagenia*, oligochaetes.

**Co-occurring fish community**: [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*, dominant, mostly YOY), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[River Carpsucker]] (*Carpiodes carpio*), [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*), [[Shortnose Gar]] (*Lepisosteus platostomus*), [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*), [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus platorynchus*), [[Emerald Shiner]] (*Notropis atherinoides*).

**Diet/Trophic role**: 133 stomachs analyzed (111 with food).
- Fish were the sole food item for age group II+ sauger.
- Young-of-year and yearling sauger fed heavily on insect larvae (chironomids) and cladocerans (*Daphnia*) in June, transitioning to fish (mostly *Notropis*) by late July/August.
- [[Gizzard Shad]] was the dominant food overall, first appearing in diets July 12 onward — mirroring the "plankton-shad-game fish" food chain described for Norris Reservoir ([[Dendy 1945]]).
- Emerald shiner and other young fish each found in only ~5% of stomachs; cannibalism rare (1 sauger ate a young sauger).
- Parasites identified: *Camallanus* sp. (nematode, in YOY stomachs and protruding from an adult's anus) and *Contracaecum* sp. (visceral mesenteries).

**No definite habitat preference** was found — sauger were captured across depths and reservoir areas using gill nets, frame nets, electric shocker, trawl, and seine.

## Key Findings

### Growth Patterns
- Greatest growth increment occurs in **Year 1** of life; increments decline progressively thereafter.
- No sauger older than **age VI** was captured (contrasts with age VII in Norris Reservoir, age X in Lake of the Woods and Lake Winnipeg).
- Reservoir sauger grow faster overall and in later years compared to river sauger, though early-year growth is similar between locations.
- A "reverse Lee's phenomenon" was observed: older fish showed *larger* calculated lengths at annulus 1 than younger fish at the same annulus, suggesting genuine differences in first-year growth between year classes rather than sampling artifact.
- Multiple regression (IBM 7074 computer, n=254 fish) modeled total length as a function of age, location, year class, sex, and their interactions — all found to be significant contributors, though sex effect was only marginally significant (p≈0.05).
- Growth in weight increases through year 3 (reservoir) or year 4 (river) before declining, even as length increments continuously decrease.
- A marked growth-rate decline between years 3 and 4 may correspond to onset of sexual maturity.

### Seasonal Growth (1962)
- First YOY sauger captured June 25 (seine); by mid-October, averaged ~8 inches — well above the average first-annulus length of 6.3 inches, indicating 1962 was a **better-than-average growth year**.
- Age groups 0, I, and II exceeded 100% of their average yearly increment by mid-October; older groups (III, IV, V) had not yet reached full expected increments by season's end.

### Year Class Strength
- The **1956 year class** (first year of impoundment) was the most abundant, dominating catches in 4 of 5 years sampled.
- 1957 year class also relatively large; 1958 and 1959 year classes were comparatively small.
- An inverse relationship was noted (except 1959) between first-year growth and growth in subsequent years across year classes.

### Comparative Growth (Table 9)
- Lewis and Clark Lake sauger grow **faster than** those in [[Lake Of The Woods]], [[Lake Erie]], and [[Lake Winnipeg]].
- [[Norris Reservoir]] sauger outgrow Lewis and Clark sauger in the first two years, but Lewis and Clark sauger surpass them at later annuli.

### Sex Ratio
- Of 343 sexed fish, 212 (62%) were female.
- Chi-square testing showed significantly skewed sex ratios (favoring females) in age groups V and VI and in pooled data (p<0.01).
- Relative abundance of males decreases with age after year 3, consistent with patterns in [[Lake Of The Woods]] (66% female, Carlander 1950) and [[Norris Reservoir]] (Hassler 1958: equal through age II, female-biased age III–VI).

### Statistical Notes
- Analysis of covariance indicated heterogeneity of variance between river/reservoir datasets (F=4.236, p<0.05), so covariance results are approximate.
- Regression coefficients (slopes) between river and reservoir not significantly different at 0.05 (but significant at 0.10); adjusted means significantly different (p<0.01) — supporting distinct length-weight relationships between the two habitats.
