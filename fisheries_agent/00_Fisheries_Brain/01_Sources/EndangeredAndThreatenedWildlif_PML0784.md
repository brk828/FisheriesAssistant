# Proposed Critical Habitat for Spikedace and Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service), published in the Federal Register on December 20, 2005 (Vol. 70, No. 243), proposes to designate approximately 633 river miles (1018.7 km) of [[Critical Habitat]] for the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), both listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The proposed areas lie in New Mexico and Arizona within the [[Gila River Basin]]. The rule identifies [[Primary Constituent Elements]] (PCEs), defines five river complexes, proposes exclusions for tribal lands under section 4(b)(2), and questions the value of critical habitat designation in general. Comments were accepted until February 21, 2006, with public hearing requests due February 3, 2006.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Spikedace and loach minnow are small, benthic-associated freshwater fishes of the [[Cyprinidae]] family, native to the southwestern United States. Both were listed in 1986 and have recovery plans (USFWS 1991a, 1991b).
- **Range reduction:** Spikedace now occupies about 10 percent of its historical range, found in Graham, Pinal and Yavapai counties (Arizona) and Grant, Catron and Hidalgo counties (New Mexico). Loach minnow occupies about 18 percent of its historical range, in Graham, Greenlee and Pinal counties (Arizona) and Catron, Grant and Hidalgo counties (New Mexico).
- **Regulatory history:** A 1999 court order required a critical habitat designation; a final designation was published April 25, 2000 (65 FR 24328) and later set aside in August 2004 by the U.S. District Court for the District of New Mexico. The present rule is a new proposal.
- **Legal framework:** Critical habitat is defined under section 3 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Protection operates through [[Section 7 Consultation]], which applies only where there is a Federal nexus. The rule notes the Ninth Circuit's [[Gifford Pinchot]] decision (2004), which invalidated the Service's prior definition of adverse modification.
- **Policy stance:** The Service argues that critical habitat designation provides little additional protection for most listed species while consuming large conservation resources. Of 1,253 listed species under its jurisdiction, only 470 (38 percent) have designated critical habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements (spikedace):**
- Prefers intermediate-sized streams with swift, turbulent flows, and sand/gravel substrates.
- Adults: slow to swift velocities of 20–90 cm/s in shallow water (10 cm to 1 m deep).
- Larvae are most common in slow-velocity stream margins; juveniles occur in slow to moderate flows.
- Substrates with moderate fine sediment embeddedness; areas with cobble and gravel.

**Habitat requirements (loach minnow):**
- Lives on the bottom of small to large rivers, preferring shallow, swift, turbulent riffles among clean, loose gravel-to-cobble substrates.
- Adult flows recorded from 0.6 to 31.2 in/s (2.0 to 79.2 cm/s) in occupied areas; Aravaipa Creek adults preferred 15.0–20.4 in/s.
- Larvae occupy slower areas (3.6–19.2 in/s); juveniles in faster water than larvae.
- Water temperatures tolerated: about 8.2–21.8 °C (48.2–71.6 °F) in studied areas; dissolved oxygen should exceed 3 ppm.
- Diet: opportunistic benthic insectivores; mayfly nymphs made up as much as 77 percent of food volume in one Aravaipa Creek study.

**Threats identified:**
- [[Nonnative Fish Species]] (e.g., [[Red Shiner]], channel and flathead catfish) compete with and prey on the native fishes.
- Water diversions and impoundments reduce riffle habitat and alter flows.
- Livestock grazing, mining, road construction, recreation, wildfire and associated sedimentation.
- Water pollutants including copper, arsenic, mercury, cadmium and fuels.

**Flooding:** Natural flooding is described as beneficial, cleaning riffles, reducing embeddedness, boosting recruitment and displacing nonnative fishes.

## Key Findings

**Proposed designation**
- Approximately 633 river miles (1018.7 km) across five river complexes: Verde River; Black River; Middle Gila/Lower San Pedro/Aravaipa Creek; San Francisco and Blue rivers; and Upper Gila River.
- Critical habitat is proposed to include the stream channel plus a lateral width of 300 feet on either side of bankfull width, used as a surrogate for the 100-year floodplain.
- The proposal covers portions of 10 streams for spikedace and 23 streams for loach minnow.

**Notable units**
- Upper Gila River (102.1 mi) is the largest single unit.
- Aravaipa Creek (28.1 mi) supports the largest remaining spikedace and loach minnow populations in Arizona.
- San Francisco River (126.5 mi) for loach minnow, the longest loach minnow stretch.
- Blue River (51.1 mi) for loach minnow.

**Exclusions and non-proposed areas**
- Upper San Pedro River was not proposed due to nonnative fishes and absence of both species; comment is sought.
- Fossil Creek was not proposed because it is currently unoccupied; comment is sought.
- Lands proposed for exclusion under section 4(b)(2) include the San Carlos Apache Tribe (Eagle Creek, ~27.7 km) and White Mountain Apache Tribe (East Fork White River, 20.1 km), both covered by management plans. The Yavapai-Apache Nation lands may be considered for exclusion in the final rule.

**Economic and procedural status**
- A draft economic analysis was being prepared; the regulatory flexibility finding was deferred until it is complete.
- The Service anticipates no significant impact on energy supplies and no federal mandate on states or local governments.
- Peer review by at least three independent specialists is planned.
- Under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] and the Tenth Circuit ruling (Catron County Board of Commissioners, 1996), a NEPA analysis will be undertaken.

**Service position**
- The Service argues that the [[Gifford Pinchot]] standard and tribal management plans give conservation benefits comparable to or greater than critical habitat designation.
- The Service considers that designation on tribal lands would intrude on tribal sovereignty and damage government-to-government relations.
