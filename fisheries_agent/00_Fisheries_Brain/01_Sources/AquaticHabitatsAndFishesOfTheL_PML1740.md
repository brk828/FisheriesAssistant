# Aquatic Habitats and Fishes of Topock Gorge, Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This appendix describes the aquatic habitats, fishes, benthic invertebrates, aquatic plants and algae, water-borne pigments, and physical-chemical conditions of the [[Topock Gorge]] Division of the lowermost [[Colorado River]]. The reach is a 22.5 km stretch of rocky canyon river that enters the slack waters of the upper [[Lake Havasu]]. Eighteen fish species have been recorded historically, and 12 were taken or observed during the 1974–76 survey. Fishes were scarce, and catches were concentrated in backwaters. Only one native species, the [[Razorback Sucker]], has been recorded in recent years. Benthic diversity was low, with five taxa dominated by chironomid midges, the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]], and oligochaetes. Water temperatures, conductance, and dissolved oxygen were relatively uniform, and nutrient levels were moderate.

## Background & Description

The Topock Gorge Division lies between rocky canyon walls, with banks unmodified apart from inundation. Backwaters are mostly natural, though they are augmented by operations at [[Davis Dam]] and [[Parker Dam]]. Lentic habitats make up approximately 2.0% of the total surface water area.

- **Substrate:** The channel is mainly boulder and gravel, with bedrock exposed in faster sections. Sand forms limited lateral bars in the upper reach and becomes much more important downstream. Backwater substrates range from sand to heavily organic material.
- **Water levels:** Fluctuations are marked in the upper reach. Farther downstream, Lake Havasu's slack waters dampen vertical fluctuations. A pronounced lag occurs between high and low water, reflecting time-of-flow variations from Davis Dam.
- **Depth:** Channel depths range from 2 to 4 m, with pools up to 9 m deep where the river turns sharply against steep canyon walls (Devil's Elbow).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian and aquatic vegetation.** Steep, stony banks limit riparian development. Cottonwoods, willows, seep willows, and arrowweeds fringe backwaters and sandbar areas. Sedges (dominated by *Scirpus olneyi*), cattail, and some giant African reed occur at the upper end of the reach. Aquatic plants were rare in the main channel. Backwaters supported beds of sago pondweed, coontail, hornwort, and other submerged species, along with duckweeds and bladderwort. Thin beds of *Cladophora*-type filamentous algae were the most conspicuous algae, with encrusting blue-green algae and diatoms on boulders and cobbles.

**Food limitation.** Invertebrate diversity was low, which may reflect food limitation. Particulate matter (plankton) from Lake Havasu is depleted by the time water reaches the gorge. Declines in filter-feeding Corbicula may illustrate this limitation.

**Pigments and productivity.** Water-borne pigment concentrations were similar to those in the upstream [[Mohave Valley Division]]. Chlorophyll A was lowest from midnight to noon and highest in the evening. It appeared to track algal flotation and discharge changes, which are driven by dam operations. Animal carotenoids were somewhat lower than upstream, possibly reflecting a decline in zooplankton from Lake Havasu.

**Physical-chemical conditions.**
- **Temperature:** Relatively constant, ranging from 17 to 20 °C. Cool hypolimnetic water from Lake Havasu moderates channel temperatures. Surface backwater temperatures can exceed 25 °C in summer (1971 data).
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Higher in daytime than at night, though the nighttime decline was not pronounced. Deeper backwaters showed some oxygen depletion near the bottom.
- **Conductance and turbidity:** Conductance remained near 950 µmho in the channel, rising to about 1,000 µmho. Turbidity was low and undetected in many samples.
- **pH:** Not reported because of equipment failure.
- **Nutrients:** Of 48 orthophosphate (PO4-P) analyses, 28 ranged from 0.01 to 0.09 mg/L, 9 from 0.1 to 0.19 mg/L, and 11 exceeded 0.2 mg/L (maximum 0.38 mg/L). Of 40 nitrogen determinations (OCR-garbled, likely NO3-N), 24 fell between 0.1 and 0.19 mg/L, and 15 exceeded 0.2 mg/L (maximum 0.24 mg/L). A slight nutrient depletion occurred during periods of high photosynthesis.

## Key Findings

### Fishes
- **Species richness:** 18 species have been recorded historically from the division, and 12 were taken or observed in the 1974–76 survey.
- **Survey species:** The 12 species taken or observed included threadfin shad, rainbow trout, carp, goldfish, red shiner, channel catfish, black bullhead, yellow bullhead, mosquitofish, striped bass, largemouth bass, and bluegill. The remaining historically recorded species were golden shiner, fathead minnow, green sunfish, redear sunfish, and black crappie. The table marks (asterisks and "x") are partly garbled in the OCR, so the survey list is approximate.
- **Scarcity:** Seining over more than 1,200 m at various locations yielded no catches. Hoop nets were also unsuccessful. Small fishes and juveniles of larger species were essentially absent during the study period.
- **Gear results:**
  - *Entanglement nets* (gill, seine, and trammel) caught yellow bullhead, carp, bluegill, largemouth bass, channel catfish, black bullhead, striped bass, and softshell turtle (*Trionyx spiniferus emoryi*), in decreasing order of numerical abundance. Most catches came from backwaters, especially downstream near the upper end of Lake Havasu.
  - *Mainstream gill nets* took only striped bass, at 0.50 fish and 0.35 kg per night.
  - *Trammel nets* produced the highest catch per unit effort. Bullheads, channel catfish, carp, bluegill, and largemouth bass were taken at a rate of 33 fish weighing about 20.1 kg per night.
  - *Mesh-size comparison:* The smallest mesh (net type 13) averaged 2.50 individuals and about 1.50 kg of carp and striped bass per night. The intermediate mesh (net type 14) caught 8.2 individuals totaling 7.6 kg per night. The largest mesh (net type 15) caught no fish in daytime and averaged 0.8 animals (1.5 kg) per night set.
- **Notable observations:** Red shiners were observed in schools of 5 to 25 in shallow, flowing water near the lower end of the division. A goldfish was found in a largemouth bass stomach, and a rainbow trout was seen in an angler's creel near RM 469.0.
- **Native species:** Only the razorback sucker has been recorded from the division in recent years.

### Benthic Invertebrates
- **Low diversity:** Only five taxa were recorded: chironomid midges, oligochaetes, the introduced Asiatic clam (*Corbicula*), a small snail (*Gyraulus*), and a tiny clam (*Pisidium*). The last two were rare.
- **Dominance shift:** Corbicula made up 98.5% of total weight (15.9 g/m²) in the upper reach, dropping to 61.7% (0.9 g/m²) downstream. Chironomid larvae rose from 1.0% to 55.2% of total biomass.
- **Chironomid density:** Mean numbers tripled, from 132 individuals/m² upstream to 366 individuals/m² downstream, with weight per larva unchanged.
- **Oligochaetes:** Numbers were similar up- and downstream (about 43/m²), with stable weights.
- **Absent groups:** Mayflies, other dipterans, caddisflies, and beetles were conspicuously absent. Their scarcity may reflect the coarse substrates and high flow rates, which made the Ekman dredge the main sampler.
- **Backwaters:** Backwaters held the same major taxa, in similar proportions, minus Corbicula. Corbicula was present in moderate numbers on harder bottoms but was not captured in formal sampling.

### Aquatic Plants and Algae
- Submerged plant beds in backwaters were diverse, but the main channel had few plants. Water-level fluctuations and unstable sandy substrates likely suppress growth.
- Drifting algal mats were scarce, but some mats floated in mid-afternoon in backwaters and then entered the current.

### Water Quality
- Temperatures were stable across the reach, and the backwaters were not much warmer than the mainstream.
- Conductance was low and uniform. Dissolved oxygen was adequate, with depletion only in deeper backwater bottoms.
- Nutrient levels were moderate, with a slight depletion during high photosynthesis.
- pH data are missing because of equipment failure.
