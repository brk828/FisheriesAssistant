# A Study of the Pollution Sources and Their Effect on the Aquatic Habitat of Eagle Creek Watershed, Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest, Arizona

#aquatic-ecology #stream-habitat #water-quality #pollution-assessment #native-fish #macroinvertebrates #Arizona

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive study assessed pollution sources and aquatic habitat quality in the [[Eagle Creek Watershed]], [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]], Arizona, from April 1975 to February 1976. The research examined physico-chemical parameters (temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, nutrients, sediment), biological indicators (macroinvertebrate diversity, native fish populations), and the taxonomic status of trout in [[Chitty Creek]]. Key findings revealed that [[Eagle Creek]] below [[Dry Prong Creek]] experiences summer temperatures exceeding recommended standards for trout (mean daily high 23.1°C vs. 20°C standard), high sediment loads (35-42% fines <0.85 mm), and reduced macroinvertebrate diversity (H=1.688, E=0.432) compared to [[Chitty Creek]] (H=3.451, E=0.786). Five native fish species ([[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Gila Mountain Sucker]], [[Gila Sucker]]) remained abundant with normal condition factors, indicating adequate habitat quality despite degradation. The [[Chitty Creek]] trout population was identified as a unique subspecies of [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*), differentiated from both [[Gila Trout]] and hatchery [[Rainbow Trout]]. No pollution quantities of nitrates or phosphates were detected from campgrounds or livestock facilities, though [[Dry Prong Creek]] contributed elevated sediments and warm water to [[Eagle Creek]].

## Background & Description

The [[Eagle Creek Watershed]] originates along the [[Mogollon Rim]] at approximately 7,600 ft elevation and flows 40 miles south to the [[Gila River]] near Morenci, Arizona. [[Chitty Creek]], a tributary, flows south from the Rim and joins [[Eagle Creek]] approximately 7 miles from its origin, separated by a 10 m waterfall. The study area encompasses a pine forest transition life zone with riparian vegetation dominated by cottonwoods, sycamores, and ponderosa pine. The watershed supports recreational uses including camping at [[Honeymoon Campground]], fishing, hiking, and hunting, as well as cattle grazing throughout the area.

Previous biological surveys documented native fish distributions (Miller and Lowe 1964, Minckley 1973) but comprehensive habitat quality assessments were lacking. The watershed represents one of the last semi-natural riparian systems with intact native fish fauna in southern Arizona. [[Eagle Creek]] receives annual Easter stockings of 500 catchable [[Rainbow Trout]] for put-and-take fishery at [[Honeymoon Campground]]. [[Chitty Creek]] above the waterfall harbors an isolated trout population of unknown taxonomic status, accessible only by horseback or hiking trail from Strayhorse Camp.

The study employed monthly sampling from April 1975 to February 1976 at six stations across three watershed sections: (1) [[Chitty Creek]] above and below falls, (2) [[Eagle Creek]] at [[Dry Prong Creek]], and (3) [[Eagle Creek]] at [[Honeymoon Campground]]. Sampling required horseback access above Four Drag Ranch, with horses and support provided by local residents Mack and Stella Hughes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Eagle Creek Watershed]] exists within a complex ecological gradient from cool headwater streams to warmer lower reaches. Water sources include snowmelt, springs, and runoff from the [[Mogollon Rim]], maintaining year-round flow in [[Eagle Creek]] from [[Dry Prong Creek]] downstream for 10-15 miles, though upper reaches are intermittent. [[Chitty Creek]] flows over bedrock for much of its 1.5 mile length before cascading over the waterfall, below which it becomes intermittent before joining [[Eagle Creek]].

Stream temperatures varied dramatically by location and season. [[Chitty Creek]] maintained coldwater conditions suitable for trout with summer daily minimums of 10-14°C and maximums of 17-18°C. [[Eagle Creek]] above [[Dry Prong Creek]] remained at or below 20°C in summer, but warmed drastically below the confluence where [[Dry Prong Creek]] contributed water up to 10°C warmer than [[Eagle Creek]]. At Four Drag Ranch, mean daily high temperatures during July 26-August 24 reached 23.1°C, exceeding Federal Water Pollution Control Administration standards for resident trout waters (20°C) by 3.1°C and rearing area standards (15°C) by 8°C.

Dissolved oxygen levels remained above 5-6 mg/l during daytime sampling, meeting minimum trout requirements. However, nighttime oxygen depletion may occur in [[Dry Prong Creek]] due to low flow, lack of riffles, dense aquatic vegetation, and warm temperatures. The pH ranged from 7.0-8.5 across the watershed, within acceptable limits for fish wellbeing and indicating a well-buffered system with minimal risk of pH shock for stocked trout.

Nutrient levels remained low throughout the watershed. Nitrate-nitrogen (NO₃-N) rarely exceeded 0.05 mg/l, well below the 0.3 mg/l threshold associated with problem algae growth. Ortho-phosphate (PO₄) levels varied dramatically by location: [[Chitty Creek]] and upper [[Eagle Creek]] at [[Dry Prong Creek]] showed elevated levels (0.5-1.0 mg/l), while lower [[Eagle Creek]] maintained levels at or below 0.05 mg/l, suggesting uptake by aquatic plants. No pollution quantities of nutrients were detected from [[Honeymoon Campground]], Four Drag Ranch corrals, or other point sources.

Total dissolved solids (TDS) remained low (120-190 mg/l) across the watershed, with [[Dry Prong Creek]] contributing elevated TDS to [[Eagle Creek]]. Conductivity measurements correlated well with TDS, and levels posed no problems for aquatic life or stocked trout acclimation.

Bottom composition analysis revealed substantial sediment loads throughout the system. [[Chitty Creek]] contained approximately 15-20% fines (<0.85 mm), similar to spawning sites of [[Arizona Trout]] in [[Big Bonito Creek]] (15%). [[Eagle Creek]] locations showed higher percentages: 35-42% fines, with approximately 50% of substrate <4 mm in size. [[Dry Prong Creek]] contributed significant amounts of fine sediments to [[Eagle Creek]], with bottom composition differing strikingly above versus below the confluence. Summer flooding did not significantly reduce fine sediment percentages, indicating a relatively stable but degraded substrate condition. Such high sediment concentrations (<20% survival to emergence expected for salmonid embryos) preclude successful trout reproduction in [[Eagle Creek]] below [[Dry Prong Creek]].

The aquatic plant community included abundant green algae (*Stigeoclonium* sp., *Spirogyra* sp., *Ulothrix* sp.), blue-green algae (*Oscillatoria* sp.), and vascular plants (*Potamogeton pectinatus*, *P. foliosus*, *Ranunculus aquatilis*, *Chara globularis*). Dense aquatic vegetation in lower [[Eagle Creek]] likely sequesters phosphates, explaining the dramatic decrease from 0.5-1.0 mg/l at [[Dry Prong Creek]] to <0.05 mg/l at Four Drag Ranch over a two-mile distance.

## Key Findings

**Physico-chemical Water Quality:**

1. **Temperature**: [[Chitty Creek]] maintained coldwater trout habitat (summer highs 17-18°C), while [[Eagle Creek]] below [[Dry Prong Creek]] exceeded trout temperature standards (mean daily high 23.1°C vs. 20°C recommended maximum). [[Dry Prong Creek]] contributed water up to 10°C warmer than [[Eagle Creek]], potentially forming a thermal barrier during low flow periods.

2. **Dissolved Oxygen**: Daytime levels consistently exceeded 5-6 mg/l minimum for trout wellbeing. Nighttime oxygen depletion may occur in [[Dry Prong Creek]] due to dense vegetation, warm temperatures, and low flow.

3. **pH**: Values ranged from 7.0-8.5, within acceptable limits for fish. The well-buffered system showed minimal variation, with no risk of pH shock for stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] (hatchery pH ~7.0, [[Eagle Creek]] at [[Honeymoon Campground]] pH 8.0-8.5 in April-May).

4. **Nutrients**: Nitrate-nitrogen remained low (<0.05 mg/l typically, never exceeding 0.1 mg/l except one May sample). Ortho-phosphate levels varied dramatically: [[Chitty Creek]] and upper [[Eagle Creek]] showed 0.5-1.0 mg/l (exceeding 0.1 mg/l threshold for excessive plant growth), while lower [[Eagle Creek]] maintained <0.05 mg/l due to plant uptake. No pollution quantities detected from campgrounds or livestock facilities.

5. **Total Dissolved Solids**: Low levels (120-190 mg/l) posed no problems for aquatic life. [[Dry Prong Creek]] contributed elevated TDS but not to problematic levels.

6. **Sediment**: High sediment loads throughout watershed: [[Chitty Creek]] 15-20% fines (<0.85 mm), [[Eagle Creek]] 35-42% fines, with ~50% of substrate <4 mm. [[Dry Prong Creek]] identified as major contributor of fine sediments. Summer flooding did not reduce sediment percentages, indicating stable but degraded conditions. Sediment levels predict <20% survival to emergence for salmonid embryos.

**Biological Indicators - Macroinvertebrates:**

Benthic macroinvertebrate analysis using Shannon-Weiner diversity indices revealed striking differences among watershed sections:

- **[[Chitty Creek]]**: Highest diversity (H=3.451) and evenness (E=0.786), indicating least degraded habitat. Mean abundance 61.9 insects/ft², biomass 0.53 g/ft². Twenty-one insect families collected, dominated by *Rhyacophilidae* (Trichoptera, 22.87% abundance).

- **[[Eagle Creek]] at [[Dry Prong Creek]]**: Lowest diversity (H=1.688) and evenness (E=0.432), indicating most degraded habitat. Mean abundance 118.8 insects/ft², biomass 0.73 g/ft². Fifteen families collected, with 68.10% abundance dominated by *Tendipedidae* (Diptera), suggesting environmental stress.

- **[[Eagle Creek]] at [[Honeymoon Campground]]**: Intermediate diversity (H=2.505) and evenness (E=0.601). Mean abundance 90.9 insects/ft², biomass 0.85 g/ft². Eighteen families collected, *Tendipedidae* abundance decreased to 36.24%.

EPA standards suggest E values of 0.6-0.8 for undegraded streams and <0.5 for degraded habitats. Results indicate [[Eagle Creek]] at [[Dry Prong Creek]] experiences moderate to severe habitat degradation, likely related to increased temperature and/or sedimentation. Riffles supported higher standing crops than pools across all locations. The dramatic improvement in diversity indices from [[Dry Prong Creek]] to [[Honeymoon Campground]] (2 miles downstream) suggests partial recovery despite continued sediment loading.

**Biological Indicators - Native Fish:**

Five native fish species documented by Miller and Lowe (1964) and Minckley (1973) remained present in substantial numbers:

- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*): Most abundant in vegetated pools of [[Dry Prong Creek]], with good recruitment of young fish. Length frequencies indicated normal growth, no stunting. Condition factor K=1.24-1.38 across seasons.

- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): Occurred in all three habitats (riffles, raceways, vegetated pools). Strong recruitment evident in [[Dry Prong Creek]]. K=1.26-1.41.

- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*): Concentrated in vegetated pools with good numbers of adults and juveniles in [[Dry Prong Creek]]. K=1.02-1.25.

- **[[Gila Mountain Sucker]]** (*Pantosteus clarki*): Numerically dominant species, occurring in all three habitats. Most abundant in summer sampling. K=1.25-1.71. Length frequencies did not reveal large numbers of small fish, suggesting different rearing areas or recruitment patterns than cyprinids.

- **[[Gila Sucker]]** (*Catostomus insignis*): Primarily in raceway habitats. K=1.28-1.30. Limited small fish in samples.

Seasonal sampling (April, August, February) revealed that P. clarki, G. robusta, and C. insignis were most abundant in summer, while A. chrysogaster and R. osculus showed less seasonal variation. [[Dry Prong Creek]]'s vegetated pools served as critical rearing habitat for young A. chrysogaster, R. osculus, and G. robusta. Only four juvenile salmonids captured in [[Eagle Creek]] during entire study, likely displaced from [[Chitty Creek]].

Condition factors for all species fell within normal ranges reported for similar species elsewhere (Carlander 1969), indicating adequate habitat quality for general wellbeing despite measured degradation. The persistence of all five native species suggests habitat changes have not exceeded their compensatory capacity, though long-term population trends remain unknown without historical abundance data.

**Taxonomy of [[Chitty Creek]] Trout:**

Morphometric analysis of 36 trout from [[Chitty Creek]] above the waterfall compared with 28 hatchery [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), 15 [[Gila Trout]] (*S. gilae*) from Main Diamond Creek, New Mexico, and 30 [[Arizona Trout]] (*S. apache*) from upper [[Big Bonito Creek]], Arizona yielded definitive taxonomic conclusions:

1. **Not hybridized with [[Rainbow Trout]]**: Discriminant function analysis placed [[Chitty Creek]] trout at great morphological distance from hatchery [[Rainbow Trout]]. If hybridization had occurred, group centroids would show intermediate positioning, which they did not.

2. **Not [[Gila Trout]]**: Despite [[Eagle Creek]]'s location in [[Gila River]] drainage (leading to Minckley's 1973 hypothesis), [[Chitty Creek]] trout showed greatest morphological distance from *S. gilae*. Four character ratios showed 90% non-overlap (C.D. ≥1.28) with [[Gila Trout]].

3. **Unique subspecies of [[Arizona Trout]]**: [[Chitty Creek]] trout most closely resembled *S. apache* from [[Big Bonito Creek]] but differed significantly in multiple characters. Discriminant function analysis correctly reclassified 100% of individuals to their proper groups. T-test comparisons revealed significant differences (p<0.05 to p<0.001) for most character ratios.

4. **Key diagnostic characters**: [[Chitty Creek]] trout distinguished from [[Arizona Trout]] primarily by smaller adipose fin length (mean ratio 0.069 vs. 0.084, 90% non-overlap) and shorter maxillary bone length (mean ratio 0.092 vs. 0.104, 87% non-overlap). Characters contributing most to discrimination included head length, dorsal fin position, adipose fin length, dorsal and anal fin sizes, and mouth size.

5. **Conservation significance**: This represents the first reported subspecies of [[Arizona Trout]]. The entire population exists in only 1.5 miles of stream above the waterfall, isolated from downstream fish by the 10 m barrier and intermittent flow. This extreme geographic restriction and genetic uniqueness warrant special conservation consideration.

**Management Implications:**

1. **Trout Fishery Limitations**: Summer temperatures and high sediment loads in [[Eagle Creek]] below [[Dry Prong Creek]] preclude establishment of self-sustaining trout populations. Current put-and-take [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery (500 catchable fish stocked at Easter) represents appropriate management given habitat constraints. A few fish over-summer but habitat remains marginal even for adult [[Rainbow Trout]].

2. **Alternative Species Concerns**: Introduction of [[Brown Trout]] or [[Smallmouth Bass]] not recommended despite potentially suitable temperature regimes. Both species would likely prey heavily on native fish, potentially causing drastic reductions or eliminations. [[Brown Trout]] establishment in similar White Mountain streams has led to difficult-to-catch populations and unknown impacts on native fauna. [[Smallmouth Bass]] show stunted growth in nearby [[Black River]] for unknown reasons.

3. **Native Fish Conservation**: [[Eagle Creek]] represents one of the last semi-natural riparian watersheds with large, healthy native fish fauna in southern Arizona. All five historically documented species persist with normal condition factors and apparent successful reproduction. Establishment of permanent non-native predatory fish populations would likely lead to eventual demise of some or all native species.

4. **Habitat Degradation Sources**: [[Dry Prong Creek]] identified as major non-point pollution source, contributing: (a) elevated water temperatures (up to 10°C warmer than [[Eagle Creek]]), (b) significant fine sediment loads (increasing [[Eagle Creek]] fines from ~20% to 35-42%), and (c) elevated total dissolved solids. The watershed drained by [[Dry Prong Creek]] loses many tons of topsoil annually during snowmelt and rainfall runoff.

5. **Potential Restoration**: Stream shading through riparian vegetation planting along [[Dry Prong Creek]] and [[Eagle Creek]] below the confluence might lower temperatures to meet trout requirements, but successful reproduction remains doubtful given sediment loads.
