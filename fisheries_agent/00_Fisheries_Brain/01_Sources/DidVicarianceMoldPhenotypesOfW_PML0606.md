# Vicariance and the Morphological Variation of Gila Chubs in the Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether historical tectonic and drainage changes explain the morphological variation of [[Gila Chub|Gila]] cyprinids in the [[Gila River Basin]] of southwestern North America. The authors (Michael E. Douglas, W. L. Minckley, and Bruce D. DeMarais) compared fish body shape against three competing models: [[Ecotypy]] (local ecological adaptation), [[Hybridization]] or intergradation, and [[Vicariance]] (historical separation of once-continuous populations). Using [[Mantel Test]] methods on 33 sampling sites, they found that the ecological and hybridization models offered little explanatory power, while [[Pliocene Epoch]] and [[Miocene Epoch]] paleodrainage patterns were significantly associated with present-day shape variation. The authors conclude that deep, geologically driven history, rather than recent or Pleistocene processes, is the more important influence on the distribution of western North American freshwater fishes.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Chub]] group was long confusing taxonomically. Earlier workers split these fishes into many taxa or regarded them as ecological variants of a few species. The authors treat the taxa as a *Gila robusta* complex and focus on three forms:

- **[[Gila Robusta]]** (roundtail chub), the type form of the complex.
- **[[Gila Intermedia]]**, which is morphologically intermediate in some populations.
- **[[Gila Nigra]]**, which shows discordant, intermediate morphology and is hypothesized to be of hybrid origin from robusta and intermedia.

Other named taxa discussed include *G. elegans*, *G. cypha*, *G. seminuda*, and *G. grahami*, the last regarded as a synonym of *G. robusta*. The authors note that nigra's distinctiveness has been doubted because some populations are morphologically intermediate.

The study region is basin-and-range topography in southern Arizona and northern Sonora. Northward-flowing tributaries drain the southern escarpment of the [[Colorado Plateau]], with the [[Mogollon Rim]] at about 1700 m above mean sea level. Isolated peaks exceed 3000 m, while valley floors range from a few meters above sea level in the west to about −1000 m in the east.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling and measurements**

- Data came from 1106 adult Gila (standard length above 50 mm) at 33 sampling sites, with at least 25 fish per locale.
- Sites were grouped into seven subbasins, with the listed locality counts: Santa Cruz (4), upper San Pedro (4), lower San Pedro (2), Gila (4), Verde (7), Salt (6), and Agua Fria (3). These sum to 30 rather than 33, a discrepancy in the source.
- Morphometric measurements were taken to 0.1 mm following [[Hubbs and Lagler Measurement Protocol]] conventions, then converted to size-free shape scores using [[Sheared Principal Component Analysis]].

**Environmental and historical matrices**

| Matrix | Description |
|---|---|
| SIZ (stream size) | Four discharge classes (0 to >1.42 m³/s), used as a proxy for watershed size |
| ELE (elevation) | Site elevation to the nearest 30 m, used as a surrogate for thermal conditions |
| BAR (barrier) | Three isolation states: 0 (no barrier), 1 (partial), 2 (absolute, e.g., waterfalls or the Santa Cruz Basin) |
| Paleodrainage | Six matrices (Oligocene, Early Miocene, mid-Miocene, Pliocene, Pleistocene, Recent), reconstructed from [[Paleodrainage Reconstruction]] maps by Nations et al. (1982) |

Elevations across the region range from below 350 m to above 2000 m, which the authors judged to mask latitudinal temperature effects.

**Statistical framework**

- [[Mantel Test]] comparisons used 1000 permutations per evaluation, with a nonparametric design that tolerates non-normal data and mixed data types.
- The [[Bonferroni Correction]] was applied, with significance set at 0.05/n, where n is the number of matrix comparisons.
- [[Three-Way Mantel Analysis]] (Smouse et al. 1986) allowed correlations and partial correlations among shape, mid-Miocene, and Pliocene hydrography.

## Key Findings

1. **Model I (Ecotypy) was not supported.** The authors expected shape to correlate positively with elevation and stream size if ecotypic responses existed. Ecotypy was historically rejected for these chubs by Rinne (1976) and DeMarais (1986), and this study found little support for it.

2. **Model II (Hybridization or Intergradation) was not supported.** Hybridization predicted a negative association between shape and barrier isolation. The authors found the model offered little explanatory power. Nigra's hybrid origin, if any, is treated as an ancient event that cannot be tested with present distributions.

3. **Model III (Vicariance) was strongly supported.** Historical distributions of physiographic features, particularly those of the mid-Miocene and Pliocene, were paramount in explaining the present mosaic of morphological variation.

4. **Three-way analysis highlighted the Pliocene.**
   - Shape correlated significantly with Pliocene hydrography. The text reports r = 0.22 (P < 0.0005) for the simple correlation and a partial correlation of r = 0.26 (P < 0.008). The associated table lists 0.255 (P < 0.0008) for the same comparison, so values differ slightly between text and table.
   - Shape was not significantly correlated with mid-Miocene hydrography once Pliocene was accounted for (partial r ≈ −0.04, P > 0.76 in Table 2).
   - Adding Pliocene hydrography to a model that already included mid-Miocene raised the coefficient of multiple determination (R²) from about 0.011 to 0.087, while adding mid-Miocene to a model with Pliocene produced almost no gain.

5. **Several environmental correlations were intuitively expected.** Stream size correlated with mid-Miocene basins, likely because large geographic features underlie higher-discharge watersheds. Barrier status and stream size were also associated, plausibly because larger streams cross more erodible formations, producing waterfalls and dry reaches.

6. **Deep history outweighs shallow history.** The authors argue that Pleistocene and glacial-interglacial events, which some workers have credited for diversification (see Riddle 1996), are less important than long-term tectonic history for these fishes.

7. **Methodological novelty.** The study statistically links existing phenotypic variation to ancient hydrography controlled by tectonism, which the authors say had not previously been done. They describe it as finer-grained than regional biogeographic studies, which is why they call their approach "local" rather than "regional."

8. **Broader implication.** The authors conclude that western North American freshwater fishes frequently show phenotypic diversity when examined morphologically, and that vicariance is a primary driver of this pattern. They recommend that future work test alternative models simultaneously rather than discarding them a priori.
