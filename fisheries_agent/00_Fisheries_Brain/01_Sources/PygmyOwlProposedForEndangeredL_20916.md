# Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl Listing Proposal and Related Arizona Conservation Disputes (1994)

#ornithology #freshwater-ecology #strigidae

## Executive Summary

This newspaper compilation from *The Arizona Republic* (December 1994) covers three linked conservation issues in Arizona. The U.S. [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] proposed listing the [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl]] as endangered in Arizona and threatened in southern Texas, with 290 miles of desert river habitat proposed as [[Critical Habitat]]. The article questions whether this action is too late, since fewer than 30 sightings were recorded in Arizona over the past decade. The second issue concerns delays in protecting the [[Mexican Spotted Owl]], which led a federal judge to demand progress reports and threaten contempt. The third is a settlement under which [[Kaibab Forest Products]] paid a $300,000 fine for unlawfully cutting about 1,200 trees in the [[Kaibab National Forest]]. Several items on the pages are unrelated local news and were excluded.

## Background & Description

**Species profile (pygmy owl).** The source gives the following details, drawn from the National Audubon Society field guide:
- Size: 6.5 to 7 inches; a sparrow-sized, rust-colored owl.
- Voice: a monotonous, repeated harsh "poip" and other whistles.
- Habitat: mesquite or other streamside growth.
- Nesting: 3 or 4 white eggs in a tree cavity.

**Regulatory background.**
- Environmentalists first petitioned for protection of the pygmy owl in May 1992.
- The formal proposal was announced in early December 1994 under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- The proposal followed legal action by environmentalists, similar to the earlier case of the Mexican spotted owl.

**Mexican spotted owl case.**
- The Fish and Wildlife Service missed several deadlines, including a Dec. 1 deadline for proposing critical habitat.
- The proposal was published in the *Federal Register* on Dec. 7 but omitted an economic analysis that the court had ordered nearly three months earlier.
- The proposed area covers 4.8 million acres of mostly federal land in the Southwest.

**Kaibab timber case.**
- The Forest Service had permitted timber firms to cut a set number of trees, and the trees to be cut were supposed to be marked by the Forest Service.
- Forest Service personnel first discovered the theft of unmarked trees in May 1991 and began the investigation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Desert river habitat.** The pygmy owl depends on desert rivers and the vegetation on their banks. The proposed critical habitat includes portions of the Salt, Verde, Gila, San Pedro, New, and Santa Cruz rivers, plus parts of Arivaca, Rillito, and Bonita creeks. Unlike many threatened species in Arizona, much of the potential habitat lies close to large urban populations near Phoenix and Tucson.

**Threats to river systems.** According to the article, the proposal could affect:
- cattle operations and water wells, both of which can alter the flow and condition of desert rivers;
- dam operations;
- the [[Central Arizona Project Canal]], which carries Colorado River water to Phoenix and Tucson.

**Population status.** Fewer than 30 pygmy owl sightings were recorded in Arizona in the past decade, according to [[Peter Galvin]] of the [[Greater Gila Biodiversity Project]].

**Forest harvest context.** Kaibab Forest Products harvested more than 650,000 trees from 14 timber sales between 1988 and 1990. The company said the 1,200 trees in question represented less than 0.2 percent of that total. The firm also planned to close its Fredonia lumber mill because too few trees were permitted for cutting in the Kaibab National Forest. It continues to operate a mill in southern Utah, supplied by the [[Dixie National Forest]].

## Key Findings

1. **Pygmy owl listing proposal.** The Fish and Wildlife Service proposed endangered status in Arizona and threatened status in southern Texas. The proposal designates 290 miles of desert rivers and riparian vegetation as critical habitat.

2. **Timeliness concerns.** Environmentalists argued the proposal may be too little, too late. [[Robin Silver]], a Phoenix environmental activist, said the delayed action shows the Endangered Species Act is ineffective, since agencies act only after a species nearly disappears and litigation forces them to act.

3. **Court-driven action.** In both the pygmy owl and Mexican spotted owl cases, environmentalists had to go to federal court to force protection. [[Judge Carl Muecke]] scheduled a hearing on whether Fish and Wildlife should be held in contempt for its delays on the spotted owl. He ordered daily progress reports and set a May 30 deadline for final critical habitat designation.

4. **Economic analysis gap.** The December 7 *Federal Register* proposal for spotted owl critical habitat lacked the economic analysis Muecke had ordered. Environmentalists proposed that Interior Secretary [[Bruce Babbitt]] personally sign the daily reports, along with [[Mollie Beattie]] and regional director John Rogers.

5. **Political opposition.** [[Gov. Fife Symington]] criticized federal judges' power over environmental regulation. He said he hoped the newly elected Republican Congress would curtail that power and said he had spoken with Senate Majority Leader Bob Dole, who agreed with him. Silver countered that Arizonans consistently support environmental protection.

6. **Kaibab settlement.** Kaibab Forest Products agreed to pay a $300,000 fine, which amounts to about $250 per tree, and to a diversion program to prevent further cutting of unmarked trees. No criminal charges were filed. U.S. Attorney [[Janet Napolitano]] said the government settled because it could meet its objectives without a trial and that the settlement would deter similar cutting.

7. **Disputed value of trees.** Kaibab said the 1,200 trees were worth about $15,000. Environmentalists claimed finished wood from one ponderosa pine can be worth up to $1,000, and [[Charlie Babbitt]] of the [[Maricopa Audubon Society]] called the deal a "slap on the wrist." He urged the Forest Service to impose tougher sanctions, including a ban on the company cutting in the forest north of the Grand Canyon.

8. **Shared agency responsibility.** Kaibab argued that the Forest Service improperly marked trees and failed to mark timber-cutting boundaries properly. The Forest Service said it had not yet reviewed the settlement and that any action it might take against Kaibab would be speculative.
