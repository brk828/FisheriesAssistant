# Projection of Aquatic Conditions on the Lower Colorado River, 1976–2000

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) report, prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under Contract 9-07-30-X0066, projects physical and biological changes to the Lower [[Colorado River]] from a 1976 base year to 2000. It is a general guide to aquatic resources, compiled from literature searches and communication with state fish and game agencies and federal agencies. Its central conclusion is that aquatic resources will continue to decline overall between 1976 and 2000, driven by water-management projects, flood-control operations, bank stabilization, recreational development and nutrient depletion. Information on endangered species is for general reference only and is not official for formal Section 7 consultation.

## Background & Description

The report covers the river's main channel and selected tributaries, and it accounts for scheduled project actions and anticipated natural changes. Because data on future operations were limited, and because quantifying physical and biological effects was difficult, many projections are qualitative.

The main projects assessed are:

- **[[Glen Canyon Peaking Power Study]]**: a Bureau of Reclamation evaluation of peaking power from Glen Canyon Dam, concluded in October 1981. The [[Glen Canyon Powerplant]] was being uprated from 1250 MW to 1336 MW, raising maximum flows from 32,000 to 33,700 cfs.
- **[[Hoover Powerplant Modification]]**: uprating and addition of generating capacity. Three alternatives were considered, with capacity rising to 2060 MW or 2300 MW and peak releases up to 62,000 cfs. Construction was unlikely to begin before 1987, so peak flows of 62,000 cfs were not expected until the early 1990s.
- **[[Hoover Dam Flood Control]]**: nine alternative release schedules, with Alternative 1 preferred.
- **[[Colorado River Front Work and Levee System]]**: mostly maintenance, with riprapping and dredging.
- **[[Salinity Control]]**: the [[Colorado River Water Quality Improvement Program]], aimed at reducing salinity by 2000.
- **[[Central Arizona Project]]**: water pumped from [[Lake Havasu]] for urban and agricultural use, with first diversions expected in 1985.
- **[[Section 10/404 Permit Program]]**: a proposed General Permit covering five activities in designated river areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The river is described as not nutrient-rich to begin with. Several processes shape its aquatic system:

- **Flow regime and fluctuation.** Hydropower operations alter velocities and water-level fluctuations. Weekly fluctuations at [[Lake Mohave]] could damage centrarchid nests and threadfin shad spawning habitat.
- **Nutrients and productivity.** Reservoir productivity depends on retention time. Declining retention and nutrient stripping by the Central Arizona Project could reduce productivity from [[Lake Mead]] down to [[Lake Havasu]] and beyond.
- **Riparian and periphyton habitat.** [[Cladophora]] mats in [[Black Canyon]] support invertebrates that are key forage for [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Riprapping, dredging and bank stabilization remove shallow and riparian habitat.
- **Introduced species.** [[Striped Bass]], [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Tilapia]] are expanding or established. Stocking of salmonids, largemouth bass and channel catfish will continue.
- **Recreation.** Recreational demand is rising, with more boat docks, beaches and anglers, which adds pressure on fish populations.

## Key Findings

**Project effects**

- **Glen Canyon peaking power** would likely have had adverse effects on trout and periphyton at low flows, though the extent was unquantified. Uprating alone is expected to have only minor effects.
- **Hoover Powerplant modification** would reduce the rainbow trout fishery in Black Canyon by 15% (USFWS 1982), based on lost fishing opportunity from high velocity rather than population decline. Lake Mohave fluctuations could reduce hatching success for centrarchids, threadfin shad, razorback suckers and bonytail chub. Re-regulation of Lake Mohave would have more severe effects, with possible near-elimination of reproduction for shallow-spawning fish.
- **Flood releases** above 30,000 cfs are detrimental to all areas. Flows of up to 20,000 cfs between Davis and Imperial dams and 5,000 to 9,000 cfs below Imperial Dam are beneficial. Spring and summer releases are more harmful than fall and winter releases. The California Department of Fish and Game recommended maintaining a 4,000 cfs winter flow.
- **Levee and bank work** will eliminate riparian habitat and invertebrate production, and annual dredging will remove shallow habitat, including striped bass spawning habitat in the Topock Settling Basin.
- **Salinity control** will improve water quality, but total system water will be somewhat reduced. The [[Yuma Desalting Plant]] was scheduled to begin operation in 1985.
- **Limitrophe Division** dried up because of Wellton-Mohawk diversions. Mitigation includes a warm-water hatchery and enhancement of wildlife areas, approved in July 1981.
- **Section 10/404 General Permit** would permit five structure types, including bulkheads and boat docks, in areas that are already heavily developed. The Corps of Engineers does not expect cumulative effects to degrade the environment, but habitat losses are still expected.

**Biological outlook**

- Overall aquatic resources are projected to decline from 1976 to 2000. Lake Mohave and the General Permit areas could see significant change, while the Grand Canyon and Lake Mead may change little.
- Nutrient depletion is identified as a growing problem, potentially more important than any single construction project.
- Endangered species are highly uncertain. The bonytail chub (about 401,000 young stocked into Lake Mohave in 1981) has had no recent successful reproduction recorded. The humpback chub population in Grand Canyon is self-sustaining. The Colorado River squawfish no longer occurs in the lower river and may not be reintroduced. The razorback sucker reproduces in Lake Mohave, but stocking is under consideration.
- Central Arizona Project diversions are projected to reach 1.5 MAF per year, with a maximum of 1.7 MAF per year by 1997. Metropolitan Water District diversions from Lake Havasu were set at 550,000 af per year.

**Regional outlook (by river region)**

- **Region 1 (Glen Canyon Dam to Separation Rapids):** the Lees Ferry trout fishery should keep its trophy quality if invertebrate populations hold.
- **Region 2 (Lake Mead):** the reservoir is becoming increasingly nutrient-deficient, and striped bass fishing depends on threadfin shad.
- **Region 3A (Lake Mohave and Black Canyon) and Region 3B (Mohave Valley and Topock Gorge):** these face the most severe fishery impacts.
- **Region 3C (Lake Havasu):** CAP uptake near the Bill Williams arm may affect forage production that supports striped bass, largemouth bass and catfish.
- **Regions 4–5 (Lower Havasu through Limitrophe):** Yuma Division sustained flows of 2,000 cfs for two years improved backwaters. Nutrient loss from Lake Havasu may reduce productivity downstream.

**Summary**

The report concludes that the aquatic resources of the Lower Colorado River will continue to decline between 1976 and 2000. Long-term enhancement is unlikely. Biological values will persist, but at lower population levels than at present.
