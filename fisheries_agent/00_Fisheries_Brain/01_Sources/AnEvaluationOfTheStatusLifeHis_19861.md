# Evaluation of the Status, Life History, and Habitat Requirements of Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the Upper Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 report (FWS/OBS-77/62), prepared by Ecology Consultants, Inc. for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, evaluates the status, [[Life History]], and [[Habitat Requirements]] of the threatened and endangered fishes of the upper [[Colorado River]] system (basin area about 109,580 square miles, above Lee's Ferry, Arizona). It is Part 2 of a three-part study. The authors state that the basin holds a unique fauna, with more than two-thirds of native fishes endemic, and that water development for energy and agriculture creates conflict with species maintenance. The report covers abiotic conditions (flow, salinity, sediment, temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, water chemistry), biological components, species accounts, basin descriptions, threat factors, and research priorities. Much of its conclusions rest on professional judgment because the data base is incomplete, a caveat the authors state explicitly.

## Background & Description

The upper basin begins as clear, cold, first-order mountain streams in Wyoming, Utah, and Colorado, and becomes large third- to fourth-order desert rivers flowing through deep canyons. The native fish fauna has been isolated for a very long time and shows high endemism; the native species include 13 native, 7 endemic, at least 27 exotic, and 6 threatened or endangered species. The [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] is divided into three hydrologic subregions: Green River, San Juan-Colorado, and Upper Main Stem.

The report organizes the river into three ecological zones:

- **Upper zone:** cold, clear, high-gradient "trout water" dominated by salmonids, with periphytic algae and benthic invertebrates as key food sources.
- **Intermediate zone:** warmer, more turbid water with mixed substrates; catostomids and cyprinids increase, and primary production is highest here.
- **Lower zone:** warm, very turbid, sand-dominated large rivers where primary production is nearly absent and energy comes mainly from allochthonous (terrestrial) material.

Major species accounts cover the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Kendall Warm Springs Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Piute Sculpin]], [[Mottled Sculpin]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Mountain Sucker]], and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. The four large-river endemics (squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub, razorback sucker) are the central concern.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and water budget.** Average annual discharge at Lee's Ferry was 12,500,000 acre-feet (1896-1975), against an undepleted average of 14,900,000 acre-feet. Depletions rose from 250,000 acre-feet in 1896 to 3,823,900 acre-feet in 1975. Irrigation accounted for 56 percent of 1975 consumptive use, transmountain exports 19 percent, and reservoir evaporation 19 percent. The Upper Main Stem supplied about 50 percent of 1975 flow.

**Dams and reservoirs.** The [[Colorado River Storage Project]] added major reservoirs, including [[Lake Powell]], [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], Navajo, Fontenelle, Blue Mesa, and Morrow Point. Dams trap roughly 75-80 percent of the sediment that formerly reached Lake Mead. Flows below dams became clearer and colder, and May flows at several gauges reached record lows after 1960.

**Salinity.** Total dissolved solids rise downstream through evaporation, leaching, and irrigation return flows. Headwaters are below 100 mg/l, while the lower basin can exceed 1000 mg/l at low flows. The report estimates that each mg/l increase in salinity at Imperial Valley costs users $230,000 annually (Kleinman et al. 1974). Natural diffuse sources, mineral springs such as Dotsero and Glenwood Springs, and the Paradox Valley salt anticline contribute significantly.

**Temperature.** Flaming Gorge Dam shifted outflow temperatures: pre-impoundment means ranged from 0.0°C to 19.5°C at Greendale, and post-impoundment means from 4.0°C to 9.5°C. Cold hypolimnetic withdrawals below dams suppress spawning temperatures for native species.

**pH and dissolved oxygen.** Basin pH generally falls between 7 and 8. Acid mine drainage and trona-related alkaline spills (pH up to 10.5) pose lethal threats. Deep reservoirs, especially Flaming Gorge, develop anaerobic bottom waters.

**Primary production and food web.** Production is greatest in the upper and intermediate zones, limited by water clarity and scouring. Phosphorus is generally not limiting. Benthic invertebrates (Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Trichoptera, Diptera) dominate the upper zone and shift toward burrowing and turbidity-tolerant taxa downstream. Terrestrial insects are a significant diet component, and surface feeding follows rainstorms.

**Threats.** The report identifies livestock grazing, parasites and diseases, dams, introduced fishes, hybridization, industrial and municipal pollution, logging, and water depletion. Introduced predators and competitors, including [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Red Shiner]], are considered serious threats to native young fish.

## Key Findings

- **Endemism and status:** Of the native fishes, the Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub, and razorback sucker are endangered or threatened large-river endemics. The Colorado cutthroat trout (pure *pleuriticus*) is one of the rarest native fishes in the basin, with only two known pure populations in Wyoming streams.
- **Historical decline:** Squawfish abundance declined from the 1930s-1950s, with sharper declines after dams closed in the 1960s. Razorback suckers were commercially harvested in the late 1800s and early 1900s, and a more precipitous decline began in the late 1960s.
- **Squawfish spawning:** Spawning is inferred to occur at about 70°F, based on ripe fish and young-of-the-year. Actual spawning in nature has never been observed.
- **Hybridization:** Hybridization with introduced rainbow trout is the main cause of Colorado cutthroat decline. Gila hybrids (*cypha*, *elegans*, *robusta*) are not known to be fertile. The report treats *G. elegans* and *G. cypha* as valid species and considers intergrades to be humpback chub for the purposes of the document.
- **Flow and temperature:** Large dams eliminated large-river endemics from about 120 miles of the Green River above the Yampa confluence. The Yampa River mitigates Flaming Gorge effects, making the lower Green River the best remaining habitat.
- **Proposed developments:** The Juniper Springs project on the Yampa River would likely inundate squawfish habitat and could threaten all four large-river endemics. The Savery-Pothook project could alter Yampa temperatures critical to Green River spawning. The Yellow Jacket project on the White River would affect a recent squawfish collection site.
- **Flood control concern:** Flooding creates the backwaters used by young large-river fishes, so flood prevention may remove critical nursery habitat.
- **Research needs:** Priorities include locating undiscovered isolated Colorado cutthroat populations, defining squawfish and razorback spawning and nursery habitat, measuring minimum river size and flow, clarifying Gila taxonomy, and inventorying bonytail distribution in the Colorado mainstem.
- **Data limitations:** The authors note that speculation and professional judgment make up a large share of conclusions, and that a comprehensive data base is lacking.
