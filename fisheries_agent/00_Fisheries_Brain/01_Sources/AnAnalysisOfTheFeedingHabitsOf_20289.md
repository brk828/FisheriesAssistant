# Feeding Habits of Rainbow Trout and Lake Trout in Birch Lake, Cass County, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the diet of introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Lake Trout]] in [[Birch Lake]], an oligotrophic lake in [[Cass County Michigan]]. Trout were stocked to create a sport fishery in a region of Michigan that normally lacks salmonids. Stomachs were collected by [[Creel Census]] clerks between 1941 and 1946, from 322 rainbow trout and 25 lake trout. The authors tested whether the trout threatened the native [[Cisco]] population, which supports a gill-net fishery. They found that neither trout species preyed on or competed with cisco for food. The two trout species did compete with each other for the same invertebrate and fish prey. Aquatic insects and fish made up most of the diet, and yellow perch and centrarchids were rarely eaten. The findings informed changes to the open season and creel limits.

## Background & Description

- **Source context:** Written by J. W. Leonard and F. A. Leonard of the Michigan Department of Conservation. The paper was reprinted from Volume 76 (1946) of the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* and published in 1949. It is a contribution from the [[Michigan Institute For Fisheries Research]].
- **Management motivation:** Rainbow trout sport fishing in Birch Lake was successful enough that the Michigan Conservation Commission extended the open season into spring and fall and reduced creel limits.
- **Data-collection rationale:** A continuous creel census has run on the lake since 1941. Census clerks checked catches and removed stomachs from fish not already cleaned by anglers.
- **Prior knowledge gap:** Little had been published on rainbow trout feeding in a lake of this type. Most Michigan game lakes support piscivorous species such as [[Northern Pike]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Yellow Perch]].
- **Lake morphometry:** Birch Lake covers 308.8 acres, with a maximum depth of 95 feet. It is 1.1 miles long and 0.77 mile wide, and is fed by year-round springs.
- **Fish community:** Of 31 fish species known from the lake, four trout species were introduced from hatchery stock. The native fauna includes [[Cisco]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Mud Pickerel]] and numerous [[Cyprinid Shiners]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions (survey of July 8, 1937):**
- A [[Thermocline]] was present between 15 and 36 feet.
- Dissolved oxygen was 8.3 p.p.m. at the surface, 8.3 p.p.m. at 45 feet, and 4.6 p.p.m. at 95 feet.
- pH was 8.4 at the surface and 7.5 at the bottom. Methyl-orange alkalinity was 122 p.p.m. at the surface and 140 p.p.m. at the bottom.
- Free CO₂ was absent from the surface to 27 feet. It was 0.3 p.p.m. at 45 feet and 0.6 p.p.m. at the bottom.
- Secchi disc visibility was 17 feet.
- Water temperature was 80°F at the surface, 75°F at 12 feet, 50°F at 39 feet, and 45°F at 95 feet.

**Substrate and vegetation:**
- Shoals are sandy and narrow, ending in an abrupt drop-off. Marl dominates between the 5- and 70-foot contours. Pulpy peat lies below 70 feet.
- Aquatic plants are mostly confined to shoal areas. [[Anacharis]] reaches 18 feet, and [[Chara]] reaches 10 feet.

**Benthic and plankton fauna:**
- [[Ekman Dredge]] samples from five stations (2 to 62 feet) yielded 5 to 204 organisms per square foot. Midge larvae, oligochaete worms, clams, dragonfly nymphs and crayfish were the main taxa.
- Plankton hauls gave 2.7 cc/m³ (vertical haul, 90 feet to surface) and 2.1 cc/m³ (horizontal surface haul). Entomostraca dominated both.
- Invertebrate production was judged similar to that of other surveyed oligotrophic lakes in southwestern Michigan, though the authors noted more year-round sampling was needed.

## Key Findings

### Sample and Methods
- **Rainbow trout:** 322 stomachs were examined. 165 came from fish 7.5 to 12 inches total length, and 157 from fish 12.5 to 22.5 inches.
- **Lake trout:** 25 stomachs were analyzed (Table 6).
- **Collection period:** May 23 to December 19, covering 1941 to 1946. With one exception, no smaller trout were taken after August 31.
- **Capture methods:** Most fish were angler-caught using night crawlers, minnows, cheese, meat, fish viscera, flies, spinners, plugs and spoons. A few came from experimental gill nets.
- **Bait exclusion:** Bait made up 24.8% of stomach volume and was excluded from later calculations. Only gullet and stomach contents were analyzed.
- **Metrics:** Frequency of occurrence, numbers, and fluid-displacement volume were all recorded. The authors argued that volume alone can mislead, especially with small samples.

### Rainbow Trout Diet (All Stomachs Combined)
- **Aquatic insects:** Found in 89% of stomachs and 23% of total volume. Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and Diptera dominated. Chironomid midge larvae and pupae were the most frequent single group.
- **Fish:** Found in 41% of stomachs and 43% of volume.
- **Mollusks:** Found in about one-third of stomachs of both size groups, and 3% of volume. Gastropods made up 9 of 10 genera, with one fingernail clam.
- **Crayfish and scuds:** 3% of volume, mostly from 11 large crayfish (*Cambarus* sp.) taken by larger trout. Scuds were rare.
- **Terrestrial insects:** Found in 15.3% of stomachs in the combined data, with heavy intake in October and early November.
- **Microcrustaceans:** Cladocerans and copepods were taken by about one-quarter of smaller trout and one-tenth of larger trout.
- **Algae and higher plants:** Plant intake rose with predator size.

### Size-Class Differences
- In smaller trout (7.5 to 12 inches), aquatic insects made up 40% of volume and fish 30%.
- In larger trout (12.5 to 22.5 inches), fish made up 48% of volume and insects 18%.
- Nearly half (49%) of larger trout had no fish in their stomachs, so they still fed heavily on insects.

### Fish Prey
- 17 of the 31 known fish species were found in the stomachs. Cyprinids were the most important fish prey, and every cyprinid known from the lake was represented.
- The [[Mimic Shiner]] (*Notropis volucellus*) was the most frequent fish prey and most numerous per stomach. Yellow perch had a larger volume share but occurred less often.
- **Yellow perch:** About one-fifth of fish volume, but found in only 3% of stomachs. Only 11 perch were counted in total.
- **Centrarchids:** 14% of fish volume and 7% of stomachs. Only 90 centrarchids were counted in total.
- Scales of four other species (cisco, white sucker, mudminnow and Iowa darter) appeared in one stomach each. They were judged accidental ingestions and omitted.

### Aquatic Insect Patterns (Table 5)
- Mayfly [[Hexagenia Limbata]] (as *H. limbata occulta*) nymphs appeared all season and reached fair numbers to 60 feet.
- [[Ephemera Simulans]] was eaten mainly during its late-May emergence. Nymphs far outnumbered subimagoes, suggesting trout fed while nymphs rose to the surface.
- Aquatic Coleoptera (six families) appeared only in 1945 stomachs. Simulium larvae and a stonefly (*Isoperla* sp.) were rare and possibly taken near the screened outlet.
- Aquatic Hemiptera occurred steadily but in low numbers, despite being considered unpalatable.

### Annual Variation
- Crayfish and scuds were absent from stomachs collected from 1941 to 1943.
- Fish occurred in two-thirds of stomachs in 1942 but only one-third in 1943.
- Mollusks ranged from 25% of stomachs in 1941 to 88% in 1943.

### Lake Trout Diet (25 stomachs)
- The lake trout were more strongly piscivorous than rainbow trout, with fish in 16 of 25 stomachs (76 percent of stomachs; 83 percent of total volume).
- Aquatic insects appeared in 8 stomachs (11% of volume) and were frequent enough to make lake trout direct competitors with rainbow trout for insects and fish.
- No cisco were found in lake trout stomachs. Two fish had eaten adult June beetles, and one had eaten a large hawkmoth larva.

### Management Conclusions
- Neither trout species significantly preyed upon the cisco or game fish. The authors concluded that trout stocking did not threaten the cisco fishery.
- The two trout species compete for the same food organisms, which should be considered when stocking either one.
- Fly fishing for rainbow trout would succeed mainly during spring and fall, when terrestrial insects and emergences are available. Summer surface temperatures discourage surface feeding.
- Summer anglers must fish baits at about 15 feet or deeper, where temperatures are tolerable.
- If managers want to reduce perch and centrarchid populations, increased trout stocking could push trout to prey more heavily on young bass, bluegills, sunfish and perch in colder months.
