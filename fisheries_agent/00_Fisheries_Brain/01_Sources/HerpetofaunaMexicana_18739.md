# Annotated List of the Amphibians and Reptiles of Mexico, Recent Taxonomic Changes, and New Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #herpetofauna

## Executive Summary

This Carnegie Museum of Natural History Special Publication (No. 17, 1993), authored by [[Oscar Flores-Villela]] and edited by C. J. McCoy, presents an annotated checklist of the valid amphibian and reptile species of Mexico, together with a summary of taxonomic changes and new species recorded since 1966. The document is a herpetological reference work rather than a fish study. It records roughly 65 species described or first recorded for Mexico in the previous 16 years and about 130 taxonomic and nomenclatural changes. The author places Mexico among the six most biologically rich countries and notes that its amphibians and reptiles are among the least well known vertebrates in the country.

## Background & Description

The publication is framed by a preface from the editor, who notes the rapid growth of Mexican herpetology since the early 1970s, when native-born university programs began producing trained herpetologists. The stated goals are to encourage Mexican biology students toward herpetology and to provide a framework for future research filling gaps in knowledge of the [[Mexican Herpetofauna]].

The introduction summarizes the earlier national works that this list updates, including [[Smith and Taylor Checklists]] (1945, 1948, 1950, 1966) and [[Synopsis of the Herpetofauna of Mexico]] by Smith and Smith (1976–1979). The present work uses the Smith and Smith (1976a, 1976b) lists as its base and notes where it departs from them.

The main sections are:
- Annotated list of valid species, with notes on uncertain or disputed taxa.
- Species of possible occurrence in Mexico that have not been reported.
- Recent taxonomic changes, species described or first recorded since 1966, nomenclatural changes since 1976, changes above the species level, and changes in type localities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Mexico's physical setting is described in detail. The country covers 1,976,183 km² (plus 5,364 km² of islands), with 9,903 km of coastline: 7,147 km on the Pacific Ocean and Gulf of California and 2,756 km on the Gulf of Mexico and Caribbean Sea. Major mountain systems include the [[Sierra Madre Occidental]], [[Sierra Madre Oriental]], [[Sierra Madre Del Sur]], and the [[Transverse Volcanic Axis]]. Principal drainages are the Atlantic, Pacific, and Interior basins; the major rivers listed are the Usumacinta, Grijalva, Papaloapan, Coatzacoalcos, Pánuco, Balsas, and Santiago.

Following West (1971), the country is divided into ten natural regions grouped as extratropical dry lands (regions 1–2), tropical highlands (3–5), tropical lowlands (6–7), extratropical highlands (8–9), and a subhumid extratropical lowland (10). Region 6 (Gulf and Caribbean lowlands, including the [[Yucatán Peninsula]]) and Region 7 (Pacific lowlands and the [[Balsas Basin]]) hold the highest species richness. Table 2 gives richness by region, with totals of 320 species in Region 7 and 326 in other comparisons as reported; the highest regional totals are Region 7 (320) and Region 3 (270), while Region 9 (103) and Region 10 (102) are lowest. Vegetation types range from tropical forests and thorn forest to oak, pine and cloud forest, and alpine vegetation above 4,000 m.

Endemism is high: 60.7% of amphibians, 53.7% of reptiles, 7.6% of birds and 30.2% of mammals are endemic, and 55.7% of Mexican amphibian and reptile species are endemic.

## Key Findings

- **Species count and additions:** About 65 species were described or first recorded for Mexico in the previous 16 years, and about 130 taxonomic and nomenclatural changes were made. The author projects the species total to the year 2000 based on known forthcoming descriptions.
- **Endemism:** 55.7% of Mexican amphibian and reptile species are endemic, making the herpetofauna among the most distinctive in the world.
- **Taxonomic groups:** The list is organized into orders and suborders of Amphibia (Gymnophiona, Caudata, Anura) and Reptilia (Testudines, Crocodylia, Squamata with Sauria, Amphisbaenia and Serpentes). Taxonomic treatment follows Dundee (1989) for higher categories.
- **Regional richness:** Table 2 shows the highest richness in Regions 7 and 3, reflecting diverse tropical and highland habitats. Colubridae is the most species-rich snake family across most regions.
- **Species of possible occurrence:** A separate list includes taxa not yet reported from Mexico, such as several introduced geckos and skinks and some species of uncertain status.
- **Disputed and uncertain taxa:** Numerous explanatory notes (about 63) flag taxa whose validity or distribution is contested, including certain Anolis species, Sceloporus subspecies, and parthenogenetic Cnemidophorus taxa.
- **Recent changes:** The list incorporates new species such as [[Nyctanolis Pernix]], [[Abronia Mitchelli]], [[Micrurus Nebularis]], and [[Porthidium Olmec]], along with many genus reassignments (for example, Hyla to Ololygon, Ptychohyla to Duellmanohyla, and Bothrops species moved to Bothriechis and Cerrophidion).
- **Type localities:** The work updates type localities for many species, following uniform citation practice where possible.
- **Aquatic and marine taxa:** Sea turtles (e.g., Chelonia, Lepidochelys, Dermochelys) and crocodilians (Crocodylus acutus, C. moreleti) are included in the checklist, and the Testudines list covers freshwater turtles such as Kinosternon, Staurotypus and Trionyx. The document contains no fish data.
