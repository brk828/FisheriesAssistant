# Baits Used in Winter Fishing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #aquatic-entomology #fishing-practices

## Executive Summary

This 1939 publication from the Michigan Department of Conservation's Institute for Fisheries Research documents the variety of baits used in winter ice fishing and provides detailed identification and collection guidance for aquatic insects and other organisms. The document addresses the growing winter bait industry, conservation concerns regarding aquatic insect removal from trout streams, and proper handling techniques for various bait types including worms, aquatic insect larvae and nymphs, and bait fishes.

## Background & Description

Winter fishing popularity increased tremendously in the years preceding 1939, transforming the bait industry from seasonal summer operations to year-round businesses. The document provides comprehensive descriptions of aquatic insect life stages, distinguishing between larvae (soft, light-colored bodies) and nymphs (hard bodies with long, jointed legs).

**Major bait categories covered:**

- **Worms**: Fish worms, night crawlers, and manure worms (striped worms, red worms). Storage recommendations include wooden buckets or earthenware crocks filled with rich loam or sphagnum moss, maintained below 60°F but above freezing. Feeding with chopped beef suet, hard-boiled eggs, cereals, or milk at weekly intervals.

- **[[Mayflies]]**: Over 500 species in United States and Canada. Burrowing nymphs ([[Hexagenia]] sp.) measuring 1¾-2½ inches, known as "wigglers" or "sea horses," are most important as bait. Recognizable by long feathery gills arranged in pairs along abdomen, three long tails, and pointed tusks on jaws for burrowing. Found in mud bottoms containing sand, fine gravel, or organic debris.

- **[[Caddisflies]]**: Larvae known as "stick worm" or "reedamites." Most species construct portable cases from sticks, pebbles, sand grains, reeds, or shells. Cases may be cylindrical, quadrangular, triangular, or circular in cross-section. Caterpillar-like larvae with strong legs found around weedy lake shores and quiet stream sections.

- **Hellgrammites**: Larval form of dobsonfly. Found under rocks in fast-flowing streams, more abundant in warm water than trout streams. Aquatic carnivores identifiable by slender, fleshy appendages along abdomen. Can be fed hamburger in captivity.

- **[[Stoneflies]]**: Flat-bodied nymphs found clinging to undersides of stones in rapid streams or rocky, wave-washed lake shores. Long, flat body with pair of tails, gills sometimes absent or located beneath body.

- **[[Dragonflies]] and [[Damselflies]]**: Nymphs distinguishable by enormous, hinged grasping lower jaw with spiny grabhooks. Dragonfly nymphs broad and flat; damselfly nymphs thinner. Wings develop externally as large pads behind head.

- **Other insects**: Leather-jackets (cranefly larvae), goldenrod gall worms (moth or fly larvae), meal worms (beetle larvae), wood borers or sawdust worms (beetle larvae).

- **Bait fishes**: Lake shiner (lake runner) most popular for [[Perch]] and [[Walleye]]. Small golden shiners (1-1.5 inches) preferred for panfish. Chubs favored for [[Pike]]. Large suckers, chubs, carp, and goldfish used as decoys for spear fishing. Salmon eggs and fish chunks used for [[Bluegill]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document emphasizes conservation of aquatic insects as fish food resources. It is unlawful to take aquatic insects from trout streams except for personal use on such streams or under permit from the conservation department director. Removal of insects decreases natural food essential for fish production in streams.

**Habitat associations:**
- [[Mayflies]] inhabit all unpolluted fresh waters, adapted to diverse habitats
- Burrowing mayflies require slightly porous mud bottoms
- [[Caddisflies]] occupy all aquatic habitats; large species most abundant around weedy lake shores
- Hellgrammites prefer fast-flowing stream sections under rocks
- [[Stoneflies]] require rapid streams or wave-washed rocky shores
- [[Dragonflies]] and [[Damselflies]] occupy nearly every aquatic situation around lake shores and stream beds
- Leather-jackets live in stream gravel, leaf drifts, and weed beds

**Collection and handling considerations:**
- Burrowing mayflies are delicate; screens used to sift bottom materials for capture
- Lower water temperatures extend nymph survival in captivity
- Avoid crowding; aerate water when necessary
- Some caddis larvae are carnivorous and may turn cannibalistic in captivity despite normally vegetarian diet
- Hellgrammites are tougher and more tenacious of life than mayflies and caddisflies
- [[Stoneflies]] require running water or well-aerated quiet water
- [[Dragonflies]] and [[Damselflies]] are hardy swimmers, tolerant of oxygen and temperature extremes

## Key Findings

**Target species preferences:**
- [[Bluegill]], [[Crappie]], sunfish, and [[Perch]]: Mayflies, caddisflies, leather-jackets, goldenrod gall worms, meal worms, wood borers, small minnows (golden shiners 1-1.5 inches), salmon eggs, fish chunks
- [[Bass]]: Hellgrammites (excellent but rather large for panfish)
- [[Perch]] and [[Walleye]]: Lake shiners (lake runners)
- [[Pike]]: Chubs
- Spear fishing: Large suckers, chubs, carp, goldfish as decoys

**Storage and maintenance:**
- Worms: Cool cellar storage (below 60°F, above freezing) in loam or sphagnum moss
- Aquatic insects: Tanks or aquaria with dead leaves and leaf mold on bottom; lower temperatures extend survival
- Hellgrammites: Can be kept for weeks in cool basement in wooden bucket with dampened sod and decaying leaves
- Goldenrod gall worms: Can be held in gall during winter if left in cold place
- Meal worms: Successfully reared in boxes of cornmeal or other cereal
- Wood borers: Sometimes kept successfully in sawdust

**Conservation practices:**
- Practice conservation of fish bait as of fish themselves
- Avoid damaging vegetation when removing larvae or nymphs from non-trout streams
- Legal restrictions on aquatic insect collection from trout streams protect natural food resources

The winter bait industry had become as important and necessary as summer bait business by 1939, with numerous bait stores offering variety of worms, wigglers, and minnows year-round.
