# Fishes of the Arkansas River Basin: Cowley, Chautauqua and Elk Counties, Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 University of Kansas Museum of Natural History monograph by Artie L. Metcalf surveys the fishes of a portion of the [[Arkansas River Basin]] in south-central Kansas, covering Cowley, Chautauqua and Elk Counties (about 2,430 square miles). Collections were made at 60 numbered stations during 1956, using seines, nets and pole-and-line gear. The study records the species present, their habitat preferences, faunal associations across streams, and the effects of drought, flooding, pollution and stream intermittency on fish distribution. The author concludes that the regional fish fauna is transitional between [[Ozarkian Fauna]] and [[Great Plains Fauna]] elements.

The document also contains publication front and back matter (lists of earlier University of Kansas Museum numbers in volumes 1–10) which carry no scientific data and are not summarized here.

## Background & Description

- **Aims:** (1) determine which species occur in the streams; (2) determine habitat preferences; (3) identify faunal associations within streams; (4) compare fish faunas among streams; (5) relate findings to east-west fish distribution in Kansas; (6) assess effects of stream intermittency.
- **Study area:** Cowley and Chautauqua counties form part of Kansas's southern border; Elk County lies north of Chautauqua. Population in 1950 was 50,960; the largest towns were Arkansas City (12,903) and Winfield (10,264).
- **Physiography:** three sections: the Wellington formation and [[Arkansas River]] floodplain in western Cowley County; the [[Flint Hills]] in the centre; and the wooded [[Chautauqua Hills]] in the east.
- **Major drainages:** [[Walnut River]] (gradient 2.8 ft/mile in county), [[Grouse Creek]] (9 ft/mile), [[Big Caney River]] (15.4 ft/mile in study area) with tributaries such as [[Cedar Creek]], [[Otter Creek]] and [[Spring Creek]], [[Middle Caney Creek]], and [[Elk River]] (14.4 ft/mile).
- **Methods:** a four-foot nylon net, several woven and knotted-mesh seines (1/4 to 3/4 inch mesh), and pole and line. Percentages reported represent the relative number taken in the first five seine hauls at each station.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and hydrology.** The mean annual temperature is 58 °F and mean annual precipitation ranges from 32.9 in (Cowley) to 38.5 in (Chautauqua). Drought conditions began in 1952 and prevailed during the study, while local summer floods (e.g., 22 June 1956) affected some streams but not others. Big Caney River monthly mean discharge at Elgin fell from 103.00 cfs (October 1954) to 69.60 cfs (October 1955) in the table, and daily discharge in May ranged from 0.03 to 20.0 cfs in 1956 versus 0.7 to 9,270.0 cfs in May 1944.

**Land use and pollution.** Agriculture, urbanization and industrialization were identified as the main human modifications. Sewage from towns (only nine municipalities had sewage systems in 1957, several discharging raw sewage) and petroleum and brine wastes from oil fields and refineries had degraded streams. Clapp (1920) stated that the Walnut River was "now a murky oil run" and that the Fall and Verdigris rivers were "practically ruined." Metzler (1952) recorded fish kills from the mid-1940s to 1952, the largest in 1944 attributed to brine pollution.

**Flora.** Aquatic plants were sparse in the Arkansas River. Key species recorded include [[Justicia Americana]], [[Myriophyllum Heterophyllum]], [[Nelumbo Lutea]], [[Najas Guadalupensis]] and [[Potamogeton Foliosus]]. The surrounding landscape ranges from mixed bluestem prairie to the oak-dominated Chautauqua Hills.

**Habitat characters.** The Arkansas River has sand bottoms and is turbid in wet years; Flint Hills streams are predominantly rubble and bedrock; Walnut River has gravel and rubble upstream; lower alluvial reaches have mud bottoms.

## Key Findings

- **Species inventory:** The annotated list covers roughly 70 species across about 20 families. Collectively, 40 species were taken in Big Caney River, 35 in Grouse Creek, 31 in Elk River, 24 in the Walnut River system and 18 in the Arkansas River. The most widely distributed species was the green sunfish, *Lepomis cyanellus*, recorded at 45 of 60 stations, the greatest number for any single species.
- **Longnose gar:** 84 specimens were taken, 27 of them young-of-the-year, mainly in shallow isolated pools.
- **Gizzard shad:** In [[Fall River Reservoir]] studies cited, shad made up 97% by number and 83% by weight of fishes.
- **Golden redhorse:** Reached 7.5% of catch at Crab Creek station G-12, the highest relative abundance recorded for this sucker.
- **Bigeye shiner:** Largest relative abundance (14.11%) at station C-11 on Big Caney River; the species is otherwise known in Kansas only from the Spring River drainage.
- **Red shiner:** 20–25% of the catch in the Arkansas River; up to 80% at the uppermost station on Silver Creek, an intermittent tributary.
- **Intermittency effects:** In severely intermittent tributaries (Crab, Beaver and Grouse tributaries), faunas were dominated by a few tolerant species, chiefly [[Lepomis Cyanellus]] and [[Ictalurus Melas]]. At a foul pool on the East Fork of Big Caney River, 153 green sunfish under 5 inches were taken in one haul. Species reappeared rapidly upstream after rains.
- **Ozarkian versus Plains fauna:** Ozarkian species such as Notropis boops, N. camurus, N. rubellus and Pimephales tenellus are sensitive to intermittency and largely absent west of the study area, whereas plains species such as Notropis lutrensis tolerate it.
- **Faunal associations:** Four were identified: (1) [[Arkansas River Fauna]] (Notropis girardi, N. blennius, Hybopsis aestivalis tetranemus, Fundulus kansae); (2) lower Walnut River fauna, rich in deep-bodied suckers and carp; (3) Caney-Elk-Grouse main-stream fauna; and (4) upland tributary fauna, separated into Walnut/Grouse (species-poor, intermittent) and Big Caney (more stable flow, Ozarkian affinities).
- **Range limits:** Seven species reached their westernmost records in the study area, including Notropis boops (station G-5) and Percina copelandi (station G-1). Four species reached their easternmost limits here, including Notropis girardi and Fundulus kansae.
- **Historical notes:** An 1899 Winfield Courier editorial complained of seine and hoop-net overfishing. Graham (1885) listed 18 introduced species in Kansas before 1885.
- **Sampling caveats:** The author notes that seines are species-selective, that seasonal and diurnal differences occurred, and that station classification into lower, middle and upper segments was partly arbitrary.
- **Summary conclusion:** The fish fauna is transitional between Ozarkian and Great Plains faunas. Fluctuations in water level, driven by climate and accentuated by human habitat modification, were the most important factor in fish distribution.
