# An Ichthydium, Probably Sulcatum, of the Mountain Lake, Virginia Area

#gastrotricha #ichthydium #ichthydium-sulcatum #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1962 note by [[Charles E. Packard]] of Randolph-Macon College, published in the [[Turtox News]], reports observations of smooth and near-smooth gastrotrichs (microscopic aquatic invertebrates) from the [[Mountain Lake Biological Station]] in Giles County, Virginia, during the summers of 1958 and 1960. The author evaluates whether the form described by [[Stokes]] as *Ichthydium sulcatum* is a valid species, and concludes that several specimens from Mountain Lake very likely belong to it. The paper is largely a morphological description of individual specimens, with notes on cultivation and slide-mounting methods. It does not present a formal taxonomic revision or statistical analysis, and the author states that analyses of the accumulated data are planned for future papers.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic history.** Stokes (1918) described *Ichthydium sulcatum* from Trenton, New Jersey, based on deep transverse furrows on the back and sides, a transparent, soft and flexible body, a narrowed posterior region, and a short esophagus (no more than one-sixth of body length). [[Brunson]] (1950) gave a fuller account of the same species, collected in Michigan in July 1944. The genus *Ichthydium* was first described by Joblot in 1718 (as reported by Murray) and has been recorded sporadically from all continents except Australia. [[Remane]] (1936) listed eight species but did not mention *I. sulcatum*. The most frequently cited species in the literature is *I. podura* Müller (1773).

**Author's prior observations.** Packard reports "smooth" gastrotrichs seen in New Hampshire, Maine, New York and Virginia from the early 1930s onward. Whether a consistent pattern of cuticular thickenings resembling scales is present was never resolved.

**Study methods.** Work was supported by a [[National Science Foundation]] grant administered through the University of Virginia. Of 176 gastrotrichs isolated for data recording in June, July and early August 1958, several resembling *Ichthydium* were selected. Specimens were measured under the microscope, isolated into methocel, and supported on slides with crushed glass bits. Cultivation in artificial media failed beyond a few days. Mounting trials used a modified [[Hoyer Technique]] (with Dr. J. C. Riggin advising), which yielded mounts usable for some months but not permanently. In 1960, trials with Dekophane looked promising.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Specimens came from submerged water plants scraped from rocks along the north shoreline of [[Mountain Lake]], from marshy areas near the station laboratory, from a small pasture pond in western Giles County (Nunn's pond), and from "Lake Rana," a small frog pond on station grounds fed by a watercourse from Salt Pond Mountain.
- **Behavior:** Animals were described as rapid swimmers and darters. One specimen moved leisurely, while another darted and snapped its body "like a minnow."
- **Seasonal variation:** Rippled body surfaces appeared in mid-June and seemed to be temporary. Late July and early August 1960 were characterized by low precipitation and almost no moisture in the formerly wet marsh near the laboratory. Lake samples in 1960 contained a considerable number of chaetonotids but not the abundance seen in 1958, and no circularly ringed forms were found. Frog pond samples yielded very few gastrotrichs.
- **Observation of drying:** When the water drop evaporated, the body flattened and thickened.

## Key Findings

- **Probable identity:** Specimen #106, collected from the north shoreline of Mountain Lake on July 18, 1958, was judged "clearly of the *Ichthydium sulcatum* type" and "very closely akin" to the species described by Stokes and Brunson.
- **Measurements of specimen #106:** Overall length 133.2 µm; esophagus 33.3 µm; toes 16.05 µm; mouth width 3.33 µm; head width 23.31 µm. Circular sulcations were clearly visible even at low magnification.
- **Specimen #116** (recovered July 19, 1958, from Nunn's pasture pond; a mate was also seen): overall length 129.87 µm; esophagus 29.97 µm; narrowed posterior zone 16.65 µm long and 11.1 µm wide; body width 16.65 µm; head width 17.76 µm; neck width 14.43 µm.
- **Specimen from July 21, 1958:** Overall length about 159 µm; esophagus and narrowed posterior end of equal length, together a little over one-quarter of total length.
- **Specimen #122** (examined July 23, 1958): 149.85 µm. Its mouth was "ribbed" or "beaded," a condition not previously noted. Frontal shield width was 6.60 µm.
- **Specimen #164** (August 8, 1958, from marsh near the laboratory): the most elongated of the smooth species, about 173 µm, with delicate transverse striations in the esophagus and a jointed condition in the caudal prolongations.
- **Specimens 128 and 129** (July 28, 1958): data were meager; diagnosis was "probably *J. sulcatum*."
- **Morphological features:** Clearly S-lobed head with shield and subterminal mouth; a pair of lateral clear vesicles; two pairs of stiff dorsal "hairs" with reflexed tips; dense short ventral ciliation; darkly granular intestine; an elongated, lobulated egg.
- **Cuticular patterns:** Various delicate cuticular patterns appeared in some specimens. The genus *Heterolepidoderma* was repeatedly considered as a comparison, and evidence suggested that a member of this genus, apparently unreported from the United States, may have been obtained.
- **Open questions:** The author asks whether grooves persist through successive generations and whether the species is valid. He argues that cultivated clones would be the surest method of resolving this, because slight variations occur within populations.
- **Prospects:** Analyses of data accumulated in 1958 and 1960 were planned for future papers, but the note itself provides no statistical summary.
