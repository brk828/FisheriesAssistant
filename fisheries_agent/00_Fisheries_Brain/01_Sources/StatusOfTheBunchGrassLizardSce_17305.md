## Executive Summary
This note documents a dramatic population decline of the bunch grass lizard ([[Sceloporus scalaris]]) in the Chiricahua Mountains of southeastern Arizona, attributed to cattle grazing-induced destruction of its obligate bunch grass microhabitat. Historical densities of 100–200 individuals per hectare (recorded in the 1970s) had collapsed to near-zero observations by the mid-1990s, with survey areas showing complete loss of grass cover (0%) where 80–100% cover previously existed. The authors call for immediate conservation action, including cessation of grazing, habitat surveys, and exclusion fencing to prevent local extinction.

## Background & Description
- No detailed morphological data (e.g., body size, coloration, scalation) provided in this note; the paper is focused on population and habitat status.
- Life history is described as typical of **short-lived temperate sceloporine lizards**, exhibiting winter activity patterns (per [[Ballinger and Congdon]] 1980, 1981; [[Newlin 1976]]).
- Referenced taxonomic works ([[Smith 1939]]; [[Thomas and Dixon 1976]]) place *S. scalaris* within the broader *Sceloporus scalaris* species group (Sauria: Iguanidae, per original classification).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat specialist**: obligate dependence on dense bunch grass cover, used both for:
  - Shelter from predators
  - Favorable microclimatic/thermal conditions, particularly during winter months
- Lizards occupying more open/exposed sites exhibited **lower body temperatures** than those in dense grass cover ([[Smith, Ballinger, and Congdon 1993]] — thermal ecology study).
- **Key grass species** comprising optimal habitat (~90% cover) in the Chiricahua Mountains:
  - *Blepharoneuron tricholepis*
  - *Muhlenbergia virescens*
  - *Bromus frondosus*
- Historical grass structure: stands up to 0.5 m high with 80–100% ground cover.
- Degraded habitat state (1994–1995): 0% grass cover, bare ground with occasional dicots or *Iris* spp.
- Similar bunch-grass habitat use noted in high plateau grasslands south of Animas, New Mexico (Barney Tomberlin, pers. comm.).
- Broader range: Sierra Madre Occidental (Mexico) extending into montane "sky island" grasslands of southwestern New Mexico and southeastern Arizona.

## Key Findings
- **Historical baseline (1970s)**: Population densities of 100–200 individuals/hectare were typical across large areas of upper mountain slopes.
- **Decline timeline**:
  - Cattle presence first noted in study area in 1975 (late 1970s).
  - Major grazing impact not recognized until early 1990s (flagged by Laurie Vitt, pers. comm.).
  - August 1994: Congdon observed complete loss of grass cover and no lizards in former study sites.
  - Early 1990s: Researcher Tom Mathies reported increasing difficulty locating specimens; observed only **one male lizard** at Rustler Park (spring 1995).
  - March 1995: Congdon located only **4 juveniles** in Study Area II after extensive search.
  - May 6, 1995: Ballinger found **zero lizards** and complete absence of grass cover in Study Area II; cattle observed within 100 m.
- **Causal driver**: Cattle grazing within Coronado National Forest identified as the primary cause of bunch grass habitat destruction and consequent lizard population collapse.
- **Recovery signs**: Some spring regrowth from bases of former grass clumps observed (1995), but full recovery unlikely absent cattle removal.
- **Conservation recommendations proposed**:
  1. Immediate cessation of grazing at high-altitude lizard habitat sites.
  2. Quantitative population assessment of *S. scalaris* in affected areas.
  3. Qualitative survey of bunch grass microhabitat across the mountain range's upper elevations.
  4. Development of strategies for population and habitat rejuvenation, including exclusion fencing to protect seed banks and remaining basal stock of lizards.
- **Risk assessment**: Authors describe the situation as posing a credible risk of **local extinction** of *S. scalaris* in the Chiricahua Mountains absent intervention.
