## Executive Summary

This 1970 study by Paul B. Liao (University of Washington) represents one of the first systematic investigations into the **pollution potential of salmonid fish hatcheries**, a topic previously overlooked in scientific literature. Through literature review, a questionnaire survey of 63 North American fisheries agencies, and field testing at three Washington State hatcheries ([[Green River Salmon Hatchery]], [[Cowlitz Trout Hatchery]], and [[Seward Park Trout Hatchery]]), the study demonstrates that salmonid hatchery effluents constitute a legitimate—though historically dismissed—source of water pollution. Key pollutant categories identified were **organic matter (BOD/DO depletion)**, **nutrients (nitrate, ammonia, phosphate)**, and **solids (suspended/settleable)**. The paper quantifies pollutant production rates relative to fish size, feeding rate, loading density, and water temperature, and proposes operational and treatment-based mitigation strategies.

## Background & Description

While this is primarily a pollution/aquaculture engineering study rather than a morphological one, several fish-size-related physiological parameters relevant to hatchery management are reported:

- **Fish size range tested:** 2–26 inches in length; weight range corresponding to 0.52–26.0 lb fish/gpm water supply loading.
- **Water requirement scaling:** Water use is **directly proportional to water temperature** and **inversely proportional to fish size** (e.g., at 50°F with 40 g/fish average size, water use = 1 gpm per 25 lb of fish).
- **Loading density (pond carrying capacity):** Proportional to fish size, inversely proportional to water temperature (per [[Haskell]], [[Westers]], and [[Burrows]] loading models).
- **Dissolved Oxygen (DO) tolerance:** Reported limiting DO value for salmonids is **2–3 mg/L**, though sublethal reductions in activity and growth rate reportedly occur below **6 mg/L**.
- **Feeding physiology:** Young fry fed dry floating mash up to 8 times/day; older fingerlings/adults fed sinking pelleted food (e.g., Oregon moist pellet: <35% moisture, >35% protein, >5% fat, <4% fiber) 2–3 times/day, at <10% of body weight/day.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper frames salmonids within an **artificial propagation and receiving-stream ecosystem** context rather than a natural niche:

- **Habitat:** Hatchery raceways, rearing ponds, and incubators, discharging into natural rivers/streams (e.g., Jordan River, MI; San Joaquin system, CA; Rifle Falls, CO; Green/Cowlitz Rivers, WA).
- **Trophic/ecosystem impacts of effluent:**
  - Nutrient enrichment (nitrate, ammonia, phosphate) stimulating **algae and weed growth**, occasionally causing algal blooms.
  - Deposition of fish fecal matter and uneaten food on stream substrates, supporting **pollution-tolerant benthic organisms** (e.g., tubifex worms/"blood worms") and altering benthic community structure.
  - **DO depletion** in receiving waters due to fish respiratory uptake, benthal oxygen demand, and atmospheric losses—directly threatening downstream aquatic life, including the hatchery-reared salmonids themselves.
- **Interactions with regulatory ecosystems:** Documented conflicts with state/federal water quality standards (Michigan, Colorado, California), including a Colorado Supreme Court-upheld civil action against the [[Rifle Falls Trout Hatchery]].

## Key Findings

1. **Pollution existence confirmed:** Contrary to prior assumptions, field data and case studies (Jordan River National Fish Hatchery, San Joaquin Hatchery, Rifle Falls Trout Hatchery) confirm salmonid hatcheries are measurable point sources of water pollution.

2. **Survey results (63 agencies, 46 with salmonid hatcheries):**
   - Total annual salmonid production reported: **24,176,684 lbs**
   - 13 agencies reported pollution problems; 9 conducted studies; 8 installed special control units; 18 felt it was a potential problem.

3. **Organic pollutant loading:**
   - Average water consumption: **34,000 gpd per 100 lb of fish**.
   - Normal-operation BOD effluent concentration: <10 mg/L (often meets water quality standards).
   - Average BOD production: **1.34 lb per 100 lb fish/day** (equivalent to ~10 people's sewage output).
   - During pond cleaning, BOD concentrations exceeded **35 mg/L** when loading density >25 lb fish/gpm—exceeding typical secondary sewage effluent strength.
   - Average DO depletion: **0.727 lb O₂ per 100 lb fish/day** (range 0.246–1.754).

4. **Nutrient pollutant loading (average effluent concentrations):**
   - Nitrate: 1.68 mg/L
   - Ammonia: 0.53 mg/L
   - Phosphate: 0.077 mg/L

5. **Solids pollutant loading:**
   - Normal operation: Total solids avg. 85 mg/L (92% dissolved, 35% volatile); suspended solids avg. 7 mg/L (~50% settleable).
   - During pond cleaning: Total solids avg. 174 mg/L (45% dissolved, 62% volatile); suspended solids avg. 96 mg/L (~89% settleable) — indicating rapid downstream deposition and stream-bed blanketing.

6. **Operational relationships identified:**
   - Pollutant production rates (BOD, DO uptake, nitrate, ammonia, phosphate) are **directly proportional to feeding rate** and **inversely proportional to fish size and loading density**.
   - Higher loading densities correlate with **more efficient food conversion** and reduced pollutant output, likely due to reduced uneaten food and lower fish activity.
   - Seward Park Trout Hatchery trial: optimized feeding (1.3 lb food/100 lb fish/day) reduced BOD production to **0.75 lb/100 lb fish/day**, versus 1.34 lb/100 lb fish/day at higher (4.2 lb) feeding rates — demonstrating feeding optimization as a key pollution control lever.

7. **National-scale pollution estimate:** Extrapolating to an estimated 36 million lb annual salmonid production across North America, total BOD output would be roughly equivalent to the untreated sewage load of **2.4 million people**.

8. **Recommendations:**
   - Adopt optimized **fish loading density and water supply rates** (per [[Burrows]], [[Haskell]], and [[Westers]] models) to minimize pollutant production while maintaining DO ≥5 mg/L in raceway effluent.
   - Implement **precision feeding schedules** calibrated to water temperature, fish size/species, and food type to minimize uneaten food waste.
   - Install **effluent treatment systems** (aeration + detention ponds) capable of withstanding quantitative/qualitative shock loading, particularly during pond-cleaning events.
   - Call for **large-scale governmental investigation** into hatchery effluent characterization and receiving-water impacts across North America.
