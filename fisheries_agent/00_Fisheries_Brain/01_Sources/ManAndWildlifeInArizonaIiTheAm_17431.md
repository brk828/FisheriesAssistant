# Man and Wildlife in Arizona II: The American Settlement Period, 1865-1912

#wildlife-history #conservation-history #arizona

## Executive Summary

This document is the front matter of *Man and Wildlife in Arizona II: The American Settlement Period, 1865-1912*, edited by [[Neil B. Carmony]] and [[David E. Brown]]. It consists of a table of contents, an editors' preface, and scanned handwritten pages that are largely illegible. The volume continues the work of [[Goode P. Davis]] on pre-settlement wildlife, covering the period from the end of Davis's account through statehood. The editors describe this as a period of transcontinental railroads, livestock expansion, irrigated farming, and the founding of most present-day cities, all of which drove major wildlife and habitat change.

## Background & Description

The book is organized into two parts and a reporter's notebook section:

- **Part I, "From Soldiers to Ecologists":** Covers U.S. government surveys, including the [[Wheeler Survey]], [[Edgar A. Mearns]]'s boundary survey work, and [[C. Hart Merriam]]'s role in founding the [[U.S. Biological Survey]].
- **Part II, "Naturalists and Collectors":** Profiles [[Edward W. Nelson]], who took up ranching in the White Mountains, and [[Herbert Brown]], described as Arizona's first resident ornithologist. It also covers collectors who explored Papagueria, the Pinacate Region, and related areas.
- **Sportsmen and Journalists:** Draws on books, magazines, and newspapers to document hunting for meat, furs, and plumes, the decline of game, and the rise of sport hunting. A section on sport fishing reviews reports of native trout, opinions on Colorado River salmon and other native warm-water fishes, and the absence of lake fishes in the territory.
- **Legislatures and Bureaucracies:** Covers territorial and county bounties on predators, Forest Service management, national game preserves, the Arizona fish commissions, and the introduction of carp and other exotic fishes.
- **Predators, habitat change, and early conservation:** Covers declines in grizzlies, wolves, and jaguars; shifts in prairie dog, pronghorn, javelina, and bird populations; the loss of great mesquite forests; and the early game laws that protected game animals, songbirds, and eggs.

The appendix lists Territorial Game Codes and amendments. The references section was not legible in the source.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The preface frames the book around the environmental changes of settlement. These included:

- Market hunting for food, pelts, and sport, which caused wholesale destruction of some game populations.
- Livestock grazing across the territory, which conflicted with large predators.
- Irrigated agriculture and the transformation of riparian and wetland habitats, including the loss of [[Mesquite Forest]] systems.
- Shifts in species distributions, with some animals adapting to settlement and others declining or disappearing.

The editors pose questions central to the volume: which ecological changes were most significant, which animals adapted and why, which species were lost entirely, which early conservation measures succeeded or failed, and whether these changes are irreversible. They state the goal is to use past conditions to improve future management.

## Key Findings

The source is a front matter and only partly legible, so the findings below are drawn from the table of contents and editors' preface rather than from primary data:

- **Scope:** The book covers Arizona Territory from 1865 to 1912, extending the earlier [[Man and Wildlife in Arizona]] volume by Davis, which was first prepared as a Master's thesis and reprinted in 1986.
- **Method:** Documentation relies on the reports of early explorers, government surveys, and newspaper accounts of the day.
- **Fisheries content:** Native trout fishing was productive, Colorado River salmon and other native warm-water fishes were discussed, lake fishes were absent, and carp and other exotic fishes were introduced.
- **Predator control:** Bounties and campaigns targeted predators, including the wolf, and grizzlies, wolves, and jaguars faced decline due to conflicts with livestock grazing.
- **Institutions:** The Forest Service, the [[Grand Canyon National Game Preserve]] (established by Executive Order), and the Biological Survey became central to federal wildlife management, though jurisdictional conflicts arose.
- **Legal change:** Early game laws reflected growing awareness of wildlife values, and laws later prohibited commercial use of wildlife. Forest Service and other public lands were expected to protect wildlife.
- **Editors' goal:** The volume aims to give a fuller understanding of past conditions and their effects on wildlife, to support better future decisions.
