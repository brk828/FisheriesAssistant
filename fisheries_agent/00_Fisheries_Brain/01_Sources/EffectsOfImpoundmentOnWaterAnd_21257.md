# Effects of Impoundment on Water and Sediment in the Arkansas River at Pueblo Reservoir

#limnology #water-quality #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation-funded study, conducted by the University of Southern Colorado, documents how the formation of [[Pueblo Reservoir]] changed the inorganic chemistry of the [[Arkansas River]] in Colorado. Water was sampled from the river before impoundment (1972–1974) and from the reservoir, inlet, and outlet from June 1974 to March 1976. Sediments were analyzed before and after impoundment. The authors concluded that dissolved and suspended concentrations of nearly all parameters were far below recommended or maximum permissible limits for public water supply, fisheries, irrigation, and recreation. Dissolved oxygen and temperature were judged nearly ideal for [[Salmonid Fishes]], with rainbow trout growth described as spectacular. The main concern was sediment enrichment in zinc, iron, and manganese near the reservoir inlet, indicating that the reservoir is acting as a sink for some metals.

## Background & Description

**Objectives.** The primary objective was to determine how concentrations of major and minor inorganic chemicals in the Arkansas River would change after [[Pueblo Dam]] was completed. Secondary objectives were to track the fate of selected chemicals in the reservoir, predict future water-quality changes under changing operations, and evaluate water quality against criteria for beneficial uses.

**Historical context.** Pre-impoundment monitoring began in September 1972 under an NSF-supported heavy-metals program, with monthly samples from two river sites. Impoundment began in June 1974. Data were reported as a four-year culmination of pre- and post-impoundment work.

**Sampling design.**
- Nine water sites: six reservoir sites (A–F) at 3–5 m depth intervals using a non-metallic Van Dorn sampler; inlet site G, which shifted with reservoir level; outlet site H (about 2 miles downstream); and site I below the stilling basin.
- Monthly sampling from June 1974 to March 1976, with gaps due to ice cover and access.
- Sediment cores were taken from 31 flood-plain sites in fall 1973, and triplicate cores were collected by [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] divers at sites B, D, and E in June 1974, with additional cores from C, F, and G.

**Analytical approach.**
- Water was filtered through 0.45 µm membranes. The filtrate was the [[Dissolved Fraction]] and the retained material was the [[Suspended Fraction]].
- Trace metals were measured by [[Atomic Absorption Spectrometry]] (direct and APDC-MIBK extraction) and flame photometry; mercury by flameless AA.
- Sediments were digested for AA, and some elements were measured by [[X-Ray Fluorescence Spectroscopy]].
- Parameters were grouped as "gross limnological parameters" (temperature, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, pH, conductivity, alkalinity, chloride, sulfate, hardness, and related measures) and trace metals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and seasonality.** The dominant seasonal pattern was high winter values during low runoff and low summer values during high runoff, which produced a dilution effect. Temperature and turbidity were exceptions. The reservoir appeared to buffer normal seasonal fluctuations, keeping dissolved solids higher into spring.

**Physical and chemical controls.** Evaporation concentrated dissolved solids, and some metals were precipitated into sediments. Iron, manganese, and zinc did not follow classic seasonal patterns. Their depth profiles showed higher dissolved concentrations near the bottom, which the authors attributed to water-sediment exchange. Sediments sometimes released metals and more often took them up.

**Local sources.** A significant zinc source in the Arkansas River is mine drainage from the Leadville area, particularly [[California Gulch]]. Lead also enters from mining and from gasoline-powered boats, though no boat-related lead effect was observed.

**Aquatic life thresholds.** The report cites [[Water Quality Criteria]] for:
- Dissolved oxygen: not below 6 mg/l for cold-water biota, and not below 7 mg/l for successful spawning.
- Turbidity: not above 25 JTU for warm-water lakes or 10 JTU for cold-water lakes.
- pH: 6.0–9.0 for freshwater organisms.

## Key Findings

**1. Dissolved oxygen and temperature (salmonid habitat).**
- Pool mean dissolved oxygen was 8.2 mg/l (1974) and 8.8 mg/l (1975), with monthly highs of 9.6 and 10.6 mg/l in December and lows of 6.4 and 6.7 mg/l in July.
- Grand mean temperature was 13.5 °C (56.3 °F) for 1974–75 and 12.4 °C (54.3 °F) for 1975–76. Highest monthly means were 22.9 °C (July 1974) and 21.3 °C (August 1975); lowest were 4.4 °C and 4.9 °C in December.
- Mean summer maxima slightly exceeded the 20 °C (68 °F) recommended maximum for salmonid growth.
- The authors concluded that trout growth should not be adversely affected, but successful spawning may be a problem for certain trout species.

**2. Turbidity.** Pool mean annual turbidity was 24 JTU (1974) and 25 JTU (1975). Inlet values were usually higher, especially during high discharge. Pool turbidity periodically exceeded the 25 and 10 JTU aquatic-life guidelines. Effects on fishes were beyond the study's scope.

**3. pH.** Ranges were 6.9–8.7 (1974) and 7.0–8.8 (1975). The lowest values were near the bottom and the highest near the surface. Values stayed within the freshwater range and never fell below 6.0.

**4. Conductance and total dissolved solids.**
- Mean annual specific conductance was 511 µmhos (1974) and 480 µmhos (1975).
- TDS never exceeded the 500 mg/l permissible maximum, and was frequently below the desirable 200 mg/l for public water supply.

**5. Alkalinity, chloride, sulfate, and hardness.**
- Mean annual total alkalinity was 116 mg/l and 177 mg/l as CaCO3. The authors classed the reach as an "average" carbonate river.
- Chloride annual means were 9.8 and 9.3 mg/l, close to the North American river mean of 8 mg/l.
- Sulfate annual means were 152 and 121 mg/l. The pool rarely met the desirable 50 mg/l level but never exceeded the 250 mg/l permissible maximum.
- Total hardness annual means were 215 and 232 mg/l as CaCO3.

**6. Lithium.** Pool dissolved lithium averaged 0.017 ± 0.004 mg/l (1974–75) and 0.014 ± 0.004 mg/l (1975–76). This was well above the North American river median of 0.0011 mg/l cited from Durum and Haffty, but far below the 2.5 mg/l limit suggested for plant toxicity.

**7. Copper.** Dissolved copper averaged 0.004 ± 0.001 mg/l in 1974–75, consistent with solubility predictions at pH about 8. The report noted that sediment copper rose from 31 ± 17 (pre-impoundment flood plain) to 37 ± 13 in the reservoir, with 59 at site F near the inlet.

**8. Zinc.**
- Mean dissolved zinc was 0.010 ± 0.015 mg/l (1974–75) and 0.007 ± 0.002 mg/l (1975–76). Suspended zinc averages were 0.021 ± 0.030 and 0.010 ± 0.010 mg/l.
- Zinc tracked suspended matter loads, and reservoir sediment zinc was 4 to 5 times the pre-impoundment flood-plain level. The greatest loading was near site F.

**9. Manganese.**
- Pool dissolved manganese averaged 0.022 ± 0.090 mg/l and 0.016 ± 0.016 mg/l. Inlet averages were 0.016 and 0.020 mg/l.
- A single high of 0.63 mg/l was recorded at the bottom of site B in August 1975. Sharp depth profiles were most consistent for manganese.
- During November 1974, manganese was the only parameter that increased from outlet to inlet, indicating precipitation in the reservoir and sediment enrichment at sites E and F.

**10. Iron.** Pool dissolved iron averaged 0.006 ± 0.004 mg/l, compared with 0.030 ± 0.016 mg/l pre-impoundment. Part of this difference reflects a change to a more sensitive analytical method. Sediment iron rose from about 1.6% (pre-impoundment flood plain) to 2.6% at site E and 2.7% at site F after one year.

**11. Lead.** Pool dissolved lead averaged 0.001 ± 0.001 mg/l in 1974–75 and was below detection in 1975–76. Sediment lead averaged 65 ± 34 pre-impoundment and 100 ± 68 in the reservoir, with 157 at site F and 209 at site D. The authors suggested possible enrichment near the inlet.

**12. Cadmium and mercury.**
- Most cadmium values were below the 0.001 mg/l detection limit. The highest monthly average was 0.011 ± 0.001 mg/l (May 1975), which the authors noted exceeded the drinking-water maximum, though this was atypical.
- Mercury was detected in about half of dissolved samples, mostly below 1 µg/l. The highest value was 0.54 µg/l (August 1974, inlet). Pool means were 0.02 ± 0.01 µg/l and 0.02 ± 0.3 µg/l.
- Sediment cadmium averaged 4.2 ± 1.4 mg/g pre-impoundment and 4.4 ± 1.1 mg/g in the reservoir, with 6.1 at site F.

**13. Other metals.** Cobalt, nickel, silver, and arsenic were mostly at or below detection limits, and all reservoir means fell within or below reported national ranges.

**14. Overall water-quality status.** Where criteria exist, trace-metal concentrations almost never approached or exceeded them. The authors concluded that the Arkansas River and Pueblo Reservoir are "fairly average North American surface waters."

**15. Sediment enrichment.** Recent inlet sediments (sites E, F, and G) showed increases in iron, manganese, and zinc relative to pre-impoundment flood-plain sediments. Magnesium, copper, cadmium, and lead also appeared to be increasing. Inlet sediments approached or exceeded those of the Arkansas River just downstream of California Gulch, the largest heavy-metal source in the upper river. The environmental effect of this downstream loading was not yet clearly identified.

**16. Conclusions and recommendations.**
- Normal seasonal trends were slightly altered by reservoir formation.
- Reservoir sediments should be sampled and analyzed every 3 to 4 years.
- Additional statistical analysis is needed on the dissolved and suspended fractions, discharge, suspended matter, and sediments to quantify loading rates.
- The authors recommended a one-year study of the water fractions be repeated on an approximate four-year basis.
- The data have predictive value for similar projects in assessing water-quality impacts.
