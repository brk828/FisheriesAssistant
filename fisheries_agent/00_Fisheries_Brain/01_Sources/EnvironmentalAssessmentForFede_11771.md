# Environmental Assessment for the Virgin River Resource Management and Recovery Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) Environmental Assessment (EA), prepared under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) and dated June 2001, evaluates federal agency participation in the proposed Virgin River Resource Management and Recovery Program (VRRMRP). The program aims to recover imperiled aquatic species, chiefly the endangered [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Chub]], while protecting water supplies for growing human demand. It compares three alternatives: a No-Action Alternative (A), the Preferred Alternative (B, the VRRMRP), and a Basinwide Recovery Program (C). The preferred alternative would start recovery actions immediately in Utah and introduce a "recovery banking" mechanism to balance species protection against water development. The EA concludes that the preferred alternative would benefit all native and sensitive aquatic species and would not produce cumulatively significant impacts.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and need.** Federal recovery under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) has not halted declines of listed fishes. Section 7 consultation has been the main protective tool, but the USFWS increasingly struggles to find reasonable and prudent alternatives (RPAs) that avoid jeopardy while supplying water for growth. The stated goals of the VRRMRP are to (1) implement actions that recover, conserve, and protect native species and (2) enhance water supplies for human needs.

**Listed and imperiled species.** The woundfin was listed as endangered in 1970; the Virgin River chub was listed as endangered in 1989 and reclassified as *Gila seminuda* following [[Demarais Et Al 1992]]. The [[Virgin Spinedace]] was proposed as threatened in 1994, managed under a conservation agreement, and its listing was withdrawn in 1996. Four other state-sensitive species (desert sucker, flannelmouth sucker, speckled dace, and southwestern toad) are also covered.

**Basin.** The Basin covers about 5,900 square miles. In Utah it comprises 1,831,017 acres, of which about 67% is federal land. The Virgin River flows about 200 miles southwest from its headwaters to Lake Mead, forming the boundary between the [[Colorado Plateau]] and the [[Great Basin]].

**Regional context.** The Basin is a major biogeographic overlap zone. The EA lists 366 vertebrate species coexisting with imperiled species (81 state-sensitive), 13 sensitive plant species, and four sensitive invertebrates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and flow regime.** Flows are highly variable, with winter rain and spring snowmelt producing high flows from December through May, summer dewatering, and flash floods. The [[Virgin River Gage]] (USGS 09406000) is the key long-term record. Annual discharge ranged from 69,000 acre-feet (1977 and 1990) to 337,000 acre-feet (1922). The highest recorded peak was 22,800 cfs (December 6, 1966), a 100-year flood. Effective discharge at Hurricane was estimated at about 1,000 cfs; the statistically defined bankfull discharge (4,200 cfs) is much larger, and channel-forming flows are governed mainly by spring runoff.

**Water quality.** Headwater water quality is excellent, but salinity, temperature, and sediment load increase downstream. [[La Verkin Springs]] (Pah Tempe) contributes 12 to 19 cfs at 100–109°F with an average salinity of 9,650 mg/l.

**Riparian and floodplain systems.** The floodplain supplies nutrients and land-based food to a river that produces little organic matter. Tamarisk (*Tamarix ramosissima*) has largely replaced native vegetation along the mainstem. Riparian zones support 354 wildlife species, and about 90% of Washington County wildlife uses riparian corridors.

**Non-native species.** Thirteen introduced fish occur in Utah waters, led by the [[Red Shiner]], which has been especially damaging to endangered fishes. Chemical treatment since 1988 has reduced red shiner populations. Crayfish, present in tributaries, may prey on larval and young fish.

**Human pressure.** Washington County may grow from 48,560 people (1990) to 380,592 by 2040. Projected water demand in 2050 is 267,000 af/yr, or 200,600 af/yr with a 25% per-capita reduction. The estimated shortfall is 130,900 af. Agriculture uses about 87,800 af/yr, and the county has a developed supply of 69,700 af/yr.

**Tribal water rights.** The [[Shivwits Band]] received a 2000 settlement entitling it to 4,000 af/yr from the Virgin, Santa Clara, and groundwater sources. The VRRMRP is expected to make these projects easier to permit.

**Culture and recreation.** The Basin contains prehistoric Virgin-Kayenta Anasazi sites and Southern Paiute heritage. Recreational pressures include rafting, camping, and off-highway vehicle use in the floodplain.

## Key Findings

**Recovery actions and costs**
- The VRRMRP is projected to cost more than $14 million. Average annual funding is $450,000 each from federal, state, and local sources in Alternative B. Alternative A provides about $30,000 per year, and Alternative C would cost as much as $20 million with $650,000 per year from each source.
- Under Alternative B, recovery of listed species would be expected to take 15–20 years; Alternative C would start after a 3–5 year development period.

**Recovery banking**
- The "recovery bank" uses "recovery units" that accumulate as conservation actions are completed and are withdrawn as water development impacts occur, keeping a net positive balance.
- "Sufficient progress" requires (1) a net gain in the bank ledger, (2) maintained recovery actions, and (3) the expected biological response.

**Environmental consequences**
- The preferred alternative is expected to benefit all native and sensitive aquatic species, the endangered fishes, riparian resources, and other threatened and endangered species (desert tortoise, southwestern willow flycatcher, bald eagle, Mexican spotted owl).
- Negative impacts are intended for non-native fishes; reduced sportfishing opportunities are expected.
- The VRRMRP would not produce cumulatively significant impacts; instream-flow reservation may accelerate water projects that would proceed regardless.
- Economic impacts are described as minimal under both "with fish" scenarios.

**Population status**
- Woundfin numbers have declined drastically since standardized monitoring began in 1986, and reproducing populations are restricted to reaches above Washington Fields Diversion.
- Virgin spinedace populations are generally stable. Spinedace were observed at each monitored station in 1994–1998, with two stations not monitored in 1998 because of high flows.

**Quail Creek controversy**
- A 1982 biological opinion assumed minimum flows of 86 cfs or natural low flow above the Washington Fields diversion. Environmental groups contend the project has not met these conditions.
- The VRRMRP baseline uses flows from [[Lentsch Et Al 1998a]]; critics want the 86 cfs standard incorporated instead.

**Public comment**
- The draft EA received 13 written comments. Responses address the need for more detail, participant selection, recovery banking, stakeholder priorities, regulatory abdication, flow requirements, and baseline flows.

**Outstanding requirements**
- A biological opinion must be completed before the final NEPA decision. The Utah Field Office has started an intra-service Section 7 consultation.
