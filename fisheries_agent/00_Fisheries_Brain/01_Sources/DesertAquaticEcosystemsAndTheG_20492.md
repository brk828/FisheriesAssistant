# Desert Aquatic Ecosystems and the Genetic and Morphological Diversity of Death Valley System Speckled Dace

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the morphological and genetic diversity of [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) across 13 populations in the [[Death Valley System]], the [[Lahontan Basin]], and the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The authors use these data, together with a review of endemic wetland organisms, to argue that isolated desert wetlands should be managed as [[Evolutionarily Significant Unit]]s and that conservation should target whole wetland communities rather than single aquatic species.

Meristic and mensural differences among populations were highly significant, while body shape varied only slightly and fell into two forms. [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] of 23 loci showed isolated populations to be genetically distinct, with very limited [[Gene Flow]] among Death Valley system (DVS) populations. The authors identify 59 endemic wetland taxa in the DVS and conclude that both the genetic structure of the dace and the endemic community support community-based protection.

## Background & Description

Desert fish faunas in western North America are relict assemblages in isolated, endorheic basins. Taxonomic work on these fishes began during pre-settlement land surveys, and invertebrate study followed soon after. The authors note that the fauna is depauperate compared with more mesic regions, yet many endemic plants and animals occupy wetlands ranging from less than 0.1 ha to several thousand hectares. Small habitat size and population isolation make these fishes vulnerable to habitat degradation and nonnative species. The document states that approximately 60% of North American fish species listed as threatened or endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service occur in the desert Southwest.

The DVS comprises the Owens, Amargosa, and Mojave river basins, which were tributary to Pleistocene Lake Manly (present-day Death Valley) during pluvial periods. Most DVS fishes had been well studied, but speckled dace, which occupy the Owens and Amargosa basins, had not. Declining dace populations prompted morphological, genetic, and status studies intended to guide conservation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** Morphological analyses covered 17 collections, of which 14 collections from 13 localities were used for meristic and mensural analysis. Thirteen collections were used for allozyme analysis, with two nearby Bishop-area collections combined. Sampled sites include Smoke Creek, Reese River, Huntington Creek, East Fork Walker River, several Owens basin springs (for example, Whitmore Hot Springs and Little Alkali Lake), Bishop-area drains and creeks, Amargosa River localities (Tecopa, Beatty), Ash Meadows, Bradford Spring, and Beaver Dam Wash in the Colorado drainage.

**Habitat of endemic taxa.** Of the 59 endemic taxa, most aquatic members occupy springs (SP), spring margins (SPMAR), or streams (ST). Plants and mammals are associated with mesic alkali meadows (MAM) or alkali flats (MAF). Several mollusks and amphibians are semiterrestrial. Many endemic taxa live some distance from fish habitat.

**Biogeographic context.** Genetic clustering places DVS dace separately from Lahontan populations. The authors infer that ancestral DVS dace invaded the DVS from the Colorado River rather than from the Lahontan basin, although they recommend further Colorado River sampling.

## Key Findings

**1. Morphology: highly significant population differences with two body forms.**
- Meristic and mensural characters differed significantly among all populations (P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA).
- Morphometrics were based on 18 truss measurements, fin lengths, and six meristic variables recorded from 484 fish longer than 25 mm standard length.
- Body shape differences were slight. Clustered factor scores from sheared principal components II and III suggested two forms: a short, deep-bodied form and a long, slender form.

**2. Genetics: strong population structure.**
- Electrophoresis assayed 23 gene loci in 311 fish from 13 localities.
- Polymorphism ranged from 4% (lower Marble Creek and Little Alkali Lake) to 35% (Huntington Creek).
- Mean observed heterozygosity ranged from 0.008 (Little Alkali Lake) to 0.072 (East Fork Walker River).
- Allele frequencies differed significantly among populations at all polymorphic loci (P < 0.05, chi-square tests).
- The mean fixation index across the 13 populations (F_ST) was 0.538, and the mean individual index (F_IT) was 0.621.
- Whitmore Hot Springs and Little Alkali Lake shared a fixed difference at the PEPA* locus, the only fixed allelic difference between populations.
- Only Smoke Creek (sAAT*), Bishop (PGM-1*), and Huntington Creek (PGD-1* and PEPD*) departed from [[Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium]] at some loci.

**3. Gene flow and drainage structure.**
- Hierarchical analysis showed significant allele frequency differences among Owens basin populations and among Amargosa basin populations.
- Reese River and Huntington Creek (Humboldt drainage) did not differ in allele frequency, which the authors interpret as higher gene flow within the Humboldt drainage.
- The population-to-drainage fixation index was 0.525, and the population-to-total index was 0.528. Gene flow was therefore approximately the same within and between drainages.
- Genetically effective migration was estimated at about one individual per DVS population every five generations (Nm = 0.21). This is well below the level needed to prevent divergence under a neutral model.

**4. Genetic diversity and drift.**
- Mean heterozygosity across DVS populations was 0.014, substantially lower than Lahontan and lower Colorado populations and below the cyprinid average of 0.052 cited from Buth et al. (1991).
- The authors attribute low DVS heterozygosity to [[Genetic Drift]] in small, isolated populations.

**5. Endemic wetland community.**
- 59 endemic taxa are documented from DVS wetlands: 16 fishes, 1 amphibian, 22 mollusks (mostly [[Springsnail]]s), 7 aquatic insects, 3 mammals, and 10 plants.
- Examples include the [[Owens Pupfish]], [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], [[Ash Meadows Poolfish]], [[Amargosa Toad]], and [[Amargosa Niterwort]].
- Many endemic plants, mammals, and mollusks depend on mesic soils moistened by distant springs. The [[Amargosa Niterwort]], for instance, relies on soil moisture from springs about 8 km away.

**6. Conservation implications.**
- Protection has historically been limited to taxonomically recognized forms. The authors argue that even widespread species may contain unique populations that merit protection as evolutionarily significant units.
- Existing strategies by [[Williams et al. 1985]] and [[Moyle and Yoshiyama 1994]] focus on aquatic species. The authors contend this is inadequate because many endemic organisms depend on adjacent mesic soils as well as water.
- Programs must be designed around the entire ecological community, including terrestrial habitats that depend on spring discharge. Narrowly focused programs could unintentionally alter soil moisture and subsurface percolation, damaging distant endemic species.
- The authors conclude that the same community-based principle applies to wetlands in more mesic regions, and that conserving populations requires conserving the ecosystems in which they evolve.
