# Report on Progress of Fish Management Studies on Strip-Mine Lakes

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #fisheries-management #Centrarchidae #strip-mine-lakes

## Executive Summary
This report, authored by Charles Burner (Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Commission), documents a multi-year (1949–1953) investigation into the fish populations and limnological conditions of **strip-mine lakes** (strip-pits) in southeastern Kansas. These artificial impoundments, created as a byproduct of coal strip-mining, were surveyed to determine their suitability for sport fisheries and to develop management techniques to counteract declining fishing success. The study found that while many strip-pits are viable fisheries habitat, a substantial fraction are unsuitable due to low pH, small size, or inaccessibility. A recurring management problem was **[[Sunfish]] Overpopulation** and stunting, which suppressed [[Largemouth Bass]] populations through competition and egg/fry predation. The Commission tested total and partial fish removal (via chemical treatment), differential restocking, and water fertilization as corrective management strategies, with promising early results.

## Background & Description
The report is primarily population- and management-focused rather than morphological, but includes relevant size/growth data used as proxies for habitat quality and stocking success:

- **[[Largemouth Bass]]**: Legal size defined as ≥10 inches. Three-inch fingerlings stocked in June grew to 9.5 inches by September in a fertilized pit; in another restocked pit, three-inch fingerlings reached 10+ inches after ~15 months.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]**: Fingerlings stocked at three inches grew to 12 inches within ~15 months post-stocking.
- **[[Sunfish]] (Bluegill-type)**: Characteristically undergo **stunting** in older strip-pits — individuals grow larger than the size preferred by bass as prey, yet remain smaller than desirable for anglers, creating a size bottleneck that perpetuates overpopulation.
- **[[Walleye]]**: Stocked as fry (15-acre pit, spring 1953); no recovery of stocked walleye was documented after partial poisoning treatment in fall 1953, raising unresolved questions about fry survival in this habitat type.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**Habitat type**: Strip-mine lakes are anthropogenic lentic systems formed from water accumulating in abandoned coal strip-pits, primarily seepage-fed with minor input from sparsely vegetated spoil banks.

- **Areal extent**: As of a 1946 U.S. Forestry Service report, water covered ~8% of the 25,000 acres of strip-mined land in Kansas (~2,000 acres of water), with expansion continuing at a rate of ~320 acres/year per major coal company.
- **Size range**: Individual pits ranged from <0.5 acre to >30 acres; ponds/pits ≤1/3 acre were deemed too small to support a satisfactory fishery, so management efforts were restricted to pits >0.5 acre.
- **Water chemistry**:
  - **pH**: Ranged from 2.8 (strongly acidic, locally termed "alkali" water) to 9.4 (alkaline). Conditions were considered unfavorable for fish at pH ≤4.5. Most productive pits fell between pH 7.0–8.4, near optimal for fish and fish-food organisms. ~20% of surveyed strip-mine lakes were too acidic to support fish life.
  - **Dissolved oxygen, carbonate/bicarbonate alkalinity, CO₂**: Generally within acceptable ranges for warm-water fisheries.
- **Productivity/Trophic status**: Strip-mine lakes exhibited notably **lower fertility** than natural farm ponds/lakes because they lack agricultural surface drainage/fertilizer input. Water transparency often exceeded 12 feet, which was linked to poor spawning success in [[Black Crappie]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Bullhead]], and reduced spawning success in [[Largemouth Bass]] as well.
- **Fertilization experiment**: A cooperative project with the Hume Sinclair Coal Company tested artificial fertilization to boost fish-food organism abundance; success was indicated by reduced water transparency (target ≤24 inches visibility).
- **Community dynamics**: Older strip-pits followed a successional decline pattern similar to natural Kansas lakes — initial good fishing after stocking, followed by a shift toward sunfish dominance, stunting, and bass decline, attributed to competition for food and sunfish predation on bass eggs/fry.

## Key Findings

### Fish Yield Data by Site (unmanaged/unstocked pits)
| Location | Area | Total Yield (lb/acre) | Species Breakdown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crawford Co. pit (with shad) | 2 acres | 224 lb/ac | 199 bass (20 lb); 1,984 sunfish (85 lb); 800 shad (119 lb); 7 legal bass (15 lb) |
| Crawford Co. pit | 14 acres | 136 lb/ac | 237 bass (23 lb); 40 crappie (7 lb); 7,407 sunfish (105 lb); ratio 31 sunfish : 1 bass |
| Cherokee Co. pit | 6 acres | 51 lb/ac | 15 lb bass (5 legal bass/acre); 36 lb other fish (incl. 5 lb bullhead) |

- Older, unmanaged strip-pits averaged approximately **13 legal-sized bass (≈20.5 lb)/acre**, consistent with a general pattern of bass decline and sunfish overpopulation.

### Management Techniques Trialed
1. **Total fish removal and restocking** — chemical treatment (poisoning) to eliminate existing fish community, followed by restocking with game species.
2. **Partial eradication** — chemically treating a portion of a pit to reduce fish density, then restocking with game fish to restore [[Sunfish]]-[[Largemouth Bass]] balance.
3. **Marginal (shallow-water) treatment** — targeted chemical treatment of shallow zones during summer (when large bass occupy deeper water) to selectively remove sunfish while sparing bass.
4. **New-pit stocking** — stocking newly formed strip-pits with hatchery fish soon after mining operations cease and pits fill with water.

### Growth Outcomes Post-Management
- Restocked pits surveyed in 1953 (~15 months post-stocking) showed strong growth: bass fingerlings (3 in.) reached 10+ inches; channel catfish fingerlings (3 in.) reached 12 inches — indicating management interventions can substantially improve growth rates relative to unmanaged, overpopulated pits.

### Experimental Species Introductions
- **[[Walleye]]** fry stocked in a 15-acre pit (spring 1953) were not recovered after partial poisoning in fall 1953; survival status remains undetermined, pending further investigation.
- **Trout** stocking was also being monitored experimentally, though no specific data are reported yet.

### Broader Significance
- Kansas was noted as likely the **first U.S. state** to conduct intensive fisheries investigations specifically on strip-mine lakes, positioning this work as foundational for a novel and expanding freshwater fisheries habitat type.
- The report emphasizes that strip-pit environmental characteristics (seepage-fed hydrology, low fertility, high transparency, variable pH) differ markedly from natural ponds/lakes/streams, meaning management techniques developed for conventional waters cannot be assumed to transfer directly — necessitating dedicated, multi-year strip-pit-specific research before conclusions can be drawn.
- Program expansion plans included opening more coal-company-owned strip-land to public fishing via agreements with the [[Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Commission]].
