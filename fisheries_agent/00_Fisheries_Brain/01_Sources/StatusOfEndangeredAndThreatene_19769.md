# Status of Endangered and Threatened Fish Species in Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 [[Bureau Of Land Management]] (BLM) Technical Note, prepared by James E. Johnson of the Colorado State Office, reviews the status of endangered, threatened and state-listed fishes in Colorado. It covers four cutthroat trout subspecies (*Salmo clarki*) and four warm-water species of the [[Colorado River]] system: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The note concludes that nearly all of these taxa persist only at greatly reduced levels. Main threats are hybridization with exotic salmonids, habitat alteration by dams and water development, and loss of the turbulent-river habitat the native fishes need. The note recommends habitat and population inventories on BLM-administered National Resource Lands (NRL), cooperation with the Colorado Division of Wildlife, and the recovery actions outlined under the [[Endangered Species Act Of 1973]].

## Background & Description

**Legal status.** The 1974 U.S. list designated three Colorado fishes as endangered: greenback cutthroat trout, humpback chub and Colorado squawfish. The Fish and Wildlife Service's threatened list for Colorado included the Rio Grande cutthroat trout. Three further taxa were listed as "status undetermined": yellowfin cutthroat trout, Colorado cutthroat trout and humpback sucker. The State of Colorado's endangered list added bonytail chub. Its threatened list also included Arkansas River speckled chub, Arkansas darter, central johnny darter and plains orange-throat darter. The author notes that taxonomy and status are confused for several of these taxa. The two darters are widespread, and the two Arkansas River endemics need further study.

**Cutthroat trout (*Salmo clarki*).** All four Colorado subspecies are part of a highly variable gene pool, and their taxonomy remains debated. Each subspecies has a distinct description and range:

- [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. c. stomias*): originally headwaters of the South Platte and Arkansas rivers. Large, prominent spots and brilliant golden-to-orange coloration.
- [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. c. virginalis*): upper Rio Grande and upper Pecos drainages. Smaller spots and more pastel coloration.
- [[Colorado Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. c. pleuriticus*): cold headwaters of the Green and Colorado rivers, and the San Juan River. Large spots and gaudy crimson or orange coloration.
- [[Yellowfin Cutthroat Trout]] (*S. c. macdonaldi*): known from Twin Lakes (Arkansas drainage). Small, irregular spots and silvery coloration with yellow tints on the lower fins.

**Warm-water species (Colorado River system).** The three large endangered fishes and the bonytail evolved in the turbulent, silt-laden Colorado River. They share a streamlined, bottom-hugging body plan with small heads and eyes, wedge-shaped anteriors, thin caudal peduncles and large pelvic and caudal fins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hybridization and exotic species.** All four cutthroat subspecies hybridize readily with other cutthroat trout and with rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*). Competitive exclusion and genetic swamping follow from introducing exotic salmonids into small native populations. The only "pure" native populations left in Colorado are those isolated from stocked trout by a permanent barrier such as a waterfall. A 1971 test of Beaver Creek cutthroat trout (Moffat County) found 7 of 15 specimens lacking basibranchial teeth, a sign of rainbow trout hybridization. Holden and Stalnaker (1970) found abundant hybridization between *Gila cypha* and *Gila elegans*. The author suggests that man-made alterations of the river have broken down the isolating mechanisms between these species.

**Dams and altered flow.** Most large Colorado River drainage rivers are now dammed. Ponded water above dams and clear, cold water below them favor exotic salmonids, centrarchids and ictalurids over the native fauna. Migratory species also lost access to spawning tributaries.

**Refugia.** Small headwater streams in the Southwest act as refugia for native fishes retreating from human disturbance and exotic competition (Minckley and Deacon, 1968). Inventories in these streams are difficult but necessary to find the last native strongholds.

**Decline and future pressure.** Within 30 to 40 years, populations of the large river species have declined steadily until all four are virtually extinct over most of their range. Remaining habitat is expected to be important for energy and water development. The author warns that further degradation could make the native Colorado River fauna the first major fauna to undergo mass extinction.

**Management principles.** For the cutthroat trout, the proposed steps are: inventory headwaters; eliminate exotic fishes in suitable isolated areas; restock only within the natural drainage of each fish; monitor reproduction; and discourage fishing until populations can sustain harvest. The author notes that such "brood populations" may never support a general harvest.

## Key Findings

- **Greenback cutthroat trout:** Thought extinct, pure strains were later found in Como Creek and other Colorado streams. Albion Creek fish were stocked into Black Hollow Creek in 1967 after eradication of existing species, and a self-reproducing population now exists. In summer 1974, 30,000 eggs were taken for hatching, but hatching success was relatively poor. The DOW Work Plan requested $12,500 for collecting 50 brood fish and rearing their progeny. Fishing was closed on existing populations for 1975. BLM's potential public-domain lands are limited to high Arkansas River tributaries in the Canon City District, with the Huerfano watershed given special inventory priority.
- **Rio Grande cutthroat trout:** Known from upper Trinchera Creek and other headwaters on Trinchera Ranch, Costilla County, Colorado, plus streams in New Mexico national forests. Trinchera Ranch is being subdivided for land sales. A Colorado restocking attempt in one lake was unsuccessful, and hatchery rearing has met with little success. No trout of this subspecies are known to maintain populations on NR Lands.
- **Colorado cutthroat trout:** Pure populations include Trapper's Lake, which the author calls "surprisingly close" to pure stock despite 1943–1950 Yellowstone cutthroat stocking. Populations on NR Lands are at North Beaver Creek (Wyoming) and Northwater Creek (Colorado). BLM should survey Colorado River highland tributaries above about 8,000 feet, with attention to Parachute, Roan, Piceance and Little Snake drainages.
- **Yellowfin cutthroat trout:** Probably extinct, with no collections since 1891. A transplant to Island Lake (Grand Mesa) in the early 1900s was probably unsuccessful. The State DOW no longer recognizes the subspecies. BLM interest is minimal, with some priority for Eagle River inventory.
- **Colorado squawfish:** Once reached nearly 100 pounds and 6 feet, the world's largest minnow. Modern fish are typically up to 10 pounds and 3 feet. Declining populations remain in the Colorado River (Lake Powell to Grand Junction), the Green River below Ouray, and the Yampa River below Craig. In spring 1974, pre-spawning adults from the Yampa were transported to Willow Beach Federal Fish Hatchery. They spawned after pituitary injections, and their offspring are being reared, with stocking planned for late 1975. A recovery team has been formed. Priority inventory areas include the Colorado River around Grand Junction, the Yampa from Craig to Dinosaur National Monument, the lower Dolores, the Gunnison, and the lower White.
- **Humpback sucker:** Reported from the Colorado River above and below Grand Junction, the Yampa below Craig and the White below Piceance Creek. Reservoir populations persist in Mohave and Havasu, with a warning from Saguaro Lake, where an annual commercial harvest of six tons in 1949 fell to zero by 1965. Adults were artificially spawned at Willow Beach in spring 1974, and several thousand offspring are being reared. The author argues that less work should go to this species because of its undetermined federal status and apparent abundance in some localities.
- **Humpback chub:** First described in 1946 from Grand Canyon, when fewer than a dozen specimens were in museums by 1965. Recent collections come from the Grand Canyon, below Lake Powell, and the Green, White and Yampa confluence areas. DOW took 32 specimens above and below Grand Junction on August 10, 1974, the largest known concentration of young and adult fish. The State prohibited capture in 1975. The Grand Junction reach is the only known breeding population, and it is bordered by NR Lands that need prompt inventory.
- **Bonytail chub:** Now known only from the Green and Yampa rivers and very small numbers below some mainstem dams. Reports from the White, Colorado and Gunnison rivers are unconfirmed. There are no known management plans. The Yampa population is one of the few known and should be inventoried as a potential source stock for other sites.
- **Policy recommendation:** BLM should begin gathering habitat-requirement and distribution data for these large-river species "immediately," anticipating conflicts over water and energy development. The author describes this as one of the most critical tests of endangered species legislation in the country.
