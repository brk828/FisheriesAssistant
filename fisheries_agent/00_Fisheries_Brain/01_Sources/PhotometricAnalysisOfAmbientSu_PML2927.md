# Photobehavioral Analysis of Subsurface and Surface Illumination on the Colorado River, Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This January 1991 research proposal by Michael D. Yard of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program, submitted to David L. Wegner, Program Manager, defines a methodology for testing how light illumination shapes the temporal and spatial behavior of [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the Colorado River, Grand Canyon. The study is framed as a supporting component of the [[Endangered Species Program]] and is designed to be integrated with radiotelemetry work by [[BIO/WEST Inc]] and with scheduled GCES research trips. The proposal addresses whether [[Negative Phototaxis]] and light avoidance, alongside velocity and depth preferences, explain habitat selection in this endangered fish.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is a large endemic cyprinid of the Colorado River system. It was described as a distinct species by Miller in 1946 from a Grand Canyon specimen. The species was considered uncommon, was listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1967, and was presumed extirpated from Grand Canyon before 1975, although it persists in the mainstem. Knowledge of its original distribution and life history is limited.

The species evolved under an [[Allochthonous]] system of high, seasonally variable sediment loads and temperatures. Its morphology and feeding ecology are thought to be adapted to foraging across a range of depths and food types. Humpback chub are described as well adapted to swift, silt-laden rivers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dam effects:** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] regulated flows, reduced sediment transport, and increased water clarity, producing an abrupt shift from an allochthonous to an [[Autochthonous]] system. Historic pre-dam suspended sediment exceeded 10,000 ppm (the figure is written "1oroob ppm" in the OCR and is read here as 10,000 ppm); present levels are below 200 ppm (Schmidt and Graf, 1988).
- **Sediment sources:** Sediment enters the system from (1) periodic input from perennial tributaries such as the [[Little Colorado River]], Paria River and Kanab Creek, and infrequent debris flows; and (2) degradation of alluvial sand deposits.
- **Light and turbidity:** Subsurface illumination is controlled by turbidity and ambient light. Turbidity is a function of suspended sediment and flow. Wind-driven surface agitation reduces light penetration.
- **Food base:** Primary food items include the filamentous green alga *Cladophora glomerata*, diatom assemblages, the amphipod *Gammarus lacustris*, and other macroinvertebrates. Productivity is hypothesized to decline with distance downstream of the dam because of nutrient depletion, suspended sediment, and seasonal light shifts.
- **Other fishes:** Species composition shifts toward natives with distance from the dam, and native fishes are associated with major tributaries (Maddux et al., 1987).
- **Comparative behavior:** Brown trout avoid direct sunlight, and walleye show a negative phototactic response linked to retinal reflecting pigments (Bulkowski and Meade, 1983) and increased feeding at low light (Ryder, 1977).

## Key Findings

The proposal sets out three objectives plus a hydrological evaluation:

1. **Temporal light shifts:** Determine diel, seasonal and annual changes in ambient surface light. Illuminance is to be logged at 0.25-hour intervals, with monthly measurements of daily maxima and percentage change.
2. **Longitudinal light attenuation:** Measure the proportional decrease in subsurface illuminance along the river at sample stations every 10 miles, with depth readings at 0.25 m intervals from the surface to the point of light extinction, plus Secchi disk transparency.
3. **Avoidance behavior:**
   - 3.a: Test whether subsurface light affects temporal activity patterns in adult humpback chub.
   - 3.b: Test whether spatial distribution responds to subsurface light, using longitudinal surveillance during crepuscular periods and fixed-point monitoring of tagged fish.
4. **Hydrological records:** Evaluate historic and present discharge and suspended sediment data for the Paria River (gage since 1923) and Little Colorado River (gage since 1946, near Cameron, Arizona, 45 miles upstream of the confluence).

**Equipment:** An irradiameter with a photocell (spectral range 400 to 720 nm) will measure illuminance in lux. The ambient cell has a diffuser disk with about 1% transmission. A depth transducer and CR-10 datalogger will record data remotely. Spectral filters (blue and green) will isolate wavelength bands.

**Radiotelemetry limits:** Radio signal extinction in water of 850 to 950 µmhos/cm conductance is about 4.75 to 5 m depth (Kaeding et al., 1990; Yard et al., 1990). The subsurface photocell will sit above this extinction depth. Fish below this depth can therefore be separated from fish above it.

**Logistics and budget:** All field work will be performed by GCES personnel alongside scheduled endangered species trips, with no additional personnel. Equipment is largely already available. The listed items total $430.00 for the CR-10 datalogger, $125.00 for spectral filters, $235.00 for data cable, $35.00 for underwater deployment equipment, and $35.00 for a digital photometer. The schedule includes research trips from 91-05 to 92-17 (February 1991 to February 1992), a semi-annual report, a draft final report on March 15, 1992, and a final report on April 15, 1992.

**Key hypothesis:** Humpback chub habitat selection may reflect both avoidance of light and preference for velocity. Avoidance could force fish into preferred habitat more often, with possible effects on foraging efficiency and condition.
