# Biotic and Abiotic Effects of Flash Flooding in a Montane Desert Stream

#aquatic-invertebrates #freshwater-ecology #belostomatidae

## Executive Summary

This study measured the effects of a [[Flash Flood]] on a 122 m reach of the [[North Fork Cave Creek]] in the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] (Chihuahuan Desert, Arizona). Substrate, [[Organic Detritus]], and aquatic animal richness and abundance were sampled 8 to 16 days before and within 7 days after a flood on 29 July 1994. The flood reshaped pool morphology and removed most coarse detritus and most aquatic animals, while substrate composition at the reach scale stayed unchanged. Reach-wide taxonomic richness fell from 35 to 21 taxa, and [[Ephemeroptera]] were eliminated. The author argues that flash floods in montane desert streams cause greater mortality and detritus removal than floods in [[Mesic Stream]] systems, making [[Recolonization]] pathways and [[Population Bottleneck]] effects central to population persistence.

## Background & Description

Flash floods in desert streams arrive suddenly, last briefly, and have strong physical and biological impacts. They are generated by spatially patchy [[Desert Convective Thunderstorm]] activity. Individual floods are unpredictable over short time spans, but regional flood frequency and timing show clear long-term patterns. Storms typically cover small areas (20 to 100 km²) for short durations (typically under 40 minutes). Deeply cut canyons limit lateral dispersal onto floodplains, which maintains the stream power needed to move large substrates. The introduction quotes a 1995 eyewitness account describing rolling rock and debris beneath a flood that arrived with no local rain.

The study site was a series of 14 bedrock pools (wetted area 1.3 to 9.4 m²) connected by less than 1 L/s of baseflow. The North Fork is a permanent, spring-fed, first-order drainage at 1935 m elevation, with a 5.4 km² upstream drainage area of mixed deciduous and coniferous forest. The flood frequency for streams in the Chiricahua Mountains was about 0.8 floods per year. No rainfall above 20 mm had occurred for at least five months before the flood.

The study had three objectives: (1) measure mortality to identify flood-resistant and flood-susceptible taxa; (2) measure changes in organic detritus, a key food resource; and (3) measure changes in substrate, which defines the physical environment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** Pools were mapped before and after the flood using a grid of point estimates of substrate type, with depth and detritus cover recorded. Invertebrates were sampled with a 0.1 m² [[Box Sampler]]. [[Abedus Herberti]], [[Gerridae]] water striders, and [[Oncorhynchus Mykiss]] were too mobile or large for the box sampler and were counted visually and by grabbling. Detritus was separated into four classes: deciduous leaf, coniferous leaf, [[Fine Particulate Organic Matter]] (FPOM, particles under 2 mm), and woody debris.

**Flood characteristics.** The flood was observed from the [[Southwestern Research Station]] as a mud-saturated front moving at several m/s. Discharge at the study site rose from under 1 L/s to at least 29 L/s. That value probably underestimated the peak, because it was measured 12 h after the flood began, and scouring 1 to 2 m above the water level suggested the peak was at least twice that. Discharge fell to 8 L/s within 24 h and 2 L/s within 5 days. The adjacent Middle Fork Cave Creek, of similar drainage area, stayed at baseflow.

**Recolonization context.** Montane desert streams differ from lowland ones. Waterfalls and bedrock channels limit upstream movement and deep-substrate refugia. The authors suggest that downstream drift and aerial colonization are therefore the main recovery routes. Headwater refugia may supply drifting and aerial colonists to downstream reaches.

## Key Findings

**Substrate and morphology**
- The flood strongly altered pool morphology, scouring some pools to bedrock and filling others with substrate.
- Percent substrate composition per pool did not change significantly (MANOVA, Wilks' lambda = 0.75, F = 1.444, p = 0.248, df = 5, 22). Substrate was moved within and among pools, but reach-level composition stayed constant.

**Organic detritus**
- Pool area covered by detritus fell from nearly 50% to under 5% (paired t-test, p < 0.0001, df = 13).
- Detritus dry mass declined significantly overall (MANOVA, p < 0.0001). Declines in deciduous leaf, coniferous leaf, and woody debris were significant (ANOVA, p < 0.05).
- FPOM did not decline significantly, and it was the only detritus class that did not.

**Aquatic animals**
- Mean taxonomic richness per 0.1 m² fell from 14 to 6 taxa (paired t-test, p = 0.0001, df = 9).
- Reach-wide richness fell from 35 to 21 taxa.
- Mean abundance fell by 95%, from 4804 to 228 individuals/m² (paired t-test, p = 0.0021, df = 9). Diptera, which dominated before the flood (82% of individuals), were heavily reduced, and [[Chironomidae]] abundance drove most of the variability in total abundance.
- All Ephemeroptera were eliminated (100% loss). Trichoptera fell by 95%, Coleoptera by 80%, and [[Oncorhynchus Mykiss]] by 80%.
- Early-instar Plecoptera and the riffle beetle *Cleptelmis* increased, but each increase came from a single high post-flood sample.
- Among taxa with low losses, *Abedus herberti* lost only 14%, and [[Gerridae]] also showed low loss, though the exact value is partly illegible in the source.

**Interpretation**
- Compared with mesic-stream studies, the authors conclude that flash floods in montane desert streams cause higher mortality and remove more detritus. The reported invertebrate loss (95%) is comparable to values from lowland Sonoran Desert streams (96% and 98%) and from a New Mexico montane desert stream (94%).
- Losses probably occurred through three routes: stranding on the floodplain, downstream transport into drying reaches, and grinding by moving substrate. Most taxa were obligately aquatic juveniles, so any of these routes could cause death.
- *Cricotopus* larvae encased in [[Nostoc]] cases may survive flood and drought, since the cases retain moisture and serve as food.

**Behavioral and life-history strategies**
- *Abedus herberti* detects rainfall on the stream surface and leaves the stream in response, which the author links to its low loss rate. Not all individuals respond, and cannibalism is well documented in this taxon.
- [[Phylloicus Aeneus]] is univoltine. Its adults emerge just before the mean date of the first monsoon flood, and its larvae shift to FPOM when leaf litter is removed by floods.
- Ephemeroptera have fast life cycles, with larval development as short as 8 days in some lowland taxa. The author proposes that this reduces flood encounter probability.

**Fish distribution**
- Native [[Rhinichthys Osculus]] were the only fish in the North Fork in the early 1960s. By 1994 they had been replaced by introduced [[Oncorhynchus Mykiss]]. A late-1994 flood, worsened by forest fires, reduced trout to a remnant population about 1 km upstream. Native dace then returned to the lower reaches. By 1999 trout had reinvaded the study reach.
- The future balance between trout and dace is unresolved. Possible outcomes include trout excluding dace through predation, flooding favoring dace, or a dynamic flood-mediated coexistence.

**Population and evolutionary implications**
- Flash floods extirpated or severely reduced taxa at both pool (1 to 20 m) and reach (over 100 m) scales, suggesting low flood resistance in montane desert taxa.
- Repeated extirpation and recolonization by small numbers can produce population bottlenecks, which reduce heterozygosity and fitness in desert fishes.
- Flash floods may also impose strong selection that favors flood-avoidance traits and local adaptation. The author states that this remains to be tested empirically.
