## Executive Summary
This 1954 field report by Charles C. Bowers (Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources) documents an early applied fisheries management technique: the selective poisoning of [[Gizzard Shad]] using low-concentration [[Rotenone]] to reduce overpopulation without significantly harming sport and pan fish populations. Building on observations of shad's unusual sensitivity to rotenone and a 1950 pilot experiment on Kentucky Lake, the study successfully treated the 70-acre Carpenter Lake in October 1954. The treatment removed approximately 285 lbs of shad per acre while killing less than 1 lb per acre of game/pan fish, resulting in a rapid, noticeable improvement in sport fishing success within three days.

## Background & Description
- **Species of Interest**: [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), a pelagic forage fish known to reach nuisance population densities in Kentucky impoundments.
- **Key Biological Observation**: Over five years of population studies (on Kentucky, Cumberland, Herrington, and Dewey Lakes), biologists noted that gizzard shad were consistently:
  1. The **first species to show distress** following [[Rotenone]] application.
  2. Susceptible to **lingering sub-lethal concentrations** — shad swimming into treated areas days later continued to die even after toxicity had dropped below the lethal threshold for other fish species.
- **Rationale for Selective Poisoning**: This differential sensitivity suggested that a carefully controlled, low-dose rotenone application could selectively cull shad populations while sparing more toxin-tolerant game and pan fish, such as [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Crappie]], [[White Crappie]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Longear Sunfish]], [[Warmouth]], [[Bullhead]], and [[Freshwater Drum]].

### Prior Experiment: Kentucky Lake (1950)
- **Method**: Standard rotenone concentrations (1 lb/surface acre) sprayed directly onto surfacing pelagic shad schools during midday summer feeding.
- **Hypothesis**: A lethal concentration would form in the top few feet of water (where shad congregate) and dilute to non-lethal levels in deeper water (where other species reside).
- **Outcome**: Successful in avoiding bycatch mortality, but logistically inefficient — the effort of targeting individual surface schools did not justify the small proportion of the total shad population killed. Aerial spraying was considered as a more efficient alternative but not pursued at the time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Location**: Carpenter Lake, a 70-acre state-owned impoundment approximately 9 miles northeast of Owensboro, Kentucky.
- **Water Chemistry at Treatment (October 1954)**:
  - Temperature: 65°F
  - pH: 7.4
  - Total Alkalinity: 60 ppm
- **Lake Morphometry**: Average depth 5 ft; maximum depth 11 ft.
- **Management Motivation**: Severe shad overpopulation was identified in summer 1954; managers hypothesized that reducing shad biomass would improve habitat/resource conditions (competition, forage, oxygen) for desirable [[Largemouth Bass]] and panfish species.
- **Community Impact Context**: Twenty-seven lakeside homes made shad carcass removal a public necessity, highlighting the human/recreational dimension of nuisance shad die-offs.

## Key Findings

### Treatment Protocol
- **Target Concentration**: 0.10 ppm (one-tenth part per million) — significantly lower than standard rotenone dosing.
- **Formulation**: 5% powdered rotenone mixture.
- **Application Method**: Sprayed over the lake surface via gasoline-powered centrifugal pump mounted on an outboard motor boat.
- **Application Strategy**: Concentration carefully modulated — targeting no more than 0.10 ppm in shallow areas, and at least 0.10 ppm in deeper areas, to account for shad's preference for surface/pelagic waters.

### Toxicological Response Timeline
- **15 minutes**: Shad began showing signs of distress in each sprayed area.
- **1 hour**: Majority of shad died; no additional rotenone was required.
- **By nightfall**: A few individual shad still surfacing.
- **By next morning**: All shad mortality activity had ceased.
- **Day 2**: Shad began floating to the surface in large numbers (delayed buoyancy post-mortem).

### Quantitative Kill Results
| Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Shad recovered & buried (7 days) | 14,050 lbs |
| Shad estimated decomposed/sank (uncollected) | ~6,000 lbs |
| **Total shad removed** | **~285 lbs/acre** |
| [[Largemouth Bass]] killed (8–10 in) | 9 |
| [[Largemouth Bass]] killed (5–6 in) | 130 |
| [[Bluegill]] killed (6–7 in) | 35 |
| [[Bluegill]] killed (1–4 in) | 1,000 |
| [[Bullhead]] killed (6–8 in) | 4 |
| [[Minnows]], [[Freshwater Drum]], [[Crappie]] killed | 0 (none recovered) |

- **Non-target Mortality**: Estimated at **less than 1 lb of game/pan fish per acre** — a highly favorable selectivity ratio relative to shad biomass removed.
- **Likely Cause of Non-target Kill**: Bycatch mortality attributed to localized over-concentration of rotenone in shallow zones despite dosing precautions.

### Verification & Follow-up
- One area was **re-sprayed three days later** to test for shad survivors — no further shad kill was observed, suggesting high treatment efficacy, though long-term population elimination could not be confirmed immediately.
- **Fishing Success**: A "definite increase" in angler success was noted within **three days** post-treatment, with increased [[Largemouth Bass]] activity observed along the shoreline — supporting the hypothesis that shad reduction improves conditions for game fish.

### Programmatic & Management Implications
- The Carpenter Lake project was met with strong local approval from resident anglers.
- Based on this success, the Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources planned to expand selective shad poisoning to a **900-acre lake in eastern Kentucky**, and potentially additional smaller lakes — indicating this technique was being scaled up as a viable fisheries management tool.
- **Logistical Note**: Original removal crew (biologist + 1 assistant) was insufficient for the unexpectedly large shad die-off; two additional laborers had to be hired, underscoring the importance of accurate biomass estimation for future project planning.
