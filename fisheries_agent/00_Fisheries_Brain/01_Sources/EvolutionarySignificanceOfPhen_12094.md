# Evolutionary Significance of Phenotypic Plasticity in Plants

#botany #plant-ecology #phenotypic-plasticity

## Executive Summary

This is a review essay by A. D. Bradshaw (University College of North Wales, Bangor, and University of California, Davis) on the evolutionary significance of [[Phenotypic Plasticity]] in plants. Bradshaw defines plasticity as the extent to which environmental influences alter the expression of a genotype's characters, and he argues that plasticity is specific to individual characters, to particular environmental influences, and to direction. It is under genetic control, is not necessarily linked to heterozygosity, and can be altered rapidly by selection. The essay links plasticity to four types of selection (disruptive in time, disruptive in space, directional, and stabilizing) and reviews mechanisms ranging from continuous to discrete switches. It concludes that plasticity is widespread in plants because they lack the behavioral escape available to animals, yet most plant species still consist of genetically distinct, locally adapted populations.

The source contains no fish or other aquatic animal data. Its aquatic content concerns water plants, mainly heterophyllous species of [[Ranunculus Aquatilis]] and related taxa.

## Background & Description

Bradshaw distinguishes plasticity from related terms:

- **Plasticity** is any non-genetic intragenotypic variation. It can be morphological (permanent, developmental) or physiological (possibly reversible, at any life stage). All plasticity is ultimately physiological.
- **Lack of stability (instability)** is non-genetic variation with no observed environmental cause, often attributed to developmental noise (Waddington, 1957). Bradshaw argues this is probably environmentally caused and folds it into plasticity.
- **Phenotypic flexibility** (Thoday, 1953) refers to functioning across a range of environments and may include stable responses, so it is not equivalent to plasticity.
- **[[Homeostasis]]** (Cannon, 1932) is avoided because its usage has become confused.

The essay also covers the genetic control of plasticity. Plasticity can be linked to developmental pattern: characters formed over long meristematic periods (size, leaf number) tend to be more plastic than characters formed rapidly or fixed early (reproductive structures, bud scales). Bradshaw notes this rule does not explain all cases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Bradshaw argues that plants are especially dependent on plasticity because they are static, cannot escape unsuitable conditions, and have less buffering than animals. Their seeds disperse passively, so an annual's offspring may land in very different environments from generation to generation. Plasticity therefore matters most in:

- **Annual weeds**, which show extreme seed and capitulum plasticity relative to related perennials. For example, [[Sonchus Oleraceus]] has been recorded with 1 to 1600 capitula.
- **Seasonal and drought environments**, where deciduousness is treated as a highly developed form of plasticity, and where desert shrubs such as *Cereidium floridum* produce leaves only after rain.
- **Aquatic habitats**, where heterophylly (submerged versus floating or aerial leaves) and cleistogamy (flowers that self-fertilize underwater or in drought) are common.
- **Competitive and density-dependent settings**, where plants adjust seed output per plant so that seed output per unit area stays roughly constant.

Bradshaw also discusses the cost of plasticity. Permanent characters can only be modified by losing and regrowing organs, which is costly. Plasticity may also fail when change is sudden or when a modification cannot be reversed, which can make genetic adaptation more effective.

## Key Findings

**1. Plasticity is specific, not whole-genotype.** Bradshaw concludes that plasticity is (a) specific to individual characters, (b) specific to particular environmental influences, (c) specific in direction, (d) under genetic control unrelated to heterozygosity, and (e) radically alterable by selection. He argues that neither the developmental-pattern model nor a general whole-genotype property model explains the data alone.

**2. Examples of character-specific plasticity:**
- In *Polypogon monspeliensis*, spikelets per panicle varied a hundredfold between low and high fertility, while glume and seed size varied by only about 10%.
- In linseed (*Linum usitatissimum*), capsules per plant were 5.6 at high density and 77.5 at low density (L.S.D. at 5%: 22.6). Seeds per capsule were 8.1 versus 9.3 (L.S.D. 0.86), and seed weight per 100 seeds was 6.0 versus 5.9 (L.S.D. 0.34).
- In *Nicotiana rustica*, Paxman (1956) found that stability differed between characters, and that unrelated characters did not share stability.
- In *Euphrasia*, host presence increased vegetative size about 2.2-fold but flower size only 1.1-fold (Wilkins, 1963).

**3. Heterophylly is patchily distributed.** Heterophylly occurs in some species but not in close relatives. In British [[Potamogeton]] and [[Ranunculus]] (subgenus *Batrachium*), it appears in some species and not others. Bradshaw also notes that populations within species can differ in their capacity for heterophylly, as in *Polygonum amphibium* (Turesson, 1950).

**4. Mechanisms range from continuous to discrete.** Continuous plasticity ("dependent morphogenesis") covers traits like height and seed number. Discrete plasticity ("autoregulatory dependent morphogenesis") covers two-state switches such as leaf form in [[Ranunculus Peltatus]], with few intermediates. Bradshaw suggests the switch is often an indirect stimulus such as [[Photoperiod]], which allows anticipatory responses before the main environmental change.

**5. Selection can alter plasticity quickly.** Selection in [[Drosophila]] changed the induction of venation phenocopies and bithorax, and increased adaptive response to salt. Bradshaw uses these results to argue that plasticity is heritable and evolvable. Genetic assimilation (Waddington, 1961) can turn environmentally induced effects into genetically determined ones.

**6. Selection types favour plasticity in different ways:**
- **Disruptive selection in time** favours plasticity when environmental change is as fast as or faster than generation time, because genetic adaptation arrives too late (Kimura, 1955; Crow, 1955).
- **Disruptive selection in space** may favour spectacular plasticity when habitat changes over very short distances. *Plantago maritima* illustrates this, with shorter individuals in exposed areas and taller ones in shallow depressions.
- **Directional selection** can be supplemented by plasticity when genetic variation is limited.
- **[[Stabilizing Selection]]** can produce a uniform phenotype from different genotypes, analogous to dominance. The strongest evidence is from Gregor (1956a), where a wild *Plantago maritima* population in Iceland showed less than one-quarter the variation in plant size seen when grown in a garden.

**7. Conditions disfavouring plasticity.** Plasticity is less likely where stabilizing selection is strong (seed size and flower size are very stable), where changes are sudden, where modifications are irreversible in early life, and where the cost is too high. Bradshaw concludes that most plant species are made up of genetically distinct, locally adapted populations shaped by their most adverse conditions rather than their optimum.

**8. Open problems.** Bradshaw identifies four main gaps: the amount of genetic variation for plasticity in natural populations, how several characters are linked in one plastic response (as in [[Cleistogamy]] and the deciduous habit), how the direction of response is controlled, and the practical use of plasticity in crop improvement. He notes that plasticity in crops, such as yield stability under variable density, is essential to plant breeding.

**9. Summary conclusion.** Adaptation by plasticity is widespread in plants and has evolved in different ways in different species. Plasticity of one character can allow stability in another, so a species' plasticity should be assessed trait by trait.
