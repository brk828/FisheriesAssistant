# Lake Powell Post-Impoundment Investigation 1965 Progress Report

#ichthyology #reservoir-ecology #centrarchidae #salmonidae #limnology

## Executive Summary

This third annual progress report documents the fisheries and limnological investigation of [[Lake Powell]] from February 1965 to January 1966, during the reservoir's early filling phase. The study, financed under Section 8 of the Colorado River Storage Act, was conducted cooperatively by the Utah State Department of Fish and Game and Arizona Game and Fish Department. [[Lake Powell]] rose from elevation 3,491 feet (53,500 acres) to 3,535 feet (70,720 acres) during the study period, with exceptionally high runoff (over 14 million acre feet) significantly affecting reservoir limnology. The investigation focused on physical and chemical regimens, fish stocking programs, population dynamics of stocked sport fish (primarily [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]), and food habits. Key findings indicate successful establishment of bass and trout populations with excellent growth rates, particularly in the upper reservoir sections, and confirmation of natural bass reproduction. Water quality remained generally suitable for fish, though summer stratification and localized oxygen depletion occurred in bay environments.

## Background & Description

[[Lake Powell]] is a 186-mile-long, narrow, canyon-walled reservoir created by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]]. At full capacity, it will contain 28,040,000 acre feet with a surface area of 162,700 acres and approximately 1,800 miles of shoreline. Impoundment began in January 1963. The reservoir is characterized by predominantly cliff and talus slope shorelines with sparse vegetation of insignificant nutrient value. During 1965, boating access was available at [[Wahweap]], [[Hall's Crossing]], and [[Hite]].

The reservoir experienced unusual hydrological conditions in 1965. Following a relatively stable period from February to June (rising less than 0.6 feet), exceptional precipitation produced high runoff exceeding 14 million acre feet. Calculated inflow varied from 20,200 cfs to 75,500 cfs during peak runoff. Part of this water was immediately released downstream to relieve critical shortages in [[Lake Mead]], while the remainder was retained, raising the reservoir to elevation 3,531 feet by August. At the study period's end, the reservoir contained 8,804,000 acre feet with mean depth of 125 feet and maximum depth at the dam of 405 feet. The reservoir extended approximately 172 miles from the dam to 16 miles above [[Hite Ferry]].

Fish stocking in 1965 included approximately 3,500,000 [[Rainbow Trout]] fingerlings distributed by airplane in May, 40,000 five-inch rainbow stocked by truck in [[Wahweap Bay]] (October-November), 30,000 one-inch [[Black Crappie]] planted in July, and 4,700 four-inch black crappie in December. This continued the cooperative stocking program between Utah and Arizona Fish and Game Departments and the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and Chemical Regimens:**

Water chemistry and temperature monitoring occurred at multiple stations in March, June, September, and November, with additional temperature profiles in April, May, July, October, and December. Surface temperatures ranged from winter minimums of 40-51°F (January-February) to summer maximums of 79-82°F (July-August) in the lower reservoir and 76-79°F in the upper reservoir. The reservoir stratified in late June with a well-defined thermocline at 15-30 feet depth in the lower 40 miles, characterized by temperatures of 77°F at the upper limit and 67°F at the lower limit.

A significant density current, produced by the cold [[Colorado River]] water (approximately 34°F in winter), penetrated the reservoir to approximately [[Rock Creek]] (40 miles above the dam). This current, evident in winter and spring, contributed nutrient and oxygen-laden water far into the reservoir. The phenomenon appeared primarily temperature-driven rather than turbidity-driven, as silt settled rapidly.

Dissolved oxygen generally remained at levels sufficient for fish (typically 5-10 ppm), though late summer and fall readings between 2-3 ppm occurred along bay bottoms, with anaerobic conditions where vegetation was present. The upper reservoir showed higher oxygen values (6-10 ppm below 50 feet in March) compared to lower sections, reflecting Colorado River influence. pH values ranged from 7.6-8.5, with seasonal patterns showing increases during the growing season and minimums in fall/winter.

Alkalinity (primarily bicarbonate) showed pronounced seasonal fluctuations: winter values of 130-155 ppm at surface and 140-170 ppm in deeper water, declining to summer minimums of 85-100 ppm (surface and upper 125 feet) and 130 ppm below 200 feet. Total hardness followed similar patterns, ranging from 350-400 ppm before runoff to 40-170 ppm in the upper reservoir during peak runoff. Calcium hardness declined from 200-230 ppm (winter surface) to 115 ppm (lower lake) and 185 ppm (upper reservoir) by September. Magnesium-calcium ratios varied significantly, with the [[San Juan River]] contributing 50-75% less calcium hardness and 25-50% less magnesium hardness than the Colorado River.

Turbidity increased dramatically during runoff, with surface values below 15 ppm in the lower reservoir but exceeding 400 ppm in the upper 30 miles near the Colorado River confluence. By September, turbidity declined to 10 ppm or less reservoir-wide. Secchi disc transparency ranged from 0.5 feet near the Colorado River during runoff to 28-30 feet in the lower reservoir by September.

**Biological Communities:**

Non-game fish populations were dominated by [[Carp]] (51.5% of occurrence) and [[Green Sunfish]] (36%), with lesser numbers of [[Black Bullhead]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. Small forage minnows ([[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Mosquito Fish]]) that were abundant in 1963 remained largely absent from collections, though occasional red shiner schools were observed in the upper lake. [[Bluegill Sunfish]] appeared for the first time since impoundment, with several individuals and apparent hybrids with green sunfish collected.

## Key Findings

**Largemouth Bass Population Dynamics:**

Scale analysis from 428 bass revealed significant regional growth differences. Bass in the upper reservoir (Rock Creek to Hall's Crossing) grew 41-71 mm larger during their first year than those in the lower 30 miles, with total differences of 78 mm after two years for the 1963 year-class. Combined data showed bass in the lower reservoir reached average total lengths of 209 mm (first year) and 290 mm (second year), while upper reservoir bass attained 249 mm and 385 mm respectively. Growth rates compared favorably with leading bass waters including [[Conchos Reservoir]], [[Lake Mead]], and [[Norris Reservoir]].

Condition factors (K) increased with total length from 2.12 (115 mm fish) to 3.65 (455 mm fish), with bass 115-285 mm showing better condition than in 1964. Factors were comparable to Minnesota standards (average to excellent condition for 200-455 mm fish) and favorable compared to Tennessee and Iowa waters.

Natural reproduction was confirmed through seine collections in [[Wahweap Bay]] (July 21-22, 1965), which captured approximately 500 young-of-the-year bass less than 50 mm and numerous age-1 fish (100-150 mm). Catch rates of 0.64 age-1 bass and 10.1 young-of-the-year per 1,000 square feet indicated good year-class populations for both 1964 and 1965. Spawning appeared discontinuous, starting in March, interrupted during April-June due to water fluctuations and turbidity from intermittent canyon runoff, and resuming in July. Surface temperatures suitable for spawning (60°F+) occurred from late April onward.

Vertical gill netting experiments (July-August) revealed bass inhabited depths in or very close to the top of the thermocline, at temperatures between 71-78°F. This depth distribution coincided with the level of the inflowing Colorado River and may explain reduced summer harvest when bass occupy depths greater than those typically fished.

**Rainbow Trout Population Dynamics:**

Age-growth analysis of 177 trout scales showed first-year growth of approximately 190 mm standard length (8.5 inches total length) and second-year growth to 333 mm standard length (14.7 inches total length). This growth placed Lake Powell trout in the top 50% nationally and exceeded [[Fish Lake]] (Utah) rainbow, which required two years to reach 7.5 inches and three years for 12.4 inches. No significant difference existed between 1963 and 1964 plant growth rates.

Condition factors for 135 trout appeared slightly better than 1964, remaining steady for fish under 300 mm (K=1.80-1.84) but declining from 300-400 mm, then improving for fish larger than 400 mm (K=1.90 at 550 mm). Vertical distribution during stratification showed trout concentrated in or just above the thermocline (30-50 feet depth, 67-76°F), with capture rates ten times greater at these depths than elsewhere. Some trout appeared near the surface in Wahweap Bay, possibly for feeding.

Fin-clipped trout (274 marked in April) showed limited movement from Wahweap Bay to the main channel, with two individuals recovered in the tailrace below Glen Canyon Dam (August 9-10), indicating some downstream passage through the dam.

**Food Habits:**

Analysis of 203 bass stomachs (42.4% empty) revealed fish as the dominant food item. In the upper reservoir, fish occurred in 31.2% of age-I, 84.6% of age-II, and 71.0% of age-III stomachs, comprising 96.6%, 95.5%, and 86.6% of food volume respectively. [[Green Sunfish]] dominated across all age groups, with [[Carp]] significant in older fish and [[Channel Catfish]] occasionally taken. Lower reservoir bass showed similar patterns (>80% occurrence and volume), with green sunfish, carp, largemouth bass, and red shiner taken in that order. Zooplankton occurred only in age-I bass from the upper reservoir (26.8% occurrence), possibly due to higher turbidity protecting small fish and denser plankton populations. Insects (primarily Diptera and Anisoptera) decreased in importance with age.

Analysis of 91 trout stomachs (7.1% empty) showed zooplankton (cladocerans: Daphnia, Bosmina, and copepods) as the prevalent food item, contributing 99% by number, 49% by occurrence, and 53% by volume. Zooplankton occurrence was higher in smaller trout (80% for 190-249 mm) than larger fish (43% for 250-535 mm). Fish (red shiner, green sunfish) and insects (Diptera pupae) appeared only in trout over 249 mm, contributing less than 10% of occurrence and volume. Food habits remained relatively stable between 1964 and 1965.

**Fisheries Harvest:**

Creel census data from 407 bass and 162 trout showed excellent fishing, particularly March-May and autumn for bass. Most bass fishing occurred from [[Escalante River]] to [[Last Chance Arm]], coinciding with original 1963 planting sites and the zone between turbidity drop-off and farthest density current penetration. Bass harvest ranged 170-490 mm total length, with peak frequency at 330 mm (13 inches). The 1964 year-class comprised 78% of harvest, with the 1963 year-class contributing 22%. Trout fishing concentrated within five miles of the dam and in Wahweap Bay, with most harvest at 280-340 mm total length (peak at 300 mm/12 inches). The 1964 plants comprised 93% of trout harvest, with 7% from 1963 stocks.

**Sampling Methods Evaluation:**

Electro-shocking proved most effective, producing 22 trout and 28 bass per hour in 14 hours of operation (84% of trout and 63% of bass collected, excluding creel). Gill netting was largely ineffective (0.048 trout and 0.043 bass per net hour over 1,100 hours). Seining captured 7.3 bass per hour and was effective for young-of-the-year centrarchids. Hook-and-line sampling produced better per-unit-effort results than netting but was impractical given the reservoir's size and extensive shoreline. Vertical gill netting showed promise for determining depth distribution, with capture rates varying significantly by depth and correlating with thermocline position.

**Other Species:**

[[Channel Catfish]], the third-ranking game fish and only species present pre-impoundment, showed evidence of successful reproduction with nine individuals between 100-180 mm collected (none under 184 mm observed in 1964). [[Black Crappie]] from 1964 plants were occasionally taken in Wahweap Bay, with one individual reaching 8.4 inches by April 1965. Two large [[Walleye]] were taken in spring, representing recruitment from upstream sources. [[Carp]] remained the most numerous non-game fish, abundant in shallow bays and observed in large surface schools near river confluences. [[Green Sunfish]] were common in all shallow environments, with apparent year-classes at 85 mm (1964), 120 mm (1963), and 160 mm (1962) total length, showing condition factors (K=3.82) favorable compared to Minnesota and Iowa populations.
