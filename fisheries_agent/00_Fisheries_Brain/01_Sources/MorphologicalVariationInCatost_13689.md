# Morphological Variation in Catostomid Fishes of the Grand Canyon Region, Middle Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #systematics #morphometrics

## Executive Summary

This 1980 report by W. L. Minckley examines morphological variation in suckers of the genus [[Catostomus]] from the middle [[Colorado River]] region, including [[Grand Canyon]], [[Glen Canyon]], [[Virgin River]], and [[Little Colorado River]] basins. The study reveals that populations previously identified as [[Catostomus latipinnis]] (Flannelmouth Sucker) exhibit remarkable morphological diversity that challenges the traditional concept of this species as uniform. Using morphometric analysis of body proportions, fin dimensions, scale counts, and lip morphology across multiple populations, the author documents significant geographic variation and proposes that a complex of forms exists in the region rather than a single widespread species. The findings suggest the presence of either undescribed species, environmentally induced morphs, or hybridization following anthropogenic habitat modifications from dam construction.

## Background & Description

[[Catostomus latipinnis]] was originally described by Baird and Girard (1854) from the San Pedro River, southern Arizona, characterized by a streamlined body with thin caudal peduncle, short head with bulbous snout, small mouth with fleshy lips, expansive fins (especially dorsal and caudal), and fine scales. The species was thought to occupy much of the [[Colorado River Basin]] with relatively stable nomenclature due to its distinctive features.

However, Minckley detected far greater variation than anticipated during examination of large specimen series. Populations from the [[Little Colorado River]] basin exhibited thick bodies, small fins, and small lips, referred to as "Catostomus sp." A similar form was recognized in the [[Virgin River]] basin, initially presumed to be an introduced species. Large adults from mainstream [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]] also proved heavy-bodied with small fins and tiny mouths, quite unlike typical C. latipinnis.

The study examined specimens from multiple localities: Virgin River basin (mainstream, Santa Clara River, Meadow Valley Wash), Little Colorado River basin (East Clear Creek, Chevalon Creek, Silver Creek), Grand and Glen canyons (Kanab Creek mouth, Shinumo Creek mouth, Paria River mouth), and upper Colorado River (San Juan River, La Plata River, Strawberry River). All specimens are housed in the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Catostomid fishes of the American Southwest exhibit remarkable intra- and interpopulation morphological variation attributed to multiple factors: geographic isolation in periodically disrupted drainage basins, responses to local environmental conditions (elongated "swift-water forms" in torrential canyon-bound rivers versus thick-bodied forms in quieter tributary waters), considerable ontogenetic change with increased age and size, and pronounced sexual dimorphism.

The [[Grand Canyon]] region represents a biogeographically distinctive area with high endemism. The genus [[Lepidomeda]] (spinedace) is endemic to the middle Colorado River with distinctive species/subspecies in each major tributary. A complex of [[Pantosteus]] (mountain suckers) also exists, including nominal P. intermedius in Pluvial White River and undescribed forms in the Virgin River.

Environmental modifications from dam construction profoundly altered the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. Closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in the early 1960s changed discharge patterns to nearly tidal variation, vastly reduced and stabilized temperatures, altered chemistry, and curtailed sediment transport resulting in consistently clear water. These conditions now resemble headwater streams of the [[Little Colorado River]] basin (clear, cold, relatively stable waters above 1,500 m elevation) far more than historic mainstream conditions.

## Key Findings

**Allometric Changes with Growth:**
Eight morphological features showed pronounced allometric changes with size. Larger fish had relatively smaller heads (7.0-9.9% reduction), orbits (41.0-48.1% reduction), depressed dorsal fin lengths (3.5-13.6% reduction), and lip lengths (7.0-34.3% reduction). Predorsal length was proportionately greater in smaller fish (2.3-7.7%) except in tributaries above Grand Canyon where it remained isometric. Caudal peduncle length showed population-specific patterns: relatively greater in large Virgin River fish (-6.5%), shorter with increased length in Little Colorado River fish (16.1%), and isometric in other samples.

**Sexual Dimorphism:**
Males generally have larger fins than females, demonstrated by depressed dorsal fin length and dorsal fin base measurements, but differences were not significant enough to influence taxonomic decisions. Other features were generally similar between sexes.

**Body Shape Variation:**
- Predorsal length: least in mainstream Virgin River, greatest in populations above Grand Canyon and East Clear Creek
- Postdorsal length: least in Santa Clara River and Little Colorado tributaries (East Clear, Chevalon creeks)
- Caudal peduncle depth: least in Virgin River basin, greatest in Little Colorado River basin
- Caudal peduncle length: shortest in Chevalon Creek (spectacularly so), longest in Virgin River; other samples variably intermediate

**Head Morphology:**
Head length varied little among samples (ca. 23-26% of standard length) with no apparent geographic patterns. Differences were cranial rather than opercular. Virgin River fish had greater snout length accompanied by larger orbit and shorter postorbit. Fish from Grand Canyon and lowermost Glen Canyon had narrower heads and isthmus than others.

**Lip Morphology:**
Virgin River basin and populations above Grand Canyon had by far the longest lip measurements and widest mouths. Fish from Santa Clara River, Silver and Chevalon creeks (Little Colorado basin), and Grand/Glen canyons had much shorter lips and narrower mouths. Numbers of papillae crossing imaginary midlines on upper lip and left lobe of lower lip did not differ significantly among populations (means 9.4-10.6 for upper lip).

**Fin Dimensions:**
Pectoral and pelvic fin lengths were least in Santa Clara River, uppermost Little Colorado River (Silver Creek), and Grand/Glen canyons populations. This pattern held for vertical fins. Only Virgin River populations were distinctive in fin base measurements (index of fin expansiveness). Dorsal fin-ray counts: Virgin River populations extreme with means near 13 rays (range 12-15), exceeding literature limits for C. latipinnis (11-13). All other populations fell within traditional limits.

**Scale Counts:**
Scales in lateral line lowest in Virgin River basin (mainstream 97.7 ± 11.6, range 90-111), intermediate in Little Colorado River and Grand/Glen canyons (102.7-113.4), highest in upper Colorado River basin streams (106.7-112.0). Virgin River's lower counts contrast sharply with upstream populations.

**Qualitative Observations:**
- **Coloration:** Little Colorado and Santa Clara river fish sharply bicolored (dark above, silvery-white below). Mainstream Virgin River and juveniles from Grand/Glen canyons and upper Colorado nearly unicolored (sandy-brown or yellowish, slightly lighter below). Grand Canyon adults darker above and lighter below but not as intensely bicolored as Little Colorado/Santa Clara forms.
- **Fin shape:** Only mainstream Virgin River fish had expanded, falcate dorsal fins and visibly enlarged caudal fins with pronounced procurrent rays. Other populations had fins tending toward emarginate or straight distal margins.
- **Skin texture:** Grand/Glen canyon and upper Colorado River specimens leathery in external appearance with deeply embedded scales on nape and dorsum. Skin on head and leading fin margins appeared thickened, possibly adaptive response to abrasive canyon-bound river habitats.

**Population-Specific Patterns:**
Only mainstream Virgin River population closely fits the conceptualized C. latipinnis as represented in recent literature. Other populations present a mosaic of characteristics, each distinctive. Santa Clara River fish resemble Little Colorado basin populations in head characteristics, lip characters, and fin sizes, but share other features with mainstream Virgin River and Meadow Valley Wash (caudal peduncle dimensions, lateral line scale counts). Variation among Little Colorado River tributaries is great, masking distinctiveness and casting doubt on earlier proposals of an undescribed species. Grand/Glen canyon specimens unique in head measurements, small fins, and relatively high lateral-line counts, tending toward intermediacy between Little Colorado and upstream populations (though influenced by large specimen size).

**Hypothesized Explanations:**
The author proposes a combination of factors: (1) presence of a "middle Colorado River" catostomid fauna including undescribed forms, and (2) presence of a "Catostomus latipinnis superspecies" consisting of environmentally induced and maintained morphs. The hypothesis suggests C. latipinnis evolved in swifter waters developing distinctive features for that environment. The Virgin River represents a special biogeographic area with high endemism where the population may have differentiated to special conditions. The Little Colorado River form is considered distinct from C. latipinnis sensu stricto, and its presence may have affected mainstream populations after Lake Powell closure in early 1960s. Little Colorado fish, pre-adapted to clear, cold, stable headwater conditions, could have invaded the modified mainstream. Present mainstream variation and intermediate characteristics may result from hybridization and introgression in the modified Grand Canyon reach, promoted by tributary spawning behavior.

**Recommendations:**
The author recommends: (1) additional studies utilizing pre-impoundment specimens from pertinent museums, (2) examination of type material of C. latipinnis, (3) examination of additional specimens from Grand Canyon and environs to detail variations. Until investigations are complete, suckers of genus Catostomus within Grand Canyon should be designated as members of the "Catostomus latipinnis complex" to underline apparent differences from the conceptualized taxon.

**Precedence for Middle Colorado River Fauna:**
The minnow genus [[Lepidomeda]] is endemic to the region with distinctive species/subspecies in each major tributary: L. vittata in Little Colorado, L. mollispinis (two subspecies) in Virgin River, L. albivallis and L. albivelis in Pluvial White River. Thermal waters support [[Crenichthys baileyi]] (springfish) and [[Moapa coriacea]] (Moapa dace). [[Gila robusta]] represented by nominal subspecies in different drainages. A [[Pantosteus]] complex exists including nominal P. intermedius in Pluvial White River and undescribed Virgin River forms. P. discobolus exists as two morphs in mainstream Colorado River (one stubby and thick, the other elongated with pencil-thin caudal peduncle).
