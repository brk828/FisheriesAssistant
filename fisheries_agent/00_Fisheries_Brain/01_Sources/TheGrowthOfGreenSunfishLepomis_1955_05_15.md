# The Growth of Green Sunfish (Lepomis cyanellus) in Little Wall Lake, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #freshwater-ecology #Lepomis-cyanellus

## Executive Summary

This 1955 study by George Sprugel, Jr. examines the growth dynamics of [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) in Little Wall Lake, Iowa, based on 467 specimens collected between June 1948 and April 1950. The research validates scale-based age determination for this species, establishes body-scale and length-weight relationships, and documents growth acceleration in survivors following two significant winterkill events (1949 and 1950). Notably, the dominant 1946 year-class exhibited suppressed early growth due to population density, followed by markedly accelerated growth after winterkill reduced competition. The study suggests slower-growing individuals may have had a survival advantage during winterkill conditions.

## Background & Description

**Species**: Green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus* Rafinesque), family [[Centrarchidae]], typically regarded as a "nuisance fish" in the Midwest due to its usual failure to reach catchable size. However, in spring 1948, Little Wall Lake produced unusually large specimens (7–9 inches), prompting this investigation.

**Study site**: [[Little Wall Lake]] is a shallow (~230 acres), eutrophic prairie lake in Hamilton County, Iowa, with a maximum depth of only ~6 feet. The lake had been completely dry from 1936–1941 and was restocked in 1945 as part of a largemouth bass–bluegill management program. By 1948, dense aquatic vegetation severely restricted fishing access.

**Co-occurring fish community**:
- Northern black bullhead (*Ameiurus m. melas*)
- Northern bluegill (*Lepomis m. macrochirus*)
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- Northern fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*)
- Bluntnose minnow (*Pimephales notatus*)
- Yellow perch (*Perca flavescens*)
- Black crappie (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*)
- Common white sucker (*Catostomus c. commersoni*)

**Methods**: Fish were collected via angling, minnow seine, wire basket trap, experimental gill net, and hoop net — each method exhibiting size-selectivity biases (seines/traps favored smaller fish; angling/gillnets/hoopnets favored larger fish). Standard lengths were measured to the nearest millimeter, weights to the nearest gram. Scales were sampled from a standardized region below the lateral line, mounted dry, and examined at 42× magnification via micro-projector, with each scale read five times for consistency.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Winterkill events**: Little Wall Lake did not exhibit strong thermal stratification, but was prone to severe winter oxygen depletion due to shallow depth, decaying vegetation, and prolonged snow/ice cover:
- **Mid-February 1949**: Dissolved oxygen approached zero for about a week, first in near-shore vegetated areas, then mid-lake. Relatively few bullheads, green sunfish, and fathead minnows survived; other species were eliminated entirely. Reproduction was strong in survivors during the following summer.
- **Early February 1950**: A more severe event — dissolved oxygen was undetectable at all stations for over four weeks. No green sunfish and only a few bullheads/fathead minnows survived.

**Density-dependent growth**: The 1946 year-class was highly dominant but grew more slowly in its first year of life than other year-classes, likely due to increased intraspecific competition. This suppressed growth pattern persisted until the 1949 winterkill reduced population density, after which surviving fish showed markedly accelerated growth — evidence of strong [[Density-Dependent Growth]] effects in fish populations.

**Differential winterkill survival**: Survivors of the 1949 winterkill (sampled July 1949) had a history of slower prior growth compared to fish that died in the March 1949 kill event. Similarly, fish that died in the 1950 winterkill (but survived 1949) had also shown slow early growth followed by an excellent 1949 growth increment. This suggests slow-growing individuals may possess a survival advantage under winterkill/hypoxic stress — a potentially important finding for [[Fish Population Resilience]] under environmental stress.

## Key Findings

### Scale Method Validity
- Larger green sunfish possessed more annuli than smaller individuals, consistent with prior validation by Hubbs and Cooper (1935) for the species.
- The dominant 1946 year-class showed 2 annuli in 1948 and 3 by July 1949, confirming annual annulus formation, estimated to occur in **May**.
- **False annuli** were common and not attributable to a single consistent cause:
  - >50% of 1944 and 1945 year-class specimens showed ≥1 false annulus.
  - Only ≤10.5% of 1946 year-class fish (pre-winterkill) showed false marks in any given year.
  - 75% of 1946 year-class winterkill survivors developed a false mark during the rapid growth period of summer 1949.
  - False marks appeared in both sexes and were not strictly tied to spawning condition, contradicting a purely spawning-check explanation (cf. Hubbs & Cooper 1935 for Michigan populations).

### Body–Scale Relationship
- Linear regression: **L = 4.75 mm + 1.176818 S** (L = standard length mm; S = scale anterior radius × 42)
- Curvilinear (3rd-degree polynomial): **L = 10.281 mm + 0.776459 S + 0.00655259 S² − 0.00002897 S³**
- Goodness-of-fit (sum of squared deviations): Curvilinear = 16.14 vs. Linear = 55.22 — curvilinear fit statistically superior.
- However, computed growth histories differed by <3.5% between the two methods (except for the small-sample 1944 year-class), so the **simpler linear formula was adopted for practical use**.

### Standard-to-Total Length Conversion
- Based on 426 specimens; unweighted average conversion factor = **1.218** (Total Length = 1.218 × Standard Length).
- No consistent sex-related differences detected.
- Factor decreased somewhat with increasing size, suggesting the caudal fin grows more slowly than the body overall.

### Growth Analysis (Calculated Standard Lengths by Year-of-Life, mm)
| Year Class | Yr 1 | Yr 2 | Yr 3 | Yr 4 | Yr 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1946 | 26.4 | 66.3 | 85.0 | 139.2 | – |
| 1945 | 38.0 | 91.4 | 139.0 | 189.0 | – |
| 1944 | 37.5 | 94.2 | 132.6 | 172.0 | 193.5 |
| **Grand average** | **29.1** | **67.2** | **94.3** | **163.2** | **193.5** |

- Growth was relatively slow in the first 1–2 years of life, followed by rapid growth in later years — contrasting with growth patterns reported for green sunfish in other waters.
- Sex-related growth differences (1946 year-class): males consistently slightly outgrew females at each annulus (e.g., Annulus 1: males 26.7 mm vs. females 25.7 mm; Annulus 3: males 74.9 mm vs. females 70.0 mm), consistent with findings by Hubbs & Cooper (1935) in Michigan populations, where dimorphism increased in later years of life.

### Winterkill Growth Comparison (1946 Year-Class)
- March 1949 winterkill victims showed **faster prior growth** than July 1949 survivors.
- Statistical tests (t-values) confirmed significant differences between March and July 1949 samples at all three annuli (t = 1.506–2.908), supporting the hypothesis that slower-growing fish were more resilient to winterkill stress.

### Length-Weight Relationship
- **Log W = -4.76800 + 3.16876 Log L** (W = grams, L = standard length mm)
- Exponent (3.16876) indicates weight increases slightly faster than the cube of length (positive allometric growth).
- **Coefficient of condition (K)** = W × 10⁵ / L³, showed a general trend of increasing plumpness with length (K ranged from ~3.16 at 21-30 mm to ~4.36 at 131-140 mm), with no consistent sex-based differences.

### Reproduction
- Spawning in 1948 occurred from early June through mid-August.
- A single 45 mm, one-year-old male (captured July 7) was found fully mature with ripe milt — indicating very early sexual maturity is possible.
- By the third summer of life: <1% of 212 males and only 4% of 145 females examined were sexually immature; all older fish were mature.
