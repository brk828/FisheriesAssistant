# Morphology and Diet of *Catostomus insignis*, *Pantosteus clarki*, and Their Putative Hybrids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests a common assumption in hybrid fish identification: that the offspring of two parental species are intermediate in both morphology and behavior. The authors compare putative hybrids between the [[Sonora Sucker]] (*[[Catostomus Insignis]]*) and the [[Desert Sucker]] (*[[Pantosteus Clarki]]*) with their parental forms, using morphometric, meristic, and dietary data from two Arizona streams. Hybrids were intermediate in measured morphological characters, though tended toward *C. insignis*. Their diets averaged intermediate but varied widely, and they used a broad food base spanning both parents. The authors conclude that dominance and/or interallelic suppression can produce deviations from the assumed intermediacy of hybrid phenotypes.

## Background & Description

**Hybrid intermediacy premise.** Hybrid identification has long rested on the expectation that F1 individuals are intermediate between parents for characters that distinguish those parents (Hubbs, 1955). The authors note that this expectation may not hold where dominance or interallelic suppression occurs, and that the assumption of additive inheritance for most fish morphological traits may be questionable. Prior work reports that hybrid behavior may be intermediate in some cases and extreme (resembling one parent) in others.

**Study species.**
- *Pantosteus clarki* (desert sucker) is described as one of the most specialized products of *Pantosteus* evolution. It has cartilaginous sheaths on the jaws for scraping diatoms and other materials from solid substrates, a black peritoneum, and an elongate digestive tract, all linked to herbivory.
- *Catostomus insignis* (Sonora sucker) is a generalized carnivore that forages in pools and slower runs on aquatic invertebrates. Its feeding is described as facultative, since it also ingests algae, mud, seeds, and plant material seasonally. It lacks the specialized jaws, darkened peritoneum, and lengthened gut of *P. clarki*.

The two species are broadly sympatric, and their natural intermediates are among the most clear-cut examples of apparent hybridization in North American freshwater fishes. Hybridization is geographically widespread and not tied to any fixed zone.

**Materials and methods.**
- **Specimens:** *C. insignis*, *P. clarki*, and presumed hybrids were collected from [[Bonita Creek]] and [[Aravaipa Creek]], southeastern Arizona, between 1964 and 1982, and are housed in the [[Arizona State University Collection Of Fishes]]. Both are small, gravel-bottomed tributaries of the [[Gila River Basin]].
- **Morphology:** Ten morphometric characters (e.g., standard length, head length, snout length, lower lip width, cartilaginous ridge of lower jaw, mouth width, caudal peduncle depths, and others) and seven meristic characters (e.g., lateral line scale counts, dorsal and pelvic fin ray counts) were measured.
- **Hybrid index (HI):** Calculated after [[Hubbs And Kuronuma]] (1942), using characters that distinguish the parental populations.
- **Multivariate analyses:** [[Canonical Discriminant Function Analysis]] (DFA) used a priori group assignments, and [[Principal Components Analysis]] (sheared PCA) quantified shape differences independent of size. Both were used to assess hybrid identity and backcrossing.
- **Diet:** A point-system estimate of relative stomach content volumes, with empty guts excluded. Invertebrates were identified to family, and parasites and diatoms were recorded separately. Relative volumes were compared using DFA.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stream conditions.** Both streams are small, gravel-bottomed Gila River tributaries. Only these two catostomid species occur in them, which the authors cite as evidence that hybridization occurred between them and not with other catostomids.
- **Reproductive timing.** Hybridization likely occurred by chance because both species reproduce at similar times (winter, spring) and in similar habitats (riffles).
- **Hydrologic variability.** Sampling in Aravaipa Creek spanned roughly 14 years, and Bonita Creek sampling spanned roughly 2 years. Aravaipa sampling in part covered conditions from drought through normal to flood, which is relevant because desert stream flow variation affects habitat diversity and food availability.
- **Food availability.** Diet differences between streams may reflect stream-specific differences in invertebrates, algae, and detritus, as well as temporal sampling.
- **Introgression context.** Blood protein studies suggest introgression in evolutionary time. *C. insignis* was the only species of its genus examined that carried a cathodally migrating hemoglobin element typical of *P. clarki*, which may reflect hybridization as a contributor to genetic variability in its history.

## Key Findings

**Morphology**
- Hybrids showed demonstrable intermediacy in morphological characters that differed between parents, although tendencies were toward *C. insignis*.
- Coefficients of variation were similar between hybrid and parental samples. Together with relatively low variation in canonical variables, this implied a lack of backcrossing.
- DFA scores of hybrids clustered along the first canonical variate (CV-I) with *C. insignis* and *P. clarki* forming distinct groups. Overlapping DFA scores would have indicated ongoing backcrossing, but they were not observed.
- Some characters (e.g., ML, RW, MW, HD, BL, and AP at Aravaipa Creek; ML, LU, MW, HD, and BL at Bonita Creek) trended toward hybrids, which the authors attribute possibly to greater sample size compared with [[Hubbs Et Al 1943]], sampling error, or combinations of characters.
- Five unique *P. clarki*-like specimens from Bonita Creek, with obvious affinity to *P. clarki* in mouth morphology, did not group with either parental or hybrid clusters. The authors are uncertain whether these reflect natural variation, polymorphism, or aberrant forms.
- The HI of the sample was reported as near 0.5, compared with a value of 0.48 from earlier work (Hubbs et al., 1943). The scanned value is partly illegible.

**Diet**
- Hybrid diets averaged intermediate to parental forms, but variation was high.
- At Aravaipa Creek, parental diets were distinct, and hybrid feeding was intermediate in a way that supported the hypothesis of hybrid intermediacy.
- Hybrids used a broad food base, with feeding habits that collectively spanned those of both parents.
- The algal component of hybrid diets resembled that of *P. clarki*. DFA placed average hybrid feeding as intermediate, though its distance from the *C. insignis* average was probably not significant.
- Parental diet differences were less pronounced at Bonita Creek, where both species consumed similar food kinds and quantities.
- Parasites were recorded but not used to infer hybrid status. Cestodes were common, and the reported occurrences varied between streams.

**Interpretation**
- The authors conclude that additive inheritance of morphological and behavioral traits appears influenced by [[Dominance And Interallelic Suppression]]. These processes tend to produce deviations from the commonly assumed intermediacy of hybrid fishes.
- Feeding behaviors appear facultative, and hybrids may exploit food resources in ways that resemble either parent depending on availability and location.
- The authors propose that the absence of intermediacy could extend through the genetic structure of hybrids, affecting behavior as well as morphology.

**Limitations and future directions**
- Because F2 and backcross morphology cannot be predicted, the presumed hybrids are treated as F1 generation.
- Morphological analyses may not be sensitive enough to distinguish parents, F1, and backcrosses. The authors suggest that genetic markers would help resolve this.
- Comparisons between streams are confounded by unequal sampling periods, so differences in diet may partly reflect temporal variation rather than hybrid behavior alone.
