# Avoidance Reactions of Salmonid and Centrarchid Fishes to Low Oxygen Concentrations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Centrarchidae #fish-behavior #dissolved-oxygen #water-pollution

## Executive Summary

This 1960 laboratory study examined the ability of four juvenile freshwater fish species — [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Coho Salmon]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Bluegill]] — to detect and avoid water with reduced dissolved-oxygen (DO) concentrations using a custom "channeled avoidance tank." Across 174 experiments, all four species avoided the lowest tested oxygen concentration (~1.5 mg/L), with avoidance generally decreasing as oxygen concentration increased toward saturation. Juvenile chinook salmon showed the strongest and most consistent avoidance behavior, particularly at high summer temperatures, avoiding concentrations as high as 4.5 mg/L. Coho salmon showed more erratic but broader avoidance, responding even to ~6 mg/L. The centrarchids (largemouth bass and bluegill) showed avoidance mainly at the lowest concentration (1.5 mg/L) and little response above 3 mg/L. Notably, avoidance thresholds were often well above lethal DO concentrations, suggesting avoidance is not purely a function of acute respiratory distress. Temperature strongly modulated the intensity of avoidance behavior, with warmer water producing stronger avoidance responses.

## Background & Description

- **Study Purpose**: Part of a broader cooperative investigation (Oregon State College and U.S. Public Health Service) into effects of reduced dissolved oxygen on fish survival, development, growth, activity, and movement — motivated by concerns over organic pollution (domestic/industrial wastes) depleting oxygen in inland waters.
- **Prior Work**: Builds on and critiques earlier studies by [[Shelford And Allee 1913]] (gradient tank studies) and [[J. R. E. Jones 1952]] (glass tube studies), which showed some avoidance behavior in fish but differed in interpretation regarding whether avoidance is an instinctive response or simply a byproduct of increased random activity ("dyspnoea"-driven struggling).
- **Species and Origins**:
  - Chinook salmon (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) and coho salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*): hatchery-reared, 63–110 mm total length.
  - Largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*) and bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*): wild-caught, mostly 50–90 mm total length.
  - Fish held ≥2 days in 250-gallon wooden tanks supplied with Marys River water prior to testing.
- **Apparatus — "Channeled Avoidance Tank"**:
  - Rectangular wooden tank (112 in. long × 24 in. wide × 12 in. deep), water depth ~4.5 in.
  - Four parallel 36-inch channels (A, B, C, D) at one end; two channels served as DO-reduced experimental channels, two as aerated controls (pairing alternated between trials to control for bias).
  - Oxygen reduced via nitrogen bubbling through 4-ft glass Raschig-ring-packed columns.
  - Flow rates carefully balanced (~1,200 mL/min inflow per channel; ~1,500 mL/min drains) to minimize channel-to-body mixing.
  - Fish confined to a 62-inch section of tank via wire screen; observed through polarized glass window under fluorescent lighting.
  - DO measured via Alsterberg (azide) modification of the Winkler method.
- **Behavioral Metrics ("Avoidance Indices")**:
  1. **First-line crossings**: entries into a channel (immediate directional response).
  2. **Second-line crossings**: crossing a second marker 24 inches inside the channel (delayed penetration).
  3. **Periodic counts**: number of fish present in channels at 60-second intervals (duration/occupancy-based).
  - Formula: `Avoidance Index = 100(M − A)/M`, where M = mean expected entries (all channels/2), A = actual entries into experimental channels. Index ranges from −100 to +100 (100 = complete avoidance).
- Tested DO concentrations: ~1.5, 3.0, 4.5, and 6.0 mg/L, compared against aerated control channels (typically 8–10 mg/L).
- No temperature control was used — testing occurred at ambient river water temperature (range ~6.5°C–25.5°C depending on season).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pollution Relevance**: Dissolved-oxygen depletion in natural waters is frequently caused by organic pollution from domestic/industrial waste, making fish avoidance behavior ecologically and regulatorily significant — early detection/avoidance could protect fish from lethal conditions, but could also displace fish from otherwise suitable habitat.
- **Habitat Associations**:
  - Juvenile salmonids ([[Chinook Salmon]], [[Coho Salmon]]) typically inhabit cool, well-oxygenated streams, consistent with their stronger, more sensitive avoidance responses to moderately reduced oxygen.
  - Centrarchids ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]]) commonly inhabit ponds, lakes, and sluggish streams that naturally experience larger DO fluctuations and higher temperatures, consistent with their reduced sensitivity/avoidance thresholds.
- **Temperature Interaction**: Increased susceptibility to oxygen deficiency at high temperatures is well documented in related studies ([[Davison Et Al 1959]] for coho salmon; [[Burdick Et Al 1954]] for trout and smallmouth bass). This study confirms that avoidance behavior intensifies with temperature, particularly in chinook salmon.
- **Natural vs. Experimental Conditions**: The authors caution that the abrupt DO gradient in the tank (transition over inches) does not mimic most natural gradients (which occur over much larger distances, e.g., miles), though abrupt gradients can occur naturally at pollution outfalls, stream confluences, or in thermally stratified lakes. Abrupt transitions may be more readily detected/repellent than gradual ones, so lab avoidance thresholds should not be assumed to directly predict field avoidance behavior.
- **Confounding Variable — CO₂**: In nature, low DO is often accompanied by elevated CO₂ tension, to which fish are also known to react strongly ([[Powers And Clark 1943]]; [[Collins 1952]]). In this experiment, CO₂ was likely reduced (not elevated) due to nitrogen bubbling, so observed avoidance is attributed specifically to oxygen deficiency rather than CO₂ effects — though the authors note CO₂ variation could modulate O₂ sensitivity in nature.
- **Schooling Behavior**: Chinook salmon exhibited schooling behavior (especially in summer), influencing channel entry patterns — schools tended to enter outer channels (A, D) more than inner channels (B, C), and splitting of schools at channel entrances affected raw avoidance index calculations. No schooling was observed in coho salmon or bluegill; minimal schooling noted in juvenile bass only.

## Key Findings

### Species-Specific Avoidance Patterns

**Chinook Salmon**
- Marked avoidance of DO concentrations near 1.5, 3.0, and 4.5 mg/L in summer (high temperatures); no avoidance of ~6.0 mg/L at any time.
- Summer (June–July 1956) avoidance indices (first line / second line / periodic count):
  - 1.5 mg/L: 61.0 / 90.1 / 89.5
  - 3.0 mg/L: 46.7 / 67.6 / 71.9
  - 4.5 mg/L: 27.6 / 38.4 / 35.3
  - 6.1 mg/L: −1.9 / −4.7 / −1.9 (no avoidance)
- Fall (Oct–Nov 1956) avoidance markedly reduced at all concentrations due to lower temperatures (e.g., 1.5 mg/L: 27.8 / 72.1 / 67.4).
- Reacted with sharp, decisive turns at channel entrances, often without visible distress signs — suggesting rapid sensory detection rather than mere disorientation.
- Strong seasonal/temperature dependence confirmed via paired comparisons (Table 3): higher avoidance indices consistently correlated with higher water temperatures for the same DO concentration.
- Schooling behavior biased raw indices; using outer-channel-only data increased avoidance indices substantially (e.g., 1.5 mg/L: first line 76.0, second line 94.8, periodic count 93.0).

**Coho Salmon**
- Showed avoidance of all tested concentrations, including 6 mg/L (unique among the four species), but behavior was more erratic and less pronounced at low concentrations (≤4.5 mg/L) compared to chinook salmon.
- July 1956 avoidance indices:
  - 1.5 mg/L: 25.2 / 53.2, periodic count ~18.0
  - Avoidance indices declined steadily with increasing DO for first-line crossings, but second-line/periodic-count indices dropped sharply between 1.5–3 mg/L then plateaued up to 6 mg/L.
- Movements were rapid, erratic, "darting" — fish often entered low-oxygen channels then quickly found their way out, unlike the calmer directional reversal of chinook.
- No schooling behavior observed.

**Largemouth Bass**
- Marked avoidance only at 1.5 mg/L (periodic count index: 69.5; first line: 23.6; second line: 48.8) — fish left low-O₂ channels much sooner than control channels even though initial entry rates were similar.
- Avoidance dropped sharply at ≥3 mg/L (indices <12); some limited avoidance detected at ~4.5 mg/L.
- No avoidance detected at 6 mg/L.
- Schooling noted only in two experiments using very young bass.

**Bluegill**
- Avoidance observed at 1.5 and 3 mg/L (weaker at 3 mg/L, detected mainly via periodic counts, not entry frequency).
- No detectable avoidance at 4.5 or 6 mg/L.
- Some individuals exhibited territorial "position defense" behavior potentially influencing group movement patterns.
- No schooling observed.

### Cross-Species Comparisons
- All four species avoided the lowest tested concentration (1.5 mg/L) markedly.
- Only salmonids (especially chinook) showed strong avoidance at concentrations ≥4.5 mg/L.
- Avoidance thresholds were generally **well above known lethal DO thresholds** (~2 mg/L or less lethal within 24 hrs at 20°C for salmonids per [[Katz Pritchard And Warren 1959]] and [[Davison Et Al 1959]]; largemouth bass lethal threshold <1 mg/L per preliminary 1957 Corvallis trials) — indicating avoidance is not simply a response to imminent lethal stress.
- Salmonids > centrarchids in sensitivity to moderately low O₂, consistent with their cool, well-oxygenated stream habitat preferences versus centrarchids' tolerance of warmer, fluctuating-O₂ habitats.

### Behavioral Mechanism Insights
- Large differences between first-line (immediate entry decision) and second-line/periodic-count (delayed/duration) avoidance indices across all species indicate **active directional avoidance** (deliberate reversal of swimming direction), not merely increased random activity or accelerated swimming induced by oxygen deficiency (contra [[J. R. E. Jones 1952]]'s dyspnoea-driven random movement hypothesis for stickleback/minnow/trout).
- Authors argue the prompt, targeted avoidance response (particularly in chinook salmon) suggests some capacity for rapid, near-instantaneous detection of oxygen concentration changes at channel entrances — though learning and social/schooling effects cannot be ruled out.
- No influence of fish size/age on avoidance behavior was detected within the tested size ranges.

### Methodological Notes
- A novel avoidance index formula was devised because the earlier formula used by [[Jones Et Al 1956]] (pulp-mill effluent avoidance study) could yield unbounded negative values; the new formula is bounded between −100 and +100.
- Caution urged in extrapolating lab-tank avoidance thresholds (abrupt DO gradients) to natural systems (gradual gradients over much larger spatial scales), and regarding the confounding role of CO₂ tension in natural low-oxygen waters, which was not replicated in this nitrogen-stripping experimental design.
