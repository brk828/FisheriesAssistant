# Diet Comparison of Gambusia sexradiata and Gambusia puncticulata yucatana (Deep-bodied Form) on Two Dates in the Dry Season

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the diets of two sympatric [[Gambusia]] species, [[Gambusia Sexradiata]] and the deep-bodied form of [[Gambusia Puncticulata Yucatana]], collected from a single [[Reed-Pond Habitat]] in [[Belize]] on two dates one month apart during the [[Dry Season]] (2 February and 2 March 1976). Because both species shared the same homogeneous habitat, the authors hypothesized no diet difference. Diets nonetheless diverged. The deep-bodied form consistently ate fewer, larger prey items than *G. sexradiata*, and the diet overlap coefficient (C-lambda, [[Horn Overlap Index]]) fell from 0.85 to 0.67 between dates. The authors attribute the differences to fine-scale, behavior-mediated feeding responses to microhabitat and prey rather than spatial separation.

## Background & Description

*Gambusia* in Belize comprises small tropical littoral fishes that vary in morphology and occupy environments from lotic to marine habitats (Greenfield et al., 1982). This paper is the third in a series by the authors on resource use of *G. sexradiata* relative to sympatric congeners. The earlier studies were:

- A lotic comparison with the slender [[Gambusia Uma]], in which diet non-overlap was spatially mediated.
- A savannah-pond comparison with the shallow-bodied form of *G. p. yucatana*, in which *G. sexradiata* showed a strong affinity for shoreline vegetation and the shallow-bodied form favored non-vegetated shoreline.

The deep-bodied *G. p. yucatana* is usually found near the sea but occasionally occurs inland in reed-ponds. It is convergent with *G. sexradiata* in body depth and coloration but can be distinguished by pigmentation (Greenfield et al., 1982). Since most resource-partitioning studies emphasize spatial separation (Schoener, 1974), the authors tested an hypothesis of no diet difference between the two species.

The authors also note that foraging in tropical *Gambusia* may be more specialized within broad limits than in the temperate [[Gambusia Affinis]] (Bence, 1983).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling site and conditions.** Fish were collected with a 3 m seine from a reed-pond on the east side of Burell Boom Road, 10.8 km north of Hattieville, Belize District. The first sample was taken 14:00–14:30 hrs on 2 February 1976 and the second 13:30–14:00 hrs on 2 March 1976. Water temperature was 24°C on the first date and 32.5°C on the second. The reed-pond habitat is characterized by heavy cover of rushes and sedges and black organic sediment.

**Seasonal context.** The dry season is a period when declining food resources have often been documented in tropical waters (Lowe-McConnell, 1975; Zaret and Rand, 1971; Schoener, 1982). Food abundance has been positively associated with water levels in the tropics. The authors note that February samples were taken when water levels were "somewhat high," and that diet overlap values from receding-water periods were lower, consistent with ecological theory.

**Prey community.** Prey were identified mostly from genus to order level. The dominant items included [[Cladocera]], [[Chironomid Larvae]], [[Corixidae]] (water boatmen), and the unicellular alga [[Closterium]]. Chironomid pupae, copepods, filamentous algae and protozoans were also present.

## Key Findings

**Sample composition and size control**

- Gambusia sexradiata was more abundant than the deep-bodied form: 141 versus 37 individuals in the March sample.
- Subsamples were length-matched to control for predator size:
  - February: *G. sexradiata* n = 48, SL 24.72 ± 0.56 mm (range 19–32 mm); *G. p. yucatana* (db) n = 49, SL 25.31 ± 0.68 mm (range 17–35 mm).
  - March: *G. sexradiata* n = 48, SL 22.25 ± 0.35 mm (range 17–29 mm); *G. p. yucatana* (db) n = 37, SL 23.08 ± 0.47 mm (range 15–31 mm).
- Sample sizes were judged adequate because species-area curves were leveling off. Only one large deep-bodied fish in February lacked food.

**Feeding intensity and prey size**

- Mean number of food items per stomach declined fourfold for *G. sexradiata*, from 132.2 ± 17.3 to 32 ± 2.3 (P < 0.001).
- Mean number declined twofold for the deep-bodied form, from 47.3 ± 7.6 to 20.8 ± 1.8 (P < 0.005).
- The deep-bodied form consumed significantly fewer items than *G. sexradiata* on both dates (P < 0.001).
- Mean food-item volume rose threefold for *G. sexradiata* (0.97 to 3.09) and twofold for the deep-bodied form (2.73 to 6.49). Units are ×10⁻ⁿ ml; the exponent is garbled in the source scan, and item volumes were not individually measured, so variance estimates are unavailable.
- Mean food volume per individual did not differ between species on the first date (P > 0.2) or between dates for the deep-bodied form (P > 0.5; 1.58 ± 0.09 to 1.61 ± 0.10 ×10⁻² ml).
- Food volume per individual differed between species on the second date (P < 0.02) and between dates for *G. sexradiata* (P < 0.01; 1.66 ± 0.11 to 1.27 ± 0.09 ×10⁻² ml). Because the size decrease was similar in both species, the authors conclude fish size does not explain this difference.

**Diet diversity and niche breadth**

- In February, 38 food categories were found in *G. sexradiata* and 37 in the deep-bodied form (46 total; 17 not shared).
- In March, 33 and 30 categories were found, respectively (39 total; 15 not shared).
- [[Levins Niche Breadth]] values:
  - February: 11.7 (deep-bodied form) and 9.9 (*G. sexradiata*).
  - March: 5.9 (deep-bodied form) and 11.4 (*G. sexradiata*).

**Diet overlap**

- The overlap coefficient (C-lambda) based on prey volume fell from 0.85 to 0.67 between dates. The authors describe this as a substantial reduction driven by decreased overlap across most major food categories.
- Zaret and Rand (1971) treated values near 0.60 as indicating effective ecological segregation.

**Prey-type shifts**

- The 17 most prevalent prey types comprised 87% (February) and 96% (March) of *G. sexradiata* volume, and 82% and 91% of deep-bodied form volume.
- Cladocera, chironomid larvae and corixids together made up 44% and 37% of *G. sexradiata* diets, and 40% and 59% of deep-bodied form diets (February and March).
- *Closterium* was a consistent component of *G. sexradiata* diets (12% in February, 10% in March) and disappeared from the deep-bodied form's diet in March.
- Cladocera use declined in both species to about one-third of February values.
- Corixid use stayed about the same in *G. sexradiata* but doubled in the deep-bodied form in March.
- Chironomid larvae rose by half in *G. sexradiata* and doubled in the deep-bodied form.
- *G. sexradiata* increased use of protozoans, copepods and filamentous algae in March, and gerrids became a notable prey item.

**Delta (relative change in resource use)**

Values of delta among the three main prey types were:

- Cladocera/corixids: 0.83
- Cladocera/chironomid larvae: 0.32
- Corixids/chironomid larvae: 1.46

All differed from zero, showing the two species shifted their diets differently relative to each other.

**Comparison with other Belize populations**

- Cladocera made up 3% of the *G. sexradiata* diet at Burell Boom km 3.2 (allopatric reed-pond) versus 22% in this study. Corixids were 5% versus 10%.
- Previously reported diet overlap values for comparable Belize populations:
  - 0.94: allopatric *G. sexradiata* and shallow-bodied form in bare clay-mud ponds (February).
  - 0.52: reed-pond *G. sexradiata* versus clay-mud shallow-bodied form (February).
  - 0.67: mixed habitat (February).
  - 0.62: river versus side-pond *G. sexradiata* (December).
  - 0.90: lotic [[Gambusia Uma]] versus *G. sexradiata* (April).
  - 0.57: both species in clay-mud pond (May, lowest water).
  - 0.90 to 0.76: lotic *G. uma* versus *G. sexradiata* as water receded.
  - 0.85 to 0.67: the present study.

**Interpretation**

The authors conclude that:

1. Diet differences between these two species occurred despite shared habitat, so they likely result from behavior-mediated responses to microhabitat and prey rather than spatial segregation.
2. *G. sexradiata* generally takes smaller items and feeds on submerged, autochthonous items, while the deep-bodied form takes fewer, larger prey.
3. Foraging differences may reflect the profitability of subhabitats and prey types, following guppy switching behavior (Murdoch et al., 1975).
