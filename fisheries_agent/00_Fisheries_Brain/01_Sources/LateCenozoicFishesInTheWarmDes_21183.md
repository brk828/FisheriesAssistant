# Late Cenozoic Fishes in the Warm Deserts of North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a book chapter, "Late Cenozoic Fishes in the Warm Deserts of North America" by Michael Leonard Smith (University of Michigan), followed by the opening abstract of a companion chapter by Robert Rush Miller on pupfishes of the genus [[Cyprinodon]]. The core argument is that the warm deserts of North America are geologically young as regional environments, even though desert-adapted organisms are often assumed to be ancient. The fish fauna now living in the deserts has persisted through brief desert intervals separated by longer pluvial (wet) episodes. The author therefore interprets desert fishes as generalists that accumulated in the region, not as specialized organisms that evolved there. Coastal marine and estuarine habitats are proposed as the likely source of the physiological tolerances these fishes display.

## Background & Description

**Scope and definition.** Following Goodall (1976), deserts are defined biologically as areas where "biological potentialities are severely limited by lack of water." The chapter covers the [[Mohave Desert]], [[Sonoran Desert]], [[Chihuahuan Desert]] and Hidalgan deserts, plus intervening arid areas. The central question is time: did ancient desert regions match present ones, and when did desert environments originate?

**Fossil record.** The chapter compiles fossil fish records from 29 localities, ranging in age from Miocene to Holocene. The total assemblage includes representatives of 12 families. Most sites yielded limited material, often a single species. Significant concentrations occur in two regions: southern California and central Mexico. Key sites include:
- Ridge Formation (Pliocene, California): cyprinodonts and a stickleback (*Gasterosteus haynesi*).
- [[Bouse Formation]] (Pliocene): the atherinid *Colpichthys regis*, now restricted to the northern Gulf of California.
- [[Lake Bidahochi]] (Pliocene, Arizona): a diverse cyprinid community.
- [[Mesa Central]] (Jalisco, Mexico): the most diverse fossil-fish assemblage in southwestern North America.

**Miller companion abstract.** Pupfishes (*Cyprinodon*) are described as about 30 species of small, oviparous fishes typical of southwestern U.S. and northern Mexican deserts. About 10 species live outside arid regions. The known fossil record is poor, with one species, probably Miocene, described from near Death Valley. Distributional and phylogenetic evidence suggests the genus is not very old, and much of its differentiation probably occurred before the Pleistocene.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tectonic setting.** Late Cenozoic geology of the region is controlled by interactions between the Pacific and American plates, with the [[San Andreas Fault]] as a key boundary. Basin-and-range topography and drainage isolation developed from faulting. The Sierra Madre Occidental ignimbrites were erupted mainly 22 to 30 million years ago, and the [[Gulf of California]] opened largely within the last four million years. The [[Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt]] is linked to a fault system that may continue the San Andreas system. Gastil and Jensky (1973) estimated about 175 km of movement in the late Cretaceous or early Tertiary and an additional 260 km in the Miocene or Pliocene.

**Climate.** Deserts are controlled by subtropical high-pressure cells, rain-shadow effects from mountains (for example, the Chihuahuan and Mohave deserts), and cold offshore currents along northern Baja California. Floras and faunas were repeatedly displaced in response to climate change. The paleobotanical record shows that:
1. Regional desert environments are geologically new, emerging mainly during Pleistocene interglacials and especially after the [[Wisconsinan Glaciation]].
2. Climates have been most rigorous since the beginning of the last glaciation.
3. Segregated biotic communities typical of today are less typical of most of the Cenozoic.

**Pluvial and desert intervals.** Pluvial periods of the Quaternary lasted on the order of 100,000 years, whereas interpluvial desert climates lasted only about 10,000 to 20,000 years, according to oxygen-isotope and pollen-stratigraphic data. Woodland communities occupied much of the present Chihuahuan, Sonoran and Mohave deserts during pluvial climates. The shift from woodland to desert occurred about 8,000 years ago in these deserts. Large [[Pluvial Lakes]] occupied intermontane basins, and [[Packrat Middens]] record mixed desert and woodland communities during the last glaciation.

**Aquatic habitat extremes.** Desert aquatic habitats (small streams, marshes, isolated springs) show high temperatures, intense radiation, high mineralization, limited volume, unusual chemistry and large fluctuations. The author states these habitats most closely resemble marginal marine habitats. Desert aquatic systems are often dominated by cyprinodontoid fishes, whose distribution centers on coastal and estuarine habitats.

**Mixed communities.** Fossil evidence shows desert and woodland species coexisting, for example a bass associated with trouts on the Mesa Central. Van Devender et al. (1977) proposed cooler summers and moderate winters to explain these mixtures. Extreme seasonal temperature differences are considered a Holocene phenomenon.

## Key Findings

- **Desert age:** Expansion of xeric habitats into regional desert environments is geologically new, and the present fauna has persisted through brief desert intervals interrupted by longer pluvial episodes.
- **Fish affinities:** The oldest fossil fishes from the warm-desert region have marine affinities. Cyprinodonts were widespread under low-to-moderate relief until the middle-to-late Tertiary. Primary freshwater fishes are not known from southwestern North American deposits older than middle Pliocene.
- **Ecological origin:** Desert fishes are best interpreted as generalists that accumulated in the region rather than specialized autochthonous organisms. Their physiological tolerances may be inherited from estuarine ancestors.
- **Refugia contrast:** Terrestrial desert organisms had dry-site refugia during wet periods, but aquatic organisms did not. Isolated aquatic habitats were repeatedly inundated by pluvial lakes, and freshwater fish distributions shift slowly relative to terrestrial ones.
- **Extinction pattern:** Limited fossil data suggest higher extinction among larger fishes. Large piscivorous salmonids and a bass disappeared from the Mesa Central in the late Pleistocene, synchronous with reduction of lacustrine habitat. Large cyprinids became extinct in Arizona after the Bidahochi phase.
- **Mesa Central diversity:** The western Mesa Central fauna comprises remains of 22 species in seven families from seven sites, ranging from Miocene to late Pleistocene. The oldest freshwater fossil fish in the present desert region is the Miocene goodeid *Tapatia occidentalis*.
- **Pupfish origins:** Miller's companion abstract concludes that *Cyprinodon* is relatively young and that most of its differentiation probably preceded the Pleistocene, with a very poor fossil record.
- **Research implications:** The author argues that paleontological data are essential for addressing time-dependent questions in desert biology, and that desert fish tolerances should be evaluated against a long-term, pluvial-dominated history.
