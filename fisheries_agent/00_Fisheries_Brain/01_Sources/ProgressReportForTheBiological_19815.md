# 1980 Progress Report: Biological Survey of the George Whittell Wildlife Preserve

#ichthyology #marine-biology #herpetology #ornithology #mammalogy

*Note: this source is a terrestrial biological survey and contains no fish or marine taxa. The tag line above follows the template; the [[Amphibians and Reptiles]], [[Birds]] and [[Mammals]] sections are the substantive content.*

## Executive Summary

This final 1980 progress report, edited by Terry B. Johnson, presents four parts of a biological survey of the [[George Whittell Wildlife Preserve]] (26,104 acres) and the wider [[Aravaipa Creek Watershed]] in Graham and Pinal counties, Arizona. The survey was funded by the Defenders of Wildlife Trust and is the second of three consecutive annual reports, intended to provide baseline data for a management plan. Parts cover vegetation (Warren and Anderson, not reproduced here), amphibians and reptiles (Johnson), birds (Mills) and mammals (Spicer). The herpetofauna survey recorded 8 amphibians and 46 reptiles, plus 14 hypothetical species. The bird survey documented 209 species on the Preserve, and the mammal survey listed 46 species. The report closes with management recommendations focused on water rights, grazing reduction, vehicle restriction and riparian protection. All results are described as preliminary.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Gather baseline information on the flora and fauna of the Aravaipa canyonlands to support land-use decisions, with monitoring every third year from 1984.
- **Setting:** The Preserve lies on the north slopes of the [[Galiuro Mountains]], between the [[Aravaipa Canyon Primitive Area]] and the lowlands of the San Pedro River. Physiographic subdivisions are the East End, West End and Uplands.
- **Study area:** Elevations range from about 2,200 ft in the West End lowlands to 6,254 ft on Little Table Mountain. Annual rainfall is 14–20 inches.
- **Biotic communities:** Six communities comprising 24 vegetation associations: [[Sonoran Desertscrub]], [[Desert Grassland]]/Semi-desert Scrubland, [[Chaparral]], [[Evergreen Woodland]], [[Deciduous Riparian Forest]] and Second Growth.
- **Water:** Aravaipa Creek has perennial flow through the canyon. Stock tanks and springs add permanent water, which is relatively scarce elsewhere in southern Arizona.

### Amphibian and Reptile Survey (Terry B. Johnson)

- **Objectives:** Compile a species list, estimate relative abundance and seasonality, assess habitat preferences and recommend mitigation.
- **Methods:** Seasonally repeated transect censuses (700 m or longer) with variable strip widths (2 m to 5 m), spot sampling of microhabitats, and evening road censuses. A total of 108 field man-days were recorded (the Summary gives 52 man-days for the Preserve itself, so the figures differ by scope). Nomenclature follows Collins et al. (1978, 1979). Ranking categories were Abundant, Very Common, Common, Uncommon and Rare.

### Bird Survey (G. Scott Mills)

- **Objectives:** Build an annotated checklist with densities and seasonal status, plus species accounts and nest records.
- **Methods:** Transects in each major habitat, with densities calculated by a modified [[Variable Strip Method]] (Emlen 1971) and a "pairs" method that doubles pair counts. Numerous spot checks supplemented the transects.

### Mammal Survey (Robert B. Spicer)

- **Objectives:** Annotated list of current and historical mammals, with habitat, relative abundance, life history and management notes.
- **Methods:** Direct observation and reconnaissance, 26 field days in 1979 and 52 in 1980, and permanent live-trap grids in six associations using [[Lincoln-Petersen Index]] population estimates. Literature, museum (University of Arizona) and local-resident records were also compiled.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biogeography:** The area sits where [[Great Plains Fauna]], [[Chihuahuan Fauna]], [[Sonoran Fauna]] and [[Madrean Fauna]] overlap. Author Spicer notes it is at the edge of the known breeding ranges of at least nine small mammals.
- **Water as a driver:** Leopard Frogs and most aquatic and riparian species depend on permanent or semi-permanent water. Stock tanks have benefited the Leopard Frog but may not have affected other amphibians.
- **Land-use pressures identified:**
  - [[Red Brome]] (introduced grass) forms thick carpets that exclude small lizards and may have reduced Chihuahuan Spotted Whiptail numbers.
  - Over-grazing by cattle has removed beargrass, sotol and native grasses, and has eroded soils.
  - Channelization and lowered water tables threaten the Southwestern Woodhouse's Toad.
  - Off-road vehicles destroy habitat and must be restricted to existing roads.
  - A proposed BLM campground at the West End may affect a possible [[Gila Monster]] den.
- **Historical context (mammals):** The report summarizes Sobaipuri and Apache occupation of the San Pedro and Aravaipa valleys, ending with the Apache departure to San Carlos after the Camp Grant Massacre of 1871.

## Key Findings

### Amphibians and Reptiles
- **Confirmed resident species:** 54 (8 amphibians, 3 turtles, 20 lizards, 23 snakes). Hypothetical species: 14 (1 salamander, 3 toads and frogs, 3 lizards, 7 snakes). Summary totals are 8 amphibians and 46 reptiles.
- **Amphibians:** All 8 species are native anurans. The Leopard Frog is the most ubiquitous amphibian. The Southwestern Woodhouse's Toad is considered most at risk of extirpation from the watershed.
- **Turtles:** The [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] is the most frequently encountered; the [[Desert Box Turtle]] and [[Desert Tortoise]] are uncommon.
- **Lizards:** The Tree Lizard is likely the most abundant species, and the Chihuahuan Spotted Whiptail is the most conspicuous. The Arizona Night Lizard was recorded only once (August 1979) and not seen in 1980.
- **Snakes:** The Blackneck Garter Snake was the most commonly observed (42 observations in 1980). Three of the five most frequently seen snakes were rattlesnakes. Six venomous species occur, and the [[Gila Monster]] is the only fully protected species on the Arizona list.
- **New locality records:** Arizona Night Lizard, Pima Leafnose Snake and Tiger Rattlesnake.
- **Federal status:** No federally endangered or threatened amphibians or reptiles are known from the watershed.

### Birds
- **Richness:** 209 species recorded on the Preserve, plus 17 more in the adjacent watershed. Of the Preserve species, 53 are permanent residents, 40 summer residents, 35 winter residents and 81 migrants or visitors. Nesting was recorded for 70 species, with 20 more probably nesting. The report's summary cites an annotated checklist of 231 species.
- **Habitat-specific richness:** Riparian Forest has the greatest summer richness, while [[Desert Grassland]] has the largest area and most varied habitat.
- **Population changes, 1979 to 1980:** Cassin's Sparrow appeared in large numbers in 1980 after being absent in 1979. Blue-gray Gnatcatcher and Bushtit declined sharply in Chaparral. Fall migrants were much less numerous in 1980.
- **Special-status species:**
  - [[Black Hawk]]: listed as threatened in Arizona; the Aravaipa population is the southernmost viable one in the state.
  - [[Zone-tailed Hawk]]: listed as threatened; nests heavily near the Preserve.
  - [[Beardless Flycatcher]]: listed as threatened; one of the northernmost populations in the state.
  - [[Buff-collared Nightjar]]: a range extension and only the third known Arizona location; special study recommended.
  - [[Peregrine Falcon]]: federally endangered; possible return to nesting.
- **Recommendations:** Prohibit off-road vehicle use; reduce and eventually remove cattle; allow floodplain fields to return to native riparian forest; maintain stream flow; and continue monitoring.

### Mammals
- **Richness:** 46 species listed as current or historical inhabitants of the watershed. 41 (including humans) occur there now, and 31 are documented on the Preserve. Beaver, muskrat and gray wolf are historical.
- **Newly recorded on the Preserve:** Bailey's Pocket Mouse, Cactus Mouse and Yellow-nosed Cotton Rat (previously unknown from the watershed).
- **Threatened and Unique Wildlife of Arizona:** Coati, desert bighorn and Mexican gray wolf (the wolf is also federally listed as endangered).
- **Density results:** Seven nocturnal rodent species were sampled across six associations. Total nocturnal rodent density was highest in Lower Desertscrub in late summer 1979 (49.8 animals/ha) and in Riparian Forest East in late summer 1980 (23.2/ha, versus 16.6/ha in spring 1980). Biomass was highest in Riparian Forest East in late summer 1980 (1.78 kg/ha). Densities are estimated to be lowest in Chaparral in late summer 1980 (1.3/ha). Density and biomass were highest in Lower Desertscrub, Semi-desert Scrub with Juniper and Riparian Forest, which the author links to high vegetation diversity.
- **Notable records:**
  - A coati troop of 15–20, including young of the year, was seen in Virgus Canyon in 1979.
  - Ringtail and Raccoon are documented along the creek.
  - A yellow-nosed cotton rat was recorded about 30 miles northeast of its previous northernmost record.
- **Observed population patterns:** Trends are not yet clear; the author suggests a spring dip followed by a fall recovery in some associations.

### Management Recommendations (Whole Report)
1. Secure as many water rights in the Aravaipa watershed as possible, especially bottomland aquifer rights and canyonland rights to the south and east.
2. Monitor cattle grazing closely; the Wood's Corral area shows erosion, compaction and poor salt-lick placement.
3. Monitor all vehicle traffic, especially in the Uplands, and restrict use to existing roads.
4. Prevent grazing in all riparian habitats.
5. Work to secure upland grazing rights, remove cattle for at least 10 years, and reseed native grasses.
6. Work with the BLM to provide a limited West End campground for Primitive Area permittees, sited to minimize wildlife impact, and protect any Gila Monster den found there.
7. Continue the biological survey and prepare a natural history book on the Aravaipa canyonlands.
