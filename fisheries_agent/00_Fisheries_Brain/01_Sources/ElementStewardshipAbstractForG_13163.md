# Gila chub (*Gila intermedia*) Element Stewardship Abstract

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Element Stewardship Abstract, prepared for The Nature Conservancy's Arizona Field Office and compiled by [[Stephen P. Vives]] of Georgia Southern University, summarizes management-relevant information on the [[Gila Chub]], a freshwater cyprinid endemic to the upper [[Gila River Basin]]. The species is a thick-bodied minnow of springs, small streams, and [[Cienega]] marshes. It is considered a separate species from [[Roundtail Chub]] by most recent authors, is state-listed as threatened in Arizona, and has been extirpated from New Mexico. Populations have declined because of habitat loss and predation or competition from [[Non-Native Fishes]]. The abstract concludes that recovery potential is good only if critical habitat is vigorously protected, and it lists monitoring, research, and management priorities.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** Fishes of the genus *Gila* in the Colorado River basin range from the streamlined *G. elegans* of large rivers, through *G. robusta* of intermediate rivers, to the thick-bodied *G. intermedia* of creeks and cienegas. Although the American Fisheries Society (Robins et al. 1980) listed it as a subspecies of *G. robusta*, Rinne (1976) and DeMarais (1986) provided evidence for full species status, which most authors now accept (Douglas et al. 1989; Sublette et al. 1990). DeMarais's support rests on morphological differences and on contiguous populations that maintain independence, although intermediate phenotypes of unknown origin complicate identification.

**Morphology.** The Gila chub is a robust, darkly colored minnow, typically about 150 mm long. Key diagnostic traits include:
- 8 dorsal, anal, and pelvic fin rays
- Fewer than 80 and more than 61 lateral-line scales; large, thick, broadly imbricate scales with basal radii usually present
- 38–45 vertebrae
- No barbels; pharyngeal teeth in two rows (2,5–4,2, with variation)
- Head length divided by caudal peduncle depth of 3.0 or less

Both sexes develop breeding tubercles, less extensively in females. Breeding coloration is intense: orange-red along the lower sides and caudal peduncle, yellow eyes in males, and a blue-black dorsal surface.

**Similar species.** The [[Roundtail Chub]] is lighter, less robust, has smaller scales with weak basal radii, nine fin rays, 81 or more lateral-line scales, 43–49 vertebrae, and a head/peduncle ratio of 3.3–4.3. [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Sonora Chub]], and *G. elegans* differ in scale radii, mouth position, and basicaudal spotting. *G. elegans* has been extirpated from areas the Gila chub occupies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The species is endemic to the upper Gila River basin, occurring in springs and small streams in Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico. Arizona drainages include the Santa Cruz, Upper and Lower San Pedro, Gila, Verde, Salt, and Agua Fria systems. New Mexico records include Apache Creek, Duck Creek, and San Simon Cienega; the species has since been extirpated from the state. Today it is found in fewer than 15 streams in central and southern Arizona and is abundant at no more than 10 locations. Young fish (25–75 mm TL) use swifter areas, while adults use undercut banks and vegetated margins. The species occupies both riffles and pools.

**Associated fauna.** Native companions include [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], Sonora sucker, and desert sucker. Several species historically present, including [[Woundfin]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Gila Topminnow]], are now extirpated from the Gila basin. Gila chub and roundtail chub can occur within tens of meters of each other, but they have never been collected together at the same site.

**Biology and ecology.**
- Most individuals mature in their second or third year.
- Spawning in Monkey Spring occurred from late winter into early autumn, possibly over aquatic plant beds.
- Redfield Canyon fish (N = 113) measured 45–222 mm TL. Scale-based mean lengths were 90, 135, 160, and 183 mm at ages 1–4; males are typically smaller than females.
- Larger chub feed in the evening and early morning, whereas juveniles feed throughout daylight. Diet consists mainly of terrestrial insects and filamentous and diatomaceous algae. Of 27 Redfield Canyon fish examined, four contained fish remains, three of them [[Speckled Dace]].

**Threats and drivers.** Watershed changes (impoundment, channel downcutting, sedimentation, diversion, and groundwater pumping) and the spread of non-native predators and competitors, such as [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]], have acted together, so no single cause can be isolated. Destruction of [[Cienega]] habitats has also reduced populations. Largemouth bass decimated the Monkey Spring population, and green sunfish abundance correlated with chub declines in the San Carlos River. Warmer water raises mortality risk; heat deaths of roundtail chub at 30.5% were recorded in Salt River pools.

## Key Findings

**Conservation status**
- Federal Notice of Review species (September 18, 1985), considered for possible listing.
- Arizona: threatened. New Mexico: Group I endangered (1975), now considered extirpated.
- Declines documented by Minckley (1973), comparing current data with 1920s–1950s field notes of Hubbs and Miller.

**Element occurrence quality.** A quality occurrence must show all year classes present for at least 3 years, demonstrating generational overlap. Over a shorter term, quality depends on (1) healthy fish of all age classes with evidence of reproduction, (2) habitat quality (bank stability, riparian vegetation, substrate, flow, and non-native fish abundance), and (3) freedom from threats such as water control structures, grazing, and logging.

**Protection status.** Protection is minimal except on lands held by The Nature Conservancy, the U.S. Forest Service, and the Bureau of Land Management. The most secure populations are at Redfield Canyon, Canelo Cienega, and Muleshoe Ranch. Recovery potential is good only if critical habitat is protected. Reestablishment into the former range is problematic until causes of decline are corrected.

**Monitoring needs**
- Identify all existing populations and assess potential sites in Sonora, Mexico, and on San Carlos and White Mountain Apache lands.
- Monitor known populations biannually (spring breeding season and late autumn recruitment), using standardized techniques.
- Sample entire headspring–cienega–stream systems every 2–3 years rather than small areas annually.
- Electrofishing is recommended because chub flee and hide under structures, which makes seining difficult.
- Resurvey Mule Creek, New Mexico, and Turkey Creek, New Mexico.

**Research needs.** Gaps include habitat requirements at all life stages, natural abundance variation, dietary preferences, interactions with non-native fishes, effects of habitat modification, movement patterns, and taxonomic relationships. Molecular genetic studies by DeMarais and Dowling aim to clarify origin, closest relatives, and hybridization.

**Management priorities**
- Protect existing populations and remaining cienega and headwater habitat.
- Maintain perennial flows with natural variation in flow regime.
- Stop transport and stocking of non-native fishes, including downstream to the first absolute barrier.
- Build barriers where they do not harm chub life stages, and close potential immigration routes.
- Reintroduce chub to selected historic streams using local stocks and AFS guidelines.
- Secure threatened populations in a hatchery such as the Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico.

**Riparian and fire management.** Riparian health is essential. Only Aravaipa Creek, where cattle have been excluded since 1973, showed successful reproduction and dominance of broadleaf riparian vegetation (Rucks 1984). Cattle browsing favors scrub over broadleaf communities, changing shade, energy flow, and temperature. If fire is needed, controlled burns during non-spawning winter periods are preferred, since crown fires can fill riffles with sediment.

**Current programs.** No programs are underway specifically for this species. The Bureau of Land Management ranks riparian habitat as its top priority and plans to hire a fisheries biologist in 1990 for Arizona public lands. The Safford district has proposed Bonita Creek, Turkey Creek, and Swamp Springs–Hot Springs (Muleshoe Ranch) as areas of critical environmental concern.

**Summary.** Existing populations must be identified, monitored, and protected from detrimental water and land use and from non-native fishes. An ecological approach to habitat management is needed for all native fishes, and degraded habitat should be reclaimed before reintroduction where success is likely.
