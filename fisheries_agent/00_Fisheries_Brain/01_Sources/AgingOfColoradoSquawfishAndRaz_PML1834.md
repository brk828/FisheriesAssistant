# Aging of Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker by Otolith Examination

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, transmitted in May 1989 by [[Arizona State University]] (Department of Zoology) to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], evaluates whether polished sagittal [[Otolith]]s can reliably age two imperiled [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes: the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]]. Conventional scale methods are unreliable for these fish because the sub-tropical climate lacks cold winters that form clear annuli. The study found that otoliths examined under [[Phase-Contrast Microscopy]] are usable for age estimation, that hatchery-reared fish were aged precisely, and that investigator-to-investigator agreement was reasonable. Aging becomes difficult in large, old fish, where growth slows markedly. Preparation technique was identified as the main source of error.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Little is known about the age and growth of native Colorado River fishes, many of which are threatened or endangered. Reliable age data are needed for population assessment and recovery planning.
- **Objectives:** (1) extend otolith aging to Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker from the upper Colorado River basin; (2) produce preliminary age estimates for these species.
- **Samples:**
  - Otoliths (whole or sectioned) from wild-caught Colorado Squawfish. The exact number is partly illegible in the scan; per-investigator sample sizes in [[Table 1]] range from 41 to 45.
  - Otoliths from 13 additional known-age, hatchery-reared Colorado Squawfish.
  - Otoliths from 10 wild-caught Razorback Suckers.
- **Preparation:** Wild-caught otoliths were mounted on glass slides in [[Thermoplastic Cement]], then ground and polished. Hatchery otoliths were mounted similarly but examined unpolished. Sections and whole otoliths were examined by transmitted light at 40–400× (typically 40–100×). Each preparation was aged independently by three investigators; disagreements were resolved by re-examination, with final decisions made by the lead author.
- **Collection data:** Capture location and size were withheld to avoid bias, and dates of capture were supplied only for some individuals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate and aging:** The sub-tropical climate with mild winters means that winter growth checks are often weak or absent. This is why conventional aging is difficult in these basins.
- **Annulus formation:** Annuli in hatchery fish were marked by closely spaced [[Circuli]] (slow winter growth) and wider-spaced circuli (faster summer growth). Summer growth ran from March–April to October–November.
- **Growth strategies:**
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]]: slow growth for the first 1–2 (sometimes 3) years, faster growth for about 2–3 years, then slow, irregular growth in older year-classes. Growth patterns were similar among individuals.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]]: rapid growth for the first 3–5 years, reaching roughly half of maximum size, then growth typically below 1 cm per year for the rest of life. Values for the upper and lower basin populations are only partly legible in the scan (about 65 and 70 cm total length).
- **Reservoir-linked recruitment:** Four Razorback Suckers collected in spring 1987 averaged 25.5 years old, placing them in a year-class hatched near the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962. A similar pattern occurs at [[Lake Mohave]], where most Razorback Suckers belong to year-classes at or after the construction and closure of [[Davis Dam]]. Recruitment likely peaked soon after impoundment, when the expanded habitat was not yet dominated by non-native predatory and competitive species.

## Key Findings

### Validation and Hatchery Fish
- All 13 known-age hatchery Colorado Squawfish (year-classes 0, I, II, IV, and V) were aged precisely from unpolished otoliths. Annuli were absent in year-class 0 and present in older fish, and resembled putative wild annuli.
- Razorback Sucker otolith validation had already been established using hatchery fish ([[McCarthy and Minckley 1987]]) and by observing growth in tagged fish over several years ([[Marsh and Minckley]], unpublished).

### Investigator Agreement
- Average age estimates from the three investigators were similar. Individual estimates for some fish, however, differed by 2 or more years.
- Investigator tendencies: one was markedly conservative, another liberal, and the third made independent estimates that sometimes varied widely for the same fish.
- Concordance was highest for younger fish and depended mostly on whether a first-year (inner) annulus was present. Re-sectioning or re-polishing often revealed an inner annulus that had previously gone unnoticed.
- Older fish were aged more reliably, probably because larger otoliths are easier to handle and polish evenly.

### Summary Statistics (Mean ± SE)

| Estimate | Colorado Squawfish (Table 1) | Razorback Sucker (Table 3, n = 10) |
|---|---|---|
| Investigator I | 6.3 ± 0.35 | 25.9 ± 2.9 |
| Investigator II | 7.1 ± 0.35 | 26.6 ± 2.9 |
| Investigator III | 5.8 ± 0.54 | 25.2 ± 2.7 |
| Final (IV) | 7.7 ± 0.51 | 28.7 ± 2.5 |

- The final compilation (IV) produced higher mean ages than the individual investigators. Investigator III's data were reduced by one year, and the edge of the otolith was counted as the last annulus only for fish sacrificed in April–May.
- Razorback Sucker maximum age was estimated at 40–50 years, similar in upper and lower basin populations.

### Sources of Error
- Most variation came from sections that were too thick (requiring re-polishing), incorrectly oriented or embedded in cement (requiring re-setting), or broken (requiring multiple fragments to be viewed).
- Breakage was linked to storage (dryness) and rough handling, such as heat from hurried polishing, and resulted in loss of a few samples.
- Cement bubbles, overheating, and scratches complicated examination.

### Limitations and Recommendations
- Slow growth in large, old fish makes annuli at older ages difficult to distinguish and probably introduces error. [[Scanning Electron Microscopy]] was recommended if accurate ages are required for old fish.
- No otolith-to-body-length relationship had been established for either species, so [[Back-Calculation]] of length at each annulus was considered tenuous, though feasible where annuli are clear.
- One trained technician should prepare and age all otoliths to ensure consistency.
- Validation using hatchery fish appears adequate, but further validation with re-introduced fish is needed as they become available in the wild. Both species are being reintroduced in the lower Colorado basin, with some augmentation upstream.
