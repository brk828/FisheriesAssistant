# Comparison of the Number and Area of Backwaters Associated with the Colorado River in Glen, Marble and Grand Canyons, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft report by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program, counts and measures [[Backwater]] habitats along the regulated [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Backwaters serve as rearing habitat for larval and juvenile native fishes in Marble and Grand canyons. Using aerial videography at steady 227 cms (8,000 cfs) flows, the study found that the number of backwaters changed significantly among 1990, 1992, 1993 and 1994, while mean backwater area did not. The main driver of the change was sediment and water input from the January and February 1993 floods of the [[Little Colorado River]].

## Background & Description

Backwaters are bodies of water with zero to extremely low velocity that are surrounded on at least three sides by land. The study defined two types:

- **Type I:** backwaters associated with [[Eddy]] return-current channels and [[Reattachment Bar]]s. These are the backwaters of prime concern, because they are warmer than the mainstem.
- **Type II:** shoreline cavities or pocket waters associated with bank irregularities or channel roughness.

Type I backwaters form during higher flows, when reattachment bars are built and sediment is deposited during receding flows. They occur in two forms. *Connected* backwaters have an open connection to the mainstem. *Isolated* backwaters are cut off when river stage drops, but they can reconnect when stage rises. Isolated backwaters were included in the analysis because they may still be useful fish habitat.

**Methods.** A video camera was mounted under a helicopter flying about 2,000 feet above the river. Observers narrated locations while the [[Aerial Videography]] covered the full study length. Videotapes were analyzed with a 486-66 microcomputer and the MIPS image-processing package. Only Type I backwaters of at least 100 m² were counted. For each year, a random subsample of 55 backwaters was measured for area. Measured areas were calibrated against [[U.S. Geological Survey]] topographic maps and checked by ground surveying.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fishes.** All four native species, [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Speckled Dace]], were found in backwaters. The [[Razorback Sucker]] was not. Only a few adults have been recorded in Marble and Grand canyons, none in backwaters, and no spawning population is known. Larval and young-of-year razorback suckers use backwaters elsewhere in the Upper Colorado River Basin.

**Thermal and food conditions.** Backwaters are significantly warmer than the mainstem during the daytime from late April to October. Larval and juvenile fishes were more common in these warmer waters. AGFD found that backwaters also hold more zooplankton, benthic invertebrates and detritus than the mainstem along reattachment bars.

**Regional habitat context.** Anderson et al. (1986) reported that backwaters made up only 2.5% of the river habitat studied, while eddies made up 52.4% to about 58%. Eddies are important for adult fish, especially humpback chub.

**Dam operations.** [[Interim Flows]] began August 1, 1991, and were officially implemented November 1, 1991. Minimum releases are 142 cms at night and 227 cms from 7 am to 7 pm, and maximum releases are 566 cms. Before interim operations, standard operations fluctuated flows over a wider range, from 28 or 85 cms (depending on season) up to a regulated high of 892 cms.

**Reaches.** The river is divided into 12 geomorphic reaches (plus Lake Mead). Wide reaches include reaches 4 and 10. Crucial reaches for native fishes are reach 4 (RKM 57.9–99.0) and reach 5 (RKM 99.0–124.5), which bracket the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence, and reach 10 (RKM 257.4–344.0), below the [[Havasu Creek]] confluence.

## Key Findings

**Backwater numbers (227 cms constant flows, backwaters ≥100 m²)**

- Numbers differed significantly among years ([[G-Test]], p<0.025):
  - 1990: 146
  - 1992: 70
  - 1993: 113
  - 1994: 89
- The 1990 count was probably an underestimate. Videography stopped at the start of reach 12 (RKM 378.1), and a poor-quality segment (RKM 350.8–372.5) was missed. Even so, 1990 had the highest count of all years.
- More backwaters occurred in wide reaches than in narrow reaches (G-test, p<0.025), particularly reaches 4 and 10.

**Backwater area (227 cms constant flows)**

- Mean area did not differ significantly among years ([[Kruskal-Wallis Test]], H = 6.269, p = 0.099; n = 55 per year):
  - 1990: 733 m²
  - 1992: 450 m²
  - 1993: 649 m²
  - 1994: 798 m²

**Comparison of 142 cms and 227 cms (1990, RKM 89.3–122.3)**

- Significantly more backwaters appeared at 142 cms (42) than at 227 cms (21) (G-test, p<0.05).
- Mean area was 493 m² (n = 42) at 142 cms and 741 m² (n = 21) at 227 cms. The difference was not significant ([[Mann-Whitney U Test]], U = 371, p = 0.307).

**Drivers of change**

- The decline from 1990 to 1992 is attributed to erosion. Sediment input to the canyon was limited, and a system-wide decrease in sand deposits was reported from 1965 to 1991.
- The increase in 1993 followed the January–February 1993 Little Colorado River floods. These floods brought high sediment loads and raised Colorado River flow to 850 cms below the confluence. New backwaters formed, and existing sandbars were reformed or built up.
- Many new small backwaters (<100 m²) appeared in 1993, mostly in the lower reaches. These were excluded from the analysis.
- The decline from 1993 to 1994 is attributed to loss of sediment from sandbars. Sediment deposited by the 1993 floods was fine and easily eroded, and no high flows occurred afterward to rebuild sandbars.
- Some reattachment bars were barely inundated in 1993 at 227 cms, suggesting sediment loss, higher tributary inflows, or both.

**Weiss (1993) review data**

- Weiss (1993) found more backwaters at lower flows than at higher flows:
  - 1985: 568 backwaters at lower flow levels, versus fewer at high flows (the high-flow figure is unclear in the scanned text).
  - 1991: 114 backwaters at 142 cms and 46 at 425 cms.
- High research flows in 1983–1986 and 1991 created backwaters that appeared at lower flows afterward. Counts at lower flows may therefore be inflated relative to high-flow counts.
- Mean area in 1991 was only slightly different between 142 cms and 425 cms. The mean at 425 cms was 737 m².

**Humpback chub and spawning**

- Backwaters in reach 5 are very important to the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], which spawns in the Little Colorado River. Chub were found mostly in reach 5.
- Adult humpback chub likely spawned in 1993 near RKM 49.4 (reach 4) or in reaches 9–10. Larvae were found near RKM 70.8 and 328.2.
- In April 1994, AGFD caught three chubs (29, 41 and 43 mm total length) near RKM 70.8 and 94.4. They were probably from the 1993 cohort, but recruitment into the adult population is not documented.
- On July 14, 1994, larval chub (mean 24 mm) were documented at a spring source at RKM 49.4, indicating spawning in the mainstem during 1994.
- The highest catch rates of bluehead sucker, flannelmouth sucker and speckled dace occurred in reach 10, followed by reach 5.

**Recommendations**

- Controlled flood flows of about 850 cms for one to two weeks in spring, in years when stored water allows, to aggrade sandbars and maintain backwaters. Floods should be timed to the chub spawning period.
- Low, steady flows of about 142 cms during spawning periods. Flows should be steady between 142 and 227 cms. Low steady flows may also improve conditions for mainstem rearing of adults and eggs.
- Monitoring to continue, to assess how changes in flow affect backwater habitat and fish survival.
