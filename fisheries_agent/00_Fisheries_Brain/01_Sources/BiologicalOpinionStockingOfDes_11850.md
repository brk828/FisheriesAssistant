# Biological Opinion: Stocking of Desert Pupfish into Cold Spring Seep and Big Spring, Graham County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae #Poeciliidae #endangered-species #conservation-management

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biological opinion, issued December 1, 1989, evaluates a Bureau of Land Management proposal to stock endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*) into two spring systems in Graham County, Arizona that already support populations of endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*). The consultation addresses the proposed stocking of 100-200 desert pupfish from captive populations at Flowing Wells High School and Dexter National Fish Hatchery into [[Cold Spring Seep]] and [[Big Spring]], along with existing and foreseeable land management activities including livestock grazing, recreation, vegetation management, and habitat maintenance. The opinion concludes that the proposed action is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of either species and is expected to benefit desert pupfish conservation by establishing additional wild populations.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] was listed as endangered on March 11, 1967, without critical habitat designation. This small livebearing fish was historically among the commonest fishes in the [[Gila River]] drainage of Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico. Habitat destruction through water diversion, stream downcutting, channelization, and competition with nonnative species, particularly [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), resulted in extirpation throughout most of its range. At the time of this opinion, only nine naturally occurring localities remained in the United States. Populations at Cold Spring Seep and Big Spring were introduced in 1985 as part of recovery efforts and were thriving under full Endangered Species Act protection.

The [[Desert Pupfish]] was listed as endangered on March 31, 1986, with critical habitat designated at Quitobaquito Spring in Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona, and three California locations. Historically common throughout the lower Gila River system, lower Colorado River system, and Rio Sonoyta system in Arizona, California, and Mexico, the species declined due to factors similar to those affecting the Gila topminnow. Natural populations persisted only in three California and Arizona localities and several Mexican locations.

[[Cold Spring Seep]] is located on the northern rim of the Gila River floodplain near Fort Thomas, Arizona (T.5S., R.24E., NE 1/4 of the NE 1/4 Sec. 17). The site consists of hillside seepage with estimated flow of 0.5 gallons per minute, impounded in two artificial earthen-bermed ponds. The northernmost pond (pond 1) measures approximately 20 feet in diameter, varying from 1-2 feet deep at edges to 4 feet deep in the center. The southern pond (pond 2) is approximately 12 feet in diameter with 2-3 feet depth. Both ponds contain clear water with considerable submergent vegetation and support Gila topminnow populations. The ponds overflow into a grassy meadow area. A separate spring system to the northwest contains [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus* sp.) and mosquitofish.

[[Big Spring]] is located in a wash on the north rim of the Gila River floodplain near Pima, Arizona (T.6S., R.25E., NE 1/4 of the SE 1/4 Sec. 5), approximately 8 miles southeast of Cold Spring Seep. Flow is estimated at 2.5 gallons per minute with moderately saline water. The spring system extends approximately 200 feet within a vertical-walled gully 5-10 feet deep, emerging from under a 5-6 foot tall caliche ledge. A springbox pipes water to a downstream trough. The system includes a headspring pool (10x15 feet, 1-2 feet deep), approximately 150 feet of stream channel, and a pool behind a concrete dam (15 feet long, 5-10 feet wide, several feet deep). The site is within a 2.5-acre livestock exclosure with extensive riparian and emergent vegetation including cattails. Topminnow are abundant throughout the system.

The proposed stocking would involve 100-200 desert pupfish from Flowing Wells High School (Santa Clara Slough genetic stock) supplemented with stock from Dexter National Fish Hatchery (same genetic lineage). Fish would first be stocked into Cold Spring Seep in fall 1989 or spring 1990, with later transfer from Cold Spring Seep to Big Spring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Both species historically occurred sympatrically throughout much of the Gila River system. The [[Gila Topminnow]] occupies the upper water column while [[Desert Pupfish]] utilize mid-column and bottom habitats, reducing direct competition. Both species are omnivorous with wide food utilization. The vertical habitat partitioning and dietary flexibility suggest compatibility in shared habitats.

Existing land uses at both sites include cattle grazing managed under Bureau of Land Management Allotment Management Plans. At Cold Spring Seep, ephemeral grazing occurs only when rains produce sufficient annual plant growth, typically mid to late summer. Cattle access the seep directly for watering. BLM holds a water right of 0.40 acre-feet/year for wildlife and fisheries use. At Big Spring, a 2.5-acre exclosure excludes livestock except during drought when insufficient water fills the outside trough. The grazing permittee holds a 1917 State water right to 1000 miner inches. BLM filed in 1982 for a water right of 0.862 acre-feet/year for livestock, wildlife, and fisheries use.

Recreational use at both sites is very light, including incidental non-consumptive activities and hunting. No exotic game fish are present and the sites are unsuitable for game fish stocking. Road access to Cold Spring Seep is located approximately 100 yards uphill, separated by fencing. At Big Spring, a dirt track ends at a corral on the west side of the wash, with a bladed "ranch road" crossing the wash approximately 15 feet upstream from the spring source.

Wildlife and native vegetation management under Habitat Management Plans includes native plant planting (willow and other species), pond/pool maintenance for native fish, water quality monitoring, and at Big Spring, riparian vegetation restoration ongoing since 1980. Exotic [[Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*) are present at Cold Spring Seep. Two candidate snail species, *Apachecoccus arizonae* and *Tryonia gilae*, occur at Cold Spring Seep and likely at Big Spring.

## Key Findings

**Biological Opinion Conclusion**: The proposed stocking of desert pupfish into Cold Spring Seep and Big Spring, along with existing and foreseeable land management activities, is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of either endangered Gila topminnow or endangered desert pupfish. The overall effect is expected to be beneficial to desert pupfish survival by establishing additional self-sustaining wild populations.

**Effects of Stocking on Desert Pupfish**: The Dexter National Fish Hatchery population is sufficiently large to provide needed stock without adverse effects. Removal from Flowing Wells High School is not expected to substantially affect that population, though adverse effects through genetic variation depletion could occur if only a small number remain, particularly during periods of low reproduction. Stocking into Cold Spring Seep is expected to benefit the species by establishing an additional wild population. Direct adverse effects to the Cold Spring Seep population could occur if stock is removed for Big Spring or other sites before the population becomes sufficiently established.

**Effects on Gila Topminnow**: No adverse effects to Gila topminnow populations are expected from desert pupfish stocking. Historical records document sympatric occurrence of both species. Habitat partitioning (topminnow in upper water column, pupfish in mid-column and bottom) and omnivorous diets with wide food utilization reduce competition. Production at both sites should support large numbers of both species.

**Livestock Grazing Effects**: Current ephemeral grazing at Cold Spring Seep appears to have minimal impact on Gila topminnow, with similar minimal impacts expected for desert pupfish. The Big Spring system is not grazed except under drought conditions, resulting in good aquatic habitat condition. Grazing under existing Allotment Management Plans appears compatible with survival of both species. However, springbox and pipeline maintenance at Big Spring may cause adverse effects through soil disturbance, vegetation removal, turbidity, and potential pollutant introduction. High turbidity may depress dissolved oxygen levels and cause respiratory inhibition. Livestock gathering may adversely affect both species if large stock concentrations occur near springs or wetted areas, causing bank trampling, vegetation damage, and increased nutrient loading.

**Vehicular Access Effects**: Existing roads and maintenance at current levels are not expected to adversely affect either species. However, the bladed track crossing the wash upstream from Big Spring adversely affects both species through substantial turbidity and sedimentation. Excessive sediment causes ongoing problems by filling pool habitat and directly reducing available fish habitat.

**Habitat Management Effects**: Wildlife enhancement projects are expected to have long-term beneficial effects, though short-term adverse effects must be minimized. Exotic vegetation removal and native vegetation planting should benefit both species through ecosystem restoration, but potential adverse effects include excessive soil disturbance, turbidity, sediment introduction, loss of shading (increasing invasive emergent plant growth), and introduction of species that may invade watered areas and clog flows.

Maintenance, repair, and replacement of earthen berms and concrete dams, along with sediment dredging and vegetation control, are expected to have overall beneficial effects by maintaining vital habitat. However, potential adverse effects include increased turbidity, pollutant introduction (oils, concrete, wet cement), loss of ponded water, lowered productivity from bottom sediment removal, loss of cover from vegetation removal, and direct mechanical fish mortality. High turbidity may depress dissolved oxygen and cause respiratory inhibition. Sediment dredging may substantially depress food availability, as the top substrate layer is an important productivity source. Vegetation removal may cause short-term adverse effects including increased turbidity, lowered food availability, and reduced cover. Aquatic plants are important for introduced topminnow population success and provide important pupfish habitat. Both species use vegetation as cover from predators including bullfrogs and giant water bugs (Belostomatidae). Partial vegetative cover loss may increase short-term predation losses.

Construction of additional ponded water at Cold Spring Seep is expected to provide additional habitat benefiting both species. Sediment control structures upstream from Big Spring should have overall beneficial effects, though short-term increases in sedimentation, turbidity, and pollutant introduction during construction are possible. Exotic animal removal (e.g., bullfrogs at Cold Spring Seep) will benefit both species long-term, though adverse effects depend on removal methods employed.

**Incidental Take Statement**: Because reliable population estimates are not obtainable due to sampling difficulties and rapid population changes in short-lived, high-fecundity species, incidental take cannot be quantified. Population parameters triggering consultation reinitiation include: (1) mortality exceeding 20 percent during capture, stocking, holding, or transport; (2) rapid declines in gross abundance following BLM actions or slower declines continuing over the following year; (3) lack of detectable reproduction during the next reproductive season following action completion. Loss due to natural drying or water source failure does not constitute incidental take if not resulting from direct BLM action.

**Reasonable and Prudent Measures**: As many Gila topminnow and desert pupfish as possible (minimum 200 of each species or all present, whichever is less) must be captured and held during actions involving draining, dredging, vegetation removal, dam/berm repair or replacement, or other actions with substantial take potential. Holding facilities must provide adequate size, water chemistry, and temperature to sustain fish during the action and until turbidity returns to near post-project levels. Fish must not be held where contamination with other fish species is possible. Captured stock must contain representative samples of adult females, males, and juveniles of both species.

**Terms and Conditions**: Populations and habitat must be checked following completion of specified actions at minimum time periods: one week, one month, six months, and one year post-completion. Baseline information must be recorded prior to actions, including gross abundance, presence/absence of young, general distribution, water level and turbidity, general habitat condition, and other pertinent data. Post-project information must include the same parameters. Copies of all baseline, project, and follow-up records must be furnished to FWS immediately after each step completion.

**Conservation Recommendations**: Key recommendations include: (1) leave at least 100 desert pupfish at Flowing Wells High School following stock removal; (2) remove pupfish from Cold Spring Seep only after the population has at least tripled from original stocking numbers; (3) avoid large livestock concentrations near springs or wetted areas; (4) minimize soil and vegetation disturbance during springbox maintenance; (5) do not permit increased spring flow diversion; (6) prevent pollutant entry; (7) avoid actions increasing nonnative fish introduction likelihood; (8) close the bladed track crossing the wash upstream from Big Spring; (9) remove salt cedar gradually while allowing native trees to replace shading; (10) avoid introducing plant species causing open water habitat loss; (11) continue cattail control; (12) coordinate exotic animal removal methods with FWS and Arizona Game and Fish Department; (13) conduct actions during spring, summer, or fall reproductive periods; (14) minimize turbidity and sedimentation; (15) maintain U-shaped bottom profiles in pools; (16) maintain action records with documentation, sketches, and photographs.

**Additional Biological Concerns**: Snail samples from Cold Spring Seep confirmed presence of *Apachecoccus arizonae*, with *Tryonia gilae* also known from the site. Both are Federal Category 2 candidate species. One or both likely occur at Big Spring. Consideration of these species' needs and survival in management actions is requested.
