# American Fisheries Society Protocol for Introductions of Threatened and Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-biology #endangered-species #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This draft protocol, developed by the [[American Fisheries Society]] Western Division Endangered Species Committee, addresses the widespread but poorly standardized practice of introducing (translocating) threatened and endangered fishes as a recovery tool. An analysis of 37 approved [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] recovery plans found that 81% (30 of 37) called for some form of introduction — to supplement existing populations, establish new populations, create genetic reserves, support artificial propagation, provide educational exhibits, or serve as biological control agents. Despite the frequency of these actions, success rates have been poor (e.g., only ~2 of 12 [[Mohave Tui Chub]] introductions succeeded; zero success across many thousands of [[Razorback Sucker]] introductions; ~30% success rate for rare Arizona fishes overall). The paper proposes a three-part protocol — (1) site selection, (2) conducting the introduction, and (3) post-introduction monitoring — intended to standardize practice and improve outcomes, illustrated by the cautionary extinction of the [[Amistad Gambusia]] and phenotypic problems observed in translocated [[Devils Hole Pupfish]].

## Background & Description

- **Regulatory driver**: The [[Endangered Species Act of 1973]] requires USFWS to develop recovery plans for listed species; introductions have become a prominent recovery tool in recent decades.
- **Case study — Amistad Gambusia (*Gambusia amistadensis*)**: Known only from Goodenough Spring, inundated by construction of Amistad Dam on the Rio Grande. Roughly 30–40 individuals were collected on 3 August 1968 as the habitat flooded, taken to Brackenridge Field Laboratory (Austin, TX), then partially transferred in 1974 to Dexter National Fish Hatchery (NM) for propagation. By 1979, the entire captive stock was found to consist solely of contaminating [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) — the species was declared extinct (Hubbs and Jensen 1984).
- **Historical success rates**:
  - [[Mohave Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor mohavensis*): only 2 of 12 introductions established populations (Hoover and St. Amant 1983).
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): no successful reintroductions despite many thousands of individuals released (Johnson 1985).
  - Rare Arizona fishes generally: ~30% success rate (Minckley and Brooks 1985).
- **Recovery plan survey**: 37 approved USFWS recovery plans reviewed; 30 (81%) included provisions for introductions. Species *without* proposed introductions (due to highly restricted, unusual, hard-to-duplicate habitats) included:
  - [[Borax Lake Chub]]
  - [[Kendall Warm Springs Dace]]
  - [[Ozark Cavefish]]
  - [[Alabama Cavefish]]
  - [[Slackwater Darter]]
  - [[Fountain Darter]]
  - [[Snail Darter]]
  - [[Bayou Darter]] (introductions permitted only if loss of the existing population is imminent)
- **Documented introduction counts** (by species, purpose = supplement/establish populations):
  - [[Little Kern Golden Trout]] (*Salmo aguabonita whitei*) – 43
  - [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki stomias*) – 32
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) – 14
  - [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) – 13
  - [[Pecos Gambusia]] (*Gambusia nobilis*) – 32
  - [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) – 97

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Genetic homogeneity risk**: Fishes from homogeneous habitats (e.g., isolated thermal springs) often possess narrow genetic variability, limiting adaptive capacity in new environments.
- **Phenotypic plasticity case study**: The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*), native to a limestone cavern (Devil's Hole), was introduced to a more exposed cement refugium below Hoover Dam with higher primary productivity and food abundance. This produced pupfish with larger, more brightly-colored bodies and altered mensural characteristics (Williams 1977) — demonstrating unintended phenotypic divergence from habitat mismatch.
- **Hybridization risk**: Many isolated-drainage western fishes lack strong reproductive isolating mechanisms. [[Catostomidae|Suckers]] in particular readily hybridize, including intergeneric hybrids. Introductions must be prohibited where hybridization risk with resident species/subspecies exists.
- **Non-target impacts**: Pre-introduction surveys must assess risks to co-occurring rare/endemic invertebrates. Example: introduction of [[Owens Pupfish]] into Fish Slough (eastern California) inadvertently eliminated a population of an endemic hydrobiid snail (later rediscovered elsewhere in the slough) (Landye 1983).
- **Pathogen/parasite transfer**: Fish transfers are a known vector for introducing unwanted parasites and diseases (Hoffman and Schubert 1984); culture facility crowding elevates parasite loads.
- **Population viability considerations**: Allendorf and Ryman (1987) recommend an effective population size (Ne) of 200 for sustaining hatchery salmonid stocks; wild populations require much larger census sizes to buffer against skewed sex ratios, age structure imbalance, and stochastic events (floods, drought, fire).

## Key Findings

1. **81% of reviewed recovery plans** (30/37) for U.S. threatened/endangered fishes included introduction provisions as part of recovery strategy.
2. **No standardized introduction protocol previously existed**, contributing to slow recovery progress and, in some cases, elimination of other rare native species by introduced fish.
3. **The proposed protocol has three components**:
   - **I. Selecting the Introduction Site**
     - Restrict to native/historic habitat whenever possible; introductions outside historic range warrant great caution and should occur only if historic habitat is unsuitable/unrestorable, imminently threatened, or the introduction is in a controlled setting (e.g., hatchery).
     - Restrict to protected, secure sites (ideally under formal management agreement or dedicated land status).
     - Evaluate dispersal potential relative to introduction goals (contain vs. allow range expansion).
     - Ensure the site meets full life-history requirements (food, spawning, rearing habitat, water quality) across all life stages.
     - Ensure sufficient habitat to support a viable population (potentially requiring many thousands of individuals to sustain an effective population size of 200).
     - Prohibit introductions risking hybridization with resident species/subspecies.
     - Prohibit introductions that could adversely affect other rare/endemic taxa (requires pre-survey by relevant taxonomists, e.g., malacologists, entomologists).
   - **II. Conducting the Introduction**
     - Select introduction stock using clear criteria (genetic purity, rarity, geographic/ecological proximity); avoid mixing stocks absent genetic justification.
     - Verify taxonomic identity of stock prior to transport.
     - Screen for pathogens (recommended minimum sample size of 60 fish per Ossiander and Wedemeyer 1973; quarantine ≥2 weeks per Hoffman and Schubert 1984).
     - Use sufficient numbers/sex ratio/age structure — minimum suggested benchmark of 25 males:25 females for salmonids (Allendorf and Ryman 1987; Ryman and Stahl 1980); never take more than 10% of source population annually.
     - Transport carefully to minimize handling stress, mucus/scale loss, and mortality (per Stickney 1983; Johnson 1979).
     - Time introductions for favorable weather/hydrology, thermal-shock avoidance, and reduced predation (e.g., release at night where sight-feeding predators are present).
     - Fully document each translocation (personnel, taxon, source, numbers, age/size distribution, date, precise release location) in accessible scientific/administrative literature.
   - **III. Post-Introduction Activities**
     - Conduct systematic monitoring (first-year survival, recruitment, long-term persistence through stochastic events).
     - Restock if warranted, using the same source population unless genetic mixing is justified.
     - Determine causal factors for any failures before considering restocking.
     - Document and widely disseminate findings/conclusions from post-introduction monitoring.
4. **Case examples underscore protocol rationale**: The extinction of [[Amistad Gambusia]] via unmonitored hatchery contamination, phenotypic divergence in the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] refugium population, and loss of an endemic snail during [[Owens Pupfish]] introduction all illustrate failures the protocol seeks to prevent.
5. **Purpose categories for introductions** identified across recovery plans: (1) information/education, (2) genetic reserve/artificial propagation, (3) biological control agent, (4) supplementing existing populations, (5) establishing new populations — with sport fishery creation as a secondary goal for certain rare trout introductions.
