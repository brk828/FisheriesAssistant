# Mass Hybridization Between Two Genera of Cyprinid Fishes in the Mohave Desert, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Hubbs and Miller (1943, reprinted from the *Papers of the Michigan Academy of Science, Arts, and Letters*, Vol. XXVIII, 1942) document extensive natural hybridization between two native minnows of the [[Mohave River]] system: [[Gila Orcuttii]] (a stream-adapted fish) and [[Mohave Lake Chub]] (*Siphateles mohavensis*, a lake-adapted fish). The authors argue that Quaternary desiccation, followed by the confinement of both species to shrinking permanent waters, forced them into intimate contact. Hybrids made up about 8 per cent of the basin-wide minnow population and up to 24 per cent locally, a level the authors call exceptional for the Cyprinidae. Interspecific hybrids were interpreted as first-generation products with low fertility, with only a small amount of backcrossing. The study rests on morphological, meristic, and ecological evidence, with a hybrid index for each character.

## Background & Description

The Mohave Desert lies within the Great Basin, and its permanent waters are characterized by interior drainage. The Mohave River rises on the northern slopes of the San Bernardino Mountains and runs roughly northeast for more than 100 miles to Soda Lake, a playa south of Baker. The river supports five regions of perennial flow: the headwater region (about 90 per cent of the water supply), the Victorville vicinity (about seven miles of flow), a short flow south of Harvard, Afton Canyon (six or seven miles), and a spring pool on the west side of Soda Lake.

Pleistocene lakes, including [[Lake Mohave]], [[Manix Lake]], and [[Little Mohave Lake]], once connected these habitats. The authors suggest that the ancestral lacustrine conditions favored [[Siphateles Mohavensis]].

Only two native fish species occur in the basin. Both belong to the Cyprinidae, and both represent genera that are widespread in western North America:

- **[[Gila Orcuttii]]**: a fluviatile (stream) fish, more streamlined and darker, with strong, hooked pharyngeal teeth suited to a rapacious, insect-based diet, short gill slits, and few rudimentary gillrakers.
- **[[Siphateles Mohavensis]]**: a lacustrine (lake) fish, with a more uniform metallic color, weaker grinding-type pharyngeal teeth, deeply cleft gill slits, and numerous slender gillrakers, consistent with a plankton diet.

Trout (*Salmo gairdnerii irideus*), introduced into the headwaters, probably restricted minnow numbers and range. Several other exotic species had apparently little effect as of the study period. Catostomid fishes may once have occurred in the system but appear to have been extirpated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Precipitation drops sharply from the mountains to the desert. Annual rainfall is about 13 to 35 inches in the headwaters, about five inches at Victorville, and under two inches near Afton Canyon. Severe downpours in the mountains periodically flush the river, especially its lower reaches.

The major flood of March 1938 filled Soda Lake, overflowed into Silver Lake, and transformed stream beds. The lower end of Deep Creek, a five-foot pool, became a sand-choked channel. Flood effects were uneven across the two species. The Gila population was relatively unaffected and increased in several sites, while the Siphateles population decreased 89 to 93 per cent in the affected headwater areas. The Siphateles was apparently carried farther onto the desert playas.

The authors note that the two species partition habitat only partly. The Gila ascends streams farther and prefers current; the Siphateles favors quieter pools. Soda Lake spring is the only site where Siphateles occurs alone, and the authors describe the two species as commonly swimming together elsewhere. Where they co-occur, the authors conclude that competition is limited because the species are largely complementary in food habits. In these desert waters, physical rather than biotic factors dominate the struggle for existence.

## Key Findings

**1. Frequency of hybridization.** Random samples totaled 5,604 cyprinids from the stream system, with composition approximately Gila 60%, hybrids 8%, Siphateles 32%. Excluding the 608 Siphateles from Soda Lake spring, the composition was Gila 67%, hybrids 9%, Siphateles 24%.

**2. Deep Creek census.** The entire population of 1,471 minnows from the lowest Deep Creek pool, preserved on 1 September 1934, comprised Gila 57%, hybrids 8%, and Siphateles 35%.

**3. Flood-related shifts.** Before the flood (1934 and 1937), the basin-wide ratio was Gila 56.1%, hybrids 8.6%, Siphateles 35.3%. After the flood (May 1938 to 1940), it was Gila 76.6%, hybrids 9.3%, Siphateles 14.1%. In Afton Canyon the hybrid share reached 24% (Gila 254, hybrids 164, Siphateles 277).

**4. Hybrid index for morphology.** The authors used a hybrid index scaled so that Gila = 0 and Siphateles = 100. Hybrids were intermediate in pharyngeal teeth, gillrakers, scale radii, and fin-ray counts. Gillraker averages were about 8 (Gila), 13 (hybrids), and 24 (Siphateles). The hybrid coefficient of variation was about 13%, compared with about 10% for Gila and 8% for Siphateles.

**5. Pharyngeal teeth.** Gila most commonly showed the formula 2, 5–4, 2; Siphateles typically 0, 5–5, 0; hybrids most commonly 1, 5–4, 1. A single weak tooth in the outer row is treated as a marker of hybridity.

**6. Scales and fins.** Gila scales are longer than high and have more radii. Siphateles scales are shield-shaped with fewer radii. Hybrids were intermediate in most scale and fin counts. Pelvic rays were typically 8 (Gila), 9 (hybrids), and 10 (Siphateles).

**7. Aberrant hybrid features.** Hybrids were more extreme than either parent in head size, body depth, fin size, and pectoral-ray number. The authors attribute these to heterosis (hybrid vigor), comparing them with sunfish hybrids and with gradients in size and productivity among fish races.

**8. Backcrossing.** Backcrossing appears to be limited. The authors note a positive correlation between pelvic-ray and gillraker counts in the Afton Canyon hybrids (r = 0.34 ± 0.07), but only three hybrids had fewer than 10 gillrakers. The authors conclude that the hybrids were probably first-generation products with low fertility.

**9. Parallel case.** The authors describe the Lahontan system pairing of *Siphateles obesus obesus* and *S. obesus pectinifer*, which they treat as subspecific intergradation rather than hybridization. Snyder had described *Richardsonius microdon* from four Lake Tahoe specimens, which the authors consider a hybrid.

**10. Conclusion.** The breakdown of the isolating mechanism is attributed to the shift from separate, complementary habitats in the Pluvial period to forced cohabitation in isolated remnant waters. The authors conclude that mass hybridization of this extent is rare among fishes and that the hybrids do not show a fixed, distinct species pattern.
