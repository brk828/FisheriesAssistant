# Orconectes causeyi, sp. nov.: A New Crawfish from New Mexico

#carcinology #freshwater-biology #astacidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 taxonomic paper, published in [[The American Midland Naturalist]] (vol. 77, no. 2, pp. 518–526), describes a new crayfish species, *Orconectes causeyi*, from [[Conchas Reservoir]] in San Miguel County, New Mexico. The author, [[Douglas B. Jester]], based the description on a [[Holotype]] male, an [[Allotype]] female and a [[Morphotype]] male, plus 43 paratypes. The species is named in honor of [[David Causey]]. The paper also documents its distribution across three drainages and proposes that human-mediated bait transport introduced it outside its native range. Manuscript dates: received 23 March 1966, accepted 28 April 1966.

## Background & Description

**Discovery and naming.** Three specimens were collected during a fishery investigation of Conchas Lake in 1960. The author could not identify them, so they were sent to [[Horton H. Hobbs Jr.]], who judged them to be a new species and encouraged a formal description. The species is named for Dr. David Causey, emeritus professor of zoology, who taught the author and supported his work.

**Diagnostic features (holotype male, Form I).** The description is in the standard crayfish morphology format used by [[Penn]] (1952):

- Body stocky, subovate and depressed; abdomen slightly shorter than the cephalothorax.
- Cephalic groove and areola are typical of the group; the areola is open, a feature cited by [[Ortmann]] (1905) as diagnostic for the [[Virilis Group]].
- Rostrum long and excavate, with a weak lateral spine on each side. The acumen is slightly longer than one third the rostrum length.
- Chelae are unequal. The right chela has an inflated palm, depressed fingers, and two rows of inner palm tubercles (seven in the inner row). The fingers terminate in short corneous tips.
- Ischiopodites of the third pereiopods bear hooks (Fig. 5). Hook length is more than two-thirds the diameter of the ischiopodite base.
- First pleopod tip is deeply split. The central projection is slender and the mesial process is noncorneous and similarly curved.

**Morphotype male (Form II).** Differs mainly in having a considerably smaller body and chelae of intermediate size compared with the holotype. The first pleopods reach the posterior end of the second pereiopod coxopodite. The author notes that the chela size may reflect regeneration, so the morphotype's measurements are listed as "questionably" comparable.

**Allotype female.** Similar to the holotype in general appearance. The annulus ventralis is immovable and spindle-shaped, with an anteromedian depression.

**Type series and deposition.**
- Holotype (Form I male), allotype (female) and morphotype (Form II male) are deposited in the [[United States National Museum]] under Nos. 115699, 115699 and 115700 respectively.
- Paratypes sent to Dr. Hobbs in 1960 are presumed to be in his collection, later donated to the U.S. National Museum.
- Five Form I males and five females are held at the [[Tulane University]] Department of Zoology.
- Thirty paratypes (17 Form I males and 13 females) are held at the Division of Wildlife Management, New Mexico State University.

**Measurement note.** Measurements are given in a table of millimeters for the three types. Because of OCR damage and the regeneration question, the table is summarized here rather than reproduced. The author reports that the holotype and allotype are the more reliable reference measurements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Type locality.** The holotype, allotype and morphotype were collected by the author on 15 August 1960 from [[Conchas Reservoir]], an impoundment of the [[South Canadian River]] in the headwaters of the [[Arkansas River]] system. The site is about 32 miles northwest of Tucumcari, New Mexico.

**Collection methods and habitats.**
- Paratypes were mainly taken with experimental gill nets in water 1 to 22 m deep.
- Thirty paratypes were taken by rod-and-reel baited with dead and spoiled minnows, at depths of 6 to 10 m around a minnow dock and fish-cleaning facility.
- Most specimens from Conchas Lake came from deep water. Specimens from streams were taken where depth does not exceed 1 m.
- Substrates at collection sites include sandstone, bedrock, coarse gravel, fine gravel and sand, silt, and mixtures of these.

**Known distribution.**
- *Rio Grande drainage (native-region records):* Jemez River, 4 mi S of Jemez Springs; 3.9 mi N of Peña Blanca; Rio Grande 2 mi S of Embudo; and Rio Grande at Arroyo Hondo crossing, Taos County. The author also reports that the species is abundant in Bluewater Lake and tributary streams just east of the [[Continental Divide]].
- *Arkansas River drainage (probable origin):* Conchas Reservoir and the South Canadian River.
- *Gila (Colorado) drainage, west of the Continental Divide:* four specimens received from Jack Dean of the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife. These include one mature female from the Little Colorado River at Springerville, Arizona (9 February 1966), and one mature male, one juvenile male and one juvenile female from Nutria Lake, Zuni Indian Reservation, McKinley County, New Mexico (October 1965).
- *Introductions:* The species was introduced into Wall Lake and Lake Roberts (Catron and Grant counties) in 1964, both in headwaters of the Gila River.

**Dispersal.** The author concludes that the species probably originated in the Arkansas River drainage and was moved by people into the Rio Grande and the Colorado River drainage west of the Continental Divide. Fish-bait traffic from the South Canadian River to the Rio Grande is documented, with no known reversal of this pattern. Bait transport to westerly streams in Colorado is also reported. Impoundment of Conchas Reservoir may have isolated a native population.

**Water temperature.** The species is definitely known from water temperatures of 1 to 28 °C (the scanned value reads "1 to 2B°C"; 28 °C is the probable intended value). The author describes it as a species of relatively cool headwaters.

**Seasonality and life history.**
- Form I males were collected from August through October. The Form II morphotype male was collected in August.
- No females carrying eggs were found, so the egg-carrying period cannot be estimated.
- Small juveniles (one from the Rio Grande collected 17 July 1962) were abundant in gill nets and in the stomachs of [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]. Similar-sized specimens appeared in the stomachs of small [[White Crappie]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] from Conchas Reservoir in April–May 1960.

**Relationships.** The species is placed in the *virilis* section because it shows the traits described by Ortmann (1905) and [[Penn]] (1957), chiefly the relatively large first pleopods of males. Hobbs (personal communication) judged it most closely related to [[Orconectes palmeri longimanus]] among known species.

## Key Findings

- **New species described:** *Orconectes causeyi* is a new crayfish in the *virilis* section of *Orconectes* (family [[Astacidae]]), based on a holotype male, an allotype female, a morphotype male and 43 paratypes from New Mexico.
- **Type locality:** [[Conchas Reservoir]], collected 15 August 1960.
- **Natural range:** Records from the Rio Grande and the [[South Canadian River]] / [[Arkansas River]] system, with a probable native origin in the Arkansas drainage.
- **Human-mediated spread:** Bait transport and two 1964 lake introductions (Wall Lake and Lake Roberts) extended its range into the [[Gila River]] headwaters west of the [[Continental Divide]].
- **Habitat:** Reservoir deep water (1–22 m) and shallow streams (under 1 m); water temperatures 1–28 °C; sandy, gravel, silt and bedrock substrates.
- **Life history:** Form I males appear August–October; no egg-carrying females were collected, so the egg-carrying period is unknown. Juveniles are eaten by trout, crappie and bass.
- **Systematic affinity:** Closest known relative is *Orconectes palmeri longimanus* (per Hobbs).
- **Type deposition:** [[United States National Museum]] (types), [[Tulane University]] (5 male and 5 female paratypes), and New Mexico State University (30 paratypes).
