# Annotated Key to the Fishes of Indiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1968 draft by Joseph S. Nelson and Shelby D. Gerking (Department of Zoology, Indiana University, Bloomington; Indiana Aquatic Research Unit, Project 342-303-815) is an annotated dichotomous key for identifying the fishes present in, or recorded from, [[Indiana]]. It combines recent nomenclature changes, additional diagnostic characters and distributional updates with earlier work by Gerking (1915, 1955). The Indiana freshwater fauna comprises 179 species in 28 families. The key uses paired couplets to lead the user from family to species, and each species entry gives a brief total range and a statewide distribution by drainage.

## Background & Description

**Geography.** Indiana covers 36,291 square miles and lies between roughly 37°46′ and 41°46′ N latitude. Its northern sixth drains to the [[Lake Michigan]]–[[Lake Erie]] watershed. The remainder drains to the [[Mississippi River]] system, chiefly the [[Wabash River]] and [[Ohio River]] and their tributaries. Elevation ranges from 1,257 feet in Wayne County to 335 feet in Posey County.

**Glacial history.** The [[Wisconsin Glaciation]] covered about two-thirds of the state at its maximum, roughly 21,000 years ago, and left about 16,000 years ago. Older Pleistocene glaciation (Kansan and Illinoian) left an unglaciated wedge in the south. The authors argue that all native Indiana fishes derive from a Mississippian refugium, and that northward invasion routes into the Great Lakes drainage included the Maumee River, the Kankakee–St. Joseph connection and the Chicago outlet.

**Key structure.** Couplets are numbered, and the number in parentheses after each couplet permits the user to retrace the path. Counts such as dorsal and anal fin rays, lateral-line scales and pharyngeal teeth follow the methods of [[Hubbs and Lagler]] (1964). The key is not intended for hybrids, teratological specimens or very young fish. Families are arranged following [[Greenwood et al. 1966]], while Table II compares four classification systems (Regan 1929, Jordan 1923, Berg 1940, Greenwood et al. 1966).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Northern lakes:** Glacial lakes are numerous, physically and chemically variable, and usually rich in aquatic vegetation. They freeze for up to about 3½ months. [[Lake Wawasee]] (2,618 acres) is the largest natural lake wholly within Indiana, and [[Lake Tippecanoe]] (123 ft maximum depth) is the deepest.
- **Southern and central Indiana:** No natural lakes occur except oxbows. Streams are abundant, and caves and sinkholes are common. [[Monroe Reservoir]] (about 10,700 acres) is the largest body of water wholly within the state. Many reservoirs and thousands of farm ponds have been created.
- **Climate:** Mean annual temperature is 51°F (reported as "318 F", likely OCR error, see note below) and mean summer temperature is 76°F. Annual precipitation ranges from about 36 inches near Lake Michigan to about 50 inches along the Ohio River.
- **Flow:** The Wabash drains about two-thirds of the state and averages about 26,500 cubic feet per second in its lower reaches.
- **Human influence:** Bait transport and fish stocking have altered natural distributions, and pollution has restricted the ranges of many species. The authors note that the effect of anglers is hard to quantify.
- **Cave fauna:** Cavefishes such as [[Northern Cavefish]] and [[Southern Cavefish]] inhabit limestone caves in southern Indiana.

## Key Findings

- **Diversity:** 179 freshwater species in 28 families occur in Indiana. Thirteen of the 57 orders with living representatives worldwide are represented. Some species were last collected more than 70 years ago and may be extinct.
- **Regional comparison (Table I):** Indiana's fauna is similar to that of adjacent areas. Cyprinidae (about 48 species) and Percidae (30 species) dominate. No species is endemic to the state.
- **Great Lakes differences:** About 30 Indiana species are absent from the entire Great Lakes watershed. No native species of the Great Lakes drainage in Indiana is absent from all parts of the Mississippi drainage.
- **Introductions:** Eight species have been introduced into Indiana or adjacent areas. Notable examples include [[Common Carp]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Coho Salmon]]/[[Chinook Salmon]] (recent stocking in Lake Michigan).
- **Notable records:**
  - [[Threadfin Shad]] was first captured in Indiana in 1957.
  - [[Sea Lamprey]] is recorded from Lake Michigan and a single specimen from the Maxinkuckee Lake outlet.
  - [[Northern Pike]] was first recorded from New Harmony, Posey County.
  - [[Crooked Lake]] records [[Ninespine Stickleback]], the first verifiable Indiana record and the second in the Mississippi drainage.
- **Historically important taxa:** [[Harelip Sucker]] has not been taken since about 1900 and may be extinct.
- **Ichthyological history:** Several notable ichthyologists, including [[David Starr Jordan]], [[Charles Gilbert]], [[Carl Eigenmann]], [[Barton Evermann]] and [[Seth Meek]], worked on Indiana fishes.
- **Editorial notes:** The document is a draft. Several passages carry OCR-related errors, and some counts and citations are incomplete in the source text. Figures and table data are summarized here rather than reproduced in full.
