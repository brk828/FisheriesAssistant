# Loach Minnow Research Proposal (Artificial Propagation Study)

## Executive Summary
This 1997 memorandum and attached research proposal from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Alchesay-Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery outlines a plan to study **artificial propagation techniques** for the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Rhinichthys cobitis*, historically *Tiaroga cobitis*), a federally threatened cyprinid fish native to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico. The hatchery had maintained approximately 40 individuals in a wet lab for nine months and sought collaboration with the Bureau of Reclamation to formalize research into captive spawning, egg incubation, and larval rearing as a conservation tool supporting genetic refugia for wild populations threatened by habitat loss and non-native species competition.

## Background & Description
- **Body form**: Small, slender, elongate fish
- **Distinctive feature**: Markedly upwardly-directed eyes (dorsally oriented), an adaptation associated with its benthic, riffle-dwelling lifestyle
- **Maximum size**: ~7.3 cm (total length)
- **Life stages**: Four distinct stages — embryo, larval, juvenile, and adult
- **Lifespan**: 1.5–3 years, with most individuals surviving ~2 years (Britt 1982; Propst & Bestgen 1991)

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Bottom-dwelling inhabitant of shallow, swift-water riffles over gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates; occurs below 2,200 m elevation in intermediate-sized rivers with moderate gradients (Rinne 1989; Propst and Bestgen 1991)
- **Microhabitat use**: Utilizes interstitial spaces between and in the lee of larger substrate particles for resting and spawning; largely absent from sites where fine sediment fills these interstitial spaces, indicating sensitivity to sedimentation
- **Habitat quality indicator**: Presence of filamentous algae may be an important habitat component (Barber and Minckley 1966)
- **Diet**: Exclusively aquatic insects (Schreiber 1978; Abarca 1987) — an obligate insectivore
- **Thermal range**: Daytime water temperatures at occupied habitat sites range from 9.0–32.0°C
- **Reproduction**:
  - Primary spawning period: March–May at 16–20°C
  - Secondary spawning window: late June–early July at 14–23°C following declining high flows (Meyer, pers. comm. 1995)
  - Autumn spawning also documented at 16–20°C following declining high flows (Vives and Minckley 1990)
  - Incubation period: 5–6 days, temperature-dependent (Britt 1982)
  - Eggs are attached to the underside of a rock forming the roof of a small substrate cavity on the downstream side
  - Evidence suggests males may guard nests during incubation (Propst et al. 1988; Vives and Minckley 1990)
- **Historic range**: Verde, Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco, and Gila river basins (Minckley 1973; Sublette et al. 1990)
- **Current range**: Persists in limited segments of the upper [[Gila River]], San Francisco, Blue, Tularosa, and East Fork White River, plus Aravaipa, Eagle, Campbell Blue, and Dry Blue Creeks in Arizona and New Mexico
- **Threats**: Competition and predation by non-native fishes (e.g., red shiner, *Cyprinella lutrensis*), and habitat disturbance/destruction (Miller 1961; Williams et al. 1985; Marsh et al. 1989); range reduced by an estimated 85% (USFWS 1986)

## Key Findings
- **Conservation status**: Listed as a threatened species on October 28, 1986; critical habitat designated March 8, 1994, covering portions of the San Francisco, Tularosa, Blue, and upper Gila rivers, and [[Aravaipa Creek]]
- **Genetic conservation program**: The Arizona Fishery Resources Office and Alchesay-Williams Creek NFH collected and held loach minnow from the East Fork White River (Fort Apache Indian Reservation) under USFWS Endangered Species Permit PRT-676811, in response to Biological Opinion 2-23-96-F-01 concerning irrigation diversion repairs
- **Captive husbandry advances**:
  - Loach minnow sustained in captivity for 9+ months at Alchesay NFH
  - Custom artificial feed regimes developed
  - Systems refined to control parasitic disease previously problematic in captive holding
  - Wet lab equipped with mechanical, biological, and chemical filtration, backed by a 5,000-watt standby generator
- **Prior propagation successes**: Wild-collected eggs hatched in aquaria (Turner, pers. comm. 1996); captive-induced spawning with larvae reared temporarily on cultured zooplankton (Minckley, pers. comm. 1996) — indicating strong feasibility for a formal propagation program
- **Proposed research objectives**:
  1. Simulate natural temperature and photoperiod cycles to induce captive spawning
  2. Use spawning substrate techniques developed at Arizona State University
  3. Transfer egg masses to separate hatching aquaria
  4. Develop phytoplankton/zooplankton feeding regimes tailored to larval nutritional and size requirements
  5. Establish captive populations as **genetic refugia** for imperiled wild populations
- **Knowledge gaps identified**: Riffle-specific behavioral patterns (station fidelity vs. random/diel movement) and fine-scale physical habitat requirements (depth, velocity, substrate size, embeddedness) remain poorly studied
- **Proposed annual budget**: $13,000 total (Biological Technician half-time: $9,000; Equipment/Supplies: $2,500; Facility Support/Utilities: $1,500)
- **Reporting structure**: Quarterly progress reports and comprehensive annual reports submitted through the White Mountain Apache Tribal Outdoor Recreation and Wildlife Department, then distributed to Arizona/New Mexico Fishery Resources Offices and funding partners
