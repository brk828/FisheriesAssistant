# Farm Fish Pond Management Guide (Texas Agricultural Extension Service)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Texas Agricultural Extension Service publication, authored by Edwin H. Cooper (Extension Specialist in Wildlife Conservation, Texas A. & M. College System), is a practical manual for building, stocking, fertilizing, fishing and maintaining small [[Farm Pond]]s for family recreation. It covers site selection, pond dimensions, [[Fertilization Program]] timing, balanced stocking of [[Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Crappie]] and [[Channel Catfish]], removal of [[Rough Fish]] with [[Rotenone]], and control of aquatic vegetation and algae using [[Sodium Arsenite]], [[2,4-D]] and [[Copper Sulfate]]. The guide also addresses common problems such as fish die-off, parasites, snakes, crayfish and unbalanced fish populations.

## Background & Description

The document is a revised (1958) extension bulletin, not a research study, and reports no original sample sizes or statistics beyond management guidelines. Its stated premise is that fishing is a popular outdoor recreation and that a properly managed farm pond can provide excellent fishing at reasonable cost. It frames the pond as a family project that can also support swimming, boating and camping.

The publication is organized into sections on pond location, size and depth, dam and spillway construction, seepage control ([[Bentonite]] sealing), fertilization, fishing, fish shelters and spawning aids, rough fish removal, aquatic plant control, chemical treatment calculations, pond beautification and other problems, followed by references and acknowledgments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site and watershed.** Ponds should sit on clay soils that hold water; gravelly sites require water-sealing materials. Grass-sodded watersheds are preferred, because cultivated watersheds silt up ponds and shorten their life. Roughly 25 to 50 acres of watershed are said to suffice for one acre of pond, depending on local rainfall.

**Size and depth.** A surface area of one to three acres is recommended. Ponds under about one-third of an acre are described as hardly worth managing, and ponds above 3 acres are difficult and costly to manage. The water area should be split roughly into thirds: shallow, medium, and 10 to 12 feet or deeper.

**Oxygen and plankton dynamics.** Fertilization builds plankton blooms that shade out submerged plants. Late-season fertilization (after June 1 in Texas) is described as hazardous because it can deplete dissolved oxygen enough to kill fish. Dense plant growth and decaying vegetation can also cause oxygen depletion, and fish gathering at the shore or surface signals this condition.

**Aquatic plants.** Emergent plants (cattails, bulrushes, water lilies), floating plants ([[Water Hyacinth]]) and submerged "pond mosses" (waterweed, parrotfeather, coontail, bladderwort, [[Chara]]) are discussed. Plants supply oxygen, invertebrate habitat, spawning substrate and waterfowl food, but excessive growth interferes with fishing and disrupts predator-prey balance.

**Invasive mammal.** The [[Nutria]] was introduced to control vegetation with only limited success, and state and federal agencies discouraged further release due to crop and habitat damage.

## Key Findings

**Pond design and fertilization**
- Clay soils are best for ponds; a 1-acre pond needs roughly 25 to 50 acres of watershed, depending on rainfall.
- Fertilization timing: January 1 to June 1 in the lower Rio Grande Valley and March 1 to June 1 in northern Texas. Applications after June 1 risk fish kills.
- Method: apply about half the total fertilizer in the first application, then divide the rest into three to five applications guided by the "cupped hand" test (hand visible at elbow depth, about 18 inches, means another application is needed).
- Do not fertilize if the pond will not be fished at least 300 man-hours per acre per year, if fish are badly out of balance, or if natural fertility is already sufficient.

**Stocking and fishing management**
- Research cited indicates an acre needs 300 to 500 man-hours of fishing per year to keep production at maximum and prevent stunting.
- Fishing may begin after about one year, once seine samples show young of all stocked species.
- Sunfish tend to overpopulate: for every bass or catfish removed, at least 10 to 12 sunfish should be removed; small sunfish should not be returned to the water.
- Shelters (brush, logs, rock piles) and nesting structures (sand/gravel beds, gravel boxes, milk cans or nail kegs for channel catfish) improve fishing and spawning.

**Species-specific fishing notes**
- Bass: best in spring, summer and fall; live baits include minnows, crayfish and frogs; surface and shallow lures work in warm months.
- Sunfish: best in spring and summer; light cane poles and small hooks with no weight; nests visible in shallow water.
- Crappie: best in early spring; feed around submerged brush in early morning and late afternoon.
- Channel catfish: mainly night fishing; trotlines, cane poles and rod and reel; caught near bottom.

**Rough fish removal**
- Rotenone dosage: 5 lb of 5% powder per acre-foot; 3 lb per surface acre when water exceeds 65 °F. A 3-foot-average pond needs 15 lb.
- Rotenone affects only the gills, fish may be eaten if cleaned fresh, and it loses strength within 7 to 14 days before restocking.
- Treat in late spring or summer at 65 °F or higher, when water is at its lowest and warmest.

**Aquatic plant control**
- Emergent and floating plants: cut cattails and bulrushes before seed maturity; mow water lilies 6 inches or more below the surface; apply herbicide per the Table 2 mixtures (e.g., 40% amine salt of 2,4-D, 2,4,5-T, or Dalapon for cattails), with pressure sprayers.
- Algae and chara: copper sulfate at 0.5 to 2 ppm (about 1.66 to 16.60 lb per million gallons for 0.2 to 2.0 ppm), treating one-quarter to one-third of the pond per application in spring or early summer.
- Submerged plants: sodium arsenite (40% solution) at 4 ppm for ponds of 2 acres or less, and 5 ppm for 2 to 10 acres, applied early in the season on calm days. A single application controls weeds for one summer; annual treatment may be needed for 2 to 3 years.

**Safety and regulation**
- Sodium arsenite is poisonous: keep animals and children away for at least 2 weeks, do not use irrigation water for at least 1 week, and never treat rice-culture water.
- The use of 2,4-D is restricted by law in certain counties; permits are obtained from the State Commissioner of Agriculture, Austin.
- Treating too much or too quickly risks fish suffocation, and fresh water should be introduced if oxygen depletion appears.

**Other problems and assistance**
- Fish die-offs may result from oxygen depletion, silt run-off or agricultural chemical pollution; remedies include fresh water inflow, aeration with an outboard motor, and restocking.
- Crayfish burrows can be treated with coal tar creosote dip at 1 part per 100 parts water.
- Assistance is available from county agricultural agents, the Soil Conservation Service, the Texas Game and Fish Commission, and the Department of Wildlife Management at A. & M. College.
