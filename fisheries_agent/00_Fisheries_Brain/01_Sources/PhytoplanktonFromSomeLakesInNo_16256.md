# Phytoplankton from Some Lakes in Northern Saskatchewan and Great Slave Lake

#phycology #freshwater-biology #bacillariophyceae #cyanophyta #chlorophyta

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper by J. W. G. Lund examines net phytoplankton samples from [[Great Slave Lake]] and six Saskatchewan lakes: [[Lake Athabaska]], [[Churchill Lake]], [[Cree Lake]], [[Lac La Ronge]], [[Reindeer Lake]] and [[Lake Waskesiu]]. The samples were sent for identification by the late [[D S Rawson]], who was troubled by inconsistent names that different specialists gave to the same algae. Lund lists about 60 taxa, states which authority he follows for each group, and gives diagnostic characters for the groups most often confused. The paper also reports new or unusual records and suggests that the diatom-dominated, oligotrophic lakes contrast with more eutrophic lakes that have a mix of diatoms and Cyanophyta.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Resolve nomenclature and identification problems in Canadian lake phytoplankton, so that trophic interpretations based on species (especially [[Melosira]]) are not misread.
- **Authorities followed:** [[Hustedt]] (1930-62) and Huber-Pestalozzi (1942) for diatoms; Knudsen (1952, 1953) for [[Tabellaria]]; Komárek (1958) for Microcystis, Aphanothece and Anabaena.
- **Scope:** Table I lists the taxa per lake. Lake sample dates range from 1940 to 1960, and each lake is coded by its initial letters (A, Ch, Cr, Re, Ro, S, W).
- **Taxonomic groups covered:**
  - Diatoms (Bacillariophyceae): 18 taxa in Table I
  - Chrysophyceae: 3 taxa (including [[Chrysosphaerella Longispina]])
  - Cyanophyta (blue-green algae): 16 taxa
  - Chlorophyta (green algae, including palmelloid forms): 15 taxa
  - Desmids and other green algae: 8 taxa
- **Figures:** 75 figures document cell outlines, striation, sulcus structure and spore shape.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Trophic indicators:** Following Rawson's work, the more oligotrophic lakes are dominated by diatoms, while the more eutrophic lakes contain a mixture of diatoms and Cyanophyta.
- **Melosira as a trophic indicator:** [[Melosira Granulata]] and [[Melosira Ambigua]] are typical of strongly eutrophic waters and are rare or absent in oligotrophic lakes. [[Melosira Italica Subsp Subarctica]] usually replaces them in oligotrophic lakes, and [[Melosira Islandica]] is found mainly in large northern lakes.
- **Seasonality:** Lund notes that M. italica subsp. subarctica and M. islandica subsp. helvetica are usually most common from autumn to spring, probably because of changes in vertical density stratification. Earlier work by Lund (1954) suggested this was a stratification response rather than a cold-water preference. An English lake overturned artificially in August showed a rapid increase in M. italica, similar to the natural autumn overturn.
- **Great Slave Lake:** Described by Rawson (1956) as very oligotrophic. In the samples studied, only M. italica subsp. subarctica and M. islandica were recorded among the Melosira.
- **Distribution of Melosira across lakes:**
  - M. italica subsp. subarctica: Great Slave Lake, Athabaska, Cree, Reindeer and La Ronge (most common in La Ronge)
  - M. granulata: only Waskesiu, Churchill and La Ronge
  - M. ambigua: predominant in two La Ronge samples and one Waskesiu sample; as common as M. granulata in the single Churchill sample; also present in Athabaska, Cree and Reindeer
  - M. islandica subsp. helvetica: abundant in Athabaska

## Key Findings

1. **Asterionella formosa variation:** Cell length varies from ca. 40-70 µm, while width varies only ca. 1-2 µm, and length and breadth are unrelated. Cells in Lake Athabaska reached 145 µm, which is above the maximum (about 100 µm or less) recorded for the English Lake District and above the maximum in Huber-Pestalozzi (1942). Lund considers that the Athabaska population may differ genetically, but an environmental effect on auxospore size cannot be excluded.

2. **Synedra acus var. angustissima:** Some cells from Lake Athabaska were also unusually long, up to 540 µm as recorded in the source.

3. **Melosira identification:** Five Melosira types are recognized, and the paper gives separating characters:
   - *M. granulata*: terminal spines of varied size, large punctae, shallow V-shaped sulcus
   - *M. islandica*: small, evenly spaced terminal spines, shallow sulcus; Great Slave Lake cells resemble var. *vanernsis* with very thick walls
   - *M. ambigua*: small terminal spines, U-shaped sulcus that appears as a white "eye" in optical section (the most constant diagnostic feature)
   - *M. italica* subsp. *subarctica*: larger spines than M. ambigua, V-shaped sulcus with a thick internal ledge
   - Thin threads of M. ambigua and M. italica are easily confused.

4. **Stephanodiscus niagarae:** Distinguished from S. astraea by closer, coarser markings. It is characteristic of large North American lakes and is almost unknown elsewhere. S. astraea, which is cosmopolitan, was not seen in the samples.

5. **Diplocystis taxonomy:** D. wesenbergii and D. aeruginosa were often recorded under the names Microcystis flos-aquae and M. aeruginosa. The two species can be separated at low magnification by the refractive mucilage of D. wesenbergii and the apparent absence of mucilage around D. aeruginosa. Both are associated with markedly eutrophic lakes.

6. **Aphanothece clathrata:** Some workers treat this small coccoid colonial form as a bacterium. Lund follows Komárek (1958) in treating it as a blue-green alga, based on pigments. Live cells are light bluish-green, while formalin-preserved material may appear colorless.

7. **Anabaena identification:** Three filament types were recognized. Straight or curved filaments were A. solitaria. Regularly spiralled filaments were A. spiroides, distinguished by oval-ellipsoid spores and dense pseudovacuoles. Irregularly tangled filaments with a central spore mass were A. flos-aquae, which the Canadian specimens (with small cells) placed in that species under Komárek's system.

8. **Possible new records:**
   - *Oscillatoria amphigranulata*, from Lake Waskesiu, is probably a new Canadian record.
   - *Coenocystis subcylindrica* is a new record for North America.

9. **Lyngbya:** A very large species with gas vacuoles in Lac la Ronge (threads up to 27 µm broad) is identified as *L. birgei*. Constrictions at cross-walls varied within one sample, so Lund regards this as an unreliable character unless live material confirms that constrictions are real and not preservative artifacts.

10. **Chrysosphaerella longispina:** Common in the Cree Lake sample. Only the silicified spines and cysts survived preservation, and these matched earlier descriptions.

11. **Desmids:** Uncommon even in the more oligotrophic lakes.

12. **Nomenclature problems:** Lund concludes that many records of these algae are "almost universally useless" unless supported by figures or preserved material, and that much more is needed on clonal variation before a satisfactory taxonomy can be devised for Melosira.
