# Third Workshop on Ecology of Pumped-Storage Research at Twin Lakes, Colorado and Other Localities

#limnology #reservoir-ecology #pumped-storage #environmental-impact #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This workshop proceedings document, compiled by James F. LaBounty in February 1976, summarizes the third annual workshop on ecological effects of pumped-storage hydroelectric operations, with primary focus on the [[Mt. Elbert Unit]] at [[Twin Lakes, Colorado]]. The workshop brought together approximately 60 participants from federal agencies (Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Forest Service, EPA), state agencies (Colorado Division of Wildlife), universities (Michigan State, Arizona State, University of Washington, Colorado State, University of Nevada-Las Vegas, West Virginia University), and utility companies to share research findings on pumped-storage environmental impacts. The preoperation phase of the Twin Lakes study was nearing completion, with the first 100-megawatt unit scheduled for operation in fall 1977. Studies encompassed physical and chemical limnology, fisheries (particularly [[Lake Trout]] and their primary food source, [[Mysis relicta]] shrimp), zooplankton, phytoplankton, benthic organisms, bacteria, sediments, and physical modeling. The workshop expanded beyond Twin Lakes to include reports on pumped-storage operations at [[Ludington Pumped-Storage Plant]] (Michigan), [[Banks Lake]] (Washington), [[Salt River Reservoirs]] (Arizona), and planning studies for [[Lake Mead]] (Nevada-Arizona), [[Kortes and Alcova Reservoirs]] (Wyoming), and the [[Upper Colorado Region]].

## Background & Description

Pumped-storage hydroelectric facilities store energy by pumping water from a lower reservoir to an upper reservoir during periods of low electricity demand, then generating power by releasing water back through turbines during peak demand periods. The [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] is part of the [[Fryingpan-Arkansas Project]], a multipurpose water project extending from central Colorado across the Continental Divide to southeastern Colorado. The powerplant is located on the northwest shore of lower [[Twin Lakes]], at elevation 2802 meters (9193 feet), with maximum depths of 28 meters (upper lake) and 27.1 meters (lower lake). Surface areas are approximately 263 hectares (upper) and 736.5 hectares (lower). Twin Lakes provide one of the best [[Lake Trout]] fisheries in the Western United States, supported primarily by populations of [[Mysis relicta]] (opossum shrimp) introduced in 1957.

The first pump-turbine unit will develop 170,000 horsepower at design operating head of 122-140 meters (400-460 feet), producing 100 megawatts. The unit will pump 74 m³/s (2,600 ft³/s) at 100 percent gate opening and use 98 m³/s (3,450 ft³/s) during generation. A second 100-megawatt unit is scheduled for installation in 1978-1979. The forebay reservoir capacity is 102,206,610 m³ (27 billion gallons) with vertical fluctuation of 20 meters possible within 10 hours. The discharge channel was designed at a 27° angle to the plant centerline, 18.3-meter bottom width, with a 1.5-meter high underwater barrier to encourage withdrawal from higher levels during pumping and influence the inflowing jet during generation.

Historical context reveals that Twin Lakes were created by glacial action and first scientifically documented in the Hayden Geological Survey (1873-1874). By 1889, David Starr Jordan reported the lakes contained suckers, minnows, and two native trout species including the yellowfin trout. In 1901, the Twin Lakes Reservoir Company converted the lakes to irrigation storage by damming the outlet, permitting extreme water level fluctuation of 7.8 meters (lower lake) and 5.8 meters (upper lake). Chancey Juday's 1902-1903 limnological study for the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries documented greater diversity and abundance of zooplankton and fish than presently observed, with Secchi depths averaging 5.5 meters compared to 3 meters in recent years. [[Mackinaw Trout]] (lake trout) were introduced around this time, and [[Mysis relicta]] were successfully introduced by W.D. Klein in 1957, becoming an important food source for the lake trout fishery by 1969.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Twin Lakes are characterized as oligotrophic, cold-water mountain reservoirs with water chemistry showing soft water, calcium as principal cation, and bicarbonate and sulfate as principal anions. Conductivity averaged about 48 µmhos and pH was generally neutral to slightly basic. Temperature regime showed ice cover from December through May, with maximum inverse stratification in April (bottom temperatures 4°C, under-ice temperatures 0.2-1.2°C). Maximum direct stratification occurred in August with surface temperatures of 14.4°C (upper lake) and 16°C (lower lake), and surface-to-bottom differences of 8.2°C and 6.2°C respectively. Fall overturn occurred in early October (lower lake) and about 2 weeks later (upper lake).

Dissolved oxygen profiles paralleled temperature cycles with April and September hypolimnetic minima of 0 mg/l (upper lake) and 2.4-2.75 mg/l (lower lake). Maximum hypolimnetic values of 8.5-10.4 mg/l occurred after spring and fall overturns. Secchi depths ranged from 1 meter (upper lake) to 2 meters (lower lake) in July, increasing to 9 meters (upper) and 4.5 meters (lower) in September, with 3 meters most commonly observed. Primary productivity measurements indicated oligotrophic conditions comparable to [[Lake Tahoe]]. Chlorophyll a measurements showed algae biomass rising toward midsummer in the lower lake then declining, while in the upper lake the rise continued into early winter.

A critical environmental problem emerged during winter 1974-75 when heavy snow cover and prolonged ice cover caused the upper lake hypolimnion to become anaerobic. Heavy metals abundant in bottom sediments (from historical mining runoff and organic debris from a former marsh) became dissolved in toxic form, killing aquatic life. When spring overturn occurred in May, these toxic substances distributed throughout the upper lake. The lower lake was somewhat affected by water from the upper lake but did not become anaerobic. This condition prompted installation of a molecular oxygen bubbling system in winter 1976 to prevent future anaerobic conditions by overcoming biochemical oxygen demand and keeping heavy metals in particulate form.

Sediment core studies using in situ freeze-core sampling revealed a sedimentation rate of 0.62 ± 0.20 mm/yr. Elevated concentrations of copper and zinc in recent sediments of the upper lake corresponded to onset of mining activity approximately 100-125 years ago. Diatom frustule analysis (araphidineae/centrale ratios) indicated that prior to 100-125 years ago both lakes were very similar and oligotrophic, but a sharp increase in A/C ratios in the upper lake (not evident in lower lake) corresponded to cultural activity onset. The upper lake appears to function as a nutrient trap in the system.

## Key Findings

**Twin Lakes Fisheries and Populations:**

- Fishing pressure estimates for 1975: 31,087 fisherman trips, 108,806 hours effort, harvest of 44,589 fish (43,470 [[Rainbow Trout]], 1,119 [[Lake Trout]]), catch per unit effort 0.41
- Marked rainbow trout return of 66 percent, much higher than previous years
- Gill netting in May, August, November yielded 248 lake trout (only 14 more than 1974), indicating stable populations
- Lake trout growth rate slower during first 8-9 years due to dependence on [[Mysis relicta]]
- Fish reach catchable size near fifth year with approximately 50 percent survival rate
- Spawning occurs late October to early November, lasting approximately 2 weeks

**Mysis relicta (Opossum Shrimp) Ecology:**

- Two-year life cycle: recruitment in spring, breeding after 18 months in second winter, males die after breeding, females may survive into second breeding season
- Vertical migrations variable: general pattern of rise to surface after sunset, return below thermocline on summer nights, rapid descent at first morning light; juveniles migrate more extensively than adults
- Horizontal distribution highly clumped (negative binomial distribution), prefer deeper colder water in summer, migrate to shallow water as temperatures drop in fall
- Peak populations in late August-early September with estimates of nearly 135 shrimp per square meter over two-thirds of lower lake bottom
- Mortality greatly increased by 8 hours daily turbulence exposure; mysids show definite avoidance reaction to turbulence
- Photographic counts showed nearly twice as many shrimp per square meter as trawl counts (correlation r = 0.9183)

**Physical Modeling and Hydrodynamics:**

- Angled channel (27° to right of plant centerline) with 18.3-meter bottom width, 1.5-meter high underwater barrier
- During pumping cycle, most flow entered from north shore with velocities about 37 cm/s (1.2 ft/s)
- During generating cycle, average velocities usually less than 30 cm/s (1.0 ft/s) when entering lake
- Temperature measurements showed stratification not affected by two-unit operation, though ambient temperature affected by generating flow temperature
- Cold water inflow lowered lake temperature, warm water inflow increased temperature, but gradient remained essentially the same over 2-week operating period
- Nineteen days simulated operation did not affect upper lake temperature
- Dye tracers in glacial flour deposits did not move during initial 2-3 days operation but gradually assimilated into surrounding water

**Benthic and Plankton Communities:**

- Zooplankton fauna limited to copepods and mysis shrimp (historically more diverse)
- Copepods always more abundant in lower lake; both show definite seasonal trends
- Zooplankton concentrations always higher at thermocline
- Benthic fauna: midge fly larvae and fingernail clams; significant difference between lakes
- No clams found in deepest portion of upper lake
- 100-800 times more midge larvae in lower lake than upper lake
- Seasonal trends indicate continued hatching throughout summer
- Vegetation ranged from dense 2-meter tall [[Potamogeton]] (pondweed) at east end to sparse 6-inch rooted stonewort to 20 meters depth
- Littoral zone (greatest vegetation density) ranged from 3-9 meters depth
- Most rooted vegetation heavily covered with glacial flour

**Bacteriological Studies:**

- Low total and fecal coliform density in water column
- Bottom muds relatively free from coliform bacteria
- High density of iron bacteria in muds; sulfur bacteria detected
- Corrosion problems predicted in buried pipe areas with anaerobic conditions

**Comparative Pumped-Storage Studies:**

[[Ludington Pumped-Storage Plant]] (Michigan): Largest in world, 1,872 megawatts capacity, operating since January 1973. Intake volumes average 312 m³/s (11,000 ft³/s) per turbine (six turbines). Upper reservoir 4 km long, vertical fluctuation 20 meters possible in 10 hours. Little effect on water temperatures, light penetration, turbidity in adjacent [[Lake Michigan]]. Phytoplankton (diatoms) and zooplankton densities monitored; 24 fish species taken, dominated by eight species including [[Yellow Perch]], [[Spottail Shiner]], [[Smelt]], [[Alewife]]. Mortality rates 55-65 percent for pumping modes, 41 percent for generating modes. Rapid fish colonization of reservoir occurred; all Lake Michigan species collected in reservoir during first year.

[[Banks Lake]] (Washington): Created 1951 as equalizing reservoir for [[Columbia Basin Irrigation Project]]. Six pump-generating units being installed at [[Grand Coulee Dam]]. Lake behaves as river-run reservoir during summer with turbulence preventing stratification in northern end. Thermal stratification occurs in southern half. Nitrates generally limiting; oligotrophic system. Nineteen fish species captured; three most abundant: [[Yellow Perch]] (67.5%), [[Lake Whitefish]] (15.0%), [[Kokanee Salmon]] (8.0%). Estimated 1975 entrainment through irrigation canal: 127,787 kokanee (62.6 tons), 242,712 yellow perch (11.8 tons), 19,272 lake whitefish (12.1 tons). Entrainment rates through feeder canal much lower during generation mode.

[[Salt River Reservoirs]] (Arizona): Unique on-channel pumped-storage with pump-turbines at each end of [[Canyon Lake]]. Pumping capacity 6,627 m³/min (Mormon Flat), 6,457 m³/min (Horse Mesa). Pumping operations destroyed thermal stratification within 300 meters of dam; stratification began reestablishing within one hour after pumping ceased. Long-range thermal loading of Canyon Lake appears likely; hypolimnetic temperatures increased from 8°C (1971) to 10.4°C (1974). First observed inverse thermal stratification occurred October-December 1974. Substantial numbers of fish pumped back; highest mortality in first few minutes of operation. [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Walleye]] most affected. Estimated 300-400 kilograms suspended solids moved per 8-hour pumping cycle.

[[Lake Mead]] (Nevada-Arizona): Warm, highly productive, monomictic lake. Develops negative heterograde oxygen profile during stratification; hypolimnion oxygen remains above 60% saturation in deep water. Minimum oxygen concentrations may fall below 1 mg/l in thermocline in most productive areas. Metalimnetic oxygen depletion most severe in [[Las Vegas Bay]], less severe in [[Boulder Basin]] and [[Virgin Basin]]. Zooplankton distributions correspond almost exactly with oxygen depletion curve; zooplankton respiration suggested as primary cause. Total phosphorus 13-67 µg/l; nitrate nitrogen 200-510 µg/l in hypolimnion. Nitrogen becomes limiting in epilimnion during summer stratification. Phytoplankton dominated by diatoms, dinoflagellates, green algae in winter/spring (below 1,000 units/ml). [[Cyclotella atomus]] pulses in summer and autumn reaching 53,000 cells/ml. [[Anabaena circinalis]] dominant August-October (over 2,000 filaments/ml). Autumnal pulse associated with mixing and nitrate recharge from hypolimnion. Concerns raised that destratification from pumped-storage could increase primary productivity, phytoplankton and zooplankton biomass, ultimately causing hypolimnetic oxygen depletion and damage to summer trout fishery.

**Planning and Future Research:**

- Preoperation phase of Twin Lakes study essentially complete; reports being prepared
- Post-operational studies formulated to begin tentatively in 1977
- Need identified for fish passage monitoring at Mt. Elbert plant during construction phase
- Daily and annual shrimp population distribution data can be used to schedule pumping cycles to minimize fishery damage
- Upper Colorado Region investigating up to 15,000 MW additional peaking power needed between 1985-2000, primarily through pumped-storage development
- 115 potential sites identified by Federal Power Commission and Bureau of Reclamation
- Preliminary criteria developed for site selection related to biological, archeological resources, recreation, aesthetics
- [[Kortes and Alcova Reservoirs]] (Wyoming) appraisal investigations scheduled to begin fiscal year 1977
- [[Hoover Dam]] pumped-storage concept involves four 500-megawatt pump generators in underground powerhouses, total capacity 2,000 megawatts
- West Virginia University study initiated January 1976 on influence of pumped-storage flows on thermal stratification in reservoirs
- Workshop conclusions emphasized critical need for baseline information, recognition that each ecological system is unique, difficulty of accurate impact prediction, and need to publish results
