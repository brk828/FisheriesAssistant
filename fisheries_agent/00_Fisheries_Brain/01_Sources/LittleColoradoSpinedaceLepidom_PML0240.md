# Little Colorado Spinedace (Lepidomeda vittata): Biology and Genetics of an Endangered Southwestern Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, submitted to the [[Coconino National Forest]] by Dean W. Binn, Jessica Hagan, and Fred Govedich of the [[Northern Arizona University]] Department of Biological Sciences (September 1996), studies a captive population of [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] held in the [[Arboretum Stream-Pond]] near Flagstaff, Arizona, over three years (1993–1995). The species is restricted to north-flowing tributaries of the Little Colorado River and was listed as threatened by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. The study covered spawning, growth, diet, behavior, and preliminary genetics, and aimed to provide information for management policy.

## Background & Description

The Little Colorado spinedace is a small native cyprinid (maximum about 130 mm total length) in the tribe Plagopterini. It was first described from specimens collected near the headwaters of the Little Colorado River in the White Mountains by the [[Wheeler Expedition]] (Wheeler, 1889). Only four collection records existed before 1939, although [[Miller (1961)]] considered the species once abundant.

Its distribution and abundance have been erratic. A single fish was collected from East Clear Creek in 1960, yet a large population was reported there in 1961. The species was described as endangered in 1961 and listed as threatened by the USFWS in 1987. Surveys in 1994 suggested abundance was rising, but distribution was not expanding. Current populations occur in Chevelon Creek, Nutrioso Creek, Rudd Creek, and Leonard Canyon (a tributary of East Clear Creek, over a 3–5 km section). In 1992, 44 fish were transplanted from Rudd Creek to the Arboretum pond, and summer spawning has continued there since.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The Arboretum stream/pond is a modified cattle tank at 2,167 m elevation (about 6.5 km SSW of Flagstaff). The oval pond covers about 1,408 m² with a maximum depth of 3.6 m. A recirculated stream (about 35 m long, 1–2 m wide, under 0.6 m deep) feeds from it. The pond had no fish before the introduction, although tiger salamanders (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) were present, and dense *Elodea* covered the bottom each summer.

**Thermal and chemical regime.** A thermocline formed at 1–2 m by mid-April and deepened to 2.6–2.8 m by late July. Dissolved oxygen exceeded 8.0 mg/L in the epilimnion, while the bottom 0.75 m stayed anoxic (≤0.5 mg/L) for much of summer. Under-ice temperatures averaged 1.5 °C. Water transparency was low (Secchi depth ≤75 cm) in April–May and rose above 250 cm by late June in 1994 and 1995.

**Predation and competition.** Field enclosure work by [[Blinn et al. (1993)]] showed that introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) strongly influence spinedace distribution. Trout occupied the upper 10 km of a 40 km reach of Nutrioso Creek, while spinedace remained in the lower 30 km. Other threats include [[Green Sunfish]], belostomatid insects, [[Crayfish]] (*Orconectes causeyi*, non-native), kingfishers, garter snakes, and raccoons.

**Invertebrate food base.** Dipteran larvae, oligochaetes, and nematodes made up over 50% of sediment invertebrates by number. Benthic energy was highest in late May (about 16,500 J/m² in 1994) and fell sharply by autumn.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction and spawning**
- Males became ripe at about 70 mm TL and females became gravid at about 80 mm TL.
- Spawning occurred at water temperatures above 14 °C in turbid water, mainly May–June. No spawning occurred in clear water even when temperatures were above 14 °C.
- Broadcast spawning took place over gravel in flowing water (mean velocity 14.2 cm/s; depth 3.9 cm) and was repeated three to four times per summer.
- A 3–4 week delay in spring warming reduced age-0 fish size by up to 16 mm TL, and fish under about 40 mm TL were less likely to overwinter.

**Early life history and growth**
- Fry hatched at 6–7 mm TL and stayed in shallow water near the shore until about 18–20 mm TL. Adults were highly cannibalistic on fry.
- Modal size increased 3–4 mm TL per week from late May to late August. Growth slowed to under 2 mm per month by mid-September.
- Fish reached about 50% of maximum TL (about 100 mm) within three months of hatching. They did not reach maturity in their birth year. Lifespan was typically under 4 years.

**Behavior**
- Schooling occurred in all age classes; the captive fish formed schools of 15–60 fish, which contradicts earlier reports.
- Adults entered the stream at spawning, in small schools of 10–40 fish, mainly between 1030 and 1530 h, when the stream mouth was in direct sunlight.

**Diet**
- The diet was largely opportunistic. Adult flying dipterans made up about 59% (±1.8%) and unidentified eggs about 21% of the summer diet.
- Consumption of adult diptera rose through summer, while aquatic invertebrate use declined.

**Population estimates and mortality**
- About 960 fish over 70 mm TL were estimated in the pond on 1 September 1994, and about 9,000 fish of 35–60 mm TL were estimated at the end of August 1994.
- Mortality appeared high, likely from cannibalism and heteropteran predation.

**Genetics (preliminary)**
- AFLP analysis of three cohorts (under 20 mm, 40–60 mm, over 60 mm) showed very little genetic diversity among cohorts, with no evidence of selection.

**Management implications**
- Temperature and turbidity, possibly with photoperiod (about 14.5 h daylength in May–June), act as proximate cues for reproduction. Natural spring spates that cut water transparency may extend spawning and help explain the species' sporadic distribution.
- Non-native trout, sunfish, and crayfish are major threats, and the species' vulnerability to both predators and erratic distribution makes its status difficult to predict.
