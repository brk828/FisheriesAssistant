# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Research Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #dam-impacts #river-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document outlines a comprehensive research plan for the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program, designed to investigate the ecological and economic impacts of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on the [[Colorado River]] ecosystem through [[Grand Canyon]]. The plan addresses multiple interacting resources including sediment dynamics, [[Rainbow Trout]] fisheries, native [[Humpback Chub]] populations, aquatic productivity, recreation, archaeological sites, and hydroelectric power generation. The research framework identifies controlling variables (both dam-dependent and natural), critical habitats for significant resources, and the interactions between these components. The plan proposes controlled flow experiments during 1990-1996 to test specific hypotheses about how flow regimes, fluctuations, and ramping rates affect downstream resources. A monitoring program is proposed to track long-term responses to management decisions.

## Background & Description

The [[Glen Canyon Dam]] creates a highly managed river system where water releases are controlled primarily for hydroelectric power production, resulting in daily flow fluctuations between approximately 2,000 and 33,500 cfs. The dam traps virtually all sediment from the upper [[Colorado River]] basin in [[Lake Powell]], releasing cold, clear, sediment-free water downstream. This fundamentally altered the pre-dam warm, sediment-laden river ecosystem.

The research plan recognizes the complexity and linkages within the [[Grand Canyon]] ecosystem, spanning from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to upper [[Lake Mead]]. Key controlling variables are categorized as:

**Dam-Dependent Variables:**
- Flow volumes and temporal patterns
- Flow fluctuations (daily, seasonal, annual)
- Ramping rates (rate of change between high and low releases)
- Water temperature and quality (nutrients, salts)

**Downstream-Centered Variables:**
- Canyon geomorphology
- Sediment input from tributaries
- Sediment dynamics (transport, storage, recirculation)
- Vegetation dynamics (riparian and aquatic)
- Bank storage
- Nutrient dynamics
- Invertebrate population dynamics
- Recreational activities
- Management and research activities

The plan identifies significant resources requiring management attention: fish (trout and native species), aquatic food base species (algae like [[Cladophora]], invertebrates including chironomids and simuliids), terrestrial vegetation, wildlife, recreation (fishing and rafting), cultural resources, water storage, and power production.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]] represents a highly altered ecosystem where natural flood regimes have been replaced by hydropower-driven flow patterns. The ecological context is defined by several critical factors:

**Sediment Dynamics:** Sediment is identified as the most critical variable because it creates habitat for multiple resources. With the dam trapping upstream sediment, the system depends entirely on tributary inputs (primarily from the [[Little Colorado River]] and other side canyons) to supply sand and finer materials. Beaches and recirculation zones serve as temporary storage areas, but the balance between aggradation and degradation depends on flow patterns.

**Thermal Regime:** Cold water released from deep in [[Lake Powell]] maintains temperatures suitable for [[Rainbow Trout]] but potentially unsuitable for native warm-water species like [[Humpback Chub]]. The [[Little Colorado River]] provides a critical warm-water refuge and spawning habitat for chub.

**Aquatic Productivity:** The clear, cold, nutrient-poor tailwater supports a food web based on attached algae (particularly [[Cladophora]]) and aquatic invertebrates. Productivity patterns differ from the pre-dam turbid, warm system and may be influenced by flow fluctuations, nutrient availability, and substrate stability.

**Habitat Compartments:** The river system includes distinct habitat types: main channel, recirculation zones (eddies), backwaters, beaches, and tributary mouths. Each responds differently to flow management and provides different ecological functions.

**Species of Concern:**
- [[Rainbow Trout]]: Non-native species supporting a valuable recreational fishery in the upper 15 miles below the dam (Lee's Ferry area)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha): Endangered native species with primary spawning population in the [[Little Colorado River]]
- Other native fish species (not specified in detail)
- Predatory and competitive exotic species including [[Striped Bass]], catfish, and [[Rainbow Smelt]]

## Key Findings

### Research Framework and Approach

The plan establishes a systematic six-step research process: (1) problem identification, (2) literature search, (3) problem refinement into testable hypotheses, (4) research design with sound statistical analysis, (5) research and analysis with periodic monitoring, and (6) long-term monitoring following management decisions.

Research is organized around three overlapping components:
1. Understanding controlling variables within the system
2. Identifying characteristics and critical habitats for significant resources
3. Understanding the magnitude of influence of controlling variables on resources

### Proposed Controlled Flow Experiments

The plan proposes two series of controlled research flows to test hypotheses:

**First Series (February-May, 1990 or 1991):**
- February 1-14: 2,000-20,000 cfs range, 11,000 cfs average, steep ramping
- February 15-28: 2,000-20,000 cfs range, 11,000 cfs average, shallow ramping
- March 1-14: 6,000-16,000 cfs range, 11,000 cfs average, steep ramping
- March 15-28: 6,000-16,000 cfs range, 11,000 cfs average, shallow ramping
- March 29-April 2: constant 11,000 cfs
- April 3-16: 2,000-14,000 cfs range, 8,000 cfs average, steep ramping
- April 17-May 1: 2,000-14,000 cfs range, 8,000 cfs average, shallow ramping
- May 2-15: 6,000-10,000 cfs range, 8,000 cfs average, steep ramping
- May 16-20: constant 8,000 cfs

**Second Series (July-August):**
- July 1-14: 5,000-30,000+ cfs range, 15,000 cfs average, steep ramping
- July 15-28: 5,000-30,000+ cfs range, 15,000 cfs average, shallow ramping
- July 29-August 11: 10,000-20,000 cfs range, 15,000 cfs average, steep ramping
- August 12-25: 10,000-20,000 cfs range, 15,000 cfs average, shallow ramping
- August 25-30: constant 15,000 cfs

Ramping rates defined as: Steep = <4 hours from minimum to maximum flows and return; Shallow = >6 hours from minimum to maximum flows and return.

### Key Hypotheses to be Tested

**Sediment Hypotheses:**
1. Fluctuating flows play a significant role in beach degradation, with large fluctuations and steep ramping rates more degrading than small fluctuations and gradual ramping
2. Net sediment exchange occurs from beaches to channel storage under fluctuating flow regimes
3. Recreational activities contribute to beach degradation, with effects increased by greater flow spreads
4. Peak loading prevents sediment budget equilibrium under present conditions
5. The system loses more sediment than tributaries input regardless of dam operations
6. Fluctuating flows prevent long-term maintenance of backwaters
7. Greater flows and fluctuations increase sediment transport from upper reaches to lower reaches or out of the system
8. Vegetation stabilizes sediment deposits but is increasingly degraded as flows increase
9. Specific flooding event patterns tied to tributary sediment inputs will benefit beach aggradation

**Trout Hypotheses:**
1. Increasing spread in flow fluctuations causes increasing stranding and loss of spawning beds
2. Stranding does not have detrimental impact on trout population and may benefit other ecosystem components
3. Flows below 5,000 cfs (or other threshold) significantly impact the trout population
4. No alternative dam operation will allow trout recruitment to reach equilibrium with fishing pressure through natural reproduction alone

**Nutrients and Productivity Hypotheses:**
1. Wide spreads in fluctuating flows have greater impact on primary and secondary productivity relative to maintaining food base for trout than narrow fluctuations
2. Steep ramping rates reduce productivity availability in upper reaches but enhance it in lower reaches
3. Greater spread in fluctuating flows increases nutrient availability in the stream system
4. Fluctuating flows help maintain sufficient nutrient levels for fish food base

**Humpback Chub Hypotheses:**
1. Flows with wide fluctuations and peak maxima increase potential for reduction of young in the [[Little Colorado River]] chub population
2. The [[Little Colorado River]] is the only viable habitat for chub reproduction in the [[Grand Canyon]] system
3. No dam operational alternative will allow chub to invade the mainstem on a broad population scale
4. Minimum flow of 5,000 cfs (or other threshold) is necessary to prevent large losses of young chub from the [[Little Colorado River]]
5. The [[Little Colorado River]] chub population, along with mainstem adults, is healthy, viable and stable

**Recreation Hypotheses:**
1. Greater spread in fluctuating flows reduces quality of recreational experience

**Archaeology Hypotheses:**
1. Greater spread in fluctuating flows increases basal erosion at near-river archaeological sites

**Economics-Operations Hypotheses:**
1. A flow regime exists that creates an economic balance point between power revenue losses (short and long term) and non-power benefits of the canyon

### Critical Research Questions

**Sediment Dynamics:**
- What flow regime is required to move sediment through the system without net storage changes?
- What flows result in no net change in recirculation zone storage?
- What is the rate and magnitude of channel geometry change under various flow regimes?
- How does sediment move through the system after floods versus after years of power plant flows?
- Are there critical discharges or flow patterns at which processes change?
- Under what combinations of flow rates and fluctuations are beaches most and least degraded?
- Do different eddies respond differently to flows based on local geometry or sediment supply?
- What are beneficial and detrimental effects of floods on sediment storage and transport?
- What level of flood frequency reduction is most beneficial to sediment dynamics?

**Water Quality and Temperature:**
- How does exchange of sediment between eddies, backwaters, and mainstem influence nutrient availability?
- What is the role of tributary clays and fine sediments in nutrient input and adsorption?
- Do different flow regimes influence downstream water chemistry changes?
- How does bank storage affect stream water quality?
- Do varying flows influence residence time of nutrients in the system?
- How is fecal contamination influenced by flow fluctuations?
- Do inundation pulses from flow fluctuations serve as nutrient pulses in backwaters?

**Fish Habitat and Populations:**
- Are backwaters viable fish habitat?
- Does marsh vegetation in backwaters reduce or enhance habitat for fish?
- Do fluctuating flows enhance (through vegetation loss) or degrade backwater use by fish?
- What is the area of spawning habitat under various flows?
- How does distribution of each species relate to flow and temperature?
- What is the relative significance of fish habitat in different eddy types?
- What are survival rates of different life stages to temperature gradients?
- What are relationships between fish productivity and flow and substrate?
- Are there ramping rates detrimental to trout survival?
- Can productivity rates of algae and primary consumers be used to model fish productivity?
- How does angler success affect the trout population?
- What are relative effects of fishing pressure versus fluctuating flows on trout survival?

**Recreation:**
- Is quality of rafting experience influenced by beach availability?
- Is beach availability causing congestion in rafting trips?
- What is relative importance of beach size, accessibility, vegetation cover, and location on beach use?
- How does interaction of flow regimes and debris hydraulics influence rafting experience?
- Do different flow regimes cause downstream clumping of rafting parties?
- Does National Park Service carrying capacity affect quality of rafting experience?
- What flow regimes restrict boat access to upper reach trout fisheries?
- How do different flow regimes influence quality or value of upstream trout fishing experience?
- What alternatives reduce safety and equipment damage problems from flow fluctuations?
- What management techniques should be used to assure maintenance of a blue-ribbon fishery?
- What is the "value" of hatchery versus river-spawned trout?
- How does perception of catching river-spawned trout affect quality of fishing experience?

**Vegetation:**
- What is relationship between sediment aggradation/degradation and establishment of riparian vegetation by species?
- Are there flow regimes that enhance vegetational establishment on beaches to detriment of beach use?
- Are there flood event levels beneficial and/or detrimental to riparian species establishment and maintenance?

**Cultural Resources:**
- What levels of flows (including flood events) are detrimental to identified archaeological locations?

**Hydroelectric Power:**
- How do additional constraints on release patterns affect net benefits of power production?
- How do changes in values of downstream resources affect net value of power production and system value?
- What would be new resource requirements and environmental impacts if hydroelectric production were reduced or altered?

### Proposed Monitoring Program

The plan proposes establishing a long-term monitoring program based on research findings to track responses to operational decisions. Key monitoring variables include:

**Sediment:**
- Rates of beach erosion through topographic changes
- Sediment lost (bedload transport) relative to tributary inputs

**Trout:**
- Stability of food source
- Reduction in stranding and desiccation of redds (spawning beds)

**Humpback Chub:**
- Population in [[Little Colorado River]], mainstem, and other tributaries
- Stability of spawning areas (if known)
- Status of predatory fish

### Research Development Needs

The plan identifies need for Request for Proposals (RFPs) for external literature search and analysis on:
1. Non-game or non-endangered fish species including [[Striped Bass]], catfish, [[Rainbow Smelt]], and other predatory or competitive species, focusing on temperature tolerances, sediment tolerances, reproductive requirements, food requirements, and projections of success under selected operational scenarios
2. Other topics to be determined

### Projected Research Timeline (1990-2000)

The plan outlines a decade-long research program including:
- Canyon geometry studies
- Sediment models (flow routing, solute transport, tributary/debris, flow dynamics, beach sediment, eddy mechanics)
- Biological process models (primary productivity/trout population, vegetation/beach dynamics, secondary productivity/food chain dynamics, backwaters, total river nutrient budget models)
- Recreation models (beach degradation)
- Economics models (environmental/power tradeoffs)
- Other research ([[Lake Powell]] profiles, water/sediment chemistry, [[Humpback Chub]] conservation measures, autotroph/heterotroph stream interface, archaeological impacts)
