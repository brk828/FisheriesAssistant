# Population Trends of the Native Fish Assemblage in Bonita Creek, Arizona, USA

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tracked five native fish species in [[Bonita Creek]], a desert stream within the [[Gila Box Riparian National Conservation Area]] in southeastern [[Arizona]], over 2005–2016. Four fixed sites were surveyed each spring by [[Electrofishing]], and [[Mesohabitat]] proportions were mapped from 2011 to 2016. Populations were largely stable after protective measures were applied. [[Speckled Dace]] increased significantly at one site, while [[Sonora Sucker]] declined significantly at two sites. The authors conclude that exclusion of human activities and [[Nonnative Fish]] helped halt further decline, although sustained monitoring and restoration are still needed. Mesohabitat proportions did not explain most population trends.

## Background & Description

The native fish assemblage of Bonita Creek includes five species:

- [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*): listed as endangered under the US Endangered Species Act and extirpated or reduced in most of its historical range in the [[Gila River]] basin.
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarkii*)
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)

Context and background:

- The Bonita Creek Basin covers approximately 1269 km² and is partly within the Gila Box.
- The Gila Box was established under the Arizona Desert Wilderness Act of 1990 to protect water bodies, riparian areas, and their native fish. Roads were closed, and all-terrain vehicle use and livestock grazing were prohibited.
- Within the Gila River basin, 57% of the 21 historically native fish species are imperiled or have been extirpated.
- At least 40 nonnative fish species have established in the Gila River since the 1900s. Nine nonnative species have invaded the lower 5-mile section of Bonita Creek.
- In Arizona, nonnative fish outnumber native fish 2:1.
- Creek segments are separated by an [[Infiltration Gallery]] operated by the City of Safford, where most surface flow is diverted for municipal use. In 2008, the Bureau of Reclamation built a [[Fish Barrier]] to block nonnative fish moving upstream from the Gila River and lower reaches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian importance.** Riparian zones are among the most ecologically productive lands in the southwestern United States, despite covering a small fraction of the land surface (<2%). They provide thermal buffering, invertebrate food, and woody debris and root wads as habitat for fish. Their protection and restoration are considered top environmental priorities.

**Threats.** Declines in native fish result largely from long-term water development and poor watershed practices that have altered riverine habitat. Many desert fishes have restricted distributions and are often unique genetically and considered evolutionarily significant units. Nonnative fish threaten them through predation, competition, hybridization, habitat alteration, and parasite and pathogen transmission.

**Habitat preferences (from literature).**

- Longfin dace: riffle habitat with smooth, laminar flow over gravel-sand substrate.
- Speckled dace: riffles with swift, turbulent flow over gravel-pebble substrate.
- Gila chub: traditionally considered a calm-water and pool species, though large individuals in Bonita Creek may use moderate to shallow, currented water.
- Sonora sucker: a "pool dweller" that favors slow current over sand or fine gravel.
- Desert sucker: turbulent water, rapids, and flowing streams with gravelly bottoms.

**Mesohabitat types surveyed.** Pools (deep, nonturbulent, still), runs (turbulent, swift, moderately shallow), riffles (shallow, moderately turbulent, fast), and glides (nonturbulent, low to moderate velocity; transitional between pool and riffle).

## Key Findings

**Study design and methods**

- Four fixed 200-m fish-monitoring sites: [[Reservation Boundary]], [[Midnight Canyon]], [[Red Knolls]], and [[Lee Trail]]. All were above the infiltration gallery.
- Annual surveys in May, 2005–2016, by crews of about 4–6 people using backpack electrofishers. Each survey lasted about one day.
- Mesohabitat surveys at all sites, 2011–2016, following American Fisheries Society methods.
- Abundance was expressed as [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (CPUE, fish per hour).
- Trends were analyzed with the [[Mann-Kendall Trend Test]], with Sen's slope as the effect size.
- Site differences used Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA with pairwise Mann-Whitney U tests and Bonferroni correction.
- Associations between abundance and mesohabitat proportions used [[Kendall Tau Correlation]].
- Sites were analyzed separately because fish are generally sedentary and populations do not communicate.

**Abundance and variation**

- Interannual variation was high for all species.
- Speckled dace was the most abundant species, with mean CPUE ranging up to 323.0 fish/h (SD 221.5) at Midnight Canyon. The lowest mean was for longfin dace at Lee Trail, at 11.6 fish/h (SD 8.9).
- Desert sucker was the least abundant species in the survey.

**Population trends**

- Speckled dace showed a significant positive monotonic trend at Midnight Canyon, driven by an increase during 2012–2016.
- Sonora sucker showed a significant negative trend at Reservation Boundary and Red Knolls.
- Longfin dace, desert sucker, and Gila chub showed no significant trends.
- Overall, populations were mostly stable across the 12-year period, with the two exceptions above.
- Abundance of all native species mostly increased shortly after the 2008 fish barrier, especially during 2010–2012, then stabilized near pre-2008 levels, except for the Sonora sucker.

**Between-site differences**

- Gila chub, Sonora sucker, and desert sucker showed no significant differences among sites (Kruskal-Wallis H = 3.056, P > 0.353).
- Longfin dace differed significantly among sites (H = 7.031, P = 0.030). It was more abundant at Reservation Boundary, which had higher proportions of riffles, runs, and glides, than at pool-dominated Lee Trail (Mann-Whitney U = 24.000, P = 0.006).
- Speckled dace also differed significantly (H = 8.662, P = 0.034). It was more abundant at Midnight Canyon than at Lee Trail (U = 24.000, P = 0.006).

**Habitat associations**

- Mesohabitat proportions varied strongly over time at Reservation Boundary and Midnight Canyon. Pools dominated at Red Knolls and Lee Trail.
- Speckled dace and Sonora sucker were significantly positively associated with riffles at Red Knolls.
- Sonora sucker was significantly negatively associated with glides at Reservation Boundary.
- All other associations were highly variable and nonsignificant.
- Changes in abundance were not correlated with changes in mesohabitat proportions, suggesting other factors drive abundance variation.

**Interpretation**

- The authors suggest that the desert sucker's historical abundance was linked to attached algae, which were grazed by livestock before grazing was banned. As riparian shade returned, algal food declined.
- The Sonora sucker decline at two sites cannot be explained by the reported habitat trends. The authors single out Red Knolls, which is pool-dominated.
- Although no nonnative fish were recorded in this 12-year survey, earlier work found persistent populations of green sunfish, fathead minnow, western mosquitofish, and yellow bullhead in lower Bonita Creek. Predation and competition from these species may still pressure native fish.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- Exclusion of human activities and nonnative fish helped halt further decline of the studied populations, although the benefit was short-lived in the upper creek.
- Full recovery requires more effort.
- Continued monitoring of population dynamics and habitat is needed. Reinvading nonnative fish should be detected early to guide targeted restoration.
