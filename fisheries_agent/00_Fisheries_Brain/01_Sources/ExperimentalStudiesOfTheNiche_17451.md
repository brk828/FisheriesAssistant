# Experimental Studies of the Niche

#community-ecology #niche-theory #experimental-ecology

## Executive Summary

This review, published in the *Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics* (Volume 6, 1975) by Robert K. Colwell and Eduardo R. Fuentes, synthesizes experimental studies of how biotic interactions shape the [[Ecological Niche]] of populations within co-evolved communities. The authors restrict scope to studies involving two or more species from the same community, excluding most population-cage experiments, microcosms, natural experiments, and applied fields such as pollution biology and parasitology. The synthesis organizes evidence by interaction type: competition, predation and parasitism, mutualism, one-sided dependency, and complex interactions. The central conclusions are that (1) populations usually occupy a narrower range of conditions than they could physiologically tolerate, (2) interference mechanisms are common in competition, (3) when niches are nested, the specialist typically interferes with the generalist, and (4) community structure may be predictable even when species composition is not. Fish appear only marginally, mainly in citation lists and brief mentions.

## Background & Description

The review frames the niche as a phenotypic attribute of a conspecific population, defined as a hypervolume whose axes represent biotic and abiotic factors. The set of niches in a community is termed the "columbarium" by the authors, a deliberately coined term. A positive response to an environment (persistence, growth, abundance, reproduction, or dominance) marks a point as part of the niche, and response level is treated as a measure of fitness.

Key conceptual distinctions include:

- **[[Exploitation Competition]]:** indirect competition through depletion of a shared resource.
- **[[Interference Competition]]:** direct interaction that prevents a competitor from realizing its maximum exploitation efficiency, such as shading, aggression, or territoriality.
- **[[Fundamental Niche]] vs. [[Realized Niche]]:** the authors apply Hutchinson's distinction in a broad sense, acknowledging that the fundamental niche is hard to define operationally.
- **Niche expansion vs. contraction:** results are reported as the expansion of a niche under experimental reduction of an interacting species, or contraction under experimental removal of a dependent partner.

The paper also defines the criteria for interaction patterns:

- **[[Niche Inclusion]]:** the fundamental niche of one species is a subregion of another's.
- **[[Reciprocal Niche Overlap]]:** each species has an exclusive region of niche space, so coexistence is assured across heterogeneous conditions.
- **[[Coextensive Niches]]:** fundamental niches overlap completely, and coexistence depends on each species being superior in a different subregion.
- **Nonoverlapping niches:** presented as a possible evolutionary outcome of past competition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The review's scope covers a wide range of systems. Notable examples include:

- **Parasitic helminths in rats:** [[Holmes Helminth Experiments]] tested [[Hymenolepis Diminuta]] (tapeworm) and [[Moniliformis Dubius]] (acanthocephalan) in laboratory rats. The acanthocephalan displaced the tapeworm from the anterior intestine, and the authors suggest interference, with carbohydrate gradients as a possible resource basis.
- **Thermal algal mats:** Castenholz and colleagues studied [[Oscillatoria Terebriformis]] and [[Synechococcus Lividus]] in hot spring outflows. Oscillatoria shades out Synechococcus below about 54°C. Grazing by the ostracod *Potamocypris* limits Oscillatoria to temperatures above about 46–48°C, creating a refuge.
- **Intertidal mussels:** [[Mytilus Edulis]] predominates in sheltered bays, while [[Mytilus Californianus]] dominates exposed sites. Harger's work showed that silting smothers young M. californianus in calm habitats, while wave action tears M. edulis free in exposed habitats.
- **Salamanders:** Jaeger's work on [[Plethodon Cinereus]] and [[Plethodon Shenandoah]] showed that the moisture-specialist P. cinereus has a fundamental niche included within that of the physiological generalist P. shenandoah, with food competition as the likely mechanism.
- **Drosophila and cacti:** Fellows & Heed studied three cactiphilic *Drosophila* species with roughly coextensive fundamental niches, and [[Drosophila Pachea]] is monophagous on the [[Senita Cactus]], which contains a sterol the fly requires.
- **Bumblebees and flowers:** Brian showed that bumblebee tongue length matched flower tube depth, but that the dominance hierarchy was the reverse of tongue-length order, suggesting interference.
- **Mutualisms:** Janzen's [[Acacia-Ant Mutualism]] and the Pleurocapsa–Calothrix association illustrate protection and nutrient exchange. Penguin nesting beneath shrubs in the Straits of Magellan enriches otherwise nitrogen-poor soils.

Fish are touched upon only briefly. The review cites fish in a list of coextensive-niche studies, and mentions a manuscript on three Nicaraguan cichlid species in which the guarding of fry links competition with predation. It also notes that in many fish and amphibians, adults may prey on the young of another species while competing with its adults.

## Key Findings

1. **Niche expansion under reduced interaction is common.** Many experiments show that populations occupy only a subset of the conditions they could physiologically tolerate. Interspecific competition, predation, and parasitism are all shown to restrict realized niches.

2. **Interference is often underestimated.** The authors argue that it is very difficult to prove that interference is absent. In a hypothetical shading example, selection for shading can increase fitness without any gain in photosynthetic efficiency.

3. **Specialists tend to win interference contests.** In every case where interference was shown in niche inclusion, the specialist interfered successfully with the generalist. Morse (1974) reached a similar conclusion from broader categorization.

4. **Predation can create refuges and maintain coexistence.** In hot springs, the thermal refuge of Oscillatoria from grazing ostracods is a clear example. Differential predation can allow species with coextensive niches to coexist, as summarized by [[Joseph Connell]] in his reviews.

5. **Mutualism is context-dependent.** Mutualistic benefits often exist only within specific communities. For instance, the ant–acacia relationship has no benefit without herbivores and competing plants.

6. **Community structure may be more predictable than composition.** Artificial substrate studies suggest that structure may be relatively predictable in similar abiotic environments, while species lists are much less so. This points to general patterns that may emerge from careful study of biotic interactions.

7. **Higher-order and frequency-dependent interactions complicate niche models.** The niche hypervolume is rarely a simple intersection of pairwise interactions. A field example of a [[Batesian Mimicry]] system illustrates how fitness can depend on the densities of several species and on frequency.

8. **Experimental evidence is limited for vertebrates.** Controlled experiments on vertebrate niche relationships are difficult, and most reviewed examples face methodological criticisms. Rodents provide the most cases, with a single bird study (Davis 1973) suggesting niche inclusion between juncos and golden-crowned sparrows.

9. **Priority in the mosaic environment matters.** The authors note that priority and stochastic effects affect community development, which may complicate predictions based on niche theory alone.

10. **The authors call for future work.** Suggested priorities include experiments on pollinator and plant interactions, manipulation of the floral environment, testing of parasite-mediated interference hypotheses, and experiments on thermophilic algae to examine limiting similarity under varied thermal regimes.
