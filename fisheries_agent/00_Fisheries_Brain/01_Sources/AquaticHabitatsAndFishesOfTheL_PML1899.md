# Aquatic Habitats and Fishes of the Lower Colorado River, Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation final report (contract 14-06-300-2529, May 1979, by W. L. Minckley, [[Arizona State University]]) surveys the lowermost [[Colorado River]] mainstream (Davis Dam to the International Boundary, 1974–1976). It characterizes physical, chemical and biological habitats, quantifies fish abundance by habitat parameter, analyzes food habits, and discusses the historical and present fish assemblage. The river is highly modified, flow-regulated and dominated by introduced fishes; native species are extirpated or extremely rare. The authors conclude that the present fauna is a guild of generalists that persists on a narrow food base, and that stocking-based management should follow careful ecological assessment.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** About 453 km of river from Davis Dam (RM 422.0) to the International Boundary (RM 704.2), divided into 10 operational divisions (Mohave Valley, Topock Gorge, Havasu, Parker, Palo Verde, Cibola, Imperial, Laguna, Yuma, Limitrophe). The river forms the Nevada–Arizona, California–Arizona, and Arizona–Sonora/Baja borders.
- **Climate:** Sonoran Desertscrub; local rainfall seldom exceeds 12 cm/yr; summer air temperatures often exceed 40 °C.
- **Historical context:** Before closure of [[Hoover Dam]] (1935), the river carried very high sediment loads (more than 190 million metric tons per year in 1925–1935) and extreme discharge variability. Subsequent dams, channelization, and riprap converted it to a constrained, flow-regulated channel.
- **Channel dimensions:** Channelized width averages about 150 m; depths average about 3.5 m in channelized segments.
- **Study objectives:** Identify and quantify aquatic habitats; obtain relative abundance of organisms (principally fishes); identify food-web relations; and determine habitat contributions to ecosystem maintenance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat structure.** Bottoms are armored by gravel and boulder below dams, while sand dominates the lower reach (over 65% of samples below Imperial Dam). Velocities are generally higher in channelized reaches (more than 30% of records exceeded 1.0 m/s there, against 16% in unchannelized Parker reach). Shoreline diversity is lowest on cut banks and sandbars and highest on uncut, vegetated banks.

**Temperature and oxygen.** Water below [[Davis Dam]] is cool (15–16 °C in summer, 12–14 °C in winter) because of hypolimnetic release from [[Lake Mohave]]. Downstream temperatures rise; summer drains in Limitrophe Division exceeded 40 °C in August 1974, with localized fish mortality. Dissolved oxygen generally remained above 60% saturation, with diurnal supersaturation in vegetated reaches and deoxygenated deeper backwater areas.

**Chemistry.** Conductance increased downstream from about 900 µmhos/cm (Mohave Valley) to above 4,000 µmhos/cm in Limitrophe Division after the [[Gila River]] inflow. Drains were more saline (up to about 2,000 µmhos/cm). Phosphate-phosphorus was generally ≤0.1 mg/L in upper divisions; nitrate-nitrogen was higher near Davis Dam. Turbidity rose markedly in Limitrophe Division (1.0–160 JTU; mean 14.5 JTU).

**Biota.** Submergent macrophytes (notably sago pondweed, *Potamogeton pectinatus*) were moderate to dense in Mohave, Havasu and Parker divisions. Standing crops reached 805 g/m² in Parker Division. Drift of particulate organic matter exceeded 100 g/m³ in most Parker Division samples. Benthic invertebrates were sparse in the shifting-sand channel and most abundant in backwaters and the lower divisions; the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula manilensis*) was dominant in some segments, with biomass sometimes exceeding 68 kg/ha.

**Fish assemblage.** Forty-four kinds of fishes have been recorded; 20 constitute the present fauna, of which 12 were taken in the survey. Only one native fish, the bonytail chub, persists in marginal numbers in Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu, and the native flannelmouth sucker, razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish are rare or extirpated from the mainstream.

## Key Findings

**Fish assemblage and status**
- Nine native species were historically present; all are now extirpated from the mainstream or exceedingly rare. Only one native fish was taken in the survey.
- Of 44 species recorded, 15 were seen only once, recently introduced without reproduction, or too rare to collect; 13 fishes are classed as "hypothetical" occurrences.
- Introduced species include [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Mosquitofish]].

**Relative abundance (seine data, 111 mainstream sets; 24 backwater sets)**
- Mainstream: red shiner dominated (average 42.4% of individuals); about 52,000 fish were taken, averaging 1.7 individuals/m².
- Backwaters: about 21,700 fish were taken (0.83 individuals/m²); red shiner comprised 56.5%.
- In Yuma Valley canals, seines captured an estimated 2.7% of fish killed by a 1974 pesticide spill (9,940 seined vs. 32,400 killed).

**Gill and hoop netting (487 mainstream sets; 128 backwater sets)**
- Channel catfish were the most abundant netted fish in the mainstream (17.1%), followed by bluegill (16.2%), redear sunfish (14.3%) and carp (11.7%).
- Backwater nets were dominated by threadfin shad and carp; softshell turtles were rare there.

**Habitat relations**
- Red shiner was the only species with little regard for current type or bottom, and it fed on drifting materials; it was a major prey item.
- Most small fishes avoided strong currents and cut or modified banks; juvenile largemouth bass and sunfishes were closely associated with submergent vegetation in backwaters.
- Carp, channel catfish and largemouth bass occupied a broad range of habitats, and adults moved into deep water (>2 m).

**Food habits**
- Detritus formed the main stomach contents of threadfin shad, red shiner, sailfin molly, mouthbrooder and striped mullet.
- Chironomid larvae were a near-universal food. Asiatic clams were eaten by carp, channel catfish, yellow bullhead and redear sunfish; crayfish were major foods of large carnivores.
- Striped bass in the Davis Dam area fed almost entirely on threadfin shad drawn through penstocks (Edwards 1974); 45% of 100 stomachs examined were empty.

**Historical and trophic inferences**
- The pre-dam river supported a small fauna of three large-river fishes in the channel. Flows, sediment loads and shallow braided channels likely supported lentic, detritus-based food webs.
- Recent faunal change is attributed mainly to predation by introduced adults and juveniles on early life stages of native fishes, and to conversion of lentic habitats to a fauna of generalists.

**Management conclusions**
- The current fauna is a guild of generalists supported by a narrow food base. Stabilization is lacking, and the native fauna cannot be re-established while predatory species persist.
- Monitoring of food base and consumers, and assessment of habitat requirements before further stocking, are recommended.
