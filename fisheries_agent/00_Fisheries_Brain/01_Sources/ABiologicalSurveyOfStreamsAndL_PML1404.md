# Biological Survey of Streams and Lakes, Coconino National Forest, Arizona (1935)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1935 report by M. J. Madsen, Temporary Assistant Biologist, records a biological, chemical and physical survey of streams and lakes in the [[Coconino National Forest]] carried out by the [[Bureau of Fisheries]] field party from late spring to 27 August of the survey season. The survey aimed to supply the data needed to choose fish species, sizes and numbers for stocking, and to identify habitat improvements. The forest's waters were generally in poor condition because of a severe multi-year drought. The report makes recommendations on stream improvement, lake management, and stocking for trout and warm-water species.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and methods.** The survey was intended as a broad condition assessment, not a complete study of any single water body. Its stated goals were to (1) establish the biological, chemical and physical conditions of streams and lakes; (2) use these data to decide the species, size and number of fish to stock; and (3) identify environmental changes that could increase productivity.

**Field methods.**
- Stream stations were 200-yard reaches. Lake stations were areas that could be worked from an anchored boat.
- Altitude and gradient were measured with an altimeter and a clinometer. Stream area was calculated from measured widths and depths.
- Temperatures were taken with a Negretti-Zambra reversing thermometer. Turbidity was measured with a standard white disc (Secchi-type) reading.
- Field chemistry included pH (colorimetric tubes with bromthymol blue), free carbon dioxide (phenolphthalein titration against N/44 sodium hydroxide), dissolved oxygen (Winkler method), and alkalinity (titration with 0.02 N sulfuric acid).
- Fish were sampled with gill nets, seines, dip nets, wire traps, angling and direct observation.
- Bottom food was sampled with a square-foot net in streams and an Ekman dredge in lakes. Plankton was collected with a standard net.

**Survey party.** M. J. Madsen, with assistant biologists Paul Kllnsenberg (spelling uncertain in scan), Harry G. Moser, and Ivan Hirdspoth (?). Acknowledgments go to the Arizona State Game Commission, the Forest Service and local sportsmen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Setting.** The forest covers about 3,000 square miles on the Colorado Plateau, north of Flagstaff, and contains headwaters of two major drainages: the Colorado (about 1,000 square miles) and the Verde (about 1,000 square miles). Most permanent water lies in the Verde drainage, which flows to the Salt River and then the Colorado. Elevations range from roughly 1,600 feet to about 11,500 feet, with an average near 6,800 feet (scan-garbled values, approximate).

**Drought.** The survey took place during an unusually severe drought. Many streams had lost much of their flow, and most lakes had receded to very low levels.

**Stream conditions.**
- Streams are steep and rapid in the upper reaches and sluggish in the lower reaches. The average gradient was about 150 feet per mile (approximate, scan-affected).
- Upper-stream temperatures were cool, with a maximum reported near 70°F. Lower reaches were warmer, near 80°F.
- Pool and riffle conditions were below normal in most streams. [[Oak Creek]] was the only stream that had not suffered a serious water shortage and showed normal pool and riffle relations.
- Drought-stranded pools produced dense, but temporary, algal and higher-plant growth.

**Lake conditions.**
- The lakes sit in closed basins on the Coconino Plateau, mostly at about 7,000 feet. They have no permanent inflow or outflow and depend on storm runoff.
- Water-level fluctuations of one to eight feet per year were recorded, depending on the lake.
- Most lakes are very shallow, with soft mud bottoms and sparse cover. Turbidity is high because of muddy shores and shallow water.
- The lakes formed largely from the ponding of cattle-trampled, water-tight ground in former grass flats. Some were built as stock tanks, irrigation reservoirs, or for logging transport.

**Food organisms.** Sampling found mayflies, caddisflies, stoneflies, midges, water mites, dragonfly and damselfly nymphs, snails, scuds, crayfish-type crustaceans, black fly larvae, and dobsonfly/hellgrammite larvae. Terrestrial insects that fell into the water also made up a large share of the summer diet of trout and bass, though these were not quantified.

**Predators and disease.** Great Blue Heron and kingfishers were present but not considered a serious threat. Game fish showed little evidence of disease or parasites. Suckers from West Clear Creek carried a small flatworm-like parasite on their pectoral fins.

## Key Findings

**Streams surveyed.** Four watersheds were covered, with total and permanent lengths as follows:
- [[Beaver Creek]] (Verde drainage): 40 miles total, 20 miles permanent.
- [[East Clear Creek]] (Colorado drainage): 50 miles total, 9 miles permanent.
- [[Oak Creek]] (Verde drainage): 42 miles total, 38 miles permanent (per text).
- [[West Clear Creek]] (Verde drainage): 40 miles total, 30 miles permanent.

**Lakes surveyed.** Fourteen named lakes were surveyed, including [[Mormon Lake]] (about 6,400 acres at high water, per Forest Service estimate), Mary, Stoneman, Annex, Kinnikinick, Ashurst, Boot, Cow, Horse, Long, Mud, Marshall, Soldier and Vail lakes. Only five held water in the drought: Annex, Kinnikinick, Mary, Mormon and Stoneman. Roughly eleven others, including Dax, Deep, Duck Nest, Fisher and Fry, Hay, Myrtle, Potato, Pickett, Rogers and Red lakes, were dry when first visited in early June.

**Benthic food abundance.** Bottom-fauna counts were classed as:
- Grade I (richest): more than 175 animals per square foot.
- Grade II (average): 75 to 175 per square foot.
- Grade III (poor): fewer than 75 per square foot.

The overall average was about 812 organisms per square foot across all streams (scan reading). Mayflies averaged 76 per square foot, caddisflies 64, and stoneflies 80 (scan readings). Oak Creek and West Clear Creek had the richest food supplies, while East Clear Creek and Beaver Creek were poor because of low water.

**Game and other fish in streams.** Game fish listed in order of abundance were rainbow trout, brown trout, rock bass, catfish, and bluegill sunfish. These were present in most streams but not abundant. Suckers and bonytails (as written in the text) occurred in every stream, mostly in lower, warmer reaches. Current stocking was not enough to meet fishing demand.

**Lake fish.** Yellow perch, largemouth black bass, bullhead catfish, rock bass, golden shiner, crappie, bluegill sunfish, and rainbow trout were present. Yellow perch growth was slow (about four years to reach a small, catchable size). Largemouth bass growth was poor. Rainbow trout grew well. Lake fish appeared free of disease and parasites.

**Recommended improvements.**
- Streams: Improvement of Beaver Creek, West Clear Creek and the main East Clear Creek was not recommended, because flooding made such work impractical. The smaller tributaries of East Clear Creek were judged best suited for improvement. Water conservation, check dams and large, deep pools were advised.
- Lakes: Close the black bass season during spawning (about May through early July). Remove size limits on yellow perch. Install spawning boxes for bass. Add artificial cover and aquatic and shore vegetation (except at Stoneman Lake). Build dikes to reduce wave action and enlarge natural drainage channels.
- Mary Lake: Stabilize water levels by permanently filling the underground drainage channels, or by building a permanent dam at a suitable point. A geologist was recommended before any such work.

**Stocking recommendations.**
- [[Smallmouth Black Bass]] for all of Beaver Creek, West Clear Creek from Bull Pen Ranch to the Verde River, and 21 miles of Oak Creek below Sedona. Gravel bars and abundant forage were noted. Plantings were recommended about once every third year.
- [[Brown Trout]] for isolated sections of Oak Creek and West Clear Creek that are separated from upstream waters by impassable falls.
- [[Rainbow Trout]] or cutthroat trout for cooler upper stream sections. Heavy fishing was expected to remove an entire planting within two weeks.
- Mormon Lake should receive both trout and warm-water species to determine which performs better.
- The stocking tables for trout (by fish weight class per acre) and warm-water fish were based on a planting table by Dr. G. C. Sumner (name partly garbled in scan). The report notes that the supporting data were incomplete and that the plans were provisional.

**Caveat.** The report states that these recommendations are provisional. Accurate data on fish removed annually, production, and growth rates were not yet available, and the authors called for further study before definitive conclusions could be drawn.
