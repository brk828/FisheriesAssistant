# Channel Catfish Abundance, Growth, and Diet in the Green and Yampa Rivers, Colorado and Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the introduced [[Channel Catfish]] (*[[Ictalurus Punctatus]]*) in the [[Green River]] (517 km sampled) and lower [[Yampa River]] (74 km sampled) in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], Colorado and Utah, in 1987 and 1988. The fish was most abundant in rocky canyon habitats with average stream gradients above 2 m/km. Growth was slow compared with other U.S. populations, which the authors attribute to cold water, a short growing season, and elevated summer flows. Diet was omnivorous, with piscivory restricted to larger fish. The authors conclude that the species' abundance, wide distribution, and feeding habits may threaten rare and endangered native fishes, although direct evidence of decline is limited.

## Background & Description

Channel catfish are native to large streams tributary to the [[Mississippi River]], [[Great Lakes]], and [[Gulf of Mexico]]. They have been widely introduced elsewhere. Stocking in the [[Colorado River Basin]] dates to 1892, and introductions to Utah lakes occurred about 1888. Stocking was originally intended to enhance sportfishing in a faunally depauperate river. Angling for channel catfish in the Green and Yampa rivers is now virtually nonexistent.

Little was known previously about growth, food habits, or interactions with native fauna in these rivers. Young fish examined at the end of their first growing season were small, and large individuals were rare. This led the authors to hypothesize that environmental conditions in the Green River basin may not be optimal for growth. A prior slow-growth report from the Yampa River (23 fish) was considered too small a sample to interpret.

Methods included:
- **Capture:** [[Electrofishing]] (spring shoreline sampling), seining, and [[Angling]] (July to August) by U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service personnel.
- **Sampling:** Seven Green River reaches (River Kilometer 35 to 552) and one Yampa River reach (0 to 74 km) were sampled. Reaches were chosen to contrast low-gradient sand/silt sites (0.2 to 0.4 m/km) with higher-gradient boulder/cobble sites (1.3 to 2.1 m/km).
- **Aging:** [[Pectoral Spine Aging]] with sectioning and annulus counts, with lengths estimated by [[Back-Calculation]]. Annulus formation was assumed to occur on 1 January by convention.
- **Statistics:** [[Analysis of Variance]] (ANOVA) for back-calculated lengths, [[Condition Factor]] (K), and diet-related size differences. Simple linear regression related growth to discharge and temperature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and abundance.** The fish was most abundant in rocky, turbulent canyon reaches. Electrofishing catch rates were highest in the Yampa River (19.6 fish/h) and upper Green River stratum G (11 fish/h). Angling in eddies gave 1.3 fish/h (n = 46, 34.5 h) in canyon habitats, compared with 0.5 fish/h (n = 19, 36 h) in sand/silt-laden sections.

**Community composition.** The most abundant species in spring electrofishing (6,097 fish total) were [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (31.8%), [[Common Carp]] (29.6%), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (16.2%), channel catfish (8.3%), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (5.5%).

**Water temperature and flow.** Water temperatures in the Green River exceeded 21°C only from mid-July to early September. Channel catfish grow poorly below 21–22°C, and the summer growth period averaged 18 to 22°C, well below the 30 to 35°C optimum reported by Layher and Maughan (1985). The fish may also lose weight below 15°C.

**Sediment and turbidity.** Sediment loads in the Green River ranged from 19 to 66,000 mg/l, and suspended loads from about 110 to 1,700 mg/l. Layher and Maughan (1985) reported optimal water clarity below 30 mg/l. High turbidity may limit predation on fish.

**Nursery habitat.** Shoreline embayments served as young-of-year nurseries. These were about 1.5 times more numerous in low-to-average discharge years. Under high summer flows, embayments were inundated, and young fish were smaller and less numerous.

**Native fish interactions.** Channel catfish co-occur with [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], roundtail chub, and speckled dace. Shared use of foods and habitats, and documented predation on native fish, suggest possible negative interactions.

## Key Findings

**Abundance and distribution**
- Channel catfish were widely distributed across 517 km of the Green River and 74 km of the Yampa River in 1987–1988.
- Adult fish comprised 14% of electrofishing catch and 70% of angling catch, as printed in the abstract (the OCR text is ambiguous here).
- Catch rates peaked in rocky canyon habitats, and the authors describe a clear preference for high-gradient reaches.

**Age and growth**
- Pectoral spine sections from 364 fish (27–756 mm total length) gave ages from 0 to 22 years.
- Average annual growth increments ranged from 17 to 53 mm across the first 15 years, with the greatest growth in the first year.
- Fish averaged more than 400 mm by age 9 and more than 599 mm by age 17.
- Growth was much slower than in midwestern and southwestern U.S. populations.
- Of the fish aged (n = 89), 24% were older than 10 years. The maximum age of 22 years is notably long compared with the usual 6 to 10 years.
- No difference in growth or condition was detected among the eight reaches.
- The length-weight relationship (log W = 3.12 log TL − 5.41) was similar to other populations, suggesting healthy fish.

**Environmental drivers**
- October age-0 size was inversely related to July–September discharge (r = −0.83; P = 0.02) and positively related to water temperature (r = 0.88; P = 0.01).
- The authors attribute slow growth to limited food resources, suboptimal temperatures, a short growing season, and unfavorable riverine conditions.

**Diet**
- Of 755 stomachs inspected, 76.2% (575 stomachs) contained food.
- Frequency of occurrence among stomachs with food: aquatic invertebrates 31%, vascular plants 28% (including horsetail, juniper berries, and seeds), terrestrial insects 22% (including Mormon crickets), algae and detritus 10%, fish 7%, and mice 1.5%.
- Diet included human camp garbage (rice, corn, potatoes, chicken bones), indicating [[Omnivory]] and scavenging.
- The alga *Cladophora* was transported from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] tailwaters and was a common food item.

**Piscivory**
- Fish remains occurred only in the largest individuals. The abstract gives an average length of 420 mm; the body text gives 392 mm (n = 28) for fish-containing stomachs.
- Whole fish were found in 12 stomachs, including channel catfish, suckers, sculpin, and speckled dace.
- Carp scales were found in 10 individuals, suggesting scale-feeding.
- Piscivory in this population was lower than reported for smaller (50–200 mm) fish elsewhere.

**Conservation implications**
- There is little direct evidence that channel catfish caused the decline of rare and endangered Colorado River fishes.
- Potential adverse impacts include predation on native fish, competition for food and space, and reduced habitat and food availability.
- Records of [[Colorado Squawfish]] with channel catfish lodged in their throats indicate adverse impacts.
- Warmer water and longer growing seasons from proposed flow or thermal changes may increase channel catfish growth and predation on native species.
- The authors recommend careful evaluation of habitat modifications before implementation, and further investigation of channel catfish–native fish interactions.
