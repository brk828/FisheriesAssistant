# Predator Avoidance and Exercise Conditioning in Hatchery-Reared Razorback Sucker and Bonytail

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary USGS and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service study tested whether physical exercise (swimming against currents) and prior predator exposure improve predator avoidance in hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*). Hatchery fish were naive to predators such as [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). Exercised and predator-exposed razorback suckers suffered lower predation losses than controls. Bonytail results were not statistically significant, owing to disease-related loss of the exercise group and small sample sizes. The authors conclude that conditioning may improve post-stocking survival, though field validation is still needed.

## Background & Description

The report addresses the poor survival of hatchery-reared endangered fishes after release into the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Since 1980, more than two million bonytail and 1.5 million razorback suckers have been reintroduced, yet survival has been below 10 percent or absent (Marsh and others, 2005). Stocking size is a documented factor: first-year survivorship was 10 percent for fish released at 30 cm and 26 percent at 35 cm. The Lake Mohave Native Fish Work Group recommended increasing the target stocking size from 35 to 50 cm, though larger fish raise per-fish production costs and reduce numbers.

The authors draw parallels with terrestrial and salmonid repatriation programs, where predator-recognition and survival-skill training are standard (for example, gray wolf, California condor, black-footed ferret, and [[Natural Rearing System]] programs for salmon). Such approaches are rare for warmwater species.

Species involved:
- **Razorback sucker:** juveniles averaged about 198 to 222 mm total length across years.
- **Bonytail:** juveniles averaged about 196 mm total length in 2007.
- **Predators:** flathead catfish (580 mm mean total length, 2.2 to 14.8 kg) and largemouth bass (mean 1.3 kg).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predator behavior:** Flathead catfish were nocturnal and used shelters (cut plastic garbage cans) during daylight. Largemouth bass fed during the day.
- **Prey behavior:** Naive razorback suckers approached and gathered around predators, showing curiosity rather than avoidance. Predator-exposed bonytail and razorback suckers adopted a defensive posture, swimming at the upper sides of tanks to minimize the attack angle.
- **Stress and pigmentation:** Bonytail stressed by disease became darkly pigmented, lethargic and often died. Under 1 percent of razorback suckers showed darker pigmentation, possibly linked to stress, without associated losses.
- **Water conditions:** Experiments maintained 1 m depth and used flowing water (up to 30 cm/s) for exercise. Fish were exposed to a formalin treatment for ich, which caused significant losses in exercised bonytail.
- **Social learning:** Treated and control fish schooled together, which may have transferred predator-avoidance behavior to controls.

## Key Findings

**Predator naivety and learned avoidance**
- Predator-naive juveniles (20 to 25 cm TL) showed no preference between areas with and without large flathead catfish (52% and 48%, n = 16; 46% and 54%, n = 24).
- After a predation event, use of predator-free areas nearly doubled (36% and 64%, n = 54; 33% and 67%, n = 12).
- In a stricter test, predator-savvy razorback suckers used predator-free areas 88 percent of the time (n = 21).
- The authors conclude predator avoidance is a learned behavior, not inherited.

**Swimming performance (razorback sucker)**
- Exercised fish (n = 31) and unexercised fish (n = 29) showed no significant difference in mean critical flow velocity.
- Using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, exercised fish reached critical velocities of 2.2 body lengths/s or higher more often than controls (21 vs. 14 fish; D = 0.33, p = 0.035), about 50 percent more.
- Drafting behavior in a "sweet spot" near the flow-straightener was observed. The authors added a screen to limit it.

**Predation trials, razorback sucker (2006)**
- Nine trials with 20 fish each (10 exercised, 10 control) showed mean mortality of 3.33 ± 0.62 SE in exercised fish versus 5.11 ± 0.74 SE in controls (Wilcoxon matched-pairs, n = 9, Z = 1.69, p = 0.046).

**Predation trials, razorback sucker (2007)**
- Twenty-three trials compared exercised-exposed, exercised-naive, and control fish.
- Mean percent consumed for exercised-exposed fish was 30.98 ± 4.41 SE, versus 45.72 ± 4.88 SE for controls (MRPP, n = 42, test statistic = -2.85, p = 0.017).
- Exercised-exposed fish showed a 22 percent survival advantage in 2006 and a 19 percent advantage over controls for naive exercised fish in the four longer trials. The authors note these are small samples.

**Bonytail trials**
- Exercise was attempted but abandoned: exercised bonytail died from ich (Ichthyophthirius multifiliis) and a later infection.
- Predator-savvy bonytail suffered 38 percent fewer losses than controls, but the difference was not significant (χ² = 1.536, df = 1, p = 0.143).
- In 2007 the trials used 88 treatment and 88 control bonytail: 18 (20%) treatment and 28 (32%) control fish were lost.
- Baseline endurance of unexercised bonytail averaged 3.36 BL/s, versus 2.24 BL/s for razorback suckers. Trials were capped at 4.5 BL/s.

**Major conclusions (as stated by the authors)**
- Hatchery-reared bonytail and razorback suckers are naive to largemouth bass and flathead catfish.
- Hatchery fish initially approach predators.
- Predator avoidance is a learned behavior.
- Predation losses were reduced for flow-conditioned razorback suckers, for razorback suckers and bonytail with prior predator experience, and for all treatments overall.

**Limitations and recommendations**
- Physical trauma from failed attacks may have reduced control performance and biased results toward treatments.
- Mixing treated and control fish may have improved control survival through social learning.
- Recommended future research: duration of predator avoidance, effective mass exposure methods, optimal predator species, flow duration and velocity, and actual wild survival benefit.
