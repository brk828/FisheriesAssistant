# Microbial Origin of Travertine Fabrics: Pleistocene Stuttgart and Miocene Riedöschingen Travertines

#ichthyology #marine-biology #microbial-calcification

*Note: This document is a sedimentological and microbiological study of non-marine carbonates. It contains no fish or fish-focused data; the aquatic focus is on cyanobacteria, bacteria, plants, and invertebrates within spring and lake carbonate systems.*

## Executive Summary

Koban and Schweigert (Stuttgart) compare two travertine deposits in Southern Germany that differ in age and depositional setting: the Quaternary [[Stuttgart Travertines]] (Pleistocene, Holsteinian and Eemian) and the Middle Miocene [[Riedöschingen Travertine]] on the Swabian Alb. They propose a genetic classification separating [[Travertine]], [[Tufa]], [[Calcareous Sinter]] and [[Lacustrine Limestone]] based on facies types and controlling factors. The central claim is that in travertines, microbial activity is the dominant factor in carbonate precipitation, producing recognizable fabrics such as laminar crusts, bushy ("shrub") layers, peloidal layers and gas bubble layers. Abiotic and pedogenetic processes modify these deposits, producing sinters, speleothems, tepees and caliche pisoids.

## Background & Description

Non-marine carbonates in Southern Germany have been labelled inconsistently in the literature. The authors argue that the terms can be differentiated by associated facies, and they present a triangular diagram (Fig. 1) linking deposits to inorganic versus organic precipitation.

- **Travertines** are laminated carbonates precipitated from mineral and/or thermal waters under harsh conditions that exclude higher organisms. They are highly porous (mostly vadose) and finely laminated, probably reflecting seasonal changes in water depth, temperature or detrital input.
- **Tufas** are built around incrusted plant remains (mosses, green algae, reed, angiosperm leaves) and have large moldic pores. They lack fine horizontal lamination and are typical of supersaturated spring and fluvial waters, with precipitation assisted by photosynthesis.
- **Calcareous sinters** are dense, sparitic, commonly laminated limestones formed abiotically, mainly at spring fissures, caves and pores, where microbial activity is present but not dominant.
- **Lacustrine limestones** are recognized by fossils such as planorbid and lymnaeid gastropods, ostracods, characean stems and gyrogonites, and by [[Seekreide]] carbonate mud.

The authors note that transitions between categories exist, since speleothems and hot springs can host microbial activity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stuttgart Travertines.** The deposits formed from thermal and mineral springs on Quaternary terraces of the River Neckar. Several interglacial phases (Pre-Holsteinian, Holsteinian, Eemian) and postglacial periods are distinguished using elevation, fossil content, prehistoric artifacts and radiometric data. Fossils document Quaternary fauna, flora and climate in continental Europe. The succession typically begins with a tufa horizon at the base, followed by laminated travertine, then massive lacustrine limestone, and is capped by Riss Glacial loess. Natural spring orifices no longer exist; water comes from artificial wells and subsurface springs, and precipitation has stopped because the water no longer flows across the surface.

**Riedöschingen Travertine.** This dome-shaped deposit, up to 20 m thick, formed at a single spring orifice active during Middle Miocene volcanism on the western Swabian Alb near Blumberg. It overlies marine marly limestones (Upper Marine Molasse) or caliche carbonates (Helicidenmergel), the latter containing Early Miocene terrestrial gastropods. Fossils are extremely rare, mainly vertebrate bones. Fluvial gravels of Late Miocene age overlie it. Seasonal detrital input, including iron-rich material from lateritic weathering, reflects cycles of dry and wet periods. Lacustrine facies are absent because the dome morphology prevented deep ponds from forming.

**Microbial and environmental controls.** Calcification by cyanobacteria is not obligatory. It depends on water chemistry, temperature, current velocity and supersaturation. Microbial activity can create microenvironments that encourage precipitation. Gas bubbles were probably generated by free oxygen from photosynthesis within sediment and, possibly, by volcanic CO2.

## Key Findings

**1. Microbial fabrics (main travertine facies)**
- **Laminar crusts:** thinly laminated micritic carbonates, often called stromatolites in a morphological sense. They are among the most common travertine fabrics. The authors interpret thicker crusts as bacterial mats that developed on sunken fragments of calcite rafts from the water surface. The "Schizothrix"-type calcification is compared with modern mats containing the cyanobacterium *Schizothrix calcicola*.
- **Bushy layers ("shrubs"):** bush-like calcite crystals, typically 1 mm to 2 cm thick (up to 10 cm at Riedöschingen). They show a false-branching, [[Dichothrix]]-like morphotype. Some Stuttgart examples preserve central molds indicating a filamentous origin. In the Holsteinian of Stuttgart, these layers formed during summer or early autumn, based on a fossil dragonfly.
- **Peloidal layers:** very small allochems, often clustered, forming a clotted fabric. Peloids are interpreted as autochthonous, formed by direct microbial calcification or by mechanical destruction of bushy structures. They grade into pure micritic microbial mats.
- **Gas bubble layers:** hollow cavities a few millimeters across, each separated by a thin micritic incrustation, formed within the uppermost sediment. They are compared with the elongated "foam rock" described from Italy.

**2. Fabrics with partial microbial participation**
- Cements in intraclastic layers and sheet cracks at Stuttgart are bored by branching filaments (3-12 µm in diameter), probably molds of endolithic cyanobacteria such as *Hyella*. A second type of fine, unbranched filament (about 1 µm) is also present, and both types coexist with goethitic peloids.
- [[Frutexites]] crusts occur in horizontal cavities at both sites. They are enriched in manganese and show micropores of about 3 µm, interpreted as microbial filament molds. The authors cannot exclude abiotic precipitation, but microbial traces are evident.

**3. Tufas**
- Stuttgart tufas are a minor component. Basal layers contain molds of *Phragmites* (reed), *Schoenoplectus* and *Sparganium*. Rare moss-tufa built by *Philonotis calcarea* and Cladophorites-tufa (calcified tubes of filamentous green algae, 0.3 mm thick) occur. Holsteinian topmost layers contain angiosperm leaves, fruits and twigs (ash, box, hazel, oak).
- Riedöschingen tufas are very rare and consist of small moss-tufa layers in gullies.

**4. Lacustrine limestones**
- The upper Holsteinian Stuttgart profile is dominated by grey, finely porous, non-laminated lacustrine limestone containing ostracods, characean stems, planorbid and helicid gastropods and sometimes large vertebrate remains. Limonitic root molds indicate later pedogenetic overprint.

**5. Abiotic fabrics**
- **Sinters:** at Riedöschingen, feathery calcite crystals grow perpendicular to the spring fissure walls in "cone-in-cone" structures, with small detrital layers marking seasonal changes. Aragonite is recorded in comparable Italian travertines, but not preserved at Riedöschingen.
- **Speleothems and cave pearls:** Riedöschingen cavities contain fragments of microbial crusts, intraclasts and pisoids. Cave pearls reach up to 15 mm in diameter. The authors separate the layered, non-branching calcite fabric from the horizontal bushy layers, arguing they differ in shape and setting.
- **Pedogenetic features:** desiccation cracks, loferitic fabrics, caliche pisoids, cortoids (red hematitic coatings) and *Microcodium* occur. Riedöschingen has a pedogenetic layer with caliche pisoids and *Microcodium* at the base of the travertine complex.
- **Tepee structures:** well developed at Stuttgart, where many early-lithified layers, including all microbial facies, are disrupted together. The authors attribute these to an abiotic origin from desiccation, since biotic mechanisms cannot explain disruption of so many layers. At Riedöschingen, only centimeter-scale tepees occur.

**6. Conclusions**
- The two deposits share very similar facies despite different ages and settings. Most fabrics that define travertine result from microbial calcification.
- Travertine formation is restricted to harsh water conditions favourable to specialized cyanobacterial and bacterial genera, in vadose environments with very shallow ponds.
- Travertines are shaped by primary microbial activity together with desiccation, resedimentation and pedogenesis.
- Increasing water depth and normalizing chemistry (for example through mixing with meteoric water) can shift a travertine environment toward lacustrine conditions, indicated by a rapid increase in fossils of higher organisms.
- Three principal microbial fabric types are recognized: laminar mats, bushy layers and peloidal accumulations. Peloids are largely autochthonous, which is relevant to interpreting peloidal fabrics in marine settings.
- Microbial products can resemble abiotic precipitates, but fabrics in synsedimentary caverns and spring-fissure sinters are morphologically distinct.
