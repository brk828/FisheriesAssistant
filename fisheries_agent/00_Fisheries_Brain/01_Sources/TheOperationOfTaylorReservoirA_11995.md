# The Operation of Taylor Reservoir and Its Effect on Gunnison River Fishing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1950 fisheries report, published by the [[Colorado Game and Fish Department]] (Fisheries Management Division) and written by Harvey J. Williams, examines whether the operation of [[Taylor Dam]] affected trout fishing on the [[Gunnison River]] between Gunnison and Sapinero. The study ran from June 9 to October 7, 1950, within the fishing season of May 25 to October 31. The author concluded that Taylor Dam, as operated in 1950, did not noticeably affect fly fishing for trout. Water temperatures were lowered somewhat but stayed within a satisfactory range. Volume changes were found to be the main operational effect, and the author warned that abrupt, larger releases could change this conclusion.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The study covered the Gunnison River from Sapinero to Almont, the [[Taylor River]] from Almont to Taylor Dam, and short lengths of the larger tributaries above their confluences. The Gunnison River forms at Almont, where the East and Taylor rivers meet. The report describes it as an excellent trout stream with moderate gradient, good pool and riffle proportions, a rubble and gravel bottom, boulders for cover, and frequent undercut banks. Most angling pressure fell on the stretch between Gunnison and Sapinero. Major tributaries above Sapinero include [[Ohio Creek]], [[Tomichi Creek]] and [[Cebolla Creek]].

**Taylor Dam and reservoir.** [[Taylor Dam]] was built in 1938 by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. It is a 170-foot-high earth-fill dam. Water is released through two outlet tubes in the dam bottom or over a concrete spillway. When full, [[Taylor Park Reservoir]] is 3 miles long and 1.8 miles wide, covers 2,000 acres, and holds 106,230 acre-feet. The surface level varies considerably each year and at times drops nearly 50 feet below full.

**Water use.** Reservoir water supports the [[Uncompahgre Irrigation Project]] between Colona and Delta. The project covers 103,000 acres, of which 75,800 acres are irrigated and 27,600 acres are irrigable. Water is diverted from the Gunnison River through the [[Gunnison Tunnel]], which begins in Black Canyon about 25 miles below Sapinero.

**Methods.**
- **Temperature monitoring:** Fourteen temperature stations were sampled, usually five days a week, at fixed morning and afternoon windows. From July 21 to 23, eight stations were read hourly or half-hourly to track the effect of the first cold-water releases.
- **Creel census:** The [[Creel Census]] divided the Gunnison River into two sections: Gunnison to Iola (12 miles) and Iola to Sapinero (14 miles). The checker floated the river by [[Kayak]] over two afternoons, with trips lasting two to four hours. Trips were made every day except Monday and Tuesday and on days when weather or water conditions were unsuitable.
- **Flow data:** Volumes for the East River at Almont, the Gunnison River near Gunnison and the Gunnison River at Iola came from the [[United States Geological Survey]] Water Resources Branch.
- **Turbidity:** A [[Jackson Turbidimeter]] was used in September.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Factors affecting fly fishing.** The author lists seven factors known or suspected to affect fly fishing: fishing pressure, weather, turbidity, insect hatches, water temperature, water volume, and stocking. The report states that it is very difficult under field conditions to isolate which factor, or combination of factors, causes good or poor fishing.

**Temperature and metabolism.** Citing [[Belding (1928)]], the author notes that lower water temperatures reduce fish metabolism, which in turn lowers growth rate through reduced feeding.

**Turbidity.** Nearly all samples contained fewer than 25 parts per million of suspended solids. One sample taken less than 200 yards below a bridge-construction project at Station 7 read 97 parts per million. Such murky conditions occurred only occasionally and for a few hours at a time. The author concluded that turbidity was not a limiting factor. [[Taylor Park Reservoir]] also acts as a settling basin, reducing turbidity in the downstream study sections.

**Insect hatches.** A small [[Caddisfly]] species hatched in great numbers on both study sections during the first two weeks of September. Trout were seen feeding at the surface much more often than earlier in the season. The author suggests this hatch may explain the September peak in fishing quality.

## Key Findings

### Fishing quality

- The fly-fishing catch per man hour (CPMH) for the 1950 season was **0.93**. It ranged from a low of **0.57** in July to a high of **1.91** in September.
- The bait-fishing CPMH for the season was **0.9**.
- The 1949 CPMH for all fishermen on checked Colorado trout streams was also **0.93**.
- Fly-fishing quality peaked in mid-July, then declined, and then rose gradually to the September high. The cause of the mid-July decline is unknown. Bait fishing peaked in August and fell in September.
- Insufficient fishermen were checked to show a daily link between fishing quality and pressure or weather.

### Temperature effects

- Releases through the outlet tubes lowered the Gunnison River temperature between Gunnison and Sapinero. The author judged the water still within a satisfactory range for trout fishing, which the report puts at roughly 50–60°F.
- Mean temperatures on July 23 were **57.3°F** at Gunnison and **61.1°F** at Iola. On July 22–23, the East River at Almont averaged **55.4°F** and **55.7°F**, and the Gunnison near Gunnison averaged **58.4°F** on July 22.
- Comparing 20 days before and 19 days after the July 21 release (Table II), the East River (unaffected by a large dam) averaged about **3°F warmer** afterward. The Gunnison at Iola averaged about **3°F cooler**. The report therefore estimates the Gunnison at Iola was about **6°F cooler** for roughly nineteen days after the release.
- Below the East River confluence, no temperature changes attributable to Taylor releases were detected within three days. The East River tempered the reservoir water.

### Water volume

- Releases ranged from 0 to nearly 1,000 cubic feet per second. Increases were made gradually over nearly six weeks, and most were made around 9:00 P.M. Table I, which uses daily means, does not show the exact size of each increase. It does show that no large increase occurred over several consecutive days.
- The author states that sudden, larger increases would produce "an entirely different picture."
- Comparing Gunnison and East River volumes before and after 1939, the author estimated Taylor Dam's effect (Figure IV). Gunnison River flows were **lower** in May, June and July and **higher** in August and September. Only part of the total decrease could be attributed to the dam.
- None of the data showed that these volume changes affected fly fishing.

### Turbidity

- Turbidity was not considered a limiting factor for fly fishing, except during a few isolated hours.

### Stocking

- Stocked fish were **rainbow trout**, except for one plant of brown trout. All fish were seven inches or longer.
- Fewer fish were planted in the Iola to Sapinero section, even though it is the longer section, because landowners closed much of it to free public fishing.
- The limited creel data showed **no correlation** between the number of fish stocked and fishing quality. The author notes that more extensive catch data might reveal one.

### Conclusions

1. Taylor Dam, as operated in 1950, did not noticeably affect trout fishing between Gunnison and Sapinero.
2. Sudden or larger volume changes could alter this conclusion, so further monitoring was recommended.
3. Cold-water releases lowered temperatures for about nineteen days, but the water remained within a satisfactory range.
4. Taylor Reservoir reduces downstream turbidity.
5. Good or poor fishing cannot easily be attributed to any single factor under field conditions.
