# Convective and Advective Circulation of Lake Powell, Utah-Arizona, 1972-1975

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #reservoir-hydrodynamics

## Executive Summary

This paper documents the physical circulation of [[Lake Powell]], a canyon reservoir on the Colorado River behind [[Glen Canyon Dam]], over 1972-1975. Sampling was roughly quarterly for temperature, conductivity, turbidity and dissolved oxygen, supplemented by monthly [[Bureau of Reclamation]] chemistry data. The authors show that the lake mixes convectively only in winter, never to the bottom, and that dissolved salt is an important stabilizing factor in winter. Circulation is driven mainly by inflow density currents (spring overflow, autumn interflow, winter underflow) and by a mid-depth [[Withdrawal Current]] created by dam operations. The paper concludes that the lake is best described as warm monomictic and meromictic. The document contains no fish, invertebrate or biological community data; its biological relevance is limited to dissolved oxygen dynamics.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Lake Powell fills about 260 km (160 mi) of the former Glen Canyon sector of the [[Colorado River]], completed as a reservoir with Glen Canyon Dam in 1963. It lies on an arid [[Colorado Plateau]] with sandstone bedrock and no natural lakes. Its design functions were hydropower and upper-basin water storage, with recreation as a significant later use.

**Climate and water balance.**
- Mean monthly temperatures range from 27°C (July) to -1°C (January).
- Mean annual rainfall over the lake area is about 20 cm.
- Average annual evaporation is about 180 cm at the dam and about 110 cm in the upper lake.

**Pre-dam hydrology at Lee's Ferry.**
- About 79% of discharge came from the upper Colorado and Green rivers, 16% from the [[San Juan River]], and 5% from local watersheds.
- The river showed a single annual snowmelt flood.
- Summer water temperatures averaged 28°C.
- Salinity averaged 606 ppm, ranging from 324 ppm in summer to 1164 ppm in late fall.
- The water was sulfate-dominated with strong bicarbonate buffering.
- Suspended load averaged about 4100 ppm (as reported).

**Post-dam regime.** Penstocks draw water from mid-depth, so releases below the dam are cold, clear and of even quality year-round. Seasonal fluctuations in flow and quality at Lee's Ferry are largely eliminated.

**Morphometry.**
- Design capacity is 3.3 × 10¹⁰ m³ at full pool, with a maximum depth of 170 m.
- Operating levels average about 20 m below full pool.
- Mean residence time is about 2 years.
- At full design volume the shoreline is about 2980 km in the text (Table 1 gives 2930 km), longer than the US Pacific coastline.
- Almost half of the capacity lies in the upper 30 m.
- The channel floor has a nearly uniform grade of 0.038%.

**Methods.**
- Heat, salt, dissolved oxygen and suspended load were surveyed every 3 months during 1972-1975, with each survey taking 2-3 days.
- Measurements used thermistor probes, [[Hydrolab]] conductivity and [[Transmissometry]] optical density, with depth from a Hydro Products sensor.
- Dissolved oxygen was determined by the [[Winkler Method]] (azide-modified) on Van Dorn bottle samples.
- Bureau of Reclamation monthly chemistry came from seven stations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime.** Lake Powell is classed as a [[Monomictic Lake]] (warm monomictic). It mixes only from early October to early March. The summer epilimnion extends 10-20 m below the surface. The annual heat budget averages about 40 kcal/cm², described as comparable to other large monomictic lakes. The heat budget was not greatly affected by changes in lake volume during the study.

**Salinity and meromixis.** Winter salinity is a critical stabilizing factor. The bottom at the Wahweap station was consistently saltier than the surface, so the lake did not circulate fully top to bottom and is described as [[Meromixis]].

**Stratification and oxygen.** A pervasive [[Oxygen Minimum Layer]] forms just below the epilimnion-thermocline interface in July-September. It is attributed to biologic respiration of settling organic matter. Autumn convection breaks down this layer, and winter underflow flushes the oldest hypolimnetic water out through the penstocks.

**Turbidity as a tracer.** Suspended sediment makes inflow plumes visible, allowing the paper to trace overflow, interflow and underflow currents.

**Biological relevance.** The document reports no fish, invertebrate, plankton or habitat-biota data. Its only biologically relevant output is the seasonal dissolved oxygen structure, which the paper does not link to any organisms.

## Key Findings

**Stability and convection**
1. Schmidt stability is always positive, from about 1 kg cm/cm² in early spring to about 14-15 kg cm/cm² in late summer (the text gives about 14 and the summary about 15).
2. Diminishing stability occurs only from about January through March.
3. Summer stability is dominated by temperature (thermal/total ratio above 0.9). In winter, salinity dominates (ratio below 0.5, and below zero in January-February 1973).
4. Convection never penetrates below about 1020 m (3400 ft), roughly mid-depth, and is blocked by the withdrawal zone at 1020-1050 m (3400-3500 ft).
5. The Birgean work function averages a maximum of about 8 kg cm/cm² in late summer. Wind work is limited to waters above 1020 m, while advective work occurs below that level.

**Advective currents by season**
6. **Spring:** a warm, fresh overflow current sets the summer epilimnion thickness and metalimnion depth. Large spring floods produce deeper, more diffuse metalimnia.
7. **Summer:** the overflow current dominates. A return current and mixing zone lie between the surface overflow and the penstock-level withdrawal. A persistent upwelling or convergence zone occurs at depth in the central lake.
8. **Autumn:** an [[Interflow Current]] forms as denser, saline water closes the overflow, operating alongside the withdrawal current.
9. **Winter:** a full-lake-length [[Underflow Density Current]] replaces and uplifts bottom waters, with upwelling concentrated near the dam.

**Withdrawal currents** (based on the [[Bohan and Grace Model]], confirmed by dye tracer studies)
10. The withdrawal current thins as stratification increases.
11. Surface waters are entrained only with no stratification and a lake level below 1080 m (3600 ft).
12. The normal withdrawal zone does not affect waters below 1000 m (3350 ft). Emergency outlet tunnels at 1000 m (3347 ft) could draw water down to 970 m (3240 ft), within 18 m of the bottom.
13. The uplake extent of the withdrawal zone is greatest during falling lake levels and smallest during rising levels.

**Overall conclusions**
14. Lake Powell acts as a cooling basin for Colorado River water released below Glen Canyon Dam.
15. Hypolimnetic water is replaced each year by the winter underflow.
16. Seasonal variability in downstream water quantity and quality has been largely modulated by the reservoir.
17. The Bohan and Grace model describes penstock withdrawal flows reasonably well.

**Methodological note.** Schmidt and Birgean calculations used a volume-weighted lakewide average over 18 geographic domains, with 20 ft (6.1 m) integration increments. Temperature was converted to density with the [[Tilton and Taylor Approximation]], and conductivity to salinity with the [[Hendrick Equation]]. The Birgean reference density was taken from the 12-month profile with minimum work.
