## Executive Summary

This document is a 1987 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service bibliography compiling research (1980–present) intended to update the [[Humpback Chub]] Recovery Plan. Rather than a primary research paper, it is a curated reference list spanning life history, reproductive ecology, taxonomy, genetics, physiology, hatchery propagation, and population/habitat monitoring of the [[Humpback Chub]] and its co-occurring endangered congeners in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The compiled works collectively establish [[Gila Cypha]] as a canyon-adapted cyprinid endemic to swift, turbid whitewater reaches of the Colorado River system, facing conservation challenges from altered flow regimes, hybridization, and habitat loss. The bibliography reflects a decade of intensive interagency research (USFWS, Bureau of Reclamation, state wildlife agencies, universities) aimed at clarifying taxonomy, reproductive biology, and recovery needs for this federally listed endangered species.

## Background & Description

Although this is a bibliography rather than a primary data source, the referenced studies collectively document key morphological research on the [[Humpback Chub]]:

- **Distinguishing dorsal hump**: Taxonomic and distributional status addressed by Valdez (1980), who examined range and species identity of *Gila cypha* in the Upper Colorado River.
- **Morphometric analysis**: Rosenfeld (1986) conducted principal component analysis (PCA) on chubs of genus *Gila* from the Green and Colorado Rivers to statistically discriminate species-level morphology.
- **Larval and juvenile identification**: Muth and Carlson (1986) and Muth, Haynes, and Carlson (1985) developed taxonomic keys for identifying larval and early-juvenile *Gila* native to the Upper Colorado River Basin — a notoriously difficult task due to morphological similarity among sympatric [[Gila]] species (humpback, bonytail, and roundtail chub).
- **Skeletal anomalies**: Haynes and Muth (1983) documented **lordosis** (spinal curvature deformity) occurring in *Gila* populations in the Yampa River.
- **Hematological baselines**: Berry (1984) established hematological profiles for four rare Colorado River fishes, providing physiological reference data potentially useful for identifying stress or disease in these species.
- **Cytogenetics**: Rosenfeld (1983) used chromosome analysis to discriminate between *Gila* species, addressing persistent taxonomic ambiguity caused by [[Hybridization]] among Colorado River chubs.
- **Genetic/electrophoretic analysis**: Rosenfeld (1986) performed a preliminary electrophoresis study of the *Gila* complex to clarify species boundaries at the molecular level.
- **Hybridization**: Smith (1983) discussed the significance of hybridization within *Gila* (and *Agosia*), a critical consideration given the morphological continuum observed between [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Roundtail Chub]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The compiled literature situates the [[Humpback Chub]] within the following ecological context:

- **Habitat specialization**: Multiple studies (Bulkley et al. 1982; Bulkley and Pimentel 1983; Pimentel and Bulkley 1983) document tolerance and preference for specific habitat parameters, including **total dissolved solids (TDS)** concentrations, indicating adaptation to the naturally high-TDS, turbid conditions of the Colorado River system.
- **Geographic range**: Populations and habitat documented across the [[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], [[White River]], [[Little Colorado River]], Grand Canyon, and Cataract Canyon reaches (Haynes 1980 documented a range extension; Valdez and Williams 1985, 1986 conducted Cataract Canyon fish studies).
- **Reproductive ecology**: Burdick and Kaeding (1984) described preliminary reproductive ecology of humpback chub and roundtail chub in the Upper Colorado River. Kaeding and Zimmerman (1982, 1983) provided detailed life history and population ecology data for humpback chub in the Little Colorado and Colorado Rivers within Grand Canyon — considered foundational works for the species.
- **Movement and habitat selection**: Valdez and Nielson (1982) used **radiotelemetry** to assess humpback chub movement patterns and habitat selection, an important methodological advance for understanding fine-scale habitat use.
- **Thermal ecology of early life stages**: Marsh (1985) and Marsh and Pisano (1982) examined the influence of **incubation temperature** on embryo survival and hatching success in native Colorado River fishes, highlighting temperature as a limiting factor for recruitment.
- **Limiting factors research**: A multi-year series by Haynes and Muth (1982, 1984) and Haynes, Muth, and Nesler (1983, 1985) specifically identified habitat requirements and limiting factors constraining Colorado squawfish and humpback chub populations.
- **Flow regime impacts**: Maddux et al. (1987, in preparation) evaluated varied flow regimes (relevant to Glen Canyon Dam operations) on aquatic resources of Glen and Grand Canyons — directly relevant to hydropower-driven flow alteration as a threat to the species.
- **Community context**: Tyus et al. (1982) and related distribution/status reports (Tyus, Valdez, and Williams 1985) place the humpback chub within the broader endangered fish assemblage of the Upper Colorado River Basin, alongside [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- **Swimming performance**: Berry and Pimentel (1985) measured swimming performance of three rare Colorado River fishes, relevant to understanding capacity to navigate swift canyon currents and engineered structures (e.g., fish passage).

## Key Findings

- The [[Humpback Chub]] is consistently documented as a taxonomically challenging species due to morphological overlap and hybridization with [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]], necessitating chromosome, electrophoretic, and morphometric (PCA) studies to resolve species identity (Rosenfeld 1983, 1986; Smith 1983).
- Long-term population and habitat monitoring programs (Radant 1982–1986; Wick et al. 1980, 1981, 1985) tracked Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub, and razorback sucker across Utah and Colorado river reaches throughout the 1980s, forming the core demographic dataset for recovery planning.
- Successful **captive propagation** was achieved at Willow Beach and Dexter National Fish Hatcheries, with induced spawning and hybrid culture techniques developed for humpback chub and bonytail chub (Hamman 1980–1985), supporting a translocation/refugia strategy for the species.
- Habitat degradation and altered flow regimes (linked to Glen Canyon Dam operations) were identified as major threats, prompting dedicated flow-regime impact studies (Maddux et al. 1987) and TDS/habitat protection initiatives.
