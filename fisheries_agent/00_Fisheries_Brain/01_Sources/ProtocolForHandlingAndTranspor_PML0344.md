# Protocol for Handling and Transport of Wild Bonytail

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila

## Executive Summary

This June 1987 draft protocol, prepared by the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], directs field personnel to retain every wild [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) they encounter alive and move it to refugia. It sets out how to identify the species in the field, how to hold and transport a captured fish, which equipment field teams must carry, who to notify, and which federal hatcheries serve as temporary and permanent holding facilities. The goal is to maximize the survival of captured fish during recovery.

## Background & Description

The protocol gives a three-part field identification test for bonytail. A fish is a candidate if it meets all of the following:

- **Minimum total length:** 300 mm.
- **Fin-ray counts:** dorsal and anal fin-ray counts of 10 or greater.
- **Body-proportion criterion (either a or b):**
  - (a) caudal-peduncle length divided by head length is greater than 1.0 (after Minckley, 1973), or
  - (b) head length divided by caudal-peduncle depth is greater than 5.0 (after Minckley, 1982).

Measurements should follow the method in *Ichthyology* by Lagler, Bardach and Miller. The document also includes a standard [[Vernier Caliper]] and key for measuring the morphometric characters needed to identify *Gila*.

The protocol is framed as a recovery action for two listed Colorado River fishes, the bonytail and the [[Humpback Chub]]. Teams sampling reaches where either species has been captured are expected to be ready to handle a bonytail.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Priority river reaches.** The UCRBCC Sensitive Reach document lists about ten reaches where humpback chub or bonytail were captured within the previous five years. Examples include [[Westwater Canyon]], [[Cataract Canyon]], [[Moab Canyon]], [[Black Rocks]], [[Gray Canyon]], [[Whirlpool Canyon]], [[Yampa Canyon]], and [[Cross Mountain]] in the [[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], and upper [[Colorado River]]. Teams sampling these reaches with [[Electrofishing]], entanglement nets, seines, or angling must carry transport equipment. Teams working elsewhere need not carry it at all times.

**Water-quality and temperature requirements.** Holding water should come from the capture area when possible. Temperature is managed by aeration, by partial water exchange, by adding ice in sealed containers, and by partly submerging the holding container in river water for longer holds.

## Key Findings

### 1. Field handling and holding protocol
- Retain every wild bonytail that meets the criteria, and avoid leaving it unattended at a remote site for later pickup.
- Place the fish promptly in a cooler filled with fresh water, preferably from the capture site.
- Keep handling to an absolute minimum. Defer recording length and weight until later.
- Aerate with the portable aerator and diffuser only when water temperature rises above the target temperature. Cool with sealed ice or frozen containers as needed.
- For holds longer than about 13 hours, partly submerge the container in river water.
- Do not use a holding net, which may abrade the fish.
- Keep the container closed. Partly exchange the water at intervals of one to several hours, and replenish it only if the fish shows respiratory stress, such as rapid ventilation or gaping at the surface.
- If the fish has not been injured, release it at the capture site as soon as possible, unless transport is feasible.
- Record capture-site information, including location, date, time, and anecdotal notes. Send these records with the fish.

### 2. Equipment carried by field teams
The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] provides the following equipment, which field teams carry when sampling bonytail habitat:
- One cooler of roughly 100 to 160 quarts.
- One to two oxygen cylinders with regulator, rubber tubing, and carbon diffuser, weighing about 5 lb each.
- One live net.
- One vernier caliper.
- One key for measuring the morphometric characters.

Cooperators should obtain this equipment through the Vernal or Grand Junction [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] field stations.

### 3. Coordination and notification
- The Colorado River Fishery Project coordinates transport from the river to temporary and permanent facilities.
- Teams should notify the appropriate facility one week before each trip. The notice should give departure and takeout dates, takeout point, trip length, and the person in charge.
- Teams sampling the Green River subbasin notify the Vernal station, led by Harold Tyus.
- Teams sampling the Colorado River subbasin notify the Grand Junction station, led by Lynn Kaeding.
- When a bonytail is captured, the team contacts the relevant station to arrange pickup.
- If neither station can be reached, the team contacts the regional Endangered Species Office. The Region 6 coordinator is John Earnill in Denver, Colorado.

### 4. Temporary and permanent holding
- **Temporary facilities:**
  - [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] in Vernal, Utah, for the Green River subbasin. Fish are held in earthen ponds that are isolated from the mainstem Green River.
  - [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] in Hotchkiss, Colorado, for the Colorado River subbasin. Fish are held in an eight-foot circular fiberglass tank with continuous groundwater.
- **Transport between sites:** A 250-gallon [[Fish Distribution Tank]] mounted in a pickup truck carries fish from the takeout point to the temporary facility. Air transport is preferred for the next leg.
- **Permanent facilities:** [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in Dexter, New Mexico, and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in Willow Beach, Arizona. Transfer from temporary to permanent holding should occur within two weeks.
- **Fallback contact:** If neither permanent facility can be reached, the Regional Endangered Species Office in Albuquerque, New Mexico, is the contact.
