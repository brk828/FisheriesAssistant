# Alabama Banded Sculpin: A New Subspecies of Cottus carolinae from Below the Fall Line

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic study describes [[Cottus Carolinae Infernatis]], a new subspecies of the [[Banded Sculpin]] (*Cottus carolinae*) from Alabama. It is the southernmost population of the genus *Cottus* known. The subspecies occupies the [[Alabama River System]], the [[Tallapoosa River System]] below the [[Fall Line]], the [[Lower Tombigbee River System]], the [[Cahaba River System]], and one stream in the [[Black Warrior River System]]. It differs from the northern and upland forms in body banding, chin pigmentation, preopercular spination, lateral-line completeness, and low fin-ray counts. The authors conclude that its habitat is being seriously reduced by pollution, dredging, and dams, and they note that the future of this sculpin and other endemic Alabama fishes is jeopardized.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic context.** The banded sculpin is widely distributed in eastern North America, west of the Appalachians and south of the Ohio Valley. It tolerates warmer, more downstream water than members of the [[Cottus Bairdi Complex]]. Earlier work by [[Robins 1954 Revision]] recognized two subspecies in the Mobile drainage: an undescribed form known from eight specimens in the lower Tallapoosa system, and [[Cottus Carolinae Zopherus]]. A 1961 survey by Robins and Bohlke found many otherwise suitable streams industrially polluted and devoid of fishes.

**Sampling.** The senior author surveyed the Tallapoosa and adjacent systems from 1963, obtaining much of the study material. Specimens came from as far south as lower Alabama system localities less than 50 miles north of Mobile (Lat. 31°30'N).

**Methods.** Counts and measurements followed [[Robins and Miller 1957]] and general methods followed [[Hubbs and Lagler 1958]]. The authors defined a "mottled" chin as one with unevenly distributed pigment, and a "uniformly pigmented" chin as one with evenly distributed pigment that may range from pale to nearly black. Four body saddles (bands 1 to 4) were defined for comparison. Specimens are held in collections at UAIC, AU, CU, TU, USNM, and UMMZ.

**Diagnostic and meristic features of *infernatis*:**
- Dark, broad body bands, each about equal to or broader than the pale interspace.
- Chin pigmentation relatively uniform.
- Preopercular armature reduced to a well-developed upper spine, a small second spine, and a reduced or knob-like lowest spine.
- [[Lateral Line]] complete, usually with 30 to 32 pores anterior to the hypural plate.
- Dorsal spines modally 7; dorsal soft rays modally 16; anal rays modally 12; pectoral rays modally 15; branched caudal rays typically 9.
- Vertebrae 30 or 31.
- Small body size; seldom exceeds 80 mm SL (largest known 87 mm).
- Pelvic fins fall anterior to the triangular mark above the anus, and the genital papilla is short.

**Type material.** The [[Holotype]] is USNM 203762, an 81 mm SL male from Chewacla Creek, Chewacla State Park, about 3 mi S of Auburn, Lee County, Alabama, collected 3 April 1964. Paratypes are listed by locality across Lee, Macon, Clarke, Elmore, Chilton, Autauga, Monroe, Bibb, Shelby, and Tuscaloosa counties, among others.

**Name.** The epithet *infernatis* is the genitive of the Latin *infernas*, meaning "of the lowlands," referring to the form's distribution below the fall line. The authors note that the epithet *carolinae* (after Miss Caroline Henry, type locality near Maysville, Kentucky) has been misunderstood.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Within the [[Mobile Basin]], *infernatis* occurs from the lower Alabama system north into the lower Tallapoosa system and into the Cahaba system, except for the Little Cahaba and some northeastern tributaries. Elsewhere it is known from one spring run in the lower Tombigbee system and one tributary to Valley Creek in the Black Warrior system. Earlier records of *Cottus bairdi* from Will's Creek in the Escambia drainage were referred to *zopherus* in the Coosa system.

**Habitat.** The subspecies is adaptable, occupying small to large tributaries and rivers, but is most frequent in small to moderate creeks over [[Gravel and Rocky Riffles]]. Its absence from parts of the lower Cahaba and central Alabama drainages is largely due to unsuitable habitat. Streams draining the [[Black Belt]] have chalky marl bottoms with little or no flow. Exceptions are larger tributaries and rivers with sand and gravel flood plains.

**Reproduction.** Ripe males and females with eggs ready to shed were collected in late January and early February, at water temperatures of 9 to 13 °C. No nests were found, supporting the view that carolinae-complex sculpins lay eggs singly or broadcast them. Two females from UAIC 1828 (Cahaba system), measuring 47.8 and 56.8 mm SL, carried 123 (67 left, 56 right) and 150 (65 left, 85 right) eggs of 2.5 to 2.6 mm diameter. These eggs are smaller and more numerous than those reported for the bairdi complex. April collections had small gonads.

**Threats.** The authors identify several pressures:
- [[Industrial and Mining Pollution]] of streams, the most serious.
- [[Siltation]] from dredging, road construction, and poor agricultural practices.
- [[Dam Construction]], which floods riffle habitat.

**Related fauna.** [[Cottus Pygmaeus]], described by Williams (1968), also occurs in the Alabama River system. It lacks palatine teeth, has three pelvic rays, and has an incomplete lateral line.

## Key Findings

1. **New subspecies.** *Cottus carolinae infernatis* is described as the southernmost *Cottus* population, distinguished from *zopherus* and *carolinae* by its morphology.

2. **Body pattern.** Broad dark bands (bands 2 and 3 are straighter-edged and nearly as wide as or wider than the pale interspaces) and a uniformly pigmented chin, which the authors describe as extreme within the carolinae complex for larger specimens.

3. **Lateral line.** Complete and uninterrupted in nearly all specimens. Only 5 of 176 specimens had one small posterior break, compared with about 16% of *zopherus*. Pore counts range 29 to 34 (usually 30 to 32), with a mean of 30.86 (N = 176). The *carolinae* (Midlands) mean is 32.90.

4. **Fin-ray counts.** Lower than *zopherus* and *carolinae* in several characters. Pectoral rays for *infernatis* average 14.76 (N = 241), versus 14.99 (N = 359) for the Coosa *zopherus* and 16.52 (N = 1,491) for the Midlands *carolinae*. Dorsal soft rays average 15.88 (N = 233), versus 16.81 (N = 159) for Coosa *zopherus* and 16.55 (N = 727) for Midlands *carolinae*. Dorsal spines average 7.18 (N = 233).

5. **Preopercular armature.** Reduced to two spines in most specimens, with a third spine present only as a short knob, unlike the three or four sharp spines of *carolinae*.

6. **Relationship.** *Infernatis* is most closely related to, and probably derived from, *zopherus*. The authors treat *zopherus* and *infernatis* as subspecies of *carolinae* pending further study. *Zopherus* has longer pelvic fins and a mottled chin.

7. **Size.** A small form, seldom reaching 80 mm SL.

8. **Conservation status.** Habitat is "seriously limited" by pollution, siltation, and dams, and the future of this sculpin and other Alabama endemics is considered jeopardized.
