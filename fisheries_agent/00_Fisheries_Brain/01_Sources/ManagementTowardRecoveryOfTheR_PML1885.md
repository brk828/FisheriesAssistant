# Management Toward Recovery of the Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter reviews the decline of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large, uniquely shaped catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], and describes the development of a recovery program built on [[Hatchery Propagation]] and [[Reintroduction]]. The authors reconstruct the species' historic abundance from the historical and archaeological record, document its collapse across the lower and upper basin, and summarize biology (reproduction, movement, larval ecology) that informed recovery. Reasons for decline are discussed in terms of [[Non-Native Fishes]], reservoir effects, and [[Recruitment Failure]]. The chapter closes with an interim evaluation of reintroduction results and a list of future needs, arguing that recovery requires long-term commitment, habitat protection, and sustained research.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Xyrauchen* is a monotypic genus. The species was first described as *Catostomus texanus* (Abbott, 1861) from the "Colorado and New Rivers," and later placed in *Xyrauchen* by Eigenmann and Kirsch.
- **Morphology:** Adults have a high, sharp-edged predorsal keel-like hump, a flattened head, and a subterminal mouth. Breeding males are more robust with larger fins and nuptial tubercles; females are thicker-bodied with lower, broader keels. Species reaches about 60 cm and roughly 16 lb (about 7 kg).
- **Range:** Historically from Wyoming to northwestern Mexico, including the Gila, Salt, Verde, Green, Colorado, and Gunnison systems and the Salton Sea basin. Once common across the lower basin.
- **Conservation status:** Listed as endangered; a formal proposal to list as threatened was withdrawn, and a later petition sought endangered status.
- **Lineage-related issues:** Hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] has been documented, with substantial controversy over its extent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic use:** The fish was harvested by Native Americans (named by Yuman and Cocopah peoples) and appears in archaeological sites. Indians used V-shaped weirs on a paleo-shoreline of the Salton Sea basin, which corresponded to depths used by spawning aggregations.
- **Abundance records:** Early accounts describe it as "extremely abundant" near Yuma (Gilbert and Scofield, 1898) and as a commercial food fish, but quantitative data are sparse. Historic collections were overwhelmingly adults; juveniles were essentially absent.
- **Lower basin:** Natural populations disappeared from the Gila basin. Lake Mohave holds the only large adult population. Lake Mead and Lake Havasu populations declined after impoundment, with reproduction not detected for decades.
- **Upper basin:** Scarce across the Green and Colorado sub-basins. Holden and others recorded few fish, with recent data indicating roughly 900–1,000 adults in the upper Green River above Desolation Canyon (estimate 979 ± 232 by Lanigan and Tyus).
- **Spawning habitat:** Lake Mohave spawning occurs over coarse cobble and gravel in water 0.5–1 m deep, with depressions clearing fine sediment. Upper-basin spawning occurs in flat-water, sand-bottom and gravel-bar reaches.
- **Threats:** Dams and altered temperature regimes, predation by non-native fishes (carp, channel catfish, green sunfish, largemouth bass, threadfin shad), hybridization, and entrainment into canals.

## Key Findings

1. **Decline:** Razorback suckers have disappeared from the Gila basin and most of the Colorado mainstream. Lake Mohave holds the only major adult population, and downstream catches are sporadic.
2. **Recruitment failure:** Wild juveniles are rare; no natural recruitment into the Lake Mohave adult population has been detected for nearly three decades. Larvae (around 9–13 mm TL) vanish from the reservoir, consistent with predation.
3. **Predation hypothesis:** The authors conclude that predation by non-native fishes is the most likely factor preventing recruitment. Exclosure and isolated-pond experiments showed larvae survived and grew when predators were removed (for example, 80% survival of 50 larvae in exclosures) and disappeared when green sunfish were present.
4. **Hatchery success:** Egg hatch at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] commonly exceeded 90%. Growth rates were high, with fish reaching maturity in 2–3 years under hatchery conditions.
5. **Reintroduction results (interim):** Of about 1.2 million (roughly "nearly 12 million" in the source; reported as "nearly 12 million" larvae and juveniles stocked in central Arizona), only 118 fish were recaptured through autumn 1988, a very low return. None was recovered from the Colorado mainstream despite 2.9 million larvae and juveniles stocked in 1987–1988.
6. **Genetic concerns:** Electrophoretic work (Buth et al. 1987) found Lake Mohave brood fish were razorback suckers with negligible introgression, supporting continued use of brood stock.
7. **Policy and legal context:** The 1981 memorandum of understanding between AZGFD and USFWS enabled reintroductions, with a commitment to produce up to 100,000 fish per year. Legal protection was extended to all five states by 1987, and the Endangered Species Act provisions shaped reintroduction practice.
8. **Recommendations:** Priorities include removing or blocking non-native predators in habitat reaches, using isolated backwaters and reservoir nurseries, rearing larger fish before release, and establishing centralized data repositories. The authors urge publication in peer-reviewed outlets over reliance on gray literature.
9. **Long-term outlook:** Given possible lifespans of 40–50 years, the authors estimate that populations established by 1990 could persist until about 2030–2040, and state that recruitment success remains unknown. Recovery criteria have not been formally defined.
