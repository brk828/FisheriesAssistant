# From Balance of Nature to Hierarchical Patch Dynamics: A Paradigm Shift in Ecology

#ecology #ecosystem-ecology #patch-dynamics

## Executive Summary

This 1995 review in *The Quarterly Review of Biology* by Wu and Loucks argues that the [[Balance Of Nature]] assumption, which holds that undisturbed ecosystems are ordered, harmonious, and return to equilibrium after disturbance, has shaped ecological theory for centuries and has limited it. The authors contend that the [[Classical Equilibrium Paradigm]] failed because equilibria are rare in nature and because it could not incorporate heterogeneity and scale. They propose [[Hierarchical Patch Dynamics]] as an emerging paradigm that merges [[Patch Dynamics]] with [[Hierarchy Theory]]. The framework treats ecosystems as nested hierarchies of patch mosaics, explains system dynamics as composites of patch changes, and relies on the concepts of [[Incorporation Principle]] and [[Metastability]]. The paper is a synthesis and conceptual review. It reports no new empirical data, and its quantitative content comes from cited studies and a single modelling example.

## Background & Description

**Historical roots.** The authors trace the balance-of-nature idea to early Greek cosmologies and to Chinese yin-yang philosophy. Carl Linnaeus named it *oeconomia naturae* in 1749. The notion moved from divine order to a self-maintaining natural order, and it was reinforced by 19th-century thinkers including Darwin, Marsh, and Forbes. Frederic Clements' supraorganismic view of plant communities (1916) is cited as a direct product of this tradition. Critics such as Gleason (1926) and Elton (1930) challenged it, but it remained influential.

**Stability concepts.** The paper distinguishes four components of stability: resistance, resilience, persistence, and variability or constancy. Resistance and resilience presuppose an equilibrium, whereas persistence and variability do not. Botkin and Sobel (1975) proposed Θ-persistence, meaning fluctuation within bounds without a static equilibrium, and [[Homeorhesis]] (Waddington, 1957) describes return to a pre-perturbation trajectory rather than to a fixed state.

**Nonequilibrium and multiple-equilibrium views.** Several alternatives are reviewed:
- Chesson and Case (1986) identified four types of nonequilibrium community theory.
- [[Dissipative Structure]] theory (Prigogine) explains order emerging from fluctuation in open systems, with the related idea of [[Stratified Stability]] (O'Neill et al., 1986).
- Multiple-equilibrium perspectives (Levin, 1974, 1976, 1979) describe spatial patchiness as supporting alternative locally stable communities.
- Bifurcation and threshold phenomena, along with chaos and catastrophe theory, show that determinism does not guarantee stability.

**Patchiness.** A patch is a spatial unit that differs from its surroundings. The authors catalogue definitions from more than ten studies and emphasise that patches are scale- and organism-dependent. Their conceptual framework (Fig. 1) divides patchiness into physical (abiotic) and biological causes, with disturbances, vegetation pattern, and biotic interactions among the drivers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors argue that the balance-of-nature assumption has practical consequences. Its "nature knows best" corollary has influenced conservation, resource management, and environmental protection. Their examples of patch-scale systems include:
- **Forests:** gap dynamics, in which canopy-tree death creates gaps that feed a forest-wide "shifting mosaic steady state" (Bormann and Likens, 1979).
- **Intertidal communities:** wave-generated patches of different successional age (Levin and Paine, 1974; Paine and Levin, 1981).
- **Grasslands:** patches formed by burrowing animals, grazing, soil heterogeneity, and fire.
- **Aquatic systems:** phytoplankton and zooplankton aggregates of different sizes (Steele, 1978).
- **Landscapes:** mosaics of ecosystem patches, each of which is a patch hierarchy at finer scale.

Holling's (1992) cross-scale analysis gives approximate ranges for three structuring process classes:
- Vegetative processes: centimetres to tens of metres in space, days to decades in time.
- Contagious disturbances (fire, insect outbreak, disease): hundreds of metres to hundreds of kilometres, years to decades.
- Geomorphological processes: hundreds to thousands of kilometres, centuries to millennia.

The paper also notes that small systems face higher extinction risk from biotic feedbacks or stochastic instability than large systems do, because their dynamics are faster and higher in frequency.

## Key Findings

1. **The balance of nature is not supported empirically.** Wild animal numbers vary irregularly in period and amplitude. Direct evidence that ecological systems are inherently in equilibrium is lacking. Hall (1988) found that none of the data he examined agreed with the predictions of the logistic and Lotka-Volterra models they were said to support. Gilbert (1980) reached similar conclusions for [[Island Biogeography]] equilibria.

2. **Heterogeneity and scale are central.** The classical paradigm generally neglects spatial patchiness. Observed stability depends on the scale of observation, so the same dynamics may appear transient or steady depending on the observer's "observation window."

3. **Hierarchical patch dynamics integrates prior approaches.** The paradigm combines five elements:
   - Ecological systems as nested, discontinuous hierarchies of patch mosaics.
   - System dynamics as a composite of patch dynamics, with emergent properties that are not simply sums of patch behaviour.
   - A pattern-process-scale perspective, in which pattern and process are inseparable from scale.
   - A nonequilibrium perspective, in which small scales show continuous transient dynamics and very large scales show long-term change.
   - [[Incorporation Principle]] and [[Metastability]], in which nonequilibrium processes at one level become quasi-equilibrium at a higher level.

4. **Incorporation is illustrated across systems.** Single treefalls create gap-level nonequilibrium that is absorbed into stand-level steady states. Large blowdowns and wildfires may be incorporated only at landscape scale. Loucks (1970) described wave-form dynamics in southern Wisconsin forests, with long-term steady state composed of transient responses to fires at intervals of 30 to 200 years. Rahel (1990) found that phytoplankton assemblages fluctuate at short time scales but are more stable over longer periods.

5. **Five stabilising mechanisms are proposed,** building on [[Deangelis And Waterhouse Classification]] of stable, unstable, and weakly interactive communities:
   - (A) Spatial incorporation of localised biotic feedback instability, through quasi-isolation and asynchronous patch dynamics.
   - (B) Environmental disturbance control of biotic feedback instability, which keeps populations within bounds and prevents exclusion.
   - (C) Biotic compensatory control of stochastic instability, such as density-dependent recruitment at low densities.
   - (D) Spatial incorporation of stochastic instability, or "spreading of risk" (den Boer, 1968), in which local extinctions are offset by colonisation from disturbance-free patches.
   - (E) Absorption of instability through heterogeneity and functional redundancy.

   The authors note that quantitative expressions for these mechanisms remain preliminary and need field testing.

6. **Modelling needs improvement.** The patch demographic model of Levin and Paine (1974; Paine and Levin, 1981) uses age- and size-structured patch distributions. The paper describes spatially explicit approaches: [[Gap Dynamics]] models on grids, and the patch-based PatchMod of Wu and Levin (1994), which handles overlapping patches and explicitly models two hierarchical levels. Turner et al. (1993) modelled a 100 × 100 cell grid with eight seral stages and found landscapes that ranged from steady state to unstable, with bifurcation or crash behaviour. No existing framework fully incorporates more than two hierarchical levels.

7. **Metastability is the closest technical equivalent to balance of nature.** Its authors describe it as scale-dependent persistence, found "at the meta-scale," and summarise the idea as "order within disorder." It differs from static stability in being dynamic and multi-scale.

8. **Practical implications.** The paradigm emphasises rather than avoids disturbance, heterogeneity, and multiple spatiotemporal scales in management. It is presented as a common ground for [[Metapopulation Theory]], landscape ecology, and conservation biology. Leopold's 1927 remark that the balance of nature "has been upset long ago" is quoted to argue for managing each area according to its intended use.

9. **Status and limitations.** The authors acknowledge that the paradigm is still emerging, that several mechanisms lack empirical tests, and that long time series and robust statistics are scarce. Table 1 rates the paradigm as satisfactory as a conceptual framework, with high potential but preliminary testing, while the balance-of-nature view is rated untestable and questionable.
