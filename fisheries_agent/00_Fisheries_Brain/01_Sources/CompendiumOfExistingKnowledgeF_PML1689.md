# Compendium of Existing Knowledge for Habitat Management Recommendations, Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 6) memorandum transmits a compendium, compiled from 1989 and dated May 1990 by Allen S. Miller and Wayne A. Hubert, that synthesizes existing knowledge on the habitat needs of the rare riverine fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The compendium is intended to identify and quantify the physical, chemical, and biological conditions required by [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and to show where those needs are not being met. It is designed to support habitat management recommendations under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]], to assess impacts of proposed projects, and to identify research gaps. The memo asks reviewers for corrections, missing literature, and comments on limiting variables.

## Background & Description

The memorandum (Assistant Regional Director, Fish and Wildlife Enhancement, Region 6, Denver) is addressed to a management group and to consultants and interested parties, including recovery team members. The attached compendium is organized into chapters covering:

- Physical description and study history of the basin
- Legal and institutional constraints (e.g., [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922, [[Colorado River Storage Project]] Act of 1956)
- Hydrology, species response to flow, and channel maintenance
- Temperature, habitat use, and water depths, velocities and substrates
- Pollutants and toxicants, life history and distribution, biotic interactions, and foods

The Upper Basin is defined under the Upper Colorado River Basin Compact and covers roughly 293,955 square km. It is divided into the Grand, Green, and San Juan sub-basins (Joseph et al. 1977 also classified stream zones as Trout Waters, Transition Zones, and Large River Channels, the last of which holds the rare fishes). Basin rocks range from 625 million years old to recent alluvium. Mean annual precipitation is about 40 cm, and the basin has the lowest runoff per unit area of any major U.S. drainage. Roughly 9.5 MAF of precipitation falls annually, with about 8.0 MAF lost to evapotranspiration.

The compendium is a literature synthesis. Its authors report facts, relationships, hypotheses and opinions as stated by original investigators, and they flag areas of consensus versus controversy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regulation.** Dams including [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]], [[Blue Mesa Reservoir]] and [[Navajo Dam]] have altered hydrographs. Reported effects include reduced peak flows, reduced annual fluctuation, increased daily fluctuation, increased salinity, reduced water temperature, and reduced turbidity. Peak flows declined by 28.7% (May) and 36.9% (June) below Flaming Gorge Dam, and by 24.4%, 34.6%, 36.7%, and 15.3% for May, June, July and August on the upper Colorado mainstem. Mean annual flow in the San Juan Basin declined about 3% after Navajo Dam closure.

**Sediment and channel processes.** Channel equilibrium varies by reach. The Palisade reach is near equilibrium; the Browns Park reach (river miles 363-396) is degrading by about 4 feet since 1951; the Green River reach below the Duchesne is aggrading. In the Yampa River, the Little Snake River contributes about 70% of the sediment load while supplying about 27% of the flow. Cobble spawning substrates in the Yampa canyon depend on bedload transport and periodic flushing flows.

**Water depletion.** The upper basin contributes about 15 MAF of runoff after 80 MAF of evapotranspiration. Irrigated agriculture accounted for 62% of depletions in 1975. Water demand was projected to grow about 50% between 1960 and 2000.

**Temperature and climate.** Optimal hatching and larval survival occur near 20 °C for all four species. Growth ceases below about 12-13 °C for Colorado Squawfish. Growing seasons are under five months in occupied reaches, a potential limiting factor.

**Contaminants.** Colorado Squawfish preferred TDS of 560-1,150 mg/L, while concentrations above 4,400 mg/L were avoided. Oil shale wastes were more toxic to channel catfish, cutthroat trout, and fathead minnows than to Colorado Squawfish.

**Introduced species.** Common carp, channel catfish, red shiner, fathead minnow, green sunfish, largemouth bass and others now occupy the basin. Many prey on or compete with native young, and they thrive in perennial backwaters that native fishes can avoid.

## Key Findings

**Species response to flow**

- Colorado Squawfish spawn on the recessional limb of the hydrograph as discharge declines toward baseflow; flow spikes may also cue spawning.
- Humpback Chub spawn on the recession shortly after peak spring flows.
- Razorback Sucker spawn on the ascending limb up to about peak spring flow.
- No spawning-timing data exist for Bonytail Chub.
- Humpback Chub and Roundtail Chub may hybridize when historic flow regimes are altered.
- Native fishes reproduce better than introduced species in high-peak-flow years. In the Yampa River, Colorado Squawfish larval catch was 800% higher in years with peaks above 20,000 cfs, a flow with about a 20-year recurrence interval.
- Daily fluctuations below dams strand young fish and reduce larval survival.
- Late-summer high flows reduce backwater nursery habitat for Colorado Squawfish larvae; Jones and Tyus found mean daily discharge explained 80% of variation in catch per unit effort.

**Temperature thresholds (summary)**

- Optimal hatching for Colorado Squawfish, Humpback Chub and Razorback Sucker is about 20 °C; Bonytail Chub, 15-21 °C.
- Preferred temperatures: Colorado Squawfish and juveniles 25 °C; Humpback Chub 24 °C; Bonytail 24.2 °C; Razorback Sucker 22-24 °C.
- Peak swimming ability: Colorado Squawfish about 20 °C; Humpback Chub above 26 °C; Bonytail about 26 °C; Razorback Sucker about 20 °C.
- Colorado Squawfish migration begins at 16-19 °C; spawning at 18-25 °C, with peak activity near 20-22 °C.
- Larval drift begins near 16 °C, with 65% of drift occurring at 18-21 °C.

**Habitat use**

- Adult Colorado Squawfish use shoreline, eddy and run habitat in summer, backwaters during spring runoff, and deep pools in winter.
- Humpback Chub adults are most often found in eddies, especially those formed by boulders and rapids; they show high habitat specialization to deep, swift canyon reaches.
- Bonytail Chub habitat is poorly known; recent captures are from canyon reaches with swift currents.
- Razorback Sucker adults use low-velocity habitats, gravel pits and backwaters; some were stranded in gravel pits as flows declined.
- Juveniles and young-of-year of Colorado Squawfish use backwaters and low-velocity zones (0.0-0.15 m/s), typically at depths near 0.3 m.

**Habitat assessment methods**

- Aerial photography is the most accurate backwater mapping method but is costly. Video imagery costs about half as much (e.g., $9,600 vs. $18,400 for one Green River segment) and is slightly less precise.
- The Physical Habitat Simulation model (PHABSIM) quantifies habitat availability at different discharges using depth, velocity and substrate; it requires field data across a range of flows and assumes a stable channel.

**Flushing flows and sediment**

- Yampa River canyon: between 1,220,000 and 1,400,000 acre-feet per year is believed necessary to keep cobble substrate clean. Estimates of depletion available range from 100,000 acre-feet (Butler 1988, the more prudent figure) to 288,000 acre-feet (O'Brien 1984).
- Palisade reach (Colorado River): a flow of 10,000-20,000 cfs for a number of hours is required to flush fines (recurrence interval about 1.65 years).
- Butler's modeling indicated that peak flows reduced below about 71% of historic levels, combined with depletion, cause aggradation in the Yampa canyon.

**Key knowledge gaps**

- No spawning timing data for Bonytail Chub.
- No substrate or velocity data for non-spawning Bonytail Chub.
- No quantitative flow-habitat relationships for Bonytail or Razorback Sucker.
- Limited information on food availability in the upper basin.

**Management program goals (Recovery Implementation Program)**

- Colorado Squawfish: maintain and protect self-sustaining populations and natural habitat.
- Humpback Chub: maintain or establish and protect five self-sustaining populations, natural habitat, and two refugia.
- Bonytail Chub: prevent immediate extinction; establish and protect six self-sustaining populations.
- Razorback Sucker: establish and protect self-sustaining populations (candidate for listing).

**Key sources of data** include Tyus and Karp (1989) on Yampa River flows, Archer et al. (1986) on distributions, Miller et al. (1982c) on habitat and flow, and Butler (1988) on sediment budgets. The compendium gives a species-by-species count of about 40 tables and over 300 figures in its appendices; these are summarized rather than reproduced here.
