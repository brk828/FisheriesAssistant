# Rearing Methods and the Return of Fall-Migrating Chinook Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by the [[Washington State Department of Fisheries]] tested whether the length and salinity of a pre-release sea-water phase improved the survival of hatchery-reared [[Chinook Salmon]] (fall-migrating, "[[Fall Chinook]]") planted as juveniles in the [[Samish River]] estuary. Roughly 125,000 juveniles were reared in fresh water for 78 days at the [[Samish State Salmon Hatchery]], split into four experimental groups, and released as 3- and 4-year-old spawner returns were tracked in 1955 and 1956. The group given a 5-day conversion to 75 percent sea water after fresh-water rearing returned at the highest rate (0.75 percent). Fish released directly from fresh water returned at 0.47 percent. Longer sea-water rearing (25 or 60 days) produced the lowest returns (0.26 and 0.19 percent). Returns were not linked to larger release size, and marked and unmarked fish showed the same migration timing.

## Background & Description

Artificial rearing aims to increase the survival of planted salmon, measured by their return as adult spawners. Approaches include improved diets, disease control, size control, and selective breeding. For [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Coho Salmon]], and [[Sockeye Salmon]], which feed during a juvenile stage in fresh water, fresh-water rearing had already been shown to raise survival. [[Pink Salmon]] and [[Chum Salmon]] young feed little or not at all in fresh water, so no fresh-water procedure had been shown to increase their returns. The department had instead run an extensive program showing that extended feeding and rearing in sea water could substantially increase returns of pink and chum salmon.

Earlier work on fall chinook reared 30 to 100 days in sea water after a roughly three-month fresh-water period showed no benefit from the extra salt-water phase, based on marked fish returns. The present study therefore varied the length of salt-water exposure before planting, from a 5-day conversion to an 8-day conversion followed by 60 days of sea-water rearing, and compared these with fish planted directly from fresh water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rearing and release sites**
- Fish were reared in fresh water at the [[Samish State Salmon Hatchery]], on a bench of the [[Samish River]] in western Washington.
- Group 1 was released immediately after fresh-water rearing and marking into estuarine water at the river mouth near the [[Edison Bridge]]. Salinity at high tide there was about 22 parts per thousand.
- Groups 2, 3, and 4 were trucked about 30 miles by water to the [[Bowman's Bay Marine Biological Station]], which had both fresh and salt water for controlled conversion and rearing. (The abstract gives "about 10 miles," but the body text gives 30 miles.)

**Conversion and rearing conditions**
- Group 2 was moved over 5 days to 75 percent sea water (close to the estuary's salinity) and planted in the same manner as Group 1.
- Group 3 was moved over 8 days to 100 percent sea water and reared there for 25 days before planting.
- Group 4 was handled like Group 3 but reared in 100 percent sea water for 60 days.
- Fish stayed in excellent condition through conversion and salt-water rearing. Mortality never exceeded 1 percent per week and was usually below 0.5 percent per week.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design (Table 1 summary)**

| 1 | Dorsal | 78 days | None | None | 150 | 0.47 |

| 4 | Adipose | 78 days | 8 days (to 100%) | 60 days | 25 | 0.19 |

- Total planted: 122,022 marked fish. Recorded returns: 496 over the two return years.

**Returns and survival**
- Highest return: Group 2 (5-day conversion to 75% sea water), 0.75 percent.
- Second: Group 1 (released directly from fresh water), 0.47 percent.
- Third: Group 3 (8-day conversion, then 25 days in sea water), 0.26 percent.
- Lowest: Group 4 (8-day conversion, then 60 days in sea water), 0.19 percent.
- Group 2 was superior to the other groups in all respects except average weight.

**Size and survival**
- Released sizes were about 150 (Groups 1 and 2), 49 (Group 3), and 25 (Group 4) fish per pound.
- The usual expectation that larger fish survive better was not supported. Group 4, the largest fish at release, had the lowest return, while the smallest fish (Group 2) had the highest.

**Sex ratio and weight of returns**
- Males predominated among 3-year-old returns in 1955 (193 of 194, as printed in the OCR text).
- Returns of 4-year-old fish in 1956 were about evenly split between males and females (134 and 133, per OCR).
- Average weight of 4-year-old returns was slightly higher in Group 4 (16.1 pounds per fish, per OCR) than in Group 2 (14.9 pounds). The authors attributed this to a smaller proportion of females, which are smaller than males in chinook, among Group 2 returns.

**Migration timing**
- Marked and unmarked fish returned to the [[Samish Hatchery]] with the same run pattern (Figure 2).
- Both groups peaked in early October in both years, and no important differences were found between marked and unmarked returns.

**Conclusions**
- A short pre-release sea-water conversion (5 days to 75% sea water) after 78 days in fresh water gave the best return.
- Extended sea-water rearing (25 to 60 days) did not improve returns and was associated with lower survival.
- The study supports [[Estuarine Acclimation]] as a useful hatchery step for fall chinook, but the effect appears to depend on the length and salinity of exposure rather than on release size.
