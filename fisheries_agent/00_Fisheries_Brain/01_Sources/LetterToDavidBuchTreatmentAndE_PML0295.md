# Lower Colorado River Surface Water and Shoreline Change, 1927/1903 to 1982

#aquatic-ecology #riverine-habitat #hydrology #lower-colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This 1983 cover letter and accompanying data set from the [[Southwest Natural History Association]] report a reach-by-reach comparison of surface water, shoreline, island, and canal extent along the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The work was done under [[Purchase Order 3-PG-30-09150]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], Lower Colorado Regional Office. Historic conditions are drawn largely from 1903 survey maps (cited as 1927 in the tables), and current conditions from 1982 maps. The document contains no fish, invertebrate, or water chemistry data. Its value for aquatic ecology lies in documenting habitat change: overall surface water increased by almost 50%, shoreline length was nearly unchanged, islands declined markedly, and canals increased almost fourfold.

## Background & Description

The letter, dated July 1983, was sent by Mrs. Louella B. Brown, President of the Southwest Natural History Association in Phoenix, Arizona, to David Busch of the Bureau of Reclamation in Boulder City, Nevada. It accompanied tables, maps, and worksheets for each river segment. The author offered to submit a proposal for an expanded treatment and explanation of historic changes if the Bureau was interested.

The letter defines the categories used in the mapping:

- **River:** the normal river channel(s) and all water areas connected to the main channel, including reservoirs.
- **Lakes:** water bodies completely separated from the main channel but within the Colorado River flood plain.
- **Islands:** lands completely surrounded by the main river channel or by lakes.
- **Shoreline:** both shores of the main channel, length of secondary river channels, lake shorelines, and island shorelines.
- **Canals:** man-made water conduits.

Working notes add further rules. Islands were subtracted from river and lake measurements. Lakes were defined as water bodies separated from or connected only by canals or channels. Shorelines included levees, dikes, and jetties. Canals were identified by their straight form and symbolic designation on 1982 maps, and were measured only by length. The author noted that 1982 maps appear to include less of the river valley than the 1903 maps, probably causing an underestimate of 1982 canal length. Natural-origin channels were also measured by length only, and the 1982 maps appear to show far fewer channels than the 1903 maps, especially in Divisions 1 and 11.

Maps were reported at different water levels. Reaches 1 and 3 used high water levels, while some reaches used low water levels or the mean of low and high levels. Measurement conversions were applied to both map series.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reaches run from the Davis Dam area in the north to the Mexican border in the south. They include several named features of aquatic and riparian importance:

- [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]], cited as a stabilized wetland upstream of Laguna Dam.
- [[Laguna Dam]], [[Imperial Dam]], [[Headgate Rock Dam]], [[Palo Verde Diversion Dam]], and [[Morelos Dam]], which regulate flow and create reservoirs.
- [[Cibola Lake]] and the reach north of it to the 35°N latitude line.
- [[Bill Williams River]] and its delta, which were measured together and noted as contributing to upstream water area changes.
- [[Gila River]], which was measured to a point above its junction with the Colorado and was treated separately in the Division 13 and 14 mapping. Because the Colorado River's channel changed, some Gila channel mapped in 1903 appears as Colorado channel in 1982.
- [[All-American Canal]], a major canal included in canal totals.
- An industrial waste reservoir for a power plant, excluded from totals (0.56 km² of water and 4.57 km of shoreline were noted).

The letter notes that changes in water and shoreline area were driven by reservoir formation, altered mean flows, and channel shifts. Island loss is linked to changes in the river channel and to the spread of canal networks.

## Key Findings

- **Overall shoreline:** The letter states that shoreline length was almost exactly the same in 1982 as in 1927 (1903). Losses in the lower reaches were offset by greater shoreline along upstream reservoirs.
- **Overall surface water:** There was an overall gain in surface water of almost 50% between 1927 (1903) and 1982. Losses below Laguna Dam were more than offset by increased mean flows, reservoirs, and stabilized wetlands upstream.
- **Islands:** The major overall loss occurred in islands. Both island shoreline length and island area decreased substantially during the interval.
- **Canals:** Canal length increased almost fourfold.
- **Reach 1 (Davis Dam to T.19N/T.18N boundary):** River water area rose from 7.49 km² to 8.66 km²; lake water area rose from 0.67 km² to 0.79 km² (change of about +1.29 km² in total water, based on high water levels).
- **Reach 3 (Topock to T.6N/T.5N boundary):** Total water area rose from about 3.48 km² to 8.26 km². River shoreline rose from about 49.16 km to 85.91 km.
- **Reach 4 (T.6N/T.5N to north of T.13N/T.12N):** Total water area rose from about 5.02 km² to 57.87 km² (change of about +52.85 km²).
- **Reach 7 (north of T.13N/T.12N to north of Bill Williams River):** Total water area rose from about 4.16 km² to 26.76 km². River shoreline rose from about 43.57 km to 148.99 km (about +105.42 km).
- **Reach 9 (Palo Verde Diversion Dam to Riverside/Imperial County line):** Water area was reported at about 47 km² in 1927 with large shoreline and canal changes; several values in the scanned table are partly illegible.
- **Reach 13 (Imperial Dam to Laguna Dam):** Total water area rose from 3.20 km² to 3.60 km². Total island area fell from 45.91 km² (1927) to 4.45 km² in one listing; the scan quality makes some values uncertain.
- **Reach 17 (whole river, Davis Dam to Sonora border):** Totals were reported for water, canals, shoreline, and islands, but several entries are too garbled to reproduce reliably.
- **Data limitations:** Tables were produced from different map scales and survey dates. Canal and channel mapping differs between the two series, so canal change may be underestimated. Values are reported at high or low water levels, which affects comparability.
- **Future work:** The author offered to prepare a proposal for an expanded historical analysis of Lower Colorado River changes if the Bureau of Reclamation requested it.
