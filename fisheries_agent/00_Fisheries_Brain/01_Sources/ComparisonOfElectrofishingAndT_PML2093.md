# Sampling Variability of Trammel Netting and Electrofishing for Rare Riverine Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares [[Trammel Netting]] and [[Electrofishing]] for monitoring three native fishes of the mainstem [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]: the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. Using 1992 and 1993 data from an 8 km reach, the author evaluated catch variability, gear agreement, size selectivity and the sample effort needed to detect population change. Trammel nets gave lower and more consistent [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (CPUE) variability and captured a higher proportion of adult fish. Electrofishing was biased toward smaller individuals. Catches of all three species were too low for either gear to detect annual changes with practical effort. The author concludes that long-term trend analysis, possibly with [[Mark-Recapture]] stock assessment models, is more appropriate than annual CPUE point estimates.

## Background & Description

The three species are native to the Colorado River basin and are considered large-river fishes. The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is listed as endangered by the U.S. Government, which made its status a priority concern. Its recovery goals define adult size as ≥200 mm total length (TL), and the study applied the same threshold to the two sucker species for consistency. Native fish abundance in Grand Canyon has declined since the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] began impounding the river in 1963. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed that the humpback chub could be downlisted if the abundance of fish ≥200 mm did not decline over a five-year period, which makes reliable trend detection important.

The study was framed by a general concern in fisheries science: relative abundance indices are often assumed to reflect true abundance, but this assumption is frequently not met. Gear bias and high variability can mask year-to-year differences, and low-abundance populations are especially prone to these problems. The objectives were to:

- measure the precision of trammel netting and electrofishing for relative abundance and size structure;
- compare the length frequencies collected by each gear;
- estimate the sample sizes needed to detect changes in CPUE.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Sampling occurred in an 8 km reach of the Colorado River (rkm 96.6–104.7) near the confluence of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), the largest tributary in Grand Canyon. The LCR is the primary spawning area for native fishes, which explains why this reach has higher native fish densities than other mainstem areas. Catches elsewhere on the mainstem are generally low, probably because of effects from Glen Canyon Dam and interactions with non-native species.

**Habitat use by gear.** Electrofishing was conducted along shorelines. Trammel nets were mostly anchored to the shore and stretched across the channel, though some were suspended in mid-water, typically in deepwater eddies or areas known to be frequented by the study species. Adults of all three species mainly occupy deepwater eddies, tributary mouths and offshore habitats. Juveniles (<200 mm) mostly use shoreline areas with vegetation or cover. The author argues that gear selectivity therefore reflects both gear design and where each fish life stage lives.

**Sampling design.** Trammel nets were 22.9 m long and 1.8 m deep, with two outer walls of 30.5 cm mesh and an inner wall of 2.5 cm mesh. Nets were checked about every 2 h (median 2.21 h), with a total soak time mean of 4.9 h. Electrofishing used a pulsed-DC system (Coffelt Mark XX Complex Pulse System) on an inflatable boat, with output typically 200–250 V and 8–10 A. To limit variability linked to sample duration, electrofishing samples of 0.15–0.75 h and trammel samples of 1.5–3.5 h were analysed. Samples were grouped into seasons (spring: February–April; summer: May–July; autumn: August–October) and into [[Diel Period]]s (night and day). November through January were excluded because sampling was limited.

## Key Findings

**Overall effort and catches**
- Sampling covered 272 electrofishing stations and 303 trammel net sets from February to October 1992 and 1993.
- Electrofishing captured only 20 humpback chub, 17 flannelmouth suckers and 1 bluehead sucker ≥200 mm TL.
- Trammel netting captured 222 humpback chub, 85 flannelmouth suckers and 31 bluehead suckers ≥200 mm TL.
- Mean CPUE was below 0.27 for electrofishing and ranged from 0.05 to 0.34 for trammel netting, depending on species.

**CPUE variability (coefficient of variation)**
- [[Coefficient of Variation]] (CV) values ranged from 128 to 575 across gear types and species, showing very high variability even in the targeted habitat.
- For humpback chub, mean CV was significantly lower for trammel nets than for electrofishing (P = 0.004).
- For flannelmouth sucker, CV tended to be lower for trammel nets (P = 0.12).
- Season and diel period had no significant effect on mean CV for either species.
- Bluehead sucker could not be compared between gears because electrofishing captured only one fish ≥200 mm. Among trammel-net samples, CV did not differ by season or diel period.
- Variability was not related to sample duration for any species, for either gear.

**Agreement between gears**
- Paired samples (same date, location and hour) showed no positive relationship between gears for humpback chub (r = −0.31, n = 9, P = 0.43) or flannelmouth sucker (r = −0.27, n = 11, P = 0.46), as tested with [[Spearman Rank Correlation]].
- In many paired samples, one gear caught fish while the other caught none.
- The author concludes that conclusions drawn from one gear may not hold for the other.

**Size structure and selectivity**
- For humpback chub, 89–97% of fish caught by trammel nets were ≥200 mm, compared with only 1–6% of those caught by electrofishing. The difference was significant (P < 0.001).
- Flannelmouth suckers ≥200 mm made up a lower proportion of electrofishing catches than trammel catches, and electrofishing caught more smaller fish (P < 0.001).
- Electrofishing also captured a higher proportion of smaller bluehead suckers (P < 0.001), as tested with the [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]].
- The author attributes these differences to the habitats each gear samples and to the life stages present there.

**Sample size requirements (α = 0.10)**
- Detecting a 10% change in humpback chub CPUE at power 0.9 required up to 3,844 trammel net sets.
- Detecting the same change in flannelmouth sucker CPUE required up to 5,279 trammel net sets.
- Detecting a 25% change in humpback chub CPUE required between 170 (trammel, power 0.6) and 2,944 (electrofishing, power 0.9) samples.
- A minimum of 304 trammel net samples was needed for a 25% change in flannelmouth sucker CPUE at power 0.6.
- Bluehead sucker estimates ranged from 489 to 8,521 trammel samples.
- Even the smaller requirements were logistically impractical and would effectively require sampling the entire reach.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Trammel netting is recommended over electrofishing for long-term monitoring of adult native fishes in the mainstem Colorado River. It produced lower CPUE variability and captured a higher proportion of fish ≥200 mm.
- Annual CPUE point estimates are not adequate to detect changes in native fish relative abundance. Long-term trend analysis is more appropriate.
- Intensive [[Mark-Recapture]] estimates using stock assessment models may be needed to determine population trends. The author suggests this applies to other rare or endangered riverine fishes as well.
- Sampling should be consistent in season and diel period, and conducted when logistically feasible.
- Nearshore gears such as seines, hoop nets or small-mesh trammel nets, which were not tested here, may be better suited to monitoring juvenile abundance and recruitment.
