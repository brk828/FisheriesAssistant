# Influence of Light and Temperature on the Reproductive Cycle of the Eastern Brook Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 experimental study by Nancy E. Henderson, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, tests how [[Photoperiod]] (4 to 20 hours of light per day) and [[Water Temperature]] (8.5 °C and 16 °C) shape the [[Gonadal Maturation]] of [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*[[Salvelinus Fontinalis]]*). Female development was tracked by average ova diameter, and male development by six histological [[Spermatogenesis]] stages. The main conclusion is that the effect of a light regime depends on the phase of gametogenesis and on the light regimes experienced earlier. An [[Accelerated Light Regime]] advanced [[Functional Maturity]] only in fish that had already completed at least one reproductive cycle. Temperature had no effect under natural day lengths. The author rejects a simple additive "light-hours plus day-degrees" model and proposes that the response depends on maturation of the [[Hypothalamic-Hypophyseal Mechanism]].

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Eastern brook trout, a salmonid of the cold streams and lakes of eastern North America. Spawning is timed to the season: late August to September at 60° N, November at 35° N, and mid-October at Toronto (44° N). Normal functional maturity at Toronto was set at October 15 for comparison. Males usually mature in the first week of October and females one to two weeks later.
- **Prior work:** Earlier studies ([[Hoover And Hubbard]], 1937; [[Hazard And Eddy]], 1951; [[Corson]], 1955) showed that an accelerated light regime could induce functional maturity several months early. Those studies recorded only timing and did not examine the underlying gonadal stages. [[Atz]] (1957) criticized earlier experiments as too short and poorly controlled.
- **Aim:** To measure how different light and temperature regimes affect gonadal development at each phase of the cycle, and to test whether maturation time can be predicted from cumulative photoperiodic and thermal experience.
- **Terminology:** Ova are classed as recruitment stock (under about 1 mm), maturing ova (about 1 to 4 mm), and atretic (degenerating) ova. Oogenesis is split into a primary growth phase and a secondary growth phase, the latter being the period of rapid yolk growth from July to October. Males pass through six maturation stages, Stage 5 being the functionally mature (strippable) period.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal range:** Brook trout occurred between the 7 °C and 21 °C isotherms in two Nova Scotia lakes ([[Hayes]], 1946). In Redrock Lake, Ontario, they were distributed between the 12 °C and 20 °C isotherms during summer stratification ([[Baldwin]], 1948). Field records show they rarely occur where water exceeds about 20 °C. The two experimental temperatures therefore bracket the main growing-season range.
- **Photoperiod:** Brook trout inhabit streams and rivers, and so are exposed to the natural annual fluctuation in day length. The study argues that this exposure makes day length a plausible timing cue.
- **Laboratory conditions:** Fish were held in outdoor tanks at the [[Maple Laboratory]] under natural illumination and water at 8.5 ± 1 °C until experiments began.
  - *1957 (Experiments 1 to 6):* six cement tanks of 600 L, with 50 fish per tank, at a flow of 4 L/min. Artificial light intensity was 8 to 62 foot-candles.
  - *1958 to 1959 (Experiments 7 to 17):* six wooden tanks of 1100 L, with 140 fish per tank in Experiments 7 to 12. Flow was 6 to 7 L/min and artificial light was 24 to 63 foot-candles.
  - Warm water was produced by mixing heated and cold supplies. Oxygen was kept at or above 70% of air equilibrium in every sampled tank.
- **Health:** Mortality was high, at roughly 6 to 10 fish per 100 per month. Furunculosis, ulcer disease and *Saprolegnia* infection were recorded. Sulfamerazine and salt or acriflavin treatments were used. In early September, a sudden high mortality occurred in Experiments 7, 8, 10 and 12. Its cause was unknown, but a viral infection of the nervous system was suggested.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sampling**
- Each tank was sampled roughly semi-monthly (every 3 to 4 weeks in spring), usually with one male and one female per tank. Standard length and gonad weight were recorded.
- Female ova were measured in 30-egg samples. Ova under 1.3 mm were measured with a Whipple disc on a sub-sample of 10 ova per fish; larger ova were measured in grooved blocks.
- Stripping began once fish could yield gametes. Males were stripped weekly and tagged.
- Male tests showed that milt was viable for about 6 to 7 weeks after onset of functional maturity. Control males yielded milt for 7 to 8 consecutive weeks. In tested groups, at least 95% of ova were fertilized.

**Normal cycle (controls, natural day length, 8.5 °C)**
- Females: ova did not grow from late winter through spring. The secondary growth phase began in July, after the summer solstice, and ended by mid-October. Maturing ova grew from just over 1 mm to about 3.8 mm (mature range 3.5 to 4.0 mm).
- Males: spermatogonial proliferation was slow in winter. Rapid spermatogenesis (Stages 3 and 4) ran from late June to late September. Functional maturity (Stage 5) began about the autumnal equinox.
- Gametogenesis therefore tracked declining day length after the summer solstice.

**Experiments 1 to 5 (2-year-old, first reproductive cycle, 8.5 and 16 °C)**
- Experiment 1 (accelerated light, 8.5 and 16 °C) and Experiment 2 (accelerated light, 8.5 °C) did not advance maturity. Fish matured at the normal time.
- Experiments 3 and 4 (retarded light, 12 h then 20-minute weekly declines) also matured at the normal time, with males in 3 and 4 starting several weeks early.
- Experiment 5 (artificial light at natural day lengths) did not differ from controls.
- Conclusion: light intensity and probably quality had no influence. The time required for gonadal development in a first cycle was not set by the total hours of light received. Predicting maturity from additive photoperiod and thermal exposure was not possible within these limits.

**Experiments 7 to 12 (2-year-old, second cycle, short and long photoperiods)**
- **Experiment 7** (4 h light, 8.5 °C): secondary growth began about 2 months early, in May. Growth was slowed, and females matured only in late November. Males matured late August to mid October.
- **Experiment 11** (4 h light, 16 °C): growth onset and rate were normal. Maturity was delayed 2 to 6 weeks, and mature ova were slightly larger.
- **Experiment 8** (4 h then 20 h light, 8.5 °C): the current year-class of maturing ova was resorbed, and a second class of ova (1.4 to 2.0 mm) developed precociously. Males were delayed 3 to 5 months. Only 3 of 29 males reached Stage 4 or 5 in mid-summer 1958.
- **Experiment 12** (20 h light, 8.5 °C): growth was markedly retarded, and maturity was delayed 2 to 3 months, into late winter. Males reached Stage 4 about a month late and matured in late November and early December.
- **Experiment 10** (20 h light, 16 °C): most fish did not mature within the experiment, which lasted more than a year. Only one female (October) and two males (normal time) matured.
- **Experiment 9** (20 h then 4 h light, 8.5 °C): ova developed at about the normal rate. Fish were strippable in early October, a week or two early. Males were strippable in late August, about a month early.

**Experiments 13, 14 and 17 (previously mature fish, accelerated light, 8.5 °C)**
- **Experiment 14** (65 two-year-old fish that had matured the previous autumn): females began growth at least 1.5 months early and matured in late August, 7 weeks ahead of normal. Males matured about 2 months early (late July to early August).
- **Experiment 13** (20 four-year-old males, previously mature): males matured about 2 months early, in late July to early August.
- **Experiment 17** (three-year-old adults retained from Experiments 1 to 6): all 12 males matured in mid-August and all 3 females in late August.
- Comparison with Experiment 2 showed that the response depends on whether the fish have already completed a reproductive cycle, not on age.

**Experiment 15 (16 °C, natural day length)**
- Ova development at 16 °C paralleled controls at 8.5 °C. Male testicular development appeared normal. Fish were too few to measure time of maturity.
- Temperature did not alter gametogenesis under natural photoperiods.

**Reconciling earlier work**
- Earlier workers who advanced maturity with accelerated light had used only sexually mature fish that had completed one reproductive cycle. Henderson's results reconcile the apparent contradiction with her Experiments 1 and 2.

**Principal conclusions**
1. The effect of a photoperiod depends on the phase of gametogenesis in progress and on the photoperiod experienced earlier. A short photoperiod can stimulate one phase and retard another.
2. Under natural day lengths, the normal growth rate of ova depends on long photoperiods in late spring followed by declining day lengths in summer.
3. Long photoperiods delay male functional maturity mainly by delaying [[Spermiation]] (the release of sperm from the lobules into the efferent ducts), not by altering the germ cells.
4. Temperature mattered only under abnormal photoperiods, where it changed the pattern of ovarian response. The sole effect was a modest delay at 16 °C under short photoperiods.
5. Brook trout "modified" but did not "obliterate" their reproductive cycle under the conditions tested. Fecundity was not affected, although those data are reserved for later publication.
6. The author proposes that spermiation is blocked under long photoperiods because [[Luteinizing Hormone]] release is insufficient. Short photoperiods are assumed to stimulate its secretion.
7. Accelerated light advanced maturity only in previously mature fish. This fits the proposal that the stimulative effect of environmental factors depends on maturation of the [[Hypothalamus]] and its control of the [[Hypophysis]] (pituitary), analogous to puberty in mammals.
