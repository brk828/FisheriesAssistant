## Executive Summary

This study documents host-parasite relationships among seven native fish species in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, a rare Sonoran Desert stream largely free of exotic fishes. Ten parasite species across six genera were recovered, spanning Ciliata (*Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*), Myxosporea (five *Myxobolus* species), Digenea (*Ornithodiplostomum ptychocheilus*), Cestoda (*Isoglaridacris bulbocirrus*), and Nematoda (*Dacnitoides* sp. and *Rhabdochona decaturensis*). The research (1980–1981, monthly/seasonal sampling) emphasizes host- and site-specificity, seasonal infection cycles, and correlations between fish body size and parasite burden. [[Catostomus Insignis]] (Sonoran sucker) is implicated as the reservoir host maintaining *I. multifiliis* in the system, while [[Pantosteus Clarki]] (mountain/desert sucker) is identified as the primary host of *R. decaturensis* using flow-rate modeling (Holmes et al. 1977).

## Background & Description

Host fish sampled (identified via Minckley 1973 keys):
- **Cyprinids (minnows):** *Agosia chrysogaster* (longfin dace), *Gila robusta* (roundtail chub), *Meda fulgida* (spikedace), *Rhinichthys osculus* (speckled dace), *Tiaroga cobitis* (loach minnow).
- **Catostomids (suckers):** *Catostomus insignis* (Sonoran sucker) — described as having "coarse scales" potentially conferring parasite resistance, and being carnivorous; *Pantosteus clarki* (mountain sucker) — herbivorous diet, taxonomically debated as subgenus vs. full genus (Smith 1966 vs. Minckley 1973).

Parasite morphological/site notes:
- *Myxobolus catostomi* — skin of head (C. insignis)
- *Myxobolus discrepans* — gills (C. insignis)
- *Myxobolus nodularis* — body musculature (C. insignis)
- *Myxobolus macrocapsularis* — gills (A. chrysogaster)
- *Myxobolus oblongus* — pectoral/pelvic fins (P. clarki)
- *Isoglaridacris bulbocirrus* — intestinal cestode, annelid-borne intermediate host presumed
- *Rhabdochona decaturensis* — intestinal nematode, mayfly intermediate host
- *Ornithodiplostomum ptychocheilus* — metacercarial cysts in mesenteries (R. osculus)
- Ectoparasites were excluded from analysis due to damage/loss during freezing/icing prior to examination.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stream setting:** 500-m reach of riffle-pool sequence over sand/gravel, 10.2 mi NW of Klondyke, Graham Co., AZ; sampling coordinated with seasonal water level/temperature fluctuations (summer floods, low summer, fall, winter, spring).
- **[[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]] ("Ich") dynamics:** Direct life cycle, temperature-dependent (optimal 18–20°C), sensitive to low oxygen. Natural transmission is normally low due to fish dispersal, but outbreaks occur under drought-induced crowding. *C. insignis* shows relative refractility/immunity in the field despite serving as apparent reservoir host — attributed to its preference for deep, stable pools (favoring cyst retention) combined with sluggish behavior and coarse scales.
- **Myxosporidian host/site specificity:** Restricted to *Agosia chrysogaster*, *Catostomus insignis*, and *Pantosteus clarki* — all pool- or pool-edge–associated fishes, consistent with slow-water association with myxosporidian transmission (Fantham et al. 1940). Strong site-specificity within hosts suggests physiological/organ-level adaptation (Kudo 1920). No myxosporidian species shared between *P. clarki* and *C. insignis*, interpreted as evidence of marked physiological divergence, possibly linked to differing diets (herbivorous vs. carnivorous) supporting generic separation of [[Pantosteus]] from [[Catostomus]].
- **Cestode niche:** *Isoglaridacris bulbocirrus* specific to Catostomidae; occasional infection of the carnivorous *Gila robusta* linked to diet-mediated ingestion of annelid intermediate hosts. Lower infection in herbivorous *P. clarki* vs. *C. insignis*.
- **Nematode niche:** *Rhabdochona decaturensis* uses mayfly intermediate hosts; distribution across host fishes tied to diet (carnivory/insectivory increases exposure). *P. clarki*, despite herbivory, shown to be the primary host via flow-rate modeling — implying high establishment success in this species relative to low establishment (high mortality) in *C. insignis*.

## Key Findings

- **10 parasite species / 6 genera** recovered from 7 native fish species in Aravaipa Creek (1980–1981 seasonal survey).
- **[[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]]** is maintained in the creek primarily via *Catostomus insignis*, whose pool-dwelling, sluggish habits favor persistent parasite association despite apparent host immunity/resistance.
- **Myxosporidia exhibit strict host- and site-specificity**, restricted to three pool/slow-water-associated species (*A. chrysogaster*, *C. insignis*, *P. clarki*), with no myxosporidian overlap between the two sucker genera — supporting physiological (and possibly taxonomic) separation of *Pantosteus* and *Catostomus*.
- **Myxosporidian incidence rises then falls with fish size**, suggesting age/structural resistance develops in older, larger fish (consistent with Kennedy 1975; Dogiel 1961).
- *Myxobolus nodularis* infection showed **biannual incidence troughs** (autumn and early summer) in *C. insignis*, not explained by temperature or fish migration; possibly linked to differential mortality of heavily infected young fish, especially post-flood.
- **[[Isoglaridacris Bulbocirrus]]** (cestode) displayed **two annual reproductive cycles** (Sept–Oct and May–Jun) in both *Gila robusta* and *Catostomus insignis*, each ending in total worm die-off — a recruitment/maturation/death cycle common in cestodes (Kennedy 1977).
- **[[Rhabdochona Decaturensis]]** (nematode) incidence and intensity **correlate positively with host body size** in both *Pantosteus clarki* (r = 0.358) and *Catostomus insignis* (r = 0.2129), attributed to greater food intake in larger fish.
- *R. decaturensis* exhibits **two annual generations** in both major hosts, with four alternating high/low intensity phases per year; the infection cycle in *C. insignis* **lags behind** that in *P. clarki*, suggesting host-specific parasite adaptation rather than purely environmental (flood/temperature) control.
- Using **Holmes et al. (1977) flow-rate model** (R = H × A × G; R′ = H × A), *Pantosteus clarki* is identified as the **primary host** of *R. decaturensis* in Aravaipa Creek (R = 0.6229, R′ = 0.6162) versus *C. insignis* (R = 0.3767, R′ = 0.3831), despite the sucker's herbivorous diet limiting direct exposure to mayfly intermediate hosts — implying higher parasite establishment success in *P. clarki* and higher worm mortality in *C. insignis*.
- Findings support using a **single primary-host/parasite relationship** as a tractable model for predicting multihost parasite population dynamics in a locality.
