# Biological Merits of Fish Passage as Part of Recovery of Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service draft report, dated September 1990 and circulated for comment to the [[Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]] Biology Committee, evaluates whether fish passageways should be built at instream barriers to aid the recovery of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The authors, Bob D. Burdick and Lynn R. Kaeding, conclude that passage is justified at only one existing barrier, [[Tusher Wash Diversion]], where securing adequate instream flows may be more cost-effective than building a fishway. Construction at high-head dams is judged impractical, and the report stresses that the recovery value of any passageway remains uncertain. Prototype testing is recommended before broader investment.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is a large, piscivorous cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It is listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Its decline is attributed mainly to physical habitat alteration, modified discharge below dams, non-native fishes, and degraded water quality including temperature. Blockage of movement by dams and diversions has been proposed as an additional cause, though its actual role is unknown.

Radiotelemetry studies showed that adults make long-distance movements, mostly between feeding-wintering areas and spawning habitats, with individual movements over 200 miles documented (Miller et al. 1983; McAda and Kaeding 1989). Year-round upstream populations in the [[Yampa River]] suggest long-term upstream movement, possibly of juveniles (Tyus 1986).

Fish passage methods fall into two categories:
- **Active transport:** trapping and trucking, fish locks, fish elevators, and bucket hoists, in which fish are mechanically lifted.
- **Passive transport:** fish are required to swim through the passage, most commonly via fish ladders.

The report covers the upper basin above Glen Canyon Dam and excludes the San Juan River.

The report also notes new proposed or unauthorized diversions, including a Clifton Water District dam on the 15-mile reach, a White River diversion near Rio Blanco Lake, and a proposed Colorado Ute Electric diversion on the Yampa River near Craig.

**Barrier categories.** The report classifies barriers as:
- Low-head: partial or seasonal, under 10 ft.
- Medium-head: total barriers, 10 to 50 ft.
- High-head: total barriers, over 50 ft, often with cold tailwaters.

**River-reach priorities.** The Recovery Implementation Program ranks reaches by squawfish use. Barriers occur within each priority designation.

## Key Findings

**Rationale for passage**
- Passage is most valuable when adults cannot reach the only suitable spawning habitat (spawning-habitat limitation), or when nonspawning adult habitat is limiting (adult-habitat limitation). Both conditions further restrict the species' range.
- Passage is unlikely to help where it only provides access to habitat that is not limiting the population.
- No passageway is 100 percent efficient, so barrier effects can never be fully mitigated. Efficiency likely falls as dam height rises.

**Redlands Diversion Dam (12 ft, Gunnison River)**
- Proposed in 1982. Oil shale companies funded a prototype passageway as a conservation measure tied to Section 7 consultations, with funding of about $400,000 determined by the "Windy Gap" water-depletion formula.
- A 1984 Denil-fishway design (structure cost $160,000) did not assess squawfish swimming ability.
- A 1986 [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] study evaluated nine alternatives and selected a vertical-slot-with-orifice design. Estimated design and construction cost was $783,000, with $15,000 per year for operation and maintenance and $300,000 for a three-year biological study. Overall cost was near $1.1 million, and the project was postponed.
- A larval squawfish found below the plunge pool in 1986 suggests local spawning. Restoring a Gunnison adult population may require stocking juveniles and about five years of maturation.

**Taylor Draw Dam (60 ft, White River)**
- Closed in 1985 and fully blocks upstream movement. Adults were captured below the dam in 1989 (11 fish) and 1990 (14 fish) during spring monitoring.
- About $120,000 is available for passage feasibility, but no work has begun.

**Other barriers**
- Tusher Wash Diversion: all life stages occur above and below it, and a confirmed spawning area lies about 20 miles upstream. Passage during low flows is unknown.
- Grand Valley Diversion: only adults are known in the adjacent reaches. It is followed by the 10 ft Price Stub Diversion.
- [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and Flaming Gorge Dam: concrete dams over 500 ft high that inundate former habitat.

**Barrier-specific options**
- Low-head: notches or slots, adequate instream flows (for example, through the Flaming Gorge Biological Opinion), or pool-and-step passages, with water rights needed for operation.
- Medium-head: trapping and trucking until passive fishway use is proven.
- High-head: passage is considered impractical because of high costs, avoidance of cold tailwaters, and uncertainty about fish survival in reservoirs. Trapping with elevator, bucket, or truck transport is the most feasible option.

**Recommendations**
1. Install a prototype or portable experimental passageway at a low-head site. Tusher Wash is favored for its larger squawfish population, while Redlands is also suitable.
2. Radio-tag adults displaced below barriers and track their movements above them as a proxy for passage studies.
3. Prohibit new barriers to movement within the current species range until the importance of unrestricted movement is better understood.

**Summary conclusion**
- Passage is recommended at only one existing barrier, Tusher Wash Diversion, where flow provision may suffice.
- Construction at Flaming Gorge, Redlands, Taylor Draw, Grand Valley, Price Stub, and Government Highline is not justified as a practical recovery tool.
- Informational needs include clear recovery goals, identification of limiting factors, assessment of reservoir-retention risks, reproductive success across spawning areas, and gene-flow patterns that preserve stock integrity.
