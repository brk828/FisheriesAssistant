# Characteristics of Spawning Nests of Columbia River Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service bulletin (Fishery Bulletin 61, Volume 52, 1951) by [[Clifford J. Burner]] describes the spawning nests, or [[Redd]]s, of four Pacific salmon species in the [[Columbia River]] watershed. The study was undertaken to guide the relocation of salmon runs displaced by [[Grand Coulee Dam]]. Redd size, gravel composition, stream conditions and interredd spacing were measured for 850 redds. The work supplies a practical rule for estimating how many spawning pairs a stream can hold.

## Background & Description

**Management context.** [[Grand Coulee Dam]] was to be a high dam, with a tailrace-forebay difference of about 350 feet. Upstream passage for adults was possible but very costly, and juvenile salmon moving downstream would risk death over the spillway or through the turbines. Runs spawning above the dam were therefore moved to four tributaries below it: the [[Wenatchee River]], [[Entiat River]], [[Methow River]] and [[Okanogan River]]. The study was conducted to decide how many fish to place in each stream section for maximum spawn and fry yield. Its results were used in the [[Grand Coulee Fish-Maintenance Project]] but had not been published until this bulletin.

**Species studied.** Four species of the genus *Oncorhynchus* were examined:
- [[Chinook Salmon]] (*O. tshawytscha*): spring, summer and fall runs.
- [[Silver Salmon]] (*O. kisutch*).
- [[Chum Salmon]] (*O. keta*).
- [[Blueback Salmon]] (*O. nerka*), called sockeye in Puget Sound and British Columbia and red salmon in Alaska.

**Life history.** All Pacific salmon are [[Anadromy|anadromous]]. Adults return to the tributary of their birth, and nearly all die after spawning. The female digs the redd in gravel and deposits fertilized eggs under successive layers of gravel. Embryos develop in the gravel, absorbing their yolk sac until late winter or early spring. Young fish then emerge, may remain in fresh water for a variable period, and migrate to sea, where they stay 1 to 3 years before returning to spawn.

**Redd building stages.** Redd construction was divided into prespawning, spawning and postspawning stages. Prespawning digging may last up to 5 days and forms a [[Tailspill]]. Spawning enlarges the redd and moves it upstream. Postspawning digging may continue for 10 days but becomes shallow and ineffective. A redd was judged mature using three criteria: (1) a gaunt, spent female still digging, the most reliable; (2) sporadic mating as the egg supply runs out; and (3) freshly covered eggs in an unoccupied redd.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Gravel and flow.** Redd site selection depended strongly on substrate and water movement:
- Gravel size classes were large (more than 6 inches), medium (6 to 3 inches) and small (under 3 inches, above heavy sand).
- [[Percolation]] of water through the gravel appeared to be a requirement. Gravel firmly cemented with silt and clay was avoided by all species.
- Current velocities at spawning sites ranged from less than 1 foot per second to 3.5 feet per second. Redds in fast water were long and narrow, while those in quiet water were broadly oval.
- The current in the redd pot flows slightly upstream, favoring egg retention and fertilization.

**Sediment tolerance.** Silver salmon were the only species that built redds where the bottom contained up to 10 percent mud. In general, all species preferred stream bottoms relatively free of silt.

**Habitat by stream.** Stream conditions varied widely. The [[Ohanapecosh River]] is a mountain stream with abundant large rubble. The [[Kalama River]] has a fixed upstream limit created by an insurmountable falls, which restricts it to about 7 miles of spawning area. The [[Toutle River]] and its [[Green River]] tributary offer 40 miles or more of accessible gravel with relatively uniform size. The [[Entiat River]] has abundant medium and small rubble. The [[White River]] is clouded by glacial silt during summer and fall run-off, which hampered observation.

**Spawning timing.** Spring chinook spawned from mid-July to mid-September in headwaters. Summer chinook spawned from September to mid-November, and fall chinook spawned at about the same time in lower tributaries and main stems. Silver salmon and fall chinook spawned side by side in the upper Toutle and Green River tributaries.

**Body size.** Average adult weights and lengths reported in the bulletin were:
- Spring chinook: about 15 pounds.
- Summer chinook: about 30 pounds.
- Fall chinook: 20 to 25 pounds.
- Silver salmon: about 9½ pounds and 24 inches.
- Chum salmon: about 10 pounds and 26 inches.
- Blueback salmon: about 3 pounds and 20 inches.

## Key Findings

**Survey scope.** A total of 850 redds were measured across the four species. Counts by group were: [[Blueback Salmon]] 141, [[Chum Salmon]] 66, [[Silver Salmon]] 65, spring chinook 184, and summer plus fall chinook 394. The summary table covers spawning dates, stream depths, redd depths, velocities and temperatures for about a dozen streams. Only key entries are reproduced here.

**Mean redd size by species** (summary, square yards):
- Summer and fall chinook: 6.1
- Spring chinook: 3.9
- Silver salmon: 3.4
- Chum salmon: 2.7
- Blueback salmon: 2.1

**Stream-level redd sizes:**
- Ohanapecosh River (spring chinook): 2.9 square yards, 94 redds; 59 percent medium and small gravel.
- Nason Creek (spring chinook): 4.9 square yards, 90 redds; 86 percent medium and small gravel.
- Entiat River (summer chinook): 7.8 square yards, 41 redds, the largest average recorded.
- White River (summer chinook): 4.7 square yards, 9 redds; about 95 percent medium and small gravel.
- Kalama River (fall chinook): 5.7 square yards, 143 redds; 41 percent large gravel.
- Toutle River (fall chinook): 6.5 square yards, 89 redds; 11 percent large gravel.
- Green River (fall chinook): 5.5 square yards, 27 redds.
- Chum salmon (all four lower-Columbia streams): 2.7 square yards, 66 redds.

Large gravel in the Kalama River reduced redd size relative to the Toutle system, because large stones were harder to dislodge.

**Interredd space and area per pair.** Interredd space in crowded areas approached three times the redd area for chinook salmon, so the total area per spawning pair was about four times the average redd. Other species showed somewhat less space. The conservative spawning-area requirement per pair was:
- Summer and fall chinook: about 24 square yards.
- Spring chinook: about 16 square yards.
- Silver salmon: about 14 square yards.
- Chum salmon: about 11 square yards.
- Blueback salmon: about 8 square yards.

**Estimating stream capacity.** The recommended method is to divide the suitable spawning area of a stream by four times the average redd area. This gives a conservative count of spawning pairs.

**Species-specific behavior.**
- Chum salmon spawned in tidal water at Abernethy Creek, where redds were exposed at low tide. Their redds were often overlapped and in some cases superimposed.
- Silver salmon showed the greatest redd-building versatility, spawning on slate-shale rubble in Beaver Creek and building bizarre shapes around boulders and fallen trees in the Toutle River.
- Blueback salmon made stream-type redds in tributaries and lake-type redds in shallows, such as Osoyoos Lake in the Okanogan watershed. Their redds were scaled-down versions of larger salmon nests.

**Redd-size principles.** Redd size varies in direct proportion to fish size and in inverse proportion to gravel size and cementation. Slow-water redds are larger and more circular. Rapid-water redds are often deeper. Few redds of any species were built side by side, and most lay in diagonal rows across the stream.

**Conclusion.** The study concluded that transplanted salmon could be allocated to the foster rivers using the area-per-pair rule, based on the suitable gravel present in each stream.
