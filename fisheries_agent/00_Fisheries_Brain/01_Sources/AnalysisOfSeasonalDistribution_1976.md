#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 study by Van Den Avyle applies **frequency-of-capture analysis** — a technique borrowed from terrestrial mammal and insect population studies — to investigate whether behavioral segregation biases mark-recapture population estimates of young [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*). Using semimonthly shoreline electrofishing data from a 3-ha Iowa impoundment (McFarland Lake) over 1971–1972, the author tested whether age II bass of the 1970 year-class were equally catchable. Results showed that while shoreline-resident bass were captured with statistically uniform catchability, the population as a whole (including offshore fish) was **not** equally catchable. This supports earlier hypotheses (Parker and Hasler 1959; Lewis and Flickinger 1967) that largemouth bass split into distinct **shoreline** and **offshore** subpopulations during summer, a behavioral pattern that causes shoreline electrofishing to systematically underestimate population size and inflate apparent mortality rates. Evidence also suggested **year-to-year fidelity** to habitat preference (shoreline vs. offshore) among individual fish.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Micropterus salmoides* (Largemouth Bass), family [[Centrarchidae]]; co-occurring species in the study lake was [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*).
- **Age class studied**: 1970 year-class fish, aged as **Age I and Age II** at time of tagging via magnified scale impressions.
- **Size at tagging (1971)**: Mean total length ≈ **160 mm**; mean weight ≈ **55 g**.
- **Tagging method**: Numbered Monel Metal® strap tags (National Band and Tag Co., size 1, style 4-1005) affixed to the lower jaw of all fish >100 mm total length.
- **Tag loss**: An estimated **~30%** of tagged fish recaptured in 1972 had lost tags (29 of 101 total identifiable recaptures), identified instead via jaw deformities and scar tissue — a potential source of bias in behavioral inference.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: McFarland Lake, a narrow, 3-ha impoundment in Story County, Iowa, with ~two-thirds of its area deeper than 2 m. The lake stratifies both thermally and chemically in summer, though dissolved oxygen generally remained >4.0 ppm above the 3-m contour; ice-covered November–March.
- **Behavioral segregation**: During summer, the tagged bass population split into two behaviorally distinct segments:
  1. **Shoreline residents** — occupy shallow littoral home ranges, exhibiting relatively uniform and detectable catchability via boom electrofishing.
  2. **Offshore group** — inhabit deeper, open water; largely unavailable to shoreline electrofishing gear, consistent with the findings of [[Lewis And Flickinger 1967]] and [[Parker And Hasler 1959]].
- **Seasonal mixing**: Significant intermixing between shoreline and offshore segments occurs in **early spring**, evidenced by low recapture-to-capture ratios in April and early May attributed to influx of previously untagged fish to the shoreline.
- **Home range fidelity**: Recapture-to-capture ratios in summer 1972 approached 1971 levels despite tag loss, suggesting **persistent, year-to-year habitat preference** at the individual level — a form of restricted movement / home-range tendency as previously described by [[Gerking 1959]].
- **Comparative context**: Lewis and Flickinger (1967) found only ~1.2% of a total bass population was captured on a first electrofishing pass in a comparable 3.4-ha pond, indicating the shoreline-catchable segment can represent a small fraction of the total population.

## Key Findings

- **Sampling design**: 230V AC boom electrofishing conducted along shorelines during daylight, April 15 – November 15, 1972, divided into **14 semimonthly recapture periods** (~5 sampling trips of 2 passes each per period).
- **Recapture summary (1972)**: 48 of 272 originally tagged 1970 year-class bass were recaptured across the study; 31 captured once, 11 twice, 5 three times, 1 four times (72 total captures used in catchability tests). An additional 29 captures were tag-shed individuals, excluded from frequency analysis.
- **Theoretical model**: Applied Holgate's (1964, 1966) **zero-modified geometric / bivariate distribution model**, which incorporates both mortality/emigration probability (1–k) and capture probability (1–q), extending simple binomial capture-frequency models (Eberhardt 1969) to allow for migration and death.
- **Shoreline residence test**: Mean shoreline residence = 1.06 half-months; mean capture rate = 0.15/period. Chi-square goodness-of-fit = **4.80 (df=3, P > 0.05)** — could **not reject** the hypothesis of equal catchability among shoreline residents.
- **Whole-lake residence test**: Mean lake residence = 2.77 half-months; mean capture rate = 0.40/period. Chi-square goodness-of-fit = **42.54 (df=6, P < 0.01)** — **rejected** the hypothesis of equal catchability across the full tagged population, confirming non-random, heterogeneous catchability driven by offshore/shoreline behavioral splitting.
- **Implication for population estimates**: The behavioral segregation explains the previously observed anomalously high (90%) apparent mortality rate from April–November 1972 in a companion study ([[Van Den Avyle 1973]]), since shoreline-based mark-recapture estimates likely captured only the shoreline subpopulation, not the whole cohort.
- **Methodological contribution**: Demonstrates the underused value of **frequency-of-capture analysis** (common in terrestrial wildlife biology — mammals, insects, birds) as a diagnostic tool for testing equal-catchability assumptions in fisheries mark-recapture studies, independent of needing a fully independent population estimate.
- **Broader significance**: Reinforces that behavioral heterogeneity (e.g., home range fidelity, habitat partitioning) among fish can introduce serious bias into standard fisheries sampling (e.g., shoreline electrofishing), and that frequency-of-capture diagnostics can reveal such biases even when overall population estimates appear internally consistent.
