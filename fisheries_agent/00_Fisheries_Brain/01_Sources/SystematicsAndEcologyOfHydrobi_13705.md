## Executive Summary

This document is a 1985 research funding proposal submitted by the Department of Zoology, Arizona State University, to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, requesting support to systematically describe the largely undocumented **Hydrobiidae** (gill-breathing freshwater snails) of Arizona. While the primary taxonomic focus is malacological rather than ichthyological, the proposal is explicitly framed within the context of endangered [[Southwestern Freshwater Fishes|Southwestern freshwater fish]] conservation, arguing that hydrobiid snails serve as reliable bioindicators of long-term habitat permanence — a critical variable in planning reintroduction and recovery programs for threatened and endangered fish species such as the [[Gila Trout]], [[Apache Trout]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Sonoran Topminnow]], and [[Desert Pupfish]]. The proposal requests ~$56,500 over 18 months to fund taxonomic description, ecological data compilation, and publication of findings by Principal Investigator W. L. Minckley, field collaborator J. Jerry Landye (Arizona Game and Fish Department), and taxonomic specialist Robert Hershler.

## Background & Description

Although the focal taxon is a mollusc rather than a fish, the proposal details rigorous morphological/anatomical characterization protocols relevant to comparative aquatic systematics:

- **Target group:** Family [[Hydrobiidae]] — minute, gill-breathing, obligately aquatic freshwater snails.
- **Size:** Shell length <8.0 mm (extremely small-bodied).
- **Diagnostic character suite:** 50–60 morphological/anatomical characteristics per population are scored, of which 50–60% are qualitative traits.
- **Character sources include:**
  - Shell morphology
  - Head-foot structures
  - Mantle cavity
  - Digestive, excretory, and nervous systems
  - Reproductive systems of both sexes (the **female reproductive system**, involving complex duct/organ arrangements, is noted as showing the greatest inter-taxon variation)
- **Reference methodologies:** Davis & Carney (1973), Davis (1979), Davis & Greer (1980), Hershler (1985).
- **Diversity:** Approximately 18 hydrobiid species are known/suspected from Arizona (per Landye 1981a), predominantly in genera *Tryonia* and *Fontelicella*, plus at least one undescribed genus. The entire Arizona hydrobiid fauna remains formally undescribed at time of writing; one undescribed species is possibly already extinct, another persists in critically low numbers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat specificity:** Hydrobiids are restricted to springs, seeps, and cienega systems characterized by geologic permanence and hydrological stability — they do not tolerate ephemeral or highly variable waters.
- **Dispersal capability:** Very low; presumed passive dispersal only (e.g., via floodwaters or transport by aquatic birds), resulting in high rates of **local endemism** — often single-spring or single-spring-system endemics.
- **Indicator value:** Because of their obligate aquatic dependence, low vagility, and habitat fidelity, living hydrobiid populations are proposed as direct biological proxies for **long-term (geologic-scale) habitat permanence** — a key consideration for identifying candidate sites for endangered fish reintroduction and recovery.
- **Co-occurrence with fishes:** Many hydrobiid taxa share habitat with critically imperiled native Southwestern fishes, and some hydrobiid species are considered as threatened or endangered as their co-occurring fish counterparts (U.S. Dept. Interior 1984).
- **Regional context:** Broader Southwestern aquatic ecosystem in severe decline — 28 of 43 USFWS-listed endangered North American fishes (1980) are Southwestern species; 81% of native Arizona fish species (22/27) and 42% of New Mexico fish species (31/73) carry threatened/endangered status.
- **Historical survey base:** Extensive collections (1944–1980) across Arizona, New Mexico, Sonora, and Chihuahua by Minckley, Landye, and associates, yielding >100 curated Arizona hydrobiid samples plus extensive concurrent fish, crustacean, and mollusc collections.

## Key Findings

- **Conservation rationale:** Ecosystem-level (rather than isolated-habitat) approaches are necessary for successful endangered fish propagation/reintroduction; hydrobiid distribution data can fill critical gaps in assessing habitat reliability where historical hydrological records are absent.
- **Taxonomic gap:** The entire Arizona hydrobiid fauna (~18 species) is undescribed, despite decades of specimen accumulation — representing a significant unaddressed reservoir of biodiversity and conservation-relevant data.
- **Proposed deliverables** (18-month project, Oct 1985–Mar 1987):
  1. Literature review of hydrobiid and associated aquatic fauna (including fishes) relative to drainage history and habitat permanence.
  2. Identification/description of existing Landye collection material.
  3. Formal taxonomic descriptions submitted for publication.
  4. General manuscript with identification keys, distributions, and ecological summaries for Arizona hydrobiids.
- **Ecological data to be compiled:** Physicochemical water quality parameters, altitude/drainage relationships, geologic history of habitats, and basic life-history data (reproduction, size at maturity) for hydrobiid populations.
- **Budgetary structure:** Total request of $56,500, primarily funding a Faculty Associate (R. Hershler) salary/fringe, with in-kind (uncompensated) contributions from Minckley and Landye; includes field travel for live specimen collection and conference dissemination of results.
- **Broader significance:** Findings intended to support cross-referencing with existing regional hydrological/historical compilations (Brown et al. 1981; Minckley & Brown 1982; Hendrickson & Minckley 1984) and to formally evaluate hydrobiids' utility as bioindicators for Southwestern aquatic conservation planning.
