# Bonytail Chub Identification and Genetic Monitoring Study Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service study plan proposes a three-year genetic monitoring program for the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) held at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (DNFHTC), New Mexico. The plan argues that the hatchery broodstock, the only source for a population of wild-stocked fish in [[Lake Mohave]], is at a critical transition point: the broodfish are 17 years old and must be replaced. The authors propose using [[Microsatellite]] markers, with [[Amplified Fragment Length Polymorphism]] (AFLP) analysis as a supplement, to fingerprint all surviving broodfish, reconstruct parentage and relatedness, and track differential survival of hatchery and wild-caught fish without lethal sampling. The requested total is $320,100 over three years, to be granted through the [[Bureau of Reclamation]].

The document also includes a February 12, 2001 interoffice memorandum from Chuck Minckley (AZFRG Parker) asking external reviewers to comment on the plan by the end of March. It states that the memo's author had not been asked to act on the plan and did not know its status.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** Bonytail Chub is listed as federally endangered under the Endangered Species Act since 1980. No known self-sustaining wild populations are extant, apart from a population in Lake Mohave believed to derive mainly from DNFHTC broodstock. Other wild fish are known from rare takes in Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu.
- **Broodstock:** The hatchery population consists of 350 F1 progeny from between 4 and 10 wild fish. The plan states the broodfish are 17 years old.
- **Origin:** Wild broodfish were artificially spawned, and progeny were reared at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] on the Colorado River below Hoover Dam. Most F1 progeny were returned to Lake Mohave; 2,300 went to DNFHTC as foundation stock.
- **Management context:** Present goals are to stock 25,000 ten-inch fish annually. The plan cites a cost of $3.00 per ten-inch fish (R. Hamman, pers. comm.). Stocking is annual, and the plan describes the marking of 20,000 individuals per year for release.
- **Study team:** Principal investigators are [[Connie Keeler-Foster]] (Research Geneticist) and Dr. J. Holt Williamson (Research Leader), both at DNFHTC. Collaborators include Dr. Daniel J. Howard and Dr. Roy Cantrell at [[New Mexico State University]], and Dr. Brook Milligan as genetic statistical consultant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The plan describes the [[Colorado River]] system as transformed from a rigorous lotic environment into a series of large impoundments beginning in the 1950s. Combined with the introduction of exotic fish, this habitat change has severely affected what the authors call the most unique fish assemblage in North America, including the [[Gila Complex]] and other endemic fauna.

Genetic context drawn from the document:
- Hatchery supplementation can range from no effect to genomic swamping of wild stocks (Skaala et al. 1990; Hindar et al. 1991).
- Loss of genetic variation in hatchery stocks may reduce fitness, for example through loss of disease-resistance alleles (Allendorf and Phelps 1980).
- Minckley (1995) defines the goal of conservation propagation as maintaining evolutionary potential and population structure so that species can follow independent evolutionary pathways.
- Earlier allozyme work on bonytail showed low polymorphism. Previous genetic studies (Hedrick et al. 1998) assessed allozymes at three loci in F1 fish and four loci in F2 fish at DNFHTC in 1989. Hedrick et al. reportedly found 22 of 30 analyzed fish came from one mating.

## Key Findings

**Research objectives**
1. Assess genetic variability within the F1 broodfish using microsatellites; reconstruct relatedness, founder number and parentage; and design a mating strategy that avoids full-sib matings and maximizes heterozygosity.
2. Genotype 100 fish from each of three year classes (1996, 1997, 1998) to estimate the number of contributors to the F2 population and assess differential survival by parentage, fecundity and hatching success.
3. Analyze any wild fish taken, including the 15 already held at DNFHTC, for relatedness to the hatchery stock.
4. Evaluate differential survival of genetic backgrounds in the hatchery and in the wild.
5. Collect quantitative fitness trait data during broodstock handling to test correlations with DNA markers.

**Methods**
- Non-lethal fin clip or muscle plug sampling from each broodfish, linked to existing coded wire tags.
- Microsatellites are the primary codominant marker, described as average 8.7 alleles per locus in vertebrates and selectively neutral.
- AFLPs (dominant markers, Vos et al. 1995) would support microsatellite results and test parentage.
- Analyses will use Genepop, UPGMA cluster analysis, RST Calc, and a paternity method from SanCristobal and Chevalet (1997).

**Deliverables**
1. Software to select non-sibling matings that maximize heterozygosity and predict offspring genotypes.
2. A DNA fingerprint for each of the 350 F1 broodfish, entered into a database.
3. Fingerprints and parentage for sampled F2 fish.
4. Relatedness and heterozygosity for all wild fish captured, including the 15 at DNFHTC.

**Schedule and budget**
- Duration: three years, proposed start October 1, 1999.
- Budget (per annum, as itemized): salaries, laboratory equipment, travel, chemicals, miscellaneous costs and overhead. Total requested is $106,700 per annum (matched $53,000), with a total of $320,100 requested for the three-year research period.

**Administrative note**
- The February 12, 2001 memorandum requests external review of the study plan by the end of March 2001, with its current status unknown to the requesting coordinator.
