# Encroachment of Exotic Rainbow Trout into Stream Populations of Native Brook Trout in the Southern Appalachian Mountains

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests the hypothesis that the modern contraction of [[Brook Trout]] range in the southern [[Appalachian Mountains]] is caused by encroachment of exotic [[Rainbow Trout]]. Using historical surveys (1935–1936, 1972–1977) and detailed field sampling of four streams in [[Great Smoky Mountains National Park]], the authors document a shrinking allopatric brook trout range and a spatial pattern of allopatric and sympatric zones consistent with upstream rainbow trout displacement. Rainbow trout reach roughly 1.8 times the biomass of brook trout in comparable sections, and adult brook trout shift to peripheral habitats when sympatric. The authors conclude that encroachment is ongoing and, without management, could confine brook trout to small, isolated headwater refugia.

## Background & Description

- **Native species:** Brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) were historically recorded from headwaters above about 1,550 m down to about 500 m elevation in the park.
- **Exotic species:** Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*) were first stocked around 1910, most extensively at lower park elevations, with some middle- to high-elevation stockings from logging railroad trestles.
- **Prior pressures:** Logging (ending with the park's establishment in 1936) and heavy fishing initially eliminated brook trout from many streams. Brook trout streams were closed to public fishing from the mid-1970s.
- **Study design:** Kelly et al. (1980) survey data for more than 200 tributaries were reanalyzed using a map-wheel estimate of stream kilometers. Tributaries were classified into six population-structure classes (Table 1). Four study streams (Pretty Hollow–Onion Gap, Buckeye Gap, Meigs Post–Sweet Creek, Marks Creek) were electrofished in summer 1978 across 4–6 sections each.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Range contraction:** Of 59 stream sections surveyed in both periods, allopatric brook trout range fell from 157.8 km to 63.6 km (a 59.7% decline over about 40 years), while rainbow trout range increased by 94.2 km. Nine sections (23.5 km) above impassable falls remained brook trout streams in both surveys.
- **Habitat shift:** Early in the 20th century brook trout occupied stream gradients below 5%; currently they occupy average gradients above 15%.
- **Stream classes:** Sympatric zones did not exceed about 3.2 km. Brook trout–only streams (class 1) supported longer allopatric brook trout segments than classes 2 and 3.
- **Habitat use:** Age-0 fish of both species favored shallow, slow water over gravel and debris, shifting to deeper, faster water in fall. Adult brook trout occupied pools and riffle-runs near cover and off the main current; rainbow trout favored faster current and the middle-to-upstream portions of pools.
- **Physical conditions:** Platts (1976) found rainbow trout tolerate a wider range of gradients than brook trout, which are best adapted to low-gradient channels. Declining stream pH with elevation may be less tolerable to rainbow trout than to brook trout (King 1943).
- **Climate:** Climatic change was judged unlikely to be the primary factor, since brook trout retain original distributions in streams without rainbow trout.
- **Rainbow trout advantages (proposed):** Larger body size (from age 1+), possibly greater egg output per female, and a balanced sex ratio versus a reported male-biased ratio in brook trout (Lennon 1967).

## Key Findings

- **Exclusive persistence:** Brook trout keep their full longitudinal distribution only in streams lacking rainbow trout.
- **Spatial pattern:** Typical streams show allopatric brook trout upstream, a sympatric zone (up to about 3.2 km), and allopatric rainbow trout downstream. Few age-0 rainbow trout occur in sympatric zones unless adult brook trout are rare, and vice versa.
- **Density and biomass:** In sections with similar physical characteristics, allopatric brook trout and rainbow trout had similar adult densities (9.5 and 9.4 adults/100 m², P > 0.05). Rainbow trout biomass was about 1.8 times greater (445 g/100 m² vs. 245 g/100 m², P < 0.05).
- **Abundance interaction:** Age-0 abundance of either species was greatly reduced when three or more adults (per 100 m²) of the other species were present. Adult abundances showed a negative curvilinear relationship.
- **Responses to removal:** Moore et al. (1983) found brook trout increased in abundance and biomass after experimental removal of sympatric rainbow trout, supporting a competitive advantage for rainbow trout.
- **Encroachment model:** Based on stream classes, the authors propose a sequence from class 2 (sympatric at downstream edge) through class 3 and class 5 to class 6 (allopatric rainbow trout only), with class 4 streams possibly shifting directly to class 6.
- **Prognosis:** Encroachment will likely continue, with brook trout retreating to higher elevations and steeper gradients. Factors that may slow this include cascades and falls, linear stream geometry, unstable high-elevation flows, and lower pH. Without management, brook trout may persist only in small, inbreeding headwater populations vulnerable to floods, droughts, and random extinction.
- **Limitations:** The authors note that the encroachment process is difficult to document because sampling has been irregular, and that habitat preference, predation, disturbance, and illegal fishing could also influence results.
