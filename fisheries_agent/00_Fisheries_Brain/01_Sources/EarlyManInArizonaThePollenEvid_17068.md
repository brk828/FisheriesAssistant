# Early Man in Arizona: The Pollen Evidence

#palynology #paleoecology #pollen

## Executive Summary

This paper by Paul S. Martin, from the Geochronology Laboratories at the University of Arizona, uses [[Pollen Analysis]] and [[Radiocarbon Dating]] to test the [[Bryan-Antevs Model]] of Southwestern climate. The author argues that the [[Sulphur Spring Stage]] deposits at [[Double Adobe]] are early postpluvial, not pluvial, in age. He also argues that the [[Altithermal]] was not clearly hot and dry in southern Arizona and may have been wetter than the present. The pollen evidence confirms major climate change during the last pluvial period but does not support the traditional interpretation of Early Man's environment in southern Arizona.

## Background & Description

The traditional climatic model for arid North America, developed from fieldwork by Kirk Bryan, his students, and [[Ernst Antevs]], divides the post-Wisconsin climate into a threefold sequence: the [[Anathermal]], the Altithermal, and the [[Medithermal]]. The last pluvial period is regarded as cold and wet, and the Altithermal as especially hot and dry. [[Sayles and Antevs]] (1941) associated the [[Cochise Culture]] with this sequence. The Sulphur Spring stage was placed in the pluvial period, and the Chiricahua and San Pedro stages in the postpluvial.

Martin's earlier pollen chronology for the desert grassland of southern Arizona covered ten archaeological sites and three late Pleistocene geological deposits. It was first issued as a multilith monograph of 250 copies and later revised with four pollen zones instead of six. The paper responds to Antevs' (1962) objections to that chronology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pluvial vegetation.** The pollen record from the pluvial [[Willcox Playa]] (Lake Cochise) contains over 95% pine pollen, with regular spruce, fir, and Douglas fir. Today the playa lies in the [[Desert Grassland]] and its pollen rain is dominated by grass, cheno-ams, and Compositae. Pine pollen is below 20% there, and spruce, fir, and Douglas fir are below 0.01%. High conifer frequencies therefore occur in the desert grassland only in pluvial-age sediments. Similar conifer-dominated records occur in Wisconsin-age sediments of New Mexico and the [[Texas High Plains]].

**Double Adobe vegetation.** The 1959 pollen profiles at Double Adobe are dominated by cheno-ams, Compositae (probably low-spine types such as *Ambrosia* and *Xanthium*), grasses, sedges, and other herbs. Tree pollen is scarce. The author links the high Compositae zone to a riparian and cienega setting, with high water tables marked by sedge, cat-tail, smartweed, ash, and willow along [[Whitewater Draw]].

**Altithermal moisture.** Antevs proposed that intense arroyo cutting during the mid-postglacial required reduced plant cover and therefore widespread drought. Martin contests this, noting that the link between plant cover and channel erosion is unproven.

## Key Findings

- **Double Adobe is postpluvial.** Six radiocarbon dates from deep alluvium at Double Adobe range from 7756 to 9350 years B.P. Together with the pollen content, this places the Sulphur Spring-stage artifacts in the early postpluvial period.
- **Pluvial and Double Adobe pollen differ sharply.** Radiocarbon dates from the Willcox Playa cores (A-352: 23,200 ± 500 B.P.; A-353: 22,000 ± 500 B.P.), both with about 98% pine, differ from the Double Adobe dates by more than 10,000 years.
- **Pollen from a mammoth tooth matches the 1959 profile.** Matrix inside the tooth, probably from the original Cummings mammoth, gave Compositae above 20%, cheno-ams above 10%, and pine above 20%, similar to the 190 to 200 cm levels of the profile.
- **Pine is low in the Sulphur Spring zone.** Pine in the Compositae-rich pollen units (A-188, A-189, A-191) ranges from 2% to 5%, comparable to modern surface samples. The author reads this as a climate similar to the present.
- **Exotic trees are absent from the pluvial record.** Double Adobe yielded seven hickory and five elm pollen grains. In 27,000 determinations from a 40-meter Willcox Playa core, no hickory and only three elm grains appeared. The author suggests these trees arrived in early postpluvial time.
- **Riparian fossils do not require pluvial conditions.** Each fossil plant and animal Antevs cited, apart from perhaps yellow pine, is consistent with high water tables and riparian habitat. Living analogues include oak and ash in lower Sabino Canyon and *Quercus emoryi* at Lone Oak. The [[Sayles and Antevs]] molluscs are also known from Arizona today, and some came from late postglacial deposits on the San Pedro River.
- **Altithermal pollen does not indicate drought.** Three radiocarbon dates within the Altithermal (7500 to 4000 B.P.) have slightly more mesic and hygric pollen than younger sediments. Table 2 shows pine, grass, sedge, and cat-tail increasing in the 4000 to 5000 year sediments compared with the last 1000 years. Taken literally, the record indicates a wetter Altithermal.
- **Mammoth extinction does not prove drought.** Living elephants occupy arid habitats, and North American mammoths occupied varied vegetation zones before they disappeared. Martin treats the drought explanation for their extinction as unsupported.
- **Summer rain is a better explanation.** The author proposes that increased summer precipitation, not widespread drought, better explains the Altithermal in parts of the Southwest. This would also account for the introduction of corn (*Zea*) from Mexico during that period.
- **Methodological limits remain.** Martin acknowledges that the scope and limitations of arid-land pollen analysis are not fully understood. He concludes that the traditional hot, dry Altithermal cannot be verified from the fossil pollen evidence available so far, and he calls for more rigorous geobiological investigation at archaeological sites.
