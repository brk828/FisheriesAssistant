# San Pedro River Basin Water Quality Status Report, 1973–1979

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Department of Health Services report compiles water quality data for the Upper [[San Pedro River]] Basin for 1973–1979, with emphasis on surface water. It covers the [[Intensive Survey]] of 1973, a 1977 monitoring study by the University of Arizona Water Resources Research Center, and the [[Joint Survey]] of July 1979. The main event is the [[Cananea Mine]] spill in Sonora, Mexico, which released acidic, metal-rich process water into the river from December 1977 to April 1979, with a further episode from late December 1978 to March 1979. The spills killed aquatic life along about 60 miles of river. By July 1979, surface water chemistry had recovered to within state standards, and biological life was recovering slowly.

## Background & Description

- **Report:** Prepared by Sandra Eberhardt for the [[Bureau of Water Quality Control]], [[Arizona Department of Health Services]] (ADHS), Phoenix, January 1981.
- **River:** The San Pedro rises in Sonora about 25 miles (40.2 km) south of the border and flows north roughly 125 miles to the [[Gila River]] near Winkelman. The Upper Basin, from the headwaters to the narrows about 11 miles north of Benson, is the focus of this report.
- **Flow regime:** The river is predominantly ephemeral. Only three perennial segments were identified. Flow is driven by winter storms and summer convective storms, and the 1977–79 high flows came from unusually heavy late-fall through early-spring rains.
- **Ecological value:** The river and its major tributary, [[Aravaipa Creek]], support exceptional riparian habitat. The area has the highest bird species diversity in the United States and is on the nationwide rivers inventory as a potentially nationally significant wildlife ecosystem.
- **Pollution sources:** Point sources include municipal sewage plants at Bisbee, Benson, Sierra Vista, and Mammoth, plus private treatment plants. Nonpoint sources include septic seepage near Dudleyville, Naco, St. David, and Sierra Vista, sediment and fecal material from cattle grazing, and mining operations. Indirect air pollutants include lead, dust, and sulfur and nitrogen oxides.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Cananea spill mechanism.** Leaching ponds at the Cananea operation sit in the headwaters and are highly prone to runoff, filling, and overflow. Unusually heavy rains from late 1977 through 1979 overtopped the ponds, weakened earthen dams, and sent polluted runoff into the river. Residents reported smaller, earlier spills that visibly changed river flow for a day or two during heavy runoff.

**Spill impacts.** The discharge was brick-red and acidic, with pH as low as 3.1 and dissolved oxygen as low as 2 mg/L. It carried high iron, copper, manganese, and zinc, along with very high suspended solids. The Arizona Game and Fish Department reported no bottom-dwelling insects, fish, or aquatic vegetation along a 60-mile stretch. Deformed, flightless birds were found near the river, and the metal concentrations were thought to be responsible.

**Social and agricultural effects.** Farmers feared for livestock and crops, and ranchers reported horses refusing to drink. Some irrigation water diverted to bass ponds became toxic to fish. A Cochise County court temporarily enjoined the St. David Irrigation District from diverting river water. Concern also extended to groundwater, the artesian wells near St. David, and possible downstream flooding from sediment buildup.

**Recovery.** Following the diversion ditch and pipeline built by [[Anaconda Copper Company]] and the Cananea operator, and the cessation of heavy rains, the river recovered rapidly in chemistry but more slowly biologically. The July 1979 Joint Survey found no surface water chemical violations.

## Key Findings

### 1. Water quality data, 1973 Intensive Survey (ADHS)
- Surface water turbidity was higher than state standards.
- The river below the Mammoth wastewater treatment pond exceeded fecal coliform limits.
- Groundwater showed elevated sulfates and total dissolved solids, exceeding U.S. Public Health Service drinking water recommendations.
- No copper violations were found in 1973.

### 2. 1977 University of Arizona monitoring ([[Water Resources Research Center]])
- Surface water violations were recorded for pH, barium, lead, and copper (Table 5).
- Elevated copper was found in the upper basin, possibly linked to Cananea operations.

### 3. Cananea spill chronology
- **Mid-December 1977:** Orange-brown discharge first noted near Palominas.
- **February 7, 1978:** A sample showed elevated iron, copper, manganese, and zinc. Copper and pH exceeded state standards.
- **March 13 – April 6, 1978:** Continuous monitoring was conducted. Pollution abated by mid-April 1978.
- **December 26, 1978:** Red-orange acidic discharge resumed after a nine-inch rain in Mexico. It continued through March 1979.

### 4. 1979 Joint Survey (July 17–18)
- **Participants:** ADHS, Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), and Anaconda Copper Company's Environmental Engineering Division.
- **Surface water chemistry:** No parameters violated state standards. Iron and magnesium were higher than before the spill. Copper, manganese, and zinc were lower than in 1977. Concentrations of heavy metals dropped sharply at St. David, probably because of hard, sulfate-rich tributaries such as Babocomari Creek.
- **Sediment:** Sediment pH was above 8, despite the acidic pollution, reflecting alkaline southwestern soils. Copper, zinc, and iron decreased with distance from the source, with an exception for copper at Benson. A precipitate sample from Charleston contained nearly 14% iron and 0.1% copper.
- **Biology:** One fish species, the [[Longfin Dace]], was found at all four sites. Five fish families had been recorded previously (Table 15), including Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae, Poeciliidae, and Centrarchidae. Six riparian and aquatic plant species and eight aquatic invertebrate species were identified. The Summary's count differs from the Abstract's "six species of aquatic insects," and the source text is ambiguous. Invertebrate and fish numbers increased with distance from the spill source. Baetidae mayflies were the most numerous invertebrates, with two species. Filamentous algae were present at all stations except Palominas, which recorded 34 °C.
- **Groundwater:** Drinking water wells 1–6 met all standards analyzed. Irrigation well 7, abandoned and about ten yards from the river, had excessive iron, lead, and manganese. It is unknown whether the spill caused this, because no pre-spill data exist and well casing leaching is also possible.

### 5. Recommendations
- Continue ongoing surveillance of surface and groundwater to track baseline quality, long-term effects, and early warning of future spills. The [[Southeastern Arizona Governments Organization]] was supporting this program, with a report expected in spring 1981.
- Inspect and maintain the Cananea diversions and dams to withstand heavy runoff.
- Develop contingency plans for future spills, including lime or precipitating agents to reduce acidity and metal concentrations, and diversion or ponding to allow settling.
- Maintain international communication and cooperation, which the report identifies as the key long-term solution.

### 6. Pending research
Fish and insect tissue metal analyses and fish population studies were planned for the 1979–80 Statewide Fishery Investigations, as reported by the AGFD.
