# Sonoran Chub Transplant to Sycamore Canyon Side Drainages

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila

## Executive Summary

The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) proposed transplanting approximately 150 [[Sonoran Chub]] (*Gila ditaenia*) from the [[Sycamore Canyon]] main drainage into six permanent water sites in adjacent side drainages. The entire U.S. population of the species is restricted to this one drainage, so a natural or human-caused disaster could eliminate it. A June 1990 survey identified six perennial water sources as candidate transplant sites. The project proposes a two-phase transplant (fall 1991 and June 1992), supported by watershed protection such as spring exclosures and cattle drinkers, with post-release monitoring in 1991 and 1992. The package includes a project abstract, a scoring sheet, an environmental assessment checklist, and a habitat survey summary. Its conclusions point to a categorical exclusion under NEPA, pending required federal permits.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Sonoran Chub (*Gila ditaenia*). Status is listed as State Endangered and Federal Threatened in the project abstract. The environmental checklist describes it as threatened under ESA and by Arizona category. The two designations should be reconciled in the final record.
- **Study area:** Sycamore Canyon in southern Arizona, on [[Coronado National Forest]] land managed by the [[U.S. Forest Service]].
- **Access and hazards:** The [[Ruby Road]], a major forest service road, crosses the drainage above the chub population. Mining is common in the area, and vehicles carrying hazardous materials use the road. A spill upstream could eliminate the population.
- **Land use:** Cattle grazing is permitted under the [[Sear Valley Grazing Management Plan]] on all but the Gooding Research portion of the watershed. Past grazing caused siltation that filled pools the chubs prefer.
- **Correspondence:** Kenneth L. Stirn, Wildlife Manager, sent the proposal on October 1, 1991, to Tom Deecken of the Sierra Vista Ranger District. He requested a bio-evaluation and comments, which were to be forwarded to [[Francisco Abarca]], AGFD Native Fish Biologist. He also enclosed a 1979 student survey summary for transplant site selection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Population restriction:** A study by Carpenter and Maughan (University of Arizona) found substantial numbers of fish in the main canyon drainage. These fish are confined to intermittent stretches of water.
- **Drought survey:** The 1990 survey took place in mid-June, one of the driest Junes on record, to locate perennial water.
- **Habitat threats:** Siltation from past grazing has filled preferred pools. Watershed management is needed to protect them.
- **Water quality:** Oxygen and temperature readings from source and transplant sites are required to confirm habitat compatibility. The plan calls for contaminant-free water at protected springs.
- **Habitat survey summary:** A summary sheet records stream and pool measurements, including flow, water temperature, substrate composition (boulder, cobble, gravel), and turbidity. The scan is too degraded to transcribe these values reliably, so they are not reported here.
- **Environmental checklist:** The checklist flags several potential effects. These include:
  - Effects on a federally listed species.
  - Introduction of a species into a location where it does not currently occur. Chubs would be moved upstream of the furthest known location.
  - Fencing of springs and tinajas, which would exclude livestock.
  - New watering facilities and possible grazing plan changes.
  - Need for [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] permits to work with threatened and endangered species.

## Key Findings

- **Need:** Restriction to one drainage leaves the entire U.S. population vulnerable to a single disaster. Transplanting is considered critical to long-term survival.
- **Sites:** The June 14, 1990 survey found six permanent water sources. Four are springs: [[Horse Pasture Spring]], [[Tinaja Spring]], [[Casita Spring]], and [[Agua Cercada Spring]]. The other two are a pool in [[Tinaja Canyon]] (southeast of Little Tinaja Spring, described as five pools in the proposal) and a pool 0.5 mile north of Horse Pasture Spring. The two unfenced pools are the ideal immediate transplant sites.
- **Phase one (fall 1991):** About 150 chubs are to be moved to the Tinaja Canyon pools and the pool north of Horse Pasture Spring. Water quality is to be checked first.
- **Phase two (June 1992):** The four springs are to be improved with exclosures and cattle drinkers before a second transplant. AGFD and the Forest Service will develop exclosure plans before phase two.
- **Methods:** Chubs are to be collected by seining from healthy populations within the Sycamore Canyon drainage and transported to the release sites.
- **Monitoring:** Post-release monitoring is planned for summer 1991 and summer 1992.
- **Budget and duration:** The work plan lists an estimated cost of $1,600. The project abstract lists $2,600. This discrepancy must be resolved. The abstract gives a 1991–1992 duration, and the work plan lists a period beginning 3/91. Funding comes from AGFD personnel time (Region V and Nongame Branch). Partners include the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and U.S. Forest Service.
- **Scorecard:** A project scorecard ranked factors such as species ranking, habitat availability, land and land-use conflicts, logistics, and public acceptance. Its cumulative score is not legible in the scan.
- **Regulatory status:** The checklist assesses the project as a categorical exclusion under NEPA. An Environmental Assessment is marked as not attached. Several checklist items were marked "YES," so the review should confirm whether a full environmental assessment is needed. Required approvals include AGFD branch and division sign-offs and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service permits.
