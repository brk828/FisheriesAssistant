It is a terrestrial/limnetic desert oasis ecology study of small mammals (bats and rodents) and incidental larger mammal observations at Saratoga Springs, Death Valley National Monument. The structured note below has been adapted to faithfully reflect the actual content (mammalian ecology at a spring-fed oasis) rather than forcing a fish-centric framing.

## Executive Summary

This 1971 study by Bradley and Deacon (University of Nevada, Las Vegas) documents the ecological distribution, relative abundance, seasonal activity, population structure, reproduction, and food habits of small mammals at [[Saratoga Springs]], an isolated spring-fed oasis in [[Death Valley National Monument]], California. Conducted from September 1965 through March 1967, the study compared five distinct habitat types — creosote bush scrub, rocky canyon/slope, sand dunes, salt flat, and marsh — using standardized snap-trap transects and mist-netting for bats. Ten rodent species and six bat species were documented, with the mesic sand dune and marsh habitats supporting substantially higher rodent biomass and diversity than the surrounding arid creosote bush and salt flat habitats. The findings support the hypothesis that proximity to permanent water and green vegetation drives elevated population densities and prolonged reproductive seasons in desert small mammal communities.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** [[Saratoga Springs]] is a spring-fed oasis at 193 feet elevation in the extreme southern portion of Death Valley National Monument, fed by one main spring and three smaller sources draining into a 3-acre permanent marsh dammed by sand dunes.
- **Prior work:** Grinnell (1937) established a baseline checklist of 25 lower-elevation Death Valley mammal species; this study recorded 20 of those 25 species and deposited 174 voucher specimens (19 species) in university and National Park Service museum collections.
- **Methods:**
  - Rodents trapped via snap traps (Museum Special and Victor rat traps), typically in lines of 60 traps (3 per station, 50-ft intervals).
  - Sherman live traps used opportunistically for *Dipodomys deserti*.
  - Bats collected via mist nets or shooting.
  - Age class (young/subadult/adult) assigned by size and pelage; reproductive status assessed by autopsy (uterine condition, embryos, placental scars, lactation, testes condition).
  - Stomach contents visually estimated (nearest 5% by volume) and categorized as green vegetation, seeds, or insects/arthropods — method validated against volumetric techniques (Bradley 1968).
  - Climate monitored via recording thermograph and gallon-jug rain gauges with kerosene evaporation barriers.
- **Habitat types studied** (vegetation detailed in Bradley 1970):
  - [[Creosote Bush Habitat]] — dominated by *Larrea divaricata* and *Atriplex polycarpa*; sparse desert scrub ~4 mi SW of the springs.
  - [[Rocky Canyon And Slope Habitat]] — dominated by desert holly (*Atriplex hymenelytra*, ~78% of scrub cover).
  - [[Salt Flat Habitat]] — dissected by Amargosa River distributaries; dominated by salt grass (*Distichlis spicata*) and pickleweed (*Allenrolfea occidentalis*).
  - [[Sand Dune Habitat]] — dominated by inkweed (*Suaeda fruticosa*) and salt grass (>90% cover).
  - [[Marsh Habitat]] — salt grass periphery, extensive bullrush (*Scirpus olneyi*, 85% of plant cover in that zone), and reed grass (*Phragmites communis*) stands.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate:** Sub-freezing temperatures December–March; daily highs ≥40°C April–August; strong diurnal temperature swings (e.g., below freezing to 35°C+ in March). Annual precipitation recorded at 4.0–6.3 inches across two stations, concentrated in November–December 1965.
- **Habitat heterogeneity and "edge effect":** Sand dunes and marsh habitats, due to their spatial juxtaposition and abundant vegetative cover, created greater environmental heterogeneity than the more arid creosote bush and salt flat zones, correlating with markedly higher rodent biomass.
- **Water and food availability as limiting factors:** Rodent population density was consistently higher in mesic habitats (marsh, sand dunes) where green vegetation, shelter, and water were more abundant, compared to surrounding arid desert scrub — directly paralleling how proximity to permanent water shapes population and reproductive success in other desert vertebrate communities.
- **Salt flat as a depauperate zone:** Despite abundant lagomorph (rabbit) droppings indicating transient use, extensive trapping (417 + additional 300 trap-nights) yielded very few or zero rodents, consistent with Grinnell's (1937) suggestion that the salt flat may be largely devoid of resident small mammals.
- **Migratory vs. resident bat fauna:** *Lasionycteris noctivagans* and *Lasiurus cinereus* were present only during migration; *Myotis californicus* occurred in low numbers despite being common elsewhere; *Tadarida brasiliensis* occurred in moderate spring/summer numbers with uncertain regional migratory status; *Antrozous pallidus* and *Pipistrellus hesperus* were the two dominant, near year-round active species, consistent with patterns from nearby southern Nevada (O'Farrell and Bradley 1970).

## Key Findings

### Bat Community ([[Vespertilionidae]] / [[Molossidae]])
- Six species netted/collected, 70 total individuals over 13 net-nights across the study period (Table 3):
  - **[[Antrozous Pallidus]]** (Pallid Bat) — 36 individuals, most abundant, active across most seasons.
  - **[[Pipistrellus Hesperus]]** (Western Pipistrelle) — 19 individuals, second most abundant, near year-round activity.
  - **[[Tadarida Brasiliensis]]** (Brazilian Free-tailed Bat) — 10 individuals, moderate spring/summer numbers.
  - **[[Myotis Californicus]]** (California Myotis) — 3 individuals, unexpectedly scarce relative to regional abundance.
  - **[[Lasionycteris Noctivagans]]** (Silver-haired Bat) — 1 netted + 2 additional migratory observations (7 March).
  - **[[Lasiurus Cinereus]]** (Hoary Bat) — 1 individual, migratory only.

- **Habitat associations:** Sand dune and marsh habitats shared some species (*Neotoma lepida*, *Reithrodontomys megalotis*) but differed markedly in composition — *Dipodomys deserti* and *Spermophilus tereticaudus* essentially restricted to sand dunes; *Peromyscus crinitus* and *Peromyscus eremicus* essentially restricted to marsh among the mesic habitats.
- **Biomass:** Sand dunes exhibited ~3× the rodent biomass of rocky slope and marsh habitats, driven largely by the two large-bodied species *Spermophilus tereticaudus* and *Dipodomys deserti*. Creosote bush and salt flat habitats had the lowest biomass and individual counts (up to 8× fewer individuals per equal trap effort than mesic habitats).
- **Seasonal activity:** All species showed population increases in spring/summer (recruitment of young) followed by fall/winter decline. *Perognathus formosus* appeared to hibernate through most of fall and part of winter (absent from traps mid-October to late January); *Spermophilus tereticaudus* showed some winter activity but with non-synchronous hibernation/estivation across the population (active December–August, missed only September and February).
- **Population structure:** Winter populations were composed almost exclusively of adults; young and subadults were recruited mainly in late spring, summer, and fall.
- **Reproduction:** Peak reproductive activity for nearly all rodent species occurred in spring/summer. *Perognathus formosus* reproduced only in spring. *Reithrodontomys megalotis* (and to a lesser degree *Peromyscus crinitus*) showed reproductive activity across all seasons, reduced in fall. *Dipodomys deserti* showed possible early fall reproduction; *Neotoma lepida* and *Spermophilus tereticaudus* were reproductively active from winter through summer.
- **Food habits:** Green vegetation dominated diets (frequency and volume) across all species and seasons, even when scarce in surrounding desert — notably in species concentrated near the marsh (*Dipodomys deserti*, *Spermophilus tereticaudus*, *Reithrodontomys megalotis*). *Perognathus* and *Dipodomys* are otherwise typically seed-specialists; *Peromyscus* spp. are typically seed-dominant omnivores with some insect intake; ground squirrels were highly omnivorous. *Neotoma lepida*'s known reliance on green vegetation for water needs (per Schmidt-Nielsen 1964) was corroborated.

### Larger Mammals (Incidental Observations)
- **[[Lepus Californicus]]** (Black-tailed Jackrabbit) — common in creosote bush and sand dunes; abundant sign on salt flats.
- **[[Canis Latrans]]** (Coyote) — single observation.
- **[[Vulpes Macrotis]]** (Kit Fox) — repeated observations/tracks near marsh, indicating regular use by one or more individuals.
- **Species notably absent from study area:** [[Sylvilagus Auduboni]] (Desert Cottontail), [[Ammospermophilus Leucurus]] (Antelope Ground Squirrel, despite being described as common by Grinnell 1937), [[Perognathus Penicillatus]] (Desert Pocket Mouse), [[Taxidea Taxus]] (Badger), [[Lynx Rufus]] (Bobcat), [[Ovis Canadensis]] (Desert Bighorn Sheep).

### Overarching Ecological Conclusion
Mesic microhabitats associated with the permanent spring/marsh system (sand dunes and marsh) supported markedly higher rodent densities, biomass, and — for several species — more prolonged reproductive activity than the surrounding arid creosote bush and salt flat habitats. The authors propose that increased availability of water, shelter, and particularly green/succulent vegetation drives both elevated population density and extended reproductive seasons in oasis-associated desert rodent populations, a pattern paralleling similar findings in other desert small mammal studies (Chew and Butterworth 1964; Bradley and Mauer 1965; Beatley 1969).
