# Conservation Status of Freshwater Mussels of the United States and Canada

#malacology #marine-biology #unionidae #margaritiferidae

## Executive Summary

This American Fisheries Society (AFS) review, published in *Fisheries* (Vol. 18, No. 9, September 1993), lists all native freshwater mussels of the families [[Margaritiferidae]] and [[Unionidae]] in the United States and Canada. It gives state and provincial distributions, reviews the conservation status of every taxon, and compiles references on mussel biology, conservation, and distribution. The list includes 297 native taxa, of which 213 (71.7%) are endangered, threatened, or of special concern. Only 70 (23.6%) are currently stable. The main drivers of decline are habitat destruction from dams, channel modification, and siltation, together with the spread of nonindigenous mollusks such as the [[Zebra Mussel]] and [[Asian Clam]]. The authors warn of an extinction crisis that could cause the complete loss of some genera.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and size:** Freshwater mussels (naiads, unionids, or clams) are worldwide in distribution but reach their greatest diversity in North America, with about 297 recognized taxa (281 species and 16 subspecies). Adults range from 4 cm to more than 30 cm.
- **Ecology:** Mussels are planktivores and serve as food for fishes and other vertebrate predators. They are sessile, depend on good water quality, suitable physical habitat, and [[Host Fish]] populations, and are regarded as good indicators of aquatic ecosystem health.
- **Cultural use:** Native Americans used mussels for food, tools, and ornaments, as shown by large shell mounds beside productive streams (Parmalee and Klippel 1974).
- **Commercial history:** Before plastics, mussel shell supported a major button industry (Coker 1919). Today shell is valued mainly as seed material for the [[Cultured Pearl Industry]].
- **Comparative decline:** [[The Nature Conservancy]] classed 55% of North American mussels as extinct or imperiled, compared with 7% of the continent's bird and mammal species (Master 1990).
- **Purpose:** The AFS [[Endangered Species Committee]] initiated the review to inform conservation and fisheries communities and to support protection and recovery planning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats to mussels** are grouped under habitat destruction, overutilization, disease, predation, nonindigenous species, pollution, hybridization, and restricted range. The authors judge habitat destruction the single most important cause.

- **Dams:** Dams alter physical, chemical, and biological conditions both upstream and downstream. They are estimated to destroy approximately 30% to 60% of the mussel fauna (Layzer et al. 1993; Williams et al. 1992). The most damaging effect is probably the disruption of reproduction by eliminating host fish species. See also [[Dam Impoundment Effects]].
- **Siltation and contaminants:** Erosion from deforestation, poor agriculture, and loss of [[Riparian Zones]] increases silt loads and destabilizes stream bottoms. Heavy metals, pesticides, and acid mine drainage are further threats.
- **Asian clam (*Corbicula fluminea*):** First introduced to the West Coast in the 1930s, it is the most widespread nonindigenous aquatic bivalve in North America. Densities of up to thousands per square meter have been implicated in native unionid declines, though the harm varies by site and time (Belanger et al. 1990; Leff et al. 1990).
- **Zebra mussel (*Dreissena polymorpha*):** First discovered in Lake St. Clair in 1988, it spread across most of the [[Great Lakes]]. It was found in the Illinois River in early 1991, the first record outside the Great Lakes drainage. By late 1991 it had reached the lower [[Ohio River]] and [[Tennessee River]], and it has since been found in the lower Mississippi River in southern Louisiana. Its range may eventually cover most of the United States and southern Canada (Nalepa and Schloesser 1992).

## Key Findings

**1. Conservation status (297 native taxa)**

| Status category | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Endangered, possibly extinct (E*) | 21 | 7.1% |
| Endangered, extant (E) | 77 | 20.6% (Summary gives 26.0%) |
| Threatened (T) | 43 | 14.5% |
| Special Concern (SC) | 72 | 24.2% |
| Undetermined (U) | 14 | 4.7% |
| Currently Stable (CS) | 70 | 23.6% |

- 213 taxa (71.7%) are endangered, threatened, or of special concern.
- The categories sum to 297. The abstract's 20.6% for extant endangered taxa conflicts with the Summary's 26.0%. Both figures are reported as given. 77/297 equals about 25.9%.
- Within the genera *Epioblasma* and *Pleurobema* (56 taxa), 15 are possibly extinct and 31 others are extant but endangered.
- Many "currently stable" taxa may decline severely as the zebra mussel invades large southern rivers and reservoirs.

**2. Status definitions** (AFS Endangered Species Committee)
- **E:** in danger of extinction across all or a significant part of its range. An asterisk marks possible extinction.
- **T:** likely to become endangered.
- **SC:** may become endangered from relatively minor habitat disturbance; needs careful monitoring.
- **U:** historic and current distribution and abundance not evaluated in recent years.
- **CS:** distribution and abundance may be stable, and no immediate management is needed.
- Status reflects the whole range, regardless of political boundaries. The authors agree with federal [[Endangered Species Act]] listings but may revise status after pending U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service reviews.

**3. Taxonomic changes**
- The authors recognize the family Margaritiferidae.
- They recognize the genera *Pyganodon* and *Utterbackia*, previously included in *Anodonta* (Hoeh 1990).
- They corrected several spelling errors in Turgeon et al. (1988).

**4. Commercial harvest**
- The cultured pearl trade drove a resurgence in harvest in the decade before 1993. Most shell goes to Japan, where it is cut into beads that form the nuclei of cultured pearls.
- Shell prices reached $6 per pound on the Japanese market.
- As many as 75 to 100 commercial mussel boats harvested single reservoirs at once.
- Exports totaled 9,000 short tons in 1991. Demand then fell and levelled at about 4,500 short tons (Baker 1993).
- No federal regulations restrict harvest unless a species is listed under the Endangered Species Act. Several states have set species, size, location, and season limits, and some monitor the effects.
- Harvest methods:
  - **Diving** ([[Hookah Diving]] or SCUBA) lets harvesters find dense beds and pick legal-size individuals of about 15 valuable species.
  - **[[Brailing]]** drags a crowfoot dredge across the bottom. It is nonselective, and undersized mussels are discarded. One study reported up to 50% mortality of undersized mussels (Sickel 1989). The survival of discarded mussels and the effects of brailing on populations need further study.

**5. Recommendations and conclusions**
- The authors urge natural resource agencies to manage proactively for whole ecosystems rather than react species by species.
- They state that many listed mussels "obviously need emergency attention or they will perish."
- Mussels are valuable as a commercial resource, as indicators of environmental health, and as a major part of world freshwater biodiversity.
- They warn that the imperiled status of mussels may lead to the complete loss of some genera and severe impoverishment of the richest freshwater mussel fauna in the world, unless prompt conservation action is taken.

**6. Distribution and references**
- The taxa list is arranged by family and genus, with status codes and historic state or provincial ranges. Some taxa extend into Mexico.
- The reference section lists regional literature organized by state and province, plus general works such as Turgeon et al. (1988), Cummings and Mayer (1992), and Williams et al. (1989).
- Examples of listed taxa with photographs: [[Ouachita Rock-Pocketbook]] (E, Arkansas), [[Snuffbox]] (T, Illinois), [[Higgins Eye]] (E, Mississippi River), [[Louisiana Pearlshell]] (T, Alabama), [[Altamaha Lance]] (T, Georgia), and [[Western Ridgemussel]] (U, California).
