# Water Chemistry of a Stream Following a Storm, Absaroka Mountains, Wyoming

#water-chemistry #freshwater-ecology #hydrogeochemistry

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[William R. Miller]] and [[James I. Drever]] examines how the major dissolved chemistry of the [[North Fork Shoshone River]] changed during a single storm on July 20, 1973, in the [[Absaroka Mountains]] of Wyoming. The authors show that the variation cannot be explained by simple dilution of base flow by rainwater. Using [[R-Mode Factor Analysis]] on the storm runoff, split into two hydrologic components, they identify distinct chemical controls: early in the flood, dissolution of readily soluble salts from the soil zone; later, [[Dilution Effect|dilution]] of base flow. Selective [[Ferromagnesian Weathering]] and [[Potassium Leaching]] from biological material operate throughout. The authors argue that storm-time chemistry can reveal weathering processes at different soil and weathered-rock depths.

The document contains no fish data. Its subject is stream water chemistry and weathering geochemistry, not ichthyology or the broader aquatic biota.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The sampled drainage basin of the North Fork above the sampling location covers approximately 1,100 km². Vegetation is mainly conifer forest, with grass and sagebrush along the valley. Most of the basin lies within the [[Shoshone National Forest]], and the authors consider human influence essentially absent. Elevation ranges from 1,800 m at the sampling site to 2,500 m upstream, and several peaks above the basin divide exceed 3,700 m. The mean July discharge is 54 m³/s (U.S. Geological Survey, 1959).

**Geology and soils.** The basin lies entirely within the [[Absaroka Volcanic Field]], made up of deeply dissected andesitic stratovolcanoes and reworked volcaniclastic material. The main primary minerals are plagioclase and pyroxene, with locally abundant hornblende and olivine. The rocks are deeply weathered. Soils are generally thin lithosols, only a few centimetres deep, attributed to a high physical erosion rate of 78 m/m.y. The dominant clay mineral in soils and suspended sediment is [[Montmorillonite]], with traces of chlorite, illite and kaolinite in some soil samples.

**Precipitation.** Average annual rainfall across the basin is 25 to 76 cm, with more rain in spring. Rain dissolved solids were judged a low contribution and were excluded from the stream-water discussion.

**Sampling and analysis.** Water samples were collected in polyethylene bottles at the start of the storm, at ¼-hour intervals for the first 24 hours, and at 2-hour intervals for the next 24 hours. Sampling ran for 74 hours, but because several storms occurred, only the first 32 hours are analysed. Samples were filtered through 0.45 µm membranes. [[Specific Conductance]] was measured at 25 °C. Calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium and silica were determined by atomic absorption spectrophotometry, and bicarbonate was calculated from alkalinity by potentiometric titration with HCl. Field pH, gauge height and water temperature were recorded at each sampling time.

**Rain composition.** Mean rain chemistry (mg/L, from four samples, except sulfate and chloride from two) was: calcium 0.25, magnesium 0.10, sodium 0.03, potassium 0.18, bicarbonate 1.94, sulfate 1.14 and chloride 0.12.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Storm hydrology.** The storm delivered 1.3 cm of rain at the sampling site. Rainfall probably averaged 2.5 to 3.0 cm across the whole basin, with more falling at higher elevations. Storm runoff has two components: [[Overland Flow]], which reaches the stream first after contact with near-surface soil, and [[Subsurface Flow]], which has been in contact with soil longer and has penetrated deeper.

**Weathering zones.** The authors frame the storm as a natural probe of weathering. Soil-zone reactions, shallow dissolution of soluble salts and deeper [[Selective Weathering]] in the altered-rock zone each leave a distinct chemical signature in the runoff. Water from the first, overland component has not penetrated far into the altered-rock zone, so the weathering signal is weak early in the storm.

**Silica kinetics.** Kennedy (1971) showed that the silica-to-specific-conductance ratio tracks differences in release kinetics between silica and the major electrolytes. In the [[Mattole River]] basin in California, this ratio peaks near peak flow and then declines. The North Fork shows no such simple relationship, which prompted the multivariate analysis.

**Biological sources.** Potassium and, to a lesser degree, calcium and bicarbonate are linked to leaching from leaves, seeds, pollen and insects, and to leaf decay in streams. Earlier studies in New Hampshire and Maryland (Johnson et al., 1968; Likens et al., 1967; Cleaves et al., 1970) are cited as supporting evidence.

## Key Findings

**1. Simple dilution does not explain the storm chemistry.** Early in the storm, soluble salts are leached from the soil zone, giving a small initial rise in salinity. Later, these salts are largely flushed out and dilution of base flow dominates.

**2. Storm runoff splits into two components,** separated on a plot of specific conductance against gauge height (Figure 5). R-mode factor analysis was run on each component separately, using SPSS at the University of Wyoming. Factors with eigenvalues above 0.35 were varimax-rotated. Including or excluding gauge height, and using oblique rather than varimax rotation, produced no significant change.

**3. First storm component (overland flow): three factors explain 98% of variation.**
- **Factor 1 (41%)**, highly soluble material: sodium, bicarbonate, magnesium and calcium. Interpreted as soluble soil salts plus possible hydrogen-ion exchange on clays. Its gauge-height loading is essentially zero, so it is not diluted.
- **Factor 2 (37%)**, potassium leaching: negative loading on gauge height and high loading on potassium. Factor 2 accounts for 72% of potassium variation, and potassium rises with streamflow.
- **Factor 3 (20%)**, ferromagnesian weathering: silica, magnesium and bicarbonate. Its influence is small because overland water has not reached the deeper altered-rock zone.

**4. Second storm component (subsurface flow): three factors explain 94% of variation.**
- **Factor 1 (59%)**, dilution: bicarbonate, calcium and sodium, with a strong negative gauge-height loading. It accounts for 83% of calcium, 83% of sodium and 86% of bicarbonate variation. The soluble-salt factor from the first component is absent.
- **Factor 2 (22%)**, ferromagnesian weathering: silica and magnesium. Calcium and bicarbonate are masked by the dilution factor and do not load here.
- **Factor 3 (13%)**, potassium leaching: 55% of potassium variation. Its weaker influence than in the first component is attributed to leaching being more effective early in the storm.

**5. Potassium behaves differently from all other ions.** Its concentration peaks at maximum storm runoff, showing no dilution effect. This points to control by leaching of organic matter.

**6. Silica and magnesium show selective weathering.** Thin-section and bulk rock analyses show that ferromagnesian minerals (augite, olivine) are weathered selectively relative to plagioclase, mainly in the altered-rock zone below the surface soils.

**7. Conclusion.** Storm-time chemistry of a stream reflects the depth profile of weathering: soluble soil salts first, then dilution of base flow, with ferromagnesian weathering and organic potassium leaching operating throughout. The authors note that the companion work (Miller and Drever, in preparation) supports the weathering interpretation.
