# Invertebrate Drift in the Skunk River, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #invertebrate-drift #lotic-insects #benthic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1976 study, published in the *Proceedings of the Iowa Academy of Science*, examined downstream invertebrate drift in the Skunk River, Iowa, during summer 1971. Drift was sampled weekly at four stations and was composed primarily of lotic insects. Drift composition and abundance were related to substrate type, effluent from the [[Ames Sewage Treatment Plant]], and discharge. The author concluded that substrate transition and channel dredging explained most spatial differences, that municipal effluent increased chironomid immatures, and that drift density rose with falling discharge at sand-bottomed sites.

## Background & Description

Downstream drift of benthic invertebrates was considered important for dispersal and regulation of stream benthic populations. The study assumed that drift collections sample a randomly distributed benthic population, because drifting organisms travel only short distances. The aim was to determine which environmental factors shape the composition and abundance of invertebrate drift in the Skunk River.

The study area lies in the [[Wisconsin Drift]] region of north-central Iowa. The upper river runs through a narrow, forested bedrock valley between Story City and Ames, with boulder and rubble riffles and silt in deep pools. At Ames the river enters a wide preglacial valley, channelized about 1900 for agricultural drainage, where shifting sand is the dominant substrate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Discharge:** Flow falls to very low levels in late summer. North of Ames, discharge declined from 6.75 m³/s on July 6 to 0.14 m³/s by August 31, 1971 (U.S. Geological Survey, 1971). [[Aquifer Pumping]] at Ames further reduces natural flow.
- **Effluent:** Secondary-treated sewage from the Story City and Ames plants enters the river. At low flow, Ames effluent can exceed natural flow.
- **Background:** Fisheries and limnological characteristics of the upper river were described by Jones et al. (1974).

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Drift was captured in tapered nets (30.5 cm mouth, 571 µm mesh), each filtering 13.6 m³ of water, assuming 100% sampling efficiency.
- Hourly samples (June 8, 1971) and diurnal sampling (July 27–29, 1971) showed total drift density rose rapidly after sunset. No day-active drift periodicity was detected, so routine samples were taken about two hours after sunset.
- Two concurrent weekly samples were taken at four stations. Stations 1 and 2 were in the rock-substrate segment; stations 3 and 4 flanked the Ames sewage outfall in the sand segment.

**Taxonomic composition**
- 20 invertebrate genera were identified from drift.
- [[Chironomidae]] larvae (42%) and [[Baetis]] nymphs (21%) were the most common components of drift.
- [[Hyalella azteca]], *Hexagenia* sp., and *Pteronarcys* sp. were rare, together making up less than 1% of drift.

**Substrate effects**
- Taxa associated with large substrate particles (Baetidae, Heptageniidae, Hydropsychidae, Simuliidae) were most abundant at stations 1 and 2 and declined downstream of the sand transition.
- Station 3 means for Baetidae, Heptageniidae, Hydropsychidae, Simuliidae, and total individuals differed significantly (P = .05) from the mean of stations 1 and 2.
- Pteronarcidae and Rhyacophilidae, rare at stations 1 and 2, were absent from drift at stations 3 and 4.

**Effluent effects**
- Mean chironomid immature numbers differed significantly (P = .05) between stations 3 and 4, with more chironomids below the Ames outfall. The author linked this to greater chironomid abundance in the benthos below the outfall (Zimmer, 1972).
- Story City effluent appeared to have little effect on drift.

**Seasonal and discharge effects**
- Season means differed significantly for all tested taxa except Simuliidae. Period × station interactions were not significant.
- Total invertebrates per collection rose significantly (P = .05) in late summer (mean 57.4 vs. 29.4 in early summer).
- Drift density was significantly correlated with discharge only at sand-substrate stations. Correlation coefficients were −.18, +.04, −.80, and −.78 for stations 1 to 4; regressions were significant at stations 3 and 4 only.
- The author proposed that sand substrates stabilize as discharge falls, becoming more suitable for benthic colonization.

**Conclusions**
- Most differences in drift above and below Ames were attributed to valley geology and channel dredging, which could not be separated.
- Municipal wastes from Ames altered drift composition, mainly through increased chironomid immatures.
- Drift abundance increased with decreasing discharge in the lower study area.
