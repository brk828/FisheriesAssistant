# Changes in the Fish Population in the Upper Ohio River following Temporary Pollution Abatement

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #riverine-ecosystems #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #pollution-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study by Krumholz and Minckley (1963-era publication) documents a rapid and dramatic recovery of the fish community in the Upper [[Ohio River]] following the incidental abatement of industrial pollution during a steel industry shutdown (July 15–November 7, 1959). Using rotenone sampling in the auxiliary lock chamber of [[Montgomery Lock and Dam]], researchers compared fish assemblages before and after the strike began. Just 11 days into the steel shutdown, species richness more than doubled (9 to 21 species) and abundance increased more than fivefold (480 to 2,587 individuals) compared to pre-strike samples. The compositional shift was driven primarily by the influx of "clean-water fishes" — species such as the [[Stoneroller]], [[Bigeye Chub]], and [[Mimic Shiner]] — that apparently recolonized from nearby unpolluted backwaters and tributaries rather than emerging from within the degraded main channel. The authors conclude that industrial (not domestic sewage) pollution was the primary factor suppressing faunal diversity in this reach of the river, since a prior period of partial domestic sewage treatment (1958–1959) produced no comparable community shift.

## Background & Description

This paper is a community-ecology / faunal survey rather than a morphological study of a single species; no detailed anatomical descriptions are provided. However, key taxonomic identifications and life-stage notes include:

- **[[Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*) — dominant early collections were composed largely of young-of-the-year individuals.
- **[[Black Bullhead]]** (*Ictalurus melas*) — consistently dominant across nearly all samples (up to 96.7% relative abundance in the June 1959 pre-strike sample); many individuals in the third sample were young-of-the-year.
- Hybrid individuals of **Carp × Goldfish** (*Cyprinus carpio* × *Carassius auratus*) were documented, indicating hybridization occurring in the disturbed habitat.
- Species list spans at least 7 families: Clupeidae (shad), Cyprinidae (minnows/shiners), Catostomidae (suckers), Ictaluridae (catfishes/bullheads), Fundulidae (killifish), Centrarchidae (sunfishes/bass/crappie), and Percidae (perch/darters).
- Total sample weights ranged from 9.00 to 155.52 pounds across the six collections (four lock chamber, two backwater), reflecting large shifts in standing biomass and numeric abundance rather than individual body-size data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat context:**
- The study site was the auxiliary chamber of [[Montgomery Lock and Dam]] on the Ohio River, 31.7 miles downstream of the Pittsburgh confluence of the Allegheny and Monongahela rivers, with water depths of 11–17 feet during sampling.
- Comparative reference sites included polluted and unpolluted backwaters/embayments, notably an isolated ~300 ft backwater behind the lock esplanade, largely disconnected from the main channel and serving as a relatively pollution-free refuge.

**Pollution regime:**
- Prior to the steel strike, the river exhibited classic industrial pollution signatures: gray, malodorous water, oil slicks, floating debris, and dead fish; nets required cleaning 2–3 times daily.
- After strike onset, water clarity and odor improved markedly; nets required cleaning only once per 24 hours.
- Water-quality data (Anonymous, 1961 / [[ORSANCO]]) showed that during the shutdown, phenolic compounds fell to ~3.0 ppb (vs. 16 ppb after mill restart), threshold-odor intensity dropped (average index 8 vs. 29), and dissolved iron averaged 0.1 ppm (vs. 2–7× higher post-restart).
- River discharge during the study (~10,000 cfs, ~800 million cubic feet/day) provided rapid dilution and flushing capacity, facilitating self-purification once industrial inputs ceased.

**Community/recolonization dynamics:**
- "Clean-water fishes" — including [[Stoneroller]], [[Bigeye Chub]], [[Creek Chub]], [[Sand Shiner]], [[Mimic Shiner]], [[Common Shiner]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[White Sucker]], and [[Yellow Perch]] — appeared or increased sharply only after pollution abatement.
- These species are inferred to have invaded from nearby unpolluted backwaters and creeks (not from less-polluted stretches of the main channel), supported by their prior known occurrence only in clear backwaters near the dam (per [[Krumholz Et Al. 1962]]).
- Small minnow species (Cyprinidae) rose from 3.5% of total catch (32 individuals) in the first three samples to 61.1% (1,583 individuals) in the fourth sample — a key indicator of environmental improvement.
- Comparison with backwater habitat: the fourth (post-strike) lock-chamber sample was compositionally more similar to backwater collections than to any prior lock-chamber sample, reinforcing the recolonization-from-refugia hypothesis.
- Seven species were unique to the backwater (e.g., [[Spotted Sucker]], [[Golden Redhorse]], [[Longear Sunfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Logperch]]) and seven were unique to the lock chamber (e.g., [[Silver Chub]], [[Ghost Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[White Crappie]]), indicating partitioned but overlapping faunas.

## Key Findings

1. **Rapid faunal resurgence**: Within only 11 days of the steel industry shutdown, the lock chamber fish community more than doubled in species richness (9→21 species, 3→7 families) and increased more than fivefold in abundance (480→2,587 individuals).
2. **Industrial pollution as primary limiting factor**: The lack of faunal change during a preceding period of partial domestic sewage treatment (July 1958–June 1959) — contrasted with the dramatic shift during the 11-day steel strike — implicates industrial effluent (not municipal sewage) as the dominant driver of the river's prior "faunal monotony."
3. **Source of recolonizers**: New "clean-water" species most likely invaded from adjacent unpolluted backwaters and tributary refugia rather than emerging from within previously polluted main-channel reaches, based on compositional similarity between post-strike lock samples and backwater samples.
4. **Water quality corroboration**: Independent ORSANCO water-chemistry monitoring confirmed sharp reductions in phenols, threshold-odor intensity, and dissolved iron during the mill shutdown, with all indicators rebounding (often more than doubling) once mills resumed operation — providing a causal mechanistic link between industrial activity and biotic degradation.
5. **Self-purification capacity**: High river discharge (~10,000 cfs) enabled rapid dilution and flushing of pollutants, allowing biological recovery to proceed quickly once the pollution source was interrupted, consistent with earlier findings on riverine recovery after pollution events (e.g., [[Krumholz 1946]] West Fork White River study).
6. **Contrast with catastrophic pollution events**: Unlike sudden toxic spill events that eliminate entire fish populations (as in the West Fork White River case), this study addresses recovery from more chronic, continuous industrial pollution, showing that even long-standing degraded rivers can show fish community rebound within days of source reduction.
