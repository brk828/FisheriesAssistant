# Threatened Fishes of the World: Gila Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) and Fish Nematode Fragment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This note covers a species account of the [[Gila Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida* Girard, 1856), a small, threatened cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico, USA. The account is from the *Environmental Biology of Fishes* series "Threatened Fishes of the World" (Marsh, 1996). The species has been eliminated from much of its historic range and survives in a few isolated reaches. The account documents its identification, distribution, habitat, feeding, reproduction, threats, and conservation needs.

The source file also contains a second, unrelated fragment: the closing paragraph, acknowledgements, and reference list of an article on fish nematode parasites. Only the single sentence on experimental design is summarized here.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** A member of the tribe [[Plagopterini]] (spiny-rayed minnows). The source states the tribe has 7 taxa in 3 genera, 2 of them monotypic, and is endemic to the Colorado River basin.
- **Diagnostic features:** Two spinose rays at the leading edge of the dorsal fin, the first strongest and sharp-pointed. Pharyngeal teeth are listed as "1,4-4,1" (OCR-garbled in the source; the reading is probable, not certain). Scales are small, deeply embedded plates.
- **Size and appearance:** Small, slim, laterally compressed fish, maximum about 75 mm TL. Sides are silvery with vertically elongated black specks; back olive-gray to brownish; belly white. Breeding males are brassy, especially on the head and fin bases.
- **Photograph:** An adult male, 60 mm TL, from [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona (Schoenherr, in Minckley 1973).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic and current distribution**
- Historically found in large to moderate streams of the Gila River basin, including the Gila, Salt, Verde (upstream from present-day Phoenix), Agua Fria, San Pedro, and San Francisco rivers and major tributaries.
- Present range is reduced to Aravaipa and Eagle creeks and the upper [[Verde River]] (Arizona), and the Gila River with its East, West, and Middle forks (New Mexico).

**Habitat and behaviour**
- Lives in lotic waters less than 1 m deep.
- Adults occupy shear zones along sand-gravel bars, eddies downstream of riffles, and broad, shallow areas above bars.
- Young occupy shallow pool margins over soft, fine-grained sediments.
- Habitat associations vary by season, ontogeny, and geography.

**Growth, life span, and diet**
- First-year growth to 40-48 mm TL; second year to 59-74 mm TL.
- Sexes look similar. Typical longevity is 1-2 years (occasionally 3, rarely 4).
- An opportunistic, [[Drift Feeding]] carnivore, mainly on aquatic insects.

**Predators and competitors**
- Native predators: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (extirpated from the spikedace range) and [[Roundtail Chub]].
- Non-native fishes (cyprinids, ictalurids, poeciliids, centrarchids) may now prey on spikedace of all sizes.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning is presumably cued by discharge and temperature in spring-summer.
- A female is typically accompanied by 2 males and spawns in riffles.
- Gametes are released into the water column or near the substrate. Fertilized ova are adhesive and demersal.
- Young-adult sex ratio is near unity, but skewed toward females in older fish. Females may be fractional spawners.
- Fecundity is 90-250 ova, correlated with TL and age. Ovum diameter is near 1.5 mm.

**Threats**
- Habitat destruction or alteration: dewatering, dams, altered hydrologic regimes, and channel management.
- Interactions with non-native fishes, particularly the [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*).

## Key Findings

1. **Threatened status:** The species is federally listed as threatened in the USA. Formal designation of [[Critical Habitat]] was pending at the time of writing.
2. **Severe range contraction:** Only a few populations remain, in Aravaipa and Eagle creeks, the upper Verde River, and the Gila River system in New Mexico.
3. **Conservation priorities:** The [[Recovery Plan]] (USFWS 1991) recommends:
   - protecting remaining populations (critical habitat designation, land and water acquisition, barriers, improved land-use practices);
   - determining how non-native fishes interact with spikedace;
   - quantifying physical habitat requirements;
   - restoring selected historic habitats and reestablishing populations, with monitoring;
   - developing methods for captive holding, propagation, and rearing;
   - disseminating information to the public and professionals.
4. **Broader conservation concern:** The author notes that one Plagopterini taxon is recently extinct, the rest are listed or candidates, and continuing water development and non-native fishes make their status perilous. The author concludes that management must be implemented soon "or it likely will be lost."
5. **Research gaps:** The source identifies the need for basic research on life history and ecology, which is still needed despite the species being relatively well studied (Barber et al. 1970; Propst et al. 1986).
