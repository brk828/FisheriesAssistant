# Spawning and Culture of Humpback Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1981 study by Roger L. Hamman, of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], describes the first hatchery spawning and culture of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Wild adults from the [[Little Colorado River]] and the [[Colorado River]] were induced to spawn by injection with acetone-dried carp pituitary, either naturally on artificial substrate or by manual stripping. The study reports egg incubation, hatching and fry survival across four water temperatures, and growth through day 56. The author concludes that the methods are suitable for full-scale propagation to support [[Endangered Species Recovery]] and reintroduction.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub was one of the last freshwater cyprinids discovered in North America ([[Miller 1946]]). It is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and was known only from [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona, before 1950. By the time of this study it was found only sporadically in canyon reaches of the upper and lower basins. It is listed as endangered by Arizona, Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, and by the U.S. Department of the Interior (1980) under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

The study aimed to develop techniques for spawning and culturing the species. Its scope covers brood stock held at Willow Beach during 1981.

**Brood stock**
- 14 wild adults from the Little Colorado River, collected 17 May 1978, 17 October 1979 and 13 June 1980 near the confluence with the Colorado River, [[Grand Canyon National Park]].
- 16 wild adults from the Colorado River, collected 5 November 1979 at [[Black Rocks]], near Grand Junction, Colorado.
- Ages were unknown. Females were generally larger than males (Table 1).

**Spawning and rearing facilities**
- **Semi-natural spawning:** two concrete raceways (30.5 × 2.4 × 1.2 m), designated A9 and A10, connected for [[Water Recirculation]]. Water was kept at least 19 °C, which was considered necessary for spawning. Pumping was at 0.02 m³/s with a 1-hp pump. Spawning substrate was boulders (30–40 cm) beneath cobble (4–10 cm). Water depth over the substrate was 10–76 cm.
- **Artificial spawning:** a holding tank (6.0 × 0.75 × 0.75 m) with warm water at 21–22 °C flowing at 23 L/min. Eggs were fertilized with milt, water-hardened for 50 min, and incubated on 1.4-mm mesh screen trays in four aluminum troughs.
- **Rearing:** two recirculating systems or four raceways (A9, A10, C1, C2). Raceways were filled a week before spawning and fertilized with ammonium phosphate and commercial trout starter to produce a plankton bloom. [[Zooplankton]], predominantly cladocerans, appeared within 10 days.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The decline of the humpback chub has been attributed to habitat loss from dams, reduced flows below cold and fluctuating dam tailwaters, and [[Hybridization]] (Vanicek et al. 1970; Holden and Stalnaker 1970, 1975; Minckley 1973; Smith et al. 1979). Hatchery work was framed around these threats. Two of its key findings bear on them. Egg and fry mortality at low temperatures match conditions common below dams, which the author links to dam-related decline. Hybridization is also possible: the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) has been artificially hybridized with humpback chub in a hatchery (Hamman 1981).

Spawning timing differed among populations. Suttkus and Clemmer (1977) reported probable spawning in June and July in Grand Canyon. [[Holden 1978]] indicated May and June in [[Desolation Canyon]], [[Utah]], on the [[Green River]]. Spawning in this study occurred in May for Little Colorado River fish (19–20 °C) and June for Colorado River fish (21–22 °C).

## Key Findings

**Spawning**
- **Semi-natural spawning:** nine females spawned 5–6 May 1981, within 24 h of the first injection. Males had begun showing orange to red-orange breeding colors on 5 May at 19 °C. The spawning area covered 1.0 × 2.4 m in 35–45 cm of water at 19–20 °C. An estimated 30,000 eggs were spawned naturally. Egg diameters were 2.6–2.9 mm (mean 2.8 mm). Eggs adhered to the cobble and were left to hatch on the substrate.
- **Artificial spawning:** eight females ovulated 1–4 June 1981, about 20 h after the first injection. One female ovulated only after three injections. Egg diameters were 2.6–2.9 mm (mean 2.7 mm). Volumetric counts ranged from 51 to 58 eggs/mL (mean 55 eggs/mL).
- **Fecundity:** total fecundity of the eight females was 20,185 eggs. Mean fecundity was 2,523 eggs per female, or 5,261 eggs per kilogram of body weight.
- Induction used intraperitoneal injections of 4 mg acetone-dried carp pituitary per kg body weight, repeated at 24-h intervals in the artificial trials.

**Incubation and hatching**

| Water temperature | Hatching began (h) | Hatching ended (h) | Egg survival | Swim-up survival |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21–22 °C | 102 | 146 | 79% | 99% |
| 19–20 °C | 115 | 160 | 84% | 95% |
| 16–17 °C | 167 | 266 | 62% | 91% |
| 12–13 °C | 340 | 475 | 12% | 15% |

- Newly hatched fry averaged 6.9 mm total length on screen trays and 7.1 mm in raceway A9.
- At hatching, total length ranged from 6.7 to 7.4 mm and weight from 2.8 to 4.2 mg.

**Culture and growth**
- Fry began swimming to the surface 72 to 168 h after hatching. 12,381 were transferred to raceways C1 and C2. Fry hatched naturally on cobble in A9 and A10 (about 23,490 fry) were cultured in place.
- Fry placed in raceways doubled in length within 21 to 28 days.
- By day 28, zooplankton was declining and fry were fed commercial trout starter 4 to 5 times daily (0.25–0.50 kg feed per system per day). By day 35, fry averaged 22.4 and 32.9 mm total length.
- On day 55–56, 19,875 fingerlings (mean 36.9 mm, 588.5 mg) were removed from A9 and A10, with 83% survival. A further 9,318 fingerlings (mean 47.5 mm, 887.4 mg) were removed from C1 and C2, with 75% survival. The abstract gives the weight range as 586.5–887.4 mg, while the results give 588.5 mg, so the source may contain a transcription error. Fish increased 5–7-fold in total length over the 56 days.

**Water quality during culture** (weekly monitoring)
- Water temperature: 12.8–25.5 °C
- Carbon dioxide: 2.11–4.00 mg/L
- Ammonia nitrogen: 0.01–0.59 mg/L
- Dissolved oxygen: 9.1–10.6 mg/L
- pH: 7.9–8.75
- Adding river water at 12–13 °C lowered system temperature by about 7 °C within 2 h. Fish fed on the bottom at reduced temperature and returned to surface feeding as temperature rose.

**Conclusions**
- The author concludes that the methods are "suitable for full-scale propagation" and can help restore this endangered species. The future of the species may depend on hatchery spawning, culture, and reintroduction of hatchery-reared progeny to the wild.
- The author proposes that the 88% egg mortality and 85% fry mortality at 12–13 °C support the dam-related decline hypothesis.
