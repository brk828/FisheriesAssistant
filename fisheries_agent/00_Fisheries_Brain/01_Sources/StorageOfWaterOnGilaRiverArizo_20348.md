# Storage of Water on Gila River, Arizona

#hydrology #water-resources #reservoir-engineering

## Executive Summary

This 1900 U.S. Geological Survey Water-Supply Paper, written by [[Joseph Barlow Lippincott]] and verified by James D. Schuyler, assesses how to store Gila River water to restore irrigation supply to the [[Gila River Indian Reservation]]. The authors judged that at least 40,000 acre-feet must be stored annually, that [[Queen Creek]] cannot supply it, and that the [[Gila River]] is the only permanent source. They recommended that the [[San Carlos Reservoir]] dam be built first, with other sites withdrawn for future storage.

The document does not address fish, invertebrates, plankton or other aquatic biota. Its aquatic content concerns hydrology, sediment, evaporation and river discharge.

## Background & Description

- **Reservation:** The [[Gila River Indian Reservation]] covers 357,120 acres on both sides of the Gila, about 12 miles south of Phoenix (Phenix). Annual rainfall is 7 to 9 inches, so all crops depend on irrigation.
- **Peoples:** The reservation is occupied chiefly by the [[Pima Indians]] and [[Maricopa Indians]], with some Papagos. Cabeza de Vaca's account (about 1535) describes them as long-established irrigators. Their wheat crop once averaged about 2,000,000 pounds a year.
- **Cause of decline:** Diversion by the [[Florence Canal]], whose dam sits about 15 miles above the reservation, took most of the water during the growing season. Diversions far upstream later reduced the canal's own supply. The Department of Justice ultimately dropped legal action.
- **Investigation history:**
  - In 1896, [[Arthur P. Davis]] completed a preliminary investigation for about $3,500 and six months of field work. He proposed a masonry dam 170 feet high, with a provisional cost of $2,244,000. Bed rock depth was uncertain, and soundings found no rock at 40 to 50 feet.
  - Under the act of July 1, 1898, which provided $20,000, the Survey carried out core-drill exploration and gauging from 1898 to 1899. Two diamond drills and two pipe-driving machines were loaned by the [[Nicaragua Canal Commission]].
- **Population:** Agent Elwood Hadley estimated 7,250 Indians dependent on the reservation in October 1899 (Pimas 4,200; Maricopas 350; Papagos 2,700). He assumed 2 acres per Indian.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and flow**
- The drainage area above the [[Buttes Dam Site]] is 17,834 square miles. The area below [[San Carlos Reservoir]] and above the Buttes is 4,379 square miles, about 25 percent of the total.
- The [[San Pedro River]] supplied 9.4 percent of the discharge at the Buttes in May to August 1890, and about 90 percent came from above San Carlos.
- Measured mean annual discharge at the Buttes is given as 485,545 acre-feet in one section and 469,093 acre-feet in another. The text does not reconcile the two figures.

**Sediment (silt)**
- Colton's 1893 measurements averaged 2.2 percent silt by volume.
- Richins's 1895 observations averaged 10.5 percent mud, with a mud-to-dry-solid ratio of about 5:1.
- Over 1899 the solids fraction was 1.6 percent and the mud fraction about 8 percent. Total 1895–1899 solids were estimated at 9,597 acre-feet against 479,504 acre-feet of water.
- The highest mud concentration recorded was 27 percent, on July 11, 1899, described as the greatest sediment load observed in the river.
- Silt was judged the main threat to reservoir life, and sluicing and a storm channel were proposed.

**Evaporation and return flow**
- Evaporation losses from the Buttes reservoir were estimated at 22,370 acre-feet a year.
- Seepage or return water from irrigation is 20 to 50 percent of applied water. At Solomonville, 64 percent of water at the head works reappeared through underground seepage. A figure of 20 to 25 percent was expected for the new [[Gila River]] irrigation.

**Flood and channel**
- The Davis maximum flood estimate was 102,566 cubic feet per second, based on a channel cross section of 6,600 square feet and Kutter's formula.
- Bed material at the Buttes is sand and gravel up to 123 feet deep. Bed rock lies at 122.6 feet at the deepest hole.

**Climate and rainfall**
- Rainfall increases about 0.21 inch per 100 feet of elevation in eastern Arizona.
- The mean for the Gila basin above San Carlos is 12.87 inches, and the San Pedro mean is 14.13 inches.
- Rainfall in 1899 was about 80 percent of normal to August 1. Arizona's August 1899 precipitation was 85 percent below normal.

## Key Findings

**Storage sites and costs**

| Site | Dam height / ultimate | Storage (acre-feet) | Estimated cost | Years to silt-fill |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[Buttes Dam Site]] | 170 ft; rock-fill and masonry | 174,040 | $2,643,327 | 18 (about 18.6) |
| [[Riverside Reservoir]] | Masonry; raisable ~70 ft | 221,134 (ultimate ~650,000) | $1,989,605 | 67 (ultimate) |
| [[San Carlos Reservoir]] | 130 ft; raisable ~70 ft | 241,396 (ultimate ~550,000) | $1,038,926 | 63 (ultimate) |
| [[Guthrie Reservoir]] | 140-ft survey; rock-fill or earth | Not estimated | Not estimated | Not estimated |

- The Buttes masonry option was rejected because of rotten rock, great bed-rock depth and excessive height.
- Storage cost per acre-foot at the Buttes was 3.5 times that at San Carlos, and at Riverside 2.1 times.
- San Carlos was adopted at about $4.30 per acre-foot of stored water.

**Water availability**
- About 90 percent of Buttes flow is available at San Carlos.
- In the basin, the San Carlos reservoir would not lose more than about 10 percent of flow by the Buttes.
- In 1899 surplus was 179,599 acre-feet at the Buttes and 76,879 acre-feet at San Carlos.
- Reservoirs were judged likely to fill in ordinary years, and only severe drought beyond the record would leave them partly empty. Such droughts were expected about once every fifteen years.
- Less than 3 percent of Gila water near Florence was used for irrigation at the time. In 1890 only 6,619 acres were irrigated.

**Financial plan**
- Of the 241,396 acre-feet at San Carlos, 40,000 acre-feet would go to the Indians without charge. The remaining 201,396 acre-feet would irrigate about 100,698 acres at 2 acre-feet per acre.
- Estimated charge for the remaining water rights was $10.24 per acre.
- Public lands in the supplied district total 389,211 acres.
- Feeding 3,000 Indians at 10 cents per ration would cost $109,500 a year, equivalent to a capital of $2,787,500 at 4 percent.
- Estimated increase in taxable value was $7,772,400, with no public outlay.

**Recommendations**
1. Withdraw irrigable public lands and reservoir sites (Buttes, Queen Creek, Riverside, San Carlos, Guthrie) from entry pending Congressional action.
2. Maintain gauging on the Gila River.
3. Construct the San Carlos dam first.
4. Supply Indian water free of charge, with the Government recouping costs from sales of the remaining water rights.
5. Establish a Federal irrigation district to manage water delivery and canal construction.

**Conclusion:** The authors argued that restoring the water supply was an "imperative obligation of honor" to the Pima and Maricopa, and that only federal storage could achieve it. Private enterprise was judged unlikely because of the Indians' legal water rights, the mostly public land ownership, and interstate complications.
