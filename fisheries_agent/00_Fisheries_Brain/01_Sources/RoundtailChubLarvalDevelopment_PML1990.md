# Roundtail Chub Larval Development and Colorado Squawfish Predation on Channel Catfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1985 research article (*Southwestern Naturalist*, vol. 30, no. 1) with two linked studies on native and introduced fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The first describes the embryonic and larval development of [[Roundtail Chub]] reared in the laboratory, providing growth data and identification criteria for young-of-the-year chubs. The second reports that [[Colorado Squawfish]], an endangered native cyprinid, can die from choking on introduced [[Channel Catfish]], and tests this in a laboratory experiment.

## Background & Description

**Roundtail chub larvae.** Development was tracked from fertilized eggs through the larval period at 19 to 20 °C. Embryonic stages followed Balinsky's convention. The authors note that reliable larval identification is a major problem for basin biologists, who must separate young [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]] before monitoring populations or studying life history. The study's developmental series is intended to support taxonomic research by the Colorado Division of Wildlife and Colorado State University.

**Colorado squawfish predation.** The Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is the largest cyprinid native to North America and was historically widespread in the Colorado River and its tributaries. Major water developments altered the system, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed the species as endangered (*Federal Register* 39: 1175, 1974). Channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*) were established in the basin in the early twentieth century as a sport fish. Squawfish found dead with channel catfish lodged in the esophagus had been reported before, but the only documented case prior to this study was a single 1983 record.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rearing conditions (roundtail chub).**
- Incubation: water temperature 19 °C; dissolved oxygen 100 to 105% saturation; pH 7.3 to 7.7; total hardness 410 to 440 mg CaCO₃/L; water flow 12.3 L/min.
- Post-hatch larvae: troughs measuring 3.0 × 0.3 × 0.2 m; dissolved oxygen 8.0 to 9.0 mg/L; pH 7.1 to 7.9; total hardness 450 to 470 mg CaCO₃/L; temperature 19 to 20 °C; flow 6 L/min.

**Feeding.** After swim-up, larvae were fed three to four times daily with brine shrimp nauplii and commercial fry diet (Tetra Min Fry Diet and Sample Food) during the first 19 days.

**Squawfish holding conditions.** Squawfish were fed pelleted commercial trout feed or rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*) on alternate days. Both species were held at 15 ± 1 °C on a 14 L:10 D photoperiod in dechlorinated Logan City water.

## Key Findings

**Embryonic development (roundtail chub).** Notable stages, with ages at 19 to 20 °C:
- 4 h: beginning of cleavage (Stage 3)
- 20 h: blastula (Stage 11)
- 36 h: late gastrula (Stage 14)
- 44 h: late neurula (Stage 16)
- 56 h: oval eyes (Stage 19)
- 60 h: early tail-bud (Stage 20)
- 92 h: finfold (Stage 21)

Embryos were sampled every 4 h and preserved in 3% buffered formalin. Larvae were fixed in 10% buffered formalin, and all specimens were measured for total length.

**Larval growth.**
- Mean daily growth over the larval period was 0.3 mm, ranging from 0.0 to 0.7 mm per day.
- Daily growth averaged 0.2 mm in newly hatched larvae and 0.4 mm in protolarvae.
- Hatchery-reared humpback chub and bonytail grew 0.5 to 0.6 mm per day at 12.9 to 25.5 °C (Hamman, 1982a, 1982b), so roundtail chub grew more slowly.
- Length at each developmental event agreed with field-collected roundtail chub larvae reported by Snyder (1981).

**Squawfish predation study (laboratory).** The experiment asked three questions:
1. Will adult squawfish prey on channel catfish without prior conditioning?
2. Can channel catfish be swallowed, or do they lodge in the esophagus?
3. Are squawfish injured while attempting to swallow channel catfish?

Sixty squawfish were tested, ranging from 160 to 512 mm total length (scan partly illegible for the mean; SD = 29 mm). Channel catfish were hatched in June 1982 at a commercial hatchery in southern Idaho and were 51 to 125 mm total length at testing (SD = 10.5, n = 600; mean partly illegible in scan).

**Field records.** Since the 1983 report, three more cases of squawfish choking on channel catfish were recorded from the Green River, Utah. This supports the view that channel catfish predation is a real mortality source for the endangered squawfish.

**Management implications.** The authors judge that taxonomic work by the Colorado Division of Wildlife and Colorado State University will help solve the larval identification problem, and that the roundtail chub developmental series is a key reference for that work. The predation findings add channel catfish to the list of factors threatening native fishes in the basin.
