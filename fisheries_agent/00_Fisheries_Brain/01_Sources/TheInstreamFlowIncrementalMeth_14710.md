# The Instream Flow Incremental Methodology: A Primer for IFIM

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Department of the Interior, National Biological Service primer (Biological Report 29, March 1995) introduces the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM), a interdisciplinary framework for evaluating how water management decisions affect aquatic habitat in flowing waters. The primer covers the historical progression from [[Minimum Flow Standards]] to incremental, habitat-based analyses; criteria for choosing among assessment techniques; the ecological principles underlying IFIM; its philosophical and problem-solving foundations; and the five sequential phases of applying IFIM. It positions the [[Physical Habitat Simulation System]] (PHABSIM) as one specific model within the broader IFIM methodology, not a synonym for it.

## Background & Description

The primer was prepared by Clair Stalnaker, Berton L. Lamb, Jim Henriksen, Ken Bovee, and John Bartholow for the [[National Biological Service]] and is intended as a short introduction to IFIM for practitioners, with pointers to advanced certified training. It is organized into five sections: (1) history of instream flow problems; (2) choice of assessment methods; (3) ecological underpinnings; (4) philosophical underpinnings; and (5) application of IFIM.

Key historical developments described include:
- **Minimum flow standards** (1960s to early 1970s), which produced a single threshold flow for a stream reach, often below optimal habitat conditions.
- **Impact analyses** following the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) of 1970, which shifted attention to quantifying incremental changes in streamflow across alternative project designs.
- **Water budgets**, exemplified by the Pacific Northwest Electric Power Planning and Conservation Act of 1980, which established fisheries as a legitimate management purpose with reserved "fish water."
- **Multiple-use, interdisciplinary analysis**, recognizing that single-purpose reservoir management is no longer socially acceptable.
- **IFIM development**, led by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] during the small hydropower era of the late 1970s and early 1980s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The primer identifies the following ecological concepts as underpinning IFIM:

- **[[Longitudinal Succession]]**: Originating with Shelford (1911) and linked to Davis's (1909) stream-age classification, this concept holds that fish and invertebrate assemblages change predictably from headwaters to lowland rivers. It later matured into the [[River Continuum Concept]] (Vannote et al. 1980).
- **[[Habitat Segregation]]**: Fish communities segregate by velocity, depth, substrate, and cover within the same longitudinal zone (Thompson and Hunt 1930; Hubbs 1941).
- **[[Reproductive Success]]**: Salmonid redd conditions (gravel cleanliness, velocity, intragravel dissolved oxygen) strongly influence embryo survival. Centrarchids such as [[Smallmouth Bass]] are sensitive to water velocity over nests; the primer cites reported reproductive failure above about 0.15 ft/s.
- **[[Energetics]]**: Velocity is described as the most significant abiotic factor affecting stream community energetics. Fish tend to occupy low-energy microhabitats near high-energy feeding zones.
- **[[Habitat Bottlenecks]]**: Wiens' (1977) concept of ecological bottlenecks, applied to habitat limitations on individual populations. Habitat limitations typically act 1 to 3 years before maturation, and population size often reflects recruitment.

Environmental factors cited as influencing fish populations include water velocity, minimal water depths, instream cover, bottom substrate (particularly fines in interstitial spaces), water temperature, dissolved oxygen, total alkalinity, turbidity, and light penetration.

## Key Findings

- **Minimum flows offer limited protection.** Minimum flow values are almost always below optimal habitat conditions, yet they form the basis for water permits in many states.
- **Water budgets reframe the fisheries manager's role.** Fisheries managers must become water and habitat managers, deciding on daily delivery of water during critical seasons.
- **No single best method exists.** The authors conclude "there is simply no one best way"; the appropriate choice depends on whether a problem is standard-setting (planning, low controversy) or incremental (project negotiation, high controversy).
- **Method selection is also a political decision.** Analysts choose techniques as much for their fit with political and environmental problems as for scientific standards (Lamb 1986).
- **Tennant method.** Based on percentages of mean annual flow; Table 2.2 gives Tennant's recommendations (e.g., "Optimum" habitat at 60–100% of mean annual flow in both October–March and April–September seasons; "Poor" at 10%).
- **Drainage-area surrogate.** A base flow of 0.5 cubic feet per second per square mile (cfsm) is recommended for summer months in one drainage-area technique (Larson 1981).
- **7Q10 is inappropriate for fish.** The 7-day, 10-year low flow statistic was developed for water quality protection and does not address fish flow requirements.
- **Wetted perimeter technique.** Selects the inflection point of the wetted perimeter versus discharge relation, typically applied to wide, shallow, relatively rectangular riffles.
- **PHABSIM is a microhabitat model.** It computes an index of microhabitat for life stages at different flows, using habitat suitability indices (0–1) for depth, velocity, substrate, and cover, summed as [[Weighted Usable Area]] (WUA). It does not measure habitat actually used and does not predict channel change.
- **Brown trout suitability criteria.** Adults use deeper and faster water than juveniles (Figure 2.3).
- **Incremental methods require extensive work.** A comprehensive IFIM analysis may take up to 2 years for analysis alone and more than 3 years total from design to results.
- **Cost estimate.** The authors state that about 80% of IFIM studies for a single river segment could be conducted within 12 months at a cost under $45,000, while noting "it depends."
- **Habitat bottleneck findings (Bovee et al. 1994).** Multiple consecutive habitat events may affect adult populations; limiting events occur over variable time scales; habitat may be limited by both high and low flows; and the smallest habitat amount in a year may not be the limiting event.
- **Research priorities (Reiser et al. 1989).** Highest-priority needs are to (1) define the relation between flow, habitat, and fish production; (2) validate habitat measurements against fish production; and (3) develop new flow requirement methods.
- **Conflict resolution.** About 99% of instream flow problems are resolved through negotiation rather than arbitration, according to the authors' estimate.
- **Five-phase application.** Problem identification, study planning, study implementation, alternatives analysis, and problem resolution, with full and open communication required in every phase.
- **Alternatives evaluation criteria.** Effectiveness, physical feasibility, risk, and economics.
- **Best-alternative guideline.** When the present fishery is good to excellent, the best alternative is often the one that deviates least from baseline habitat ("no net loss of habitat," the official policy of the Canadian Department of Fisheries and Oceans).
- **Extending IFIM.** The authors note that ecosystem planning in federal agencies may require broader geographic scope and longer time horizons than IFIM has typically used.
- **Contact information.** The Midcontinent Ecological Science Center, Fort Collins, Colorado, handles technical questions; Colorado State University Conference Services handles training courses.
