# Age, Growth, and Production of the Lake Sturgeon in the Lake Winnebago Region, Wisconsin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #acipenseridae

## Executive Summary

This study examines a [[Lake Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser fulvescens*) population and its winter spearing fishery in the [[Lake Winnebago]] system of east-central Wisconsin. The authors combine creel-census catch estimates, length and weight measurements, age determinations from pectoral fin rays, and stomach content analysis to describe the population. The 1953 season produced the largest recorded catch, an estimated 2,828 fish weighing about 100,000 pounds, which the authors describe as exceeding any commercial lake sturgeon harvest in the United States and Canada. Ages ranged from 0 to 82 years, females dominated larger size classes, and the diet was dominated by [[Tendipedidae]] larvae.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Lake Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser fulvescens*), a long-lived, slow-growing member of the [[Acipenseridae]].
- **Study system:** Four connected river lakes on the [[Fox River]] and [[Wolf River]]: Lake Winnebago (137,708 acres; maximum depth about 21 ft, average about 15 ft), Lake Poygan (10,992 acres), Lake Winneconne (3,264 acres) and Lake Butte des Morts (4,505 acres). The smaller three lakes have maximum depths not exceeding 11 ft. All four share many characteristics of eutrophic lakes. The system covers about 156,000 acres in total.
- **Fishery:** Apart from a limited fall set-line season, the fishery is winter spearing through holes in the ice from portable shanties, using decoys and scattered bottom material to improve visibility. The authors note this practice is unique to Wisconsin, apart from a limited fishery in three Michigan lakes (Black, Mullett and Burt lakes, Cheboygan River drainage).
- **Regulations:** Anglers could take three fish per season, each marked with a $1 tail tag. The season limit was reduced from five to three in 1954, and the minimum legal length rose from 30 inches to 40 inches in September 1954.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Weather and water conditions:** Late-forming ice, winter thaws, heavy snow with high winds, and turbidity from clay-soil runoff all reduced spearing activity and success. Spearers could reach shanties up to 4 miles from shore.
- **Spatial pattern:** Most spearing on Poygan, Winneconne and Butte des Morts occurred in water under 10 ft deep, while much spearing on Lake Winnebago occurred between 10 and 18 ft, depending on visibility.
- **Feeding environment:** Stomach contents reflected local invertebrate communities. [[Tendipedidae]] larvae dominated, while [[Mayfly]] nymphs and aquatic sow bugs were also common in the smaller lakes.
- **Comparison fisheries:** Lake St. Francis (Quebec), the Nelson and Churchill rivers (Manitoba), and the Great Lakes produced far smaller annual yields. The [[White Sturgeon]] and [[Green Sturgeon]] commercial fishery on the Pacific coast produced 295,400 pounds in 1951.

## Key Findings

**Harvest and effort**
- The 1953 catch of 2,828 fish (about 100,000 lb) was an all-time high, following the 1952 opening of the smaller lakes. The previous record was 1,735 fish in 1948.
- Spearers increased from 1,427 (1951, Lake Winnebago only) to 3,074 (1953). Shanties rose to roughly 750 on Lake Winnebago and 315 on the smaller lakes in 1953.
- Success rates in 1953 averaged 1.14 fish per angler on the smaller lakes (175 creel cards) and 0.83 on Lake Winnebago (536 cards). The authors found a relatively high correlation between the number of participants and the success rate.

**Size structure**
- Speared fish ranged from 30 to 79 inches. The 79-inch fish is the largest on record from these waters.
- The length-frequency distribution for the smaller lakes was bimodal, with peaks at 37 inches and 53–54 inches. Fish 37–40 inches were most abundant.
- Lake Winnebago fish were more often over 60 inches, with a mode near 60 inches.

**Age and growth**
- Ages were determined from transverse sections of pectoral fin rays. Age was assessed for 966 fish (ages I to LXXXII, 82 years). Most were in the XV–XXX range.
- Growth was fastest in the first years. Fish reached 30 inches in about 7 years, 40 inches at about 13 years, and 50 inches at about 20 years.
- Growth was relatively constant until about 14 years, became more irregular between 14 and 19 years, and was highly variable after 19 years.
- The oldest fish was 79 inches long and about 82 years old. An 81-inch fish from Lake of the Woods, Ontario, had an estimated age of about 152 years (anonymous report, 1954).
- Lake Winnebago fish were larger than those from the smaller lakes at ages below 20 years, but average lengths over age 20 were similar across lakes.
- Males matured at about 14 years and females at about 25 years, according to [[Cuerrier]] (1949). The authors could not link maturity to growth changes with the available data.

**Length-weight relationship**
- Combined data gave: log W = −4.17096 + 3.30367 log L (W in pounds, L in inches).
- Separate equations: Lake Winnebago, log W = −4.01939 + 3.24070 log L; smaller lakes, log W = −4.12298 + 3.26625 log L.
- Male and female equations for the smaller lakes were nearly identical (males: −4.10243 + 3.25418 log L; females: −4.12597 + 3.26261 log L).
- Mean coefficient of condition was 21.7 for all 981 fish. Lake Winnebago fish were plumper (25.4, n = 184) than fish from other lakes (20.9, n = 797).

**Sex ratio**
- Sexes were nearly equal in the smaller lakes (females 53.6% of 491 fish). Females made up 72.4% of 163 fish from Lake Winnebago.
- Females accounted for 96% of fish over 59 inches (or 60 inches in the 1953 summary) and 94% of fish over 30 years old in the smaller lakes.
- Growth rates did not differ between the sexes. The authors suggest the sex difference in longevity reflects either a longer natural lifespan in females or higher male mortality from illegal snag-line harvest during spawning runs in the Wolf River.

**Diet**
- Stomachs of 554 fish (30–76 inches) were examined in February 1954. Only 11.8% of Lake Winnebago stomachs were empty, compared with 53.9% in the smaller lakes.
- [[Tendipedidae]] larvae occurred in 86.6% of Lake Winnebago stomachs and 27.6% of smaller-lake stomachs.
- Leeches (about 10% of Lake Winnebago stomachs) and fish remains, usually discarded bait minnows, were also found.

**Methodological notes**
- The authors estimated catches from returned creel-census cards, assuming successful and unsuccessful anglers reported at the same rate.
- The authors did not separate catch estimates by lake because of insufficient data.
- Alcohol immersion of fin-ray sections gave the best annulus contrast. Translucent zones were treated as valid annual marks.
