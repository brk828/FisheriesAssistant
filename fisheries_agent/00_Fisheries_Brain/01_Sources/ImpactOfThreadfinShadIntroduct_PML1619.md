# Impact of Threadfin Shad Introduction on Centrarchid Diets in Lake Powell

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Utah Division of Wildlife Resources study (authors listed on the header as Fred E. May and Charles Thompson; the OCR header is partly garbled) evaluates how the introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]] into [[Lake Powell]] changed the diets of four centrarchid predators: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Green Sunfish]]. Food habits were compared between a pre-introduction period and a post-introduction period using stomach analysis drawn from roughly 1964 to 1971. After shad became established, they became a dominant prey item for nearly every predator examined, displacing zooplankton, aquatic insects, and crayfish. The authors conclude that the overall impact of shad on centrarchid diets was substantial, although its full effect on the lake's fish populations was not yet determined.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Managers have stocked forage species in reservoirs to feed predatory sport fish. Earlier work on [[Threadfin Shad]] in western reservoirs (including the [[Colorado River]]) raised questions about its potential as forage, prompting this study.
- **Prey species:** Threadfin shad is a small, abundant clupeid that was regarded as an ideal forage fish because of its size, abundance, and morphology, which made it less vulnerable to predation than some other fishes.
- **Predators studied:** Four centrarchids were monitored: largemouth bass, black crappie, bluegill, and green sunfish. Largemouth bass was the main focus, with 1,580 stomachs examined.
- **Study scope:** The work tracked seasonal and size-class dietary changes before and after shad establishment.
- **Funding:** Supported in part by a Section 8 program for the Glen Canyon storage project.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and stocking.** Lake Powell, on the Arizona–Utah border, was stocked with threadfin shad. Donor populations came from Lake Pleasant in central Arizona, with initial eggs reportedly from Lake Mead (OCR-uncertain). Eggs were transplanted using methods described by Mauk and Smith (1971).

**Sampling methods.** Fish were collected by seine, gill net (experimental and vertical), and electrofishing, with nets set in the late afternoon and retrieved in the early morning. Stomach contents were measured volumetrically by water displacement and centrifugation. Food items were reported as percent of total stomach volume.

**Food web shifts.** Before shad, bass and crappie diets were dominated by centrarchids, unidentified fishes, zooplankton, and insects. Seasonal variation was marked; for example, black crappie and bluegill fed heavily on zooplankton in certain seasons. After shad, the predators shifted toward fish prey, and seasonal diet patterns became more uniform.

**Literature context.** The study cites prior reservoir work on threadfin shad and centrarchid feeding, including Goodson (1965) on Pine Flat Lake, California, and Applegate et al. (1966) on Bull Shoals Reservoir, where shad also became an important forage item.

## Key Findings

### Largemouth Bass (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- **Sample size:** 1,580 stomachs examined; 471 (30%) were empty. Before shad, 1,140 stomachs were examined (27.2% empty); after shad, 440 (36.6% empty). Mean stomach volume fell from 2.42 ml to 2.12 ml.
- **Seasonal diet:** Threadfin shad became a major diet item in all seasons after introduction, and it dominated the summer and autumn diets.
- **Prey composition:** Before shad, centrarchids made up the bulk of the diet (about 78% of volume in one period). After shad, shad constituted 88% of diet volume in one size class.
- **Size relationship:** Forage size increased with predator length, consistent with the general relationship between predator and prey size reported by Wright (1969) and Snow (1971).

### Black Crappie (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*)
- **Sample size:** 397 stomachs, collected and examined over five years (1967–1969 per figure labels). Fish ranged from 62 to 338 mm total length.
- **Shad effect:** Stomach volume increased by 58% after shad utilization. The change was reported as statistically significant (t = 3.79, df = 64, p = 0.05).
- **Diet:** Before shad, the diet varied strongly by season and size, with zooplankton and insects important. After shad, diets became more uniform and shad became dominant.

### Bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- **Pre-shad diet:** Zooplankton and insects dominated; fish made up only 3% of stomach volume.
- **Post-shad diet:** Threadfin shad became a prominent food item. Winter and summer diets showed the greatest shad use, consistent with greater shad availability during those seasons.
- **Growth implication:** The authors suggest that shad use could increase bluegill growth rates.

### Green Sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*)
- **Fish in diet:** Fish comprised 21% of diet volume before shad and 75% after shad was available.
- **Pre-shad diet:** Aquatic insects and zooplankton were the main prey.

### Overall Impact and Other Species
- Threadfin shad replaced many earlier forage items and became the dominant food for bass, black crappie, bluegill, and green sunfish in nearly all cases.
- Other prey, including insects, zooplankton, crayfish, and plant material, declined in importance after shad established.
- The paper reports **Rainbow Trout** and **Common Carp** among fishes found in bass stomachs, with trout ranging from about 4% to 9% of volume in some periods. Tables indicate that trout and carp were minor components of the diet.
- Cladocerans and copepods dominated the zooplankton fraction, while Diptera and Odonata were the main insects.

### Conclusions
- The authors state that threadfin shad substantially changed predator diets in Lake Powell.
- The full population-level consequences were not yet determined.
- The work recommends continued monitoring of shad's role as forage for centrarchids in western reservoirs.
