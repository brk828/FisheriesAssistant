# Evaluation of Instream Flow Requirements of Fishes in the Burro Creek Drainage, Arizona

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #habitat-modeling #instream-flow #Arizona

## Executive Summary

This study evaluated instream flow requirements for three native fish species—[[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta robusta*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*)—in the [[Burro Creek]] drainage of west-central Arizona using the Incremental Methodology. The research was conducted 1981-1982 at three study sites: [[Francis Creek]], Upper [[Burro Creek]], and Lower [[Burro Creek]]. These fishes serve as primary prey for the [[Black Hawk]] (*Buteogallus anthracinus*), a state-listed species with the largest breeding assemblage in North America in this drainage. Habitat preference data were collected via electrofishing and direct observation during spring and summer 1981. Hydraulic simulations used the Water Surface Profile (WSP) program after initial difficulties with streambed movement and low flows. Results indicate all three species show rapidly decreasing probability-of-use at water depths <0.5 ft and velocities >0.5 ft/sec, with no apparent substrate preferences. Weighted usable area (WUA) improved slowly as discharge increased from 5 to 25 ft³/sec but remained relatively stable at 3-5 ft³/sec due to large, deep pools. Maintenance of sufficient flow for fish use of riffles and runs appears critical for prey availability to black hawks. The [[Cyprus Bagdad Copper Company]] pumping station on [[Francis Creek]] withdraws water that could significantly impact aquatic resources during low-flow periods (June-August).

## Background & Description

The [[Burro Creek]] drainage in west-central Arizona supports exceptional biodiversity with over 250 plant and 300 vertebrate taxa. The [[Bureau of Land Management]] proposed managing 56,377 acres as the Burro Creek Riparian Management Area (BCRMA) to protect these resources. The drainage supports two federally endangered species ([[Bald Eagle]], [[Peregrine Falcon]]) and eight state-listed vertebrates including the [[Black Hawk]] and [[Roundtail Chub]]. Black hawk breeding territories are restricted to riparian habitats adjacent to perennially flowing water.

**Roundtail Chub** (3-7 inches TL in study): Probability-of-use = 1.00 for depths ≥0.6 ft, dropping to 0.80 at 0.3 ft and 0.00 at 0.08 ft. For velocity, probability drops linearly from 1.00 at 0.3 ft/sec to 0.25 at 0.7 ft/sec and 0.00 at 2.5 ft/sec. Adults (≥6 inches) prefer deeper pool waters; juveniles occupy pool peripheries near boulders. Species typically occupies pools and eddies, moving into chutes to feed on drifting insects. About 10% of 514 collected fish were >6 inches. Males released milt June 2-3, 1981, suggesting recent spawning (spawning in similar [[Aravaipa Creek]] occurs April-May).

**Sonora Sucker** (5-12 inches TL): Only 82 specimens collected, comprising 5-20% of sucker collections (vs. desert sucker). Probability-of-use increases from 0.15 at 0.3 ft depth to 0.98 at 0.8 ft. Velocity preference peaks at 1.00 for 0.2 ft/sec, decreasing to 0.07 at 2.0 ft/sec. Prefers deeper, slower waters than desert sucker. Ripe females collected March 27, 1981 at Lower Burro Creek. No specimens <5 inches collected at upper sites, suggesting poor 1981 year classes possibly due to thermal springs (75-80°F) maintaining elevated winter temperatures. Species prefers gravelly/rocky pools or deep quiet waters; concentrates in pools during summer but moves to shallow riffles for feeding.

**Desert Sucker** (3-9 inches TL): Most abundant species with 668 specimens collected. Age-0 fish abundant at Lower Burro Creek, growing from 2.4 inches (March 27) to 3.5 inches (June 3). Spawning likely occurred early in year (January-April typical in [[Aravaipa Creek]]). Probability-of-use increases rapidly from 0.00 at 0.09 ft to 1.00 at 1.0 ft depth. Velocity preference peaks at 1.00 for 0.3 ft/sec, decreasing to 0.07 at 2.0 ft/sec. Juveniles prefer shallower water with higher velocities than adults; strong preference for cobble substrate for periphyton grazing. Feeds mainly on algae scraped from stones with specialized mouth. No juveniles <5 inches collected at upper sites in June-August, indicating poor year-class recruitment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study sites were located in a drainage with highly variable discharge: instantaneous flows ranged from 31,500-47,500 ft³/sec during floods to <1 ft³/sec during summer low-flow periods. The 1981 water year total discharge was 5,480 acre-feet with maximum daily discharge of 108 ft³/sec. The [[Cyprus Bagdad Copper Company]] holds water rights for 2,319.36 acre-feet annually (42.3% of 1981 measured discharge), equivalent to 3.20 ft³/sec if pumped uniformly year-round.

Black hawk prey observations at two nests showed fish comprised major prey biomass: [[Sonora Sucker]] (most important by weight), [[Desert Sucker]], [[Roundtail Chub]], and [[Leopard Frog]] (*Rana pipiens*). [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) were not used as prey, likely due to small size (<4 inches) or low vulnerability. Mean weight of fish prey was ~0.1 pound (50-60 g), suggesting preference for 4-9 inch fish. Black hawks typically capture fish from shallow water (<12 inches depth) in riffles and runs by perching on branches or boulders and making feet-first strikes. Capture probability = 1.00 for depths ≤8 inches, dropping linearly to 0.00 at 12 inches based on leg length constraints.

Water quality issues include heavy metals (copper, zinc) from mining activities affecting Boulder and Burro creeks. Significantly lower hatching and fledging success observed at black hawk nests downstream from [[Boulder Creek]] mouth. Only 2 nesting territories found downstream vs. 15 pairs upstream. Recent improvements in mine water containment should reduce heavy metal concentrations.

Thermal springs (82-84°F) maintain elevated water temperatures in [[Francis Creek]] downstream from pumping station and at Upper Burro Creek site. Midday temperatures reached 75-80°F on March 24-25, 1981. Elevated winter temperatures may have contributed to poor sucker year classes in 1981 by affecting egg development or spawning cues.

## Key Findings

**Hydraulic Simulation Results:**
- Study required modification between 1981-1982 field seasons due to streambed movement and low flows
- Final analysis used Water Surface Profile (WSP) program instead of originally planned IFG4 program
- Three study sites established: Francis Creek (separated into Pool and Riffle sections), Upper Burro Creek, Lower Burro Creek
- Francis Creek: 17% pool habitat, 83% riffle habitat based on aerial photographs
- Measured discharge June 1982: ~3 ft³/sec at Francis Creek and Upper Burro Creek, 0.6-1.3 ft³/sec at Lower Burro Creek
- Lower Burro Creek becomes intermittent during low-flow periods between Boulder Creek mouth and Sixmile Crossing

**Weighted Usable Area (WUA) Analysis:**

*Francis Creek:*
- Pool section WUA 3-10 times greater than riffle section for all species
- Riffle section: lowest WUA at 5 ft³/sec; increased 24-33% at 3 ft³/sec (though simulation reliability questionable at 1-3 ft³/sec)
- WUA increased slowly from 5 to 25 ft³/sec for all species
- Combined WUA (weighted 83% riffle, 17% pool) increased continuously from 5-25 ft³/sec, with rate decreasing above 10 ft³/sec
- At 5 ft³/sec: Roundtail chub ~9,000 ft²/1000 ft stream; Sonora sucker ~6,500 ft²/1000 ft; Desert sucker ~7,500 ft²/1000 ft
- At 25 ft³/sec: Roundtail chub ~16,000 ft²/1000 ft; Sonora sucker ~11,000 ft²/1000 ft; Desert sucker ~13,000 ft²/1000 ft

*Upper Burro Creek:*
- WUA 3-4 times greater than Francis Creek at similar discharges
- Approximately 2-fold linear increase in WUA from 4 to 25 ft³/sec for all species
- Large WUA values even at lowest discharges indicate species well-adapted to low flows
- 10 ft³/sec flow would increase WUA only 30-50% over 4 ft³/sec
- Black hawk prey availability analysis: "adjusted WUA" (accounting for <12 inch depth constraint) substantially lower than total WUA but still substantial at low flows
- Adjusted WUA increases slowly up to 20 ft³/sec, suggesting prey availability varies little over 4-25 ft³/sec range
- Decreasing flows may actually enhance fish vulnerability to black hawks by concentrating prey in shallow water

*Lower Burro Creek:*
- WUA increased very slowly from 1 to 25 ft³/sec (competing effects of increased depth and velocity)
- Rate of increase much less than upper sites
- Complete dewatering of transects observed August 1981
- General absence of fish >6 inches suggests reduced survival/growth during 1980-1981 low flows
- Periodic summer dewatering reduces fish abundance and black hawk prey availability

**Habitat Preferences:**
- All three species show no apparent preference for specific substrate types; all substrates weighted as probability = 1.00
- Species tolerate shallow depths and low velocities associated with summer low flows
- Shallow water with slow currents important for Sonora sucker, especially juveniles
- Juvenile desert suckers prefer shallow riffles/runs with moderate velocities (up to 2.0 ft/sec) and larger substrates for periphyton growth
- Adult desert suckers may prefer deeper, swifter water but use shallow boulder-strewn riffles/runs extensively during low-flow periods
- Large deep pools (up to 12 feet) provide refuge during low flows but may be poor black hawk foraging habitat

**Management Implications:**
- Maintenance of flow in riffles and runs critical for fish availability to black hawks
- Pools >1 foot depth have limited value as black hawk foraging habitat based on observational data
- Voluntary curtailment of pumping during June-August low-flow periods could significantly benefit aquatic resources
- Augmentation of 3-4 ft³/sec existing flow by 4-5 ft³/sec (via stopping pumping) could substantially enhance riffle/run depths
- Any additional water withdrawals from upper drainage should be prohibited
- Flow >4 ft³/sec required to maintain perennial flow in lower Burro Creek during August
- Summer flows >5 ft³/sec occurred only a few days during June-August 1981-1982

**Recommendations:**
1. Install continuous recording flow meter on CBCC pipeline to determine actual withdrawal volumes and seasonal patterns
2. Request CBCC evaluation of feasibility for curtailing Francis Creek pumping during summer low-flow periods; compare economics of Francis Creek pumping vs. well pumping
3. Monitor black hawk fledging success at selected nests over 3-5 years with varying streamflow; estimate year-class strength and standing crop of leopard frogs and fish at five fixed stations
4. Request water rights for minimum streamflow: 5 ft³/sec in Francis Creek and upper Burro Creek; 10 ft³/sec in Burro Creek from Francis Creek to Boulder Creek mouths; or establish permanent moratorium on additional water rights upstream from gaging station
5. Evaluate impact of fall-spring temperature and discharge regime on sucker reproductive success before recommending winter pumping reductions (elevated thermal spring temperatures may affect spawning)
6. Determine relationship between March-July discharge and black hawk fledging success across different habitat types
