# Effects of Interim Flows from Glen Canyon Dam on the Aquatic Resources of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Phase I report from BIO/WEST, Inc. (Report TR-354-01, December 1994) evaluates how interim flow releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] affect fishes, macroinvertebrates, water quality and habitat in the lower [[Colorado River]] from Diamond Creek (RM 226) to below Pearce Ferry (RM 286) in [[Lake Mead]]. Sampling was carried out on seven trips between June 1992 and December 1993, administered by the [[Hualapai Natural Resources Department]] with technical support from BIO/WEST. The study area lies about 240 river miles below the dam.

The preliminary conclusion is that interim flows alone do not appear to detrimentally impact aquatic resources in the area. The authors instead attribute the effects to the combined influence of fluctuating flows, cold releases, absence of spring runoff and, especially, [[Nonnative Fishes]]. Downstream of [[Bridge Canyon]], the ecosystem is dominated by lake-influenced and nonnative species.

## Background & Description

The study was designed to integrate with earlier [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) fishery work from Lees Ferry to Diamond Creek. Before [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in 1963, the mainstem supported eight native fish species. By the time of this study, [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Bonytail]] had been extirpated from Grand Canyon. [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are federally endangered.

Interim flow criteria, in effect since August 1, 1991, limit releases to 5,000–20,000 cfs, with seasonally varying daily change limits and ramping rates. The study area was divided into two geomorphic reaches (Reach 11 and Reach 12, following [[Schmidt and Graf]] 1988) and four longitudinal sampling strata (A–D). Stratum A is a steep canyon from Diamond Creek to Bridge Canyon, Stratum B is a narrow canyon to Quartermaster Canyon, Stratum C is a braided lake-deposit channel, and Stratum D is an open alluvial delta at the Lake Mead inflow.

Field methods included electrofishing, gill and trammel nets, hoop nets, minnow traps, seines, angling, snorkeling, drift nets for macroinvertebrates, and three-pass removal estimates in [[Spencer Canyon]]. A total of 41 people took part in the seven trips, and 1,266 fish sampling efforts were recorded in the mainstem and 214 in tributaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Flow at the [[Diamond Creek Gage]] ranged from about 8,000 to 15,000 cfs, with lows usually between 2:00 and 4:00 am and peaks between 11:00 am and 1:00 pm. Maximum daily stage change was about 60 cm, and the largest multi-day change was about 90 cm over 3 days (October 2–4, 1993). Stage changes were damped by [[Lake Mead]] below about Quartermaster Canyon.

**Water quality.** Mainstem temperature varied by about 3°C longitudinally in June 1992 (17°C at Travertine Canyon to 20°C at Grand Wash Cliffs), and reached 24.5–26.0°C at Pearce Ferry because of lake impoundment. Winter temperatures were around 8.6–10.2°C. Dissolved oxygen was near saturation (11.1–11.9 mg/l in December 1992). Secchi depths ranged from 0.03 m (March 1993) to 0.90 m (October 1993), indicating high turbidity. [[Spencer Creek]] was warmer (about 14–29°C) and showed strong diel temperature and oxygen cycles.

**Macroinvertebrates.** Mainstem drift was dominated by [[Chironomidae]] and [[Simuliidae]]. Drift densities in [[Spencer Creek]] reached 14,614 per 100 m³ in May–June 1993, compared with 362 per 100 m³ maximum in the mainstem. Mainstem invertebrates were largely polyvoltine, unlike the univoltine taxa typical of temperate streams.

**Thermal and sediment context.** Cold hypolimnetic releases limit productivity throughout Grand Canyon. Below Bridge Canyon, [[Sediment Deposits]] and lake-level changes dominate habitat change more than interim flows do.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** 19 fish species in 10 families were captured. Only four are native to the basin: humpback chub, speckled dace, flannelmouth sucker and bluehead sucker.
- **Nonnative dominance:** Nonnative species made up about 93% of the 17,909 fish recorded in the study (Table 8). The red shiner was the most abundant (61.3%), followed by [[Fathead Minnow]] (18.8%) and common carp (6.9%).
- **Endangered species:** Razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish were not captured. Only one humpback chub was taken, at RM 253.2.
- **Bridge Canyon break:** Species richness rose from 11 to 18 downstream of Bridge Canyon. Red shiners were absent upstream of Bridge Canyon Rapid, and their dispersal appears limited by swift currents and sparse tributary habitat.
- **Tributary reproduction:** Young flannelmouth suckers (25 YOY) and speckled dace were found in Spencer and Surprise creeks, suggesting successful native reproduction there. Of 158 flannelmouth suckers, 95 were found in tributaries.
- **Spencer Creek fish barrier:** A series of falls about 2 miles upstream of the outflow stopped upward movement. Only speckled dace occurred above them.
- **Population estimates:** In Spencer Creek, red shiner and speckled dace densities rose from May to October 1993. At Site 2, October red shiner density was about 220 fish per 100 m².
- **Recommended introduction site:** Spencer Creek above the falls was identified as a candidate release site for young [[Razorback Sucker]], because nonnative predators are absent there.
- **Interim-flow effects:** Interim flows alone were judged not to be a detrimental factor. The authors note that the dynamic Lake Mead inflow complicates isolating flow effects, and that native fish numbers were too low to assess flow effects directly.
- **Stage and access:** Daily stage changes of 40–60 cm in canyon reaches may temporarily alter shoreline habitat, but no direct link to fish distribution was found.
- **Recommendations:** Continued surveys from National Canyon to Pearce Ferry; habitat and camera monitoring of tributary inflows; GIS mapping; quantitative surveys of Spencer and Surprise canyons; spawning surveys; water quality monitoring at river-lake interfaces; parasite sampling for the Asian tapeworm; and a risk assessment of nonnative invasion.
