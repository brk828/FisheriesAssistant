# Food and Growth of Spottail Shiners and Other Forage Fishes of Clear Lake, Iowa

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by [[Bernard Griswold]] (Iowa State University) examines the forage fish community of [[Clear Lake]], a shallow eutrophic lake in north-central Iowa. Forage species were sampled by night seining in summer 1961. [[Spottail Shiner]] (*Notropis hudsonius*) dominated the forage catch, while [[Tadpole Madtom]] (*Noturus gyrinus*) was the second most abundant species. The study covers species composition, habitat association, spawning timing, growth in relation to temperature, food habits, and the role of forage fishes in predator diets. Growth data are compared with the earlier [[McCann 1959]] study of the same lake.

## Background & Description

Clear Lake (Cerro Gordo County) is a shallow eutrophic lake supporting heavy sport fishing. The [[Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]] had studied its fish populations since 1941. Earlier work by [[Bailey and Harrison 1945]] found that minnows and other forage species were relatively scarce and contributed less to game fish diets than young game and panfishes.

Key methods:

- **Seining:** A 30-foot bag seine with one-quarter-inch mesh was used at eight predetermined shore stations, sampled once a week for eight weeks beginning July 21, 1961. Each station was sampled with two 50-foot hauls per period, starting at sundown. Sampling began at a random station and rotated around the lake so each station served as a starting point. Late evening and night seining was found most efficient, following [[Ridenhour 1960]].
- **Supplementary gear:** Occasional specimens were taken by trawl or electroshocking, and daytime seining in early summer was less productive and was discontinued.
- **Age and growth:** Scales were impressed onto plastic slides with a roller (following [[Smith 1954]]), read on a microprojector at 42 diameters, and read twice. A third reading resolved conflicts. Annuli were identified by circuli crowding and anastomosis.
- **Food habits:** Stomachs were split, contents examined under a dissecting microscope, and microscopic organisms checked with a compound microscope. Subsampling was used when many fish from one location had similar contents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Community composition.** Forage species made up only 5.6 per cent of all minnow seine catches in 1961. Young pan and game fish made up the other 94.4 per cent. Of the forage species, 71.6 per cent were spottails and 18.4 per cent were madtoms. The earlier study by [[McCann 1959]] reported spottails at 4.7 and 8.5 per cent of catches in 1956 and 1957.

**Habitat association.** Spottail catches were highest at stations with dense [[Submergent Vegetation]] extending to 5 feet or deeper. The two least productive stations were the Outlet, which had large rocks up to 2 feet across, and the Island Station, which had a very sandy bottom. Both lacked vegetation. Similar patterns were reported by [[Surber 1940]] in the Shenandoah River and by several other workers.

**Temperature and growth.** Growth was tied to temperature, as shown by [[McCann 1959]]. Mean May 1 to July 24 air temperature was 65.3°F in 1961, intermediate between 66.1°F (1956) and 65.1°F (1957), and higher than 63.0°F (1958). Young spottails averaged between the 1956 and 1957 sizes.

**Food resources.** All species initially fed mainly on [[Cladocera]] and plant material. Insects became dominant after mid-summer.

## Key Findings

### Species composition and abundance

- Total forage species counted: 744 individuals (Table 1), with the following leading entries:
  - Spottail shiner: 534 (71.6%)
  - Tadpole madtom: 137 (18.4%); the highest madtom share in any year reported
  - Bigmouth shiner: 34
  - Common shiner: 15
  - Golden shiner: 11
  - Johnny darter: 9
  - Bluntnose minnow: 2
  - Central stoneroller and rosyface shiner: 1 each
- Madtom abundance indicated a good 1961 year class.

### Spawning and recruitment

- Spawning was mostly in late May to early June. Females taken on June 15 had enlarged, crystalline eggs apparently being resorbed.
- Two gravid females were collected on August 3, and many small young-of-the-year (YOY) spottails were caught in the weeks of August 22 and September 6. This suggests a smaller, later hatch in early August, a second spawning period not previously recorded.
- Gravid female madtoms were collected July 15 and 17.
- YOY spottails made up 90 per cent of spottails collected, compared with 80 per cent in 1956 and 47 per cent in 1957.

### Growth

- The abstract gives the average growth rate as 0.05 millimeters per day. The body text gives the average daily increment from July 24 to August 28 as 0.55 mm in 1961. The 0.55 mm value matches Table 2, so the abstract figure is likely a typographical error.
- Compared with other years, the 1961 increment was lower than 0.63 mm (1956), 0.68 mm (1957), and 0.65 mm (1958), consistent with the lower August temperature (71.0°F versus 80.3°F in 1957, though slightly above 70.0°F in 1956).
- Mean total length of spottails July 21–27 was 43.0 mm in 1961, compared with 43.9 mm (1956), 41.1 mm (1957), and 36.6 mm (1958).
- Age analysis of 448 spottails (July 21–September 13) gave age-group composition of about 90% age 0, 6% age I, 3% age II, and 1% older. Only a few fish were about 3 years old. Sizes at each age were similar to those in 1956 and 1957.

### Food habits

- All species fed mainly on Cladocera and vegetable matter until mid-summer, when insects became abundant and made up most of the diet afterwards.
- Spottails were individually selective, but the population as a whole was less selective. Fish caught early in the nightly seining period had full stomachs, indicating a feeding peak just before sundown.
- [[Bigmouth Shiner]] showed little selectivity. The [[Johnny Darter]] fed almost entirely on Diptera larvae early in the season, with insects almost 100 per cent of its diet later.
- YOY made up 79 per cent of bigmouth shiners and 90 per cent of johnny darters. Neither species included individuals of age II or older.

### Parasites and health

- No parasites were observed on spottails. One fish had a hard growth behind the dorsal fin, probably from injury.
- For comparison, [[Haley and Winn 1959]] reported copepod parasites (*Lernaea cyprinacea*) on 20.8 per cent of spottails in a Maryland pond.

### Forage role

- Earlier reports cited in the paper show spottails and other forage fish as prey for smallmouth bass ([[Surber 1939]], [[Surber 1940]]), walleye ([[Maloney and Johnson 1957]]: spottails 3%, johnny darters 4% of diet), lake trout ([[Van Oosten and Deason 1937]]), channel catfish ([[Bailey and Harrison 1948]]), and young northern pike ([[Hunt and Carbine 1951]]).
- Of 150 walleye stomachs examined from Clear Lake in summer 1961, 57 contained fish, mostly unidentified. None showed evidence of eating spottails or the other forage species reported here.
