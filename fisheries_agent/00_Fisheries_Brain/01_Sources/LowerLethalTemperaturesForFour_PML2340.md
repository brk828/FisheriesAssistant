# Lower Lethal Temperatures for Fourteen Non-Native Fishes in Florida

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This controlled laboratory study identified the lower lethal and sublethal temperatures of 14 non-native fishes established in [[Florida]]. The authors, working at the Non-Native Fish Research Laboratory of the [[Florida Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission]], argue that low temperature is the main factor limiting the potential range of these exotic species. Fish were held in a semi-closed [[Aquarium Experiment|aquarium system]] while water temperature was lowered gradually, and the temperatures at which feeding declined, feeding stopped, equilibrium was lost and death occurred were recorded. The results indicate that temperature is less limiting than previously assumed, so the authors expect [[Range Expansion]] across much of the state, particularly for [[Tilapia]] species.

## Background & Description

Fourteen of the 15 non-native fishes then established in Florida are native to the tropical zones of Africa, Asia, and South and Central America. Several of these species have spread quickly, dominated certain lakes and canals, and shown the capacity to alter aquatic ecosystems. Because Florida's waterways are largely unobstructed and interconnected, these fishes can disperse widely unless an environmental factor stops them. Low-temperature lethal data existed for only a few species, and most of those values came from incidental observations. The study therefore set out to measure [[Lower Lethal Temperature]] values under controlled conditions and use them to estimate potential ranges.

The species tested were:

- Cichlidae: [[Astronotus Ocellatus]], [[Cichlasoma]] spp. (C. cyanoguttatum, C. octofasciatum, C. bimaculatum, C. meeki, C. trimaculatum), [[Hemichromis Bimaculatus]], and [[Tilapia]] spp. (T. aurea, T. mossambica, T. mariae, T. melanotheron)
- [[Belonesox Belizanus]] (pike killifish)
- [[Clarias Batrachus]] (walking catfish)
- [[Hypostomus]] sp. (armored catfish)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Fish were collected from Florida populations with cast nets, seines, and electrofishing. Typically one adult, or 1 to 15 subadults or juveniles, were stocked per aquarium. Fish were acclimated for 7 to 10 days at their collection temperature. Temperature then fell by 1 °C per day in six of eight aquaria, while two control aquaria stayed constant. Uneaten food was counted daily, fish behavior was observed, and 10% of the water was exchanged daily. Dead fish were weighed, measured and examined for parasites and disease. Heaters and thermoregulators held temperatures within ±0.1 °C. The water came from the municipal supply after aeration to remove chlorine. Reported water chemistry (mg/L) included calcium 24.9–32.3, sulfate 16.0–19.0, chloride 39.5–55.0, nitrate nitrogen 0.15–0.22, and orthophosphate 0.04 to 0.05. Ammonia nitrogen ranged from 0.07 to 0.51 ppm and pH from 7.1 to 9.0.

**Statistical design.** The authors calculated that a sample size of six or more gives the true mean lower lethal temperature within ±1 °C 99% of the time, based on a maximum standard deviation below 0.9 across the 14 species.

**Factors affecting lethal temperature.** The authors note that acclimation temperature, cooling rate and salinity all influence lower lethal values. [[Brackish Water]] appears to lower these thresholds for tilapias. Diel temperature swings may reduce cold tolerance in restricted habitats but are less harmful in large systems where fish can move between temperatures.

**Habitat selection.** Tilapias seek the warmest available water when cold-stressed. [[Tilapia Aurea]] congregate in heated power plant effluents, and occupy warm shallows by day and deeper water at night in ponds. They also move into warm spring and deep-flow refuges.

**Climate model.** Mean monthly air temperatures can predict stream temperatures in Florida (Anderson 1975). The authors compared January air temperature isotherms with the experimental lethal values to estimate where winter stream temperatures remain suitable for each species.

## Key Findings

**Lower lethal temperatures (mean, °C), from the synopsis:**

| Species | Mean lower lethal temperature (°C) |
|---|---|
| [[Astronotus Ocellatus]] | 12.9 |
| [[Hypostomus]] sp. | 11.2 |
| [[Belonesox Belizanus]] | 9.7 |
| [[Clarias Batrachus]] | 9.8 |
| [[Tilapia Melanotheron]] | 10.3 |
| [[Tilapia Aurea]] | 6.2 |
| [[Cichlasoma]] cyanoguttatum | 5.0 |

Across the 14 species, the mean lower lethal temperatures spanned roughly 5 to 13 °C. The authors concluded that these fishes tolerate lower temperatures than previously recognized.

**Key results:**

- Temperature is the chief limit on range, but it constrains these species less in Florida than was assumed.
- A [[Tilapia Aurea]] t-test showed a significant difference between juvenile (6.0 °C mean) and adult (6.5 °C mean) lower lethal temperatures (α = 0.01). Juvenile and adult values did not differ significantly for [[Astronotus Ocellatus]], [[Cichlasoma]] trimaculatum, or [[Tilapia Mariae]].
- Control fish stayed healthy except for one juvenile tilapia killed by intraspecific aggression. Post-mortem examinations showed no unusual anomalies or parasite infestations.
- Behavior and coloration changed consistently as temperature fell. Fish became darker and lost equilibrium abruptly. Species-specific signs included fin clamping, extended fins, head-down twirling, lethargic swimming, and red abdominal pigmentation. The heavy fin spines of [[Hypostomus]] turned from brown to orange-red at 12 °C.
- Brooding [[Tilapia Mossambica]] females continued to brood and sometimes stopped feeding at lower temperatures. One adult had a lower lethal temperature of 10 °C. Another adult's brood was 9 °C. Young from both females had lower lethal temperatures between 9 and 12 °C.

**Florida distribution implications:**

- Winter stream temperatures are suitable throughout Florida for [[Cichlasoma]] cyanoguttatum and [[Tilapia Aurea]].
- Suitable winter temperatures extend south of Jacksonville for [[Cichlasoma]] bimaculatum and C. octofasciatum.
- They extend south of Gainesville for [[Belonesox Belizanus]], C. meeki, [[Clarias Batrachus]], [[Hemichromis Bimaculatus]], and [[Tilapia Mossambica]].
- Tilapia aurea has been reported to cover over 75% of the state, and has overwintered in north Florida and southern Alabama.

**Cold-induced mortality in the field:** Winter kills have been documented only for [[Tilapia Aurea]], [[Clarias Batrachus]], and [[Hypostomus]] sp. Most occurred during the [[Cold Winter Mortality|coldest winter on record]] in January–February 1977. Populations recovered without measurable changes in density or range, which suggests these species are not currently limited by low temperature.

**Conclusions:** The authors conclude that a single universal lower lethal temperature cannot be derived from these data. Multiple lethal thresholds exist for each species, depending on thermal history and conditions. The brackish-water effect on tilapia lethal temperatures, together with Florida's warm springs and connected waterways, may allow further range extension. More suitable habitat exists for these fishes in Florida than was previously recognized.
