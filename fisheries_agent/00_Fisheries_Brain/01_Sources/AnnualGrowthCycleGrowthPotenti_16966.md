# Annual Growth Cycle, Growth Potential, and Growth Compensation in the Bluegill Sunfish in Northern Indiana Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Shelby D. Gerking]] (Department of Zoology, Indiana University) examines seasonal growth of the [[Bluegill Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis Macrochirus]]) in eight northern Indiana lakes during 1962 and 1963. Growth in all populations surged in spring, then declined steadily through late summer and autumn. Growth length was estimated from marginal scale growth in [[Scale Analysis]] of 11,463 samples. The [[Growing Season]] ranged from 98 to 189 days (mean 152 days) and was longer in faster-growing populations. Growth potential, measured as weight gain in the first month of the season, was similar across populations. The author proposes a new mechanism for [[Growth Compensation]] based on differences in the timing of [[Annulus Formation]] rather than hatching date, and discusses temperature, photoperiod and [[Growth Hormone]] as drivers of the annual cycle.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses three objectives: measuring the growing season of bluegills in eight lakes, quantifying variation among lakes, and testing whether annual growth rate depends on growing-season length. The work was motivated by [[Beckman]] (1943), who found that 85% of expected bluegill growth in Pasinski Pond, Michigan, was achieved by July, implying a production year of only about three months.

Study lakes were grouped by growth rate using lengths at the second and third annuli:

- **Rapid growth:** [[Sylvan Lake]], [[Carr Lake]], [[Maxinkuckee Lake]]
- **Intermediate growth:** [[Spear Lake]], High Lake
- **Slow growth:** [[Ridinger Lake]], [[Crooked Lake]], [[Pretty Lake]]

Sylvan Lake fish reached 123 mm fork length at the second annulus, versus 53 mm in Pretty Lake, a more than twofold difference. Each lake kept its growth-rank position in both years.

Methods were built on [[Hodgson's Method]] (marginal scale growth sampled through the year) and the [[Hile]] (1936) indirect approach. Fish were sampled from fishermen's catches, typically 100 or more scales per collecting period, at half-monthly intervals from June to September, with occasional spring, fall and winter collections. Scales were impressed into cellulose acetate and read by projection. Data were processed on an IBM 709 using two custom programs. Back-calculation used a 12 mm length intercept in the [[Lee Equation]], chosen from weighted means of the intercepts estimated in each lake (11.4 mm in 1962, 11.3 mm in 1963; 12.6 and 12.9 mm when Ridinger was excluded).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Latitude and climate.** Earlier studies linked growing season to latitude and temperature. [[Bennett]] (1937) estimated the growing season of largemouth bass at 9 months in Louisiana and 4–5 months in Wisconsin. [[Hubbs]] and Cooper (1935) related sunfish dwarfing in Michigan to temperature and frost-free days. The present paper argues that variation among lakes within one latitude band, not explained by climate, is also substantial.

**Lake characteristics.** Lakes ranged in area from 16 ha (Spear) to 668 ha (Maxinkuckee) and in maximum depth from 11 m (Sylvan) to 33 m (Crooked). Carr, Spear and Ridinger share a county and maximum depth, yet differ markedly in growth. Crooked Lake is described as mesotrophic, whereas Sylvan Lake is extremely eutrophic because of domestic pollution. Area was not correlated with growth rate or growing season.

**Temperature.** Mid-April surface temperatures were 7.8 °C in Crooked Lake and 11.1 °C in Sylvan Lake, yet annulus formation averaged May 3 and May 6, respectively. Crooked Lake warmed faster in spring (0.276 °C/day vs 0.110 °C/day in Sylvan). Growth ended in Crooked Lake at a higher water temperature (19.7 °C on October 5) than in Sylvan Lake (17.4 °C on October 8). Temperature therefore did not explain the timing of the growing season.

**Food.** Benthos and zooplankton, the bluegill's food, peak in spring in [[Wyland Lake]] (Gerking, 1962), consistent with the spring growth surge.

## Key Findings

**1. Seasonal growth pattern.** All populations showed a sharp spring rise from annulus formation, lasting 6 to 8 weeks, followed by a decline to low levels by September or October. Growth curves were individualistic and could not be described by one mathematical function. Growth of older age groups never exceeded that of younger groups in the same year.

**2. Timing of annulus formation.** Direct observation in Sylvan Lake (1963) showed the annulus appearing on group II fish between April 27 and May 11, with 50% of group II fish marked by May 18 (n = 309) and 50% of group III fish by June 5 (n = 68). Across 45 regression estimates, the mean first appearance was April 17. The earliest estimate was March 9 (Maxinkuckee, 1962), the latest May 19 (Sylvan, 1963). Six of eight lakes formed annuli earlier in 1962 than in 1963, with mean dates of April 8 (1962) and April 26 (1963).

**3. Length of the growing season.** The growing season, defined as the period until 90% of annual growth was achieved, averaged 152 days, ranging from 98 days (Ridinger, 1962) to 189 days (Maxinkuckee, 1962). The mean end dates were September 10 (1962) and September 27 (1963). Ridinger was the only lake showing a distinct year-to-year change (nearly one month). Spear, Crooked and Sylvan differed by no more than 11 days between years.

**4. Growing season and growth rate are linked.** Fast-growing lakes averaged 170 days, intermediate lakes 163 days, and slow-growing lakes 134 days. Fast-growing populations also began growth earlier (about April 15 vs April 27 for slow populations) and ended later (September 27 vs September 9).

**5. Equal growth potential.** Weight increments in the first month of the season were 12.6 g (rapid), 12.9 g (intermediate) and 13.9 g (slow). Ridinger Lake, with the lowest annual growth, had the largest first-month gain (16.7 g). The author concludes that differences in annual growth arise from environmental factors that determine how long growth continues, rather than from inherent differences in potential. Protein turnover mirrors this pattern: 37.6 g (rapid), 31.4 g (intermediate), 22.5 g (slow).

**6. Statistical limits of the method.** The regression method has broad 95% confidence limits, typically 3 to 7 weeks wide. Of 29 comparisons between age groups, 24 showed no statistically reliable difference in annulus timing. Of 21 year-to-year comparisons, two-thirds overlapped, though 62% of all comparisons were earlier in 1962. Only in Sylvan Lake were the spring data dense enough to resolve age effects on timing.

**7. Age effect on annulus formation.** The author cites chi-square evidence from [[Smith]] (1956) for lake herring: for age groups III and IV on May 26, 1948, chi-square = 5.93 (1 d.f., P < 0.001), indicating later annulus formation in older fish.

**8. Proposed mechanism for growth compensation.** Growth compensation, the tendency of slow-growing fish to catch up with faster-growing cohort members, is usually attributed to differences in hatching date. Here, smaller bluegills formed the annulus earlier, giving them a time advantage. In Sylvan Lake (1963), fish with an annulus were shorter than those without on May 11 (136.00 vs 144.33 mm, P = 0.015), May 30–31 (140.50 vs 154.88 mm, P = 0.005) and June 5–6 (154.22 vs 165.89 mm, P = 0.005). The model in [[Spear Lake]] (1960 year class) shows that a 3-week delay in annulus formation, if growing seasons end together, leaves a persistent gap of about 2 mm. The author states this explanation works only when the whole year class stops growing together and when the annulus timing difference is about one month.

**9. Growth compensation in bluegills.** Using [[Scott]] (1949) regression coefficients on Spear Lake age group IV: first-year vs first-to-second-year increments gave +0.5754 (P < 0.001), a depensation pattern. The second-to-third-year coefficient was -0.1736 (P = 0.200, not significant). First-year vs fourth-year increments gave -0.7278 (P = 0.001), a strong compensation. This matches the centrarchid pattern of second-year depensation followed by later compensation.

**10. Environmental drivers.** The author proposes that temperature and [[Photoperiod]] act together: spring growth results from rising temperature and day length, and autumn decline from falling values of both (Gross et al., 1965, in green sunfish). Work by [[Swift]] and [[Pickford]] (1965) on perch pituitary growth hormone found a July peak, with hormone levels falling to very low values in August while growth remained high. The author notes that these hypotheses require further testing.

**11. Heredity vs environment.** The author concludes that environment outweighs heredity in determining growth, based on equal growth potential across lakes. Inbreeding in isolated glacial lakes (about 12,000 years old) could not be fully excluded.

**12. Management implication.** Fish in neighboring lakes may grow for 4 to 8 months. Management can therefore influence production by controlling the length of the growing season, not only the spring growth rate.
