# Fishing in Twelve Michigan Lakes Under Experimental Regulations

#fisheries-management #regulations #creel-census #warm-water-species #pan-fish

## Executive Summary

This report documents a five-year experimental study (1946-1950) on twelve Michigan lakes testing liberalized fishing regulations for warm-water species, particularly pan fishes. The Michigan Legislature authorized experimental regulations on up to 20 lakes to evaluate effects of removing size limits, shortening closed seasons, and opening lakes to year-round fishing for species like [[Bluegill]], [[Crappie]], [[Yellow Perch]], and [[Northern Pike]]. Intensive creel census methods tracked angling pressure, catch rates, and species composition. Results showed that liberalized regulations did not cause overfishing or decline in fish populations. Spring fishing added only 2.7-31.5% to annual catches depending on lake category, and catch-per-hour rates remained stable or improved over the study period. These findings led to permanent liberalization of Michigan fishing laws, including removal of pan fish size limits (1949) and shortened closed seasons on bluegills and sunfish (1952).

## Background & Description

### Historical Context of Michigan Fishing Regulations

Michigan's fishing regulations evolved from early laws (1859) prohibiting seines and continuous nets in southern counties. Key regulatory milestones included:

- **1869**: [[Whitmore Lake]] closed to seines, spears, firearms; winter closed season established
- **1901**: First closed season on [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (April 1-May 20); creel limit of 50 fish for bass, sturgeon, and [[Muskellunge]]
- **1909**: Ten-inch size limit and 10-fish creel limit on black basses; seven-inch size limit and 20-fish limit on crappie and [[White Bass]]
- **1915**: Five-inch size limit on bluegills, sunfish, perch, crappies; six-inch limit on [[Rock Bass]], white bass, [[Black Crappie]]
- **1921**: Six-inch size limit on bluegills and sunfish (remained until 1949)
- **1929**: The Inland Fishing Law closed all non-designated lakes to fishing April 1-June 24
- **1945**: Amendment to Discretionary Power Act authorized experimental regulations on up to 20 lakes for five-year research periods

### Study Design and Lake Selection

The Conservation Commission designated twelve lakes in 1946 for experimental regulations, distributed across southern Michigan (eleven lakes) and one northern lake ([[Fife Lake]]). Lakes were selected based on:

- Prior population and growth rate data
- Variable fishing quality
- Existing biological surveys and mapping
- Public access availability
- Geographic distribution (one lake per county maximum)

**Three experimental categories:**

1. **Six lakes** open to year-round fishing for species not protected by closed seasons (interpreted as May 15 opening for pike and walleye): [[Craig Lake]] (Branch County, 122 acres), [[Duck Lake]] (Calhoun County, 630 acres), [[Fine Lake]] (Barry County, 320 acres), [[Lobdell Lake]] (Genesee County, 545 acres), [[Pontiac Lake]] (Oakland County, 585 acres), [[Whitmore Lake]] (Livingston/Washtenaw counties, 675 acres)

2. **Four lakes** open to year-round fishing for bluegills and sunfish (plus species not protected by closed seasons): [[Big Portage Lake]] (Jackson County, 360 acres), Fife Lake (Grand Traverse/Kalkaska counties, 575 acres), [[Minnewaukon Lake]] (St. Joseph County, 126 acres), [[Sugarloaf Lake]] (Washtenaw County, 180 acres)

3. **Two lakes** with no closed season on pan fish AND no creel limit on pan fish under six inches (to test removal of stunted populations): [[Bear Lake]] (Hillsdale County, 117 acres), [[Saddle Lake]] (Van Buren County, 271 acres)

### Census Methodology

**1946 census approach:**

- Six lakes (category 1): Two clerks contacted anglers at random, recording residence, bait type, fishing method, hours, and species caught; 25-100% daily coverage with proportional weighting
- Six lakes (categories 2-3): Official checking stations issued permits for "out-of-season" or undersized fish; clerks recorded all reporting anglers plus independent census of non-reporting anglers

**1947-1950 census approach:**

- Random angler contacts at trip completion
- Periodic boat counts (two-hour intervals) to estimate total fishing pressure
- Prearranged schedules for 2-3 lakes per clerk
- Winter "dark house" (shanty) fishermen supplied self-report forms (variable return rates: 1-95%)

**Seasonal definitions:**

- **Spring**: May 1-June 24 (56 days, 20% of fishing year)
- **Summer**: June 25-September 15 (83 days, 28% of year)
- **Fall**: September 16-November 30 (76 days, 26% of year)
- **Winter**: Ice-safe periods (approximately 77 days, 26% of year)

**Census intensity:**

- 1946: 31,548 angler trips contacted (~20% of estimated total)
- 1947-1950: Average 11,000 trips per year (~6% of estimated total)
- 1951 (post-experimental): 2,890 trips contacted

### Lake Characteristics

All study lakes shared common features:

- Agricultural land use in surrounding areas
- Boat liveries present
- Public access (state-owned or through liveries)
- Cottage and resort development (except Craig Lake)
- Connected to major river drainages ([[Grand River]], [[Kalamazoo River]], [[Huron River]], [[Manistee River]], [[St. Joseph River]], [[Saginaw River]], [[Clinton River]])

Specific lake features documented through inventory maps included bottom types (marl, sand, muck, fibrous peat), vegetation zones (emergent, floating, submerged), shore features (cottages, docks, springs, marshes), and bathymetric contours.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Species Composition and Abundance

**Six lakes without spring bluegill fishing** (five-year combined catch percentages):

- **Craig Lake**: 77% bluegill, 9.5% yellow perch, 4.3% crappie, 3.4% largemouth bass
- **Duck Lake**: 69.0% bluegill, 11.1% yellow perch, 6.5% pumpkinseed, 4.4% largemouth bass
- **Fine Lake**: 86.8% bluegill, 4.0% yellow perch, 3.2% largemouth bass
- **Lobdell Lake**: 67.8% bluegill, 14.8% yellow perch, 6.8% pumpkinseed, 6.2% crappie
- **Pontiac Lake**: 79.6% bluegill, 7.3% largemouth bass, 5.5% yellow perch, 4.8% crappie
- **Whitmore Lake**: 62.3% bluegill, 14.4% yellow perch, 9.1% crappie, 5.3% pumpkinseed

**Four lakes with spring bluegill fishing:**

- **Big Portage Lake**: 69.8% bluegill, 9.6% yellow perch, 6.0% rock bass, 5.0% largemouth bass
- **Fife Lake**: 49.6% bluegill, 12.0% [[Walleye]], 11.1% yellow perch, 10.3% yellow perch (mixed warm/cool water fishery)
- **Minnewaukon Lake**: 53.2% bluegill, 22.8% [[Bullhead]], 9.4% yellow perch, 6.5% pumpkinseed
- **Sugarloaf Lake**: 49.3% bluegill, 39.6% yellow perch, 5.4% pumpkinseed

**Two lakes with unlimited pan fish harvest:**

- **Bear Lake**: 67.7% bluegill (legal size), 13.8% yellow perch, 8.6% largemouth bass
- **Saddle Lake**: 74.0% bluegill (legal size), 12.6% yellow perch

### Productivity Metrics

**Catch per acre** (five-year averages, all fish):

- Highest: Minnewaukon Lake (170 fish/acre/year), Craig Lake (327 fish/acre/year for pan fish alone)
- Moderate: Fife Lake (63 fish/acre/year), Saddle Lake (245 fish/acre/year)
- Lower: Big Portage Lake (30 fish/acre/year), Duck Lake (85 fish/acre/year)

**Catch per hour** varied considerably by season and lake:

- Spring rates generally lower (0.2-2.7 fish/hour) due to pike fishing emphasis
- Summer rates highest and most consistent (0.5-2.2 fish/hour)
- Fall rates variable (0.2-3.5 fish/hour)
- Winter rates on Craig Lake very high (1.8-2.8 fish/hour) due to concentrated bluegill fishing

### Habitat and Water Quality

While detailed water chemistry was not reported, inventory maps documented:

- Bottom substrates: sand, marl, muck, fibrous peat, mixed compositions
- Aquatic vegetation: emergent (cattails, rushes), floating (water lilies), submerged macrophytes
- Depth ranges: most lakes had maximum depths 20-60 feet with extensive shoal areas
- Shoreline modifications: dams maintaining water levels (Lobdell, Pontiac lakes), outlets to river systems
- Encroaching vegetation and marsh areas noted on several lakes

### Reproductive Success

Seining surveys (1946 and 1950) on three lakes assessed young-of-year and yearling abundance:

**Big Portage Lake:**
- 1946: 3.5 bass and 11.2 pan fish per 100 feet of shoreline seined
- 1950: 3.1 bass and 6.1 pan fish per 100 feet

**Saddle Lake:**
- 1946: 2.9 bass and 21.6 pan fish per 100 feet
- 1950: 3.4 bass and 29.9 pan fish per 100 feet

**Sugarloaf Lake:**
- 1946: 3.3 bass and 18.5 pan fish per 100 feet
- 1950: 2.2 bass and 21.5 pan fish per 100 feet

Results indicated no adverse effect of spring fishing on bass and pan fish reproduction over the five-year experimental period.

## Key Findings

### Overall Fishing Quality Trends

**Twelve lakes combined** (catch per hour, 1946-1950): 0.9, 1.2, 1.1, 0.9, 0.9 fish per hour. The pattern showed improvement from 1946 to 1947-1948, followed by decline to 1949-1950 levels equal to the starting point. Significantly, fishing quality in 1949-1950 equaled 1946, indicating no cumulative negative effect of liberalized regulations.

**1951 comparison** (post-experimental, standard regulations resumed): Catch per hour was 0.96 for the twelve lakes combined during summer-fall-winter, virtually identical to the 1946-1950 average, confirming that experimental regulations had not depleted fish populations.

### Six Lakes Open to Year-Round Fishing (Species Not Protected by Closed Seasons)

**Fishing pressure and seasonal distribution:**

- Spring fishing comprised only 6.7% of total annual fishing trips (average 9,070 trips)
- Spring catch was only 2.6% of annual total
- Summer fishing dominated: 71.0% of trips, 72.4% of catch
- Fall fishing: 10.1% of trips, 7.2% of catch
- Winter fishing: 12.2% of trips, 17.8% of catch

**Catch per hour by season** (five-year averages):

- Spring: 0.39 fish/hour (lowest due to pike fishing emphasis)
- Summer: 1.03 fish/hour (highest and most consistent)
- Fall: 0.86 fish/hour
- Winter: 1.68 fish/hour (inflated by Craig Lake bluegill fishing)

**Annual trends:** Catch per hour values were 0.98, 1.14, 1.16, 0.88, 0.86 for 1946-1950. The decline from 1948 to 1950 occurred on only four of six lakes and was followed by recovery to 0.96 in 1951, indicating temporary rather than regulation-induced decline.

**Key conclusion:** Opening lakes to spring fishing increased fishing days by 24% but increased catch by only 2.7%, demonstrating minimal impact on fish populations.

### Six Lakes Open to Spring Bluegill and Sunfish Fishing

**Fishing pressure and seasonal distribution:**

- Spring fishing comprised 21.6% of annual trips (average 9,970 trips vs. 36,118 for remainder of year)
- Spring catch was 23.9% of annual total (average 32,024 fish)
- Spring harvest represented 31.5% increase over summer-fall-winter catch

**Annual catch per hour** (legal-size fish, 1946-1950): 0.81, 0.87, 0.88, 1.04, 0.94 fish per hour. The trend showed improvement rather than decline over the five years.

**1951 comparison:** Summer-fall-winter catch in 1951 (107,425 fish) was virtually identical to 1946-1950 average (101,792 fish) for the same seasons, with equal fishing pressure and catch per hour (0.94 vs. 0.91). This demonstrated that anglers did not compensate for lack of spring fishing by taking more fish in other seasons.

**Species composition stability:** Game fish (particularly basses) maintained consistent percentages in catches over five years, indicating successful reproduction despite spring fishing disturbance.

**Key conclusion:** Spring bluegill fishing added substantially to annual harvest (31.5%) without depleting populations or reducing fishing quality in subsequent years.

### Two Lakes with Unlimited Pan Fish Harvest (Under Six Inches)

**Bear Lake:**

- Catch per hour (legal fish) remained stable 1946-1949 (0.62-0.79) with decline in 1950 (0.42)
- Sublegal pan fish catch per hour showed no decline: 0.20, 0.25, 0.18, 0.30, 0.35 for 1946-1950
- Recovery in 1951 (0.61) and 1952 (0.77) demonstrated 1950 decline was temporary, not regulation-induced
- Annual catch of legal fish: average 12,780 (1946-1949), dropped to 4,880 (1950), recovered to 8,620 (1951) and 13,900 (1952)

**Saddle Lake:**

- Maintained constant ratio of six legal fish to seven sublegal fish over five years
- Catch per hour (legal fish) stable: 1.16, 0.90, 0.86, 1.02, 1.14 for 1946-1950
- Annual catch averaged 29,780 legal fish with no declining trend

**Key conclusion:** Angling was not an effective method for reducing stunted pan fish populations. The unlimited harvest provision did not significantly alter population structure or fishing quality.

### Comparison with State-Wide General Creel Census

Catch per hour on experimental lakes (0.9-1.2 fish/hour, 1946-1950) was consistently lower than Conservation Department Region III general creel census (1.2-1.7 fish/hour). This difference likely reflected:

- Possible bias in general census toward more successful anglers
- Representative nature of experimental lakes (not selected for high productivity)
- More rigorous census methodology on experimental lakes

The experimental lakes were considered generally representative of southern Michigan warm-water lake productivity.

### Factors Affecting Fishing Pressure

**Angling intensity declined 1948-1950** on Pontiac, Lobdell, and Whitmore lakes, attributed to:

- Increased business activity and decreased industrial strikes/layoffs in metropolitan Detroit area
- Reduced fishing time availability for urban anglers
- Correlation between fishing quality and pressure (anglers responded to quality changes)

**1947 showed elevated pressure** possibly due to:

- Release of thousands of men from armed forces post-World War II
- Novelty of experimental regulations attracting anglers

### Seining Evidence for Reproductive Success

Three lakes (Big Portage, Saddle, Sugarloaf) seined in 1946 and 1950 showed:

- Young-of-year and yearling bass abundance remained stable or increased
- Pan fish young remained abundant
- No evidence of spawning disruption from spring fishing

**Conclusion:** Spring fishing did not adversely affect reproduction of bass or pan fish over the five-year experimental period.

### Legislative Outcomes

Based on study results, Michigan Legislature enacted permanent liberalizations:

1. **September 1949**: Removed all size limits on pan fish
2. **1951**: Added lakes in southern Lower Peninsula to year-round fishing for species not protected by closed seasons
3. **1952**: Shortened closed season on bluegills and sunfish to April 1 through last Saturday in April (from previous February 1-June 24)

### Management Implications

The study provided evidence supporting several management principles:

1. **Pan fish populations are resilient** to increased harvest and do not require protection on spawning beds
2. **Size limits on pan fish are unnecessary** and may be counterproductive in lakes with stunted populations
3. **Closed seasons can be shortened** without depleting fish populations
4. **Angling pressure is self-regulating** through correlation with fishing quality
5. **Spring fishing adds to annual harvest** without reducing summer-fall-winter catches
6. **Natural reproduction is robust** and not significantly disrupted by angling during spawning
