# Proposed Rule to List Spikedace as Threatened Species

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #conservation-policy #endangered-species-act

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed on June 18, 1985, to list [[Meda fulgida]] (spikedace) as a threatened species under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended, and to designate critical habitat. This small cyprinid fish, endemic to the [[Gila River]] system upstream from Phoenix, Arizona, has experienced catastrophic range reduction from approximately 2,600 kilometers historically to only 154 kilometers (6% of historic range) at the time of proposal. The species persists in three primary locations: [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona), sections of the upper [[Gila River]] (Grant and Catron Counties, New Mexico), and the upper [[Verde River]] (Yavapai County, Arizona). Primary threats include habitat destruction from damming, water diversion, channel alteration, and competition/predation from exotic fish species, particularly [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner). The proposal includes a special rule allowing take under state permits for educational, scientific, and conservation purposes, and incidental take during recreational fishing if fish are immediately returned.

## Background & Description

[[Meda fulgida]] was first collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro in Arizona and described by Girard in 1856. The species is a small fish (less than 75 millimeters total length), characterized by very silvery sides and spines in the dorsal and pelvic fins. Breeding males develop a brassy golden color. The species is slim-bodied and adapted to flowing water habitats.

The spikedace inhabits moderate to large perennial streams in shallow riffles with gravel and rubble substrates and moderate to swift currents, as well as swift pools over sand or gravel substrates. Recurrent flooding is critically important in the life history of [[Meda fulgida]] and helps maintain its competitive advantage over invading exotic fish species.

Historically, [[Meda fulgida]] was common throughout much of the [[Verde River]], [[Agua Fria River]], [[Salt River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[San Francisco River]], and [[Gila River]] (upstream from Phoenix) systems, occupying habitat in both mainstreams and moderate gradient perennial tributaries up to 1,800-1,900 meters elevation. The historic range encompassed approximately 2,600 kilometers of river habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The native fish fauna of the [[Gila River]] system has been drastically affected by human alteration, with 35% of species federally listed as endangered at the time of this proposal, and another 35% considered threatened or endangered by Arizona and New Mexico states and/or the American Fisheries Society.

**Current Distribution (1985):**
- [[Aravaipa Creek]]: approximately 24 kilometers
- Upper [[Gila River]] (New Mexico): approximately 73 kilometers in three sections (Middle Box Canyon, Cliff-Gila Valley, lower portions of West and Middle Forks)
- [[Verde River]] (Arizona): approximately 57 kilometers from below Sullivan Lake downstream to just below Sycamore Canyon mouth
- Total occupied range: 154 kilometers (6% of historic 2,600 kilometers)

**Extirpation Timeline:**
- [[Salt River]] drainage: last found 1972
- [[San Pedro River]] drainage (except [[Aravaipa Creek]]): last found 1967
- [[Agua Fria River]] drainage: last found 1943
- [[San Francisco River]] drainage: last found 1950
- [[Gila River]] downstream from Red Rock, New Mexico: scattered individuals found as late as 1984, but no permanent populations since 1951
- [[East Fork]] of [[Gila River]]: extirpated between 1978-1984
- Upper [[San Pedro River]] (Sonora, Mexico): extirpated due to dewatering

A 1978 study by Anderson documented that the range of [[Meda fulgida]] had receded 25 kilometers upstream in the [[Gila River]] over 28 years. A 1983-1984 study by the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish documented a further 40% loss in range since 1978, including complete loss from the [[East Fork]] and an additional 10-kilometer recession upstream from Red Rock to the mouth of Middle Box Canyon.

**Land Ownership of Occupied Habitat:**

*Aravaipa Creek:*
- Bureau of Land Management: ~75% (mostly designated [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]])
- Defenders of Wildlife: most perennial stream above/below Wilderness ([[George Whittell Wildlife Preserve]])
- Private: scattered parcels

*Gila River:*
- U.S. Forest Service: large portion in [[Gila National Forest]], including sections in [[Gila Wilderness]], [[Lower Gila River Bird Habitat Management Area]], and [[Gila River Research Natural Area]]
- New Mexico Department of Game and Fish: approximately 61.5 kilometers
- Bureau of Land Management: approximately 4.5 kilometers (including Area of Critical Environmental Concern)
- The Nature Conservancy: small portion upstream from town of Gila
- Private: most of Cliff-Gila Valley and near Gila Hot Springs

*Verde River:*
- U.S. Forest Service: [[Prescott National Forest]]
- Private: interspersed inholdings and lands below Sullivan Lake
- State of Arizona: approximately 3.5 kilometers scattered parcels below Sullivan Lake

**Native and Exotic Fish Communities:**

Native predators include [[Gila robusta]] (roundtail chub). Exotic predatory and competitive species present in occupied habitats include:
- [[Lepomis cyanellus]] (green sunfish)
- [[Ameiurus melas]] and [[Ameiurus natalis]] (black and yellow bullhead)
- [[Ictalurus punctatus]] (channel catfish)
- [[Pylodictis olivaris]] (flathead catfish)
- [[Micropterus dolomieu]] and [[Micropterus salmoides]] (smallmouth and largemouth bass)
- [[Oncorhynchus mykiss]] and [[Salmo trutta]] (rainbow and brown trout)
- [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner) - particularly problematic competitor

The downstream limit of [[Meda fulgida]] in the [[Gila River]] closely corresponds to increasing abundance of flathead and channel catfish. Near lakes where game fish are heavily stocked, [[Meda fulgida]] populations are depleted. The recent extirpation from the [[East Fork]] is attributed to increased numbers of smallmouth bass and catfish.

## Key Findings

**Threats Analysis (Section 4(a)(1) Factors):**

**Factor A - Habitat Destruction/Modification:**
The majority of historic habitat has been drastically altered or destroyed through:
- Conversion of flowing to still waters by impoundment
- Alteration of flow regimes (perennial to intermittent flow, reduction/elimination of natural flooding)
- Water temperature alterations
- Changes in silt and bed loads
- Channel transformation from well-defined, vegetated channels with diverse substrate to deeply cut, unstable arroyos with little riparian vegetation and uniform substrate
- Loss of marshes and backwaters

Causes include damming, water diversion, channel downcutting, excessive groundwater pumping, lowering water tables, channelization, riparian vegetation destruction, erosion, mining, and grazing.

**Specific Habitat Losses:**
- [[San Pedro River]]: now severely downcut with only intermittent flow
- Lower [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]]: limited or no flow during portions of year due to agricultural diversion and upstream impoundments
- [[Gila River]] below Mogollon Mountains: affected by agricultural/industrial diversion, impoundment, channelization, and chemical fish management
- [[San Francisco River]]: erosion, extensive water diversion, undependable water supply

**Ongoing and Future Threats:**

*Aravaipa Creek:* Relatively protected due to [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]] and [[George Whittell Wildlife Preserve]] status, but affected by upstream groundwater pumping that could eventually reduce perennial flow.

*Gila River:* Portions in [[Gila Wilderness]] and [[Gila River Research Natural Area]] have use restrictions, but still affected by watershed uses and water diversion. Private lands lack statutory habitat protection. Agricultural use, water diversion, and flood control measures have heavy impacts.

**Critical Threat - Proposed Dam Construction:**
The Bureau of Reclamation's Central Arizona Project Upper Gila Water Supply Study proposes four alternatives:
1. High dam at Conner site: would inundate up to 29 kilometers (40% of existing [[Gila River]] range), alter downstream habitat through changed temperature, bedload, and flow regimes, eliminate natural flooding
2. Small dam at Conner site with offstream storage: would inundate approximately 14 kilometers
3. Direct pumping to offstream storage: effects include fish entrapment, impingement on screens, stream flow depletion
4. Floodplain storage basins: would require removal of 484 acres riparian vegetation, eliminate 18 kilometers aquatic habitat through construction and channelization, increase sediments, alter temperature and flow regimes downstream

Historical dam construction on [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], and [[Gila River]] resulted in complete extirpation of [[Meda fulgida]] downstream and up to 65 kilometers above reservoirs.

*Verde River:* Threatened by watershed disturbances, increasing silt, deteriorating water quality from upstream communities, and future water developments. Central Arizona Project plans include water rights exchanges and diversions. City of Prescott and Yavapai-Prescott Indian Reservation propose infiltration gallery 4 kilometers below Sullivan Lake with maximum diversion of 13 cubic feet per second from joint allocation of 7,627 acre-feet per year. Average median monthly discharge near diversion point estimated at 10 cubic feet per second or less for 10 months of year. Such reduction could cause crowding, increased predation/competition, increased water temperatures, and other negative impacts.

**Factor B - Overutilization:** No threat from overutilization known to exist.

**Factor C - Disease or Predation:**
Historically not significant, but introduction of exotic predatory fish has increased predation's role. In [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Gila robusta]] (native) and [[Lepomis cyanellus]] (exotic) are potential predators, but neither has significant effect. In [[Gila River]] and [[Verde River]], multiple exotic predators present.

Predation becomes important under altered habitat conditions. The downstream limit of [[Meda fulgida]] in [[Gila River]] corresponds to increasing catfish abundance. Near heavily stocked lakes, [[Meda fulgida]] populations are depleted. In 1983-1984, [[East Fork]] had abundant smallmouth bass and catfish but few native species, correlating with [[Meda fulgida]] extirpation.

Dam and reservoir construction exacerbates predation by increasing habitat for exotic predators, decreasing suitable [[Meda fulgida]] habitat, and supplying predators from reservoir fisheries.

**Factor D - Inadequacy of Existing Regulatory Mechanisms:**

*New Mexico:* Listed as endangered species, Group 2, under Wildlife Conservation Act. Prohibits taking except under scientific collecting permit. Provides no habitat protection.

*Arizona:* Listed as threatened species, Group 3. Listing provides no special protection. Regulations prohibit taking except by angling (unlikely capture method). Provides no habitat protection.

Neither state provides instream water rights protection for fish and wildlife habitat (Arizona only recently recognized such rights). New Mexico regulations allow use of [[Notropis lutrensis]] and other live minnows as bait in [[Gila River]] areas containing [[Meda fulgida]], encouraging spread of detrimental exotic species.

**Factor E - Other Natural or Manmade Factors:**
Continued introduction and dispersal of exotic species, particularly [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner), throughout [[Gila River]] system. The spread of exotic species correlates closely with declining numbers and distribution of [[Meda fulgida]] and other native species. [[Notropis lutrensis]] now occupies much of former [[Meda fulgida]] habitat.

In suitable unaltered habitat, [[Meda fulgida]] may hold its own against [[Notropis lutrensis]] invasion. However, in extensively altered habitats where [[Meda fulgida]] populations are stressed, [[Notropis lutrensis]] has competitive advantage and replaces [[Meda fulgida]].

Major displacement factor appears to be disturbance of natural flooding patterns. Native species like [[Meda fulgida]] are adapted to and thrive under frequent moderate to severe flooding regime; [[Notropis lutrensis]] and other exotics do not. Controlled flow from impoundment interrupts natural pattern and encourages [[Notropis lutrensis]] spread. Reservoirs increase likelihood and rapidity of exotic spread by supplying ready source from reservoir fisheries.

**Current Distribution of Notropis lutrensis:**
- Not found in [[Aravaipa Creek]]
- Found in [[Verde River]] along with [[Meda fulgida]]
- Found in upper [[Gila River]] as far upstream as Cliff, New Mexico (not present in 1978)

**Proposed Critical Habitat Designation:**

1. [[Aravaipa Creek]], Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona: perennial stream portion (approximately 24 kilometers)
2. [[Verde River]], Yavapai County, Arizona: approximately 57 kilometers from 0.8 kilometers below [[Sycamore Creek]] confluence upstream to Sullivan Lake
3. [[Sycamore Creek]], Yavapai County, Arizona: approximately 1.5 kilometers near confluence with [[Verde River]]
4. [[Gila River]], Grant and Catron Counties, New Mexico: three sections totaling approximately 73 kilometers:
   - 50 kilometers from mouth of Middle Box Canyon upstream to confluence with Mogollon Creek
   - 11.5 kilometers up West Fork from East Fork confluence to west boundary Section 22, T.12S., R.14W
   - 11.5 kilometers up Middle Fork from mouth to confluence with Big Bear Canyon

**Constituent Elements of Critical Habitat:**
- Permanent water with moderate to swift velocity
- Depth of at least 10 centimeters
- Sand, gravel, and rubble substrate
- Both pool and riffle components

**Activities That Could Adversely Modify Critical Habitat:**
- Excessive groundwater pumping
- Impoundment
- Water diversions
- Channelization
- Excessive sedimentation from mining, grazing, watershed disturbances
- Deprivation of substrate source
- Riparian destruction
- Release of chemical or biological pollutants
- Introduction of exotic predatory and competitive fish species

**Conservation Status and Recognition:**

- Listed as species of concern by Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife (1973)
- Listed as rare and possibly endangered by American Fisheries Society and Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists (1972)
- Listed as threatened by American Fisheries Society Endangered Species Committee (1979)
- Listed as vulnerable by International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources Red Data Book Vol. 4 (1977)
- Listed as threatened/endangered by Arizona and New Mexico
- Included in USFWS December 30, 1982 Vertebrate Notice of Review, Category 1
- Petitioned for listing by American Fisheries Society (March 14, 1985) and Desert Fishes Council (March 18, 1985)

Land acquired for protection by The Nature Conservancy (upper [[Gila River]]) and Defenders of Wildlife ([[Aravaipa Creek]]).

**Proposed Special Rule (Section 4(d)):**
Given that [[Meda fulgida]] is threatened primarily by habitat disturbance rather than direct taking or commercialization, and that states currently regulate taking through collecting permits, a special rule is proposed allowing take without Federal permit if:
1. State collection permit obtained and all state wildlife laws satisfied, for: educational purposes, scientific purposes, enhancement of propagation/survival, zoological exhibition, other conservation purposes consistent with ESA
2. Incidental take by state-licensed recreational fishermen, provided individual fish immediately returned to habitat

This special rule acknowledges state permit systems are adequate to protect species from excessive taking and that incidental recreational take is not significant threat. Any other taking activities remain prohibited.

**Federal Actions Requiring Section 7 Consultation:**
- Bureau of Reclamation dam construction or water projects (Upper Gila Water Supply Study, Central Arizona Project Verde River diversions)
- Changes in USFS grazing, mining, timbering, recreational activities
- Future flood control work by Federal Emergency Management Agency or U.S. Army Corps of Engineers in Cliff-Gila Valley
- Federally funded irrigation, pipeline, water diversion, and land leveling projects on private agricultural lands

**Rationale for Threatened vs. Endangered Status:**
Species still locally abundant throughout approximately 154 kilometers of stream, so not in immediate danger of extinction (does not meet endangered definition). However, drastic loss of range (94% reduction) and imminent threats to all major portions of presently occupied range warrant threatened status.
