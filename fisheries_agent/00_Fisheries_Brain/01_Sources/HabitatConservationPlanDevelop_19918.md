# Habitat Conservation Plan Development in the Aquatic Environment: An Overview of the Federal and California Endangered Species Acts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species-act

## Executive Summary

This course presentation, given by Susan K. Hori of Paone Callahan McHolm & Winton LLP at the 16th Annual Water Law Conference (February 20, 1998, San Diego, California), summarizes the [[Endangered Species Act]] (federal) and the [[California Endangered Species Act]] (state). It explains how listing, [[Critical Habitat]] designation, federal agency consultation, the take prohibition, and the [[Habitat Conservation Plan]] (HCP) permit process operate. The document is a legal overview rather than a research study. It contains no species-level data, population counts, or field measurements. Its main points are the legal mechanisms, the 1982 creation of incidental take permits, and 1997 changes to California's take authorization rules.

## Background & Description

**Federal Act.** Congress first legislated on endangered species in 1966 and amended that law in 1969. The major revisions came with the federal Act of 1973. Its stated purpose is to protect endangered and threatened plants and wildlife ("species") through three tools:

1. Listing species on an official list maintained by the Department of the Interior.
2. Protecting listed species by establishing critical habitats.
3. Regulating federal agencies through required consultation with the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] (FWS) or the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS).

The Act is organized into four operative sections:

- **Section 4 (16 U.S.C. § 1533):** Listing of species.
- **Section 7 (16 U.S.C. § 1536):** Federal agency consultation.
- **Section 9 (16 U.S.C. § 1538):** Prohibition on taking listed species.
- **Section 10 (16 U.S.C. § 1539):** Permits for taking species or their habitat.

The "Secretary" is the Secretary of the Interior or the Secretary of Commerce, depending on which agency (FWS or NMFS) has jurisdiction over the species.

**State Act.** California enacted its own Endangered Species Act to protect endangered, threatened, and candidate species. The State Fish and Game Commission maintains the state list. The state list does not exactly match the federal list: some federally listed species are not on the state list, and some state-listed species are not on the federal list.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses aquatic and marine conservation only in general legal terms. It refers to:

- The **territorial sea of the United States** and the **high seas** as places where the Section 9 take prohibition applies.
- **Section 404 permits** (Clean Water Act) as a common trigger for federal consultation, since private applicants for these permits must consult with FWS or NMFS.
- **Federal lands** such as [[Bureau Of Land Management]] leases and easements, which require consultation before agency action.

Threats the Secretary considers when listing include habitat destruction, modification, or curtailment; overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific, or educational purposes; disease or predation; inadequate regulatory mechanisms; and other natural or manmade factors. The source does not describe any specific species, habitat, or water body.

## Key Findings

**1. Listing (Section 4)**
- Anyone may petition FWS to list a species. FWS has 90 days (to the maximum extent practicable) to determine whether the petition presents substantial information that listing is warranted.
- Proposed rules are subject to notice-and-comment rulemaking, with a 60-day public comment period and at least one hearing (50 C.F.R. § 424.16(c)(2)-(3)).
- [[Emergency Listing]] is allowed when there is a significant risk to the well-being of a species. Emergency listings take effect immediately and last 240 days (50 C.F.R. § 424.20).
- The Act requires [[Recovery Plan]]s for listed species.

**2. Federal Agency Consultation (Section 7)**
- Federal agencies must ensure that any action they authorize, fund, or carry out is not likely to jeopardize a listed species or adversely modify its critical habitat.
- The acting agency first prepares a [[Biological Assessment]] to determine whether a listed or proposed species is present in the action area.
- FWS or NMFS then issues a [[Biological Opinion]]. A [[Jeopardy Opinion]] that identifies no reasonable and prudent measures to minimize impacts means the activity cannot proceed without an exemption from the Endangered Species Committee.
- Section 7 is triggered by private applicants for federal permits, by state or local projects that use federal funds or grants, and by activities on or affecting federal lands.

**3. Take Prohibition (Section 9)**
- [[Section 9 Take Prohibition]] bars any action that results in taking a listed species in U.S. waters, the territorial sea, or the high seas. "Take" is defined broadly to include harassing, harming, wounding, killing, trapping, or capturing, and attempts to do so.
- Unlike Section 7, Section 9 applies to any person, not only federal agencies.

**4. Incidental Take Permits and HCPs (Section 10)**
- Before 1982, the Act only provided permits for take for scientific or educational purposes. Landowners with listed species on their property and no federal nexus had no route to develop it.
- The 1982 amendment added [[Section 10 Incidental Take Permit]]s for take incidental to an otherwise lawful activity, conditioned on a [[Habitat Conservation Plan]].
- An HCP must describe: (a) the likely impact of the taking; (b) steps to minimize and mitigate impacts and the funding for them; (c) alternatives considered and why they were rejected; and (d) other measures the Secretary requires.
- HCPs must be made available for public review and comment. Unlike listing decisions, HCPs are subject to the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA), as held in [[Friends Of Endangered Species V. Jantzen]], 760 F.2d 976 (9th Cir. 1985).
- Before approving a permit, the Secretary must find that: the take is incidental; impacts are minimized and mitigated to the maximum extent practicable; adequate funding will be provided; the take will not appreciably reduce the likelihood of survival and recovery in the wild; and HCP measures will be met. Other assurances may also be required. Regulations are at 50 C.F.R. § 13.1 et seq.

**5. California Take Authorization (Sections 2080, 2081, 2080.1)**
- [[Section 2080 Take Prohibition]] forbids taking, possessing, purchasing, or selling state-listed endangered or threatened species.
- Until 1997, the state act had no equivalent of the federal incidental take permit. The [[Department Of Fish And Game]] (DFG) had interpreted the "management purposes" clause of [[Section 2081 Permit]] to authorize incidental take. In [[Planning And Conservation League V. Department Of Fish And Game]], 54 Cal.App.4th 140 (modified 55 Cal.App.4th 479, 1997), the Court of Appeal found that interpretation inconsistent with the statute. The California Supreme Court granted review on June 18, 1997.
- In response, the Legislature amended Section 2081 to authorize permits for incidental take, provided that the take is incidental to a lawful activity, impacts are minimized and fully mitigated, the permit is consistent with state regulations, and the applicant assures funding for mitigation and monitoring.
- Under [[Assembly Bill 21]] (Olberg, 1997), [[Section 2080.1 Federal Hcp Coverage]] lets holders of a federal Section 10(a) permit or Section 7 incidental take statement avoid a separate state permit. They must notify the DFG Director and include a copy of the federal authorization. The Director has 30 days to determine whether the federal authorization is consistent with the state act. If it is not, state authorization is required.

**Overall conclusion:** The presentation frames HCPs as the main route for landowners and developers to proceed with lawful activities where listed species occur. It also shows that California's 1997 changes were a direct response to the court's limit on the "management purposes" authority, and that federal coverage can now be recognized under state law.
