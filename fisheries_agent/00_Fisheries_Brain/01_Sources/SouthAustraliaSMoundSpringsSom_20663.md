# South Australia's Mound Springs: Some Introductory Notes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mound-springs #artesian-ecosystems

> **Note on scope:** This source is a geological, historical, and conservation-focused introduction to [[South Australian Mound Springs]] and does not directly describe fish species, morphology, or fish-specific ecology. The note below synthesizes the aquatic habitat context relevant to any [[Mound Spring Endemic Fauna]] (e.g., endemic gudgeons and hardyheads known from other mound spring literature), while remaining faithful to what this text actually covers.

## Executive Summary

This paper by C.R. Harris provides an introductory overview of South Australia's mound springs, a system of natural artesian discharge points formed where waters of the [[Great Artesian Basin]] emerge along an arc of fractures and fault lines where crystalline basement rocks meet Jurassic and Cretaceous aquifers. These springs form geomorphic mounds/cones of varying composition (sands, clays, gypseous silts, limestone) and represent unique, ecologically fragile aquatic habitats in an otherwise arid landscape. The paper catalogs the distribution, physical/chemical characteristics, and discovery history of the springs, and outlines major conservation threats. While the source does not discuss fish species directly, mound springs are broadly recognized elsewhere in the literature as refugial habitats for isolated, often endemic, aquatic fauna due to their permanent water supply in arid Australia.

## Background & Description
*(Habitat/geomorphology characteristics, in lieu of organismal morphology, since no fish taxa are described in this text)*

- **Spring mounds (cones/domes):** Vary in composition — sands, clays, gypseous silts, and limestone.
- **Relief:** Ranges from ~1 m to 20–25 m of local relief above ground level.
- **Extinct spring mounds:** Can be very large (several km² in area), e.g., [[Mt Hamilton]] near [[Coward Springs]].
- **Flow rates:** Highly variable — from mere seepages up to 14.3 megalitres/day (recorded at [[Dalhousie Springs]]).
- **Water chemistry:** Almost all springs are alkaline and saline to varying degrees; high dissolved solids content (carbonates, sulphates, chlorides). Evaporative deposition of these solids is the primary driver of cone/mound formation.
- **Water temperature:** Ranges from 20–40°C within a given locality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Permanent, naturally occurring artesian water sources in an arid/semi-arid environment, forming isolated aquatic "island" ecosystems.
- **Distribution in South Australia:**
  1. [[Lake Frome]], [[Lake Callabonna]], and [[Lake Blanche]] area — relatively minor springs with small flows.
  2. **Marree–Peake area** — includes [[Coward Springs]], [[Hermit Hill]], [[Elizabeth Springs]], [[Jersey Springs]], [[Strangways Springs]], [[Hawker Springs]], and [[Loudon Spa]]; some remain large and important, while many are extinct or have declined significantly since European settlement.
  3. **[[Dalhousie Springs]]** — located 160 km northeast of Oodnadatta; the most significant spring grouping in Australia, comprising ~60 active springs across ~60 km².
- **Broader regional context:** Similar mound spring fields occur around Cloncurry, Hughenden, and Cunnamulla in Queensland, and north of Bourke and White Cliffs in New South Wales — all linked to the same [[Great Artesian Basin]] discharge arc.
- **Ecological fragility:** These springs support sparse but specialized plant cover and (per broader mound spring ecology) can harbor isolated aquatic communities due to their permanence in arid zones; this isolation is a key driver of potential endemism in associated fauna, though this specific paper does not enumerate species.
- **Historical/anthropogenic context:** Long used by Aboriginal peoples as permanent water sources (evidenced by surrounding campsites and occupational debris); later "stepping stones" for European exploration (Babbage and Warburton, 1858; McDouall Stuart, 1859) and subsequent pastoral development.

## Key Findings

- Mound springs are surface expressions of [[Great Artesian Basin]] groundwater escaping along basement rock fracture zones, forming a broad arc across Queensland, NSW, and South Australia.
- [[Dalhousie Springs]] represents the largest and most significant mound spring complex in Australia (~60 springs, ~60 km²), with flow rates up to 14.3 megalitres/day.
- Spring water chemistry (alkaline, saline, high dissolved solids) and temperature (20–40°C) vary considerably even within a single locality, which — combined with geographic isolation — creates highly specialized and variable aquatic microhabitats.
- The mound springs are of major scientific, historical, and archaeological value but are ecologically fragile, facing multiple compounding threats:
  1. Vehicle and trampling damage exacerbated by low rainfall and saline/gypseous soils (slow recovery).
  2. Uncontrolled visitor activity threatening archaeological/mythological sites and sparse vegetation.
  3. Reduced spring flow (in some cases to extinction) due to Great Artesian Basin aquifer exploitation.
  4. Uncontrolled stock access (domestic and feral) causing vegetation destruction, drainage line trampling, and siltation/fouling of pools and overflow channels.
- These conservation pressures directly threaten the permanence and water quality of habitats that, in comparable arid-zone mound spring systems, are known to sustain relictual and endemic aquatic fauna — underscoring the ecological stakes of the described threats, even though this text does not itself catalog fish species.
