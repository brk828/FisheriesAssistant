# Biological Opinion: Central Arizona Project Water Exchange, Upper Verde River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service [[Biological Opinion]] (May 1990) addresses the proposed [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) Water Exchange on the upper [[Verde River]], Yavapai County, Arizona. The threatened [[Spikedace]] (*[[Meda Fulgida]]*) is the species of concern. The request was submitted by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The Service concludes that implementation of either proposed diversion schedule is likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the spikedace. The opinion offers [[Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives]] that, if followed, would remove the jeopardy finding. Impacts to the [[Bald Eagle]] and the experimental nonessential population of [[Colorado Squawfish]] were deferred to later consultation.

The proposal is a joint diversion by the [[City of Prescott]] and the [[Yavapai-Prescott Indian Community]] totaling 7,627 acre-feet per year (af/yr). Two schedules were analyzed: [[Alternative 1]] (direct infiltration gallery near Sullivan Dam) and [[Alternative 2]] (near the Paulden gage).

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and size:** Spikedace is a monotypic genus endemic to the Gila River basin. It is a small silvery minnow reaching about 2.5 inches.
- **Range:** Its range has been reduced by about 94 percent. Remaining populations occur in the upper Gila River (New Mexico), [[Aravaipa Creek]], Eagle Creek, and the upper Verde River. The Verde population occupies about 36 miles, roughly one-third of known existing habitat.
- **Listing:** Listed as threatened on July 1, 1986. Critical habitat was proposed in 1985 and remained pending at the time of this opinion.
- **Biology:** Spikedace live about two years. Reproduction occurs mainly in one-year-old fish. Spawning runs from March through May and appears triggered near 19°C. Fish feed mainly on aquatic and terrestrial insects.
- **Habitat preferences:** Adults use water of about 0–3 ft/s at depths of 0.1–1.25 ft. Juveniles use slightly slower, deeper water. Larvae use the slowest, shallowest edges (0–1 ft/s, 0.1–1 ft deep).
- **Project background:** In 1983 the Secretary of the Interior allocated CAP water to 120 users, eight of them on the upper Verde. The Prescott allocation is 7,127 af/yr and the Yavapai-Prescott allocation is 500 af/yr.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Environmental baseline.** The upper Verde remains comparatively unimpacted, largely because of Prescott National Forest management. Six of the ten historically known native fish species persist there. Four native species have been eliminated from the river. Cumulative impacts include livestock grazing, mining, road and railroad construction, off-road vehicle use, groundwater pumping, and urban growth.

**Non-native fish.** Eight non-native species are reported in the spikedace range, including [[Red Shiner]], mosquitofish, carp, channel catfish, smallmouth bass, and flathead catfish. Native fish dominated upstream of Sycamore Creek (96 percent at Forest Road 638 in 1986). Downstream the balance is unstable. At Tapco, natives made up only 61 percent in 1986, but were more prevalent in 1988–89 after floods. The red shiner is considered the most serious threat, and the upper Verde is the only known site where it and spikedace have coexisted for more than a few years.

**Hydrology.** Base flow is sustained by spring discharge and runs about 20–30 cfs for roughly 85 percent of the year at the Paulden gage. Floods are episodic, with a maximum recorded peak of 15,700 cfs. The 2-year flood at Paulden is 1,638 cfs and the 100-year flood is 29,102 cfs. Flow is gained downstream, and bedrock constriction below Perkinsville substantially increases discharge.

**Diversion facility.** Water would be withdrawn by an infiltration gallery (about 100 × 100 ft; roughly 300–500 linear feet of riverbed disturbed). Sheet piling would partially block subsurface flow. Construction would widen the channel where wetted width is about 12 ft (Alternative 1) or 30 ft (Alternative 2), causing turbidity, sedimentation, and riparian clearing. Periodic backwashing would add sediment.

**Historic low flows.** The minimum recorded low at Paulden was 15 cfs (May 1964), lasting 11 days. At Clarkdale the minimum was 55 cfs (August–September 1920). Records span only about 23–27 years, a period of relatively high rainfall, so drought flows lower than recorded minima are plausible.

**Genetics and recovery.** The upper Verde population is the only remaining representative of the northern historic range, and under the draft [[Spikedace Recovery Plan]] it is the only appropriate source stock for northern reintroductions.

## Key Findings

**1. Jeopardy conclusion.** The Service found that either alternative is likely to jeopardize the spikedace. Both would also adversely modify proposed critical habitat. The conference report from April 14, 1986 is amended accordingly.

**2. Flow reductions (monthly medians).**
- *Alternative 1:* reductions range from 92 percent (June, at the diversion site) to 12 percent (March, at Sycamore Creek). Reductions average about one-half of the median across the upper 27–29 miles and about one-sixth in the lower 7 miles.
- *Alternative 2:* no reduction occurs March–June or above the diversion. July–February reductions average about 57 percent over 19 miles and about 23 percent in the lower 7 miles. Monthly reductions range from 62 percent (Paulden, February) to 20 percent (Sycamore Creek, August).

**3. Low flows and drought.** Under Alternative 1 at drought flows, the diversion could eliminate all summer flow at the point of diversion for four months. Total loss would extend about 6 miles. At maximum capacity, total dewatering could reach about 16 miles, or 44 percent of spikedace habitat. Alternative 2 uses a guaranteed minimum (10–15 cfs) and would never allow flow below 10 cfs or natural flow, whichever is less.

**4. Flow variability.** The coefficient of variation of monthly medians would rise sharply:
- Alternative 1: 3.7% to 45% at the upper site, 3.4% to 22% at Paulden, and 1.5% to 6.2% at Sycamore Creek.
- Alternative 2: 3.4% to 46% at Paulden and 1.5% to 14% at Sycamore Creek.

**5. Flood flows.** Neither alternative is expected to significantly change existing flood flows. Floods are, however, essential for channel maintenance, habitat rejuvenation, and controlling non-native fish. Reduced flood flows could cause aggradation and extirpation.

**6. Spawning and larval impacts.** Alternative 1 diverts heavily in spring, the spawning and larval period. Alternative 2 avoids March–May diversion but takes 62 percent of February median flow, which may disrupt spawning cues. Larval fish in shallow edges are especially vulnerable to entrainment and thermal stress.

**7. Non-native competition.** Diversion is expected to favor [[Red Shiner]] over spikedace, which is a habitat specialist. Red shiners are pioneers that spawn across most of the year, while spikedace spawn only in spring. Drought combined with non-native predators was blamed for losses of spikedace in the San Francisco River.

**8. Isolation.** The nearest connected population is in Aravaipa Creek, several hundred river miles away. Losing the upper Verde population would make the species highly precarious. Two consecutive project-amplified drought summers could eliminate two age classes and extirpate the population.

**9. IFIM rejected.** The [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] study (1985–87) was judged not valid for this species. Reviewers cited its linearity assumption, its four equal habitat variables, the absence of interspecific interactions, and small sample sizes (n=50). Their professional judgment was used instead.

**10. Cumulative effects.** The population of the Prescott water service area is projected to grow 46 percent between 1990 and 2010. This would increase sediment, pollution, and recreation pressure, raising extirpation risk from stochastic events.

**11. Reasonable and prudent alternatives.**
- Acquisition of existing water rights near the point of use.
- Development of groundwater in the Big Chino Valley, which lies outside the Prescott Active Management Area and would require studies of connection to Verde surface flow.
- Separate development of the Yavapai-Prescott allocation from other basins or headwater tributaries.
- Water conservation and recycling. Prescott's use fell from 141 gallons per capita per day (1980) to 129 gpcd with recharge.

Rejected options: capturing flood flows for offstream storage (no feasible site, and flood flows are essential), and diversion below Sycamore Creek (high biological value and bald eagle habitat). The Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act report recommended no diversion from the Verde.

**12. Incidental take.** No incidental take is anticipated or authorized. Reclamation must notify the Service of its decision on the alternatives. Reinitiation of consultation is required if the project changes or new information emerges.
