# Life Histories of Peamouth and Northern Squawfish in the Big Blackfoot River Drainage, Montana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by Clifford W. Hill, Jr., published in the *Proceedings of the Montana Academy of Sciences*, documents the life histories of two abundant native cyprinids, [[Peamouth]] and [[Northern Squawfish]], in the [[Big Blackfoot River]] drainage of western Montana. Field work was carried out in the summers of 1956 and 1957 as part of a fisheries management program. The study covers distribution and habitat, growth (from scale analysis and length-frequency data), reproduction (fecundity, age at maturity, spawning season), and food habits (stomach analysis). Both species are classed as rough fish that compete with game fish and, in the case of squawfish, prey on young salmon. The study found that the two species grow at different rates, mature at different ages, spawn at different times, and feed mainly on insects, with fish a minor dietary item.

## Background & Description

**Rationale.** The author notes that both species grow to the size of many game fish but are little used as food and are generally considered rough fish. They may compete with trout and salmon for food and space. The Northern squawfish was previously reported as an important predator on young [[Sockeye Salmon]] (Ricker, 1941; Foerster and Ricker, 1941). Both species take bait readily and are regarded as a nuisance by anglers.

**Study area.** The Big Blackfoot River and its tributaries drain about 2,100 square miles of portions of Lewis and Clark, Powell, and Missoula counties. The highest point is about 9,000 feet, and the river's confluence with the [[Clark Fork]] (Columbia River system) lies at 3,300 feet. The average gradient is about 11 feet per mile. The [[Clearwater River]] is the largest tributary, a 32-mile narrow wooded valley that flows through eight lakes ranging from 25 to 1,200 acres. Lake depths reach 90 to 100 feet, with abrupt shorelines. Shoal bottoms are gravel or rubble on steep slopes and sand or silt on gradual slopes. Water is moderately soft, with total alkalinity of 12 to 20 ppm. Deeper lakes stratify thermally and chemically in summer, but oxygen depletion is not severe.

**Associated fauna.** Other native fishes include [[Redside Shiner]], [[Longnose Dace]], longnose and largescale suckers, [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Dolly Varden]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], and [[Mottled Sculpin]]. Introduced species include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Kokanee]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Pumpkinseed]]. Three hybrids between Northern squawfish and Peamouth were collected, consistent with an earlier Montana report by Weisel (1953, cited as 1955 in the literature list).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Sampling methods varied by habitat: [[Gill Netting]] in lakes, dynamiting on the Big Blackfoot River, angling on the lower Clearwater River, [[Rotenone Sampling]] on the upper Clearwater above Rainy Lake, and [[Electrofishing]] at other stream stations. Peamouth were abundant in certain Clearwater lakes and occurred in small numbers in slow reaches of the Clearwater and the Big Blackfoot below their confluence. Northern squawfish occurred in the same lakes, in [[Cooper's Lake]] (which drains intermittently to the North Fork Big Blackfoot River, though the species was absent from that river), and in the lower Clearwater and lower Big Blackfoot. Fish from Cooper's Lake were darker in pigmentation than those from other areas. Upstream on the Big Blackfoot, only large squawfish (11.3 to 15.7 inches total length above Arrastra Creek) were collected. No young-of-the-year were found more than 15 miles above the Clearwater mouth, so the author concluded that little or no successful reproduction occurs in the upper part of the species' range.

**Habitat use.** In lakes during summer, both species occurred almost exclusively in shallow water, with few catches below 20 feet. They favored submerged vegetation except during spawning. Under ice cover at [[Seeley Lake]] in late March 1957, both species were captured in vegetation at 8 feet or less, possibly indicating they stayed in shallow vegetated areas through winter. Young-of-the-year were in water under one foot deep along rubble or gravel shores before July 8, then moved to submerged vegetation at depths under three feet after August 1. Northern squawfish in the Big Blackfoot occupied large pools (8 to 12 feet deep, up to 100 feet long). In the Clearwater they used pools and low-gradient areas, and juveniles under six inches clustered among boulders in riffles.

## Key Findings

**Growth**
- **Peamouth, Placid Lake:** Scales from 300 fish (1956) were analyzed. Young-of-the-year averaged 2.2 inches by September 12, 1957. Calculated mean lengths at annulus formation (ages I to IX) were 2.6, 4.6, 6.7, 8.3, 9.6, 10.6, 11.5, 12.6, and 13.5 inches. Females grew slightly faster and lived longer; they averaged 0.2 inches longer than males at age five and 0.7 inches longer at age seven. The oldest female (13.5 inches) was nine years old.
- **Northern squawfish, Alva Lake:** Scales from 267 fish (summers 1956 and 1957) covered age classes IV to XV. Mean lengths at annulus formation were 2.1, 3.3, 4.6, 5.9, 7.2, 8.4, 9.5, 10.5, 11.5, 12.3, 13.4, and 14.4 inches. Females grew faster and reached greater age; the difference was 0.7 inches at the ninth annulus. The oldest male was 11 years, and one 18.3-inch female was 15 years.
- **Northern squawfish, Seeley Lake:** Scales from 72 fish showed growth slightly greater than at Alva Lake, with age classes VI to XV. A 17.4-inch female was 15 years old.
- **Length-frequency analysis:** The Clearwater River sample (127 fish, July and August 1956) showed modes at 2.4, 3.3, 4.6, and 6.1 inches, corresponding to age classes I to IV. The 3.3-inch mode was weak because that size was poorly vulnerable to both collection methods.
- **Comparison:** Taft and Murphy (1950) reported faster growth and larger size for the Sacramento squawfish in California.

**Reproduction**
- **Fecundity (March 1957, Seeley Lake):** Seven Peamouth (11.8 to 12.8 inches) carried an estimated 11,800 to 18,900 eggs. Seven Northern squawfish (11.3 to 13.8 inches) carried 6,700 to 27,500 eggs, showing much greater individual variation. No relationship between fish size and egg number was found within either species' size range.
- **Age at maturity:** Peamouth at Placid Lake matured at three to five years. About 20 percent (18 of 94) of age class III were mature males, and mature females first appeared in age class IV. Northern squawfish at Alva Lake matured later and over a longer period, with the youngest mature male at six years and the youngest mature female at seven.
- **Spawning season (1957):** Peamouth at Placid Lake began spawning around May 24 or 25, at about 54°F, with peak activity between roughly May 28 and June 9. On May 28, 39 percent (12 of 31) of females were spent, and spawned females were concentrated near the outlet, which has gravel or rubble shoals. Northern squawfish at Seeley Lake began spawning around June 1, at about 58°F. Their season was later and less abrupt: 15 percent (3 of 20) of breeding females were spent on June 3 to 5, rising to 86 percent (24 of 28) by June 26.
- **Non-breeding squawfish:** About 25 percent (29 of 117) of female Northern squawfish at Seeley Lake in June 1957 had green, non-ripening ovaries. These fish were found throughout both summers and likely retained their eggs for one or more years. This condition was not seen in Peamouth.
- **Interannual variation:** Spawning in 1956 was about two weeks later than in 1957, matching weather station data showing 1957 temperatures about two weeks ahead of 1956.
- **Spawning observations:** A school of several hundred adult squawfish was observed at Cooper's Lake on June 18, 1957, over a rubble bottom near shore, but no actual spawning was seen. Published accounts describe spawning on rubble at 2 to 12 inches depth (Jeppson, 1957) and a similar gravel-rubble site for Peamouth (Schultz, 1935).

**Food habits**
- **Peamouth (42 fish, Placid and Rainy lakes, August and September 1957):** Insects made up 51 percent of stomach contents. Adult ants (26 percent) were the most important item, from ants falling onto lake surfaces during August swarms. Cladocerans contributed 20 percent and snails 6 percent. Plant material occurred in only one stomach.
- **Northern squawfish (32 fish, Placid and Rainy lakes, August and September 1957):** Insects made up 92 percent of contents. Adult ants (23 percent) occurred in half the stomachs, and water boatmen (Hemiptera) contributed 20 percent.
- **Northern squawfish, Alva Lake (8 fish, June 1956):** Fish remains occurred in four stomachs, including Northern squawfish and Yellow Perch.
- **Northern squawfish, lower Clearwater (43 fish, mid-summer):** Insects made up 76 percent of contents, with mayfly nymphs contributing 25 percent. Fish remains (cyprinids) occurred in only two stomachs.
- **Comparison:** Ricker (1941) found Northern squawfish over 100 mm at Cultus Lake fed largely on fish except in early summer. Chapman and Quistorff (1938) reported fish in 24 percent of 37 stomachs. The author concluded that fish are a minor summer food item in the Big Blackfoot drainage.

**Hybrids**
- Three Northern squawfish × Peamouth hybrids were collected, and none were sexually developed.

**Methods (summary)**
- Gill nets measured 125 feet long with 25-foot sections of 3/4- to 2-inch square mesh. Young-of-the-year were collected with dip nets and seines. Scales were read with a projector, and calculated lengths were derived with a nomograph under a linear scale-to-length assumption. Ovary volumes were estimated by water displacement, with egg counts from 5 to 10 percent subsamples.
