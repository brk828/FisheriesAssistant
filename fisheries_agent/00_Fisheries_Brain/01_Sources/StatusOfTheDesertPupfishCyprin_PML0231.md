# Status of the Desert Pupfish (Cyprinodon macularius) in California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 California Department of Fish and Game special publication by Glenn F. Black assesses the status of the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), a species once widespread across the [[Colorado River]] and springs of the Salton Sink. Quarterly surveys of the [[Salton Sea]] and its tributaries (March 1978 to January 1979), plus two surveys of [[San Felipe Creek]], found that pupfish have declined sharply. Exotic fishes, especially the [[Sailfin Molly]], dominate most habitats. The author concludes that the species qualifies for listing as endangered under State and Federal law, and recommends protective actions including habitat acquisition, hydrological study, and exotic species eradication.

## Background & Description

The desert pupfish is a member of the killifish family (Cyprinodontidae), endemic to backwaters, sloughs, springs and seeps of the Gila River drainage in Arizona, the Sonoyta River drainage in Sonora, Mexico, and the lower Colorado River drainage (including the Salton Sink) of California and Baja California. Several isolated populations are considered separate subspecies. The Monkey Springs population in Arizona is probably extinct.

The species was first reported from California in 1859 by Girard, from unnamed saline springs in Imperial County. Recorded California localities include the Colorado River, Figtree John Spring, the Salton Sea, Fish Spring, Dos Palmas Spring, San Felipe Creek, and artesian wells near Mecca, Thermal, and Indio. Of these, the Colorado River population, Dos Palmas Spring, Fish Spring, Figtree John Spring, and the artesian wells are now lost, leaving the Salton Sea tributaries, including San Felipe Creek, as the only natural California habitats.

Declines are linked to (1) introduction of exotic predators and competitors, (2) habitat modification by water diversion, and (3) groundwater pumping for agriculture. The Salton Sea is not a natural water body; it was formed and maintained by human activities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salton Sea habitats sampled:**
- **Agricultural irrigation drains:** 18 drains, widths 1 to 5 m, with variable depth, clarity and salinity.
- **Shoreline pools:** 2 pools, 5 to 61 cm deep, with salinities up to three times that of the Sea, and daily temperature swings of 15 to 20°C in summer.
- **Natural tributaries:** Salt Creek, Whitewater River and Whitefield Creek.
- **Salton Sea proper:** 13 areas, with salinity of 36 to 38 ppt.
- **San Felipe Creek:** a spring-fed, intermittent tributary with 7.2 km of perennial flow and total dissolved solids of 8,000 to 10,000 ppm.

**Exotic and introduced species:** The [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*) was first found in irrigation drains about 23 years before the study. Other introductions include the [[Shortfin Molly]], the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), the porthole fish (*Poeciliopsis gracilis*), the red shiner (*Notropis lutrensis*), the [[Zill's Cichlid]] (*Tilapia zillii*) and the Mozambique mouthbrooder (*Sarotherodon mossambicus*). Tilapia were introduced in the late 1960s by local irrigation districts to control aquatic weeds and have been stocked annually since.

**Native associates:** Common natives included the [[Longjaw Mudsucker]] (*Gillichthys mirabilis*), the Gulf croaker and the orangemouth corvina (*Cynoscion xanthulus*). The [[Red Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*) was the only invertebrate recorded in the drains.

## Key Findings

**Survey results (quarterly, March 1978 to January 1979):**
- Total catch: 16,048 fish and invertebrates, of which 13,693 (85%) were sailfin mollies.
- Desert pupfish: 324 individuals captured, about 2% of the total catch.
- Irrigation drains (18 drains, 65 traps across all habitats): 13 fish species and 1 invertebrate species. Sailfin mollies made up 85% of the drain catch, African cichlids 5%, and pupfish 3%.
- Shoreline pools: 5 fish species. Sailfin mollies were 81% of the catch and longjaw mudsuckers 13%. Pupfish contributed 5%.
- Natural tributaries: 9 fish species and 1 invertebrate species. Sailfin mollies were 70% of the catch and red shiners 9%.
- Salton Sea proper: 6 fish species. Sailfin mollies were 87% of the catch and juvenile orangemouth corvina 7%.
- Pupfish were most abundant in the drains during summer (91 individuals) and least abundant in spring (nine).
- In the drains, predator and competitor ratios to pupfish were as high as 204:1 (spring) and 104:1 (fall), and pupfish were outnumbered wherever they were captured, with two exceptions.

**San Felipe Creek (fall and winter 1978–1979):**
- Desert pupfish made up 70% of the 421 fish captured, the only location where pupfish dominated.
- Sailfin mollies were 26%, mosquitofish 3%, and shortfin mollies 1%.
- Predator/competitor ratios to pupfish ranged from 1:4 to 12:1 across creek sections.

**Refugia:** Five refugial populations were established between 1970 and 1979 at Palm Canyon, Living Desert Reserve, Arrowweed Springs, Palm Springs and the Visitor Center. Estimated numbers were about 1,000 at Palm Canyon, 100 at Living Desert, 30 at Arrowweed Springs, 200 at Palm Springs and 200 at the Visitor Center.

**Discussion and interpretation:**
- The author argues that competition with the sailfin molly for food and space is the most likely cause of pupfish decline in shoreline pools.
- African cichlids, crayfish and mosquitofish also compete with, or may prey on, pupfish and their eggs.
- Pesticide exposure and habitat loss from irrigation drain maintenance or infilling (for example, McKinley Street Drain was filled between surveys) further threaten the species.

**Threats at San Felipe Creek:**
- Imperial County zoned the lands bordering the creek as open space, which allows agricultural development without permits.
- Approval of a housing subdivision adjacent to the creek and planned agricultural development with groundwater pumping on about 17 sections could lower surface flows.

**Status conclusion:** Pupfish qualify for listing as endangered under State and Federal law. Survival in California is in immediate jeopardy due to loss of habitat, present or threatened habitat destruction, and predation, competition and behavioral interference from exotic species.

**Recommendations:**
1. List the desert pupfish as endangered by both State and Federal governments.
2. Conduct hydrological studies to locate the groundwater source(s) supplying San Felipe Creek and assess pumping effects.
3. Request Imperial County to zone land in and adjacent to San Felipe Creek to exclude development.
4. Request the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and/or the Bureau of Land Management to acquire the remaining private land.
5. Investigate installing a concrete spillway or drop (15 to 30 cm high) downstream of State Highway 86 to block upstream movement of exotic fish.
6. Eradicate exotic fishes within the permanent water sections of San Felipe Creek.
7. Conduct biannual surveys of all known habitats.
8. Obtain biological data needed for effective management.
9. Establish at least six refugia sufficiently separated geographically to prevent a single catastrophic event from eliminating all refugial populations.
