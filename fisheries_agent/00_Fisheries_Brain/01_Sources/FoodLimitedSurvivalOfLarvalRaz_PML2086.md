# Food-Limited Survival of Larval Razorback Suckers in the Laboratory

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study tests whether food limitation contributes to the failure of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) larvae to recruit into the adult population of [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. Adults are common and reproduce annually, yet larvae disappear at about 10.6 mm total length (TL), and the few field-caught larvae show signs of incomplete transition from [[Endogenous Feeding]] to [[Exogenous Feeding]]. Three experiments conducted at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in 1985 examined the effects of food absence, delayed feeding, and food density on larval mortality and growth. The authors identify a [[Critical Period]] lasting from about 8 to 19 days after hatching, with [[Irreversible Starvation]] occurring between 19 and 23 days, and conclude that food-related mortality may contribute to [[Year-Class Failure]] in the Lake Mohave population.

## Background & Description

- **Species and status:** The razorback sucker is a large, endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Recruitment to adulthood has been poor for decades, and the species is in decline.
- **Population context:** Adults in Lake Mohave are reported as common (Minckley 1983; McCarthy & Minckley 1987), but age-class studies found no adequate recruitment, despite extensive sampling with appropriate gear (Tyus 1987; Minckley 1983).
- **Larval vulnerability:** Starvation during the transition from yolk reserves to external feeding is a widely proposed cause of larval mortality in fishes (Hjort 1914, 1926; Cushing 1976; Leggett 1986). The period of greatest vulnerability is termed the critical period (May 1974).
- **Broodstock and rearing methods:** Adult females from Lake Mohave were induced to spawn with three intramuscular injections of chorionic gonadotropin at 24-h intervals, beginning in February 1985. Eggs were stripped and fertilized with milt from naturally ripened Lake Mohave males, then incubated at 21°C.
- **Larval stocking:** Larvae first became active on 23 February 1985. On 25 February, 50 larvae measuring 9.6 to 10.7 mm TL (mean 9.96 ± 0.04 mm) were stocked into jars and studied for 50 days, until 16 April.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Mohave conditions:** Zooplankton densities in the lake are reported to be low and variable (Paulson et al. 1980; Langhorst & Marsh 1986). Lake temperatures during incubation are cooler (9 to 15°C) than the 18°C used in the experimental system (Marsh 1985).
- **Field-caught larvae:** Larvae collected from the reservoir generally have reduced or fully absorbed yolk sacs, an average yolk-sac area of about 10.3 mm² at 10.6 mm TL, mostly empty stomachs, and are estimated to be no older than 19 days (Marsh & Langhorst 1988; Langhorst & Marsh 1986). Together these suggest they were in transition from endogenous to exogenous feeding.
- **Temperature effects:** At the hatchery, larvae hatched in about two-thirds the time at 20°C (5.5 d) than at 15°C (8.5 d), though total length at swim-up did not differ significantly (8.5 vs. 8.4 mm; Marsh 1985). Lower lake temperatures may produce larger, better-provisioned larvae that can survive longer without food.
- **Prey relationships:** Food-density thresholds and feeding dynamics are discussed in relation to the broader literature on larval fish survival (Houde 1974; Werner & Blaxter 1980; Taylor & Freeberg 1984).

## Key Findings

### Experimental Design
- **Experiment 1 (starvation):** Unfed larvae were monitored to determine the pattern of mortality.
- **Experiment 2 (timing of first feeding):** Larvae were fed *Artemia* nauplii (300 nauplii L⁻¹ or fewer, per the methods) starting at 7 days and then at 4-day intervals up to 31 days after hatching, with three replicates.
- **Experiment 3 (ration size):** Larvae were fed nauplii at 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, and 1000 L⁻¹, with three replicates. Experiments 2 and 3 were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U-test.

### Experiment 1: Starvation
- **54%** of unfed larvae died within 25 days of hatching (mean 26.0 ± 1.4 d; range 10 to 50 d).
- Average TL at death was 9.6 ± 0.2 mm.

### Experiment 2: Timing of First Feeding
- No mortality occurred in larvae fed from 7 days onward.
- Mortality for larvae first fed at 11, 15, and 19 days was 0.0%, 0.0%, and 26.7%, respectively. No significant differences were found among first-feeding ages of 7, 11, 15, and 19 days.
- Highest mortality occurred when larvae were deprived of food for 27 days (86.7%) and 31 days (93.3%), with no significant difference between the last two values.
- Most deaths occurred between 20 and 30 days, at an average of 9.3 ± 0.1 mm TL (n = 35).
- Survivors fed from 7 to 50 days were larger (23.0 ± 0.5 mm) than those first fed at 31 days (about 15 mm).

### Experiment 3: Food Density
- Mortality was highest and not significantly different at 5 and 10 nauplii L⁻¹ (86.7 ± 3.3% and 73.3 ± 3.3%).
- At 50, 100, 500, and 1000 nauplii L⁻¹, mortality was below 20%, with no significant differences among these treatments.
- Mortality declined as food was sufficient to provide survivors with at least 10 to 20 nauplii per fish per feeding at 5 and 10 nauplii L⁻¹ treatments.
- Most larval deaths again occurred between 20 and 30 days, at a mean of 10.1 ± 0.2 mm TL (n = 25).
- Larvae surviving 50 days at 5, 10, and 50 nauplii L⁻¹ did not differ in TL (about 15 mm). Larvae at 500 and 1000 nauplii L⁻¹ reached 23.0 and 23.7 mm, respectively.
- Estimated minimum food density to ensure survival through the critical period is **30 to 60 nauplii fish⁻¹ day⁻¹**.

### Interpretation
- Larvae deprived of food, fed too late, or given insufficient food usually died between 20 and 30 days after hatching.
- Larvae that survived starvation for 3 days and were then fed grew to about 15.9 mm TL, while those fed continuously reached about 23.2 mm TL.
- Under laboratory conditions, the critical period appears to end between 19 and 23 days after hatching. Field conditions may extend this period, especially at lower reservoir temperatures.
- The authors caution that direct application of these laboratory results to nature is difficult, because growth and yolk utilization efficiency vary with temperature and cannot be precisely predicted for the wild population.
- The absence of larvae that passed through the critical period in Lake Mohave is consistent with food-related mortality contributing to year-class failure, though the authors note this does not prove it.

### Conclusions
- Food limitation during the transition to external feeding is a plausible mechanism for recruitment failure in razorback suckers.
- Food availability in Lake Mohave should be assessed for densities above the estimated threshold (30 to 60 nauplii fish⁻¹ day⁻¹) during the critical period, particularly after yolk absorption at about 8 days.
- Further research is needed on yolk utilization efficiency, larval size at hatching, and growth rates at ambient reservoir temperatures.
