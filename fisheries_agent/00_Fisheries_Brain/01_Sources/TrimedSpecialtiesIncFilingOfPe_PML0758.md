# Delta Smelt Critical Habitat Designation and Related Federal Rules (December 1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae

## Executive Summary

The document is a bundle of Federal Register materials dated December 19, 1994. Its centerpiece is the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) final rule designating [[Critical Habitat]] for the threatened [[Delta Smelt]] (*Hypomesus transpacificus*) under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) in California's [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary]]. The rule was coordinated with the [[Environmental Protection Agency]] (EPA) [[Bay-Delta Water Quality Standards]], which set salinity criteria, most notably the 2 ppt isohaline, intended to restore historical estuarine variability. The bundle also includes a Legal Services Corporation rule on governing-body composition for legal aid recipients, a NMFS bluefin tuna quota transfer, an APHIS notice on Mexican Hass avocado imports, and an NRC petition correction. Those non-fish items are summarized briefly below.

## Background & Description

The delta smelt is a small, short-lived (about one year) osmerid fish that is euryhaline, tolerating a wide salinity range. For much of its annual life it is associated with the freshwater edge of the mixing zone, where salinity is about 2 ppt. Historically it occurred from Suisun Bay upstream to the City of Sacramento on the Sacramento River and to Mossdale on the San Joaquin River (Moyle et al. 1992). Adults migrate upstream before spawning and disperse into river channels and tidally influenced backwater sloughs. Eggs are adhesive and are thought to attach to submerged roots, branches, and vegetation. Spawning season varies yearly, from late winter or December to early summer, with peak spawning in April in many years.

The rule notes that the species was listed as threatened on March 5, 1993 (58 FR 12854). The original critical habitat proposal was published October 3, 1991, and a revised proposal January 6, 1994 (59 FR 852). The revision expanded the area upstream to include spawning habitat in the Sacramento River and tidally influenced sloughs.

Non-fish items in the bundle include:
- **Legal Services Corporation (45 CFR Part 1607):** governance rules for recipient boards (at least 60% attorney members, at least one-third eligible clients, four meetings per year, compensation limits).
- **NMFS bluefin tuna:** transfer of 5 metric tons from the longline-south to the longline-north incidental subcategory (effective December 14–31, 1994).
- **APHIS:** reopening of the comment period on the proposed importation of Hass avocados from Michoacán, Mexico, to January 3, 1995.
- **NRC:** correction to a notice on Tri-Med Specialties' petition PRM-35-72 (comments due February 15, 1995).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** The final rule identifies five primary constituent elements (PCEs): physical habitat, water, river flow, and salinity concentrations for:
- **Spawning:** shallow fresh or slightly brackish backwater sloughs and edgewaters, with suitable water quality and egg-attachment substrates. Named areas include Barker, Lindsey, Cache, Prospect, Georgiana, Beaver, Hog, and Sycamore sloughs, the Sacramento River in the Delta, and tributaries of northern Suisun Bay.
- **Larval and juvenile transport:** adequate river flow to move larvae from upstream spawning areas to rearing habitat in Suisun Bay. Physical disturbance (dredging, diking, gravel mining) and flow disruption (diversions, barriers, reverse flows) are identified as threats.
- **Rearing:** maintenance of the 2 ppt isohaline in a food-rich, shallow environment, extending from Carquinez Strait across Suisun Bay (Grizzly and Honker Bays) and up the Sacramento River to Three Mile Slough.
- **Adult migration:** unrestricted access to spawning habitat from December to July, with adequate flow and water quality.

**Hydrology and drought.** The document records that spawning occurred in 1993 (a wet year) as far upstream as Sacramento, and that peak delta smelt diversion has increased since 1983. Recent droughts (1987–1992) are noted as severely affecting the species. Water project operations (the State Water Project and Central Valley Project) are a major source of entrainment, with a documented relationship between salvage numbers and Delta outflow.

**Threats.** Other stressors named include habitat loss and degradation (sand and gravel mining, diking, dredging, levees), contaminants, and introduced species such as the yellowfin goby, striped bass, and Asian clam (*Potamocorbula amurensis*). Comments argued the effects of these were uncertain; the Service held that diminished water quality and quantity are major contributors to the decline.

**Other estuarine species.** The Service states that the salinity regime will benefit other native species, including winter-run chinook salmon, longfin smelt, and Sacramento splittail. The delta smelt and six other native fishes are covered in the draft Delta Native Fishes Recovery Plan.

## Key Findings

**Critical habitat designation (Service final rule)**
- Designated areas include all waters and submerged lands below ordinary high water in Suisun Bay (including Grizzly and Honker Bays), the length of Goodyear, Suisun, Cutoff, First Mallard (Spring Branch), and Montezuma sloughs, and existing contiguous waters within the Delta (per Cal. Water Code §12220).
- The designation falls within Contra Costa, Sacramento, San Joaquin, Solano, and Yolo Counties, California.
- Effective date: January 18, 1995.
- No exclusions were judged appropriate. The Service found the benefits of designation outweigh the benefits of exclusion, and that the economic consequences are "relatively small."

**Economic findings**
- Four Delta aggregate operations may be affected. San Joaquin County had an aggregate production value of $13 million in 1986; California's total in 1992 was $473 million.
- Wetland restoration costs were estimated at $10,000 to $50,000 per acre. A 3:1 habitat replacement ratio applies to some diking projects; foregone agricultural income from one 1,300-acre tract was estimated at $650,000 per year.
- Levee maintenance averaged $1.24 million per mile (1981–1991, 536.6 miles).
- The EPA water-cost analysis estimates up to 376 thousand acre-feet of average annual water supply reduction, with 73% of water costs attributable to the salinity criteria.
- EPA's economic benefits estimate is at least $2.1 million per year in commercial and recreational fishery benefits and about 145 full-time equivalent jobs.

**Biological opinion and [[Section 7 Consultation]]**
- A February 4, 1994 jeopardy biological opinion found that combined State and Federal water project operations likely would jeopardize the delta smelt and destroy or adversely modify proposed critical habitat.
- The Service anticipates that adverse modification will be avoided through three initiatives: the Framework Agreement (1994), a forthcoming Delta Native Fishes Recovery Plan, and water contract renewals.

**Water quality standards (EPA)**
- EPA's standards focus on the 2 ppt isohaline location from February to June, with criteria at Roe Island, Chipps Island, and the Sacramento-San Joaquin River confluence.
- The Service argued that the variability these criteria provide is needed to resist invasion by introduced species and to support high phytoplankton and zooplankton densities.

**Public comment record**
- The October 1991 proposal drew 360 written and oral comments from 318 individuals. Of the 4 people who specifically commented on critical habitat, 3 opposed it and 1 supported it.
- The January 1994 revision drew comments from 43 parties in writing, plus about 125 oral testimonies at four hearings. Of the 43 parties, 37 opposed critical habitat, 9 supported it, and 2 were neutral.

**Other rules**
- NMFS: the longline-north incidental bluefin tuna subcategory now has 33 mt annual allocation, with about 12 mt remaining in longline-south.
- APHIS: the Mexican avocado comment period is extended to January 3, 1995.
- LSC: recipient boards must have at least 60% attorney members.

**Conclusion.** The Service concluded that the designation protects habitat essential for each life stage of the delta smelt, that it does not by itself create a management plan, and that specific management actions will come through recovery planning and section 7 consultations.
