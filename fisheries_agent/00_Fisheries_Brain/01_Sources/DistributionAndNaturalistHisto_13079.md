# Distribution and Natural History of the Snail *Pomatiopsis cincinnatiensis* (Lea)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pomatiopsidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by [[Van Der Schalie]] and [[Getz]] examines the regional and local distribution, life history, and laboratory culture of *Pomatiopsis cincinnatiensis*, an amphibious, operculate prosobranch snail that lives on river banks in the upper Mississippi drainage and lower Great Lakes region. The authors found that soil texture does not limit the species' range, while frequent flooding and silt-clay deposition, along with pollution and bank disturbance, exclude it from many streams. Within a stream, the snail occupies a narrow zone defined mainly by substrate moisture of roughly 70 to 80 per cent of saturation. The species behaves as an annual, with hatching in August to September, adult die-off in late summer and autumn, and a female-biased sex ratio. Diurnal and nocturnal activity did not differ. The paper also presents a moisture-controlled culture chamber that solved earlier rearing failures. This snail is a relative of *Oncomelania*, the intermediate host of [[Schistosoma Japonicum]], which gives the work medical zoology relevance.

Note: the source is a research paper on a snail, not a fish study. Fish-specific fields are therefore listed as N/A.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** *Pomatiopsis cincinnatiensis* (Lea), a North American prosobranch snail, compared with [[Pomatiopsis Lapidaria]] and the four nominal species of [[Oncomelania]].
- **Habitat preference:** Restricted to stream and river banks with fine-textured, moist substrate. The authors describe marshes as unsuitable because their moisture fluctuates too much.
- **Life cycle:** Eggs are laid mainly from mid-May to late June (extending to late September per the discussion). Young hatch from early August to late September. Most snails reach maturity before hibernation in late October and emerge in spring, with a mating peak in mid-May.
- **Longevity:** A few individuals may survive 14 to 18 months, but the species is treated as effectively annual, with almost all adults dying between mid-August and mid-October.
- **Study context:** Six years of field and laboratory work, supported by the Armed Forces Epidemiological Board and the Office of the Surgeon General, Department of the Army.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geographic distribution**
- Surveys were made along roads crossing streams, examining about 100 yards above and below each bridge.
- The species was not found in Illinois. It was found at a few new Michigan localities and at 14 new Ohio localities.
- Historically it was probably present in all major Ohio drainages, and possibly throughout Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, eastern Iowa, and southern Michigan before settlement.
- A list of 33 negative sites in southern Illinois is summarized here rather than reproduced. None yielded the species.

**Substrate and soil texture**
- Soil texture was tested against presence or absence. Positive localities averaged 62% sand, 26% silt, and 12% clay. Negative localities averaged 57% sand, 29% silt, and 14% clay.
- Snails were found in sand as high as 79%, so sand content alone is not limiting.
- In the Raisin River, the upper reaches were 78 to 88% sand, but the authors concluded that soil texture does not limit distribution there.

**Anthropogenic and hydrological pressures**
- Pollution from sewage and industrial wastes, dredging, and farm runoff causing floods all reduce suitability.
- Flood deposition of silt and clay (1 to 2 cm was observed after rain in August 1959) dries into a hard crust that prevents eggs from hatching.
- Suitable bank habitat in some Ohio streams extends only about five miles.
- A cut-off river loop at Tecumseh (September 1960) isolated a population. Young still appeared in August 1961, but one year was too short to judge long-term effects.

**Local zonation and moisture**
- Field data placed snails at soil moisture of 75 to 92% of saturation. Laboratory tests favored about 70%.
- Snails avoided soils at 80 to 100% saturation and became inactive at 10 to 20%.
- Steeper banks gave narrower zonation (15° to 75° slopes were tested).
- Abundance declined steadily from a tributary's backwater outlet to its trickle end.

**Temperature**
- Laboratory tests showed concentration near 21 °C. Field surface temperatures varied by only 2 to 3 degrees and did not explain zonation.
- Summer habitat temperatures averaged 20 °C and seldom exceeded 23 to 24 °C.

## Key Findings

1. **Soil texture is not the limiting factor.** Laboratory tests showed a preference for finer loam over sand and for clay over loam, but field distribution did not match soil texture.
2. **Moisture controls local zonation.** Snails clustered at about 70 to 80% substrate saturation, and zone width narrowed as bank slope increased.
3. **Flooding and silt limit regional range.** Silt and clay deposition, flood scouring, and pollution removed or killed colonies, and eggs are especially vulnerable.
4. **Life cycle is effectively annual.** Hatching occurs August to September. Most adults die between mid-August and mid-October, with a mating peak in mid-May.
5. **Sex ratio is female-biased.** Newly hatched snails showed 2 to 4 females per male. Adults showed 1.5 to 1.8 females per male. The overall ratio was 1.7:1 across 2,952 specimens, which is more balanced than the earlier estimate of 4:1 and suggests higher male survival.
6. **Maximum shell lengths** were 3.8 mm for males and 4.8 mm for females.
7. **No diel activity difference.** Movement was similar by day and night, and egg-laying occurred throughout the day and night.
8. **Dispersal.** Marked snails moved up to 12 m after 30 days. The authors estimated upstream spread of about 15 to 20 m per generation, with downstream transport by water.
9. **Temperature sensitivity.** At 27 °C, 42 of 90 snails died in 50 days, compared with 6 of 90 at 20 °C. No eggs were produced at either temperature in the moist saucers.
10. **Culture solution.** A flowerpot vivarium with a filter-paper wick maintained drier, more suitable substrate. Adults mated and produced young in it, although about 25% of the initial adults died.
11. **Historic and current range.** The species probably occupied a broad region, and its present distribution has been reduced by habitat alteration. Its northern limit is lower Michigan and its southern limit southern Kentucky.
