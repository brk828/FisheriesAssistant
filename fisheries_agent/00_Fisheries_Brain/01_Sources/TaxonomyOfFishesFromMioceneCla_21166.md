# Taxonomy of Fishes from the Miocene Clarkia Lake Beds, Idaho

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[G. R. Smith]] and [[R. R. Miller]] (University of Michigan) describes the fish fossils from the Miocene lacustrine deposits of the [[Clarkia Lake Beds]] in northern Idaho. The fauna contains three taxa: a trout, a minnow, and a sunfish, belonging to [[Salmonidae]], [[Cyprinidae]], and [[Centrarchidae]]. The trout is assigned to the Eurasian genus [[Hucho]], the minnow is referred to [[Gila]] sp., and the sunfish is described as a new species, [[Archoplites Clarki]]. The authors use the fishes to infer past biogeographic links and climate for western North America.

## Background & Description

Miocene freshwater fishes from western North America are poorly known. The samples available indicate low diversity, representing nine families, with salmonids, cyprinids, and centrarchids the most widely distributed. Several dozen Clarkia specimens, collected by [[C. J. Smiley]] and colleagues, show unusually detailed skeletal preservation.

Most fossils come from brown, ashy silts and silty clays below an ash layer in the type section (locality P-33), with additional material from other horizons at P-33 and the [[Emerald Creek Locality]] (P-37). The fish fauna is treated alongside taphonomic work by [[Smith and Elder]] (1985).

**Hucho sp.** A single nearly complete trout (estimated 668 mm total length, 588 mm standard length) was collected at P-33 on June 12, 1980. It has large jaws and teeth, large vertebrae (55-58), large scales (22-23 rows on the caudal peduncle), and 12 dorsal rays. It differs from the earlier grayling-like [[Zosalmo]] driftwoodensis from the Eocene of British Columbia, and from *Oncorhynchus*, *Salvelinus*, and *Salmo* in the features listed above.

**Gila sp.** Nineteen specimens were studied: 17 adults (9.5-18 cm) and two small individuals (55-65 mm). Diagnostic features include 8 dorsal rays (in 11 specimens), 8 or 9 anal rays, 9 or 10 pelvic rays, 42 post-Weberian vertebrae (in two specimens), and conical, hooked pharyngeal teeth in two rows. The taxonomic status is uncertain, and the authors refer the specimens to *Gila* sp. because no suitable type material exists. A difference in dorsal fin position (over the pelvics in most specimens, behind them in two larger ones) may indicate two species, but the authors caution that this character is unreliable in fossils.

**Archoplites clarki (new species).** The holotype (UMMP V 74202) is a 123 mm standard-length imprint (155 mm total length). The description draws on 21 specimens plus the holotype, ranging from 63-200 mm SL. Diagnostic features include 6-7 anal spines, 11-14 dorsal rays, 3-4 predorsal bones, strongly serrate lacrimal and preopercle, and a long pelvic spine. The four largest specimens (ca. 200 mm SL) have scales 4-5 mm in diameter. The species is named for Captain [[William Clark]] of the [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Clarkia lake supported a fish fauna of low diversity by modern standards. *Gila* sp. is described as a generalized midwater fish, similar to living relatives in the [[Basin and Range Province]] and southward into Mexico. *Archoplites clarki* was a large, predatory sunfish, probably ecologically similar to [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus*) in eastern North America.

Both groups had broader Miocene ranges than they do today. Centrarchids likely extended north, probably to Alaska. Cyprinids ranged from southern Nevada to northern Idaho. The authors suggest cyprinids reached North America from Asia across the [[Bering Strait]] in the early Miocene or late Oligocene. *Hucho* is linked to Eurasia, particularly to [[Hucho perryi]] of Japan, and was probably also present in Alaskan fresh waters during the Miocene.

Climate interpretations from the fishes are mixed:
- The [[Clarkia Flora]] indicates a warm-temperate climate analogous to the southern Appalachians (Smiley and Rember 1979).
- The Miocene distribution of *Archoplites* suggests warm winters in northwestern North America.
- The salmonid suggests moderately cool summers at Clarkia.

Later in the Miocene, three salmonid genera, including *Hucho*, coexisted with *Archoplites* in southern Idaho at [[Lake Idaho]]. By the late Pliocene, following a cooling trend, *Archoplites* was restricted in the western United States, *Hucho* had disappeared from Lake Idaho, and other salmonids were widespread.

## Key Findings

- **Three taxa in total:** The Clarkia fauna includes one trout (*Hucho* sp.), one or two minnows (*Gila* sp.), and one sunfish (*Archoplites clarki*, new species). Of these, *A. clarki* is the most abundant.
- **Eurasian affinity of the trout:** *Hucho* shows a transverse prevomerine tooth row, a basibranchial tooth patch, and large vertebrae and scales, all characteristic of the Eurasian genus. Its lineage does not appear to be ancestral to any living North American salmonid.
- **Generalized minnow:** The *Gila* material is not a particularly primitive cyprinid. It closely matches the widespread *Gila* group of the Miocene and Pliocene, and is similar to *[[Gila Turneri]]* from Nevada.
- **Archoplites diagnosis:** The genus is defined by serrate preopercles and lacrimals, a deep adductor fossa, and a long pelvic spine. The Clarkia species is the most western and most primitive of known Miocene sunfish lineages.
- **Broader Miocene ranges:** Centrarchids, cyprinids, and salmonids were distributed farther north and east in the Miocene than they are today, possibly including Alaska.
- **Climatic inference:** The fish evidence broadly agrees with the flora for warm conditions, while the salmonid indicates cool summers. The later Pliocene decline of *Hucho* and *Archoplites* in the region is linked to cooling.
- **Related fossil sites:** Similar late Miocene to Pliocene fishes are known or under study from Montana, North Dakota, California, Utah, Nevada, and Arizona (Bidahochi Formation). Their identification is still pending.
- **Taxonomic caveats:** The *Gila* identity and number of minnow species are unresolved, and the sunfish genus status depends on continuing study.
