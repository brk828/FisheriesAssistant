## Executive Summary

This is a preliminary stratigraphic report on the Pleistocene "111 Ranch beds" of Graham County, Arizona, located roughly fourteen miles east of Safford. The study documents a ~150-foot sequence of floodplain, paludal (swamp/marsh), and lacustrine sediments — including sand, gravel, clay, marl, limestone, tuffaceous silt, diatomite, and chert — exposed in badland topography near the Whitlock Hills. Although not an ichthyological paper, the report is directly relevant to [[Paleolimnology]] and [[Aquatic Paleoecology]] because it reconstructs an ancient lake/wetland system using sedimentology, diatomite deposits, and faunal indicators such as pulmonate gastropods and a fossil capybara skull. No fish remains are reported in this text; the aquatic ecosystem evidence instead comes from invertebrate (gastropod) fossils, diatom-rich sediments, and vertebrate megafauna associated with the lake margins.

## Background & Description

No fish or fish-diagnostic morphological data are present in this source. The morphological/taxonomic evidence discussed is limited to:

- **Pulmonate gastropods**: *Lymnaea reflexa* and *Pupilla muscorum* — freshwater/terrestrial snails whose shell morphology and ecological tolerances are used as proxies for moisture and climate rather than described in anatomical detail.
- **Capybara (Hydrochoeridae)**: A well-preserved skull recovered from a massive chert bed (Lance, 1958) is cited as biostratigraphic/paleoenvironmental evidence but is not morphologically described beyond its identification as capybara.
- **Diatoms**: The lower diatomite unit is composed of ~80% diatom material, indicating a diatom-dominated microfossil assemblage, though individual diatom taxa are not identified.

There is no description of fish body plans, fin structures, scale patterns, or skeletal elements in this text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report reconstructs a mosaic of ancient freshwater and wetland habitats rather than a single aquatic niche:

- **Diatomite lagoon/lake (lower unit)**: A local, lagoon-like lake paralleling the Whitlock Hills, asymmetrical and elongate, inferred from a thick basal diatomite bed (~30 ft thick where exposed, thinning to a few feet northward).
- **Paludal floodplain**: The bulk of the section (clay, marl, silt, tuff units) reflects a poorly drained, muddy floodplain with small, ephemeral ponds (generally <50 ft long) that hosted thin limestone deposition — analogous to modern [[Floodplain Wetland Habitat]].
- **Lacustrine limestone basins**: Three major limestone units record at least three episodes of coalescence into larger lake systems:
  - Lower limestone (unit 3): shallow lake, silty limestone with chert lenses, traced >2 miles along the Whitlock Hills margin.
  - Middle limestone (unit 6): the most extensive and continuous lacustrine deposit, >15 ft thick, traced ~4 miles west and further south into the San Simon valley; contains abundant turtle shell remains interpreted as residual (in-situ mortality), indicating a stable, longer-lived lake environment analogous to a [[Freshwater Lake Ecosystem]].
  - Upper limestone (unit 10): gradational with massive chert and diatomite, thinning northward and eastward, associated with well-sorted beach sands — indicating shoreline/littoral zone development.
- **Shoreline/marginal wetland facies**: A small, impure silty lime bed in unit 8 preserves shoreline features, fossil weed stems, and the pulmonate gastropod fauna, suggesting a vegetated marsh-lake margin used for paleoclimate reconstruction.
- **Chert-diatomite association**: Massive secondary chert bodies (up to 12 ft thick) are restricted to the diatomite units near the Whitlock Hills, interpreted as diagenetic replacement of fossils within a silica-rich lacustrine/paludal setting — an important taphonomic and geochemical control on preservation of any biological remains (including the capybara skull).
- **Terrestrial-aquatic transition**: Upright, silicified/calcified plant stems preserved against the mountain front indicate that lake waters periodically extended to the base of the Whitlock Hills, with rapid burial preserving standing vegetation — evidence of fluctuating shoreline dynamics in this [[Lacustrine Depositional Environment]].

## Key Findings

- The 111 Ranch beds record a long-lived, humid-to-semi-humid depositional system dominated by paludal floodplain and lacustrine (lake) environments, later shifting to arid alluvial fan deposition sourced from the Whitlock Hills.
- Diatomite units (comprising up to 80% diatoms) and associated secondary chert bodies indicate silica-rich, biologically productive lake/lagoon conditions during at least two phases of the depositional sequence.
- Three distinct limestone units mark repeated coalescence of floodplain ponds into larger, more stable lake systems, with the middle limestone (unit 6) representing the most extensive and long-lived lacustrine phase, evidenced by abundant in-situ turtle remains.
- Pulmonate gastropod fauna (*Lymnaea reflexa*, *Pupilla muscorum*) and a fossil capybara skull are used as key **paleoclimatic and paleoecological indicators**, jointly supporting a humid climate interpretation for much of the depositional history — an approach paralleling how modern [[Freshwater Fish Assemblages]] are sometimes used as bioindicators of aquatic habitat conditions.
- Contrary to classic intermontane basin models (which predict coarse sediment thickening toward bordering highlands), fine-grained lacustrine/paludal sediments in this basin lap against the Whitlock Hills without coarsening — implying sediment sourcing from outside the immediate highland, with climate (not proximity to source terrain) as the dominant control on facies distribution.
- A late shift to arid conditions is marked by the transition from paludal/lacustrine deposition to massive alluvial fan sand and gravel derived directly from the Whitlock Hills, followed by multi-stage terrace erosion and caliche-cemented gravel capping — marking the end of the lake/wetland ecosystem recorded in this section.
- **No fish fossils or fish-related data are reported in this source**; the aquatic ecosystem is instead characterized through sedimentology, diatom content, gastropod paleoecology, and megafaunal (capybara, turtle) remains.
