#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This study by W. J. Matthews (1986) examines geographic variation in thermal tolerance of the red shiner (*[[Notropis lutrensis]]*), a widespread cyprinid minnow, across 18 populations spanning 1100 km from northern Kansas to south-central Texas. Despite documented north-south climatic gradients in air and water temperatures, critical thermal maximum (CTM) showed **no significant difference** among populations, no clinal trend, and no relationship to stream size. This contrasts sharply with findings in another sympatric species, the orangethroat darter (*[[Etheostoma spectabile]]*), which shows locally adaptive thermal tolerance across much smaller geographic scales. The study concludes that *N. lutrensis* exhibits "evolutionary inertia" or thermal stasis at the species level, even though other research demonstrates the species *can* rapidly adapt physiologically and genetically when exposed to artificially altered thermal regimes (e.g., reservoir tailwaters). This suggests a species can possess a "labile" thermal physiology in principle while remaining "static" across its natural range in practice.

## Background & Description
- **Taxonomy**: *Notropis lutrensis* (Baird & Girard, 1853), family Cyprinidae; commonly known as the red shiner.
- No detailed external morphological data are reported in this physiological study; the focus is on **thermal physiology** rather than morphology.
- Fish used were adults, collected via seining, with sample sizes of n=15 per population (n=19 for Solomons River, KS population).
- Reference is made to companion work (Wooten, 1983) documenting genetic and morphological variation in natural populations of the species, with "river of origin" explaining more variation than location within a drainage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Wide, shallow prairie streams and river mainstreams/large tributaries across the American Great Plains; subject to intense solar heating in summer, with water temperatures often reaching 35–39°C.
- **Distribution**: One of the most abundant minnows across much of the U.S.A.; native to major Great Plains drainages including the Kansas, Arkansas, Red, Washita, Canadian, Brazos, Trinity, and Colorado (TX) river systems.
- **Historical biogeography**: Populations likely originated from a single ancestral stock connected via the Preglacial Plains Stream (~10,000+ years ago), later becoming isolated in separate drainages after Wisconsin glaciation with minimal north-south gene flow since.
- **Physiological ecology**: Highly tolerant of physicochemical stress; successful in harsh, thermally variable, and desiccation-prone environments. Exhibits "thermal hardening" and seasonal acclimatization of temperature tolerance (Maness & Hutchison, 1980).
- **Life history**: Exhibits a bimodal spawning pattern in southern populations (June spawning plus a second late-summer peak; Farringer et al., 1979), exposing developing young to differing thermal regimes within a single year.
- **Population connectivity**: Considered more vagile and less isolated than benthic species like *[[Etheostoma spectabile]]*; readily recolonizes rewatered streams after drought/flood disturbance, promoting intradrainage gene flow (per Wooten, 1983) but likely insufficient interdrainage gene flow to explain the observed homogeneity via mixing alone.
- **Anthropogenic influence**: In the Brazos River, Texas, a culturally altered thermal regime (cold-water reservoir release) has driven measurable population-level divergence in enzyme systems, temperature preference, and thermal tolerance within ~40 years (Zimmerman & Richmond, 1981; Calhoun et al., 1982; King et al., 1985), demonstrating latent adaptive capacity absent in unaltered systems.

## Key Findings
- **No significant difference in mean CTM** among 18 populations spanning 1100 km (ANOVA for Unequal Variance, F=1.68, P=0.0603).
- **CTM range**: 35.90°C (North Canadian River, OK) to 36.35°C (San Saba River, TX) — a narrow range despite substantial climatic variation across the study area.
- **No clinal (north-south) trend** in CTM despite documented climatic gradients: southern sites have more days >32.2°C, higher average/maximum air and water temperatures (with exception of three southernmost, higher-elevation drainages).
- **No relationship between CTM and stream size**, tested via both 5-category and 2-category stream width classifications (Wilcoxon Scores Test; 5 classes: F=0.72, P=0.581; 2 classes: F=0.16, P=0.688).
- **Heterogeneous variance in CTM** among populations (Bartlett's test, P<0.01; F-max test, P<0.05), despite homogeneous means — populations with greatest CTM variance were located in south Oklahoma/north Texas (Red, Trinity, Brazos rivers), coinciding with the hottest recorded climatic conditions.
- **Possible spring-influence trend**: Three of the four populations with lowest mean CTM occurred in at least partially spring-fed streams (Chikaskia, Paluxy, Pedernales rivers), suggesting localized cool-water influence may lower thermal tolerance, though not statistically robust.
- **Contrast with *[[Etheostoma spectabile]]***: Unlike *N. lutrensis*, this darter species shows significant, locally adaptive intraspecific variation in thermal tolerance across populations within just 60 km (Feminella & Matthews, 1984), attributed to its more sedentary, isolated population structure in small streams.
- **Evolutionary interpretation**: Following criteria from Hertz et al. (1983) for demonstrating evolutionary inertia, the author argues that (1) gene flow alone is unlikely to fully explain homogeneity across major, largely isolated drainages, and (2) thermal selective pressures are plausibly "important and operative" in these hot prairie streams. The persistence of uniform CTM despite this supports a conclusion of **evolutionary stasis/inertia** in thermal tolerance for this trait, even though the species demonstrates capacity for rapid adaptive change under strong artificial selection (thermal effluent systems).
- **Broader implication**: A species may possess a "labile" (adaptable) thermal physiology at the genomic/physiological level, yet remain "static" (evolutionarily conservative) in expressed thermal tolerance across its natural geographic range — intraspecific thermal variability must be assessed empirically on a species-by-species basis rather than assumed from theory alone.
- **Methodology**: CTM measured via heating fish at 1°C/min from a 21°C acclimation temperature (14–20 days, L:D 12:12) to loss of equilibrium, following [[Hutchison CTM Method]] as used in Feminella & Matthews (1984).
