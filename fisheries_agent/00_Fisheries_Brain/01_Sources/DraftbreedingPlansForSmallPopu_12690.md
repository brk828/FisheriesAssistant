# Breeding Plans for Small Populations, Based on the Dynamics of Quantitative Genetic Variance

#conservation-genetics #population-genetics #captive-breeding #quantitative-genetics

## Executive Summary

This theoretical paper by [[Russell Lande]] develops analytical models for designing captive breeding programs aimed at maintaining genetic diversity in small populations of endangered species. The work extends previous models by [[Soulé]] and colleagues to incorporate mutation, immigration, selection in captivity, and population subdivision. The primary management goal examined is maintaining 90% of original additive genetic variance for 200 years. Key findings demonstrate that accounting for mutation substantially reduces the required final population sizes, especially for species with short generation times, and that strategic population subdivision can preserve genetic variation even under selection pressures in captive environments.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses the genetic challenges facing small populations in nature preserves or captive breeding programs established due to habitat destruction and fragmentation. While ecological factors typically dominate extinction risk in wild populations, genetic factors become increasingly important in artificially reduced populations. In captive environments where demographic fluctuations can be controlled, genetic considerations should play a dominant role in breeding plans, particularly when the goal is eventual reintroduction to the wild.

The author develops continuous-time models analyzing the dynamics of additive genetic variance in quantitative traits (morphological, behavioral, and physiological characters). The models account for:
- Random genetic drift and mutation
- Immigration from wild populations
- Selection in captive environments
- Population subdivision strategies

The baseline management goal, proposed by [[Soulé]] et al. (1986), targets maintaining 90% of genetic variability present in the original base population for 200 years. The paper provides analytical frameworks allowing breeding plans to meet various goals (e.g., 75% variability for 100 years).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The models assume captive environments where natural selection on most traits is greatly reduced or absent, though substantial selection may occur for adaptation to captivity (docility, high reproduction). The work recognizes that newly established captive populations experience novel physical conditions (confinement), altered social factors (isolation or crowding), and exposure to different pathogens. Relaxation of natural selection may cause gradual deterioration of traits maintained by mutation-selection balance in wild populations, including sensory acuity, agility, and cognitive function.

The paper emphasizes that effective population size (N_e) may change with time and is assumed to be a constant multiple of actual population size. Generation time (T) is defined as the average age of parents of newborn individuals in a population with stable age distribution. For populations with overlapping generations, generation time depends on population growth rate, being smaller for rapidly increasing populations.

## Key Findings

**Random Genetic Drift and Mutation:**
- Accounting for typical mutation rates (V_m = 10^-3 V_e, where V_e is environmental variance) substantially reduces required final population sizes compared to models ignoring mutation
- For species with short generation times, required effective population size (K_e) never exceeds 450 individuals when mutation is included
- Mutation allows management goals to be met with founder numbers smaller than 5 individuals, previously considered the minimum
- Without mutation, extremely large final population sizes are needed for species with generation times of a few years or less

**Immigration from the Wild:**
- One effective migrant every few generations (N_e m = 1/4, 1/2, or 1) substantially reduces the effective population size necessary to achieve management goals
- Immigration rates of N_e m ≥ 2 provide stronger stabilizing influence on mean phenotype than random genetic drift effects
- Mean phenotype expected to drift less than one phenotypic standard deviation in 200 years except for populations with generation times less than 0.5 year without immigration
- Immigration rate must be comparable to selection strength (m ≥ s) to effectively counteract deleterious evolution

**Selection in Captive Environment:**
- With selection coefficient s = 1%, even infinitely large populations cannot maintain 90% of original genetic variance for 200 years unless generation time is rather long
- For species with short generation times, populations with moderate effective size maintain nearly as much genetic variance in selected characters as infinitely large populations
- Artificial selection within progeny groups, with readjustment to equal size after selection, can help counteract natural selection in captivity

**Population Subdivision:**
- Splitting a population into separate subpopulations permanently maintains genetic variability between subpopulations rather than within them
- After several N_e/n generations, genetic variance preserved among n subpopulations approaches (1 - 1/n)V_g(0)
- With mutation but no migration or selection, subpopulations continue to differentiate and total genetic variance after panmixia eventually exceeds that in the base population
- Extreme subdivision into numerous very small subpopulations may be the only method of maintaining high proportions of original genetic variation for characters under appreciable selection for adaptation to captivity
- Subdivision is most effective when random genetic drift and fixation of alternate alleles occurs faster than selection
- Subpopulations should not be so small as to experience severe inbreeding depression (effective size should exceed a few dozen individuals)

**Practical Recommendations:**
- Breeding plans should be designed to meet specific goals ranging from permanent captive display to breeding for wild release
- Period of captive propagation should encompass as few generations as possible to minimize loss of genetic variation, cultural information, and domestication effects
- Environment of release should be similar to original natural habitat
- Multiple releases at various localities often necessary for successful establishment
- Random exchange of one effective immigrant every few generations between subdivisions renders population nearly panmictic with respect to selectively neutral variation
- Degree of subdivision should balance intensity of selection (requiring more subdivision) against magnitude of inbreeding depression (requiring larger subpopulation size)

The paper provides analytical formulas in an appendix allowing construction of breeding plans for various management goals and includes graphical evaluations for the specific 90%/200-year target proposed by [[Soulé]] and colleagues.
