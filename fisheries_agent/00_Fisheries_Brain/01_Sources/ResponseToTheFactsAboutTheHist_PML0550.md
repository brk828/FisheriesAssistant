# Quail Creek Project and Woundfin Flow Requirements: Response to the WCWCD "Facts" Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1998 letter and enclosed rebuttal by [[James Deacon]], a Distinguished Professor at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, responds to a draft report by the [[Washington County Water Conservancy District]] (WCWCD), "The Facts About the History and Operation of the Quail Creek Project" (dated 14 April 1998). The dispute centers on whether the WCWCD agreed to bypass 86 cubic feet per second (cfs) or natural flow at the Quail Creek Diversion, and whether the [[Quail Creek Project]] has harmed the endangered [[Woundfin]], the [[Virgin River Chub]], and the wider native fish community of the [[Virgin River]].

Deacon argues that the [[Biological Opinion]] (BO) was based on biological needs of woundfin, that the 86 cfs stipulation applies to the 12-mile reach above [[Washington Diversion]], and that the project is not being operated as the analysis assumed. He concludes that reservoir construction and operation partially explain the woundfin decline.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Cover letter (July 2, 1998, addressed to Paul Marsh, Arizona State University) plus a formal response by Deacon (June 30, 1998).
- **Regulatory context:** The Quail Creek Project received a non-jeopardy opinion from the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). The [[Bureau Of Land Management]] (BLM) made the BO's conditions a condition of the right-of-way grant, as stated in the [[Environmental Assessment]] (EA).
- **Predecessor project:** The [[Warner Valley Project]] received a jeopardy opinion because it would have reduced minimum flows in the Virgin River to below roughly 80 to 100 cfs between Pah Tempe (LaVerkin) Springs and Washington Diversion. Quail Creek was designed to conform to the 86 cfs or natural flow standard.
- **Target species:** Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*, Cyprinidae), an endangered minnow, and other native Virgin River fishes.
- **Author's role:** Deacon conducted much of the research underlying the BO, co-authored the 1982 analysis with his student [[Thomas Hardy]], and was involved in discussions leading to the minimum flow stipulation.

### Key Conditions Cited

- A minimum of 86 cfs "will always bypass this diversion structure" (or natural flow, if lower), to satisfy downstream water rights.
- Operation is year-round, but Virgin River water is conveyed to the reservoir only during periods of high flow.
- Mean spring flows below 100 cfs are associated with poor reproduction or recruitment (Deacon 1979).
- Woundfin spawning, fry development, and recruitment are most successful when mean monthly flow is roughly 200 to 800 cfs (Deacon and Hardy, in press).
- The upper reach (LaVerkin Springs to Washington Diversion, about 12 miles) represents the upper limit of known woundfin habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and habitat.** Hardy and Deacon (1982) analyzed weighted useable area (WUA) for woundfin in two reaches: a 1.5-mile segment from Pah Tempe Springs to the mouth of Ash Creek, and a 12.5-mile segment from Ash Creek to Washington Diversion. Maximum habitat for juveniles and adults in the lower reach occurred at 90 and 85 cfs, respectively. The analysis found that flows of about 200 cfs, which maximize habitat for fry, do not occur naturally because of withdrawals at the LaVerkin and Hurricane diversions.

**Drought and flow variability.** Deacon agrees with the view of [[Hardy Et Al 1995]] that drought reduces native fish numbers, but argues that drought is only part of the cause. He cites the difference between the 1974 to 1977 drought and the 1989 to 1992 drought, which had a much greater effect on woundfin density, as evidence that reservoir operation and lower-river diversions (LaVerkin and Ash creeks) added significant impacts. Reduced flow variability and minimum flows are identified as likely causes of decline.

**Peer review and evidence base.** Deacon's main conclusions appear in peer-reviewed sources (Deacon 1979, 1988; Deacon and Hardy 1984; Gregory and Deacon 1994). Several non-peer-reviewed reports (Hardy and Deacon 1982; [[Holden And Zucker 1996]]; Minckley et al. 1997; Williams and Deacon 1998) are also cited. Deacon contends that the WCWCD's preferred analyses by Hardy and co-workers, including Hardy et al. (1995), have not undergone peer review or independent confirmation.

## Key Findings

### 1. Central dispute: the 86 cfs stipulation

- The WCWCD claims it agreed to supply 86 cfs at Washington Diversion. Deacon argues the stipulation requires bypass flows of 86 cfs (or natural flow) over the entire 12-mile reach from Pah Tempe Springs to Washington Diversion.
- The BO cites eight references. Seven are by Deacon or his students, and one is by [[Robert R. Miller]] and [[Carl L. Hubbs]]. None address water rights, which Deacon argues shows that the BO was based on biological needs.
- Water rights are protected by state statute rather than federal law. Deacon argues the repeated references to downstream water rights were included to reduce controversy, not to set the purpose of the BO.

### 2. Operating criteria in the 1982 analysis

- The [[Hardy And Deacon 1982]] analysis examined only water withdrawals. It did not include reservoir releases.
- Under this analysis, if natural flows are below 86 cfs throughout the reach, neither the BO nor the EA is violated. In that case, the WCWCD may release reservoir water to satisfy water rights but is not obligated to do so.
- Deacon states that the analysis relied on hydraulic data from the Dixie Project Simulation (Quail Creek Reservoir) User's Manual (Stauffer, 1982). He says the 1983 Creamer and Noble Simulation Program could not have been used for the 1982 report.
- Tables 1 to 9 of the 1982 report could be used to reconstruct the operating criteria.

### 3. Adverse effects of the project

- Deacon states that the Quail Creek Project is contributing to the decline of woundfin and other native Virgin River fishes.
- Williams and Deacon (1998) conclude that operation of Quail Creek Dam and Reservoir, plus diversions in lower LaVerkin and Ash creeks, have had "significant adverse impacts" on woundfin population numbers.
- Deacon notes that two independent analyses (Holden and Zucker 1996; Gregory and Deacon 1994, with Williams and Deacon 1998) agree, while the conclusions of Hardy et al. (1995) are not supported by an independent analysis.

### 4. Summary conclusions stated by the author

1. The Biological Opinion is based on the biological needs of woundfin.
2. The 86 cfs or natural flow requirement applies to the 12-mile reach above Washington Diversion.
3. Available evidence indicates that woundfin decline is partly attributable to the construction and operation of Quail Creek Reservoir.
4. The Quail Creek Project is not being operated "as presented for the analysis of the impacts of the reservoir operation on the woundfin and its habitat."
