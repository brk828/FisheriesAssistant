# Multiple-Plate Samplers as a Source of Living Food for Aquatic Animals

#chironomidae #aquatic-invertebrates #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1963 reprint from *Turtox News*, written by J. S. Dendy of the [[Auburn University]] Department of Zoology-Entomology, describes how a [[Multiple-Plate Sampler]] can supply live food for laboratory aquatic animals. The sampler, based on the design of [[Hester and Dendy]] (1962), is a stack of hardboard plates that colonizes with larval insects, mainly [[Chironomidae]] (midges), which can then be fed to fish, salamander larvae and aquatic beetles. The article gives practical guidance on placement, colonization timing, transport, holding and reuse of the samplers, with the most productive results reported from fertile [[Alabama Ponds]].

## Background & Description

The sampler was originally developed to study aquatic macroinvertebrates in streams and ponds. Dendy adapted it as a food-production tool. Its construction is simple:

- **Plates:** Tempered hardboard (“Masonite”), 1/8 inch thick, smooth on both sides.
- **Sizes:** Large plates, 3 inches square, alternating with small plates, 1 inch square. A hole is bored in the center of each plate.
- **Assembly:** Plates are strung on a bolt or cord so that large plates are separated by small ones.
- **Spacing:** The gap between large plates must be too narrow for fish to enter and feed on the insects inside. Exact plate dimensions and plate count matter little unless quantitative sampling is intended.
- **Fasteners:** A bolt with nuts (Figure 1) rusts and is hard to take apart. [[Nylon Cord]] (Figure 2) is easier to disassemble and was used successfully.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Colonization depends on habitat and conditions:

- **Fertility:** Plates in fertile ponds collected more food organisms than plates in infertile ponds.
- **Depth and distance from shore:** Samplers in water 2 to 3 feet deep near shore, suspended about 1 foot below the surface, performed best. Samplers several hundred feet offshore in deep water attracted fewer animals.
- **Streams:** Stream samplers were colonized by stream animals, but in smaller numbers than samplers in fertile ponds.
- **Temperature:** Colonization was faster in warm weather. In the coldest weather, invertebrates were less active and populations built up slowly, but the animals did not leave the samplers.
- **Emergence:** Adult chironomids emerged from populated samplers held for more than a few days, so some animals were lost during storage.

## Key Findings

**Colonization timeline**
- Immature aquatic insects, chiefly chironomids, began colonizing within about one week.
- Samplers were ready to harvest after about one month.
- In fertilized ponds, the spaces between large plates were often filled with hundreds of chironomids after two months.

**Handling and holding**
- Before removal, each sampler was enclosed in a small plastic bag of pond water to keep loosely attached animals from being lost. Insects usually showed no alarm or distress during transfer to the laboratory.
- Samplers were stored in a battery jar of pond water. For holding periods of hours, days or weeks, the water was aerated with compressed air.

**Feeding methods**
- Larvae were scraped off the plates and fed to fish in some experiments ([[Carothers 1961]]).
- In other experiments, whole plates were placed in aquaria, where fish readily fed on exposed insects. This method worked well for small fish, larval salamanders and larval aquatic beetles.

**Reuse and planning**
- Nylon cord was used repeatedly for exposure periods from one week to more than three months in pond water.
- Once organisms are removed, plates can be reassembled and returned to the pond for another colonization cycle.
- Because populated samplers are often easier to hold in the pond than in the laboratory, a schedule should be planned so that colonized samplers are ready when needed.

**Context**
- Carothers' 1961 thesis on [[Redear Sunfish]] control of snails is cited as an example of using invertebrates as fish food in experiments.
- The article does not report species-level identifications, sample counts beyond the qualitative descriptions above, or statistical tests.
