# Report upon the Collections of Fishes Made in Portions of Nevada, Idaho, Utah, California, Colorado, New Mexico, and Arizona (1871–1874)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a taxonomic report on freshwater fishes collected across the interior American West during four field seasons (1871, 1872, 1873, and 1874). The collections were gathered by several survey parties and were described and annotated by [[D. D. Cope]] with the assistance of [[H. C. Yarrow]], who also compiled the synonymy and bibliography. The report establishes several new genera and species, documents range extensions, and gives notes on the economic and ecological status of commercially important fishes such as [[Salmo Virginalis]] (Utah Lake trout) and [[Coregonus Williamsoni]]. Most material was later lost or damaged, but the surviving collections of 1872 and 1873 were in good condition when they reached Washington.

## Background & Description

The collections were made by separate survey expeditions working across Colorado, Utah, New Mexico, Arizona, and adjacent territories. A collector, F. Bischoff, made much of the 1871 material, but most of his specimens were destroyed before reaching the office; the 1872 and 1873 collections survived. The text stresses that this material gave the best available information on western fish fauna at the time.

The report is organized as an annotated systematic catalogue. Each entry typically includes:

- Diagnostic characters (fin-ray formulas, scale counts, tooth formulas, and body proportions given as ratios of head, eye, and body lengths)
- Color in life or spirits
- Locality, date, and collector, often keyed to specimen catalogue numbers
- Notes on local names, abundance, and behavior

An index covers dozens of genera and species, including *Gila*, *Catostomus*, *Pantosteus*, *Apocope*, *Lepidomeda*, *Hybopsis*, *Hybognathus*, *Rhinichthys*, and *Siboma*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Colorado River and San Luis Basin fauna.** The author notes that the Colorado River basin is home to a small group of fishes in the family Cyprinidae, the "Plagopterinae," comprising three genera: [[Plagopterus Argentissimus]], [[Meda]], and [[Lepidomeda Vittata]]. These fishes have a distinctive dorsal fin spine formed from two bony rays. The author observes that *Plagopterus* is the only fish type not known from waters outside the Colorado and San Luis basins.

**Great Basin.** Utah's lakes and streams support a distinctive assemblage. [[Utah Lake]] and [[Panquitch Lake]] host abundant trout, suckers, and [[Coregonus Williamsoni]]. [[Uranidea Wheeleri]], a cottid, was the only spiny-rayed (physoclistous) fish yet found in the Great Basin of Utah.

**Rio Grande and Southwest.** Fishes of the [[Rio Grande]] and its tributaries include the abundant chub [[Gila Pandora]] ("pescadito"), *Gila egregia*, and *Lepidomeda* species. A sturgeon (two specimens from near Albuquerque, collected by Dr. Oscar Loew) extended the known western range of the genus. A gar was reported from the Rio Grande by local informants but was not collected.

**Range extensions.** The discovery of [[Ceratichthys Biguttatus]] at Harmony, southern Utah, in 1872 was an unexpected range extension, since the Smoky Hill River had been its previously known western limit. [[Catostomus Discobolus]] was reported from the Zuni River and Arizona, and the author notes that a previously named *Catostomus* had been reclassified from the Pacific-slope *Coregonus* group.

**Human use and conservation.** Several fishes were valued locally. The Utah Lake trout (*Salmo virginalis*) supplied a large share of settlers' subsistence. The author warns of heavy overfishing with fine-mesh nets, including by outside fishermen who sold small fish in Salt Lake City markets. He recommends laws governing net mesh and enforcement to protect the fishery. The report also notes that Mormon settlers called several species "mullet," "chub," and "sucker," and that the Utah chub (*Siboma atraria*) was sold in Salt Lake City markets.

## Key Findings

1. **New genera and species.** The collections allowed the author to establish three new genera and about eighteen new species, along with several varieties from western territories. Taxa named or redescribed include *Plagopterus argentissimus*, *Plagopterus jarrovii* (*Lepidomeda*), *Lepidomeda vittata*, *Apocope henshawi*, *Apocope coues*, *Apocope osculus*, *Gila tenia*, *Gila hydrophlox*, *Gila nigra*, *Siboma atraria*, *Ceratichthys squamilentus*, and *Uranidea wheeleri*. Taxon names are as given in the text.

2. ***Plagopterus argentissimus*.** This small silver fish, with a bony dorsal spine formed from two rays, was numerous in the [[San Luis Valley]] of western Colorado. It was the only known representative of its genus.

3. ***Lepidomeda vittata*.** A small silver minnow found in the Provo River, where it was "very abundant" and served as food for trout. The author notes that trout run up the river to spawn in June, and that the male builds the nest by using body movements to displace gravel.

4. **Salmonid and coregonid fisheries.** [[Salmo Virginalis]] (the Utah Lake trout) averaged about 14 inches and 1.5 pounds, with a maximum reported length of three feet and weight of about 15.5 pounds. Fish were sold at 10–15 cents wholesale and 20–30 cents retail per pound. [[Coregonus Williamsoni]] was a prized food fish that sold for about 20 cents per pound. Its presence in the Provo River was unexpected, given its Puget Sound affinities, and it appeared only in November.

5. **Economic importance of Sibama atraria.** This fish, called "mullet" by settlers, was abundant in Utah Lake and sold at about three cents per pound in Salt Lake City. The author notes that much of the catch was wasted because fish were sometimes discarded when not wanted.

6. **Parasites.** Trout from the Yellowstone region had large tapeworms (identified as *Dibothrium*, a section of the genus *Bothriocephalus*) in their flesh. Utah Lake trout were free of such parasites and of epidemic disease.

7. **Cope's Catostomid revision.** Cope proposed the genus [[Pantosteus]] for suckers combining the characters of *Catostomus* with a complete parietal fusion, which obliterates the fontanelle. He lists *P. platyrhynchus*, *P. jarrovii*, and *P. virescens* among the species of the present report.

8. **Conservation and future work.** The author expects the list of western species to grow with further exploration, and he calls for protective fishing laws to prevent collapse of the Utah Lake trout fishery.
