# Chronic Effects of Endrin on Bluntnose Minnows and Guppies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service research report (Research Report 58, 1962) by Donald Irvin Mount of [[Ohio State University]] tests the chronic toxicity of the insecticide [[Endrin]] to [[Bluntnose Minnow]] and [[Guppy]]. Neither species survived concentrations above about 0.5 parts per billion (ppb) for more than a few days. Sublethal exposure caused hyperactivity, convulsions and loss of equilibrium, consistent with central nervous system (CNS) disruption. Endrin did not significantly reduce oxygen consumption or swimming ability, and it accumulated in carp tissues, mainly the digestive tract and internal organs. The author concludes that endrin is not cumulative, that poisoned fish can recover, and that sublethal activity changes could disrupt spawning and expose fish to predation in natural waters.

## Background & Description

The report opens with a broad review of pesticide use in the United States, including [[Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane]] (DDT), [[Dieldrin]], and [[DDD]], and their effects on aquatic life. Mount cites several documented kills:

- **Louisiana bayou (1953):** a crashed spray aircraft carrying a little over 1 gallon of actual endrin killed all fishes and reptiles, including 42 alligators, over 35 miles.
- **Alabama (Young and Nicholson, 1951):** 15 major fish kills in one summer, with apparent total eradication of fish in a 13-mile section of Flint Creek, attributed to heavy rains washing insecticides from soil.
- **Clear Lake, California (Hunt and Bischoff, 1960):** DDD applications produced up to 2,275 parts per million (ppm) in the fat of apparently healthy [[Largemouth Bass]].

Endrin, a yellowish-white crystal (1,2,3,4,10,10-hexachloro-epoxy-octahydro-dimethanonaphthalene), was chosen for its extreme toxicity to fish and its increasing use since the early 1950s. Henderson, Pickering and Tarzwell (1959) found it "by far the most toxic" of the insecticides they tested to fish. Prior to this work, no chronic-effects studies on fish had been found in the literature.

The stated objectives were to determine:

1. The maximum concentration guppies and bluntnose minnows could survive for extended periods.
2. Reproductive and physiological effects of sublethal concentrations.
3. The port of entry into, and movement within, the fish body.
4. Differences in acute toxicity among size groups.
5. Potential danger to humans and other animals that eat exposed fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Test system.** Chronic tests used a continuous-flow apparatus built in a basement room with polyethylene walls and thermostatically controlled heaters. Water temperature was held at 22.2 °C (±1 °C). Ten reservoirs (each holding a 4.5-day supply) fed constant-level chambers and test chambers. Dechlorinated [[Columbus Tap Water]] was used, with mean pH 7.8 and mean total alkalinity 23.9 ppm (73 samples, April 1959 to January 1960). Test-chamber means were: total alkalinity 24.0 ppm, dissolved oxygen 6.2 ppm, and dissolved CO2 13 ppm.

**Endrin dosing.** Endrin was dissolved in 95% ethyl alcohol. Because the chemical assay was only sensitive to 5 ppb, [[Bioassay Methods]] using sensitive fish were used to check concentrations, detecting levels as low as 0.4 ppb. The author found the concentration in TB-7 to be about 0.5 ppb, and in TB-6 somewhat lower than 0.4 ppb. Aeration reduced endrin; fish in an aerated 5 ppb solution survived beyond 48 hours, while fish in an unaerated solution all died within 12 hours.

**Fish sources.** Test fish were collected from a pool in Rocky Fork Creek. Guppies came from a local tropical-fish store. Bluntnose minnows were parasitized by tapeworm metacercariae (Proteocephalus), which the author judged did not noticeably affect them.

**Guppy disease.** Guppy chronic testing ran alongside the minnow tests but had to be stopped on October 24, 1959, due to a dropsy-like disease (edema, enlarged kidneys) probably caused by a virus with secondary bacterial infection. This confounded later guppy data.

## Key Findings

### Chronic toxicity (bluntnose minnow)

- The chronic test ran **291 days** (April 11, 1959 to January 27, 1960) with five fish per chamber.
- At **0.5 ppb**, 9 of 14 fish (64.3%) died. At **0.4 ppb**, 5 of 10 (50%) died. These percentages were close to the 96-hour TL50 from acute tests.
- At **0.1 ppb**, fish lived without visible poisoning, and the mean daily growth was 0.102 mm, higher than the control (0.069 mm). All fish at this level were males, so the growth may be sex-linked, though females grew more than males in one chamber where both sexes were present.
- Fish at 0.9 and 0.75 ppb all died within 29 days.

**Note on survival figures:** The abstract reports that fewer than 50% of fish survived 0.5 ppb beyond 30 days and about 65% survived 0.4 ppb. The summary gives about 35% survival at 0.5 ppb and about 50% at 0.4 ppb. The discussion's counts (9 of 14 and 5 of 10 died) support the lower figures, so the summary's numbers are better aligned with the raw data.

**Symptom sequence.** Poisoning began with increased ventilation and rapid, jerky movements, then heightened sensitivity to stimuli, surfacing, rotation, convulsions, and loss of equilibrium, followed by death. Weaker concentrations prolonged symptoms over weeks. Some fish were hyperactive for up to 2 months, then returned to normal while still in endrin.

### Chronic toxicity (guppy)

- Guppies were tested at 0.9, 0.75, 0.5, 0.4, 0.25, and 0.1 ppb, with results complicated by disease.
- Symptoms matched those in minnows, except that guppies sank to the bottom, while bluntnose minnows rose to the surface. The author notes this reverses the normal resting and feeding positions of the two species.
- Pregnant females in 0.75 and 0.5 ppb produced no more than one litter, with one exception: a second litter of 12 included 6 young with large yolk sacs, probably premature.
- Concentrations of 0.5 ppb and above appear to have prevented reproduction in guppies.
- Young guppies that died in endrin showed skull bursting and brain protrusion, which the author links to pressure on the CNS.

### Acute toxicity

- In continuous-flow tests, the **96-hour TL50** ranged from **0.27 ppb** (fish 30 mm standard length) to **0.47 ppb** (fish 60 mm).
- The F value for between-group differences (28.75) exceeded the critical value (18.00), indicating size-related tolerance differences. The author judged the difference too small to matter for setting safe levels.
- Fish collected in winter were less tolerant than those collected in March, possibly due to reproductive stage, parasitism, temperature, or crowding.

### Oxygen consumption

- Endrin had **no significant effect** on oxygen consumption in seven bluntnose minnows (fed and unfed, both sexes). Increases were 25% or less above normal.
- Ventilation rates rose sharply, which the author interprets as respiratory distress from a stimulus other than oxygen deficiency, possibly metabolite accumulation.

### Swimming

- Endrin had little effect on the ability of acutely poisoned fish to swim against a current of about 9 inches per second. Fish stopped convulsing when the current started. The author notes a swifter current might have revealed a difference.

### Tissue distribution and entry (carp)

- Carp exposed to 2.5 to 10 ppb for 2.5 to 28 days accumulated endrin. Maximum tissue concentrations were about **160 times** the water concentration.
- A carp heart-spleen exposed 28 days to 2.5 ppb contained approximately **400 ppb**.
- Tissues with the highest levels were the digestive tract, liver, heart-spleen-blood, and kidney. Muscle was consistently low. Gills were low or negative.
- The author concludes endrin enters through the **intestine** (the fish drinks water), not the gills, and is transported by blood. Brain and fat also held measurable amounts.
- Muscle from a carp exposed to 2.5 ppb for 4 weeks, fed to two mice for 3 weeks, caused no weight loss or symptoms.

### Histology, mode of action and recovery

- Fatty vacuolation of liver cells appeared in many fish, including controls, likely from the high-fat diet. No tissue damage was found in the brain, heart, kidney, or spleen of surviving fish.
- Optic-disk measurements showed no significant papilledema-like swelling. Mount suggests fixation shrinkage may have masked any swelling.
- Five fish that were poisoned at 0.3 ppb and then moved to pure water recovered fully within days and appeared normal 3 months later.

### Conclusions and recommendations

- Endrin does not appear to be cumulative in fish under chronic exposure. A threshold lies narrowly between **0.4 and 0.5 ppb**, above which fish die and below which they show few physical effects.
- Increased activity at 0.5 and 0.4 ppb could disrupt spawning, displace fish from habitat, and increase predation.
- The author calls for chronic-toxicity studies of many insecticides on aquatic organisms, which he found absent from the literature. He also recommends research on residual persistence in sediments and tissues and on normal fish physiology.
- Application rates would need to be very low to protect fish. If endrin can control pests at rates safe for fish, it is preferable to a less toxic compound with greater cumulative effects.
