# Water for Birds, Fish & People: Devils Lake Inlet-Outlet Plan and Non-Native Fish Introduction Dispute

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1995 policy compilation by [[Gordon Berg]], North Dakota state representative and chairman of the Devils Lake-Sheyenne-Red River Advisory Committee. It promotes a comprehensive "inlet-and-outlet" water plan for [[Devils Lake]] and [[Stump Lake]] that uses the [[Sheyenne River]] to provide flood control and drought water, and a [[Ozone Treatment Plant]] to remove biota from [[Missouri River]] water before it enters the Hudson Bay drainage. The plan is framed against Canadian objections based on total dissolved solids (TDS) limits and fears of non-native fish transfer. The file also includes correspondence and resolutions on the proposed introduction of [[Zander]] (European pikeperch) into North Dakota, opposed by Manitoba, Wisconsin, and scientific bodies.

## Background & Description

The compilation spans correspondence from 1987 to 1995 and includes news clippings, letters from government officials, an American Fisheries Society (AFS) resolution, a USDA water-quality data sheet, and a 1980 newspaper special edition on waterfowl pollution. Berg argues that the Red River Basin was once under an ocean, leaving salts in the soil that leach into lakes and rivers.

Key project features proposed by Berg:
- A dam on the Sheyenne River, with a control gate at 1440 MSL, diverting water through a roughly 9-mile channel (about 1,500 cfs capacity) to Devils Lake.
- An optimum Devils Lake level of 1426-1429 MSL.
- An ozone treatment plant at [[McClusky]] with 300 cfs capacity (100 cfs for eastern North Dakota cities, 200 cfs for migratory birds at the Lonetree Wildlife Management Area).
- Estimated total cost of $76 million, compared with the [[Garrison Diversion Project]] at roughly ten times that amount.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salinity history.** Berg reports Devils Lake reached about 8,500 TDS near Creel Bay around 1900, and that fish could not survive in the lake in 1889 despite an elevation near 1430. Salts blown from the dry lakebed during the 1930s drought are re-entering the lake. Fish did not return until 1950. Current TDS is cited as about 1,800.

**Water quality (ND Department of Health, May 15, 1995).** Sampled sites ranged from West Bay (1,000 mg/L TDS) to East Devils Lake (7,570 mg/L TDS, sulfate 3,770 mg/L). Sulfate and TDS rise steadily from west to east.

**Inflow quality (USGS, Water Year 1993).** Eleven coulee and lake inflows ranged from 329 to 593 mg/L TDS, averaging 436 mg/L. Missouri River water is reported at 420-600 mg/L TDS.

**Biota and disease.** Berg notes that the Devils Lake basin (Hudson Bay) and Missouri basin (Gulf of Mexico) have distinct biota. [[Striped Bass]] were stocked in Devils Lake in 1977 (13,000 fish). Only four were caught afterward, and the species is believed not to reproduce there. Botulism and Newcastle's disease in migratory birds are linked to water quality.

**Waterfowl waste.** A 1980 Minot newspaper special edition estimated that 100,000 ducks at J. Clark Salyer Refuge deposited 4,320 pounds of solid waste daily, more than double the daily total solids from Minot's treated sewage (2,093 pounds).

## Key Findings

- **Canadian objections:** Canada will reject transfers containing non-native fish or diseases, and water above 500 mg/L TDS, under the 1909 Boundary Waters Treaty.
- **Inlet necessity:** Berg argues an outlet-only plan will concentrate salts and cause a fish kill, and that federal emergency funding is more likely if the inlet is included.
- **Proposed flood benefit:** Diverting Sheyenne flood water could save about $2 million in annual flood damages.
- **Energy saving:** Reduced need for treated Missouri River water could save about $1 million per year in electricity costs.
- **Flow data:** Sheyenne flows were about 150,000 acre-feet in 1950 (high), about 6,000 acre-feet in 1961 (low), and average about 40,000 acre-feet over 35 years.
- **Stump Lake outcome:** Berg projects Stump Lake TDS falling from 7,000 to 2,000 mg/L over 10 years, with about 20,000 acre-feet released annually.
- **Fish reproduction threshold:** Fish will reproduce in water under 800 TDS.
- **Cost:** Total project estimate is $76 million (ozone plant $30M, Sheyenne dam $20M, Devils Lake channel $11M, Stump Lake channel $3M, property and engineering $10M, pipeline $2M).
- **Agency roles:** Berg proposes the US Army Corps of Engineers build the inlets and outlets, the Bureau of Reclamation build the ozone plant, and the US Fish and Wildlife Service manage Lonetree.
- **Zander opposition:** The [[American Fisheries Society]] (1987 resolution), Manitoba (1987, 1990), Saskatchewan (1991, supporting a non-native species law), Arkansas (1991), and Wisconsin (1991) all urged caution or opposed introduction. Wisconsin cited the risk of [[Pike Fry Rhabdovirus]] and hybridization with walleye.
- **Scientific critique:** Peter Moyle (UC Davis, 1988) argued zander would compete with walleye and deplete shared prey, and that the environmental assessment was biased toward introduction.
- **Policy requests:** Berg sought a North Dakota law barring non-native fish introduction without consent from bordering states and provinces, and a formal agreement with Manitoba to that effect.
- **Outcome:** Governor Sinner put the zander program on hold, and the [[Non-Native Fish Introduction Council]] was formed. Berg attributed the stalled [[Garrison Diversion Project]] to fish introductions, and the 1995 lake-level crisis led to requests for a Presidential Disaster Declaration.
