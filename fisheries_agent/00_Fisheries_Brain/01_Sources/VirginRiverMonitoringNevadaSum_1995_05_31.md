## Executive Summary
This memorandum summarizes the 1995 annual fish monitoring survey conducted by the Virgin River Recovery Team at three Nevada sites — [[Bunkerville Diversion]], [[Riverside Bridge]], and [[Halfway Wash]] — on 25 April 1995. Sampling targeted native fishes of conservation concern, particularly the endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and the [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*), alongside native suckers and [[Speckled Dace]]. Results indicated a dramatic reduction in native fish abundance compared to historical surveys: only a single marked Woundfin was captured across all sites, and no Roundtail Chub or Speckled Dace were detected anywhere. Native suckers were captured only at Bunkerville Diversion. In contrast, the nonnative [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) dominated catches numerically at all three sites, suggesting continued competitive/predatory pressure on native Virgin River fishes. High winter/spring flows (~500–600 cfs) had scoured the riverbed, increasing cobble substrate, and water clarity was poor (highly turbid) throughout the survey period.

## Background & Description
The report does not provide detailed morphometric or meristic data for individual specimens; data are presented as site-level abundance counts by species code. Species recorded (via standard codes) include:

- **PLAR** — *Plagopterus argentissimus* ([[Woundfin]]): federally endangered cyprinid endemic to the Virgin River system; only a single marked individual was captured during the entire survey.
- **GIRO** — *Gila robusta seminuda* ([[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]]): none captured at any site.
- **CACL** — *Catostomus clarkii* ([[Desert Sucker]]): captured only at Bunkerville Diversion (n=3).
- **CALA** — *Catostomus latipinnis* ([[Flannelmouth Sucker]]): captured only at Bunkerville Diversion (n=3).
- **RHOS** — *Rhinichthys osculus* ([[Speckled Dace]]): none captured at any site.
- **CYLU** — *Cyprinella lutrensis* ([[Red Shiner]], nonnative): overwhelmingly dominant, with 853 individuals at Bunkerville Diversion, 403 at Riverside Bridge, and 35 at Halfway Wash.
- **GAAF** — *Gambusia affinis* ([[Western Mosquitofish]], nonnative): none captured at any site.

Streamflow during sampling ranged from ~500–600 cfs, with high turbidity at all sites. Scouring from elevated winter/spring flows increased cobble prevalence relative to prior surveys, though sand remained the dominant substrate, particularly at Halfway Wash (100%). Permanent riparian overstory was limited to only a few sample reaches. The [[USGS Gauge at Riverside]] was noted to be 200 yards from the nearest water at the time of survey, indicating substantial channel/flow shift.

## Key Findings
- Only **one marked Woundfin** was captured across all three Nevada monitoring sites during the two-day survey — a stark decline from historical catch levels.
- **No Roundtail Chub or Speckled Dace** were captured at any of the three sites (Bunkerville Diversion, Riverside Bridge, Halfway Wash).
- Native suckers (Desert Sucker and Flannelmouth Sucker) were captured **only at Bunkerville Diversion** (3 each), and were absent at Riverside Bridge and Halfway Wash.
- The nonnative **Red Shiner (CYLU)** was by far the most abundant species at every site (853, 403, 35 individuals respectively), highlighting a potential ecological threat to native Virgin River fish populations via competition and/or predation.
- Overall native fish abundance across all species was **markedly reduced** compared to past survey years.
- Habitat conditions were consistent with post-flood scouring: increased cobble, turbid water, and elevated flows (500–600 cfs) during sampling.
- Sampling protocol deviated slightly from standard methodology — a 4' x 10' seine was used to sample 30–40 m² micro-habitats, with longer/wider hauls than usual due to low fish density and high water availability.
- These findings are relevant to ongoing [[Virgin River Recovery Team]] efforts to monitor and protect the endangered Woundfin and Virgin River Roundtail Chub populations amid habitat alteration and nonnative species pressure.
