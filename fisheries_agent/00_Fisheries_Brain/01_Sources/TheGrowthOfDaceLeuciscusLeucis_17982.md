# Growth of Dace, Roach and Chub in Willow Brook, Northamptonshire

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 study in the *Journal of Fish Biology* by D. [[Cragg-Hine]] and J. W. Jones examines the annual growth cycles and overall growth rates of three cyprinid fishes, [[Dace]], [[Roach]] and [[Chub]], in [[Willow Brook]], a tributary of the [[River Nene]]. Fish were sampled by [[Electrofishing]] from late 1960 to 1962 across four river sections. Ages were estimated from [[Scale Reading]], whose validity the authors confirmed for each species by showing that [[Scale Check]] formation occurs once a year over a short period. Growth in all three species followed a similar seasonal cycle, with rapid growth from spring to autumn and little growth in winter. Growth rates varied between sections and years. The authors link this variation to population density and competition, and link differences between years to water temperature.

## Background & Description

Freshwater fishery management in Britain had focused mainly on salmonids. The authors argue that the biology of coarse fish, and especially cyprinids, needed more study. Willow Brook is a small, polluted-then-recovering stream. It rises near Corby and enters the Nene at Elton after about 18 miles. Its headwaters receive sewage and steel works effluent, and self-purification and cooling occur in lakes at Deenethorpe and Blatherwycke.

Fish were sampled at monthly intervals from November 1960 to September 1962 (the introduction gives a range to autumn 1962). Four sections were defined: Apethorpe, Woodnewton, Fotheringhay and Elton. Nineteen fish species were recorded. The three focal species were the most abundant coarse fish, alongside [[Perch]] and [[Pike]]. Other species included [[Gudgeon]], [[Minnow]], [[Stone Loach]] and [[Bullhead]], which were collected for a separate study.

Methods included:
- **Electrofishing:** an a.c. generator (2.5 kW, 250 V) for deeper or faster water, and a non-pulsing d.c. machine (1.5 kW, 450 V) for small fish in quiet water.
- **Processing:** fish were preserved in 5% formaldehyde. Length was measured to the nearest millimetre and weight to the nearest gram. Gonad development, gut contents and scales were recorded.
- **Scale sampling:** scales taken from just above the lateral line, below the dorsal fin, were judged most satisfactory.

The authors define key terms, including year class, age group (Roman numerals with "+" for partial progression), growth rings, scale check, nucleus, erosion and spawning mark.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry (March 1963, Fotheringhay):** pH 8.0; calcium 180.0 mg/l; magnesium 21.9 mg/l; sodium 60.0 mg/l; potassium 25.0 mg/l; chloride 82.5 mg/l; sulphate 348.0 mg/l; nitrate nitrogen 7.2 mg/l. Alkalinity as calcium carbonate was 217.0 mg/l.

**Physical habitat:** the brook alternated between shallow gravelly riffles and slow, deep, silted sections. Average width was about 15 feet and average depth about 2 feet 6 inches. The 25-year average flow at the Fotheringhay measuring flume to September 1963 was 23.79 cubic feet per second.

**Vegetation:** *Cladophora glomerata* covered the bed year-round, and *Enteromorpha intestinalis* grew heavily in spring and summer. *Ranunculus fluitans* and *R. pseudofluitans* formed thick beds in faster reaches. *Myriophyllum* spp. and *Callitriche stagnalis* occurred near the banks.

**Barriers and distribution:** no dace were found above a weir about three-quarters of a mile upstream of Fotheringhay, despite suitable habitat, which the authors attribute to an insurmountable barrier. Dace were abundant in the lowest half mile before the Nene confluence. Chub had been heavily reduced by electrofishing by the Nene River Board before 1960, and the later chub at Elton were thought to have come from repopulation from the Nene.

**Water temperature:** October 1959 temperatures in the brook ranged from 15°C to 18°C, against a normal range of 9.5°C to 13°C. Summer 1958 was cooler than normal.

## Key Findings

**Ageing validity and seasonal growth**
- Scale checks in [[Dace]] are formed in May or June. The check is marked by a "cutting-over" of earlier rings in the latero-posterior scale region. After it forms, scale growth is rapid until mid-autumn and then almost stops until spring.
- Young dace grew most of their 1961 length between April and July. Growth slowed from early autumn until spring, and rapid growth resumed in May and June 1962.
- [[Roach]] laid down checks mainly in June and July, and most scale growth occurred from June to September/October.
- [[Chub]] formed checks mainly in May or June. Growth was rapid from spring to August/September, then slow for the rest of the year.
- For all three species, the authors fixed a "birthday" date (1 June for dace and roach, 1 May for chub) to assign age groups.
- Older dace had less-defined, closely spaced checks and more variable check timing, but the authors still accepted scale reading as valid.

**Dace**
- Of 18 monthly dace samples, the largest counts were mostly from Elton and Fotheringhay. The sample table includes 14 to 70 males and 13 to 57 females per month.
- Male dace grew faster than females at both Elton and Fotheringhay, most markedly in middle life after maturity.
- Dace grew faster at Fotheringhay for the first five or six years. After this, Elton dace grew faster, and by years seven or eight mean lengths were similar.
- Elton had more small dace, and intraspecific competition was proposed as the cause of the poorer early and middle-life growth there. Interspecific competition with chub and perch, whose diets overlap with dace, was also possible.
- Dace growth in Willow Brook was better than in the River Thames but poorer than in the River Funshion and the River Cam/Shepreth Brook.

**Roach**
- Roach were abundant at all stations. Some catches (January, June and November 1961) were sub-sampled to about 100 fish.
- A dominant 1956 year class was present. It was still prominent in February 1966, when half the fish in one sample were IX+. The progression of this class through age groups each June was taken as confirmation that scale reading is valid.
- Scale growth varied greatly between years. The 1956 class had poor growth in its third and fifth years and good growth in its fourth. The authors attributed poor growth in 1958 to cool summer conditions and good growth in 1959 to warm temperatures, citing parallel observations by Kempe (1962) and Williams (1967).
- From the fourth year onward, female roach grew considerably faster than males at all stations.
- Roach grew fastest at Apethorpe, where roach were the only large-number species and pike and chub were more common. Growth was similar across the other three stations for the first three years, then better at Elton and Fotheringhay than Woodnewton.
- Roach were far more abundant at Woodnewton and Fotheringhay than at Apethorpe. The authors suggest intraspecific competition there suppressed growth. They propose that earlier [[Electrofishing]] by the River Board removed predators such as pike and chub, increasing roach numbers but reducing size.
- Roach growth at Apethorpe was about average among British and Continental waters, and growth at the other stations was below average.
- Erosion on roach scales was observed at spawning time and is interpreted as a [[Spawning Mark]], following Jones (1953).

**Chub**
- Chub numbers were small because of earlier electrofishing. The 1960–62 samples totalled 105 males and 94 females, spread across months and stations, and most were from Elton.
- Scale checks in chub consist of two or three narrowly spaced irregular rings. The first annual check can be faint and close to the nucleus, so it is easily missed in older fish. Small fish of 70 to 80 mm taken in autumn 1961 confirmed a true first annual check.
- A dominant 1959 year class was followed through successive age groups. It was in group II+ from May 1961 to April 1962 and in III+ from May 1962.
- Female chub grew faster than males, in agreement with Lelek (1959) and Libosvarsky (1959).
- Chub growth in Willow Brook was better than in the Rokytna River and similar to the Svratka River, but poorer than in the Danube and Elbe (Reichenbach-Klinke, 1962).

**Summary conclusions**
- The three species showed a similar annual cycle in which scale growth and body growth were synchronized, and the scale check coincided with the onset of rapid spring growth.
- Variation in roach scale growth between years was linked to summer temperature.
- Poor growth in parts of the brook is likely the result of increased competition from greater fish abundance.
