# Restriction Endonuclease Analysis of Salmonid Mitochondrial DNA

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 study by [[Berg]] and [[Ferris]] applies [[Restriction Endonuclease Analysis]] to the [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) of four salmonids to test their systematic relationships. The authors estimate the [[Salmonid Mitochondrial Genome]] at about 16,670 base pairs (bp), slightly larger than the human mtDNA. Cladistic and phenetic analyses place [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] as closest relatives, with [[Brown Trout]] more distant and [[Brook Trout]] the most divergent. The results support the existing generic framework and suggest that the subgenus *Parasalmo* could be given coordinate rank with *Oncorhynchus*.

## Background & Description

The subfamily [[Salmoninae]] contains 25–35 species, provisionally divided into two tribes: Salmonini and Salvelini. Earlier work based on morphology, biogeography, paleontology, karyology, physiology, embryology, and [[Nuclear DNA Hybridization]] supports the broad generic outlines but leaves many species-level questions open. [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] offers finer resolution but is limited, partly because of the tetraploid origin of salmonids (see [[Tetraploidy]]).

The study uses mtDNA for several reasons:
- Restriction endonucleases cut DNA at short palindromic recognition sites of four to six nucleotides.
- Animal mtDNA is a covalently closed circular duplex that is maternally inherited and genetically autonomous (see [[Maternal Inheritance]]).
- It occurs in about 1,000 copies per cell and evolves 5–10 times faster than single-copy nuclear DNA.
- It lacks many complicating features of nuclear DNA, so it gives a more direct view of genotype.

The four species were:
- *Salvelinus fontinalis* (brook trout), a member of tribe Salvelini and used as the out-group.
- *Oncorhynchus tshawytscha* (chinook salmon).
- *Salmo (Salmo) trutta* (brown trout).
- *Salmo (Parasalmo) gairdneri* (rainbow trout).

Six rainbow trout, known to be at least maternal half-sibs, came from the UC Davis hatchery stock RTD. One brown trout and one brook trout came from California Department of Fish and Game hatcheries. Two chinook salmon were caught at sea.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a molecular systematics study and does not report ecological field data such as population sizes, habitat conditions, or water chemistry. Its ecological relevance lies in its use for stock identification of commercially important fishes ([[Stock Identification]]) and for population genetics.

Mitochondria are described as autonomous organelles that, in higher animals, do not undergo recombination. They are maternally inherited and mutate rapidly, so they give an independent record of evolution.

Within-species mtDNA variation in vertebrates is generally 0–3%. In a survey of 14 [[Skipjack Tuna]] (*Katsuwonus pelamis*), sequence variation was below 1%. The authors note that within-population variation in salmonids remains to be studied.

## Key Findings

**Genome size and fragment patterns**
- The SacII enzyme gave two fragments of equivalent mobility in all four species, which the authors take as strong evidence of equal genome size.
- The salmonid mtDNA genome is estimated at about 16,670 bp, roughly 100 bp larger than human mtDNA.
- The mean genome size from the sum of accurately estimated fragments is 16,062 bp, about 4% less than the SacII-based estimate.
- Thirteen enzymes gave fragment sizes in Tables 1–2 (excluding MboI and HinfI). Across the 15 enzymes used, each species had a distinct pattern, except that the two chinook salmon were identical.
- The six-base enzymes HindIII and XbaI showed a high degree of homology between rainbow trout and chinook salmon. HindIII gave an identical pattern for these two species.
- Four-base enzymes (MboI and HinfI) produced more fragments because four-base sequences occur more frequently than six-base sequences.

**Shared patterns**
- Rainbow trout and chinook salmon shared identical patterns for four enzymes and only two patterns with brown trout, and one with brook trout.

**Phylogenetic analysis**
- Character data: 272 fragment characters, of which 16 were invariant, leaving 256 variable characters. Of these, 198 were unique and 58 were phylogenetically informative.
- The shortest network (a [[Wagner Network]]) required 274 character-state changes, 18 more than the ideal lower bound of 256. Other possible networks required at least 287 changes and were rejected by [[Parsimony Analysis]].
- Of the 274 changes, 238 characters fit single nucleotide substitutions. The 18 characters identified as nonhomologous (homoplastic) were removed.
- The network was rooted with brook trout as the out-group.
- Phylogenetic analysis places *O. tshawytscha* and *S. gairdneri* as sister taxa, with *S. trutta* as their outgroup among the three Salmonini members. Brook trout is the most distantly related of the four species.

**Sequence divergence**
- Estimated divergence (using the [[Comparative Fragment Method]] and [[UPGMA Clustering]]):
  - *S. gairdneri* vs. *O. tshawytscha*: 3.4%
  - Brown trout vs. the Pacific salmonids: 7.2%
  - Brook trout vs. the other species: approximately 10%
- The 3.4% value is within the range reported for mammalian conspecifics and congenerics.

**Homoplasy and mutation bias**
- 31% (18 of 58) of the phylogenetically informative characters may reflect multiple substitutions within the same restriction site.
- The authors cite evidence from [[Sequence Divergence]] studies that mtDNA shows a bias toward [[Transition Bias]] over transversions. This makes homoplasy more likely in more divergent taxa.
- The comparative fragment method may underestimate divergence above 5%, but estimates were considered reasonably reliable.

**Taxonomic implications**
- The mtDNA results support the close relationship of *Oncorhynchus* and *Salmo (Parasalmo)* and their status as a sister group to *Salmo (Salmo)*.
- The authors argue that *Parasalmo* should be given coordinate rank with *Oncorhynchus* if nomenclature is meant to reflect phylogeny.

**Applications**
- The authors conclude that the method could help resolve systematic problems and assist fishery scientists in identifying commercially important stocks.
