# Chamberlain's 1904 Survey of Arizona Fishes, with Annotations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annotated publication reproduces the unpublished 1904 field manuscript of [[Frederic Morton Chamberlain]], an assistant with the U.S. Fish Commission (later the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries), who surveyed fishes and aquatic habitats across Arizona from 15 January to 28 April 1904, with about 30 days in the field concentrated in the [[Gila River Basin]]. The editor, [[W. L. Minckley]], supplies footnotes, tables and modern comparisons. The main result is a historic baseline: more than half of the sites and stream reaches Chamberlain visited had become dry, or were so modified that native fishes were greatly reduced or extirpated. Chamberlain attributed declines to overgrazing, flooding, erosion, irrigation diversion, mining pollution and powder fishing, and these stressors largely persist today.

## Background & Description

- **Author and context:** Chamberlain (1867–1921) was one of the first bona fide aquatic biologists to study Arizona fishes in detail. His 1904 assignment came from [[Barton Warren Evermann]], his former mentor.
- **Manuscript:** A handwritten, 52-page report held in the [[Smithsonian Institution Archives]] (RU 7258). It was a preliminary draft, reproduced nearly verbatim with punctuation and clarifications added by the editor.
- **Voucher specimens:** Housed in the [[United States National Museum]] (USNM). They have supported later studies of regional fish systematics and evolution.
- **Sampling:** More than 20 localities, labelled as numbered stations (I–XVIII and subdivisions), were visited. Methods included seining, dip-netting, hook-and-line and the use of explosives ("powder").
- **Editorial conventions:** Temperatures were kept in degrees Fahrenheit. Discharge was given in "miner's inches," converted at 1 miner's inch ≈ 0.025 cubic feet per second (cfs). Table 2 matches Chamberlain's names to current common and scientific names.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish occurrence (Table 1):**
- Historically, an average of 5.7 native species were recorded or expected at visited sites.
- Chamberlain physically sampled sites and recorded an average of 3.2 kinds.
- In the 1990s, only an average of 2.3 species remained at the same localities.

**Non-native species:**
- Chamberlain recorded only seven non-native species.
- More than 40 non-native fishes are now known from the Gila River basin alone.
- Across Arizona, [[Fuller, Nico, and Williams (1999)]] listed 84 translocated fish species.
- The editor identifies predatory and competing alien species as the most important factor in native fish decline.

**Conservation status:**
- Of the 16 native species Chamberlain caught, one is extinct and eight are listed as Threatened or Endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].
- Eleven are listed in Mexico.
- Most of the rest are considered imperiled by state agencies.

**Stressors identified by Chamberlain:**
- **Land use:** Overgrazing removed the herbaceous mulch, which increased floods, erosion and sedimentation, filled pools and raised water temperatures.
- **Irrigation:** Diversion dams blocked migration and led fish into unscreened ditches.
- **Groundwater:** Pumping lowered water tables and caused springs and wells to fail.
- **Mining:** Concentrator wastes and tailings were linked to fish kills, and powder fishing removed fish.
- **Climate:** Chamberlain suggested climate change, though the editor notes this is uncertain.

**Editorial notes on history:**
- Common carp, catfish and black bass were introduced during the late 1800s and early 1900s.
- Dams such as [[Roosevelt Dam]] (1911) and Coolidge Dam (1929) further altered flow regimes.

## Key Findings

1. **Historic baseline:** Chamberlain's 1904 data document conditions before major regional development. More than half of the sites he visited were dry or drastically modified by the 1990s.
2. **Native fish richness declined:** Average native species per site fell from 5.7 historically and 3.2 sampled in 1904 to 2.3 in the 1990s.
3. **Taxonomic value:** Twelve of the fifteen native fishes then recognized from the Gila basin had been described from localities he visited. He secured eight taxa at or near type localities and three species unrecognized by science at the time.
4. **Drivers of decline:** Land-use change, water diversion, groundwater depletion, mining pollution and introduced species are identified as the main threats, and the editor states they remain in effect today.
5. **Specific observations:**
   - [[Colorado Squawfish]] (salmon) and [[Razorback Sucker]] were still present in the lower Colorado and Gila rivers in 1904 but were extirpated or nearly so later.
   - [[Bonytail]] was common in the lower Colorado.
   - [[Monkey Spring]] held a native pupfish and Gila chub, and its isolation was later confirmed genetically.
   - [[Apache Trout]] was reported from the East Verde and Tonto drainages, although hybridization with introduced rainbow trout may have occurred.
6. **Recommendations (Chamberlain's):** Restocking was not feasible under then-current climatic and industrial conditions. He suggested limiting laws for trout streams and better pond management with fewer fish and provision of food.
