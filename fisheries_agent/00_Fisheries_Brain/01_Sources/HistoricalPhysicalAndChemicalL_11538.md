# Historical, Physical, and Chemical Limnology of Twin Lakes, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation report (REC-ERC-77-13, September 1977) documents the preoperation physical and chemical limnology of [[Twin Lakes]], a connected pair of glacially dammed montane lakes in central Colorado. The study forms the baseline for assessing the effects of the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]], a 200-MW facility then under construction within the [[Fryingpan-Arkansas Project]]. Nearly five years of field data (1971–1976) on light, temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, redox potential, sediment and water chemistry, and hydrology are synthesized. The central conclusion is that the upper lake has entered an advanced eutrophic state without passing through a mesotrophic phase, a condition the authors term "trophic disequilibrium," driven by allochthonous organic debris and winter hypolimnetic oxygen depletion. A winter oxygenation experiment was planned for 1976–77 to test this hypothesis.

## Background & Description

**Location and origin.** Twin Lakes lies on [[Lake Creek]] at the eastern foot of the Sawatch Range, at 2802 m (9193 ft) elevation, 39°05' N and 106°20' W, in the Montane/Canadian life zone. The lakes originated through morainic damming of Lake Creek, classed as type 30a in [[Hutchinson Lake Classification]] (1957).

**Morphometry.** Maximum surface areas are 263.4 ha (651 acres) for the upper lake and 736.5 ha (1820 acres) for the lower. Maximum depths are about 28 m (upper) and 27 m (lower). The lower lake is described as the largest natural mountain lake in Colorado.

**Historical background.**
- 1873–1874: First scientific mention by [[Hayden Survey]] reports, noting glacial origin and since-vanished features (a waterfall, a marshy meadow, and a roughly 2 m elevation difference between lakes).
- 1889: [[David Starr Jordan]] recorded suckers, minnows, and two native trout, including the yellowfin trout (*Salmo clarki macdonaldi*), then described as indigenous to the lakes.
- 1901: The lakes were converted to irrigation storage, permitting a 7.8 m fluctuation in the lower lake.
- 1902–1903: [[Chauncey Juday]] surveyed the lakes for the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries. Four new fish species had been introduced, including [[Mackinaw Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*), and the yellowfin trout had become extremely rare.
- 1935: The [[Twin Lakes Tunnel]] was completed, diverting Roaring Fork water into Lake Creek.
- 1957: [[Mysis Shrimp]] (*Mysis relicta*) were introduced into the lower lake from Clearwater Lake, Minnesota. Success was evident by 1969.
- 1958–1961: Nolting studied lake trout; Daphnia and the Colorado speckled dace had become extremely rare or disappeared.

**Study context.** The study began in May 1971 and was coordinated with the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] and the [[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] at Colorado State University. It is linked to parallel work at [[Banks Lake]], Washington, and the [[Salt River Project Lakes]], Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** The Lake Creek basin covers about 238 km². Mean maximum flow in 1971–75 was about 32.5 m³/s, and winter flows averaged about 0.3 m³/s. Average total annual runoff was 154.365 × 10⁶ m³ (125,145 acre-ft). Estimated flushing times at maximum elevation were about 3 months for the upper lake and 9 months for the lower. A June peak inflow would flush the upper lake in 15 days or less, while winter flows would take more than 2 years (upper) and over 6 years (lower). The upper lake acts as a settling basin for the lower.

**Light.** Mean light extinction coefficients for 1972–1975 were 0.63 m⁻¹ (upper) and 0.47 m⁻¹ (lower). Upper-lake transparency correlated strongly with inflow (r = 0.99). Compared with 16 other lakes in the literature, Twin Lakes ranks as relatively clear. Secchi transparency declined since Juday's 1902–1903 work, probably due to eutrophication and increased flows after the tunnel.

**Temperature.** Both lakes are classed as second-class dimictic lakes. Spring overturn occurs at about 4 °C. Lake Creek enters the upper lake roughly 4 °C cooler than the surface. Fall overturn occurs about two weeks earlier in the lower lake. Winter ice reaches 600–900 mm.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Oxygen is adequate year-round except near the bottom during stratification. Mean upper-lake bottom minima were about 6% saturation in winter and 45% in summer; lower-lake minima were about 26% (winter) and 31% (summer). The upper lake reached zero bottom oxygen in April 1975. Heavy allochthonous woody debris and loss of the oxygenating Lake Creek inflow in winter explain this pattern.

**Sediments.** Sediments are fine-grained glacial rock flour with a three-layered profile. The average sedimentation rate is 0.63 mm/year. Iron and manganese dominate heavy metal content. Mean sediment iron was 38.10 mg/g (upper) and 31.81 mg/g (lower). Metal enrichment is judged to be of natural origin, derived from mineralized South Fork headwaters rather than mining.

**Water chemistry.** Waters are soft, dilute calcium bicarbonate lakes, with pH neutral to slightly basic. Mean TDS was 67.4 mg/L (upper) and 68.5 mg/L (lower), compared with about 120 mg/L for average world rivers. Major anions are bicarbonate (about 25 mg/L) and sulfate (about 14 mg/L), and the principal cation is calcium (about 10 mg/L). Lake Creek inflow is about 50% more saline than the lakes, mainly because of sulfate.

**Heavy metals in the water column.** Manganese and lead concentrations in lake water are an order of magnitude higher than in inflow and outflow, indicating internal release from sediments under reducing conditions. Copper and zinc peak at spring overturn, consistent with Hutchinson's ferrous sulfide explanation.

## Key Findings

- **Light:** Twin Lakes are relatively clear by literature standards (mean extinction coefficients 0.63 and 0.47 m⁻¹), but transparency has declined since 1902–1903.
- **Stratification:** Both lakes are dimictic, with strong summer stratification and inverse winter stratification.
- **Oxygen depletion:** Upper-lake bottom waters reach near-anoxia in winter (zero mg/L in April 1975). Spring and fall overturns restore bottom oxygen to 100% or more of saturation.
- **Redox:** Bottom Eh in the upper lake fell below 200 mV in winter 1974–75, favoring release of ferrous iron and manganese from sediments.
- **Metals:** Iron, manganese, zinc, copper, and lead accumulate in sediments. The South Fork of Lake Creek is the major metal source, with inflow surveys showing high Fe, Mn, Cu, and Zn concentrations (for example, up to 42.00 mg/L Fe at site S-2 in August 1976).
- **Trophic state:** The upper lake is interpreted as being in "trophic disequilibrium," having moved directly to advanced eutrophy because of organic debris from human-altered hydrology. The lower lake is oligotrophic to slightly mesotrophic.
- **Management plan:** Winter hypolimnetic oxygenation of the upper lake, planned for 1976–77, will test whether reducing conditions and metal release can be ameliorated, with the lower lake serving as a control.
- **Pumped-storage baseline:** The dataset provides preoperation baseline information against which the effects of the Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant will be evaluated.
