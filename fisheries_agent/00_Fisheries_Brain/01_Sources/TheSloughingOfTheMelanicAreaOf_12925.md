# Sloughing of the Melanic Area of the Dorsal Fin: An Ontogenetic Process in Tylosurus raphidoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #belonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 Bulletin of the [[American Museum of Natural History]] article by C. M. Breder Jr. and Priscilla Rasquin examines how the juvenile dorsal fin of [[Tylosurus Raphidoma]] (a [[Needlefish]] in the order [[Synentognathi]]) is converted into the adult form. The juvenile fin is high and black posteriorly; the adult fin is light and high anteriorly. The authors combined morphometric data from 279 specimens, field collection records and [[Histology]] to show that the change is not caused by differential growth or resorption but by direct sloughing off of the melanic distal fin. The transformation occurs at roughly six months of age and 200–300 mm standard length, well before sexual maturity (not under 600 mm). The paper also compares the disappearing mandibular lappets and barbels of related synentognaths and reports the absence of taste buds in these structures.

## Background & Description

- **Order context:** Juvenile synentognaths (Belonidae, Hemiramphidae, Exocoetidae) often bear mandibular lappets or barbels and a dark, high posterior dorsal fin. These features regress long before sexual maturity and are not linked to maturing gonads.
- **Prior work:** [[Breder (1932)]] first documented the transformation, using material from the Carnegie Institution's Dry Tortugas laboratory. Further material was obtained in 1950 at the [[Lerner Marine Laboratory]], Bimini, Bahamas.
- **Study material:** Table 1 lists measurements from three regional series: southern Florida and Puerto Rico (10 specimens), North Carolina (19 specimens) and Bimini (about 40 specimens, collected 1948–1950). Table 2 lists collection dates and sizes across about 13 localities. Standard lengths ranged from roughly 35 mm to 327 mm.
- **Comparative material:** Barbels of [[Cypselurus Cyanopterus]] (155.5 mm specimen, barbel 51.5 mm) and [[Cypselurus Furcatus]] (52 mm specimen), plus the mandibular lappets of Tylosurus.
- **Funding and acknowledgement:** The melanosis work was partly supported by the [[American Cancer Society]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Juvenile behavior:** Small juveniles hide among floating debris and resemble twigs or stems (at Tortugas, Cymodocea-like stems; on the Florida west coast, vegetation bits; at Bimini, needles of the introduced [[Australian Pine]], *Casuarina equisetifolia*). They match the colour of their associates closely. This behavior disappears before transformation.
- **Post-transformation behavior:** Adults move into deeper, open, clear water, where they feed in schools of [[Jenkinsia]] and [[Atherina]] and feed almost continually.
- **Stomach contents:** Table 3 reports contents of 18 individuals (177–237 mm standard length, Bimini, late August 1950). Of 9 fish with recognizable remains, 9 contained fish (Jenkinsia, Atherina, *Synodus*?) and 4 contained Crustacea (shrimp, callinectid). Remarkably, a dozen fish of 150 mm or less with intact black fins had no recognizable remains.
- **Seasonality:** Breeding was inferred to occur mainly from March to July. Transitional individuals appear in summer and fall.
- **Parasitism:** Cysts of an unidentified worm were found near the fin ray bases in some disintegrating fins.

## Key Findings

1. **Mechanism:** Gross changes are attributed to direct sloughing off of the distal fin. Differential growth and resorption are considered minor or negligible, though they may occur at a low level.
2. **Sequence of breakdown:** Melanophores break down first, then the fin membrane (epithelium detaches, connective tissue becomes pycnotic), and finally the fin rays snap off at their articulations, leaving two or three basal segments.
3. **No phagocytosis:** No macrophages were found in the disintegrating fin. Pigment is eliminated directly through the surface epithelium, with no phagocytosis.
4. **Timing:** The transformation occurs at about six months of age and at 200–300 mm standard length, and is completed before the fish reach half their adult size. Sexual maturity is not reached before 600 mm and about one to one and a half years of age (Haiti data; none under 700 mm mature at Tortugas).
5. **Hormonal independence:** Because the change happens long before maturity, the authors conclude it is not controlled by endocrine changes at puberty.
6. **Proposed trigger:** A possible psychogenic basis is suggested. Loss of optical fixation on dark twigs may lead to breakdown of melanophore concentration, followed by membrane shredding and ray snapping. This remains untested.
7. **Vascular hypothesis:** Histology points to selective failure of blood supply, but no constriction by the lepidotrichia was observed, and the mechanism remains unresolved.
8. **Comparison with melanoma:** The fin's melanic stage resembles the "second stage of melanosis" in *Platypoecilus–Xiphophorus* hybrids, but the Tylosurus process is confined and shows no neoplastic overgrowth.
9. **Mandibular lappets and barbels:** Histological study shows these structures are eliminated by resorption and are controlled by smooth muscle. Coarse granular eosinophiles are abundant in involuting tissue and may relate to parasitism, resorption or fat handling. The authors note that no taste buds were found, a condition they regard as apparently unique among teleost oral appendages.
10. **Open questions:** The biological function of the black "flag" is not established. A warning function tied to a shift to predation is suggested but unproven. The function of coarse granulocytes is also unknown.
