# The Copper Deposits of the Clifton-Morenci District, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #geology-economic-deposits

Note on scope: this document is a USGS geological monograph on copper deposits, rocks and mines. It contains no fish, invertebrate or aquatic-ecosystem data beyond stream and water-chemistry descriptions. The tag line above is adapted to the content; the taxon tags requested for fish do not apply.

## Executive Summary

Waldemar Lindgren's USGS Professional Paper No. 43 (1905) describes the geology and copper deposits of the [[Clifton-Morenci District]] in southeastern Arizona, a region he surveyed from October 1901 to May 1902. The work covers stratigraphy from [[Pre-Cambrian Basement]] granite through Paleozoic, Cretaceous, Tertiary and Quaternary units, the intrusive porphyries and faulting that structured the district, and the [[Contact Metamorphism]] and [[Hydrothermal Alteration]] processes that formed its ore. Copper is the only metal mined in large quantities, and the district ranked as the second most important copper camp in Arizona in 1903. The author concludes that the copper deposits originated from water and metals released by [[Porphyry Intrusions]] of Late Cretaceous or earliest Tertiary age, with later [[Supergene Enrichment]] by descending sulphate solutions.

## Background & Description

**Setting and access.** The district lies on the San Francisco River near the New Mexico line, about 120 miles north of Bisbee. The traveler reaches Clifton from Lordsburg, New Mexico, across plains and the Duncan Valley on the Gila River. Vegetation is sparse; rainfall rarely exceeds 10 inches per year, with winter and July-August wet seasons.

**Mining history.** The deposits were discovered in August 1872 by a party from Silver City, New Mexico, forming the Copper Mountain district. Early progress was slowed by Apache raids, distance from railroads (La Junta, Colorado, was 700 miles away), and the need to work only rich ore. Development accelerated after the [[Southern Pacific Railroad]] reached the area in 1881 and after the 1893 discovery of low-grade sulphide bodies at Copper Mountain.

**Principal companies.** The [[Detroit Copper Company]] (Morenci), the [[Arizona Copper Company]] (Morenci and Metcalf) and the [[Shannon Copper Company]] (Metcalf, from 1902) dominated production.

**Literature.** The author reviews earlier work by Gunnel, Wendt (1887), Henrich (1887), Douglas and Emmons (1901), who had first described the Longfellow, Coronado and Metcalf mines.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses the aquatic and hydrological setting only in relation to mining and geology:

- **Chase Creek** drains the district through a canyon up to 1,000 feet deep. Its water is fed mainly by concentrator discharge pumped from San Francisco River, carrying roughly 100 miner's inches heavily charged with tailings.
- **San Francisco River** is the main stream. It carries saline water from springs near Clifton; samples showed sodium chloride of 4.1 to 6.9 grains per gallon and total salts of 14.05 to 20.3 grains per gallon. The Shannon smelter had difficulty obtaining fresh water.
- **Eagle Creek** is a permanent stream reported to carry about 100 miner's inches even in dry seasons, subject to sudden freshets.
- **Groundwater.** No permanent groundwater was found in any mine in the district. Workings are dry except for minor moisture from domestic sources. Soluble sulphate coatings on drift walls show seasonal infiltration.
- **Climate.** The region is exceedingly arid. Snow remains only at elevations above 5,000 to 6,000 feet.

Vegetation includes [[Fouquieria Splendens]], agave, yucca, juniper and cottonwood along watercourses. No fish or invertebrate survey is reported.

## Key Findings

**Stratigraphy (Paleozoic and younger)**
- Total Paleozoic thickness about 1,000 to 1,500 feet, comprising the [[Coronado Quartzite]] (probably Cambrian, up to 250 feet at Morenci), [[Longfellow Limestones]] (Ordovician, up to about 400 feet), [[Morenci Shales]] (probably Devonian, about 175 feet) and [[Modoc Limestone]] (Mississippian, about 160 to 180 feet).
- The Ordovician fauna at Longfellow was described as the first definite Ordovician occurrence in Arizona.
- Cretaceous [[Pinkard Formation]] sandstones and shales overlie the Paleozoic unconformably and are correlated with the Benton formation.
- Cenozoic units include [[Tertiary Lavas]] (repeated rhyolite and basalt flows, to over 1,500 feet thick) and the [[Gila Conglomerate]] (early Quaternary, up to about 900 feet thick).

**Intrusive rocks**
- Granite-porphyry, quartz-monzonite-porphyry and diorite-porphyry form stocks, dikes, sheets and laccoliths. The main stock extends about 7 miles from Eagle Creek to Metcalf.
- Diabase is subordinate and connected with a small class of copper-bearing veins, including the Coronado vein.
- Intrusion is placed in latest Cretaceous or earliest Tertiary time. The magma is estimated to have consolidated at depths not exceeding about 4,000 feet.

**Structure**
- Faulting followed intrusion and was largely confined to one epoch between latest Cretaceous and middle Tertiary. Most blocks are monoclinal, dipping about 12° on average, mainly to the west and north.
- The [[Coronado Fault]] shows a vertical throw of at least 2,000 feet at Horseshoe Gulch. The [[Concentrator Fault]] displaces Longfellow limestone by about 3,000 feet near the San Francisco River.

**Ore genesis**
- Contact metamorphism of Paleozoic limestone and shale produced garnet, epidote, diopside and tremolite, with contemporaneous pyrite, magnetite, chalcopyrite and zinc blende.
- The author attributes the ore to magmatic water carrying sodium chloride and ferric oxide, released by decreasing pressure as the magma rose. Fluid inclusions in vein quartz indicate high-temperature aqueous solutions.
- Oxidation and [[Secondary Enrichment]] produced chalcocite zones, typically 200 to 500 feet thick, and carbonate ores (malachite and azurite) in limestone.

**Production and economics**
- Total district output to the end of 1903 was estimated at about 201,600 short tons of copper, valued near $60,500,000.
- Production in 1902 was 49,500,000 pounds; in 1903 the Arizona company produced 30,228,000 pounds, the Detroit company 16,558,232 pounds and the Shannon company 6,600,000 pounds.
- Ore grades averaged 3 to 4 per cent copper. Monthly ore output in 1902 was about 60,000 short tons, of which 47,000 tons were concentrating ore and 12,000 tons smelting ore.
- Oxidized ore at Metcalf was about 13,000 tons per month, and the lowest-grade oxidized ore contained less than 3 per cent copper.
- Clifton ranked second in Arizona in 1903 (53,400,000 pounds), behind Bisbee (62,500,000 pounds).

**Mines**
- The report describes about 40 individual mines and prospects, including the [[Longfellow Mine]], [[Humboldt Mine]], [[Montezuma Mine]], [[Detroit Mine]], [[Shannon Mine]], [[Coronado Vein]] and [[Copper King Mine]].
- Most of the carbonate and oxide deposits in limestone (Longfellow, Montezuma, Detroit, Manganese Blue) were largely exhausted by the time of writing. Their volume was much smaller than the chalcocite ore supporting the camp.

**Future outlook**
- The author expects the district to remain productive for many years, based on the large extent of low-grade chalcocite ore at Copper Mountain.
