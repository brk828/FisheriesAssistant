# Factors Affecting Trout Populations in Southwestern Mountain Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared for the [[USDA Forest Service]] Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, examines how physical habitat variables relate to trout population density in montane streams of the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests, eastern Arizona. Field work covered 33 reaches across 25 streams in 1988–1989, sampling trout populations by electrofishing alongside physical and riparian measurements. Trout (Apache, rainbow, brown, brook, and hybrid forms) were captured in most reaches, and trout density was most strongly associated with total stream volume, stream size, altitude, and pool proportion, though overall predictive models explained only moderate variance. The author concludes that generalized habitat models have limited predictive value across regions, and recommends locally specific, watershed-level experimental research and watershed management over physical stream modification.

## Background & Description

Stream trout represent a major recreational resource on [[National Forest]] lands, and both trout and their habitats have been studied intensively for decades. Prior work by Lowby and Inhof (1980s) noted that predictive models developed for one fishery may apply only regionally, which the author links to the variable geographic nature of limiting factors and the resulting confusion among studies.

The study was commissioned to establish locally specific relationships between trout and physical habitat in the Mogollon Rim region. Streams were selected from a set of watersheds in the White Mountains and Mogollon Rim area. Reaches were chosen as representative of each stream, and each reach was divided into 50-m study sections bounded by block nets. Fish were removed by electrofishing (Smith-Root model 15A backpack shocker), identified, measured (total length in mm), weighed (nearest 0.1 g), and classed as fry, juvenile, or adult.

Physical variables measured included stream width, depth, substrate composition, pool/riffle/run proportions, gradient, discharge, riparian shading (densiometer), instream cover, and aspect. Regional gradient, altitude, and stream order were derived from topographic maps. Streams were grouped into small (<1.5 m mean width), intermediate (1.5–3.0 m), and large (>3.0 m) size classes. Statistical analysis used correlation, simple and multiple regression, stepwise regression, GLM, and factor analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat (33 reaches):**
- Mean stream width 1.43 m (range 0.55–8.5 m); mean depth 0.11 m (0.03–0.39 m).
- Pool averaged 34.7% of habitat (range 0–100%); riffle 54.6%; run 23.7%.
- Mean discharge 0.12 m³/s (range 0 to 0.5 m³/s).
- Substrate: silt 12.8%, sand 4.7%, gravel 4.8%, cobble 31.5%, boulder 17.1%, bedrock 1.4%.
- Riparian canopy cover averaged 43.7% (range 0–100%).
- Local gradient averaged 3.3% (0.1–11.0%).

**Biological conditions:**
- Trout per reach averaged 53.7 fish (range 0–94); total trout biomass averaged 548.6 g per reach.
- Areal trout density averaged 0.5 trout/m² (range 0–1.3); areal biomass averaged 7.2 g/m².
- Trout were absent from several reaches, while non-salmonid species (chiefly speckled dace, suckers, and sculpin-type fishes) were present in many.

**Study streams and management:**
Streams are associated with the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]] and include [[Coleman Creek]], [[Hannagan Creek]], [[Nutrioso Creek]], [[Bear Creek]], [[Beaver Creek]], [[Fish Creek]], [[Hunter Creek]], and [[Eagle Creek]] (among others). Management is reported for wild Apache trout (a native subspecies of cutthroat trout), rainbow, brown, and hybrid (Apache × rainbow) trout. Several streams carry notable land-use pressures including domestic livestock grazing, timber harvest, and road-associated effects.

## Key Findings

- **Trout density and stream size:** Trout density (numbers and biomass per unit area and per unit volume) was highest in small, high-gradient streams, while total trout biomass per reach was greater in larger streams.
- **Drainage effects:** Streams in the San Francisco River drainage had significantly greater trout densities (per unit area and volume) than Little Colorado or Salt/Black River drainages.
- **Stream order:** Total trout biomass was significantly greater in second- and third-order streams than in first-order streams; numerical density was greater in second-order streams.
- **Stream aspect:** Aspect had no significant effect on any biotic variable.
- **Habitat correlations:** Run habitat was negatively correlated with pool (R = −0.94) and riffle (R = −0.77) proportions. Undercut bank was associated with drainage and run habitat. Regional and local gradients were positively related to riparian cover in some combinations.
- **Multiple regression:** The best 8-variable model for total trout numbers (R² ≈ 0.77) included total biomass, altitude, drainage, substrate silt, gravel, cobble, boulder, and stream order. Trout density per unit volume was best predicted by total biomass and stream volume (R² ≈ 0.91), reflecting partial redundancy among metrics.
- **Factor analysis:** Trout abundance and density loaded with larger streams, deeper pools, and boulder cover; non-trout fish loaded with pool-poor, low-cover habitats.
- **Sampling variability:** Three reaches per stream was judged adequate for some variables, but more replication was needed for reliable estimates; within-stream variation was large.
- **Recommendations:** The author argues that generalized predictive models for trout density from physical habitat are not yet reliable. Management should emphasize watershed-level practices (controlling livestock grazing and destructive timber harvest), with experimental watershed studies of paired streams to test habitat manipulation (e.g., adding pools or boulders), rather than relying solely on in-stream structures.
- **Self-sustaining populations:** Trout populations in most surveyed streams appeared self-sustaining, though temporal variation was not quantified and populations may be at carrying capacity for their habitat.
