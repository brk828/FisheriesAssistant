# Antibiotic Aspects of Copper Treatment of Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #copper-toxicity #lake-management

## Executive Summary

This historical review, attributed to Arthur D. Hasler of the [[University Of Wisconsin]] Department of Zoology, examines the use of copper sulfate (CuSO₄) to control algal blooms in lakes. Copper is effective against nuisance algae, but the author argues it is a persistent heavy metal that accumulates in lake muds and can harm fish, invertebrates, snails and the basic food plants of aquatic food webs. The paper concludes that a permanent poison should not be used to combat a temporary nuisance, and it proposes alternatives: research into causes of blooms and replacement compounds, harvesting of algae and aquatic plants as a crop, zoning of lakes by use, and watershed erosion control.

## Background & Description

The document is a discussion article rather than a field study. It opens with the limnological life cycle of lakes: young lakes are unproductive, while aging lakes become enriched (eutrophic), shallower and prone to algal scums, a process accelerated by agricultural and domestic runoff. Algal scums impair bathing, boating, fishing, aesthetics and municipal water supplies through odor, taste and filtration problems.

The main management practice discussed is spraying surface waters with CuSO₄ before a bloom is expected. The author stresses that copper, as a heavy metal, is not destroyed by combining with carbonate or organic matter and therefore accumulates over years of treatment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Copper chemistry and water hardness:** Hard water precipitates copper as insoluble copper carbonate, but soft water requires a much smaller killing dose (Wisconsin Committee on Water Pollution, 1939, 1946; Ellis, 1937).
- **Sources and sinks of copper (Riley, 1939):** Precipitation dilutes copper; sedimentation adsorbs it onto organic matter; mud releases it back; and decomposing vegetation releases it in autumn.
- **Muds as reservoirs:** Surface muds of Mendota, Monona, Waubesa and Kegonsa held 85, 605, 300 and 223 mg Cu per kg dry mud, respectively. Mendota was untreated; the other three had received regular copper treatments.
- **Bottom fauna:** Summer standing crop of bottom organisms in 1944 was about 9,000 per m² in Lake Mendota muds versus 800 per m² in Lake Monona. No small clams (*Pisidium*) were found in Monona muds.
- **Food web effects:** Worthington (1946) noted that copper sulfate "may not kill fish but it certainly kills the bulk of their food supply."

Key sensitivity thresholds cited across studies:

| Organism group | Reported threshold (as cited) |
|---|---|
| Freshwater invertebrates (Riley, 1939) | 0.03 to 0.5 ppm Cu |
| Goldfish, *Daphnia magna*, tadpoles, *Cladophora* (DeGiusti, 1941) | 1.25 ppm killed within 14 days; 0.25 ppm killed none within 20 days |
| Pond snail *Lymnaea stagnalis* (Guilford and Noland, 1943) | Could not tolerate 0.25 ppm beyond 64 hours; tolerated 0.12 ppm |
| Floating plants: *Spirodela*, *Azolla*, *Lemna*, *Riccia* (Elser, 1941) | All killed at 0.3 ppm; *Lemna* and *Riccia* inhibited at 0.03 ppm |
| Planktonic algae (Churchill, 1946) | Unicellular forms killed at 0.25 ppm (some at 0.08 ppm); filamentous forms inhibited at 0.33 ppm, not killed below 0.66 ppm |

## Key Findings

1. **Short-term tests overstate safety.** The Wisconsin report tolerated up to 250 ppm CuSO₄ for short exposures in Lake Mendota water (150 ppm CaCO₃), but no longer-term studies were made.
2. **Low doses still harm organisms.** Traces of copper markedly increased goldfish death rates in distilled water (Allee et al., 1940). Snails in Madison water were sensitive to 0.25 ppm over days.
3. **Food-plant damage may be more serious than fish toxicity.** Floating plants and algae were killed or inhibited at concentrations below those used for algae control, potentially altering the base of the food chain.
4. **Dose depends on total copper, not just concentration.** The author argues that total available copper, which depends on container or lake volume, may matter more than ppm alone.
5. **Accumulation is the central concern.** Repeated treatments build copper in muds, which later release it back into the water, paralleling marine antifouling paints.
6. **Field observations are consistent with harm.** Bloomington, Illinois reportedly added about 500 lb of CuSO₄ weekly to a 900-acre lake since 1930 (personal communication, D. H. Thompson), with no aquatic plants and no blooms. Frey (1940) linked declines in vegetation and molluscs in Monona, Waubesa and Kegonsa to copper addition.
7. **Proposed alternatives:**
   - Coordinated research on bloom causes and a replacement compound, analogous to 2,4-D replacing heavy metals in land weed control.
   - Harvesting algae and aquatic plants as a crop (as in Milwaukee), with engineering research on harvesting machinery.
   - Lake zoning, with some lakes reserved for bathing and boating (where copper could be used until a non-accumulating agent is found) and others for wildlife and fish.
   - Soil erosion control in watersheds, which supply nearly all of a lake's fertility.

The author closes with an analogy to arsenic in orchards, where repeated spraying harmed soil biota and trees (Rodale, 1945), suggesting humans are indirectly affected.
