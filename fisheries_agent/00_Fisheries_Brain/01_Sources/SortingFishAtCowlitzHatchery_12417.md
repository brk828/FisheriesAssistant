# Sorting Fish at Cowlitz Hatchery: Automation in Grading Cuts Labor Costs

#aquaculture #hatchery-management #trout-culture #fish-grading

## Executive Summary

This document describes an automated fish grading and handling system designed and installed at the [[Cowlitz Trout Hatchery]] in Washington State, a joint project between the City of Tacoma and the Washington Department of Game. The facility was engineered by Kramer, Chin & Mayo Consulting Engineers to address the common hatchery challenge of efficiently grading fish for improved management and programming. The system integrates drain and transport pipes, mechanical graders, sorting tanks, and loading facilities to enable three workers to grade approximately 4,000 pounds of fish per raceway in about six hours, with reduced fish damage compared to manual grading methods.

## Background & Description

Fish grading is widely recognized as essential for effective [[Hatchery Management]] and programming, yet most hatcheries lack convenient methods for moving and handling fish, preventing frequent grading operations. The [[Cowlitz Trout Hatchery]] facility was designed following conferences with hatchery personnel including Cliff Millenbach, Quentin Edson, and Chris Jensen.

The system consists of several integrated components:

**Transport System**: Fish move from raceways through a 12-inch round drain plug into an underground flume and pipe system leading to the sorting facility. Flow enters by gravity into a crowding channel measuring 8 feet wide by 45 feet long with approximately 2.5 feet water depth, controlled by a weir overflow.

**Crowding Channel**: When fish collection is complete, the entry pipe closes and a second drain gate closes. Water is then introduced through a separate pipe from the lift end, attracting fish and raising water level to provide additional height for gravity operation.

**Crowder**: An electrically powered, track-mounted piece of equipment with adjustable carriage travel speed. Features a probe-type screen allowing the carriage to move to any point with the screen raised. The crowder lowers its screen to collect a lift load of fish and moves toward the fish lift.

**Fish Lift**: A platform-type lift with sloping top, powered by a water-driven hydraulic cylinder similar to service station lifts. Lifts fish up and onto the graders after the crowder reaches the stops.

**Grader Assembly**: Consists of two graders with stainless steel false weir, entrance apron, spray headers, and adjustment wheels and levers. Levers raise upper sections to clear fish and provide a 6-inch clear passage for moving adults. Four handwheels with lock levers set grading bar spacing. The grader separates fish into two sizes that fall into sorting tanks, while jumbo fish proceed to the end sorting tank.

**Sorting Tanks**: Three tanks, each sized for 1,000 gallons to provide one tank truck load per grade. Each tank has separate supply and circulation pump, enabling individual draining without disrupting the process. Pumps include float switches for automatic shutoff when drained.

**Measurement System**: Spray headers are separated to isolate spray water into individual sorting tanks for displacement measuring. A float well with adjustable scale and indicator is provided for each sorting tank to measure fish quantities.

**Adult Handling**: Provisions included for holding and loading adult scrap fish from the holding pond, moving them through a buried 12-inch plastic pipe to the crowding channel.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The facility was designed to minimize fish stress and physical damage during grading operations. The automated system reduces handling time and physical contact compared to manual methods. Water flow management through the attraction header and spray systems maintains fish welfare during the sorting process. The system operates by gravity where possible, reducing mechanical stress on fish.

The crowding channel dimensions (8 feet wide by 45 feet long by 2.5 feet deep) provide adequate space for fish movement while enabling efficient crowding operations. Water depth control via weir overflow maintains consistent conditions throughout the grading process.

## Key Findings

**Operational Performance**: During the first full-scale grading run, four raceways containing approximately 4,000 pounds of fish each were graded in about six hours with three workers performing all necessary tasks.

**Fish Welfare**: Fish damage was considered less than would have occurred with hand grading methods, demonstrating improved welfare outcomes with the automated system.

**Labor Efficiency**: The automated system significantly reduced labor requirements compared to manual grading, with only three workers needed for the entire operation.

**Economic Justification**: The authors conclude that in larger hatcheries, investment in this type of facility is justified through:
- Better programming capabilities
- Lower labor costs
- More effective utilization of food budgets
- Reduced fish mortality and damage

**Versatility**: Prior to the grading trial, the facility had been successfully used for moving adult fish for loading purposes, demonstrating multi-purpose functionality.

**Design Success**: The facility met its primary objective of enabling frequent fish grading operations that were previously impractical due to handling difficulties, allowing for improved [[Hatchery Management]] practices at [[Cowlitz Trout Hatchery]].
