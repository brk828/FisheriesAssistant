# Two-Year Creel Census of the Little Salmon River, Idaho

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #fisheries-management #creel-census

## Executive Summary

This 1954 study by Leon W. Murphy (Idaho Department of Fish and Game) presents a two-year (1952–1953) creel census of the lower 24 miles of the [[Little Salmon River]], Idaho, conducted in response to increasing angling pressure, an extended fishing season, and reports of declining catch success. Using a stratified random-sampling design (rather than exhaustive daily checks), the study estimated total angling effort, catch composition, and the contribution of hatchery-stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] to the fishery. An estimated 6,555–6,991 anglers fished the census area annually, producing an estimated total catch of 47,772 fish (1952) and 46,997 fish (1953). The study is notable for its methodological rigor in creel survey design, including voluntary postcard reporting, follow-up questionnaires, and statistical validation of catch estimates via [[Analysis of Variance in Creel Census Data]].

## Background & Description

This paper is a fisheries management/creel census study rather than a morphological or taxonomic analysis; no detailed morphometric data are presented. Species identification during creel inspections relied on standard field characters distinguishing:

- **[[Rainbow Trout]]** (*Salmo gairdneri*) — dominant species in the catch (95.7–96.3% of observed kept fish).
- **[[Eastern Brook Trout]]** (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) — minor component of catch.
- **[[Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Salmo clarki*) — minor component of catch.
- **[[Dolly Varden]]** (*Salvelinus malma*) — minor component of catch.
- **[[Mountain Whitefish]]** (*Prosopium williamsoni*) — minor component of catch.
- **[[Chinook Salmon]]** (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) — minor component of catch.
- **[[Steelhead Trout]]** — minor component of catch.

Hatchery-reared rainbow trout were distinguished from wild fish using **fin-clip marking**: each of five (1952) or planting events (1953) received a unique single fin clip, allowing hatchery recruits to be tracked through the fishery as the season progressed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: The [[Little Salmon River]] watershed drains approximately 582 square miles, flowing north to its confluence with the [[Salmon River]] at Riggins, Idaho. The census area comprised the lower 24 miles of the main stem, paralleled by U.S. Highway 95, and divided into six ~4-mile sections for sampling purposes.
- **Fishing season structure**: The lower 24 miles were open to all species April 15–October 31 (200-day season), 50 days longer than the general drainage season (June 4–October 31), reflecting differing management/access pressures on this reach.
- **Angler pressure**: Increased tourist/cabin development (nine locations built over 15 years on the lower 20 miles) and highway accessibility drove rising fishing pressure, prompting the census.
- **Stocking ecology**: Hatchery-reared catchable-size rainbow trout were released directly into the census area (6,000 in 1952; 8,400 in 1953) at multiple points along the six river sections, integrating hatchery supplementation directly into the wild population dynamics of the reach.
- **Species dominance**: Rainbow trout overwhelmingly dominated the sport catch, consistent with the drainage being managed and stocked primarily as a put-and-take rainbow trout fishery, with salmonid competitors/associates (brook trout, cutthroat, dolly varden, whitefish, chinook, steelhead) present only as minor incidental catch.

## Key Findings

**Census Methodology**
- Stratified sampling design: 200-day season divided into eight 25-day periods; one random Sunday, Saturday, and weekday censused per period (24 total census days/season).
- Self-addressed, stamped postcards distributed to anglers during interviews; catch data returned voluntarily by mail.
- Three data-collection categories: "postcard" (voluntary mail return), "questionnaire" (follow-up to non-responders), and "interview" (direct census-taker observation of completed fishing days).
- A test of naming anglers on cards (1952) showed a slightly higher voluntary return rate when names/addresses were recorded.

**Response Rates**
- 1952: 1,107 anglers interviewed; 940 (84.9%) returned catch data.
- 1953: 1,140 anglers interviewed; 1,042 (91.4%) returned catch data.

**Species Composition of Catch**
- Creel inspection covered 21.7% (1952) and 22.2% (1953) of fish kept on census days.
- Rainbow trout: 96.3% (1952) and 95.7% (1953) of observed kept fish.
- Remaining catch: eastern brook trout, cutthroat trout, dolly varden, mountain whitefish, chinook salmon, steelhead trout.

**Hatchery [[Rainbow Trout]] Recruitment**
- 1952: 6,000 catchable-size hatchery rainbow trout released (five plantings of 1,200 per section); fin-clip marked per lot. Estimated recovery: 2,223 fish (**37.1%** return rate).
- 1953: Planting increased 40% to 8,400 fish (including two enlarged plantings of 2,400 on June 20 and July 28). Estimated recovery: 4,867 fish (**57.9%** return rate).
- Carry-over from 1952 plantings was ruled out as a confound for 1953 results — only 4 marked fish from 1952 stock were observed in the catch during the 6 census days prior to the first 1953 plantings.

**Statistical Validation**
- Variance analysis (per [[Embody 1954]] methodology) found highly significant variation in daily reported catch **between days of the week** and **among the eight sampling periods**.
- No significant difference in catch was found **between years** (1952 vs. 1953).
- Best estimate of total reported catch per year: **39,100 fish**, with 95% confidence limits of **30,200–48,000 fish** — validating the census method as sufficiently precise for management purposes.

**Management Implications**
- The stratified random-day census method proved effective for high-traffic streams where conventional creel checkpoints were impractical (due to highway traffic conditions preventing vehicle stops).
- Results informed evaluation of hatchery stocking efficiency and supported continued use of fin-clip marking to track put-and-take rainbow trout program effectiveness in the [[Little Salmon River]].
- Study was funded under the Federal Aid in Fish Restoration program ([[Dingell-Johnson Act]], Project F 7-R).
