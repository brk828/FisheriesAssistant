# Distribution and Ecology of Bacteria in Freshwater

#bacteriology #limnology #freshwater-microbiology

## Executive Summary

This two-part paper by [[Vera G. Collins]], a senior scientist at the Freshwater Biological Association, reviews how freshwater bacteria have been studied and then reports a new method for counting them. The author argues that bacteriology of freshwater has been dominated by water-quality testing, and that the ecology of lake bacteria has been neglected. Over more than ten years of weekly sampling of [[Windermere]] and [[Esthwaite Water]], the author compared the standard [[Plate Count Method]] with a new [[Direct Count Method]]. The direct count gave values many times higher than plate counts at every depth. Bacterial numbers were linked to mixing, stratification, rainfall and the [[Sulphur Cycle]] in lake muds. The author concludes that lake bacteriology is fundamentally ecological and needs biochemical study.

## Background & Description

**Historical foundations.** The paper traces bacteriology from [[Louis Pasteur]], who established sterilisation and culture principles, to [[Robert Koch]], who introduced solid media for isolating pure colonies. Percy Frankland and later workers applied these methods to water. Routine water-quality tests culminated in the Ministry of Health Report No. 71, *The Bacteriological Examination of Water Supplies*. The author notes that the basic biology of bacteria in fresh water was largely neglected.

**Early lake studies (to 1940).** Earlier workers in Europe and North America, including Kleiber (Lake Zürich), Pfenniger, [[Fred, Wilson and Davenport]] (Lake Mendota), Ruttner (Lake Lunz), Graham and Young (Flathead Lake), Stark and McCoy (Wisconsin lakes) and Henrici (Lake Alexander), reported seasonal, vertical and horizontal patterns. Bacteria were generally more abundant in shallow bays than in open water. Stark and McCoy attributed this to organic matter rather than temperature or oxygen.

**Study sites.**
- **[[Windermere]]:** the largest and one of the deepest English lakes, about 10 miles long, averaging 0.4 mile wide, with a maximum depth of 63 m. Routine sampling was in the North Basin, which receives sewage effluent from Ambleside.
- **[[Esthwaite Water]]:** shallow (depths not exceeding 16 m; later stated as 15 m), fed by streams draining agricultural land, with farmyard drainage and sewage from Hawkshead. It is richer in dissolved chemicals and supports more algae than Windermere.

**Methods.**
- *Plate count:* the standard [[Plate Count Method]] used a modified [[Stark and McCoy Medium]] (peptone, sodium caseinate, starch, glycerol, agar and other ingredients). Plates were incubated at 20 °C for 10 days.
- *Direct count:* a 0.01 ml drop of water was stained with gentian violet in 50% glycerine and concentrated in vacuo into hand-drawn glass capillaries. Bacteria were counted under oil immersion over 60 microscope fields. The method was developed by Collins and Kipling (1957).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mixing and stratification.** Under winter mixing, bacteria are evenly distributed. During [[Stratification]], numbers fall with depth in the [[Epilimnion]], while bacterial numbers below the [[Thermocline]] are approximately uniform. Water immediately above the mud has somewhat higher counts. Taylor (1940) found that chemical composition was similar at all depths on any one occasion.

**Rainfall and runoff.** Fluctuations in bacterial numbers correlated with rainfall in the preceding week, most strongly in winter. Soil-associated bacteria washed in with heavy rain likely explain winter peaks in plate counts.

**Overturn.** Autumn [[Overturn]] raised plate counts at lower depths. Mud-derived bacteria appeared in surface waters, where they had previously occurred only at the greatest depths.

**Mud and sulphur cycling.** Sulphate-reducing bacteria in deep, de-oxygenated mud produce hydrogen sulphide, which supports [[Coloured Sulphur Bacteria]] (*Chromatium*) at the oxic-anoxic interface. In Esthwaite Water, *Chromatium* mass growths occurred at 9 to 13 m. Beggiatoa and *Achromatium* follow during overturn. [[Iron Bacteria]] such as [[Sphaerotilus natans]] and *Leptothrix* spp. form filamentous sheaths that attract other microbes in the thermocline zone.

**Biochemical potential.** The author reports that bacteria in lake water respond to added substances, and that [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]] (B.O.D.) testing is a better guide to their potential than standard fermentation tests.

## Key Findings

1. **Direct counts exceed plate counts by large factors.** In the June–July 1949 trial in the North Basin of Windermere (samples at 1, 5, 30 and 50 m), the direct-to-plate ratio ranged from 17 to 11 551 at 1 m, 6 to 9 775 at 5 m, 16 to 11 278 at 30 m, and 33 to 3 948 at 50 m. Direct counts were higher in every sample.

2. **Seasonal pattern.** The ratio between methods was lowest in winter, when plate counts were relatively highest. The highest numbers occurred in December through March, when lake temperatures were uniform and rainfall was greatest.

3. **Thermocline concentration.** After a short period of heavy rain during calm weather, Windermere plate counts were 2 400 bacteria/ml above the thermocline, 47 700/ml at its top, 1 460/ml at minimum, and 60 240/ml at its base. The effect had disappeared three days later and occurred once in six years.

4. **Cultured isolates.** About 1 000 cultures were isolated. Some 95% were Gram-negative rods, with short rods more common than long. Cocci were under 1%, and spore-formers did not exceed 10%. Dominant genera included *Pseudomonas*, *Achromobacter*, *Alcaligenes*, *Chromobacterium*, *Flavobacterium* and some micrococci. Most showed weak biochemical activity.

5. **Three bacterial categories.** Lake bacteria were classed as (a) washed-in allochthonous types, (b) indigenous types using low concentrations of organic matter, and (c) surface-dependent types associated with soil, mud and plankton.

6. **Nutrient and substrate effects.** In [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]] trials, fumarate, butyrate, propionate and lactate increased oxygen consumption. Formate and ferric chloride inhibited growth. Phosphate increased B.O.D. in the presence of glucose. Ammonium sulphate was a better nitrogen source than sodium nitrate.

7. **Container effects.** Bacterial growth in stored lake water ranked from least to most stimulating: silica, pyrex, soda-glass, polythene, nylon. Polythene and nylon tubing promoted slime formation, a possible cause of blockage in industrial pipework.

8. **Mud community.** Aerobic bacteria dominate the mud surface and decline with depth in cores of 20 to 30 cm. Sulphate-reducing bacteria dominate below about 4 cm. Denitrifiers, proteolytic and starch-hydrolysing bacteria, and cellulose and chitin decomposers were also present.

9. **Author's conclusion.** Direct counting improves measurement of cell numbers but does not identify bacteria or their functions. The author concludes that freshwater bacteriology is fundamentally ecological, requiring biochemical and ecological study rather than physiology alone.

10. **Discussion points.** Discussants (including Dr. Burman and Dr. Allen) noted that colony counts are not "total" counts, and that colony counts can be 10 to 1 000 times higher than conventional 22 °C counts. Dr. Burman asked whether the difference between direct and colony counts (around 10 000:1) could be explained by known specialised groups. Dr. Allen cited Drachev's finding that the ratio of direct to colony counts was tens of thousands in low-organic water.
