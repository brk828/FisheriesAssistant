# Growth of Freshwater Drum in Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Sciaenidae

## Executive Summary
This 1960 report (Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory Report No. 78, by Alfred Houser) summarizes growth of the [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) in Oklahoma from 19 years of data (1940–1958). Scale samples from 3,103 fish were drawn from 32 waters (16 reservoirs, 7 large lakes, 9 streams, per the Methods text). Statewide average total length at age 1 through 14 ranged from 4.6 to 33.3 inches. Growth was fastest in small lakes under 500 acres, slower in reservoirs over 500 acres, and slowest in streams. The species is a minor sport and commercial fish that is essentially unmanaged.

## Background & Description
- The freshwater drum is widely distributed in Oklahoma and was long classed as a "rough fish" that many anglers considered undesirable. Construction of many large [[Oklahoma Reservoirs]] raised angler numbers and a commercial fishery, which brought wider recognition as a sport and food fish.
- Virtually no management of the species exists, although extreme overpopulation occasionally develops in lakes.
- The report aims to give biologists comparative reference data for analysis and management recommendations.
- **Methods:** Scales were collected by [[Rotenone Sampling]], gill nets, trap nets, wire traps, seines and hook and line. Scales were viewed at 45x to identify annuli and measure scale length, assuming a direct body-length to scale-length relationship ([[Scale Analysis]] after Jenkins, Elkin and Finnell 1955). The state average is a simple average of the weighted averages for three water types. Length-weight analysis used 3,839 fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Water types compared: streams, lakes up to 500 acres, and reservoirs over 500 acres. Examples of waters sampled include [[Grand Lake]] (46,300 acres; 1,092 fish), [[Lake Texoma]] (93,080 acres), [[Fort Gibson Reservoir]], [[Lake Murray]], [[Lake Overholser]], [[Canton Reservoir]], [[Tenkiller Reservoir]], and rivers such as the [[Grand River]], [[Illinois River]], [[Verdigris River]] and [[Red River]].
- Most Oklahoma lakes and reservoirs are relatively new, and many fish came from new-lake conditions where growth is usually high. The averages may therefore exceed those of older, more stabilized populations.
- Standing crop (rotenone and one mark-recapture estimate): average 26 lb/acre in 11 reservoirs (19 rotenone samples plus one mark-recapture estimate); 20.4 lb/acre from 6 samples in 4 streams. Highest single estimates were 159 lb/acre (reservoir) and 57 lb/acre (stream).
- No length, creel or season limits apply to the species, and it is harvested commercially year-round. Commercial nets and traps must have a minimum 3-inch square mesh.

## Key Findings
- **Statewide mean total length at ages 1–14 (inches):** 4.6, 9.0, 11.9, 15.3, 17.5, 20.3, 22.5, 26.0, 27.5, 28.9, 28.5, 30.9, 31.8, 33.3. Sample sizes fall with age (3,103 fish at age 1 to 3 fish at age 14; 32 waters to 2 waters).
- **Longevity:** Three fish reached age 14, comparable to the maximum ages of [[Blue Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] in the area.
- **Desirable size:** At about 12.0 inches the fish weighs roughly one pound (Fort Gibson creel data), which is age 3 on the statewide average. Slowest-growing fish take more than six years to reach one pound; the fastest reach it in their second year. The same size/age pattern was reported for [[Channel Catfish]] at Fort Gibson.
- **Length-weight relationship** (3.5–24.0 in): log(100)W = -4.7305 + 3.17866 log(10)L. Weights: 1 lb at 13.1 in, 2 lb at 16.5 in, 3 lb at 18.5 in, 4 lb at 20.3 in, 5 lb at 21.8 in, 6 lb at 23.0 in. Condition index C(TL) rises uniformly with length (34.9 at 3.5 in to 49.6 at 24.0 in). Specimens over 25 in (up to 34.1 in) were excluded because of small numbers.
- **Growth by water type (mean length at age 6):** reservoirs 18.2 in, small lakes 26.4 in, streams 16.2 in. At age 6, lake fish averaged 8.2 in longer than reservoir fish and 10.2 in longer than stream fish. Small lakes (149 fish) gave the best growth conditions.
- **Comparison with other states:** Oklahoma averages are much higher than those reported elsewhere. Oklahoma stream growth is similar to [[Missouri Streams]], Kentucky and the [[Mississippi River]]. Oklahoma lakes under 500 acres grew fastest of all compared. Oklahoma reservoirs outgrew [[Lake Erie]] (age 6: 11.9 in), Lake Chickamauga, Kentucky Lake and two Kansas lakes. Minnesota's state average at age 6 was 14.3 in.
- **Angler harvest:** Drum made up 3.4% of annual catch by weight in a Corps of Engineers creel census of seven reservoirs (1952–1955), and a 3.1% grand average over 24 creel-census years including Spavinaw Lake. Harvest is seasonal, with substantial catches only in spring and very few in winter.
- **Commercial catch:** Total statewide commercial catch was 16,514 lb in 1957 and 11,018 lb in 1959; drum was 2.4% of the total in both years.
- **Management outlook:** Because the species is of minor importance, changes to management are unlikely. Extreme overpopulations in large reservoirs, where rotenone reduction was not feasible, remain uncorrected. Experiments with trap nets and trawls offer some hope, but effective management is unlikely until knowledge of the species' ecology is greatly enlarged.
