# Native Arizona Fishes: Part II - Spiny-Rayed Minnows

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game & Fish Department article (*Wildlife Views*, Vol. 16, No. 6, Nov.-Dec. 1969) by [[W. L. Minckley]] of Arizona State University describes the four native [[Spiny-Rayed Minnows]] of the tribe [[Plagopterini]]. These silvery, stream-dwelling cyprinids occur only in the middle and lower [[Colorado River]] drainage. Seven forms are known (six species, one with two subspecies). Two of these have gone extinct in Nevada, and all have lost part of their original range. The article covers the diagnostic fin adaptations, species-by-species distribution and biology, and conservation concerns, and argues that these fishes matter chiefly for their scientific uniqueness.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic group:** The spiny-rayed minnows are members of the family Cyprinidae, the most diverse group of native Arizona fishes. Four of the state's native cyprinids belong to the tribe Plagopterini.
- **Diagnostic features:** The group is named for modified fin supports. The defining trait is a pair of double, spine-like rays at the leading edge of the dorsal fin, with one "spine" fitting into a groove in the other. Plagopterine fishes also have spine-like rays in the pelvic fins, which no other minnow has.
- **Habitat preference:** All four species are silvery, active, and found in streams rather than lakes.
- **Nomenclature:** [[Edward Drinker Cope]] named the genus *Plagopterus* in 1874. The name combines the Greek *plagos* ("wound") and *pterus* ("fin"), referring to the sharp dorsal spine. The species epithet *argentissimus* means "most silver."
- **Source context:** The article is part II of a series on native Arizona fishes, published in the [[Wildlife Views]] magazine of the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]]. The same issue includes unrelated pieces on deer management, elk, and Madera Canyon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Woundfin:** Adapted to the swift, sandy-bottomed channel of the lower Virgin River. The species' biology is almost unknown. Highway construction and possible future damming threaten it.
- **Spikedace:** Lives in slowly flowing, shallow pools, usually concentrating near the upper ends where swifter water enters. Spawns in spring in shallow, moving water over sand or gravel. Formerly widespread in the Gila basin upstream of the Salt-Gila junction, it has been replaced in the [[Verde River]] basin by the introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*).
- **Little Colorado Spinedace:** Inhabits creek pools and prefers deeper, quieter water than [[Spikedace]] or [[Woundfin]]. Feeds principally on aquatic insects, and its reproductive season runs through the summer.
- **Virgin River Spinedace:** Lives in pools of creeks and springs. Its biology has not yet been studied.
- **Trophic role:** The article describes these fishes as a possible link between invertebrates and larger game fishes. They are occasionally used as bait.
- **Biogeography:** Distributions reflect drainage relationships from the distant past. The fishes evolved in long isolation in the lower part of a major western drainage system.
- **Threats:** Habitat modification, competition and replacement by introduced species, and drought-related population declines.

## Key Findings

- The tribe Plagopterini comprises seven known forms (six species, one with two subspecies) confined to the middle and lower Colorado River drainage. Two of these became extinct in Nevada within the past few years.
- Plagopterine fishes are distinguished by double, interlocking dorsal fin spines and spine-like pelvic rays, which no other minnow possesses.
- The woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) has three historic records and was last collected from the Gila River system in 1894. Its only remaining Arizona population is in the lower Virgin River. Biology is largely unknown.
- Woundfin, spikedace, and Little Colorado spinedace are assigned population-status concerns in the article; the Little Colorado spinedace nearly went extinct in the early 1960s, spread back across much of its range in the mid-1960s, and showed renewed declines in 1969 sampling.
- The Game and Fish Department and Arizona State University were attempting to maintain woundfin stock in hatchery raceways and possibly introduce the species to other natural waters, with the goal of preserving the species and producing forage for game fishes.
- The Virgin River spinedace (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*) is common in many tributary creeks in Arizona, Nevada, and Utah, but its Arizona populations may be destroyed by the same habitat modifications expected to affect the woundfin.
- The article states these four small fishes have little definable economic value, and that their major worth is scientific: they are a closely knit group of relict fishes whose distributions record ancient drainage conditions.
