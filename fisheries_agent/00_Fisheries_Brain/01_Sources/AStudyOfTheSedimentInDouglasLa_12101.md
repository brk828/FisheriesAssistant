# Sediment Accumulation and Basin Morphometry in Douglas Lake, Cheboygan County, Michigan

#limnology #sedimentology #lake-sediment

## Executive Summary

This 1944 study by Ira T. Wilson examines the sediment fill of [[Douglas Lake]], a small glacial lake in Cheboygan County, Michigan. Using 111 borings and nine complete sediment cores, the author reconstructs three basin stages: the original basin during the [[Lake Algonquin]] embayment stage, a high-level basin, and the present basin. Four sediment types are identified: [[Gelatinous Black Mud]], [[Marl]], [[Pink Clay]], and [[Brown Varved Clay]]. The study finds that sediment accumulated mainly around the basin margins during the Algonquin stage, when stranded glacial ice blocks occupied the depressions. After the lake isolated, sediment settled mainly in the centers of the depressions. The author attributes this pattern to ice-block melting history, basin shape, and wave and current action.

## Background & Description

**Study design.** The author made 111 borings to the original lake bottom. The existing contour map of the present basin ([[Morphometry]]) was already available, so the borings were used to build contour maps of the original and high-level basins and cross-section profiles. Most borings were exploratory and recorded only sediment type and transition depths. Nine borings produced full cores for chemical, petrographic, microfossil and laminations analysis. Boring 74 in [[South Fish-Tail Bay Depression]] was used for [[Pollen Analysis]], published separately with Potzger (1943). Diatoms (Ruth Patrick) and invertebrates (Frank E. Eggleton) were under study, with separate publications planned. Sampling used a float method described earlier by Wilson (1941), [[Sediment Borings]] which required a float at least as broad as the maximum wave length on rough water.

**Funding and support.** Field work was supported mainly by the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]] in Ann Arbor, with additional grants from the Michigan Academy of Science, Arts, and Letters and the American Association for the Advancement of Science. Lodging and laboratory facilities were provided by the [[Biological Station of the University of Michigan]], located on Douglas Lake.

**Lake history.** Scott (1921) proposed that Douglas Lake began as a depression in an inlet of [[Lake Algonquin]]. The lake became isolated when Lake Algonquin lowered to the Nipissing level. The depressions are attributed to stranded blocks of ice left by the receding [[Wisconsin Ice Sheet]]. The author recognizes three stages:

- **Algonquin stage:** the lake was an embayment of Lake Algonquin, with the shoreline at 740 feet above sea level. A terrace at about 730 feet was cut during this period.
- **High-level stage:** the isolated lake stood higher than today, with a shoreline at 720 feet. Most shore adjustments that shaped the modern lake occurred here.
- **Present stage:** the lake is about ten feet lower than the high-level stage, with a shoreline at 710 feet.

**Depressions.** Seven isolated submerged depressions were already known: [[Fairy Island Depression]], Maple Point, Roberts Point, Grapevine Point, Stony Point, Sedge Point, and South Fish-Tail Bay. The author identified three more that are now filled: [[Bogardus Point Depression]], Boys Camp, and North Fish-Tail Bay.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment types and origin.** The sequence has four units:

- **Gelatinous black mud** forms the uppermost deposit. It is soft at the top and firm lower down. It is a warm-water deposit laid down after isolation, when the lake stratified in summer.
- **Marl** lies below the black mud. It is gray and carbonate-rich, and is also a warm-water deposit. It is usually only a few feet thick.
- **Pink clay** is amorphous and lies below the marl. It is attributed to the Algonquin period and is a cold-water deposit. Its color comes from iron oxide, probably derived from iron deposits farther north. It has almost no organic matter.
- **Brown varved clay** occurs in layers about a foot thick beneath the pink clay. The varves number 10 to 30 per inch, and the author notes they do not form in water warmer than 10 °C. The clay may predate Douglas Lake, which would make it a possible marker of the original bottom. It overlies very fine sand with enough carbonate to react vigorously with hydrochloric acid.

**Sediment chemistry (Table I summary).** The black mud is about 75 percent water. Excluding water, about 30 percent is organic matter containing pollen and plant fragments. Silicon dioxide makes up 48.80 percent of the dry weight, mostly diatom tests. The marl contains 47.99 percent water, 11.30 percent organic matter, and 27.20 percent silicon dioxide. Carbonates (mostly calcium and magnesium) make up about 50 percent of the marl, mainly as calcite and dolomite. The pink clay is 48.20 percent silicon dioxide, mostly quartz, with 1 percent organic matter. Its magnesium content is higher than in the marl, and its calcium content is lower. The varved clay is similar to the pink clay but also contains considerable calcite and limonite, which gives it a brown color.

**Core log.** The core from the center of South Fish-Tail Bay shows a transition zone of alternating mud, marl, clay and sand layers. The sequence runs from black mud at 72 to 74 feet, through marl and laminated mud, into pink and gray clays, and ends in coarse sand at 138 to 148 feet.

**Wind and current exposure.** Sediment distribution is linked to exposure. The most wind-exposed depressions (Sedge Point, Stony Point and Roberts Point) received the least warm-water sediment. The author suggests that swift currents over exposed basins prevent settling. The sheltered Fairy Island depression, at the lee end of the lake, received the most. The long axis of the lake lies along the prevailing winds, so wave action erodes the shore and carries material into the depressions. Undertow, return currents and seasonal overturns keep fine sediment suspended until it settles in the deeper basins.

## Key Findings

**1. Sediment filled the lake in two contrasting modes.** During the Algonquin stage, sediment accumulated mainly around the basin periphery, where ice was thin and melted early. In the high-level and present stages, sediment accumulated mainly in the basin centers.

**2. Basin volume and area decreased substantially.** For the lake as a whole, volume fell from 14,942,574,000 cubic feet (original basin) to 6,634,224,000 (high-level) and 2,908,403,000 (present), a total reduction of 80.54 percent. Surface area decreased 13.04 percent from the Algonquin stage to the high-level stage and 7.71 percent from the high-level stage to the present. Area is given in the table as 199,465,000, 173,458,000 and 160,088,000 square feet.

**3. Sediment volume was greater in the Algonquin stage.** The lake accumulated 4,582,776,000 cubic feet of sediment during the Algonquin stage and 2,058,089,000 cubic feet afterward. The average sediment depth across the lake was 22.97 feet (Algonquin) and 11.86 feet (subsequent). The author notes that these lake-wide figures exclude the part of the basin above the present water level.

**4. Fairy Island Depression is the largest and most filled basin.** Its area fell from 27,590,000 to 9,063,000 square feet (−67.15 percent) and then to 7,042,000 square feet (−22.30 percent). Its mean depth rose from 46.44 to 69.35 feet in the Algonquin stage, then fell to 23.08 feet. Its volume fell 50.94 percent and then 74.14 percent. Its average deposition was 30.30 feet in the Algonquin stage and 60.30 feet afterward. Boring 2 recorded 104 feet of sediment, and the basin filled more than any other depression.

**5. Bogardus Point Depression filled in the reverse pattern.** It was almost completely filled with pink clay during the Algonquin stage. Its average deposition was 52.03 feet in that stage and 11.56 feet afterward. Its area rose 74.09 percent in the Algonquin stage and then fell 74.26 percent.

**6. Ice-block persistence controlled sedimentation.** The author proposes that large ice blocks persisted for different lengths of time in different depressions. Thin ice in shallow areas melted early and became the first repositories of cold-water sediment. The deepest basins kept their ice longest and received the least pink clay. South Fish-Tail Bay had steep sides and was protected by Grapevine Point, so its ice melted late and it has relatively little pink clay.

**7. Boulder belts and sand strata record glacial processes.** Boulders up to two feet in diameter lie on deep sand at several shoreline borings. The author suggests they were deposited when floating or stranded ice blocks melted near shore. Sand and gravel layers between the Algonquin and later deposits may have been carried into the centers by wave action or dropped directly by melting ice.

**8. Varved clay and carbonates set limits on the record.** The brown varved clay may predate Douglas Lake and could serve as a marker for the original bottom. Laminations in the sediment were judged useful for building a postglacial timetable, to be published separately.

**9. Comparison with other lakes.** The average sediment depths in Douglas Lake are much greater than those in the shallower depressions of [[Winona Lake]] and [[Tippecanoe Lake]] in Indiana, which the author studied earlier.

**10. Limitations noted by the author.** Shoreline data for the original and high-level basins were partly drawn arbitrarily and are less reliable than the present-basin data. The high-level and present stage deposits cannot be distinguished in the sediment, so they are compared only as the high-level and present basins. Mineral identification was limited because the large calcite and organic content prevented separation of the clay minerals.
