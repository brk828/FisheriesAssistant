# Devils Hole Pupfish: Victim of Environmental Damage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This article by [[James E. Deacon]], a biologist at the [[University Of Nevada Las Vegas]], reviews the status of the desert pupfishes of the [[Death Valley]] region. Several populations are extinct or threatened, mainly because of groundwater pumping and drainage of marshes in [[Ash Meadows]], Nevada. The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] is highlighted as the species whose legal protection, won through court rulings on water levels, may also help protect its relatives. The author argues that a [[Pupfish National Monument]], proposed in Senator Alan Cranston's bill S. 2589, is the best long-term solution.

## Background & Description

The [[Cyprinodontidae]] pupfishes of the Death Valley area are small, endemic fishes with restricted ranges. The article covers several forms:

- **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon diabolis*): the best-known desert pupfish. It lives in a spring pool with no surface outflow on a 40-acre disjunct parcel of [[Death Valley National Monument]]. It is the smallest pupfish in the area and occupies the most restricted habitat.
- **[[Warm Springs Pupfish]]**: an endangered form in Ash Meadows, covered by a [[Recovery Plan]] under the [[Bureau Of Land Management]] (BLM).
- **[[Valley Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon nevadensis amargosae*): persists in marshy habitat along the [[Amargosa River]] below Shoshone, California.
- **[[Ash Meadows Pupfish]]** and **[[Ash Meadows Speckled Dace]]**: endemic to Ash Meadows, now restricted to spring pools and short outflow streams.
- **[[Tecopa Pupfish]]** and **[[Shoshone Pupfish]]**: the author reports these as extinct, based on a 1977 survey by Doug Selby of the [[California Department Of Fish And Game]] that found no surviving populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Groundwater connectivity:** Ash Meadows has porous soils and fracture patterns that allow groundwater to move quickly to discharge points. Pumping for irrigation by Spring Meadows Ranch, Inc. (1967–70) lowered the water level in Devils Hole. One pump was so closely connected to the aquifer of Jackrabbit Spring that the spring stopped flowing about half an hour after the pump started, which was fatal to the local [[Ash Meadows Pupfish]] population.
- **Marsh drainage:** Large marshes in Ash Meadows were drained several years before the article, destroying most habitat for the Ash Meadows pupfish and speckled dace.
- **Seasonal cycle:** The population fluctuates with food production and reproduction. It falls to an annual minimum in winter and peaks in late summer or early autumn.
- **Threats:** Irrigation, land promotion, introduced fishes, and water manipulation threaten these fishes. Most of the Ash Meadows area is considered critical habitat for the Devils Hole Pupfish.

## Key Findings

- **Extinctions:** The Tecopa and Shoshone pupfishes are considered extinct after the 1977 survey.
- **Devils Hole water level:** The water level reached an all-time minimum on September 20, 1972. A preliminary injunction by Judge [[Roger D. Foley]] (June 5, 1973) was partly undermined when the [[Ninth Circuit Court Of Appeals]] allowed levels to drop below the court-ordered limit. The [[U.S. Supreme Court]] upheld Foley's interpretation on June 7, 1976.
- **Revised water level:** Deacon's research, under way since 1974, showed that a slightly higher water level than the 1973 order was needed. Judge Foley ruled in favor of this on December 22, 1977.
- **Population goal:** Conditions were set to keep the Devils Hole population above 200 individuals at all times. The population was at 219 in March (the annual low), and a maximum above 400 was expected in August or September. The population was described as responding about as predicted and in good shape.
- **Recovery plan:** The Devils Hole Pupfish Recovery Team submitted a draft plan to the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]]. It states that Ash Meadows must be treated as critical habitat and that the land and water rights there must be acquired by the federal government.
- **Economic argument:** Large-scale farming in Ash Meadows was judged not economically feasible over the long term. Short-term profit from environmental damage is no longer allowed under the court decisions.
- **Proposed solution:** Senator Alan Cranston's bill (S. 2589) would establish a Pupfish National Monument to protect critical habitat, restore wildlife and waterfowl habitat, and provide a recreational resource. The author argues that development in the area would be costly and very difficult.
