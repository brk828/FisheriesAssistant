# Delta of the Colorado River -- Past and Present: A Cultural and Historical Overview

#cultural-history #delta-ecology #fisheries #water-management #international-relations #Cocopa #Colorado-River

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive cultural and historical overview examines the [[Colorado River Delta]], a transboundary ecosystem shared between the United States and Mexico. The document traces human habitation from indigenous [[Cocopa]] peoples through European exploration to modern agricultural and fishing industries. The imposition of the U.S.-Mexico political boundary fundamentally altered human exploitation of the deltaic area. Current agricultural practices and commercial fishing in the Mexican delta are unavoidably affected by upstream water management decisions in the United States. The [[International Boundary and Water Commission]] (IBWC) provides a diplomatic framework for binational cooperation. The author advocates for enhanced scientific study and international cooperation regarding the river and its ecologically, historically, and economically significant delta.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] originates in high mountains and descends through desert terrain, gaining erosive power and carving deep canyons. Before damming, the river annually deposited 160 million tons of suspended sediment. Drilling near [[Holtville, California]] in 1958 reached 8,300 feet without encountering bedrock, demonstrating the massive accumulation of alluvial deposits.

The delta is situated between latitudes 31°-32°31'N and longitudes 113°30'-116°W. It extends from where alluvium forms the dominant soil surface south to [[San Felipe]], east to the [[Santa Clara Mesa]], and west to the peninsular ranges (Sierras Juarez, Cucopah, and Pinta). The delta's three geographic cornerstones are [[Pilot Knob]] (west of [[Yuma, Arizona]]), [[Cerro Prieto]] (an extinct volcano and current geothermal energy site), and [[Punto San Felipe]].

Geologist William Blake in 1853 recognized that the delta included the [[Colorado Desert]] west of Yuma, an area below sea level. He identified the [[Salton Sink]], at 287 feet below sea level the lowest terrain in the Western Hemisphere. Blake determined that the Colorado River historically oscillated between flowing to the Gulf and filling this depression, with the pattern repeating every few hundred years as sediment built land barriers.

The [[Morelos Dam]], completed in 1950 just south of the U.S.-Mexico border, diverted the river into the [[Alamo Irrigation Canal]], effectively drying up the river's path to the Gulf of California. Heavy upstream precipitation in the 1970s-1980s resumed flow to the sea and caused widespread sheet flooding.

### Indigenous Inhabitants

The [[Cocopa]] (Yuman tribe) were the primary documented inhabitants, living chiefly between what are now the [[Hardy River]] and [[Colorado River]]. Population estimates: 22,000 in 1605 (Onate) and 5,000 in 1827 (Hardy). The Cocopa fished the river for native species including [[Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Sucker]] (razorback sucker), and [[Mullet]], using dip nets and willow basket traps. They were primarily agricultural, cultivating beans, pumpkins, squash, watermelons, gourds, corn, and sugar cane in flood areas. They gathered wild rice, *[[Distichlis]]* grass, quelite seed, tree bark, and screwbean mesquite pods.

The 1853 [[Gadsden Purchase]] created an international border dividing the Cocopa into two countries. Construction of 20th-century dams restricted river flows and concomitantly affected fish, fauna, flora, and agricultural systems the Cocopa had depended upon for centuries. As of June 1986, the Cocopa numbered only around 776 individuals: approximately 571 in [[Yuma, Arizona]], 145 in [[Baja California]] near the Hardy River, and 60 at [[Pozos de Arvizu]] south of [[San Luis, Sonora]]. Nearly all are mestizos with Spanish as their primary language.

The Cocopa at [[El Mayor]] (Kilometer 55 of the Mexicali-San Felipe Highway) depend mainly on fall and winter tourism, functioning as guides to sport fishermen and duck hunters. They hold a 143,000-hectare communal ejido with 43 legal member families. They are forming a fishing cooperative (*unidad comunal de produccion pesquera*) with credits from the [[Instituto Indigenista]] for nets and motors. There is renewed interest in cultivating *[[Distichlis]]* (referred to as "wild wheat"), a plant cultivated prehistorically that can be irrigated with saline water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Hydrological Alterations

The [[Treaty of 1944]] between the United States and Mexico divided waters of the Colorado and [[Rio Grande]], guaranteeing Mexico 1,500,000 acre-feet of Colorado River water annually. The treaty established the [[International Boundary and Water Commission]] (IBWC) to manage water deliveries and resolve future issues.

### Salinization Crisis

In 1960, farmers in the [[Mexicali Valley]] noticed white salt residue coating their fields. The [[Wellton-Mohawk Irrigation Project]] in Arizona was pumping saline water from a fossil aquifer into the [[Gila River]], which flowed into the Colorado. Water salinity increased from 800 parts-per-million to 2,700 ppm. The U.S. initially stated that the 1944 treaty specified quantity but not quality of water delivered to Mexico.

The IBWC designed an annex guaranteeing Mexico's water would have the same salinity as the [[Imperial Valley]]'s. Solutions included construction of the [[Wellton-Mohawk Canal]] to the [[Santa Clara Slough]] for hypersaline discharge and authorization of the world's largest reverse osmosis desalinization plant at Yuma. [[Minute 242]] addressed the salinity problem; [[Minute 261]] (1979) expanded the treaty to cover sanitation and water pollution.

### Current Water Management

Mexico receives annual information containing either a low table (1,890,000,000 cubic meters) or high table (2,100,000,000 cubic meters) of expected water. The high table has been consistent in recent years. Information flows from the [[Comision Internacional de Aguas y Limites]] (CILA, Mexico's IBWC component) to the state of Baja California and [[SARH]] (Secretaria de Agricultura y Recursos Hidraulicos). Regional officials meet with the Rural Bank, crop insurance agency, seed producers, and farmer associations to determine irrigation plans.

An informal agreement exists that water arriving at the border will not exceed 800 cubic meters per second (29,248 cfs), though this was exceeded in 1983. Engineers report that fluctuating water levels increase erosion of dikes and natural river banks, creating maintenance challenges.

### Habitat Changes

Before recent water increases, agriculture was limited to one cultivation per year. Mexico now plants 256,675 hectares total (as of 1986), including 11,475 hectares of land without irrigation rights and 41,500 hectares of successive crops. At least 12,000 hectares have been affected by salinity to varying degrees. SARH estimates 15,341,257.544 metric tons of salt enter the region annually, with 13,722,002.919 metric tons leaving, theoretically depositing 1,619,254.625 metric tons in the area.

## Key Findings

### Agricultural Developments

**Historical Context**: The [[Imperial Valley]] and [[Mexicali Valley]] were developed through irrigation beginning in 1900. The [[California Development Company]] formed a Mexican subsidiary and purchased rights to the [[Alamo Wash]] to divert Colorado River water. By 1904, 10,000 settlers had moved onto 100,000 acres irrigated by 400 miles of canals. The [[Colorado River Land Company]] (controlled by Harry Chandler) controlled 800,000 acres of Baja California, using Chinese coolies rather than Mexican labor.

**1905-1907 Flood**: In winter 1904-05, unusual snowfall in the Rockies and Arizona highlands caused unprecedented flooding. The entire Colorado River flowed through [[Alamo Wash]] and [[New River]], filling the Imperial Valley and creating the [[Salton Sea]]. Much of Mexicali's infrastructure was destroyed. The [[Southern Pacific Railroad]] finally closed the gap on February 10, 1907, by dumping boulders from a thousand rail cars.

**Modern Agriculture**: Salinity, collapse of world cotton prices, and [[Pink Boll Worm]] infestation (entering from the United States) eliminated three-fourths of the cotton industry in the Mexicali Valley. Mexico diversified toward salt-tolerant crops like livestock-rye grass. A $97.8 million World Bank loan in 1968 improved the irrigation system.

Heavy Colorado River flow in the early 1980s temporarily made water plentiful, flushing salinity from fields. Many farmers returned to cotton cultivation, but faced prohibitive harvest costs due to Chinese competition. Farmers increasingly turned to wheat and vegetables.

American vegetable companies are renting lands formerly in cotton, bringing laser leveling equipment, tractors, and fumigation. Farmers report profits up to 300% over normal from renting rather than growing. U.S. companies assist with machinery parts that have become expensive under peso devaluation. Increased North American involvement is predicted, ensuring continued Mexican demand for Colorado River water.

### Fisheries

**Gulf of California Shrimping**: In January 1985, [[Cooperativa Venustiano Carranza]] in [[El Golfo de Santa Clara, Sonora]] reported shrimp catches dropped to near zero. Total harvest for their ten boats had been 150 metric tons/year in previous years but reached only 60 tons by January 1985 (season: October 15 - May 31). By June 1986, they captured 119 metric tons during the 1985-86 season. Summer shark catches showed an 80% decline from the previous year.

Possible explanations included: (1) illegal fishing by non-cooperative members using small-mesh nets taking immature shrimp, (2) poaching by approximately 60 non-cooperative free fishermen, (3) aquatic predators consuming shrimp, (4) alterations in sea and riverbed structure affecting breeding areas, and (5) variations in Colorado River flow affecting estuarine spawning habitat.

The cooperative has 64 members and is the only one in El Golfo licensed for shrimp. Two other cooperatives ([[Santa Clara]] and [[Escamera]], with 30 and 70 members respectively) use skiffs for scale fish only. Scale fish are relatively rare at El Golfo; only [[Shark]], [[Sierra]] (mackerel, *Scomber* spp.), and [[Mullet]] appear seasonally.

An eight-year ban on [[Totuava]] was widely ignored. One fisherman reported that in a particularly good year five years prior, truckloads of 15-80 kg totuava were leaving El Golfo daily. Black market shrimp trade is significant: at least 24 million pounds of Mexican shrimp (nearly 1/3 of total catch, worth $144 million) arrived illegally in the United States in 1983.

**Delta Freshwater Fishing**: A levee 100 kilometers long runs from [[Riito, Sonora]] curving northward to where Highway 1 bridge washed out below [[Ledon]]. Between KMs 56-80, three sets of pumps (75-125 hp each) pump irrigation drainage water across the dike. Aquatic avifauna proliferate at pumps, feeding on [[Tilapia]] congregating around oxygenated water.

In January 1985, fishermen's income was based mainly on [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) at $180/kilo (81 cents USD), [[Mullet]] at $100/kilo (45 cents), and [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) at $100/kilo. [[Tilapia]] was considered a plague with no market; buyers from Mexicali would not purchase it. Fishermen spent significant time cleaning gill nets of tilapia. The vast majority of tilapia observed were small, with only a few exceeding one pound.

Several fishermen reported seeing [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) in the past year. Shrimp harvesting during spawning season was reportedly rampant in delta floodwaters, with allegations that [[Pesca]] officials accepted $10,000 peso ($45 USD) bribes from fishermen caught with shrimp. Fishermen reported hanging nets across culverts of the San Felipe Highway during August, collecting tons of shrimp.

By December 1985, largemouth bass catches had dropped approximately 50%, now paying $400 pesos (65 cents) per kilo. Catfish paid $170 pesos/kilo and mullet $150 pesos/kilo. Shrimp had almost disappeared—a negligible quantity was taken during August breeding season, whereas the previous year they had "been jumping like popcorn." One fisherman reported total catch of 206 kilos for the entire year, whereas the previous year he had taken "two tons in two weeks."

By June 1986, water levels had stabilized. [[Catfish]] and [[Mullet]] were most abundant, selling for approximately $200 and $250 pesos (25 and 32 cents USD, respectively) per kilo.

**Introduced Species**: [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Striped Bass]] introduced in the U.S. portion of the Colorado have established populations in the delta. Sport fishermen from Mexicali were observed taking striped bass. Fishing contests for striped bass were held in the [[Alamo Canal]] at KM 15 of the San Felipe Highway. Egg-carrying female stripers were taken in the delta near the Ledon bridge. Local fishermen speculated that these introduced carnivorous predators might prey on breeding shrimp in delta floodwaters.

### Socioeconomic Impacts

At least 300 people are employed in freshwater scale fishing in delta floodwaters, some of whom are farmers whose fields now lie underwater. This industry is dependent on water releases upstream by the United States. The freshwater fishermen are both plagued and benefitted by introduced fish species; as some species establish themselves at the expense of others, catches and market success will vary accordingly.

The [[Cerro Prieto]] geothermal field in the western delta has become an important electrical energy source for northern Mexico. Officials believe the geothermal source is related to underground flow of the river, making them sensitive to upstream water management.

### Recommendations

The author concludes that human and aquatic predators with easy access to breeding shrimp in delta floodwaters, combined with variations in breeding space availability (depending on drainage and upstream releases), may be affecting shrimping in the Gulf of California. Upstream increases of fresh water have had both positive effects (enlarged breeding environment) and negative effects (bringing in aquatic predators and attracting human predators).

With increased attention to the U.S.-Mexico border, increased American involvement in the Mexicali and San Luis Valleys, and growing urban demands on the Colorado River, a binational study of current cultural and biological conditions of the delta is recommended for diplomatic and scientific purposes. Such a study, updating Sykes's 1937 landmark effort, has not occurred since construction of Colorado River dams or since the 1944 treaty and subsequent annexes. The ecology and economics of both countries are affected by and affect the river and its terminus. Enhanced cooperation through binational delta study is highly recommended and overdue.
