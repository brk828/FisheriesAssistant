# Bonytail Chub: Colorado River Recovery and Riparian Notes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This newsletter-style document covers two topics. The first is the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*[[Gila Elegans]]*), a large-bodied cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin. The species was federally listed as endangered in 1980, its wild population is nearly extinct, and recovery depends on a [[Repatriation Program]] that relies on [[Captive Propagation]] at hatcheries. The second is a short field account of a birding trip to [[Bonita Creek]] in Arizona, which describes riparian recovery and bird diversity. The fish article is the substantive recovery account. The creek piece is a field note on riparian habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Endemism and history:** Bonytail are found nowhere else in the world. Ancient bonytail bones have been recovered from prehistoric Native American fishing camps. Early 20th-century surveys misidentified or undercounted the species, so documented historical abundance is uncertain.
- **Identification:** Bonytail are recognized by a slender, flattened head, a dorsal hump that tapers to a narrow caudal peduncle, and a forked tail. Coloration is olive-green on the back with silvery sides and a white underside.
- **Size and age:** Adults captured in [[Lake Mohave]] averaged 46 cm (18 in), with the largest measuring 56 cm (22 in). One exceptional individual from [[Lake Havasu]] exceeded 81 cm (32 in). Individuals in Lake Mohave were estimated at 30 to 50 years old, making the species long-lived.
- **Classification:** Although the species is a minnow (family Cyprinidae), it is considered a "big-river" fish of the Colorado basin.
- **Feeding and habitat use:** Bonytail occupy the limnetic zone during the day. At night they move into shallow pools, eddies, gravelly shorelines, marshy sloughs, and oxbow lakes. They feed on aquatic and terrestrial insects, drifting organic matter, plant debris, and algae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article identifies several interacting causes of decline:

- **Knowledge gaps:** Knowledge of optimal habitat for all life stages, and of the habitat needed for successful reproduction, is limited. Only one spawning observation has been recorded outside captivity: in May 1954, about 500 adults were seen spawning over a gravel shelf in Lake Mohave.
- **Flow alteration:** Depletion, diversion, and dams have altered the historically dynamic Colorado River, reducing or destroying bonytail habitat.
- **Dams:** Dams can directly kill fish by drawing them through turbines and limit access to food delivered from upstream.
- **Non-native species:** Competitive and predatory non-native fish and invertebrates occupy the shallow habitats bonytail use and prevent larval survival and juvenile recruitment through predation.
- **Riparian dependence:** The article cites Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife (1995) figures that 80 percent of Arizona and New Mexico vertebrates use riparian areas for at least half their life cycles, and that 60 to 75 percent of Arizona's resident wildlife species depend on riparian areas. Healthy riparian habitat is therefore directly tied to aquatic ecosystem health.

**Bonita Creek:** The field account describes a 20-year-old riparian area that has improved considerably and is now "lush." The trip recorded 52 bird species, including Brown-crested Flycatcher, Gila Woodpecker, Western Wood-Pewee, Warbling Vireo, Plumbeous Vireo, Yellow Warbler, Lucy's Warbler, MacGillivray's Warbler, Black-throated Gray Warbler, and Townsend's Warbler, along with a desert bighorn ewe observed from about 150 feet above the creek. The author notes that the riparian habitat is well developed.

## Key Findings

**Bonytail recovery status and history**
- Bonytail were listed as endangered in 1980. By then, the population was considered dangerously close to extinction, which led to the creation of the bonytail repatriation program.
- The wild population is now mostly restricted to Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu, and is consistently captured only in Lake Mohave during periodic scientific sampling.
- Successful reproduction is thought to be blocked by predation from non-native fish and by degraded physical habitat. Only a few repatriated fish have survived to sexual maturity.

**Captive propagation and repatriation protocol**
- Since 1980, stocking of hatchery-produced fish has been considered the only viable management option to prevent extinction.
- Adults are collected each year from Lake Mohave during the March to June spawning season using trammel nets along gravelly shorelines.
- Fish are held at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] for recovery, then transported to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center in New Mexico, where they are quarantined, treated for bacterial and fungal conditions, internally tagged, and held in ponds.
- Eggs hatch after about four to seven days. Larvae are reared to juveniles, then returned to the lower Colorado River for growth in backwaters or released into Lake Mohave or Lake Havasu.
- Per the article, more than 200,000 bonytail have been repatriated since 1981. The reported figure is partly garbled by OCR, so it should be verified against the original source.

**Management outlook**
- The article warns that if dedicated, exotic-free habitats are not established, the species may survive only in hatcheries or museum collections.

**Field note (Bonita Creek)**
- The author describes riparian recovery at Bonita Creek near the Gila River confluence and notes the value of bird song identification for surveys.
