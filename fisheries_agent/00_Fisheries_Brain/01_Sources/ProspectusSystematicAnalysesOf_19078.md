# Systematic Analyses of Colorado River Chubs (Genus Gila) - Research Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #systematic-analysis #proposal

## Executive Summary

This document is a research proposal prospectus submitted by Dr. W.L. Minckley and Dr. M.E. Douglas of [[Arizona State University]] Department of Zoology to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] for consideration at the Colorado River Coordinating Committee session on 10 December 1987. The unsolicited prospectus proposes systematic analyses of [[Colorado River]] chubs in the genus [[Gila]], addressing what the investigators describe as a "problematic area" that has divided the committee and prevented disbursement of available research funds. The proposal requests funding for a three-year study period from 1 April 1988 to 31 March 1991, with total costs of $239,458.00 across all budget periods.

## Background & Description

The genus [[Gila]] comprises cyprinid fishes native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The proposal addresses systematic and taxonomic questions regarding these chubs that require resolution before management decisions can be made. The investigators note that bureaucratic problems and lack of resolution on methodological approaches have created a "state of near-catatonia" within the Colorado River Coordinating Committee, preventing the disbursement of funds already available for this research area.

The research design includes extensive travel components to both field research areas and museums across the United States, necessitating off-campus indirect cost assessment. Dr. Harold Tyus of [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in Vernal, Utah is identified as the agency contact.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] system represents a unique and threatened aquatic ecosystem. The proposal emerges from ongoing management and conservation efforts coordinated through the Colorado River Coordinating Committee, suggesting that systematic uncertainties regarding [[Gila]] species are impeding effective management decisions for these native fishes. The need for museum visits indicates the research will incorporate historical specimen data and comparative morphological analyses across the species' range.
