# Ground Water in San Simon Valley, Arizona and New Mexico

#hydrology #groundwater #artesian-systems #arid-valley-agriculture #water-resources

## Executive Summary

This 1917 U.S. Geological Survey report by A. T. Schwennesen documents the groundwater resources of [[San Simon Valley]], an 85-mile-long arid basin in southeastern Arizona and adjacent New Mexico. The valley, bounded by the [[Peloncillo Mountains]] to the east and the [[Chiricahua Mountains]], [[Dos Cabezas Mountains]], and [[Pinaleno Mountains]] to the west, experienced rapid agricultural development following the 1910 discovery of artesian water at San Simon. The study identifies two distinct groundwater horizons: an upper unconfined aquifer in younger stream deposits and a lower confined artesian aquifer beneath a 300-400 foot thick blue clay layer. Field investigations in 1913 and 1915 measured 127 flowing wells with yields ranging from less than 1 to 300 gallons per minute. Water quality analyses show predominantly sodium carbonate type waters with moderate dissolved solids (224-468 ppm in most samples), suitable for irrigation and domestic use. The report documents concerning decreases in well head pressure (39% average decline) and flow rates (43% aggregate decline) between 1913-1915, attributed partly to poor well construction but potentially indicating aquifer depletion. Agricultural sections describe irrigation farming, dry farming supplemented by flood waters, and livestock grazing as the primary land uses.

## Background & Description

### Geographic Setting

[[San Simon Valley]] extends north-northwestward for 85 miles from the head of [[San Bernardino Valley]] (20 miles north of the Mexican boundary) to the [[Gila Valley]]. The valley averages 10 miles wide in its southern portion, widens to a maximum of 35 miles where crossed by the [[Southern Pacific Railroad]], then narrows to 25 miles before opening into the Gila Valley. Elevation ranges from approximately 4,700 feet above sea level at the [[San Bernardino Divide]] to about 3,000 feet at the lower end, yielding an average northward gradient of 20 feet per mile.

The valley occupies a broad, shallow trough with alluvial slopes extending from the bordering mountains. Drainage flows through [[San Simon Creek]], which originates at the lower end of [[San Simon Cienaga]] (a 1,200-acre marsh) and follows the valley axis to the Gila River. The creek channel has been deeply incised since American settlement, with high vertical banks formed primarily in recent decades.

### Bounding Mountain Ranges

**Western Ranges:**
- [[Chiricahua Mountains]]: Extend 35 miles from San Bernardino drainage to [[Apache Pass]]; elevations exceed 8,000 feet ([[Cochise Head]] reaches 8,100 feet); heavily timbered with pine, oak, and juniper; included in [[Chiricahua National Forest]]; principal watercourses include Sulphur, Cave, Turkey, and Whitetail creeks
- [[Dos Cabezas Mountains]]: Extend 20 miles northwestward; named for prominent twin peaks rising to ~8,000 feet; nearly bare with no important streamways to San Simon Valley
- [[Pinaleno Mountains]]: Extend 30 miles to Gila River; culminate in [[Graham Peak]]; northern portion heavily timbered and included in [[Crook National Forest]]

**Eastern Range:**
- [[Peloncillo Mountains]]: Single ridge for most of length; average crest elevation ~5,500 feet; prominent peaks include [[Ash Peak]], [[Doubtful Peak]], [[Stein Peak]], and [[Cienaga Peak]] (all ~6,000 feet); southern portion timbered and included in Chiricahua National Forest; major canyons include Tule Wells, Ward, Cottonwood, Post Office, Skull, and Skeleton

### Settlement History

First white settlers arrived in the early 1820s, establishing cattle ranches in a region well-suited for stock raising with summer grass in valleys and fall/winter pasturage in mountains. The valley remained sparsely settled until 7-8 years before the report (circa 1907-1910), when rapid settlement followed the discovery of artesian water at San Simon in late 1910.

Principal agricultural areas identified:
- **[[San Simon Area]]**: Artesian water irrigation
- **[[Artesia Valley]]**: Artesian water irrigation  
- **[[Bowie Area]]**: Pumped well irrigation
- **[[Rodeo Area]]**: Dry farming supplemented by flood water irrigation

### Vegetation

Native vegetation includes:
- **Higher elevations**: Pine forests, juniper, oak, walnut
- **Stream courses**: Sycamores, cottonwoods
- **Valley bottom**: Mesquite (valuable for fuel, posts, nutritious pods)
- **Original grasslands**: Extensive sacaton flats (now largely degraded by overgrazing and erosion)
- **Current vegetation**: Grama grass, six-weeks grasses, opuntias, saltbushes, sotol, creosote, cat's-claw, sagebrush
- **Introduced species**: Alfileria (winter feed)

Stockmen characterize the valley as "brushy" or "browse" country rather than typical grassland.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Climate and Precipitation

The region experiences an arid climate with bimodal precipitation distribution:

**Seasonal Patterns:**
- Primary maximum: July-September (local thunderstorms induced by mountain topography)
- Secondary maximum: Winter months (general storm systems from Gulf of California and lower Colorado Valley)
- Minimal precipitation: Late autumn and April-June

**Station Records (mean annual precipitation):**
- [[Bowie]] (3,747 ft elevation, 1867-1908 records): 13.51 inches
- [[San Simon]] (3,594 ft, 1881-1915): 9.24 inches  
- [[Rodeo]] (4,300 ft, 1910-1915): 13.60 inches
- [[Paradise]] (5,400 ft, 1908-1915): 20.76 inches

The striking difference between Bowie (13.51") and San Simon (9.24"), despite only 16 miles separation and 153 feet elevation difference, demonstrates the strong orographic influence of the [[Dos Cabezas Mountains]] on local precipitation patterns. Higher mountain stations like Paradise receive substantially more precipitation.

### Geology and Hydrogeology

**Pre-Quaternary Basement:**
- Core of western mountains: Pre-Cambrian gneisses and schists (Gilbert, 1875)
- Overlying sedimentary rocks: Paleozoic limestone, sandstone, shale, quartzite
- Volcanic rocks: Probable Tertiary age lavas widely distributed
- Peloncillo Mountains: Principally Tertiary igneous rocks and volcanic fragments

**Quaternary Valley Fill (oldest to youngest):**

1. **Older Stream Deposits**: Exposed in gravel hills near [[Railroad Pass]] and south of San Simon Cienaga; gray color, coarse well-assorted gravel, mature erosional topography; reached in some deeper wells; thickness at least 750 feet

2. **Lake Beds**: Deposited in ancient lake occupying the basin
   - Upper member: Dense, homogeneous, non-water-bearing blue clay (300-400 feet thick); serves as artesian confining layer
   - Lower members: Gray, yellow, reddish clays interbedded with sand/fine gravel and thin hard sandstone layers; contain artesian water
   - Exposed in lower dissected valley portions; form flat syncline with axis coinciding with valley axis
   - Penetrated in wells to reported depth of 1,230 feet at San Simon; 1,000 feet at Rodeo

3. **Younger Stream Deposits**: Surface mantle over ~2/3 of valley area; overlie lake beds
   - Thickness: ~150 feet at San Simon/Bowie; 300-400 feet near Rodeo
   - Composition: Clay, silt, sand, gravel, boulders; lenticular beds, variable sorting
   - Finer, better-stratified sediments in valley interior; coarser unsorted debris along edges
   - Contains upper (unconfined) groundwater horizon

4. **Lava Beds**: Grayish-black or reddish-brown vesicular basalt; small areas near east edge between Rodeo and Apache; larger areas near Moore's Spur and San Bernardino divide; rests on or interbedded with valley fill

### Soils

Valley soils consist of:
- **Heavy impervious clays**: Derived from lacustrine deposits; yellow and bluish colors
- **Sandy or gravelly loams**: Derived from mountain alluvium
- **Alluvial fans**: Poorly sorted boulders, gravel, sand extending from canyon mouths
- **Organic-rich soils**: Black marshy soil at [[San Simon Cienaga]] from accumulated vegetation

**Soil Chemistry:**
- Generally fertile under irrigation but deficient in nitrogen and humus (typical of semiarid Southwest)
- Sodium carbonate ("black alkali") present due to granitic source rocks in drainage basin
- Effect: Clay deflocculation, increased plasticity and imperviousness
- Limited alkali accumulation due to steep gradients and good drainage
- Volcanic ash beds ("gyp rock") exposed in places, used locally as building stone

### Groundwater Systems

**Two Distinct Horizons Identified:**

**Upper Horizon (Unconfined Aquifer):**
- Occurrence: Younger stream deposits (gravel, sand, mixed materials)
- Water table conditions: Little or no pressure; water does not rise appreciably above static level
- Local terminology: "Surface water" in "surface wells"

**Lower Horizon (Confined Artesian Aquifer):**
- Occurrence: Deeply buried sediments beneath blue clay confining layer
- Pressure conditions: Hydrostatic pressure causes water to rise above upper groundwater level
- Flowing wells: Water reaches surface in [[San Simon Flowing-Well Area]]
- Non-flowing artesian: Water rises but remains below surface in [[Bowie Area]] and margins of San Simon area

### Water Quality and Temperature

**Chemical Characteristics:**
- Predominant type: Sodium carbonate ("black alkali")
- Total dissolved solids: 224-468 ppm in 24 of 25 samples analyzed
- Exception: E.W. Burress well (No. 47) with 6,578 ppm (highly mineralized)
- Regional pattern: Waters from large-flow areas (east and south of San Simon) less mineralized than small-flow areas (west of San Simon)
- Quality assessment: Most waters "good" for irrigation and domestic use; some western area waters "fair" (require good drainage)

**Temperature Patterns:**
- Normal range: 77-84°F
- East and south of San Simon: Very uniform temperatures within normal range
- West of San Simon: Much warmer waters common (>90°F)
- Anomalously high temperatures: Lyday well (No. 28) and Baker well (No. 48) at 105°F; Triangle Cattle Co. well (No. 29) at 109°F
- Interpretation: Heating by uncooled lavas in subsurface flow paths

**Source of Artesian Water:**
- Primary source: Rain and snow on mountainous portions of San Simon drainage basin and upper valley
- Intake areas: Elevated outcrop zones along valley edges (4,000-4,500 ft elevation)
- Flow path: Downward into water-bearing beds beneath blue clay to valley center (3,000-3,500 ft elevation)
- Alternative theory (considered improbable): Seepage from [[Animas Valley]] through Peloncillo Mountain barrier

## Key Findings

### San Simon and Bowie Areas - Upper Groundwater Horizon

**Depth to Water Table:**
- Less than 100 feet throughout axial belt including San Simon flowing-well area and strips along San Simon Creek
- Shallowest: San Simon Cienaga (water table at surface)
- San Simon station: 60-70 feet below surface
- Triangle Cattle Co. well (4 mi NNW of San Simon): 35 feet
- Posey ranch (15 mi below San Simon): ~40 feet
- Depth increases outward from valley axis toward mountains

**Yield and Development:**
- San Simon area: All irrigation from lower artesian horizon; upper horizon supplies railroad (two 12-inch wells, 183 feet deep), stock, and domestic needs
- Supply never known to fail; indications suggest large volume potentially available for irrigation
- Bowie area: Upper horizon supply small; wells generally must reach lower artesian horizon even for stock/domestic use

### San Simon and Bowie Areas - Lower Artesian Horizon

**Aquifer Characteristics:**
- First pressurized water-bearing bed: Immediately under blue clay at 400-600 feet depth
- Additional water-bearing beds: Encountered at intervals to maximum drilled depth of 1,230+ feet
- Separating layers: Clay, shale, sandstone, conglomerate, cemented gravel, hardpan
- Water-bearing bed composition:
  - San Simon area (east of creek and southern west side): Coarse sand and fine gravel
  - San Simon area (north and west of town): Fine sand
  - Bowie area: Coarse white sand

**Flowing Well Area:**
- Total wells documented: 127 flowing wells (October 1915)
- Flow rates: <1 to ~300 gallons per minute
- Average flow (116 wells measured 1915): 52 gallons per minute
- Combined yield: ~15 second-feet or 11,000 acre-feet per year (if continuous flow)
- Geographic variation:
  - Largest flows: East of San Simon Creek (avg ~80 gpm) and southern area west of creek (avg ~90 gpm)
  - Smallest flows: North, west, and south of San Simon (avg ~12 gpm)

**Hydraulic Head:**
- Fall 1915 measurements: ~60 wells measured
- Head range: Fraction of a foot to 42 feet above surface
- Average head (all wells): ~15.5 feet
- Comparison 1913 vs 1915 (8 wells):
  - 1913 average head: 31 feet
  - 1915 average head: 19 feet  
  - Decrease: 39% (12 feet)
  - Individual well decreases: 14-50% in 5 wells
  - Individual well increases: 44-71% in 2 wells (Nos. 57, 104)

**Non-Flowing Artesian Wells:**
- Bowie area and margins of San Simon area: Pressure insufficient to reach surface
- Depth to water: Few feet to >100 feet below surface
- Large area west and north of Bowie: Water rises to <50 feet (considered economical pumping limit)
- Higher ground west and south of Bowie: Water does not rise high enough for economical irrigation pumping
- Bowie pumping yields: 600-800 gpm reported (10-20 gpm per foot of drawdown under steady pumping)

**Performance Decline 1913-1915:**
- Flow comparison (12 wells): 43% aggregate decrease over 2 years
- Individual well flow decreases: 20-80%
- Potential causes identified:
  1. Faulty well construction: Caving and sand filling of uncased portions (primary cause)
  2. Possible aquifer depletion: Increased well numbers and heavier drafts
  3. Drought effects: Exceptionally dry summer of 1915
- Evidence against widespread depletion:
  - No noticeable shrinking of flowing-well area
  - Water levels in non-flowing wells not greatly lowered
  - Marginal wells with initially small flows still flowing
  - Cleaned-out wells show immediate flow increase
  - New wells yield much more than older wells in same locality

**Well Construction:**
- Typical dimensions: 4-6 inches diameter
- Common casing practice: 150-200 feet (short distance into blue clay) - INADEQUATE
- Problem: Caving and sand filling of uncased portions
- Recommended practice: Standard casing to bottom, perforated at water-bearing beds
- Construction method: Jetting process (almost exclusively used)
- Contract cost: $0.50-0.75 per foot
- Average cost (partly cased flowing wells, San Simon): $500-600
- Larger pumping wells (Bowie, cased to bottom): $1,000-2,000
- Cooperative drilling: Even lower costs achieved through joint rig ownership

### Rodeo Area Groundwater

**Occurrence and Depth:**
- Water-bearing formations: Younger stream deposits covering most of area
- Intake mechanism: Coarse gravels along valley border absorb mountain runoff
- Favorable area: Apache vicinity north to San Simon Cienaga (central valley and adjacent lower slopes)

**Depth to Water Table (Central Valley):**
- Northeast of Apache: First water 100-120 ft; second 150-170 ft; third 200-250 ft
- Rodeo vicinity: First water 70-80 ft; second 90-100 ft; third ~150 ft
- North toward cienaga: First water 50-60 ft; second ~70 ft; third 80-90 ft
- San Simon Cienaga: Water virtually at surface; bordering wells <25 ft

**Area with Depth <100 feet:**
- Extent: ~45 square miles from 4 miles south of Rodeo to San Simon Cienaga
- Includes: Central drainage depression and narrow strips on either side
- Outside this tract: Depth 100-300 feet (except near canyon mouths with shallow water in gravels)

**Yield:**
- Example: El Paso & Southwestern Co. pumping plant at Rodeo
  - Well: 18 inches diameter, 160 feet deep
  - Normal water level: 80 feet below surface
  - Pump setting: 100 feet below surface
  - Discharge: 32 feet above ground into tank
  - Pumping rate: ~200 gpm continuous
  - Drawdown: 27 feet total (7 feet below pump)
  - Specific capacity: ~7.3 gpm per foot of drawdown
