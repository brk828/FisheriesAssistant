# Survival of Fish Sperm After Freezing and Storage at Low Temperatures

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-gamete-cryopreservation

## Executive Summary

This 1956 study in *The Progressive Fish-Culturist* by Kermit E. Sneed and Howard P. Clemens tested whether fish [[Milt]] (sperm) could be kept alive through [[Cold Storage]] and [[Cryopreservation]]. Work was done on [[Carp]] and [[Goldfish]] using [[Frog Ringer's Solution]] and other [[Extenders]], with [[Glycerol]] as the protective agent. The authors reported that sperm survived freezing only when glycerol or sugar was present, that [[Glycerol Concentration]] and dilution technique strongly affected results, and that about 20 percent of frozen carp sperm remained motile after 60 hours at -73 °C. The authors stated that the storage duration depended mainly on the quality of the diluting medium. They had not yet shown that frozen fish sperm could fertilize eggs.

## Background & Description

The authors describe several practical reasons for storing fish sperm. Stored milt could be shipped between [[Fish Hatcheries]], fewer males would need to be kept between seasons, selective breeding could use males after they had died, and sperm could be held until females ripened.

Earlier work set the context:

- [[Mann]] (1954) reviewed semen physiology and storage methods.
- [[Rucker]] (1949) preserved the fertilizing capacity of [[Sockeye Salmon]] milt for 14 days in a refrigerator.
- [[Butcher]] (1944) stored [[Brown Trout]] milt under paraffin for 5 days at 2.2 to 3.3 °C.
- [[Smith and Quistorff]] (1943) kept [[Steelhead Trout]] and [[Chinook Salmon]] milt for 2.5 to 5 hours at temperatures given as 6 to 19 (exact range garbled in the scan).
- [[Polge Method]] work (Polge, Smith, and Parkes, 1949) showed fowl sperm survived low temperatures in a Ringer-glycerol solution. The same [[Glycerol Method]] was then applied to bull semen, which yielded successful cow inseminations, and to human sperm.

The study was supported under the [[Saltonstall-Kennedy Act]] for commercial fisheries research, as University of Oklahoma Research Institute Project 120 (Contract No. 14-19-008-2382), directed by Dr. Carl D. Riggs.

Methods used in the study:

- Milt was hand-stripped from fish, collected free of urine, feces, blood, and water, and placed in 1 mL corked vials.
- Fish were sometimes injected with 2 to 3 mg of freeze-dried [[Pituitary Injection]] material from [[Black Buffalo]] 24 hours before stripping. The injection date given is February 14, 1956.
- Samples were frozen by direct placement in a dry-ice chest (about -73 °C) or after slow cooling to -15 °C. Thawing was done in tap water, on the bench, or gradually in a refrigerator.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses hatchery reproduction, not wild population ecology. It does note some relevant conditions:

- Carp milt could be obtained by hand-stripping in almost any month of the year.
- Goldfish were transferred from winter ponds to the laboratory one month before testing and were kept at 72 °F (about 22 °C).
- Out-of-season carp sperm were kept immotile in Ringer's solution. Sperm from ripe fish became immotile only when potassium chloride was added.
- Storage in these extenders was limited by bacterial growth and evaporation of seminal plasma, especially in undiluted samples.
- Adding 1,000 units of [[Penicillin]] per mL of diluted milt impaired motility and did not significantly prevent bacterial growth.
- The authors linked the immotility to ion composition, the osmotic balance of the medium, or both, and did not establish a direct role for potassium.

## Key Findings

**Extender trials**

- Normal frog Ringer's solution was isotonic to out-of-season carp and goldfish sperm and kept it inactive. A few sperm stayed capable of motility after 30 days at about 3 to 5 °C.
- Motile cell numbers declined progressively after about 10 days of storage.
- Adding 200 to 500 mg of KCl per 100 mL of Ringer's solution made sperm from ripe fish immotile. The required amount varied with donor ripeness.
- A [[Phosphate Buffer]] (pH 7.4, with 6 percent glycerin) usually kept out-of-season sperm immotile but not sperm from pituitary-injected fish. A 3 percent sodium citrate dihydrate solution (pH 7.6) gave similar results.
- At about 3 °C, sperm in phosphate or citrate with 6 percent glycerin stayed intermittently motile for more than one week. The authors suggested the effect might relate to higher oxygen tension, as seen in sea-urchin semen.

**Glycerol concentration**

- Sperm placed directly into 6 percent glycerin in Ringer's solution kept motility.
- Direct placement into 10 percent or higher gave little motility.
- Stepwise addition of glycerin allowed some motility at all concentrations up to 30 percent.
- Above 9 percent, dilution with water had to be done very carefully. Cells diluted too quickly swelled and stopped moving. The authors proposed that water balance damage caused this, but did not test dialysis.

**Freezing results (more than 200 samples)**

1. No sperm survived freezing in pure seminal plasma or in any extender without glycerol or sugar. Glycerol was far more effective.
2. The greatest motility occurred in milt frozen in Ringer's with 6 percent glycerin, or 12 percent added in three steps. Samples frozen for 5 to 10 minutes showed an estimated 75 percent motility after thawing and dilution. After 60 hours of storage, about 20 percent of cells were motile.
3. In citrate and phosphate solutions with 6 to 7 percent glycerin, sperm clumped and seminal plasma sometimes coagulated. About 5 to 15 percent of cells were still motile after freezing. Egg yolk did not significantly change these results.
4. Five percent cane sugar gave slight protection, alone or with glycerin. Higher sugar concentrations were harmful.
5. Slow freezing (1 to 2 hours) to -15 °C before storage at -73 °C appeared beneficial.
6. Slow thawing may also be beneficial.
7. Equilibration times of 15 minutes to 24 hours made no significant difference compared with immediate freezing.

**Conclusions**

- Carp milt can be frozen and stored at -73 °C in isotonic salt solutions with 6 to 12 percent glycerol, with 20 percent of sperm motile after 60 hours.
- Results depend as much on the tonicity and composition of the extender as on glycerol's protective action.
- Long-term survival of frozen fish sperm probably depends on improving the diluting medium.
- The authors had no evidence yet that frozen fish sperm would fertilize eggs. They noted that cattle and human studies suggested this might be possible.
