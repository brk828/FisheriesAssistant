# Lead Versenate Time Mark on Fish Scales

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1960 methods paper by Fry, Cucin, Kennedy and Papson, from the [[Ontario Department of Lands and Forests]] and the [[University of Toronto]], evaluates lead disodium versenate ([[Lead Versenate]]) as an injected agent for placing a time mark on fish scales. Injection at 50 mg per kg body weight (5 to 7 percent solution) produced consistent lead deposits in [[Lake Whitefish]] and [[Lake Trout]] scales and was judged superior to the original [[Lead Acetate Method]] of Hiyama and Ichikawa in efficacy and toxicity. The authors report no detectable effect on mortality or growth, though whether handling produced a growth check could not be determined.

## Background & Description

The [[Lead Line Method]] for marking bone and scales was developed by Hiyama and Ichikawa (1952, 1953) to record the time of injection at the scale margin. The authors first injected lake whitefish with lead acetate in South Bay, Lake Huron, but found it difficult to inject the large liquid volume needed. On the suggestion of Dr. H. Boroughs (Hawaii Marine Laboratory), lead versenate was substituted; it is stable in neutral solution, can be injected at higher concentration, and appears less toxic.

Two preparation routes are described:
- **Acetate-derived method:** 10% lead acetate is precipitated with 20% sodium hydroxide (pH approximately 8), redissolved with 20% disodium dihydrogen versenate, neutralized, and diluted to a 5% solution. Final concentration is about 7% expressed as lead versenate.
- **Lead oxide method:** supplied via R. D. Spitz of the [[Dow Chemical Company]]; 5.40 g versene acid, 4.10 g lead oxide, and 7.5% sodium hydroxide are combined, neutralized to pH 7.0, and diluted to 200 mL (5% solution).

Scale development follows Ichikawa and Hiyama (1954) with modifications: sulphide excess is removed with methanol rather than water, and [[Gold Chloride]] staining produces a pinky-blue line, followed by [[Sodium Thiosulphate]] treatment and mounting in Euparol.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Experiments (1955–1956) compared lead acetate and lead versenate in several species, including brook trout, smallmouth bass, white sucker, rock bass, common carp, yellow perch and muskellunge, held in running lake water at the South Bay laboratory, well water at the Maple laboratory, or hatchery facilities at Deer Lake. Field trials were conducted in South Bay and Georgian Bay, with fish captured in pound nets and anesthetized with [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222). Injections were given into dorsal muscle using a 25-gauge needle, with dosage set from a length-based chart or estimated by experienced fishermen (0.5 mL of 5% solution per pound estimated).

Scale growth was checked against body growth using recaptured [[Georgian Bay]] whitefish at large for one year. Growth was closely proportional across the sample, and the release season had little current-year scale growth, which complicated assessment of any handling-induced check.

## Key Findings

- **Dosage:** 50 mg/kg of lead versenate was effective; the authors concluded the dose could safely be doubled, and field dosages were based on this.
- **Relative efficacy:** Versenate was "somewhat more effective and somewhat less toxic" than lead acetate in the 1955–1956 comparisons (Table 1 summarizes species, dosage, solution strength, effect, toxicity and first appearance of the mark in days).
- **Timing:** Lead is deposited at the scale margin within a few days of injection (probably 1 or 2 days).
- **Survival and growth:** No difference in survival between injected and control fish was observed, and growth rates did not appear to differ (Table 3).
- **Recapture returns (through August 1959, South Bay):** Lake trout releases of 358 (1957), 348 (1958) and 80 (1959) injected fish; whitefish releases of 145 (1958) and 398 (1959). Detailed counts are in Table 2.
- **Mark success:** Whitefish gave 86 percent positive findings versus 69 percent for lake trout. Lake trout scales are difficult to flatten and were not examined critically.
- **Scale-body growth validation:** Scale-growth ratios differed from body-growth ratios by small amounts: +0.8 percent (26 mounts, average deviation 3.7 percent) and −0.4 percent (25 impressions, average deviation 2.2 percent). Mean body length increase over the year at large was 13.5 percent. Mounted scales averaged 1.3 percent larger than their pre-treatment impressions (30 cases).
- **Open question:** Whether tagging and injection caused a growth check remained undetermined, because the false annulus would merge with the existing annulus at release.
