# Fishes of Aravaipa Creek, Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona

#ichthyology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #desert_streams #native_fish_assemblage

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: This survey documented nine species and one hybrid from [[Aravaipa Creek]], a spring-fed tributary of the [[San Pedro River]]. Seven native species persist: [[Gila robusta]] (roundtail chub), [[Rhinichthys osculus]] (speckled dace), [[Agosia chrysogaster]] (longfin dace), [[Tiaroga cobitis]] (loach minnow), [[Meda fulgida]] (spikedace), [[Catostomus insignis]] (Sonora sucker), and [[Pantosteus clarki]] (Gila sucker). Two introduced species, [[Ictalurus melas]] (black bullhead) and [[Lepomis cyanellus]] (green sunfish), were restricted to an artificial pond lateral to the creek. Hybrids of C. insignis × P. clarki were documented at three stations. Age and maturity data: Not reported. Fecundity: Not reported. Growth parameters: Not reported. The [[Gila robusta]] population represents intergrades between G. r. robusta and G. r. intermedia based on meristic characters: lateral-line scales ranged 73-86 (mean 78.6), dorsal fin-rays mean 8.81, anal fin-rays mean 8.70, intermediate between the two subspecies.

**Habitat Associations**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] drains 1,200 km² with approximately 50 km of permanent surface water. Stream gradient averages 5.7 m/km above the canyon and 8.2 m/km within the canyon. Mean discharge ranged 0.50-1.46 m³/second historically (1932-38 average 0.79 m³/second). Water temperatures during the survey ranged 8.9-20.6°C; summer temperatures can exceed 27°C but are moderated by percolation through coarse sediments, cliff shading, and riparian vegetation. Bottoms consist of unconsolidated sand, gravel, and boulders with sparse aquatic vegetation (watercress, filamentous algae). Water is slightly turbid with fine suspended silt and hard (marl deposits on riffles). Ground water typically contains <600 mg/L dissolved solids, with some areas >1.5 mg/L fluoride. No organic pollution was evident. Riparian vegetation includes dense shrubs ([[Baccharis]]), gallery forest of cottonwood ([[Populus]]), sycamore ([[Platanus]]), willow ([[Salix]]), ash ([[Fraxinus]]), box elder ([[Acer]]), and xeric species (walnut, oak, mesquite, acacia). The stream flows through Upper Sonoran vegetation dominated by cacti, creosote bush, and grasses.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Not reported. Young [[Agosia chrysogaster]] disperse rapidly downstream into normally-intermittent stream sections when opportunity presents, persisting under severe environmental conditions. Young suckers (both [[Catostomus insignis]] and [[Pantosteus clarki]]) inhabit warm, quiet backwaters, moving into faster waters as juveniles, then into riffles or pools as adults.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Extreme habitat partitioning occurs among congeners and within families. [[Gila robusta]] occupies deep pools, particularly behind irrigation diversions; 39 adults (56-182 mm SL) were gill-netted from one pool at Station 5. [[Agosia chrysogaster]] dominates sandy, shallow pools in moderate current, comprising 100% of fauna at downstream stations and >80% at several upstream stations; schools frequent shallow sandy areas while large adults seek pools with cover (algal mats). [[Meda fulgida]] prefers moving water <1 m deep, living in deep swift pools or downstream ends of riffles, but was found on flat riffles <30 cm deep during this survey. [[Tiaroga cobitis]] is restricted to riffles in turbulent areas with coarse gravel/small rubble bottoms in water <20 cm deep; seine-hauls with Tiaroga generally contained no other species. [[Rhinichthys osculus]] habitat overlaps Tiaroga on riffles (but swims actively above substrate while Tiaroga remains on bottom) and [[Meda]] in pools (but Meda is more mid-water). [[Catostomus insignis]] is most common in pools behind irrigation diversions and on deep riffles; small individuals occur in turbulent waters. [[Pantosteus clarki]] reaches peak abundance in canyon reaches; large individuals occur in pools, small adults and young predominate on riffles over gravel/rubble bottoms. This habitat partitioning minimizes interspecific competition in the relatively depauperate southwestern fauna.

**Environmental Drivers**: Spring-fed discharge maintains permanent flow despite minimal surface runoff from the 1,200 km² drainage. Occasional severe flooding is indicated by debris 3 m above normal water level. Irrigation diversions create artificial pools that concentrate certain species (G. robusta, C. insignis). Cliff shading and riparian vegetation moderate summer temperatures. Substrate type (sand vs. gravel/rubble vs. bedrock) influences discharge and habitat suitability for different species.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] serves as a refugium for seven of 12 known native fish species from the [[San Pedro River]] drainage. Five species historically present in the drainage do not persist in Aravaipa: [[Ptychocheilus lucius]] (Colorado squawfish), [[Catostomus latipinnis]] (flannelmouth sucker), [[Xyrauchen texanus]] (humpback sucker), [[Cyprinodon macularius]] (desert pupfish), and [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] (Gila topminnow). The first three may never have occurred in the creek (characteristic of large, swift-flowing waters); the last two probably were present historically in marshes near the mouth. Extensive marshes along lower Aravaipa and the San Pedro River existed historically (documented by malaria incidence at Camp Grant in 1870s-1880s, water rights data, trappers' records). Major vegetative change since 1870s: expansion of mesquite replacing Sacatón grass stands. [[Tiaroga cobitis]] may have declined spatially and numerically: Simon collected 6 adults near Station 7 in 1943, Miller collected 106 specimens near Station 13 in 1950, but this survey found the species uncommon (total catch 53 individuals, most abundant at Stations 13-14). [[Meda fulgida]] was not collected by Simon (1943) or Miller (1950) but was found at multiple stations in this survey. [[Rhinichthys osculus]] remains rare: one specimen in this survey, one in 1943, 35 in 1950.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Increasing water demands in the American Southwest threaten native aquatic fauna. Irrigation along Aravaipa: approximately 3 km² of downstream floodplain under irrigation in 1941, similar use observed in 1964-65; 12.5 km² under cultivation in upper valley in 1941. Pumps removing subsurface water for irrigation were noted near Klondyke in 1964-65, though no diminution in average flow was reported. Movement of sand into southwestern streams during late 1800s erosion cycle probably decimated [[Gila robusta]] populations even in streams maintaining permanent flow. Introduced species pose limited threat currently: [[Ictalurus melas]] and [[Lepomis cyanellus]] restricted to artificial pond (Station 4); [[Micropterus salmoides]] (largemouth bass) reported in same pond. Swift current and lack of muddy pools make large populations of these species unlikely in mainstream. However, introduction of [[Notropis lutrensis]] (red shiner) disrupts habitat partitioning patterns in many Arizona streams. No other Arizona stream supports so large a segment of aboriginal fauna with so few exotic forms.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Recognition that [[Aravaipa Creek]] habitats have changed little in recent years (except near mouth and possibly through entrenchment in upper stream) is imperative. The undisturbed area should be maintained as a reserve for native animals, allowing preservation of a unique native fauna for study and appreciation by subsequent generations. Prevent introduction of additional exotic species, particularly [[Notropis lutrensis]], which disrupts habitat partitioning. Monitor effects of groundwater pumping for irrigation on stream discharge. Protect irrigation diversions that create pools supporting [[Gila robusta]] and [[Catostomus insignis]] populations while recognizing these are artificial features. Maintain riparian vegetation and cliff shading that moderate summer temperatures. Prevent organic pollution and excessive siltation.
