# Bogs: Living Climate Archives and a Possible Carbon Source

#peatland #sphagnum #paleoecology

## Executive Summary

This 1958 reprinted *Scientific American* article by [[Edward S. Deevey Jr.]] describes the [[Bog]] as a distinct ecosystem that forms when a lake fills with silt and organic debris. Bogs are wet, nutrient-poor peat systems whose layered deposits record past climates, and the article argues they may influence the global [[Carbon Cycle]]. The author proposes that peat oxidation could be a major source of the rise in atmospheric [[Carbon Dioxide]] observed since about 1870. The document contains no fish, and the biota it covers are plants, insects and mammals.

## Background & Description

- **Definition.** The true bog is distinguished from the reedy [[Marsh]], which forms near salt water and is dominated by grasses. Bogs need rainfall, are absent from deserts, and occur in both continental interiors and coastal areas where trees can grow on the uplands.
- **Origin by lake infilling.** Most bogs begin as lakes. Silt from entering streams, debris from shoreline plants and runoff from swampy flats fill the basin. Sedge and [[Swamp Loosestrife]] then bridge open pools, and a floating mat supports shrubs and trees.
- **Raised bog (Hochmoor).** A raised bog can form in any wet meadow without a lake. It depends on [[Sphagnum]] (peat moss), which absorbs water by capillary action and holds more water than absorbent cotton. Accumulated peat draws groundwater upward, and the dome-shaped mass grows upward and outward, sometimes uphill.
- **Peat stratigraphy.** Below the bog's surface, peat does not decay because oxygen is excluded. Plant remains, including human corpses, persist for centuries. A forest on a raised bog suggests a recent shift to a drier climate.
- **Chemistry.** Bog water is poor in salts and bases for three reasons: much of it is rain, peat absorbs dissolved matter like an ion-exchange resin, and granite bedrock contributes few minerals. Bog plants are therefore starved for lime, phosphorus and nitrogen, and the deeper the peat, the more severe this becomes.
- **Biota.** Bogs are "enclaves of subarctic life," with [[Black Spruce]], [[Cotton Grass]] and [[Labrador Tea]]. Cranberries occur on Cape Cod and blueberries in New Jersey. Subarctic animals such as the [[Bog Lemming]] and the olive-backed thrush occur far south of their normal ranges. [[Insectivorous Plants]] (sundews, butterwort and [[Pitcher Plant]]) solve nitrogen deficiency by trapping prey. Pitcher-plant leaves hold rainwater that serves as larval habitat for the [[Pitcher Plant Mosquito]] (*Wyeomyia smithii*), which has followed its host northward to near the arctic tree line.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Zonation.** Photographs and diagrams show a clear succession from open water to forest:

1. Deep water with pondweed.
2. Shallows with floating-leaved pondweeds.
3. Loosestrife forming a creeping floating mat.
4. Leatherleaf, bog rosemary and sphagnum.
5. Drier ground with black spruce, cottongrass and sedge.
6. Tall shrubs such as smooth alder and shining willow.
7. Edge forest of red spruce, mountain ash or larch, separated from upland trees by a water-filled ditch, or "fossa," that marks the original shoreline.

**Sensitivity to climate.** Bogs are sensitive climate indicators. A moister or cooler climate renews growth both upward and outward. A drier or warmer climate stabilizes the surface and deepens the zone of decay. Prolonged drought can weather the peat deeply, and the bog "will dwindle away in the sun like an ice cube." Because bogs respond slowly, they integrate short-term weather into climate.

**Stratigraphic record.** Peat layers are alternately:
- *Bright, raw sphagnum peat* with cottongrass remains, marking cooler, wetter flooding periods.
- *Dark, humus-rich peat* with heather, birch or alder, marking drier, warmer stable or decaying periods.

The Hochmoor example at [[Bemidji]] and the layered cross-section in the figures show dated layers. These include a cold, dry phase near 600 B.C., when tree stumps overlie the moss layer.

**Dating methods.** The [[Pollen Analysis]] method, founded by [[Lennart von Post]] in 1916, uses ratios of fossil pollen grains to date climate periods. It is relatively coarse, since one pollen period spans two or three bog layers. Radiocarbon dating refines these chronologies.

**Regional climate history.** [[Weber's Grenzhorizont]], a widespread bog-rejuvenation horizon in northern Germany, coincided with von Post's "sub-Atlantic climatic deterioration," when northern Europe became colder and rainier, around 600 B.C. Upland vegetation responded more slowly than bogs. Bogs thereafter spread across the British uplands as [[Blanket Bog]].

**Human history.** The article links bog expansion to human events:
- Greek decline and Roman rise coincided with climatic change shortly after 600 B.C.
- Rome abandoned its conquest of Britain around 400 A.D., partly for climatic reasons.
- By the Middle Bronze Age (about 1200 B.C.), British bogs were encroaching. Wooden trackways were laid to keep trade routes open. These are datable by pollen, artifacts and radiocarbon.
- Most European forests were cut long ago. Drained raised bogs were dug for peat fuel in Ireland, Scotland, Denmark, western Scandinavia, northern Germany, and to a lesser extent in Maine and New Brunswick.

**Global peat reserve.** Russian peat expert [[George Kazakov]] estimates about 223 billion dry tons of peat on Earth, more than half in the U.S.S.R.

## Key Findings

- **Rising carbon dioxide.** Atmospheric CO₂ has increased by 11 per cent since about 1870 and apparently continues to rise. Radiocarbon assays by [[Hans Suess]] show that most of the added carbon is modern, too young to derive mainly from fossil fuels. Fossil fuel burning can therefore account for only a small part of the increase.
- **Peat as a suspected source.** The author suggests that bogs are a likely source of the remaining added CO₂. Warming since the last century may have accelerated surface oxidation of peat by soil bacteria.
- **Scale of potential release.** Complete oxidation of world peat would release about 366 billion tons of CO₂. This is a sixth of the amount present in the atmosphere. The total carbon in land plants and animals is only about 15 times that amount. Humus in ordinary soils is not included in this estimate.
- **Cause and effect uncertain.** Climate and CO₂ have changed in parallel, but the author notes that causality is not yet established. Extra CO₂ absorbs more heat, so warming may itself have been caused by the added CO₂.
- **Projected consequences.** A doubling of atmospheric CO₂ would almost certainly melt glaciers enough to raise sea level perhaps 100 feet. The author notes that CO₂ should eventually dissolve in the oceans. It is "a major mystery" why the extra 11 per cent has not dissolved already.
- **Bogs as climate archives.** Bog layers record climate changes, and simultaneous layers across many bogs imply a repeated regional climate change rather than local water-table fluctuation.
- **Bogs and human history.** Climatic shifts after 600 B.C. and around 400 A.D. are linked to changes in European civilizations, and bog expansion affected trade routes and land use.
