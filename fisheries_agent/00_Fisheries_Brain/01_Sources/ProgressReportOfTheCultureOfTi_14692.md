# Progress Report of the Culture of Tilapia mossambica (Peters) Hybrids in Southern California

#ichthyology #aquaculture #cichlidae #tilapia #hybrid_production #warmwater_fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1966 progress report documents the [[California Department of Fish and Game]]'s experimental culture of all-male [[Tilapia mossambica]] hybrids produced by crossing Zanzibari males with Javanese females. Initiated in December 1963, the study aimed to develop culture techniques, assess growth rates, determine temperature tolerances, and evaluate potential applications for sport fishing and biological control in southern California waters. The research successfully produced over 5,000 all-male hybrids using laboratory breeding protocols at the [[Chino Fisheries Base]]. Hybrids demonstrated growth rates of 1.0–2.4 inches per month in holding ponds under warm conditions but proved intolerant of prolonged exposure to temperatures below 50–55°F. The study concluded that fingerlings stocked at 2–3 inches in spring could reach catchable size (6+ inches) by late summer, though temperature limitations restrict their use to warmwater environments.

## Background & Description

[[Tilapia mossambica]] is an ancient African cichlid with a history of aquaculture dating to before 2000 B.C. in Egypt. The species is a maternal mouth brooder: females hold fertilized eggs and subsequently fry in their buccal cavity until the young become free-swimming. The Javanese subspecies originated from the [[Serang River]] of East Java (initially misidentified as a new species, "ikan mudjeir"), while the Zanzibari subspecies is indigenous to brackish swamps of [[Zanzibar]]. During World War II, Japanese forces disseminated the species throughout Java, and it subsequently became an important protein source across Indonesia, Malaysia, Taiwan, and Ceylon.

[[Hickling]] (1963) and colleagues discovered that crossing Zanzibari males with Javanese females produced fertile, exclusively male offspring—an intriguing development for aquaculture, as it could prevent the overpopulation and stunting common in mixed-sex tilapia populations. The species had already been introduced into Arizona canals and ditches tributary to the [[Colorado River]] by 1963, raising concerns about potential establishment in California waters. In November 1963, the [[California Fish and Game Commission]] authorized importation of both subspecies for experimental purposes.

The study obtained 111 tilapia from Dr. William J. McConnell at the [[University of Arizona]] in December 1963: 103 Javanese (7 males, 96 unsexed juveniles) and 8 Zanzibari (4 males, 4 females). By December 1965, the program had expanded to 5 adult male Javanese, 33 adult female Javanese, 7 adult male Zanzibari, 5 adult female Zanzibari, 250 adult male hybrids, 319 hybrid fingerlings, and 1,450 hybrid fry—with zero females among all hybrids produced.

**Morphological Characteristics**: [[Tilapia mossambica]] is distinguished by 27–29 dorsal fin rays, 12–15 anal fin rays, 14–19 gill rakers, unicuspid outer teeth in mature males, approximately 29–32 lateral line scales, and variable coloration (dark brown, grey to silver grey, sometimes with six vertical dark bars). Javanese subspecies typically display more intense yellowish coloration on cheek and chin, a spectacle mark across the nose, more prominent vertical bars in females, pectoral fins reaching the base of the third anal spine when placed horizontally, and fewer gill rakers (14–16) compared to Zanzibari (22–33).

**Sexual Dimorphism**: Males exhibit a distinct genital papilla posterior to the anal opening and anterior to the ureter. Females display an oviduct opening between the anal opening and ureter. Sex determination becomes reliable when fish exceed 2.5 inches in length. Independent examinations of 87 fish (2.7–4.0 inches) by two observers showed 100% agreement, later confirmed by internal examination and subsequent sexual maturation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature Requirements**: Temperature emerged as the critical limiting factor for [[Tilapia mossambica]] culture and survival. North Vietnamese researchers (Long, Giay, and Vinh 1961) established 30°C (86°F) as optimum rearing temperature, with upper lethal limit at 43°C (108°F), upper sublethal at 37–38°C (98.8–100.4°F), lower sublethal at 16–20°C (60.8–68°F), and lower lethal at 6°C (42.8°F). This study confirmed upper tolerance near 108°F and lower lethal around 42°F. [[Allenson and Noble]] (1964) reported upper lethal temperature of 36°C (100.8°F). [[Swingle]] (1957) experienced total loss at 41°F during cold-room tests at [[Auburn University]].

Field observations at [[Indio City Golf Course]] pond demonstrated that hybrids survived brief exposure to 42°F on November 15, 1964, but complete mortality occurred when temperatures stabilized at 50–55°F for extended periods through December 23. At [[University of California Riverside]] experimental ponds, all 48 hybrids died during the week prior to November 16, 1964, when water temperatures dropped to 42–43°F lows. Feeding activity slowed in low 70s°F and ceased below 60°F, while higher temperatures (within tolerance limits) increased activity and food consumption.

**Reproductive Biology**: Spawning begins with males constructing nests by removing sand or cleaning specific bottom areas. Females lay eggs in the designated nest site, males fertilize them, and females collect and hold eggs in the buccal cavity. Under laboratory conditions (66–82°F), fry normally hatched 7–10 days after egg deposition and became free-swimming 3–5 days later. Optimum temperatures would shorten these periods. Free-swimming fry return to the mother's mouth when frightened through cooperative behavior. Females can spawn monthly under suitable conditions.

Fecundity varied with female size. Brood Javanese females averaged approximately 200 fry per spawning. Individual production ranged from 65 fry (4.0-inch, 0.39-ounce female) to 304 fry (4.5-inch, 0.53-ounce female). A 6.2-inch, 1.2-ounce female produced only 95 fry, suggesting factors beyond size influence fecundity. Ripe Javanese females exhibited typical maturation characteristics including mouth pouch development, oviposition, and egg storage even in the absence of males, though these infertile eggs were presumably consumed. Mature male hybrids displayed spawning behaviors including coloration and nest building despite absence of females.

**Feeding Ecology**: [[Tilapia mossambica]] proved highly adaptable in diet. Laboratory trials tested guinea pig pellets (rejected), rabbit feed (disintegrated rapidly), filamentous green algae ([[Cladophora]]), frozen daphnia, mosquito larvae, cooked fish, chopped liver, hamburger, chicken, turkey, and canned dog food—all accepted by adults and juveniles. Adults consumed live [[Mosquitofish]] ([[Gambusia affinis]]) and guppies ([[Lebistes reticulatus]]); fingerlings ate fresh fish small enough to ingest. Zanzibari and hybrids proved more piscivorous than Javanese.

The study adopted dry trout meal (crumbles or pellets: 37% protein, 35% carbohydrate, 5% fat, 9% ash, 2.86% fiber, 10% moisture, 1.14% vitamins/additives) as standard diet with occasional supplements of filamentous algae and frozen or live daphnia. [[Uchida and King]] (1962) demonstrated significantly superior growth on high-quality trout feeds versus livestock feed (white wheat middlings), though they suggested prolonged use might impair spawning potential. After 18 months on trout meal diet, no impairment in maturation or fecundity was detected in this study.

Feeding rates of 3–5% body weight per day proved optimal for fingerlings and adults fed once daily. Rates exceeding 5% resulted in unutilized residues, reduced dissolved oxygen, and decreased water clarity. Fry under 14 days old required at least two (preferably more) daily feedings of pulverized trout meal or fine tropical fish meals.

Stomach analysis of hybrids from [[Santee Pond]] revealed consumption of dragonfly and damselfly larvae, water boatmen adults, [[Ephemeroptera]] (mayfly larvae), unidentified amphipods, and unidentified adult insect parts—demonstrating opportunistic invertivory.

**Disease and Parasites**: [[Tilapia mossambica]] demonstrated low susceptibility to diseases and parasites. [[Chimits]] (1955) stated no serious diseases or parasites of Tilapia had been recorded. Fungus ([[Saprolegnia]]) infections occurred only twice: once from handling during seining, once presumably from poor conditions associated with low water temperatures. All fish recovered completely after two 30–60 second dips in 1:15,000 malachite green solution; prompt recovery suggested single treatment might suffice. No fungus outbreaks occurred during warm water periods. Susceptibility to disease transmission from other warmwater fishes remained untested.

**Deformities**: Only seven deformed fish appeared among entire hybrid production (less than 1% of two affected batches). Malformations included spinal curvature or shortening (four fish), absence of right eye (two fish), and absence of caudal peduncle and caudal fin (one fish).

**Mortality Factors**: Female Javanese were killed by Zanzibari males during spawning trials in 20-gallon aquaria. Deaths occurred when females were not ready to spawn, males were larger, and no cover was available. Abundant plastic strip cover eliminated this problem. Other losses resulted from jumping out of tanks (eliminated by polyethylene film covers), handling, and predation. The semi-opaque film covers also reduced alarm activity from personnel movements. Most fry losses in breeding troughs resulted from parental cannibalism before implementation of early separation protocols.

## Key Findings

**Hybrid Production Success**: The study successfully developed techniques for culturing all-male [[Tilapia mossambica]] hybrids by crossing Zanzibari males with Javanese females, confirming [[Hickling]]'s findings. From March 23, 1964 through the report period, over 5,000 hybrid fry were produced with zero females—100% male offspring. By December 1965, stocks included 250 adult male hybrids, 319 hybrid fingerlings, and 1,450 hybrid fry.

**Optimal Culture Methods**: The most efficient production technique involved: (1) placing one Zanzibari male with 1–5 Javanese females in aquaria no smaller than 20-gallon capacity with abundant cover but no bottom material required; (2) daily inspection for females carrying eggs or fry (10-day intervals may suffice); (3) transferring egg-carrying females to separate tanks and removing fry from females' mouths for placement in nursery tanks; (4) feeding fry at least twice daily on finely ground trout meal until advanced fry stage, then transitioning to larger food.

**Production Potential**: Under suitable hatchery conditions with a basic breeding unit of five females per male, the study estimated 20,000 hybrids could be produced per unit per year, though maximum production potential remained to be determined. Individual Javanese females averaged approximately 200 fry per spawning, with monthly spawning capability under optimal conditions.

**Alternative Production Methods**: Eggs could be successfully hatched in aerated jars (99% survival with one batch of 120 eggs), though Arizona workers reported average 50% mortality. Spawning cages placed in ponds proved impractical due to difficulty determining fry production and potential cannibalism from different-sized hybrids occupying the same pond. Pond breeding (male and females released together) produced 56+ hybrids from one male and three females over approximately three months, but lacked control necessary for consistent large-scale production.

**Growth Rates**: Hybrids held in [[Chino Fisheries Base]] holding ponds grew 1.0–2.4 inches per month, averaging 1.4 inches per month. At [[Santee Pond]], hybrids averaged 1.4 inches per month. Maximum size obtained was 8.7 inches (fish held in pond for 5.7 months, with most growth in first 3 months). Well-fed hybrid fry in heated tanks attained 3 inches in 2–3 months. First Javanese fry (0.2 inches at hatching) grew approximately 1 inch by day 18 and 2 inches by day 38.

Temperature proved the main limiting factor for growth. Hybrids held 59 days at average 76.5°F grew 2.4 inches per month, while hybrids held 87 days at average 71°F grew only 1.0 inches per month. Feeding slowed in low 70s°F and ceased below 60°F ([[Allenson and Noble]] 1964). Little growth occurred in October or November due to low water temperatures.

**Survival Rates**: Recovery of fish stocked in [[Chino Fisheries Base]] ponds varied from 19–83%. Highest recovery rates (83% and 79%) resulted from ponds stocked with large fry. Lowest recovery rates (19%, 49%, and 51%) occurred in ponds stocked with small fry. The 19% recovery occurred when fry were taken directly from female's mouth and released into pond. Optimal survival required holding young hybrids to at least free-swimming fry size before stocking.

**Temperature Tolerance**: Hybrids tolerated brief periods as low as 40°F but could not survive prolonged exposure to temperatures below 50–55°F. At [[Indio City Golf Course]] pond, hybrids survived 42°F on November 15, 1964, but complete mortality occurred when temperatures stabilized at 50–55°F through December 23. At [[University of California Riverside]] ponds, all 48 hybrids died when temperatures dropped to 42–43°F lows during the week prior to November 16, 1964. Temperature emerged as the critical limiting factor for use as sport fish in California waters.

**Stocking Recommendations**: For maximum growth and survival during warm months, fingerlings should be stocked at 2–3 inches length in spring (April), thereby obtaining sufficient growth to provide catchable-sized fish (6+ inches) by late summer and fall (June or July, depending on water temperatures and available food). Stocking smaller fry resulted in significantly lower survival rates.

**Catchability**: No problems were anticipated concerning catchability, though angler acceptance remained to be ascertained. Spin fishing with earthworms as bait on September 22, 1964, in 74°F water produced three hybrids (7.9–8.4 inches, 3.0–3.6 ounces) within minutes. An earlier attempt with artificial flies failed to produce strikes (water temperature not recorded). [[McConnell]] (personal communication) found Arizona hybrids readily accepted both artificial flies and worms.

**Biological Control Applications**: Tests at [[University of California Riverside]] experimental ponds (September 17–November 16, 1964) demonstrated hybrids did not appreciably reduce larval midge populations (predominantly [[Chironomus californicus]]). Sixteen hybrids per 1/150-acre pond (approximately 205 pounds per acre stocking rate) failed to control midges, though general observations indicated mosquito larvae were controlled. Hybrids appeared no more effective than [[Carp]] for aquatic plant control, with control realized mainly through nest-building activities rather than herbivory.

**Fish Kill Events**: Two fish kills occurred at [[Santee Pond]] on August 25 and September 8, 1965, apparently resulting in complete elimination of all fish. Heavy blooms of the algae [[Microcystis]] were noted during both kills. The August 25 kill included approximately 15 dead tilapia, several [[Red-ear Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis microlophus]]), [[Threadfin Shad]], and an 11-inch [[Largemouth Bass]] ([[Micropterus salmoides]]).

**Management Implications**: The study prepared California for potential management needs if [[Tilapia mossambica]] appeared in state waters, as the species had already been introduced into Arizona canals and ditches tributary to the [[Colorado River]]. [[St. Amant]] (1966) subsequently reported T. mossambica (Javanese) established in a tributary of the [[Salton Sea]], [[Imperial County]], California. The rapid spread of [[Threadfin Shad]] ([[Dorosoma petenense]]) throughout Imperial County canals and wasteways and its appearance in the [[Salton Sea]] suggested similar potential for tilapia dispersal.

**Future Directions**: The study planned to continue through at least 1966 with goals including: (1) developing parent stock of both subspecies through selective breeding to produce low-temperature-tolerant hybrids; (2) producing 10,000–20,000 fingerlings for introduction into a heavily-fished southern California water to provide data on catchability, angler reception, growth rates, effects on California ichthyofauna, and how hybrids are affected by other fishes.
