# Mitochondrial DNA Diversity Within and Among Populations of Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*) as Determined by Cytochrome b Sequences

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State University honors thesis (Jeffery Chow, May 2000) uses mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) cytochrome b sequences and single-stranded conformational polymorphism (SSCP) screening to assess genetic distinctiveness among [[Speckled Dace]] populations, focusing on Nevada. The study addresses long-running taxonomic debate over the many morphologically distinct, sometimes endangered, subspecies and isolated forms of *Rhinichthys osculus*. Results identify major phylogenetic lineages, quantify population structure, estimate divergence times against known [[Great Basin Geologic History]], and propose [[Evolutionarily Significant Units]] (ESUs) and [[Management Units]] (MUs) for conservation.

## Background & Description

*Rhinichthys osculus* is a polytypic cyprinid distributed through streams and rivers of western North America, from Washington and Idaho to southern California and Arizona. It is described as the most ubiquitous freshwater fish in the western United States and the only native species occurring in all seven major drainages in the region. Its morphological variation is extensive, with numerous local variants, and Nevada populations have been assigned to several subspecies, including *R. o. carringtoni* (Snake River), *R. o. robustus* (Lahontan), *R. o. nevadensis* (Amargosa), *R. o. velifer* (White River), *R. o. yarrowi* (Virgin River), and *R. o. moapae* (Moapa). Additional subspecies from isolated springs and valleys (e.g., *R. o. oligoporus*, *R. o. relictus*, *R. o. lariversi*) have also been described, though their genetic status was unknown.

The study's stated goal was to measure genetic divergence within and among Nevada populations and to use these data to identify conservation units. Samples comprised 20 localities in Nevada, Utah, Arizona, Oregon, and California, plus outgroup specimens of *R. atratulus* and *R. cataractae* from Michigan. Tissue was preserved in 100% ethanol or frozen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors attribute the species' distribution to stream captures, headwater connections, and stream shifting across divides, followed by dispersal. In the Great Basin, drying since pluvial times isolated populations in small springs and disconnected stream segments. Anthropogenic groundwater pumping, surface water diversion, habitat loss, hybridization, and competition from exotic species were identified as further threats to southwestern desert fishes.

Geologic history is central to the interpretation. Tectonic events, basalt flows, rotation of the Klamath-Sacramento area, Colorado Plateau uplift, Pluvial Lakes Lahontan, Bonneville, and others, and climatic cycles of wet and dry periods are all linked to observed lineage splits and connections.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- Mitochondrial cytochrome b (5′ end, ~329 bp) screened by SSCP; representative haplotypes were sequenced for the 5′ region (with a complete cyt b sequence for select major-lineage individuals).
- Phylogenetic analysis used parsimony (TBR branch swapping, 10 random-addition sequences) and neighbor-joining (Kimura two-parameter distances), with 1000 bootstrap replicates.
- Population structure was assessed with AMOVA-based fixation indices (F-statistics) and permutation tests (1000 permutations), with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons. Migrant estimates (Nm) were derived from pairwise F_ST values.
- Divergence times were calibrated at 1% per pairwise million years (±10–25%), with rate variation handled per lineage (0.5% per Ma).

**Genetic diversity**
- SSCP identified one to six haplotypes per population across 20 populations.
- Haplotype diversity was highest in the Willamette River sample (h = 0.8667 ± 0.1291 overall) and highest among Nevada populations at Maynard Spring (Pluvial White River; h = 0.7000 ± 0.2184).
- Big Smokey Valley and Bradford Spring (Amargosa) had zero haplotype diversity.
- Nucleotide diversity was highest in Laverkin Creek (Virgin River) and in Mesquite, Utah (Virgin River; π = 0.029311 ± 0.015663, the highest among Nevada samples). Hendricks Creek (Snake River) had the lowest.

**Phylogenetic lineages**
- Five well-supported monophyletic lineages were recovered (bootstrap > 70%):
  1. Snake-Lahontan-Big Smokey-Monitor (including Death Valley/Amargosa as a sister clade)
  2. Colorado-Bonneville-Los Angeles (including Pluvial White River, Bonneville Basin, Virgin River, Gila, and San Gabriel)
  3. Willamette River (*R. osculus* and *R. falcatus*) and Sacramento/Salinas
  4. Sacramento (Salinas River)
  5. A single unique haplotype from the Bonneville Basin (Lake Creek)
- The Virgin River carried haplotypes similar to both the Pluvial White River and Bonneville Basin, and Park Creek (Shoal Creek drainage) showed a similar mixture, indicating a recent connection.

**Population structure**
- Snake-Lahontan-Big Smokey-Monitor lineage: 73% of variation was among localities (F_ST = 0.73), largely within drainages (F_SC = 0.64), with limited differentiation among drainages (F_CT ≈ 0.18).
- Pluvial White-Virgin-Bonneville complex: 72% of variation was among localities; within-drainage variation was largely due to the Virgin River and Park Creek localities.
- Restricting analysis to Pluvial White and Bonneville samples: 86% of variation was among drainages, with little variation among localities within drainages.
- Most pairwise comparisons showed Nm < 1, indicating strong differentiation; a few pairs (e.g., Humboldt River–Little Goose Creek, Big Smokey Valley–Humboldt River) had 1 < Nm < 4.

**Divergence times**
- The four major lineages diverged in the mid-Miocene; the *R. osculus* lineage in this study dates to 9.7–11.1 Ma (late Miocene to early Pliocene).
- Death Valley and Snake-Lahontan-Big Smokey-Monitor lineages shared a common ancestor 4.3–5.4 Ma.
- Oasis Valley and Ash Meadows shared a common ancestor 1.6–2.3 Ma.
- The Virgin River–Bonneville connection is estimated at 0.5–1.7 Ma, and Park Creek's Bonneville-related allele at 0.1–1.0 Ma.
- The Colorado Basin's Gila River split from middle-Colorado localities at 5.8–7 Ma, reflecting reintegration with the formation of the Grand Canyon at about 5 Ma.

**Conservation recommendations**
- The authors identify ESUs: the Amargosa/Death Valley lineage, Snake-Lahontan-Big Smokey-Monitor lineage, Pluvial White River, Bonneville Basin, and Virgin River/Shoal Creek lineage (the last treated as a suture zone of Bonneville and Pluvial White forms).
- MUs include Oasis Valley (Amargosa/Death Valley), Ash Meadows (Amargosa/Death Valley), and Hendricks Creek (Snake River), along with differentiated localities within the Snake-Lahontan-Big Smokey-Monitor and Bonneville-Virgin-White groups.
- Management should preserve natural isolation and genetic structure. Artificial mixing of populations is considered inappropriate because the isolated states are natural. Priorities are protecting water supplies, removing exotic competitors and predators, and preventing further habitat deterioration.
- The authors note that Bonneville Basin sampling was limited and that additional sampling there is needed to test the origin of the unique Bonneville haplotype.

**Taxonomic implications**
- Major phylogenetic groups correspond closely, but not exactly, with named Nevada subspecies. *R. o. nevadensis* formed a single monophyletic lineage; *R. o. velifer* and *R. o. robustus* were paraphyletic; and *R. o. yarrowi* was polyphyletic.
- The data suggest *R. falcatus* may be a localized form of *R. osculus*, although hybridization remains a possibility.
