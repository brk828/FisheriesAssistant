## Executive Summary

This document summarizes the minutes of a November 22, 1995 [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) meeting held at Lake Mohave, Cottonwood Landing, focused on conservation and recovery efforts for two federally listed endemic fishes of the Lower Colorado River Basin: the [[Razorback Sucker]] (RBS) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (BTC). Topics covered included broodstock genetics, larval collection programs, telemetry-based habitat use studies, ephemeral pond rearing capacity, and coordination between the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BR) and the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (LCRMSCP). The meeting reflects an active, multi-agency captive propagation and reintroduction program aimed at reversing population declines caused by recruitment failure in the wild.

## Background & Description

- **Razorback Sucker (RBS)**: Wild-collected fingerlings reared at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (WBNFH) reported to average >100 mm total length prior to stocking into ephemeral ponds; fish stocked into Lake Mohave/Lake Havasu after PIT tagging were >250 mm.
- **Bonytail Chub (BTC)**: 1995 year class fish at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] ranged 50–100 mm; a subset of this cohort was targeted for growth to 250–300 mm prior to release into Lake Mohave in fall 1996.
- Tagging methodology has evolved over time: **Spaghetti tags** used 1988–1990, transitioned to **PIT tags** since 1991, enabling individual tracking across recapture events.
- Telemetry study (Gordon Muller, Office of Biological Services, initiated 1994) used sonic tags implanted in 40 RBS (20 adults, 20 juveniles) to study post-release movement and habitat use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Both species occupy [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the Colorado River, with adult RBS showing strong shoreline affinity — typically remaining within 100 meters of shore despite large home ranges.
- **Movement Ecology**:
  - Adult RBS exhibited high mobility, with one individual traveling >51 of the reservoir's 61-mile length; average range across tracked fish was ~30 miles.
  - Fish rarely moved downstream of Cottonwood Basin, and some moved upstream toward Hoover Dam.
  - Juvenile RBS dispersed independently post-release, with some individuals moving >2 miles within 48 hours; no cohesive schooling behavior was observed among stocked juveniles.
  - Seasonal patterns: adults moved toward Owl's Point (May–June), "disappeared" during summer, then staged near spawning sites (>100 m from shore) October–November.
  - During the spawning season, adults visited multiple spawning aggregations; one individual crossed the reservoir three times and was recorded at four separate spawning groups. Five of 11 tracked mobile adults were detected at **Tequila Cove**.
- **Rearing Habitat**: [[Ephemeral Ponds]] and net-pen **enclosures** (12 total downriver of Davis Dam in 1995: located at Imperial NWR, Cibola NWR, Imperial Canyon Golf Course, and Havasu NWR) serve as intermediate grow-out habitats before release into main-stem/reservoir habitat.
- **Carrying Capacity Estimates**: Based on Davis Cove renovation data (1992–1995), estimated carrying capacity for Davis and Yuma Coves is 520–630 kg/ha; planning assumed 500 kg/ha for stocking calculations.

## Key Findings

### Population Status & Trends
- Lake Mohave RBS population estimated at ~60,000 fish in the early 1990s (broad confidence limits), declining to a current estimate of ~25,000 fish (narrow confidence limits), which has remained **relatively stable over the past three years**.
- BTC wild adult captures have totaled only ~90 individuals since 1980; the FWS has not successfully produced a new wild-origin year class since 1991.

### Genetics
- The 1981 BTC spawning event at WBNFH involved 10 broodfish (5M/5F) and produced >100,000 fry, but chemical/genetic analysis revealed **fewer than 5 females actually contributed** to the surviving 1991 year class — indicating a genetic bottleneck in current broodstock.
- RBS ephemeral pond releases show high genetic diversity (7 of 10 sampled fish from different mothers).
- BTC genetic diversity in captivity, while lower than RBS, is described as "relatively high" compared to other North American [[Cyprinidae|cyprinids]], despite a donor pool of ≤10 fish.
- Three RBS genetics publications were nearing completion at Arizona State University (W.L. Minckley et al.); prior BTC genetics work cited includes Minckley, Buth & Mayden (1989) and research by DeMarais.

### Propagation & Stocking Program (1995 activities)
- **RBS larvae collection**: >20,000 wild larvae collected (Jan–April 1995); 91% survival to stocking/transport stage. Distribution: 10,800 to seven ephemeral ponds, 7,500 to WBNFH.
- **Adult BTC netting**: 13 adults captured from Lake Mohave in 1995.
- **Fall removal/tagging (in progress at time of meeting)**: >1,500 RBS and 500+ BTC expected to be PIT-tagged and released into Lake Mohave.
- **Downriver enclosure stocking**: ~500 RBS and 116 BTC (all >250 mm) PIT-tagged and released downriver in 1995 (BTC to Lake Havasu; RBS to Lake Havasu or the Colorado River below Parker, AZ).
- **Dexter NFH&TC**: Holding >100,000 BTC (1995 year class); 20,000 transferred to protected growth areas; remainder (30,000–35,000) to be over-wintered by FWS pending lack of available Lake Mohave rearing facilities, targeting a 25,000-fish stocking commitment at 250–300 mm in fall 1996.
- **1996 target**: Minimum 40,000 wild RBS larvae to be collected, following a trend of collection totals doubling in each of the prior two years.

### Infrastructure & Institutional Coordination
- Davis Cove renovation completed successfully in 1995; berm repair still pending (unscheduled).
- [[Bureau of Reclamation]] confirmed continued/increased funding support for NFWG activities in 1996 and committed $100,000 in FY1996 toward Interim Conservation Measures (ICMs) under the LCRMSCP, citing Section 7 [[Endangered Species Act]] consultation obligations.
- Multi-agency spring survey ("RBS roundup") has continued since the mid-1980s; debate arose over whether once-a-year monitoring adequately captures recruitment/introduction success, prompting a proposal from Gordon Muller to expand BTC monitoring efforts.
