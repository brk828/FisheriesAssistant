# Distributional Ecology of the Cisco (Coregonus artedii) in Indiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coregonidae

## Executive Summary

David G. Frey (Indiana University, published in *Investigations of Indiana Lakes and Streams*, Vol. IV, No. 7, October 1955) examines why the [[Cisco]] (*Coregonus artedii*) persists in some northern Indiana lakes and has disappeared from others. The cisco is documented in 41 lakes of northern Indiana. Shriner Lake (41° 14′ N) is the southernmost known natural occurrence of the family Coregonidae. Cisco populations are concentrated in definite geographic clusters, and the paper links that pattern to regional geologic and geomorphic controls on lake limnology. Temperature-oxygen data from 37 cisco lakes and 53 non-cisco lakes show that cisco lakes generally hold a thicker layer of water that is cooler than 20 °C and contains more than 3 ppm oxygen. Stocking attempts appear to have failed and did not extend the pre-Columbian range.

## Background & Description

The coregonids are closely allied to trouts and are circumboreal in distribution. The cisco is the most widely distributed coregonid in the glacial lakes of the northern United States and Canada, extending from New York to Minnesota and northward to Hudson Bay. It is a pelagic, plankton-feeding species with a terminal mouth. Koelz (1931) recognized 24 subspecific groups, but [[Hile]] (1936, 1937) concluded that these lacked sufficient basis, and noted strong environmental control of phenotypic expression.

Indiana cisco records date from [[Jordan]] (1875), who described the Tippecanoe Lake population as *Argyrosomus sisco*. A chronology of records from 1875 to 1945 (Table 1) distinguishes:

- **Specimen-verified lakes:** 10 lakes, including Tippecanoe, Shriner, Crooked, James, Royer, Lake of the Woods, McLish, Indian Village, Gordy and Hindman.
- **Reported lakes:** 14 additional lakes.
- **Questionable or erroneous reports:** three lakes, including Blue River Lake and Oswego Lake.

Many early records were uncritical. Blatchley's 1938 list, for example, was judged incomplete.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Summer bathymetric distribution.** The cisco is generally a deep-water, cold-water fish in summer. It occupies the coldest water that still has enough oxygen. Its position is limited above by temperature and below by oxygen. As oxygen declines, the population is pushed upward from the hypolimnion into the thermocline. Reported tolerances are up to about 20 °C and oxygen down to about 3 ppm, possibly lower. Literature from Wisconsin, Ontario (Lake Nipissing, [[Fry]] 1937) and Green Bay (Smith, unpublished) is reviewed. Several of these studies show cisco overlapping with predators such as [[Northern Pike]] and [[Bowfin]].

**Oxygen typology.** The paper uses the Aberg and Rodhe (1942) classification: orthograde, clinograde, plus-heterograde (oxygen maximum in or near the thermocline) and minus-heterograde (the "oxygen notch"). Cisco lakes are more often plus-heterograde, and non-cisco lakes are mostly clinograde. A plus-heterograde maximum, often created by photosynthesis, appears to be a key factor enabling survival in small, shallow lakes. The best-documented case is [[Myers Lake]], where a maximum of 23.2 ppm occurred at about 8 m and was observed in each summer from 1951 through 1954 (the abstract cites five successive summers, 1951–1955).

**Regional controls.** Cisco lakes form clusters: the Twin Lakes group (Marshall Co.), Tippecanoe group (Kosciusko Co.), Indian Village group (Noble Co.), Tri Lakes group (Whitley Co.), Oliver Lake group (LaGrange Co.) and James Lake group (Steuben Co.). Cluster persistence is linked to regional geology. Dr. W. J. Wayne suggested these lakes lie in glacial outwash rather than till.

**Age and life stage.** Young coregonids are more tolerant of high temperature and low oxygen than adults. Adults are the first to die in severe years. Loss of young age classes can make a residual population more vulnerable.

**Comparison with other systems.** Lake Muskellunge and Silver Lake (Wisconsin) show cisco forced into narrow strata, while [[Coregonus albula]] in European lakes shows different thermal behaviour. European lakes are generally less sharply stratified.

## Key Findings

1. **Known range.** 41 Indiana lakes are known or reported to contain cisco. Of these, 14 are substantiated by actual specimens (including Lake and Stream Survey catches in 1951–1952). The 1951 questionnaire of 44 replies from 129 licensed fishermen identified 29 actively fished lakes and 12 more where ciscoes were reported but not fished.
2. **Lost populations.** Cisco have probably disappeared from at least Big Lake (Noble Co.), Manitou Lake and Big Long and Turkey Lakes, as well as possibly other lakes.
3. **Stocking failure.** Whitefish and cisco stocking (including 2,000,000 whitefish fry in Wawasee, 1896; 100,000 hybrid fry, 1893–94; cisco eggs in the Tri Lakes area, 1914; and Hamilton Lake, 1947) produced no lasting range extension. The paper concludes that the pre-Columbian distribution was not extended by man.
4. **Oxygen-temperature comparison.** Temperature and oxygen data cover 37 cisco lakes and 53 non-cisco lakes. Cisco lakes have a thicker stratum below 20 °C with oxygen above 3 ppm and a higher mean oxygen content. Many non-cisco lakes lack a suitable layer entirely.
5. **Depth is not decisive.** The paper finds no relationship between maximum depth and cisco presence. Some of the shallowest lakes (Indian Village, 6.5 m; Hindman, 6 m) harbour the species.
6. **Shallow-lake survival.** Shallow cisco lakes often show a photosynthetic oxygen maximum in the thermocline, which the paper considers important for survival. Indian Village Lake was clinograde in two of three years, showing that plus-heterograde is not essential.
7. **Feeding and predation.** Cisco caught in shallow lakes were associated with warm-water species such as bluegill, bullhead, bowfin and warmouth bass. Predation by northern pike was noted in Lake Geneva.
8. **Fishery regulation.** Cisco fishing was legal from 1 November to 31 December with gill nets of up to 150 feet, a licence fee of $2, and a 2½-inch extension measure. Nets could not fish more than 15 feet deep. Anglers were directed to return milt and spawn to the water, though the paper notes eggs and sperm were not required to be mixed.
9. **Fall-surfacing hypothesis.** [[Scott]] (1931) proposed that September surfacing in Snow Lake results from fish trapped below the oxygen notch. Frey finds the data do not support this, and notes that surfacing occurs in lakes with varied oxygen types.
10. **Conclusion.** The cisco is more physiologically plastic than traditionally assumed. Persistence in Indiana depends on regional geologic controls that maintain adequate buffer capacity and oxygen in the thermocline.
