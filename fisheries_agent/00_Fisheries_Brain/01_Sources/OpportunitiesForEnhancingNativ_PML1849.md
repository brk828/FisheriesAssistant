# Enhancing Native Fishes of the Lower Colorado River: An Issue Paper

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 letter from [[Arizona State University]] zoologist to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Arizona Projects Office) presents an "issue paper" on opportunities to enhance native fishes along the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The author states it is not a proposal for action, but a set of considerations to be pursued through agencies. The paper reviews (1) prehistoric conditions, (2) present conditions, (3) a desirable future state, and (4) the probability of success and practical suggestions for a management program.

The author judges the probability of initiating and succeeding with a comprehensive "master" plan as very low, because of the many agencies and interests involved. He strongly supports short-term, piecemeal actions. These would maintain the four endangered native species on [[National Wildlife Refuges]] and would likely be most effective if they focus on excluding [[Non-Native Fishes]] from isolated waters.

## Background & Description

The paper draws heavily on [[Minckley]] (1979, 1985, 1991) and other sources. It describes an unusually depauperate native fauna. Before major modification, the lower river was characterized by:

- Large seasonal and annual variation in discharge, with early-summer floods and low autumn-to-spring flows.
- High sediment transport and a very low gradient (roughly 0.3 m/km, as rendered in the OCR text).
- Floodplain heterogeneity (oxbow lakes, marshlands) that was extensive, possibly dominated by the aquatic habitats of the [[Colorado River Delta]].

The native mainstream fauna is summarized as 4 minnows, 2 suckers, 2 semi-marine species, and 1 cyprinodontid. Minckley noted that [[Woundfin]], [[Colorado Chub]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] were rare, recorded only 1 to 3 times in the reach from [[Davis Dam]] to the Delta. Species with specialized roles included:

- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: abundant, predatory, likely feeding largely on [[Striped Mullet]].
- [[Razorback Sucker]]: abundant, detritivorous.
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: common and widespread, feeding on drifting organic material and terrestrial invertebrates.
- [[Desert Pupfish]]: restricted to shorelines and quiet, marginal waters.
- [[Striped Mullet]] and [[Machete]]: entered the river from the [[Sea of Cortez]] and were restricted to the lowermost reach.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Channel straightening, bank stabilization with rip-rap, and dredging increased flow velocity and lowered water tables, which drained floodplain habitats. Regulated daily, weekly, and seasonal fluctuations replaced natural cycles. The Colorado Squawfish disappeared in the 1960s. Bonytail and razorback sucker persist in small numbers, and the desert pupfish survives only where reliable backwaters and oxbows remain.

All four of the listed species (Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, bonytail, desert pupfish) are federally listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]], making them eligible for recovery action. Other rarer native fishes are considered gone.

The present fauna is dominated by non-native species, most of them intentionally stocked for sport fisheries in reservoirs. Groups mentioned include:

- Threadfin shad ([[Threadfin Shad]]) and [[Common Carp]].
- Catfishes, including channel and flathead catfish.
- Livebearers such as [[Gambusia]] and sailfin molly.
- Centrarchids (sunfishes and largemouth bass).
- [[Striped Bass]].
- African cichlids (*Sarotherodon* spp.).

The author notes that these fishes compete with, prey on, or otherwise negatively interact with natives. Their popularity with anglers creates a dilemma for agencies. Apart from funding limits and variable agency commitment, the presence and desirability of non-native fishes are described as the main obstacles to native fish recovery.

## Key Findings

**1. A desirable future state is technically feasible.** The author states that the technology exists to re-establish the four endangered species. Habitats can be created on public trust lands, and non-native competitors and predators can be removed or managed. Stocks are available. Less-than-token water surface area could be dedicated to native fish management.

**2. Re-created habitats are not self-sustaining.** Natural processes that once generated transitory habitats (flooding, deposition, erosion) are now controlled. Oxbows must be dredged or dug, and marsh and backwater habitats require human maintenance. Re-created systems will need to be maintained, and they will change over time as organic material accumulates.

**3. Native fishes were themselves transitory.** Adults and juveniles moved among habitats in response to flow. Managed habitats must therefore allow fish movement into new habitats as old ones fail, and they must maintain genetic variability in stocks.

**4. Probability of a comprehensive program is very low.** The author considers a "master" plan unlikely, given the number of federal, state, and non-governmental interests. Multi-agency committees are seen as inherently fragmented by self-interest. A high-level commission appointed by the Secretary of the Interior is suggested as one possibility.

**5. Piecemeal actions have long-term value.** Squawfish, bonytail, and razorback sucker are long-lived. Establishing viable populations today could provide a 30- to 50-year buffer, or longer, against extinction. This buys time for future actions and for research on long-term management.

**6. Recommended short-term program.** The author strongly supports developing a series of projects to maintain the four endangered fishes on National Wildlife Refuges. Re-created habitats could be integrated into existing refuge programs for waterfowl or other species. Key requirements include:

- Excluding non-native predators and competitors, which requires the ability to dry sites or otherwise remove undesired species.
- Periodic maintenance to sustain suitable habitat.
- Once high-quality stocks are established, enhancing habitats to stimulate natural reproduction and population sizes proportionate to habitat size.
- Aggressive, proactive management of both habitat and stock quality.
- As the program expands, incorporating the habitats and fishes into the water management plans for the whole river system.

**7. Trade-off with naturalness.** The author acknowledges that managed, human-maintained populations are less than ideal from a "naturalness" standpoint. He considers them far preferable to the loss of biodiversity and regional extirpation now occurring.
