## Executive Summary

This 1994 memorandum, authored by Jeff Baumgartner (The Nature Conservancy) to Pat Nelson (USFWS), critiques the goals and strategies of the **Flooded Bottomlands Restoration Program**, a component of the Colorado River endangered fishes **Recovery Program**. The memo argues that program goals — "restoring floodplain habitat" and "restoring habitats for razorbacks" — are too vague and inconsistently applied across stakeholders. It proposes adopting the **National Research Council (1992)** definition of ecological restoration (recreation of ecosystem structure, function, and natural dynamic processes) rather than a narrower hydrologic-reconnection definition. The document is substantively about habitat requirements of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and, secondarily, [[Bonytail]], within a dynamic floodplain-fluvial system in the Green and Colorado River sub-basins. A second, unrelated attached document ("Calhoun Report," 1974) presents 31 national policy recommendations on public fish hatchery management, stocking responsibility, and disease control — included as a historical comparison exhibit rather than as primary science content.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Floodplains are described as "a mosaic of dynamic habitat types...created, maintained, and connected primarily by fluvial processes related to hydrologic and sediment regimes." The specific habitat types most utilized by [[Razorback Sucker]] and other big-river fishes are those **seasonally or semi-permanently flooded** (per [[Cowardin Wetland Classification|Cowardin et al. 1979]] wetland classification), rather than the entire floodplain extent.
- **Hydrologic connectivity**: Restoration is defined as reconnecting floodplain habitats to the main river channel such that flooding occurs at near-historical **frequency, timing, and duration** — access by big-river fishes to bottomland habitat is closely tied to these flood parameters.
- **Groundwater–surface water interaction**: Cooper's baseline studies of five original bottomland sites found bottomland hydrology to be highly complex, involving multiple water sources and significant groundwater-surface water interaction; river connectivity to a given site may be limited. This complexity affects habitat structure/function independent of simple stage-discharge (flood-ease) metrics.
- **Trophic/productivity considerations**: The scope-of-work explicitly deprioritized "ecosystem productivity," which the author argues is inappropriate, since fish utilization of bottomlands — and recovery outcomes — are "inextricably linked to the productivity function of these habitats." Cooper's baseline invertebrate data showed **seasonal invertebrate productivity varied considerably site-to-site** based on species composition and habitat structure, suggesting plankton sampling alone is insufficient to characterize site biological suitability.
- **Geomorphic dynamics**: Floodplain landforms are naturally created, maintained, and destroyed by hydrologic/geomorphic processes; the memo cautions against engineering approaches (channel stabilization, long-term site "endurance") that conflict with a restoration philosophy based on operating natural fluvial processes.
- **System-level framing**: The author recommends treating bottomland habitats as a **dynamic system** across the [[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]] sub-basins rather than a collection of independent, static sites, echoing [[Floodplain Ecosystem Restoration]] principles.

## Key Findings

1. **Ambiguous goal definitions**: Terms like "restore," "floodplain habitats," and "habitats for razorbacks" were used inconsistently among program participants (ranging from intensively managed grow-out ponds to unmanaged natural habitats) and require explicit, agreed-upon definitions.
2. **Recommended restoration definition**: Adopt NRC (1992) definition — restoration = return of ecosystem to near pre-disturbance condition, with structure/function recreated and natural dynamic processes operative — rather than the narrower "hydrologic reconnection" definition in the original scope-of-work.
3. **Proposed program goal**: "Develop a dynamic system of seasonally and semi-permanently flooded bottomland habitats that is maintained by natural fluvial processes in both the Green and Colorado River sub-basins," with near-historical structure, function, and process.
4. **Unsupported site-selection assumptions**: The Burdick and Irving site scoring/ranking approach relied on untested assumptions — that sites flooded in June 1993 are more valuable/easier to restore, that proximity to spawning sites increases habitat value, and that networked (spatially clustered) sites outperform isolated sites. The memo recommends these be treated as testable hypotheses within a formal experimental design.
5. **Hydrologic screening limitations**: Stage-discharge relationships and "ease of flooding" metrics are inadequate alone; groundwater-surface water interaction studies (per Cooper) should be incorporated into site screening.
6. **Geomorphological screening critique**: Emphasis on channel stability and long-term site endurance contradicts a natural-process-based restoration philosophy; screening should instead identify sites compatible with dynamic fluvial processes and characterize expected timescales of site stability.
7. **Biological screening critique**: Plankton sampling alone is insufficient for assessing food-resource suitability; seasonal invertebrate productivity analysis (as per Cooper's baseline studies) is recommended.
8. **Experimental design proposal**: A three-way factorial design (Level of Manipulation × Distance from Spawning Area × Proximity to Neighbor Site) is proposed to rigorously test restoration and site-selection hypotheses, comparing highly engineered/high-O&M sites against naturally-maintained, low-O&M sites.
9. **Unresolved success criteria**: The memo highlights unresolved questions about how to define/measure when "conditions necessary to sustain razorback suckers (and possibly bonytail) are restored" have been met, and calls for an overarching, ecosystem-level strategy integrating bottomland restoration with the broader [[Colorado River Endangered Fishes Recovery Program]].
10. **Secondary/attached document**: The appended 1974 "Calhoun Report" (National Task Force for Public Fish Hatchery Policy) provides 31 policy recommendations on state vs. federal responsibility for fish stocking, hatchery management, disease control, and salmonid culture — offered in a 1994 cover memo as a historical parallel to contemporaneous National Fish Hatchery System (NFHS) review recommendations, but contains no direct data on the razorback sucker/bonytail restoration program.
