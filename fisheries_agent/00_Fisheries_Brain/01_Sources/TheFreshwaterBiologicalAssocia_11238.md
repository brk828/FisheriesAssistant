# The Work of the Freshwater Biological Association (1958 British Association Symposium)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1959 reprint from *The Advancement of Science* collects four papers presented at a joint session of Sections D (Zoology) and K (Botany) of the [[British Association For The Advancement Of Science]] at Glasgow on 2 September 1958. The papers review the work of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] (FBA), founded in 1929 and based at a laboratory on [[Windermere]] in the English Lake District. The contributions cover the Association's history, its physical and chemical limnology, the algal ecology of Windermere, and a long-term experiment on fish populations (perch and pike) subjected to intensive fishing. The central theme is that freshwater ecosystems are controlled by interacting physical, chemical, biological and climatic factors, and that long-term, field-based experiments are needed to understand them.

## Background & Description

**Origins of the Association.** [[William Pearsall]] traces the FBA to a 1928 joint discussion of the British Association, chaired by [[Fritsch]], which recommended a freshwater laboratory modelled on the [[Marine Biological Association]]. The Association was formally constituted on 11 June 1929 at the Linnean Society. Britain was described as having more than 500 square miles of freshwater surface but lagging behind continental Europe in freshwater laboratory provision.

**Site selection.** Candidate sites were the Norfolk Broads, an unpolluted southern chalk stream, and pools at Sutton Coldfield. Moving water was judged too difficult for preliminary work, and southern waters already resembled continental studies. [[Windermere]] was chosen for its access to lakes and tarns of many sizes, including the Caledonian type, and a wide range of nearby habitats.

**Policy.** The Association committed to fundamental research rather than ad hoc fishery or water-supply enquiries, though it came to value applied-sector supporters.

**Laboratory history.** The laboratory opened at [[Wray Castle]] in September 1931, funded by membership subscriptions, the [[Royal Society]], the Fishmongers' Company and a Development Commission grant of £450 (March 1931). The Development Commission grant rose to £2,200 in 1937, allowing a full-time Director, Dr. [[E. B. Worthington]]. [[H. C. Gilson]] succeeded him in 1946. The [[Ferry House Laboratory]] was acquired in 1947 and formally opened in 1950. The [[International Limnological Congress]] met in Britain in 1952, and the laboratory received the [[Einar Naumann Medal]].

**Staff and intellectual lineage.** Early naturalists [[P. Ullyott]] and [[R. S. A. Beauchamp]] worked on flatworms in a field-to-laboratory ecological approach. Later figures include [[J. W. G. Lund]] (algae), [[F. J. H. Mackereth]] (chemistry), [[C. H. Mortimer]] (physics and chemistry of lakes), [[T. T. Macan]] (invertebrate populations) and [[E. D. Le Cren]] (fish). Pearsall credits the Association's success to enthusiasm, the Easter classes that trained recruits, and the staff's ability to identify significant problems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nutrient control of algal growth.** Mortimer's review of 1935-1957 work identifies spring sunshine as driving diatom growth, with a concurrent fall in dissolved silicate, nitrate and possibly phosphate. Phosphate was extremely dilute (about 1 mg/m³ in one estimate) and near the detection limit (about 0.001 mg P/l) in Windermere. Research grouped into three lines:

- **Regular observations** in selected lakes, including Lund's work on the spring maximum of [[Asterionella Formosa]].
- **Laboratory studies** of algal nutrient requirements and luxury phosphorus uptake.
- **Mechanisms of nutrient transport**, including thermal stratification, oxygen depletion and sediment chemistry.

**Stratification and motion.** Windermere, like other temperate lakes, stratifies in spring into a warm, aerated upper layer (the site of production) and a cold lower layer (the site of decomposition and oxygen depletion). Phosphate release from sediments depends on oxygen: when oxygen is lost, ferric iron is reduced and adsorbed phosphate is liberated. Wind drives internal seiches. Thermistor records showed that each basin oscillates at its own resonant period, for example about 0.6 days for the north basin of Windermere (7 km long, 65 m deep).

**Catchment influence.** Mortimer and Mackereth emphasised that catchment processes, including soils, rain chemistry and inflows, strongly shape lake chemistry. Nitrate decline, for example, appeared to reflect catchment soil processes rather than in-lake events.

**Algal diversity.** Lund notes that algae span plants, animals and bacteria-like forms, including mixotrophic golden-brown algae (Chrysophyceae). Size ranges from the single-celled *Chlorella* to the giant kelp *Macrocystis*. Lund describes lakes as "inland seas" whose base of the food web is planktonic algae, while many streams are dominated by benthic organisms.

**Fish community.** Windermere holds ten fish species, of which seven are ecologically important: trout, char, pike, perch, eel, minnow and stickleback. Diets link them to algal-based food chains: char and young perch eat zooplankton; trout, medium perch, eels, minnows and sticklebacks eat bottom fauna; pike, larger perch and larger trout are predators on fish.

## Key Findings

**1. Physical and chemical controls on algal growth.** Mortimer's work on *Asterionella formosa* showed:
- Population increase in early spring tracked sunshine. Shallower basins (maximum depths 15, 33 and 65 m) reached a given population level earlier than deeper ones.
- Peak numbers of 5,000 to 10,000 cells/ml coincided with dissolved silicate falling to about 0.5 mg/l SiO₂, after which a sudden mortality occurred.
- In 1949 in Esthwaite Water, chytrid parasitism kept numbers low until other diatoms (*Fragilaria* and *Tabellaria*) depleted silicate.
- In 1948 in the north basin of Windermere, a dry-weather halt in growth was attributed to a lack of an unknown, possibly organic, trace nutrient supplied by inflows.

**2. Nutrient budget.** Mackereth determined that one million *Asterionella* cells (0.3 mg dry weight) contain 0.14 mg SiO₂, 0.06 mg C, 0.007 mg N and 0.00006 mg P (minimum). Windermere phosphate could support at least twice the spring maximum population, so phosphate did not appear to limit growth there. Mackereth also demonstrated luxury phosphorus uptake: in culture, cells absorbed added phosphate beyond their minimum requirement, and growth resumed.

**3. Stratification and transport.** Thermal stratification confines nutrient uptake to the upper layer, while oxygen depletion in the lower layer and sediment surface releases phosphate. Wind-driven seiches and thermocline exchange transport materials and nutrients throughout the basin.

**4. Algal ecology and methods.** Lund reports that:
- Large planktonic algae appear largely immune from grazing, although the extent of animal grazing is poorly known.
- Nannoplankton, poorly studied, may be an important food source for planktonic animals.
- Chu's culture medium (with soil extracts or metal-binding organic compounds) became a widely used general medium for freshwater algae.
- Diatom cell walls preserve in lake sediments. Jenkin's coring technique reaches back to the end of the last ice age, when Lake District waters were richer in calcium. A recent shift in diatom assemblages, attributed to tourism and changes in sanitation, is also recorded.

**5. Fish population experiment.** Le Cren describes the intensive fishery begun in 1940 by Worthington. Its aims were wartime food production, improved trout and char angling, and a large-scale ecological experiment. About 80 tons of perch and 10 tons of pike and eels were sold.
- Perch numbers fell to about 3% of their original density by 1948 and have remained near that level; biomass stabilised at about 8% of the 1940 level.
- Pike catches declined to about 10 to 12% of pre-1944 levels, rising to about 25% after a temporary increase in young pike.
- Perch growth increased markedly. Perch hatched in 1952 and later were growing up to about eight times as fast as adults did before 1940.
- Average perch survival has been about 60% of its 1941-1945 level. Perch year-class survival varied up to a hundredfold between years, and Le Cren attributes extreme fluctuations at least partly to climate.
- Pike egg numbers fell to about 25% of pre-1944 levels, but early survival appears to be about four times greater.
- Predation by young pike and larger perch is likely a major natural mortality source for young perch.

**6. Position of the research.** Le Cren argues that long-term, medium-scale experiments such as this occupy an essential middle ground between small controlled-tank studies and commercial fishery statistics. Lund calls for further experiments in natural waters and for enlarged chemical research. Mortimer also highlighted the importance of catchment processes for lake chemistry.
