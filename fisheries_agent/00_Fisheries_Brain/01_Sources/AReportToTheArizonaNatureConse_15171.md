# Status of Native Fishes in Bass and Hotsprings Creeks, Muleshoe Preserve, Arizona

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #desert-streams #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This report documents the status, distribution, and habitat use of native fish species in [[Bass Creek]] and [[Hotsprings Creek]] within the [[Muleshoe Preserve]], Arizona, based on surveys conducted in January, March, and June 1987. The study area comprises 18 km of perennial stream in the [[Gila River]] drainage, though the two creeks are often separated by intermittent flow from late May through early July. Five native species were documented: [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*). Two introduced species were also detected: [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*). The study employed DC electrofishing in blocked pools and riffles, with population estimates calculated using Seber-Lecren and Moran Zippin depletion estimators. Habitat parameters including depth, cover, substrate composition, and flow were measured at each sampling site.

## Background & Description

[[Bass Creek]] and [[Hotsprings Creek]] join to form 18 km of perennial stream habitat, though separated by 200 m to 1 km of intermittent flow during late spring and early summer. Lower [[Bass Creek]] contains approximately 6.1 km of surface water. Fish access to the upper watershed is blocked by a 1-km stretch of deep alluvium in Sec. 17, 20, T12S, R21E, which only flows during flash floods. The two creeks differ substantially in physical characteristics: [[Bass Creek]] has a well-developed riparian zone with large trees, cobble and small boulder substrate, and pools formed by deflection around riparian vegetation. [[Hotsprings Creek]] lacks extensive riparian development over most of its length, is bordered by bedrock walls, has sand over bedrock substrate, and forms pools primarily through bedrock configurations. [[Hotsprings Creek]] is consequently less shaded, warmer, and provides less cover than [[Bass Creek]].

Fish and habitat parameters were sampled three times in 1987: January (1/5-1/10), March (3/15-19), and June (5/27-6/3). Eight pools and eight riffles were randomly selected in [[Bass Creek]] for intensive sampling. Longitudinal distribution surveys were conducted in May and August. Fish were captured using DC electrofishing equipment in habitats blocked with 4.7-mm mesh seines. Population estimates were calculated from two-pass depletion sampling. Up to 45 fish per habitat unit were measured to nearest millimeter fork length. Habitat measurements included area, volume, maximum and average depth (measured at 1.0-m transects), substrate composition and embeddedness (visual estimation following Platts et al. 1983), cover (undercut banks, overhanging vegetation, large debris), stream flow (pygmy current meter), and water temperature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area represents critical habitat for native [[Sonoran Desert]] fish species within the [[Gila River]] basin. The perennial nature of these streams provides refugia in an otherwise arid landscape, though seasonal intermittency creates connectivity challenges. The alluvium block in upper [[Bass Creek]] effectively isolates lower stream populations from upstream areas except during rare flash flood events. Stream temperatures averaged 10.5°C in January, 11.6°C in March, and 17.8°C in June. Mean stream flow varied substantially between seasons: 0.127 m³/s in January, 1.17 m³/s in March, and 0.153 m³/s in June, reflecting spring snowmelt and monsoon patterns.

The riparian zone condition strongly influences fish habitat quality. [[Bass Creek]]'s well-developed riparian vegetation stabilizes stream banks, promotes channel down-cutting rather than lateral meandering, and creates deeper pools with undercut banks and root cover. These features are critical for species like [[Gila Chub]] and [[Sonoran Sucker]]. In contrast, [[Hotsprings Creek]]'s limited riparian development and bedrock-dominated channel provide fewer deep pools with cover, restricting habitat for these species. Substrate composition varies from predominantly sediment (up to 80% in some pools) to cobble-dominated riffles, affecting habitat suitability for different species and life stages.

## Key Findings

**Species Distribution and Abundance:**

[[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Desert Sucker]] were found throughout both [[Bass Creek]] and [[Hotsprings Creek]], including seasonally available tributaries. [[Sonoran Sucker]] occurred from midway up [[Hotsprings Creek]] (where first deep pools were encountered) to the alluvium block on [[Bass Creek]]. [[Gila Chub]] had the most restricted distribution, found only from the pipeline crossing on [[Bass Creek]] upstream to Pine Creek (approximately 6 km), with no individuals in [[Hotsprings Creek]]. No fish were found above the alluvium block on [[Bass Creek]].

**Longfin Dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*):**

Second most common fish in pools (38% of pool fish, 7% of riffle fish). Population estimates indicated 86% of all longfin dace inhabit pools, true for all size classes. In pools, no strong habitat associations were detected. In riffles, more common in areas with maximum depths >10 cm and abundant cover (>0.6 m²). Found in shallower average depths (<2 cm) than any other species. Showed seasonal movement from riffles into pools during winter. Average sizes: 43 mm (January), 48 mm (March), 53 mm (June).

**Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*):**

Most abundant species overall, comprising 43% of pool fish and 88% of riffle fish. Population estimates indicated 66% occur in riffles. Size distribution similar between pools and riffles, though largest individuals slightly more common in pools. In pools, more abundant in areas with greatest maximum depth. In riffles, associated with substrates having less sediment (higher gravel/cobble percentage). No seasonal movements observed. Average sizes: 61 mm pools/57 mm riffles (January), 55 mm pools/51 mm riffles (March), 55 mm pools/56 mm riffles (June).

**Gila Chub (*Gila intermedia*):**

Comprised only 1-2% of fish in pools and riffles, much less than the 8% reported by Johnson (1983). Population estimates averaged 2.5 fish/100 m², compared to 20.3/100 m² in nearby [[Redfield Creek]]. Total population estimates for 6 km of [[Bass Creek]]: 594 fish (March), 451 fish (June), based on capture of 29 fish from 2.7% of stream length. Habitat use varied with age: most young-of-year in riffles, all fish ≥1 year old in pools. Only one fish >2 years old collected, suggesting potential habitat limitation for large adults. Pools with chub always had maximum depth >20 cm and cover >0.5 m² (preferably undercut banks or tree roots). Riffles with chub had maximum depths >10 cm, cover >0.6 m², and higher percentages of cobble. All individuals disappeared from sampling areas in January, indicating seasonal movements to unknown locations. The species is considered for federal listing as threatened.

**Sonoran Sucker (*Catostomus insignis*):**

Found only in pools, comprising 2.3% of pool fish. Required pools >30 cm maximum depth with average depths >10 cm. These pools typically had >48% sediment substrate and were associated with stable riparian vegetation forcing channel down-cutting. Habitat similar to [[Gila Chub]] but without the same cover requirements, explaining their presence in deeper [[Hotsprings Creek]] pools where chub are absent. Individuals >125 mm showed winter movements out of sampled areas to unknown locations. Males and females in spawning condition collected in March; males develop tubercles on anal fins during spawning. Juvenile suckers collected from both pools and riffles in June.

**Desert Sucker (*Catostomus clarki*):**

Comprised 15% of pool fish and 5% of riffle fish. Population estimates indicated 53% occur in riffles. Size distribution similar between pools and riffles. More abundant in pools with greater cover. In riffles, associated with higher percentages of cobble substrate, though will use areas with nearly 100% sediment. Individuals >115 mm moved out of sampling areas during winter. Smaller fish moved from riffles into pools during winter. Spawning condition fish collected in March. Juveniles collected from both pools and riffles in June.

**Introduced Species:**

Two [[Largemouth Bass]] observed in [[Bass Creek]], likely originating from small impoundments high in watershed. No evidence of established reproducing populations. [[Fathead Minnow]] collected in [[Hotsprings Creek]]: one individual in June, three in August. Source unknown, possibly bait bucket introduction. Fatheads prefer quiet muddy waters and generally do not persist well with other cyprinids present.

**Management Recommendations:**

Populations of [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Desert Sucker]] are widely distributed, abundant, and represented by multiple size classes, requiring only basic habitat protection. [[Gila Chub]] and [[Sonoran Sucker]] have restricted distributions and should be monitored. All native species would benefit from riparian zone improvement and stream bank stabilization. The cattle grazing prohibition will help protect and stabilize the watershed. Reducing sediment flow to [[Hotsprings Creek]] would improve pool habitats by reducing siltation. Sources of exotic species introductions should be located and prevented. Before habitat modification is considered, further research on [[Gila Chub]] and [[Sonoran Sucker]] habitat requirements is needed.

**Electrofishing Guidelines:**

To minimize fish mortality during research: use lowest equipment settings that effectively stun fish (particularly important with mixed size classes, as large fish more susceptible to shock); pulse unit on/off when fishing areas where stunned fish cannot be seen; aerate water in holding buckets with battery-operated aerators.

**Recommended Further Research:**

1) Taxonomic study of [[Gila Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] using electrophoretic analysis to determine if separate species (potential federal listing implications); 2) Documentation of [[Gila Chub]] habitat requirements at multiple sites, especially where more abundant; 3) Determination of seasonal movements for [[Gila Chub]] and larger [[Sonoran Sucker]] and [[Desert Sucker]] individuals using radio tracking and mark-recapture, particularly investigating potential winter movements into [[Hotsprings Creek]] and summer returns to [[Bass Creek]].
