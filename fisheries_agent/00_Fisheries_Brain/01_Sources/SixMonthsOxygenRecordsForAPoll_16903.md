# Six Months' Oxygen Records for a Polluted Stream

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #rainbow-trout

## Executive Summary

This study examines six months of automated [[Dissolved Oxygen]] records (1 January to 26 June 1958) from the polluted [[River Hiz]], near Hitchin, Hertfordshire. The records were made with an automatic recorder developed at the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]], Stevenage. The authors did not set out to explain the causes of oxygen variation. They asked how much the oxygen content varies and whether regular single samples can reliably indicate its condition. They also asked whether oxygen and temperature alone could support [[Rainbow Trout]], a test species studied at the laboratory.

The stream's oxygen varied considerably within each day. Samples taken at a single fixed time gave a poor picture of the stream. A 6 a.m. sample came closest to the daily minimum, which matters most for fish. Oxygen never fell below 1.6 p.p.m., but it dropped below 3 p.p.m. on many occasions. Using published survival data, the authors concluded that oxygen and temperature alone were unlikely to kill fish in the stream.

## Background & Description

The study builds on an automatic dissolved-oxygen recorder described in earlier papers by the same group. The paper is not a full river survey. It does not relate oxygen changes to flow, pollution load, photosynthesis, or temperature in a systematic way. Its aim is to describe variation and to test the practicality of judging oxygen from regular samples taken at a set time of day.

The [[Oxygen Recorder]] was sited on the River Hiz, which is about 10–15 ft wide and 1 ft deep upstream of the site. The flow during the period was probably generally above 5 Mgal/d (the OCR text reads "5 mug.d.", so this figure is uncertain). It was much greater during some periods. Just under half a mile above the sampling point, the Hiz is joined by the River Purwell, a stream of about the same size. About 100 yards below the confluence, a continuous but variable discharge of [[Gas Liquor]] enters the river. Several smaller sewage and effluent discharges lie further upstream. The authors found no other important pollution source.

Recording ran from 1 January to 26 June 1958. The instrument was still in field trials. Its camera, which photographed the dials every half hour, often failed, so records are incomplete on about one-third of the days. This gap made interpretation harder.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diurnal variation.** Within single days, oxygen varied considerably. Two example days (5 February and 4 May 1958) showed a minimum in late morning, followed by a rapid rise to a maximum. Over the six months, the daily diurnal range (maximum minus minimum) varied between 3 and 63 per cent of saturation, with a mean of 27 per cent. In p.p.m., the mean diurnal range was 3.0 p.p.m.

**Average diurnal curve.** Averaging over six months produced a smoother mean daily cycle. Its range was only about 1 p.p.m. The lowest values occurred between midnight and 6 a.m., and high values formed a plateau from noon to 6 p.m. The pattern resembled what [[Photosynthesis]] alone would produce. The authors caution against reading too much into this curve. Variation in the timing of daily maxima and minima flattens the average. Weirs and turbulent reaches near the confluence and upstream tend to push oxygen toward [[Oxygen Saturation]] and reduce the swings.

**Timing of maxima and minima.** Maxima could occur at any time, but about two-thirds fell between 11:30 a.m. and 6 p.m. Many maxima and minima fell at midnight because calendar days were used and oxygen was trending over time. In January–March, maxima were spread across the 24 hours. In April–June, all maxima occurred between 8:30 a.m. and 10 p.m. About 85 per cent of minima fell within the 12 hours ending at 7:45 a.m. The minima in the first three months tended to occur a few hours earlier than in the second three.

**Representative sampling.** The 11 a.m. Tuesday samples were not representative of the stream. Whether a sample is representative depends on the purpose. For general chemical condition, the daily average matters most. For fish populations, the [[Daily Minimum Oxygen]] is more useful. The 6 a.m. samples tracked the daily average and minimum better than the afternoon samples. Up to mid-March, both 6 a.m. and 4 p.m. samples were fairly representative. By mid-May neither was. A 4 p.m. sample was judged to be of little value for assessing fish-support capacity.

**Week-scale distribution.** For two sample weeks, the half-hourly oxygen values were roughly normally distributed. An arithmetic-probability plot confirmed this.

**Fish-survival assessment.** The authors treated oxygen and temperature as necessary but not sufficient evidence. Constant saturation would not prove that a river supports fish, because toxic discharges could still kill them without affecting oxygen. Conversely, prolonged zero oxygen would show that fish cannot survive. Using survival-time data from [[Downing and Merkens]], the authors found that at Hiz temperatures, a fall to 1 p.p.m. for two consecutive half-hours would cause large mortality. If oxygen stayed above 3 p.p.m., oxygen and temperature alone were unlikely to kill any fish. The [[Median Survival Time]] at each oxygen level was the key comparison.

## Key Findings

- **Coverage:** Oxygen records cover 1 January–26 June 1958. About one-third of days are incomplete because of recorder failure.
- **Extremes:** The highest recorded concentration was 12.4 p.p.m. The lowest was 1.6 p.p.m. The mean diurnal range was 3.0 p.p.m.
- **Diurnal variation:** The daily range was 3–63 per cent of saturation, with a mean of 27 per cent. The averaged daily curve had a range of about 1 p.p.m.
- **Minimum timing:** About 85 per cent of daily minima fell within the 12 hours ending at 7:45 a.m.
- **Maximum timing:** Two-thirds of maxima fell between 11:30 a.m. and 6 p.m. In April–June, all maxima were between 8:30 a.m. and 10 p.m.
- **Sampling bias:** Samples at 11 a.m. on Tuesdays were not representative. The 4 p.m. sample was of little use for fish assessment.
- **Best single sample:** The 6 a.m. sample tracked the daily minimum most closely. It was about 1 p.p.m. above the daily minimum (the Conclusions section gives 1.1 p.p.m., and the Results section gives "about 1 p.p.m. below", which is consistent with this reading). Samples at 6 a.m. and 4 p.m. together would have differed by only 1.3 p.p.m. on average, compared with the 3.0 p.p.m. average range.
- **Fish survival (5 June):** At 15 °C, the median survival time for rainbow trout was about 50 h at 2 p.p.m. and about 20 h at 1.8 p.p.m. The observed oxygen was not expected to cause 50 per cent mortality.
- **Fish survival (18–19 June):** At 13 °C, the median survival time was about 40 h at 1.8 p.p.m. and about 10 h at 1.6 p.p.m. Substantial mortality was not expected unless oxygen stayed below 1.6 p.p.m. for most of the 6 a.m.–11 a.m. period.
- **Occasions below 3 p.p.m.:** Oxygen fell below 3 p.p.m. on many occasions. In nearly every case, temperature was too low, oxygen too high, or the low period too short to harm fish. Three periods were examined in detail (Fig. 10).
- **Toxicity:** In two of the three worst periods, all test fish died, but the stream's poisonous substances, not low oxygen, were judged sufficient to explain the deaths. No fish were on test on 5–6 June. The authors state there is no natural fish population in this stream.
- **Generalizability:** The authors judged that these values are probably typical of many polluted streams in England, though wider ranges have been observed. They caution against generalizing from one stream. Sewage or effluent discharges may create diurnal cycles with timing very different from photosynthesis-driven cycles. Such streams need at least 24-hour studies on several occasions before choosing a sampling time.
