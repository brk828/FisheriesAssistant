# Recovery of Marked Fish Following a Second Poisoning of the Population in Ford Lake, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Robert C. Ball of the Department of Zoology, Michigan State College, reports the second rotenone poisoning of [[Ford Lake]], a 10.7-acre lake in Otsego County, Michigan, on August 26, 1946. The study had two goals: to compare the standing fish crop with that of a complete kill in 1936, and to test how completely fish can be recovered after [[Rotenone]] treatment. Fish marked 3 to 4 days before poisoning were recovered at rates of 58.9 percent for [[Bluegill]] and 44.7 percent for [[Brook Trout]]. The author concludes that the common assumption of near-complete recovery, and the production estimates built on it, is doubtful.

## Background & Description

Ford Lake had become unusable for anglers because of a stunted [[Yellow Perch]] population. It was poisoned in 1936 with rotenone and dynamite, and the population was analyzed at that time. Later management attempts failed:

- **1937:** Experimental planting of [[Montana Grayling]] failed, presumably because of unauthorized bluegill introductions made at the same time.
- **1941:** 5,000 fingerling [[Brook Trout]] were stocked. They could not compete with the increasing bluegill population and were last reported in a gill net set in September 1944. None were taken in 1945.
- **1943:** [[Walleye]] were introduced to reduce the stunted bluegills. Of 168 young-of-the-year planted, 17 survived to 1946.

The author notes that 35 Michigan lakes had been treated with rotenone in the preceding decade, and that several were later repopulated with poorly suited species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Rotenone was applied as powdered derris root with 5 percent rotenone content, at a concentration of 0.6 p.p.m. A centrifugal pump mixed and distributed the poison from a boat. Of the 300 pounds of derris used, 80 pounds were discharged at about 15 feet of depth through a weighted garden hose, and the rest was applied in shallower water.

Water conditions at the time of poisoning:

- Lower waters were devoid of oxygen.
- Carbon dioxide was 12 p.p.m.
- pH was 6.6.
- The bottom was covered with pulpy, fibrous peat.
- Weed beds were visible to 18 feet, the lower limit of vegetation.
- A dense mat of [[Chara]] covered the bottom.

Bluegills are largely invertebrate feeders and sit closer to the primary food chain, while yellow perch are piscivorous. The author suggests this difference may explain why the 1946 catch weighed more than the 1936 catch.

## Key Findings

**Population composition and biomass, 1946**

- The total weight recovered was 1,293 pounds, which the text describes as 2.4 times the 1936 weight. The Abstract's "more than twice" and "111.5 pounds per acre" differ from the Results' figures and from a direct calculation (1,293 lb / 10.7 acres ≈ 120.8 lb/acre); the source gives both without reconciliation.
- [[Bluegill]] were dominant: 37,383 individuals weighing 1,222.2 pounds. Only 18 were legal size (6 inches or longer), about one legal fish per 2,000 bluegills. Mean length was 3.9 inches and mean weight about one-half ounce, indicating a severely stunted population.
- 17 [[Walleye]] weighed 34.2 pounds (17 to 19¾ inches; 1¼ to 2¾ pounds).
- About 4,293 [[Central Mudminnow]] weighed 24.5 pounds. The author estimates no more than 50 percent of dead mudminnows were recovered.
- Only one [[Northern Mimic Shiner]] was taken.

**Comparison with 1936**

- In 1936, the population was 4,817 yellow perch, 27 brook trout, and four minnow species, weighing 516 pounds (about 50 pounds per acre). Perch were absent in 1946.
- The only species common to both years was the mudminnow.

**Recovery of marked fish (the study's central result)**

| Group | Marked | Recovered | Percent recovered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bluegill | 246 | 145 | 58.9 |
| Brook trout | 210 | 94 | 44.7 |

- Bluegills were fin-clipped 4 days before poisoning, and brook trout were dorsal-fin clipped 3 days before. The brook trout had come from a hatchery.
- The author writes that 54 percent of marked fish were recovered overall. The counts in the table give 239 of 456 (about 52.4 percent). This difference is not explained in the text.
- The author states that the recovery rates were "lower than anticipated" and question population estimates from post-poisoning pickup.

**Comparison with other Michigan studies**

- [[Third Sister Lake]] (Ball, 1944): 23.4 percent of 1,395 marked fish recovered, after 9 to 22 months in the lake, so natural mortality is a confounding factor.
- [[Deep Lake]] (Carbine, unpublished): 13.7 percent of 699 marked fish (10 species) recovered, after up to 2 years in the lake.
- [[Twin Lake]] (Krumholz, 1940): 86 percent of marked fish recovered, in a bass-bluegill lake. This was a check on a [[Population Estimation]] made with nets, using [[Mark-Recapture Estimation]] methods.

**Fate of unrecovered fish**

Possible explanations offered by the author:

1. Affected fish buried themselves in the [[Chara]] mat.
2. Fish sank into deep, anoxic water and disintegrated before surfacing. The author considers this the most likely explanation.

**Conclusion**

If the recovery rate in Ford Lake applies more widely, estimates of inland lake fish production based on [[Rotenone]] poisoning would need to be "revised sharply upwards." The author cautions that other lakes may differ, and that only further checks on future poisonings can resolve the question.
