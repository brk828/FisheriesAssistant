# Life-History of the Bullhead (*Cottus gobio* L.)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1950s life-history study by W. J. P. Smyly, based at the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] in Ambleside, examines the [[Bullhead]] (*Cottus gobio*) in the Lake District, near the northern limit of its British range. Fish were sampled between January 1951 and December 1955: 1,555 from [[Windermere]], 771 from the [[River Brathay]] and 542 from other waters, 2,868 in total. The study covers behaviour, sex identification, reproduction, age and growth, feeding, and parasites. Its central conclusions are that fish in the lake grow faster and mature earlier than river fish, that males guard the nest and eggs alone, and that the species feeds mainly on benthic invertebrates.

## Background & Description

*Cottus gobio* is the only freshwater member of the Cottidae in Britain. The popular name "Bullhead" is shared with unrelated fishes, including the marine [[Pogge]] and the American [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus nebulosus*). In America, the Cottidae and related families are called sculpins.

The species ranges across Europe from Greenland and Scandinavia to Italy. It is replaced by a related species in Siberia. In the Alps it occurs from sea level to 2,000 m. In Britain it is common in England and Wales, rare in Scotland and absent from Ireland.

**Identification of sex:**
- Males have a triangular genital papilla, present at all times of year, and usually a larger, broader head.
- In the breeding season, males show a dark head and a whitish-yellow edge on the anterior dorsal fin. Females show a distended abdomen.

**Behaviour:**
- Fish are negatively phototactic and seek dark surfaces, which is why they live under stones.
- Non-breeding fish are solitary, inactive by day and non-migratory. Fish of either sex will not share a stone with a [[Stone Loach]].
- A marking experiment in the River Brathay found strong site fidelity. 87% of 30 recaptured fish six days later were marked, and 65% of 29 after eighteen days.
- Fish return to a chosen stone even after it is swapped for a similar one nearby, indicating a strong homing instinct.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and habitat:** The fish is locally distributed around Windermere. It occurs in Windermere, Rydal Water and Grasmere but not in Esthwaite Water. In running water it prefers weak to moderate flow and is scarce where the flow is swift. It is mostly found on firm sand or gravel, rarely on rock or mud. It was collected only at wading depth, and the author estimates it is rare below 6 to 9 m.

**Sampling stations:** Windermere stations were Scout Camp (1,405 fish, combined with High Wray Bay and Red Nab), Troutbeck Mouth (150) and other shore sites. The River Brathay station was Purdom's Dub (771). Other waters yielded smaller catches, such as Rydal Water (97), Malham Tarn (43), River Alwen (66) and River Bela (46).

**Competition and co-occurrence:** In the River Brathay, when [[Stone Loach]] are abundant, Bullheads are usually scarce, and vice versa.

**Seasonal and altitudinal breeding:** Breeding is in April in the Lake District. In the Alps it varies with altitude, from February near sea level in the Tessin to late May or early June in high alpine streams and lakes.

**Parasites and predators:** The only identified parasite is the urinary bladder trematode [[Phyllodistomum Folium]]. It was more frequent in River Brathay fish (about 41–64% infected per month) than in Windermere fish (generally under 31%). Serious predators are not recorded; only occasional fish and bird predation has been noted.

## Key Findings

**1. Reproduction and nest guarding**
- Breeding occurs in April. No spent females were caught before 31 March, and no ripe females after 10 May. Egg clumps were found from 31 March to 15 May.
- Males dig a nest under a stone, a necessary step for breeding, which was never observed on hard bottoms. Only males fan the eggs with their pectoral fins. Fertilisation is external.
- At 10 °C, eggs hatch in about four weeks. Fanning rates reported by [[Morris 1954]] were 140 beats per minute at 11.5 °C and 190 at 15.5 °C.
- Females lay eggs in a clump on the stone's underside. Clumps are never found on the ground. Eggs are 2.0–2.4 mm in diameter, with 50–250 per clump.

**2. Maturity and fecundity**
- In Windermere, 49% of females and 66% of males bred at eleven months old. In the River Brathay, only 6% of females and 11% of males bred at that age.
- Lake fish have more and larger eggs than river fish at equivalent ages. One-year-old mature Windermere fish carried more ripe eggs than the largest three-year-old River Brathay fish.
- Males mature one to two months before females and are less numerous than females in the lake during the breeding period.

**3. Age, growth and size**
- Age was determined from otoliths. A distinctive structural feature, recognised between April and June, was assumed to be annual. Residual eggs confirmed that a female was at least one year old.
- Lake fish grow faster and reach a greater size than river fish. Growth is most rapid in the first two years and effectively stops after the third.
- Males grow faster and are longer and heavier than females in both habitats. Lake fish of both sexes are larger than river fish of the same sex.
- Measured ranges were 2.2–8.1 cm and 0.09–6.4 g in the Lake District, and up to 11.2 cm and 27.1 g in hard waters.
- Growth is faster in hard waters than soft waters (Table 5).

**4. Feeding**
- The fish feeds mainly on moving benthic invertebrates: Ephemeroptera and Plecoptera nymphs, Trichoptera larvae, [[Gammarus]] and [[Sialis]].
- Gammarus and Sialis are eaten only by lake fish. River fish have less food in their stomachs throughout the year, and grow more slowly.
- Only two cannibal fish were recorded across the whole study.
- The author concludes that Bullheads rarely eat [[Salmonid]] eggs in nature. Predation on alevins is considered more serious than on eggs, although the author admits he has no direct data on this.

**5. Overall conclusions**
- The study finds a clear lake–river contrast in growth, maturation and diet, which the author links to food availability and temperature.
- The species is considered to have few serious predators, and parasite burdens are moderate.
