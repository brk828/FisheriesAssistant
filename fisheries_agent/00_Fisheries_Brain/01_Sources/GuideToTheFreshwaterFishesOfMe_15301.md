## Executive Summary

This work, Robert Rush Miller's *Guide to the Freshwater Fishes of Mexico*, is the first comprehensive treatment of Mexico's freshwater fish fauna, synthesizing more than fifty years of fieldwork and over 3,000 collecting stations. The book documents **503+ species** across **48 families**, representing roughly 65% of the combined ichthyofauna of the United States and Canada despite Mexico's much smaller land area. The fauna is biogeographically tripartite — derived roughly equally from Nearctic/Holarctic, Neotropical, and marine-derived lineages, with a small but ecologically important autochthonous component (the family [[Goodeidae]] of the Mesa Central). A dedicated appendix addresses taxonomic uncertainty in the genus *Agonostomus* (American mountain mullets), questioning whether American populations warrant separation into the genus *Dajaus* and how many valid American species exist.

## Background & Description

- **Diagnostic taxonomy conventions**: Species identification in the guide relies on field-distinguishing characters (illustrated via photographs, drawings, and color plates) rather than exhaustive morphological description — emphasizing counting and measuring methods per Hubbs and Lagler (1958), with exceptions noted for [[Cyprinodontiformes]].
- ***Agonostomus* / mountain mullets** (family [[Mugilidae]]): 
  - Type species *A. telfairii* Bennett, 1831, from Mauritius; related to *A. dobuloides* (validity questioned) from the Western Indian Ocean.
  - New World form: *Agonostomus monticola* (Bancroft in Cuvier, 1834) — most of six previously recognized American species (Regan 1906–08) have been synonymized under *A. monticola* by later workers (Meek and Hildebrand, 1916).
  - *Agonostomus hancocki* Seale, 1932, described from two specimens from Chatham (San Cristóbal) Island, Galápagos, but never compared against congeners — validity unresolved.
  - Life history note: only prejuveniles of *Agonostomus* and the related genus [[Joturus]] are marine; adults are essentially freshwater, inhabiting swift, clear streams, rapids, and waterfall bases.
- **Nomenclatural structure**: Species names follow binomial Latin/Greek convention (genus capitalized, species lowercase, both italicized); gender agreement required for adjectival species epithets (e.g., *Poeciliopsis fasciata*, not *fasciatus*).
- The author retains broad, conservative generic concepts (e.g., *Notropis*, *Arius*, *Cichlasoma*) despite known non-monophyly, to avoid rendering remaining species paraphyletic upon partial splitting.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Salinity boundary for "freshwater" classification**: arbitrarily set at 1.0 ppt, following Castro-Aguirre (1978); some included populations (e.g., [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] basin, Coahuila; San Diego springs, Chihuahua) tolerate salinities up to 1.5 ppt or higher.
- **Euryhaline exceptions among "primary" freshwater fishes**: several species classified as intolerant of sea water are documented in brackish/marine conditions worldwide:
  - *Rutilus rutilus* (European roach) in 10–18 ppt (Nellen 1965)
  - *Tribolodon brandti* in full seawater (cannot complete life cycle there; Gritsenko 1974)
  - *Mylocheilus caurinus* (peamouth chub) taken in ocean waters
  - *Ictalurus punctatus* (channel catfish) in 15.1 ppt (Schwartz 1964)
  - *Lepomis macrochirus* (bluegill) in 17.4 ppt (Renfro 1960)
  - *Astyanax mexicanus* (Mexican tetra, [[Characidae]]) invading mangrove-lined brackish estuaries on Mexico's east coast (Miller 1966)
- **"Borderline freshwater" taxa** included for completeness: *Lile stolifera* (herring), many [[Ariidae]] (ariid catfishes), *Menidia beryllina*, *Pseudophallus starksi*, genus *Centropomus*, gerreids, *Pomadasys*, mullets, flatfishes, species of *Eleotris*, and both species of *Dormitator* (family [[Eleotridae]]) — the latter two normally brackish/estuarine.
- **Distribution of *Agonostomus* mountain mullets**: 
  - Atlantic drainage: southeastern/southern United States, through Caribbean islands, Mexico, into Colombia and Venezuela.
  - Pacific drainage: Gulf of California southward through mainland Middle America into central Panama and the Galápagos Islands.
  - Habitat preference: swift, clear streams, rapids, and waterfall bases; considerable food/fishery value.
- **Biogeographic drivers of Mexican diversity**: highly varied physiography, complex geology, large latitudinal range (32°30'N to 14°30'N), isolation of the tropical highland Mesa Central (harboring the endemic Río Lerma fauna), marine intrusion events producing permanent freshwater residents, and the tropical Río Grijalva-Usumacinta system (largest river system in Middle America) supporting a highly endemic fauna.
- **Endorheic Mexican Plateau**: refuge largely for Nearctic/Holarctic-derived fish lineages.

## Key Findings

- Mexico's freshwater ichthyofauna totals at least **503 species**, ~25% primary freshwater fishes (127 spp.) and ~43% secondary freshwater fishes (218 spp.), with the remainder marine-derived or autochthonous.
- Three largest families: [[Poeciliidae]] (86 species), [[Cyprinidae]] (76 species), [[Cichlidae]] (45 species).
- Fauna origins split roughly equally (~30% each) among Nearctic/Holarctic, Neotropical/Middle American, and marine-derived sources, with <10% comprising the endemic [[Goodeidae]] radiation on the Mesa Central.
- Conservation crisis: **22 fish taxa** have been lost from Mexico — 12 extinct (including 2 extinct genera) and 10 extirpated — underscoring urgent need for aquatic biodiversity conservation.
- Major knowledge gaps identified: (1) no complete life history known for any Mexican freshwater fish; (2) badly needed generic/phylogenetic revisions; (3) insufficient Cenozoic geological/paleolimnological research to resolve evolutionary history; (4) urgent conservation action needed.
- Taxonomic case study (*Agonostomus*) remains unresolved: no formal genus-level revision has been completed since Hubbs (1953) flagged the need, and it remains uncertain whether New World mountain mullets should be split into genus **Dajaus**, and whether more than one valid American species exists (per Ebeling 1961, Follett 1960, Gilbert 1978).
- Methodological note: family name endings follow traditional euphonious usage (e.g., retaining "Eleotridae" over the proposed "Eleotrididae"), reflecting the author's stance against excessive nomenclatural correction per Ernst Mayr (1989).
- The genus [[Anguilla]] (American eel) is confined to the Atlantic basin; a reported Pacific-slope Río Balsas capture is flagged as a likely erroneous intracontinental transfer, illustrating pitfalls in range-extension reporting.
