# Phytoplankton Succession and Hydrology in the River Bure and Its Broads

#phytoplankton #limnology #freshwater-ecology #bacillariophyceae #cyanobacteria

## Executive Summary

This document is the second part of a two-part study (Moss & Balls, published in the *Journal of Plankton Research*, received 28 October 1988) on phytoplankton distribution in the lowland [[River Bure]] and its connected shallow lakes, the [[Norfolk Broads]], in eastern England. Data were gathered fortnightly from February 1979 to March 1981 at 21 river, Broad and dyke stations between Hoveton Bridge and Thurne Mouth. The authors organise the data by "time for development", an estimate of how long river water has been developing algae before reaching a station. Phytoplankton succession follows this gradient, moving from centric diatoms to cyanobacteria and finally to pennate diatoms. The authors attribute this to changing nutrients and declining turbulence, and they use the results to inform restoration options. The reference list, which is extensive, is condensed here.

## Background & Description

The R. Bure is a slow-flowing, eutrophic river fed by sewage effluent and agricultural run-off. It is linked to a series of shallow lakes (Broads), some adjacent and others at the ends of dykes. The study area ran from Hoveton Bridge downstream toward Thurne Mouth. Prior work (Moss et al., 1989) found no relationship between flushing rate and chlorophyll *a*, and it concluded that crops are set by total phosphorus supply. The less-flushed Broads act as "nurseries" that seed the river with phytoplankton.

Upstream reaches are dominated by diatoms, whereas downstream reaches have more cyanobacteria, which are typical of heavily eutrophic lakes. The authors frame the seasonal and downstream changes as a successional sequence moving along a water mass, and they compare it with Reynolds' (1980) model for stratified lakes.

Methods: time for development was calculated from a 2-day baseline at Hoveton Bridge, plus travel times at 5.8 km/day (Hoveton Bridge to Swan Bend) and 4.25 km/day (Swan Bend to Church Bend), plus retention times. Retention times were 7 days (Wroxham, Salhouse, Hudsons Bay, Decoy Broads), 28 days (Hoveton Great, Malthouse, Ranworth Broads) and 56 days (South Walsham Broad). Soluble reactive phosphorus and silicate were measured by the method of [[Mackereth Water Analysis Methods]], and phytoplankton was counted with an inverted microscope at ±20% precision for dominant species, preserved in Lugol's solution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Broads delay downstream water movement. Upstream Broads effectively added several weeks to development time. Back-projection suggested nursery areas could add the equivalent of about 12 weeks.
- **Phosphorus:** Total phosphorus was steady along the upper river and correlated with chlorophyll *a* across the system (r = 0.75, P < 0.005 with all stations). This relationship depended on the South Walsham stations. Without them, there was no significant relationship (r = −0.13, P > 0.1).
- **Nutrients:** Soluble reactive phosphorus and nitrate fell with time for development, while silicate rose. Summer phosphate peaks occurred at Hoveton Bridge, where high flows dilute sewage effluent. Winter nitrate peaks fell downstream, possibly from denitrification.
- **Anomalous sites:** South Walsham Broad receives sediment-derived phosphate and silicate and is affected by surge tidal injection of sewage effluent. Burntfen Broad is fed by a tributary contaminated with stock effluent.
- **Carbon and pH:** Carbon dioxide uptake in this highly calcareous, high-alkalinity system led to small pH increases, with values over 9 only in South Walsham Broad.
- **Turbulence:** Melosira, which has heavy cells, is associated with mixing. Its decline downstream may reflect reduced turbulence.
- **Zooplankton:** Grazing is short-lived because refuges for large Cladocera are few once young fish hatch in June. Cyclopoid copepods and rotifers then dominate.

## Key Findings

1. **Chlorophyll rises along the gradient.** The slope was 1.28 µg chlorophyll *a* l⁻¹ day⁻¹ without South Walsham and 1.7 µg l⁻¹ day⁻¹ with it. Negative intercepts (−83 and −58 days) suggest development times are systematically underestimated.
2. **Nutrient availability shifts.** Soluble reactive phosphorus and nitrate decreased with time for development, and silicate increased. Nutrient stress ratios increased with time, suggesting increasing scarcity of all three nutrients.
3. **Succession sequence.** The sequence starts with *Cyclotella meneghiniana*, *Stephanodiscus hantzschii* and *Melosira* species, then moves to *Oscillatoria* species, then *Diatoma elongatum*, *Synedra ulna* and *Anabaena planctonica*. Over 80 additional taxa were recorded, none as abundant as the main species. In total, 85 taxa were identified.
4. **Mechanism.** The authors propose that succession is driven by decreasing SRP and nitrate, rising Si:P ratios, declining CO₂, and steadily decreasing turbulence. These factors are mutually correlated, so experiments are needed to separate them.
5. **Nutrient-limited Broads.** Wroxham Broad was probably not nutrient limited. The occurrence of *A. planctonica* at Hoveton Great Broad suggests summer nitrogen scarcity.
6. **Restoration implications.** The authors suggest that phosphorus loading must be reduced, and that zooplankton grazing and reduced development time through nursery-area modification are secondary options. Complete damming of the Broads was rejected as impracticable because of fish migration and navigation issues. Widening Broad openings to increase flow-through was suggested instead. The plan is to restore the upper, diatom-dominated section above Swan Bend.
