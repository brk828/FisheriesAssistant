# Rare and Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodon

## Executive Summary

This is an essay by [[Robert Rush Miller]] on the conservation of rare and endangered fishes, with emphasis on freshwater species of the American Southwest. Miller argues that fishes have received far less conservation attention than birds and mammals, that habitat alteration caused by human activity is the main driver of freshwater fish decline, and that diversity in aquatic ecosystems must be retained. He reviews the national and international organizations working on threatened fishes and presents two case histories of desert [[Cyprinodon]] pupfishes: the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], whose survival depends on protecting its groundwater source, and the [[Owens Pupfish]], which recovered after refuges were established. Internal references date the text to about 1973.

## Background & Description

Miller opens with a tribute to [[Rachel Carson]], whose book [[Silent Spring]] helped launch the environmental movement of the 1970s. He recounts a campaign, led by Miller with [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]], to halt federal-state poisoning of the [[Green River]], a Colorado River tributary. The effort failed to stop the project, but it prompted a moratorium on toxic chemicals in federal fishery management.

Miller defines endangered species as those whose numbers decline faster than they can replace themselves, and rare species as those with few individuals or restricted ranges that require monitoring. Fishes are estimated at 20,000 or more species, probably more than all other vertebrates combined. Yet conservation has largely ignored them. Miller notes that the [[National Park Service]] once permitted stocking of exotic fishes for anglers, damaging native species, and that ichthyologists have lacked popular field guides comparable to those for birds and mammals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline.** Freshwater fish losses stem mainly from habitat alteration by humans. The causes cited include:
- Destruction of forest cover, with silting and flooding
- Dam building, irrigation, and excessive groundwater pumping
- Draining of marshes
- Introduction of exotic species and their parasites
- Toxic chemicals, including insecticides
- [[Channelization]], which Miller highlights as a serious problem in the southern United States

Channelization widens, deepens, and straightens streams. On the Tippah River in Mississippi, one acre once yielded an average of 241 pounds of fish. Three years after ditching, it yielded 4.4 pounds. Miller argues that diversity promotes ecosystem stability, use of solar energy, and resilience against environmental stress, citing [[K. E. F. Watt]].

**Desert pupfishes.** Pupfishes comprise about two dozen species. They inhabit desert springs, briny creeks, alkaline marshes, and coastal waters from eastern North America and California to northern South America. Their environments range from 68°F to 104°F, with annual temperature swings of up to 70°F. They tolerate salinities up to 142 ppt, about four times that of sea water, and live from below sea level to 6,000 feet. Miller states that their desert habitats were once isolated but are now increasingly settled, and that these fragile ecosystems cannot withstand rapid change.

**Devils Hole.** The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] lives in a cave-like spring without outlet east of [[Death Valley]]. Its water comes from a 93°F aquifer. The only macroscopic invertebrates are a flatworm (Planaria), a beetle ([[Stenelmis]]), and a small snail (Tyronia). The pool measures about 60 feet long and 8 to 10 feet wide, possibly the smallest habitat known for any vertebrate. Lowered water tables from ranching pumping in nearby [[Ash Meadows]] threaten the population.

## Key Findings

**Conservation organizations and lists**
- The [[IUCN]] (founded 1948, based in Morges, Switzerland) includes 27 governments and 250 organizations from 25 countries. Its [[Survival Service Commission]] runs the [[Red Data Books]], and its Fish Group produced the first world list of threatened freshwater fishes in 1969, with 79 kinds, to which 13 were added in 1972.
- The U.S. [[Office of Endangered Species]] has issued the U.S. Redbook of Rare and Endangered Wildlife since 1964.
- The [[Nature Conservancy]] (founded 1950) buys endangered habitats. The [[Defenders of Wildlife]] helped secure wilderness status for much of [[Aravaipa Canyon]], Arizona, home to seven native fishes.
- The [[Environmental Defense Fund]] (founded 1967) pursues legal action and public education.
- The [[American Fisheries Society]] Endangered Species Committee published a 1972 account listing 305 threatened U.S. freshwater fish kinds and is preparing a national list and a model law.
- The [[Desert Fishes Council]] (founded 1970) shares information on North American desert fishes.

**Devils Hole Pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*)**
- Population probably fluctuated between 300 and 900 individuals before 1969.
- By spring 1972 the population was at least 50 percent below normal.
- Water level had dropped at least three feet since 1969.
- SCUBA studies in 1972 found most individuals migrate daily to depths of up to 80 feet, with the bulk at 25 to 50 feet.
- Unlike other cyprinodonts, it lacks aggressive territorial behavior, and it may spawn year-round.
- Transfers to five refugia in California and Nevada mostly failed or produced few fish. Aquarium culture has repeatedly failed.
- Miller concludes that survival requires preserving the original habitat by stopping excessive pumping. Federal legal action to halt pumping was pending as of January 15, 1973.

**Owens Pupfish (*Cyprinodon radiosus*)**
- Endemic to [[Owens Valley]], California, along the eastern Sierra Nevada escarpment.
- Thought extinct when described in 1948, after no sightings since 1942.
- Causes of decline were diversion of water by the [[Los Angeles Aqueduct]], which drained marshes and sloughs, and predation by [[Largemouth Bass]], introduced by 1913.
- Rediscovered in 1956 and 1964 in [[Fish Slough]] north of Bishop.
- The species now occupies three refuges, including its original spring area. The [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]] (established 1970) is intended to protect three other threatened native fishes.

**Central message**
- Miller calls for more pupfish-style recovery efforts across the Southwest.
- He states that human environmental damage usually results from ignorance of ecosystem complexity rather than intent.
- Protecting native fishes is presented as a responsibility for humans to keep other species alive.
