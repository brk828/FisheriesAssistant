# Reintroduction and Monitoring of Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in Arizona, 1985

## Executive Summary
This 1986 report by James E. Brooks (Arizona Game & Fish Department) for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service documents the first coordinated 1985 reintroduction effort of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) into its historic lower Colorado River basin range, specifically the Salt and Verde rivers of Arizona. Extirpated from the lower basin since the early 1960s due to habitat modification and mainstream damming, the species persisted only in the upper Green and Colorado river systems. Following precedents set by reintroductions of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (1981) and [[Gila Topminnow]] (1982), over 96,000 fingerlings and 442 larger juveniles/subadults were stocked at six sites across the Salt and Verde drainages. Limited post-stocking monitoring via trammel netting and seining confirmed short-term survival, feeding activity, and continued site presence of stocked fish through at least four months post-release (January 1986), though sample sizes were small and long-term establishment remained uncertain.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Ptychocheilus lucius* — a large, piscivorous [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]] (minnow family) native to the Colorado River drainage.
- **Stocked size classes**:
  - Fingerlings: 15–125 mm Total Length (TL)
  - Larger juveniles/subadults: 355–405 mm mean TL (x̄ TL ≈ 355 mm)
- **Recaptured fish (Verde River, Perkinsville, Sept 1985)**:
  - n = 7; x̄ TL = 333 ± 15 mm (range 312–358 mm)
  - x̄ Weight = 253 ± 48 g (range 195–350 g)
  - All individuals were dead or dying upon net retrieval despite live-capture net design; stomachs were empty (possible regurgitation or full digestion during set).
- **Recaptured fish (January 1986 survey)**:
  - n = 5; x̄ TL = 362 ± 34 mm (range 333–412 mm)
  - x̄ Weight = 314 ± 103 g (range 218–462 g)
  - All alive upon capture; stomachs empty but visceral fat present, indicating active feeding had occurred.
- Fish reared by Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery (AGFD-stocked cohort) were marked with **tetracycline** for identification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historic range**: Widely distributed throughout the entire [[Colorado River Drainage]] until the early 1900s; now restricted in the wild to the upper basin (Green and Colorado rivers and larger tributaries).
- **Study area habitats**:
  - **Salt River**: Confined canyon topography with high relief; features deep pools, shallow riffles, long deep runs, and ephemeral backwaters.
  - **Verde River**: Lower-relief topography; characterized by channel braiding, large stable backwaters, and finer substrates.
  - **Tributaries** (Cherry Creek, Fossil Creek, West Clear Creek): Narrow canyons/valleys subject to frequent flash flooding; generally native-fish dominated with fewer introduced predators, considered more ecologically natural.
  - Upstream boundary of suitable habitat marked by **Roosevelt Lake** (Salt) and **Horseshoe Lake** (Verde) reservoirs.

- **Predation threats**: Introduced [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) identified as significant predators on stocked native fishes, based on parallel razorback sucker reintroduction data.
- **Movement ecology**: Adult Colorado squawfish are documented to migrate long distances (>200 km), presumably for spawning; contrasts with more sedentary, bottom-oriented behavior observed in razorback sucker. Homing behavior is suggested by some radiotelemetry studies (Tyus 1985) but disputed by others showing minimal movement (Tyus et al. 1981; Miller et al. 1984).

## Key Findings
- **Stocking effort**: 96,000+ fingerlings and 442 larger squawfish (355–405 mm TL) released across six sites in the Salt River drainage (Cherry Creek, Salt River) and Verde River drainage (Fossil Creek, West Clear Creek, Verde River) between August–October 1985 (see Table 1).
- **Short-term survival confirmed**: Recaptures in September 1985 and January 1986 at the Verde River/Perkinsville site demonstrated persistence of stocked fish at least 4 months post-release, with evidence of feeding activity (visceral fat) by the January survey despite empty stomachs at capture.
- **Mortality concern**: All squawfish captured in the initial September 1985 survey were dead or dying despite use of trammel nets designed for live capture — cause undetermined (possible net-related stress/injury).
- **Unverified anecdotal report**: A fisherman reported catching a squawfish at Perkinsville in November 1985 (AGFD, Bill Silvey), suggesting persistence in the local population beyond formal survey dates.
- **Management implications**:
  - Legal status as "nonessential experimental" (10(j) ESA designation) permits flexible recovery efforts without full Section 7 protections, following the successful [[Razorback Sucker]] reintroduction model in the Gila River Basin.
  - Upstream tributary stocking (vs. downstream mainstem sites) is recommended to reduce predation exposure and stranding risk from irrigation diversions, mirroring lessons learned from prior razorback sucker work.
  - Stocking larger-sized fish may reduce predation losses.
  - Future studies (planned fall 1986) will assess short-term dispersal of stocked fingerlings and evaluate predation impacts and homing behavior — critical unknowns for optimizing recovery protocol.
  - Authors argue reintroduced squawfish should behave ecologically similarly to other native fishes, benefiting from natural flow regimes that disadvantage non-native species (per [[Minckley and Meffe 1986]] flood-selection hypothesis).
- **Comparative species movement data** (from cited upper-basin telemetry studies):
  - Tyus et al. (1981): of 6 radiotagged squawfish, 3 stationary, 1 moved 25 km, 2 moved 356–455 km.
  - Tyus et al. (1985): 6 squawfish moved a mean of 261.5 km during a June–July 1981 spawning migration.
  - Miller et al. (1984): of 22 squawfish tracked in 1982, 21 moved <65 km (1 moved 320 km); of 21 tracked in 1983, movements were <50 km — largely contradicting strong homing/migration patterns.
