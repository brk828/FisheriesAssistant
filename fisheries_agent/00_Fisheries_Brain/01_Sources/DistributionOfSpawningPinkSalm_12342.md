# Distribution of Spawning Pink Salmon in Sashin Creek, Southeastern Alaska, and Survival of Their Progeny

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #pink-salmon #spawning-ecology #freshwater-survival

## Executive Summary

This study examined the 1963 brood year of [[Pink Salmon]] (Oncorhynchus gorbuscha) in [[Sashin Creek]], [[Southeastern Alaska]], focusing on spawner distribution, density, and progeny survival from egg deposition through fry emergence. The escapement totaled 16,757 fish (8,740 females), with overall freshwater survival of 20 percent from potential egg deposition to outmigrant fry. The spawning ground was divided into three areas (upper, middle, lower) differing in gradient and substrate composition. Spawner density was highest in the middle area, but survival and fry production per square meter were highest in the upper area (31% survival, 360 fry/m²) due to coarser substrates and better intragravel conditions. The study challenges the assumption that small escapements automatically utilize only the most productive spawning habitat, demonstrating that large escapements may be necessary to ensure full utilization of optimal spawning areas.

## Background & Description

[[Sashin Creek]] is a small stream on [[Baranof Island]] approximately 4,000 m long. A waterfall 1,200 m from the head of tide limits upstream migration. The main spawning ground encompasses 13,629 m², with 13,084 m² divided into three study areas: upper (2,945 m²), middle (4,067 m²), and lower (6,072 m²). These areas differ substantially in physical characteristics:

- **Upper area**: Steep gradient (0.7%), coarse substrates (81% cobbles >12.7 mm diameter), 16% pebbles/granules, 3% sands/silts
- **Middle area**: Intermediate gradient (0.3%), 61% cobbles, 26% pebbles/granules, 13% sands/silts  
- **Lower area**: Shallow gradient (0.1%), 47% cobbles, 36% pebbles/granules, 17% sands/silts

The 1963 spawning run began August 10 and concluded September 6. One hundred females were tagged August 20-21 with colored plastic disks to track distribution by size (50 large >49.9 cm with pink tags, 50 small <49.9 cm with green tags). Three observers conducted systematic surveys on a weekly rotating schedule to count spawners and locate tagged individuals. Average female fecundity was 1,900 eggs.

Chronological events for the 1963 brood year:
- Spawning: mid-August to late September (1.5 months)
- Hatching: 85% complete by December 12 (2.5 months from spawning start)
- Fry emergence: March 25 to June 1 (began 3.5 months after hatching)
- Total freshwater residence: 9.5 months

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Sashin Creek]] flow is well-regulated by watershed lakes that provide temporary storage during high runoff. Maximum discharge over 12 years (1952-64) was 21 m³/sec; maximum affecting the 1963 brood was 15 m³/sec. This regulation prevents major bed disturbance and debris accumulation.

**Dissolved oxygen in intragravel water** varied substantially among areas and seasons. In August, the upper area maintained relatively high oxygen levels while middle and lower areas showed depressed levels:

- Upper area (August 1963): 7.3 mg/L (64% saturation)
- Middle area (August 1963): 5.1 mg/L (41% saturation)  
- Lower area (August 1963): 5.9 mg/L (47% saturation)

By September, all areas showed improved oxygen levels (8.2-9.2 mg/L). The coarse substrates and steep gradient in the upper area likely promoted better intragravel water circulation and oxygen supply throughout the spawning period.

**Weather patterns in 1963** were exceptional. The early spawning period (August 10-September 2) was the third driest on record (average 0.25 cm daily rainfall), while the late period (September 3-29) was the wettest in 26 years (4.52 cm daily rainfall). This dramatic shift may have influenced spawner distribution, as tagged females showed a downstream "shift" after September 3, possibly seeking less turbulent water in low-gradient areas as discharge increased from 0.3 to 3.0+ m³/sec.

**Substrate composition** strongly influenced egg retention and survival. The upper area's coarse materials (large cobbles) appeared to reduce egg loss during [[Redd Superimposition]], as females could not dislodge large cobbles while digging and eggs drifted into large interstices rather than being washed away. Conversely, finer materials in middle and lower areas allowed greater disturbance during redd construction.

**Predation and scavenging** may have been important mortality factors, particularly in the upper area during autumn. Coarse substrates provide easier access for predators and scavengers to salmon eggs. Water temperatures were relatively warm in autumn (2-7°C) when feeding rates would be high, compared to winter (0-2°C) when metabolic rates decline.

## Key Findings

### Spawner Distribution and Density

Spawner density was approximately 50% higher in the middle area than in upper and lower areas:
- Upper: 0.6 females/m²
- Middle: 0.9 females/m²
- Lower: 0.6 females/m²

Chi-square analysis of tagged female distribution confirmed non-uniform density (X² = 5.22, 2 df, p = 0.07). Of 86 tagged females observed spawning, 13 used the upper area, 36 the middle, and 37 the lower area.

**Female size did not influence distribution**: Large (>49.9 cm) and small (<49.9 cm) females spawned with equal frequency in all three areas. Of 86 tagged spawners, 43 were large and 43 small, with no significant size-related spatial pattern.

**Spawning timing affected distribution**: Early spawners (before September 3) favored upstream areas while late spawners concentrated downstream (X² = 7.87, 2 df, p = 0.02). This shift occurred after stream discharge increased dramatically and was not related to date of stream entry, as all tagged fish entered August 20-21.

### Egg Deposition Efficiency

Despite highest spawner density in the middle area, **egg density at spawning end was highest in the upper area**:
- Upper: 885 eggs/m² (78% of potential deposition retained)
- Middle: 538 eggs/m² (31% of potential retained)
- Lower: 433 eggs/m² (38% of potential retained)

The percentage of spawning ground used (indexed by samples with >3 eggs per 0.1 m²) was highest in the middle area, consistent with spawner density. However, **recruitment efficiency** (percentage of potential eggs actually deposited in redds) was dramatically higher in the upper area due to coarse substrates that reduced egg loss from redd superimposition and other disturbances.

### Survival Through Four Life-History Periods

**Period 1 - Egg deposition (1.5 months, mid-August to late September)**:
- Upper: 67% survival (M = 0.27 month⁻¹)
- Middle: 20% survival (M = 1.07 month⁻¹)
- Lower: 28% survival (M = 0.85 month⁻¹)

Mortality was 3-4 times higher in middle and lower areas. Most loss resulted from eggs discharged during spawning but not retained in redds. Post-mortem examination of 435 females showed only 4% of eggs retained in body cavities, and predation on live spawners was minimal. The primary cause was redd superimposition and egg displacement, which was reduced in the upper area by coarse substrates.

**Period 2 - Deposition to hatching (2.5 months, late September to mid-December)**:
- Upper: 49% survival (M = 0.29 month⁻¹)
- Middle: 85% survival (M = 0.07 month⁻¹)
- Lower: 101% survival (M = -0.004 month⁻¹)

The upper area experienced substantial egg and alevin disappearance (estimated 58% by mid-December), while middle and lower areas showed stable or slightly increasing numbers. Predators or scavengers may have consumed eggs in the upper area where coarse substrates provided easier access. Feeding rates would be highest in autumn when water temperatures were 2-7°C.

**Period 3 - Hatching to fry emergence (3.5 months, mid-December to late March)**:
- Upper: 97% survival (M = 0.009 month⁻¹)
- Middle: 98% survival (M = 0.006 month⁻¹)
- Lower: 57% survival (M = 0.16 month⁻¹)

The lower area showed elevated mortality during winter, with many dead alevins present in late March samples. Poor water circulation in fine substrates with low gradient may have produced weak alevins with reduced winter survival. Dead alevins remained in the gravel, ruling out predation or washout as causes.

**Period 4 - Fry emergence and migration (2.0 months, late March to late May)**:
- All areas: 97% survival (M = 0.02 month⁻¹)

High survival throughout the stream indicated minimal predation during emergence. Of 3,342,000 alevins estimated in the streambed at emergence start, 3,256,000 (97%) were counted at the weir as outmigrating fry.

### Overall Survival and Production

**Total survival from egg deposition to migrant fry**:
- Upper area: 31% (360 fry/m²)
- Middle area: 16% (268 fry/m²)
- Lower area: 15% (174 fry/m²)
- Stream-wide: 19-20%

Despite comprising only 22% of the study area, the upper area produced approximately one-third of total fry due to superior survival. Each area produced similar total fry numbers because size differences compensated for survival differences.

### Historical Patterns and Management Implications

Analysis of 22 brood years (1940-63) revealed that survival from the 11 larger escapements averaged 8.2% compared to 4.5% for the 11 smaller escapements, though this difference was not statistically significant (t = 1.27, 20 df). 

**Critical observation**: In years of high abundance (1959, 1963), spawners concentrated in the middle area but also utilized the productive upper area. In years of low abundance (1958, 1964), spawners concentrated in the lower area with minimal use of the upper area. This contradicts the hypothesis that small runs automatically select the most productive spawning habitat.

The study questions the validity of assuming that only highly productive spawning beds are used when escapements are small. At [[Sashin Creek]], relatively large escapements appear necessary to ensure complete utilization of productive spawning areas. Crowding in preferred areas may cause "overflow" into the upper area, which ultimately proves highly productive despite not being initially selected.

**Management consideration**: Behavior traits affecting spawner distribution should inform [[Pink Salmon]] management. Escapements may need to exceed levels calculated assuming uniform distribution to ensure utilization of all productive habitat. Crowding on certain spawning beds may beneficially force some spawners to use areas with more favorable conditions for embryo and alevin development.
