# Ecological Evaluation of Game Fishes at Deer Island Lake, Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation (Michael Kenichi Satoh, University of Arizona, 1976) evaluates the game fishery of [[Deer Island Lake]], a 40.7 ha backwater of the [[Lower Colorado River]] in Arizona. Field work ran from October 1973 to August 1975. The study examined water quality, water levels, bottom sediments, aquatic flora, invertebrates and fish biology, and placed these within the broader system of reservoirs, backwaters and the mainstream. The author concludes that the backwater is poorly flushed, that forage supply is adequate for growth and condition, but that few fish survive past ages 2 or 3, so few trophy-sized fish are available to anglers. Management recommendations focus on increasing flushing and removing culvert gratings.

## Background & Description

The Lower Colorado River has been transformed by large mainstream reservoirs and channelization, which reduced pools, riffles and backwater habitat. A 1964 multipurpose management plan authorized artificial backwaters to mitigate these losses, and several have since been built. The author argues that a sound fishery management plan requires understanding the interactions among reservoirs, backwaters and the mainstream, which had not previously been studied together.

Deer Island Lake is a holomictic backwater joined to the Slough and river by a culvert 0.75 m in diameter, grated with vertical steel rods spaced 2 cm apart. Average and maximum depths are 2.0 m and 3.0 m. Shoreline development is 5.9. The climate is hot and dry, with less than 10 cm of annual rainfall and evaporation often exceeding 219 cm per year. Bottom substrates range from silt, sand and gravel in shallows to clay, sand and organic muck in deeper water.

The aquatic flora includes [[Cattail]] (Typha spp.), [[Spiny Naiad]], [[Sago Pondweed]] and stonewort (Chara sp.). The area also supports bullfrogs, softshell turtles, waterfowl, herons and mammals such as mule deer, beaver and muskrat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Temperatures ranged from 8.0 to 34.4 °C. Thermal stratification was absent, so the lake is classed as warm polymictic. Mean monthly dissolved oxygen ranged from 4.9 to 9.7 mg/L. Bottom waters fell below 2.0 mg/L at times from May to October, and anaerobic conditions occurred once (23 July 1974). pH ranged from 7.7 to 8.5 and total alkalinity from 176 to 276 mg/L. Total dissolved solids ranged from 580 to 1310 mg/L, and conductivity from 1420 to 2400 µmhos/cm.

**Water level and sediments.** Daily water-level fluctuations were usually under 0.15 m, with annual fluctuations up to 0.50 m. Bottom sediments averaged 98.7% clay-sand-gravel and 1.3% organic matter by dry weight.

**Biota.** Cattail growth lasted about 9 to 10 months, and submergent macrophytes grew from roughly May to November. Zooplankton peaked in March at 10.1 ml/m³, with a second peak in January, and were low from May to October. Thirty-one macrophytic and benthic invertebrate taxa were recorded. Benthic standing crops peaked in January 1974 at 2.1 g/m², and their abundance was inversely related to that of macrophytic invertebrates. This provided year-round forage.

**Fish community.** Thirteen species and one hybrid were recorded, all exotic to the Lower Colorado River system. The most abundant were largemouth bass, bluegill, redear sunfish, threadfin shad, channel catfish and carp. Channel catfish and rainbow trout are stocked annually, since spawning was not confirmed for them.

**Historical context.** Before dam construction the river was seasonally variable and turbid, and it supported few native fishes. Mainstream reservoirs such as Lake Mead, Lake Havasu and Imperial Reservoir stabilized flows and clarified water, and exotic centrarchids and ictalurids became dominant. Surviving native fishes include the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and bonytail chub, which were reported in earlier surveys.

## Key Findings

- **Limited flushing:** Only a single culvert and minor percolation link the backwater to the river. Its dilution factor is over 40 times lower than that of backwater A-7B, so Deer Island Lake behaves more like a lake than a flowing backwater.
- **Stable levels:** Water-level fluctuations were not detrimental to fishes. Prolonged river drawdowns (1–2 weeks) lowered backwater levels.
- **Adequate forage:** Condition coefficients and growth rates indicated sufficient forage for a good fishery. Growth was average to above average compared with other Lower Colorado River waters. Bluegill reached 6–7 cm in one year and 16–17 cm in two. Largemouth bass reached about 19 cm in one year and 30 cm in two.
- **Few old fish:** Length-frequency distributions showed few individuals past ages 2 or 3 for shad, bluegill, redear sunfish and bass. Angler harvest was negligible, so natural mortality is implicated.
- **Bass starvation:** Bass of 25–35 cm declined in condition between March and June 1974, when shad were too large or too small for predation and bluegill became the main prey. The author attributes the loss of large bass to starvation-related mortality.
- **Emigration not responsible:** Culvert width limits meant only small fish (shad under 11.4 cm, bluegill under 13.7 cm, redear under 14.0 cm, bass under 14.9 cm) could leave the backwater.
- **Shad movement:** Thousands of 2–4 cm shad immigrated through the culvert in July 1974. Shad distribution contracted in mid-winter and expanded later, suggesting seasonal migrations between the backwater and river.
- **Reproduction:** Bluegill, redear sunfish and largemouth bass nested in shallow sand-gravel areas. Carp spawned in shallow weedy water in March 1975. Threadfin shad spawning was not observed directly, but larvae were captured in May 1974.
- **Food habits:** Carp and channel catfish were omnivorous. Bluegill fed mainly on insects. Redear sunfish fed on mollusks and insects. Threadfin shad were planktivorous. Largemouth bass fed on shad and crayfish when large, and on bluegill and insects when small.
- **Management recommendations:** (1) Increase the flushing rate of the backwater, using an additional culvert at the northwest end and sliding gates to regulate flow. (2) Remove culvert gratings to allow migration and reduce crowding of larger fishes.
- **Future threats:** Increased withdrawals from Lake Havasu and salinity increases could reduce river flows and degrade backwater habitats. The author predicts that predominantly non-game, salt-tolerant species such as mosquitofish and carp would benefit.
