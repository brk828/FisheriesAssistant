# Responses of Minnows to Flood and Drought in an Intermittent Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Andreas A. Paloumpis]] (Iowa State College, 1958) examines how the fish community of [[Squaw Creek]], an unstable intermittent stream in central [[Iowa]], responds to repeated floods and droughts. Sampling covered 1953 to 1956 using seines, wire traps, an electric shocker and rotenone. The creek bed is mostly shifting sand, and during droughts it shrinks to isolated pools. Only the [[Bigmouth Shiner]] was abundant. Most species persisted by using refuges termed "[[Stream Havens]]": tributaries during floods, and pools, bordering [[Flood Plain Ponds]] and the [[Skunk River]] during droughts.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** [[Squaw Creek]] is about 40 miles long, drains 210 square miles, and is part of the [[Skunk River]] drainage. Its bed is composed mainly of shifting sands, with only a very small area of riffles or rubble.
- **Hydrological regime:** Floods usually occur in spring but may also follow heavy rain in summer or fall. Flow ceased when the gauge at Station 8 read below 19 inches; stages above 55 inches were classed as high water.
- **Drought and flood periods:** The stream was reduced to isolated pools from July 1953 to February 1954 and from October 5, 1955 to February 1956. High-water periods occurred in 1954 (April 30 to May 17, May 22 to 29, June 10 to 29, August 23 to 31) and 1955 (June 15 to 21, July 14 to 19).
- **Sampling methods:** A 20-foot common sense minnow seine (catch per haul as an abundance index), two types of wire traps (migration), a gasoline-powered electric shocker, and rotenone treatments of roughly 100-yard reaches bounded by block seines.
- **Species inventory:** The study recorded about 29 species across the Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae and Centrarchidae. Examples include [[Quillback]], [[White Sucker]], [[Carp]], [[Creek Chub]], [[Stoneroller]], [[Emerald Shiner]], [[Northern Common Shiner]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Johnny Darter]]. Several records are single or very few specimens, probably from stocking or flood-plain ponds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat instability:** Drought exposes productive areas and concentrates fish in remaining water. Floods displace adults and young, destroy spawn and reduce food supply.
- **Dissolved oxygen and ice:** In the winters of 1953–54 and 1955–56, dissolved oxygen was fully depleted in several isolated pools, killing fish by suffocation. Ice froze to the bottom of some pools.
- **Recolonization:** In March 1956, 55 seine hauls captured only four species (bigmouth shiner, red shiner, bluntnose minnow, creek chub) in low numbers. Connection with the Skunk River was restored on May 13, 1956. By June 2, 1956, 13 species were found in a roughly 100-yard section.
- **Migration barriers:** Low water blocks upstream fish movement (Larimore, 1952). Delayed reconnection in 1956 postponed the spring migration.
- **Spawning cues:** Temperature is usually the main spawning trigger, but unspawned creek chubs in early June 1956 suggested that suitable habitat also regulates spawning time.

## Key Findings

1. **Abundance pattern:** The [[Bigmouth Shiner]] was the most abundant and widest-ranging species, occurring in most seine hauls with catches usually over 50 per haul. The [[Red Shiner]] occurred in 71.8% of hauls but made up only 5.88% of the total catch. It was collected in large numbers during drought pool conditions, which suggests better tolerance of crowding.
2. **Abundance at low water:** Fish appeared more abundant during low-water periods (per haul), but this reflected concentration in a reduced habitat. Total population was much lower in droughts.
3. **Carp:** Carp were more abundant in the Skunk River and bordering ponds than in Squaw Creek. Scale analysis of 231 carp from Pond C showed that 193 bore slow-growth bands, probably from crowding as the pond shrank. Sixteen of 45 creek carp showed similar bands.
4. **Creek chub:** Upstream spawning migration began in late March. Males arrived first, then females by mid-April. Spent females were collected in late April 1955 at 58–60°F.
5. **Emerald shiner:** Numbers fell sharply after the stream became isolated pools and none were collected after August 1955. The species could not tolerate restricted habitat and concentrated fish.
6. **Common shiner:** Spawns from mid-April to late May in Squaw Creek. Traps caught many common shiners in spring, though seine catches were small.
7. **Bigmouth shiner life history:** Spawns in mid-July. Reaches about 1.4 inches by the end of year one and 2.4 inches by year two. No individuals over 3.2 inches were collected. Scale analysis indicated a two-year life span. The spring 1956 seine data showed a major population drop over the 1955–56 winter.
8. **Bluntnose minnow:** Absent in 1953 and until July 13, 1954, probably brought in from tributaries or ponds by the June 10–29 flood. Spawning extended from late May to late August, with young present in July–September.
9. **Fathead minnow:** Favored muddy, slow-flowing habitat, mostly found in Ponds C and D. Breeding extended from May through August. Of 1,698 fish measured in Pond C (November 1954), length distribution suggested one age class, while scale analysis showed two.
10. **Source populations:** Carp, bluntnose and fathead minnows are maintained largely by flood-plain ponds. Creek chubs, stonerollers, common shiners and suckers are maintained largely by migration from the Skunk River.
11. **Stream havens:** Survival depends on refuges. Tributaries serve during floods. Isolated channel pools, flood-plain ponds and the Skunk River serve during drought. Havens were not always safe, due to crowding, predation and suffocation.
12. **Overall conclusion:** Most species persisted despite drastic habitat change. Fish population changes were small compared with observed habitat changes, and only the bigmouth shiner was abundant.
