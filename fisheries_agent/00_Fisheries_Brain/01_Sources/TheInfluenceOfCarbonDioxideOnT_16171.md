# The Influence of Carbon Dioxide on the Toxicity of Un-ionized Ammonia to Rainbow Trout

## Executive Summary
This 1960 study by Lloyd and Herbert (Water Pollution Research Laboratory, Stevenage) investigates why the toxicity of un-ionized [[Ammonia]] to [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) varies across different dilution waters, despite equal concentrations of the toxic un-ionized ammonia molecule. The authors hypothesized that variation in **free carbon dioxide (CO₂)** concentration in the test water — not just [[Bicarbonate Alkalinity|carbonate alkalinity]] — was responsible. As fish respire, CO₂ excreted across the gill epithelium locally lowers the pH at the [[Gill Surface|gill surface]], reducing the concentration of un-ionized ammonia available for uptake relative to the bulk solution. This effect is proportionally greater in waters with initially low free CO₂, making ammonia less lethal in such waters and comparatively more lethal in high-CO₂ waters at a given bulk un-ionized ammonia concentration. The study combines controlled toxicity bioassays with a theoretical model of gill-surface pH depression, successfully reconciling laboratory findings with field data and earlier experiments using different dilution waters.

## Background & Description
- **Test organism:** Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdnerii* Richardson), average length 5.6 cm (juvenile/fingerling stage).
- No detailed external or internal morphological description of the fish is provided; the paper focuses instead on **gill physiological function**, specifically:
  - The **secondary lamellae** of the gills are identified as the site of CO₂ excretion, generating a pH gradient across the respiratory water film.
  - Gas exchange assumptions: salmonids absorb ~80% of dissolved oxygen from respiratory water ([[Van Dam 1938]]), and the respiratory quotient of trout on a protein-rich diet is assumed to be 0.8.
  - These parameters permit calculation of the increase in CO₂ concentration (mg/L) in water passing over the gill surface, approximated as 0.88 × the dissolved oxygen concentration of the ambient water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Freshwater; experiments conducted using untreated borehole water (bicarbonate alkalinity 250 p.p.m. as CaCO₃; total dissolved solids 420 p.p.m.) to simulate conditions relevant to polluted streams and effluent-receiving waters.
- **Environmental context:** The study is directly motivated by discrepancies between laboratory ammonia toxicity data and field observations of trout in a stream affected by [[Sewage Effluent]] pollution (Allan, Herbert & Alabaster, 1958), highlighting the ecological relevance of water chemistry (CO₂, alkalinity, pH, dissolved oxygen) in determining pollutant toxicity in situ.
- **Physicochemical interactions:** The research underscores that ecological toxicity thresholds for ammonia cannot be generalized across water bodies without accounting for site-specific free CO₂ and alkalinity, since these variables control the effective pH microenvironment at the respiratory surface, not merely the bulk water chemistry.
- Findings have broader implications for [[Water Pollution Research|water pollution regulation]] and the assessment of other pH-sensitive toxicants affecting fish physiology.

## Key Findings
- **Toxicity variation confirmed:** The toxicity of un-ionized ammonia to rainbow trout varies significantly with the free CO₂ concentration of the test solution (Fig. 1), even when un-ionized ammonia concentration is held constant.
- **Mechanism proposed:** CO₂ excreted at the gill surface lowers local pH more sharply in low-CO₂ waters than in high-CO₂ waters, thereby reducing the effective un-ionized ammonia concentration at the site of uptake in low-CO₂ conditions — explaining why ammonia appears less toxic in low free-CO₂ waters.
- **Quantitative gill-surface model:** Using assumed values (80% O₂ absorption, 0.8 respiratory quotient), the increase in CO₂ across the gill was estimated as 0.88 × dissolved O₂ concentration; average gill-surface pH was modeled as the pH after half of this CO₂ increase had occurred.
- **Table 1 results (500 min. TL₅₀ estimates at ~18.5–20.7°C):**

| pH (bulk) | Free CO₂ (p.p.m.) | Un-ionized NH₃ in bulk solution (p.p.m. N) | Estimated un-ionized NH₃ at gill surface (p.p.m. N) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8.2 | 3.2 | 0.84 | 0.39 |
| 7.8 | 7.7 | 0.62 | 0.42 |
| 7.37 | 21.5 | 0.42 | 0.36 |
| 7.0 | 48.0 | 0.49 | 0.45 |

  - The gill-surface un-ionized ammonia concentrations are **far more consistent (~0.36–0.45 p.p.m. N)** across treatments than the bulk-solution values, strongly supporting the validity of the gill-surface pH depression hypothesis.
- **pH depression independent of alkalinity:** The magnitude of pH decrease for a given increase in free CO₂ is constant regardless of water alkalinity (e.g., at 18.5°C, increasing CO₂ from 5→10 p.p.m. reduces pH by ~0.31 units whether alkalinity is 25 or 250 p.p.m. as CaCO₃).
- **Unifying toxic threshold:** Assuming a constant lethal gill-surface un-ionized ammonia concentration of **0.40 mg/L as N** for a 500 min TL₅₀, the authors constructed a theoretical curve (Fig. 2) relating free CO₂ concentration in bulk solution to the bulk un-ionized ammonia concentration required for this toxic endpoint.
- **Validation against independent data:** This theoretical curve matched both the present experimental results and earlier data from low-alkalinity waters ([[Downing and Merkens 1955]]; DSIR 1959), reinforcing the generality of the CO₂-mediated gill-surface pH mechanism.
- **Broader implication:** The approach for estimating gill-surface pH could be extended to evaluate the toxicity of other pH-dependent toxicants to fish, beyond ammonia alone.
- **Methodological notes:** Median survival times were determined via geometric means (log-normal distribution) when all ten test fish died, or graphically (per [[Bliss 1937]]) when only partial mortality occurred, reflecting a "biological truncation" effect where resistant individuals survived disproportionately longer.
