## Executive Summary

This document is a multi-agency **Conservation Agreement** (draft) for the [[Pecos Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon pecosensis*), a small cyprinodontid fish endemic to the [[Pecos River Basin]] in New Mexico and Texas. The agreement, signed among the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department, New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, New Mexico State Parks, U.S. Bureau of Land Management, and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, was formulated in response to the species' proposed listing as Endangered under the Endangered Species Act (January 30, 1998). The species has experienced a range contraction of more than 50% since 1980, primarily due to hybridization with the introduced [[Sheepshead Minnow]] (*Cyprinodon variegatus*), compounded by historic habitat degradation from damming, dewatering, and groundwater over-pumping. The agreement outlines specific management, monitoring, refugia establishment, and bait-fish regulation commitments by each signatory intended to alleviate threats sufficiently to avoid a final endangered listing.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide direct morphological descriptions (e.g., meristic counts, coloration, body shape) of the Pecos pupfish. Key taxonomic/genetic points noted include:

- **Species identity**: *Cyprinodon pecosensis*, distinct from the congener *Cyprinodon variegatus* (sheepshead minnow).
- **Hybridization susceptibility**: The two *Cyprinodon* species exhibit **little premating isolating mechanism**, readily hybridizing where sympatric (Cokendolpher 1980), resulting in a hybrid "swarm" that has morphologically and genetically replaced pure Pecos pupfish populations in portions of the mainstem river.
- **Genetic purity concerns**: Populations at Phipps Gravel Pits (Pecos County, TX) were found to be **genetically impure** (hybridized), while populations in the upper reaches of [[Salt Creek]] (Culberson and Reeves counties, TX) remain the only known genetically pure Texas populations (Wilde and Echelle 1992).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic range**: The Pecos River from near [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Bottomless Lakes State Park]] (near Roswell, NM) downstream ~650 km (404 mi) to the mouth of Independence Creek near Sheffield, Terrell County, Texas (Wilde and Echelle 1992).
- **Habitat types**:
  - Gypsum sinkholes and saline springs (Bitter Lake NWR, including Salt Creek Wilderness Area)
  - Sinkholes, springs, and spring runs (Bottomless Lakes State Park)
  - Salt Creek (Reeves County, TX)
  - Mainstem Pecos River (New Mexico segment, north of Loving upstream to Bitter Lake NWR)
  - Off-channel wetlands: [[Overflow Wetlands Wildlife Habitat Area]] (BLM), Laguna Grande, Phipps Gravel Pits
- **Current range (as of document)**: Reduced by more than half historic extent (Echelle and Connor 1989); pure populations now largely restricted to off-channel refugia and isolated New Mexico river reaches.
- **Threats**:
  1. **Hybridization** with the non-native sheepshead minnow, introduced near Pecos, Texas, between 1980–1984, likely via bait-bucket transfer; hybrid swarms have spread both upstream and downstream despite six irrigation diversion dams.
  2. **Habitat degradation** predating the hybridization crisis: damming, dewatering of the Pecos River, and excessive groundwater pumping causing spring and sinkhole desiccation (Minckley et al. 1991).
  3. **Algal bloom fish kills** (1985, 1986) in the Texas Pecos River (Rhodes and Hubbs 1992), which paradoxically could create future reintroduction opportunities by depopulating hybrid/sheepshead minnow-dominated reaches.
  4. **Nonnative species introductions**, including green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*) at Bottomless Lakes State Park sites.
- **Co-occurring/competing species**: [[Sheepshead Minnow]] (*Cyprinodon variegatus*), green sunfish, rainbow trout (managed as winter fishery only in select lakes), and various baitfish species (fathead minnow, red shiner, common carp, gizzard/threadfin shad, sunfish, golden shiner, goldfish) subject to new bait-use regulations.

## Key Findings

- **Range reduction**: Post-1980 range contraction exceeds 50% of historic distribution, driven primarily by hybridization, not habitat loss alone (Echelle and Connor 1989).
- **Hybridization dynamics**: By 1984, hybridization was documented at four sites below Red Bluff Reservoir, TX (Echelle 1985); near Pecos, TX, pure Pecos pupfish have been **completely replaced** by hybrids; hybrid influence detected from 50 km upstream to 250 km downstream of the introduction point.
- **Genetically pure populations remaining**: Only the upper reaches of Salt Creek (Culberson/Reeves counties, TX) confirmed pure in Texas; Phipps Gravel Pits population confirmed **not** genetically pure.
- **Refugia sites identified**: Three off-channel New Mexico sites (Bitter Lake NWR, Bottomless Lakes State Park, Laguna Grande) and two Texas sites (Phipps Gravel Pits, Salt Creek) considered critical for species security, pending viability assessment.
- **Management commitments** (selected):
  - USFWS: fund life-history research (ESA Section 6), construct fish barrier at Bitter Lake NWR South Weir (by Sept 30, 1998), annual pupfish monitoring across six impoundments, assess/introduce pupfish to Sinkhole W3, evaluate Dexter National Fish Hatchery wetlands as refugium.
  - NM Dept. of Game and Fish: regulate live bait to fathead minnow and red shiner only within historic pupfish range; prohibit live bait at Bitter Lake NWR and Bottomless Lakes SP.
  - TX Parks and Wildlife: assess Phipps Gravel Pits and Salt Creek populations; establish ≥4 new secure off-channel populations of >500 individuals each; initiate 1998 fish eradication/restocking at Lake Balmorhea to remove a key sheepshead minnow source.
  - BLM: implement Overflow Wetlands Area of Critical Environmental Concern management prescriptions (No Surface Occupancy oil/gas restrictions, mineral leasing closures on 1,040 acres, OHV closures, land acquisition of ~3,300 acres of riparian/wetland habitat).
- **Broader habitat rehabilitation goal**: Long-term commitment to converting invasive salt cedar (*Tamarix* sp.) stands to native vegetation within the Pecos River basin.
- **Regulatory context**: This agreement was explicitly designed to serve as evidence in USFWS's final listing determination — if conservation measures prove insufficient, signatories agreed to support formal ESA listing of the species.
- **Potential future reintroduction pathway**: Natural fish-kill events (algal blooms) or successful localized eradication of sheepshead minnow/hybrids could open windows for reintroducing pure Pecos pupfish into mainstem Pecos River habitat.
