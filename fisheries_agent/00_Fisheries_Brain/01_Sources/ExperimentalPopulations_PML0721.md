# Endangered Species Act Section 10(j) Experimental Populations and Enforcement Provisions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species-law

## Executive Summary

The document is a compilation of U.S. federal material on the Endangered Species Act of 1973 (ESA), as amended in 1982. It contains three components: (1) the statutory text of ESA sections on experimental populations (Section 10(j)), penalties and enforcement (Section 11), and authorization of appropriations (Section 15); (2) the Fish and Wildlife Service final rule published in the Federal Register of August 27, 1984, which establishes procedures for designating experimental populations under [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 10(j) and amends 50 CFR Part 17; and (3) the rule's preamble, including the agency's responses to public comments. The rule was signed by Assistant Secretary G. Ray Arnett on August 1, 1984, and took effect September 26, 1984. Its first listed example is the [[Marianas Fruit Bat]] (*Pteropus mariannus*), with several Western Pacific populations. The document is largely legal and regulatory; it contains no original field data on fish or aquatic ecosystems.

## Background & Description

Congress amended the ESA through Public Law 97-304 (October 13, 1982), creating Section 10(j) to allow the Secretary of the Interior to designate reintroduced populations of listed species as "experimental populations." Before the 1982 amendments, the Service could translocate listed species only under narrower authority. A population qualifies as experimental only if it is geographically separate from nonexperimental populations of the species.

Key statutory provisions summarized:

- **Section 10(j)(2)(A):** The Secretary may authorize release of any population (including eggs, propagules, or individuals) of an endangered or threatened species outside its current range if release will further conservation of the species.
- **Section 10(j)(2)(B):** Before release, the Secretary must identify the population by rulemaking and determine whether it is "essential" to the species' continued existence.
- **Section 10(j)(2)(C):** Members of experimental populations are treated as threatened species, except that non-essential populations outside the [[National Wildlife Refuge System]] or [[National Park System]] are treated as proposed species for Section 7 purposes.
- **Section 11 (Penalties):** Civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation for knowing violations by importers or exporters; criminal penalties up to $20,000 and/or one year imprisonment for knowing violations of core provisions. Good-faith defense exists where a person acted to protect themselves or others from bodily harm from an endangered or threatened species.
- **Section 15 (Authorization):** Appropriations were authorized for fiscal years 1984 through 1986 (amounts given in the OCR text are partly illegible).

The 1984 rule establishes a new subpart H of 50 CFR Part 17 with four sections: 17.80 (definitions), 17.81 (listing and special rules), 17.82 (prohibitions), and 17.83 (interagency cooperation). Special rules for vertebrates, invertebrates, and plants (17.84–17.86) were reserved.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The rule's listing table includes the Marianas fruit bat and several other species, mostly in the Western Pacific (Guam, Rota, Tinian, Saipan). Entries specify the status of experimental populations as essential ("X_E") or nonessential ("X_N"). Aquatic and marine species are not individually discussed in the text available, though the regulations cover fish and wildlife generally.

The preamble addresses the ecological rationale for experimental populations:

- Translocation is generally inappropriate outside historic range because it may subject populations to "doubtful survival chances" and alter gene pools.
- Releasing species into non-native habitat conflicts with Executive Order 11987, which prohibits introduction of exotic species into U.S. ecosystems.
- Habitat enhancement that causes natural range expansion is not eligible for Section 10(j) treatment.
- Fish stocking as traditionally practiced by the Service is not covered by the rule. Stocking to augment existing populations is considered a separate activity.
- Commenters from several Colorado River water agencies expressed concern about introducing experimental fish populations into rivers with natural populations; the Service said such cases would be reviewed carefully.

## Key Findings

- **Essential vs. nonessential status:** The rule defines an "essential experimental population" as one whose loss would be likely to appreciably reduce the likelihood of survival of the species in the wild. The final rule adopted "would be likely to" in place of earlier wording, following public comment. The Service declined to narrow the definition to "imminent danger of extinction."
- **Critical habitat:** Critical habitat may be designated only for essential experimental populations, and not for nonessential ones.
- **Regulatory protections:** Experimental populations are treated as threatened species for protective-regulation purposes under Section 4(d). Special rules must specify prohibitions and exceptions.
- **Public participation:** The Service rejected several requests for a formal Environmental Impact Statement and concluded that an environmental assessment was adequate. It did not adopt additional procedural requirements such as a 60-day public meeting before proposal.
- **Interagency cooperation:** Nonessential populations outside refuges and parks are treated as proposed species under Section 7. Essential populations and those inside refuges or parks are treated as threatened species. Biological opinions must consider experimental and nonexperimental populations together as a single listed species.
- **Consultation with states:** The rule encourages cooperation with state wildlife agencies and affected Federal land managers. Memoranda of Understanding can support recovery but cannot substitute for the rulemaking required to designate an experimental population.
- **Historic range limit:** The Service generally will not authorize translocation of listed species outside historic range, except in extreme cases where the primary habitat has been unsuitably and irreversibly altered or destroyed.
- **Scope of comments:** Responses addressed roughly two dozen commenters. Among them, Colorado River and Northern Colorado water districts, the National Forest Products Association, Conoco Inc., and the Marine Mammal Commission each raised concerns about scope, economic impact, or essentiality.
