# Hydrodynamic and Fluvial Morphologic Processes: Implications for Fisheries Management and Research

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This synthesis by [[Heede]] and [[Rinne]] (North American Journal of Fisheries Management, 1990) argues that fisheries science and hydrology have developed separately, and that stream habitat for fish is dictated by the interaction of streamflow, sediment transport, and channel morphology. The authors show how hydrodynamic and fluvial morphologic variables shape habitat quantity and quality for different fish life stages, and they warn that habitat enhancement projects fail when the [[Dynamic Equilibrium]] state of a channel is ignored. The paper is a conceptual review with examples from the southwestern USA, Rocky Mountains, Pacific Northwest and Midwest, rather than a primary data study.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** Most North American rivers and streams are no longer in a natural state. Human activity, including dessication, siltation, bank erosion, logging, and grazing, has reduced riverine habitat, particularly in the southwestern USA ([[Hastings]] and [[Turner]] 1965; [[Minckley]] 1973).
- **Native fishes at risk:** The Gila and Salt rivers and their tributaries once supported over 20 native fish species (Minckley 1973). [[Gila Trout]] and [[Spikedace]] are highlighted as threatened or endangered examples.
- **Management gap:** Habitat improvement projects, especially for [[Salmonids]], were often not based on physical parameters, and their benefits have frequently not been verified (Platts and Rinne 1985).
- **Purpose:** The paper aims to bridge fishery, hydrologic and hydrodynamic disciplines so that managers and researchers include hydrologic parameters in their work.
- **Prior frameworks:** [[Ziemer]] (1971) linked watershed geomorphology to fish habitat, and [[Platts]] (1979) related stream order to fish populations in the Idaho Batholith.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Large woody debris and riparian links**
- Logging "cleanups" disrupt streamside forest–stream interactions that create habitat complexity; [[Weaver]] (1983) estimated logged areas received 25–35 cm less annual precipitation in Pacific Northwest mountains due to lost fog drip (the source text's figure).
- Large woody debris, especially stems and root wads, is a preferred cover type for salmonids (Bisson et al. 1981; Bryant 1981, 1985).
- [[Angemeier]] and Karr (1984) found a positive relationship between fish diversity and abundance and woody debris in a small Midwestern warmwater stream.

**Sediment and water temperature**
- Viscosity is inversely related to temperature. [[Colby]] (1964) found that cooling a flume from 26.7 to 4.4 °C increased sediment discharge for 0.125–0.250 mm sand by 2540%.
- [[Alexander]] and Hansen (1986) reported a 50% population reduction of [[Brook Trout]] in a Michigan stream when bedload was artificially increased four to five times.
- [[Rinne]] and Medina (1989) recorded a significant inverse relationship between salmonid population and substrate fine content in first-order headwater streams in central Arizona.
- [[Reiser]] and White (1988) reported reduced egg survival as fines (<4.6 mm) increased in artificial stream habitat.

**Flow and velocity**
- Low-pressure (velocity) areas behind boulders and obstructions create resting, feeding and spawning sites. Rinne (unpublished) noted three- to fivefold increases in a native cyprinid in New Mexico habitat around 1 m boulders placed as improvement structures.
- [[Bisson]] et al. (1988) found juvenile salmonid habitat use was dictated by fish morphology and channel hydraulics.

**Channel and bed processes**
- [[Knickpoints]] indicate channel instability and can propagate upstream, causing bank undercutting and widening.
- [[Pools]] and riffles tend to occur at intervals of five to seven times channel width. Rinne (1978) found pool structure and quality, not linear pool-riffle ratio, limited [[Gila Trout]] populations in New Mexico headwaters.
- In the South Fork Salmon River, Idaho, habitat deteriorated after 15 years of logging and road-building and 10 years of flooding, then improved after a logging moratorium (Platts and Megahan 1975; Megahan et al. 1980).

## Key Findings

1. **Equilibrium state controls project success.** Neglecting whether a channel is in dynamic equilibrium or disequilibrium raises the probability of enhancement failure. Structures should not be installed in reaches with shooting (supercritical) flows.
2. **Water viscosity and temperature matter.** Viscosity influences sediment transport, and cooler, denser spring waters reduce fine sediment loading of spawning gravels.
3. **Velocity and flow structure shape habitat.** Pools, riffles, runs and glides depend on subcritical flow energy; zones behind obstructions are key resting and spawning sites.
4. **Hydraulic indices are recommended.** The authors encourage the Froude number (Fr = v/√(gD)) and Reynolds number (Re) to objectively quantify flow conditions. A workable flow-resistance coefficient for gravel-bed streams is still needed.
5. **Sediment overload drives morphologic change.** In [[Redwood Creek]], California, a 1964 flood left deposits 1.5 times those found in 1947, widening the channel, reducing depth and pool area, and increasing scour susceptibility (Madej 1984).
6. **Natural recovery can outpace intervention.** Following a moratorium, the South Fork Salmon River's sand content fell dramatically and gravel-cobble substrate increased. Working with natural processes is typically less costly and yields faster success.
7. **Log steps outperform gravel bars.** In small mountain streams, log steps created deeper pools at waterfalls than gravel bars. Removing all log steps led to increased bedload movement and new transverse gravel bars replacing about 14% of former steps after 5 years (Heede 1985).
8. **Artificial structures often fail through poor design.** Structures in McKnight Creek, New Mexico, were placed at right angles to banks, whereas natural large woody material lies at about 30° or less to banks. Bilby (1984) found initial stormflows moved 60% of remaining debris after artificial cleaning.
9. **Base level and knickpoints govern bank stability.** Neglecting knickpoints downstream of a planned enhancement can cause failure through upstream undercutting. Gradient-control structures (e.g., submerged or fence dams with aprons) can stop headcutting, though dams are undesirable where they affect fisheries.
10. **Life-stage dependence.** Whether a habitat change is beneficial or detrimental depends on the fish life stage. More hydraulic and geomorphologic factors considered, the greater the information content and reliability of results.
11. **Core recommendation.** Habitat characteristics should be modified only if (1) the causes of an undesirable condition are known and (2) the modifications are compatible with future stream development. Including hydrodynamic and fluvial morphologic variables gives more precise habitat quantification.
