# Life History of the Banded Pygmy Sunfish (Elassoma zonatum) in Western Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study describes the life history of the [[Elassoma Zonatum]] (banded pygmy sunfish) in a cypress swamp on the floodplain of [[Mayfield Creek]], western Kentucky. The authors collected 555 specimens on 16 dates between 9 March 1979 and 11 October 1980, and kept mature fish from this site and from [[Wolf Lake]], Illinois, in aquaria to observe spawning and larval development. The species is short-lived: most individuals die after their first spawning season, and the maximum recorded age is 25 months. Males defend territories during a spring breeding season, and females lay adhesive eggs on submerged vegetation. The diet is dominated by small crustaceans, and parasite infection is common. The study was undertaken partly to provide comparative data on geographic variation in life history, since the site lies near the northern edge of the species' range.

## Background & Description

*Elassoma* contains three described species and at least three undescribed forms. All are small (maximum length under 50 mm), cryptic fishes of the southern and southeastern United States that favor vegetated, sluggish waters. Earlier work on the genus was mostly taxonomic or based on aquarium observations, especially for [[Elassoma Evergladei]], so ecological data for *E. zonatum* were scarce. Earlier studies include [[Barney and Anson]] (1920) on mosquito control in Louisiana and [[Gunning and Lewis]] (1955) on habitat, diet and predation in southern Illinois.

The species has a broad coastal-plain distribution, from North Carolina to central peninsular Florida, west to Texas and Oklahoma, and north to southern Illinois. Populations above the Fall Line are relict and occur in the Wabash, Tradewater and Green River drainages. It was probably extirpated from the Wabash River. Its salinity tolerance is disputed: one source listed it as possibly marine, while [[Bailey et Al.]] (1954) considered it intolerant of saline water.

Adults are olivaceous to bronze with 8–11 dark vertical bands on each side. Breeding males turn dark blue to black with pale to light-blue background coloration and iridescent speckles. Females show no notable breeding colour change. Males and females differ only in a few measurements (pelvic and anal fin lengths, vent to pelvic base length), and external sexing is reliable only during breeding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The site is an extensive bald cypress (*Taxodium distichum*) swamp on the north shore of Mayfield Creek, a channelized, silt-laden tributary of the [[Mississippi River]]. The area is disturbed by road construction, nearby businesses, debris dumping and runoff, and has high turbidity year-round. Water depth in most areas is 0.2–1.0 m, with one pool at least 1.8 m deep. Water temperatures range from 5–7 °C in December and January to 32 °C in August. The swamp supports at least 25 fish species. The most abundant are [[Lepomis Symmetricus]] (25.3%), [[Lepomis Macrochirus]] (19.1%), and [[Lepomis Gulosus]] (18.0%), with [[Aphredoderus Sayanus]] at 11.0%. *E. zonatum* occurred alone only in shallow (15–30 cm), clear, well-vegetated areas during spring and early summer.

**Habitat use.** The species occupies lentic and sluggish waters with protective cover such as logs, stumps, cypress knees, overhanging banks and dense vegetation, typically over soft substrates. The dominant plants include coontail, milfoil, duckweed, smartweed and filamentous algae.

**Population density.** Estimates from enclosed sampling areas ranged from 3.8 fish/m² in October to 30.4 fish/m² in June, with 11.5 fish/m² in February. The June area was a nursery, where young-of-the-year made up 96.7% of the *E. zonatum* collected. The authors attribute the October low to summer mortality, water-level changes, dispersal, or sampling inconsistency.

**Predation and parasites.** Only one predation event was documented: a 21.8 mm SL female found in the stomach of an 86.8 mm SL [[Lepomis Gulosus]] in February 1980, out of 13 Lepomis gulosus and about 60 other potential predators examined. Parasite prevalence was high. Of 265 specimens examined, 79.2% carried endoparasites, and no ectoparasites were found. The most common parasite was an encysted trematode larva, found in 64.9% of fish.

## Key Findings

**Growth and longevity**
- Maximum length was 32.3 mm SL; the largest individuals were 23-month-old males (mean 31.6 mm SL). The maximum lifespan was 25 months, but most fish did not survive beyond one year.
- Length growth decelerated: half of the first year's mean growth was reached in about eight weeks. Body weight grew linearly, with half of first-year growth reached in about 5.5 months.
- One-year-olds averaged 22.9 mm SL and 316.3 mg adjusted body weight.
- The length–age regression for both sexes was SL = 7.36 + 14.94 log A (r = 0.921; N = 555). Males and females did not differ significantly in growth rate.
- Length and weight were highly correlated (log W = −1.44 + 2.87 log SL; r = 0.987; N = 269).

**Population structure and survival**
- Of 555 specimens, 94.9% were up to one year old, 4.7% were 1–2 years old, and 0.4% were older than two years.
- The overall sex ratio (males:females) was 1:0.98 among 514 sexed fish, not significantly different from 1:1.
- Adult survival is very low after the first spawning season. The authors suggest reproductive energy cost and high spawning-season mortality are responsible. Large fluctuations in density are offset by high reproductive output.

**Reproduction**
- Males were in breeding condition from late February to mid-June. Testes reached functional maturity at 10–12 months of age, and spermatogenesis peaked from late March to June.
- Ovaries grew from August to May, peaking in late March and April. Gravid females' ovaries averaged 14.1% of adjusted body weight (range 10.2–19.6%; N = 28).
- Mature and intermediate oocytes numbered 43–255 per female (mean 144.9; N = 16). The total count of oocytes above 0.40 mm ranged from 350 to 797 (mean 537.1).
- Spawning peaked from late March through April and was observed in captive fish from late March to 12 April 1981. Clutches averaged 37.9 eggs (range 6–76; N = 11), and females spawned repeatedly, with clutch size declining (63, 15, then 6 eggs over four days in one case).
- No nest was built. Spawning occurred in or above submerged vegetation. The demersal, adhesive eggs were guarded by the male for up to 48 hours.
- Polyandry was observed on five occasions, with two males spawning with the same female. Earlier work had recorded only one case.
- Breeding ceased in mid-to-late May 1980, when water temperature reached about 27 °C.

**Development**
- Embryos hatched in 97–116 hours at 21 ± 1 °C. Newly hatched protolarvae measured 3.0–3.6 mm TL (mean 3.23 mm; N = 26), with heartbeats of 114–143 beats per minute.
- Yolk was nearly or completely resorbed by days 6–7, when larvae began feeding on *Artemia* nauplii. Mortality was high beyond the first week, attributed to starvation and saline toxicity from *Artemia* feeding.

**Diet and feeding**
- Diet was over 99% small crustaceans, mollusks and aquatic insects across 362 stomachs. Cyclopoid copepods (36.2%) and cladocerans (39.4%) were most common, followed by ostracods (10.2%), chironomid and ceratopogonid larvae (3.9%), and isopods (3.1%).
- Feeding was diurnal, with 80–100% of stomachs containing food between 0800 and 2000 hr. Peak feeding occurred at 0800 and 1200 hr, and the species feeds mainly on moving prey.
- Diet composition varied seasonally, with ostracods most common from November to March and immature mayflies most common from June to October.

**Taxonomic and distributional notes**
- The authors recognise a northern boundary population near the Kentucky site. Southern populations reach larger adult sizes (up to 38.5 mm SL, and 46.6 mm TL in one Louisiana specimen) because of earlier spawning and longer growing seasons.
