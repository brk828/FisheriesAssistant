# Mitochondrial DNA Variation and Evolution of the Death Valley Pupfishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This study uses [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) [[Restriction Site Analysis]] to test the evolutionary history of the [[Death Valley Pupfish]] group, the *Cyprinodon nevadensis* complex of desert fishes from [[Ash Meadows]], [[Death Valley System]] and [[Owens Valley]]. The authors examined 26 samples representing 16 composite mtDNA haplotypes, drawn from 9 nominal taxa plus outgroups, and reconstructed phylogenies with [[Maximum Parsimony]] and [[UPGMA]] clustering. The results contradict the traditional view that the *C. nevadensis* complex is monophyletic. The authors instead favor a scenario in which the Death Valley System was colonized twice by pupfishes carrying divergent mtDNA lineages.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** The genus *Cyprinodon* in the American Southwest, with emphasis on the *C. nevadensis* complex (Death Valley System) and its relatives, including the *C. macularius*, *C. eximius*, *C. variegatus* and Guzman Basin groups.
- **Prior hypothesis:** [[Miller Hypothesis|Miller (1981)]] proposed that the *C. nevadensis* complex is monophyletic, which would make it a single invasion lineage.
- **Study focus:** Whether mtDNA relationships support that monophyly, and what the Death Valley pupfishes' history implies about their colonization routes and timing.
- **Taxa and specimens:** 26 samples from 22 localities, with specimens deposited in the Oklahoma State University Collection of Vertebrates (OSUS). Sample numbers are keyed to localities in Appendix 1 and Figures 1 and 2.
- **Key taxa:** *C. radiosus* (Owens Valley), *C. salinus* (salinus and milleri), *C. nevadensis* (with five named subspecies), *C. diabolis* (Devils Hole), *C. macularius* (two subspecies), *C. fontinalis*, the undescribed "Palomas" and "whitefin" pupfishes of the [[Guzman Basin]], and outgroups *C. eximius*, *C. meeki* and *C. variegatus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Desert spring systems and marshes. *C. diabolis* lives in [[Devils Hole]], an open fault cavern where it occupies a small sunlit rock shelf. The cavern was apparently not open to the surface until about 50,000 years ago.
- **Population size:** *C. diabolis* often numbers fewer than 200 adults, which the authors suggest may have facilitated rapid evolution.
- **Geology and dispersal routes:** The authors consider several water connections between drainages:
  - [[Lower Colorado River]] and Owens River–Death Valley connections (Miocene and Pliocene).
  - Guzman System–[[Gila River]] connections (Plio-Pleistocene).
  - The [[Garlock Fault]] as a dispersal route into Owens Valley and Death Valley.
  - A Late Pleistocene "Pluvial Las Vegas River" connection to Ash Meadows.
  - Desiccation of [[Lake Manly]] as a possible isolating event.
- **Reproductive compatibility:** Prior work shows high reproductive compatibility among morphologically divergent pupfishes, which the authors cite to support introgression and mtDNA replacement.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Mapping used 107 restriction sites in total. Of these, 22 were present in all samples (about 22%), 38 were unique to particular haplotypes, and 47 were cladistically informative.
- Divergence was estimated from the proportion of shared sites (Nei et al., 1985) and clustered by UPGMA.
- Parsimony analysis (PAUP, Swofford) was run with outgroup rooting. Node support came from 1,000 [[Bootstrap Analysis]] replicates (Felsenstein, 1985).
- Alternative trees were compared with [[Templeton Test]] at alpha = 0.10, which the authors chose because the test is conservative.

**Sequence divergence**
- Intraspecific divergence was below 0.6% (all estimates under 0.650%).
- Interspecific divergence was generally much greater, with two exceptions:
  - *C. diabolis* versus *C. nevadensis* subspecies (0.32–0.49%).
  - Palomas versus whitefin pupfish (0.16%).
- The *C. nevadensis* mtDNAs differed by 0–2 sites, except *C. n. mionectes*, which differed by 3–5 sites from other members of the species.
- *C. meeki* was among the most divergent taxa, clustering with *C. variegatus* at a relatively high divergence level.
- *C. radiosus* differed by 3.2–3.9% from the *C. nevadensis* complex, and by 2.7–2.8% from *C. macularius*.
- *C. salinus* was the most divergent member of the Ash Meadows–Death Valley cluster, at 1.2–1.3%.
- *C. macularius* subspecies differed by 0.34%.

**Phylogenetic results**
- The analysis yielded 54 maximum parsimony trees of length 80 (consistency index 0.562), which collapsed to two trees once trichotomies were considered. An additional 135 trees of length 81 resolved to five.
- Two trees of length 80 were significantly better than four of the length-81 trees (0.10 > P > 0.05).
- The strict consensus tree (Figure 5) shows three key features:
  1. *C. diabolis* mtDNA nests inside the *C. nevadensis* subspecies clade.
  2. *C. radiosus* is the sister taxon of *C. macularius*.
  3. The sister group of the Ash Meadows–Death Valley species (*C. diabolis*, *C. nevadensis*, *C. salinus*) is *C. fontinalis* from the distant Guzman Basin. This last feature appears only in the three trees underlying Figure 5.
- The *C. nevadensis* complex is therefore not monophyletic according to mtDNA.

**Interpretation and conclusions**
- The authors reject the single-invasion model and favor two separate invasions of the Death Valley System. Their reasoning is that morphological similarity between *C. radiosus* and *C. macularius* does not match the mtDNA lineage distribution, and that the geology supports two water connections.
- Alternative explanations (genetic introgression after a second invasion, or ancestral mtDNA polymorphism followed by lineage sorting) are considered but judged less likely.
- Timing of divergence:
  - *C. diabolis* and *C. nevadensis*: less than about 50,000 years, given the virtual lack of mtDNA differentiation. This is inconsistent with an earlier estimate of 10,000–20,000 years for *C. diabolis*.
  - *C. salinus*: more recent, possibly around 12,000 years, following desiccation of Lake Manly.
  - *C. macularius* subspecies: possibly 13,000–17,000 years, since the Pinacate lava flows separated the Rio Sonoyta drainage. Protein electrophoresis (Nei's I = 0.96) supports this low divergence.
- The two whitefin populations are united as one species by a white dorsal fin in breeding males. Their low mtDNA divergence suggests recent exchange across the Mexican highlands.
- The authors note that the Guzman Basin species are poorly sampled, and that past introgression may have blurred the mtDNA picture for the Palomas and whitefin pupfishes.
