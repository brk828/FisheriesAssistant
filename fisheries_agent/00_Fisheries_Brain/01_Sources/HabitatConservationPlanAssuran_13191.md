# Habitat Conservation Plan Assurances ("No Surprises") Rule

#regulatory-policy #endangered-species-act #conservation-planning #federal-register

## Executive Summary

This final rule, published February 23, 1998, codifies the Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP) assurances provided through section 10(a)(1)(B) permits issued under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). The "No Surprises" policy, first announced in 1994 by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS), provides regulatory and economic certainty to non-Federal landowners who develop HCPs. The rule ensures that permittees implementing approved HCPs in good faith will not be required to commit additional land, water, or financial compensation beyond the agreed-upon level if unforeseen circumstances arise, even if additional mitigation is needed for covered species. The Services retain responsibility for addressing unforeseen circumstances through their own authorities and resources. This policy aims to incentivize private landowners to factor endangered species conservation into land management activities by offering long-term regulatory certainty in exchange for conservation commitments.

## Background & Description

Congress enacted section 10(a) amendments to the ESA in 1982 to address concerns of private landowners facing section 9 "take" prohibitions. The amendments allow the Services to issue permits authorizing incidental take of listed species during otherwise lawful activities, provided activities follow an approved conservation plan that minimizes and mitigates take and avoids jeopardy. Congress recognized that long-term HCP permits (often spanning decades and involving millions of dollars) required "adequate assurances" to the development community to encourage participation. The 1982 Conference Report explicitly stated the Secretary should provide "long-term assurances to the proponent of the conservation plan that the terms of the plan will be adhered to and that further mitigation requirements will only be imposed in accordance with the terms of the plan."

The Services developed the No Surprises policy based on this Congressional direction and a decade of HCP experience. The policy was included in the draft [[HCP Handbook]] (December 1994) and final Handbook (December 1996), with public comment opportunities. Following litigation in *Spirit of the Council v. Babbitt*, the Services agreed to codify the policy through formal rulemaking. The proposed rule was published May 29, 1997, receiving over 800 comments from Federal, State, County, and Tribal agencies, industry, conservation groups, and private individuals.

Key definitions established in the final rule include: **"adequately covered"** species (those satisfying section 10(a)(2)(B) issuance criteria); **"properly implemented conservation plan"** (commitments and provisions fully implemented, permittee in compliance); **"changed circumstances"** (reasonably anticipated changes that can be planned for); **"unforeseen circumstances"** (changes that could not reasonably have been anticipated and result in substantial adverse change to covered species status); **"conserved habitat areas"** (areas designated for restoration, acquisition, protection); and **"operating conservation program"** (conservation management activities expressly described in the HCP).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The rule addresses the reality that more than half of ESA-listed species have 80 percent of their habitat on non-Federal lands. Prior to the No Surprises policy, only a handful of HCPs had been developed since 1982, while thousands of acres of species habitat disappeared annually. The policy was designed to rechannel uncontrolled habitat loss through the regulatory structure of section 10(a)(1)(B) by offering regulatory certainty in exchange for long-term conservation commitments.

HCPs provide multiple ecological benefits: conservation of lands and waters upon which species depend, decreased habitat fragmentation, removal of threats to candidate or unlisted species, and advancement of listed species recovery. Large-scale regional HCPs enable ecosystem planning, providing benefits to more species than small-scale plans while allowing efficient analysis of cumulative effects. The rule encourages incorporation of [[Adaptive Management]] provisions in HCPs with significant biological data gaps, allowing adjustments in operating conservation programs based on monitoring results without increasing permittee costs.

The Services retain extensive authorities to provide additional species protection beyond HCP terms, including land acquisition or exchange under section 5 of the ESA, cooperation with States under section 6, habitat restoration and enhancement, translocation, and other management techniques. Federal lands managed by the Department of the Interior can be utilized for listed species protection. Subsequent section 7 consultations and section 10 permits must account for existing HCPs and species status. Section 9 prohibitions against unauthorized take by other landowners provide additional protection.

## Key Findings

**Assurances Framework**: The final rule provides one level of assurances (eliminating the proposed two-tier system) to all permittees with approved HCPs that are properly implemented. Assurances apply only to species adequately covered by the conservation plan and specifically listed on the section 10(a)(1)(B) permit. Assurances last for the permit duration and do not apply to Federal agencies, which retain continuing conservation obligations under section 7(a)(1).

**Changed Circumstances**: For changed circumstances provided for in the HCP's operating conservation program, permittees must implement specified measures. For changed circumstances not provided for, the Services will not require additional conservation measures without permittee consent, provided the plan is properly implemented. All reasonably foreseeable circumstances, including natural catastrophes normally occurring in the area, should be addressed in the HCP.

**Unforeseen Circumstances**: The Services will not require commitment of additional land, water, financial compensation, or additional restrictions on natural resource use beyond the agreed-upon level without permittee consent. If additional measures are deemed necessary, they must be limited to modifications within conserved habitat areas or to the operating conservation program, maintaining original terms to the maximum extent possible. The Services bear the burden of demonstrating unforeseen circumstances exist using best scientific and commercial data, considering factors including: size of current species range; percentage of range affected and conserved by the HCP; ecological significance of affected range; knowledge level about the species; and whether failure to adopt additional measures would appreciably reduce survival and recovery likelihood.

**Funding and Implementation**: Section 10(a)(2)(B)(iii) requires applicants to ensure adequate funding for plan implementation. The Services issue permits only when concluding the operating conservation program will be adequately funded. If major HCP components (like funding strategies) are never initiated, No Surprises assurances lapse. For HCPs relying on proposed bonds, reevaluation mechanisms allow species coverage adjustment if funding is not secured within specified timeframes.

**Monitoring and Adaptive Management**: Monitoring is required under existing regulations (50 CFR 17.22(b)(1), 17.32(b)(1), 222.22) and should be properly designed to address reasonably foreseeable changes. When significant biological data gaps exist, adaptive management becomes integral to the HCP, allowing strategy adjustments based on monitoring results without increasing permittee costs. The Services committed to strengthening adaptive management provisions in future HCP Handbook revisions.

**Public Oversight**: The rule does not eliminate public comment periods for permit decisions or prevent citizens from challenging inadequately issued permits or bringing compliance issues to the Services' attention. All Service decision documents remain part of the Administrative Record and available to the public. The Services or designated parties monitor projects for permit and HCP compliance, with periodic reporting required from permittees.

**Permit Shield Provision**: After review of legal authorities and public comment, the Services removed the proposed legally binding permit-shield provision from the final rule. However, the Services maintain a policy of not enforcing section 9 prohibitions against permittees in full compliance with permit terms and conditions. The current statutory and regulatory framework already provides permittees certainty regarding legal obligations when acting within permit authorization.

**Multispecies HCPs**: Each species in a multispecies HCP must adequately address section 10(a)(2)(B) issuance criteria. While "guilding" or habitat-based approaches may be appropriate for preserve design, each species receiving assurances must be specifically named in the HCP with adequate conservation measures included. Third-party beneficiary mechanisms (e.g., certificates of inclusion) are supported for large-scale regional HCPs, provided the overarching permit holder has legal authority to enforce terms and funding mechanisms.

**Unlisted Species Coverage**: Unlisted species are "adequately covered" only if treated as if listed under section 4 and the HCP meets section 10(a)(2)(B) issuance criteria. This standard encourages landowners to conserve unlisted species and provides certainty that subsequent listing will not trigger additional requirements beyond HCP terms. Species (listed or unlisted) will not be included without adequate data unless data gaps can be overcome through adaptive management.

**Regulatory Determinations**: The rule imposes no additional economic costs or information collection requirements on non-Federal entities beyond existing HCP permitting processes. The Department of the Interior certified the rule will not have significant economic impact on a substantial number of small entities. Implementation requires no additional data beyond existing HCP requirements. Benefits are shared between the Services (less habitat fragmentation, habitat management, species protection) and landowners (regulatory certainty for future economic land uses).
