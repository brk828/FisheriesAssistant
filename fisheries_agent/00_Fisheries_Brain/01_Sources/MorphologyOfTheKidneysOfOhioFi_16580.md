# Morphology of the Kidneys of Ohio Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1933 monograph, Contribution Number 5 from the [[Franz Theodore Stone Laboratory]] of The Ohio State University, by [[Robert A. Moore]] (Western Reserve University), reports the gross and microscopic structure of the kidneys of ninety-one species of freshwater fishes collected in the summer of 1930. The study's primary aim was to locate a freshwater aglomerular fish (one lacking glomeruli) for further morphological and physiological work; the search was unsuccessful. The paper also reviews the anatomical and physiological literature on [[Teleost Kidney]] types, particularly the glomerular versus aglomerular condition, and compares excretion of nitrogen, salts, carbohydrates and dyes across marine species.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with the rationale that functional kidney studies in lower vertebrates (frog, mudpuppy) must rest on sound anatomy, and that [[Teleostean Kidney]] structure had been restudied only recently by [[Marshall and Smith]] and [[Edwards]]. The fish were examined at the Stone Laboratory with identifications made by Dr. R. C. Osburn, and classification followed [[Gregory]] (1905), with Ohio species following [[Osburn]] (1930).

The literature review distinguishes:

- **Aglomerular kidneys**: first described by [[Huot]] in lophobranchs (genera *Syngnathus*, *Siphonostoma*, *Entelurus*, *Nerophis*, *Hippocampus*). About twenty-five teleost species were then known to have an aglomerular mesonephros, including *Lophius piscatorius*, *Opsanus tau* and *Lepadogaster gouanii*.
- **Glomerular kidneys**: the majority of teleosts, with glomeruli of varying size and number.

Tubule epithelium was also contested. [[Edwards]] described three tubule segments (proximal, distal, collecting) in aglomerular kidneys, whereas [[Marshall]] found only one epithelial type in *Opsanus tau*, and the author concluded that no general scheme could yet be drawn.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Physiological data reviewed concern mainly marine teleosts. Urine flow is strongly stimulus-dependent: freshly caught fish showed two to three times more urinary nitrogen than captive fish (Grollman). [[Smith]] concluded that normal urine flow in marine fish should be as low as 5 per cent, possibly 1 per cent, of that in freshwater fish. Marine teleosts swallow seawater and excrete monovalent salts through the gills in hypertonic solution, while the kidney produces isotonic or hypotonic urine that removes divalent ions. Freshwater fish instead absorb water through oral membranes. Excretion of carbohydrates (glucose, phlorizin-induced glycosuria, xylose) and dyes (ferrocyanide, phenolsulphonephthalein) was reduced in aglomerular kidneys.

The author notes that the Ohio fishes studied were mostly freshwater species, so the marine comparisons are indirect, and that the gross kidney shape depends largely on the size and position of the [[Air Bladder]].

## Key Findings

**Glomerular development and distribution**

- Ninety-one freshwater species were examined; most are Teleostei.
- All species examined, except possibly *Dorosoma cepedianum* (Gizzard shad), have good glomerular development.
- The gizzard shad had few glomeruli, confined mostly to the kidney periphery, and small in size (about 39 to 57 microns in diameter minimum to maximum). Its placement in Class III of the Marshall and Smith scheme is proposed. The author notes that its herbivorous diet cannot explain the morphology, since [[Common Carp]] and *Campostoma anomalum* share that diet but have large glomeruli.
- Glomerular diameters across the studied species generally ranged from about 70 to 130 microns, with no apparent relation to fish size.
- Table I gives minimum, maximum and average glomerular diameters for 23 species or species groups. For example, *Amia calva* averaged 93.0 microns, *Hiodon tergisus* 70.3 microns, *Notropis atherinoides* 52.9 microns, and *Ambloplites rupestris* 60.2 microns.
- Each capillary loop of a glomerulus averages 10 to 20 per glomerulus.

**Unusual glomerular forms**

- *Micropterus dolomieu* (Largemouth Bass): a count of one kidney from a three-year-old specimen gave 4,279 glomeruli (2,156 right, 2,123 left). Large composite glomeruli over 1 mm in diameter with more than 20 lobules occur, from which 4 to 20 tubules arise. These have a distinct ciliated neck segment about 0.3 mm long.
- *Perca flavescens* (Yellow Perch): double glomeruli arising from a single vascular pole.
- *Umbra limi* (Central Mudminnow): one giant glomerulus divided into more than 15 lobules.
- *Cottus bairdii bairdii* (Mottled Sculpin): counts of 515 glomeruli in a 62 mm specimen and 422 in a 69 mm specimen.

**Kidney gross anatomy and vasculature**

- Kidneys lie dorsal to the body cavity, usually separate in the cephalic half to two-thirds and fused across the midline caudally.
- Segmental arteries from the dorsal aorta supply the kidney in all species examined; the caudal vein continues as the right posterior cardinal vein.
- Morphological evidence for a left-sided renal portal circulation was found in Percidae and Esocidae; whether it is physiologically functional remains undetermined.
- Ameiuridae (catfishes) concentrate kidney tissue in a caudal mass. Catostomidae (suckers) have kidney shapes that follow the hourglass [[Air Bladder]].

**Tubule structure**

- In most species the tubule is lined by a single epithelial type resembling mammalian proximal convoluted tubule, often with a brush border.
- A distal convoluted segment with basophilic cuboidal cells is present in a few species.
- The author concluded that the absence of a [[Loop of Henle]] in teleosts is linked to inability to excrete hypertonic urine.

**Evolutionary conclusions**

- Teleost kidneys are independent derivatives of the vertebrate stem, appearing in the Mesozoic after the Devonian origin of Amphibia from ganoid fish.
- The aglomerular condition is interpreted as a loss from a primitive state (Lophius retrogression) or possibly a secondary development, with embryological studies recommended.

**Summary of results (author's numbered conclusions)**

1. Gross and microscopic structure of 91 freshwater species was studied.
2. Kidney shape depends largely on the size and position of other viscera, especially the air bladder.
3. Ducts, arteries and veins conform to a type pattern with minor variations.
4. Good glomerular development occurs in all species except possibly *Dorosoma cepedianum*.
5. Multiple composite glomeruli occur in Perca flavescens and Micropterus dolomieu; giant single glomeruli occur in Umbra limi.
6. Tubular epithelium is generally of one cell type; exceptions are a distal convolution in some species and a neck segment in Micropterus dolomieu.
7. Teleost mesonephric peculiarities represent a separate evolutionary line.
