# Habitat Selection of Spawning Razorback Suckers Observed in Arizona Bay, Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation study, conducted on February 16-17, 1982, examined the spawning habitat of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endemic [[Colorado River Basin]] sucker that has persisted in [[Lake Mohave]] after the closure of [[Davis Dam]] in the early 1950s. A portion (690 sq m) of a spawning area in [[Arizona Bay]] was mapped using survey techniques and [[SCUBA Diving]] observations. Substrate size appears to be a critical factor in spawning site selection, and all observed spawning sites lacked fines. In a reservoir without flushing river currents, wave action combined with fluctuating lake elevations may maintain substrate suitability.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a river-oriented fish that has apparently adapted to reservoir conditions. Very little is known about its ecology or habitat requirements. Habitat modifications and competition from non-native species have reduced native Colorado River fishes, and Lake Mohave supports the last remnant population of [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) as well as the largest concentration of razorback suckers. Propagation efforts for both species are underway for reintroduction.

Spawning has been documented in Lake Mohave, but spawning habitat requirements remained poorly understood. Large numbers gather between January and April at Arizona Bay, [[El Dorado Canyon]], [[Cottonwood Cove]], [[Six Mile Cove]], and other locations. The study was intended to inform impact assessments of proposed Bureau projects and mitigation options.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site:** Arizona Bay was chosen for its high spawning numbers. It lies on the Arizona side, upstream of the Cottonwood Marina, and is adjacent to a backwater isolated from the lake by a gravel bar formed by wave action. The backwater was fish-free during the study and was completely isolated from the lake at lake elevations reached in June.

**Geomorphology:** Alluvial material from the Yuma Wash formed a shallow cove with a lake bottom slope of about 10 degrees. Near the wash mouth, bottom substrate was predominantly fine material at depths greater than 12 feet, while shallower areas ranged from particle sizes up to large cobble. Wave action at different lake elevations created bands of differing substrate size, roughly 2 to 4 meters wide, parallel to shore. Shoreline substrate was largely large gravel and pebbles, with gravel bands mixed with coarse and fine sand offshore.

**Sediment and hydrology:** Without flushing river currents or seasonal runoff, wave action and lake level fluctuation appear to reduce fine sediments in shallow areas, producing conditions resembling native riverine habitat.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Behavioral observations used snorkeling, bank observation, underwater video, and 35mm photography.
- The mapped area measured approximately 105 by 70 feet. Mapping used plane tables and alidades on a 150-foot baseline, with two scuba divers swimming transects and marking redds with numbered wire flags.
- Substrate samples were taken from inside nests, outside nests, and outside the spawning area, then washed in a borax solution and sieved.

**Spawning behavior**
- About three hours of video were edited into a 20-minute presentation showing large numbers of fish moving through the redd area, with spawning activity occurring.
- Spawning groups of 3 to 5 fish formed in the shallows or over redds. Females were flanked by two or more males; spawning convulsions lasted 2 to 5 seconds, and the entire act took 1 to 2 minutes.
- Plankton tows confirmed the presence of razorback larvae. Nighttime observations suggested spawner or possible predator activity.

**Redd mapping**
- A total of 63 redds were recorded, 45 of which were inside the mapped area. Redd depth, size, substrate, and egg presence were recorded.
- Redds were concentrated at the wash mouth over large gravel to cobble, relatively free of fines.
- Spawning was observed to depths of 9 feet, with redds found at depths up to 3 feet along the shallows and deeper nests extending to 26.3 feet.
- Eggs were found in several redds, but the number of eggs was never large; fewer than 5 eggs were typical per inspected nest.

**Nest density**
- Redds covered about 9 percent of the total area (excluding shoreline areas shallower than 1.3 feet). High-use areas in the 2.0 to 3.5 foot zone had densities of about 20 percent, with densities of 9.7 and 13.5 percent in deeper zones.
- Extrapolation suggested about 34 redds and an additional 30.3 square yards in the spawning zone, giving a total density of about 12.0 percent.

**Conclusions**
- Spawning occurs annually in specific areas used repeatedly, with the population in Lake Mohave possibly numbering several hundred thousand (Paulson, personal communication).
- Razorbacks spawn over substrate of large gravel to cobble that is generally free of fines. Repeated spawning cleans the substrate, and the coarsest material is retained in heavily used redds.
- Although spawning occurs at depths greater than 10 feet, most spawning occurs in shallow water and decreases with depth.
- Redd density depends on substrate suitability, and sediment loading, depth, and substrate size may be important factors in site selection.
- Artificial spawning habitat could be built to mimic depth and substrate size, but reducing sediment load would be more difficult and costly to duplicate.
- Further quantitative study is needed to determine razorback spawning effort along shallow shorelines.
