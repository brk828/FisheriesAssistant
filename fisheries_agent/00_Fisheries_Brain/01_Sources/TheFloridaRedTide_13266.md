# The Florida Red Tide

#ichthyology #marine-biology #dinoflagellate

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service staff publication (Bureau of Commercial Fisheries Biological Laboratory, Galveston, Texas) reviews the [[Florida Red Tide]], a recurring fish-killing phenomenon on Florida's Gulf coast. The 1946–47 outbreak, which followed a roughly 30-year absence, killed several hundred million pounds of food and game fishes. Research traced the mortality to the marine dinoflagellate [[Gymnodinium Breve]], which was shown to be the primary toxic agent. The text also summarizes the history of outbreaks, control trials, laboratory culture breakthroughs, and the environmental conditions believed to trigger blooms.

## Background & Description

- **Name and nature:** Local observers called these events "poison water," "black water," "yellow water," and "rotten water." The term "Red Tide" is a misnomer, since no tide is involved and the water is often green, yellow, or brown rather than red.
- **Recorded history:** Outbreaks were recorded in Florida in 1844, 1854, 1878, 1880, 1882, 1883, 1908, and 1916, followed by the 1946–47 event.
- **Symptoms:** Water could appear oily and slimy, and fish died quickly after entering patches. An odorless gas caused coughing, eye irritation, and throat and nose burning, especially when waves broke and winds blew from the water.
- **Victims:** Nearly all fish species were affected, including tarpon and jewfish. Most oysters in affected areas died, and horseshoe crabs died by the thousands. True crabs and sponges were apparently unharmed.
- **Organism:** A minute (under one thousandth of an inch) single-celled marine flagellate in the dinoflagellate group, classed in the genus *Gymnodinium*. It moves in an irregular spiral using two flagella and contains chlorophyll.
- **Global occurrences:** Similar red-water events were reported in Chile (Darwin, 1832), Port Jackson, Australia (1891), Japan (24 outbreaks, 1899–1934), California (1902–1935), the Malabar coast of India, Washington and British Columbia, and Narragansett Bay, Rhode Island.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Mass mortality:** In the 1946–47 event, dead fish washed ashore at more than 100 lb per linear foot of shoreline near Fort Myers. Some species, such as mackerel, do not float when dead, so offshore losses were likely larger than beach counts.
- **Triggering conditions:** Blooms appear to require a shift from offshore to onshore winds after abnormally heavy rainfall and land drainage. This traps nutrient-rich water near shore. Decaying fish may release further nutrients that intensify the bloom.
- **Rainfall link:** Dense *G. breve* populations occurred only in years when rainfall was well above normal.
- **Physiology:** In culture the organism depends on light for energy, which indicates a plant-like nutrition method. It is a tropical marine flagellate, adversely affected by brackish or fresh water and by sudden temperature drops.
- **Economic impact:** Losses included interrupted sport and commercial fishing, damage to resort centers, and lower real estate values. Dollar figures were not determined.

## Key Findings

- **Cause identified:** Early theories blaming Lewisite gas from war munitions were rejected because the only recorded disposal was off Mobile, about 300 miles away, in autumn 1947, almost a year after the bloom began.
- **Pure cultures are fatal:** Research at Galveston established *G. breve* as the primary toxic agent. Pure cultures proved fatal to fish within short periods.
- **Laboratory culture:** Earlier culture attempts at Galveston, Haskins, and Beaufort failed. Artificial cultivation succeeded in 1953 and became a key research tool.
- **Defined medium:** A fully synthetic medium supports growth. Several vitamins and trace metals were found essential, and an organic nitrogen requirement was suggested.
- **Copper sulfate trials:** Three small trials in 1952–54 and a large 1959 application (105 tons, St. Petersburg Beach to Tarpon Springs) reduced or eliminated the organism for only 1 to 2 weeks. Cost, short duration, and harm to other marine life made the method inadvisable.
- **Chemical screening:** About 5,000 compounds had been tested by the time of writing. Several were more toxic per unit weight to *G. breve* than copper sulfate.
- **Monitoring program:** Field stations at Sarasota (1948–52), Fort Myers (1953–55), Naples (1955–57), and St. Petersburg Beach (1957 onward) tracked weather, tides, water samples, and aerial surveys.
- **Open question:** Whether the Florida Red Tide can be stopped remained unanswered. The text argues that the research would also benefit other industries and fields.
