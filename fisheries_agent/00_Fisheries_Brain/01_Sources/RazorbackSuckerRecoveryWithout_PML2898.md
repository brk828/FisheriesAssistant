# Razorback Sucker Recovery Without Listing: 1981 Correspondence on Transplants, Hatcheries, and Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a July 1981 letter from an ichthyologist (signature partly garbled by OCR, rendered as "E. Williams, Ph.D.") to Bill Loudermilk of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], enclosing an earlier letter to Jim Johnson of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. Both letters respond to the Service's proposal to "recover the [[Razorback Sucker]] without listing it" under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The author accepts the pragmatic appeal of transplanting and hatchery-based recovery but argues that habitat protection must come first. A numbered list of seven questions accompanies the letter, asking whether the proposal is biologically and legally sound. The author's core position is that habitat, not only individual populations, should drive recovery.

## Background & Description

The correspondence centers on two fish groups:

- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large catostomid of the [[Colorado River]] system. The proposal would stock hatchery-propagated fish into the lower basin.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*), a small endangered cyprinodontid of California. The author uses it as the main example of transplant-based management and specifically mentions the **[[San Felipe Creek Pupfish]]** stock.

The letter also refers to the **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) through an interagency memorandum of understanding, described as a comparable "transplant" approach.

The author contrasts two management philosophies:
- The **traditional approach**: protect the species in its native habitat.
- The **newer approach**: intensive management and transplantation, covered by memoranda among agencies.

The author states that the Service's acceptance of the newer approach reflects frustration with a past record that "often falls short of our goals."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat loss is the central concern.** The author warns that managing a species while its native habitat is destroyed "has not protected the species." Transplanted populations would require periodic restocking.
- **Refugia are a partial solution.** The author argues that 30 transplanted populations in refugia are "better than no populations at all," but still favors protecting the natural habitat.
- **Natural selection matters.** The author notes that a species can change "drastically over the period of a few generations" in artificial environments, so maintaining natural genetic selection is "vital."
- **Upper and lower basins.** The letter treats the upper Colorado River populations as "possibly the only viable populations remaining." The concern is that stocking the lower basin could divert attention from upper-basin problems.
- **Exotic species.** The lower Colorado River system is described as already "worked over" with "dozens of exotic species," raising doubts about whether the system can support a new population.
- **Hatchery genetics.** The proposal would stock hatchery-propagated fish from "broodstock," and the author questions whether genetic diversity can be preserved across hatchery generations.

## Key Findings

**1. Habitat must precede species management.** The author states that a recovery program should "include both habitat and species recovery," and that San Felipe Creek, the habitat that naturally shaped the pupfish, should remain the main focus. A refuge population at Palm Canyon is acceptable as a supplement.

**2. Seven unresolved questions accompany the proposal.** Summarized:
1. Does stocking the lower basin threaten the upper-basin populations, which may be the only viable ones?
2. Is it biologically feasible to place large numbers of razorback suckers in habitats where they cannot reproduce successfully?
3. Can lower-basin habitat be restored to support natural reproduction and selection?
4. Can lower Colorado River systems support new populations given the dozens of exotic species already present?
5. Can hatchery stocks be maintained without loss of genetic diversity over successive generations?
6. Is the Service establishing a formal policy for recovering endangered species, and is hatchery propagation the right method?
7. Does the proposal satisfy the Service's legal obligations under Section 2 of the Endangered Species Act, which states the Act's purposes?

**3. Questions should be answered before stocking.** The author writes that these questions "must be addressed before any stocking program is undertaken," and that the recovery of the razorback sucker ultimately depends on securing it in the upper river.

**4. Broader consultation is needed.** Because the action may affect the species throughout its range, the author calls for consultation with additional parties, including the States of [[Utah]] and [[Colorado]], the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]], and the [[Desert Fishes Council]].

**5. Continued debate is endorsed.** The author concludes by calling for "continued debate over this issue" and for close scrutiny of transplant-based recovery efforts.
