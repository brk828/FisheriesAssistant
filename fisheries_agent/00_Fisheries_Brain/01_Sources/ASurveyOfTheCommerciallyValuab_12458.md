# Commercially Valuable Mussels of the Wabash and White Rivers, Indiana

#malacology #freshwater-biology #unionidae

## Executive Summary

This survey assessed the commercially valuable freshwater mussels (family [[Unionidae]]) of the [[Wabash River]], [[White River]], and [[East Fork of the White River]] in Indiana. Shells from these mussels are cut into nuclei for the [[Cultured Pearl Industry]] in Japan. The Indiana Department of Natural Resources commissioned the study, carried out by the University of Louisville in 1966 and 1967, covering about 500 stream miles. Of 30 unionid species collected, 10 were commercially important, and together they made up 77.1% of the total catch. The most abundant commercial species was the [[Quadrula Quadrula]] (mapleleaf). Survey and market data showed serious depletion of stocks in 1966, largely due to diver-assisted harvesting. This led to [[Discretionary Order No. 136]], which banned mechanical dredges and diving with auxiliary air for commercial musseling. Because the mussels grow slowly, yields of marketable shells were expected to stay low at least until 1970.

## Background & Description

Unionid mussels have existed since the early Mesozoic and include roughly 500 to 600 living species in the continental United States. Although their soft parts are edible, no freshwater clam market comparable to the marine clam market developed. Shell value has peaked twice in U.S. history. The [[Pearl Button Industry]] flourished at Muscatine, Iowa, for about 50 years until it essentially disappeared in 1953. The cultured pearl industry then took over demand. Shells from the Wabash and Tennessee river systems are especially translucent and prized for nuclei, which are spherical beads, usually ¼ to ¾ inch in diameter, implanted in oysters. Pearls take 1 to 5 years to form.

Mussels were collected by [[Crowfoot Bar]] (a towed iron-pipe bar with hooks), by [[Handpicking]], and by [[Scuba Diving]] with auxiliary air. Sampling covered 99 collections at 63 sites, with one mile of each 10-mile stretch sampled intensively. Sex was determined by inserting a hypodermic needle through the incurrent siphon to sample gonadal tissue, and the method was judged to have no deleterious effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors attribute the decline of unionids mainly to two factors: overexploitation for commercial purposes and deteriorating habitat from pollution. Pollution, whether industrial, domestic, or agricultural, was described as a leading cause of mussel decline in most Indiana streams. Earlier work cited in the paper, including studies by [[Ortmann]] and [[Baker]], documented mussel elimination from polluted rivers, and recovery downstream after pollution abatement.

Mechanical dredging was considered the most destructive method, since it lifts about the uppermost 2 feet of streambed. Crowfoot bars disturb only small areas to a depth of a few inches, and handpicking is the least destructive.

The [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*) has spread widely through Indiana streams. It does not produce [[Glochidia]] and so needs no fish host, giving it a competitive advantage. By late 1969, many sections of the Wabash and White rivers were heavily infested.

Unionid larvae (glochidia) are obligate temporary parasites of fishes. If they do not find a host within a few days, they die. Survival is low when adult populations are dense or host fishes are scarce.

## Key Findings

**Survey scope and catch composition**
- 99 collections, at 63 sites, covering about 500 stream miles, yielded 30 unionid species. Only 10 were of commercial value.
- The 10 commercial species made up 77.1% of the total catch. The top three were *Quadrula quadrula* (34.0%), *Obovaria olivaria* (10.2%), and *Quadrula pustulosa* (9.0%), together 53.2%.
- Of the 20 species in the main abundance table, 15 were represented by more than 25 specimens.
- The paper concludes that the 10 most sought-after species are also the 10 most abundant in the aggregate.

**Distribution and trends**
- Most species have restricted ranges and lower abundance than in earlier studies. Only *Q. pustulosa* and *Q. quadrula* were as abundant as or more abundant than earlier reports.
- *Elliptio dilatatus* and *Quadrula cylindrica* appeared only as dead shells and were likely extirpated within the last two decades.
- *Tritogonia verrucosa* (pistolgrip) is limited mostly to the Wabash upstream of Vincennes, where it made up 3.7% of the catch in 1966.
- *Quadrula quadrula* and *Obovaria olivaria* were the only species found in more than half of the Wabash collections. *Quadrula quadrula* was the only commercial species found in over half of all collections.

**Gear efficiency**
- Handpicking yielded 22.4 mussels per collection versus 17.4 for the crowfoot bar. Crowfoot collections took about 2 men more than 2 hours, while handpicking took 2 men under 30 minutes, so handpicking was judged more than four times as efficient on time alone.
- In a 10 ft × 170 ft plot near Americus, the crowfoot bar took 8 mussels (8.4 lbs). Two divers then took 220 legal-size mussels, 201 of them commercial, weighing 138.8 lbs for the commercial share.
- One instance was recorded in which 3 divers with auxiliary air took more than 3000 lbs of commercially valuable mussels from one bed in under 2 days.

**Population decline, 1966 to 1967**
- Upper Wabash sampling (10 one-mile sections) found 297 specimens of 21 species in 1966, versus 56 specimens of 11 species in 1967. This is an 81% drop in numbers and a 48% drop in species diversity.
- Commercial species taken rose from 7 in 1966 to 23 in 1967. Because 1967 specimens were concentrated in one collection, the authors describe the population decline as spectacular.

**Market data**
- Indiana shells sold for Japanese pearl production: 2000 tons (1965), 4200 tons (1966), 1080 tons (1967), and no more than 250 tons (1968).
- In 1969, commercial musselers reported many small mussels in the Wabash. Most proved to be Asiatic clams.

**Growth and regulation**
- Mapleleaf and threeridge mussels need 4 to 5 years to reach the legal size of 2.5 inches.
- Discretionary Order No. 136 (April 1967) limited commercial harvest to handpicking, short forks, tongs, and brail bar, and prohibited mechanical dredges and diving with auxiliary air.
- Removing all legal-size adults also impairs reproductive capacity, and recovery may take another 4 to 5 years.

**Reproduction (369 gonads from 15 species)**
- *Quadrula quadrula* was studied in detail: 167 specimens (78 male, 89 female).
- Mature *Quadrula quadrula* are larviparous. Glochidia form 2 to 4 weeks after fertilization.
- Subfamily Unioninae species are short-term breeders, usually gravid between April and August. Anodontinae and Lampsilinae are long-term breeders, with fertilization in mid to late summer and embryos carried until spring.
- *Actinonaias carinata* (21 specimens) was in breeding condition from late July to mid-October, indicating a long-term breeder. *Obovaria olivaria* (39 specimens) showed similar patterns.
- *Amblema costata* breeds from early May to early July, *Megalonaias gigantea* from early June through August, and the three studied *Quadrula* species through May, June, and July.
- *Tritogonia verrucosa* appears to be a long-term breeder, though the authors note more data are needed. It is also the only species showing sexual dimorphism: females have long posterior valve extensions.

**Conclusions**
- Stocks were seriously depleted in 1966, and the authors expected low marketable yields until at least 1970, and perhaps longer.
- Protecting breeding stock, controlling pollution, and limiting the spread of the Asiatic clam are needed for recovery.
