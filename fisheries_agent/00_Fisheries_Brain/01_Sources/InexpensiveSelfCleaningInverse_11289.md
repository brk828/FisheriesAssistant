## Executive Summary

This 1960 technical note, published in *The Progressive Fish-Culturist*, describes the design, construction, and operation of an inexpensive, self-cleaning **inverse aquarium filter** intended for use by aquarists, hatchery attendants, and experimental biologists. Unlike conventional gravity-fed filters that trap debris atop filter media (glass wool, charcoal, sand) and require frequent disassembly for cleaning, this design reverses water flow — pushing water **upward** through the filtrant — so that heavier debris settles into a sloped collection chamber and can be flushed out via a drain valve without dismantling the unit. The filter is built from low-cost materials (Plexiglas or plywood) and is scalable for use in aquaria up to 100 gallons or larger hatchery systems. While the paper does not address fish morphology, ecology, or wild habitat directly, it is highly relevant to captive fish husbandry, [[Aquaculture]], and [[Fish Hatchery Management]].

## Background & Description

*Not applicable in the traditional biological sense — this source concerns aquarium equipment, not fish anatomy.* However, the physical/engineering "morphology" of the filter itself is described in detail:

- **Overall dimensions:** 12 in (long) × 6-1/4 in (wide) × 5-1/2 in (high)
- **Filter compartment (Z):** 3-7/8 × 6 × 9-3/8 in, filter volume ≈ 3,000 cm³
- **Panel C:** solid divider panel (4 × 6 in)
- **Panel D:** perforated "filter bed" panel with 759 holes (33 rows × 23 holes), each hole 1/8 in diameter spaced 1/4 in apart (6 × 9-1/2 in)
- **Floor (E):** slanted at a 7° angle (6 × 11-13/16 in) to direct debris toward a flushout opening
- **Flushout opening (O):** fitted with a No. 7 rubber stopper and molly anchor screw for periodic drainage
- **Total water capacity:** ~1-1/8 gallons (4,258 cm³) filled to within 1/2 in of the top
- **Operating filter capacity:** ~0.324 gallon (1,226 cm³), depending on filtrant volume

Construction materials: 1/8-inch Plexiglas (cost ~$5.49 total) or 1/4-inch plywood (cost ~$1.24 total), assembled using Weldwood Contact Cement, lath nails/brads, and asphaltum waterproofing for wood surfaces. Comparable commercial filters of the era cost $12–$25.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This section is reframed to reflect the **captive/artificial ecosystem context** described in the source, given the absence of wild habitat or species-specific ecological data:

- **Intended environment:** Closed-system aquaria (up to 40 gallons standard design; scalable to 100 gallons with a 2-inch width increase per 20 gallons) and hatchery troughs.
- **Function within the system:** Removes silt, decaying organic matter, and debris from water, mimicking natural filtration processes that maintain water quality for captive fish — a critical factor in [[Water Quality Management]] and prevention of disease in confined fish populations.
- **Applications noted by the author:**
  - Standard aquarium filtration for hobbyists and researchers.
  - Hatchery use as a **spawning chamber** — compartment Z (without filtrant) could hold spawning fish, with enlarged holes in panel D allowing eggs or newly hatched fry to escape into a protected lower chamber, functioning similarly to a [[Hatchery Jar]] or fry-sorting device.
  - Sorting of fingerlings by size using variable hole dimensions.
- **Relevance to fish culture practice:** Reduces labor and maintenance costs associated with traditional gravity filters, indirectly supporting fish health and survival in hatchery and experimental settings by maintaining cleaner water with less operational disruption.

## Key Findings

- The **inverse (upflow) filtration principle** — passing water upward through filtrant rather than downward — allows heavier debris to settle by gravity into a separate collection area rather than clogging the filter media, enabling **self-cleaning** operation.
- A **7° sloped floor** and a narrow gap (Y) beneath the filter bed create a current that sweeps both heavy and light debris toward a single flushout point (O), simplifying maintenance to occasional flushing rather than full teardown.
- The filter is **significantly cheaper** to build than commercial equivalents: $1.24 (plywood) to $5.49 (Plexiglas) versus $12–$25 for comparable commercial units.
- Design is **modular and scalable**: suggested modifications include spillway lips, sliding-door cleanout access, spigots at the flushout point, and fully removable filter compartments for rapid servicing — particularly valuable in time-constrained hatchery operations.
- Beyond filtration, the apparatus has **dual-use potential** in aquaculture: as a spawning enclosure, egg/fry separator, or fingerling sorting device, broadening its utility beyond simple water purification.
- This design represents an early, low-cost engineering solution aimed at reducing labor in [[Fish Hatchery Management]] and small-scale aquarium maintenance, reflecting practical innovation in mid-20th-century fish culture technology.
