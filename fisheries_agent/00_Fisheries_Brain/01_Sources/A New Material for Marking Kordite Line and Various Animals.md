# A New Material for Marking Kordite Line and Various Animals

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #herpetology #malacology #entomology #field-methods #[[Kinosternon]] #[[Crayfish]] #[[Amphipoda]]

## Executive Summary

This 1962 methodological note by John G. Weise (Arizona State University) describes an inexpensive, accidental discovery of a marking tool for use in [[limnology|limnological]] field research. The tool—a felt ink pencil marketed by Carter's Ink Company (Cambridge, MA)—was originally intended for general marking purposes but was found to be highly effective, durable, and versatile for marking both **equipment** (Kordite clothesline) and a wide range of **live aquatic animals**. The paper is not a taxonomic or morphological study but a practical contribution to field research methodology, particularly valuable for [[mark-recapture studies]], population dynamics, and migratory tracking of aquatic organisms.

## Background & Description

- **Marking medium**: Felt-tip ink pencil, ~3 inches long, ~¼ inch diameter, waterproof cap, available in 8 colors.
- **Substrate compatibility observed**:
  - **Kordite line / polyethylene**: Dye "strikes in immediately," remains visible after 4+ months of continuous use.
  - **Glassware**: Successfully marked.
  - **Chitinous exoskeletons** (crayfish, amphipods): Successful marking with no noted degradation.
  - **Hard-shelled organisms** (turtles - [[Kinosternon sonoriense]], molluscs): Marking persisted with "no sign of wear or loss of stain" after over one month of aquatic immersion.
  - **Scaled vertebrates**: Noted as suitable, implying applicability to [[fish marking]] and tagging studies, though no specific fish species trials are detailed in this excerpt.
  - **Plant tissue**: Also functional as a marking medium.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This paper does not describe a specific ecological niche of a single species but rather addresses a **cross-taxon field methodology** applicable broadly across freshwater and terrestrial-aquatic interface ecosystems. The technique is positioned as a tool for:

- **Population studies** of aquatic and semi-aquatic organisms
- **Migratory movement tracking** of marked individuals
- Studies involving **chitinous invertebrates** (e.g., [[Crayfish]], [[Amphipoda]])
- Studies involving **hard-shelled vertebrates and invertebrates** (e.g., [[Kinosternon sonoriense|Sonoran mud turtle]], molluscs)
- Broader application to **scaled aquatic vertebrates**, implicitly including [[fish]], suggesting utility in ichthyological mark-recapture research

The described technique represents a low-cost, low-tech advancement in field ecology equipment marking, applicable within freshwater [[limnology]] and general aquatic ecosystem research programs.

## Key Findings

- A commercially available felt ink pencil (Carter's Ink Company), costing **59 cents**, was found to be an effective and inexpensive marking tool for both field equipment and live animals.
- **Kordite line durability**: Dye marks remained visible after **4 months** of active field use, with immediate ink absorption ("strikes in immediately").
- **Animal marking durability**: Turtles (*[[Kinosternon sonoriense]]*) marked and kept in water for **over one month** showed no wear or stain loss.
- **Broad taxonomic applicability** confirmed successful marking across:
  - [[Hemiptera]] (true bugs)
  - [[Crayfish]]
  - [[Mollusca]]
  - [[Kinosternon sonoriense]] (turtles)
  - [[Amphipoda]]
  - Plant material
- The pencil is marketed in **8 different colors**, enabling multi-cohort or multi-individual distinguishing in population studies.
- The author suggests strong potential utility for **ichthyological tagging studies** on "various scaled vertebrates," extending the tool's relevance to fish population and migratory research, though this application was not directly tested in the reported experiments.
- All experimental trials across the tested taxa were reported as **successful**, with no failures or negative results noted.
