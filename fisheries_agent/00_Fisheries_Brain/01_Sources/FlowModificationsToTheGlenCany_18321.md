## Executive Summary

This 1995 report evaluates proposed modifications to the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) "Interim Flows" regime, specifically (1) increasing the up-ramp rate from 2,500 cfs/hr to 4,000 cfs/hr, and (2) increasing the maximum discharge from 20,000 cfs to 25,000 cfs. While the document is fundamentally a hydrological and geomorphological review (focused on sediment transport, beach erosion, and dam operations), it contains embedded ecological findings on the response of the [[Lees Ferry]] trout fishery and native fish habitat to the 1991 Interim Flow regime. The central fish-relevant finding is that stabilization of sediment and flow fluctuations improved [[Rainbow Trout]] recruitment and condition at Lees Ferry, while simultaneously causing the loss of backwater habitats historically used by native Colorado River fish species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report situates fish populations within a highly engineered lotic (flowing-water) ecosystem downstream of [[Glen Canyon Dam]], structured by controlled discharge parameters:

- **Flow regime (Interim Flows, established August 1991):**
  - Maximum release: 8,000 cfs (7am–7pm)
  - Minimum release: 5,000–6,000 cfs (day) / 5,000 cfs (night)
  - Allowable daily fluctuation: 5,000–8,000 cfs (volume-dependent)
  - Ramp rates: 2,500 cfs/hr upramp; 1,500 cfs/hr downramp

- **Trophic/habitat linkages:**
  - Expansion of [[Cladophora]] algal beds provided increased primary production/structural habitat in the tailwater fishery.
  - Substantial increases in the amphipod *[[Gammarus lacustris]]* in the Lees Ferry drift represent an enhanced invertebrate food base, plausibly driving improved trout recruitment and condition.
  - Backwater habitats — historically important nursery/rearing habitat for **native Colorado River fish** — were reported to be "filling in with sediment," progressively eliminating this habitat type under the stabilized flow regime. This represents a documented trade-off: sediment storage and beach stabilization benefits versus loss of native fish backwater habitat.

- **Physical habitat drivers:**
  - Sediment trapped in the main channel and backwaters under the capped 20,000 cfs regime.
  - Beach/sandbar erosion slowed and stabilized, indirectly protecting riparian and marginal aquatic habitat structure.
  - Downramp rate (not upramp rate) identified as the primary control on beach/sandbar erosion (Budhu & Gobin, 1995), which has downstream implications for maintaining stable nearshore aquatic habitat structure.

- **Spatial context:** The critical reach of concern for cumulative flow/sediment/habitat effects was identified as River Mile 1 to River Mile 61, encompassing the confluence with the [[Little Colorado River]] — a key spawning area for native fish (e.g., humpback chub) in the broader Grand Canyon ecosystem, though not explicitly named as such in this text.

## Key Findings

- **Trout fishery response (Lees Ferry):** Since implementation of Interim Flows in August 1991, the trout population "has begun to rebuild," with increased numbers of naturally produced (wild) fish and improved fish condition factors — attributed to more stable, conservative flow operations.
- **Food base increase:** *Gammarus lacustris* densities increased substantially in the Lees Ferry drift, alongside expansion of Cladophora beds, suggesting a bottom-up improvement in the invertebrate/algal food web supporting the trout fishery.
- **Native fish habitat loss:** Backwaters — important habitat for native fish — are "in the process of filling in" with trapped sediment, effectively eliminating this habitat type as a direct consequence of the sediment-storage objective of the Interim Flows.
- **Sediment vs. flow trade-off:** The 20,000 cfs cap successfully trapped channel sediment but did not fully halt beach erosion in critical reaches (RM 1–61); tributary debris flows were, in some cases, more impactful to beaches than dam operations.
- **Proposed flow changes assessed as low-risk:** Based on hydrologic and geomorphic modeling (Smith & Wiele 1994; Nelson et al. 1993; Budhu & Gobin 1995), neither the upramp rate increase (to 4,000 cfs/hr) nor the maximum flow increase (to 25,000 cfs) was expected to substantially increase sediment loss or beach erosion, provided downramp rate (1,500 cfs/hr) and daily fluctuation limits (5,000–8,000 cfs) remained unchanged.
- **Need for future high-flow management:** Despite short-term sediment storage benefits, a future controlled high-flow release was identified as necessary to redistribute stored channel sediment back onto beaches — an action with likely secondary implications for backwater/native fish habitat restoration.
- **Recommendation on experimental design:** Scientists recommended not changing both flow parameters (ramp rate and maximum flow) simultaneously, to allow clear attribution of any observed ecological or geomorphic effects — an implicit call for controlled monitoring of biological responses (including fish) to each variable independently.
- **Monitoring directive:** Both proposed changes were recommended for inclusion in an ongoing "transition monitoring program," with reversal of operations if scientific evidence later indicated negative impacts on the ecosystem, implicitly including fish and aquatic habitat resources.
