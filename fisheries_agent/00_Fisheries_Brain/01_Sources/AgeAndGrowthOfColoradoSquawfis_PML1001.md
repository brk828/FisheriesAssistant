# Age and Growth of Colorado Squawfish from the Upper Colorado River Basin, 1978-1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This completion report by John A. Hawkins (Larval Fish Laboratory, Colorado State University), prepared for the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under contract 60181-0-1431, examines the age, growth, and condition of [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), an endangered cyprinid of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It synthesizes tagging records from 1978 to 1989, uses [[Scale Ageing]] with back-calculation, and fits [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Model]] parameters. The report also includes [[Weight-Length Relationship]] regressions and [[Relative Condition Factor]] analyses. The author notes that the work will be expanded in a master's thesis due in late fall 1991.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish is a large endemic minnow of the Colorado River system. Earlier aging work by [[Musker 1981]] (152 fish) and [[Vanicek and Kramer 1969]] used scale methods, but the author judged that small sample sizes, a lack of small fish, and a misidentified first annulus compromised those estimates. Musker's reported lengths at age I+ (195 mm for the Yampa River, 399 mm for the White River) are therefore considered unreliable.

Stated objectives were to:
1. Evaluate scales as a standard ageing method.
2. Validate scale ages using other bony structures and mark-recapture data.
3. Determine growth rates and compare among populations.
4. Determine age composition of each population.
5. Compare growth, size at maturity, and longevity between sexes.

Objectives 2 and 5 were not fully met. Otoliths, vertebrae, and opercula were attempted but expertise was insufficient, and too few fish were sexed to compare sexes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** The Colorado, Green, White, and Yampa rivers of the Upper Colorado River Basin, sampled 1978-1989.
- **Data sources:** Tagging records from the USFWS Grand Junction field station, plus agency reports from Bio/West, Inc., Colorado Division of Wildlife, Colorado State University, New Mexico Department of Fish and Game, New Mexico State University, Utah Department of Natural Resources, and USFWS field stations.
- **Life history:** Fish sampled from March through November. The Yampa River lacked fish under about 450 mm, which suggests that fish recruit into the Yampa after reaching sexual maturity (>428 mm TL), probably after migrating to spawning areas on the lower Yampa. Tyus (1990) described net downstream movement of small fish and upstream movement of larger fish.
- **Impoundments:** The author suggests historical distributional differences may have been altered by dams that blocked upstream redistribution.
- **Management context:** The database also covers [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and other endangered or native fishes (see Table 5 entries for [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], and [[Northern Pike]]), linking tagging work to [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]] goals.

## Key Findings

**Sample and tagging**
- Tagging records from 1978-1990 totalled 2,176 (Colorado 422, Green 1,086, White 146, Yampa 465). Of tagged fish, 279 were recaptured once, 42 twice, and 3 three times.
- Of 165 fish sexed, 79% (130) were male. Musker's sexing ratio was similar (83% male in Seethaler 1978).

**Length frequency**
- Mean total length across all rivers was 536 mm (range 81-896 mm). The modal group was 500-550 mm.
- Mean lengths by river: Colorado and Green rivers were smaller than the White (498 mm) and Yampa (577 mm). All Yampa fish exceeded 350 mm TL.
- Length-frequency distributions were consistent across the 10-year period, with 500-600 mm fish most common each year.

**Weight-length relationships**
- Slopes were not significantly different among months for mature fish, but intercepts were, so regressions were pooled by river (log10 W = a + b log10 L; W in g, L in mm). Pooled equations:
  - Colorado: log10 W = -6.384 + 3.463 log10 L
  - Green: log10 W = -5.692 + 3.206 log10 L
  - White: log10 W = -5.555 + 3.156 log10 L
  - Yampa: log10 W = -6.026 + 3.339 log10 L
  - Green and Yampa combined: log10 W = -5.54 + 3.16 log10 L (per the report's listing; Colorado-Yampa similarity noted)
- Colorado and Yampa fish were heavier at a given length than Green and White fish.

**Relative condition (Kn)**
- Fulton's K was rejected because it assumes isometric growth (b = 3).
- Kn peaked in June before spawning and fell in July or August, probably from migration or spawning, then recovered in late summer or early fall.

**Age and growth**
- Back-calculated lengths were shorter than lengths at age in previous studies; fish were often about one year older for a given length than earlier estimates.
- The oldest fish aged was 18 years (Yampa River, over 800 mm). Other maxima: 16 years (Green and White rivers) and 12 years (Colorado River).
- Growth increments exceeded 60 mm between ages 3-4 or 4-5 and declined to about 30 mm per year after age XII, with a tendency to increase again near age XVI.
- Relative growth fell from about 50% to 10% with age.
- Recaptured fish with known time intervals showed discrepancies between predicted and observed annuli, attributed to first-annulus misidentification and variable annulus timing.
- Age composition: most mature fish were age VII in the Green River, age VIII in the White, and age IX in the Yampa. The Yampa contained the widest range of older fish.

**Tagging data management**
- The consolidated database contained 3,740 records. Corrections covered transposed tag numbers, truncated tags (42 cases), duplicate records across agencies, and averaged recapture lengths.
- Carlin tags are being phased out because of possible effects on growth and behavior; PIT tags are expected to replace them.

**Recommendations**
- Continue tagging adult fish to support life-history and radio-telemetry work.
- Extend tagging to juveniles in lower reaches to document recruitment.
- Review past tagging accomplishments during the Carlin-to-PIT transition.
