# Age and Growth of Four Species of Warm-Water Game Fish from Three Montana Ponds

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Centrarchidae #Percidae

## Executive Summary
This 1960 study (Brown & Logan, published in *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*) presents age-and-growth data for four warm-water game fish species — [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Crappie]], and [[Yellow Perch]] — recovered from three seepage-fed gravel-pit ponds (East, Middle, and West Ponds) near Three Forks, Montana. Fish became available for study after the ponds were treated with piscicidal toxicants in July 1957 as part of a fisheries management/removal effort. Scale-based back-calculation revealed exceptionally slow growth rates across all four species relative to populations reported in other U.S. states and even other Montana waters, attributed to Montana's short growing season, low water temperatures, and dense, possibly stunted, populations. The [[West Pond]], which had a smaller fish population due to ongoing gravel dredging disrupting reproduction, consistently showed comparatively better growth for bass, bluegill, and perch.

## Background & Description
- **Measurement methods:** Total length measured to nearest 0.1 inch; weight to nearest 0.01 pound.
- **Aging method:** Scales taken from area between dorsal fin and lateral line; scale impressions made on plastic; annuli counted and increments measured along the anterior radius using a projector. A constant scale-radius-to-body-length ratio was assumed for back-calculation.
- **Largemouth Bass:** Longest specimen 16.2 in (2.6 lb); heaviest specimen (female) 16.1 in, 3.3 lb. Useful size (10 in) not reached until year 5; 1 lb not achieved until year 6–7.
- **Bluegill:** Largest specimen was a 7.3 in male (0.3 lb) from Middle Pond. Did not reach 5 in total length until year 5.
- **Black Crappie:** Largest specimen 8.2 in (0.27 lb); oldest specimens (age VII, n=3) averaged 7.0 in.
- **Yellow Perch:** Largest specimen a female, 11.2 in (0.75 lb); oldest specimens (age VI) averaged 10.7 in. All large perch came from West Pond; East/Middle Pond perch were uniformly under 6 in.
- Sex was determined in mature fish, but growth data were pooled since no significant sex-based differences in growth were detected.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** Three adjacent, unconnected gravel-pit ponds (East: 12.9 acres/12 ft depth; Middle: 23.3 acres/18 ft; West: 12.8 acres/19 ft) near Three Forks, Montana, fed solely by groundwater seepage (no inlets/outlets).
- **Thermal regime:** Daytime summer surface temps 65–75°F; subsurface temps 2–6°F cooler; no marked thermal stratification due to frequent wind-driven mixing. Ice cover typically early November–early April.
- **Pond age/history:** East Pond >30 years old, Middle Pond >25 years, West Pond ~20 years and still actively dredged at time of study — ongoing dredging in West Pond is hypothesized to disrupt reproduction and limit population density.
- **Co-occurring species:** [[Carp]], [[Longnose Sucker]], [[White Sucker]], [[Common Sucker]], [[Golden Shiner]], and [[Black Bullhead]] were also present in varying abundance across the three ponds (see Table 1 for species rankings by pond).
- **Community structure:** Species composition and relative abundance varied by pond — Yellow Perch and Bluegill dominated East and West Ponds, while Bluegill and Black Bullhead dominated Middle Pond; Black Crappie was entirely absent from West Pond.

## Key Findings
- **General pattern:** All four species exhibited unusually slow growth compared to populations in other states (Wisconsin, Minnesota, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Ohio, Iowa, Connecticut) and even compared to other Montana ponds (e.g., Raymond Reservoir, eastern Montana farm ponds studied by [[Brown and Thoreson 1951]]).
- **Largemouth Bass:** Average total length at annulus I was only 1.9 in (vs. 2.1 in in eastern Montana ponds); no comparable record found elsewhere of equivalently slow growth. Growth was fastest in West Pond.
- **Bluegill:** Average length at third annulus was substantially below most other regions except Ohio, which showed even poorer growth. Growth was fastest in West Pond at annuli III–IV, but fastest in Middle Pond at annuli I–II.
- **Black Crappie:** Reached 6 in (a "useful size") only by the fifth summer — much slower than Raymond Reservoir, Minnesota, Ohio, or Tennessee populations. Absent from West Pond entirely.
- **Yellow Perch:** Average length at third annulus (5.7 in) was slower than most comparison populations except Ohio and Lower Thompson Lake, Montana. Growth was markedly faster in West Pond (7.0 in at annulus III) than East/Middle Ponds.
- **Population density hypothesis:** The comparatively superior growth of Yellow Perch, Bluegill, and Largemouth Bass in [[West Pond]] is attributed to lower population density, likely due to dredging activity interfering with reproduction and preventing the pond from reaching full numerical carrying capacity — an example of density-dependent growth suppression in stunted warm-water fish populations.
- **Regional implication:** Findings support the broader observation that Montana's cold climate and short growing season allow warm-water game fish to survive and reproduce but severely constrain growth rates, often producing stunted populations of limited value as sport fisheries.
