# Solubility of Oxygen in Pure Water and Sea-Water

#aquatic-chemistry #water-quality #dissolved-oxygen

## Executive Summary

This 1955 paper by G. A. Truesdale, A. L. Downing and G. F. Lowden re-determines the [[Saturation Solubility]] of atmospheric oxygen in pure and saline water between 0° and 40°C using a modified [[Winkler Titration Method]]. The new values are, in some cases, up to 4% lower than the accepted values, which were largely based on [[Gasometric Method|gasometric]] determinations by Fox. The authors show that the accepted values fail the [[Adeney And Becker Law]] of aeration in slowly stirred water, while the new values satisfy it. They provide empirical equations and a coefficient table for calculating oxygen saturation from temperature and salinity, and estimate that using the new values reduces the calculated daily oxygen absorption of the Thames estuary by about 40 tons.

## Background & Description

The rate at which oxygen dissolves into water controls the [[Self-Purification of Streams]] of polluted waters. In uniformly mixed water, this rate is proportional to the difference between the saturation solubility at the water temperature and the actual [[Dissolved Oxygen]] concentration. Accurate solubility values are therefore essential for calculating oxygen balances in polluted streams and estuaries.

Published values for oxygen in pure water differed by up to 0.57 ppm (about 6% of solubility) between 0° and 35°C (Table I of the source). Solubility data for sea-water were scarce, which made their reliability hard to judge. Table I compares values from the American Public Health Association, Winkler, Roscoe & Lunt, Bohr & Bock, and the Royal Commission on Sewage Disposal. Values were calculated for air-saturated water at 760 mm pressure.

The authors' earlier tests showed that the [[Adeney And Becker Law]] was not satisfied when slowly stirred water was analysed using the Winkler method against accepted saturation values. Plots of the logarithm of the oxygen deficit against time were curved rather than straight. The authors judged the published gasometric values to be the more likely source of error. Supersaturation from vigorous agitation during gasometric determinations was suggested as the cause. Jacobsen's saturation values, obtained by rotating water in a bottle with air, gave reasonable agreement with the law. The authors noted, however, that inadequate mixing in that method could bias the result.

Two teams at the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] investigated the problem. Wheatland & Smith compared the Winkler method, including a sea-water modification, against a new gasometric reference procedure and confirmed the Winkler method's accuracy. This allowed the present authors to use it.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study concerns the physical chemistry of oxygen transfer rather than any organism. Its ecological relevance lies in aeration of rivers and estuaries, where dissolved oxygen supports aquatic life and where oxygen demand from pollution is balanced by atmospheric re-aeration. Errors of a few percent in saturation values translate into large differences in estimated oxygen budgets for large water bodies such as the [[Thames Estuary]].

The sea-water used was drawn from the Thames estuary at Knock John Buoy. It had a 5-day [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]] of about 1 ppm. The authors attempted to remove this demand by filtration (kieselguhr and porous candle), heat treatment and sterilization, but a small residual demand of about 0.1–0.2 ppm over 5 days persisted. This was too small to affect the results at high stirring speeds.

## Key Findings

**Experimental method**
- Solubility was measured by bringing water, initially either deoxygenated or supersaturated, into equilibrium with a continuously renewed air supply. The air was free of dust and CO₂ and saturated with water vapour.
- Water in a 10-litre aspirator was stirred at 12.7 r.p.m. (pure water) or 38 r.p.m. (sea-water). Three confirmatory sea-water runs used 400 r.p.m.
- Saturation concentrations were corrected to 760 mm pressure using a [[Henry's Law]] relation, assuming air was fully saturated with water vapour (humidity checks gave 97–100%).
- Air contained 20.88–20.97% oxygen by volume (mean assumed 20.93%), slightly below the 20.99% in the International Critical Tables. The authors attributed this to local or seasonal variation rather than analytical error.
- Absorption of oxygen by the rubber bung and propeller was below 0.002 ppm.
- Winkler titration used an amperometric end-point. Six replicate tests on distilled water gave 9.606 ± 0.024 ppm (standard Winkler) and 9.601 ± 0.026 ppm (modified method), so the two were considered equivalent.
- Sea-water samples used the [[Azide Modification of the Winkler Method]] to remove interference from nitrites.

**Pure water results**
- Twenty-nine determinations were made between 0° and 36°C. The saturation value was approached from one side in all but two experiments at 10.1° and 22.9°, where both directions gave the same result.
- A typical run at 10.1° gave 10.92 ± 0.02 ppm, against the generally accepted 11.31 ppm.
- The fitted equation is: C′₀ = 14.161 − 0.3943T + 0.007714T² − 0.00006467T³ (Equation 1), where T is temperature in °C. The root-mean-square deviation is 0.05 ppm and the maximum deviation is 0.11 ppm.
- The discrepancy from accepted values is about 0.4 ppm (3.4%) at low temperatures, falling to 0.025 ppm (0.3%) or less at 35°C.
- Compared with Winkler's earlier results, the new values are about 0.2 ppm (2%) lower between 0° and 25°C. The authors estimated that bubble over-pressure in Winkler's method could explain about 0.6%, leaving 1.4% unexplained.
- Agreement with Jacobsen's titrimetric results was good. The value at 25°C agreed with a recent gasometric measurement made with care to avoid supersaturation.

**Sea-water results**
- Sixteen observed values at salinities of 30.0 ppt and 15.0 ppt were fitted to a salinity-correction equation (Equation 2). The root-mean-square deviation is 0.04 ppm and the maximum is 0.08 ppm.
- Solubility is assumed to decrease linearly with salinity (Equation 3): C′_S = C′₀ − S(0.0841 − 0.00256T + 0.00003874T²), where S is salinity in ppt.
- Three checks at about 15 ppt gave a mean departure from prediction of +0.01 ppm and a root-mean-square value of 0.055 ppm. The expected values were zero and 0.047 ppm. The authors described this as too few to be conclusive, but consistent with linearity.
- A worked example: at 30.1°C and 15.00 ppt, the table gives a = 7.52 and b = 0.04082, so C′_S = 6.91 ppm. The observed value was 6.84 ppm, a difference of −0.07 ppm.
- Table III gives coefficients a and b at 0.1° intervals from 0° to 40°C and salinities of 0–30 ppt for practical use.

**Rate-of-aeration tests (Figure 1)**
- Pure water at 11.6°C, stirred at 12.7 r.p.m.: using the accepted value (10.91 ppm) gave a curved plot. Using the experimental value (10.52 ppm) gave satisfactory agreement with the law.
- Sea-water (salinity 30 g/1000 g) at 20.5°C, stirred at 41 r.p.m.: the accepted value (7.71 ppm) gave curvature. The experimental value (7.46 ppm) gave agreement with the law.

**Conclusions and applications**
- The new solubilities satisfy the [[Adeney And Becker Law]] in well-mixed water, which the authors took as strong evidence for their correctness.
- The authors state that substituting the observed values for the accepted values reduces the calculated weight of oxygen absorbed daily by the Thames estuary by about 40 tons. This affects the accuracy of [[Oxygen Balance]] calculations for polluted streams and estuaries.
- Remaining uncertainties include whether the accepted values were affected by supersaturation, and the 1.4% discrepancy relative to Winkler's data, which the authors could not fully explain.
