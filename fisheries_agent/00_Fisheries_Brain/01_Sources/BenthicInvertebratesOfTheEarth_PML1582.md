# Benthic Invertebrates of the Earthen Coachella Canal, California

#benthic-invertebrates #freshwater-ecology #macroinvertebrates #corbicula-fluminea

## Executive Summary

This baseline study by [[Paul C. Marsh]] and [[Carolyn R. Stinemetz]] (published in *California Fish and Game*, 1983) describes the benthic macroinvertebrate community of the [[Earthen Canal]] section of the [[Coachella Canal]]. The canal carries [[Colorado River]] water for irrigation in southeastern California. Sampling took place in October–November 1980 (the canal was dewatered 10–13 November). At least 19 invertebrate taxa were recorded, and the community was dominated by the [[Asiatic Clam]] (*[[Corbicula Fluminea]]*), a caddisfly (*[[Smicridea Utica]]*), oligochaetes, and chironomid midges. The study was motivated by plans to line earthen canals with concrete, an alteration whose biological effects were unknown. The authors present the data as a pre-lining baseline.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** The [[Coachella Canal]] supplies agricultural water to the Imperial, Coachella and Indio valleys. Its capacity at the turnout from the [[All American Canal]] is given as 70.8 (OCR-uncertain) m³/s, reduced by withdrawals to 36.8 m³/s downstream. Depths range from 1–5 m mid-channel, and the open-reach width is about 20 m.
- **Setting:** The region lies in the [[Colorado Desert]], is mostly below sea level, and drains entirely to the endorheic [[Salton Sea Basin]].
- **Management context:** To cut seepage losses and improve transport efficiency, the southernmost (upstream) 78.5 km of canal was re-aligned into a concrete structure in mid-November 1980. The paper was submitted for publication in November 1981.
- **Prior work:** The authors note that North American canal macroinvertebrates are poorly known. Earlier studies include the [[Delta-Mendota Canal]] (by the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]]) and studies in the Baltic Canal, the Worcester-Birmingham Canal and Lombardy irrigation canals.
- **Affiliations:** Marsh is with the [[Center For Environmental Studies]] at [[Arizona State University]]. Stinemetz is with the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Substrate and hydrology**
- Substrate was mainly fine to medium sand with sparse pea-sized gravel. Silt and clay occurred in near-shore areas where rooted vegetation reduced current velocity.
- Current velocity was relatively uniform at 0.3–0.7 m/s in open reaches. It was slower near banks and immediately upstream of constrictions.
- Concrete structures at check drops, siphons and bridge crossings provided hard surfaces for colonization.

**Vegetation and organic matter**
- Rooted aquatic vegetation occurred in water less than 1.0 m deep. The dominant emergent plants were common reed (*Phragmites australis*) and cattail (*Typha*). Occasional beds of *Potamogeton pectinatus* and *Myriophyllum spicatum* were also present.
- Near-shore sites with vegetation held more particulate organic matter than areas of higher current.
- Filamentous algae (*Cladophora glomerata*) occurred only on rock riprap or concrete structures.

**Sampling methods**
- Quantitative samples used an [[Ekman Dredge]] (0.023 m²) on 23–24 October 1980, taken along both banks and at mid-channel.
- Samples were sieved through a 420 µm mesh and preserved in 10% formalin.
- Qualitative observations covered current velocity, vegetation and substrate. Additional observations were made near stations 1, 3 and 7 during 10–13 November, when the canal was dewatered.
- Asiatic clam weights were taken as wet weight after blotting. Dry weight was estimated as 0.1 × wet weight, following [[Winberg]] (1971).

## Key Findings

### Community composition
- **Taxa:** 17 taxa were recorded in the 23–24 October quantitative samples (the abstract reports at least 19 taxa for October–November 1980, including two odonate species collected 10–13 November).
- **Dominants:** Asiatic clam, the caddisfly *Smicridea utica*, the oligochaetes *[[Aelosoma]]* sp. and *[[Chaetogaster]]* sp., and chironomid midges together made up more than 90% of individuals.
- **Other taxa:** Ephemeroptera (*Baetis* sp.), Odonata (*[[Hetaerina Americana]]*, *[[Hyponeura Lugens]]*), Lepidoptera (*[[Parargyractis]]* larvae), Diptera (Chironomidae, *Chrysops* sp.), Turbellaria, Nematoda, Ostracoda, Hydracarina and Physidae.

### Densities and biomass
- **Density range:** Mean densities ranged from 158 to 3,678 individuals/m² between mid-channel and near-shore habitats. The near-shore figure was 3,678 ± 1,354 and the mid-channel figure was 158 ± 56.
- **Biomass range:** Biomass ranged from 2.02 to about 7.5 g dry weight/m² (table values 2,015 ± 1,101 mg and 7,625 ± 4,205 mg dry weight/m²).
- **Near-shore vs. mid-channel:** Near-shore habitats had more than 20 times as many individuals and nearly four times the biomass of mid-channel habitats. They also supported more taxa.
- **Clam size structure:** Clams averaged about 1,277 individuals/m² in the sampled area (OCR-uncertain). Large clams (shell length >4 mm) dominated biomass, and the smallest size classes were concentrated at the lowest-velocity sites.

### Concrete versus natural substrates
- Concrete structures supported very high densities of *Smicridea utica* and *Parargyractis* larvae. The exact densities are garbled in the scan, but the authors state they were far greater than the density of any invertebrate on natural substrates.
- Concrete surfaces were also used by filter-feeding and grazing fauna such as Chironomidae, which were densely colonized on these surfaces.

### Distribution drivers
- Invertebrate distribution reflected substrate size and stability and the presence of organic matter.
- Filter feeders (Asiatic clam, *Hydropsyche*-type caddisflies) and collectors dominated where current carried particulate food.
- Detritivores and grazers (Chironomidae, Oligochaeta) dominated areas of fine sediment and organic accumulation.

### Implications for canal lining
- The authors argue that concrete lining will change invertebrate assemblages. Hard surfaces may favor a few dense, sessile or cling-adapted taxa and reduce burrowing habitat.
- They suggest that retaining some vegetation, bank holes and habitat diversity could reduce this disturbance.
- Concrete canals may also reduce primary production and total stream-level productivity, since the lining removes the sediment and plant-associated habitat that supports the benthic food web.

### Limitations
- The study is a single-season (autumn), single-year baseline. Sample sizes are small, and the authors caution that differences between habitats cannot always be tested statistically.
- Several table entries and numbers in the source are OCR-garbled, so values should be checked against the original paper before quantitative reuse.
