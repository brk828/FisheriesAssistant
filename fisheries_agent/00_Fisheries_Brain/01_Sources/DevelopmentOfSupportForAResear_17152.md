# Development of Support for Research Emphasis in Evolutionary Genetics

#evolutionary-genetics #population-genetics #ecological-genetics #research-policy #biotechnology

## Executive Summary

This 1986 NSF memorandum from James P. Collins (Program Director, PBPE) to John L. Brooks (Division Director, BSR) outlines the rationale for developing a research emphasis in evolutionary genetics for the FY 88 budget. The document traces the historical convergence of genetics, ecology, and evolutionary biology from Darwin through the Evolutionary Synthesis (1936-1947) to contemporary applications. Collins demonstrates that BSR programs are funding an increasing number and diversity of genetics studies using nonbiomedical organisms, and argues this investment has created a research community uniquely positioned to address emerging challenges in deliberate release of genetically engineered organisms. The memorandum advocates continued or increased support for basic research in evolutionary genetics as both scientifically important and nationally necessary.

## Background & Description

The document provides extensive historical context for the integration of genetics into ecology and evolutionary biology. Collins identifies four principal areas of modern genetics: molecular genetics (genic organization and expression), physiological genetics (developmental processes), Mendelian genetics (individual variation), and population genetics (application of genetic principles to populations). Population genetics extends to two key areas: ecological genetics (study of qualitative trait differences like butterfly coloration) and quantitative genetics (study of continuous traits like height or weight).

The historical narrative traces how Darwin's 1859 *Origin of Species* lacked a mechanism for inheritance, leading to the "provisional hypothesis of pangenesis" and subsequent confusion between neo-Darwinism and neo-Lamarckism. Mendel's 1866 laws of heredity, rediscovered in 1900, provided the materialistic base for particulate inheritance. The foundations of population genetics were established 1920-1930 by [[R. A. Fisher]], [[J. B. S. Haldane]], [[Sewall Wright]], and [[S. Chetverikov]].

Early 20th-century ecology emerged from field natural history and experimental physiology, initially focusing on physiological plant-environment interactions rather than evolutionary explanations. The [[Evolutionary Synthesis]] of the 1930s-1940s, led by [[Theodosius Dobzhansky]], [[Ernst Mayr]], and others, demonstrated that natural selection of small variations could direct evolutionary change. By the 1960s, three empirical developments affected ecology: (1) demonstration of genetic bases for ecologically significant traits, (2) evidence that evolutionary change occurs on ecological timescales, and (3) demonstration that natural selection operates over small spatial scales.

The document describes how [[Ernst Mayr]]'s 1961 distinction between "proximate" (mechanistic) and "ultimate" (evolutionary) explanations helped structure ecological research, particularly regarding population regulation. [[G. C. Williams]]' 1966 critique of group selection in *Adaptation and Natural Selection* further refined thinking about levels of selection, encouraging ecologists to seek traits responsible for population regulation at the organismic level.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Collins emphasizes that contemporary evolutionary ecology explicitly recognizes selection at multiple levels from genome to community. The approach is distinguished not by topics investigated but by how ecological problems are conceived using ecological, genetic, and evolutionary principles. Two novel conceptual elements emerged in the 1960s: (1) sensitivity to partitioning phenotypic variation into environmental versus genetic components, and (2) awareness of the need to specify the level at which selection operates most effectively.

The document notes that total genetic variation can be partitioned into amounts attributable to selection within versus between groups, with the magnitude and direction of selection at each level determining how gene frequencies change or are maintained. This theoretical framework became increasingly relevant as investigators studied topics ranging from competitive ability to tolerance of abiotic factors like temperature.

BSR funding data (FY 83-85) show consistent increases in support for genetics research across four programs: [[Ecology Program]], [[Systematics]], [[Population Biology and Physiological Ecology]] (PBPE), and [[Biological Research Resources]] (BRR). The [[Ecosystems Biology]] program indirectly supports genetics through Long Term Ecological Research (LTER) projects. PBPE showed genetics components in 37.3% of awards (FY 83) increasing to 42.6% (FY 85), with preliminary FY 86 data showing 71-72% of awards including genetics.

Research spans diverse nonbiomedical organisms including nonvascular and vascular plants, invertebrates (especially insects including [[Diptera]]), fish, herps, birds, and mammals. Less than 5% of BSR proposals are simultaneously submitted to other agencies, making NSF "perhaps the principal national and international source of support for research on the genetics of nonbiomedical models."

## Key Findings

**Historical Integration of Disciplines**: The document establishes that genetics, ecology, and evolutionary biology have progressively integrated since 1900, with major conceptual advances in the 1920s-1930s (population genetics theory), 1936-1947 (Evolutionary Synthesis), and 1960s (evolutionary ecology incorporating proximate-ultimate distinction and levels of selection).

**Funding Trends in BSR**: Analysis of FY 83-85 data reveals:
- Consistent increase in percentage of funds for genetics research across BSR programs
- PBPE genetics funding increased from 37.3% (FY 83) to 42.6% (FY 85), with $10,387K total over three years (N=197 awards)
- [[Systematics]] genetics funding: 11.5% to 14.6%, $4,608K total (N=85)
- [[Ecology Program]]: 1.0% to 2.1%, $352K total (N=7), with substantial increase expected in FY 86 for microbial ecology
- BRR (living organism stock centers): 11.9% to 16.8%, $3,687K total (N=37)
- Combined BSR genetics funding: 14.8% to 19.4%, $19,033K total (N=326 awards)

**Taxonomic Diversity**: PBPE supports genetics research across broad taxonomic range with average funding per year: theory ($25-45K/year), nontracheophyte plants ($32-49K/year), tracheophyte plants ($33-38K/year), invertebrates ($40-49K/year), insects ($40-42K/year), fish ($34-45K/year), herps ($31-35K/year), birds ($31-36K/year), mammals ($31-40K/year).

**Convergence with National Need**: The document identifies critical convergence between basic research agendas and applied challenges of deliberate release of genetically engineered organisms. Key applied questions requiring basic research foundation include:

*For microbes*:
- How are plasmids transferred in complex systems and at what rates?
- What environmental conditions affect horizontal gene transfer?
- What are population densities, generation times, and seasonal dynamics under field conditions?
- Are population dynamics density-dependent or density-independent?

*For gene expression and stability*:
- How do genetic and external environments affect likelihood of stable transfer?
- How does environment affect gene expression in novel hosts?
- Will selection favor or oppose novel characters?
- How do organisms maintain genetic integrity despite frequent input of new genetic material?

**Policy Recommendations**: Collins argues the Foundation should continue or increase support for basic research in BSR programs, citing [[Vannevar Bush]]'s 1945 principle: "Under the pressure for immediate results...applied research invariably drives out pure." The 35-year NSF investment has created infrastructure of researchers with expertise in genetics of nonbiomedical organisms, positioned to address both theoretical questions and practical challenges of genetic engineering at a time when such talent is nationally needed.

**Research Community Characteristics**: Success rates in PBPE declined from 33% (1977-1979) to approximately 20% (current), attributed to: (1) higher cost per project for genetics research, and (2) larger number of proposals including genetics components. This reflects both the increasing sophistication of genetics research and growing recognition of its importance across ecological and evolutionary disciplines.
