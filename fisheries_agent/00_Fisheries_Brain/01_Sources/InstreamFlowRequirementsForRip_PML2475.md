# Instream Flow Requirements for Riparian Vegetation

#botany #salicaceae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Julie C. Stromberg]] and Duncan T. Patten argues that instream flow standards, which are usually set for fish, do not necessarily maintain [[Riparian Vegetation]]. Plants may need flows that differ in timing and magnitude, such as [[Flood Pulse|floodplain-wetting]] events, to sustain tree growth and to allow seedling establishment. The authors develop a model for maintaining mature [[Black Cottonwood]] growth on [[Rush Creek]], a diverted stream in the [[Eastern Sierra Nevada]]. The study uses tree-ring data and a streamflow-based growth model. The authors conclude that maintenance of established trees and seedling establishment have different flow requirements, and both must be met for long-term persistence of the riparian community.

## Background & Description

Stream diversion for water supply and hydropower is increasing across the United States. Approval of diversion projects often requires an instream flow, defined as the water needed to maintain the physical and ecological functions of a stream system. Most instream flow methods were developed for fish or fisheries habitat, and riparian vegetation has seldom been considered. The authors note that vegetation helps stabilize stream banks, reduce siltation, and create fish habitat, so flows designed for fish may be inadequate for plants.

The paper reviews the main instream flow approaches for fisheries, grouped into three categories:
- The [[Tennant Method]] (1976), a widely used hydrological rule of thumb.
- Methods based on habitat simulation, including the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM; Trihey and Wegner 1981).
- Other approaches, such as those reviewed by Wesche and Rechard (1980) and Morhardt (1986).

The authors do not endorse a single method, because costs, effort, and data needs vary by stream system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and geomorphology.** Riparian vegetation depends on the timing and magnitude of flows. Gaining, high-gradient streams with coarse substrate differ from losing, low-gradient streams with deep alluvium, and the authors suggest their flow requirements differ accordingly.

**Bishop Creek example.** A study of vegetation change along [[Bishop Creek]] (Patten and Stromberg 1988), where diversion supports hydroelectric power, found that:
- Upper high-gradient reaches responded mainly to climatic cycles, such as droughts and scouring high flows.
- Lower deep-alluvium reaches responded mainly to stream diversion, with climate as a secondary influence.
- Diversion did not reduce water availability enough in the lower reaches to reduce riparian vegetation cover.

**Life-cycle needs.** Flow requirements must address every life stage of each riparian species. Creating flows for regeneration is as important as maintaining the adult community.

**Rush Creek.** Lower Rush Creek, below [[Grant Lake]], is a diversion reservoir for the [[Los Angeles Department of Water and Power]]. Its irregular pattern of releases, with some years of reduced flow, caused high mortality of black cottonwood and willows. The authors infer that the flow regime before 1941 was inadequate for maintaining woody riparian species.

## Key Findings

**1. Fish-based flows may not meet riparian needs.** The authors state that flows sufficient for fisheries are assumed to be sufficient for vegetation, but no empirical evidence supports this assumption. Riparian plants may not need perennial flow, as shown by riparian communities along ephemeral streams in the southwest.

**2. Tree-growth model for Rush Creek.** Using tree-ring records, the authors built a model linking standardized ring width to annual flow and precipitation. The reported fit is R² = 0.71. The model uses flow in the current year (SF), flow in the previous year (SF t-1), and annual precipitation (PR), plus a constant term of 0.4947. Some coefficients are illegible in the source, so the equation should be checked against the original publication before use.

**3. Flow-to-growth relationship.** The model shows that the amount of water released to the stream strongly affects growth. The authors state that the predicted growth is about 50% of its potential at the present release, which is close to the 19 cfs flow then in place, compared with 80 to 100 cfs rates they associate with adequate growth. Figure 2 compares actual and predicted standardized growth.

**4. Seedling establishment needs different flows.** Seedling establishment depends on timing: seed dispersal and germination must coincide with moist surfaces. Seedlings also need to survive their first summer, and this depends on the location of the water table relative to the stream and the distance from the channel. Strahan (1987) found that establishment was favored by fluctuating moisture conditions rather than constant ones. Sand and gravel bars were the main sites of successful establishment, and some seedling recruitment occurred after reservoir releases.

**5. Ecological models.** The authors propose a conceptual model linking riparian species response to hydrologic events, and they acknowledge that empirical data are limited. Their literature review lists several models relating flow to vegetation, including Taylor's (1982) relationships between flow rate, number of riparian habitats, and channel width.

**Conclusion.** Maintenance of established riparian trees and shrubs and their seedling establishment require different flows. Maintenance flows can be expressed through growth models, while establishment flows depend on the timing and pattern of wetting. Both must be met for long-term persistence of woody riparian species.
