# Glen Canyon NRA Fish Management Plan (April 1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae #moronidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 fish management plan, prepared jointly by the National Park Service (NPS), the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (UDWR), and the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), sets a five-year framework for fisheries within [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]]. The plan addresses Lake Powell, the Lees Ferry tailwater, flowing river reaches of the Colorado, San Juan, Dirty Devil and Escalante rivers, inflow zones, and perennial streams. Its core premise is that the area can support both an outstanding sport fishery and the preservation of native fish, including four endangered [[Colorado River Endemic Fishes]]. The plan sets goals and objectives for each habitat type, establishes an annual workplan process, and outlines education and outreach actions.

## Background & Description

Glen Canyon NRA includes [[Lake Powell]], which spans the Arizona–Utah border, covers 255 square miles, drains a watershed of 111,700 square miles, and extends nearly 2,000 miles of shoreline. Lake Powell is the second largest reservoir in volume in the United States. Created by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (closed 1963), the reservoir is managed under the overlapping authority of the NPS, UDWR, AGFD, the Navajo Nation, the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR) and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS).

The plan has two main purposes: to coordinate fish management between agencies, and to identify concerns and issues. Its objectives include:
- Providing a mechanism for formal coordination between the NPS and the states of Utah and Arizona.
- Identifying fish management issues.
- Promoting interagency cooperation to maximize efficiency and expertise.

The plan is a five-year document, and it is tied to the NPS General Management Plan, the [[Resource Management Plan]], and state strategic plans. It was called for in a 1990 Memorandum of Understanding between UDWR, AGFD and the NPS.

Key legal and policy frameworks include the [[Endangered Species Act]], the [[Clean Water Act]] (1973), the [[Wild and Scenic Rivers Act]], and [[Executive Order 12962]] on Recreational Fisheries (June 1995). Coordination with partners includes the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]s, the [[Lake Powell Native Fish Work Group]], the [[Grand Canyon Adaptive Management Program]], and the Navajo Nation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The region is semi-arid to arid, with annual rainfall of 6 to 10 inches. Evaporation exceeds precipitation in all but winter months, with an estimated net loss of about 500,000 acre-feet per year. The Colorado River historically had spring floods of 80,000 to 300,000 cfs and low summer and winter flows near 3,000 cfs. Post-dam operations eliminated spring floods and increased summer and winter flows. Water temperature in the historical river reached nearly 80°F in summer and near freezing in winter.

**Tributaries.** The four main tributaries into Lake Powell are the Colorado, San Juan, Dirty Devil and Escalante rivers. The Paria River enters below the dam at Lees Ferry. The San Juan River, the largest tributary, has a historic mean flow of about 2,177 cfs (reported as "average discharge"; the document gives partly illegible values).

**Lake Powell.** It is a warm, monomictic, thermally stratified reservoir with a thermocline that varies seasonally. Its clear, oligotrophic water favors nonnative fish.

**Lees Ferry Tailwater.** Cold water (about 46°F) released from deep penstocks supports coldwater species and a trout fishery. The 15-mile reach is shaped entirely by dam releases.

**Biological history.** Before impoundment, native species such as the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and several chub species were common. After closure, habitat changed from turbid to clear water, and nonnative species such as [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Threadfin Shad]] became established through intentional and unintentional introductions. The shad–striped bass predator-prey cycle has fluctuated widely since the 1980s.

**Habitat types** (plan Table 1 lists species by habitat):
- Flowing rivers: native-dominated but nonnative species now dominate catches.
- Inflows: artificially created transition zones, highly productive and important for young fish.
- Lake Powell: sport fishery dominated by introduced species.
- Lees Ferry tailwater: trout fishery.
- Perennial and intermittent streams: largely unsurveyed.

## Key Findings

**Issues identified (10 main concerns).**
1. Dam operation effects on fisheries are poorly understood.
2. Impacts on endangered fish below Glen Canyon Dam and critical habitat in flowing reaches have not been fully evaluated.
3. Causes of continued declines of endangered endemic species are unidentified.
4. General lack of information on native species status and habitat needs.
5. Conflicts between state and federal laws.
6. Trace elements and contaminants in fish tissue are of concern.
7. NPS is not fully involved in Recovery Implementation Programs.
8. Invasive organisms, parasites and disease threaten aquatic systems.
9. A coordinated interagency approach to recreational fishing has been lacking.
10. A public education program on fishery issues is needed.

**Endangered and native species status.**
- Four endemic species are listed as endangered: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- Fewer than ten confirmed bonytail are known in the upper basin, and none were taken in Lake Powell over the last 20 years of surveys. The last known bonytail in the lake was caught in May 1985.
- Humpback chub: only three were captured in Lake Powell in UDWR gill-net surveys.
- Razorback suckers in Lake Powell and the San Juan inflow area appear to be declining sharply.
- Flannelmouth suckers persist in Lake Powell but show a declining trend, and no reproductive recruitment has been documented in the lake.

**Management goals.**
- Flowing rivers: protect, restore and recover native fish populations.
- Inflows: conserve native fish and enhance survival.
- Lake Powell: maintain a stable, balanced sport fishery, with an average catch rate of 0.5 fish/hour for all species combined.
- Lees Ferry tailwater: maintain a "blue ribbon" trout fishery and restore native fishes such as flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker and speckled dace.
- Streams: protect native species and habitats in streams and isolated springs.

**Lake Powell fishery and contaminants.**
- The striped bass population reproduces without stocking, and it is the most numerous sport fish.
- Selenium in plankton ranged from 3.3 to 14.6 µg/g dry weight; striped bass fillets contained 12.7 to 19.9 µg/g. The plan recommends angler education and possible consumption advisories.

**Lees Ferry tailwater.** Trout condition has improved under interim flow restrictions. A slot limit of 16 to 21 inches requires release of trout within it. Rainbow trout Age III fish should be at least 5.3 inches from natural reproduction.

**Stocking history.** Records from 1963 to 1995 document stocking of largemouth bass, rainbow trout, kokanee salmon, black crappie, smallmouth bass, striped bass and threadfin shad. Stocking records for Lees Ferry tailwater (1978–1995) list rainbow, brook and cutthroat trout, with totals of 33,000 to 310,000 per year.

**Annual process.** Representatives from UDWR, AGFD and Glen Canyon NRA meet each December to approve the annual workplan. Proposals must conform to the plan, and permits are required from UDWR, AGFD, Glen Canyon NRA and USFWS. The plan is to remain in effect for five years, and it can be renewed by written agreement.

**Education and outreach.** Components include ranger-led programs, a UDWR toll-free fishing hotline (1-800-ASK-FISH), displays and brochures, educational videos, and school partnerships.
