## Executive Summary

This 1981 study by Todd, Smith, and Cable investigated the relative contributions of **genotype** versus **environment** to morphological variation among four species of Great Lakes ciscoes: *[[Coregonus alpenae]]*, *[[Coregonus zenithicus]]*, *[[Coregonus hoyi]]*, and *[[Coregonus kiyi]]*. Wild-caught parents from Lake Michigan (alpenae, zenithicus) and Lake Superior (hoyi, kiyi) were spawned artificially, and their progeny were reared under identical hatchery conditions at the Northville Hatchery (Michigan). By comparing morphological differences between wild parents and hatchery-reared offspring, and between offspring of different species raised under common conditions, the authors sought to disentangle genetic versus environmental (developmental temperature, hatchery stress) sources of morphological variation — a critical issue for the notoriously difficult taxonomy of the [[Coregoninae]].

Key conclusion: Morphological differences between parents and their own hatchery-reared offspring were often **larger** than differences between different species, demonstrating strong environmental (developmental) effects on cisco morphology. However, genetic differentiation was still detectable: *C. hoyi* and *C. kiyi* retained consistent genetic differences under common-garden conditions, while *C. alpenae* and *C. zenithicus* did not — suggesting the latter two may represent a single variable species rather than two distinct species.

- **C. alpenae**: Parents SL 233.0±13.7 mm → progeny 133.0±20.9 mm; gill-raker number nearly stable (38.3±2.6 P vs 37.3±1.4 F1).
- **C. zenithicus**: Parents SL 226.4±14.5 mm → progeny 170.8±15.9 mm; gill-raker number stable (41.4±3.2 P vs 40.2±2.2 F1).

**Universal hatchery artifact:** All hatchery-reared fish (regardless of species) exhibited abnormal thickening of the frontal bones — interpreted as an artifact of artificial rearing conditions rather than a taxonomically meaningful trait, consistent with earlier observations by Koelz (1929) in aquarium-raised *[[Coregonus clupeaformis]]*.

**Most environmentally labile characters:** Interorbital width and dorsal fin height (significant parent-offspring differences in *all* species); fin lengths generally highly plastic.

**Most environmentally stable character:** Head length (only *C. zenithicus* showed a significant parent-offspring difference).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- All species inhabit sub-thermocline (deepwater) zones of the Great Lakes, are morphologically very similar, and spawn during nearly overlapping seasons — factors that make the [[Biological Species Concept]] difficult to apply.
- *C. alpenae* and *C. zenithicus* were sampled from Lake Michigan (off South Haven, MI); *C. hoyi* and *C. kiyi* from Lake Superior (Apostle Islands, WI).
- Prior laboratory work (Garside and Christie 1962; unpublished Cable and Todd data) demonstrated that several Great Lakes *Coregonus* species are completely interfertile, paralleling similar findings in European coregonines (Svärdson, Kajosaari, Gąsowska).
- The authors distinguish **species** (phenotypically and genetically distinct populations) from **stocks** (spatially/chronologically isolated populations maintained by homing behavior and spawning-time differences, less distinct than species) — a framework relevant to [[Fisheries Management]] of diverse but cryptic coregonine populations.
- Diet shifts during rearing mimicked natural ontogeny: live *Artemia* → live *Daphnia* → ground beef liver → trout pellets.

## Key Findings

1. **Environment dominates most morphological variation**: Differences between wild parents and their own hatchery-reared progeny generally exceeded differences between species, implicating developmental temperature and hatchery stress as major drivers of cisco morphological plasticity.

2. **Genetic differentiation confirmed between *C. hoyi* and *C. kiyi***: Principal-components and discriminant-function analyses showed consistent, near-equal cluster separation in both parents (20.6% overlap) and progeny (24.1% overlap), indicating a genetically based, stable morphological distinction between these two species independent of rearing environment.

3. **No genetic differentiation detected between *C. alpenae* and *C. zenithicus***: Discriminant-function analysis showed progeny occupied an *intermediate* position between the parental clusters (unlike the hoyi:kiyi pattern), and control experiments with random and gill-raker-based subsamples of *zenithicus* progeny produced separations comparable to those between the alpenae/zenithicus parents themselves — suggesting the original parental samples may simply represent extremes drawn from a single variable population rather than two true species.

4. **Gill-raker number and length show species-specific developmental control**: Gill-raker number was strongly environmentally influenced in *hoyi*/*kiyi* (significant parent-offspring differences) but genetically fixed (no environmental effect) in *alpenae*/*zenithicus* — and vice versa for gill-raker length. This contradicts prior assumptions (Svärdson 1952; Loch 1974) that gill-raker number is purely genetically controlled, urging caution in using this trait alone for coregonine taxonomy.

5. **Taxonomic and management implications**: The results support treating *C. hoyi* and *C. kiyi* as genetically distinct, locally adapted forms, but suggest that samples labeled *C. alpenae* and *C. zenithicus* may represent a single species subject to strong environmental morphological plasticity. The authors recommend that Great Lakes fisheries management address diversity at **both** the species level and the stock (locally adapted population) level to preserve genetic diversity in the face of exploitation and potential extinction — a conclusion with direct relevance to [[Lake Whitefish]] and other [[Coregonus]] conservation efforts, especially given that *C. alpenae* is now considered extinct in the wild.

6. **Methodological note**: Principal-components analysis and discriminant-function analysis produced consistent results for the hoyi:kiyi comparison but diverged for alpenae:zenithicus, where discriminant analysis found a separation (mostly driven by size, Canonical Axis I) not evident in principal-components ordination — highlighting the importance of cross-validating multivariate morphometric methods, following the frameworks of [[Sneath and Sokal 1973]] and [[Blackith and Reyment 1971]].
