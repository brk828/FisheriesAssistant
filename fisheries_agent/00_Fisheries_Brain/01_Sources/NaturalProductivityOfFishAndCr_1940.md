# Natural Productivity of Fish and Crayfish in Riffles

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #percidae #crayfish #stream-ecology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1940 study by B.L. Wickliff (Ohio Division of Conservation and Natural Resources) presents one of the earliest quantitative assessments of natural forage fish and crayfish productivity in stream riffles, conducted as part of a WPA-funded stream research project on [[Blacklick Creek]], Ohio. Over a 2.5-year period (Sept 1937–Feb 1940), researchers depleted 52 riffles at four stations to calculate standing population densities and biomass of riffle-dwelling organisms per acre. The study revealed extraordinarily high and highly fluctuating populations of [[Darters]], minnows, and [[Crayfish]] — at peak, exceeding four organisms per square foot of riffle — and demonstrated rapid recolonization of depleted or newly-wetted riffles (within 48 hours to one month). The findings were used to argue that abundant natural forage production in riffles may explain why supplemental stocking of hatchery-raised game and forage fish often produced disappointing results, since stocked fish were released into environments already saturated with natural competitors adapted to local flow conditions.

## Background & Description

- **Dominant taxa**: [[Rainbow Darter]] (referred to as "soldier darter") and [[Fantail Darter]] dominated all riffles studied, together comprising **88.4%** of the entire darter population (Rainbow: 46.9%, Fantail: 41.5%).
- **Associated darter species**: [[Johnny Darter]], [[Greenside Darter]], [[Banded Darter]], [[Orangethroat Darter]], [[Blackside Darter]], and [[Variegate Darter]] — eight darter species total recorded from riffles.
- **Minimum sizes recorded at first capture** (smallest individuals retained by 1/8-inch mesh nets):
  - Rainbow darter: 17 mm
  - Fantail darter: 17 mm
  - Johnny darter: 21 mm
  - Greenside darter: 27 mm
  - Banded darter: 31 mm
- **Size range**: Most darter species rarely exceed 3 inches (~76 mm) total length.
- **Sexual dimorphism/coloration**: Males of several darter species display brilliant coloration during winter and spring breeding season.
- **Sex ratio findings**:
  - Adult male rainbow ("soldier") darters: 67.9% of adult population of that species.
  - Mature female fantail darters: 54.5% of adults of that species.
- **Other notable species**: [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[Common Shiner]], [[Stoneroller]], and [[Hog Sucker]] were periodically important components of riffle fish assemblages.
- **Taxonomic diversity**: 53 fish species recorded in 30 miles of creek (~1/3 of all species recorded for the state of Ohio); 31 species specifically identified from riffle habitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Stream riffles with rubble, gravel, and sand-gravel substrates; riffle area per station ranged from 138 to 1,029 square feet.
- **Study stations** (Blacklick Creek), representing a habitat/drainage gradient:
  - **Station 3**: Headwater conditions; 12 sq mi drainage (largely pasture); riffles dry out and disconnect into pools during summer.
  - **Station 4**: Mid-basin; 30 sq mi drainage; subject to serious erosion.
  - **Station 7**: Middle third of stream; 55 sq mi drainage; most productive for game fish, especially [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Rock Bass]]; most flood-modified station.
  - **Station 8**: Near stream mouth; 61 sq mi drainage; best cropland along stream; least productive station overall (sand/gravel substrate).
- **Microhabitat behavior**: Darters live primarily on the riffle bottom, under or between stones, for much of the year.
- **Spawning ecology**: Stable gravel riffles in spring show large concentrations of breeding rainbow darters; fantail darters spawn under stones in pools/shallow shoreline water rather than on open riffle, explaining their lower riffle counts at some stations (e.g., Station 5).
- **Seasonal population dynamics**:
  - Maximum abundance in **summer and fall**, driven by young-of-year fish and crayfish.
  - Minimum abundance during **winter months** across all four stations.
  - Populations fluctuate substantially by year, season, and even month-to-month.
- **Recolonization dynamics**: Riffles rebuilt fish/crayfish populations within one month after depletion or substrate disturbance; some evidence suggests recolonization within 48 hours. A riffle that had been completely dry recovered a population of 46,927 organisms/acre within two days of rewetting.
- **Disturbance regime**: Populations appear to build up rapidly following major disturbances such as floods, droughts, ice action, and erosion — Station 7 was particularly flood-modified, as documented over four years of mapping/photography.
- **Broader relevance**: Findings inform [[Fish Stocking Policy]] debates by suggesting native forage populations (darters, minnows, crayfish) are already abundant and may compete with or limit success of introduced hatchery fish, especially [[Forage Fish]] releases into "occupied" habitat with unfamiliar current regimes.

## Key Findings

- **Methodology**: Riffles were blocked at head and foot with 1/8-inch square mesh nets; fish/crayfish removed via seine netting with 4–8 workers per riffle, repeating hauls 11–19 times per depletion until catch reached zero. This is an early quantitative depletion/removal method for benthic riffle census.
- **52 riffles depleted** total across the study period; all fish preserved, weighed to 0.1 g, measured to nearest mm, and sexed when possible.
- **Station 3** (May 1938): 19,800 adult rainbow darters/acre (23.6 lbs); 32,340 total darters/acre; total fish+crayfish = 67,155/acre, weighing 220.5 lbs.
- **Station 5**:
  - May 1938: 27,427 adult rainbow darters/acre.
  - May 1939: 54,859 rainbow darters/acre (105.2 lbs); crayfish = 65,335/acre (651.8 lbs).
  - Total fish+crayfish = 127,856/acre, weighing 789.36 lbs — **~3 organisms per square foot**.
  - December 1937: population crashed to only 384 darters total (lowest recorded).
- **Station 7** (most productive station):
  - August 1938: 40,414 fantails + 24,684 rainbows/acre; total darters = 67,035/acre (44.08 lbs); total fish = 102,850/acre; crayfish = 89,056/acre (272.8 lbs).
  - **Grand total = 191,906 organisms/acre, weighing 346.28 lbs — over 4 organisms per square foot**, recorded just two weeks after a major flood.
  - Rapid recolonization case study: a previously dry riffle depleted in June 1938 yielded 46,927 organisms/acre within days of rewetting.
  - 48-hour comparison (Sept 1937): first depletion = 26,341 darters/acre + 8,417 crayfish/acre; second depletion two days later = 11,924 darters/acre (14.24 lbs) + 6,070 crayfish/acre — indicating recovery to **45.3% of original darter density and 72.1% of original crayfish density within 48 hours**.
- **Station 8** (least productive station):
  - August 1938: peak of 11,491 rainbow darters/acre (13.2 lbs); crayfish = 6,250/acre (26.9 lbs); total = 31,516/acre (64.68 lbs).
  - December 1938: only 1,016 fish/acre (4.34 lbs), zero crayfish — lowest winter production recorded at this station.
- **February 1940** (winter minimum, all stations): total fish ranged 722–4,064/acre; crayfish ranged 310–4,284/acre.
- **Stocking context**: The paper references a prior 10-year tagging study of hatchery channel catfish and Lake Erie breeder fish releases in Ohio waters; best return was **15% from 5,000 adult channel catfish** released behind two dams (65 ft and 32 ft) on the [[Scioto River]] — fish dispersed downstream past both barriers and were caught within a 140-mile range within one year.
- **Central conclusion**: Native riffle fish and crayfish populations are naturally abundant, highly dynamic, and capable of rapid recovery after disturbance or depletion, suggesting that streams already possess substantial natural forage productivity — a factor likely contributing to inconsistent success of artificial fish stocking programs when hatchery fish are introduced into already "occupied" habitats with unfamiliar current environments.
