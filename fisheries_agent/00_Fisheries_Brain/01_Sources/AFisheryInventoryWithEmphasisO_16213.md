# Lower Colorado River Fishery Inventory and Razorback Sucker Status (1981 Survey)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game Region 5 Informational Bulletin (0012-9-1985, September 1985) reports a January–June 1981 trammel and hoop net survey of the lower [[Colorado River]], covering roughly 160.7 river miles from [[Parker Dam]] to Morelos Dam. The survey aimed to determine the status of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). No razorback suckers were captured or observed despite sampling timed to the January–May spawning period. The authors conclude the population persists at severely diminished levels, with reproduction still occurring only sporadically, and they recommend agency action on exotic fish, habitat, and reintroduction. The work was partially funded by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under a cooperative agreement.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a native Colorado River catostomid listed as endangered by California and Nevada and as a federal candidate species. The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] recommended upper-basin threatened classification and lower-basin evaluation in 1991. A 1981 Memorandum of Understanding between the USFWS and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] initiated a ten-year stocking program in the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers.

Documented decline since the early 1950s is attributed to dams and riverine modification, channelization, and predation by [[Exotic Fish Species]]. The survey used the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] division system (10 administrative divisions; seven sampled: Havasu, Parker, Palo Verde, Cibola, Imperial, Laguna, Yuma). Sampling was conducted at roughly 1.5 km intervals using 2–4 trammel nets (49.2 m × 2.6 m) and 4–6 baited hoop nets (4.9 m long) per set. Mainstream bathymetry was mapped with a [[Lowrance Sonar Graphing]] unit.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning habitat:** Potential spawning substrates resembling those at Senator Wash Reservoir and Lake Mohave are extremely limited, occurring only in the Parker and Havasu Divisions. Spawning areas at both sites had similar substrate, slope, and water temperatures of 12–17 °C.
- **Temperature and flow:** Dams releasing cool hypolimnetic water delay summer warming. Stored water has reduced and attenuated annual peaks, increased water clarity, and altered channel dimensions and substrates. Razorback egg development was reported as impaired at 0, 10, and 30 °C but normal at 20 °C; adults preferred 22.9–28.8 °C.
- **Habitat change:** Bank stabilization prevents seasonal flooding of side channels, and backwaters are succeeding toward marsh and upland. Gravel bars and wash-mouth alluvial fans, historically important spawning areas, have declined through dredging and lower spring peaks.
- **Sampling constraints:** Turbulent flows, eddies, organic drift (*Typha*, *Myriophyllum*, *Potamogeton* detritus), submerged objects, shifting substrates, and high conductivity reduced trammel net efficiency above about 5,000 cfs.
- **Migrants:** Tenpounder and striped mullet move upstream from the Gulf of California only when surplus flows reconnect the river to the Gulf.

## Key Findings

- **Razorback sucker absence:** No razorback suckers were captured in 1,176 hours of trammel-net effort, 1,324 hours of hoop-net effort, and about 125 miles of main-channel graphing. The authors state this does not prove absence below Parker Dam, but it indicates severely diminished status.
- **Prior record:** Routine agency sampling in the 3–5 years before the survey found razorbacks only in Senator Wash Reservoir, where adult numbers were estimated at fewer than 200 in 1981.
- **Fishery composition:** Assemblage is dominated by exotic species. Native exceptions were striped mullet (25 specimens, Yuma Division only; mean TL 41.3 cm, mean weight 0.79 kg) and tenpounder (one specimen, 40.5 cm, 0.31 kg, possibly a record).
- **Dominant species:** Carp, bluegill, largemouth bass, and channel catfish were the four most abundant. Carp dominated the Imperial Division catch, with the 40–70 cm size class most common across all divisions.
- **Non-native expansion:** [[Cichlidae]] (tilapia complex, introduced in the 1970s for weed control) were sampled in every division downstream of Palo Verde Diversion Dam except Laguna, with 299 hoop-net and 90 trammel-net individuals in the Yuma Division. Flathead catfish were also expanding, with young and adults reported in Lake Havasu.
- **Gear comparison:** Mann-Whitney U tests found no significant difference in catch rates between trammel and hoop nets in most divisions. The Imperial Division (trammel 1.14 vs. hoop 0.22 fish/hr, Z = 3.66) and the Yuma Division's trammel data were significant. Trammel nets caught the larger, open-water species; hoop nets caught sunfishes and bullheads. The two gear types were judged complementary.
- **Effort trend:** Hoop-net effort increased relative to trammel effort, and total effort per river mile rose slightly as sampling moved downstream.
- **Reproduction:** Successful razorback reproduction to the larval stage was documented in the lower river, but recruitment beyond that stage is limited. Slow growth to maturity, low adult abundance, restricted spawning habitat, and predation on early life stages were cited as causes of a very limited reproductive potential.
- **Management recommendations:** The authors recommend a moratorium on exotic fish introductions, habitat enhancement through mitigation for proposed projects, adjusted size and bag limits on exotic sportfishes, and a major razorback reintroduction and monitoring program in the lower river.
