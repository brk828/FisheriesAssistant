# Recovery Plan for the Aquatic and Riparian Species of Pahranagat Valley

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan (1998, Region 1, Portland, Oregon; prepared by the Reno Fish and Wildlife Office) covers three federally endangered, endemic fishes of [[Pahranagat Valley]]: the [[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]], the [[White River Springfish]], and the [[Hiko White River Springfish]]. The plan updates chub life-history information, describes springfish biology for the first time in a recovery context, and sets out criteria, actions, and costs for delisting.

The chub is confined to about 3.5 km (2.2 mi) of [[Pahranagat Creek]] and 2.5 km (1.6 mi) of irrigation ditch, with an estimated 150 to 260 adults, against a historical range of more than 30 km (18.4 mi). The [[Crystal Spring]] population of Hiko springfish is in danger of extirpation, while the [[Hiko Spring]] population is stable. Primary threats are nonnative species, habitat alteration, and disease.

The recovery goal is delisting of all three species, with delisting potentially initiated in 2015 if criteria are met. The estimated total recovery cost is approximately $992,000, as read from the scanned text; the cost for a pipeline could not be determined.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Pahranagat Valley lies in south-central Lincoln County, about 148 km (92 mi) north of Las Vegas. The valley supports three endangered fishes, one endangered bird, and one threatened bird, plus 22 other species of special concern (1 fish, 2 snails, 2 aquatic beetles, 12 mammals, 5 birds). The plan does not cover the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] or [[Bald Eagle]] recovery in detail.

**Pahranagat Roundtail Chub** (*Gila robusta jordani*). Originally described as a species by Tanner (1950), later treated as a subspecies, and confirmed as distinct by DeMarais (1993). It is greenish with black blotches, has more lateral-line scales than Colorado River roundtail chub, and reaches about 25 cm (10 in). Listed as endangered on October 13, 1970 (35 FR 16047); recovery priority 3.

**White River Springfish and Hiko White River Springfish** (*Crenichthys baileyi*). Small, deep-bodied fishes; White River springfish average about 30 mm total length, and Hiko adults exceed 40 mm. Williams and Wilde (1981) recognized five subspecies in the White River system, and the Ash Springs form is the endangered *C. b. baileyi*. Both were listed as endangered with critical habitat on September 27, 1985 (50 FR 39123); recovery priority 3C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and climate.** The valley is a transition zone between the [[Mojave Desert]] and [[Great Basin]], with average annual precipitation of 17.5 cm (7 in). Spring flows are the main water source; they total about 37,000 cubic meters annually, and much of the water originates at distant points in neighboring valleys. Elevations range from 1,160 m at Hiko to 915 m at the Pahranagat Lakes.

**Springs.**
- [[Ash Springs]]: the southernmost, largest, and warmest, at 31 to 36 °C with mean discharge of 0.56 m³/s. Dissolved oxygen is 1.8 to 5.1 mg/L.
- [[Crystal Spring]]: the most heavily altered, with discharge of 0.169 to 0.31 m³/s. Its water is cooler and has been cooling over time. Dissolved oxygen ranges from 1.3 to 6.4 mg/L.
- [[Hiko Spring]]: the northernmost, at about 27 °C with mean flow of 0.167 m³/s; a historical maximum of 32 °C was recorded in 1934.

**Creek and ditch.** About 90 percent of Pahranagat Creek has been converted to irrigation ditches or dewatered during the irrigation season. Water temperatures range from 18 to 32 °C, and summer temperatures of 29 to 32.2 °C are described as very stressful and potentially lethal for chub. Downstream water quality degrades, mainly from total phosphorus and suspended solids.

**Vegetation and invertebrates.** The plant community is typical of the Mojave Desert, dominated by creosote bush and bursage. Riparian cover includes ash, cottonwood, willow, and grape. Benthic invertebrates include caddisflies, midges, and riffle beetle larvae; the nonnative snail *Melanoides tuberculatus* is predominant downstream.

**Land tenure.** Roughly 98 percent of the riparian ecosystem and listed-species habitat is privately owned. Habitat for the listed species lies in waters surrounded by private land, except 0.04 ha (0.1 acre) at the Ash Springs source managed by the [[Bureau of Land Management]].

**Associated species of concern.** These include the [[Pahranagat Speckled Dace]] (the most common native fish), the [[Pahranagat Montane Vole]], the [[Moapa Warm Springs Riffle Beetle]], the [[Amargosa Naucorid]], the [[Grated Tryonia]] snail, and the [[Pahranagat Pebblesnail]]. Extirpated or extinct native fishes include the Pahranagat spinedace and the White River desert sucker.

## Key Findings

**Population status**
- Pahranagat roundtail chub: 150 to 260 adults (1986 to 1989 estimates); juveniles from 14 in winter to 405 in summer. More recent counts (Stein et al. 1997) show 94 to 306 adults and 18 to 505 juveniles.
- Ash Springs White River springfish: approximately 1,200 to 9,800 fish over the past 10 years; stable to increasing after habitat manipulation ended.
- Hiko White River springfish: Hiko Spring about 12,000 (1990), declined to relatively few by 1994; Crystal Spring fewer than 125; Blue Link Spring about 5,500 and 12,000 (figures as given for 1995).

**Threats**
- Nonnative species were the major cause of decline. Introduced mosquitofish (1963), convict cichlid, and shortfin and sailfin mollies (1964) reduced springfish numbers; mollies and cichlids also prey on eggs and larvae.
- Anchor worm (*Lernaea*) infestation increased sharply at Ash Springs in 1964 and caused a dramatic decline in the native fish population.
- Habitat alteration includes irrigation diversion, concrete channels, algae removal, and swimming in spring pools. Loss of juvenile chub in irrigation channels and springfish stranding in outflows were identified as correctable impacts.
- Riparian degradation from grazing and loss of canopy reduces shade; without it, temperatures may approach lethal levels.

**Reclassification and delisting criteria (chub)**
- Reclassification to threatened: cool-water pools that persist through summer; a self-sustaining population of at least three age-classes in about 75 percent of either 6.8 km (4.7 mi) of Crystal Spring outflow or 10 km (6.2 mi) of creek/ditch, for three generations (minimum 15 consecutive years).
- Delisting: a minimum year-round flow of 1.75 cubic feet per second at the start of the Pahranagat Ditch; enhanced riparian corridor along the Crystal Spring outflow; reduced habitat impacts; and the population criterion across the combined reaches.

**Reclassification and delisting criteria (springfish)**
- White River springfish: self-sustaining population of three or more age-classes in Ash Spring pools for three generations (minimum 6 consecutive years).
- Hiko White River springfish: the same standard in the pools of Hiko and Crystal Springs.

**Critical habitat.** Designated in 1985 for the Hiko springfish (Hiko and Crystal Springs with outflows and 15 m surrounding land) and the White River springfish (Ash Springs and outflow, 15 m surrounding). No critical habitat has been designated for the chub.

**Recovery actions (four narrative tasks)**
1. Maintain and enhance aquatic and riparian habitats, including conservation agreements with willing landowners and a county-wide [[Habitat Conservation Plan]].
2. Develop and implement monitoring plans for fishes, mammals, and invertebrates.
3. Provide public information and education, given that most habitat is on private land.
4. Establish and maintain refugia populations at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico), [[Blue Link Spring]] (Mineral County, Nevada), and preferred new sites at Key Pittman Wildlife Management Area and [[Pahranagat National Wildlife Refuge]].

**Timeline and cost.** Recovery could be achieved by 2015 if actions proceed on schedule. Delisting requires that the five ESA listing factors (habitat loss, overutilization, disease or predation, inadequate regulation, and other factors) be shown to be no longer threatening, per 50 CFR 424.11. Estimated costs for the first four years are 152 (1998), 126 (2000), and 105 (2001), with the 1999 figure illegible in the scan; units are as given in the source.
