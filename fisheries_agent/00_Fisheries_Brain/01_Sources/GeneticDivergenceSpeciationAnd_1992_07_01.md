## Executive Summary

This study by Sturmbauer & Meyer (1992) uses mitochondrial DNA sequencing (control region and cytochrome *b*) to investigate genetic divergence, speciation, and morphological evolution in the endemic **[[Tropheus]]** lineage of cichlid fishes from **[[Lake Tanganyika]]**. Despite encompassing six taxonomically described species and over 50 distinct color "races," the *Tropheus* lineage shows striking **morphological stasis** coupled with extraordinarily **high genetic divergence** — nearly double that of the entire Lake Malawi cichlid flock and six times that of the Lake Victoria flock. The findings suggest that historical lake-level fluctuations, rather than morphological adaptation, structured genetic diversification and speciation in this lineage, and that coloration (a sexually/socially selected trait) diverges much faster than body morphology.

## Background & Description

- *Tropheus* is morphologically **distinct** from other genera in the tribe Tropheini (e.g., *[[Simochromis Babaulti]]*) but internally **highly conserved** across all six recognized species.
- Species are distinguished taxonomically mainly by **meristic characters** (e.g., number of anal spines) and **coloration**, not by substantial skeletal or body-shape divergence.
- *Tropheus duboisi*, the most genetically divergent species (sister to all other *Tropheus*), differs from congeners only subtly — a **narrower mouth** and minor **dentitional differences**.
- No intermediate morphotypes exist between major lineages, consistent with rapid ecological niche-filling followed by long-term **stabilizing selection** on morphology.
- Coloration, in stark contrast to body morphology, is **highly variable** — over 50 distinctly colored geographic "races" are recognized, including sympatric sister species (e.g., at Bulu, 3.1% sequence divergence) that are "remarkably differently coloured."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- *Tropheus* are **strictly rock-dwelling (rheophilic/petricolous)** cichlids, confined to rocky littoral habitats for both **foraging** and **mating**.
- They possess **very limited dispersal capacity across open water**, making them highly sensitive to habitat fragmentation caused by shoreline and lake-level changes.
- Populations are geographically structured around specific rocky shore localities (e.g., Bemba, Kabimba, Kigoma-area villages), with genetic lineages often tracking historical shoreline configurations rather than current taxonomic boundaries.
- Major **lake-level fluctuations** (a drop of ~600 m occurring 75,000–200,000 years ago) likely split Lake Tanganyika into three isolated sub-lakes, driving **allopatric fragmentation**, localized extinction in shallow zones, and survival of populations on steep rocky shores — directly shaping the modern biogeographic and genetic pattern.
- Secondary lake-level shifts (a further ~400 m drop ~20,000–30,000 years ago in the Mpulungu Bay area) appear to have caused **secondary admixture** between previously isolated populations.
- Broader context: Lake Tanganyika hosts 171 cichlid species in 49 endemic genera across at least seven ancient, independently evolving lineages, making it the most ecologically, morphologically, and behaviorally diverse of the African Great Lakes' cichlid assemblages — compared to the more recent, explosively radiated flocks of **[[Lake Malawi]]** (~500–700 species, ~700,000 years old) and **[[Lake Victoria]]** (~300 species, ~200,000 years old).

## Key Findings

- **Extraordinary genetic diversity despite morphological stasis**: Maximum corrected mtDNA control-region sequence divergence within *Tropheus* = **14.5%**, compared to 8.2% for the entire Lake Malawi flock (~500 species) and 2.3% for the Lake Victoria flock (~300 species) — despite *Tropheus* comprising only six species with minimal morphological differentiation.
- Cytochrome *b* divergence within *Tropheus* reaches **5%**, versus <2% across the whole Malawi flock, reinforcing the control-region results.
- Estimated age of the *Tropheus* lineage: **~1.25 million years**, substantially older than the Malawi (~700,000 yr) and Victoria (~200,000 yr) flocks, despite far less morphological diversification.
- **Phylogenetic structure**: *T. duboisi* splits off first as sister to all other *Tropheus*. Following this split, **two successive radiation events** occurred:
  - A **primary radiation** producing six genetically distinct lineages (mean corrected divergence 8.2% ± 1.4% in control region), likely coinciding with initial colonization of rocky shores lake-wide.
  - Multiple **secondary radiations** within each primary lineage (mean divergence 2.7% ± 1.0%), possibly triggered by the same major lake-level drop, suggesting synchronous diversification across lineages.
- One lineage is **geographically widespread** across the lake and shows evidence of **cross-lake colonization**, contrasting with the other five lineages' restricted distributions — interpreted as evidence that low lake-level periods created land/shoreline bridges permitting dispersal.
- Genetically near-identical populations across the lake (e.g., Kabimba vs. Ikola: 0.9% divergence, lower than divergence to geographically closer populations on the same shore) support historic trans-lake colonization events during low-stand periods.
- Within-population variation was low at some sites (Bemba: 8/10 fish identical) but substantial at others (Mpulungu: 8/10 fish showed 1–17 substitution differences, 3.8% divergence), consistent with historical admixture from neighboring populations (Kasanga, Kala, Wampembe) following lake-level–driven bay drainage events.
- The molecular phylogeny **conflicts with current taxonomy** based on coloration and anal-spine counts, prompting the authors to recommend **taxonomic revision using molecular data**.
- Supports **Greenwood's hypothesis** that major Tanganyikan cichlid lineages diversified rapidly upon initial lake colonization and then underwent long-term morphological stasis, likely maintained by **stabilizing selection** within tightly packed ecological communities.
- Proposes that **coloration**, as a sexually/socially selected trait important in conspecific mate recognition, can diverge much faster than naturally-selected morphological traits — potentially acting as a **reproductive isolating mechanism** that facilitates speciation without concurrent morphological change, a model relevant to the broader "explosive speciation" phenomenon in East African cichlid **[[Species Flocks]]**.
