# Growth of Juvenile Tilapia Reared on Different Diets

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This short report by J. J. Hauser, Assistant Research Biologist at the University of California, compares the growth of juvenile [[Tilapia Zillii]] fed four different diets. The fish were obtained from the [[Imperial Irrigation District]], which was seeking to raise large numbers of fish for stocking in a short time at reasonable cost. The report is an unpublished internal document and was not to be cited without the author's approval.

Trout pellets and catfish pellets produced the best growth. Rabbit pellets and lettuce produced slow growth. The author recommends catfish pellets as the practical feed because they are cheaper and more readily available. The author also recommends avoiding rabbit pellets and terrestrial vegetable matter as feed.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** The Imperial Irrigation District wanted to stock fish that reach maturity quickly and cheaply. The experiment aimed to evaluate juvenile growth on different diets.
- **Stock:** Juveniles were obtained from the Imperial Irrigation District rearing ponds. Only juvenile fish were used.
- **Design:** Four feeds, each with two replicates, were tested. Feeds were trout pellets, catfish pellets, rabbit pellets, and lettuce ([[Diet Composition]], Table 1).
- **Stocking density:** Initial stocking was one fish per 10-gallon aquarium.
- **Feeding:** Fish were fed to excess nearly every day.
- **Conditions:** Photoperiod was natural and increasing. Temperature was nearly constant, with a lowest average of 85°F and a highest of 86.7°F.
- **Measurements:** Average weights and lengths were calculated by combining fish from both replicates of each diet. Growth was tracked until the fish reached maturity or the trial ended. Condition was expressed with the [[Condition Factor]] (K-factor), calculated as K = 10⁵ × weight / length³. Other biological factors were assessed qualitatively.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Growth and condition were measured under controlled, constant-temperature tank conditions. Results are therefore tied to warm water and the fixed tank setup.
- The herbivorous diet (lettuce) was compared with the natural diet of herbivorous fish in the wild. The author notes that wild herbivorous fish eat a much more balanced diet, including large quantities of microscopic animals and plants attached to the vegetation they ingest.
- Fish that approached maturity showed increased aggression, and growth of the smallest fish may have been suppressed. The author noted this as a possible effect of crowding or social dominance.

## Key Findings

1. **Best diets:** Trout pellets and catfish pellets gave similar and the best growth of the four diets.
2. **Poor diets:** Rabbit pellets and lettuce produced slow growth to a desirable size. Lettuce growth increased during the final 22-day period.
3. **Protein and condition:** A direct correlation was found between K-factor and the proportion of protein in the diet. No definite correlation was found between diet and size at maturity or fecundity.
4. **Intestinal fat:** Catfish-pellet-fed juveniles had more stored intestinal fat than trout-pellet-fed juveniles, even though trout-pellet-fed fish had the higher K-factor. The author suggests internal organ or muscle development may be greater in the trout-pellet group. Table 2 reports intestinal fat deposits as moderate to abundant for the diets.
5. **Lettuce consumption:** Juveniles fed lettuce ate an average of 1.15 times their body weight per 24 hours over a 23-day period.
6. **Waste:** Rabbit pellets and lettuce both left large amounts of residue in the tanks.
7. **Comparison with earlier work:** Growth on trout and catfish pellets was similar to that of caged juveniles reported in the first annual report.
8. **Recommendation:** Catfish pellets are probably the best feed choice for practical use because they are cheaper and more readily available than trout pellets.
9. **Limitations:** The author states that the results are a guide for rearing, not conclusive proof of the best diet. The sample was small, and final proof would require raising much larger numbers of fish and evaluating maturity onset, fecundity, offspring health, and other parameters. The author did not feel such a study was necessary at that point.
