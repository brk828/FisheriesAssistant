# Grand Canyon Humpback Chub Funding Proposal — Correspondence Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #conservation-policy #endangered-species

## Executive Summary
This document is a 1989 administrative letter from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 6, Denver) to Duane L. Shroufe, Director of the Arizona Game and Fish Department, addressing a funding dispute over Grand Canyon [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) studies. The Bureau of Reclamation and Western Area Power Administration had proposed diverting funds earmarked for the [[Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin]] (the "Recovery Program") to support humpback chub research in the Grand Canyon — a population geographically outside the Program's defined scope. Following interagency discussion, Reclamation and Western agreed to withdraw the proposal and instead fund Grand Canyon studies through alternative, non-Recovery-Program funds. The letter reaffirms the biological importance of the Grand Canyon humpback chub population to the species' overall recovery and calls for improved coordination between upper and lower Colorado River Basin management entities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat/Range**: The [[Humpback Chub]] population discussed inhabits the **Grand Canyon** reach of the **Colorado River**, extending into the **Little Colorado River** confluence — a region administratively and biologically distinct from the **Upper Colorado River Basin** (excluding the San Juan River Basin) covered by the Recovery Program.
- **Population significance**: The Grand Canyon population is explicitly identified by the Service as **"critical to the survival of the species"** and a key component of the overall recovery strategy for *Gila cypha*, as detailed in the current [[Humpback Chub Recovery Plan]].
- **Management framework fragmentation**: The species' range spans two jurisdictionally separate basins (upper and lower Colorado River Basin), each governed by different recovery programs, funding streams, and state-level participants. Arizona was not a signatory participant in the Upper Basin's Recovery Program, despite hosting a biologically critical population.
- **Co-occurring taxa**: The letter references other **"big river" fishes** of the Colorado River system as also being subjects of coordinated recovery efforts, implying a broader multi-species conservation context (likely including [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]], the other Colorado River Basin endangered "big river" fishes typically grouped with humpback chub).

## Key Findings
- The **Grand Canyon humpback chub population falls outside the geographic scope** of the Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Program, creating a funding and jurisdictional conflict.
- Reclamation's and Western's original proposal to redirect Recovery Program funds to lower-basin (Grand Canyon) studies was **withdrawn** after review by the [[Implementation Committee]], with acknowledgment they may resubmit a similar proposal in the future.
- Reclamation committed to funding Grand Canyon humpback chub studies via **alternative funding sources**, preserving the integrity of Upper Basin recovery funding.
- **Arizona's non-participant status** in the Recovery Program was identified as a structural barrier requiring resolution before joint recovery actions in the Grand Canyon/Little Colorado River could proceed.
- The Service proposed **increased inter-basin communication**, including sending [[Dean Hendrickson]] to brief the Upper Basin's Technical Group (chaired by [[Jim Bennett]] of the Colorado Division of Wildlife) on lower-basin recovery activities at a scheduled June 1989 meeting.
- The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] offered to review the lower basin's draft implementation plan once available, signaling a move toward **coordinated, range-wide recovery planning** for *Gila cypha*.
- This correspondence illustrates a broader theme in endangered species management: **administrative/jurisdictional boundaries** (Upper vs. Lower Colorado River Basin) can create friction with **biological realities** of species distribution and conservation priority.
