# A Preliminary Key to the Identification of Larval Fishes with Particular Reference to Canton Reservoir, Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #larval-fish

## Executive Summary

This document is Bulletin Number 5 of the [[Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory]], published in August 1967 by Edward B. May and Charles R. Gasaway. It presents a preliminary dichotomous key for identifying the larval and early juvenile stages of freshwater fishes, with particular reference to [[Canton Reservoir]] in Oklahoma. The authors also provide a selected bibliography of early life history references, a summary of larval development terminology, and general notes on morphological development. The work was conducted as part of a federal project (F-5-R) and is explicitly described as preliminary, intended to be revised as additional information becomes available.

## Background & Description

The authors note that fisheries investigators often need to identify young fishes to address ecological and biological problems, but that identification information is scattered through life history studies. The key was built from specimens collected from Canton Reservoir by meter-net hauls through [[Plankton Net Sampling]] (a 3/4-inch square-mesh meter net towed by a 24-foot outboard boat) during two years of investigation, from early March through August 1965 and 1966. Additional specimens came from other Oklahoma and regional sources, including the [[University of Oklahoma Museum of Zoology]] and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

The reservoir fish fauna was estimated at approximately 28 species, including 2 species of [[Catostomidae]] (suckers), 7 [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes) and 7 [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows and carps), with the most difficult groups to identify being the minnows, sunfishes and suckers.

Terminology was defined for early life stages: yolk-larva (Y), larva (L), prolarva (pL), early postlarva (Ep), late postlarva (Lp) and juvenile (J). The authors also state that the key is based on characters useful for identification, omitting detailed allometric and meristic data, and that scale counts for juveniles were omitted in most cases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors stress that environmental factors such as light, temperature and dissolved substances can strongly influence meristic characters and pigmentation. Variation among specimens of the same species was a major complication, and fishes collected from natural environments often looked strikingly different from those reared in artificial conditions. Preservation also affects appearance: most fishes fade when preserved, and pigment loss is progressive. Specimens should be fixed and stored in buffered formalin (2–5%), and light exposure slows pigment loss.

The authors also explain that prolarvae were not included in the key because yolk-bearing stages are short relative to later stages, and prolarvae of many species were collected but could not be reliably identified. Identifications should remain tentative until large series of a species have been examined, and the key is recommended for use with alternate couplet reading and comparison to figures and literature.

## Key Findings

- **Purpose and scope:** The key addresses larval and juvenile freshwater fishes of Oklahoma, emphasizing Canton Reservoir, and covers taxa such as [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Gar]], [[Longnose Gar]], [[Shortnose Gar]], [[Alligator Gar]], [[Walleye]], [[Sauger]], [[Sunfish]] species, [[Black Bullhead]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]].
- **Species excluded:** Several taxa were omitted due to rarity or uncertain identification, including [[Paddlefish]] (Polyodon spathula), [[Bowfin]] (Amia calva) and [[Mooneye]] (Hiodon tergisus) in some cases.
- **Key structure:** The key is a dichotomous series of couplets, with features such as fin ray counts, pigmentation patterns, gut shape, body depth, barbels and the position of the dorsal fin relative to the anal fin.
- **Development of fins:** Pectoral and caudal fins usually develop first; anal and dorsal fins are most important for identification and generally develop at about the same rate, while the pelvic fins are the last to develop.
- **Gut morphology:** The length, shape and visibility of the gut is described as one of the best characters for identifying larval fishes.
- **Pigment development:** Most fishes have little or no pigment at hatching, and it takes several days to develop. Pigment fades rapidly in preserved specimens.
- **Bibliography:** The selected bibliography, arranged by family, lists roughly 200 references on larval identification, research methods, materials and techniques, including works by [[Moore]] (1954, 1957), [[Hubbs]] and [[Lagler]] (1958), and [[Trautman]] (1957).
- **Usage cautions:** Users are advised to compare specimens with the plates and literature, avoid over-confident identification, and be aware that environmental variation and aberrant specimens can produce misidentification.
- **Authors' stated limitation:** The authors state that the work is preliminary and should be revised as additional information becomes available.
