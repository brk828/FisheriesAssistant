# Effects of Brook Trout Predation on a Crayfish Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by Walter T. Momot, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, examines how [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) predation affects a population of [[Virile Crayfish]] (*Orconectes virilis*) in [[West Lost Lake]], a closed marl lake in Michigan's [[Pigeon River Trout Research Area]]. The work quantifies energy transfer between trophic levels and assesses fish predation as an interspecies interaction. Trout predation proved to be a weak control on crayfish numbers, removing a small share of the standing stock and net production. The author concludes that predation was not an important population control mechanism and that the crayfish stock could sustain considerably more predation.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** The small marl lakes of the Pigeon River Trout Research Area have no inlets or outlets, restricted drainage, and a single stocked fish species, making them useful natural laboratories for [[Trophic Ecology]] research.
- **Fish population:** Brook trout are the only fish in West Lost Lake. Because there is no natural reproduction, fish averaging 126 mm are planted annually at 3,300 per surface hectare. Trout population density was estimated each spring and fall in 1962 and 1963 using the [[Petersen Mark-and-Recapture Method]] with [[D-C Electrofishing]].
- **Crayfish population:** Virile crayfish are abundant in the lake. Young-of-the-year crayfish stay near the shore, while adults are distributed throughout the lake. Crayfish were marked by pleuron clipping and sampled by seining, and population sizes were estimated with the [[Schumacher Mark-and-Recapture Method]].
- **Diet analysis:** A total of 273 trout stomachs were examined between August 1962 and September 1963, representing a major portion of the fish present (population did not exceed 600 fish).
- **Analytical framework:** Equilibrium yield was calculated using the [[Ricker Equilibrium Yield Equation]] by substituting predation rates for fishing mortality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predator size selection:** Only trout larger than 229 mm fed extensively on crayfish. The smallest trout containing a crayfish was 196 mm, and only 4 of 29 crayfish-containing fish were under 229 mm.
- **Prey size selection:** Trout were effective predators only on crayfish up to about one year old. Older crayfish were too large to be captured.
- **Seasonal pattern:** Winter lacks emerging insects, which are important in early summer, so predation on crayfish intensifies in midwinter. Crayfish consumption declined abruptly in April. In October and November no crayfish appeared in stomachs, but trout eggs did.
- **Other predators:** Dragonfly nymphs, small frogs, water snakes, painted turtles, fish-eating birds, and mammals were judged unlikely to regulate population size. Ice cover limits bird and mammal predation in winter.
- **Other mortality:** Molting was observed to influence mortality, with sudden drops in certain age groups following molts.
- **Energy flow:** Much of the energy entering the crayfish population was not transferred to trout. It returned to the lake through decomposition or was used within the population, including through cannibalism.
- **Biomass state:** Crayfish biomass was probably near the food-producing capacity of the lake, as shown by little change between summer 1962 and summer 1963.

## Key Findings

- **Low predation mortality:** Trout predation removed less than 3% of age-0.0 to 0.5-year crayfish from June to January and 16% of age-0.5 to 1.0 crayfish from January to May.
- **Winter share:** Trout predation accounted for 56% of total young-of-the-year mortality between October and June.
- **Summer share:** Predation between July and October accounted for 2.3% of total young-of-the-year mortality.
- **Annual utilization:** Trout used only 2.9% of the total net production of crayfish biomass and 4.2% of the available net production of young-of-the-year.
- **Standing crop figures:** Standing crop was 150.2 kg in summer 1962, 179.1 kg in spring 1963, and 137.6 kg in summer 1963. Annual net production was estimated at 310.8 kg, about 2.3 times the summer standing crop.
- **Energy bottleneck:** The author describes this low energy transfer as an important bottleneck, making trout an ineffective predator and a minor factor in controlling crayfish size.
- **Sustainable predation:** Equilibrium yield analysis indicated maximum crayfish yield at about twice the current predation rate, with a twofold increase raising total young-of-the-year yield by 33%.
- **Population response:** Predation changed the age distribution through survivorship rather than fecundity. The stock could withstand much more predation than it received.
- **Management options:** The author suggested stocking larger trout (above 126 mm) to reach the 229 mm effective predator length, introducing the smaller [[Ohio Crayfish]] (*Orconectes propinquus*), or stocking a fish species that eats large adult crayfish.
- **Comparative energy:** One adult female (9.3 g) was judged more valuable energetically than six young (4.2 g), so cropping young is less efficient for trout.
- **Overall conclusion:** Predation was not an important population control mechanism in West Lost Lake.
