# Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This is the 1993 draft U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 2) recovery plan for the [[Desert Pupfish]], *Cyprinodon macularius*, listed as endangered throughout its range. Prepared by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) and Donald W. Sada (Bishop, California), with assistance from the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the Tonto National Forest, the plan covers three recognized forms: the [[Colorado River Pupfish]] subspecies (*C. m. macularius*), the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*C. m. eremus*), and an undescribed form in Rio Sonoyta, Sonora.

The recovery objective is to downlist the Colorado River form (delisting is not considered feasible in the foreseeable future) and to protect the other two forms; downlisting of the Quitobaquito form appears unattainable. Projected downlisting of *C. m. macularius* is expected in 2009 if criteria are met. The estimated total cost is about $1,272,000 (in thousands, $1,272 k), excluding acquisition costs.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and form:** The species is native to the lower Colorado River system and to the Salton Sink. Natural populations of the Colorado River form have been extirpated from Arizona, and the species is restricted to three natural locations in California and irrigation drains around the Salton Sea, plus restricted areas of the Colorado River Delta in Sonora and Baja California.
- **Morphology:** The body is thickened and chubby or markedly compressed in adults, with a high and deep profile and a broad, short, sharply sloping dorsal profile. Males are blue-tinted during breeding, a trait long recognized by Baird and Girard (1853) and Miller.
- **Distribution:** The Quitobaquito form persists in a single modified spring at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Arizona. The Rio Sonoyta form's distribution is unknown but believed to be quite limited.
- **Reproduction and growth:** Breeding is extended (early spring into winter, when water temperature exceeds about 20°C), and growth varies with temperature.
- **Food and feeding:** Covered in an appendix on [[Foods and Feeding Habits]] and co-occurring native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat tolerance.** The species tolerates wide temperature fluctuations, low dissolved oxygen, and high salinity, but it does not cope effectively with introduced non-native fish.

**Threats.** Habitat loss, habitat modification, pollution, and competition and predation from non-native fishes are the principal threats. Key non-native predators and competitors include:
- [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*)
- [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*)
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and juvenile sunfish
- African cichlids (*Tilapia* spp.)
- Golden shiner, which was introduced into Quitobaquito Spring in 1968 or 1969 and eradicated at high cost
- Black bullhead, found in Quitobaquito Spring in 1993
- Non-native [[American Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*), which in one artificial pond at Arizona State University reduced a population of thousands to near extinction with fewer than 20 adults

**Other threats.** Aerial pesticide drift near populations (e.g., Rio Sonoyta, lower San Felipe Creek) and the ongoing diversion, impoundment, and groundwater pumping in both countries are continuing risks. Mexican populations face proliferation of non-native fishes in recent years.

**Salton Sea.** Interactions with introduced mosquitofish were early contributors to pupfish decline in the [[Salton Sea]]. Pupfish now persist in tributary streams, a few shoreline pools, and irrigation drains, where invasion by centrarchids, cichlids, ictalurids, and poeciliids remains a threat.

## Key Findings

**Recovery criteria**
- Secure, maintain, and replicate all extant natural populations.
- Re-establish replicate populations in the most natural, identifiable habitats within the probable historical range.
- A population is considered established only after persisting for at least ten years.
- Protect and establish refugium populations of Quitobaquito and Rio Sonoyta forms.
- A viable population is defined as not fewer than 500 overwintering adults (or existing numbers, whichever is greater) in a normal sex ratio with in-situ reproduction and recruitment.

**Tiered population strategy**
- **Tier 1:** Extant wild populations, the most valuable remaining reservoir of original genetic material, with the highest level of protection.
- **Tier 2:** Re-established populations in the most natural available habitats, expected to persist without human intervention.
- **Tier 3:** Populations in natural or "quasi-natural" refugia, which must still be suitable for long-term maintenance.
- Planned target numbers (in Arizona, California, Colorado Delta, and Rio Sonoyta) total roughly 45 tier 2 and 27 tier 3 populations, with tier 1 totals listed for each region.

**Recovery tasks (six to seven)**
1. Protect natural populations and their habitats (identify landowners, secure water rights, remove non-native fish, and renovate contaminated sites).
2. Re-establish populations across tiers.
3. Establish a refugium population of Quitobaquito pupfish (minimum of about 500 fish, approximately 1:1 sex ratio).
4. Develop a protocol for exchange of genetic material, using quantitative modelling.
5. Monitor natural, re-established, and refugium populations twice yearly, with periodic genetic examination at five-year intervals.
6. Determine factors affecting population persistence (life history, habitat preferences, and habitat criteria).
7. Information and education.

**Downlisting and delisting**
- Downlisting of *C. m. macularius* is expected to take about 15 years after plan approval; total recovery (delisting) is not considered feasible in the foreseeable future.
- Water rights and habitat are considered secure when a government or organization with statutory authority, or a legally binding agreement of more than 25 years, provides adequate rights and management for ten years or more.

**Historical transplant record**
- Tens of known transplantations (more than 100 entries in the appendix) were made across Arizona, California, Sonora, and other states. Of about 20 Arizona populations listed as of 1989, 8 were lost to habitat modification, 2 were destroyed by exotic fishes, 1 was renovated, and 9 failed for unknown reasons.
- Several stocks of questionable genetic purity (e.g., Bog Hole Tank, Finley Tank) were found outside the historic range and are recommended for renovation.

**Public comment and responses**
- The public draft received 18 letters from agencies and individuals. Notable issues included the feasibility of protecting aquatic habitat, the concept of historic range, and the one-way genetic exchange recommended by the plan.
- Comments on Salton Sea populations, irrigation drains, and the San Felipe Creek and Salt Creek populations led to revisions acknowledging populations within irrigation drains.
- The Bureau of Reclamation and the International Boundary and Water Commission raised concerns about extraterritorial application and groundwater treaties; implementation had not begun at the time of the comments.
