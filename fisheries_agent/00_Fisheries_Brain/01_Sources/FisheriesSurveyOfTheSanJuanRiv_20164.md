# Fisheries Survey of the San Juan River, Utah, 1987

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] (UDWR) and the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR) in February 1988, documents the fish community of the San Juan River, Utah, and its transition zone with the San Juan Arm of [[Lake Powell]]. Sampling ran March through September 1987 using [[Electrofishing]], seines, and gill and trammel nets. The survey confirmed reproduction of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (15 young-of-the-year collected), documented 12 adult [[Razorback Sucker]] in the reservoir transition zone, and found the fish community dominated by [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Channel Catfish]] in riverine habitat, and by exotic [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] in backwaters. The authors conclude that the San Juan River should be integrated into recovery planning for endangered Colorado River fishes.

## Background & Description

The report was produced under Cooperative Agreement 7-FC-40-05050 between UDWR and BOR. Funding came from Section 6 of the [[Endangered Species Act]], BOR, and Utah nongame checkoff monies.

The four endangered or candidate fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin are the Colorado squawfish (protected), humpback chub and bonytail chub (protected), and razorback sucker (candidate for listing). Historically these species were restricted to the mainstem Colorado River and large tributaries. Main threats include dam construction, watershed degradation and water withdrawal, and introduction of exotic species.

The San Juan River drains 99,200 km² and rises in the San Juan Mountains of southwestern Colorado. The study covered about 136 river miles (RMI) from the U.S. Highway 160 bridge in Colorado to Piute Farms Marina on Lake Powell. Above Chinle Creek the channel is broad and braided; below it, the river is entrenched in canyons cut into the Monument Upwarp. [[Navajo Dam]] (completed 1963) regulates flows, and the average discharge at the Bluff gaging station (1914–1986) was 2,577 cfs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Flows were historically highly variable, with a recorded peak of 70,000 cfs (September 10, 1927) and zero-flow periods in 1934 and 1939. Navajo Dam has moderated these extremes and lowered the annual temperature regime, particularly above the Animas River confluence. Flows in 1987 were well above post-dam monthly means from January through July and resembled pre-dam regimes.

The river was divided into three geomorphologic areas:
- **Four Corners to Sand Island (RMI 136.2–78.0):** broad floodplain with braided channels, the most backwater habitat.
- **Sand Island to Mexican Hat (RMI 78–60):** transitional zone where the river enters the San Juan Canyon.
- **Mexican Hat to Grand Gulch (RMI 60–16):** deep, narrow, single-channel canyon with few backwaters.

Backwaters were prominent mainly at low flows in late summer and fall. Canyon sections had few backwaters and little apparent chub habitat (no large, deep eddies comparable to Desolation or Westwater canyons).

## Key Findings

**Endangered and rare fishes**
- **Colorado Squawfish:** One adult (637 mm) was tagged by BOR in the Lake Powell arm on April 7, 1987, and recaptured by UDWR at RMI 89.8 on September 8, 1987. Fifteen YOY (26–34 mm TL) were seined in fall (six by UDWR between RMI 102 and 93; nine by BOR between RMI 14 and 6). Hatching was estimated to occur in early to mid-July 1987, indicating reproduction in the braided reach.
- **Razorback Sucker:** Twelve adults were netted at RMI 0.0 near Piute Farms Marina in March–April 1987; eight were ripe males. Six were recaptured at the original site. No razorbacks were found in riverine habitat.
- **Gila spp. (probably [[Roundtail Chub]]):** Two specimens (one juvenile, one YOY) were seined, at RMI 103 and RMI 93.5. Historical records of humpback and bonytail chub from the San Juan are lacking.

**Fish community**
- Electrofishing (UDWR, 94 hrs total, catch 1,979 in standardized reaches) was dominated by unidentified suckers (30%), flannelmouth sucker (30%), carp (27%), and channel catfish (10%).
- Backwater seining (UDWR, 38 sites) was dominated by red shiner (51%) and fathead minnow (25%); mosquitofish made up 8%.
- The transition zone electrofishing catch was dominated by channel catfish (60%) and carp (31%). Gill and trammel nets (spring 5,616 net-hours) caught mainly carp (68%) and striped bass (20%).
- Native suckers, carp, and flannelmouth sucker were significantly more abundant in braided reaches; channel catfish were more abundant in canyon reaches.
- Striped bass, an exotic predator, were encountered widely, including 114 fish in RMI 9–5 (28.5 fish/mile).

**Habitat**
- Backwaters were significantly more numerous than expected in the braided area (29 found) and fewer than expected in the lower canyon (5 found).
- Backwaters averaged 736 m² in the braided area, 631 m² in the middle reach, and 367 m² in the canyon.
- Spring channel width averaged 59 m (braided), 80 m (middle canyon), and 64 m (lower canyon); spring average depth was 1.2, 2.0, and 2.7 m respectively.
- Cobble substrate suggests potential spawning habitat, but sedimentation may limit embryo survival. Sand increased downstream.

**Management recommendations for 1988**
- Target spawning areas with spring electrofishing, radio telemetry, larval drift nets, and fall backwater seining.
- Evaluate genetic identity of Colorado squawfish stocks and consider hatchery maintenance and supplemental stocking, preferably in the braided reach where predators are fewer.
- Survey the San Juan further for razorback sucker, including off-channel impoundments near Bluff, and begin spring electrofishing by mid-March.
- Net for Gila spp. in canyon reaches RMI 74–69 and RMI 52–44.
- The authors suggest that high summer flows may favor native reproduction and suppress exotic fishes, a hypothesis that needs testing.

**Overall conclusion:** The San Juan River supports limited but reproducing populations of endangered fishes, and it should be added as a third sub-basin to Upper Colorado River recovery planning.
