# Protocol for Introductions of Threatened and Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #conservation

## Executive Summary

This draft document, prepared by the Western Division Endangered Species Committee of the [[American Fisheries Society]], presents a standardized protocol for introducing threatened and endangered fishes. Introductions are a common component of recovery programs for these species, with approximately 80% of listed fishes having recovery plans that call for them. Few introductions have followed a systematic procedure, and some have failed or harmed other rare native organisms.

The protocol applies mainly when an introduction is proposed to supplement an existing population or establish a new one, though parts may be useful in other situations. It has three components: (1) selecting the introduction site, (2) conducting the introduction, and (3) post-introduction monitoring, reporting and analysis. The authors call for better documentation and analysis of introduction attempts so future efforts have a greater chance of success.

## Background & Description

The document opens with the case of the [[Amistad Gambusia]]. In August 1968, 30 to 40 individuals were collected from a spring about to be flooded by the rising waters of the Rio Grande reservoir. The stock was later moved to a facility in Texas, then to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico in 1974. When the stocks were examined in the 1980s, they consisted solely of [[Mosquitofish]] that had hybridized with the gambusia, and the species was considered extinct. The authors use this case to show why planned introductions matter.

Most recovery programs for listed fishes are planned over a longer period than this emergency. Under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service must develop recovery plans for listed species, and introductions have become a prominent feature of many of them.

Key background points from the literature:

- **Reporting gaps:** Results of introductions with endangered and threatened fishes are seldom reported, and even less often analyzed to determine why they succeeded or failed.
- **Prior review:** One earlier review reported that only two of 12 introductions of a chub were successful. The source text is partly illegible at this point, so the exact species and figures should be checked against the original.
- **Arizona fishes:** Introduction attempts were reviewed for a variety of rare Arizona fishes, including the [[Razorback Sucker]].
- **Recovery plans reviewed:** Plans for 37 listed threatened and endangered fishes in the United States were examined. Of these, 30 fishes (81%) had recovery plans that used introductions, usually in addition to habitat protection and other recovery actions.
- **Species without introductions:** Listed fishes without proposed introductions included two cyprinids (Borax Lake chub and Mohave chub), several cavefishes (Ozark and Alabama cavefishes), and several darters (slackwater, fountain, and snail darters). The [[Bayou Darter]] has provisions for introductions if the existing population appears in imminent danger of loss.

The authors note that species without proposed introductions often have highly restricted ranges and difficult habitats. In such cases, conservation effort is better spent protecting the existing habitat than expanding the species beyond its natural range. Fish from non-native locations, or from warm springs, may also carry little of the genetic variation needed to adapt to new environments.

Introduced stocks can also face problems when they are moved to habitats that differ greatly from their native ones. When [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] were moved from their cavern to a cement refugium at [[Hoover Dam]], productivity and food supply were higher and the environment more exposed. The resulting population had larger, more brightly colored bodies and differed in several measurable characteristics (Williams 1977).

For the 30 fishes with proposed introductions, recovery plans cite five main purposes:

1. Information and education
2. Genetic reserve or artificial propagation
3. Biological control agent
4. Supplementing an existing population
5. Establishing new populations

Creating a sportfishery is a secondary goal for some trout introductions. Procedures for each introduction will vary with its intended purpose.

Examples of species in recovery programs using these approaches include [[Little Kern Golden Trout]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Owens Pupfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and [[Gila Topminnow]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The protocol is built around the risks of moving fish into new habitats. Its main ecological concerns are:

- **Native range:** Introductions outside a species' historic range can change ecosystems and should be approached with great caution. Introductions within the historic range are preferred. Where a species is clearly threatened within its native range and cannot be restored there, out-of-range introduction may be a last resort, for example to a hatchery.
- **Protected sites:** Sites must be secure from imminent or future habitat destruction. Public or private land dedicated to protecting the species is preferred, backed by a management agreement with the landowner or land-management agency.
- **Dispersal:** Depending on the goal, sites should either have little opportunity for further dispersal (for out-of-range introductions) or, when expanding an existing population within its range, allow dispersal routes.
- **Hybridization:** Introductions should not be made where the listed fish could hybridize with other species or subspecies. This is especially important for fishes from isolated drainages, which have had little opportunity to evolve reproductive barriers. The Amistad case shows the outcome when this is ignored.
- **Other rare species:** Pre-introduction surveys should identify rare invertebrates or aquatic plants that the introduction could harm. Consulting taxonomists in entomology, malacology or invertebrate zoology could have prevented the loss of a snail population at Fish Slough, California, during an Owens pupfish introduction.

## Key Findings

### 1. Site selection

- Restrict introductions to native or historic habitat where possible.
- Choose secure, protected sites with a management agreement.
- Match the site to dispersal goals and to the species' life history (food, spawning and rearing habitat, with conditions similar to undisturbed habitat).
- Ensure the site can support a viable population. Allendorf and Ryman (1987) recommended an effective population size of at least 200 for hatchery stocks of salmonids. In the wild, a much larger census population is needed to offset unbalanced sex ratios, age structure and stochastic events such as floods and droughts. This may require habitat able to support many thousands of individuals.
- Avoid sites where hybridization is possible.

### 2. Conducting the introduction

- **Stock source:** Use stock from the most appropriate source. Where a species has several populations, choose the one most similar geographically or ecologically to the target site. Avoid mixing stocks unless studies show it is safe.
- **Taxonomy:** A qualified taxonomist should verify that only the intended species or subspecies are present in the stock before transport.
- **Disease and parasites:** Stock should be screened by a qualified fish pathologist. Hatchery stock is often crowded and can carry parasites and diseases. Stock should be quarantined for at least two weeks, and a sample should be examined for parasites before release.
- **Founding numbers:** Introduce enough individuals to capture the genetic diversity of the source population. Sex ratio near 1:1 and a range of age classes improve the chance of success. Researchers have suggested an effective population of 200 as a minimum.
- **Transport:** Stock is most often stressed by handling, crowding and temperature shock. Transport should be quick and careful, using containers that hold water saturated with oxygen. Water temperatures should be equalized before release.
- **Documentation:** Every translocation should be documented in detail, including source, numbers, condition, release site and dates.

### 3. Post-introduction activities

- **Monitoring:** Conduct systematic monitoring of introduced populations. Surveys should track survival, reproduction and dispersal, and may need to continue through environmental events such as floods, droughts or disease outbreaks.
- **Restocking:** Restock only when warranted. If failure has occurred, identify and address the causal factors before restocking.
- **Failure analysis:** When an introduction fails, efforts should begin immediately to determine its cause. The authors argue that understanding failures may be more valuable for recovery than successes.
- **Reporting:** Monitoring results and causes of failure should be made available in the scientific literature or in administrative reports, and widely distributed.

### Overall conclusion

The protocol is a draft meant to make introductions of listed fishes more systematic and to encourage their adoption by the [[American Fisheries Society]]. Its central message is that thorough site selection, careful stock handling, rigorous documentation and failure analysis are needed for introductions to contribute to recovery.
