# Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan (Technical Review Draft)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This technical review draft, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 2), sets out a recovery strategy for three freshwater fishes of the Rio Yaqui system: the [[Yaqui Chub]], the [[Yaqui Catfish]], and the [[Beautiful Shiner]]. The Yaqui chub is federally listed as endangered; the other two are listed as threatened. The draft states that it is not an approved government document and that goals and funding depend on appropriations, priorities and review comments.

All three species have been extirpated from the United States or nearly so. The Yaqui chub survives at [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (NWR) and at Leslie Creek, while the Yaqui catfish and beautiful shiner no longer occur in U.S. waters. The plan's central strategy is to restore self-sustaining populations on the refuge, supported by a refugium at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], habitat protection against nonnative fishes, monitoring, research, and public education. The master plan for the refuge sets a 10-year goal of establishing self-sustaining populations of six Rio Yaqui fish species.

## Background & Description

**Protection status.** The Yaqui chub is federally endangered and listed as a Group II species by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. The beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish are Group I species on the Arizona list, so they receive no state legal protection. All three are listed as endangered by the [[International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources]] (IUCN).

**Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*).** This chubby-bodied fish rarely exceeds 150 mm. It lives in small streams, typically in pools or under undercut banks fed by underflow or springs. Males turn metallic blue during breeding, while females remain straw-colored.

**Yaqui catfish (*Ictalurus pricei*).** This species occurs in moderate to large streams from highland areas at 2,100 m elevation to the delta. It rarely exceeds 500 mm and closely resembles the [[Channel Catfish]]. Its relationship to the [[Headwater Catfish]] is poorly understood.

**Beautiful shiner (*Notropis formosus*).** The species is similar in size and appearance to the [[Red Shiner]]. Breeding males are bright metallic blue with red-orange fins. Earlier authors recognized subspecies: *N. f. mearnsi* in the northern Rio Yaqui basin and *N. f. formosus* in the Casas Grandes system, including the Mimbres River. Chernoff and Miller (1982) placed all nominal forms in synonymy with *N. formosus*, with no recognized subspecies. The draft nevertheless keeps the two stocks separate for recovery purposes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical and present range.** The Yaqui chub historically occurred in upper San Bernardino Creek, artesian springs in Cochise County, and Turkey Creek (Morse Canyon), Graham County, Arizona, as well as in the upper Rios Yaqui, Matape, and Sonora. It was first collected in 1853. [[Astin Spring]] was the last natural U.S. locality where it was abundant. The spring dried in 1969, and before that it held thousands of chubs and topminnows in a 2 x 12 m pool. The Yaqui catfish was introduced to the upper Santa Cruz River in 1899 and persisted there until the late 1950s. The beautiful shiner was historically found in San Bernardino Creek and the Mimbres River, New Mexico, and it has been extirpated from the United States.

**Current status.**
- *Yaqui chub, U.S.:* Three localities on the refuge (House Pond, North Pond, San Bernardino Creek) plus Leslie Creek in the Swisshelm Mountains. North and House Ponds support viable self-sustaining populations. The San Bernardino Creek population is low and fluctuates with flow. A few persist in artesian bore holes.
- *Yaqui chub, Mexico:* Severe decline. A 1978 survey of the Rio Yaqui system produced one specimen despite extensive effort.
- *Yaqui catfish:* Persists in larger and moderate streams of the Rio Yaqui and Casas Grandes basins. No quantitative abundance data exist.
- *Beautiful shiner:* Found in Mexico (Rios Yaqui, Casas Grandes, Santa Maria, Carmen). It was relatively scarce in streams but abundant in a large earthen tank at the head of Arroyo Moctezuma, Chihuahua.

**Habitat and biology.** The Yaqui chub is secretive and highly localized, occupying the most permanent habitats: deep pools, areas behind boulders, and deeply undercut banks. Spawning occurs in spring and early summer. Small adults eat mainly algae and some terrestrial insects and spiders. The beautiful shiner has been collected from muddy pools, riffles, intermittent pools, rapids exceeding 1 m/s over rocky substrate, and stock ponds. Little is known about the Yaqui catfish, which is assumed to resemble the channel catfish.

**Associated species.** Hendrickson et al. (1980) most often found the beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish alongside longfin dace, Mexican stoneroller, Yaqui sucker, Sonoran topminnow, a suspected hybrid topminnow, river carpsucker, roundtail chub, and carp.

**Threats.**
- Stream channelization and large-scale water diversion, identified as the primary cause of decline.
- Overgrazing, which increases erosion and sediment load and reduces flow permanence.
- Pollution from industrial and domestic growth, including mine and smelter effluents and sewage.
- Groundwater pumping, which has reduced the spring flows that sustained habitat.
- Introduced predators and competitors, including [[Largemouth Bass]], bluegill, black bullhead, channel catfish, mosquitofish, and green sunfish.
- Hybridization and replacement by the exotic red shiner and channel catfish.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective.** Recovery depends on the master plan for San Bernardino NWR, whose goal is self-sustaining populations of six Rio Yaqui fish species within 10 years. The plan's emphasis is the U.S. portion of the range, but the authors commit to working with the Mexican government.

**Delisting and downlisting criteria.**
- *Yaqui chub:* Downlist when six stable populations are established at the refuge and the Leslie Creek population is secure. Delist when a secure viable population exists within its historic range in Mexico.
- *Yaqui catfish:* Delist when four stable populations are established at the refuge.
- *Beautiful shiner:* Delist when five stable populations are established at the refuge and the *N. f. formosus* subspecies is reestablished in the Mimbres River.

**Stocking targets (refuge master plan).**
- Yaqui chub, after habitat enhancement: North Pond 10,000; House Pond 20,000; Black Draw 2,000; Cienega Spring 2,000; Cottonwood Spring 2,000; Twin Pond 1,000.
- Yaqui catfish (four populations, carrying capacities): House Pond 1,000; Black Draw 500; two border springs at 500 each.
- Beautiful shiner (five populations): House Pond 50,000; Twin Pond 6,000; North Pond 2,000; Tule Pond 1,000; Upland Spring 1,000.

**Sampling and stock acquisition.**
- The 1978 Hendrickson et al. survey covered 90 site collections in the Rio Yaqui drainage. The catfish was found at 11 sites, the shiner at 23, and the chub at only one site, represented by one specimen.
- The plan proposes collecting at least 200 individuals each of Yaqui catfish and both shiner stocks, with 400 preferred for population genetics. Stock would be obtained under Mexican permits, with offspring returned to Mexico and research results shared.

**Monitoring.**
- Chub populations should be surveyed annually in mid to late summer, when numbers peak, until delisting.
- Monitoring must be standardized. Photographic habitat records are required, and no more than 20% of captured fish may be preserved.
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service compiles an annual report, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department monitors Leslie Creek.

**Culture and genetics.** The Dexter hatchery has cultured Yaqui chub since 1976 and has a beautiful shiner stock, but that stock is infected with Asian tapeworm, so a fresh stock from Mexico is desirable. Yaqui catfish culture is expected to follow [[Channel Catfish Culture]] techniques. First-generation offspring are to be analyzed by starch-gel electrophoresis to set a genetic baseline. Dexter NFH will serve as the refugium until recovery is complete.

**Management actions.**
- Build a barrier on Black Draw near the U.S./Mexico border to block nonnative fish movement.
- Check all ponds, stock tanks and streams upstream of or adjacent to the refuge for nonnative fish and remove them, in cooperation with the U.S. Forest Service and Arizona Game and Fish Department.
- Secure a habitat protection agreement with the Leslie Creek landowner, followed by a habitat management plan.
- Review federal projects affecting these fish under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Research priorities.** Basic biology and ecology studies (spawning and growth in culture), habitat utilization studies, and competition and predation studies. The last is needed because the full suite of historical refuge species, including roundtail chub, Mexican stoneroller, longfin dace, Yaqui sucker and the endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]], is to be reintroduced together.

**Education and outreach.** Develop an interpretive program at the refuge, contact private landowners at Leslie Canyon and the Mimbres River, prepare an information pamphlet, produce a video for schools and public broadcasting, and issue press releases on major milestones.
