# Electrofishing and Its Harmful Effects on Fish

#ichthyology #Salmonidae #electrofishing #sampling_methodology #fish_injury

## Survey Objectives & Target Population

**Primary Sampling Purpose**: This comprehensive literature review synthesizes knowledge on electrofishing techniques, fish responses to electric fields, and harmful effects (particularly spinal injuries and mortality) to inform interim guidelines for sampling endangered fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The review addresses concerns raised after documentation of substantial spinal injuries (44-67%) in large [[Rainbow Trout]] collected with pulsed direct current, prompting questions about impacts on threatened and endangered species including [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].

**Target Population & Spatial Boundaries**: Review encompasses freshwater fishes across North America and internationally, with emphasis on salmonids (particularly [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]]) and endangered cypriniform and catostomid fishes of the Colorado River Basin. Spatial contexts range from low-conductivity mountain streams (5-64 µS/cm) to moderate-high conductivity rivers and reservoirs (100-10,000 µS/cm), marine environments (>50,000 µS/cm), and controlled laboratory settings. Geographic focus includes Montana, Wyoming, Colorado, Alaska, Pennsylvania, Ontario, and the Colorado River system from Glen Canyon through Grand Canyon.

## Gear Specifications & Standardization

**Equipment & Mesh/Gear Parameters**: Three principal current types are used: (1) **Direct Current (DC)**—smooth or rippled unipolar current of constant voltage; (2) **Pulsed Direct Current (PDC)**—interrupted unipolar current with various waveforms (square, half-sine, quarter-sine, exponential) at frequencies typically 15-120 Hz, pulse widths 0.2-30 ms, duty cycles 6-90%; (3) **Alternating Current (AC)**—bipolar sinusoidal or triangular waveforms at 50-300 Hz. Specialized PDC includes [[Complex Pulse System]] (CPS): three 240-Hz pulses delivered 15 times/second (12% duty cycle). Electrodes vary: spherical (15-30 cm diameter), cylindrical cables, Wisconsin-ring dropper arrays (1-m rings with 20-cm droppers), vertical plates. Boat systems use throwable anodes with trailing cathodes or metal boat hulls as cathodes. Backpack units employ hand-held anodes with trailing cathode screens. Output voltages range 100-760 V (peak or mean); field intensities 0.01-15 V/cm voltage gradient.

**Deployment & Effort Standardization**: Field intensity measured with voltmeters or oscilloscopes connected to probes with exposed tips set at fixed distances, oriented along flux lines for maximum voltage differential. Peak voltage gradient = voltage differential per unit distance between isopotential surfaces. Standardization requires: (1) documenting water conductivity (ambient or normalized to 25°C) and temperature; (2) measuring or calculating peak field intensity at reference distances from electrodes; (3) verifying waveform characteristics (shape, frequency, pulse width, duty cycle) with oscilloscope; (4) recording electrode specifications (size, shape, position, surface area); (5) calibrating control-box settings and meters periodically. For comparable responses across conductivity ranges, target voltage gradients can be held constant (e.g., 0.6 V/cm for narcosis in 200-1,500 µS/cm water). Exposure duration typically <10-15 seconds in rivers; experimental exposures 1-300 seconds. Multiple-pass depletion sampling (3-4 passes) documented to cause cumulative injuries.

**Selectivity & Bias Considerations**: Electrofishing fields are heterogeneous in natural waters, with intensity highest at electrode surfaces, decreasing geometrically with distance. Response thresholds vary with: (1) **Species**—[[Salmoninae]] most susceptible to injury (up to 98% incidence), [[Burbot]] and [[Cottidae]] to mortality; cyprinids generally less susceptible; (2) **Size**—larger fish respond at lower thresholds but size-injury relationship inconsistent; (3) **Orientation**—maximum voltage differential when fish parallel to current lines, but injuries may occur more frequently when perpendicular; (4) **Condition**—spawning fish with skeletal decalcification more vulnerable; (5) **Water conductivity**—voltage-gradient thresholds decrease rapidly <100 µS/cm, stabilize >500 µS/cm; (6) **Temperature**—lower temperatures reduce metabolism and responses. Fright response in zone of reactive detection (outermost field) causes many fish to escape before capture. Fish not exhibiting taxis or stunned beyond netter reach are missed. Brands (dark skin marks) indicate injury in only ~40% of injured fish; 44% of fish without external signs have internal spinal damage detectable only by X-ray or necropsy.

## Experimental & Spatial Design Framework

**Stratification & Allocation**: Controlled experiments typically use homogeneous fields in raceways, troughs, or tanks with constant cross-sectional profiles and electrodes approximating that profile at each end, providing constant voltage gradient regardless of location between electrodes. Field studies employ heterogeneous fields around spherical, ring, or cable electrodes in natural streams, rivers, or lakes. Experimental designs compare: (1) current types (AC, DC, PDC variants, CPS); (2) field intensities (threshold levels for twitch, taxis, narcosis, tetany; or fixed voltage gradients 0.1-15 V/cm); (3) exposure durations (1-300 seconds); (4) pulse frequencies (1-512 Hz); (5) waveform shapes; (6) species and size classes. Sample sizes for injury assessment: minimum 10-50 fish per treatment, with X-ray and necropsy of subsamples or all specimens. Controls include unexposed fish, fish collected by other gear (angling, gill nets, seines), or fish from unelectrofished stream segments.

**Temporal Scheduling**: Sampling conducted year-round but timing affects fish condition and vulnerability. Spawning aggregations targeted for broodstock collection but spawning fish may be more susceptible to injury due to skeletal decalcification. Winter sampling (low temperatures 0-8°C) may reduce injury and mortality due to reduced metabolism. Multiple-pass depletion estimates conducted within 1-3 days; mark-recapture studies with intervals of weeks to years. Repeated annual electrofishing in same stream reaches causes cumulative injury (externally detectable injuries increasing from 2-5% in year 1 to 14-23% by year 3). Recovery from physiological stress requires >6-24 hours; full recovery from spinal injuries (if occurs) takes weeks to months. Intervals <3 months between repeated exposures significantly reduce growth rates.

**Detection & Bias Mitigation**: **Injury Detection Protocol** (Reynolds, 1996): (1) **Spinal damage** (X-ray analysis)—Class 0: none; Class 1: compression only; Class 2: misalignment including compression; Class 3: fractures or complete separation of ≥2 vertebrae. Record position of affected vertebrae (count from first separate vertebra behind head). (2) **Internal hemorrhage** (necropsy of fillets along spine)—Class 0: none; Class 1: mild, one or more wounds in muscle separate from spine; Class 2: moderate, small wounds on spine (<2 vertebrae width); Class 3: severe, large wounds on spine (>2 vertebrae width). Fish should be frozen or iced within 1 hour of capture to allow blood clotting; fillet both sides as hemorrhages often visible on only one side. Photograph worst injuries. **Bias mitigation**: (1) Minimize field intensity and exposure time; (2) use DC, low-frequency PDC (<20 Hz), or CPS rather than AC or high-frequency PDC; (3) maximize electrode size to reduce tetany zone; (4) avoid repeated sampling at <3-month intervals; (5) conduct pre-treatment X-rays to distinguish natural anomalies from electrofishing injuries; (6) include controls from unelectrofished areas to establish baseline injury rates (natural anomalies 0-16% in some populations); (7) document brands, abnormal swimming, bleeding at gills/vent immediately after capture; (8) hold fish in well-oxygenated water until equilibrium and respiration recover; (9) provide artificial respiration if needed (move fish back-and-forth to pump water over gills).

## Field Data Schema & Variable Definitions

**Primary Biological Measurements**: **Fish metrics**: total length (TL), fork length (FL), or standard length (SL) in cm or mm; weight in grams; species; age (scale analysis, back-calculation); sex; maturity stage; condition factor (weight/length³ × 100,000). **Injury assessment**: presence/absence and class (0-3) of spinal damage and hemorrhages; number and location of injuries (vertebra number from head); presence of brands (temporary dark skin marks), bent backs, bleeding at gills or vent; abnormal swimming behavior. **Response observations**: threshold field intensities (V/cm) for twitch (reactive detection), taxis (forced swimming toward anode), narcosis (loss of equilibrium, limp muscles), tetany (sustained muscle contraction, rigidity); recovery time (seconds to minutes) for respiration and equilibrium after current cessation; immediate, short-term (1-7 days), and long-term (weeks to years) mortality. **Physiological stress indicators**: plasma cortisol, glucose, lactate, catecholamines; blood pH, hematocrit, hemoglobin; ventilation rate; swimming performance.

**Environmental & Spatial Covariates**: **Water quality**: conductivity (µS/cm, specify ambient or standardized to 25°C), temperature (°C), dissolved oxygen (mg/L or % saturation), pH. **Habitat**: stream/river/lake/reservoir; substrate composition (fine sediments, gravel, rubble, bedrock); depth (cm or m); current velocity (m/s); presence of cover (vegetation, woody debris, undercut banks). **Spatial**: distance from electrode (cm or m); position in electric field (near anode, cathode, or between); orientation of fish relative to current lines (parallel, perpendicular, or angle). **Electrical parameters**: current type (AC, DC, PDC, CPS); waveform shape (sine, square, half-sine, quarter-sine, exponential, triangular); output voltage (V, specify peak or mean/rms); output current (A, specify peak or mean/rms); pulse frequency (Hz); pulse width (ms); duty cycle (%); voltage gradient at reference points (V/cm, specify peak or mean); current density (µA/cm² or mA/cm²); power density (µW/cm³); electrode type, size, shape, position, surface area (cm²); distance between electrodes (m); exposure duration (seconds).

**QA/QC & Data Entry Protocols**: **Calibration**: Verify control-box settings and meters with oscilloscope before and periodically during sampling; document actual waveform characteristics (frequency, pulse width, duty cycle may differ from settings). Measure voltage gradients in water at standardized reference points with calibrated voltmeters or oscilloscopes connected to probes. **Standardization**: Record whether voltage, current, and field-intensity values are peak or mean (rms for AC). Normalize conductivity to 25°C if needed using c₂ = c₁/(1.02^(t₁-t₂)) or c₂ = c₁/(1 + 0.023(t₁ - t₂)). **Injury documentation**: X-ray fish in lateral and dorsal views; photograph fillets showing hemorrhages; record position of all affected vertebrae; distinguish fresh injuries (separation, misalignment, associated hemorrhage) from old healed injuries (heavy calcification, fusion, misalignment) and natural anomalies (compression-fusion without misalignment). **Controls**: Include fish collected by other methods (angling, netting) or from unelectrofished stream segments; X-ray and necropsy controls to establish baseline injury rates. **Data entry**: Record all electrical parameters, environmental conditions, fish metrics, injury classifications, and observations in standardized datasheets or electronic databases; include unique fish identifiers for mark-recapture studies; photograph representative injuries and waveform oscilloscope traces.
