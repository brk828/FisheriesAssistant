## Executive Summary
This 1952 report by Ralph V. Beck documents the discovery and eradication of an introduced, non-native population of suckers (genus *Catostomus*, probably *[[Catostomus tahoensis]]*) in Starkweather Lake, a barren alpine lake in the Upper San Joaquin River drainage, California. The suckers were likely illegally introduced by bait fishermen crossing over from the eastern Sierra Nevada slope (Lahontan drainage system). To protect the native/stocked trout fishery and prevent the spread of "rough fish" into the previously barren San Joaquin River system, the California Department of Fish and Game conducted a rotenone treatment of the lake in July 1951. The treatment successfully achieved an apparent complete eradication of the sucker population, though incidental mortality of stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] also occurred. The paper serves as a historical record for future researchers and highlights ongoing concerns about further illegal introductions in the region (e.g., a suspected population in nearby Sotcher Lake).

## Background & Description
- **Target Species:** *Catostomus* sp., probably *C. tahoensis* (a [[Lahontan Species]]). Positive taxonomic identification was not made in the field.
- **Size Range:** Population ranged from newly hatched fry up to adults of approximately 14 inches in length, indicating an established, reproducing, multi-generational population rather than a single recent introduction event.
- **Comparative Species:** *Salmo gairdneri* (Rainbow Trout), the incumbent stocked sportfish, was the primary non-target species affected by the piscicide treatment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Type:** Starkweather Lake is a small (3.5-acre), high-elevation (8,000 ft) lake in Madera County, located near the Devil's Postpile National Monument. It sits in a basin of rock, sand, and pumice soil, surrounded by lodgepole pine forest.
- **Hydrology:** The lake is characterized by a lack of permanent inlet or outlet streams. It only connects hydrologically to the Middle Fork San Joaquin River during high-water spring runoff events, creating a largely closed system.
- **Biogeography:** The suckers represent a translocation from an eastern-slope Sierra Nevada drainage (Lahontan Basin) to a western-slope drainage (San Joaquin/[[Sacramento-San Joaquin River System]]). This mirrors a similar cross-drainage introduction event documented by Kimsey (1950) regarding Lahontan fishes in the Sacramento River drainage.
- **Native Fish Community Context:** The Upper San Joaquin River and its tributaries (specifically the 45 miles of stream above the cascades near Mammoth Pool) were historically barren of suckers or any other "rough fish," relying entirely on artificial stocking of trout for its fishery.
- **Vector of Introduction:** Suspected illegal introduction via [[Bait Fishermen]] transporting live bait fish across the Sierra Nevada crest from the eastern slope (Mammoth Lakes area).

## Key Findings
- **Population Density:** Estimated at 10 to 20 times the number of trout present in the lake prior to treatment, indicating a "moderately heavy" but not extremely large population for the lake's size.
- **Treatment Method:** The lake was treated with 165 lbs of cube powder (4.8% rotenone content), achieving a concentration of 0.058 p.p.m. rotenone in the water. An additional 15 lbs were used to treat two adjoining smaller ponds.
- **Efficacy of Treatment:**
    - Fish began surfacing ~1 hour post-treatment.
    - Approx. 50 Rainbow Trout were recovered (with an estimated 5x that number, ~250, unrecovered/undetected).
    - An undetermined number of suckers were killed.
    - **Live Car Bioassay:** Five weeks post-treatment, fingerling rainbow trout placed in the lake died within one hour, confirming sustained rotenone toxicity and supporting the conclusion of a total kill.
- **Management Rationale:** The primary motivation for eradication was not just protecting the local trout fishery, but preventing the sucker population from escaping downstream into the San Joaquin River during seasonal high-water connectivity, which would introduce "[[Rough Fish]]" into a previously pristine salmonid-only watershed.
- **Unresolved Risk:** The paper acknowledges the possibility that some suckers may have already escaped downstream prior to the treatment date.
- **Follow-up Investigation:** An unconfirmed report from late 1951 suggested suckers were also present in **Sotcher Lake**, located ~3 miles south of Starkweather Lake, warranting further investigation and potential eradication efforts in the region.
- **Documentation Purpose:** The report explicitly serves as a historical record to prevent taxonomic/ecological confusion for future researchers who might otherwise interpret a future discovery of suckers in the upper river as evidence of a native population.
