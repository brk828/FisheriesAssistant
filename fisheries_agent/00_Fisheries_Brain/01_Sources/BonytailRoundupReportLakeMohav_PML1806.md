# Bonytail Broodstock Collection at Lake Mohave, May 2005

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (Arizona Fishery Resources Office, Parker, June 2005; prepared by C.O. Minckley and Mitch Thorson) describes a one-week trammel-net effort to collect wild [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) from [[Lake Mohave]] between 16 and 20 May 2005. The goal was to recover wild fish for the captive [[Broodstock Management|broodstock]] maintained at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center, because the existing broodstock is aging and has limited [[Genetic Diversity]]. Only one wild bonytail was captured, a small running-ripe male. The catch was dominated by non-native [[Common Carp]] (85%). The report also documents the capture of two [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), one of them a recapture, and argues for continued long-term monitoring of the species.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** Wild bonytail are virtually extinct in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Historical records came from Grand Canyon, Lake Mead, Lake Mohave, the Colorado River from Parker Dam to Yuma, the historic delta in Sonora, Mexico, and the Salton Sea.
- **Regulatory history:** Bonytail were listed as Endangered in 1980 under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The recovery plan was completed in 1991, and [[Critical Habitat]] was designated in 1993.
- **Broodstock origin:** In 1981, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service produced progeny from 10 or fewer wild bonytail available at the time (Hamman 1982). The current broodstock comprises 132 fish from this cohort plus adults captured in Lake Mohave from 1990 to present (R. Hamman, pers. comm., 28 June 2004). The F1 fish are described as genetically sound, and they carry a representation of each original cross.
- **Hatchery use:** Progeny from these fish supply [[Captive Breeding|stocking programs]] throughout the Colorado River basin.
- **Rationale for collecting:** The report states that the broodstock are aging, so recovering wild fish is increasingly important. A single wild fish would increase broodstock genetic diversity by about 20% (Hedrick et al. 2000).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** The survey covered the reach of [[Lake Mohave]] between Gasoline Alley (UTM 011 0720 3903781) and Arrowhead Cove (UTM 011 0720 3902944), about 5 km north of Katherine's Landing. This reach was selected because bonytail had been collected there in the previous five years. Waters fished were generally less than 5 m deep, and the crew targeted shoal areas where spawning was suspected.

**Field methods:**
- Seven [[Trammel Netting|trammel nets]] (100 m × 2 m; 25 cm outer wall, 2.5 cm inner wall) were used. One net was fished one night and six were fished for three nights.
- Nets were tied to shore and set perpendicular or at an angle to the shoreline.
- Nets were fished for 12 hours and set in the early evening, then checked in the morning.
- Non-native fish were identified, counted, and removed. Native fish were processed and released, except bonytail, which were transported to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] as soon as possible.
- Fish were held in aerated water containing MS-222 anesthetic and a salt solution. Fish were weighed, measured, and fin-clipped for [[Genetic Analysis]] before transfer.
- Hatchery staff stood by to receive any bonytail captured.

**Community composition:** Captured species, in decreasing order of abundance, were common carp, striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*), bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*), largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*), bullhead species, and razorback sucker. Non-native species made up most of the catch.

## Key Findings

**Effort and catch**
- A total of 25 twelve-hour trammel-net sets was made, representing a net length given in the OCR text as "7s00 m"; the exact value is unclear.
- Daily catch ranged across the week. Catch per net ranged from 10 to 43 fish. Catch per 30 m of net ranged from 3.3 to 14 fish.
- Carp dominated the catch at 85% (Table 2).

**Native fish captures**
- **Bonytail:** One small running-ripe male was captured. It measured about 280 mm total length and was tagged (#5345134A7e). It had been stocked at Princess Cove, a short distance from the capture site, and was released after processing.
- **Razorback sucker:** Two adults were captured. One was a recapture (tag #5326057...) originally stocked in El Dorado Canyon on 17 July 2001. It measured 620 mm total length when recaptured. The second was newly tagged and released.

**Management implications**
- The authors conclude that monitoring is essential to evaluate current management actions and to guide future decisions. Current management of the species, including Lake Mohave management and recovery activities, depends on three decades of basic monitoring.
- Monitoring of bonytail is to continue, and the report indicates that results will be used to maintain populations for the future, even though the possibility of capturing wild fish has ended.
