# The Kelp Bass (Paralabrax clathratus) And Its Fishery, 1947-1958

#ichthyology #marine-biology #serranidae

## Executive Summary

Fish Bulletin 122 (California Department of Fish and Game, 1963), by Parke H. Young, is a long-term study of the [[Kelp Bass]] (*Paralabrax clathratus*) as a target of the southern California [[Sportboat Fishery]]. It combines 1947-1958 sport-catch logs, dockside verification, tagging (5,817 bass released, 956 recovered), growth, age, weight-length, food, maturity and hermaphroditism data, and an estimate of natural mortality. The study concludes that the fishery was deteriorating after World War II, that a stepped [[Size Limit]] (10.5 in 1953, rising to 12 in 1959) was warranted, and that the fishery was recovering by 1961-1962.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** Kelp bass, *Paralabrax clathratus*, first described by Girard (1854) from San Diego. Called "bull" bass when large and "calico" bass when small.
- **Range:** Non-migratory; from about Monterey Bay to Magdalena Bay, Baja California, but few occur north of Point Conception or south of Abreojos. Abundant at Guadalupe and San Benito Islands and Point Eugenia.
- **Depth and habitat:** Adults occupy the surf zone to 130 ft or more, distributed through the water column above and below any [[Thermocline]]. Fishing concentrates in and near [[Kelp Beds]] along mainland and island shores.
- **Fishery status:** Among the top five species in the California sportboat catch. Barracuda (*Sphyraena argentea*) and rockfish (*Sebastes* spp.) often outnumber it, but it is considered the pre-eminent species for popularity, desirability and all-season availability.
- **Study setting:** Research began in 1950 under the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] after the fishery was seen to be deteriorating. Data were collected through the [[Sport-Catch Log System]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishery structure.** Sportboats fished from Port Hueneme to San Diego, with a maximum of 371 boats (1953) and a minimum of 210 (1958). Four boat types served anglers: all-day, half-day, charter and barge. Live anchovies (*Engraulis mordax*) were the principal bait, and a circle-chumming method was used to draw bass and other species.

**Competing species.** Game fish (barracuda, yellowtail, white seabass, bonito, bluefin tuna, jack mackerel) and incidental or substitute species (Pacific mackerel, sheephead, sculpin, white croaker, cabezon, giant sea bass, shallow- and deep-water rockfish, California halibut) shared the catch and effort.

**Water temperature.** Warm years (notably 1954, 1957 and 1958) brought game fish northward and were accompanied by higher catches in several areas. Radovich (1961) linked changes in the distribution of yellowtail and barracuda to temperature, and noted that resident kelp bass were caught in greater numbers in warm years.

**Kelp and pollution.** At [[Horseshoe Kelp]], the loss of surface kelp was associated by local observers with pollution. The Los Angeles Harbor and White Point outfall were suspected sources of light-absorbing substances that inhibit [[Giant Kelp]] (*Macrocystis pyrifera*), but a causal link was not established.

## Key Findings

**Fishery history and regulation**
- Post-war fishing pressure (1947 onward) by returning servicemen and industry workers depleted the large "bull" bass accumulated during the war, and by 1950 most were caught.
- Sale of kelp bass was prohibited and a 10.5-inch [[Minimum Size Limit]] imposed in 1953. The limit increased to 11 in 1956, 11.5 in 1957 and 12 in 1959. The 12-inch level was chosen as the most beneficial based on the study.
- Partyboat sportboat landings of kelp and sand bass were 613,000 (1961) and 789,000 (1962), described as comparing favorably with better postwar years.

**Catch statistics**
- Annual kelp bass catch on sportboats ranged from a low of 470,362 (1956) to a high of 876,667 (1954, the statewide record).
- Dockside verification (1947-1951, 587 boats): logs overestimated total fish by 3.7 percent (47,878 logged vs. 46,160 counted) and kelp bass by 8.4 percent (17,152 vs. 15,837).

**Area-level results (12 principal grounds, May-September)**
- **[[San Clemente Island]]:** The outstanding bass ground, with 136,415 bass at the 1949 peak. Angler success 7.6 bass per angler in 1947 and 5.6 in 1958.
- **[[Santa Catalina Island]]:** Kelp bass provided about half the sportboat catch. Landings ranged from 65,204 (1955) to 197,608 (1947).
- **[[Point Loma-La Jolla]]:** Bass contributed about 75 percent of the overall catch in the early years, with success falling to 1.8 bass per angler in 1957.
- **[[Northern Channel Islands]]:** One of the best grounds; 1957 was the record year for total bass (105,898) with 5.1 bass per angler.
- **[[Horseshoe Kelp]]:** More than 44,000 angler days per year. Annual landings ranged from 68,967 (1947) to 13,609 (1955). Success was steady at about one fish per angler day.
- **[[Coronado Islands]] (Mexico):** Game fish were the main target. Bass catches were low, ranging from 5,096 (1952) to 45,455 (1949).
- **Santa Monica Bay:** Consistently poor for kelp bass (546 in 1956 to 19,773 in 1947). Substitute species sustained the fishery.
- **Newport Beach-Dana Point:** Success near 1.1 bass per angler over 12 years, the lowest of six similar mainland areas.
- **Oceanside:** Peak catches of slightly over 50,000 bass; success 3.9-5.3 bass per angler (1947-1955) falling to 2.5-3.4 later.

**Tagging and movement**
- About 400 bass tagged in Baja California (as far south as Abreojos Point); none was recovered in California.
- Total tagged in California (1950-1957): 5,817. Recovered: 956 (16.4 percent), of which about 50 percent were returned within 2 months and 68 percent within 6 months.
- Of 458 movement records, 364 bass were recovered at the release site. 62 moved up to 5 miles, 14 moved 5-10 miles and 18 moved 10 miles or more.
- Movement was not correlated with fish length. About 20 percent of recovered fish showed recognizable movement.
- Tagging experiments showed the transplant of 331 San Clemente Island bass to Santa Catalina Island yielded 27 percent recovery, versus 15.7 percent for resident bass.

**Growth and age**
- Tag-return growth: Y = 2.94 + 0.857X, based on 81 recoveries; theoretical maximum length about 20.5 inches.
- Age-length growth: Y = 2.47 + 0.913X; theoretical maximum about 28.4 inches.
- Age was determined from scales of 432 fish (scales preferred over otoliths). The oldest fish, aged from an otolith, was 32 years old, a 14-pound 9-ounce female.
- A captive fish lived 12 years in the Scripps aquarium (Hinton, 1962).

**Weight-length, food and maturity**
- Weight-length relationship: W = 0.00876 L^3.x (356 fish; the summary gives W = 0.00376 L^3.x); largest measured fish was 22.5 inches and 100 ounces (6.25 lb).
- Juveniles (3-7.5 in) fed mainly on amphipods and decapod shrimp; larger bass (over 8 in) increasingly ate fish (anchovies), squids and octopuses.
- Maturity: some fish were mature by April at 325 mm and above; by June fish as small as 201 mm were mature; spawning was complete by September-October. Quast (n.d.) found first maturity at about 178 mm and a possible second spawning in the largest females.
- Hermaphroditism: 65 bass examined (1960). Ovarian tissue appeared in one male gonad, possibly from laboratory processing.

**Natural mortality and yield**
- Natural mortality was estimated at 25 percent per year from 293 bass taken at unfished grounds (San Clemente, Santa Cruz and Santa Barbara Islands, 1950).
- A year-class reaches maximum total weight at about 13.4 inches (25 percent mortality) or 12 inches (30 percent mortality), supporting the 12-inch size limit.
- The authors define best catch as the greatest total weight of fish, which is most achievable through size regulation.

**Conclusions**
- Bass yield declined in several areas during the late 1940s and early 1950s due to heavy harvest of large fish. Sub-legal bass became abundant after 1954, which the authors expect will harvest as a larger crop after the 12-inch limit takes full effect.
- Warm water years increased game fish availability and temporarily competed with bass.
