# Levee Removal Strategic Plan (1996 Habitat Restoration Program)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft plan, prepared for the [[Upper Basin Recovery Implementation Program]] (UBRIP) and dated October 4, 1995, sets out a strategy for removing or breaching levees on selected [[Floodplain Wetlands]] in the Upper Colorado River Basin. The goal is to restore floodplain function that supports recovery of endangered fishes, chiefly the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The plan uses a randomized block design to compare restored ("application") sites with naturally flooding sites, and it measures fish populations, food organisms, water quality, physical conditions and contaminants over three field seasons (1996–1998). A central concern is whether enhanced floodplain habitat will also benefit [[Nonnative Fish Predators]], which could offset gains for native species.

## Background & Description

Inundated floodplains and terraces are shallow, slow-moving waters that are more productive than the adjacent main channel. They typically have higher nutrient concentrations, primary productivity and invertebrate densities. The document argues that these wetlands can serve as rearing and feeding areas for native fishes. Their productivity comes from several sources:

- A large food base of phytoplankton and other organisms.
- Structurally complex habitat from emergent plants, flooded vegetation and algal mats.
- Warmer water temperatures than the river.
- A large decomposition pathway fed by decaying plant material.

Young-of-the-year (YOY) native fish that move into floodplain wetlands are expected to grow larger, which should reduce their vulnerability to predation by most nonnative fish in the main channel. The plan also suggests that adult razorback suckers use these low-velocity areas as post-spawning resting habitat to recover energy reserves. Several researchers are cited as documenting this use, including [[Tyus]] (1989, 1990), with some observations attributed to personal communications.

The document links the loss of habitat to a general reduction in channel and floodplain complexity over the last 100 years, which it associates with dam construction and flood management. The authors note that earlier UBRIP work focused on "manipulated" sites with water control structures, while naturally flooding wetlands received little attention.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Levee removal issues.** The plan addresses several issues raised by stakeholders:

1. **Naturally flooding wetlands** should inform management criteria. Restored sites will be compared directly with natural floodplain areas of similar size within the same reaches.
2. **Manipulated sites** (e.g., Old Charlie, Walter/Walker, Johnson, Boat Bottom) will be used to answer hypotheses that are difficult to test in open wetlands. Their control structures can also improve fish sampling.
3. **Native versus nonnative enhancement.** Channel Catfish, Northern Pike, Green Sunfish and [[Smallmouth Bass]] are identified as significant threats to native survivorship. Because these species also use littoral habitats in Midwestern lakes and reservoirs, the plan must determine whether floodplain habitat will mainly benefit nonnatives. Nonnative predators are treated as potential limiting factors for native recruitment.
4. **Site prioritization.** A randomized block analysis is used to rank and schedule sites, a process that has been contested in the habitat enhancement program for years.

**Implementation framework.** Sites were selected across four criteria:

- Geographic location, above and below [[Desolation Canyon and Gray Canyon]].
- High versus low nonnative fish density.
- Depression versus terrace floodplain type.
- Natural versus application (levee removal or breaching) sites.

The strategy assumes initial work in the Green River system, two to three years of evaluation per application activity, and adjustments as new information arrives. Final site selection depends on flood-ability and drain-ability, landowner willingness, and contaminants.

## Key Findings

**Study objectives and hypotheses**

- Goal: restore and enhance natural floodplain habitats that support endangered fish recovery in the Upper Colorado River Basin.
- Objectives include reconnecting floodplains with the channel, evaluating system responses, using manipulated sites to test specific questions, and applying [[Adaptive Management]].
- H1: Floodplain habitats increase food resources and available habitat for native fishes.
- H2: Terraces and depressions differ in food resources and fish communities.
- H3: Nonnative predators use the river channel, terraces and depressions differently.
- H4: The interaction of nonnative predator use and floodplain geomorphology will produce differential survivorship of nonnative fishes.

**Block design (1996)**

- *Green River*: 10 application sites and 10 natural sites, split above and below Desolation/Gray Canyons. Block 1 lists 12 sites above the canyons and Block 2 lists 8 sites below. About 30 candidate sites were identified to fill the design.
- *Colorado River*: a potential design of 2 application and 2 natural sites, with 13 candidate sites (Block 3). Nonnative density is treated as a continuous covariate here because no clear pattern exists.

**Monitoring design**

- *Native fishes*: all life stages are sampled in depression, terrace and main channel habitats. Larval fish are collected with light traps during high flow, and sampling is biweekly.
- *Nonnative fishes*: target species are centrarchids, cyprinids, Channel Catfish and Northern Pike. Sampling is weekly, using gear such as electrofishers, seines and minnow traps.
- *Food organisms and water quality*: biweekly macroinvertebrate sampling, weekly water samples analyzed with an autoanalyzer, and Hydrolabs for other parameters.
- *Physical and vegetation*: measured at each fish sampling visit (not in the main channel). Levee-breaching techniques will be evaluated with the geomorphology/hydrology support project.
- *Contaminants*: sampled at the same frequency as fish.

**Schedule and deliverables**

- Final site selection: December 1995.
- Levee breaching or removal: February–April 1996.
- Three field seasons, May–October, with additional levees identified after the first full year of evaluation (1997).
- Reports include field trip summaries, season summaries, annual data analysis progress reports, an annual Habitat Restoration Program report and a Three Year Completion Report.

**Coordination and recommendations**

- A Program Coordinator oversees three components (native fish, nonnative fish and food organisms), each with a designated lead and regular coordination meetings.
- Field sampling is to be collaborative, possibly with combined agency crews.
- Recommended outputs include design criteria for levee removal, an evaluation of trade-offs between levee removal and flow management, and guidance on future actions.
