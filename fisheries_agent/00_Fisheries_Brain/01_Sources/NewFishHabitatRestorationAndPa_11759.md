# New Fish Habitat Restoration and Partnership Initiative Claims First Success Story

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Poeciliidae #endangered-species #habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

The [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]] (AzFRO) successfully restored [[Gila Topminnow]] habitat at the Bylas Springs complex on the [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] through a multi-agency partnership. The project addressed the near-extinction of [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis]], once abundant in the [[Gila River]] basin but reduced to only 11 natural populations worldwide. Through removal of invasive [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia affinis) and [[Salt Cedar]] (Tamarix chinensis), construction of fish barriers, and restoration of native riparian vegetation, two springs (S2 and S3) were successfully reintroduced with [[Gila Topminnow]], increasing the number of established reintroduced populations from 18 to 20.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] (Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis) historically ranged throughout the [[Gila River]] basin of Arizona and New Mexico, USA, and the rios Gila, Concepcion, and Sonoran basins of Sonora, Mexico. This small fish is listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Prior to this project, naturally occurring populations persisted in just 11 locations, with only 18 of 175 reintroduced populations having been successfully established since 1936.

The [[Bylas Springs]] complex consists of three small springs (S1, S2, and S3, labeled west to east) adjacent to the [[Gila River]] on the [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] in Graham County, Arizona. All three springs historically contained [[Gila Topminnow]]. The S1 system supports one of the 11 remaining natural populations found in the world.

Historical timeline of the springs:
- 1968: Discovery of [[Gila Topminnow]] populations
- 1980: Flood in [[Gila River]] watershed; [[Mosquitofish]] invaded S1 and S3 populations
- 1983-1984: Cement fish barriers and fences constructed around all three springs to protect from [[Mosquitofish]] displacement and livestock disturbance
- 1987-1988: S2 population samples stocked into Upper and Lower Headquarters ponds in [[Roper Lake State Park]] as refugia
- 1990: [[Gila Topminnow]] disappeared from S2 due to surface water depletion caused by increased vegetation after livestock exclusion, entrained sediment, vegetative debris, and invasion by [[Salt Cedar]] (Tamarix chinensis), a non-native tree consuming large amounts of water daily

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The decline of [[Gila Topminnow]] resulted from multiple factors: non-native species invasions (primarily [[Mosquitofish]], Gambusia affinis), land and water-use practices, and habitat degradation. The invasion of [[Salt Cedar]] was identified as a major contributor to the demise of the S2 system through excessive water consumption.

The restoration project addressed this fragile [[Sonoran Desert]] ecosystem, benefiting not only the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] but also 15 species of birds, six mammals, four reptiles, and two snails. Replacing non-native [[Salt Cedar]] with native vegetation (black willows and cottonwood trees) was expected to increase water quality and quantity over time.

Partnership structure included:
- [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]]: Access privileges, support, and equipment
- [[Arizona State University]]: Propagation of wild stock until restoration completion
- [[Arizona Game & Fish Department]]: Technical expertise and survey assistance
- [[U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service]]: Design, financing, coordination, and supervision through three divisions (Hatcheries, Ecological Services, and Fish & Wildlife Management Assistance)

## Key Findings

**S2 Spring Restoration (Summer 1996):**
- Exotic [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Salt Cedar]] removed
- Fish barriers constructed
- S2 lineage (maintained in captivity since the 1980s) successfully reintroduced
- Population now thriving

**S3 Spring Restoration (1997-1998):**
- May 14, 1997: 48 [[Gila Topminnow]] captured from S1 spring and transferred to [[Arizona State University]] Fisheries Laboratory
- April 1, 1998: Approximately 440 progeny from original S1 fish held at [[Arizona State University]]
- 30 days of work in 100-115°F temperatures completed:
  - 720 meters of S3 spring cleared of non-native [[Salt Cedar]]
  - All non-native [[Mosquitofish]] removed
  - Eight ponds and two fish barriers constructed
  - Over 30 huge piles of [[Salt Cedar]] burned
  - Thousands of [[Mosquitofish]] removed
- Eight ponds totaling 100 m² of surface area created as preferred [[Gila Topminnow]] habitat
- January 1, 1998: S3 riparian area replanted with 100 black willows and 25 cottonwood trees (native species)
- Spring 1998: Surveys revealed high replant survival and extensive growth
- April 29, 1998: 244 [[Gila Topminnow]] transported from [[Arizona State University]] and released into newly restored S3 spring habitat

**Overall Project Outcomes:**
- Number of established reintroduced [[Gila Topminnow]] populations increased from 18 to 20 (11% increase)
- Project funded through [[U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service]]'s Fish Habitat Restoration and Partnership Initiative
- Multi-species benefits: 15 bird species, 6 mammals, 4 reptiles, 2 snails, plus [[Gila Topminnow]]
- Long-term benefits to fragile desert ecosystem and local human communities
