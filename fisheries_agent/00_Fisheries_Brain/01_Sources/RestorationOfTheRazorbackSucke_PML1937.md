# Restoration of the Razorback Sucker in the Colorado River, Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This World River Conference proceeding (1997) reviews the decline of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the [[Colorado River]] basin and the restoration efforts undertaken since the 1960s. The river has been heavily regulated by dams and water diversions, and native fishes have nearly disappeared. The paper contrasts an early large-scale stocking program that failed with a later, more adaptive program centred on [[Lake Mohave]], where wild larvae are collected, reared in low-cost facilities, and released. The authors argue that restoring whole riverine ecosystems, rather than single species, is the real challenge.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] is the largest river in the southwestern United States. It rises in the Rocky Mountains of Colorado, flows through Utah, cuts the Grand Canyon, and runs about 3,200 km to the Gulf of California. It is described as the most regulated river in the Western Hemisphere, with more than 40 major dams or diversion structures built in the past century, including [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Powell]]. Water depletions are so large that the river seldom reaches the ocean, and it supplies water to more than 25 million people in the United States and Mexico.

The razorback sucker is a large, uniquely shaped fish with a lifespan of about 50 years. It is endemic to the Colorado River system and is one of four endangered Colorado River fishes, alongside the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Its range has been reduced to less than 25% of its former extent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat change:** Channelization and levees have reduced flooding but damaged riparian and wetland communities. Less than 5% of fish in the Colorado River are reported to be native.
- **Population status:** Nearly all collected suckers over three decades were adults. Upper basin populations number as few as 500 individuals, and the largest population is in Lake Mohave, a reservoir formed by Davis Dam in 1954. The paper states that about 98% of wild razorback suckers are found there.
- **Recruitment failure:** Adults spawn and larvae hatch, but few young survive. Young suckers longer than 25 mm are rarely found because predators not present a few decades ago consume them.
- **Non-native species:** More than 40 non-native fish species have been introduced. Channel catfish, striped bass, and sunfish are identified as major threats through predation and competition, often linked to recreational fisheries.
- **Management context:** Most U.S. fish management is funded through fishing licence sales, which historically favoured sport fish over natives. Recent regulations have restricted new introductions and limited recreational stocking to specific waters.

## Key Findings

1. **Failed large-scale stocking (1980s):** A political compromise in 1980 allowed a 10-year stocking program in Arizona if federal listing were postponed. Over 12 million razorback suckers were stocked, yet follow-up sampling recaptured fewer than 100. No successful recruitment or spawning was reported. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) listed the species as endangered in 1990.
2. **Predation as a key bottleneck:** Channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*) consumed on average six razorback suckers each. Researchers estimated that several thousand stocked fish could be lost in 72 hours.
3. **Recovery Program:** The first Colorado River fishes recovery team began in 1975. The Colorado River Fishery Project office opened in 1979, and a 1980 conference consolidated knowledge.
4. **Native Fish Work Group (NFWG):** Formed in the late 1980s, the NFWG built low-cost rearing sites by blocking shoreline coves with nets or earthen barriers, removing non-native fish to create predator-free nurseries.
5. **Pilot spawning success:** In 1990, 90 old adults were held in an isolated backwater and spawned successfully. Only 150 offspring were produced, which was far below the target.
6. **Larval collection method:** Larvae are phototactic and can be captured in large numbers with night-time light traps (Mueller 1993). Collection in 1994 yielded over 11,000 larvae, with numbers roughly doubling each year. The 1996 season yielded over 107,000 larvae, which produced over 60,000 juveniles.
7. **Growth and culture:** The target is to rear fish to 30 cm before release. Warm backwaters and low stocking densities allow growth in one year, whereas hatchery fish often need 2 to 3 years. Municipal golf course ponds in three cities serve as grow-out sites at 500 fish/ha, requiring no feeding. The program has also been incorporated into local high school curricula.
8. **Reintroduction results:** About 6,000 juveniles had been reintroduced into Lake Mohave as of 1996, with an estimated survival of about 50%. Young adults are now being captured alongside old adults for the first time in three decades.
9. **Goal:** The long-term target is a young adult population of 50,000 suckers. The Bureau of Reclamation and USFWS were negotiating resources to achieve it.
10. **Broader conclusion:** Successful restoration has mainly occurred in springs, small tributaries, and headwaters. Large, complex river systems remain difficult, and sustained management commitment is required because ecological processes are poorly understood.
