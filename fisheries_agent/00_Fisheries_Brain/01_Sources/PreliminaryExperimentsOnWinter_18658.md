# Preliminary Experiments on Winter Feeding Small Fathead Minnows

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Cyprinidae #fathead-minnow #winter-feeding

## Executive Summary

This document reports preliminary experiments conducted at the [[Alabama Polytechnic Institute]] Agricultural Experiment Station on winter feeding protocols for small [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) to raise undersized fish to marketable sizes. The research addresses a critical production bottleneck: 20-40% of fatheads produced in fertilized rearing ponds failed to reach salable size (2.5-3.0 pounds per thousand for crappie bait, 4+ pounds per thousand for bass bait) within one year. Multiple feeding trials tested different feed types (soybean cake, soybean-cottonseed meal mixtures, peanut meal, fish meal, and Auburn No. 1 fish feed), stocking densities (80,000-276,000 fish per acre), and feeding rates (1-4% body weight daily) during winter months when water temperatures dropped into the high thirties to low forties Fahrenheit.

## Background & Description

[[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) is a highly popular bait minnow, particularly among crappie fishermen, and has been raised extensively by commercial producers over the preceding 10 years. The species exhibits periodic spawning from early spring until water temperatures drop below 60-65°F in fall. This extended reproductive period results in cohorts of varying sizes, with a substantial proportion remaining too small for market when ponds are drained.

Market specifications require fatheads weighing 4 pounds or more per thousand for bass bait, while 2.5-3.0 pounds per thousand are acceptable for crappie bait. Smaller sizes are not generally salable. Peak demand occurs February through May, necessitating that the bulk of production reach suitable size for late winter and early spring sales. Previous experiments at [[Auburn]] showed that even with supplemental feeding during fall and winter, approximately 15% of minnows failed to reach salable size within one year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The experiments were conducted in fertilized rearing ponds at [[Auburn, Alabama]]. Ponds received three applications of fertilizer equivalent to 200 pounds of 8-8-4 per acre per application in initial trials, though later experiments omitted fertilizer to isolate feed effects. Water temperatures during winter feeding periods ranged from the high thirties to low forties Fahrenheit, representing challenging conditions for fish growth.

Spawning activity was observed when water temperatures reached approximately 60°F, with recently hatched fry present by mid-April. Adult fatheads die soon after spawning, which may have contributed to higher mortality in ponds drained latest in spring. The natural productivity of ponds contributed to growth beyond that attributable to supplemental feeding alone, though conversion factors were calculated discounting this natural food contribution.

## Key Findings

**Initial High-Density Feeding Trials (Table I):**
- Small fatheads (0.5-1.9 pounds per thousand) grew rapidly during cold weather on both soybean cake and soybean meal-cottonseed meal (1:1) mixture
- Stocking rates: 100,000-120,000 fish per acre
- Feeding: 30-60 pounds per day, six days per week
- Survival: 64-87% over 39-87 day trials
- Growth: Fish increased from 0.53 to 5.0 pounds per thousand in 87 days (soybean meal-cottonseed meal, 86% survival)
- Average daily gain per acre: 4.70-5.46 pounds
- Conversion factors: 7.11-8.17 pounds feed per pound gain
- Gross income above feed and fertilizer costs: $533.26-$721.54 per acre

**Extreme High-Density Trial:**
- Stocking at 248,000-276,000 fish per acre (0.45 pounds per thousand) failed to produce growth over five weeks
- When restocked at 100,000 per acre and fed 60 pounds per day four times weekly, fish achieved four-fold weight increase in one month with 10% mortality
- Heavy feeding rates appeared wasteful; lower rates with daily feeding deemed more desirable

**Peanut Meal and Fish Meal Trials (Table II):**
- Stocking: 120,000-136,000 fish per acre (0.7-0.8 pounds per thousand)
- Feeding: Fish meal one day per week, peanut meal six days per week (including double ration Saturday)
- Two feeding rates tested: 2% and 4% initial body weight daily, doubled after two months
- Duration: 152-187 days
- No fertilizer used
- Lower rate (2%) results: 87% average survival, 1.88-2.27 conversion factor (average 2.08), gross income $475.65-$923.59 per acre
- Higher rate (4%) results: 47% average survival, 2.77-5.70 conversion factor (average 4.34), gross income $201.42-$748.05 per acre
- Direct relationship observed between feeding rate and conversion factors
- Most efficient gains occurred in ponds with highest survival

**Auburn No. 1 Fish Feed Trials (Table III):**
- Feed composition: 35% soybean oil meal, 35% peanut oil meal, 15% fish meal, 15% distillers dried solubles
- Stocking: 80,000-190,000 fish per acre (1.2-1.67 pounds per thousand)
- Feeding rates: 1%, 2%, 3%, and 4% body weight daily, six days per week (double ration Saturday)
- Duration: 65-132 days
- No fertilizer used
- Survival: 78-89% across feeding rates (84% overall average), substantially higher than peanut meal-fish meal trials
- Conversion factors: 2.0-4.0 pounds feed per pound gain (average 2.5 at 1% level, 3.5 at 4% level)
- More efficient gains at lower feeding rates
- Gross income above feed costs: $507.44 per acre at 1% feeding rate, $638.38-$689.18 per acre at 2-4% rates

**Disease and Management Notes:**
- Multiple diseases and parasites encountered during trials
- Copper sulfate at 1 ppm tested for [[Columnaris]] control but not extensively; did not provide successful control where used
- Feeding continued at water temperatures in low forties to high thirties Fahrenheit
- Feeding amounts were not varied according to temperature

**General Conclusions:**
- Winter feeding of small fatheads is feasible and can produce rapid growth even in cold water
- Lower feeding rates (1-2% body weight) generally produced more efficient conversion and higher survival than heavy feeding (4%+)
- Auburn No. 1 mixed feed produced superior survival (84%) compared to peanut meal-fish meal combination (67%)
- Optimal stocking density appears to be 100,000-120,000 fish per acre for winter grow-out
- Daily feeding six days per week more effective than intermittent heavy feeding
