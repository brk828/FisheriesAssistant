# Gila Taxonomy Project: Solicitation and Research Plan for Colorado River Basin Chubs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Upper Colorado Region) federal solicitation, No. 0-SP-40-09700, for a total small business set-aside contract to carry out the [[Gila Taxonomy Project]]. The project seeks to resolve long-running uncertainty about the taxonomy of endangered [[Gila]] chubs in the [[Colorado River Basin]] and to support recovery and management decisions. The bulk of the text is the solicitation package: a schedule of bid items, research scope, and the full set of Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) and Reclamation contract clauses. It also attaches the Phase I, Parts 2 and 3 report (dated 5 March 1990, final draft), which sets out the taxonomic problems, management concerns, priority research needs, proposed methods and sampling guidelines.

## Background & Description

**Project context.** Recovery programs for endangered Gila chubs have been hampered by questions about which taxa are present. The Colorado River Recovery Implementation Committee requested a definitive study under a cooperative agreement between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 6) and the [[Smithsonian Institution]], which supplies a Project Manager (Wayne C. Starnes, Ph.D.). A Review Panel of scientists and agency biologists provides oversight. Funding comes from Utah, Wyoming, Colorado, the Fish and Wildlife Service and Reclamation.

**Taxonomic background.** The genus Gila is a complex of large western North American minnows. Its monophyly is uncertain. The Colorado drainage forms are collectively called the "Gila robusta complex". The traditional view held that mainstem populations comprised three species:
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), described by Miller (1946), mostly confined to canyon reaches
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*), now endangered and rare in the upper basin
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*), which also inhabits smaller rivers and creeks

Intermediate specimens have complicated the picture. Competing explanations include hybridization, intraspecific variation, incomplete speciation and a "superspecies" concept. Tributary and lower-basin forms, including the [[Virgin River Chub]] (*G. seminuda*), *G. jordani*, *G. intermedia* and *G. grahami*, also have unresolved status.

**Solicitation structure.** The package includes:
- Standard Form 33 (Solicitation, Offer and Award), with offers due at 4:30 PM at 125 South State Street, Salt Lake City, Utah
- Part I, Schedule (Sections A to H), covering objectives, scope, the four research schedules and administration
- Part II, Contract Clauses (Section I), which contains roughly 80 FAR and Reclamation clauses, summarized here rather than reproduced
- Part III, Attachments (Section J)
- Part IV, Representations and Instructions (Sections K and L)
- Section M, Evaluation Factors

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Endangered status and sampling constraints.** The humpback, bonytail and Virgin River chubs are federally listed as endangered. Sampling must therefore minimize mortality. The project prioritizes [[Non-Lethal Sampling]] methods, including imagery and small tissue clips, and cryogenic storage of tissues.

**Geographic scope.** Studies cover Gila populations across the Colorado River Basin, including disjunct pluvial tributaries. Sampling is organized into regions:
- Gila River Basin (AZ, NM)
- Bill Williams System (AZ)
- Virgin and Moapa-Pluvial White systems (AZ, NV, UT)
- San Juan River System (CO, NM)
- Upper Colorado River System (including Dolores and Gunnison, CO)
- Western Green River tributaries (UT)
- Upper Yampa River and Lake and Snake systems (CO, WY)
- Upper Green River System (WY)
- Tributaries of the Gulf of California (NW Mexico), funded only if resources permit

Large-river work covers the Yampa-Upper Green area, the Middle Green River, the Colorado River from Debeque, and the Grand Canyon to Lower Little Colorado River area.

**Habitat concerns.** Many populations have been extirpated by habitat alteration since the 1800s. The bonytail is virtually extirpated from the Gila River system and the lower Colorado, except possibly in Mohave and Havasu reservoirs. Impoundment, including Glen Canyon Dam and Flaming Gorge, is cited as a possible cause of hybridization and gene loss. Many Gila River populations are isolated by dewatering or natural barriers.

## Key Findings

**Project objectives (Section C.2):**
1. Develop non-lethal techniques for obtaining morphological and genetic data from endangered Gila.
2. Document the extent of morphological and genetic variation across the Colorado Basin.
3. Determine whether mainstem chub populations, especially canyon reaches, comprise two or more discrete taxa.
4. If multiple taxa exist, determine whether introgressive hybridization is occurring.
5. Determine the taxonomic status of tributary populations.
6. Improve knowledge of larval-juvenile taxonomy (deferred to a separate RFP).
7. Facilitate future genetic monitoring and recovery management.

**Research schedules (Section C.4).** Contractors may bid on any combination of four schedules:
- **Schedule I, Morphological Investigations:** detailed analyses on specimens with genetic data, using imagery-based non-lethal methods.
- **Schedule II, [[Protein Electrophoresis]]:** allozyme and isozyme analysis, able to identify heterozygotes and document hybridization. Initially 30 to 50 loci are to be screened on a small sample.
- **Schedule III, [[DNA Analysis]]:** range-wide study of nuclear and mitochondrial DNA, including restriction-site screening.
- **Schedule IV, Chromosomal Investigations:** banding studies, funded only if resources exceed those needed for Schedules I to III.

**Timeline and deliverables.**
- Phase II (research) lasts 3 years from contract execution, plus 6 months for analysis refinement and report support.
- Semi-annual progress information is due about 15 March and 15 September each year.
- The final report is due 3 months after input submission, with 12 months' notice to investigators.
- Two Denver meetings are planned about one year apart, the first tentatively in June 1991.

**Pricing structure.** The contract is firm-fixed price. Small tributary sampling and Denver meetings are indefinite-quantity items, with prices required per sampling trip (3 locations per trip in most regions; 2 in some; 8 in the Gulf of California tributaries). Large-river participation is priced separately. Specific dollar amounts are blank in the document.

**Key contract conditions.**
- Phase II runs under the Schedule of "[[Cooperative Data Sharing]]": contractors must freely exchange information, and failure to do so is grounds for dismissal.
- Funds are not presently available beyond Fiscal Year 1990. Funding is contingent on appropriations, with projected availability of 10% (current year), then 30% per year from 1990 to 1993.
- The solicitation is a total small business set-aside under FAR 52.219-6.
- Collecting permits for endangered species are obtained from federal and state agencies, with Project Manager assistance.
- Final acceptance is by the Contracting Officer, with verification by the Contracting Officer's Technical Representative.

**Attachment 1 (Phase I, Parts 2 and 3) key points:**
- The taxonomic problems divide into (1) the status of variable mainstem chubs, whether intraspecific variation or introgression, and (2) the status of tributary chub populations.
- Previous studies of narrower scope failed to resolve these questions.
- Opinions on the Yampa and Green chub populations, the Black Rocks area and the Grand Canyon humpbacks conflict, based on published biochemical and morphological evidence cited in the document.
- Sampling guidelines (Part 3) cover personnel, collection methods, specimen handling, non-lethal procedures, deposition of materials and a table of collection localities (summarized here; not reproduced).
