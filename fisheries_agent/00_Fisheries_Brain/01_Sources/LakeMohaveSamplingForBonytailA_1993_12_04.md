## Executive Summary
This 1993 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum outlines a cooperative, multi-agency sampling effort at [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona/Nevada border) targeting two endangered Colorado River fishes: the [[Bonytail Chub]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]]. The operation, scheduled for 13-20 March 1994, had dual objectives: (1) collecting wild adult bonytail chub to bolster captive broodstock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]], and (2) continuing a long-term tag-recapture program on razorback suckers to monitor a documented, severe population decline. The document reveals critical demographic data showing the wild razorback sucker population collapsed from an estimated 66,000 adults in 1984 to approximately 25,000 by 1992, underscoring the urgency of the conservation and monitoring effort.

## Background & Description
- **Bonytail Chub ([[Bonytail Chub]])**:
  - Target retention size: **>35 cm total length (TL)**.
  - Individuals below this threshold or bearing fin clips are identified as **hatchery-produced** rather than wild-origin fish.
  - Hatchery-derived fish were to be measured, weighed, and photographed prior to release (not retained for broodstock).

- **Razorback Sucker ([[Razorback Sucker]])**:
  - Tagging size threshold: **>300 mm total length (TL)** for PIT tag application.
  - Species is identifiable in the field by external morphology sufficient to distinguish adults for tagging protocols (characteristic keeled nuchal hump typical of the species, implied by common name, though not explicitly detailed in text).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Both species inhabit [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the lower [[Colorado River]] formed by [[Davis Dam]], spanning the Arizona-Nevada border. This lentic (reservoir) environment differs substantially from the historic riverine habitat of both species.
- **Population Structure**: The razorback sucker population in Lake Mohave represents one of the last significant wild remnant populations of the species in the Colorado River Basin, making it a critical focus for [[Endangered Species Conservation]].
- **Recruitment Failure**: The population is described as **not expected to persist indefinitely**, implying a lack of natural recruitment (likely due to predation on larvae/juveniles by non-native fishes), a well-documented issue in Colorado River razorback sucker biology.
- **Stocking Augmentation**: Smaller, hatchery-reared razorback suckers produced in **growout facilities** are being tagged and released by the **Native Fish Group** into Lake Mohave, representing an active augmentation strategy to counteract wild population decline.
- **Spatial Distribution**: Sampling divided the lake into distinct management/geographic zones:
  - **Arizona Bay** – U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Arizona Fishery Resource Offices).
  - **Cottonwood Basin (west)** – U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, Nevada Department of Wildlife, U.S. Park Service.
  - **Cottonwood Basin (east)** – Arizona State University.
- **Movement Ecology**: Prior tagging data (from >10,000 razorback suckers captured since 1980) has generated inference on **local and long-distance movement patterns** and **site fidelity** (or lack thereof) within the lake system.

## Key Findings
- **Population Decline Trajectory (Razorback Sucker, Lake Mohave)**:
  - 1984: ~66,000 estimated adults
  - 1990: ~44,000 estimated adults
  - 1992: ~25,000 estimated adults
  - This represents a **~62% decline** over an 8-year period, corroborated by independent catch statistics (P. Marsh, pers. comm.).
- **Tagging History**: Since 1980, over **10,000 razorback suckers** captured; approximately **5,000 tagged**, forming one of the most extensive individual-based datasets for this endangered catostomid.
- **Tagging Methodology**: Standard protocol shifted toward **PIT (Passive Integrated Transponder) tags** for all qualifying fish, with traditional **spaghetti-type tags** retained as contingency backup.
- **Conservation Genetics/Broodstock**: Wild bonytail chub >35 cm TL are actively being removed from the wild population and translocated (via [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]) to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] to diversify/support the captive broodstock — indicating the wild population is at critically low, unsustainable levels, consistent with the bonytail chub's status as one of the rarest freshwater fishes in North America at the time.
- **Multi-agency Collaboration**: The effort exemplifies coordinated recovery action under the framework of the [[Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], involving USFWS, Bureau of Reclamation, Arizona State University, Nevada Department of Wildlife, and the National Park Service.
- **Monitoring Rationale**: Explicitly framed as tracking a **predicted, ongoing decline** in wild razorback sucker numbers over the coming decade, alongside assessment of recruitment success from hatchery-reared, growout-released juveniles.
