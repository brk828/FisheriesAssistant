# The Epimera of North American Fresh-Water Species of Gammarus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Gammaridae #Amphipoda #Crustacea

## Executive Summary

This 1970 taxonomic paper by Gerald A. Cole addresses persistent identification difficulties among North American freshwater species of the amphipod genus **[[Gammarus]]** by providing detailed comparative descriptions of the **epimera** (abdominal side plates / metasomal plates) of the first three abdominal segments. These structures — rarely illustrated in depth in prior literature — are shown to carry diagnostic setal and spinal armature patterns that can reliably distinguish morphologically similar and ecologically overlapping species, including the taxonomically confused **[[Gammarus minus]]**, **[[Gammarus fasciatus]]**, and **[[Gammarus tigrinus]]** complex. The paper presents species-by-species descriptions for ten taxa (including two subspecies of *G. lacustris*) and situates them within a broader discussion of subgeneric relationships (*Gammarus* vs. *Rivulogammarus*) and biogeographic distribution across glaciated and unglaciated North America.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic context:** Gammarid amphipod identification is notoriously difficult due to:
- Variation among instars and size classes
- Sexual dimorphism
- Differences between summer and winter populations
- Lack of systematic value in genitalic characters
- Overlapping ecological and geographic ranges
- Morphological divergence among isolated conspecific populations

Prior to Bousfield's (1958) foundational work applying European taxonomic refinements to Nearctic fauna, confusion was widespread — e.g., **[[Gammarus minus]]** has historically been conflated with *G. pseudolimnaeus*, *G. troglophilus*, *G. pecos*, and *G. lacustris*. Similarly, the status of **[[Gammarus fasciatus]]** vs. **[[Gammarus tigrinus]]** remained unsettled for decades (Hynes 1954; Hynes et al. 1960; Bousfield 1958, 1969).

**Anatomical structures examined — the Epimera:**
The paper focuses on three paired abdominal side plates (epimeron I, II, III), described generically before being detailed per species:

- **Epimeron I:** Rounded plate with a faint diagonal chitinous line running antero-proximal to postero-ventral; anterior setae near the antero-ventral angle become stouter marginally; postero-ventral angle usually marked by a sharp notch bearing a seta; posterior margin convex with short setae.
- **Epimeron II:** Marked by a proximal anterior concavity followed by a broad antero-distal convexity; ventral surface straight to faintly convex; postero-ventral corner ranges from quadrate to conspicuously produced; bears **facial armature** (spines/setae on the plate face) — a key diagnostic feature not present on epimeron I.
- **Epimeron III:** Broader than epimeron II; shallower anterior concavity; lacks the diagonal chitinous line seen in I and II; facial armament very rare on this plate; posterior setae numerically fewer than epimeron II.

**Reference species used to illustrate general morphology:** **[[Gammarus duebeni]]** (a euryhaline species found in tide pools and tidal fresh waters from southern Labrador to Massachusetts) was chosen because no freshwater species overlaps with it geographically or ecologically, allowing it to serve as an uncomplicated morphological baseline — though its epimera closely resemble those of *G. minus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Each species occupies characteristic — and sometimes overlapping — freshwater or brackish habitats:

- **[[Gammarus tigrinus]]**: Brackish and tidal fresh waters, Gulf of St. Lawrence to North Carolina, plus a disjunct Florida (St. John's River) population; co-occurs with sibling species **[[Gammarus daiberi]]** in the Chesapeake–Delaware Bay region. Introduced to England, Ireland, Germany, and the Netherlands.
- **[[Gammarus fasciatus]]**: Lower tidal and non-tidal reaches of Atlantic rivers/streams (St. Lawrence to Chesapeake drainage); present in the Great Lakes and possibly the Mississippi system.
- **[[Gammarus bousfieldi]]**: Known only from a single cave stream in Meade Co., Kentucky, coexisting with aberrant *G. minus*.
- **[[Gammarus pecos]]**: Restricted to a limnocrene and spring brook near Fort Stockton, Pecos Co., Texas; related, taxonomically uncertain populations occur north along the Pecos River drainage to Roswell, NM.
- **[[Gammarus pseudolimnaeus]]**: Widespread in the Mississippi drainage and southwestern St. Lawrence system; occupies large rivers, lakes, tributary streams, springs, rheocrenes, and cave streams; co-occurs with *G. fasciatus*, *G. troglophilus*, and *G. minus*.
- **[[Gammarus troglophilus]]**: Common in cave streams, springs, and rheocrenes south of Wisconsin glaciation limits (Illinois, Missouri); co-occurs with *G. pseudolimnaeus*, *G. minus*, or *G. acherondytes*.
- **[[Gammarus lacustris]]** (subspecies *limnaeus* and *lacustris*): *G. l. limnaeus* occupies the St. Lawrence drainage basin; *G. l. lacustris* occurs in the Hudson Bay drainage, western North America, and northwestern Europe — raising unresolved questions about whether this represents one polytypic species or two distinct species. Notably, lotic (flowing-water) specimens tend to show more acutely produced abdominal side plate corners than lentic (still-water) forms.
- **[[Gammarus acherondytes]]**: Restricted to cave streams of Monroe Co., Illinois (north of the Illinois Ozarks); co-occurs with *G. troglophilus*.
- **[[Gammarus minus]]**: Found in an east-west band south of Wisconsin Glaciation limits and north of the Mississippi Embayment's maximal Eocene Sea extent, ranging from Philadelphia/Washington regions west into the Ozark Plateau. Shows pronounced geographic variation in general morphology, but epimeral structure is remarkably uniform across its range.
- **[[Gammarus duebeni]]**: Euryhaline; tide pools and tidal fresh waters, southern Labrador to Massachusetts.

This distributional patchwork — glaciated vs. unglaciated refugia, cave-stream endemism, and coastal brackish-water introductions — underscores how isolation and habitat type (lotic vs. lentic, cave vs. surface, tidal vs. non-tidal) likely drive the morphological divergence documented in the epimera.

## Key Findings

- **Epimeral armature is a robust diagnostic tool**: Shape, setation density, and presence/absence of spines on the three abdominal epimera reliably distinguish species that overlap ecologically and are otherwise difficult to separate (e.g., *G. tigrinus* vs. *G. fasciatus*).
- **Species-specific armature patterns**:
  - **G. tigrinus**: Facial spines on epimeron II form a sharply oblique row leading to the penultimate/ultimate ventral spine — considered diagnostic. Ventral spines common on epimeron III.
  - **G. fasciatus**: Much more heavily setose across all three plates than *G. tigrinus*; spines are extremely rare on epimeron III (versus common in *G. tigrinus*), providing the key distinguishing feature between the two.
  - **G. bousfieldi**: Unique among all species examined in having **no spines whatsoever** on any epimeron — all armature is setal.
  - **G. pecos**: Epimera resemble those of *G. tigrinus*, supporting the hypothesis that *G. pecos* arose from a *G. tigrinus*-like ancestor; helps blur the boundary between subgenera *Gammarus* and *Rivulogammarus*.
  - **G. pseudolimnaeus**: Uniquely, epimeron III bears ventral spines each accompanied by a short seta — a combination not seen in other species. Second plate resembles *G. bousfieldi*; first plate is intermediate between *G. fasciatus* and *G. bousfieldi*.
  - **G. troglophilus**: Most easily confused with *G. pseudolimnaeus*, but consistently distinguishable via epimeron III ventral armature.
  - **G. lacustris** (both subspecies): Postero-ventral corners of epimera II and III are "remarkably produced" — more so in North American *G. l. limnaeus* than in English or western North American *G. l. lacustris* populations, per Bousfield (1958) and Hynes et al. (1960).
  - **G. acherondytes**: Unique oblique ventral margin on epimeron I; epimeron II resembles *G. minus*; epimeron III armature resembles *G. troglophilus*/*G. lacustris*/*G. minus* but shaped like the latter.
  - **G. minus**: Sparsest epimeral armature of all species examined; most similar to *G. acherondytes* and *G. duebeni* in this regard. Facial armature on epimeron II particularly reduced.
  - **G. duebeni**: Though geographically isolated from *G. minus* by 400+ km, shares strong epimeral similarity; differs via more posterior setae on all plates, an additional marginal seta near the anterior convexity of plates II/III, more convex ventral margins, and vertically (rather than diagonally) arranged facial armature on epimeron II.
- **General setation gradient**: Across the ten taxa, species can be ranked from most setose (**G. bousfieldi**) to least setose (**G. minus**), providing a useful comparative framework.
- **Facial armature** (spines/setae on the plate face, distinct from marginal/submarginal setae) is essentially restricted to epimeron II and is characteristically arranged diagonally, leading toward the penultimate or ultimate ventral seta/spine — a pattern most pronounced in *G. tigrinus* and least pronounced in *G. bousfieldi*.
- **Taxonomic/nomenclatural note**: The subgenus **[[Rivulogammarus]]** remains incompletely defined; the epimeral evidence presented herein suggests that *G. pecos* may bridge *Gammarus* s.s. and *Rivulogammarus* more closely than previously appreciated.
