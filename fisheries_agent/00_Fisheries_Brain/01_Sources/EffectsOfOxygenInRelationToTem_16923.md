# Effects of Oxygen in Relation to Temperature on Trout Embryo Development

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by E. T. Garside (University of Toronto), published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, examines how [[Water Temperature]] and [[Dissolved Oxygen]] jointly affect the embryonic development and [[Hatching Time]] of [[Brook Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]. Development accelerated with rising temperature and slowed progressively as dissolved oxygen fell. Hypoxia delayed development more strongly at higher temperatures. The work extends an earlier study on [[Lake Trout]] and critiques the use of [[Van't Hoff Rule]] analyses and linear "developmental zero" extrapolations in fish embryology.

## Background & Description

Salmonid embryos are sensitive to two environmental variables: temperature and dissolved oxygen. The study continues work begun in autumn 1957 (Garside, 1959, on Lake Trout). Its aims were to measure:

- the velocity of embryonic development, using the times needed to reach selected stages;
- the time of hatching and the length of the hatching period;
- the influence of temperature and oxygen on both.

Development was staged using an expanded 18-stage series (Garside, 1959). Four stages (4, 8, 12, and 16) were singled out for detailed analysis. Stage 4 is one-quarter epiboly, stage 8 is blastopore closure, stage 12 is one-quarter vitelline venation, and stage 16 is notochord upturning.

**Experimental design**

| Experiment | Species | Source and scale | Temperatures (°C) | Oxygen levels |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1957 | Brook Trout | ~12,000 fertilized eggs, Mount Pleasant hatchery, 26 Oct 1957; 16 lots | 2.5, 5.0, 7.5, 10.0 | ~2.5, 3.5, 4.5 ppm (hypoxic) and control near air-saturation (~10 ppm) |
| 1958 | Rainbow Trout | ~15,000 eggs, Normandale hatchery, 16 Apr 1958; 11 lots | 2.5, 5.0, 7.5, 10.0 | ~2.5 and 3.5 ppm (hypoxic) and control (~10.5–11.3 ppm) |
| 1959 | Rainbow Trout | ~3,500 eggs from a wild female, Nottawasaga River, 23 Apr 1959; 3 lots | 12.5, 15.0, 17.5 | Control (near air-saturation) only |

Hypoxic treatments were terminated when embryos were advanced, and control oxygen was then restored. Each lot was sampled with five eggs at set intervals and preserved in Davidson's solution. A stage was recorded when at least three of the five embryos showed its features. Brook Trout and Rainbow Trout eggs at 2.5 and 5 °C were sampled every other day, those at 7.5 and 10 °C daily, and the 1959 eggs every 12 hours.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study frames oxygen supply and temperature as two primary controls on early life history in cold-water salmonids. Key points from the literature cited:

- **Oxygen and metabolism:** Lower oxygen reduces developmental rate across all species tested (Kinne and Kinne, 1962; Silver et al., 1963). The retardation may reflect a metabolic adjustment: embryos slow their developmental metabolism and develop normally, but more slowly.
- **Sensitivity differences:** Lake Trout suffered teratologic development and mortality at 2.5 and 3.3 ppm at 10 °C. Brook and Rainbow Trout showed less frequent abnormalities, and some normal-looking embryos developed in every hypoxic environment.
- **Comparison with other salmonids:** Silver et al. (1963) found survival of Rainbow Trout and Chinook Salmon embryos at 2.5 ppm, but total mortality at 1.6 ppm. Alderdice et al. (1958) predicted oxygen requirements of 5.6 to 7.2 ppm for Chum Salmon, which Garside considers much higher than the levels observed here. He notes that the models used (Krogh, 1941) do not fit embryo physiology.
- **Hatchling size:** Silver et al. (1963) reported that alevin length rose with oxygen and water velocity. Temperature has the opposite effect: warmer, faster-developing embryos hatch smaller (Gray, 1928). Garside calls the reasons for these opposing effects "completely obscure."

## Key Findings

**1. Temperature accelerates development.** Development time decreased with rising temperature in both species. Brook Trout took 94 days to pass all 18 stages at 2.5 °C and 34 days at 10 °C (control oxygen). Rainbow Trout took 93 days at 2.5 °C and 14.5 days at 17.5 °C (control oxygen). The rate curves are not linear; stage 12 and stage 16 curves bend, and Garside concludes that Van't Hoff analysis does not apply.

**2. Hypoxia delays development, and the delay grows with temperature.**
- *Brook Trout:* The lowest oxygen level (~2.5 ppm) delayed stage 18 by 11 days at 2.5 °C and by 9 days at 10 °C. The relative delay was almost three times greater at 10 °C.
- *Rainbow Trout:* The lowest oxygen level delayed stage 18 by 4 days at 2.5 °C and by 8 days at 10 °C.
- The delay began at progressively earlier stages as temperature rose. At 2.5 °C, Rainbow Trout metabolism was apparently satisfied at ~2.9 ppm until the iris pigment stage (stage 13). At 10 °C a limiting effect appeared as early as stage 3 at 2.5 ppm.

**3. Hatching timing follows the same pattern.**
- *Brook Trout (control):* Hatching began 99 days after fertilization at 2.5 °C and 44 days at 10 °C. The hatching period lasted 43 days (median day 31) at 2.5 °C and 11 days (median day 7) at 10 °C. Incubation ran from 130 days to 50 days.
- *Brook Trout (hypoxia):* Extension of incubation was 9 days (7%) at 2.5 °C and 7 days (14%) at 10 °C.
- *Rainbow Trout (control):* Hatching began at 95 days (2.5 °C), 29 days (10 °C), and 17 days (17.5 °C). Incubation ranged from 106 to 18 days.
- *Rainbow Trout (hypoxia):* Hatching was delayed by 3 days at 2.5 °C and 8 days at 10 °C.

**4. Methodological critiques.** Garside argues that:
- Linear extrapolation of the central segment of the rate curve to find a "developmental zero" is invalid. True x-intercepts lie further left, and in most fish embryos they fall a few degrees below freezing.
- "Day-degree" calculations based on that zero are therefore invalid.
- Using equal thermal-time products to define equal developmental proportions is improper. Equal proportions of development should be defined by morphological criteria.

**5. Consistency with prior work.** The results agree with Lindroth (1942, 1946), Einsele (1956), Alderdice et al. (1958), and Garside (1959), and confirm that dissolved oxygen markedly influences both development rate and hatching time in these trout species.
