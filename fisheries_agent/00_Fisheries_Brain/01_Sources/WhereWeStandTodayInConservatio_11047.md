# Where We Stand Today in Conservation

#conservation-history #wildlife-management #natural-resources

## Executive Summary

This address, delivered by Seth Gordon, Vice President of the North American Wildlife Foundation, to the 25th [[North American Wildlife & Natural Resources Conference]] in Dallas, Texas (March 7–9, 1960), traces the history of organized conservation in North America. It argues that conservationists have repeatedly failed to learn from earlier efforts, and it reviews the chain of conferences that shaped current policy. The speech is a historical and retrospective review, not a research study. It contains no new field data, species surveys, or measurements. Fisheries are discussed only in passing, so this note covers the wider resource-conservation context rather than fish biology.

## Background & Description

The address was given to a meeting of wildlife and natural resource professionals. Its central claim is that conservation progress depends on studying the record of past efforts.

- **Origins:** The speaker identifies [[Theodore Roosevelt]]'s White House conference with the governors (May 13, 1908) as the starting point for organized public conservation. Roosevelt framed the conservation of natural resources as "the chief material question" facing the nation, second only to morality. A [[National Conservation Commission]] was appointed to inventory resources, followed by a Conservation Congress in December 1908 and a North American Conservation Conference in February 1909.
- **National Conservation Congresses (1909–1923):** A series of five civilian-led congresses began in Seattle (August 26–28, 1909). The last was held in Washington, D.C., in 1913, and reportedly drew about 1,000 delegates. The theme was "Let Us Conserve the Foundations of Our Prosperity." Topics included forestry, river development, water pollution, soil erosion, commercial fisheries, game protection, and migratory birds. British Columbia was represented, which the speaker sees as an early international approach.
- **Lineage of the current conference:** Although numbered the 25th, the meeting is described as the 45th in a continuous chain. The first series of 21 conferences was run by the [[American Game Association]], beginning with the "National Conference on Game Breeding & Preserving" at the Waldorf-Astoria Hotel on March 1, 1915. A second series of 25 was sponsored by the [[American Wildlife Institute]] (organized 1935) and its successor, the [[Wildlife Management Institute]]. The series was interrupted only in one war year.
- **Outdoor Recreation Conferences (1924–1928):** Initiated by President Calvin Coolidge, this series was planned by a committee of five cabinet members. The first meeting (May 22–24, 1924) had 309 delegates from 28 national organizations and adopted fifteen resolutions. These covered federal land policy, wilderness, wildlife refuges, and water pollution. The speaker notes that many of today's conservation workers have never heard of this series.
- **First North American Wildlife Conference (1936):** Called by President [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] and held in Washington, D.C., February 3–7, 1936. The proceedings ran to 675 pages, and the conference led to the founding of the [[National Wildlife Federation]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The address places fish and wildlife management within a broader resource framework. The main environmental concerns it names are:

- **Fisheries and water:** Early congresses covered commercial fisheries, water pollution, and the harnessing of rivers. The speaker calls for recognition of water use for fish, wildlife, and recreation as a legitimate public use in all states. He also says that "more training is needed in the field of fisheries management."
- **Wetlands and waterfowl:** The address criticizes the drainage of prairie potholes "under the guise of aiding agriculture." It also refers to the federal waterfowl refuge system and to waterfowl restoration.
- **Watersheds and forests:** Forest fire control, watershed protection, and small watershed flood control programs are described as ongoing concerns.
- **Pollution:** Water pollution control is described as a public demand. The address also asks how synthetic detergents and compounds should be handled in sewage treatment plants.
- **Pesticides:** The speaker notes that serious attention was being paid to pesticides and poison sprays, which he says "aroused the public" after a cranberry-related incident.

## Key Findings

- **Historical continuity:** Modern conservation policy grew out of a sequence of conferences from 1908 onward. The speaker argues that these meetings created a shared base of concepts and techniques.
- **Repeated mistakes:** The speaker says conservationists have often failed to study past records, and so have "repeated, over and over, the same foolhardy blunders."
- **Policy legacy:** Many later measures trace back to conference recommendations. These include:
  - The 1916 Treaty with Canada to protect migratory birds.
  - The [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]] (1918).
  - The [[American Sportsmen's Platform]].
  - The Federal Waterfowl Refuge System (started 1920, enacted 1929).
  - The American Game Policy (adopted 1930).
  - The [[Duck Stamp]] law, which the conferences pushed for.
  - The [[Pittman-Robertson Federal Aid Program]] (enacted 1938, first proposed at the 1931 conference as an alternative to the Duck Stamp).
  - The [[Dingell-Johnson Program]] (1950), which supported fisheries work.
- **Professionalization:** Technically trained wildlife and fisheries workers were scarce in early conferences. Ding Darling, then head of the U.S. Biological Survey, announced in 1935 a funding plan for training. It included about $30,000 a year from the American Wildlife Institute, $2,000 a year from the Biological Survey, and matching state contributions.
- **Cooperative research units:** The [[Cooperative Wildlife Research Units]] began with Virginia's unit, and by the end of 1935 seven more were underway. The speaker reports sixteen units in operation by 1960. They had produced 808 master's and 121 doctoral degrees, and 2,283 bachelor's degrees are credited to students trained by them. About 156 students were then training in wildlife at master's and doctoral levels, and about 54 in fisheries.
- **Workforce:** The speaker rejects the idea of an oversupply of trained graduates. He says most are now employed by state and federal agencies, in universities, or in allied fields.
- **Progress since 1936:** The speaker lists advances in national forest wildlife and recreation programs, water pollution control, waterfowl restoration, federal-aid programs, soil conservation, small watershed programs, fisheries research, and a national recreational resources survey.
- **Remaining problems:** Prairie pothole drainage, forest fire control and watershed protection, synthetic compounds in sewage, recognition of water for fish and wildlife, and pesticide use.
- **Guiding principles:** The speaker concludes that no nation can build a balanced economy by wasting its resources. Resource programs must be planned in advance alongside forestry, water development, agriculture, and industry. Nations must also cooperate on international problems. He quotes Wisconsin Governor [[Walter J. Kohler]] (March 1951): "The real test of our democracy will come when our resources begin to run out."
- **Conclusion:** The speaker frames the 1960 meeting as "an occasion for a celebration, not one for lamentation," and expects continued "honest cooperation" among groups, drawing on a 1915 participant's view of the value of cooperation.
