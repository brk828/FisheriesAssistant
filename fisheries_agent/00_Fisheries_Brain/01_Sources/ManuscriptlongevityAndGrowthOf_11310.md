# Cui-ui Longevity and Opercular Ageing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This manuscript, circulated with a 1987 cover letter from [[G. Gary Scoppettone]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to [[James E. Deacon]], refines the age and growth of the endangered [[Cui-Ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), an endemic catostomid of [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. Using opercular bones rather than scales, the authors validate annulus formation over an eight-year interval (1978–1986), age fish to 41 years, and develop separate growth curves for males and females. They argue that scale-based ageing, long standard for Cypriniformes, substantially underestimates age, and that several large western North American Cypriniformes are far longer-lived than the literature reports. They also propose that long life in these fishes may reflect selection for iteroparity under drought-driven salinity regimes in terminal lakes.

## Background & Description

The paper begins from the premise that accurate age determination is central to fisheries management, and that errors are most likely for non-game species, which have received less study than game fish. Ageing methods must be validated before use, but validation is difficult. The standard mark-and-recapture approach requires recapturing marked fish, which is hard in large systems, and scales are unreliable for many catostomids (see [[Scale Ageing]]). Otolith daily growth rings can be validated with chemical marking ([[Otolith Daily Growth Rings]]), but processing is tedious.

The cover letter describes the manuscript as a springboard for an assigned chapter and notes an upcoming April 20 recovery meeting.

Study material and methods included:
- **Opercular preparation:** bone cleaned by boiling; museum specimens scraped with a scalpel. Annuli were identified following [[Casselman 1973]].
- **Hidden annuli:** the fenestrated support bone over the hyomandibular socket was ground away with a Dremel tool to count annuli obscured beneath it.
- **Scales:** taken from the left side below the dorsal fin and above the lateral line, read after [[Tesch 1971]], and compared with opercular ages.
- **Growth modelling:** ascribed age regressed on fork length, with least-squares fits and coefficients estimated by Quasi-Newton minimization. Separate curves were fitted by sex.
- **Sources:** adult mortalities from the 1982–1986 spawning migrations, gill-netted prespawning fish from 1978, hatchery losses, selective sacrifice, museum specimens, and juveniles from tribal gill netting, seining, and museums.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Cui-ui are lacustrine-potamodromous spawners in desert environments. The authors link their longevity to the variable hydrology of [[Great Basin Lakes]]. The 1930s drought is cited as a cause of the extinction of the Pyramid Lake [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], and prolonged drought is thought to have occurred repeatedly in the past ([[Benson 1978]]). Lake level fluctuations drive salinity increases in terminal lakes, which can block recruitment.

The authors also note that body size in western North American Cypriniformes appears to scale with habitat size, following [[Smith 1981]]. Larger, older fish seem to occur in large, stable water bodies, where environmental constraints on growth may be reduced. Tahoe and Sacramento suckers, which occur in both streams and lakes, reach their largest size and age in lacustrine settings.

Terminal lakes named in the discussion include [[Pyramid Lake]], [[Eagle Lake]], and [[Walker Lake]]. Sierra east-slope freshwater lakes ([[Lake Tahoe]], Donner Lake, and Independence Lake) are predicted to host the shortest-lived populations.

## Key Findings

**Age validation**
- Annulus formation on the opercle was validated over eight years, extended from the earlier two-year study by [[Scoppettone et al 1986]].
- Validation followed two dominant year classes, 1950 and 1969, through time. In 1978 these fish were 9 and 28 years old. In 1982 they were 13 and 32. In 1986 they were 17 and 36.
- The 1969 year class made up over 85% of mortalities in the four years before 1986, then dropped to 70%. The 1950 year class has made up less than 5% of mortalities on the upstream migration since 1983.
- Cui-ui year-class structure is sparse, with few dominant cohorts. The authors describe the paucity of year classes as a limit on how quickly growth gaps can be filled.

**Hidden and false annuli**
- Old cui-ui had 0 to 3 hidden annuli. Fish younger than about 5 years had none, and one hidden annulus appeared by age 6.
- Among post-juveniles over six years, females had more hidden annuli than males, which the authors attribute to larger size. By age 9, about 50% of females had two hidden annuli compared with 20% of males.
- False annuli occurred rarely and only in the first four years of life. They appear to form in late summer or early fall, and in the 1950 year class they served as a secondary identifier.
- Annuli form typically in June and early July, so May-collected fish were aged in anticipation of the next annulus.

**Operculae versus scales**
- Scales were unreliable and underestimated age in older fish. The oldest opercular fish (41 years) was aged only 11 by scales (based on Figure 4, which compares opercle and scale ages for 174 cui-ui).
- Scale ages in fish older than 28 years ranged from 7 to 12, roughly the age at which cui-ui first reproduce. Scale ageing becomes unreliable around sexual maturity.
- The earliest cui-ui recorded entering the spawning run was 6 years old, but many appear not to spawn until 9 or 10 years and some as late as 12.

**Growth**
- Three growth phases were identified: rapid growth through about years 9–10, slower growth until about years 18–20, and very slow or virtually no growth thereafter.
- Growth curves were fitted separately by sex, with separate equations for ages up to 10 and above 10:
  - Females, ages ≤10: FL = 105.4 × AGE^0.714 (N = 233, r = 0.988)
  - Females, ages >10: FL = 402.7 + 60.7 × ln(AGE) (N = 458, r = 0.626)
  - Males, ages ≤10: FL = 110.9 × AGE^0.670 (N = 243, r = 0.989)
  - Males, ages >10: FL = 448.7 + 29.6 × ln(AGE) (N = 260, r = 0.426)
- The text gives the first-phase female correlation as r = 0.968 and the male as 0.989, which differs from the figure value of 0.988 for females.
- The collection covered 28 different ages (1–41 years) and a complete annual series from 1–17 years.

**Longevity in other Cypriniformes**

Table 1 lists maximum ages for 10 species, taken from the oldest specimens encountered rather than from the oldest in each population:
- [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*): 43 years, the oldest Cypriniform examined
- [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*): 41 years
- [[June Sucker]] (*Chasmistes liorus*): 37 years
- [[Eagle Lake Tui-Chub]] (*Gila bicolor*): 32 years, the oldest cyprinid examined
- [[Sacramento Blackfish]] (*Orthodon microlepidotus*): 30 years, from a large Clear Lake specimen collected in 1927, before commercial harvest

The authors note that ages in these species exceed previous published upper limits by large margins: Sacramento blackfish by a factor of six ([[Moyle 1976]]), Pyramid Lake tui-chub by a factor of three ([[Galat et al 1981]]), and Eagle Lake tui-chub by a factor of six ([[Kimsey 1954]]).

**Discrepancy with earlier work**
- The authors' age-class structure conflicts with [[Sigler et al 1985]], even though both studies examined fish collected in 1978. Sigler et al. relied on unpublished scale-based work by [[Robertson 1979]].
- The authors state that Robertson did not account for hidden annuli and overlooked closely spaced outer annuli. They also suggest that the large head size of old fish is the only clear external difference in older cui-ui.

**Management implications and hypotheses**
- The authors recommend that ages in suspected long-lived species be validated before being used, with otolith daily growth rings as one option.
- They propose that fishery biologists have been biased by experience with salmonids and centrarchids, which are short-lived and often captured before reaching their age potential, and that scales have been wrongly assumed reliable for Cypriniformes.
- They hypothesize that iteroparity is widespread among western North American Cypriniformes and is selected for by drought-driven lethal salinity in terminal lakes. Longevity should be greatest in lakes most affected by drought, such as [[Eagle Lake]], with [[Walker Lake]] and [[Pyramid Lake]] following. The authors describe this as untested.

**Abstract conclusions**
- Cui-ui were aged to 41 years, and old opercula carried up to three hidden annuli.
- Cui-ui have three growth phases: rapid growth (0–10 years), slow growth (10–20 years), and extremely slow growth after 20.
- Fork length correlates strongly with age for roughly the first 10 years, weakening thereafter around the time of reproductive maturity.
- Scales also become invalid for ageing at about the time of maturity.
- Iteroparity may be a common feature of large temperate-zone Cypriniformes.
