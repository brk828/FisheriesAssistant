# Comanche Springs Pupfish Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan, prepared by the [[Rio Grande Fishes Recovery Team]] and approved by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on April 20, 1981, addresses the [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon elegans*), a species listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The species historically occupied two isolated spring systems in the Pecos River drainage of West Texas. Its type locality, Comanche Springs, went dry in 1955, and the remaining wild population survives mainly in irrigation canals and at Giffin and San Solomon Springs near Balmorhea. The plan identifies three major threats: competition with introduced species, hybridization with introduced *Cyprinodon variegatus*, and habitat loss from declining springflow. Its stated goals are to perpetuate the species in its natural habitat, maintain genetic diversity, and downlist it to threatened status. The team judged that delisting is unlikely because of the species' restricted range and declining spring flows.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and morphology.** *C. elegans* is one of two most distinctive of the 12–14 U.S. pupfish species; the other is the endangered [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*C. diabolis*). Its most diagnostic trait is the "speckled" color pattern of males. It also has a more streamlined body than is typical for the genus and lacks vertical bars.

**Local differentiation.** Extant populations differ in body form and belly scalation from specimens collected about 40 years earlier at the now-extinct type locality. [[Phantom Lake Spring]] and Toyah Creek populations differ in belly scalation and in dorsal and caudal fin-ray counts. The Giffin and San Solomon populations are intermediate.

**Historical distribution.** The species occurred in two spring systems about 190 km apart: Comanche Springs at Fort Stockton (Pecos County) and an interconnected spring system near Balmorhea (Reeves County). Comanche Springs supported irrigation of more than 6,000 acres by 1875. Historical Balmorhea-area marshes drained into Toyah Creek, a flood tributary of the Pecos River.

**Present distribution and abundance.** The population is sparse in most of the canal system but can reach up to 200 young-of-year and adults in single seine hauls in short segments. Individuals occasionally occur in Toyah Creek. Present habitat is mostly earthen and concrete irrigation canals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and salinity.** The species is known only from freshwater, with spring salinities up to about 1 ppt. Other Pecos River pupfishes (*C. bovinus*, *C. pecosensis*) occupy waters of 10 ppt or higher, which the authors cite as evidence of long ecological isolation of *C. elegans*.

**Thermal tolerance.** [[Gehlbach et al. 1978]] reported critical thermal maxima of 40.4 °C and 39.6 °C for aquarium-held individuals, similar to other *Cyprinodon* species.

**Ecology.** Feeding occurs mostly on the bottom but also at the surface and mid-water. Small specimens occur through most months, suggesting breeding across much of the year. Spawning occurs in both spring outflows and standing pools. The species is most abundant in shallow canal areas with low current velocity.

**Competitors and associated fauna.** Dominant co-occurring species include *Gambusia geiseri* (introduced), *Dionda episcopa*, and *G. nobilis* near spring heads. Canals also hold *Lepomis megalotis*, *L. cyanellus*, *Notropis lutrensis*, *Cyprinus carpio* (introduced), and many others. Lake Balmorhea's inflow canal fauna totals roughly 20 species, most of them recorded as introduced, and *C. variegatus* first appeared there in the mid-1960s.

**Major threats:**
1. **Competition** with introduced species, especially *C. variegatus*, which may exclude *C. elegans* from unstable upstream habitats.
2. **Hybridization** with *C. variegatus*. In the hybrid zone, *C. variegatus* outnumbers *C. elegans* by more than 10 to 1 in the inflow canal, and *variegatus* genes have introgressed into the population.
3. **Habitat loss** from declining springflow and surface water.

**Hydrology.** Phantom Lake Spring is failing faster than San Solomon and Giffin Springs because of its higher discharge elevation. The plan cites a prediction that Phantom Lake Spring may dry within 50 years. Groundwater mining for municipal and agricultural use has lowered the water table; the 1950s failure of Comanche Springs eliminated about half of the presumed historic habitat.

## Key Findings

**Conservation status:** Endangered under the Endangered Species Act (*Federal Register* Vol. 32:4001, March 11, 1967); also protected under Chapter 68 of the Texas Parks and Wildlife Code and listed by the [[American Fisheries Society]], the Texas Organization for Endangered Species, and the [[International Union for Conservation of Nature]].

**Recovery objectives:**
- Perpetuate the species in its natural habitat.
- Maintain genetic diversity by improving habitat quality, increasing habitat quantity, and establishing ongoing management and public information programs.
- Downlist from endangered to threatened. Delisting is considered unlikely.

**Conservation efforts to date:**
- A refugium at [[Balmorhea State Recreation Area]], completed in 1974 by the [[Texas Parks and Wildlife Department]], supports several thousand fish.
- A genetic stock at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], derived from about 30 individuals from a Giffin Springs-fed ditch, provides backup for reintroduction and research.

**Step-down recovery actions (summarized):**
- **1.0 Maintain and enhance habitat:** monitor populations (July and January sampling) and habitat; enhance habitat through periodic ditch flooding and expansion at Phantom Lake Spring; enter cooperative agreements with landowners and agencies; protect Phantom Lake Spring and the upper 1 km of Phantom Lake Canal; review regional hydrology; prepare management plans; and consider critical habitat.
- **2.0 Genetic reserve:** maintain stocks at Dexter and Balmorhea.
- **3.0 Public information:** produce a pamphlet and a 15–20 minute film.
- **4.0 Enforcement:** enforce state and federal protective laws.

**Implementation cost estimates:** The schedule lists per-task costs in the low thousands of dollars for most actions, with the hydrology review estimated at $10,000. The team provided no total budget.

**Agency review and disputes:**
- The [[Water and Power Resources Service]] (March 1981 memo) criticized the plan for lacking specifics. It recommended designating Phantom Lake Spring and about 100 yards of bench flume as critical habitat. It also opposed constructing a new refugium canal because of evaporation and percolation losses, and it reported that Phantom Lake Spring averaged 5.3 ft³/s over the previous four years.
- The Reeves County Water Improvement District No. 1 asked to be consulted on flooding, canal construction, and any comprehensive management plan.
- The Texas Parks and Wildlife Department found its recommended changes mostly cosmetic.
- The recovery team declined critical habitat designation at that time, judging it unnecessary. It accepted the suggested wording changes and noted that the monitoring program would be revisited if needed.
