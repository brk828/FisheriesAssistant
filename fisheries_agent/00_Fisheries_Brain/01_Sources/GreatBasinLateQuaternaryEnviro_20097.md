# Great Basin Late Quaternary Environments and Chronology

#paleoclimatology #quaternary-ecology #great-basin-lakes

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Peter J. Mehringer Jr.]] (Washington State University), published in the [[Desert Research Institute]] symposium volume edited by [[Don D. Fowler]] (1977), synthesizes evidence for the magnitude and chronology of late Quaternary environmental change in the [[Great Basin]]. The author argues that climatic change is poorly correlated across the basin, that local resources and events such as volcanism and tectonics may have mattered more to people than regional climate, and that variability within a single year was as demanding as the long-term changes of the last 10,000 years. Aquatic systems, including [[Pluvial Lakes]], spring-fed marshes and their fish faunas, serve as key indicators throughout.

## Background & Description

The paper is a review rather than a primary study. It separates the evidence into geological sources (lake levels, glaciation, volcanism and tectonics, soils and sediments, wind action) and biological sources (plant macrofossils, fossil pollen, dendroclimatology, fossil animals). The author notes that generalized models linking culture to synchronous, unidirectional climatic change had dominated Great Basin archaeology for over 20 years, and that site-specific data increasingly showed chronological and geographic complexity. Data are arranged chronologically and regionally. The last 40,000 years are included, with emphasis on the last 12,000.

Table 1 in the source compares climatic changes inferred from pluvial lake water budgets against historic means. Estimates range from roughly 15 to 20 degrees F cooler temperatures to precipitation changes between -0.6 and +12 inches, depending on the author and assumed evaporation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lakes and hydrology.** Four well-studied pluvial systems (Bonneville, Lahontan, Searles and Mohave) anchor the regional chronology. Lake Bonneville is interpreted as not overflowing after about 12,500 B.P., falling below the level of [[Danger Cave]] by about 11,000 B.P. Lake Searles drained through [[Owens River]], [[Owens Lake]] and [[China Lake]] into [[Death Valley]], making its overflow chronology critical to the whole Death Valley system. Recent lake history at [[Pyramid Lake]] and [[Carson Sink]] is controlled by the [[Truckee River]]; before the 1905 diversion, Pyramid Lake fluctuated only about 20 feet, between 3880 and 3860 feet. [[Great Salt Lake]] shows higher-than-present levels near 6000, 3600 and 2800 B.P. in a preliminary core.

**Tectonics and isostasy.** Post-Bonneville isostatic rebound changed strandline elevations and may have been archaeologically more important than climate in northwestern Utah. The Great Basin has been tectonically active through the late Cenozoic. Over 200 earthquakes were recorded from 1961 to 1970, and 1173 shocks with Nevada epicenters from 1852 to 1961.

**Volcanism and ash marker beds.** Volcanic ash is the most useful dated stratigraphic marker in the basin. The [[Mount Mazama]] eruption (about 7000 B.P.) produced ash that is widespread across the northern Great Basin. [[Newberry Volcano]] eruptions occurred from about 6400 to 1200 B.P., and more than 40 pumice deposits from [[Mount St. Helens]] fall within the last 35,000 years. Five [[Mono Craters]] ash falls date from about 5000 to 1500 B.P.

**Glaciation.** The Sierra Nevada record includes Hilgard (10,500-9000 B.P.), Recess Peak (2600-2000 B.P.) and Matthes (about 700 B.P.) advances. A radiocarbon date of about 12,000 B.P. from the [[Raft River Mountains]] sets a minimum age for ice withdrawal.

**Soils, sediments and wind.** A widespread paleosol forms on surfaces older than 7000-6000 B.P. and is overlain by deposits younger than 4500 B.P. Dune activity is recurrent. In the [[Amargosa Desert]], dunes were active before 5300 to about 2500 B.P., from 2000 to 1000 B.P., and within the past 400 years. Dune-dammed springs created marshes at [[Saratoga Springs]] and [[Ash Meadows]] that supported waterfowl, mammals and endemic desert pupfish.

**Vegetation history.** Woodrat middens, ground sloth dung and cave deposits provide most plant macrofossil data. Junipers descended by up to 3000 feet into the [[Las Vegas Valley]] during the pluvial period. Bristlecone pine ([[Bristlecone Pine]]) and limber pine also grew at much lower elevations. Pollen from [[Swan Lake]] shows coniferous forest about 12,000 B.P., Artemisia steppe by 10,000 B.P., and the warmest semiarid interval between 8000 and 3000 B.P.

**Fauna and fish.** Extinction of North American megafauna by about 10,000 years ago is described as the most important biological event of the late Pleistocene. Pika at [[Connley Cave]] before about 8000 B.P. and pika and marten at [[Deer Creek Cave]] indicate cooler early postpluvial conditions. [[Hubbs and Miller]] (1948) remains the most comprehensive study of Great Basin fish distribution and hydrographic history. Late Pleistocene fishes of [[Lake Bonneville]] have also been described, and the endemic [[Desert Pupfish]] persisted in isolated spring systems such as Saratoga Springs.

## Key Findings

- **Chronology of aridity:** Pluvial lakes began drying about 12,000-10,000 B.P. Conditions were much like the present by 7500 B.P. Relative aridity persisted to about 4000 B.P., followed by increased effective moisture.
- **No uniform regional climate:** Correlation of lake histories is problematic because of differences in dating materials, basin connections, tectonics, volcanism, and atmospheric circulation. The author questions whether a single climatic signal applies across the whole basin.
- **Variability over scale:** The author argues that the last 10,000 years of instability are no greater than the ecological variation experienced within one year, and that variability may have shaped technological adaptation more than long-term trends.
- **Local resources matter most:** Climate's influence on people is best assessed through its effect on local resources such as marshes, springs, pinyon and mesquite, and playa-edge plants.
- **Non-climatic drivers:** Volcanic eruptions and isostatic adjustment may have had larger local effects than regional climate. Ash falls can cause loss of life and changes in productivity, and can also enrich soils.
- **Multiple high-water episodes:** Great Salt Lake levels rose in several episodes, including about 6000, 3600, 2800 and 3500-2200 B.P. Pyramid Lake and Carson Sink each record several cycles over the last 4000 years.
- **Aquatic and fish indicators:** Fish distribution, mollusks, diatoms and marsh pollen indicate past salinity and water depth. Spring-fed marshes record changes in discharge, and endemic fish isolation reflects past hydrologic connections.
- **Research priorities:** The author calls for more data to resolve the 7500-4000 B.P. interval, improved radiocarbon control, and tree-ring records to refine regional patterns for the last 2000 years.
