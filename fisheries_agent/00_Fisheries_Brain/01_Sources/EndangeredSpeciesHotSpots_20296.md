# Science Letters: Green Crab Taxonomy, Tritium Supply, and Endangered Species "Hot Spots"

#ichthyology #marine-biology #crustacea

## Executive Summary

This Science letters page is not a fish research paper. It contains reader correspondence, a short publisher's note, and several advertisements. The main scientific debate is a response dispute over [[Dobson Et Al]]'s analysis of [[Endangered Species Act]] "hot spots." Critics argue that the study's national-scale, single-component approach can be misused to justify weaker habitat protection. The authors reply that national hot spots are an essential, non-exclusive priority and that most listed species occur largely on private land. The page also includes brief letters on the taxonomic placement of exotic green crabs, the supply of tritium, and the history of atomic discovery. Two product advertisements (PCR beads and reference-management software) are present but carry no scientific content.

## Background & Description

The page contains the following items:

- **Green crab letters (three):** Hugh Smith, Gordon A. Fox, and John K. Pope respond to a Random Samples item, "Green crabs advance north" (11 Apr.). They question an apparent reclassification of the European green crab from Arthropoda to Mollusca, which they treat as an error. Pope's letter is facetious.
- **Tritium supply:** Harold M. Agnew proposes buying tritium from Russia each year rather than producing it in the United States. He claims this would save money (a factor of 10 over an accelerator and a factor of 5 over a reactor) and would have essentially no U.S. environmental impact.
- **Rutherford correction:** Alexander Small corrects a 14 March news article. Ernest Rutherford discovered that atoms have a small, positively charged nucleus, and he worked at the [[Cavendish Laboratory]], Cambridge University, not at Oxford.
- **Endangered species debate:** A critique by Christopher P. Dunn, Marlin L. Bowles, George B. Rabl, and Kris S. Jarantoski, followed by a joint response from David Ehrenfeld, Reed F. Noss, and Gary K. Meffe on behalf of the editors of [[Conservation Biology]], with rebuttal from the original authors.
- **Advertisements:** A [[Millipore]] MultiScreen Resist plate advertisement, a [[Ready-To-Go PCR Beads]] advertisement from Pharmacia Biotech, and an EndNote advertisement from Niles & Associates. None provides research data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species-protection geography.** Dunn et al. argue that the [[Dobson Et Al]] study examines only one component of biodiversity (listed species distribution). They note that:

- [[Illinois]] has 9 federally listed threatened or endangered species but more than 500 state-listed endangered species.
- About 9 million ha of [[Tallgrass Prairie]] existed before settlement. About 950 ha remain in pristine condition, and only 17% of that area is in units larger than 4 ha.
- Of 862 plant species recorded across the tallgrass prairie range, 851 persist in Illinois, though none is endemic. Each remaining site holds about 100 plant species.
- Seventeen species of mammals, birds, insects, and plants have been lost from Illinois prairies. Another 117 species are state-listed as threatened or endangered, including 95 plants.
- Federal land makes up 30% of the United States, with 95% of that ownership in the West and Alaska. Private ownership dominates in the Midwest and Northeast, where there are fewer habitat restrictions. Destruction of one private Kansas prairie caused the local loss of two plant species.
- The loss of a [[Bay Checkerspot Butterfly]] population on a Stanford preserve shows the risk of protecting species rather than habitats.

**Database concerns.** Dunn et al. state that the ESA list used in the study (924 species as of August 1995) is biased. In July 1995, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service removed nearly 4000 "category 2" candidate species, a decision they describe as based on political liability and cost rather than science. They cite the [[Nature Conservancy]] natural heritage database (January 1996), which lists 6246 U.S. species globally: 1339 critically imperiled, 1831 imperiled, and 3076 vulnerable.

**Private land.** Ehrenfeld et al. and the authors' response agree that private land matters. The response cites a study finding that over half of federally listed species have more than 80% of their habitat on nonfederal land. It proposes tax incentives, including estate tax breaks, to reward stewardship. It also argues that subsidies for extractive industries on federal land should be replaced with realistic pricing.

## Key Findings

- **Dobson et al. (24 Jan.)** find that federally listed species cluster in regional "hot spots." Critics argue this reflects endemism more than species richness, and that it is one component of biodiversity only.
- **Critics' main concern:** The findings could be misused to justify reduced conservation spending, especially for critical habitats, and to allow development in areas with fewer listed species. They also argue that the conclusion that "a relatively small proportion of the land mass" needs management is simplistic and conflicts with the authors' own caveats about large-range species such as grizzly bears.
- **Authors' response:** National hot spots remain an essential priority that does not preclude state, county, or private initiatives. The study was not intended to define the only criteria for conservation priorities.
- **Policy direction:** Both sides agree that habitat protection, ecosystem-based planning, and conservation of locally uncommon but nationally unendangered species are needed. They disagree on the weight given to national species-distribution data.
- **Other letters:** The green crab letters dispute a taxonomic placement. The tritium letter proposes overseas purchase with claimed cost savings. The Rutherford letter corrects a historical attribution of atomic discovery and the location of his work.
