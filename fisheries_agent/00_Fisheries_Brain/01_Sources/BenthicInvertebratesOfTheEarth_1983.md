## Executive Summary

This study provides baseline ecological data on the benthic macroinvertebrate community of the earthen **Coachella Canal**, an agricultural irrigation conveyance in southeastern California fed by [[Colorado River]] water. Sampling in October–November 1980, prior to a planned conversion of the upstream 78.5 km of canal from earthen to concrete lining, identified at least 19 invertebrate taxa. The community was numerically and functionally dominated by the Asiatic clam **Corbicula fluminea**, the hydropsychid caddisfly **Smicridea utico**, oligochaetes (**Aelosoma sp.**, **Chaetogaster sp.**), and chironomid midges. Mean densities ranged from 158 to 3,678 individuals/m², with biomass ranging from 2.02 to 7.63 g dry wt/m², varying strongly between mid-channel and near-shore habitats. The paper's central ecological argument links invertebrate distribution to substrate stability, particle size, and organic matter content, and offers predictions about how conversion to concrete lining will restructure the invertebrate community — a management-relevant baseline for canal engineering projects in arid regions.

## Background & Description

While this is a community-ecology paper rather than a morphological study, several taxon-specific size/weight metrics are reported that are relevant to secondary production estimates:

- **[[Corbicula fluminea]] (Asiatic clam):** Two distinct size classes co-occurred at Station 8 (west bank): juveniles (shell <4 mm, mean individual dry weight ~0.01 mg) and larger clams (shell 1–2 cm, dry weights 10–550 mg). Juvenile clams (<4 mm) predominated numerically at Station 1, while large clams (shells >1–2 cm) reached densities of 1,277/m² at Station 8.
- **Weighing methodology:** Invertebrates were weighed en masse (wet weight) except clams, which were measured individually after valve removal (>4 mm) or decalcification in 0.1 N HCl (<4 mm). Dry weight was estimated as 0.1 × wet weight (per [[Winberg 1971]]).
- **Mean individual dry weights** of non-molluscan taxa (oligochaetes, chironomids, caddisflies) were far smaller than those of clams, explaining the disproportionate contribution of *C. fluminea* to total biomass despite comparable or lower numerical abundance.
- **[[Smicridea utico]] (hydropsychid caddisfly):** A net-spinning filter feeder; on concrete substrates reached densities of ~25,000 individuals/m² with biomass near 2,500 mg/m², far exceeding densities on any natural substrate.
- **[[Parargyractis confusalis]] (pyralid moth larva, aquatic Lepidoptera):** A scraper/shredder taxon, rare in quantitative sand-substrate samples but reaching ~1,500 individuals/m² on exposed concrete surfaces, presumably associated with microalgal films.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and physical setting:**
- The [[Coachella Canal]] is an earthen irrigation channel delivering [[Colorado River]] water via the [[All American Canal]] to the Imperial, Coachella, and Indio valleys, lying within the endorheic [[Salton Sea]] basin ("Colorado Desert").
- Capacity: 70.8 m³/sec at turnout, reduced to 36.8 m³/sec by 137.8 km downstream; depths 1–5 m mid-channel; open-reach width ~20 m.
- Substrate was predominantly fine-to-medium sand with sparse gravel; silt/clay accumulated near shore where emergent vegetation reduced current velocity.
- Water velocity ranged 0.3–0.7 m/sec in open reaches, slower near banks and upstream of constrictions (e.g., check drops, siphons, bridge crossings), which also provided the only solid (concrete) substrate patches.
- Emergent macrophytes included **Phragmites australis** (common reed) and **Typha domingensis** (cat-tail), with occasional **Potamogeton pectinatus** (sago pondweed) and **Myriophyllum spicatum** (milfoil); rooted vegetation was confined to <1.0 m depth and covered <5% of area. **Cladophora glomerata** (filamentous algae) occurred only on concrete side-walls and aprons.

**Trophic structure and functional feeding groups:**
- **Filter feeders:** *C. fluminea*, most chironomids, and the dominant caddisfly *S. utico* — relying on zooplankton, phytoplankton, and fine organic detritus.
- **Sediment/detritus ingesters:** Oligochaeta (*Aelosoma*, *Chaetogaster*).
- **Predators:** Turbellaria, Hydracarina, and odonates *Hetaerina* and *Hyponeura*.
- **Collector-gatherers:** Baetidae (mayflies), *Nectopsyche*, *Chrysops*.
- **Scrapers:** Gastropods (Physidae) and *P. confusalis* — nearly absent on natural substrate, reflecting low autumn periphyton production on sand; locally abundant only on stable concrete surfaces.

**Spatial distribution patterns:**
- Near-shore habitats supported >20× the individual density and ~4× the biomass of mid-channel habitats, and more than twice the taxonomic richness (17 vs. 7 taxa) — attributed to slower currents, finer sediments, and organic matter accumulation near banks.
- Oligochaetes were abundant near shore at Stations 1, 2, and 8; chironomids near shore at most stations; caddisfly larvae concentrated at Stations 1, 2, 5, and 7 along the east bank where gravel substrate occurred.
- No evidence supported upstream-derived filter-feeder food sources (via the All American Canal); autochthonous production and aeolian (wind-blown) particulate inputs were considered more likely food sources for filter feeders.

**Community-level comparison:**
- The canal fauna was **depauperate** relative to natural lotic systems: chironomid densities (<2,300/m²) were far below the >50,000/m² considered unexceptional in natural streams (per [[Merritt and Cummins 1978]]).
- Mayflies (Ephemeroptera) were poorly represented, and aquatic Coleoptera were entirely absent, despite their typical ubiquity — attributed to the unstable, shifting sand substrate and/or seasonal (autumn) sampling timing.

## Key Findings

- At least **19 invertebrate taxa** were documented in the earthen Coachella Canal (17 in quantitative October samples; 2 additional odonates from qualitative November sampling during de-watering).
- Overall mean densities ranged **158–3,678 individuals/m²**; biomass ranged **2.02–7.63 g dry wt/m²**, with pronounced differences between mid-channel and near-shore (bank) habitats.
- **[[Corbicula fluminea]]** dominated biomass (>98% at both bank and mid-channel sites), despite comprising a minority of total individuals — a function of its large individual body size relative to insects and oligochaetes.
- **Concrete structures** (check drops, siphons, bridge crossings) supported vastly higher invertebrate densities than any natural substrate: *S. utico* at ~25,000/m² and *P. confusalis* at ~1,500/m², implicating substrate stability as a primary driver of secondary production potential.
- Invertebrate distribution was governed primarily by **substrate type/stability and organic matter presence**, rather than by proximity to the canal's water source, ruling out simple upstream-transport explanations for filter-feeder food supply.
- The shifting sand substrate of the earthen canal appears to **limit colonization** by taxa typical of stable lotic systems (e.g., mayflies, aquatic beetles), constraining overall community diversity and abundance.
- The authors predict that **conversion to concrete lining** (already underway for the upstream 78.5 km at time of study) will:
  - Increase periphyton (e.g., *Cladophora*) productivity and support higher secondary production of grazers and filter-feeders on stabilized substrate;
  - Limit burrowing taxa until sediment accumulates via aeolian dune input;
  - Improve water clarity (reduced bank erosion), potentially benefiting primary/secondary production;
  - Eliminate habitat complexity (bank holes, vegetation), reducing habitat diversity;
  - Be vulnerable to productivity loss from routine canal-cleaning disturbance.
- This baseline dataset is explicitly framed as a **point estimate** with limited applicability to annual standing crop, given known seasonal and life-history-driven variability in benthic invertebrate populations ([[Rosenberg 1979]]).
- The study underscores the broader significance of canal invertebrate communities as a **prey base for fish populations** in these systems ([[St. Amant et al. 1974]]), despite canals being an understudied habitat type relative to natural lotic systems.
