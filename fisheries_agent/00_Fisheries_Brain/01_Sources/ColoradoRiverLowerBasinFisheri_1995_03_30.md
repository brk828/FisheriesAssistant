## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary scientific study but a **regulatory comment letter** from the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (LADWP) responding to a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service "Lower Basin Fisheries Management Prospectus" concerning four indigenous, endangered "big-river" fish species of the [[Colorado River]] Lower Basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. The letter addresses management strategy, conservation feasibility, and stakeholder coordination rather than presenting direct morphological, physiological, or population data. It provides useful context on the regulatory and conservation framework surrounding these species during the mid-1990s recovery planning period.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** All four species are characterized as indigenous "big-river" fishes of the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]], implying reliance on large-river mainstem habitats, including main channel flows and associated **backwater habitats**.
- **Backwater grow-out facilities:** Identified as important nursery/rearing habitat used in conservation efforts, suggesting these species (particularly [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]]) depend on slow-water backwater zones for early life stages.
- **Riparian zone linkage:** Riparian restoration is cited as a management action, indicating an ecological dependency between river channel fish populations and adjacent riparian vegetation/habitat structure.
- **Biotic threats:** Non-native fish species are identified as significant **competitors and predators**, contributing to the decline of native big-river fish populations — a key ecological pressure noted in the [[Colorado River]] Lower Basin ecosystem.
- **Flow regime dependency:** The prospectus proposed "water release alteration studies," implying that river discharge/flow management (dam operations) is considered an ecologically relevant variable affecting habitat suitability for these species, though LADWP disputes the near-term utility of such studies.

## Key Findings

- Four indigenous big-river fish species are formally recognized as being under a coordinated federal/state recovery **Management Prospectus**: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Humpback Chub]].
- LADWP expresses skepticism that **full recovery** of these populations is an achievable management goal, favoring more modest objectives: preventing extinction and preserving genetic variability.
- Supported conservation tools include:
  - **Backwater grow-out facilities**
  - **Hatchery propagation**
  - **Riparian habitat restoration**
- LADWP objects to proposed **water release alteration studies**, arguing:
  - Limited ecological benefit until populations are stabilized.
  - Potential significant negative impacts on water and power (hydropower) users.
- **Non-native fish species** (introduced competitors/predators) are broadly acknowledged as a major contributing factor to the decline of native big-river fish populations; the letter recommends that all future management actions be evaluated specifically for their benefit toward mitigating this threat.
- The letter endorses a **cooperative, multi-stakeholder governance approach**, supporting integration of:
  - Lower Basin States/Tribes efforts
  - The USFWS **Lower Colorado River Ecoregion Team**
- This correspondence reflects the broader institutional and political context of the mid-1990s [[Endangered Species Act]] recovery planning process for Colorado River basin fishes, illustrating tension between conservation goals and water/power resource management interests.
