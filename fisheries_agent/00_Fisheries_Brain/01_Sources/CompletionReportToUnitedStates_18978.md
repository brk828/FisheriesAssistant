# Distribution of Fishes in Selected Streams of the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest (1976–1977)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This completion report, prepared by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Fisheries Management Division) for the USDA Forest Service, documents a cooperative survey of fishes, benthic invertebrates, and physical habitat in selected streams of the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]] in 1976–1977. The primary objective was to define existing and potential habitat for the native [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*), but the survey also yielded broader distributional data. Eighteen streams totaling more than 241 km (150 miles) were inventoried in the Black, Little Colorado, and Blue River drainages. The authors state that the State has more than 475 perennial waters, roughly half of which had been sampled at least once.

Seven of the eighteen systems contained no fish. Native and introduced salmonids, native cyprinids and catostomids, and several habitat-limited streams are described, along with recommendations for stream improvement, barrier construction, and future surveys.

## Background & Description

The authors note that the impact of human activity on Arizona's aquatic communities was poorly understood compared with terrestrial systems. A comprehensive faunal distribution for the State's fishery resources was described as "grossly incomplete" despite roughly 125 years of investigation. The study was undertaken to fill basic data gaps needed for management under recent federal legislation.

Four study areas were selected, and surveys were conducted between May 1976 and August 1977. Sampling stations were chosen at random within predetermined zones to reduce bias, and locations were recorded by Township, Range, Section, and quarter-quarter section, as well as by stream kilometers (SK) above confluence. Station lengths were typically 25–50 m.

Methods included:
- **Physical surveys:** On-foot, horseback, or vehicle surveys to map perennial flow, barriers, and stream improvement structures. Discharge was computed from width, depth, and velocity.
- **Water chemistry:** Dissolved oxygen and pH measured in the field with a Hach AL-36B kit; temperature recorded with a mercury pocket thermometer.
- **Benthos:** Qualitative sampling with a Surber-type sampler (1,024-micron mesh), hand dredging, and kick-drift sampling in pool and riffle habitats. Organisms were identified to family level.
- **Fishes:** [[Electrofishing]] with both 115 V AC and 12 V DC equipment. Voucher specimens were preserved in 10% formalin and deposited in the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes. Nomenclature follows [[Minckley]] (1973).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water temperatures across the surveyed systems ranged from 3 °C to 29 °C, with many lower-elevation and unshaded reaches marginal for salmonids. Campbell Blue Creek, for example, recorded 29 °C at mid-afternoon, though the authors caution these were maxima and that diurnal variation of about 6 °C is typical. Dissolved oxygen was generally 7–10 ppm, though Raspberry Creek Station I measured 3.0 ppm and Strayhorse Creek Station I measured 5.0 ppm. pH values were commonly 8.0–9.5.

Many streams were affected by flood scouring, livestock grazing, beaver decline (notably after severe flooding in 1959 on Campbell Blue Creek), and loss of riparian overstory. Several headwater reaches were ephemeral or snow-bound at survey time, so the reported discharges may not reflect annual means.

Benthic diversity varied widely. [[Campbell Blue Creek]] had the most diverse fauna of the surveyed streams (nine orders, 28 families), while [[Home Creek]] had only three invertebrate taxa. The [[Blue River]] yielded eight orders and 21 families. Many low-diversity collections were attributed to sampling timing, ice cover, or low flow, and the authors recommend further sampling before management decisions are made.

Barriers, both natural (vertical falls of 0.3–1.0 m, bedrock slides, and up to 9 m drops) and artificial (diversion structures), play a major role in isolating fish populations. The authors repeatedly note that barriers could both protect native populations from exotic invasion and restrict expansion.

## Key Findings

**Overall survey results**
- Eighteen streams were inventoried. Seven (39%) had no fish. Two of these (K-E Canyon and Cat Creek) were ephemeral and considered unsuitable for fisheries. The remaining five fishless perennial or potentially perennial streams were Davis, Milk, Turkey, Pace, and Jackson creeks.
- Eleven systems supported fish populations of native, exotic, or mixed origin.

**Arizona trout (*Salmo apache*)**
- [[Home Creek]]: Possibly a native population. Five specimens were collected in the SK 3.03–4.86 reach. Small size classes were absent, suggesting little recent reproduction.
- [[Mamie Creek]]: A population from introductions on 21 August 1965 (50 fish), 7 September 1967 (100 fish), and 14 October 1968 (200 fish). Eight specimens were collected between Stations II and III, with at least three year classes present. Spawning habitat was abundant but pool habitat was limiting.
- [[Mineral Creek]]: Introduced in 1967 (100 fish) and 1968 (300 fish). Only three specimens (174–206 mm) were collected, all at Station V, with no small size classes. The authors suspect poor spawning substrate, with excessive fines, and recommend a water analysis for possible toxicity.
- [[Buckalou Creek]] and [[Hayground Creek]] contain hybrids of Arizona trout and rainbow trout. The Buckalou Creek record is only the second known collection of *S. apache* from a naturally occurring population in the Blue River drainage (the previous record being four specimens from K.P. Creek, 1914).
- [[Coleman Creek]]: Presents an opportunity to establish Arizona trout in about 5 km of habitat isolated above a 5.0 m barrier.
- [[Milk Creek]] and [[Davis Creek]]: Considered candidates for Arizona trout establishment after stream improvement.

**Hybridization and introduced salmonids**
- [[Hayground Creek]]: Hybrid trout (*S. apache* × *S. gairdneri*) showed three size groups, indicating reproduction. Brown trout dominated the lower reach. A 1939 stocking of 2,550 rainbow trout is noted as a likely source of hybridization.
- [[Campbell Blue Creek]]: A moderately high brown trout fishery with four to five age groups, supported by historic stocking since at least 1933. Beaver activity historically aided the fishery.
- [[Coleman Creek]]: A single brown trout population restricted below the barrier, probably a migrant extension of the Campbell Blue Creek fauna.

**Native non-game fishes**
- [[Blue River]]: Six species were collected (five native, one exotic), the greatest fish diversity recorded in the study. Native species included [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Gila Mountain Sucker]], and [[Gila Sucker]], with a single [[Loach Minnow]] specimen. The loach minnow record was described as important, since only two previous population centers were known in Arizona.
- [[Campbell Blue Creek]]: Six species, including two loach minnows (at Stations I and IV). The authors describe this as only the third record of the species from the Blue River drainage.
- [[Strayhorse Creek]] and [[Raspberry Creek]]: Minor native non-game fisheries. Strayhorse Creek had four species, including the numerically dominant longfin dace. Raspberry Creek had three native species. Neither supports salmonid development.

**Management recommendations**
- Maintain and enhance native trout populations in Home, Mamie, and Mineral creeks; conduct late-spring and summer surveys to document reproduction.
- Evaluate alternatives to vertical drop barriers at Hayground Creek, which could provide about 4.0 km of Arizona trout habitat.
- Establish Arizona trout in Coleman Creek above the barrier, and in Milk Creek above a proposed barrier at SK 2.46 (about 1 km).
- Prioritize brown trout maintenance in Campbell Blue Creek and monitor loach minnow populations.
- Consider Pace and Jackson creeks as research sites for native cyprinids and catostomids, given their fishless status.
- Recognize Turkey, Davis, and Milk creeks as lower-priority systems requiring stream improvement before introduction.

**Study limitations**
- The authors describe the work as qualitative rather than quantitative. Sampling was conducted at one time of year, and several conclusions depend on sampling conditions, including snow cover and low flow. Many recommendations call for further surveys.
