# The Rate of Growth of Bluegill Sunfish in Lakes of Northern Indiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1942 study by William E. Ricker (Indiana University) uses scale-based age determination to measure growth of [[Bluegill Sunfish]] (*Lepomis macrochirus macrochirus*) in natural lakes of northern Indiana. Scales were gathered from 53 lakes, mostly through the [[Indiana Lake and Stream Survey]], and length-at-age was estimated for each lake. Under normal conditions, the average length at the third annulus is 121 mm fork length, with a range of 90 to 160 mm. The paper concludes that sampling bias, especially [[Selective Sampling]] by anglers, strongly shapes apparent growth rates, and that [[Lee's Phenomenon]] is probably an artifact. Population density is proposed as a likely major control on growth, and the author recommends fisheries management aimed at raising growth rates in slow-growing lakes.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** Bluegill are members of the [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfish family). The study relies on scales collected over many years, starting with [[Bolen]] (1924) and [[Hile]] (1931), and continuing with collections by Will Scott, A. E. Weyer and D. F. Opdyke, and Division of Fish and Game employees. Collections totalled about 1,300 sport fish, roughly one-third bluegills, in the earliest combined set.

**Methods of age determination.**
- Scales were mounted in glycerine jelly and read at 60 diameters using a projector ([[Scale Reading]]).
- Three reading difficulties were noted: identifying the first annulus in slow-growing fish, distinguishing accessory checks from true annuli, and reading older fish (four years or more), where annuli are crowded.
- The author believes that no serious error remains in average growth rates.

**Length measurements.** Five length types were compared: fisheries standard length, scale-edge length, [[Fork Length]], natural tip length, and total (extreme tip) length. Fork length was chosen as the standard unit for convenience. Conversion factors applied:
- Standard to fork length: 1.22
- Scale-edge to fork length: 1.17
- Total to fork length: 0.94 (up to 100 mm), 0.95 (100–149 mm), 0.96 (150–199 mm)

**Scale-body relationship.** Scale radius and fish length showed a linear relationship in Indiana bluegills. A line projected to an origin of 20 mm was used for back-calculation, with errors estimated at 3 to 5 mm near the origin and 0 to 1 mm at the last annulus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling gear and bias.** Fish were taken by hook and line, traps, gill nets, and seines. Each gear is selective:
- Seines (small mesh) caught year-old fish well but older fish only if slow-growing.
- Traps caught fish down to about 100 mm and sampled faster-growing yearlings best.
- The gill net used by Hile (1929) captured mainly 175–225 mm fish.

**Legal limit and angler selection.** Indiana law requires anglers to release bluegills under 5 inches total length, equal to about 121 mm fork length. Anglers also vary in their own cutoffs, and fish tend to school by size, so angler catches are often size-restricted.

**Depth and season.** Bluegill size increases with depth in summer, especially during the daytime. Winter ice catches usually contain more large fish and cover a wider length and age range, making selection easier to correct. Annulus formation occurs from February to June, mostly in May.

**Density and food.** The author identifies population density relative to food supply as the most likely major control on growth in natural lakes. Crowded or coarse-fish-dominated waters grow slowly, while depopulated waters grow fast.

**Lake-specific factors.** Sylvan Lake receives sewage inflow with heavy organic loading, and winter asphyxiation may have reduced its population. Bass Lake was muddy and vegetationless from an excess of coarse fish before removals in 1936–1937.

## Key Findings

**Growth standards**
- Under normal conditions, the average length at the third annulus is **121 mm fork length** (about 4¾ inches), which is the legal size limit.
- The range across normal lakes is **90–160 mm** (3.5 to 6.8 inches).
- The standard deviation is **±16.0 mm**. The length distribution shows some tendency toward bimodality, though not significantly so.
- Mid-range lakes (about 115–130 mm at three years) make up the central half of the lake list.

**Extremes**
- Slowest: Bass Lake (1934), at 2.9 inches at the third annulus. Shoe Lake, at 3.5 inches, was the slowest normal-conditions lake.
- Fastest: Round Lake (Elkhart County, 1939), at 6.9 inches. This was attributed to winter kill in 1936, which left an uncrowded population. Carr Lake, at 6.3 inches, was the fastest normal-conditions lake.
- Mean lengths at three years vary strongly among lakes, with Carr Lake fish at least six times heavier than Shoe Lake fish at that age.

**Sampling and bias**
- Selective sampling is the central problem. Summer angling is the least reliable approach, while winter ice fishing samples several age classes more evenly.
- In the Shoe Lake creel analysis (21 June 1941, 12 catches), catch means differed significantly (F = 9.47, critical value 2.44), and the differences held even after excluding small fish.
- Using only a size-restricted gill net, with mean lengths near its efficiency maximum (about 200 mm for Hile's net), gives the least-biased estimates.

**Lee's phenomenon**
- The apparent decline in growth rate with increasing age is attributed to selective sampling. It is not demonstrated as a real population trait and is "at most of small importance."
- Earlier mortality of faster-growing fish could not be shown after correcting for sampling.

**Sex differences**
- Male and female bluegills grew at almost identical rates in several lakes.
- The only significant difference was in Shoe Lake, where females grew faster during the June spawning season. This may reflect earlier maturity in males.

**Stability over time**
- Growth was stable over periods up to about 20 years in several lakes, including Wawasee (1926–1941) and Dewart Lake (1929–1941).
- Two lakes showed decreases over about 12 years: Little Chapman (about 20 mm lower at three years) and Silver Lake, Kosciusko County (about one inch lower at three years in 1941 than in 1929).
- Sylvan Lake showed a significant change between 1939 (6.7 inches at the third annulus) and 1941 (5.1 inches).

**Age-class abundance and variability**
- Strong year-classes were seen in [[Lake Wawasee]], with spawnings of 1934 and 1937 notably numerous.
- Variability within age classes increases with length until about 200 mm, then declines in older fish because fast growers slow earlier.

**Management implications**
- Stocking young bluegills is most useful in fast-growing lakes. Stocking slow-growing lakes may be unproductive or harmful.
- The author argues that growth in the slowest lakes is below optimum and that management should aim to raise growth rates, not reduce them by further crowding.
- Suggested methods: encouraging predators such as [[Largemouth Bass]], relaxing fishing restrictions, removing young bluegills from slow lakes, and discouraging competing fish.
- Changes to [[Fishing Regulations]] are seen as the most promising but also the most controversial.
- Table V ranking should not be read as a measure of fishing quality, because growth rate and angler catch show little or a negative correlation.

**Summary statements (from the paper)**
- Scale samples came from 53 natural lakes of northern Indiana.
- Excessive crowding in artificial lakes, or abundant coarse fish, produced growth below the normal range. Depopulated waters produced growth above it.
- Scale annuli form mainly in May, between February and June.
