## Executive Summary

This 1987 Bureau of Land Management (BLM) Environmental Assessment (EA No. AZ-040-7-28) outlines a management action to eradicate non-native, invasive "exotic" fish populations—primarily [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*)—from approximately 100 stock tanks in the Aravaipa Creek watershed, Safford District, Arizona. The impetus is protection of two federally Threatened native cyprinids, the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), along with other native fish including the [[Round-Tailed Chub]], which inhabit [[Aravaipa Creek]] downstream. Exotic fish washed from upland stock tanks into the creek during flood events were identified as a predation and competition threat to native fish assemblages. The approved action (Alternative A) combines fish sampling, partial tank draw-down via pumping, and chemical treatment with antimycin and/or chlorine to eliminate exotic populations while minimizing risk of ichthyocide reaching the main channel of [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]].

## Background & Description

The source document is a regulatory/management EA and does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of the fish species involved. However, taxonomic and status identifications are given:

- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*) — small-bodied native cyprinid; federally listed as Threatened; part of the Aravaipa Creek native fish assemblage.
- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*) — small-bodied native cyprinid; federally listed as Threatened; co-occurs with spikedace.
- **[[Round-Tailed Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*) — native cyprinid listed among the "most conspicuous" vertebrate species of the watershed.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) — native poeciliid proposed by Dr. W.L. Minckley (ASU) as a candidate species for reintroduction into treated/reclaimed stock tanks.
- **[[Green Sunfish]]** (*Lepomis cyanellus*) — the principal exotic/invasive species targeted; a centrarchid known for high tolerance of degraded habitat and strong establishment ability in stock tank environments; historically documented to persist in tanks for 20–30 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat structure**: Aravaipa Creek is described as containing "the largest assemblage of native fish in the State of Arizona." The surrounding watershed includes approximately 100 dirt/masonry stock tanks distributed across four BLM grazing allotments (South Rim, Aravaipa, Dry Camp, Painted Cave), draining into 27 named canyons plus numerous unnamed side canyons, all ultimately connecting to [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]].
- **Elevation range**: 2,500–6,200 feet, spanning Sonoran desert scrub, desert grassland, chaparral/oak, and broadleaf riparian communities.
- **Connectivity threat pathway**: Exotic fish (notably green sunfish) established in isolated upland stock tanks are transported into Aravaipa Creek via flash flooding and dam overflow/failure events, creating recurring colonization pressure on the native fish community.
- **Predation/competition dynamics**: Once in the mainstream, exotic species prey upon and competitively displace native [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], and other native fishes, elevating extinction risk for the Threatened species.
- **Two confirmed exotic-positive tanks**: Stone Cabin Tank (SW¼, Sec. 27, T.6S., R.18E.) and Rim Rock Tank (NE¼, Sec. 20, T.6S., R.18E.) were specifically identified as harboring exotic fish populations targeted for treatment.
- **Non-target fauna interactions**: Fish-eating wildlife potentially affected by exotic fish removal include black hawk, great blue heron, migrating osprey, mergansers, and scavengers (coyotes, raccoons, turkey vultures) — though no records confirmed these species foraging at the two known-positive tanks.
- **Chemical ecology of control agents**: Antimycin and chlorine were selected as ichthyocides because they are non-toxic to homeotherms (birds, mammals) at use concentrations and degrade within 1–7 days depending on temperature, water chemistry, and substrate composition — allowing tank ecosystems to recover with seasonal rains.
- **Alternative agent discussed**: In the appended 1982 correspondence, Dr. W.L. Minckley (ASU) recommends rotenone as an alternative toxicant, emphasizing precise dilution calculations against mainstream discharge volume, timing treatments to coincide with low tributary flow and mainstream flooding for dilution safety, and cautioning that chemical detoxification (e.g., potassium permanganate) can itself introduce toxicity risk.

## Key Findings

- **Regulatory driver**: The action is framed as consistent with BLM's obligations under the [[Endangered Species Act]] due to the presence of two federally Threatened fish species in Aravaipa Creek.
- **Selected alternative**: Alternative A (sampling + partial draw-down + chemical treatment with antimycin/chlorine) was formally adopted via Record of Decision, chosen over four other alternatives (B: drain-only without chemicals; C: winter pumping; D: sampling only, no treatment; E: no action).
- **Risk mitigation design**: Treatment was to proceed tank-by-tank with days-long intervals between treatments specifically to ensure that even under worst-case dam failure, insufficient ichthyocide would ever be simultaneously present in the watershed to harm [[Aravaipa Creek]] fish populations.
- **Historical persistence of invasion**: Some tanks were noted to have supported exotic fish populations for 20–30 years, indicating long-term establishment and the ineffectiveness of natural drying cycles alone as a control mechanism (supporting rejection of the passive "no treatment" alternatives).
- **Winter pumping caveat**: Alternative C's biological limitation — some target fish can survive in wet bottom mud even after a tank is pumped dry, requiring the mud itself to fully desiccate to guarantee mortality — highlighting a species-specific desiccation-tolerance trait relevant to invasive centrarchid control.
- **Reintroduction potential**: Treated and reclaimed tanks were proposed (both in the EA mitigation section and in Minckley's letter) as future reintroduction habitat for native species, specifically the [[Gila Topminnow]], turning former invasive-species reservoirs into conservation assets.
- **Expert consultation network**: The project involved coordination among BLM, [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Forest Service, and academic input from Arizona State University's Desert Fishes Recovery Team (W.L. Minckley), reflecting the broader institutional structure of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] active in Southwest native fish conservation during this period.
- **Broader significance**: The document exemplifies an early, systematic watershed-scale approach to invasive fish eradication designed explicitly to protect endangered/threatened desert stream fish assemblages from upstream propagule sources in artificial water bodies (stock tanks).
