# Food Habits of the Larval Tiger Salamander (Ambystoma tigrinum)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ambystomatidae #amphibian-ecology #limnology

> **Note:** This source is primarily an amphibian (larval salamander) trophic ecology study conducted in a freshwater pond system. It is included here because it documents the broader aquatic community, including a co-occurring fish species ([[Pimephales promelas]]), zooplankton, and macroinvertebrate food webs. Data has been synthesized faithfully to its actual (non-fish-centric) focus.

## Executive Summary

This 1955 study by Clarence F. Dineen (Indiana Academy of Science, University of Notre Dame) examines the food habits of larval [[Tiger Salamander]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) in a single shallow pond in Cass County, Michigan. The study tests the hypothesis proposed by Manion and Cory (1952) that salamander larvae shift feeding strategy based on ambient light intensity — snapping at individual prey items under high light, and switching to plankton-filtering ("gulping") behavior under low light/darkness. A total of 329 larvae were collected via minnow seine across day and night sampling periods, and stomach contents were quantitatively analyzed via individual counts and centrifuged volumetric measurement. Results support a **dual feeding strategy** strongly correlated with declining light intensity, with a marked ecological interaction between [[Chironomidae]] (bottom-dwelling) and [[Chaoboridae]] (vertically migrating) dipteran prey. The study also documents a single co-occurring fish species, *Pimephales promelas* (fathead minnow), within the pond community, alongside anuran tadpoles and larval [[Jefferson Salamander]].

## Background & Description

- **Study organism:** Larval *Ambystoma tigrinum* (Tiger Salamander), not adult/terrestrial form.
- **Total length range:** 70–182 mm (mean = 102.9 mm across full sample).
- **Cohort size variation:** Larvae from the August 7th collection were older and larger (mean = 112.6 mm) than the July cohort, indicating an age/growth gradient within the season.
- **Life history endpoint:** All larvae underwent metamorphosis to the terrestrial adult form and emigrated from the pond approximately one week after the final collection — indicating sampling occurred near the end of the larval trophic period.
- **Fish reference species:** *Pimephales promelas* (fathead minnow) — only one individual noted in the pond; family [[Cyprinidae]]. No morphometric data given for this fish; it is mentioned solely as a co-occurring vertebrate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Shallow pasture pond, maximum depth 5 ft, Cass County, Michigan.
  - Emergent vegetation at margins: *Polygonum* and *Ambrosia*.
  - Submerged/floating cover (>50% of pond): *Potamogeton*, *Hydrodictyon*, *Spirogyra* — provided shelter for larvae.
  - Bottom substrate: firm, irregular, with livestock-trampled depressions up to 1 ft deep, creating heterogeneous microhabitats.
- **Co-occurring fauna:** Numerous anuran tadpoles, few larval [[Jefferson Salamander]] (*Ambystoma jeffersonianum*), and one fish (*Pimephales promelas*) — suggesting low fish predation pressure/competition in this system.
- **Trophic role:** Larval salamanders functioned as generalist predators occupying both benthic and pelagic feeding niches — a temporary but ecologically significant role in small pond community trophic dynamics, historically underestimated by researchers.
- **Feeding strata:** Larvae were **not surface feeders** — terrestrial insects and surface-dwelling invertebrates were an insignificant dietary component, distinguishing their niche from many fish or surface-feeding amphibians.
- **Diel prey dynamics:**
  - [[Chironomidae]] (non-biting midge larvae): strictly benthic/bottom-dwelling at all times.
  - [[Chaoboridae]] (phantom midge larvae): bottom-associated during early hours, migrating into the water column ("plankton") at night (especially after 9:00 PM), where they fed on smaller plankters — a classic diel vertical migration pattern.
  - Zooplankton community: dominated by Cladocera (*Simocephalus*, *Chydorus*, *Daphnia*) and Copepoda (*Diaptomus*, *Cyclops*).

## Key Findings

- **Dominant prey taxa:** Diptera (chiefly [[Chironomidae]] and [[Chaoboridae]]) comprised >50% of macroscopic food items in every collection (Table I).
- **Secondary prey:** Odonata, Ephemerida (mayflies), Hemiptera, and Coleoptera were also significant dietary components; minor contributions from Trichoptera, Arachnida, Mollusca (*Physa*, *Musculium*), and anuran tadpoles.
- **Light intensity as key driver:** A clear inverse relationship was observed between light intensity and feeding mode:
  - Early evening collections (6:00–9:00 PM, clear skies) showed higher proportions of benthic [[Chironomidae]] in the diet.
  - Late collections (10:30 PM–12:00 AM, following cloud cover and wind-generated ripples reducing light penetration on July 23) showed a pronounced **decrease in chironomids** and **increase in chaoborids/plankton** in stomach contents.
  - Volumetric data (Table II) confirm this shift: plankton & detritus volume rose from ~2.0–4.0 cc (early collections) to 9.8–11.3 cc (late collections), per 50 larvae.
- **Proportion of plankton-rich stomachs:** <22% of stomachs in early collections (≤9:00 PM) contained large amounts of zooplankton, versus 54% and 64% in the two late-night collections — direct behavioral evidence of a nocturnal dietary shift.
- **Mechanism confirmed:** Individual counts of zooplankton verified that increased volumetric measurements reflected a true increase in **organism numbers**, not merely detritus accumulation — supporting active planktivorous "gulping" feeding rather than passive filtering of debris.
- **Conclusion — Dual feeding strategy confirmed:** Larval tiger salamanders exhibit two distinct feeding modes:
  1. **Snapping/selective predation** on larger benthic/epibenthic macroinvertebrates under higher light conditions.
  2. **Gulping/plankton-seining** behavior under reduced light (night, cloud cover, turbidity), consistent with and supporting the earlier hypotheses of [[Manion and Cory (1952)]], [[Nicholas (1922)]], and [[Powers (1907)]].
- **Environmental modulators of light-dependent feeding:** cloud cover, moonlight, water turbidity, angle of light incidence, and microhabitat structural complexity were all identified as factors regulating effective light intensity reaching larvae, and thus feeding mode.
- **Community significance:** The study underscores the ecological importance of larval salamanders as a transient but trophically active predator guild in small pond ecosystems — a role the author argues has been historically underappreciated relative to fish and other aquatic vertebrates.
