# Food Selection and Feeding Habits of Tilapia mossambica in Different Ecological Habitats

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 study by C. M. Aravindan (Department of Aquatic Biology and Fisheries, University of Kerala, Trivandrum) examines whether [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica* Peters) changes its feeding selectivity across three contrasting habitats in Kerala, India: a temple-tank pond, the [[Karamana River]], and the brackish [[Kayamkulam Lake]]. Using gut-content analysis and the [[Ivlev Selectivity Index]], the author found that the species is selective in its feeding, that its preferred food differs by habitat, and that it favours plant matter over animal matter when given a choice.

## Background & Description

*Tilapia mossambica* is a mouthbrooding cichlid native to South Africa. It was introduced into Indian waters as early as 1952 to assess its suitability for [[Fish Farming]]. Its prolific breeding and tolerance of varied conditions allowed it to become a dominant member of the fish fauna of almost all water bodies in Kerala. Earlier work on its diet (Vaas & Hofstede 1952, Chen 1953, Panikkar & Tampi 1954, Kelly 1956) had not tested whether habitat influences feeding. The study addresses this gap, drawing on comparable work on *Tilapia zillii* in [[Lake Quarun]] by Alkholy & Malek (1972).

Methods: Fish were collected in April 1974 (sample size given in the source as about 20 per locality, citing John 1958 for the Kayamkulam survey context). Total length, weight and gut weight were recorded, and guts were preserved in 5% formalin. Gut contents were analysed by the percentage occurrence method. Plankton samples were collected from the same habitats early on the collection day to measure food availability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pond (temple tank, c. 10 ha):** a confined freshwater body. Filamentous algae, unicellular algae, copepods and detritus were abundant, along with higher aquatic plants.
- **River (Karamana River, near Trivandrum):** an open-water freshwater system with unicellular algae, filamentous algae, ostracods and copepods.
- **Estuary (Kayamkulam Lake):** a brackish lake where salinity may rise to high levels. The author notes that estuaries are highly fertile and productive (citing Ketchum 1967), support large numbers of individuals, and offer reduced competition (Kinne 1966). Suyehiro (1942) is cited for the restricted food range in freshwater habitats.

## Key Findings

- **Selectivity is habitat-dependent.** Feeding selectivity was measured using the Ivlev index (E = (ri − pi)/(ri + pi)), which ranges from +1 (complete positive selection) to −1 (complete negative selection).
- **Pond fish:** strongly positive selection for filamentous algae, higher aquatic plants and detritus; negative selection for unicellular algae, copepods and crustacean larvae. Preference for aquatic plant material was marked despite abundant algae and copepods.
- **River fish:** positive selection for filamentous algae and ostracods; index of 0 for unicellular algae, which were available in fair amounts. Crustacean larvae were absent from guts and copepods were present in small numbers. Copepods were also preferred.
- **Estuarine fish:** higher selectivity for filamentous algae and detritus, very low selection for unicellular algae. The fish supplemented plant matter with fish eggs and larvae, and mysids.
- **Negative selection across habitats:** fish eggs and larvae, coelenterates, polychaetes and molluscan larvae showed negative indices in all cases where they were tested.
- **Central conclusion:** given a choice, *T. mossambica* prefers plant to animal matter, and shows discernible selectivity even within plant material. Its ability to exploit the available food in each habitat is interpreted as a key reason for the species' success, marking it as a versatile, omnivorous feeder.
- **Limitations noted:** the author acknowledges that selection is shaped both by fish behaviour and by the environment in which prey items are available.
