# Lower Colorado River Basin Native Fish Management Plan

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #river-ecology

## Executive Summary

This draft management plan addresses four federally endangered "big-river" fishes in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]: [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Submitted by the Parker Fishery Resource Office to USFWS Region II, the plan emphasizes preventing extinction and stabilizing populations through circumvention of recruitment failure caused primarily by predation from non-indigenous fishes on eggs and juveniles. The strategy centers on creating isolated natural and seminatural habitats (>5 ha) where native fish can survive to maturity (ca. 300 mm TL) before reintroduction to larger systems. The plan divides the lower basin into five ecoregions and proposes a six-level recovery framework from preventing extinction (Level I) through delisting (Level VI), with immediate focus on Levels I-III for bonytail and razorback sucker.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] basin encompasses approximately 650,000 km² of western North America, draining portions of seven U.S. states plus Mexico. The lower basin, separated from the upper basin near [[Glen Canyon Dam]], includes the mainstem Colorado and five major subbasins: [[Little Colorado River]], [[Paria River]], [[Virgin River]], [[Bill Williams River]], and [[Gila River]]. Prior to impoundment, the mainstem consisted of slow-moving turbid reaches alternating with violent rapids in canyons, with discharge reflecting Rocky Mountain snowmelt—running high and cold in spring, then decreasing and warming in summer.

Under these extreme conditions evolved four of the largest and most morphologically distinctive freshwater fishes in the world. All four species are federally listed as endangered with critical habitat determinations pending. Current status varies by species: [[Colorado Squawfish]] are extirpated from the lower basin (Minckley 1973); [[Bonytail]] are caught only in Lakes Mohave and Havasu (Minckley 1985); [[Humpback Chub]] persist in a viable population primarily in the lowermost [[Little Colorado River]] within [[Grand Canyon]] (Minckley 1990); and [[Razorback Sucker]] maintain their largest known population in [[Lake Mohave]], with wild individuals sporadically captured in Grand Canyon and below [[Davis Dam]].

These taxa share life-history characteristics indicating a coevolved assemblage: all attain large sizes and great age, have extended reproductive periods producing large numbers of eggs, and grow quickly during early life stages. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] evolved as an apex predator with specialized adaptations (large head, raptorial pharyngeal teeth, ambush behavior), while the three [[Gila]] species and [[Razorback Sucker]] display hydrodynamic shapes and falcate fins suggesting adaptations to avoid predation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Declines of these taxa are attributed to: (1) habitat loss and modification, and (2) negative interactions with non-indigenous species. Large lower-basin impoundments blocked historic movement corridors, released water too cold for native spawning requirements, and removed innate variance from the system. Torrential floods alternating with prolonged drought were natural features to which native taxa were adapted. The most significant impact identified is predation by non-indigenous fishes on native eggs, fry, and juveniles, causing recruitment failure (Meffe 1983, Minckley 1991, Blinn et al. 1993, Burke and Mueller 1993).

The lower basin is subdivided into five ecoregions based on fish distribution and watershed relations:
- **Ecoregion I**: Mainstem Colorado from Glen Canyon Dam to Hoover Dam, including Paria, Virgin, and Little Colorado confluences
- **Ecoregion II**: Mainstem Colorado from Hoover Dam to U.S.-Mexico boundary, including Bill Williams River
- **Ecoregion III**: Middle Colorado system (Little Colorado above Grand Falls, Virgin River)
- **Ecoregion IV**: Gila River from Colorado River upstream
- **Ecoregion V**: Mexican drainages (Ríos Yaqui, Sonoyta, Magdalena, and Willcox Playa)

Artificial reservoirs, canals, and agency stocking programs provide constant sources of predators and competitors. As more non-indigenous taxa establish, fewer natives are found. The substantially longer timeframe for modifications in the lower basin (compared to upper basin) has resulted in stabilization of development, providing a relatively stable base for planning. No comprehensive management plan previously existed for threatened and endangered fishes in the lower Colorado basin.

## Key Findings

**Management Framework**: The plan proposes a six-level hierarchical approach:
- **Level I**: Prevent extinction through securing reproductive adults and confirming survivability of young to maturity
- **Level II**: Perpetuate existing genetic variability through planned genetic management and expanded broodstocks
- **Level III**: Stabilize populations with sufficient numbers, population structure, and genetic variability
- **Level IV**: Expand populations through habitat restoration and dispersal corridors
- **Level V**: Achieve self-sustaining populations with natural recruitment and no human intervention
- **Level VI**: Recovery leading to downlisting or delisting

**Immediate Actions (Levels I-III)**: Within three years, the plan calls for:
1. Identification of natural/seminatural habitats (>5 ha) that are isolated from other waters, amenable to piscicide treatment, and suitable for protective custody of wild-caught and cultured fish
2. Removal of non-indigenous fishes and stocking of endangered taxa, beginning on National Wildlife Refuges and expanding to other public lands
3. When natives reach sizes minimizing predation pressure (ca. 300 mm TL), use for: (a) augmentation of existing populations, (b) reintroductions elsewhere, and (c) buffer against catastrophic events

**Hatchery Infrastructure**: [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] currently holds broodstocks of all big-river fishes except humpback chub and serves as the pivotal facility for propagation, rearing, and distribution. Additional facilities include [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], Arizona Game and Fish Department's Bubbling Ponds hatchery, and Colorado River Indian Tribe ponds. Primary roles are: (1) maintenance of broodstocks to supply genetically representative progeny and serve as refugia, and (2) production of sufficient quantities and qualities of fish for recovery requirements.

**Genetic Management (Level II)**: To maximize and conserve genetic variability, the plan requires:
1. Maximization of numbers of individuals contributing equally to recruitment; for razorback suckers, collection of wild-caught larvae at different times during the extended breeding season at various Lake Mohave locations exploiting phototactic response
2. If wild-caught larvae unavailable, artificial propagation based on strict pedigree with measured gamete contributions from individuals with known genetic attributes, or statistical breeding regimens ensuring all broodstock alleles are represented in progeny
3. Broodstock management including: (a) maximal use of wild individuals, (b) infusion and mixing of wild individuals with F1 "domestic" broodfish to minimize inbreeding, and (c) application of strict pedigree to conserve all alleles present in F1 generation

**Population Expansion (Level IV)**: Within five years, develop large isolated habitats (≥80 ha) dedicated for long-term management of adult populations:
1. Plan and implement creation and stocking of large seminatural isolated habitats, initially on Refuges compatible with refuge management including waterfowl, riparian, and neotropical bird habitat enhancement
2. Modify habitats to encourage natural reproduction and self-sustaining populations (Level V) as sources for reintroduction and long-term genetic management
3. Within 10 years, expand to floodplain habitats on other federal lands and tributaries in Ecoregions I (including humpback chub) and IV (Gila River basin)
4. Opportunistically exploit reservoir drainage events for maintenance or drought by stocking adults and larvae coincident with refilling to take advantage of: (a) absence of predators allowing natural recruitment, (b) rapid growth and longevity of natives establishing long-lived populations, and (c) dramatically expanded population size allowing selection on diverse gene pools
5. Restoration of free-flowing river reaches (Salt, Gila, Verde, Bill Williams, mainstem Colorado) through habitat improvement, barriers to non-indigenous invasion, removal and control of non-indigenous species, and reestablishment of native fish communities

**Habitat Maintenance**: Perpetual maintenance requires:
1. Periodic renovations to control non-indigenous fishes appearing in dedicated habitats, with repeated stockings and exchange of fish among sites, broodstocks, and for reintroductions
2. Evaluation of interactions between non-indigenous and native fishes, particularly large non-indigenous predators
3. Human intervention for habitat maintenance (dredging, vegetation control) since natural processes creating oxbows and backwaters no longer exist due to river controls; alternatively, habitats changing through succession will be incorporated into refuge management as open-water components of wider wetland and riparian communities

**Species-Specific Considerations**: The plan primarily addresses [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in the lower basin. [[Humpback Chub]] in Grand Canyon is excluded from specific recommendations pending Environmental Impact Statement production mandated by the Secretary of the Interior (July 1989) and provisions of the Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1993, coupled with Conservation Measures required by Jeopardy Opinion on [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations (USFWS Region II, 13 October 1993). [[Colorado Squawfish]] reintroduced as "experimental, nonessential" in Salt and Verde rivers technically cannot contribute to recovery; the plan recommends modifying designation to "experimental, essential" so those populations and habitats may be included in recovery planning. USFWS requested withdrawal (2 August 1993) of earlier Arizona Game and Fish Department proposal for experimental non-essential designation in lower Colorado mainstem.

**Program Coordination**: A Lower Basin Coordinator position is necessary to form a Lower Basin Recovery Team and maintain order in site-specific management plan development. The coordinator must have quick access to the Regional Director or appointed representative and strong ties with regional and local endangered species personnel. The Recovery Team would consist of biologists chosen for: (1) knowledge of the species, (2) knowledge of the lower Colorado River basin, and (3) interrelated expertise (e.g., hydrology). No more than half should be from agencies actually or potentially affecting the species. One Recovery Team would address all four species rather than individual teams per species. Annual working sessions convened by the coordinator will explore state of knowledge, ascertain best information and resolve conflicts, settle upon broodstock acquisition and production means, and consolidate information on stabilizing and perpetuating populations.

**Fish Health and Monitoring**: Natural/seminatural habitats must be examined for health-related problems prior to stocking by examining non-indigenous fishes. Health of native fish in hatcheries and stocked habitats should be monitored annually at minimum and prior to any transfers to maximize health and minimize disease transfer probabilities.

**Rationale and Scientific Basis**: This research-based effort centers on a large scientific literature (reviewed by Minckley 1973, Deacon and Minckley 1974, Minckley and Deacon 1992). The rationale emphasizes circumventing recruitment failure as the principal means for initiating recovery, justified by: (1) immediate availability of amenable habitats, (2) concentration of personnel and expertise, (3) larger population sizes in lower basin than elsewhere, and (4) extensive site-specific research base allowing fact-based recovery actions. This differs considerably from existing recovery plans and upper basin documents due to: (1) longer timeframe for lower basin modifications resulting in development stabilization, (2) larger lower basin population sizes, and (3) extensive site-specific research information combined with upper basin data increasing confidence in positive results.

**Native Fish Assemblage**: Appendix A lists native fishes occurring within the five ecoregions, including listing status and recovery plan existence. The four big-river fishes form a natural management unit, collectively comprising a unique geographically sympatric fauna throughout the mainstem Colorado and major tributaries with shared life-history patterns indicating a coevolved assemblage driven by predation versus response to predation. They responded similarly to human impacts, becoming rare at approximately the same time (early 1950s) apparently through recruitment failure. Experimental data indicate all four taxa share common capability to survive to maturity if isolated in habitats free of other fishes during early life-history stages.

**Cooperating Entities**: Appendix B lists agencies and departments available to form a Lower Basin Management Plan Coordination Team, including federal agencies (Bureau of Reclamation, Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Indian Affairs, Fish and Wildlife Service divisions, Forest Service units, National Park Service areas), state agencies (Arizona Game and Fish Department, Nevada Division of Wildlife, California Fish and Game Department), Indian tribes (White Mountain Apache, San Carlos Apache, Hualapai Nation, Hopi Nation, Navajo Nation, Colorado River Indian Tribes, Chemehuevi Nation), and other organizations (universities, The Nature Conservancy, Arizona Riparian Council, American Rivers).
