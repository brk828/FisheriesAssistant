# Desert Fishes Recovery Team Memorandum and Minutes: Aravaipa Creek and Desert Pupfish Planning (1990)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a December 1990 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) memorandum and the draft minutes of the November 1990 [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] meeting in Phoenix, Arizona. It covers three linked issues: public comment on draft recovery plans for [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]; the management of nonnative fishes, chiefly [[Red Shiner]], in [[Aravaipa Creek]]; and downlisting criteria for the [[Desert Pupfish]]. The Team could not agree on whether red shiner currently constitutes a problem in Aravaipa Creek, but it agreed on a set of monitoring, barrier, propagation, and oversight actions.

## Background & Description

The FWS memo reports about 59 comments on the draft spikedace and loach minnow recovery plans. Most came from southeastern Arizona, southwestern New Mexico, ranchers, and landowners, who argued that the plans constitute a federal "taking" of private property. Most requested a takings implications assessment and an environmental assessment (EA). The formal comment period closed November 26, 1990, but comments were still accepted until about the end of December. The Solicitor's office was asked to help answer some of the comments.

Other Team business included:

- **Leadership:** [[W.L. Minckley]] withdrew his resignation as Team leader.
- **Gila topminnow:** Stock sources for reintroduction were proposed for Team review. Topminnow were virtually extinct at Salt Creek and Bos/Medicine Spring and were extinct at Middle Spring, which dried in 1989. Stock from Middle Spring was doing well at [[Roper Lake]].
- **Quitobaquito pupfish:** Removal from Bog Hole was planned, with draining preferred over poisoning. The [[Sonoran Tiger Salamander]], known from Bog Hole, was under consideration for federal listing, so any poisoning or draining plan had to protect it. At Finley Tank, poisoning was delayed while Audubon Society permission was verified.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aravaipa Creek data.** The Team reviewed a summary of October 1990 sampling and of seining records from 1943 and from 1963 to the present. Minckley cautioned that the data contain considerable "noise" and should be used only for broad patterns. Nonnative species rose from 3 in the 1960s to 7 in the 1980s, but nonnatives made up a small share of the fish community with no definite trend. No trend was evident in native species abundance, though spikedace and loach minnow fluctuated widely over 27 years.

**Red shiner.** Small numbers were scattered from about 2.5 miles above the lower wilderness boundary downstream. Red shiner were abundant in the San Pedro River at the mouth of Aravaipa Creek. The summer 1990 flood, at about a 20-year level, may have allowed them to move upstream. A female red shiner can produce 50 eggs every 10 days in warm water, with a lifetime production of about 30,000 eggs. Minckley predicted that red shiner would make up 20% of fish below the canyon by summer 1991 and could displace small natives within several years. He linked this to channelization and downcutting in upper Aravaipa Creek.

**Coexistence elsewhere.** Spikedace and red shiner have coexisted for about 15 years in the [[Upper Verde River]] and 9 years in the [[Upper Gila River]], without significant changes in abundance. In the upper Gila, red shiner surged after their 1980–81 detection, then declined to very low numbers by 1990. Propst argued that red shiner do not displace spikedace where habitat modification is minor and no major upstream diversion or impoundment exists, which he considered true of Aravaipa Creek.

**Flows.** Median flows in Aravaipa Creek are about 20–25 cfs, and the upper Verde is about 24 cfs. Gila median flows vary along the river and were not known.

**Habitat.** Channelization above the upper end of perennial flow has started channel incision, which is worsening and is now documented on [[The Nature Conservancy]] lands. Over 100 stock tanks lie on the south side of the drainage; one had green sunfish, which was eliminated. One north-side tank with nonnatives could not be treated because the landowner refused permission.

## Key Findings

**1. The Team did not reach consensus on whether red shiner are a problem in Aravaipa Creek.** Members agreed that the range-wide status of spikedace and loach minnow has deteriorated (evidenced by their threatened listing), that nonnatives are undesirable, and that some habitat deterioration has occurred. They agreed to monitor and be ready to act.

**2. Competing management proposals:**
- Minckley proposed immediate construction of three barriers and removal of red shiner, probably with [[Rotenone]]. His threshold for a problem was the appearance of red shiner, and his trigger for poisoning was a survey with zero spikedace and loach minnow collected, where a zero was confidently not a sampling artifact.
- Stefferud and Propst opposed acting on a worst-case assumption and favored waiting for a decline outside natural fluctuations. Propst proposed an "action continuum" and suggested comparing poisons such as [[Antimycin]].
- Dahlem's "banking" (seining and shocking to remove red shiner) was judged of limited value and expensive in labor.

**3. Poisoning goals and risks.** All agreed that a 100% kill was the only acceptable goal, since less would fail and might worsen the problem. Concern was raised that nonnatives are more resistant to poisoning than natives, and that the size and complexity of the system make a complete kill very difficult. Bullheads were noted as hard to poison, so nonnatives beyond red shiner would also need removal. The Team agreed that a full assessment of other affected fauna (invertebrates, herps, piscivorous raptors) must precede any poisoning. Stefferud framed three questions: whether red shiner will substantially affect the natives, whether poisoning risk exceeds the risk of inaction, and whether 100% kill is achievable.

**4. Barriers.** The Team agreed to plan a lower barrier to stop further nonnative invasion from the San Pedro and Gila Rivers and the [[Central Arizona Project]]. Expected costs include losses of native adults and larvae that cannot return upstream. Minckley judged these losses insignificant, and a probable benefit is removal of nonnatives from the upstream reach. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] agreed to design the lower barrier, but no construction funding existed. Minckley recommended three barriers: the lower barrier, one above Turkey Creek to protect the upper creek, and a middle buffer. The Team noted that red shiner had been found above the mouth of Turkey Creek on November 8. Whether wilderness law permits barriers was disputed, and the Forest Service was cited as having built several in wilderness.

**5. Recommended actions (summarized):**
- **Distribution survey** of red shiner, with a field trip on December 8–9, 1990.
- **Monitoring** of the full fish community, including larvae, monthly March–October and less often in winter. Each effort needs about 2 field days with 3 people plus lab time. [[The Nature Conservancy]] offered half the effort, and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and [[Bureau of Land Management]] were expected to cover the rest. Francisco Abarca agreed to start monitoring and draft a [[Monitoring Protocol]].
- **Genetic significance** of Aravaipa populations to the species, using about $33,000 available from barrier funds.
- **Captive propagation** of spikedace, loach minnow, and the other five native fishes, with stocks held in case poisoning becomes necessary.
- **Oversight group** of all agencies, organizations, and private and academic biologists.
- A planned larval drift study was judged low priority, and its budget was to be redirected to these actions.

**6. Downlisting and delisting of the desert pupfish.** The Team agreed that no delisting criteria should apply to *Cyprinodon macularius macularius*, which is not expected ever to be delisted because of insoluble threats and limited habitat. It also agreed that *C. m. eremus* should have no downlisting or delisting criteria, given its limited range and continuing threats. A tiered system of recovery populations was adopted for *C. m. macularius*:
- **Tier 2** populations receive high protection and must survive without human intervention for 10 years before counting toward recovery.
- **Tier 3** populations receive lesser protection and may come and go, but the specified total must be maintained for 10 years.
- A viable population is set at **1,000 overwintering adults** (up from 500 in the draft), based on [[Soule's 50-500 Rule]] plus a buffer.
- Security must include protection of water rights as well as habitat, demonstrated over 10 years.
- For *C. m. eremus*, one backup population will be established at [[Organ Pipe National Monument]].

**7. Other plan revisions.** Nonnative fish removal will be decided case by case rather than as a blanket requirement. Site-specific monitoring protocols were requested to ensure consistency and records. The 50-fish-per-population sampling in biannual monitoring was judged misplaced and applicable only to five-year genetic monitoring. A glossary will define terms such as *cienega* and *ejido*.

**8. Team business and next steps.** Letters will reconfirm Propst, Juarez-Romero, Burke, J. Stefferud, and Minckley. Gene Maughan declined an invitation to fill the vacancy left by Bill Kepner. Suggested candidates included Jim Brooks, Phil Pister, Richard Strauss, and Dean Hendrickson. A jointly drafted letter on Aravaipa Creek was to go to the Regional Director after team review. Minckley is working to archive the plan's source literature. The next meeting will likely address the draft revised [[Gila Topminnow]] recovery plan, which Hendrickson is preparing. No date was set.
