# Red Shiner Invasion Survey of Lower Aravaipa Creek (October 1990)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of 1990 field materials from [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AZGFD), [[Arizona State University]] (ASU) and cooperating agencies. It covers a fall fish count, a logistics plan, a formal survey summary, and historical annual count tables for Aravaipa Creek. The central subject is the invasion of the non-native [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) into Aravaipa Creek, a stream that is a key refuge for the federally listed native minnows [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). The survey, led by [[W. L. Minckley]] on 20 October 1990, confirmed that red shiners are well established in the region and have penetrated substantially into the creek. The authors date the invasion to early or middle 1990.

## Background & Description

**Species of concern**
- Red shiner: an aggressive non-native minnow. The source describes it as implicated in the decline and disappearance of native spikedace and loach minnow across their ranges.
- Spikedace and loach minnow: endemic, federally listed minnows. Aravaipa Creek is described as one of the best refuges for both.

**Fall Fish Count letter (9 October 1990, AZGFD)**
- Lower end of the creek: about 68% native (n=189) and 32% exotic (n=87) fishes. The OCR of this sentence is partly garbled, so these percentages are read from the surviving text.
- Upper end: a dominant native fish fauna, 100% native (n=233).
- Four sites were sampled: Woods Ranch Diversion, Brandenburg Road Crossing, Sycamore Tree (near the San Pedro River), Mouth of Turkey Creek, and Bill's Crossing (first crossing above the wilderness area). Species tallies per site are not reproduced here because the table is garbled in the source.

**Survey plan (memo for 19–21 October 1990)**
- Goal: determine the distribution and population status of red shiners in lower Aravaipa Creek.
- Method: collect juvenile fishes and retain them for laboratory identification. Juveniles are the most abundant age class, so they give the highest chance of catching shiners, and their loss has minimal effect on populations.
- Teams of untrained and trained personnel used fine-meshed [[Seine Sampling]] gear and numbered preservative buckets. Adult native fishes were released; juveniles were preserved; all introduced fishes were removed to reduce their numbers.
- Work was on partly private land. Landowners had been notified and were to be treated courteously.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study reach and sampling design**
- The survey covered about 4.8 km of Aravaipa Creek in Pinal County. It ran from a point about 4.8 km upstream of the western boundary of the [[Aravaipa Wilderness]] to the point where surface flow percolated into the bed, about 3.2 km upstream of the Aravaipa–San Pedro River confluence.
- The stream was divided into seven sections (I to VII, upstream to downstream). Seven parties of three to five people, each including at least one person familiar with the fish fauna, sampled one section each. Each party flagged its upper point and sampled downstream.
- Gear: 3.2-mm mesh seines, 1.0 to 3.0 m long and about 1 m deep.
- Young-of-the-year (YOY) too small for field identification were preserved in 10% formalin.

**Non-native fishes**
- Recorded non-native species: [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*).
- Most non-native fishes were concentrated in the lowermost sections (V through VII).

**Habitat and physical setting**
- The creek flows through desert and riparian terrain with a floodplain, backwaters, quiet bank areas and an orchard/old-field area at the Nature Conservancy headquarters.
- Surface flow ends where the stream percolates into its bed, which limits continuous downstream connectivity.

**Historical context**
- Annual count and percentage tables cover three reaches of Aravaipa Creek: upper (headwaters to above Turkey Creek), middle (Turkey Creek to Sawmill/Hanley area), and lower (Woods Ranch to the San Pedro River). The years run from the 1940s–1950s to 1990, with many "no data" years.
- The OCR of these tables is heavily garbled. Their numbers are summarized here rather than reproduced. They show long-term records for natives such as [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) and [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), together with non-native species.

## Key Findings

**1. Red shiners are established and have penetrated Aravaipa Creek**
- Adult red shiners (≥35 mm total length) were taken in small numbers, three to ten per section, in all sections except Section II (where large numbers of larvae/juveniles were preserved) and Section VII (no larvae or juveniles preserved).
- The most upstream record was about 4.0 km upstream of the wilderness area boundary, where eight adults were recognized and preserved directly in the field.
- Only one red shiner was a large individual (>60 mm), suggesting a second summer of life. All others could have hatched in spring 1990.

**2. Native fishes were scarce in the survey sample**
- Only four adult spikedace and a single adult loach minnow were recorded across all field parties.
- Preserved samples from Section I contained YOY [[Roundtail Chub]] (<30 mm TL) and YOY loach minnow (<15 mm TL).
- Section II yielded YOY loach minnow (five individuals, 9–14 mm TL) and YOY spikedace (at least three, <14 mm TL).
- Overall, [[Longfin Dace]] were dominant, followed by [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Sonoran Sucker]]. A few adult roundtail chubs were taken in Sections I to III.

**3. Invasion extends to adjacent waters**
- Collecting on 21 October in the [[San Pedro River]], 0.5 km above and below the mouth of Aravaipa Creek, yielded mosquitofish, longfin dace, one [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), and red shiners.
- Red shiners were also taken that day in the [[Gila River]] mainstream, upstream of Winkelman.

**4. Timing of the invasion**
- The last major seining/electrofishing survey of the lower reach known to the author was by an ASU ichthyology class and volunteers. Six 0.5 km reaches were sampled. The source gives the date as "April 1999", which cannot be correct for a 1990 report; the most likely intended year is 1989 (inference).
- The 1990 AZGFD October fish count also failed to record red shiners.
- The author therefore dates the invasion to early or middle 1990.
- The absence of juvenile shiners may indicate a late-summer penetration coinciding with summer rains and flooding, with shiners coming from the abundant San Pedro River population.

**5. Coordination and participation**
- The project was coordinated among AZGFD (Nongame Branch, Tucson Regional Office, and Phoenix Main Office), the [[Bureau Of Land Management]] (Safford District and State Office), the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Ecological Services, Phoenix, and Endangered Species Office, Albuquerque), and [[The Nature Conservancy]] (Aravaipa Preserve and Tucson Main Office).
- Tom Collazo (TNC) arranged camping and facilities and coordinated access with private landowners.
- A total of 39 field participants, both professional and nonprofessional, represented AZGFD, ASU (Department of Zoology), BLM, the Bureau of Reclamation (Phoenix Projects Office), USFWS, TNC, the University of Arizona Cooperative Fisheries Unit, and the private sector.
- Juvenile specimens were to be housed in the ASU fish collection.

**6. Management implications**
- Red shiners had not been stopped by the barrier the project had hoped to build "within the next few years." The survey shows the species had already reached the creek.
- The survey recommends continued communication among all parties and advance notice of any further surveys.
- Removing introduced fishes during sampling was intended to reduce their populations and enhance the native fauna.
