# Spawning Biology, Age, and Growth of the Endangered Cui-ui of Pyramid Lake, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Scoppettone]], [[Coleman]], and [[Wedemeyer]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) examines the endangered [[Cui-ui]] (*[[Chasmistes Cujus]]*), a [[Catostomidae|catostomid]] endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. Adults congregate off the mouth of the [[Truckee River]] from mid-March to early June before spawning. Spawning runs are largest in years with high spring flows. The authors describe migration timing, spawning behavior, [[Fecundity]], larval emigration, predation, and dam-related mortality. They also validate an [[Opercle Bone Aging]] method, which shows that cui-ui can live more than 40 years and identifies the population's age structure and growth patterns.

## Background & Description

The [[Cui-ui]] is a large sucker restricted to [[Pyramid Lake]] and its tributary, the [[Truckee River]]. Adults spawn in the river, and larvae emigrate to the lake soon after hatching. Spawning success depends on river flows that allow fish to pass the delta, which has historically been restricted in low-water years.

The study estimates age using opercle bones rather than [[Scale Annuli]], which are considered unreliable for catostomids. Opercles were collected from fresh specimens taken in 1982–84, and from museum material (including fish collected in 1956 and 1978). Age validation followed individual [[Year Class]] cohorts through successive collections over time, and annuli were counted from the centrum outward. Growth was back-calculated from opercle measurements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Spawning migration and river conditions.** Upstream migration through the [[Fishway]] lasted up to about 19 days (value partly illegible in the scan) and peaked when daily mean water temperatures were roughly 12 to 15 °C. Radio-tagged males remained in the river about 6.5 to 16.5 days, and females about 10 to 10.5 days. Fish spawned over predominantly gravel substrate at depths of about 21 to 110 cm, where water velocities were moderate. The run was largest in years with high spring flows in the Truckee River.

**Larval emigration and dam effects.** Most larvae emigrated to [[Pyramid Lake]] soon after swim-up, mainly at night. The interval between peak adult spawning migration and peak larval emigration was 26 days in 1982 and 29 days in 1984. [[Marble Bluff Dam]], built in the Truckee River in 1905 (date partly illegible in the scan), appears to cause substantial mortality of emigrating larvae. In a [[Larval Passage]] experiment, about 100 hatchery larvae were released above the dam and recaptured below it. Losses were attributed to impingement on the recapture net as much as to dam passage.

**Predation.** [[American White Pelican]] predation caused substantial mortality of adult cui-ui during spawning migrations in 1982 and 1983.

**Diet.** Larvae in the Truckee River fed mainly on [[Chironomids]] and [[Zooplankton]]. Yearlings in [[Hud Lake Slough]], near the river mouth, and adults in the prespawning aggregation fed mostly on zooplankton.

**Fecundity and sex ratio.** Fecundity ranged from about 24,000 eggs in a 432 mm (fork length) female to about 196,000 eggs in a 657 mm fish. The spawning-run sex ratio of males to females was 1:1.3 in 1981 and 1983, and 1:2.5 in 1984.

## Key Findings

**Longevity and age structure.**
- Eleven year classes were identified, with fish from 1 to 41 years old. The oldest validated fish (28–41 years) are believed to be the oldest validated catostomids in the literature.
- In 1983, about 92% of the reproductive population belonged to the 1969 year class. About 5% were from the 1950 or earlier year classes, and the remainder were divided among six year classes (1967, 1971, and 1973–76).
- Of the 11 year classes, eight hatched before [[Marble Bluff Dam]] was installed. These were high-water years when spring flows were sufficient for fish to pass the delta, which likely contributed to population persistence.
- The authors argue that the species' longevity has had major survival value because high-water years that allow spawning are scarce.

**Aging validation.** Annuli were validated by following year classes over time. In 1978, of 79 fish collected, 32 belonged to the 1969 year class (9 years old). By 1983 these fish had 14 annuli, and by 1984 they had 15. False annuli were a problem mainly in fish of the 1950 year class, which had several within the first 6 years of life. The authors compared these with other year classes to identify the most plausible annuli.

**Comparison with other catostomids.** Validated ages for [[White Sucker]] (17 years, from [[Beamish]] 1973) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (about 10 years, from [[Minckley]] 1983) are lower than those for cui-ui.

**Growth.**
- Males and females grew at the same rate until about 6 years of age, when fish first entered the spawning run. Thereafter, females grew faster and lived longer than males, a pattern common in catostomids.
- Length growth was virtually complete by about age 20.
- Growth of females to age 14 was somewhat faster in the 1969 year class than in the 1950 year class. The authors attribute this to higher population density and competition for food and space in the early 1950s, when the population was larger.
- Growth patterns resemble those of [[Longnose Sucker]] in [[Yellowstone Lake]], where females also grow faster after maturity.
- Two growth curves were constructed for the 1950 year class, one based on proportional growth of opercle width to body length and another assuming growth ceased at age 20. The authors favor a modified curve for 33-year-old females.

**Population structure.** The prespawning aggregation consisted of two subpopulations:
- *Young fish*: year classes from 1967 onward.
- *Old fish*: the 1950 year class and earlier. Old fish were mostly female, often had abraded or missing anal fins from previous spawning, and had a notably larger head-to-body length ratio. They concentrated at the fishway ladder and seldom passed beyond it.

**Population estimates.** The prespawning aggregation off the mouth of the Truckee River was estimated at 18,000 adults in 1982 and about 103,000 in 1983 (the year in the scan is partly illegible).
