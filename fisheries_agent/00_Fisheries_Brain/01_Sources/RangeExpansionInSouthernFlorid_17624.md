# Spotted Tilapia Range Expansion in Florida and a Proposed Marine Park at Suheli Lagoon, Laccadive Islands

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two items. The first is a research paper by [[Walter R. Courtenay]] and [[Dannie A. Hensley]] on the rapid range expansion of the introduced [[Spotted Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mariae*) in southern [[Florida]], with preliminary evidence that it may displace native and other exotic fishes. The second is a proposal by A. G. K. Menon, of the Zoological Survey of India, to establish a [[Marine Park]] at [[Suheli Lagoon]] in the [[Laccadive Islands]], an unspoiled atoll in Indian waters.

## Background & Description

**Spotted Tilapia (Florida study).** The species was first reported in Florida by Hogg (1974) from the [[Snapper Creek Canal]] in South Miami, near a concentration of aquarium fish farms, which are the presumed source of the introduction. The species is native to coastal streams of the Gulf of Guinea, West Africa, where it prefers brackish water. Unlike many tilapias, it is not a mouthbrooder. It lays small turquoise-blue eggs on the substrate and guards the newly hatched young. Juveniles bear dark lateral bars that give way to the adult spotted pattern at sexual maturity. Adults reach about 230 mm in length. The figure specimen measured 205 mm standard length.

**Suheli Lagoon (marine park proposal).** The lagoon is about 18 km long and enclosed by an extensive reef. It contains two uninhabited islands, Valiyakara (48.56 ha) and Cheriyakara (33.08 ha). Corals grow up to within about 30 cm of the surface. The author describes the site as one of the few unspoiled atolls in Indian territorial waters, with national significance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Florida.** The Spotted Tilapia has spread across canals and lakes in [[Dade County]], [[Broward County]] and [[Collier County]]. Its range now covers about 2,000 km². It has been recorded from near [[Homestead]] northward into the [[South New River Canal]]. A pond in Collier County suggests a westward spread via the [[Tamiami Canal]], the same route used by the introduced [[Walking Catfish]] to invade the [[Big Cypress Swamp]], [[Everglades National Park]] and Collier County. Virtually all occupied canals also hold other exotic fishes, notably the [[Oscar]] and [[Black Acara]], along with several centrarchids and native species. The Spotted Tilapia is highly resistant to [[Rotenone]], a common piscicide used in fish sampling and management.

**Laccadive Islands.** Coral reefs are central to the ecology here, and the author states that the islands' very existence depends on them. Tourism is growing on [[Bangaram Island]], where there are no restrictions on coral collection, sport fishing, spear fishing, boating or diving. Bait fishing inside Suheli Lagoon supports tuna fishing around Bangaram and [[Agatti Island]].

## Key Findings

### Florida range expansion and impact

- The Spotted Tilapia was not found in the Snapper Creek Canal system during 1970–72 surveys, which suggests introduction between 1972 and 1974.
- Hogg (1976) documented an expansion of 8–16 km in several directions from the original Snapper Creek Canal site and an increase in abundance. He also reported disjunct populations in the Biscayne–Opa Locka and Little River canals and in a quarry lake near Perrine.
- In 1978 collections, the species was dominant in many canals and lakes of eastern Dade County. Estimates in many canals exceeded 50% of total fish biomass. The authors state that these estimates were not validated with quantitative sampling.
- The authors observed Spotted Tilapia excluding Black Acara from shallow spawning areas and killing Black Acara of equal size in aquaria. Some canals showed fewer native fishes and declines in certain established exotics, including [[Pike Killifish]] and Oscars.
- The authors conclude that the species is aggressive and has the potential to disrupt other fishes, but they describe these conclusions as preliminary. They suggest that the competitive exclusion principle ([[Competitive Exclusion]]) will be tested in these waters.
- The summary states that the species appears to be established in southern Arizona. This rests on a photograph and juvenile description that match *Tilapia mariae* rather than the Nile Tilapia reported by Minckley (1973).
- The study was supported by a contract from the US Department of the Interior, Fish and Wildlife Service.

### Proposed Suheli Lagoon marine park

- Suheli Lagoon was selected from several candidate sites in the Laccadives, after a survey conducted from 24 October to 6 November 1976.
- The proposal recommends legislation for controlled visitation and wildlife protection. The lagoon's water clarity and reef diversity are cited as qualifying factors.
- Protection would allow breeding stocks to be maintained. Large, highly fecund individuals within the park are expected to seed surrounding areas that remain open to fishing and recreation.
- Tourism would be channelled toward less destructive activities such as underwater photography, and the park would have an educational role.
- Enforcement would be complex because of boat access and difficulty observing divers. The proposal recommends wardens who could also act as guides and naturalists. The Laccadive Administration's [[Department of Fisheries]] spends about 40% of its total budget on fisheries development and could take on enforcement.
- The two islands are low and fertile, with coconut plantations owned by residents of Kavaratti. The author recommends compensation so the Administration can take over the plantations. The islands have no fresh water and are not habitable.
- Tuna fishing could continue, but bait fishing inside the lagoon would be restricted.
- Access would require an aerodrome. Indian Airlines had surveyed Agatti Island for this purpose. A helicopter service from Cochin to Agatti is not feasible because the distance exceeds helicopter range, so speedboats would connect Agatti to Bangaram and Suheli.
- The author argues that a park at Suheli would increase tourist traffic and place the Laccadive Islands on the world tourism map.
