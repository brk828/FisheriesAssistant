# Fish and Fishing in Leon and Gadsden Counties, Florida

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1954–1955 bulletin from the [[Florida Game and Fresh Water Fish Commission]] (Fish Management Division) reports the first part of the [[Florida Lake and Stream Survey]] for [[Leon County]] and [[Gadsden County]]. Authors Richard H. Macomber (Project Leader) and Edward Crittenden (Assistant Project Leader) inventoried five lakes and the [[Ochlockonee River]], recording physical and biological data, fish species abundance, estimated catch, fishing pressure, and management recommendations. The survey was funded under the [[Dingell-Johnson Act]] as a cooperative federal-state study. The bulletin is a capsule summary and a semi-official fishing guide, and it functions as a research document rather than a management directive.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The Survey aimed to inventory public lakes and streams, document basic physical and biological conditions, evaluate fish types, abundance, and fishing quality, determine each water body's importance, and formulate management plans. The authors noted that the Commission had never had a complete inventory of the state's open-access lakes and streams.

**Scope and limitations.** Only public lakes over 150 acres and the larger rivers were surveyed, because lakes of that size are usually public while smaller ones often are not. The Survey covered roughly three years and was acknowledged to be a short-term study rather than a comprehensive one for any single water body. Private waters such as [[Lake Lafayette]] and [[Orchard Pond]] were excluded. [[Lake Miccosukee]] was dry at the time and deferred until it refilled.

**Study area.** Leon and Gadsden Counties lie in Northwest Florida on the Georgia line, separated by the Ochlockonee River drainage. Resident populations at the time were 51,590 (Leon) and 36,457 (Gadsden). Proximity to Georgia and Alabama brings heavy non-resident fishing pressure. The counties straddle the boundary between the Lower and Middle Coastal Plain, and most lakes sit in troughs between the [[Tallahassee Hills]], except Lake Talquin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and hydrology.** Underlying limestone shapes the region's fisheries. Many lakes, including Jackson and Iamonia, formed partly through limestone solution, producing a slumping land surface and numerous sinks that connect to subterranean caverns. Sinks can cause periodic severe drawdowns, such as at Lake Miccosukee. The authors attributed the high fertility of local waters to nutrient minerals leached as water seeps through the limestone. Most lakes are interconnected underground, and water levels depend on rainfall in South Georgia and Alabama as much as on local rainfall. Water is thought to come mostly from ground water that rises through sinks under hydrostatic pressure.

**Vegetation and habitat.** Vegetation varied widely among waters. [[Hyacinth]] (water hyacinth) was the dominant nuisance: it choked Lake Talquin and the Ochlockonee, and an estimated 3,000 acres of Talquin were covered in 1948. A citizen group, Talquin Hyacinth Eradication, Inc., worked with the Commission to bring it under control. Other plants included maiden cane, American lotus, water lily, spatterdock, water shield, and [[Pickerelweed]]. Cypress fringes surrounded Munson and Bradford.

**Pollution.** Lake Munson receives treated municipal sewage from Tallahassee via a tributary. The authors found no evidence that it harmed fish, fishing, or fishermen, but it reduced the area's aesthetic appeal and deterred some local anglers.

**Water fluctuation.** Water levels varied considerably:
- Lake Munson: normal in 1954, not varying over three feet.
- Lake Iamonia: frequent drying historically; less than three feet per year fluctuation since a dam was built around the sinkhole.
- Lake Jackson: dry in 1933, abnormally full in 1948 (about 20 feet above 1954 level), then falling, with a recent drop of about one inch per week.
- Lake Bradford: about 15 feet below normal at the time of survey, its lowest level on record.
- Lake Talquin: about 3,000 acres of hyacinth in 1948 reflected high-water and plant conditions of the period.

## Key Findings

**Lake Munson (235 acres, Leon County).** Fishing is mainly "cracker" pole fishing for bream (bluegill and shellcracker) from a skiff, along cypress edges. A limit of bream weighing one-half to one pound or more is "run-of-the-mill." Black crappie fishing is noted in winter; bass fishing is "only fair." Species abundance: bluegill and black crappie abundant; warmouth and shellcracker common; bass few; [[Short-Nose Gar]] very abundant; brown bullhead common; channel catfish and bowfin reported but not observed. Estimated catch: 10,000 lbs/year. Estimated fishing pressure: 3,000 fisherman days/year. Evaluation: very good pole fishing for bluegills.

**Lake Iamonia (5,344 acres, Leon County).** Only about 200 acres are open water; the rest is marsh, which is difficult to describe and fishes sporadically. Catches can swing dramatically between days as fish move in and out of the marsh. Fish are exceptionally large. A September 1954 creel census gave average stringer sizes of: bluegill just under one pound, black crappie one pound, flier (coon bream) almost three-quarters of a pound, and chain pickerel about two pounds. A later population sample confirmed these averages. Several pickerel came within one pound of the world rod-and-reel record. Occasional bass of 10 to 15 pounds are taken. Species abundance: bass, bluegill, black crappie, and chain pickerel abundant; warmouth common; chub sucker very abundant. Estimated catch: 40,000 lbs/year. Estimated fishing pressure: 12,000 fisherman days/year. Evaluation: excellent, with outstanding aesthetics.
- Recommendations: close the lake to alligator hunting to preserve and increase alligator populations, which help maintain open water in boat trails; and investigate all possibilities for increasing open water, especially boat trails and small holes in the marsh.

**Lake Talquin (6,700 acres, reservoir).** The reservoir is a major fishing destination with 24 fishing camps and fishing "spot" concentrations of boats. Spring plug-casting along shorelines is productive for bass, and in late summer bass school in the upper reservoir feeding on shad. Species abundance: shellcracker extremely abundant; black crappie very abundant; bass, bluegill abundant; warmouth common; non-game species including bowfin, brown bullhead, channel catfish, and short-nose gar common. Estimated fishing pressure: 100,000 fisherman days/year. Estimated catch reported in the OCR as a fraction of a million pounds (figure garbled in the scan). History: good crappie, bass, and bream fishing in the late 1920s, declining average sizes as the fishery aged, and curtailed fishing during hyacinth infestation. Evaluation: very good fishery.
- Recommendations: the current creel limit (25 fish per day, no more than 8 black bass) may be an undue restriction and could safely be increased.

**Lake Jackson (3,520 acres, Leon County).** Noted for "plate-size" bluegills from April through October, with spawning beds visible through clusters of boats. Bass trolling is popular year-round, and an excellent 1953 spawn was expected to support good bass fishing. Species abundance: bass, bluegill, black crappie, and shellcracker abundant; chain pickerel common; threadfin shad very abundant. Estimated catch: 100,000 lbs/year. Estimated fishing pressure: 30,000 fisherman days/year. Drought had reduced fishable acreage to about one-third of 1948 levels, though fishing remained remarkably good. Evaluation: excellent bass-bream lake. Recommendations: none.

**Ochlockonee River (48.5 miles, Georgia line to Lake Talquin).** The river supports good bass, bream, and catfish fishing; log jams and brush piles impede boat travel, but fish concentrate in pools next to them. Tailwaters below the reservoir can be excellent. Estimated catch: 20,000 lbs/year. Estimated fishing pressure: 8,000 fisherman days/year. No population sample was taken; the assessment relied on interviews. Evaluation: very good bream-catfish stream. Fishing was excellent in the first two years after hyacinth control.

**Lake Bradford (115 acres, Leon County).** A small suburban recreation lake, historically a good bass-bluegill lake, but at survey time it was at its lowest recorded level. Species abundance: bluegill abundant; bass common; chain pickerel few; brook silverside abundant. Estimated catch: 6,000 lbs/year. Estimated fishing pressure: 2,500 fisherman days/year. By August 1955 the lake had shrunk to about one-third of its surveyed area. Evaluation: good fishery, mainly for bluegill.

**Regional findings and recommendations.**
- Non-resident anglers are a major user group: 11,341 fishermen bought non-resident licenses worth $56,912.00 annually. Camp operators felt their needs were under-served.
- Pan-fish regulations could be relaxed. Lakes Munson and Talquin appear overstocked with pan fish, and many anglers take limit catches. Lakes Iamonia and Jackson have pan fish of excellent size and show no sign of overstocking or overfishing, and relaxing limits there would likely have little effect because few anglers currently reach the limit.
- Rough fish are a problem to a varying degree, though the authors did not consider it too serious.
- Further improvement in these fisheries depends largely on more rainfall.

**Survey methods noted.** Crews used gill nets for population sampling during daylight and after dark, recorded weights and measurements, performed water chemistry analyses, and used an electronic depth recorder to map lake bottoms. Contour maps show depth in feet at set intervals.
