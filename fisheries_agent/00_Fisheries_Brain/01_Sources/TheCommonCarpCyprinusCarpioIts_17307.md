# Common Carp (Cyprinus carpio): Wild Origin, Domestication and Nishikigoi

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This issue of [[Guelph Ichthyology Reviews]] (No. 3, 1995) combines two essays: Eugene K. Balon's review of the wild origin, domestication and colour selection of the [[Common Carp]], and Richard C. Hoffmann's paper on environmental change and carp culture in medieval Europe. Editor David L.G. Noakes frames the issue as an interdisciplinary attempt to explain why a widespread, economically vital, yet culturally disparaged fish has such a contested history. Balon argues that the wild carp originated in the Black, Caspian and Aral Sea drainages, spread to the [[Danube River]], and was first domesticated, probably by Romans and later monasteries, while the carp's wild populations are now endangered. The colour-selected [[Nishikigoi]] (koi) is presented as a recent Japanese artificial selection built on earlier European imports.

## Background & Description

- **Editorial framing:** Noakes notes that carp are a "metaphor" for invasive and domesticated species generally, comparing them with [[Brown Trout]], rats, goats, pigs and starlings, and with the [[Sea Lamprey]] and [[Zebra Mussel]] of the Laurentian Great Lakes.
- **Economic scale:** Citing FAO data for 1992, common carp produced 1,022,887 metric tons (mt) of the 9,417,153 mt of world aquaculture fish production, with 125,893 mt from European ponds, 706,119 mt from China and 87,000 mt from Indonesia. All carp species together provided 57% of cultured fish, and common carp alone about 19%, roughly twice all salmonids combined.
- **Domestication criteria:** Balon defines domestication by four or five conditions: value for a specific purpose, human control of breeding, altered behaviour, morphological variation never seen in the wild, and dependence on humans for survival. He describes it as having two phases: association with humans and later conscious artificial selection.
- **Wild form:** The wild carp is torpedo-shaped, with large regular golden-brown scales and a smooth dorsal head profile. Balon's personal field work on the Slovak Danube (1953–1967) and sampling near Koločovo in 1954–1956 underpin the description.
- **Comparative parallel:** The [[Aurochs]] (Bos primigenius), extinct in 1627, is the model for an ancestor lost to domestication. Balon argues the wild carp is "an orphan" in a similar position, comparable to [[Przewalski's Horse]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Wild range:** The ancestral form likely evolved in the Caspian region in the late Pliocene and spread into the Black and Aral basins. Postglacial warming carried descendants to the Danube, and possibly eastern Asia. The Japanese natural occurrence is considered dubious.
- **Danube distribution:** Naturally, wild carp occurred only in the Danube downstream of its piedmont zone. Linguistic evidence (vernacular names such as *ponty*, *šaran* and *sazan*) supports this limit, with the Celtic-derived root "carp" spreading west.
- **Spawning ecology:** Wild carp are open-substrate, egg-scattering plant spawners. They spawn on flooded grass meadows at about 17 °C, with eggs adhering to grass blades; spawning stopped below 16 °C. The 1955 Koločovo spawning was recorded on 6–7 May and resumed 5–7 June at 18 °C.
- **Threats:** Dyke construction, pollution, overfishing and river regulation (including the Gabčíkovo project) nearly eliminated the spawning schools. Holčík (1995b) cited in the text reports that only 5 of 1,536 fish (0.3%) tagged at Gabčíkovo in 1993 were carp, and only one showed wild characters.
- **Introgression:** Escaped domesticated carp threaten genetic purity. Feral Rhine populations documented by Lelek (1987) show reproductive allochrony with domesticated forms, though morphometrics identify them as feral.

## Key Findings

- **Wild origin:** Balon places the closest wild ancestor in the Black, Caspian and Aral drainages, with the Danube as the westernmost natural limit. He treats the Danubian wild carp as the *forma typica*.
- **Subspecies:** Balon finds only two taxa justifiable at most, the European *C. c. carpio* and the Asian *C. c. haematopterus*, distinguished by gill raker counts (means of 32 and 27, with overlapping ranges). He rejects further subspecific splits.
- **Morphometrics (Danube sample, n = 87):** Wild carp averaged 57 males and 30 females; females were larger (498.5 ± 11.17 mm vs. 447.5 ± 6.95 mm SL). Body depth was 24.6–30.9% of standard length in the sample, compared with 31–39% for domesticated carp.
- **Meristics:** Dorsal fin rays most frequently 19–20; lateral line scales most frequently 37–39; vertebrae constant at 38; chromosome number 2n = 100 (pseudo-tetraploid).
- **Life history:** Males in the Koločovo spawning stock were aged 3–15 years (mostly age 5); females 5–9 (mostly age 7). Each female spawned in two or three portions at 10–14 day intervals.
- **Roman hypothesis:** Balon argues Romans in Pannonia (four legions guarded the Danube frontier, with over 100,000 people in total) likely kept carp in *piscinae*. Archaeological fish remains from the Lednyvar fort near Iža, identified by Karol Hensel, include wild carp bones "in abundance," more than any other fish. Balon regards this as the first direct evidence that Romans knew the wild Danube carp.
- **Monastic and medieval continuity:** Balon sees no reason to abandon Roman and monastic involvement, while accepting that Hoffmann's documentary evidence supports later medieval introductions west of the Danube (10th–15th centuries). Albertus Magnus (1260s) and a Count of Champagne (1258) are cited as early pond-culture references.
- **Domestication effects:** Domesticated carp became deep-bodied, scale-reduced and faster-growing, with a larger mouth and longer intestine (wild gut-to-mass ratio 3.0 vs. 2.2 in domesticated). Wild carp had 18–19% more erythrocytes and higher muscle vascularisation. Wild carp are considered better stocking material for natural rivers.
- **Breeding forms:** Bryliñska (1986) recognises four domestic scale types (scaled, line, mirror and leather/naked), controlled by a two-allele system.
- **Nishikigoi origin:** Koi were selected from colour aberrants in Yamakoshi Village, Niigata Prefecture, with intensive selection after the Second World War. Japanese recognise about 15 basic colour classes (Table 5). Balon suggests possible descent from German mirror carp imported around 1904 or earlier Dutch imports to Nagasaki. Grand champions can fetch over US$1 million.
- **Conservation warning:** Balon concludes the wild carp is "an orphan" headed for extinction, and that domestication has made its wild form irrecoverable, as with cattle and the aurochs.
