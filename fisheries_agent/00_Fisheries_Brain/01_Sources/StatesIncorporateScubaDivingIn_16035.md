# States Incorporate Scuba Diving in Fisheries Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #survey-methodology

## Executive Summary

A 1957 survey of U.S. state fisheries programs reveals widespread adoption of scuba diving as a fisheries management tool. Of responding states (89.6% response rate), 14 states actively use scuba diving, four plan to implement it, and one utilizes volunteer skin diving clubs. The technology enables direct observation of fish behavior, habitat assessment, and verification of management interventions, though visibility limitations constrain its application in turbid inland waters.

## Background & Description

[[Scuba Diving]] emerged as a novel tool for [[Fisheries Management]] in the mid-1950s. State game and fish departments began incorporating underwater observation techniques to supplement traditional survey methods. The survey conducted by the [[North Dakota Game and Fish Department]] documented adoption patterns across U.S. states.

Equipment preferences varied by depth and application. Snorkels were preferred in shallow waters where scuba air bubbles disturbed fish behavior. [[Scuba Equipment]] was reserved for deep water studies where direct surface breathing was impractical.

One state prohibited scuba use following a fatality during personal (non-professional) diving operations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Scuba diving applications addressed multiple ecological questions across freshwater and marine environments:

**Habitat Assessment**: Divers documented vegetation types, bottom conditions, and physical habitat characteristics. [[Brush Shelters]] received particular attention for species utilization studies.

**Species Surveys**: [[Stream Surveys]] employed divers to determine species composition and population densities. California utilized scuba diving in the [[American River]] for these assessments.

**Behavioral Observations**: Direct observation revealed fish reactions to nets, movement patterns of tagged fish, feeding habits, spawning site utilization, and migration behaviors.

**Visibility Constraints**: Turbidity represented the primary limiting factor for scuba applications, particularly in inland waters. Ideal water clarity was essential for effective observations, and even slight turbidity significantly reduced observation efficiency.

## Key Findings

**Adoption Statistics** (as of early 1957):
- Survey response rate: 89.6% of U.S. states
- 14 states actively using scuba diving
- 4 states planning implementation ([[Connecticut]], [[Indiana]], [[North Dakota]], [[Oregon]])
- 1 state using volunteer club cooperation
- 24 states not employing scuba diving
- 1 state prohibiting use due to safety concerns

**Active Programs**: [[California]], [[Colorado]], [[Florida]], [[Georgia]], [[Illinois]], [[Massachusetts]], [[Michigan]], [[Minnesota]], [[New Hampshire]], [[North Carolina]], [[Ohio]], [[Pennsylvania]], [[Rhode Island]], [[Texas]], and [[Wisconsin]]

**Primary Applications**:

1. **Eradication Verification**: The most widespread application involved assessing fish kill success. [[Michigan]] applied dye to toxicants to track penetration rates. Post-treatment transects with underwater fish counts quantified eradication effectiveness.

2. **Netting Studies**: [[Florida]] conducted [[Trawl Net]] operation studies using underwater sleds, with cooperation from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]].

3. **Angler Support**: Divers located fish concentrations to assist recreational anglers and collected data to regulate public skin diving activities.

4. **Documentation**: Underwater photography of spawning beds and specimen collection for laboratory analysis.

**Implementation Models**: States employed various approaches including cooperative agreements with diving clubs, volunteer divers, contracted professional divers, and state-equipped diving units with trained personnel.
