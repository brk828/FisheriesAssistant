# Fishes of Arizona's Climatic Zones

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #arizona-fauna

## Executive Summary

This section of the source document surveys the freshwater fish fauna of Arizona, noting a total of 61 species inhabiting the state's waters — 28 native and 33 introduced. Introduced species include game fish, baitfish (mostly minnows), and rough fish (mainly carp). The native fauna is presented as scientifically remarkable, featuring the world's largest known minnow species, some of North America's smallest fishes, and unusual hump-backed morphologies unique to the Colorado River system. The text also notes that Arizona's diverse native fish fauna is "rapidly disappearing," an early indicator of the conservation crisis that would later affect many of these species (several are now federally listed as threatened or endangered).

## Background & Description

- **[[Arizona Native Trout]]** (*Salmo gilae*, also called Gila Trout): Yellow-golden coloration; reaches ~16 inches in length and ~2 pounds in weight. Now extensively hybridized with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]].
- **[[Colorado River Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): One of the world's largest minnows (family [[Cyprinidae]]); reaches approximately 5 feet in length and ~100 pounds in weight.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*): A live-bearer (family [[Poeciliidae]]); among the smallest North American fishes, at 1–2 inches in length.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*): Small-bodied, 2–3 inches in length.
- **[[Humpback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Large-bodied sucker (family [[Catostomidae]]) with a pronounced dorsal hump; adaptive significance of the humpback morphology is noted as unknown.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): Large minnow with hump-backed morphology, co-occurring with the Humpback Sucker in the Colorado River / Grand Canyon.
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila robusta*, "Verde Trout" or "Gila Trout" colloquially): Large minnow reaching 16 inches and ~2 pounds; superficially trout-like in appearance and readily takes a fly, despite not being a true trout and being considered "considerably bony to eat."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution**: Native trout (*Salmo gilae*) occurs in the headwaters of the [[Gila River]], with a remaining pure population restricted to the headwaters of Eagle Creek (Greenlee County), a Gila River tributary.
- **Habitat range**: Warm water sport fisheries are concentrated in rivers and lakes across central Arizona, from the [[Colorado River]] in the west to Lyman Reservoir (Greenlee County) in the east.
- **Trout waters**: Primary trout habitat spans central/northern Arizona — from the Colorado River and [[Grand Canyon]] in the north to Coolidge Dam on the Gila River in the south, and east to the Blue River.
- **Colorado River system**: Hosts several endemic remarkable species — the Colorado River Squawfish, Humpback Sucker, and Humpback Chub — reflecting isolated evolutionary adaptation within this river system, echoing the isolated speciation patterns discussed for [[Scaphinotus]] ground beetles in the coniferous forest zone.
- **Aquatic insect fauna linkage**: The permanent streams of Arizona's higher-elevation coniferous forests support a diverse aquatic insect fauna, distinct from that of lower intermittent streams/ponds and the Colorado River, serving as an indirect but economically significant food source for fish.
- **Introduced species impact**: Hybridization between native *Salmo gilae* and introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] has substantially diminished pure native trout populations, confining genetically pure stock to a single tributary.

## Key Findings

- Arizona supports 61 fish species: 28 native, 33 introduced (game fish, baitfish/minnows, and rough fish such as carp).
- Only one native game fish exists in Arizona: the Arizona native trout (*Salmo gilae*), now largely hybridized with introduced rainbow trout.
- Warm water game fishes present include channel catfish, yellow (striped) bass, smallmouth bass, largemouth bass, green sunfish, bluegill, white crappie, black crappie, and yellow perch.
- Four introduced trout species supplement the native trout fishery: rainbow trout, brown trout, cutthroat trout, and eastern brook trout.
- Arizona's native fish fauna includes extremes of size: the ~100-lb Colorado River Squawfish (one of the world's largest minnows) contrasts with the 1–3 inch Gila Topminnow and Desert Pupfish (among North America's smallest fishes).
- The Colorado River and Grand Canyon host unique hump-backed native species (Humpback Sucker and Humpback Chub) whose morphological adaptation remains scientifically unexplained.
- The Bonytail (*Gila robusta*), though not officially a game fish, is a hardy, fly-taking native minnow colloquially called "Verde trout" or "Gila trout" across the Colorado, Salt, Gila, and Verde rivers.
- The author explicitly flags Arizona's native fish fauna as being of high recreational and scientific value, yet **rapidly disappearing** — an early conservation warning consistent with the later listing of species such as the Humpback Chub, Desert Pupfish, and Gila Topminnow under the U.S. Endangered Species Act.
- Aquatic insect communities differ markedly between high-elevation permanent streams, lower intermittent streams/ponds, and the Colorado River, with direct trophic relevance to fish populations.
