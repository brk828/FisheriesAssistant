# The Warmouth, Chaenobryttus coronarius (Bartram), in Red Haw Hill Reservoir, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #warmouth

## Executive Summary

This 1949 study by [[William M. Lewis]] and Thomas S. English, of the Department of Zoology and Entomology at [[Iowa State College]], examines the [[Warmouth]] (*Chaenobryttus coronarius*) in [[Red Haw Hill Lake]], an 80-acre recreational impoundment in south central Iowa. Although the species is fairly abundant, anglers rarely catch it: only four warmouth were taken in 6,513 man-hours of fishing. The authors describe age and growth, the length-weight relationship, spawning timing, diet, and parasites. They conclude that the warmouth contributes little to angling but may be valuable as a predator of young [[Bluegill]] and of other fish.

## Background & Description

Red Haw Hill Lake was built in 1935 and is used only for recreation. Together with the neighboring East Lake, it receives most of the fishing pressure within a 35-mile radius. The maximum depth is slightly more than 30 feet, and about 90 per cent of the lake is deeper than 7.5 feet, the depth limit for higher aquatic plants. The irregular shoreline reduces wind action and produces a shallow epilimnion. The watershed is well protected from erosion.

The authors note that the following fish species are well represented in the lake:

- [[Largemouth Black Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
- [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigro-maculatus*)
- [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*)
- [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*)
- Warmouth (*Chaenobryttus coronarius*)

Warmouth occupy shallow water among weeds along the bank during summer, where angling is difficult.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Plankton growth is abundant, and surface algal blooms are frequent in late summer. During much of midsummer and late summer, the algal growth is heavy enough to interfere with fishing.

Specimens were collected with [[Basket Traps]], [[Fyke Nets]], experimental [[Gill Nets]], seining, and angling. Of the warmouth caught, 51 came from basket traps, 18 from gill nets, 6 from angling, and 5 from fyke nets. Basket traps sampled all sizes effectively, while gill and fyke nets mainly sampled larger individuals. [[Seining]] with a four-foot minnow seine caught no warmouth of any size, although young-of-the-year of other species were readily collected.

The authors report the following study parameters:

- Length measures: standard length ran from the snout tip to the end of the hypural plate; total length from snout tip to tail tip, with lobes compressed; fork length from snout tip to tail fork.
- Length conversion factors: standard to fork length 1.179 (based on 65 specimens); standard to total length 1.248 (based on 72 specimens). Both were derived from fish 40 to 177 mm long.
- Study project: Iowa Cooperative Fishery Research Unit Project No. 42, sponsored by the Industrial Science Research Institute of Iowa State College and the Iowa State Conservation Commission, with cooperation from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

## Key Findings

**Abundance and angling**
- Only four warmouth were caught in 6,513 man-hours of fishing, despite the species being fairly abundant.
- A total of 80 warmouth were collected by all methods (51 basket traps, 18 gill nets, 6 angling, 5 fyke nets).

**Age and growth**
- Scales were taken from three locations on each fish and read with a microprojector. Annuli were distinct on most scales.
- A [[Body-Scale Relationship]] was fitted to 55 warmouth. The line has a slope of 0.77459 and a length-axis intercept of 13 mm, and the growth calculations used 13 mm as the base.
- Growth was not sexually different, so data for both sexes were combined.
- Annual length increase was greatest during the first four years and declined each year afterward.
- Annual weight increment rose through the fourth year. In the seventh year it exceeded the combined first two years.
- Specimens represented seven year-classes. No young-of-the-year were obtained. The 1945 and 1946 classes were best represented among fish two or more years old. The authors suspect the 1947 and 1948 classes may have been more abundant than the sample indicates.
- Red Haw warmouth grew at a similar or slightly slower rate than warmouth from [[Muskellunge Lake]], Indiana, and [[Reelfoot Lake]], Tennessee. Total length at capture for age II, III, and IV Red Haw fish was 5.2, 6.5, and 7.6 inches (samples of 17, 23, and 3 fish).

**Reproduction**
- Among 65 warmouth examined for sexual condition, the earliest spawning occurred in the last week of June.
- Spawning was not complete until late July, when ripe individuals were still found.

**Length-weight relationship and condition**
- For 76 warmouth (40 to 177 mm standard length), the relationship is Log W = −4.683 + 3.138 Log L, with W in grams and L in millimeters. See [[Length-Weight Relationship]].
- The average [[Condition Factor]] (K) was 3.95, with no detectable change with length or sex.

**Diet**
- Of 29 stomachs (40 to 177 mm, collected April through July), 12 were empty.
- Among the 17 stomachs with food, items were: 2- to 4-inch fish (7), crayfish (4), unidentified insect larvae (4), vegetable debris (2), unidentified insects (2), leech (1), dragonfly naiad (1), and snails (1).
- By volume, fish and crayfish were the most important food items, so warmouth are predacious.

**Parasites and disease**
- Of 66 warmouth (50 to 169 mm, collected April to early September), every fish carried [[Posthodiplostomum Minimum]] metacercariae in at least one organ, and most in two or more.
- Metacercariae encased the heart of 56 fish and occurred in the liver of 42, the kidneys of 14, the mesenteries of 6, and the spleen of 1. The authors describe parasitism as extremely high. Hearts were almost always fully encased, and livers and kidneys held uncountable numbers.
- Parasitic copepods were found on the gills of 17 fish, with 2 to 30 or more per fish.
- Leeches were attached to the fins of 9 fish and the palate of 1, with 2 to 5 per fish.
- Nematodes (1 to 3) were found in the mesenteries of 6 fish.
- One fish had over 100 larval strigeids (black grubs) in its body muscles.
- [[Proteocephalus Ambloplitis]] plerocercoids were found on the livers of 2 fish (2 and 3 organisms) and in the ovaries of 3 fish (7 or 8 per ovary).
- Two fish had regenerated caudal fins, which the authors attribute to recovery from [[Saprolegnia]] infection.

**Management implications**
- The warmouth contributes little to the angler's catch. Its main value is as a predator, and the authors suggest it rivals the largemouth bass in controlling the abundance of young bluegills and similar fish.
- Little work has been done on the species. The authors propose it may prove useful as a predator of [[Bullhead]] or as a supplement to the largemouth bass, but this remains untested.
