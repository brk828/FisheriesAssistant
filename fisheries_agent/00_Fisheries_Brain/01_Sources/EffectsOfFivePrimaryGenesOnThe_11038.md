# Effects of Five Primary Genes on the Site of Melanomas in Fishes and the Influence of Two Color Genes on Their Pigmentation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1948 paper by [[Myron Gordon]], published in the Special Publications of the New York Academy of Sciences (Vol. IV, pp. 216-268), reports genetic experiments on melanoma formation in hybrids between the [[Mexican Platyfish]] (*Platypoecilus maculatus*) and the [[Swordtail]] (*Xiphophorus hellerii*). It identifies five dominant, sex-linked macromelanophore genes (Sp, N, Sr, Sd, Sb) that determine where melanotic tumors arise in hybrids, and shows that two color genes, golden (st) and albino (i), modify the degree and reversibility of melanin pigmentation.

## Background & Description

- **Study organisms:** The platyfish has two classes of black pigment cells, micromelanophores and macromelanophores. Over 8,000 wild specimens were examined, revealing about 150 patterns and pattern combinations built from thirteen common basic melanotic types, plus three red patterns. The swordtail is comparatively uniform; a faintly spotted variety occurs at under one per cent in natural populations.
- **Sexual isolation:** In the wild, both species coexist and breed in the same season in the Jamapa, Papaloapan, Coatzacoalcos and Usumacinta rivers, yet no natural hybrid was found among over 10,000 wild fishes. Under laboratory conditions they hybridize readily.
- **Historical context:** Early amateur breeders (Löesslein, Haffner, Gramsch) first noted cancer-like growths in black hybrids. Later work by [[Haussler]], [[Kosswig]], [[Breider]] and Gordon built the genetic and histological basis for [[Fish Melanoma]].
- **Sex determination:** Domesticated platyfish follow a WZ/ZZ system, whereas wild stocks follow an XY/XX system. The author proposed that the domesticated system arose through hybridization with the swordtail during domestication.
- **Materials:** Domesticated spotted (Sp) and black (N) strains were used, along with wild stocks from the Rio Jamapa and Rio Papaloapan collected on 1932 and 1939 Mexican expeditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is primarily a genetics and pathology study conducted in laboratory culture rather than a field ecology report. Its environmental relevance lies in the native distribution of the two species (southern Mexican and Guatemalan rivers) and in the observation that sexual isolation prevents gene exchange in nature. Hybridization in domestication was shown to produce hybrid pigmentation abnormalities. The paper also reviews melanotic tumors across other fish groups, including the [[Pollack]], [[Mackerel]], [[Skate]], [[Herring]], [[Plaice]], [[Silverside]] and [[Lungfish]], noting these records span eleven orders of elasmobranch and true fishes. Comparative records from axolotls, pine snakes, chickens, domestic mammals and mice are also summarized.

## Key Findings

- **Five macromelanophore genes control melanoma site.** Sp (irregular spots, tumors at unpredictable sites), N (black bands, tumors along the band), Sr (striped sides, tumors rare and only after accumulated modifiers), Sd (dorsal-fin spots, predictable dorsal tumors appearing within about a month of birth in hybrids), and Sb (ventral darkening, mid-ventral tumors about eight months after birth).
- **Macromelanophores, not micromelanophores, drive tumors.** Melanomas occurred in hybrids lacking micromelanophores (st st and i i genotypes), confirming the role of macromelanophores.
- **Swordtail modifiers A and B intensify melanosis.** Backcrosses to swordtails increased melanosis; backcrosses to platyfish generally reduced it. Multiple factors, rather than general heterosis, best explain the intensity.
- **Golden gene (st) suppresses macromelanophores** in Sp, Sr, Sd, Sb and N platyfish, but could not suppress tumor behavior in most hybrids except Sr hybrids.
- **Albino gene (i) produces amelanotic melanomas.** Pink-eyed albino hybrids (e.g., Sd i, Sp i) developed melanomas lacking melanin; in five albinos with Sd i, amelanotic melanomas of considerable size developed.
- **Melanin loss and restoration are genetically reversible.** Melanotic to amelanotic and back to melanotic cycles were demonstrated across generations (cultures 136, 138, 192), with cell types largely unchanged, suggesting genic rather than chromosomal causes.
- **Wild Sp and Sd genes differ in effect.** Wild Sp produced less macromelanophore proliferation in F1 hybrids than domesticated Sp, but became intensely black after backcrossing to swordtails.
- **Sex-linked systems differ between wild and domesticated stocks.** Domesticated stocks use WZ/ZZ; wild stocks use XY/XX, with X'X hybrids being fertile females. Most Sd hybrids were sterile, and in one Sd F2 cross 100 of 104 F1 hybrids were female.
- **Sr melanomas occur rarely.** Striped hybrids developed melanomas only after several backcrosses to swordtails, and their histology resembled Sp and N melanomas.
- **Comparative vertebrate context.** The paper argues melanomas are common across vertebrates and that heterosis as an explanation for hybrid tumor incidence is insufficient; a multiple-factor model fits the data better.
