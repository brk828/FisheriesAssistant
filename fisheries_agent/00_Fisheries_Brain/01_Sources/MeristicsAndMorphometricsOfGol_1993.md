# Meristics and Morphometrics of Golden Shiner, Rudd, and Their Reciprocal F1 Hybrids

## Executive Summary

This study, conducted by [[Douglas Tave]] and colleagues, addresses a critical taxonomic and regulatory problem in U.S. baitfish aquaculture: distinguishing the native [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*) from the introduced European [[Rudd]] (*Scardinus erythrophthalmus*) and their reciprocal F1 hybrids. Because rudd is banned for sale/importation in states like Arkansas (Commission Order 90-029) due to its invasive potential, farmers attempted to circumvent restrictions by producing golden shiner x rudd hybrids, which were hypothesized to be sterile due to significant karyotypic differences between the parental species. The researchers examined standard meristic and morphometric traits across 11 golden shiners, 30 rudd, and 60 reciprocal F1 hybrids (30 of each cross direction). The key diagnostic finding is that **keel squamation** is the single most reliable and rapid character for differentiating all three groups: golden shiner has a naked, scaleless keel; rudd has 6–9 scales on the dorsal keel surface; and hybrids have scales originating from the sides of the keel rather than its ventral edge, with different scale shape (unnotched anterior/posterior margins) compared to rudd. No morphological trait could distinguish between the two reciprocal hybrid crosses (Golden Shiner♀ x Rudd♂ vs. Rudd♀ x Golden Shiner♂); mitochondrial DNA analysis is recommended for that distinction.

### Pharyngeal Teeth
- Golden shiner: predominantly **0,5-5,0**
- Rudd: predominantly **3,5-5,3**
- F1 hybrids (both crosses): intermediate/variable distributions; not diagnostic on its own but supports species differences.

### Keel Squamation — The Key Diagnostic Trait
- **Golden Shiner**: Keel raised, entirely naked (scaleless).
- **Rudd**: Keel covered with 6–9 scales (mean = 8.0); scales positioned on top/ventral edge of keel, easily counted; scales indented (notched) along anterior and posterior edges to overlap the keel.
- **F1 Hybrids (both reciprocal crosses)**: Keel at least partially scaled, but scales originate from the **sides** of the keel rather than the ventral edge, making counting difficult. Hybrid scales lack the anterior/posterior notching seen in rudd.
- This trait alone provides complete, unambiguous differentiation among golden shiner, rudd, and hybrids — no other single phenotype achieves this.

### Coloration
- Adult Rudd: bright orange fins.
- Adult Golden Shiner: light yellow fins.

### Cytogenetics (referenced from companion studies)
- **Golden Shiner**: 50 chromosomes; 7 pairs metacentric, 11 pairs submetacentric, 7 pairs subtelo-/telocentric; diploid arm number = 86.
- **Rudd**: 50 chromosomes (per Cataudella et al. 1977; alternate report of 48 by Chiarelli et al. 1969); 6 pairs metacentric, 9 pairs submetacentric, 10 pairs subtelo-/telocentric; diploid arm number = 80.
- **F1 Hybrids**: 13 metacentric, 20 submetacentric, 17 subtelo-/telocentric chromosomes; diploid arm number = 83.
- Significant karyotypic divergence and differential NOR (nucleolar organizing region) expression (nucleolar dominance) in hybrids suggest gametic incompatibility and likely partial hybrid sterility ([[Li et al. 1991]]; [[Gold et al. 1991]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **[[Golden Shiner]]**: Native North American cyprinid; the most economically important farm-raised baitfish species in the U.S., especially in Arkansas (75% of state baitfish production; 3.7 million kg produced in 1990, valued at $20.4 million). Raised in earthen pond aquaculture systems using standard commercial baitfish culture practices.
- **[[Rudd]]**: Introduced European cyprinid, first recorded in U.S. waters (New York, New Jersey) nearly 100 years before this study (Myers 1925). Historically established but largely extirpated populations existed in New York and Wisconsin, with a persisting population in Cobbosseecontee Lake, Maine. By the time of this study, expanding introduced populations were documented in Kansas, Nebraska, Missouri, Arkansas (Horseshoe Lake), Oklahoma (Lake Texoma), and Texas (multiple lakes/reservoirs), largely attributed to angler releases of bait fish. Rudd grow larger than golden shiners and display more vivid orange coloration, driving farmer interest despite regulatory bans.
- **Hybrids**: Produced in 0.4-ha earthen ponds via controlled reciprocal crosses (Rudd♀ x Golden Shiner♂ and Golden Shiner♀ x Rudd♂) using spawning mats for egg deposition, followed by standard pond-rearing baitfish culture. Intended as a legally compliant alternative baitfish combining desirable rudd traits (size, color) with golden shiner's native/legal status, contingent on hybrid sterility to prevent ecological risk from bait-bucket releases.
- **Regulatory Context**: Concerns over exotic species introductions (competition, predation, hybridization impacts on native fish, per [[Ono et al. 1983]], [[Johnson 1987]], [[Williams et al. 1989]]) led Arkansas Game and Fish Commission to prohibit rudd importation/sale (Commission Order 90-029), motivating the hybridization approach as a workaround.

## Key Findings

1. **Complete species differentiation** between golden shiner and rudd was achieved using scales before dorsal fin, gill raker counts, pharyngeal teeth formulas, and especially keel squamation — every meristic trait examined differed significantly (P=0.05).
2. **Keel squamation is the single best diagnostic character** for distinguishing all three groups (golden shiner, rudd, F1 hybrids) rapidly and reliably; it is proposed as a practical field/hatchery identification tool to ensure rudd are not illegally sold as bait.
3. F1 hybrids differed significantly from **both** parental species in 5 of 8 meristic counts, but only keel squamation morphology cleanly separated hybrids from both parents simultaneously.
4. **No phenotypic trait could distinguish between reciprocal F1 hybrid crosses** (Golden Shiner♀ x Rudd♂ vs. Rudd♀ x Golden Shiner♂); differentiation of these crosses would require molecular tools such as mitochondrial DNA analysis ([[Gold et al. 1991]]).
5. This work corroborates and extends findings by [[Burkhead and Williams 1991]], who independently reported keel squamation as diagnostic and used principal component analysis to separate golden shiner, rudd, and one hybrid direction (though they did not examine the reciprocal Rudd♀ x Golden Shiner♂ cross).
6. The combination of significant karyotypic divergence (diploid arm numbers: 86 vs. 80 vs. 83) and nucleolar dominance in hybrids strongly suggests **at least partial hybrid sterility**, supporting the feasibility of using golden shiner-rudd hybrids as a legally and ecologically safer baitfish alternative — pending confirmation of sterility (reported elsewhere by the same research group).
7. Findings have direct **aquaculture management implications**: enabling hatchery technicians to visually confirm absence of pure rudd or backcrossed rudd genetics in bait stock via simple keel examination, supporting compliance with state exotic species regulations.
