# Succeeding in Science: Some Rules of Thumb

#ichthyology #marine-biology #science-careers

## Executive Summary

This is a reflective essay by [[James Watson]], adapted from a talk given on March 2, 1993, at [[Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory]]. The talk honored the 40th anniversary of the [[Watson-Crick Model|Watson/Crick discovery of the DNA double helix]]. It is a career-advice piece, published in the *Science* Careers in Science 93 special issue (vol. 261, 24 September 1993), and it contains no aquatic, fish, or ecological data. The note records its content in the required structure, with the main points set out as "Key Findings." Watson argues that success in science requires more than intelligence. It requires learning from stronger peers, taking risks, keeping a fallback of mentors, doing work one enjoys, and staying in close contact with other scientists.

## Background & Description

- **Format:** Personal reflection and advice, presented as "rules of thumb" for ambitious researchers.
- **Author:** [[James Watson]], co-discoverer of the DNA double helix with [[Francis Crick]].
- **Occasion:** Talk at [[Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory]], 2 March 1993, marking the 40th anniversary of the double helix.
- **Publication:** *Science*, vol. 261, 24 September 1993, Careers in Science 93 special issue.
- **Origin of interest:** Watson says he turned to genetics at age 17, after reading *What Is Life* by [[Erwin Schrödinger]], which treats the gene as the essence of life. Before that he had been studying birds.
- **Training path:** Watson was turned down by [[California Institute of Technology|Caltech]] for graduate school and went to Indiana, where his professor was [[Salvador Luria]]. He later moved from Copenhagen to Cambridge, where he worked with [[Max Perutz]] and [[John Kendrew]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The essay does not address aquatic organisms, fish, invertebrates, plankton, water chemistry, habitats, or ecosystems. Its "context" is the professional and social environment of laboratory science in the late 20th century. Key settings it names include:

- **Academic institutions:** [[University of Chicago]], Indiana University, Caltech, and the [[University of Cambridge]] (including the [[Cavendish Laboratory]] environment implied by the crystallography work).
- **Scientific rivalry:** The race to determine the structure of DNA. Watson names [[Rosalind Franklin]] as a competitor who was isolated and lacked support.
- **Mentorship and institutional power:** Figures such as [[Paul Weiss]], [[Ralph Cleland]], and [[Linus Pauling]] appear as examples of how supervisors, departments, and reputations can block or shape careers.

## Key Findings

Watson sets out five "rules" for success in science. He presents them as personal experience, not as tested or quantified results.

1. **Learn from the winners.** Always seek out people who are brighter than you. Winning against weaker opponents gives little satisfaction. The aim is to "go somewhere beyond your ability and come out on top."
2. **Take risks.** Major advances often need work for which you are "unqualified … by definition." Watson's move from zoology to [[X-ray Diffraction|X-ray diffraction]] crystallography, against the view that DNA was an unrealistic goal, is his main example. Ambitious goals will attract criticism. Being willing to ignore the judgments of people with power over your career can be painful, and may mean breaking with a mentor, lab head, or department chair.
3. **Have a fallback.** Keep someone who will support you when you run into serious trouble. Watson and Crick each had senior figures who helped them, such as Perutz and Kendrew. Watson notes that Franklin had no one who would or could save her.
4. **Never do anything that bores you.** Work you dislike will not be done well. Watson adds that you need people you can turn to for intellectual help, and gives [[George Kreisel]] as an example of a friend Crick relied on for difficult mathematics.
5. **Stay connected to your peers.** Expose your ideas to informed criticism and attend key meetings, where you may learn facts you would otherwise miss. Watson says he "knew almost everyone I needed to know," whatever their personal qualities. His final rule is: "If you can't stand to be with your real peers, get out of science."

### Points of Emphasis
- **Luck is necessary but not sufficient.** Watson credits his career to chance encounters, including reading Schrödinger and being rejected by Caltech, but argues that intelligence alone is insufficient.
- **Isolation as a risk factor.** Watson attributes the failure of rival efforts to reach the double helix partly to isolation. He cites [[Linus Pauling]], whose reputation discouraged disagreement, and Franklin, who did not talk to Crick.
- **Attitude to people.** Watson presents engagement with other scientists as necessary to research, even when the people are unpleasant.
