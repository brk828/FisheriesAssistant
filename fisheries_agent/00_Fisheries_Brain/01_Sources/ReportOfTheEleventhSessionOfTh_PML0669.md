# Report of the Eleventh Session of the Joint EIFAC/ICES Working Group on Eels (Silkeborg, Denmark, 1999)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anguillidae

## Executive Summary

This report from the Eleventh Session of the Joint [[EIFAC]]/[[ICES]] Working Group on Eels (Silkeborg, Denmark, 20–24 September 1999) reviews the status of the [[European Eel]] (*Anguilla anguilla*) across its range. The group concluded that eel yields are declining throughout Europe, that [[Glass Eel]] recruitment remains at very low levels, and that monitoring and research are poorly coordinated. Because data are fragmented and lack age and size information, the group judged that firm management advice was not yet possible. It recommended a precautionary approach, including provisional reductions in fishing on all life stages, the development of [[Spawner Escapement]] targets, a permanent monitoring system, and an international research coordination body. The report also covers [[Re-Stocking]] practice, which the group discussed although it lay outside the original Terms of Reference.

## Background & Description

The working group was mandated by the 85th Statutory Meeting of ICES (1999) to:

- assess recruitment trends and their causes, fishery yields and effects on stock;
- investigate the impact of fisheries on spawner escapement;
- explore options for escapement targets;
- define relevant management units;
- suggest management actions to achieve required escapement;
- advise on future international research coordination.

The previous meeting was held in IJmuiden, Netherlands, and its report was adopted by EIFAC (Póvoa de Varzim, Portugal, 1998) and ICES/ACFM (Copenhagen, May 1998). Attendance figures differ within the report: the abstract gives 41 participants from 27 countries, including 21 European countries plus Canada, USA, Japan, Taiwan, Australia and New Zealand, while the introduction states 41 participants from fifteen countries. The abstract reports 55 contributions; the introduction says more than fifty, mostly posters. Selected papers were later published in *Dana* (Vol. 12).

The report was prepared by the FAO (EIFAC Occasional Paper No. 84, Rome, 2001). Its structure follows the Terms of Reference, with an added chapter on re-stocking.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life cycle and species.** The European eel's spawning and maturation stages have never been observed in the wild. Silver eel escaping continental fisheries are better described as pre-spawners. There is no means of assessing losses between freshwater emigration and the oceanic spawning phase. The [[American Eel]] (*A. rostrata*), which also spawns in the [[Sargasso Sea]], shows similar downward trends, suggesting common limiting factors during the continental phase of both species.

**Recruitment and stock status.** Fourteen river catchments in 12 countries, plus North Sea and whole-Europe estimates, were analysed. No upward trends were found. Over the full time series, declines followed the high levels of the 1970s. Most series showed stable low levels in the 1990s. The exception was the River Erne (north-west Ireland), where no trend was apparent in the 1980s.

**Yellow and silver eel.** Ten yellow eel and five silver eel series were examined. Six yellow eel series declined and four were stable. All silver eel series declined. The majority of series were under 20 years long and derived from fishery-dependent sources.

**Other species.** American eel data (Peterson, 1997; Richkus and Whalen, 1999) show many downward trends, although some series were stable or increasing. Yellow eel counts at the Moses Saunders Dam on the St. Lawrence peaked in the early 1990s and then fell sharply. New Zealand commercial catches peaked in the mid-1970s and were relatively stable from 1982 to 1991.

**Aquaculture.** Total annual aquaculture production was reported for Sweden (1983–98), Denmark (1984–99), Italy (1984–97) and the Netherlands. Trends were increasing in the cases reported, and the report notes rising demand for [[Glass Eel]] seed stock.

**Contaminants and parasites.** Long-term contaminant monitoring is under way in Belgium, and the eel is considered a useful bioindicator. The swimbladder parasite [[Anguillicola Crassus]] is now endemic across most of the European eel's range, often at high infestation rates. Its effects on silver eel survival during oceanic migration and on egg production are unknown but may be important.

**Obstructed habitat.** Of about 87,335 km² of continental waters (figure as printed), 3.6% is classified as artificially obstructed. Hydropower stations reduce successful silver eel emigration, particularly for larger females.

**Genetics.** Genetic evidence supports a single spawning stock in the ocean. Preliminary work suggests three possible sub-groups: Northern European (Icelandic stocks), Western European (Mediterranean, western European and Baltic stocks), and Southern European (Moroccan stocks).

## Key Findings

### 1. Recruitment and yield trends

- Declining yields are reported across Europe for both capture fisheries and scientific indices.
- Glass eel recruitment remains at very low levels; the River Erne is the only recorded exception.
- Downward trends in recruitment and catch are "beyond any reasonable doubt," but the lack of age and size data severely limits the series.

### 2. Impact of fisheries on escapement

- Estimates of fishing mortality are few and inconsistent. The large, geographically discrete fisheries contribute only about 5% of the total European catch.
- The [[Baltic Sea]] silver eel fishery is relatively efficient and takes larger, more fecund females. Concerns exist about whether stocked eel can migrate successfully out of the Baltic.
- A conservative estimate of silver eel escapement from European waters is printed as 5951 (unit unclear in the scan). It is derived from a stated yield of 1,929 t and an assumed 12% escapement, a derivation the report itself treats as crude.
- The [[Ijsselmeer]] yellow eel fishery has an estimated fishing mortality of F = 1.0. Escapement is low for males and practically nil for females.
- In [[Lough Neagh]], yellow eel exploitation is high and escapement of silver eel is thought to be low.
- Italian lagoon fisheries are mostly closed and stocked systems, where escapement is generally zero.
- In France, a decline in glass eel recruitment on the [[River Loire]] was followed 8–15 years later by a reduction in yellow eel populations, consistent with fewer silver eel catches later.
- On the obstructed [[River Vilaine]], a reduction in glass eel fishing mortality (from 99.6% to 96%) combined with a fish ladder may have increased yellow eel density about 2.8-fold (Briand, 1999).

**Conclusion:** The overall effect of fisheries on European escapement cannot be assessed with confidence. Fisheries on all life stages can impact spawner escapement locally, and some can preclude escapement from a catchment entirely. Further controls on all stock components are therefore appropriate.

### 3. Escapement targets and reference points

- Three strategies were identified: input-output estimates (glass eel recruitment versus silver eel escapement), comparisons of exploited and unexploited populations, and escapement relative to habitat capacity (analogous to Atlantic salmon). Mortality models (F = M, %SPR) were judged not yet applicable because baseline data are lacking.
- **Provisional conservation limits:** The group recommended holding exploitation at current levels (based on average catches or effort over the last five years) as a minimum. It also advised that, under the [[Precautionary Approach]], a provisional reduction in catch and/or effort on all life stages be considered, initially by an arbitrary percentage (figure illegible in the scan). The group stressed that the absence of data cannot justify delaying action.
- **Management target levels:** Described as idealistic and not achievable in the short term, but worth consideration.
- **Management units:** Genetic and biological characteristics do not provide a basis for natural units. The group recommended jurisdictional units (countries, regions), with differentiation by life stage and catchment.

### 4. Management actions

- Options include prohibition of fishing (commercial glass eel fishing is banned in countries with low elver recruitment, including Sweden, Denmark, Germany, Ireland, UK, the Netherlands and Belgium), total allowable catches and quotas, gear controls, minimum landing sizes (which would favour larger female pre-spawners), closed seasons, closed areas, and licensing of fishermen and dealers.
- Quotas were judged probably appropriate given the difficulty of monitoring scattered inland fisheries. Rapid management responses require legislative frameworks that also cover recreational fishing.
- Recovery plans under the precautionary approach should aim to restore stocks within roughly 1–2 generation times, up to about 20 years.

### 5. Re-stocking

- Re-stocking is considered a cost-effective way to restore yields, especially in obstructed catchments. Re-stocking of inland waters has dropped to about 33 t per year, roughly 6% of the total glass eel catch.
- Re-stocking may exceed natural recruitment in all but glass-eel exporting countries.
- Experiments show reduced growth at high density, and a shift toward males has been observed after re-stocking. Transfers risk spreading diseases and parasites.
- Silver eel from re-stocked French glass eel showed conflicting homing results in the Baltic.
- Yield per re-stocked recruit ranges from 20 to 90 g in the Baltic; figures for Lough Neagh and Lough Erne are substantially lower. The Lough Erne fishery depends entirely on re-stocking.
- Intra-catchment re-stocking is preferred to minimise disease transfer risk.

### 6. Monitoring and research coordination

- The Working Group concluded that current monitoring is fragmented, ad hoc and dependent on individual interest. Continued time series alone will not show whether management measures work.
- A minimum permanent monitoring system across all eel-producing countries is regarded as essential. A small task force should develop a detailed protocol.
- Recommended monitoring for major fisheries covers total catch, effort by gear type, length and weight, age and life stage, sex distribution, and contaminant and parasite burdens. For whole catchments, observations of migration and frequent length sampling are also recommended.
- International research coordination should involve management bodies and industry. The [[North Atlantic Salmon Conservation Organization]] (NASCO) was cited as a possible model.

### 7. Research priorities (Appendix C)

- Quantifying how growing and fished stocks contribute to viable spawner production.
- Developing methods to assess silver eel escapement and understanding triggers for silvering and migration.
- Investigating the oceanic and leptocephalus phases, including the effects of climate change and oceanographic events.
- Clarifying estuarine mortality and carrying capacities of continental habitats.
- Confirming stock integrity through genetic assessment and developing biological databanks for all life stages.
- Establishing standard definitions for "silver eel" and "glass eel" and intercalibrating methods across institutions.

### 8. Recommendations (summary)

1. Re-stocking of glass eel in obstructed waters should preferably use seed material from the same river system, and escapement should be enhanced.
2. An ad hoc study group should be convened to set monitoring standards.
3. Research should be coordinated by an international research management body involving national and international managers and fishing industries.
4. Management should be based on monitoring of natural recruitment, spawning escapement, and national stock characteristics, supported by uniform national standards for data collection.
