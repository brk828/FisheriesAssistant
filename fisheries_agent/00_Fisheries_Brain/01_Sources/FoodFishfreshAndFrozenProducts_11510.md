# Food Fish Situation and Outlook - November 1971

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-economics #groundfish #commercial-fisheries #market-research

## Executive Summary

This quarterly market research report from the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] analyzes the U.S. commercial fisheries market during January-September 1971, with particular focus on groundfish products (blocks, sticks, portions, and fillets), [[Pacific Halibut]], and [[Salmon]]. The document examines supply chains, pricing dynamics, consumption patterns, and the impact of President Nixon's [[New Economic Policy]] (wage-price freeze) on the fishing industry. Major species covered include [[Cod]], [[Haddock]], [[Flounder]], [[Pollock]], and [[Ocean Perch]]. The report reveals significant market disruptions: supplies of fish blocks increased 10% over 1970 despite lower beginning inventories, driven by record imports in July (47.1 million pounds). Wholesale prices rose substantially for most groundfish products, with cod showing the largest increases. The analysis incorporates international supply dynamics from [[Canada]], [[Iceland]], [[Norway]], and [[Denmark]], and projects fourth-quarter 1971 and early 1972 market conditions under evolving economic controls.

## Background & Description

The report covers the U.S. commercial fisheries market from multiple analytical perspectives: supply (domestic landings, imports, inventories), utilization (consumption, disappearance), and pricing (exvessel, wholesale, retail). The groundfish sector dominates the analysis, reflecting its economic importance and heavy reliance on imports. Fish blocks—frozen rectangular blocks of minced fish used to manufacture sticks and portions—serve as a key industrial input, with [[Cod]] traditionally representing the dominant species (though its share declined from 80% in 1970 to 67% in 1971).

The document distinguishes between product forms: blocks (raw material), fillets (consumer-ready cuts), sticks (breaded cooked products in 8-oz packages), and portions (raw breaded 2-4 oz servings). Geographic coverage spans [[New England]] ports (particularly [[Boston]] and [[Gloucester]]), [[Pacific]] fisheries ([[Alaska]], [[Seattle]]), and major foreign suppliers. The [[International Commission for the Northwest Atlantic Fisheries]] ([[ICNAF]]) quota system significantly influences supply, particularly for [[Yellowtail Flounder]] and [[Haddock]].

Market dynamics reflect seasonal patterns: block imports and production typically peak in March and October, with July traditionally showing low activity. The 1971 season disrupted these patterns dramatically, with July imports reaching a record 47.1 million pounds—33% above the previous April peak.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report addresses fisheries management through quota systems administered by international bodies. For [[Yellowtail Flounder]], [[ICNAF]] established 13,000 metric tons for western grounds (west of 69° longitude) and 16,000 metric tons for eastern grounds, with 58% and 83% respectively reached by October 16, 1971. Biologists expected eastern grounds closure in early November. The 1972 western quota was reduced to 10,000 metric tons, reflecting conservation concerns.

[[Haddock]] faced severe restrictions: the 1971 annual quota of 30,000 metric tons would be halved to 15,000 metric tons in 1972 "in an attempt to preserve the remaining stock." U.S. landings through September were 6 million pounds (fillet weight), 23% below 1970, and the quota was not expected to be reached. [[NMFS]] biologists attributed declining [[Cod]] landings in [[Newfoundland]] to "competition from foreign fishing in both the inshore and offshore fishery."

The [[International Pacific Halibut Commission]] set a 50-million-pound quota for 1971, but combined U.S. and Canadian landings fell considerably short at approximately 26.4 million pounds. Lower catch per unit effort reflected "less abundance" of [[Pacific Halibut]]. [[Salmon]] troll fisheries showed improved performance: king salmon landings were 2.7 million pounds (33% above 1970) and silver salmon 4.5 million pounds (versus 3.4 million in 1970).

Environmental factors influencing markets included seasonal fishing patterns, stock abundance trends, and the geographic distribution of fishing effort. The transfer of halibut fishermen to the [[Albacore Tuna]] fishery (attracted by record $630/ton prices) exemplified economic-ecological interactions affecting harvest patterns.

## Key Findings

**Supply and Inventory Dynamics:**
- Total fish block supplies January-September 1971: 284.7 million pounds, 10% above 1970's 258.7 million pounds
- Block imports peaked at record 47.1 million pounds in July 1971, driven by: (1) rising prices attracting supplies, (2) processors guarding against shortages, (3) anticipation of further price increases, (4) concern about longshoremen's strike
- October 1, 1971 block inventories reached record 74.3 million pounds (sufficient for 3.5 months' use), 26 million pounds above year-ago levels
- [[Cod]] blocks maintained 164.5 million pounds of imports (essentially unchanged from 1970's 162 million), but declined from 80% to 67% of total block market share
- [[Flounder]] fillet supplies remained unchanged at 84.8 million pounds despite 13% lower domestic landings and 2% lower imports, sustained by record-high January inventories

**Production and Consumption Patterns:**
- Fish stick and portion production January-September: 252 million pounds, 4 million pounds below 1970
- Portions increased to 70% of total stick/portion production (versus 65% in 1968-70), reflecting growing institutional demand from drive-in restaurants and fish-and-chips franchises
- Block disappearance January-September: 210.4 million pounds, essentially even with 1970 despite supply fluctuations
- [[Flounder]] consumption through September: 74 million pounds, 6% above 1970, despite prices increasing since May
- [[Cod]] fillet consumption: 77.5 million pounds, 17% below 1970, primarily due to lower supplies
- [[Haddock]] consumption: 26.7 million pounds, 22% below 1970, reflecting inventory-building rather than sales
- [[Ocean Perch]] consumption: 65.2 million pounds, 10% above 1970, benefiting from abundant supplies and stable prices

**Price Movements:**
- [[Cod]] blocks: stable at 40¢/lb December-March, climbing to 45¢/lb by October (table 6)
- [[Cod]] portions (raw breaded, 2-4 oz): increased from 49.5¢/lb (January) to 57.0¢/lb (October)
- [[Cod]] sticks (breaded cooked, 8-oz packages): rose from 66.3¢/lb to 72.9¢/lb
- [[Haddock]] blocks: 41.5¢/lb (January) to 46.8¢/lb (September)
- [[Flounder]] exvessel (yellowtail): averaged 16¢/lb through September, 3% below 1970; August spike to 22.1¢/lb as quota approached
- [[Flounder]] wholesale (Canadian 5-lb frozen): below 1970 prices January-April, then exceeded 1970 levels May-September
- [[Ocean Perch]] wholesale: remained comparable to 1970 levels; retail prices averaged 10¢/lb higher than 1970
- [[Pacific Halibut]] wholesale: 2-5¢/lb lower than 1970, ranging 53-55¢/lb versus 1970's 55-57¢/lb
- [[Salmon]] (troll-caught): exvessel prices for kings 15-16¢/lb lower than 1970; silvers 10-24¢/lb lower

**International Supply Situation:**
- [[Canada]]: groundfish landings declined through August 1971 for all major species; cod down 17% to 304 million pounds (drawn weight); flounder down 16%; block/slab freezings increased to 79.1 million pounds (versus 63.3 million in 1970) despite lower landings
- Canadian inventories worked down to levels "substantially below the low holdings of last year" by fall 1971
- [[Iceland]]: cod landings January-August 345,000 metric tons versus 390,000 tons in 1970; announced 50-mile fishing limit effective September 1972
- [[Norway]]: spawning cod catches 153,000 metric tons in spring 1971, 50% above 1970; gained 9.2% of U.S. fillet market and 21% of block market
- [[Denmark]], [[Greenland]], [[Faroe Islands]]: Denmark doubled block exports to U.S. (25.2 million pounds through August); Faroe exports quadrupled over 2 years due to modern factory trawlers

**Economic Policy Impacts (Phase I - August 15-November 13, 1971):**
- Wage-price freeze critically affected seasonal fisheries (king crab, oysters, canned salmon, anchovies) whose seasons began during freeze period, preventing reflection of new season's higher costs
- Raw fishery products exempted "until shucked, shelled, skinned, or scaled," but exemption potentially negated if processors (covered by freeze) offered harvesters same or lower prices
- Import surcharge effects limited: cod blocks increased only 1¢/lb (2% of 45¢ price); Canadian fish exports estimated 3% price increase
- Currency revaluation: Japan's yen revalued 7% (Japan represented 24% of U.S. fishery imports—363 million pounds in 1970); Norway's 4% revaluation
- Groundfish import patterns unchanged August 15-November 1: price increases continued at same magnitude as pre-freeze months; imports remained high in August-September
- Domestic processors/distributors with large inventories accumulated storage costs while unable to raise prices, tying up capital

**Fourth Quarter 1971 and 1972 Outlook:**
- Block imports projected 60-65 million pounds (5-10 million above 1970 Q4, but 33% below 1971 Q3), due to: depleted foreign stocks, potential currency revaluation effects, uncertainty about Phase II price controls
- Block disappearance estimated 85-90 million pounds versus 78.6 million in Q4 1970
- January 1, 1972 block inventories projected ~50 million pounds (7 million above 1971)
- Stick/portion production projected 95-100 million pounds; consumption ~100 million pounds if prices hold at October levels
- [[Flounder]]: Q4 landings estimated 7 million pounds (30% below 1970) due to eastern yellowtail grounds closure; imports ~13 million pounds; consumption ~22 million pounds
- [[Cod]]: Q4 landings ~3.5 million pounds; imports ~16 million pounds; consumption ~25 million pounds dependent on inventory release rates
- [[Haddock]]: Q4 landings ~1 million pounds; consumption ~8.5 million pounds as high inventories worked down; potential seller's market if inventories decline rapidly
- [[Ocean Perch]]: Q4 landings ~5 million pounds (seasonally heavy); imports ~13 million pounds; consumption 14-15 million pounds
- [[Halibut]]: supplies and consumption slightly lower than Q4 1970 due to October 1 inventories of 17.3 million pounds (versus 21.7 million in 1970)
- [[Salmon]]: abundant frozen supplies expected; strong consumption anticipated with lower wholesale prices

**Phase II Considerations:**
- Pay Board announced 5.5% maximum annual wage growth rate (November 9)
- Three-category reporting structure proposed: Category 1 (500-1,000 largest companies, possibly those >$100 million revenue) must request permission before price/wage increases; Category 2 (6,000-10,000 companies) report regularly but need not delay increases; Category 3 (remaining ~10 million companies) follow criteria, subject to spot checks
- Domestic fish processors generally not in Category 1, but subsidiaries of large corporations (representing >50% of stick/portion value in 1970) may face de facto freezes pending review
- Price increase goal: 2-3% annually, requiring wage increases closely related to productivity; case-by-case approach likely for fishing industry given variability in mechanization, plant size, product lines
- Liberalized "raw seafood" definition during post-freeze period permits shelling, shucking, skinning, scaling, eviscerating, removing heads, and icing
