# Native Fish Losses on the Virgin River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #plagopteridae #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary
This 1988 letter from an Arizona State University zoologist to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service documents a significant unintended ecological event on the [[Virgin River]] system (Utah, Arizona, Nevada). A planned ichthyocide (rotenone) treatment intended to eradicate the non-native [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) from a targeted Utah reach—undertaken to benefit the endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*)—"got out of control." The toxin spread far beyond the intended treatment zone into downstream reaches in Arizona and Nevada, resulting in substantial, though at the time unquantified, mortality of native fish species, potentially affecting up to 80% or more of the remaining natural range of the [[Woundfin]] and the candidate [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuca*). The author frames this as both a conservation crisis and a rare scientific opportunity to study recolonization, community ecology, and population genetics following a large-scale, unplanned extirpation event.

## Background & Description
The source document is a management/policy communication and does not provide morphological descriptions, meristic data, or physical characteristics of the species involved. Taxonomic identity is noted only via scientific nomenclature:
- **[[Woundfin]]** — *Plagopterus argentissimus*, federally endangered status referenced.
- **[[Virgin River Chub]]** — *Gila robusta seminuca*, described as a subspecies and federal "candidate" species (under consideration for listing) at time of writing.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** — *Notropis lutrensis*, non-native cyprinid, the target of the eradication effort.
- Other "possibly endemic" native fishes of the Virgin River are referenced but not named or described.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- The [[Virgin River]] is characterized as supporting a native fish assemblage with high endemism, including habitat specialists adapted to the river's naturally harsh desert-stream conditions (implied by later references to unique life-history and conservation status, though specific abiotic data are not detailed in this excerpt).
- The [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Chub]] occupy overlapping ranges within the same river segment, making them both vulnerable to a single disturbance event.
- The **[[Red Shiner]]** is identified as an invasive competitor/threat to native Virgin River fishes, motivating the original eradication effort — a classic case of non-native species management via [[Ichthyocide|piscicide (rotenone) application]].
- The unintended downstream spread of rotenone illustrates the interconnectedness of lotic (flowing river) systems, where chemical treatments cannot be easily contained to a single reach, impacting community ecology far beyond the intended management boundary.

## Key Findings
- An agreed-upon, targeted rotenone treatment in a Utah segment of the Virgin River — intended to remove non-native [[Red Shiner]] to benefit the endangered [[Woundfin]] — inadvertently spread downstream into Arizona and Nevada river segments.
- This "out of control" spread caused substantial (unquantified at time of writing) native fish mortality outside the intended treatment zone.
- Potentially **80% or more of the remaining natural range** of both the endangered [[Woundfin]] and the candidate [[Virgin River Chub]] may have been affected by the kill.
- Other native, possibly endemic, Virgin River fishes were also reportedly "decimated," though species-level data were not yet available.
- The author warns of a dual risk/opportunity framing:
  - **Risk**: Public and professional backlash against the use of [[Ichthyocide|ichthyocides]] as a fisheries management tool, threatening future authorization of such methods.
  - **Opportunity**: The event could serve as an unplanned, large-scale natural experiment to empirically study previously theoretical questions in fisheries management, including:
    - Patterns of repopulation and recolonization following mass mortality.
    - Changes in community ecology post-disturbance.
    - Genetics of repopulating, recolonizing, or reintroduced fish stocks.
- Arizona State University (ASU) and University of Nevada, Las Vegas (UNLV) researchers were reportedly beginning to formulate a joint monitoring/research plan to track ecological and genetic recolonization dynamics in the affected reach, with anticipated requests for USFWS support.
- The letter was circulated to multiple USFWS regional offices (Denver, Portland, Phoenix, Salt Lake City), indicating a coordinated multi-jurisdictional response to the incident.
