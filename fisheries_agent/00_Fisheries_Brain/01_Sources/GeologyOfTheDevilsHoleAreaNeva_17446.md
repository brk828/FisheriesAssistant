# Geology and Hydrogeology of the Devils Hole Area, Nevada

#hydrogeology #karst #geology #carbonate-aquifer

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey open-file report by W.J. Carr maps the local geologic structure around [[Devils Hole]], a 50-foot-deep opening to the water table in southwestern Nevada, less than 1 mile up-gradient from the spring discharge of [[Ash Meadows]]. The study covers about 3 square miles of Paleozoic carbonate ridges and links the structure to ground-water flow in a regional carbonate aquifer. The document contains no fish, invertebrate, or other biological survey data. Its ecological relevance lies in the aquifer, spring, and cave-like habitat that supports the Ash Meadows discharge system.

The main conclusion is that northeast-striking faults and fractures, which are minor structurally, are the most hydrologically important features. They control Devils Hole, most collapse depressions, and many calcite veins, and they likely channel ground-water flow toward [[Crystal Pool]]. Low-angle faults beneath the ridges may help open these northeast-striking fractures.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The mapping covers the [[Amargosa Desert]] ridges, composed mainly of the Cambrian [[Bonanza King Formation]]. This is a carbonate unit roughly 5,000 ft thick regionally, of which about 1,500 ft is exposed in the mapped area.
- **Field work:** Geologic mapping was done in late 1985 and early 1986 at several scales. The report includes three partly overlapping geologic maps and a preliminary tape-and-compass sketch map of the Devils Hole area.
- **Regional framework:** The bedrock forms the base of the [[Lower Carbonate Aquifer]], the main regional ground-water unit. Springs of the Ash Meadows ground-water basin lie along a steep contact between carbonate rock and impermeable basin-fill sediments. This contact likely reflects a high-angle north- to northwest-striking fault zone.
- **Stratigraphic units:**
  - Banded Mountain Member of the Bonanza King Formation, mainly limestone with subordinate dolomite and minor clastic beds in the lowest unit.
  - A distinctive marker, the [[Curly Limestone Bed]], an 8 to 18 inch cuspate limestone layer in the upper part of unit c.
  - Fine-grained, poorly indurated "[[Lake Beds]]" of latest Tertiary age, lying below about 2,400 ft elevation.
  - Pleistocene and Pliocene alluvium and colluvium, and Holocene alluvium.
- **Units:** The report uses feet and miles. Elevations are referenced to the National Geodetic Vertical Datum of 1929.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquifer and spring habitat.** The Devils Hole area lies within a highly transmissive carbonate aquifer. Its discharge supports the springs of Ash Meadows. Several features show ground-water-related habitat and flow:

- **Devils Hole:** Roughly 75 by 25 ft in plan and 50 ft deep above the water table. It extends more than 300 ft below the water table and connects to a network of passages extending at least 300 ft northeast. Average passage width is about 6 ft. An uncollapsed opening above the water table, called "[[Brown's Room]]", sits within this system. Passage walls are largely controlled by fault planes.
- **Water temperature:** School Spring is 34.5 °C, which the report notes is 1.5 °C higher than the water in Devils Hole. Ground water in the subsurface is described as warm, about 32 °C.
- **Collapse depressions:** Twelve collapse features, including Devils Hole, were identified. Most are small, holes about 1 ft across up to larger openings. All but three occur within 600 ft of Devils Hole. Most are associated with northeast-striking faults and fractures.
- **Breathing openings:** Two collapse fractures on Point of Rocks ridge (one near Devils Hole, one at about 2,940 ft elevation, roughly 600 ft above the regional water table) were damp and exhaled warm moist air in winter 1985–86. Moss grew around them and went dormant in June. Winter convection through the openings is attributed to temperature contrast between warm subsurface air and cooler surface air.

**Geologic and hydrologic controls.**

- Calcite veins, both older recrystallized types and younger laminated types, are mapped along faults. Their elevations mark former spring and water-table positions.
- The "lake beds" overlie Tertiary rocks. Their age of 2 to 4 million years is mostly Pliocene, based on dated ash beds.
- Several Pleistocene calcite veins and laminated deposits are being studied as records of Quaternary paleohydrology and climate.

## Key Findings

**1. Structural grain and hydrologic importance.**
The dominant structural trend is northwest, with folds and faults that parallel the Amargosa ridges. However, the northeast-striking faults and fractures are the most permeable features. They control Devils Hole, most collapse depressions, and many calcite veins.

**2. Burro Trail Canyon fault zone.**
The one major northeast-striking zone in the area strikes about N 55°–60° E. It has more than 100 ft of cumulative displacement, down to the northwest. If projected southwest, it would pass through School Spring to Crystal Pool. This is the proposed major channelway for ground-water flow.

**3. Devils Hole fault.**
The main fault exposed in Devils Hole strikes N 40°–50° E and dips 80°–85° SE. It has about 2 ft of stratigraphic displacement, making it a high-angle reverse fault.

**4. Northwest-striking faults.**
These dip about 70° on average, and their throw is typically 50 to 100 ft. A few have up to 200 ft of offset.

**5. Low-angle faults.**
A low-angle fault dipping 10° to 25° NE lies within the shaly lower unit (a) of the Banded Mountain Member on Point of Rocks ridge. Its upper plate is highly fractured and brecciated. The report suggests these faults may help open northeast-striking fractures by allowing pull-apart in the direction of minimum principal stress. The same structure may extend beneath Devils Hole, following Naff (1973).

**6. Stress model.**
Following Carr (1974), the minimum principal stress has been oriented about N 50° W over the last 5 million years or so. This favors opening of northeast-striking faults and closure of northwest-striking ones. The moist-breathing openings and sinkhole relations are cited as evidence that these stresses are still active.

**7. Crystal Pool carbon-14 anomaly.**
Carbon-14 content is about 11.1 percent modern at [[Crystal Pool]], roughly five times the value of about 2.4 percent modern at other springs analyzed. This indicates a preferred, rapid flow path from recharge areas to the northeast. The report notes that a longer highly permeable channel than the mapped Burro Trail Canyon zone appears required. This is described as an unsolved problem.

**8. Age of structural events.**
Most structural displacements in the Devils Hole area are judged to predate about 4 million years ago. The "lake beds" are only slightly disturbed, so the Tertiary folded rocks of Ash Meadows, possibly middle Miocene or older, are critical to dating. The age of these rocks remains unresolved.

**9. Lower aquitard depth.**
The top of the regional lower clastic aquitard lies about 3,500 ft below the water table in the Devils Hole area.

**10. Recommendation for further work.**
The report calls for further mapping of the Amargosa ridges to clarify folds and faults. It also calls for detailed work on whether carbonate composition controls cavity development, and for dating of Tertiary rocks in Ash Meadows.
