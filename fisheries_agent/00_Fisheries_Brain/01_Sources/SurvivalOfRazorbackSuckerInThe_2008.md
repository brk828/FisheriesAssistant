# Survival of Razorback Sucker in the Lower Colorado River: Post-Stocking Assessment 2006–2008

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered-species #stocking-assessment #predation-ecology

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is an endangered catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. This study assessed post-stocking survival of subadult and adult fish (mean TL 39.0 cm, range 28.3–65.0 cm) released into the [[Lower Colorado River]] between [[Parker Dam]] (RM 192) and [[Laguna Dam]] (RM 43.5) from January 2006 to April 2008. Of 2,329 processed captures, sex ratios were 777 male (mean TL 38.8 cm, range 29.0–58.5), 468 female (mean TL 45.4 cm, range 34.2–65.0), 836 juvenile (mean TL 34.9 cm, range 28.3–39.9), and 200 unknown. Growth was approximately linear for three years post-stocking; Von Bertalanffy growth parameters were L∞ = 108.2 cm, K = 0.0818, with estimated annual growth of 6 cm (year 1) and 5 cm (year 2). Females grew faster than males (5.9 vs. 2.0 cm/year extrapolated). Spawning behavior was observed and 40 larvae were collected (March 2007–2008 in [[A-10 Backwater]]), confirming reproduction. Fish generally exhibited spawning readiness: 74.3–100% of males were tuberculate across zones, though only 8.5% of females were ripe. Most fish (77.8%) retained hatchery wire tags. Time-at-large for PIT-tagged fish ranged 2–913 days, but median was 90 days; 55.3% were recaptured within 100 days of release. Only 15 fish stocked in A-7 were captured after ≥150 days at large, representing <0.01% of releases.

**Habitat Associations**: Razorback sucker were stocked into three primary zones: [[Parker Strip]] (RM 192–169.5, 22.5 km of swift-flowing discharge with eddies and lagoons), [[A-7 Backwater]] zone (RM 124–109, 24.4 km including four named backwaters with open connections to main channel), and [[A-10 Backwater]] (RM 114, 10.1 ha semi-isolated via culvert pipes). Fish exhibited high site fidelity: 83% of PIT-tagged fish were recaptured at release location. A-10 accumulated stocked fish (CPUE increased annually), whereas A-7 showed rapid dispersal and consistently low CPUE. Water physico-chemistry (temperature 8.2–32.7°C, dissolved oxygen variable but generally adequate) was not identified as a primary mortality factor, though localized summer conditions approached tolerance limits. Backwaters provided hospitable conditions for fish communities; parasitism (anchorworm *Lernaea cyprinacea* 28% in A-10, white grub *Posthodiplostoum* sp. 2.1% overall) was not correlated with handling stress or considered detrimental to survival.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Telemetry studies (sonic and radio tags) revealed contrasting dispersal patterns. In A-7 (open connection to river), 50% of 12 sonic-tagged fish dispersed within 50 days; dispersed fish were never relocated despite extensive surveys. All 12 fish in A-7 died or disappeared within 70 days. In contrast, no dispersal occurred from semi-isolated A-10; all 12 fish remained within the backwater, but only 2 survived 200 days. Radio-tagged mature fish (n=12, >50 cm TL) released in A-7 and C-7 (December 2006–February 2008) showed limited movement; three moved between backwaters within the A-7 zone, but most remained in A-7 Upper until tags expired (April 2008). No spawning aggregations were located outside regularly sampled areas. Peterson population estimates for A-7 zone ranged 50–84 fish (February–April 2007); A-10 estimates were 2,719 (95% CI 1,502–5,438, February–March 2007) and 6,054 (95% CI 3,145–12,746, January–March 2008). Remote PIT scanning in A-10 (January 2008) detected 536 individual fish over four nights (1–86 scans/hour, mean 13.3).

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Total catch (24,602 fish, 21 species) was dominated by [[Bluegill Sunfish]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*, 21.7%), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*, 11.84%), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*, 19.47%). Razorback sucker comprised 9.9% of catch. Evidence of piscine predation was observed on 4.2% of captures, highest in A-7 (12.6%) and Parker Strip (15.8%). Bite marks implicated [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) as primary predator; large specimens (mean 13.1 kg, range 4.1–22.7; mean TL 96.0 cm, range 70.5–126.0) were rare but capable of significant impact. Largemouth bass population in A-10 was estimated at 459 (95% CI 205–1,147, March–April 2007); mean TL 26.5 cm (range 12.3–59.2). [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) was more common in flowing habitats (Parker Strip) than backwaters. Partially digested razorback sucker mortalities (head digested, post-nuchal body intact) suggested incomplete predation attempts by gape-limited predators. [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) stocking (6,425 fish, 2006–2007) resulted in 177 individual captures (0.7% of total catch); mean TL 33.4 cm (range 27.0–41.0), 93.9% retained wire tags, only 27 had PIT tags.

**Environmental Drivers**: Depth use shifted post-stocking: >50% of fish occupied upper 2/3 of water column for 44 days post-release, declining to 1.7% probability at surface by 100 days. Probability of demersal (substrate) capture increased from <50% to >75% over 100 days, indicating behavioral transition from pelagic to benthic habits. This shift may reduce avian predation vulnerability but occurs slowly. Surface feeding in hatcheries was hypothesized to cause surface orientation; experimental subsurface feeding trials (January and October 2007 releases) showed no significant treatment effect on survivorship in mark-recapture models. Temperature acclimation at stocking was inadequate in numerous events; video documented 60 fish breaching surface in 103 seconds post-release, indicating thermal shock stress response.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: More than 2.5 million razorback sucker have been stocked into the lower Colorado River over three decades (1974–2008), but <1% have ever been recaptured. Of 12,405 PIT-tagged fish released since 1993, only 1,014 (8%) were captured post-stocking, and only 62 (6% of recaptures) were captured twice. Long-term survival (>1.5 years at large) was extremely rare despite continued stocking of >80,000 subadult fish since 2000. Stocking has not established persistent populations. A-10 showed highest accumulation (CPUE increased annually 2006–2008: electrofishing 22.5 fish/1,000 sec, trammel netting 5.1 fish/net-hour), but long-term survival remained nil. A-7 showed rapid post-release decline; cumulative CPUE remained consistently low (electrofishing 0.76 fish/1,000 sec, trammel netting 0.19 fish/net-hour). Parker Strip CPUE was 19.3 fish/1,000 sec (electrofishing), <1 fish/net-hour (netting). Revised recovery goals call for stable populations of 5,000 individuals; present stocking locations do not support this objective.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: **Avian predation** was the most significant mortality factor. Evidence of avian predation (AVP: scratches, punctures, lacerations from beaks/talons) increased annually 2003–2008, reaching 23% of cumulative catch (675 of 2,970 records). Nearly half of fish with AVP were at large ≤43 days. Seasonal variation was significant: winter peaks exceeded 30%, with 60% of January catch in A-10 Upper showing AVP. A-10 had significantly more AVP than A-7 or other locations (X²=85.92, df=1, p<0.001); A-10 Upper exceeded A-10 Lower (X²=11.36, df=1, p<0.001). Survivorship modeling indicated monthly survival of 83.8% (95% CI 77.7–88.4) for 35 cm fish in non-summer months, 80.8% (95% CI 73.1–86.8) in summer (constant, not size-dependent). Annual survivorship was 9.4% for 35 cm fish, 27.7% for 40 cm fish, asymptoting at ~28% due to constant summer mortality. Smaller fish were under-represented in catch, suggesting size-related vulnerability. **Piscine predation** was ubiquitous; flathead catfish, largemouth bass, and striped bass populations, though low in certain localities, were sufficient to impact stocked populations. Handling stress and PIT-tagging did not increase parasite infection rates (Yates-corrected X²=45.2, df=1, p<0.001).

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Continued stocking under current practices is **not recommended**; it maintains only low abundance of newly-released fish without achieving population augmentation goals. Stocking should occur **only into predator-free habitats**. Present and proposed nonnative fish-free habitats exist but do not mitigate avian predation; **control measures to discourage avian piscivore residency are strongly advocated**. If stocking is mandated, implement: (1) **Pre-release predator avoidance training**: fish learn avoidance by observing conspecific predation; brief exposure may enhance short-term survival. (2) **Natural foraging regime in hatcheries**: subsurface feeding to encourage demersal behavior and reduce surface orientation, potentially accelerating behavioral transition and reducing avian vulnerability. (3) **Adequate temperature acclimation**: perform in transport tanks to minimize handling; backwater surface temperatures often exceed transport temperatures, causing thermal shock and stress. (4) **Flow conditioning for river stockings**: pre-release rearing in flowing water may enhance survival in swift currents (e.g., Parker Strip). (5) **Full PIT-tagging of all releases** (razorback sucker and bonytail): wire tags have high loss rates (25.8% undetectable after 18 months) and lack individual identification; PIT tags enable growth/dispersal tracking and remote scanning (recapture rate increased four-fold to 39% with remote scanning vs. <1–9% with electrofishing/netting alone). Parker Strip maintains high predator abundances (flathead catfish, striped bass, largemouth bass) but lower avian predation (likely curtailed by recreational boating). A-7 has notable piscine predators and ample avian habitat. A-10 has few large piscine predators but consistent avian presence. No lower river location is absent of both threats.
