## Executive Summary
This 1965 note by Don A. LaFaunce (California Department of Fish and Game) documents exceptionally long-term retention of external identification tags—**staple** and **disk-dangler** tags—on four freshwater fish species in California. Tag returns spanning **8 to over 10 years** were recorded for [[White Catfish]], [[Brown Bullhead]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]] from three water bodies: the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta, Clear Lake, and Sutter Bypass. The findings validate the durability of cellulose nitrate disk tags secured with tantalum wire ([[Kimsey Tagging Method]]) and provide indirect evidence of the longevity and low exploitation rates of California catfish populations.

## Background & Description
- **Tag Design**: Disk-dangler tags consisted of **cellulose nitrate disks** secured with **0.020-inch tantalum wire**; staple tags were a simpler mechanical attachment.
- **Physical evidence of aging**: Recovered tags showed "considerable evidence of long exposure to the elements," supporting authenticity of long-duration returns rather than tag misapplication or data errors.
- **Fork lengths at tagging** (used as a proxy for age/size class):
  - White catfish (Delta): 7.0–7.1 inches
  - White catfish (Clear Lake): 9.4–10.0 inches
  - Largemouth bass (Clear Lake): 9.0–11.2 inches
- Aged specimens of white catfish recovered at capture were estimated at **Age XIV–XV**, among the oldest documented for this species in California waters.
- The recovered largemouth bass was estimated at **Age II at tagging** and **Age XI at recapture**, described as "an exceptionally old bass for California."

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat contexts**:
  - [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]]: brackish/freshwater tidal delta system supporting [[White Catfish]] populations.
  - [[Clear Lake (California)]]: a lightly exploited warmwater lake fishery supporting [[White Catfish]], [[Brown Bullhead]], and [[Largemouth Bass]].
  - [[Sutter Bypass]]: a flood-control channel of the Sacramento Valley supporting [[Channel Catfish]].
- **Exploitation pressure**: All study fisheries, especially Clear Lake, were characterized as **lightly exploited**, a key factor permitting fish to reach advanced ages and retain tags over multi-year periods.
- **Life history implications**: Catfish species (Ictaluridae) in these systems were found to **live longer than most other warmwater fish** in California, a notable ecological distinction supporting the value of long-term tagging studies for age and mortality estimation.

## Key Findings
- **Maximum tag retention duration**: 10 years, 8 months — recorded in two staple-tagged [[White Catfish]] from Clear Lake (tagged Aug. 1952, recovered May 1963).
- **Longest largemouth bass retention**: 9 years, 6 months (tagged Aug. 27, 1953; recovered Mar. 23, 1963) — [[Largemouth Bass]] tag returns, unlike catfish, were not received every single year, a noted anomaly.
- **Species-specific retention records**:
  | Species | Tag Type | Area | Max. Time at Liberty |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | White Catfish | Staple | Clear Lake | 10 yr 8 mo |
  | White Catfish | Disk-dangler | Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta | 8 yr |
  | Brown Bullhead | Disk-dangler | Clear Lake | 8 yr |
  | Channel Catfish | Disk-dangler | Sutter Bypass | 8 yr 2 mo |
  | Largemouth Bass | Disk-dangler | Clear Lake | 9 yr 6 mo |
- **Validity criteria for long-term recoveries** were established based on: (i) timing coinciding with peak fishing seasons, (ii) physical evidence of prolonged environmental exposure on tags, (iii) consistent annual returns (except for bass), and (iv) low fishing exploitation rates at recovery sites.
- **Historical significance**: The white catfish tag-retention records were considered possibly the longest documented for any freshwater fish tagging study **in California, and potentially in the United States**, at the time of publication.
- **Management implication**: Supports the use of staple and disk-dangler tags (per [[Kimsey Tagging Method]]) as reliable, durable tools for long-term freshwater fish population studies, including mortality, movement, and growth research (cf. [[McCammon and LaFaunce 1961]], [[McCammon and Seeley 1961]]).
- Study conducted under **Dingell-Johnson Project California F-2-R**, "A Study of the Catfish Fishery of California," funded through Federal Aid to Fish Restoration.
