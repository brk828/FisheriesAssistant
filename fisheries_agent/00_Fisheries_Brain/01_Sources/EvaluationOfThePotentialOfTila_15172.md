# Evaluation of the Potential of Tilapia Populations to Support a Commercial Fishery in the Lower Colorado River Area

## Executive Summary

This 1985–1986 University of Arizona study (Project No. 5878) evaluated whether [[Tilapia Aurea]] (blue tilapia, family [[Cichlidae]]) populations in the Lower [[Colorado River]] area near Yuma, Arizona could support a viable commercial fishery. The primary concern raised by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] was potential tissue contamination of marketable-sized tilapia from agricultural biocides and heavy metals. Tissue samples from four sites (two agricultural drainage canals and two Gila River locations) showed contaminant concentrations largely below detectable limits, with no values exceeding EPA action levels or National Academy of Sciences/Engineers water quality guidelines. Beyond contamination, the study assessed practical constraints to commercial harvest, including incidental catch of valued sport fish, gear limitations, public use conflicts, and site suitability, ultimately ranking agricultural drainage canals as most suitable and the Colorado River as least suitable for commercial tilapia harvest.

## Background & Description

- **Target species**: *Tilapia aurea* (now generally classified as [[Oreochromis Aureus]]/blue tilapia), an introduced cichlid established in the Lower Colorado River drainage.
- **Study area**: Colorado and Gila Rivers and associated agricultural drainage canals near Yuma, Arizona, including:
  1. Main Drain at San Luis (~0.3 km from the international boundary)
  2. Main Outlet Drain ("Salt Canal"), near the Gila–Colorado confluence
  3. Gila River, 3.5–4.0 km above the confluence with the Colorado River
  4. Castle Dome Wash at its confluence with the Gila River
- **Sampling design**: Two composite tissue samples per site (each pooling flesh from 3–5 fish), following USFWS Resource Contaminant Assessment Program / EPA Priority Pollutant Monitoring Program protocols to buffer individual variability in contaminant burden.
- **Collection methods**: High salinity/dissolved solids precluded effective electrofishing; fixed-position nets (gill nets, block nets, trap/hoop nets) were impractical in canals due to high flow and heavy filamentous algae/debris load, which quickly fouled nets. The most effective technique was **seining with a large panel of gill net (minimum 2-inch stretch mesh)**.
- **Tissue analysis**: Conducted by Western Technologies Inc. (Phoenix, AZ) using EPA methods for arsenic (206.2), beryllium (210.1), cadmium (213.1), lead (239.2), selenium (270.3), and organochlorine pesticides/PCBs via EPA Method 8080 (DDE, DDD, DDT, toxaphene, aldrin, dieldrin, endrin, heptachlor epoxide). Mercury data were lost due to a digestion procedure error (zero spike recovery).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat contrasts**: The three aquatic systems studied differ substantially in physical character and fish community composition:
  - **Drainage canals**: High salinity, concrete-lined in places, high flow, heavy debris/filamentous algae load; most depauperate fish fauna and largely disconnected from natural river systems. Tilapia aggregated most densely where canal linings were cracked/heaved or near fallen structures providing refuge.
  - **[[Gila River]]**: Slower, warmer than the Colorado River, likely supporting greater tilapia abundance; also harbors sport fish such as [[Largemouth Bass]].
  - **[[Colorado River]]**: Supports the most diverse and valued sport fishery; highest public use; tilapia not found to be particularly abundant based on prior harvest records (Jacobson 1981, Marsh 1983).
- **Bycatch/incidental catch community** (Table 3, from drainage canals and Gila River sites): Flathead catfish, Largemouth bass (juvenile and adult), Black crappie, Common carp, and Tilapia. Non-target species outnumbered tilapia by more than 2:1 in Gila River samples.
- **Gear-habitat conflict**: Towed gear (seines, trawls) is impractical in the Colorado and Gila Rivers due to irregular bottoms and abundant structure (vegetation, debris, boulders); most effective in canals but conflicts with tilapia's preference for refuge structure in cracked/heaved canal linings.
- **Public use context**: High levels of recreational angling occur in the Colorado and Gila Rivers and even in some drainage canals, creating potential for user conflict and enforcement challenges if commercial gear differs from sport gear.

## Key Findings

### Tissue Contaminant Analysis (Table 2 / Appendix)
- **Metals**: Arsenic, beryllium, and cadmium were below detection limits (<1.0, <0.2, <1.0 mg/kg respectively) at all sites.
- **Lead**: Detected at low levels in most samples (range: <0.02–0.52 mg/kg); highest value (0.52 mg/kg) at Salt Canal sample 1.
- **Selenium**: Detected at Gila River sites (1.0–1.8 mg/kg) and below detection (<1.0 mg/kg) at drain/canal sites in most samples.
- **Organochlorine pesticides** (DDE, DDD, DDT, toxaphene, aldrin, dieldrin, endrin, heptachlor epoxide): All below detectable limits at every site.
- **Mercury**: No data — lost during tissue digestion (0% spike recovery).
- **Conclusion**: No contaminant exceeded EPA action levels or NAS/NAE (1973) water quality guideline recommendations. Tilapia generally show lower contaminant burdens than co-occurring fish species (per USFWS personal communication), so results should not be generalized to other species caught alongside tilapia.

### Fish Size Data (Table 1)
- Tilapia total lengths ranged approximately 17.2–37.0 cm; weights ranged approximately 103–673 g across all sites.
- Largest individuals were captured in the Main Drain at San Luis and Main Outlet Drain (Salt Canal); smallest were from the Gila River at Yuma site.

### Management Considerations
1. **Incidental catch risk**: Drainage canals have the most depauperate fish fauna (due to high salinity, lack of habitat, disconnection from rivers) and thus the lowest risk of incidental sport fish bycatch. Both the Gila and Colorado Rivers support diverse sport fisheries with high bycatch potential (>2 non-target fish per tilapia in Gila River samples).
2. **Gear selectivity**: Hoop/trap nets could allow safe release of bycatch but are untested for tilapia and impractical in high-flow/high-debris canal waters; may also require constant monitoring against tampering in heavily used river areas. Gill/trammel nets are ineffective in canals due to fouling but could work in rivers, with significant bycatch risk. Towed gear (seine/gill net panels, trawls) is best suited to canals but conflicts with tilapia's preferred refuge habitat in cracked linings/structures.
3. **Public use conflicts**: High angler use of the Colorado and Gila Rivers (and some canals) creates strong potential for user conflict and public perception issues (anglers may misattribute sport fish declines to commercial operations), plus enforcement complications from restricted commercial gear types.
4. **Site suitability ranking** (based on tilapia abundance, sport fish abundance, and public use level):
   - **Most suitable**: Agricultural drainage canals — least sport fish impact, least public use conflict, but tilapia abundance/productivity there is unknown and requires further research.
   - **Intermediate**: Gila River — probable higher tilapia abundance than Colorado River due to warmer, slower conditions, but notable Largemouth bass bycatch and high public use at access points.
   - **Least suitable**: Colorado River — high sport fish diversity, high public use, and no indication of high tilapia abundance per existing harvest records.
5. **Research gaps**: Standing stock, productivity, and growth rate of tilapia in drainage canals remain unknown; further study is needed to determine whether canal habitat and tilapia productivity can sustain a viable, sustained commercial harvest.
