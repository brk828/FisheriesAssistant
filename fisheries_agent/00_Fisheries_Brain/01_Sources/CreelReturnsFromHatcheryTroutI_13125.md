# Creel Returns from Hatchery Trout in June Lake, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1943 report by Elden H. Vestal, of the California Division of Fish and Game, evaluates the yield of [[Hatchery Stocking]] programs at [[June Lake]], a high-elevation glacial lake in Mono County. From May 1939 through 1942, the study used intensive [[Creel Census]] methods and [[Fish Marking]] of four annual plants of sized [[Rainbow Trout]] reared at the [[Hot Creek State Hatchery]]. The lake's fishery had declined to a low in 1930–1936 and was restored after sized plantings began in 1937. Naturally spawned rainbow failed to survive, so the fishery depended entirely on stocking. The author concluded that the plants sustained the yield despite heavy fishing pressure.

## Background & Description

June Lake lies at 7,612 ft in the southern part of the [[Mono National Forest]]. It is a glacial lake shaped by the Tioga glaciation, with a rocky, steep shoreline and rush margins (*Scirpus acutus*) covering more than two-thirds of its edge. The surface area is about 310 acres, and the maximum depth recorded on May 27, 1939, was 140 feet. The lake has no inlet and is fed by surface runoff and marginal springs. Its outlet, created in 1939 after an overflow, was screened to keep resident fish from leaving the lake.

The lake is frozen from January to April, and summer surface temperatures reached 69°F. It supports abundant marginal flora, a plankton community rich in cladocerans, and many bottom and marginal food organisms. The site was chosen for its accessibility, its measurable fishing effort at three boat concessions, its landlocked setting, and its expected lack of natural trout reproduction.

**Fish species recorded:**
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), the principal sportfish
- [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*)
- [[Loch Leven Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), a single specimen (62.5 cm) taken in 1941
- [[Lake Chub]] (*Siphateles obesus*)
- [[Sand Bar Sucker]] (*Catostomus arenarius*)
- [[Three-Spined Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*), present in the thousands

All fishes other than rainbow were introduced. Non-rainbow species made up less than 1% of the catch, chiefly brook trout.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Natural reproduction failure.** Rainbow attempted to spawn on the wave-washed north-end beach, but no naturally produced fingerlings were observed from 1939 onward. Egg experiments in 1941 gave the following results:
- 620 naturally spawned eggs excavated on June 12, 1941: 48 clear with oil globules, 572 opaque; none developed.
- 135 artificially fertilized eggs (placed June 11) showed no development by July 12; 21 were attacked by fungus.
- 203 naturally spawned eggs from nearby redds showed no development.

**Species interactions.** Stickleback did not appear to harm the trout fishery, and stomach samples showed them forming a large share of the diet of rainbow over 10 inches long. Sucker spawn annually in deep springs near the south end, and no sucker under nine inches was taken in gill nets. No sucker was found in trout stomachs. Lake chub showed no observed spawning or young.

**Management regulations.** The trout season was open only on Sundays and holidays in May during 1939–1940, extended to all of May in 1941, and shortened in 1942 by excluding October. The northern one-fifth of the lake was closed to angling in 1939–1940 for egg-taking, then closed only May 1–June 30 in 1941, and open in 1942. The Mono National Forest limited boats to 100 at one time (20 for each of the three concessions, 40 for tourists and residents).

## Key Findings

**Stocking program**
- Annual plantings were 70,000 rainbow in 1939–1941 and 100,000 in 1942, released as early as possible each season.
- Marked fish numbered 30,000, 30,000, 30,000 and 20,000 for 1939–1942, using different fin clips (adipose, left ventral, right ventral).
- Average size at planting rose from 1.3–2.6 inches (1939–41 range as reported) to 4–6 inches, with the 1942 plant at 0.85 inches.

**Creel results (estimated totals)**

| Year | Estimated total catch | Estimated anglers | Reliability |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1939 | 26,000 | 14,452 | Lower |
| 1940 | 29,000 | 19,830 | Lower |
| 1941 | 38,451 (minimum) | Not fully recorded | Slightly lower |
| 1942 | 55,965 | 19,830 | Highest |

- The 1942 census sampled 5,647 hours by 1,243 anglers, catching 3,536 trout.
- Catch per angler-hour ranged from 0.55 to 0.62 (recorded), and catch per angler from 2.8 to 3.1 trout.
- Catch per angler-hour stayed above 0.5, so no depletion of stock was indicated.
- Shore fishing made up no more than 2% of effort, so totals are slightly underestimated.

**Age-group yield**
- Most fish caught in a season were from that season's plant. The minority was from the previous year's plant.
- In 1942, 77.6% of the catch were 1942 fish, compared with a 1942 plant of 100,000.
- Hypothetical yield per 1,000 angler-hours for a 70,000 five-inch plant: 420 in the planting year, 120 in the second, 12 in the third and 1 in the fourth.

**Survival and yield to creel**
- Using recorded catches for 1939–1941, the minimum survival to the creel was 30.7% (the printed 380.7% figure is an OCR error for approximately 30.8%).
- Using estimated catches for the same period, survival was 44.5%.
- The 1941 estimate gave 55% yield to the creel.
- The 1942 plant gave an estimated 43% yield in its first year, with a projected total of at least 54% after 1943.
- The author noted that 1940 marked-fish counts were likely undercounted by census takers, not caused by higher mortality among marked fish.

**Growth**
- 1939 plant: 4.0 in at planting, 12.3 in at June 1940, 14.5 in at May 1941 (10.5-inch gain in nearly 24 months); weight gain 21.3 oz.
- 1940 plant: 4.6 in at planting, 10.9 in after 12 months (6.3-inch gain).
- 1941 plant: 4.6 in at planting, 11.6 in after 12 months (7.0-inch gain).
- Growth was fastest in the first year.

**Condition factor**
- Hatchery fish before planting had CF 1.36–1.89.
- Within three months of planting, CF fell to 1.22–1.28.
- A "normal" CF for June Lake rainbow is about 1.25, which the author considered limited by the lake environment.

**Conclusions**
- Annual sized plants built the fishery from a low level and sustained yield under heavy fishing.
- Natural rainbow reproduction did not succeed, so the fishery depends on stocking.
- The lake supports rapid growth, indicating high productivity.
