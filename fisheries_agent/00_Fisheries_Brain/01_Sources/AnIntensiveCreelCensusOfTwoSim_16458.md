## Executive Summary

This paper, presented by George G. Fleener at the Fourteenth Midwest Wildlife Conference (1952), details the first year (1951) of an intensive [[Creel Census]] designed to evaluate the efficacy of stocking hatchery-reared [[Smallmouth Bass]] fingerlings in Ozark streams. The study utilized a controlled comparison between the **Big Piney River** (stocked with ~71,000 fingerlings in 1951) and the **Niangua River** (left to natural reproduction), two hydrologically and biologically similar Missouri Ozark streams. The census measured angler effort, catch rates, species composition, and yield (lbs/acre) to establish a baseline before the effects of stocking could be assessed in subsequent years. The methodology relied on sampling three river segments via "float stations" and projecting data using specific mathematical factors for fishable days and daily "turnover" of anglers.

    *   Big Piney River: 11.1 inches (n=216)
    *   *Finding:* Niangua River smallmouth bass were consistently larger despite being less numerous in the catch relative to the Big Piney.
*   **Stocked Fingerlings:** Approximately 71,000 hatchery-reared smallmouth bass, ranging from 1 to 6 inches in length, were stocked at 11 locations on the Big Piney River in 1951.
*   **Largemouth Bass Length:** Niangua (12.5 in) vs. Big Piney (10.2 in).
*   **Channel Catfish Length:** Niangua (17.7 in) vs. Big Piney (16.6 in).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

*   **Habitat Type:** Both rivers are classified as "float-fishing" Ozark plateau streams, characterized by an average fall of 5 feet per mile, flowing northward into the [[Missouri River]].
*   **Drainage Area:** Niangua River (710 sq mi) vs. Big Piney River (768 sq mi).
*   **Stream Length (Study Area):** Niangua (~81 miles) vs. Big Piney (~82 miles) of floatable water.
*   **Species Endemism:** The report notes that the Smallmouth Bass is a species "endemic to these waters," representing the primary game fish driving angler effort (float fishing) on both systems.
*   **Community Structure:**
    *   **Big Piney River:** Dominated by [[Longear Sunfish]] (37.3% of total catch) and [[Rock Bass]] (25.1%). Smallmouth bass made up 12.0% of the total catch.
    *   **Niangua River:** Dominated by [[Longear Sunfish]] (17.7%) and [[Bluegill]] (16.5%), with significant influence from Tunnel Dam Lake (a lentic habitat) increasing bluegill numbers. Smallmouth bass made up 13.0% of the total catch.
*   **Micro-habitat influence:** The presence of an impounded lake (Tunnel Dam Lake) on the lower Niangua float station significantly skewed species composition data, increasing the proportion of lentic-adapted species like [[Bluegill]] (28.1% of catch in that specific lake segment vs. 12.3% in the flowing river segment).

## Key Findings

*   **Fishing Pressure:** The Big Piney River experienced significantly higher fishing pressure (~300 anglers/mile) than the Niangua River (~170 anglers/mile), representing only 56% of the Big Piney's intensity.
*   **Catch Rates (Fish/Hour):** Nearly identical between streams for all species combined (Niangua: 0.484; Big Piney: 0.477).
*   **Smallmouth Bass Catch Rate:** Higher on the Big Piney (1 bass every 1.13 hours) compared to the Niangua (1 bass every 2.66 hours), though this is attributed primarily to fishing pressure differences rather than population density.
*   **Yield Normalization:** When normalized for fishing pressure, the Niangua River would theoretically have yielded a smallmouth bass every 50 feet of stream, outperforming the Big Piney's actual rate of one bass every 65 feet.
*   **Float vs. Non-Float Fishing:** Float fishermen represented a small minority of total anglers (Niangua: 5.6%; Big Piney: 6.4%) but accounted for a disproportionately high percentage of the smallmouth bass catch (Niangua: 38%; Big Piney: 18%).
*   **Biomass Yield:** The Big Piney River yielded a higher biomass per acre (16.7 lbs/acre) compared to the Niangua River (13.9 lbs/acre), despite the Niangua yielding more pounds of fish per individual fisherman (1.3 lbs) due to lower angler density.
*   **Baseline Equivalence:** The report concludes that total smallmouth bass yield was statistically very similar between the two streams once fishing pressure differences are accounted for, successfully establishing a valid baseline for the multi-year stocking experiment.
*   **Methodological Innovation:** The study pioneered a rigorous creel census methodology for warmwater streams in Missouri, using "float stations" (8–13 miles long) and mathematical projection factors (including a "turnover" factor of 2.9168 for non-float bank fishermen) to estimate total seasonal yield from incomplete daily samples.
*   **Non-Game Species:** Non-game species (including [[Longear Sunfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], and suckers) constituted the majority of the catch by volume (>87%) on both rivers, despite Smallmouth Bass being the primary target species for anglers.
