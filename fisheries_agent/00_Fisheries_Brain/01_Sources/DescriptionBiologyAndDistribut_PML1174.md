# Description, Biology and Distribution of the Spotfin Chub, *Hybopsis monacha*, a Threatened Cyprinid Fish of the Tennessee River Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 *Bulletin of the Alabama Museum of Natural History* paper by Robert E. Jenkins and Noel M. Burkhead provides the first detailed description of the [[Spotfin Chub]] (*Hybopsis monacha*), a small cyprinid endemic to the [[Tennessee River]] drainage. The species had a wide historical range across 12 tributary systems in four physiographic provinces, but it has disappeared from most of that range and survives in only four systems. The authors combine morphology, life history, diet, aging, reproduction, habitat and a detailed historical distribution record (including negative collecting data) to support its status as a Threatened species, designated nationally in 1977. The paper also discusses the taxon's uncertain phylogenetic placement within *Erimystax* and *Cyprinella*.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described by [[Edward Drinker Cope]] (1868) as *Ceratichthys monachus* from specimens seined near Saltville, Virginia. It is currently placed in the subgenus *Erimystax* of *Hybopsis*; the authors suggest *Erimonax* may warrant resurrection for its sole reception.
- **Morphology:** Elongate, slightly compressed body; inferior mouth with a minute or absent labial barbel; teeth 4-4; lateral line 52-62 scales; 8 anal rays; a distinctive caudal spot. Nuptial males bear antrorse head tubercles, iridescent blue body colour, and blue fins with white margins. Maximum known size is 89.5 mm SL, and males grow larger than females.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males show longer dorsal, anal and pelvic fins and a more anterior dorsal insertion; pectoral fins are tuberculate only in males.
- **Age and longevity:** Four age groups (0-III) were recognised, with maximum longevity of about three years. Aging was difficult; of 84 specimens attempted, 36 were unageable and 48 were scale-aged with some certainty.
- **Reproduction:** Spawning appears to be protracted, from roughly mid-May to mid-August. Females carried 157-791 Class I ova. The species may be a fractional, crevice spawner, which the authors suggest may mean counts underestimate fecundity.
- **Relationships:** Morphological evidence links it to [[Erimystax]], [[Phenacobius]] and [[Cyprinella]] (a subgenus of [[Notropis]]). A hybrid with *Notropis galacturus* is known from Daddys Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Occupies medium to large, relatively clean, warm streams, typically in or near moderate to swift current at depths of about 0.3-1 m, with riffles, runs and pools. Records lie between 200 and 600 m elevation. It is closely associated with largely unsilted substrates such as gravel, rubble, boulder and bedrock, and it avoids lentic habitat.
- **Diet:** Insectivorous and benthic. Of 2,539 insect items, 99.9% were dipterans, mainly midge larvae and blackfly larvae, with mayflies (6.8%) and caddisflies (1.6%) also present. Feeding is diurnal and benthic, and the authors describe it as a sight feeder and efficient taste-sorter.
- **Behaviour:** Usually found in small, loose aggregations rather than as solitary fish, and it fed near the substrate rather than taking food from the water column. Aggressive territoriality was observed among some adults during spawning.
- **Parasites:** Only blackspot metacercariae of strigeid trematodes were found, usually one or two per fish.
- **Threats:** Probable causes of decline include [[Impoundment]], cold tailwaters, channelization, pollution, turbidity, siltation, stream renovation with ichthyocides, localised intensive collecting, and interspecific competition. The 1957 rotenone treatment of [[Abrams Creek]] eliminated its population there.

## Key Findings

- The spotfin chub was historically recorded from 12 tributary systems in five states and four physiographic provinces. It is now extant in only four systems: [[Little Tennessee River]] (North Carolina), [[Emory River]] (Tennessee), [[North Fork Holston River]] (Tennessee and Virginia), and the Buffalo River subsystem of the [[Duck River]] (Tennessee; rediscovered in 1978).
- Extant populations span about 166 km of stream in total. The authors describe survival as remarkable but tenuous, with no population generally flourishing.
- The species is considered extirpated in Alabama and Georgia. It is treated as Endangered in three states, with the more precarious category applied because each state holds fewer than four tributary system populations.
- Of 841 total specimens from 96 collections and observations, 333 were extant and examined by the authors. Records were compiled from [[Museum Specimens]] and TVA surveys.
- Decline is attributed to habitat modification, with at least two impacts affecting most populations. The most frequent stresses were siltation or coal fine sediment (12 streams), pollution (10), and reservoir inundation (10).
- The authors recommend the distribution maps, including negative data, as a baseline for future work. They also note that the species should retain Threatened status nationally, as designated in 1977.
- A 1983 addendum confirms that the species is a crevice spawner, which is further evidence of its close relationship to *Notropis* (*Cyprinella*).
