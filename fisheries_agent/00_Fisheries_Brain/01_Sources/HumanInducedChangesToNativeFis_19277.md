# Human-Induced Changes to Native Fishes in the Virgin River Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 paper by Scott C. Gregory and James E. Deacon, published in the *American Water Resources Association* volume on effects of human-induced changes on hydrologic systems, uses 17 years of native fish data plus historical records to document declines in the distribution and abundance of the [[Virgin River]] native fish community. Losses are linked to successive phases of development: flooding by [[Lake Mead]] after [[Hoover Dam]] (1930s), reservoir construction, trout stocking and poisoning in tributaries (1960s–1970s), and the spread of the introduced [[Red Shiner]] together with flow alteration in the middle mainstem (1980s). Native fishes retain near-natural abundance only in [[Zion National Park]] and a reach of the [[Santa Clara River]] above [[Gunlock Reservoir]]. The authors argue that future water development threatens these refuges and that competing demands on the river raise ethical questions about resource use.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** The [[Virgin River]] rises in southwestern Utah, flows through northwestern Arizona, and enters the Overton Arm of Lake Mead in southern Nevada. Mean annual flow at Littlefield, AZ (water years 1930–1992) is 170,600 acre-feet. The highest mean daily flow was 697 cfs (1983) and the lowest was 100 cfs (1991). Instantaneous flows ranged from 38 cfs (May 1, 1975) to 61,000 cfs (January 1, 1989).

**Fish community.** Six species have persisted under natural variability in the drainage:
- Three endemics: [[Virgin Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*) and [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), both federally listed as endangered (USFWS 1992); and [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*), a candidate for threatened listing (Addley and Hardy 1993).
- Three widespread species: [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*).

**Human history.** Paiute Indians irrigated crops before Mormon settlement in the 1850s. Within about 40 years (1850s–1890s) Mormon settlers, following the Cotton Mission, displaced the Paiutes and built dams, diversions, and storage facilities. A permanent diversion at Washington Fields was completed in 1891 after earlier rock, dirt, and brush structures were repeatedly washed away.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat alteration:** Dams and diversions change seasonal flow, dewater reaches, and create barriers to dispersal. Farm and ranch practices increase bank erosion, remove streamside vegetation, and add nutrients, animal waste, and salts to runoff (Hall and Amy 1990).
- **Non-native species:** Introduced fishes alter assemblage structure (Baltz and Moyle 1993). Invaders into the lower river included carp, channel catfish, black bullhead, bluegill, green sunfish, [[Largemouth Bass]], mosquitofish, and red shiner (Cross 1975).
- **Flow regime:** Natural flow variability is reduced as water management expands, which the authors argue increases the impact of non-natives.
- **Refugia:** Reaches with the least regulated flows and minimal fisheries management retain intact native assemblages.
- **Policy and water rights:** Competing claims include [[Federal Reserved Water Rights]] (National Park Service for Zion), instream flows for endangered species (Bureau of Land Management), irrigation rights, and municipal demand (Southern Nevada Water Authority application for 70,000 acre-feet per year). The [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 consultation also applies.

## Key Findings

**Historical decline by segment**
- **Lower mainstem (1935–present):** Native fishes were eliminated when [[Lake Mead]] replaced about the lower 25 miles of river after Hoover Dam's construction. Red shiner, introduced to Lake Mead as bait in the 1950s, comprised 20–60% of the fish population upstream to Mesquite, NV by the early 1970s.
- **Tributaries (1960–present):** Schroeder Reservoir (20 surface acres, built 1960) was followed by poisoning and rainbow trout stocking. Virgin spinedace were last collected in upper Beaver Dam Wash in July 1961. Addley and Hardy (1993) found them only in the lower 8.1 of 18.3 miles of historic habitat, mostly fish under 30 mm or over 65 mm, suggesting predation pressure from trout.
- **Santa Clara River:** Winsor Dam (1930s) blocked upstream movement. Gunlock Dam (1970s) and diversions dewater the river seasonally. Native fishes are extirpated or depleted below Gunlock Reservoir, with an isolated assemblage remaining between Veyo, UT and Gunlock Reservoir.
- **Middle mainstem (1985–present):** Following Quail Creek Reservoir completion, native fish density declined near Ash Creek and above Quail Creek (Figures 4–5). Below Washington Fields Diversion native fishes have nearly disappeared. Record flow in 1983 allowed red shiner to move upstream through the Virgin Narrows. Red shiner exceeded 10% of the fauna before 1985 and dominated afterward.
- **Zion National Park and downstream:** Native fishes have not been collected in Kolob Creek or the North Fork above the park. Speckled dace and desert sucker persist in the East Fork. Historic abundance remains only in and below the park.

**Management outcomes**
- A 1988 fish barrier above the Virgin Narrows and a poisoning attempt against red shiner were unsuccessful. Poisoning between Washington Fields and Johnson Diversions removed red shiner, but red shiner recovered and native fishes did not. Below Johnson Diversion, all fish were decimated.

**Water development and flow compliance**
- Quail Creek Reservoir required a non-jeopardy biological opinion (USBLM 1983) protecting woundfin. The opinion required bypass of 86 cfs or natural flow, whichever was less. Mean daily flow data show this standard has not been met. Operators maintain 86 cfs at the reservoir outlet, about 9 miles below LaVerkin Power Plant, which leaves lower flows between the power plant and outlet. The authors describe a probable causal link to fish decline and note USFWS reopened Section 7 consultation.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Dewatering is the most damaging process, with effects depending on diversion duration and stream length affected.
- Non-native introductions, particularly red shiner and trout, are major drivers of change.
- Management plans must account for aesthetic values and wildlife habitat, not only water supply.
- The authors call for management that treats the river as a community to which humans belong, citing Leopold (1987).
