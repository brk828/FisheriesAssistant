# Classification and Scientific Names of Rainbow and Cutthroat Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Smith and Stearley (*Fisheries*, Vol. 14, No. 1, January–February 1989) argue that two taxonomic changes are required for North American trouts. First, the [[Rainbow Trout]] and the [[Kamchatka Trout]] are the same species, so the oldest available name, *mykiss*, replaces *Salmo gairdneri*. Second, osteological and biochemical evidence places [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], and their relatives closer to [[Pacific Salmon]] than to [[Atlantic Salmon]] and [[Brown Trout]], so Pacific trouts and salmons should be assigned to the genus *Oncorhynchus*, while Atlantic trouts and salmon remain in *Salmo*. The article also rejects the name *Parasalmo* and recommends *Rhabdofario* if Pacific trouts are recognized separately. The [[Committee on Names of Fishes]] of the [[American Fisheries Society]] accepted these changes, and the AFS applied them to publications after January 1, 1989.

## Background & Description

The genus *Salmo* was proposed by [[Carl Linnaeus]] in 1758 and originally included Atlantic salmon, brown trout, huchen, chars, grayling, and other soft-rayed fishes. In the early 1790s, [[Johann Walbaum]] described many North Pacific species from specimens collected by [[Thomas Pennant]]'s expedition in Kamchatka, and [[John Richardson]] redescribed many in 1836 from northwestern North America. Richardson separated chars into *Salvelinus*. Naming confusion grew because many species were collected and named repeatedly, partly due to the variation in sexual dimorphism, breeding coloration, and life history within these fishes.

Key naming history includes:
- **[[George Suckley]] (1862):** proposed *Oncorhynchus* as a subgenus of *Salmo* for anadromous species whose breeding males have a hooked upper jaw and enlarged teeth.
- **[[Albert Gunther]] (1866):** redefined *Oncorhynchus* by anal fin ray count (14 or more), a character that does not fully match *Salmo masou*, which has 11–15 rays.
- **[[C. Tate Regan]] (1914):** showed from cranial characters that Pacific trouts and salmons form a natural group and should share one genus. The authors describe this as the most important breakthrough in understanding trout relationships.
- **[[Vladykov]] (1963):** proposed *Parasalmo* for the Pacific trouts.

Taxonomic reliability depends on cranial and skeletal traits, illustrated in Figure 1 (dermethmoid, maxilla, premaxilla, palatine, and circumorbital bones of *Salvelinus confluentus*, *Salmo trutta*, and *Oncorhynchus* species).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors link classification to life history. Trends in anadromy, spawning, and juvenile behavior map onto the phylogeny:
- The Salmoninae show a stepwise progression from freshwater forms (*Thymallus*, *Brachymystax*) through intermediate anadromy (*Salvelinus*) to greater independence from freshwater in *Oncorhynchus*.
- Osmoregulatory ability for sea life is probably primitive in Salmonidae, since it occurs in related families such as Osmeridae.
- Steelhead and Pacific salmon share a similar mechanism of post-spawning death, involving rapid senescence linked to pituitary and adrenal activity (Robertson and Wexler 1962a,b).
- *Oncorhynchus masou* contains populations that die after spawning and others (sometimes called *O. rhodurus*) that repeat-spawn, so its inland and sea-run forms cannot be placed in different genera.
- *O. tshawytscha* and *O. nerka* show a small percentage of fry going directly to sea, while *O. keta* and *O. gorbuscha* reduce the freshwater phase to a minimum.

The authors also note that some inland forms (e.g., inland rainbow trout and sockeye salmon) may have been derived from anadromous forms, a possibility suggested by Thorpe (1982).

## Key Findings

**1. Species name change for rainbow trout**
- Okazaki (1984) compared 37 electrophoretic loci and found coastal steelhead more genetically similar to Kamchatka trout (genetic distance = .0026) than to inland *gairdneri* (genetic distance = .0073).
- Behnke (1966) corrected Jordan and Evermann's scale-count argument, showing morphological similarity between Kamchatka and rainbow trout.
- Vertebral counts (Savvaitova 1975) and chromosome data (Vasil'yev 1975; Thorgaard 1977) support the same conclusion.
- Result: *Oncorhynchus mykiss* (formerly *Salmo gairdneri*) is adopted as the single name. The authors advise against an appeal to the [[International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature]], since *mykiss* is already established in Asian literature.

**2. Generic placement of Pacific trouts**
- The authors evaluated four genus options: *Salmo*, *Oncorhynchus*, *Rhabdofario*, and *Parasalmo*.
- They favor two sister lineages: Atlantic trouts and salmon in *Salmo*, and Pacific trouts and salmons in *Oncorhynchus*.
- Their argument rests on monophyly: shared specialized characters indicate common ancestry more reliably than shared primitive characters, which reflect only retained ancestral features.
- The authors used 49 morphological characters across 24 species to calculate the tree in Figure 2 (analysis performed with [[Phylogenetic Analysis Using Parsimony]]).

**3. Rejection of Parasalmo and adoption of Rhabdofario**
- *Rhabdofario* (Cope 1870) is older than *Parasalmo* (Vladykov 1963).
- It is based on a partial fossil skull (Figure 4) from the Pliocene [[Glenns Ferry Formation]] of Idaho, dated to about 3.5 million years ago in the Snake River drainage.
- The authors conclude that *Rhabdofario* represents the same lineage as rainbow and cutthroat trout. If a separate name is needed, *Rhabdofario* is the required choice under nomenclatural rules.

**4. Sister-group relationships**
- Pacific salmons grade from Pacific trouts through small steps in life history, so Pacific trouts are a middle stage in one continuous evolutionary trend.
- Atlantic trouts and salmon form the complementary sister group in the North Atlantic. *Salmo salar* and *Salmo trutta* both have landlocked forms.

**5. Diversity within the two genera**
- *Salmo* includes several species with notable diversity. For example, *Salmo ischchan* in Lake Sevan has six forms, including fall, spring, and summer spawners (Berg 1948).
- *Oncorhynchus* includes eight sea-run species, four of which have inland, landlocked relatives. These include [[Amago Trout]] and [[Biwa Trout]] (*O. masou* in Japan), [[Kokanee]] (*O. nerka*), and inland rainbow, cutthroat, golden, Mexican golden, Apache, and Gila trouts related to *O. mykiss* and *O. clarki*.

**6. Adoption by the AFS**
- At the June 1988 meeting of the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists, the AFS-ASIH Committee on Names of Fishes unanimously accepted *Oncorhynchus* as the generic name for Pacific trouts and salmons. The committee had earlier accepted the *mykiss* change.
- Only scientific names change; common names recommended by the committee remain the same.
- Changes will appear in the forthcoming *World List of Fishes of Interest to North Americans* (1989) and *List of Common and Scientific Names of Fishes from the United States and Canada*, 5th edition (1990).

**7. Stated rationale**
- The authors argue that a classification reflecting evolutionary branching will better predict life history and support management. They note that stability of names is a competing priority, but in this case they consider the evidence sufficient to justify change.
