# Colorado River Law Enforcement Sub-Committee Agenda & Arrest Report

## Executive Summary
This document is **not a biological or morphological study** but rather an administrative and legal record from a tri-state (Arizona, California, Nevada) **Law Enforcement Sub-Committee** overseeing recreational fishing and hunting regulations on the [[Colorado River]]. It comprises two parts: (1) an agenda summarizing discussions on boating regulations, underwater spear fishing, live box/stringer identification rules, joint interstate patrol scheduling, and a jurisdictional dispute over a Colorado River Indian Tribe trespass permit; and (2) a detailed **arrest and citation report** (July 1961–June 1962) compiled from multiple Justice of the Peace courts (Bullhead City, Yuma, Somerton, Kingman, Quartzsite, Parker) documenting violations of fishing and hunting regulations, associated pleas, and fines. While it references regulated species indirectly (trout, bass, doves, deer), it contains no direct morphological, physiological, or ecological data on fish populations. Its scientific value lies in documenting **fisheries law enforcement practices, regulatory pressure on Colorado River sport fisheries, and indicators of angler behavior/compliance** during the early 1960s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
Although not an ecological study, the document implies aspects of the Colorado River aquatic ecosystem and its management context:
- The [[Colorado River]] mainstem and connected reservoirs ([[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Havasu]]) functioned as shared, heavily-fished, multi-jurisdictional waters spanning Arizona, California, and Nevada state boundaries.
- Regulatory concern over **underwater spear fishing bans** (already in place in California) suggests recognition of localized fish population vulnerability to spearfishing pressure in the Colorado River.
- **Live box and stringer regulations** point to common recreational practice of holding/retaining live-caught fish in the river itself, with concerns about accountability and unattended catch.
- The **Colorado River Indian Reservation trespass permit dispute** (near Parker, AZ) highlights overlapping tribal, state, and federal jurisdiction over fishing access on a legally "navigable" waterway, an important precedent for aquatic resource governance.
- Citations for "Fish w/o Colorado River Stamp" indicate a distinct river-specific licensing/stamp system, implying separate management status for Colorado River fisheries compared to general state waters.
- Frequent citations for exceeding line/pole limits, unlicensed fishing, and overlimit catches (trout, bass) suggest active harvest pressure requiring enforcement to protect fish stocks.

## Key Findings
- **Interstate cooperation**: Arizona and California committees were working toward **uniform boating regulations**, including mandatory fire extinguishers on motorboats and daylight-only water skiing.
- **Spearfishing regulation gap**: California prohibited underwater spear fishing in the Colorado River; Arizona had no equivalent regulation, prompting committee recommendation (though no formal action) for Arizona to consider adoption — an early fisheries protection measure against localized depletion via spearfishing.
- **Live box/stringer identification rule** was drafted, requiring water-resistant ID tags (name, address, license number) on all unattended live boxes/stringers, including cases of shared use, to improve enforcement accountability and reduce over-limit fish caching.
- **Joint patrol scheduling**: Supervisors from all three states (George Werden, Don Smith, Don Wingfield, Al Jonez) coordinated increased spring enforcement patrols on the shared river, reflecting elevated fishing/hunting pressure during that season.
- **Jurisdictional dispute**: The Colorado River Indian Tribe's imposition of a trespass permit for boat-based fishing within reservation boundaries raised unresolved legal questions given the river's status as a **navigable stream**; both states sought Attorney General opinions.
- **Arrest/citation data (July 1961–June 1962)** across five J.P. courts totaled **$5,075.00** in fines for combined fishing, hunting, and licensing violations, with fishing-related violations (no license, no Colorado River stamp, multiple lines/poles, overlimit catches) constituting the dominant category — evidence of substantial non-compliance and active enforcement targeting the Colorado River sport fishery.
- Notable fishery-specific violations: "Overlimit of trout," "Fish w/o Colorado River Stamp," "Illegal possession & transportation (bass)," and "Fish Lake Mead/Havasu w/o license or stamp," collectively indicating the Colorado River trout and bass fisheries were subject to defined bag limits and stamp/licensing requirements actively enforced across the tri-state region.
- Non-fish wildlife violations (deer, dove, rabbit hunting) were concurrently prosecuted by the same courts and officers, indicating integrated game and fish law enforcement infrastructure along the [[Colorado River]] corridor.
