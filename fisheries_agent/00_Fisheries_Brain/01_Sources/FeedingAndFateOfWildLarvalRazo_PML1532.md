# Feeding and Fate of Wild Larval Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 study by Paul C. Marsh and Daniel R. Langhorst in *Environmental Biology of Fishes* tests whether food limitation explains the disappearance of [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae from [[Lake Mohave]], where no recruitment has been recorded since the 1950s. Larval diets and zooplankton availability were compared between the reservoir and an isolated [[Backwater Habitat]] (Arizona Bay) in 1985. Food supply did not explain the loss: larvae in the backwater survived and grew far larger. The authors attribute larval mortality mainly to [[Predation]] by [[Non-Native Fishes]] that invaded the backwater after its berm was breached in April 1985.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large [[Catostomidae]] fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system. It was once widely distributed and abundant, but its numbers and range are now drastically reduced. The largest remaining wild population, in Lake Mohave, consists mainly of old individuals. Annual winter-spring spawning produces abundant larvae, yet these disappear at about 12 mm total length (TL) by late spring.

Three explanations were considered for the disappearance:
- Mortality
- Migration to unknown places within the reservoir
- Transport out of the system

The authors focused on [[Nutritional Constraints]] as a possible cause of high mortality.

Study design:
- **Reservoir site:** About 5 km of shoreline between Arizona Bay and [[Hammerhead Cove]], sampled January to May 1985.
- **Backwater site:** Separated from the reservoir by a wave-formed gravel spit. Maximum depths were 1.7 to 3.3 m, surface areas 0.85 to 2.10 ha, and water exchanged freely through the porous berm. Temperatures were slightly cooler than the reservoir in winter (-1.1 °C) and warmer in summer (+1.8 °C).
- **Backwater history:** Its original non-native fishes (threadfin shad, common carp, channel catfish, mosquitofish, [[Largemouth Bass]], green sunfish and bluegill) were removed by [[Ichthyocide]] in autumn 1984. It was restocked in January-February 1985 with 30 female and 150 male adult razorback suckers from Arizona Bay.

Larvae were defined as fish from hatching (6.5-8.6 mm) through full development of fin rays (35 mm). Fish were collected at night with a spotlight and dipnet and preserved in 5% buffered formalin. [[Zooplankton]] were sampled with Clarke-Bumpus nets in the reservoir and with composite grabs in the backwater.

Food selection was measured with a [[Linear Index of Food Selection]], which ranges from -100 to +100. Positive values indicate preference, negative values avoidance, and values near zero random feeding. Values with an absolute value below 10 were treated as not different from zero.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir zooplankton:** Dominated by [[Cladocera]], mainly [[Bosmina]] and [[Daphnia]], which together made up nearly two-thirds of numbers. [[Copepoda]] made up about one-third, and [[Rotifera]] were of minor significance. Total density averaged 1,554 organisms per m³.

**Backwater zooplankton:** Total density averaged 377 animals per m³, about 25% of the reservoir value. Species richness was higher in the backwater, and the most abundant taxa were the same as in the reservoir. Rotifers and copepods were relatively less abundant in the backwater than in the reservoir.

**Predation context:** The authors frame the decline of native Colorado River fishes as a result of physical habitat modification and introduced species. Their effects were roughly concurrent and difficult to separate. Other studies cited document predation on native fish eggs and larvae by [[Green Sunfish]] and catfishes, including work at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].

## Key Findings

**Lake Mohave larvae**
- First captured on 9 February 1985, about 3 weeks after spawning was first observed at that location.
- Mean TL was 10.6 ± 0.3 (SE) mm (n = 410), indicating continuous spawning and larval production.
- Neither mean nor maximum TL increased over the season. The largest individual was 12.2 mm.
- Comparisons with known-age fish reared at similar temperatures suggest the largest specimens were at most a few weeks old.

**Backwater larvae**
- First captured on 7 March 1985, about 5 to 8 weeks after adults were stocked.
- Initial mean TL was similar to that in Lake Mohave (approximately 10 mm), but larvae grew through April. The largest specimen by late April measured 20.3 mm.
- Larvae reached the middle postlarval stage, nearly twice the size of the largest wild reservoir larvae.

**Diet and selection**
- In both habitats, diets were dominated by the same primary food, *Bosmina*, and foods were similar in composition.
- Reservoir larvae selected *Bosmina* and avoided copepods. They preferred zooplankters of 400 to 999 × 10⁻³ mm³ and avoided those of 100 to 399 × 10⁻³ mm³.
- Backwater larvae selected *Bosmina* and avoided rotifers. They preferred zooplankters of 50 to 399 × 10⁻³ mm³ and avoided those of 10 to 14 × 10⁻³ mm³.
- Selection patterns differed between habitats, but neither difference was attributable to larval size or zooplankton community structure.
- The authors found no evidence that the size of ingested food increased with fish TL between 12 mm and 20 mm.

**Conclusions on food and mortality**
- Food type, number and size do not explain the total loss of larvae from the reservoir, since larvae survive and grow far better in the backwater.
- Survival, but not growth, of larval razorback suckers in experimental ponds was independent of productivity (Papoulias, unpublished data).

**Migration and transport**
- In-reservoir migration is rejected. Extensive sampling since the early 1970s, using gears that have taken thousands of other juvenile-sized fish, failed to capture small razorback suckers.
- Down-lake transport may occur, but downstream razorback populations are very small. The authors conclude that if transport removes young fish, it must result in high mortality.

**Predation as the cause of loss**
- A storm-driven wave event combined with high reservoir level (196.8 m) breached the berm on 4 April 1985.
- Within days, non-native fishes invaded the backwater. Nearly 40% of green sunfish captured over a 24-hour period contained an average of four razorback larvae.
- Larvae could no longer be collected by 5 May, and the authors attribute total mortality of the stocked cohort to predation.

**Management implications**
- Confirming larval fate in Lake Mohave and quantifying predation are described as critical to recovery. Major obstacles include identifying prey in predator stomachs after digestion, for which serological methods such as ELISA may help.
- Suggested management includes reducing predator populations, including ichthyocide removal of catfishes from selected Arizona streams followed by stocking.
- Recovery may be best achieved in backwaters, oxbows and smaller tributary habitats where non-native fishes can be repeatedly removed.
