# Nigrosine Dye as an Aquatic Weed Control Method in Arizona Waters (1946)

#aquatic-plants #limnology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1946 report, written by a fisheries worker for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]], evaluates a low-cost method of controlling submerged aquatic vegetation by darkening water with a black dye, so that less [[Light Penetration|light]] reaches rooted plants. The dye of choice was [[Nigrosine]], a black dye that was held to be far cheaper in bulk than standard laboratory stains. It was tested in ponds at the [[Hunt Bass Hatchery]] near Phoenix and then applied to two larger lakes, [[Ashurst Lake]] and [[Granite Basin Lake]]. The dye reduced weed growth and gave a notable delayed effect the following spring, but it was too expensive for large waters and its results were inconsistent. The author recommended further trials in small, important ponds.

## Background & Description

The report begins from the management problem that weeds pose to fisheries. Some aquatic vegetation is useful because its decay supports food production. Excess growth, however, makes waters hard to fish and tends to cause overpopulation of warm-water species. Dense weed cover also shelters young fish that would otherwise form a major part of the diet of large fish, particularly [[Bass]]. Weeds were also identified as a cause of losses among freshly planted [[Trout]] in Arizona waters.

The author notes that clear, shallow lakes are especially prone to weed growth, because rooted plants need enough light for [[Photosynthesis]]. Turbid water limits growth in the same way. Even slight muddying of a stream was observed to reduce photosynthesis in the plants present and cause a rapid drop in pH.

The dye idea arose in the summer of 1944 during a search for weed control solutions in Arizona. Cost was the first obstacle. Laboratory stains such as nigrosine sold for about $15.00 per pound, but producers quoted 36 cents per pound in bulk lots. Other black dyes were similarly priced, though somewhat higher. Several candidate dyes were first tested in one-part-per-million dilutions in sunlit containers in January:

- Putnam's fadeless black faded almost completely.
- Neutral black dyes showed slight fading after three months.
- Nigrosine retained nearly full strength and was therefore selected.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hatchery ponds (Hunt Bass Hatchery, about ten miles from Phoenix).** Two ponds were treated: a half-acre pond (number eight, about 16 feet deep at the dam) and a three-acre pond (number six). Initial applications were one pound of nigrosine in the small pond, reducing [[Secchi Disk|Secchi]] visibility from 14 to 4 feet, and a larger quantity in the three-acre pond (the OCR is unclear, possibly ten pounds), reducing visibility from 18 to six feet. The concentration was maintained for about three months, with periodic top-ups to offset dilution.

Treated ponds also showed a substantially lower temperature at depth than untreated ponds, with surface readings near 77°F. In late April a large increase in [[Zooplankton]] made the treated ponds markedly more turbid for about one month, possibly from decay of bottom organic matter in the absence of light. The ponds then cleared. Results in the hatchery were ultimately inconclusive: once water was fertilized and temperatures exceeded about 88°F, weeds returned despite the dye, and clean-water flushing before draining removed the dye and weeds returned quickly.

**Ashurst Lake.** This static water covers about 260 acres, with a maximum depth of 18 feet and an average of about 16 feet. It is shallow and constantly turning over. Ashurst's pH rose above 11 in the previous year, which had inhibited vegetation in deeper water, but was lower and more favorable for weeds under the dye treatment. Myriophyllum was dominant and was kept from maturing. The dye concentration fell from 16 feet to seven feet in Secchi visibility after application, which made further treatment impractical.

**Granite Basin Lake.** This is a four-acre lake. Its pH normally rose to about 9.6 in spring; after the second dye application it dropped to 7.6 and stayed below that for the rest of the summer. Long tendrils of [[Potamogeton Crispus]], the dominant weed, turned white and dropped to the bottom in water deeper than six feet. By September the dye concentration was estimated at about 60% of the level at application.

**Dye dispersal.** Dye placed in the shallows on rocky bottom dissolved and spread through a 260-acre lake within 24 hours. A dark streak moved along the bottom and crossed roughly 100 yards in under an hour. By the next morning the lake was uniformly colored. The author noted that this rapid mixing matters for other treatments such as [[Rotenone]].

**Climate.** The author notes that Arizona has over 300 cloudless days per year, which may have strengthened the dye's effect compared with other regions.

## Key Findings

1. **Dye selection:** Of the dyes tested, nigrosine was the most durable under strong sunlight and the least costly in bulk (36 cents per pound versus about $15.00 per pound for laboratory stains).
2. **Application rate:** Ten pounds per acre was judged insufficient, and more dye was added to one lake.
3. **Application method:** Dumping dye into shallow water at several points was the most practical method. Dispersal was complete in under 24 hours in the 260-acre lake.
4. **Side effects:** The treatment lowered water temperature at depth and produced a temporary increase in plankton, which made water turbid for about a month.
5. **Delayed effect:** The greatest effect appeared the following year (1946), when normally semi-permanent weeds failed to reach the surface in Ashurst and Granite Basin lakes. The author attributed this partly to cumulative light starvation, and noted that the dye concentration in the lakes was still about 50% of the original in 1946.
6. **Limitations and program status:** The method was dropped in 1946 because it was too expensive for larger waters. Its results were inconsistent in the hatchery ponds, and the author suggested that treatment in the fall, before spring growth, might work better.
7. **Recommendation:** The author judged the method promising enough for further trials, especially in small but important ponds, and suggested that its effects might be stronger in regions with more sunlight than the southwest.
