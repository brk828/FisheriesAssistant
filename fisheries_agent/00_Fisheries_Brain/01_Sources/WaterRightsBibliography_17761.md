# Water Rights Bibliography

#water-resources #legal-bibliography #resource-development

## Executive Summary

This document is a comprehensive bibliography compiled by Eleanor J. Boyles of the [[Department of Resource Development]] and [[M.S.U. Library]] at [[Michigan State University]]. The bibliography assembles references on water rights law, water resource management, and related legal issues, drawing from research at the Congressional Library in Washington, D.C., and university resources. The compilation emphasizes current information on legal water problems and includes contributions from natural science professionals alongside legal scholarship. Associate Professor C. R. Humphrys notes in the foreword that the bibliography intentionally avoids exhaustive legal case listings, instead focusing on providing interdisciplinary perspectives on the hydrologic cycle and water use limitations.

## Background & Description

The bibliography is organized into three main sections: Technical Papers (approximately 414 entries), Law Review Articles (approximately 177 entries), and a smaller section on Opinions and Compacts. The technical papers section covers diverse topics including state water legislation, ground water law, irrigation rights, stream pollution control, interstate water compacts, and water resource development across multiple U.S. states. Significant geographic coverage includes [[California]], [[Texas]], [[Colorado]], [[Michigan]], [[Nebraska]], and other western and southeastern states. The law review articles address legal doctrines such as riparian rights, appropriative rights, percolating water law, and constitutional issues related to water ownership and use.

The compilation spans publications primarily from the 1930s through 1956, with particular concentration on post-1945 materials. Authors include legal scholars, agricultural economists, civil engineers, water resource administrators, and conservation professionals. Many entries address the tension between traditional riparian rights doctrines (prevalent in eastern states) and prior appropriation systems (dominant in western states), reflecting mid-20th century debates about modernizing water law to accommodate irrigation, industrial use, and growing municipal demands.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While this bibliography focuses primarily on legal and administrative aspects of water resources, numerous entries address ecological considerations. Several papers discuss stream pollution control legislation and its relationship to aquatic ecosystems. The compilation includes references to fish and wildlife management in relation to water rights (e.g., Wente's "Our Western water problems in relation to fish and game management"), recreational uses of water bodies, and the protection of water quality for multiple beneficial uses.

The bibliography reflects growing mid-century awareness that water law must account for the entire hydrologic cycle, including surface water, ground water, and their interconnections. Multiple entries address ground water depletion, the relationship between surface and subsurface waters, and the need for integrated water resource management. References to watershed conservation, flood control, and soil conservation demonstrate recognition of land-water relationships. The inclusion of works on water pollution, industrial waste discharge, and sewage treatment indicates emerging environmental concerns that would later crystallize in modern environmental law.

## Key Findings

The bibliography documents several major themes in mid-20th century water law and policy:

**Legal Doctrine Evolution**: Extensive coverage of the conflict between riparian rights and appropriative rights systems, with numerous states (particularly in the Southeast and Midwest) considering adoption of western-style appropriation doctrines or hybrid systems. Multiple entries discuss the "reasonable use" doctrine as a modification of traditional riparian rights.

**Ground Water Law Development**: Significant attention to ground water legal frameworks, with many states developing new statutory controls. The bibliography includes discussions of correlative rights, absolute ownership doctrines, and the application of appropriation principles to underground waters. [[Arizona]], [[New Mexico]], [[Colorado]], and other western states receive particular attention for their ground water legislation.

**Interstate Water Allocation**: Numerous references to interstate compacts, particularly regarding the [[Colorado River]], [[Rio Grande]], and other major river systems. The bibliography documents legal disputes between states and between states and the federal government over water allocation.

**Federal-State Relations**: Extensive coverage of federal power over navigable waters, federal reclamation projects, and conflicts between federal and state water rights systems. The [[Federal Power Commission]]'s licensing authority and its relationship to state water law receives significant attention.

**Pollution Control**: Growing body of literature on water pollution legislation at state and federal levels, including the Federal Water Pollution Control Act and various state regulatory frameworks. Industrial waste discharge, sewage treatment requirements, and enforcement mechanisms are addressed.

**Emerging Issues**: The bibliography captures emerging concerns including water fluoridation controversies, municipal water supply protection, irrigation development in humid areas, and the need for comprehensive state water codes. Multiple entries address the economic aspects of water allocation and the relationship between water law and agricultural development.

**Regional Variations**: State-specific analyses for [[California]], [[Texas]], [[Michigan]], [[Nebraska]], [[South Dakota]], [[Kentucky]], [[Florida]], and many other states, reflecting diverse hydrologic conditions and legal traditions across the United States.

The compilation represents a snapshot of water law scholarship during a period of significant transition, as traditional common law doctrines confronted new demands from irrigation, industrialization, urbanization, and emerging environmental awareness. The interdisciplinary scope—spanning law, engineering, economics, and natural science—reflects the complex, multi-faceted nature of water resource management challenges.
