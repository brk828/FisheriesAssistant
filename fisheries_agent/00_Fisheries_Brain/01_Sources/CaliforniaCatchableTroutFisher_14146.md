# California "Catchable" Trout Fisheries

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #Salmonidae #hatchery-programs #recreational-fishing

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive study evaluates California's catchable-sized trout stocking program from 1954-1958, during which approximately seven million 7-to-8-inch [[Rainbow Trout]] (Salmo gairdnerii) were stocked annually at a cost of $1,366,000. The research developed rapid, inexpensive survey methods to assess fishery performance and conducted extensive creel census studies across 20 representative waters (7 lakes, 13 streams). Key findings demonstrate that anglers harvested an average of 83% of stocked trout in lakes and 73% in streams, with harvest occurring rapidly (50% within 3.6 days in streams, 6.6 days in lakes). The study revealed that angling effort adjusts proportionally to stocking levels, maintaining relatively constant catch-per-hour rates regardless of stocking intensity. Critical management insights include the superiority of lakes over small streams for catchable trout programs, the problem of "truck following" caused by oversized plants, and formulas for calculating optimal plant sizes to maximize recreational benefits while minimizing costs.

## Background & Description

California's trout fishing program expanded dramatically following World War II when the [[Wildlife Conservation Board]] provided over $4 million for hatchery renovation and expansion. Production of catchable-sized trout increased fourfold to approximately 7,000,000 fish annually, distributed from southern California to roadside waters throughout the state. The program addressed heavy angling pressure that had depleted wild trout populations in accessible waters.

The study employed marked fish (fin-clipped or subcutaneously tagged) planted following routine procedures. Test fish averaged 5 per pound (3-6 per pound range). Three inventory methods were developed: (1) creel checks for tagged trout only, (2) complete angler checks, and (3) combined partial creel census with angler use counts. The Leslie-Davis method (1939) was applied to estimate total harvest by plotting daily catch per hour against cumulative catch, with the X-intercept representing total harvest and the slope indicating instantaneous total mortality rate per angler hour.

Study waters included diverse systems: [[Big Bear Lake]], [[Huntington Lake]], [[Convict Lake]], [[Lake Arrowhead]], [[East Carson River]], [[Kern River]], [[Kings River]], [[Feather River]], and numerous other lakes and streams across California counties.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Catchable trout fisheries created dramatically different ecological dynamics compared to wild trout systems. Angling intensities on stocked waters were extraordinarily high: lake effort ranged from 0.6 to 58.8 angler hours per surface acre daily (median 3.5), while stream effort ranged from 41.0 to 314.9 angler hours per mile daily (median 102.5). In contrast, comparable wild trout fisheries experienced intensities of only 0.06-0.388 hours per acre for lakes and 1.2-4.6 hours per mile for streams.

Environmental factors affecting catchability included water size, temperature, turbidity, weather, natural food availability, stream gradient, lake depth, and cover. Catchability averaged 7 times greater in streams (mean 10.71 × 10⁻⁴) than lakes (mean 1.62 × 10⁻⁴), with higher values in smaller waters. The catchability remained remarkably stable within individual waters across multiple plants and years, indicating that environmental conditions rather than fish or angler characteristics primarily determined vulnerability to harvest.

Trout dispersal patterns differed markedly between habitats. In lakes, planted trout dispersed rapidly (1-2 days to distribute throughout the water body), except when stocked near heavy inflows where fish aligned to current and remained localized. In streams, movement was minimal—most recoveries occurred within 0.25 miles of planting sites even after six months. Downstream drift occurred primarily when fish were in poor condition or when planted in extremely cold water (<50°F), though the latter was not consistently observed.

Water temperature effects were documented: at [[Castle Lake]], 43% and 9.1% of catchable trout survived to the year after planting in lightly-fished conditions, with 3.7% and 5.5% harvested 3-5 years post-stocking, demonstrating much lower natural mortality than observed in heavily-fished study waters.

## Key Findings

**Harvest Rates and Timing:**
- 17 of 20 test waters met the 50% minimum harvest required by Commission policy
- Lakes averaged 83% harvest; streams averaged 73% harvest
- Harvest was rapid: 50% of eventual catch taken within 3.6 days (streams) and 6.6 days (lakes)
- 75% harvest required average of 6.5 days (streams) and 11 days (lakes)
- Trout 8.0-8.9 inches consistently showed ~15% greater returns than 7.0-7.9 inch fish

**Mortality and Catchability:**
- Instantaneous fishing mortality rate per angler hour (catchability) was the critical management parameter
- Stream catchability: 2.6 × 10⁻⁴ to 87.8 × 10⁻⁴ (average 10.71 × 10⁻⁴)
- Lake catchability: 0.48 × 10⁻⁴ to 3.5 × 10⁻⁴ (average 1.62 × 10⁻⁴)
- Catchability remained stable within waters across multiple plants and years
- High unaccounted mortality rates (often 10-25 times natural mortality) attributed primarily to undetected angling mortality (poaching, expert anglers avoiding census, hooking mortality, underestimated effort)

**Angling Effort and Quality:**
- Angling effort was proportional to stocking levels—fish attract fishermen, not vice versa
- Average catch per angler hour: streams 0.94 (range 0.63-1.56), lakes 0.60 (range 0.40-0.75)
- No relationship between stocking rate and catch per hour; effort adjusted proportionally
- Three trout must be stocked in streams for every two in lakes to provide equal angling days
- Weekend-to-weekday effort ratios varied greatly (0.87 to 4.27, median 1.50)

**Plant Size and Truck Following:**
- "Truck following" occurred when plants were too large, causing extreme fluctuations in fish abundance and angling quality
- Plant-to-plant survival of ~0.50 prevented drops in angling effort and discouraged truck following
- Optimal plant size formula developed: R = (C/h ÷ c) × (−log_e s), where R = plant size, C/h = catch per hour, c = catchability, s = desired survival
- Small streams required very small plants (often <1,000 fish per mile) for satisfactory fisheries
- Lakes showed more stable populations (mean plant-to-plant survival 0.56) than streams (0.26)

**Distribution Among Anglers:**
- Distribution of catch was no better than in wild fisheries
- 35-70% of anglers caught nothing
- 50% of catch taken by <10% of fishermen
- At [[Erskine Creek]], 6% of anglers caught 50% of harvest
- Most successful anglers were local residents familiar with planting schedules
- Most successful anglers expended 52% of effort on day of or day after plant vs. 32% for unsuccessful anglers

**Management Implications:**
- Lakes superior to small streams for catchable programs due to: higher harvest rates, more stable populations, less truck following, greater capacity for angling effort
- Small roadside streams least suitable despite being commonly stocked
- Planting intervals should contain equal angling effort, not equal time periods
- Scattering plants in lakes unnecessary; avoid planting near heavy inflows
- Stream distribution critical—poor access and rough terrain make good distribution difficult and expensive
- Program expansion should favor lakes and larger streams over small streams

**Economic Considerations:**
- Annual cost: $1,366,000 (fiscal year 1962-63) for ~7 million fish
- Proper plant sizing maximizes recreation per fish stocked
- Waters with high overall catch per hour indicate fish not providing maximum recreation
- Frequent small plants more effective than infrequent large plants but increase distribution costs

**Movement Patterns:**
- Lake dispersal: 1-2 days for complete distribution
- Stream movement minimal: 80% of recoveries within 0.25 miles of planting site
- Upstream movement documented in [[Truckee River]] with fall-spawning Idaho strain (23-62% caught upstream)
- Downstream movement in wide rivers (>50 feet): fish crossed to opposite bank within 1-2 days
- [[Lake Pillsbury]] showed 12% downstream migration into [[Eel River]] during spilling conditions
