# Mitochondrial DNA Diversity within and among Populations of Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) as Determined by Restriction Endonuclease Analysis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Dowling]], [[Minckley]], and [[Marsh]] (Copeia, 1996) examined geographic structuring of genetic variation in the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]], a catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] of western North America. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) from 143 specimens was screened with eight restriction endonucleases, revealing 38 haplotypes. Most genetic variation lay within rather than among populations, and no geographic structure was found among haplotype networks. The authors conclude that the sampled fish are remnants of a single formerly widespread population, and that conservation should treat the species as one management unit.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large-bodied catostomid (> 500 mm SL) with long lifespan (> 50 years), historically occupying large rivers and floodplain habitats across the Colorado River basin, including the Gila River basin. Human-induced impacts, including mainstem dams, flow modification, and introduced exotic species, have extirpated it from much of its range and reduced remaining populations. The species was listed as endangered by the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1991.

The authors frame genetic structure as a key consideration for managing endangered species. Two competing expectations are highlighted: that wide-ranging, slow-water fish with high dispersal capacity would show little structure, and that fidelity to spawning sites and isolation in historical drainages could produce local divergence and loss of unique adaptations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Colorado River region underwent major Tertiary tectonism and dynamic climate cycles, with hot, dry periods that fragmented habitats and wet (pluvial) periods that connected drainages. Such alternation is expected to have isolated biota episodically, producing geographic genetic partitioning. The authors note that the Pleistocene climate, including a glacial maximum near 18,000 years ago, likely shaped population histories through range contraction and recolonization.

Remaining populations were estimated at fewer than 1,000 adults in the upper Green River basin and fewer than 25,000 in Lake Mohave, while populations in the Gila River basin were extirpated or too small to estimate. Species are cold-water adapted, and reproduction and early life stages are sensitive to temperature, with summer temperatures above 20°C limiting recruitment in warm, downstream reaches.

## Key Findings

- **Sampling:** Tissue samples were collected from Lake Mohave (n = 28), Lake Mead (n = 15), Lake Powell (n = 23), Green-Yampa rivers (n = 20), and the upper Colorado River near Grand Junction (n = 29). The Little Colorado River sample (n = 12) included putative hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and was excluded from the diversity analyses.
- **Methods:** mtDNA was cleaved with eight restriction enzymes (BanI, BstUI, HinfI, HinPI, MboI, NheI, ScrFI, TaqI), producing 38 haplotypes; two analysis subsets were used because not all fish yielded complete profiles.
- **Within-population diversity:** Haplotype diversity was highest in Lake Mohave and lowest in the upper Colorado River, declining progressively upstream. Lake Mohave showed haplotype diversity values of approximately 0.61 to 0.98 across sample sets, and the upper Colorado River had the lowest values.
- **Among-population structure:** Most variation was within populations. Genetic partitioning among populations was small and not significantly different from zero under jackknife and permutation tests, and no geographic pattern appeared in the phylogenetic or phenetic analyses.
- **Hybridization:** Some Little Colorado River fish carried flannelmouth sucker mtDNA, indicating introgression, with hybridization more common than in other sampled sites.
- **Interpretation:** The authors favored a cline in population size, with a dominant Lake Mohave haplotype (ABADA'AAA) becoming less common upstream and one haplotype fixed in the upper Colorado River. Two hypotheses were considered: nonequilibrium recolonization from a southern lower-basin refugium, or a climate-linked cline in population size at equilibrium. Serial bottlenecks during post-glacial expansion were also suggested.
- **Management conclusion:** Because historical gene exchange appears sufficient to prevent strong divergence, the authors recommend managing the razorback sucker as a single conservation unit throughout its range, while noting uncertainty about local adaptation and the need for adequate brood stock for any discrete-stock approach.
