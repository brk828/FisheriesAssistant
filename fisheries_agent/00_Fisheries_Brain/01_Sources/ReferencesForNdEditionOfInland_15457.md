# Reference List: California Fish and Aquatic Ecosystem Literature

#ichthyology #marine-biology #california-freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is not a research study. It is the closing reference section (printed pages 1109–1271) of a larger review of California fishes and aquatic ecosystems. It lists several thousand alphabetically ordered citations, from Aasen (1998) to Young (1994). The entries cover fish taxonomy, life history, genetics, habitat use, water quality, invasive species, fisheries management, and conservation status. The collection centers on native and introduced fishes of the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary]], the Central Valley, the [[Clear Lake]] basin, the [[Lake Tahoe]] region, the Great Basin, and the [[Salton Sea]] and desert springs. No new data are reported. The value of the document lies in its map of the literature on which the parent review rests.

## Background & Description

The section runs from page 1109 to 1271 of the source numbering, and the scanned copy contains about 163 page images. Entries are arranged by first author, from Aasen to Young. They include journal articles, agency reports, theses and dissertations, unpublished consultant reports, popular articles, and historical works from the 1850s onward. Several entries are early taxonomic descriptions by Ayres, Girard, Jordan, Evermann, and Snyder. Others are modern status reviews, recovery plans, and listings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The references repeatedly connect fish declines to human-driven change. Recurring themes include:

- **Water management and flow:** Studies address the effects of Delta pumping and channel changes, winter floods, flow regime, reservoir drawdowns, and instream flow requirements. Several entries focus on [[Instream Flow]] criteria for microhabitat use.
- **Temperature and dissolved oxygen:** Many citations report thermal tolerances, critical thermal maxima, hypoxia responses, and salinity tolerances for species such as [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Sacramento Blackfish]], and young [[Chinook Salmon]].
- **Contaminants:** Work on agricultural discharge and striped bass, subsurface drainwater toxicity to salmon, selenium effects on mosquitofish, and pesticide impacts on aquatic biota.
- **Invasive species:** Introductions of mosquitofish, shad, silversides, cichlids, gobies, and bullfrogs are documented. Many entries assess their ecological effects on native fish, particularly through predation, competition, and habitat displacement. See [[Invasive Species]].
- **Climate variability:** Some entries link El Niño events and climate change to striped bass declines and salmon production.
- **Genetics and taxonomy:** Allozyme, mitochondrial DNA, and microsatellite studies clarify population structure and hybridization in salmonids, sticklebacks, suckers, and sturgeon.

## Key Findings

The reference list supports several conclusions that the parent review likely develops:

1. **Native fish decline is well documented.** Entries such as [[Moyle and Williams 1990]] ("Loss of biodiversity in the temperate zone") and [[Moyle and Nichols 1974]] describe a long-term decline of California's native fish fauna.
2. **Introduced species are a major focus.** [[Dill and Cordone 1997]] ("History and status of introduced fishes in California, 1871–1996," 414 pp.) is the most comprehensive regional compilation on the topic. Other entries address the establishment and effects of introduced species in specific habitats.
3. **Estuarine fish declines have multiple, interacting causes.** [[Bennett and Moyle 1996]] ("Where have all the fishes gone?") and [[Stevens et al. 1985]] on striped bass decline suggest that water diversions, contaminants, food limitation, and climate interact.
4. **Conservation status is a central concern.** Key policy documents include [[Moyle and Yoshiyama 1992]] (a California aquatic biota protection plan, 222 pp.), [[Moyle et al. 1995]] (*Fish Species of Special Concern of California*, 2nd ed., 272 pp.), and [[Moyle and Yoshiyama 1994]] on a five-tiered protection approach.
5. **Chinook salmon status has been reviewed extensively.** [[Yoshiyama et al. 1998]] reports on the historical abundance and decline of Central Valley chinook, and [[Myers et al. 1998]] reviews chinook status across Washington, Idaho, Oregon, and California.
6. **Foundational taxonomic references are included.** These include [[Hubbs et al. 1979]] ("List of the fishes of California"), [[Moyle 1980]] (*Atlas of North American Freshwater Fishes* entries for California taxa), [[Moyle and Cech 1999]] (*Fishes: An Introduction to Ichthyology*, 4th ed.), and [[Moyle and Ellison 1991]], which presents a conservation-oriented classification of California's inland waters.
7. **Physiological and behavioral studies inform management.** Thermal and salinity tolerances, microhabitat selection, and predator-prey interactions are repeatedly tested in these studies and can be applied to flow and habitat decisions.

**Limitations:** The document contains no original data, sample sizes, or site-level measurements. Statistical findings can only be traced to the individual cited studies. Some entries contain incomplete citations or OCR artifacts, and some are marked "in press" or "unpublished."
