# Colorado River Basin Gila Taxonomy Project: Issues and Proposed Methods

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a Phase I/Phase II planning package (Parts 1–3, 1989–1990) prepared by [[Wayne C. Starnes]] of the [[Smithsonian Institution]]'s Division of Fishes under a cooperative agreement with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). It addresses whether the three large-river chubs of the Colorado River basin, [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*), are distinct species, whether intermediate "problematic" forms reflect hybridization or intraspecific variation, and what criteria can identify species and hybrids non-lethally. Part 1 reviews taxonomy, distribution, researchers and about 26 collections; Part 2 sets management concerns, priority research needs and a multi-method research plan; Part 3 gives sampling guidelines and a locality table. The document is a planning and reference statement, not a completed study.

## Background & Description

The *Gila* complex comprises medium-to-large cyprinids of western North America. The Colorado basin forms were historically treated variously as species, subspecies, or a "*robusta* superspecies" (Smith et al., 1979). Key taxonomic history includes:

- *G. robusta* described by [[Baird and Girard]] (1853) from the Zuni River, a locality later judged probably erroneous; *G. elegans* described from the lower Colorado.
- *G. cypha* described by Miller (1946) from Grand Canyon, a species that had evaded detection for about 100 years of western exploration.
- *G. elegans* elevated to species status by Holden and Stalnaker (1970) after being subsumed under *robusta* by Miller (1945).
- *G. intermedia* (lower Gila basin) was recognized as a species by Rinne (1976) and Minckley (1973), though some workers treated it as a subspecies of *robusta*.
- Tributary forms *G. r. seminuda* (Virgin River), *G. r. jordani* (Ash Springs, Nevada, pluvial White River) and *G. r. grahami* (lower Gila) have been variously ranked and remain unresolved.

Morphological separation has relied on criteria such as nuchal hump depth (Lee et al. and Valdez, 1985), meristics, and principal components analysis. Genetic tools applied to the group include allozymes (Rosenfeld and Wilkinson, 1989), mtDNA restriction analysis, karyotypes/NOR patterns and hematology (Berry, 1984). Roughly 1,600 museum lots of Colorado basin *Gila* material were located across the surveyed institutions, with about 473 lots categorized in the index.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Declines and fragmentation.** The document links taxonomic confusion to habitat alteration. Impoundments (Flaming Gorge, Glen Canyon, Navajo Reservoir) and fish-control poisonings (e.g., Flaming Gorge area 1962; Upper Little Colorado tributaries 1939–1960) reduced populations. Bonytail were extirpated from most of the lower basin by about 1960 except in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]], and had essentially disappeared from the upper Green after the closure of Flaming Gorge Dam.

**San Juan River.** Following closure of [[Navajo Reservoir]] (c. 1962), roundtail chub were initially abundant in the reservoir: 2,084 were collected 1962–1968, peaking at 727 (40.4% of catch) in 1965. Numbers then declined, and electrofishing in September 1988 at the Los Pinos and San Juan arms failed to yield the species. Mainstem San Juan records became rare (36 specimens 1963–1968). Colorado squawfish ([[Colorado Squawfish]]) recovery was documented with 25 fish collected in 1987–1988, including young-of-year cohorts indicating reproduction in Utah and New Mexico.

**Razorback sucker.** Historic reports from the San Juan were largely traced to misidentified roundtail chub. A 1976 record of two adults from an irrigation pond near Bluff, Utah, and a 1988 spawning aggregation of 10 adults at Piute Farms Marina on Lake Powell were noted.

**Community composition (San Juan, 1987–1989).** Red shiner, a nonnative species, dominated numerically (e.g., 27.3% of 64,415 specimens in the 1987–1989 summary). Native taxa including flannelmouth and bluehead suckers were abundant. Roundtail chub were very rare (about 14 specimens in one summary).

**Habitat requirements.** Humpback chub prefer canyon reaches; juvenile humpbacks were microhabitat-characterised in the Green River. Young-of-year Colorado squawfish were found in backwaters at 20–28 °C, with hatching estimated from water temperature via [[Haynes et al. 1984]] and [[Balmes et al. 1984]] equations.

**Mexican populations.** Robusta-like chubs occur in Gulf of California tributaries (Yaqui, Mayo, Fuerte, Sinaloa, Sonora). Their taxonomic status is unresolved, and canyon-adapted forms may exist there.

## Key Findings

- **Central questions (USFWS):** (1) Are roundtail, bonytail and humpback chubs three distinct species? (2) Is genetic and morphological variation adequately described? (3) Has there been identifiable change in genetic or morphological characteristics over time, and how is it related to environmental perturbation? (4) What key characters separate the species, and can definitive, non-lethal criteria be developed? (5) What degree of genetic integrity must be protected to preserve *Gila* spp. in the Colorado River system?
- **Hybridization hypotheses conflict.** Holden and Stalnaker (1970) and Smith (1983) favored hybridization and "coalescence" driven by habitat alteration. Rosenfeld and Wilkinson (1989) reported biochemical evidence of extensive *cypha × robusta* hybridization in Desolation and Westwater canyons. Douglas et al. (1989) and Tyus and Karp (1989) found little hybridization in the relatively unaltered Yampa River. Morphometric analysis (Rosenfeld, unpublished) argued that hybrids are rare and intraspecific variation may explain intermediates.
- **Hybrid frequency in the Yampa.** Karp and Tyus reported only three "intermediate" specimens among more than 1,200 examined.
- **Priority research needs (in order):** (1) develop non-lethal techniques; (2) comprehensive genetic and morphological study of mainstem Colorado populations and the Grand Canyon; (3) comparative study of tributary populations across the basin, including the Virgin River and San Juan system; (4) study of larval and juvenile *Gila*; (5) study of Mexican populations (Yaqui, Mayo, Fuerte); (6) genetic evaluation of hatchery stock versus wild populations.
- **Proposed methods:** morphometrics (including video imagery and qualitative characters), allozyme electrophoresis on about 5 individuals per population, mtDNA restriction analysis, karyotyping with NOR and C-banding, and non-lethal tissue sampling (fin clips, blood).
- **Effective population size and genetic bottlenecks** require estimation, particularly for hatchery broodstock (Dexter National Fish Hatchery). Minckley et al. (1989) documented 45 allozyme loci in hatchery bonytail, with heterozygosity comparable to wild western minnows despite a restricted Lake Mohave broodstock.
- **Museum resources:** Largest holdings include the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] (Dexter/USFWS, Fort Collins) collection, the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia, the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, Tulane University, the U.S. National Museum (USNM, including the holotype of *G. cypha* and types of several nominal species), and the University of New Mexico Museum of Southwestern Biology (58 lots, the San Juan's largest). Some older collections were preserved in alcohol rather than formalin and may yield DNA.
- **Sampling plan:** target localities include Green River, Yampa, White, Dolores, Gunnison, Virgin, Moapa, Pluvial White, Bill Williams, Upper Little Colorado, San Juan, Verde, Salt, Santa Cruz, San Pedro and Mexican drainages. Sample sizes range from 10 to 150 adults per locality; juveniles typically 20–100.
- **Status:** the document is a FINAL DRAFT (5 March 1990) for Review Panel comment. No taxonomic decisions are reported; the research phase (Phase II) was to be launched through Requests for Proposals, with synthesis expected about three years later.
