# Desert Pupfish Recovery Plan (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's 1993 recovery plan for the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) sets out the actions needed to downlist the Colorado River form of this endangered fish, and to protect its two other forms. The plan was prepared by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) and Donald W. Sada (Bishop, California) for Region 2, with help from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the Tonto National Forest. The document is dated September 1993 and runs to 67 pages.

The species is listed as endangered throughout its range. Natural Colorado River-form populations have been extirpated from Arizona. The remaining natural populations are in California, the Colorado River Delta and other Mexican sites. The [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*C. m. eremus*) survives in one modified spring at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. A third, undescribed form in the Rio Sonoyta is of unknown but probably very limited distribution.

Downlisting of the Colorado River form is expected in 2009 if criteria are met. Delisting is not considered feasible in the foreseeable future. Downlisting of the Quitobaquito form appears unattainable. The total estimated cost is 1,272 thousand dollars (in $000s), excluding acquisition costs.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described by Baird and Girard (1853) from the San Pedro River, Arizona. Two recognized subspecies are present in the U.S.: *C. m. macularius* and *C. m. eremus*. An undiagnosed form occurs in the Rio Sonoyta. *C. m. californiensis* and *Lucania browni* were synonymized with *C. m. macularius*.
- **Morphology:** Small, chubby fish; adult males are laterally compressed and turn bright blue with yellow-to-orange caudal fins during breeding. Females and juveniles are silvery with narrow vertical dark bars.
- **Genetics:** Allozyme work by [[Turner 1983]] on six populations found low inter-population differentiation, with heterozygosity values within the range reported for the killifish *Aphanius dispar*.
- **Taxonomic relevance:** The species is among the best-studied cyprinodontids, with a large body of work on behavior, physiology and genetics (see [[Cyprinodontidae]]).
- **Breeding:** Males defend territories in water usually less than 1 m deep. Females lay single eggs, and the species shows no parental care. Eggs hatch in about 10 days at 20°C. Females can produce 50 to more than 800 eggs in a season in the laboratory.
- **Growth and lifespan:** Maximum length is about 7.5 cm. Quitobaquito fish averaged 29.6 mm at age 1, 40.2 mm at age 2 and 48 mm at age 3. Lifespan in the wild varies from under one year to about three years.
- **Diet:** Opportunistic omnivores that eat algae, detritus, invertebrates and plant material. Adults show pit digging, a feeding behavior described by Minckley and Arnold (1969).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical range.** The species historically occupied the Gila River basin below about 1,500 m, the lower Colorado River, Rio Sonoyta, Puerto Penasco, and the Laguna Salada basin. It colonized the Salton Sea after the early-1900s flooding of the Salton Sink.

**Tolerances.** The fish survives water temperatures up to 45°C, dissolved oxygen of 0.1 to 0.4 mg/L, and salinity of 68 g/L, about twice that of seawater. It also tolerates abrupt salinity changes of 10 to 15 g/L and temperature shifts of 22 to 26°C that are lethal to most fishes.

**Co-occurring native fishes.** Historic associates include the Sonoran topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), Gila chub, speckled dace, desert sucker, longfin dace and Colorado River big-river fishes such as the razorback sucker and woundfin. Longfin dace was the only native fish that may have shared habitat with pupfish at Quitobaquito.

**Causes of decline.**
- Habitat loss from groundwater pumping, channel erosion and drying of springs.
- Habitat modification from impoundment, diversion, channelization, livestock grazing and mining.
- Non-native fishes, which are the greatest threat. These include [[Western Mosquitofish]], sailfin molly, [[Largemouth Bass]], African cichlids (*Oreochromis* and *Tilapia*), red shiner, carp and channel catfish.
- Bullfrogs (*Rana catesbeiana*), which nearly eliminated a population in an Arizona State University pond with fewer than 20 adults.
- Aerial pesticide drift, which contributed to the Quitobaquito decline.
- Mercury contamination near the Cerro Prieto geothermal field, where a maximum fish level of 0.14 µg/g dry weight was below the hazardous threshold for humans.
- Saltcedar, which may reduce water in small habitats through evapotranspiration.

**Land and water context.** Most U.S. populations are on Federal land or private land managed for conservation. Mexican habitats are mainly state, private or ejido land. Water rights and supply must be secured habitat by habitat.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objectives and criteria**
- Downlisting of *C. m. macularius* requires secure natural populations, re-established populations, a genetic exchange protocol and monitoring.
- Five metapopulations at 12 known natural locations must be secured: Salton Sink, Rio Sonoyta, El Doctor and Santa Clara Slough, Laguna Salada, and Cerro Prieto.
- A viable population is at least 500 overwintering adults (or existing numbers if greater), in a normal sex ratio, with self-sustaining reproduction.
- Replicate populations count only after persisting at least 10 years.
- Formal protection requires water rights and habitat held by a qualifying agency, or a binding agreement longer than 25 years.
- Downlisting is expected to take about 15 years after plan approval, with a target year of 2009.

**Three-tier re-establishment**
- Tier 1: extant natural populations, which receive the highest protection.
- Tier 2: replicates in the most natural available habitat, targeting 45 populations in Arizona, 9 in California (3 replicates of each natural population), 9 in the Colorado Delta, and 3 in Rio Sonoyta (from tier 2 or 3).
- Tier 3: quasi-natural refugia. Genetic exchange runs one way, from tier 1 to tier 2 and from tier 2 to tier 3, to avoid contaminating natural stocks.
- The San Pedro River was named a priority re-establishment site.

**Main actions (Tasks 1–7)**
1. Protect natural populations and habitats, including land ownership mapping and water acquisition.
2. Re-establish populations following the tiered specifications.
3. Establish a refugium of the Quitobaquito form from at least 500 fish of approximately 1:1 sex ratio, located near Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument.
4. Develop a genetic exchange protocol, extending allozyme work and adding mitochondrial DNA.
5. Monitor twice a year (before spawning and in late summer–autumn) and genetically every 5 years, sampling about 50 fish (25 males, 25 females).
6. Determine factors affecting persistence, including habitat criteria, non-native control and life-history data.
7. Run information and education programs in English and Spanish.

**Costs (in $000s, excluding acquisition)**
- Totals by task: 283 (Task 1), 285 (Task 2), 100 (Task 3), 224 (Task 4), 276 (Task 5), 104 (Task 6). The grand total is 1,272.
- The 1994 cost is 122 and the 1995 cost is 184, the highest annual figure. The 1999–2008 period is 525.
- Costs for Mexico and for re-establishment sites with undetermined ownership are not assigned.

**Transplant status**
- More than 100 transfers of the Colorado River form have occurred. At least 30 non-aquarium populations remain, including some of questionable genetic purity.
- Of 20 failed populations documented in 1989, 8 failed from habitat desiccation, 2 from exotic fish invasion, 1 was renovated, and 9 failed for unknown reasons.
- Populations of questionable genetic purity at Bog Hole, Finley Tank, Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum and Tohono Chul Park should be destroyed, a recommendation that drew significant comment.

**Public review**
- Technical review drew 7 letters. The public comment period drew 18 letters.
- Major issues raised include the one-way genetic exchange, the destruction of impure stocks, the role of non-native fishes at the Salton Sea, and the use of pupfish for mosquito control (rejected as secondary to recovery goals).
- The Service declined to designate experimental non-essential populations under Section 10(j) for now.

**Conservation Concerns**
- Plans assume that all sites require ongoing management. The plan's authors acknowledge that most populations will need active intervention over time.
- The Salton Sea populations are notably threatened, including by contaminants such as selenium and DDT, and by the proposed Eagle Mountain train-based landfill route past the Salt Creek population.
