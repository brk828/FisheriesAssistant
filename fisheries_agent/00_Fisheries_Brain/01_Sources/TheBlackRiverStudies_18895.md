# The Black River Studies (University of Missouri Studies, Vol. XXVI, No. 2, 1953)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

The Black River Studies is a seven-part 1953 monograph by [[Missouri Conservation Commission]] and [[University of Missouri]] Wildlife Research Unit biologists. It combines a pre-impoundment survey of a Missouri Ozark stream (Black River, 1947) with post-impoundment monitoring of the same reach after [[Clearwater Lake]] filled (1948–1950). The work covers basin physiography, [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]], small fishes, larger fishes, growth of five species, management and creel data, and an overall appraisal. The studies describe the river as a [[Smallmouth Bass]] stream whose fish and bottom fauna resemble trout streams, and document how impoundment reduced biodiversity and shifted dominance toward species that formerly lived in river backwaters.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Black River rises in the Ozark Highland of southeastern Missouri, joins the White River system, and drains 8,675 sq mi total, of which about 1,740 sq mi lie in Missouri. The main channel is 291 miles long.
- **Geology and soils:** Upper watershed is channeled in Gasconade and Potosi limestones with chert-rich, low-fertility Clarksville stony loam. Downstream lies the Southeast Missouri Lowland, dominated by poorly drained Waverly silt loam.
- **Impoundment:** Clearwater Dam, begun 1940, inundated a seven-mile stretch to form the 1,650-acre Clearwater Lake, which filled from June 3, 1948; conservation pool was reached July 4, 1948.
- **Study design:** The pre-impoundment phase was planned for two years but ran a three-month summer session at middle stations; other stations were sampled for six months in 1947 and 1948. Post-impoundment data cover 1948–1950.
- **Authorship:** [[John L. Funk]], [[Edward M. Lowry]], [[Mercer H. Patriarche]], [[Robert G. Martin]], [[Robert S. Campbell]], and [[Timothy R. O'Connell Jr.]]

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat.** The upper river has long riffles alternating with long pools and a fall of about 5 ft per mile; the lower river is wider, deeper, more turbid, and largely riffle-free. Table 1 gives elevations from 660 ft at the West/Middle Fork junction to 240 ft at the Missouri–Arkansas line. Mean discharge varies from about 900 cfs at Leeper to 8,500 cfs at Black Rock, Arkansas. Leeper's recorded discharge ranged from a minimum of 133 cfs to a maximum of 78,400 cfs (125,000 cfs known, March 1904).

**Water quality.** Upper and middle stations had turbidity under 7 ppm in low water and 16–24 ppm at the lower station. Summer surface temperatures reached about 82°F. Bicarbonate hardness ranged 64–134 ppm, and dissolved oxygen was generous because of turbulence.

**Climate and land use.** Mean annual temperature is 50–58°F; precipitation is 44–50 inches per year. Forest covers 75–85% of the upper watershed after logging and burning. Overgrazing and burning increased run-off and chert erosion.

**Plankton and plants.** Net plankton was sparse (maximum 80 organisms per liter), dominated by phytoplankton (*Oscillatoria*, *Navicula*, *Spirogyra*). Rooted plants were largely absent from the main upper channel.

**Benthos.** Bottom fauna were mainly insect larvae, with 42 taxonomic groups across 54 river and 283 reservoir samples. Riffles supported roughly twice the standing crop of pools.

**Reservoir changes.** Benthos fell from 39 groups in the river to about 23 in the lake; pondweed (*Potamogeton pusillus*) beds in 1949 were absent in 1950 after high water. Deep clay-pit areas (35–50 ft) became oxygen-depleted in late summer.

**Fish community.** Sixty-eight species were recorded, including 20 minnows. Minnows made up more than 77% of small fishes in 1947.

**Management context.** Stocking was erratic; over 1944–1948 about 112,312 largemouth bass, 35,590 smallmouth bass, and 92,400 bluegill were planted. Regulations trended toward liberalization, and gigging and snaring of suckers were permitted.

## Key Findings

**1. Benthos (O'Connell & Campbell)**
- River standing crop averaged 788 organisms and 455 mg dry weight per m²; riffles yielded 1,003 organisms versus 652 in pools.
- Black River rated as a Grade I stream by Hazzard's criterion; its production was judged average relative to 12 other rivers.
- Lake standing crop averaged 1,023 organisms (1948–1950 grand average) and 373 mg per m²; Culicidae, Chironomidae, and Oligochaeta dominated.
- Average production in Clearwater Lake was 3.3 lb dry weight per acre, comparable to Lake of the Ozarks (3.8 lb per acre) and below the 10-Connecticut-lake average of 8.3 lb per acre.

**2. Small fishes (Martin & Campbell)**
- 68 species were recorded in the 1947 river study, with 46 small-fish species collected directly.
- [[Bleeding Shiner]] (33.2% of minnows), [[Bigeye Chub]], [[Whitetail Shiner]], and [[Bigeye Shiner]] made up 75% of minnows seined.
- Lake colonization was governed by spawning success: brook silversides, bluntnose minnow, bluegill, and longear sunfish spawned in 1948 and together constituted 60% of the lake's small fishes.
- Bluegill rose from 17.8% (1948) to 47% (1949) and fell to about 11% (1950) as [[Gizzard Shad]] surged to about 58% of the 1950 collection.
- Species that spawned later in summer or that matured in a third growing season (white crappie, largemouth bass) appeared in quantity only in 1950.
- Lake species count fell from 58 identified river species (plus 10 assumed) to 49 species.

**3. Larger fishes (Funk & Campbell)**
- 34 species, 2,579 fish, and about 721 lb were collected across 1947–1948.
- Upper station: 1,536 fish weighing about 237 lb, 13 species; rock bass, longear sunfish, smallmouth bass, and yellow bullhead dominated.
- Middle stations (inundated section): 562 fish weighing 194 lb, 20 species; smallmouth bass, redhorses, longear sunfish, gizzard shad, rock bass, and hog sucker were important.
- Lower station: 479 fish weighing 286 lb, 25 species; gizzard shad, flathead catfish, redhorses, carp, longear sunfish, and largemouth bass dominated.
- Largemouth bass and bluegill at the upper station were judged to derive from unrecorded stocking.

**4. Growth and age (Patriarche & Lowry)**
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] (n = 332): 2.7 in at end of year 1, 9.3 in at end of year 3, 14.4 in at end of year 5; growth judged average for Missouri Ozark streams. Average age at 10 in was 3.6 years.
- [[Rock Bass]] (n = 585): 4.7 in at year 3, 7.3 in at year 5; slow growth, about four years to reach 6 in.
- [[Longear Sunfish]] (n = 475): 3.5 in at year 3, 5.1 in at year 5; about 1 in per year.
- [[Gizzard Shad]] (n = 218): 11.8 in at year 3 at middle station, 10.5 in at lower station.
- [[Green Sunfish]] (n = 83): 4.6 in at year 3 at the upper station; 1945 year class was exceptionally strong.

**5. Management and utilization (Funk)**
- Creel census yielded about 0.4 fish per hour in the upper Black (vs. 0.37 and 0.43 for the Ozark Highland area in 1947–1948).
- Suckers (47% in 1947, 36% in 1948), smallmouth bass (19% and 42%), and rock bass (14%) dominated the upper-Black catch.
- Smallmouth bass were more heavily exploited than in West Virginia streams (Surber & Seaman, 1949).
- Stocking was judged mostly ineffective except the 85,000 largemouth bass fingerlings placed in Clearwater Lake in 1948.
- Regulations were liberalized, with gigging and snaring for suckers deemed biologically sound.

**6. Appraisal and predictions (Campbell & Funk)**
- Upper Black resembles trout streams in channel form and benthos but has smallmouth bass as the top predator and a diverse forage base.
- Predictions for future Ozark reservoirs: bottom productivity unlikely to exceed the parent stream; early fish populations dominated by backwater species; establishment timing governed by spawning habits, life span, and filling rate.
