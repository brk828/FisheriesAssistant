# Washout Effects on a Sierra Foothill Stream Benthos and Pronotal Comb Variation in Squirrel and Chipmunk Fleas

#freshwater-ecology #benthic-macroinvertebrates #plecoptera #siphonaptera

## Executive Summary

This document contains two short scientific notes. The first, the main item, reports a year-long study (May 1973 to April 1974) of the benthic fauna of a single riffle in [[Squirrel Creek]], a foothill stream draining the [[Sierra Nevada]] in California. Autumn and winter storms caused repeated [[Washout]] events that reduced benthic standing crop by more than 95% between October and January. The disturbance reshaped trophic relations and community structure and appears to keep the riffle at a lower, intermediate level of diversity. Recovery depends on recruitment from newly hatched eggs and on autochthonous (in-stream) energy sources such as algal blooms.

The second note, on fleas, is only partly present in the excerpt. It compares pronotal comb spine numbers between the sexes of several flea species and relates these to field sex ratios. The introduction is included, but the results and discussion are not.

## Background & Description

**Benthic study (Squirrel Creek).** California receives more than 80% of its annual rainfall in November through March from Pacific storms. Lower-elevation streams respond quickly to these storms, and their discharge fluctuates sharply, producing washouts defined here as the downstream movement and transport of substrate materials during floods. Earlier work shows that flooding can strongly reduce stream invertebrate fauna. Organisms that lack deep gravel refuges are displaced into [[Catastrophic Drift]].

Squirrel Creek is an unpolluted, permanent stream that rises from springs near Grass Valley at about 710 m and flows 16.1 km westward to meet Deer Creek at 245 m. It drains a small, impervious watershed and is dominated by riffle-run habitat with widely spaced pools. The study riffle lies about 5 km above the confluence. It is about 17 m long in a channel roughly 6 m wide, with cobble (less than 10 cm diameter) over bedrock and compacted fines. Gradient in the middle third of the creek is 9 m/km, and the upper and lower reaches are steeper (over 37 m/km). Water temperature rarely exceeds 24 °C in summer and generally stays above 0 °C in winter.

Sampling used an open-ended cylindrical sampler (20 cm inside diameter) that enclosed 314 cm² of cobble substrate to about 10 cm depth, with fine material collected by dip net (0.27-mm mesh). Organisms were sorted, identified to the lowest feasible taxon (often genus, and family for [[Chironomidae]]), and measured by length class. Three samples per month were taken from May to October 1973, and 4 to 7 per month from November 1973 to April 1974. Community diversity was calculated with the [[Wilhm Diversity Index]], which is dimensionless, uses abundance data, and is relatively independent of sample size.

**Flea study.** The flea note focuses on pronotal comb spines, the rows of spines on the prothorax that are thought to help fleas lock onto host hair. Earlier work on the rabbit flea (*Cediopsylla simplex*) and cat flea (*Ctenocephalides felis felis*) found more pronotal spines in females than in males. Those differences were proposed to partly explain why females outnumber males among fleas collected off their hosts. The introduction also cites work showing that spacing of pronotal spines matches host hair diameter across 16 flea species. The two species studied in detail, *[[Orchopeas Howardii]]* (from the eastern gray squirrel and Virginia opossum) and *[[Megabothris Acerbus]]* (from chipmunks), lack genal combs, which makes them useful for testing the pronotal-comb hypothesis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and substrate.** The [[Hydrograph]] for May 1973 to April 1974 shows discharge peaks from November through April, each tied to rainfall. The study riffle had only a shallow layer (about 10 cm) of loose cobble over bedrock and compacted material. Little stable deep substrate was available, so high water produced heavy bedload movement and strong scour.
- **Canopy and primary production.** The riffle lacks a canopy. Each spring it develops a large bloom of the filamentous green alga [[Ulothrix]], which feeds herbivorous invertebrates. The bloom declines by April and leaves a large store of detritus through summer. Allochthonous (terrestrial) input appears minor for much of the year.
- **Pre-storm conditions.** Before the winter washouts, cobbles were coated with a thick film of diatoms and fine detritus. The first storms scoured much of this food layer away.
- **Regional comparisons.** Few comparable foothill studies exist. The authors note that other flood-impacted streams showed these reductions: Burns Run, Pennsylvania (Hoopes, 1974; numbers down 57% after Hurricane Agnes, with a comparable biomass loss); the River Endrick, Scotland, and Slope Creek, North Carolina (about 65% numerical reductions); and the Sagana River, Kenya (up to 83% reduction from seasonal flooding).
- **Flea hosts.** The flea species occur on squirrels, chipmunks, opossums and other small mammals. Host hair diameter and spacing of pronotal spines are central to the proposed locking mechanism.

## Key Findings

**Benthic study (Squirrel Creek)**

- **Standing crop and collapse.** Standing crop averaged about 37,000 organisms and 4.5 g dry weight/m² from May through October. November storms cut the number of benthic organisms by more than 85%, and biomass fell by more than 90%. By mid-January the fauna was below 4% of its October level, after three months of repeated floods.
- **Losses versus washout.** Autumnal emergence was not a major cause of the decline, because most late-flying insects had emerged before the October collection. Most of the October-to-November reduction was attributed to washout.
- **Recruitment buffering.** Hatching in late summer and autumn (for example *Antocha*, *Ephemerella*, *Epeorus*, *Glossosoma*, *Alloperla*) offset losses of late-flying taxa in September and October.
- **Taxon-specific vulnerability.** Trichoptera and Plecoptera suffered the greatest losses. Net-spinning caddisflies (*Hydropsyche* spp.) appeared vulnerable to scour, and stoneflies are poorly adapted to resist strong current. Free-swimming baetid mayflies and retreat-building pyralid larvae were least affected. Simuliid larvae were the only group to increase between October and November, possibly reflecting a winter-growing generation or better tolerance of current.
- **Size selectivity.** Aquatic insects longer than 4 mm made up about 10% of the fauna through summer. Washouts reduced this to about 3% in November and to under 1% by January, consistent with larger individuals being more prone to catastrophic drift.
- **Trophic restructuring.** Removal of diatoms and detritus affected detritivores and herbivores directly and higher trophic levels indirectly. By January, the carnivorous stonefly *[[Acroneuria Californica]]* made up 64% of total macrobenthic biomass, a temporary "inverted pyramid of biomass." With preferred prey scarce, the stonefly shifted toward other prey and detritus.
- **Low diversity.** Benthic diversity never exceeded 2.72 across the study. The authors cite values above 3.00 for clean water and for foothill streams without severe washouts, and values of 1.00 to 3.00 for intermediate conditions. Mean diversity before November was significantly higher than afterward (reported as significant at the 0.05 level). Diversity held steady through November and fell sharply by February.
- **Chironomid dominance.** Repeated November to January washouts, followed by weeks of stable flow, allowed winter generations of [[Chironomidae]] larvae to dominate the riffle. They made up 92% of total numbers in February, which explains the low February diversity.
- **Seasonal population pattern.** Most unaffected streams have their peak benthic density in autumn and winter. Squirrel Creek instead reaches its minimum in that period. The authors suggest that this annual disruption may keep the fauna less varied than it would otherwise be.
- **Recovery.** Recruitment from newly hatched eggs drives the spring rebound. In May, 72% of collected forms were early instars of 1 mm or less. Biomass rose nearly tenfold by June, with more than 79% of the gain from growth and recruitment of [[Hydropsyche]] larvae. Mean hydropsychid weight rose from 0.03 to 0.36 mg per individual, and density roughly tripled. In May, more than 95% of hydropsychids were 2 mm or less long; by June only 40% were. Simuliidae, [[Baetis]], and *Acroneuria* contributed a further 6%, 4% and 2% of the May-to-June gain, so four groups accounted for more than 90% of it.
- **Autochthonous energy.** Much of the benthic production appears to rely on the spring *[[Ulothrix]]* bloom, which provides readily available energy for herbivores. The authors plan a detailed energetics study of the riffle to clarify production dynamics.

**Flea study (partial text)**

- In field collections, female *Orchopeas h. howardii* had significantly more pronotal spines than males and were more numerous.
- *Megabothris acerbus* males and females had about equal pronotal spine numbers and occurred in nearly equal numbers on chipmunks.
- Structural variation appears consistent with the role of pronotal spines in lodging in host hair.
- Field sex ratios of *O. h. howardii*, *M. acerbus*, *C. simplex* and *C. f. felis* closely tracked the ratio of mean pronotal spine numbers in females to males.
