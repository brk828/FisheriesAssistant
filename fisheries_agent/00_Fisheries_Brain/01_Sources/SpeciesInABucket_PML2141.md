# Species in a Bucket: Saving the Owens Pupfish from Extinction

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a January 1993 issue of [[Natural History]] magazine. Most of its articles concern non-fish topics such as praying mantises, beetles, and natural history essays. Its one fish-focused item is a first-person essay, "Species in a Bucket," by retired fishery biologist [[Phil Pister]]. The essay describes his career with the [[California Department Of Fish And Game]] and his efforts to protect two endangered desert pupfishes, the [[Owens Pupfish]] and the [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]]. The central event is an August 1969 rescue at [[Fish Slough]], when Pister and colleagues moved the remaining Owens pupfish out of a drying pond. The essay argues that species conservation depends on personal values and that scientists and society often neglect it.

## Background & Description

**Author and context.** Edwin Philip (Phil) Pister is Executive Secretary of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] in Bishop, California. Before retiring in 1990, he worked as a district fishery biologist. He had specialized in [[Limnology]], the study of freshwater lakes, and was responsible for nearly a thousand water bodies stretching from the crest of the [[Sierra Nevada]] east to the Nevada state line. He kept more than ten thousand diary entries, summarized in pocket volumes, and the essay draws on them.

**Other career work.** The essay describes several efforts:
- A twenty-seven-year project to restore the [[California Golden Trout]] within the Golden Trout Wilderness, still in progress at the time of writing.
- Opposition to habitat-damaging commercial developments.
- Management of a reservoir fishery, which he describes as measured by tons of fish harvested.
- Work on [[Devil's Hole]], which led to a U.S. Supreme Court case.

**The pupfishes.** Pupfishes (family Cyprinodontidae) are small, territorial fish popular in aquaria. Several species evolved in isolated desert habitats of the [[Death Valley]] system in eastern California and western Nevada. The Owens pupfish (*Cyprinodon radiosus*) is the largest of the nine Death Valley pupfishes, occasionally reaching two inches. The Devil's Hole pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) rarely exceeds one inch.

**Species description and biology.**
- The Owens pupfish lives in shallow, warm marsh water that can freeze under an inch or two of ice in winter.
- The Devil's Hole pupfish lives in the upper reaches of a cavern pool at a constant 92°F.
- Pupfishes tolerate water above 100°F, and some survive about 113°F briefly. Daily temperature swings can reach 36°F.
- Some populations live in water with several times the salinity of seawater.
- All pupfishes are opportunistic feeders on immature insects and algae, and they are highly territorial.
- Pister notes that pupfish physiology (kidney function, temperature tolerance, adaptation) could be valuable research subjects, but he argues that they deserve protection regardless of their utility.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geological history.** During the Pleistocene pluvial periods, much of the [[Great Basin]] was covered by large freshwater lakes. As the climate warmed into the Holocene, these lakes shrank, leaving fishes isolated in a few permanent habitats. Pister notes that only the [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] basin in Coahuila, Mexico, has as many well-defined endemic desert fish populations as the Death Valley region.

**Devil's Hole.** Devil's Hole is a swimming-pool-sized opening into an underground aquifer, part of Death Valley National Monument. The Devil's Hole pupfish has been isolated from other pupfish populations for approximately 44,000 years. It lives in a pool about ten by fifty feet, probably the most confined habitat of any vertebrate in the world.

**Threats.** Pister identifies several causes of decline:
- Habitat change in the early twentieth century, when Owens Valley marshlands were drained or altered.
- Predatory introduced gamefish, especially [[Largemouth Bass]], to which the pupfish had no evolved defenses.
- Pumping of groundwater for ranching, which threatened Devil's Hole. The ranchers were drawing on an aquifer undisturbed since the Pleistocene.
- Unstable spring flows and loss of habitat through ongoing environmental disturbance.

**Protection milestones.**
- In 1964, researchers found a remnant Owens pupfish population in [[Fish Slough]], a desert marsh a few miles from Pister's home in Bishop. Early reintroductions were made into suitable habitats.
- The Owens pupfish was among the first fishes listed as endangered, a status it still holds.
- In 1976, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled to protect Devil's Hole from nearby ranching water use, under the [[Endangered Species Act]] framework that followed the 1973 Act.

## Key Findings

**The 1969 rescue.** The essay's central event is the near-extinction of the Owens pupfish in late summer 1969.
- The winter of 1968-69 brought heavy rains, but by August thick vegetation was transpiring large amounts of water. An unexplained drop in spring flow sped up the depletion of the pond.
- The pond, roughly room-sized, was almost completely dry when an assistant warned Pister that the species would be lost without immediate action.
- Pister and colleagues drove to the site (normally about fifteen minutes away) and removed 800 remaining individuals.
- They placed the fish in three wire mesh cages in a northwest channel of the slough, where the flow was already below two cubic feet per second.
- Their placement was flawed. The cages sat in eddies away from the main current, where velocity and dissolved oxygen were low. Overcrowding and rough handling on a hot afternoon caused many deaths.
- Only two buckets were available, because the others had already been taken back to town. The fish were moved in buckets with battery-powered [[Aerators]] to more favorable upstream conditions near the springheads, and the rescue succeeded.

**The diary record.** The diary entry for August 18, 1969, reads: "Transplanted Cyprinodon at Fish Slough; purchased alkaline D-cells, $2.00." Pister says this brief note summarizes a day that could have ended in the species' extinction.

**Lessons drawn.** Pister reflects on several points:
- Conservation depends on values, which he argues matter more than technology that often creates as many problems as it solves.
- The Owens pupfish's survival was a matter of chance, with a single misstep, such as stumbling on barbed wire, capable of causing extinction.
- Endangered species work provides only a temporary reprieve while aquatic habitats decline across North America.
- He cites [[Aldo Leopold]]'s "Land Ethic" from *A Sand County Almanac* (more than forty years old at the time), which placed humans as one species within the global ecosystem, and Leopold's principle that a thing is right when it preserves the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community.
- He answers the question "What good are they?" with the counterquery "What good are you?" and a Leopold corollary about keeping every cog in the machine.

**Conservation scientists and funding.** Pister criticizes complacency in the scientific community. He says many scientists are trapped in tenure and advancement pressures that favor quantity over quality, and that some assume someone else will take care of endangered species. He reports that at the 1992 annual meeting of the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists, a small share of the 385 research presentations focused on conservation. The exact percentage is illegible in the source text.

**Closing position.** Pister hopes public policy will be guided by Leopold's wisdom and that this will lead to a sustainable society. He describes the experience as humbling: biogeography and evolution explained why the pupfish lives where it does, while the 1969 rescue taught him why he was there. He ends with an image of himself holding an entire species in two buckets, with only himself standing between life and extinction.
