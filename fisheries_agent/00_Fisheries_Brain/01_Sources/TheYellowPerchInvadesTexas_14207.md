# The yellow perch invades Texas

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Percidae

## Executive Summary
This 1955 report by Dr. Clark Hubbs documents the first confirmed occurrence of the [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) in Texas waters, captured on June 12, 1954, in the Rio Grande approximately nine miles northwest of El Paso. The specimen likely originated from an established, successfully introduced population in New Mexican reservoirs upstream on the Rio Grande. The author concludes that despite this range extension, the species is unlikely to become established or ecologically significant in Texas due to unsuitable downstream habitat conditions, particularly high silt loads and intermittent flow.

## Background & Description
The [[Yellow Perch]] is native to quiet freshwater habitats of north-central and eastern United States and central Canada. It is taxonomically a "true perch" (family [[Percidae]]), characterized by a more or less cylindrical body shape, distinguishing it from many unrelated fish colloquially called "perch" in Texas.

- **Native range**: North-central/eastern US, central Canada.
- **Introduction history**: Widely introduced across the United States.
  - Introductions into south-central states, including all prior Texas attempts, had failed.
  - Introductions into western states (west of the Continental Divide) were notably more successful, including establishment in all Pacific Coast states and in New Mexican reservoirs along the Rio Grande.
- **Discovery event**: A single young yellow perch was seined on June 12, 1954, by Victor G. Springer and Dr. Clark Hubbs in the Rio Grande, nine miles northwest of El Paso.
- **Locational ambiguity**: At the capture site, the channelized Rio Grande crosses between Texas and New Mexico. The seine haul began in Texas and ended in New Mexico, leaving academic uncertainty as to whether the species had technically been caught "in Texas" — though the fish would have had to traverse roughly ten miles of Texas waters to reach the site from the New Mexican reservoir population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
Hubbs assesses the likelihood of the [[Yellow Perch]] establishing and spreading within Texas as very low, based on regional hydrology and water quality:

- **Habitat fragmentation**: The Rio Grande segment northwest of El Paso is isolated from other Texas fresh waters by many miles of dry riverbed.
- **Intermittent flow**: Between El Paso and Presidio, the Rio Grande frequently ceases to flow during dry seasons, and much of this stretch lacks persistent pools, precluding fish passage or survival.
- **Sedimentation/erosion**: East of Presidio, heavy soil erosion from overgrazed land has introduced high silt loads into the river, rendering the water largely uninhabitable for yellow perch or other "scaly game species" (i.e., species requiring clearer, quieter water).
- **Flow dynamics**: High-water periods, which might otherwise enable downstream dispersal, paradoxically carry even greater silt loads, further reducing habitat suitability.
- **Species habitat preference**: As a "quiet water fish," the yellow perch is poorly adapted to colonize the flowing, turbid waters that characterize much of the Rio Grande in this region.

Conclusion: the species is expected to remain restricted to the short, channelized/ditched segment of the Rio Grande northwest of El Paso, without broader ecological impact on Texas fisheries.

## Key Findings
- **First Texas record**: Confirmed capture of a young yellow perch in the Rio Grande on June 12, 1954 — the first documented occurrence of the species in Texas.
- **Source population**: Likely derived from a successfully introduced population in New Mexican reservoirs on the Rio Grande, with individuals dispersing downstream.
- **Predicted range limitation**: Environmental barriers (dry channel segments, high silt loads from overgrazing-driven erosion) are expected to prevent further spread into Texas.
- **Population dynamics in native range**: In its native range, yellow perch is often under-appreciated as a game fish due to:
  - High reproductive rates leading to overpopulation and stunted growth.
  - Insufficient food supply causing many individuals to mature at small, non-catchable sizes.
  - A self-perpetuating cycle where undersized adults continue to reproduce, compounding food scarcity.
  - Increased susceptibility to heavy parasite loads in stunted populations.
  - Where perch populations are naturally limited, they can provide quality fishing.
- **Nomenclature issue raised**: The addition of yellow perch to the Texas fish fauna highlights confusion in common naming conventions:
  - True perches (family [[Percidae]]) are cylindrical-bodied.
  - Texas anglers colloquially apply "perch" to unrelated slab-sided or deep-bodied fishes.
  - The "[[Rio Grande Perch]]" is more accurately a [[Cichlid]] (mojarra), not a true perch.
  - Other Texas fish called "perch" are more properly [[Sunfish]] or bream (family [[Centrarchidae]]), also not true perches.
- **Author's recommendation**: Calls for clarified, standardized common name usage to avoid taxonomic confusion between true perches and superficially similar but unrelated fishes (e.g., cichlids, sunfishes).
