# Realities of Providing Water for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River System

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #water-resources #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This 1981 symposium paper addresses the conflict between water development and endangered fish conservation in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] manages three endangered cyprinid species—[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)—while operating major water storage and delivery infrastructure. The document examines natural flow conditions, legal constraints from interstate compacts, and projected water depletions through 2030. Current consumptive use is approximately 4.0 million acre-feet (MAF) annually, representing 25% of natural flow, with projections reaching 5.8 MAF by 2030. The authors emphasize the need to balance human water demands with instream flow requirements for endangered fish survival.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] at [[Lee Ferry]] (below the [[Paria River]] confluence) has averaged slightly less than 15 MAF annually over 80 years, with annual flows ranging from 6 MAF to 24 MAF and instantaneous flows varying from 1,000 to 300,000 cubic feet per second (c.f.s.). Major tributaries including the [[San Juan River]], [[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], and [[Gunnison River]] exhibit erratic flows, with some drying completely during extreme drought periods. Sediment loads vary naturally from less than 1% at Lee Ferry to over 10% in lower reaches of the San Juan, [[Dirty Devil River]], [[Escalante River]], and Paria Rivers.

The three endangered fish species under consultation are all cyprinids native to the Colorado River system. All Bureau of Reclamation projects—planned, under construction, or operational—have undergone consultation with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to promote the continued existence of these species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River Storage Project]] and other multipurpose developments currently utilize approximately 25% of natural flow. Natural versus present-day (1980) flow distribution shows:

**Green River Basin**: Natural flow 36.2%, remaining flow 30.9% (5.3 percentage point reduction)
- [[Upper Green River]]: 13.1% to 10.2%
- Yampa River: 8.1% to 8.0%
- [[Duchesne River]]: 4.1% to 3.0%
- [[White River]]: 3.8% to 3.8%

**Colorado River Basin**: Natural flow 45.1%, remaining flow 32.3% (12.8 percentage point reduction)
- Upper Colorado River: 23.6% to 10.8%
- Gunnison River: 15.5% to 15.5%
- [[Dolores River]]: 5.4% to 5.4%

**San Juan River Basin**: Natural flow 14.4%, remaining flow 11.8% (2.6 percentage point reduction)

Six major reservoirs provide approximately 26 MAF of storage capacity: [[Lake Powell]], [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], [[Blue Mesa Reservoir]], [[Morrow Point Reservoir]], [[Crystal Reservoir]], and [[Navajo Reservoir]]. Initial filling of the mainstem reservoir system was completed with Lake Powell in June 1980.

Projected future flows show severe impacts on certain tributaries. By 2030, the Duchesne River will retain only 29% of natural flow (71% reduction), though the majority of reduction occurs during high spring runoff. Flow regulation may provide increased flows during critical periods for endangered fish needs.

## Key Findings

**Legal Framework**: The [[Colorado River Compact]] (1922) apportioned 7.5 MAF annually to Upper Basin States (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming) and 7.5 MAF to Lower Basin States (Arizona, California, Nevada). The Upper Basin must not deplete flow below 75 MAF for any 10-consecutive-year period. The [[Mexican Water Treaty]] (1944) allocates 1.5 MAF annually to Mexico. The [[Upper Colorado River Basin Compact]] (1948) distributed Upper Basin water: Colorado 51.75%, Utah 23%, Wyoming 14%, New Mexico 11.25%, and Arizona 50,000 acre-feet.

**Current and Projected Water Use**: Consumptive use including reservoir evaporation is currently 4.0 MAF, projected to increase to 4.9 MAF by 1990, 5.4 MAF by 2000, and 5.8 MAF by 2030. Average annual flow at Lee Ferry will decrease from 10 MAF (present) to 9.1 MAF (1990), 8.6 MAF (2000), and 8.2 MAF (2030).

**Flow Reductions by Basin** (as percentage of natural flow remaining by 2030):
- Upper Green River (above Flaming Gorge): 62%
- Yampa River: 93%
- Duchesne River: 29%
- White River: 74%
- Total Green River: 70%
- Upper Colorado River (above Cameo): 36%
- Gunnison River: 99%
- Dolores River: 86%
- Total Colorado River (above Cisco): 65%
- Upper San Juan River: 89%
- Total San Juan River: 70%
- Total Colorado River (above Lee Ferry): 61%

**Operational Considerations**: The useable or controllable yield at Lee Ferry is approximately 14.0 MAF (1.0 MAF is not controllable). Operations are governed by the "Long-Range Operating Criteria for Colorado River Reservoirs" (1970), established under the [[Colorado River Basin Project Act]] (1968) and reviewed every 5 years. Individual reservoir operations are governed by flood control criteria, downstream demands, energy production requirements, and minimum releases for fishery and recreation purposes.

**Management Challenge**: The Bureau identifies the need to determine and weigh economic and social values of food and fiber production, electric power, recreation, fish and wildlife, and flood control in a highly competitive water use situation. Operational flexibility exists within mainstem facilities to provide needed flows, but this flexibility will decrease as specific uses increase. The authors emphasize that comparing depletions with data on distribution, abundance, and flow requirements of endangered fishes should enable development of release pattern flexibility to ensure fish survival while meeting human needs.
