# Freshwater Fish of the USA: Threats, Extinction and Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This article, written by Ronald E. Watson, argues that the endemic freshwater fishes of the United States are disappearing at a rapid rate and that the causes are overwhelmingly tied to human activity. The main pressures identified are habitat drying from groundwater pumping and diversion, dam construction, agricultural and urban expansion, introduction of exotic species (including [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquito Fish]] and aquarium releases), hybridization, and direct illegal or reckless destruction. The author calls for habitat protection, better ecological understanding of rare species, and broad public engagement in [[Conservation]]. The document is an essay rather than a research study, and it provides few quantitative data.

## Background & Description

The article opens with a general discussion of public awareness of rare and endangered species, noting that people often associate extinction with dinosaurs and fail to see how species loss affects their own lives. It stresses that human activity (clear-cutting, synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, poor waste disposal and inadequate sewage treatment) is increasing the number of threatened plant and animal species. It also notes that many plants have yielded medicines, so losses may remove future cures.

The stated purpose is to highlight the plight of the endemic fishes of the United States. The author, Ronald Watson (born 1942 in Hayward, California, per the biography, age given as 42), is a student of [[Ichthyology]] and an author of scientific papers. His studies of freshwater fishes, especially cyprinids and gobiids, involved extensive field trips across the USA.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat drying and groundwater loss.** Many western species are threatened by reduction of water tables caused by pumping groundwater for urban growth and irrigation. Streams disappear by seeping into permeable valley soils or by entering high-evaporation lakes, examples given being streams entering the [[Great Salt Lake]] and the Owens River entering [[Mono Lake]]. Groundwater pumping can make underground lakes and streams disappear, and forest clearance and land use can warm groundwater, which harms cool-spring species such as the [[Flame Chub]].

**Dams and river regulation.** Dams on the [[Colorado River]] have caused changes in water quality and velocity and have restricted access to spawning grounds, threatening the [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Sucker]].

**Geological and evolutionary history.** Lowering of groundwater after the last ice age isolated streams from the wider drainage network, producing new forms. The [[White River]] of Nevada is cited as a prime example, with species, subspecies and genera including Relictus, Moapa, Crenichthys and Empetrichthys.

**Limited-range and rare species.** Species such as the [[Cape Fear Shiner]] and [[Sandhills Chub]] in North Carolina are attributed to recent evolutionary arrival, with insufficient time to spread. The [[Maryland Darter]] is known from a single riffle in the Chesapeake Bay region, and its distribution is unexplained.

**Exotic and introduced species.** Aquarium releases have harmed desert fishes in Nevada. Bait fish, especially the [[Red Shiner]], threaten the [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] in Arizona and New Mexico. The intentionally introduced [[Mosquito Fish]] has eliminated native species from ponds and springs, most severely affecting Cyprinodon, Crenichthys, Empetrichthys and Poeciliopsis.

**Hybridization.** The [[Roanoke Bass]] in Virginia is declining mainly through hybridization with introduced related species, and the hybrid is smaller than both parents.

## Key Findings

- **Extinction history:** The first recorded US freshwater fish extinction is the [[Harelip Sucker]] (Lagochila lacera), last seen towards the end of the nineteenth century. It was already rare when discovered and appears to have died out from pressures that would likely have caused its demise even without human help.
- **Principal threats identified by the author:**
  - Groundwater pumping and reduction of water tables
  - Dam construction and altered flow and water quality
  - Agricultural activities and urban expansion, including forest removal and groundwater warming
  - Exotic species introductions, from aquarium releases, bait fish and deliberate mosquitofish stocking
  - Hybridization with introduced related species
  - Pollution, such as a spill of liquefied manure that nearly wiped out the Maryland darter
  - Deliberate or reckless destruction and illegal activity
- **Snail darter case:** Percina tanasi, restricted to the lower 32 kilometres of the [[Little Tennessee River]], halted a dam closure until former President Jimmy Carter ordered the dam closed and the valley flooded. The species is now endangered but persists in a few locations, including one where it was introduced.
- **Legal framework:** The [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 prompted rescue and preservation efforts, but the author notes that most of these species are poorly understood ecologically and reproductively, so their survival requirements are unknown.
- **Illegal destruction:** In Arizona, 14 Colorado squawfish were illegally taken from a hatchery, despite warning signs and fences, killed and eaten. This was almost the entire captive stock.
- **Reckless destruction:** In Nevada, a landowner ran a pump over a desert spring containing a Cyprinodon species, in violation of a court order. The species was extinguished at that site, but it survived in several other springs and ponds.
- **Captive maintenance:** Many rare species are small and could be maintained in aquaria by naturalists, societies and public aquariums. Cyprinodon diabolis (Devil's Hole pupfish) offspring were found to differ physiologically from parents removed from their natural habitat.
- **Sport fish:** [[Apache Trout]] and [[Gila Trout]] in Arizona and New Mexico were once popular catches but are now protected.
- **Conclusion:** The author argues that protecting habitat and understanding species' needs is the only route to survival, and that conservation must be supported by public enthusiasm for local projects, treating all species as natural treasures.
