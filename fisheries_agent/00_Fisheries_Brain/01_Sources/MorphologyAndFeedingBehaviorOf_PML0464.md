# Morphology and Feeding Behavior of Hybrids Between Catostomus insignis and Pantosteus clarki (Catostomidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1982 Arizona State University M.S. thesis by Robert W. Clarkson tests whether putative F1 hybrids between [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) and [[Desert Mountain-Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) show intermediate morphology and feeding behavior. Specimens came from [[Aravaipa Creek]] and [[Bonita Creek]], two streams in the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona. Morphology was assessed with the [[Hybrid Index]], [[Discriminant Function Analysis]] (DFA) and [[Principal Components Analysis]] (PCA). Feeding was assessed by gut-content volumes analyzed with DFA. The three morphological methods agreed that hybrids were intermediate, though a dominance-like effect of *C. insignis* was detectable. Feeding results conflicted between streams. Hybrids in Aravaipa Creek were behaviorally intermediate, while Bonita Creek hybrids resembled *C. insignis*. The author concludes that the hybrids can use both parental food bases.

## Background & Description

- **Research question:** Intermediacy of hybrid phenotypes is the standard criterion for identifying hybrids in nature. Earlier work (Ross and Cavender 1981) showed that phenotypic expression may not be consistently intermediate, because dominance and/or interallelic suppression can operate. Behavioral intermediacy of hybrids has also been little documented.
- **Study taxa:** *C. insignis* is considered one of the most specialized products of catostomid evolution. It has a cartilaginous scraping sheath on the lower jaw for removing algae and diatoms from substrates, plus a lengthened gut and darkened peritoneum. *P. clarki* is more generalized and lacks these structures. The two species are nearly allopatric in Aravaipa Creek, where feeding is almost mutually exclusive (Schreiber 1978; Schreiber and Minckley 1982).
- **Study areas:**
  - *Aravaipa Creek* originates near 1310 m elevation, flows about 75 linear km (roughly 140 km of channel) to the San Pedro River at about 660 m, and has permanent surface water along about 50 creek-km. It has swift riffles, sand-bottomed channels and canyon sections, and is subject to floods that may reach 570 m³/s.
  - *Bonita Creek* drains from about 2130 m to 950 m at the Gila River confluence. Its canyon reaches are steeper, its lower channel is narrower, and it has less pool development than Aravaipa Creek. Estimated short-term peak discharges exceeded 470 m³/s.
- **Specimens:** Museum material from the Arizona State University Collection of Fishes, collected by electroshocker and seine. Collections were made largely for other purposes, not for hybrid study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native fish fauna:** Aravaipa Creek retains a large native assemblage (seven species) with little exotic presence. Bonita Creek supports only five native species, with a considerable introduced fauna in lower reaches. Differences in fauna, discharge and habitat diversity are proposed as factors in the contrasting feeding results.
- **Food base:** Five of the seven Aravaipa native species depend on ephemeropteran nymphs. *P. clarki* is largely herbivorous, and in Bonita Creek it comprised 41.4% of fish biomass (22.4 g/m²). In Aravaipa Creek it made up 41.0% of biomass (6.6 g/m²).
- **Habitat use:** *C. insignis* prefers swift riffles and pools, with juveniles in quiet margins. *P. clarki* tends to occupy relatively quiet, deep water, using swifter areas mainly as fingerlings.
- **Hydrology:** Aravaipa Creek's hydrology varied greatly over the 14-year collection period, unlike the stable 1976–1977 conditions of Schreiber's earlier study. This temporal variation probably influences habitat diversity and food availability.
- **Parasitism:** A cestode intestinal parasite (tentatively *Monobothrium*-like, per Thoane 1981) was present in both streams. Aravaipa *C. insignis* infestation was reported at 26%, but none of the Aravaipa *P. clarki* showed infestation. In Bonita Creek, infestation was heavy: about 54% in *P. clarki*, 36% in *C. insignis* and 9% in hybrids.

## Key Findings

**Morphological identification**

- Three methods (Hybrid Index, DFA and PCA) agreed in placing putative hybrids intermediate between parental samples.
- Hybrid index averages were 0.364 for Aravaipa Creek and 0.332 for Bonita Creek. Hubbs et al. (1943) had reported 0.480 for a comparable cross in the upper Gila River Basin. The Aravaipa value approached 0.5, the expected average for an F1 population.
- Length of snout and counts of dorsal and pelvic rays showed little parental difference. Excluding these characters was necessary to verify subjective hybrid classifications in Aravaipa Creek. Hybrid index values exceeded parental means in 6 of 16 cases in Aravaipa Creek.
- Coefficients of variation were essentially equivalent across groups, indicating no evidence of introgression. DFA hybrid clusters were compact and only slightly closer to *C. insignis*.
- The DFA sample from Bonita Creek included 22 typical *P. clarki* and 5 atypical specimens (with a lower lip less flared laterally and more posteriorly directed). The atypical specimens largely separated from the main group along CV II. The author concludes they are still *P. clarki*, possibly a geographic variant or developmental aberration.
- A dominance-like effect of *C. insignis* morphology was noted in most hybrids. The author concludes that simple additive inheritance may still be the main mode of gene expression in teleost hybrid crosses.
- PCA using log-transformed morphometric ratios gave a clearer separation (PC I explained 62% and 63% of variation in Aravaipa and Bonita Creeks). Raw morphometric data gave 48% and 46%. The author recommends the log-ratio form for hybrid morphology studies.

**Feeding behavior**

- Stomach fullness averaged 29–53% (excluding parasites). Between 0% and 26% of specimens in group samples had empty stomachs.
- Invertebrates occurred in 51.6% (Aravaipa) and 88.9% (Bonita) of *P. clarki* guts, but made up only 10.2% and 18.2% of relative volume, respectively. Ephemeropteran nymphs were the main invertebrate food of *C. insignis*.
- *P. clarki* ingested large amounts of fine sand (53.0% and 19.8% of relative volume in Aravaipa and Bonita Creeks). *C. insignis* contained only small amounts of sand (5.0% and 9.4%) of coarser grain.
- Vascular plant material was conspicuous in Aravaipa *C. insignis* (18.8%), hybrids (6.8%) and *P. clarki* (13.2%), but only in trace amounts in Bonita Creek suckers.
- Diatoms and filamentous algae were not used by *C. insignis*, but were significant in hybrids and *P. clarki* in both streams.
- **Aravaipa Creek:** Hybrids were almost exactly intermediate between parental averages for inorganic and animal categories. They were intermediate for diatoms and vascular plants where parental differences were small. DFA supported behavioral intermediacy.
- **Bonita Creek:** Hybrids showed little intermediacy in average relative volumes and closely resembled *C. insignis*, with the algal component as an exception. DFA placed the hybrid mean between parental means, though the hybrid distance from the *C. insignis* average was probably not significant. Hybrid CV I limits fell between parental limits.
- Individual variation in feeding was considerable in all samples, indicating that these fishes are facultative feeders.

**Conclusions**

- The author concludes that *C. insignis* × *P. clarki* hybrids are morphologically and behaviorally capable of using both parental food bases.
- Hybrids were classed as F1, supported by equal coefficients of variation and non-overlapping DFA scores. The methods used cannot distinguish F1 from backcrosses if introgression has occurred.
- The author recommends that future hybrid studies use multiple methods, since no single method is fully objective. The Hybrid Index is strongest for character-level comparisons, DFA for classification, and log-ratio PCA for interpretation.
- The Bonita Creek sample (N=10 hybrids) may have been too small to represent hybrid intermediacy accurately.
- Possible explanations for the stream differences include lower Bonita Creek discharge, lower habitat diversity, fewer native species, and a less competitive algal and invertebrate food base that may allow hybrids to shift toward animal food.
