# Recovery Plan for the Leopard Darter (*Percina pantherina*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan, prepared by Ray N. Jones of the [[Oklahoma Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] (Oklahoma State University) and dated September 1984, addresses the [[Leopard Darter]], a small percid endemic to the [[Little River System]] of southeastern Oklahoma and southwestern Arkansas. The species was listed as threatened on January 27, 1978, under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The plan states the species can be delisted only when all important habitat is identified, no longer threatened by adverse modification, and its continued existence is assured. Recovery depends on identifying and protecting important habitat, filling gaps in life-history knowledge (especially [[Reproductive Ecology]] and [[Early Life History]]), and maintaining protection through landowner agreements, agency coordination and population monitoring.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described as *Hadropterus pantherinus* by Moore and Reeves (1955); renamed *Percina pantherina* after Hadropterus was synonymized under *Percina* by Bailey et al. (1954). Closely related to the [[Blackside Darter]] (*P. maculata*).
- **Diagnostic features:** Lateral line scales 81–84 (versus 62–77 in the blackside darter); 11–14 square or round dark blotches along the lateral band; dark eye with well-developed preorbital, suborbital and postorbital bars; light olive dorsum, whitish venter. Maximum size about 3 inches.
- **Confusion species:** Smaller individuals may be mistaken for [[Channel Darter]] (*P. copelandi*) or [[Dusky Darter]] (*P. sclera*).
- **Early records:** Earliest known collections by O.P. Hay in 1884; a single specimen was taken in 1925 near Potter, Arkansas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and abundance**
- Before 1977, 64 collecting efforts at 30 locations yielded only 165 specimens: 30 from 3 upper Little River sites, 55 from 14 Glover Creek sites, 44 from 9 Mountain Fork River sites and 36 from 4 Cossatot River sites.
- Largest populations occurred in Glover Creek. The species has never been recorded from the Rolling Fork or Saline Rivers.
- From August 1977 to July 1980, 139 darters were collected at 14 Glover Creek locations; densities ranged from 0 to 0.0170/m² (0 to 27/100 m of stream).
- The leopard darter ranked second among darters in Glover Creek (119 of about 8,500 total darters; 1.4% of catch), far behind [[Radiosum Darter]] (*Etheostoma radiosum*, 98.2%).

**Habitat**
- Adults occur mainly in upland reaches with steep gradients and rubble, boulder or bedrock bottoms, mostly in larger and intermediate streams.
- Earlier literature called it a riffle species, but recent data show adults prefer moderately shallow pools.
- Highest densities occurred at depths of 20–79 cm, velocities of 0–19 cm/s, and in rubble and boulder substrates.

**Water quality** (Eley et al. 1975, in streams where the species was collected): temperature 0–33 °C; dissolved oxygen 4.0–15.0 mg/L; total dissolved solids and suspended solids 20–100 mg/L; pH 6.5–8.0; total phosphorus <0.01–0.30 mg/L; total nitrogen <0.5–6.5 mg/L.

**Food habits:** Based on seven museum stomachs (Robison 1978), diet included [[Black Fly]] larvae and pupae (*Simulium* sp.), mayfly *Pseudocloeon* sp., chironomid and chaoborid dipterans, and green algae.

**Age and growth:** Of 139 Glover Creek fish measured, total lengths ranged 45–92 mm (mean 70.2, SD 9.0). Scale-based ages were assigned: >50 mm age 0; 51–71 mm age I; 72–87 mm age II; >88 mm age III.

**Reproduction:** Spawning behavior and habitat are unknown; spawning in riffles during spring is hypothesized. Males bear enlarged midventral scales, and the sex ratio was approximately 1:1 (45 males, 43 females). Fecundity data from seven fish: 260–418 mature ova, 510–2,302 immature ova.

**Threats**
- **Impoundments:** the greatest threat, through habitat inundation and altered downstream flows. Reservoirs include Pine Creek, Broken Bow, DeQueen, [[Gillham Reservoir]], Dierks and Millwood. Only three leopard darters are known from below reservoirs.
- **Lukfata Lake** (authorized, not funded) on Glover Creek would threaten the largest known populations.
- **Silviculture** and road construction increase turbidity and sedimentation.
- **Agriculture and industry:** rising fish kills, including a creosote spill on the Cossatot River that affected 10 miles of stream.
- **Gravel removal** operations cause habitat destruction and water quality decline.

**Conservation efforts:** Primarily U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service-funded baseline research in Glover Creek and the Cossatot River.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective and steps**
- Delisting requires identified, protected and assured habitat. Quantifiable population goals cannot be set until life-history and habitat research is completed.
- Critical habitat already designated covers six reaches of the Little River system, including the main Little River, Black Fork Creek, Glover Creek (main, East and West Forks) and Mountain Fork Creek.
- The Upper West Fork of Glover Creek is recognized as important habitat based on its production.

**Inventory plan (Section 1.2)**
- About 25 additional areas are recommended for inventory, including the main Little River, Watson, Honobia, Blackfork, Cedar, Carter and Pine creeks, Big Eagle and Boktukolo creeks, Cossatot, Rolling Fork and Saline Rivers.
- Minimum sampling sites per area range from 2 to 4.
- Sampling should use snorkeling and D.C. pulse electrofishing in water under 100 cm deep, with at least 1 hour or 100 m of stream per station, during June–October, for at least two consecutive years.
- Areas with average density >4 leopard darters/100 m stream (or equivalent) should be designated important habitat. Reference densities were 3.64 (Glover Creek) and 3.44 (Cossatot River).

**Research priorities**
- Reproductive ecology (high priority): test the spring-riffle spawning hypothesis; consider laboratory rearing and spawning observation.
- Early life history: habitat use of larvae and juveniles; reviewers suggest juveniles may use pools like adults.
- Habitat: seasonal shifts in preferred habitat and water quality correlations.

**Habitat management and protection**
- Compile lists of private landholdings and water rights; pursue cooperative management agreements and conservation easements, or transfer to public ownership.
- Inform State and Federal landowners and ensure leopard darter considerations are included in their management plans. Consider a Land Protection Plan.
- Monitor populations and habitat at least every two years (preferably annually) at permanent stations, including annual flyovers and a local watch committee.
- Enforce State and Federal water quality standards, track projects by agencies, and develop a rapid-response process for spills.

**Implementation and review**
- Part III schedules tasks by priority (1–3), responsible agency (chiefly the Oklahoma Department of Wildlife Conservation with the Fish and Wildlife Service), timetable and estimated cost. Examples: inventory about $20,000 per year; private landholding acquisition about $50,000 per year.
- Reviewers (Hubbs, Orth, and Fish and Wildlife Service staff) requested clearer, more specific tasks with subtasks, a quantified objective, pesticide monitoring, sediment mapping, and clarification of Section 7 consultation duties. The reviewers also recommended that the plan be revised as new data arrive.
- The plan explicitly recommends against delisting until Lukfata Lake is no longer authorized and the recovery plan is fully implemented.
