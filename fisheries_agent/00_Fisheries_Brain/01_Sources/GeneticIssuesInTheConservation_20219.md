# Genetic Issues in the Conservation and Restoration of the Endangered Gila Trout: Update

#ichthyology #salmonidae #conservation-genetics #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This genetic study examined 11 stream populations of presumed [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*) using horizontal starch gel electrophoresis of 48 protein-coding loci to address two critical conservation issues: (1) detection of hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]] (*O. mykiss*) and (2) quantification of genetic divergence among populations. Eight loci showed fixed allelic differences between [[Gila Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], serving as diagnostic markers. Seven of eleven sampled populations showed evidence of hybridization, including native populations in [[Iron Creek]] and [[McKenna Creek]]. Only four populations ([[McKnight Creek]], [[Spruce Creek]], [[Big Dry Creek]], and [[Whiskey Creek]]) appeared to be pure [[Gila Trout]]. Substantial genetic divergence exists among populations: 28% of total variation occurs between [[Gila River]] and [[San Francisco River]] drainages, 17% among populations within the [[Gila River]] drainage, and 56% within populations. Conservation efforts should focus on maintaining all remaining pure populations to preserve the species' genetic diversity.

## Background & Description

[[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*) are native to headwaters of the [[Gila River]] drainage in New Mexico and Arizona and the [[Verde River]] drainage in Arizona. The species is federally protected as endangered under the United States Endangered Species Act. By 1993, only five native populations were believed to exist in the [[Gila River]] drainage; the species is considered extinct from the [[Verde River]] drainage.

The dramatic population decline resulted from human disturbances (vegetation reduction, increased sedimentation, reduced stream flows, elevated water temperatures) and introduction of non-native fishes. [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) eliminate [[Gila Trout]] through predation and competition. [[Rainbow Trout]] (*O. mykiss*) cause population loss through introgression and formation of random mating hybrid swarms.

Native populations are isolated in small headwater streams, making them vulnerable to extirpation from fire, flood, or drought. The [[Main Diamond Creek]] and [[South Diamond Creek]] populations were eliminated by fire and subsequent storms. The Gila Trout Recovery Plan calls for replicating all native populations and expanding distribution into larger, more stable habitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Six presumed native populations existed at the time of study: [[Main Diamond Creek]] and [[South Diamond Creek]] in the East Fork [[Gila River]] drainage, [[Iron Creek]] and [[McKenna Creek]] in the West/Middle Fork [[Gila River]] drainage, [[Whiskey Creek]] in the West Fork [[Gila River]] drainage, and [[Spruce Creek]] in the [[San Francisco River]] drainage. All native populations except [[Whiskey Creek]] had been replicated at least once through translocation efforts.

Replicate populations included: [[McKnight Creek]], [[Sheep Corral Canyon]], and Gap Creek (now extirpated) from [[Main Diamond Creek]] fish; [[Trail Canyon]], lower and upper [[Mogollon Creek]], and [[Woodrow Canyon]] from [[South Diamond Creek]] fish; [[Little Creek]] from [[McKenna Creek]] fish; Sacaton Creek from [[Iron Creek]] fish; and [[Big Dry Creek]] from [[Spruce Creek]] fish. The Mescalaro National Fish Hatchery maintained broodstocks from both Diamond Creek populations.

The severe fragmentation of populations into isolated headwater streams creates conservation challenges. [[Gila Trout]] reach sexual maturity at age 2-3 years. Stream barriers and geographic isolation have historically limited gene flow among populations, allowing genetic divergence and potential local adaptation.

## Key Findings

**Diagnostic Loci Identification**: Eight loci showed fixed or nearly fixed allele frequency differences between [[Gila Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]: ADH*, CK-C2*, FH-1*, GAPDH-4*, LDH-C*, PEPB*, PGK-2*, and PGM-1*. These diagnostic loci enable detection of hybridization and assessment of hybrid ancestry.

**Hybridized Populations**: Seven populations contained alleles characteristic of both [[Gila Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] at two or more diagnostic loci: [[Iron Creek]] (native), [[McKenna Creek]] (native), [[Little Creek]] (established from [[McKenna Creek]]), lower and upper [[Mogollon Creek]] (established from [[South Diamond Creek]]), [[Sheep Corral Canyon]] (established from [[Main Diamond Creek]]), [[Trail Canyon]] (established from [[South Diamond Creek]]), and [[Woodrow Canyon]] (established from [[South Diamond Creek]]). All samples contained later-generation hybrids, indicating hybridization occurred at least two generations prior to sampling.

**Recent Hybridization Events**: Hybridization appears recent in [[Mogollon Creek]] and [[Woodrow Canyon]]. Significant positive gametic phase disequilibrium between diagnostic loci and multiple locus genotypes indicate these populations contain mixtures of [[Gila Trout]] and descendants of first-generation hybrids. In lower [[Mogollon Creek]], only 2 of 30 fish possessed [[Rainbow Trout]] alleles; in [[Woodrow Canyon]], only 3 of 7 fish showed [[Rainbow Trout]] ancestry. Most hybrid individuals were backcrosses to [[Gila Trout]].

**Pure Gila Trout Populations**: Four populations showed only [[Gila Trout]] alleles at all analyzed loci: [[McKnight Creek]] (established from [[Main Diamond Creek]]), [[Spruce Creek]] (native), [[Big Dry Creek]] (established from [[Spruce Creek]]), and [[Whiskey Creek]] (native). With sample sizes of 19-30 fish and eight diagnostic loci, there is 95% confidence in detecting as little as 1-1.4% [[Rainbow Trout]] genetic contribution.

**Genetic Rescue Opportunity**: Since [[Gila Trout]] were extirpated from [[South Diamond Creek]] by fire, establishing a population from [[Mogollon Creek]] or [[Woodrow Canyon]] using paired matings and genotyping at diagnostic loci could recover pure [[South Diamond Creek]] lineage fish. This requires hatchery facilities to conduct controlled breeding and genetic screening.

**Substantial Genetic Divergence**: Of total genetic variation detected at seven polymorphic loci, 27.5% is due to differences between [[Gila River]] and [[San Francisco River]] drainage populations, 16.5% to differences among [[Gila River]] drainage populations, and 56.0% to variation within populations. This high divergence indicates historically restricted gene flow and potential for local adaptation.

**Drainage-Level Differentiation**: PGM-1* shows complete fixation for different alleles between drainages (PGM-1*133 in [[Gila River]] drainage, PGM-1*null in [[San Francisco River]] drainage), indicating long-term reproductive isolation. Fish from the two drainages should be managed as separate conservation units.

**Population-Specific Alleles**: Four alleles occur at high frequency in single populations: PGM-1*null (frequency 1.000) unique to [[Spruce Creek]]/[[Big Dry Creek]]; sAAT-1*null (frequency 0.632) unique to [[Main Diamond Creek]] ([[McKnight Creek]]); sMEP-2*85 (frequency 0.423) and sMEP-2*115 (frequency 0.135) unique to [[South Diamond Creek]] (lower [[Mogollon Creek]]). Two alleles (sMDH-B1,2*74 and sMEP-1*100) occur in multiple [[Gila River]] drainage populations at highly variable frequencies.

**Conservation Implications**: Preserving [[Gila Trout]] genetic diversity requires ensuring continued existence of all remaining pure populations: [[McKnight Creek]], [[Spruce Creek]], [[Big Dry Creek]], [[Whiskey Creek]], and potentially recovered [[South Diamond Creek]] fish from [[Mogollon Creek]]/[[Woodrow Canyon]]. Each native population or lineage should be considered a separate conservation unit due to substantial genetic divergence and potential local adaptation.

**Replication Priorities**: The [[South Diamond Creek]] lineage (via genetic rescue from [[Mogollon Creek]]/[[Woodrow Canyon]]) and [[Whiskey Creek]] population require replication. [[Whiskey Creek]] appears extremely small; establishing a broodstock over multiple years from at least 25 males and 25 females is recommended before attempting translocation to avoid genetic bottlenecks.

**Hybridization Concerns**: The presence of hybrid swarms in [[Iron Creek]], [[McKenna Creek]], [[Little Creek]], and [[Sheep Corral Canyon]] indicates ongoing conservation challenges. These populations should not serve as source populations for recovery efforts. However, [[Iron Creek]] and [[McKenna Creek]] habitats may be suitable for future pure [[Gila Trout]] reintroductions given their apparent habitat quality.

**Management Recommendations**: Conservation efforts should concentrate on the four confirmed pure populations and genetic rescue of [[South Diamond Creek]] fish. Between-drainage transfers should be avoided except in highly isolated situations. Within the [[Gila River]] drainage, each native population lineage should be managed separately to preserve potential local adaptations. Future population establishments could use mixed sources within the [[Gila River]] drainage to increase establishment success, but mixing [[Gila River]] and [[San Francisco River]] drainage fish is not recommended.
