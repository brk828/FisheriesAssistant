# Spikedace and Loach Minnow Habitat Relations with Introduced Red Shiner

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 final report to the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], prepared by Paul C. Marsh, Francisco J. Abarca, Michael E. Douglas and W. L. Minckley at Arizona State University, examines whether the introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) contributes to the decline of two native cyprinids of the [[Gila River Basin]]: the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). The study combined field surveys of habitat use and diet in seven stream reaches with a controlled experiment in an [[Artificial Stream System]]. Field data showed spikedace occupying significantly faster currents where it co-occurred with shiners, and the artificial-stream experiment showed shiners displacing spikedace, particularly during the August spawning period. Diets of the three species overlapped little, so the authors concluded that competition for food was unlikely to be the main mechanism, and that habitat displacement and aggression were more probable.

## Background & Description

**Project context.** The work was a 30-month contract between the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AZGF) and Arizona State University, with the aim of clarifying the role, if any, of interactions with red shiner in the decline or extirpation of native fishes. Both native species were federally listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (spikedace in 1986; loach minnow in 1987), and proposed critical habitat remained in force.

**Spikedace.** A small, slender, scale-reduced cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]], once widespread in moderate-sized, intermediate-elevation streams. Adults reach about 50 to 60 mm standard length. Breeding occurs April to June over sand-gravel substrates, with adhesive eggs (90 to 250 ova per female). Life span is typically two years.

**Loach Minnow.** A small, secretive, bottom-dwelling cyprinid of the Gila basin in Arizona, New Mexico and Sonora. It has a reduced gas bladder and is restricted to turbulent, rocky riffles up to about 2200 m elevation. Eggs are laid under rocks and guarded by males. Its historical range was far larger than its current scattered tributary populations.

**Red Shiner.** A moderately small cyprinid native to the Mississippi and Gulf drainages, introduced to the Colorado River basin in the 1950s and now abundant across the Gila system. It is tolerant of intermittency, high turbidity and temperature, and low dissolved oxygen. It is an opportunistic omnivore that spawns over an extended season, and is commonly used as bait.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats to natives.** Habitat destruction and alteration, dewatering, groundwater pumping, impoundment, channelization, livestock grazing, mining, agriculture and urban development all degraded the Gila system. Non-native predators and competitors, including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Brown Trout]], also affect native fishes.

**Fish community.** Spikedace is commonly collected with longfin dace, speckled dace, loach minnow, chubs (*Gila* spp.) and suckers (*Catostomus* spp., *Pantosteus* spp.). Red shiner occurs in most habitats occupied by spikedace, and in streams with loach minnow where those fish have been extirpated.

**Study reaches.** Seven reaches on five streams were sampled from May to September 1987 (with some work continuing to 1989): east and west [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Blue River]], [[Gila River]] at Gila and Redrock, [[Sycamore Creek]], and the [[Verde River]]. Sampling used a 2.0 × 1.2 m, 0.32 cm mesh straight seine and, where seining failed, bank electrofishing. Sample sizes per reach ranged from 89 to 239 hauls or electrofishing units.

**Physical habitat.** Mean depth ranged from 19.0 cm (Blue River) to 28.1 cm (Verde River). Mean current velocity ranged from 15.3 cm/s (Sycamore Creek) to 62.2 cm/s (east Aravaipa Creek). Weighted substrate ranged from 55.3 (west Aravaipa) to 142.3 (Verde).

## Key Findings

**Habitat use (field data)**
- Spikedace occupied significantly faster currents where it co-occurred with red shiner (Verde River and Gila at Redrock) than where it was isolated (west Aravaipa and Gila at Gila): 30.4 vs 39.6 cm/s in the weighted analysis (rounded from the reported figures), and shallower depths were not significantly different after accounting for habitat differences.
- Red shiner occupied slower and deeper water at Sycamore Creek and Gila at Redrock when spikedace was absent. At Sycamore, occupied habitat averaged 18.0 cm deep vs 11.5 cm where absent, with current velocities of 14.3 vs 24.1 cm/s.
- Loach minnow occupied shallow riffles with currents of about 37 to 70 cm/s and gravel-cobble substrate. Loach minnow and red shiner co-occurred in only two seine hauls at Blue River, so habitat comparisons were not possible.

**Diet**
- Spikedace diets were dominated by trichopterans (post-emergent adults at Verde River, where they made up 75% of volume and 61% of number) and ephemeropteran nymphs, with lesser contributions from chironomid and hydropsychid larvae. Dietary diversity was lowest at Verde River (10 items) and highest at Aravaipa Creek (23 items).
- Loach minnow is an opportunistic benthic insectivore, feeding mainly on ephemeropteran and dipteran larvae (chironomids and simuliids). Feeding was crepuscular, with fullness highest at midday and near dusk.
- Red shiner is an omnivore. Its diet varied by site: amorphous organic matter, ephemeropterans and chironomids dominated at Verde River and Sycamore Creek, and detritus and chironomids dominated in the lower Colorado River.
- Dietary overlap indices (Horn, Levins, Schoener) were all below 0.60 for spikedace and red shiner taken together at Verde River, so the authors concluded there was no significant food competition. Two hypothetical comparisons exceeded 0.60, but they were judged unjustified because overlap did not occur where the species actually co-occurred.

**Artificial stream experiment**
- The artificial stream had nine alternating pool-run sections, with current velocity ranging from 0 to 20 cm/s in designated subunits.
- Spikedace in isolation formed compact aggregations in pool headwaters. When red shiner were present, spikedace shifted toward runs (slower current), and aggregations became unstable. Shiners were more active and aggressive, chasing spikedace frequently.
- The displacement was observed clearly only in August, coinciding with shiner spawning. Shiners spawned and produced young-of-year that remained mainly in runs.
- Distributional tests (Kolmogorov-Smirnov, chi-square) confirmed a significant shift in spikedace distribution when shiners were present (p < 0.01 in August).

**Conclusions**
- The authors state that interaction with red shiner caused spikedace to occupy habitats it otherwise would not normally use. In the field this meant faster currents; in the artificial stream it meant slower currents. The shift is taken as evidence of displacement.
- The authors judged competition for food to be a minor or absent factor. They suggested that aggression, habitat displacement and possible effects on reproduction are more likely mechanisms.
- Experiments were short; a spring-season study was recommended to test effects on spawning, and no direct evidence of physical injury or predation on young spikedace was found.
- The study concludes that declines of spikedace and loach minnow were coincident with red shiner expansion, but that it remains unproven whether shiners cause those declines, because habitat alteration and non-native fishes acted together.
