# Competition for Food between Crayfish and Slimy Sculpin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study examined interspecific competition for fish eggs between two common benthic predators of the [[Great Lakes]], the [[Slimy Sculpin]] (*Cottus cognatus*) and the [[Virile Crayfish]] (*Orconectes virilis*). The sculpin's feeding rate was unchanged by the presence of crayfish, but crayfish feeding was significantly reduced by sculpin. When shelter refuges were available, crayfish fed at about half their solitary rate, and when no refuges were provided, crayfish stopped feeding altogether. The authors conclude that the competition is asymmetrical and could push crayfish toward a suboptimal diet in nature.

## Background & Description

Slimy sculpin and virile crayfish are benthic predators with substantial niche overlap in rocky habitats of lakes and streams. Both contribute to energy flow and nutrient cycling. Both favor rock substrate as protection from predators such as [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Burbot]], and in [[Lake Michigan]] they are commonly found together on rocky reefs.

Their diets overlap. Crayfish are omnivorous, eating detritus, vegetation, invertebrates and fish. Sculpin are carnivorous, eating invertebrates, fish and fish eggs. [[Fish Eggs]] are a shared food source that provides high energy and is preferred over lower-energy foods. Earlier separate laboratory studies reported that both sculpin and crayfish ate about 2 eggs per day in cobble substrate.

The paper is a contribution from the [[Great Lakes Fisheries Research Center]] in Ann Arbor, Michigan, and was submitted in partial fulfillment of a master's degree at [[Eastern Michigan University]]. It was published in the *[[Journal of Freshwater Ecology]]* (Volume 7, Number 2, June 1992).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental design.** The study used [[Laboratory Microcosm]] aquaria with a tank divider and a cobble-rock floor. Test animals were fed [[Rainbow Trout]] eggs over 24-hour periods, and the number of eggs consumed by each occupant was counted. Sample sizes were 36 sculpin and 20 crayfish in monospecific tests, plus mixed-species tests run in five replicate pairs. Mixed-species tests were run in two configurations: open (no refuge) and with small [[Shelter Refuge]]s. Excursions outside refuges, time spent outside refuges, and [[Foraging Behavior]] (feeding bouts) were recorded. Nonparametric statistics were used, including G-tests and rank tests.

**Behavioral context.** Crayfish sheltered much more than sculpin and responded to sculpin in open treatments by becoming agitated and defensive. The authors interpret the reduction in crayfish foraging as a short-term behavioral modification, following [[Collins et al. 1983]]. They note that longer exposure might allow crayfish to accept their tank companion.

**Alternative explanation.** The authors also considered whether reduced crayfish foraging came from the shelter itself rather than from the sculpin. If so, sculpin would still outcompete crayfish when the two co-occur. In a separate experiment, single crayfish with abundant cobble shelter ate about 33% more eggs than in the mixed-species/shelter treatment, which suggests that a fish's presence reduces crayfish foraging beyond any shelter effect.

## Key Findings

**Monospecific feeding rates (median egg predation per day):**
- Slimy sculpin: 2.5 eggs/d
- Virile crayfish: 3.0 eggs/d

**Mixed-species results:**
- Sculpin feeding rates were no different from monospecific tests, so sculpin were not measurably affected by crayfish.
- Crayfish feeding was significantly reduced by the presence of sculpin.
- With refuges present, crayfish predation fell about 50% below the monospecific rate, to 1.5 eggs/d.
- Relative to sculpin in the same tests, crayfish made 93% fewer excursions outside refuges, spent 94% less time outside refuges, and engaged in 83% fewer feeding bouts (P ≤ 0.05 for all comparisons).
- With no refuges, crayfish ceased feeding (0 eggs/d).

**Behavior in the mixed-species/shelter treatment:**
- Sculpin made more excursions, spent more time outside shelters, and had more foraging bouts than crayfish.
- Sculpin were active throughout the 24-hour test period and appeared to reduce crayfish excursions around the aquarium.
- Total excursions did not differ between crayfish and sculpin (G-test, P = 0.13).
- The median proportion of excursions that led to foraging was 16%.
- Shelter entry rates were similar for both species. Foraging bout frequency did not differ among the five replicates for either species.

**Conclusions:**
- The competition is asymmetrical. Sculpin are largely unaffected, while crayfish are suppressed.
- In nature, crayfish may be forced into a suboptimal diet through this interaction with slimy sculpin.
