# Gila cypha (Humpback Chub)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1946 paper by Robert R. Miller formally describes **Gila cypha** (the [[Humpback Chub]]), a remarkable new cyprinid species discovered in the [[Colorado River]] within [[Grand Canyon National Park]], Arizona. The species is distinguished by extreme morphological specialization for life in torrential, silt-laden waters — most notably a **prominent abrupt nuchal hump**, near-total loss of scales, expansive falcate fins, a flattened head, an inferior mouth, and greatly reduced eyes. The description is based on a holotype and two fragmentary/non-type specimens, all lacking robust locality data except the holotype. Miller situates *G. cypha* as the apparent "end form" of an ecomorphological gradient within the *[[Gila Robusta]]* species complex, representing the most extreme adaptation to swift-water/torrential river-channel life yet known among Colorado River cyprinids.

## Background & Description

- **Body form**: Strongly compressed laterally; sides slightly convex; prominent, abrupt hump over the occiput (nuchal hump) — the most diagnostic feature separating it from *[[Gila Robusta Elegans]]* ("bonytail"), which shows only a broad, even convexity.
- **Squamation**: Body almost entirely devoid of scales except ~80 scales retained in the lateral line; these scales possess **basal radii** (in addition to apical and lateral radii), a character shared with *[[Gila Robusta Intermedia]]* but absent in *G. r. elegans*. Degeneration of scales exceeds that of all *Gila* congeners except approached by *G. r. elegans* and, to a lesser degree, *G. r. seminuda*. Only the genera *[[Plagopterus]]* and *[[Meda]]* show more extreme scale loss among Colorado Basin cyprinids.
- **Fins**: Expansive, falcate (sickle-shaped) fins — larger than in *G. r. elegans*.
  - Dorsal fin: iii, 9 rays (first full-length ray unbranched, preceded by 3 graduated rudimentary rays).
  - Anal fin: iii, 11 rays, similar rudimentary-ray pattern.
  - Pectoral rays: 18 per fin.
  - Pelvic rays: 9 per fin.
  - Principal caudal rays: 20 (18 branched + unbranched rays above/below).
- **Head**: Dorsoventrally flattened; snout fleshy and elongate; mouth inferior; eye very small (13.0 in head length vs. 10.6 in a referred non-type specimen).
- **Gill rakers**: Total 9 (left)/11 (right), short, dimorphic — upper limb rakers slender/pointed/curved; lower limb rakers shorter, thicker, mostly forked.
- **Dentition**: Dental formula 2,5–4,1(or 2) — pharyngeal teeth thick, bluntly pointed, weak grinding surface on first two teeth of main row.
- **Pharyngeal arches**: Notably small/short for body size (21.8–22.7 mm) compared to same-sized *G. robusta* cotype (31.8–32.6 mm); upper and lower limbs nearly equal in length, both thicker than in *robusta*. Most closely resembles pharyngeal structure of *G. r. elegans*.
- **Coloration**: Brownish body — pinkish-brown on sides/belly, yellowish-brown along back; dense dark puncticulations on head, back, and sides above the lateral line, extending in patches near pectoral base, caudal base, and fin membranes.
- **Size**: Holotype ~305 mm standard length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Endemic to the swift, silt-laden, high-energy inner gorge channel of the [[Colorado River]] in the [[Grand Canyon]] — an extreme torrential environment subject to violent flood pulses (recorded flow variation: 1,800–174,000 cubic feet/second over 1917–1926) and no rooted vegetation due to scouring.
- **Adaptive suite**: The nuchal hump, flattened head, inferior mouth, degenerate squamation, and falcate fins are interpreted as **hydrodynamic adaptations** — the hump deflects current downward, forcing the fish toward the bottom/margins where flow is less torrential than in midwater; reduced scales minimize frictional drag; robust fins aid in resisting current.
- **Sensory adaptation**: Small eye may reflect degeneration from reduced light penetration (high turbidity) or direct abrasive effects of suspended sediment.
- **Sympatric associates** (Grand Canyon reach): [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus delphinus*), *[[Gila Robusta Elegans]]*, and introduced [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus lacustris punctatus*). Expected but unconfirmed associates include [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, reputed largest cyprinid in the New World).
- **Range**: Known definitively only from near the mouth of Bright Angel Creek in Grand Canyon; likely more widespread through the main-channel swift-water habitat of the Grand Canyon reach. Possibly absent below [[Boulder Dam]], where all examined *Gila* specimens were referable to *G. r. elegans*.
- **Niche partitioning**: Both *G. cypha* and *G. r. elegans* occupy the main Colorado River channel, but precise niche differentiation is undetermined; *elegans* is noted to congregate at clear tributary mouths and ascend tributaries to some degree.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomic novelty**: *Gila cypha* is described as a new species distinct from all other members of the subgenus *[[Gila (Subgenus)]]*, including *G. robusta*, *G. minacae*, and *G. nigrescens*; it is most similar to *G. r. elegans* but distinguished sharply by its nuchal hump and more extreme scale loss.
- **Revision of Colorado Basin Gila diversity**: Of 12 nominal species previously attributed to the Colorado River basin (Jordan, Evermann & Clark, 1930), Miller concludes only two species are validly recognized: *[[Gila Robusta]]* and the newly described *G. cypha*. He also argues *G. robusta* and *G. elegans* are only subspecifically distinct (not full species), bridged by intergrade specimens of *Gila seminuda* Cope and Yarrow.
- **Ecomorphological gradient**: Table 2 establishes a graded ecological-morphological series across *G. r. intermedia* (small tributary/brook form, chubby body, large scales) → *G. r. robusta* (larger tributary/river form, more streamlined) → *G. r. elegans* (swift river-channel form, highly streamlined, embedded scales, pencil-shaped caudal peduncle) → *G. cypha* (most extreme swift-water specialist). *G. cypha* is proposed as the apparent terminal/extreme form of this trend, though it does not follow all predicted trends (e.g., unexpectedly low dorsal ray count of 9 and a deeper rather than narrower caudal peduncle relative to *elegans*).
- **Comparative pharyngeal morphology**: Pharyngeal arch proportions in *G. cypha* most closely resemble those of *G. r. elegans*, supporting close phylogenetic/ecological relationship despite external morphological divergence.
- **Convergent desquamation**: Scale loss as an adaptation to swift water is noted also in other Colorado Basin fishes — *[[Ptychocheilus Lucius]]*, *[[Tiaroga Cobitis]]*, and the catostomid *[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]* — suggesting convergent evolution toward reduced-friction body surfaces in torrential habitats.
- **Etymology**: The name *cypha* derives from the Greek κύφη ("hump-backed"), proposed by Dr. Carl L. Hubbs, referencing the diagnostic nuchal hump.
- **Conservation-relevant note (historical)**: The species' apparent restriction to the untouched, unexplored inner gorge swift-water habitat suggests it may be locally endemic to the Grand Canyon reach and sensitive to hydrological alteration (e.g., absence below Boulder Dam).
