# Upper San Pedro River Native Fish Monitoring and Reintroduction Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #native-fish-management

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1989 advisory letter from Arizona State University's Department of Zoology to the U.S. Bureau of Land Management (San Pedro Project). It contains comments on a fisheries monitoring plan, a proposed general survey and artificial-habitat survey framework, and a discussion of native fish reintroduction and management for the upper [[San Pedro River]] and its Conservation Area (SPNRCA). The author argues that native fishes should be valued for their own sake, that monitoring should be expanded to four sampling locations with specified gear, and that eight of the 13 native fish species are under some form of protection in the basin. The central recommendation is to secure existing native-fish habitats first, then rehabilitate degraded streams, and then establish fishes in excavated floodplain habitats, while controlling non-native fishes.

## Background & Description

The letter is written to Erick G. Campbell, San Pedro Project Manager, BLM Safford District Office, and is accompanied by separate sheets on the monitoring plan and the Habitat Management Plan. The author describes the suggestions as possibly "off the wall" and offers to discuss them by phone.

The monitoring comments are organized by item number (I.A.1 through I.D.1.b) and propose:
- Four key sampling locations on the reach, including one near the [[Cienega]], rather than two.
- A permanent station on the [[Babocomari River]].
- Seine mesh of 1/8 inch, because small adult [[Sonoran Topminnow]] and [[Mosquitofish]] pass through 1/4 inch mesh.
- Blocking of reaches with [[Electrofishing]] passes that continue until no more fish are caught.
- Minimum effort of resampling five established sites each October.

A new General Bi-annual Survey (Section II) is proposed, sampling in May/June and October/November with standard 15 ft, 1/8 inch seines for pools and 4 ft seines for riffles, recording young-of-year and adults separately. A survey of [[Artificial Habitats]] (Section III) is also proposed, covering stock tanks, reservoirs and other sites that may source non-native fishes and [[Bullfrog]] invasions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author notes that the San Pedro has been altered since about 1880, and that restoring fishes depends on maintaining adequate surface water and developing additional habitat. Key environmental points include:

- Non-native fishes, particularly [[Mosquitofish]] and red shiner, threaten natives; floods selectively remove introduced fishes, while reservoirs and stock tanks serve as recolonization sources.
- Floodplain marsh rehabilitation and cienega restoration are expected to benefit mosquito control, raptors and other aquatic vertebrates.
- The [[Babocomari River]] watershed contains some of the most undisturbed cienega habitat remaining in the Southwest, where a dam once protected water to 3.0 m or deeper.
- Minimum flow goals of about 0.28 m³/sec or more are suggested for the mainstream, similar to minima in [[Aravaipa Creek]].

The regulatory context is also discussed. Federally listed species in the area include Spikedace and Loach Minnow (Threatened), and Colorado Squawfish, Sonoran Topminnow and Desert Pupfish (Endangered). Gila Chub and Razorback Sucker are candidates. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] additionally lists Gila chub, razorback sucker and the San Pedro roundtail chub as special concern. The author objects to the "experimental, nonessential" classification under the [[Endangered Species Act]], arguing that any biologically endangered population should be treated as essential.

## Key Findings

- **Status of native fishes:** Only Longfin Dace and Desert Sucker persist widely in the mainstream. Seven native species occur in Aravaipa Creek. Of 13 native fishes in the basin, 8 receive some physical protection, while Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, Flannelmouth Sucker and Desert Pupfish, Sonoran Topminnow are extirpated from the basin.
- **Protected areas:** Aravaipa Creek (BLM Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area and George Whittell Wildlife Reserve), Redfield Canyon (largely controlled by [[The Nature Conservancy]]), and O'Donnell Creek on the Canelo Hills Cienega Preserve.
- **Priority ordering for management:**
  1. Perpetuate species in their existing natural settings (Aravaipa Creek, Redfield Canyon, O'Donnell Creek), with no new introductions without careful evaluation.
  2. Rehabilitate damaged streams, notably the Babocomari River, and secure its watershed.
  3. Establish native fishes in excavated semi-natural floodplain habitats.
- **Selection criteria for reintroduction:** (a) probability of forming viable, self-sustaining populations; (b) contribution to other resource goals such as mosquito control, marsh rehabilitation and raptor food; (c) contribution to other recovery programs, such as the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] efforts in the Gila, Salt and Verde rivers; and (d) educational and National Showcase value.
- **Source stocks:** Hatchery Colorado Squawfish come from the upper Colorado basin; Razorback Sucker from Lake Mohave; desert pupfish from the Colorado River delta in Sonora; Sonoran Topminnow from the upper Santa Cruz River hatchery stock, with the nearest wild source in springs near Bylas, Arizona. Flannelmouth Sucker would come from the Virgin River, the most morphologically similar wild population.
- **Non-native control:** Curtail stocking of non-natives, encourage sport fishing for predators such as catfish, sunfishes and [[Largemouth Bass]], prohibit live bait to avoid introducing red shiner, and discourage mosquitofish stocking by vector control agencies in favor of native pupfish and topminnow.
- **Sequence of reestablishment:** Species of stable, erosive habitats first, then pool species, then marginal and floodplain species, and last big-river species if habitat allows.
- **Monitoring data handling:** Maps of distributions and relative reproductive success are recommended, with statistical comparison by chi-square or t-test, and a multi-year database to detect trends.
