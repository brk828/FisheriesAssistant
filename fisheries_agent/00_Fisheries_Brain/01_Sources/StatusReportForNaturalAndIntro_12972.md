# Status of Natural and Introduced Sonoran Topminnow Populations in Arizona Through 1985

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Poeciliidae #endangered-species #reintroduction

## Executive Summary

This report documents the status of natural and introduced populations of [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) in Arizona through 1985. The study monitored 71 introduction sites established between 1976-1984, finding 49 (69%) successfully occupied by topminnow. Of these, 30 populations were at least three years old, meeting the recovery plan requirement for downlisting from endangered to threatened status. All known natural populations were surveyed, revealing instability in several sites due to invasion by nonnative [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). Statistical analyses identified habitat characteristics distinguishing successful from unsuccessful introduction sites, including drainage area, substrate composition, aquatic vegetation abundance, and habitat diversity. The study recommends specific site selection criteria for future introductions and addresses genetic considerations regarding broodstock management.

## Background & Description

The [[Sonoran Topminnow]] is a small live-bearing fish endemic to the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. Two subspecies occur in Arizona: *P. o. occidentalis* (Gila River drainage) and *P. o. sonoriense* (Rio Yaqui). Both are listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act (Federal Register 32:4001). Once abundant and widespread, both subspecies suffered dramatic declines due to exploitative land and water use practices during colonization of this arid region.

The species' recovery plan (U.S.F.W.S., 1983) identified necessary steps to protect extant natural populations and increase distribution and abundance through introductions into suitable habitats. In 1981, a Memorandum of Understanding was signed by [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and [[U.S. Forest Service]] allowing introduction of the Gila form into suitable habitats on Forest Service lands in the Gila River drainage. One condition waived protection at introduction sites so water use (primarily livestock) would not be interrupted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Natural topminnow populations historically inhabited highly variable environments subject to extreme population fluctuations from flooding, thermal fluctuations, and habitat desiccation. Most naturally occurring populations are found in oak woodland and grasslands with gradual slopes and stream gradients typically less than 2%. In contrast, introduction sites represent a biased sample with little similarity to historic habitats, ranging from upper Sonoran desert scrub to mixed Pinon-Juniper communities at higher elevations, with steeper slopes and gradients.

The primary threat to natural populations is invasion by nonnative [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), which interacts aggressively with topminnow, causing fin degradation and population decline. Other nonnative species documented include [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), and [[Bluegill]] (*L. macrochirus*). Native species co-occurring with topminnow include [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) and [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*).

Environmental conditions at successful sites varied widely: elevation 544-1468 m, drainage area 0-64.11 km², water temperature 15-30°C, dissolved oxygen 2.0-14.0 mg/l, pH 6.8-9.0, conductivity 200-2800 μmho/cm. Successful sites typically featured abundant emergent aquatic vegetation (Typha sp., Scirpus sp., Nasturtium sp.), diverse substrate composition (silt, sand, gravel), and stable riparian vegetation.

## Key Findings

**Introduction Success Rates:**
- Of 71 monitored introduction sites, 49 (69%) were successfully occupied by topminnow
- 30 populations were ≥3 years old, exceeding the recovery plan requirement for downlisting
- Introductions spanned 1976-1984 on U.S. Forest Service, Bureau of Land Management, and private lands
- All introductions except [[Heron Spring]] used genetic stock from [[Monkey Spring]]

**Natural Population Status:**
- All known natural populations were monitored in 1985
- [[Bylas Springs]] and [[Salt Creek]] were renovated with Antimycin A in 1984 to remove mosquitofish; barriers constructed but Bylas Springs was reinvaded due to barrier failure
- [[Middle Spring]] topminnow population remained intact without mosquitofish
- [[Sheehy Spring]] population critically low (only 2 adult females collected); mosquitofish abundant
- [[Sharp Spring]] maintained tenuous balance between topminnow and mosquitofish
- [[Redrock Canyon]] invaded by largemouth bass and mosquitofish from upstream stock tanks
- [[Sonoita Creek]] and [[Santa Cruz River]] continued supporting topminnow despite numerous nonnative species
- [[Cottonwood Spring]], [[Monkey Spring]], and [[Cienega Creek]] populations stable

**Habitat Characteristics - Statistical Comparisons:**
- Stream gradient showed no significant difference among natural, successful, and unsuccessful sites
- Drainage area: unsuccessful sites had significantly smaller drainages (mean 0.8±1.6 km²) compared to successful (3.9±8.6 km²) and natural sites (7.2±18.4 km²)
- Bedrock substrate: introduction sites had 7× more bedrock (successful 14.0±23.3%, unsuccessful 14.1±25.8%) than natural sites (1.7±5.8%)
- Emergent aquatic vegetation cover: substantially less in unsuccessful sites (13.8±24.9%) compared to successful (30.6±28.2%) and natural sites (27.9±22.9%)
- Ponding: common in unsuccessful (43.1±50.6%) and less so in successful sites (22.6±36.6%); not a component of natural habitats (0%)

**Discriminant Analysis Results:**
- Linear discriminant function developed from 55 introduction sites explained 72% of variance (canonical correlation = 0.85)
- 13 variables identified as classification function coefficients: temperature, conductivity, bank vegetation density, substrate percentages (sand, bedrock, sapropel), cover types (emergent aquatic vegetation, submergent aquatic vegetation, filamentous attached algae), habitat types (stream, pond, marsh), and depth
- Classification function provided 89.1% correct classification of sites and correctly classified 7 natural sites as successful
- Previous classification functions from 1982 introductions (Brooks, 1985) only 50% accurate when applied to 1983 sites, indicating limited predictive value

**Site Selection Criteria Recommended:**
- Drainage area < 10.0 km²
- Elevation < 1600 m
- Stream flow < 0.1 m³/sec
- Stream gradient < 3%
- Gradual slopes, no confined stream channels
- Substrate: composite of silt, sand, gravel
- Abundant aquatic vegetation
- Diverse habitat composition, preferably spring streams and cienega-like habitats; avoid ponds
- Stable and diverse riparian vegetation
- Perennial water source with presence of snails
- Native fishes only
- Within Gila River drainage

**Genetic Considerations:**
- Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) criticized use of [[Monkey Spring]] stock due to low genetic variability
- Recommended use of heterozygous population from thermally fluctuating environment, preferably [[Sharp Spring]]
- However, [[Cienega Creek]] topminnow (from large, variable habitat) also showed low heterozygosity
- Angus and Schultz (1985) found inbred strains of related poeciliid (*Poeciliopsis lucida*) able to canalize phenotype despite homozygosity
- Sharp Spring stock acquired August-September 1985; approximately 5000 topminnow produced by August 1986 from 192 founders
- All Monkey Spring stock removed from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and transferred to Arizona facilities or stocked in Arizona sites
- Future management will follow Meffe (1986) recommendations: maintain large genetic stock and rotate hatchery stock as necessary

**Management Actions at Natural Sites:**
- Barrier construction at [[Bylas Springs]], [[Middle Spring]], and [[Salt Creek]] (winter 1983-1984)
- Renovation with Antimycin A at Bylas Springs and Salt Creek (April and June 1984)
- Fencing to protect habitats from livestock at Bylas Springs and Salt Creek
- Site improvement structures in [[Redrock Canyon]] to enhance habitat
- Conservation easements on private land ([[Sharp Spring]])
- Recommendation: renovate all stock tanks in Redrock Canyon drainage to remove nonnative fishes

**Downlisting Recommendation:**
- Recovery plan requirement met: minimum 20 introduced populations successfully reestablished for ≥3 years within historic range
- 30 populations met this criterion as of 1985
- Cautions noted: longevity uncertain, sites subject to drought, development activities, and nonnative fish introductions
- Intense follow-up monitoring requisite to downlisting

**Threats and Failures:**
- [[Mesquite Tank]] population destroyed when tank drained by unauthorized person(s); topminnow discovered in two bedrock pools 1 km downstream
- Severe flooding eliminated topminnow from several sites with narrow, confined stream beds in large drainage areas
- Mosquitofish invasion primary threat to natural populations
- Stock tanks containing nonnative fishes upstream of natural habitats pose ongoing threat
- Heavy cattle use, ephemeral water, and severe flooding characterized unsuccessful sites
