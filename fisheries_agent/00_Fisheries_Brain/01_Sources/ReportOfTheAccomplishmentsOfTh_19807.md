# Native Fish Work Group 1990 Accomplishments and Lake Mohave Backwater-Rearing Facility Management Plan

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Catostomidae #razorback-sucker #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document reports on the 1990 accomplishments of the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), a multiagency task force established to address the declining [[Razorback Sucker]] population in [[Lake Mohave]]. The NFWG successfully drafted a comprehensive Management/Research Plan and constructed a spawning cove at [[Yuma Cove]] through berm modification. The group stocked 90 sexually ripe razorback suckers in January for on-site production, with the goal of releasing a minimum of 10,000 fish (25-30 cm length) into Lake Mohave over five years to augment the aging population (estimated 60,000 individuals, ages 25-45 years). The plan addresses recruitment failure attributed primarily to predation by non-native fishes and proposes a phased approach using isolated backwater facilities for spawning and grow-out.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system of western North America. Once common throughout the mainstream and major tributaries from Wyoming to Mexico, the species is now reduced to remnant populations in the [[Green River]] (Utah), upper [[Colorado River]] (Colorado-Utah), [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada), and scattered individuals elsewhere. The species was proposed for Federal listing as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973.

[[Lake Mohave]] contains the largest remaining population of razorback suckers, estimated at 60,000 individuals. However, this population consists entirely of older fish ranging from 25 to 45 years old. Although spawning, incubation, and hatching are successful, natural recruitment is extremely limited or totally lacking. Larval razorback suckers disappear at lengths of 10-12 mm, with predation by non-native fishes identified as the single most important factor responsible for recruitment failure.

Previous experiments (1984-1986) in a temporarily isolated backwater at [[Yuma Cove]] demonstrated that young suckers could be produced in habitats devoid of exotic fish species. Larvae attained total lengths exceeding 16 mm before the berm was breached by storm-generated wave action. In 1986, growth comparable to hatchery conditions was achieved: 10 cm by June 1986, 18.2-20.4 cm by December 1986.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] system has been dramatically altered by human activities, with historical habitats greatly modified. Non-native fish species pose the critical threat to razorback sucker recruitment in [[Lake Mohave]]. Predation studies by Marsh and Brooks indicated stocked suckers were rapidly preyed upon by catfish and other non-native species, particularly [[Green Sunfish]]. Stomach content analyses verified consumption of larval razorback suckers.

The NFWG comprises technical representatives from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[National Park Service]], [[Arizona State University]], [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]], [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. Previous reintroduction efforts (1980-1990) released approximately 11.5 million larvae and juvenile fish under a 10-year Memorandum of Understanding between FWS and AGFD, with little indication of successful reestablishment.

The backwater-rearing concept utilizes semi-natural conditions for on-site spawning by adults in waters free from predacious fish, without supplemental feeding. Two backwater areas were selected: [[Yuma Cove]] (permanent facility) and [[Davis Cove]] (temporary barrier test site). The normal reservoir elevation cycle maintains minimum 5-foot depths in Yuma Cove until September-October, when depths drop to 2-4 feet, facilitating fish collection.

## Key Findings

**1990 Accomplishments:**
- NFWG drafted comprehensive Management/Research Plan identifying objectives and methodologies
- Completed berm enlargement at [[Yuma Cove]], permanently isolating the backwater from [[Lake Mohave]]
- Conducted [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) compliance and acquired necessary permits
- Poisoned backwater to remove exotic fishes
- Stocked 90 sexually ripe razorback suckers in January 1991 for on-site production
- All goals accomplished in less than 8 months

**Management Plan - Phase One (5-year study):**
- Goal: Produce and stock minimum 10,000 subadult razorback suckers (25-30 cm) from on-site facilities
- Target stocking: 30 females and 60 males for spawning
- Expected survival: 20-40% of backwater-reared fish reaching adulthood
- All reared fish to be tagged using [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tags
- Success based on recapturing backwater-reared fish from reservoir population

**Site Preparation Protocol:**
- Two ichthyocide treatments: rotenone (August-September), antimycin (autumn)
- Detoxification by December-January
- Fish salvage operation prior to poisoning
- Possible fertilization to stimulate primary production

**Rearing Strategy (Preferred Approach):**
- [[Yuma Cove]]: Spawning only (January-April)
- [[Davis Cove]]: Grow-out facility for 15 cm fish
- September collection: Young fish tagged and transported to Davis Cove
- Target size: 25-30 cm before reservoir release (12-18 months growth)
- Supplemental cage rearing facilities at marinas for public education

**Monitoring Program:**
- Bi-annual sampling (November and March) in [[Lake Mohave]]
- Collection methods: Trammel netting, electrofishing, beach seining
- March "razorback roundup" (1988-1990): 2,152 suckers tagged, 2.8% return rate
- All captured fish scanned for PIT tags
- Expected detection of stocked fish beginning following spring after release

**Tagging Technology:**
- PIT tags selected over binary coded wire tags
- Cost: $3-4 per tag, $1,200 per monitor, $30 per implanter
- 1,000 fish + 3 detectors = $7,600
- Dexter studies: 100% tag verification, 83% survival in razorback suckers ≥120 mm
- Tags implanted in dorsal musculature
- Minimum 1,000-2,000 fish tagged per stocking effort

**Population Goals:**
- Maintain present adult population level: 60,000 fish
- Release 10,000 fish (25 cm) over 5 years
- Population level may be modified based on viability dynamics and genetic data

**Future Phases:**
- Phase Two: Expand to other basin areas and species ([[Bonytail Chub]] *Gila elegans*, [[Colorado Squawfish]] *Ptychocheilus lucius*, [[Humpback Chub]])
- Phase Three: Formalize management agreement via Memorandum of Understanding for long-term population maintenance

**Reporting Structure:**
- Annual progress reports coordinated by [[Bureau of Reclamation]]
- Final report analyzing success and recommendations by end of 1995
- Public relations program: Information brochures, signs, poster displays, professional video
- Agency-specific responsibilities: FWS/ASU (monitoring), AGFD (renovation), NPS (NEPA), Reclamation (design/construction)

**1991-1992 Planned Activities:**
- January 1991: Yuma Cove stocking, conceptual designs for Davis Cove barriers
- March 1991: Major monitoring effort (Spring "round-up")
- June-August 1991: Davis Cove modification ($15,000 construction), fish salvage and renovation ($1,500)
- September 1991: Collection, tagging ($10,500-$15,500 equipment), transport to Davis Cove
- November 1991: Fall "round-up"
- January 1992: Yuma Cove restocking
- September 1992: Second collection/tagging effort ($5,000-$10,000 tags)
