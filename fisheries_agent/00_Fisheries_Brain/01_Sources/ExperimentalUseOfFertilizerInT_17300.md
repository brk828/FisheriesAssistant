# Experimental Use of Fertilizer in the Production of Fish-Food Organisms and Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-food-organisms #pond-fertilization

## Executive Summary

This 1949 Michigan State College technical bulletin (Robert C. Ball, prepared in cooperation with the [[Michigan Institute For Fisheries Research]]) reports a 1946 field experiment in which 21 hatchery ponds at three state fish hatcheries were fertilized to test whether inorganic fertilizer raises the production of fish and their food organisms in northern, hard-water Michigan waters. Results were clearest for the food base: invertebrate production was 42 percent greater and plankton production 3.3 times greater in fertilized ponds. Fish production responses were positive in every paired comparison but were confounded by unwanted "volunteer" fish, low survival of stocked fry, and variable pond bottoms, so the author treats fish-level conclusions as tentative. This is a progress report; the broader aims (rates, composition, stocking) were only partly addressed.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Growing public demand for better fishing and interest in raising productivity of natural and impounded waters through [[Pond Fertilization]].
- **Prior work:** Most fertilization research had been done in southern states (Swingle 1947; Surber 1943, 1948). The author judged southern recommendations unlikely to transfer to Michigan because of physical, chemical and climatic differences.
- **Partners:** The Institute for Fisheries Research (Michigan Department of Conservation) supplied materials and personnel; Michigan State College supplied facilities. Two fisheries technicians worked full time at the Wolf Lake and Drayton Plains hatcheries.
- **Study sites:** Three state-owned hatcheries in southern Michigan: [[Wolf Lake Hatchery]], [[Hastings Hatchery]] and [[Drayton Plains Hatchery]]. Ponds were small (1 to 1.8 acres), with a maximum depth of 6 feet and average depth of 2.5 feet. Ponds were paired (fertilized vs. control) for similar size, water source, basin shape and bottom type.
- **Water chemistry:** Wolf Lake spring water had a methyl orange alkalinity of about 160 ppm (high carbonate hardness). Hastings, fed by West Creek, had about 215 ppm. Drayton Plains drew on a variable branch of the [[Clinton River]].
- **Companion work:** A master's thesis by R. D. VanDeusen (1947) provided plankton data, and a bait-minnow propagation project ran in parallel.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fertilizer selection.** Greenhouse trials with water from all three hatcheries, directed by Dr. Peter I. Tack, found that a high-nitrogen, high-phosphorus fertilizer gave the most organic matter. A 10-6-4 (N-P₂O₅-K₂O) formulation was therefore used.

**Application.**
- Inorganic fertilizer was broadcast from a row boat. The first application was June 18, 1946 and the last August 26, 1946. Delivery was delayed about 2 months by a spring fertilizer shortage.
- Barnyard manure at 1 ton per 1.5 acres of water surface was applied early in spring at Wolf Lake and Hastings. The first inorganic application came about 60 days later than planned.
- Rate schedules at Wolf Lake (33.3 lb/acre per application at weekly, biweekly and 8-week intervals) showed no plankton advantage for the more frequent schedules over a 3-week interval, so 3-week intervals were adopted at the other sites.
- Minnow ponds received 100 lb/acre every 3 weeks.

**Biological context.** Invertebrate volume dipped in early summer, a pattern the author linked to peak emergence of insects in local lakes. In fertilized ponds, invertebrate standing crops recovered and grew after mid-July, while controls did not. Midges, which have more than one generation per year, responded first and most rapidly.

**Biological interactions.**
- [[Volunteer Fish]] and invertebrates such as tadpoles and crayfish competed for food and preyed on young fish.
- Brook stickleback (*Eucalia inconstans*) was the main volunteer at Drayton Plains; about 113,000 were in pond 6 when it was drained.
- Annelids (large earthworms, tubificids, leeches) made up 38 to more than 50 percent of invertebrate volume, but most were too deep in the sediment or too large to be available to young fish.

## Key Findings

**1. Food base responded most strongly.**
- Invertebrate production (dredge samples) was 42 percent greater in fertilized ponds.
- Plankton production was 3.3 times greater.
- Plankton numbers per liter were far higher in fertilized ponds throughout the season. Volume averaged 510 lb/acre-foot in the two fertilized ponds versus 156 lb/acre-foot in the two controls.
- Standing crop of invertebrate fish food was 68 percent greater in the fertilized Wolf Lake pair (113.3 vs. 67.2 lb/acre) and 21 percent greater at Drayton Plains (99.9 vs. 82.6 lb/acre).

**2. Suckers (Wolf Lake, 1946).**
- Eyed eggs were stocked at 10,000 to 100,000 per acre. Recovery was about 7 percent in the fertilized ponds, and the survival of eggs was mainly lost in the egg stage.
- Survival was higher in fertilized ponds, but factors such as predation and volunteer fish were uncontrolled.
- Fertilized ponds produced suckers of more uniform size.

**3. Chubs (Hastings and Drayton Plains).**
- Hastings: eight ponds (four fertilized, four controls) stocked with sac fry. Survival averaged 6.3 percent with large variation.
- Fertilized ponds produced greater total weight of organisms in every case.
- Chub yield per acre was not clearly higher in fertilized ponds. At Drayton Plains, the fertilized pond had lower chub production despite greater total organism production.

**4. Bass and bluegills (Drayton Plains).**
- Advanced bass fry (about 1 inch) survived well. Bluegill golden fry survived poorly.
- The author recommended stocking bluegills as adults and bass as fingerlings or adults.
- The data were too limited to show a fertilizer effect on bass or bluegill production.

**5. Individual size.** Chironomid midges appeared larger in fertilized ponds, though species were not identified. The author considered this suggestive only.

**6. Overall production (Table 12, averages of all ponds).**

| Category | Fertilized | Unfertilized |
|---|---|---|
| Largemouth bass (lb/acre) | 16.5 | 15.1 |
| Chubs (lb/acre) | 7.5 | 7.4 |
| Bluegills (lb/acre) | 3.3 | 1.7 |
| Suckers (lb/acre) | 15.3 | 5.8 |
| Bottom organisms (lb/acre) | 106 | 75 |
| Tadpoles and crayfish (lb/acre) | 11.3 | 7 |
| Plankton (lb/acre-foot) | 524 | 156 |

The author reported that the average for fertilized ponds exceeded the average for unfertilized ponds in every category. Individual ponds did not always follow this pattern.

**7. Trophic principle.** The author proposed, tentatively, that the closer a fish's feeding is to the base of the food chain, the more fertilizer raises its total weight. Effects were strongest for plankton, invertebrates and lower vertebrates, and weaker for fish.

**8. Limitations.** Pond-to-pond variation in bottom type and biology was large. Controls sometimes outperformed treated ponds. Operational problems hampered evaluation of fish production. The author cautioned that the results are not yet enough to justify general recommendations.
