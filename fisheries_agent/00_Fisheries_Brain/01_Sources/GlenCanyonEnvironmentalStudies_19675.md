# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies: Effects of Glen Canyon Dam Operations on Downstream Resources

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft technical report (August 1987) presents the findings of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES), a multi-agency study commissioned by the Secretary of the Interior in December 1982. The study assessed how releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] affect environmental and recreational resources in Glen Canyon and Grand Canyon along the [[Colorado River]]. Over 30 technical studies were conducted by more than 100 researchers from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[National Park Service]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Geological Survey]], [[Arizona Department of Game and Fish]], universities, and private consultants.

The report concludes that two aspects of current operations, flood releases and fluctuating releases, have substantial adverse effects on downstream resources. Modified release patterns could protect or enhance most resources. The study was not designed to recommend operational changes, which would require feasibility studies and NEPA compliance.

## Background & Description

The river below Glen Canyon Dam flows 15 miles through Glen Canyon National Recreation Area and then 277 miles through Grand Canyon National Park. The dam, completed in 1963, impounds [[Lake Powell]] and is a key facility of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] (CRSP). Its powerplant has eight generators and a capacity of 31,500 cfs. Glen Canyon Dam generates roughly $80 million annually in gross power revenues.

Post-dam operations are designed for "peaking power." Daily releases may vary from 5,000 to 30,000 cfs, changing the river level by 7 to more than 13 feet. This fluctuation prompted public concern about fishing, white-water boating, beach erosion, riparian vegetation, and native fish.

Five critical resources were targeted for study:

- [[Humpback Chub]] (federally endangered)
- [[Common Native Fish]]
- [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Trout Fishing]]
- [[Camping Beaches]] and other sand deposits
- [[Riparian Vegetation]] and wildlife, plus [[White-Water Boating]]

The GCES was conducted during 1983–1986, a period of unusually high reservoir releases after Lake Powell filled in 1980.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre- and post-dam conditions.** Before the dam, the river had seasonal floods, high sediment loads, and temperatures ranging from near freezing to about 80°F. Annual suspended sediment at Lees Ferry averaged 65.4 million tons (1948–1962). After dam closure, sediment at Lees Ferry fell to about 0.4 million tons in both 1982 and 1986, a decrease of about 99.5%. Water temperature now ranges from 46 to 54°F at Lees Ferry.

**Aquatic food base.** Clear, cold water allowed [[Cladophora]] algae to become the main primary producer. Associated invertebrates, such as the amphipod *Gammarus* and midges, support trout and other fishes. Of eight native fish species historically present, four remain in significant numbers. Several species, including the Colorado squawfish and bonytail, have been extirpated.

**Riparian changes.** Reduced flood peaks allowed a New High Water Zone of vegetation to develop on former flood-scoured sand. Tamarisk is a major component of this zone, and marshes developed in low areas. Riparian bird numbers and diversity increased. Breeding birds were estimated at five to ten times pre-dam densities for at least 10 species.

**Sediment and beaches.** Sand deposits in recirculation zones form the main camping beaches. Their supply now depends on tributaries below the dam, chiefly the Paria and Little Colorado Rivers and Kanab Creek, which together supply an estimated 2.9 million tons per year.

## Key Findings

**1. Flood releases cause severe, largely irreversible damage.**
- A flood release is defined as a discharge above 31,500 cfs. Under current operations, floods are expected in about one year in four and typically last four to six weeks in May or June.
- During the 1983 flood, vegetation loss reached 50% in some areas, and 95% of marshes were destroyed.
- Bell's vireo nest losses reached 75% at 90,000 cfs.
- Floods reduce the area of camping beaches. Losing a substantial number of beaches would reduce white-water recreation benefits by about 50%, roughly $5.2 million in an average year.
- The report concludes that floods more frequent than once in 20 years will cause loss of critical resources without substantial benefits.

**2. Fluctuating releases have mixed effects, with strong negative impacts on recreation.**
- Fluctuations, defined as daily changes over 10,000 cfs, reduce habitat for larval native fish and expose trout spawning beds in winter.
- Removing fluctuations would increase white-water recreation benefits by about $0.8 million in a typical year.
- Fluctuations also increase invertebrate drift, which may increase trout growth. Trout are the one resource that benefits.
- Stranding can be severe. More than 800 trout died after a flow drop from about 23,000 to 5,600 cfs in ten hours.

**3. Impacts to humpback chub differ from other resources.**
- The cold water restricts chub reproduction and larval rearing to the mouth of the Little Colorado River, which has spring and summer temperatures of 61–75°F.
- Spring floods above 40,000 cfs appear to benefit chub by enlarging low-velocity nursery habitat at the confluence.
- The 1978 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service opinion concluded that dam operations jeopardized the species.
- Operational changes are unlikely to expand chub range. The report concludes that reducing its vulnerability depends on non-operational alternatives, such as warming the mainstem.

**4. Fluctuations and steady flows affect common native fish and backwaters.**
- Steady flows create more backwaters. A census found 575 backwaters at 5,000 cfs compared with 191 at 28,000 cfs.
- Fluctuating flows produce less topographic relief and reduce backwater numbers and longevity.

**5. Five modified operations were developed.**
Each scenario eliminates floods and reduces or removes fluctuations. All are constrained by a minimum release of 8.23 maf per year, minimum flows of 1,000 cfs in winter and 3,000 cfs in summer, and the 31,500 cfs powerplant limit. The scenarios illustrate options and are not recommended operating plans.

- **Humpback chub:** high flows in May and June, steady flows otherwise. This benefits chub but may reduce beaches and trout growth.
- **Common native fish:** 5,000 cfs in June–August. This benefits backwaters but seriously harms white-water boating, with about $1.5 million in annual benefits lost.
- **Trout:** steady flows of at least 8,000 cfs in winter to protect spawning, with three two-week fluctuation periods. Mostly beneficial to other resources.
- **Beaches and vegetation:** steady flows of about 12,000 cfs in low-water years and 23,000 cfs in high-water years. Mostly favorable.
- **Recreation:** about 30,000 cfs in boating season and about 10,000 cfs during fishing season.

The report also evaluated a "natural conditions" pattern mimicking pre-dam flows. It concludes that natural conditions cannot be recreated without restoring warm temperatures and a large sediment supply.

**6. Recreation depends strongly on flow level and stability.**
- Commercial white-water passengers value a trip at about $900 at a steady 33,000 cfs but only about $300 at 10,000 cfs.
- The flood range (above 31,500 cfs) doubles the risk of flipping a boat at major rapids and nearly half of passengers walk around Crystal Rapid at flood flows, compared with 20% at 10,000–31,500 cfs.
- Glen Canyon anglers value a trip at about $126 at 10,000 cfs.
- Glen Canyon fishing has the highest accident risk at high flows, with 69% of recorded accidents occurring at 16,000–31,500 cfs.

**7. The Uprate and Rewind Program cannot yet be fully assessed.**
Raising the powerplant capacity from 31,500 to 33,100 cfs would raise the maximum water level by under one foot in moderate years. Sand-dependent resources may have stabilized near 27,500 cfs, so losses could be larger. The effects depend on how the new capacity is used, which has not been formalized.

**8. Non-operational options may help.**
- Supplemental trout stocking could relax the 8,000 cfs spawning-flow requirement, though natural fish are preferred by some anglers.
- A multi-level intake structure could warm releases to about 62°F in May and June. This could expand chub spawning, aid recovery of endangered fishes, and reduce hypothermia risk for boaters. Risks include warm-water exotic predators and effects on Lake Powell and Lake Mead.
- Structural measures such as revetments or sand resupply were considered but judged to have low acceptability.

**9. Management recommendations.**
The report recommends feasibility studies of operational changes, feasibility studies of non-operational measures, continued research and monitoring, and a coordinated inter-agency management mechanism for Glen and Grand Canyons. It also notes significant data gaps: a short three-year study, limited pre-flood data, and a study period that coincided with unusually high flows.
