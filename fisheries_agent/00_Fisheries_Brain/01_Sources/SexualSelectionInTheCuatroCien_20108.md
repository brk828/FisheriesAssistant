# Sexual Selection in the Cuatro Cienegas Pupfish: Mate Choice and Hybridization in Cyprinodon atrorus and Cyprinodon bifasciatus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae #sexual-selection #behavioral-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Ph.D. dissertation proposal by Anna Melina Ludlow at [[Lehigh University]] outlines a comprehensive study of sexual selection, mate choice, and hybridization in two endemic pupfish species from the [[Cuatro Cienegas]] basin in northern Mexico: [[Cyprinodon atrorus]] and [[Cyprinodon bifasciatus]]. The research aims to describe the breeding strategies of both species in natural conditions, examine mate choice processes, and investigate mechanisms of hybridization between these species. The study will employ both field observations and laboratory experiments to test hypotheses about male reproductive success, female mate preferences, habitat quality effects, male size and competitive ability, and the role of sexual selection in interspecific hybridization. The proposal is organized into five chapters covering general introduction, mating system description, sexual selection experiments, hybridization mechanisms, and general discussion.

## Background & Description

The [[Cuatro Cienegas]] basin is a small valley (less than 1500 km²) located on the eastern edge of the [[Sierra Madre Oriental]] in [[Coahuila]], [[Mexico]], within the [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. The basin contains diverse aquatic and semi-aquatic habitats including springs, marshes, rivers, lagoons, and canals. The fish fauna is unusually diverse, representing eight families with at least 16 native fish species, 8 of which are endemic.

[[Cyprinodon bifasciatus]] is restricted to headwater warm springs and their outlets, limited to warm water habitats (26.7°C to 34.5°C). This species is highly sensitive to temperature changes and may expand its range during summer when water temperatures increase. [[Cyprinodon atrorus]] inhabits shallow, saline marshy pools that warm and cool rapidly, characterized by marked variations in temperature, size, and water chemistry. Unlike C. bifasciatus, C. atrorus has adapted to highly variable habitats typical of most Cyprinodon species.

Breeding activities of C. atrorus change in intensity throughout the year, probably in relation to water temperature, with a breeding season beginning in late January, intensifying through summer, and declining in late fall. In contrast, C. bifasciatus may breed throughout the year due to its stable temperature requirements. Preliminary observations suggest C. bifasciatus has a breeding system similar to [[Cyprinodon pecosensis]], particularly regarding the presence of male alternative mating strategies.

The two species exhibit large morphological differences in both size and coloration. Despite habitat and morphological differences, C. atrorus and C. bifasciatus hybridize in some canals connecting their specific habitats and readily hybridize in laboratory conditions. This hybridization success has been attributed to low genetic divergence within the genus and similarity in egg morphology and developmental processes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Cuatro Cienegas]] basin represents a unique desert aquatic ecosystem with high endemism. The distinct habitat preferences of the two pupfish species reflect ecological specialization: C. bifasciatus occupies thermally stable warm springs while C. atrorus inhabits more variable peripheral habitats. These habitat differences should lead to dramatic differences in reproductive behavior between species (e.g., seasonal versus year-round breeding).

Habitat heterogeneity appears to influence the presence of alternative male mating strategies in pupfish. More heterogeneous habitats provide better hiding places for small males that obtain matings through "sneaky strategies." The breeding substrate (rocks versus sand/silt) plays a critical role in egg survival, as eggs deposited on sand or silt may have decreased viability due to silting and reduced aeration. Rocks provide microtopography with crevices offering protection for eggs.

The operational sex ratio (OSR) - the ratio of males ready to mate to females ready to mate - is predicted to be biased toward males in both species, resulting in more intense competition among males for females. This sex ratio bias influences the relative intensity of mating competition and sexual selection.

## Key Findings

**Proposed Research Objectives:**

1. **Mating System Description (Chapter 2):** Describe the mating systems of both species following [[Emlen and Oring]] (1977) framework, including manner of mate acquisition, number of mates, pair bond characteristics, and parental care patterns. Analyze determinants of male reproductive success in natural conditions.

2. **Male Alternative Mating Strategies:** Preliminary observations reveal C. bifasciatus exhibits three male mating strategies: (a) territorial males that aggressively defend territories with characteristic breeding coloration, (b) satellite males with territorial male coloration but no territory defense, and (c) sneaky males with cryptic female-like coloration that are significantly smaller and join spawning pairs inconspicuously.

3. **Hypotheses for Male Reproductive Success in C. bifasciatus:**
   - Males defending territories on rocks will have higher reproductive success than males on sand or silt
   - Territorial males will have higher reproductive success than satellites and sneakers
   - Males exhibiting alternative mating behaviors will associate with more successful territorial males
   - Territory size will be important for C. atrorus where habitat is homogeneous

4. **Female Behavior Predictions:**
   - Females will not defend territories but move widely across larger ranges
   - Females will spawn with more than one male (polyandry)
   - Females will prefer spawning with males defending rock territories
   - Larger females will spawn mainly with males on rocks; smaller females with males on sand/silt

5. **Laboratory Experiments (Chapter 3):** Test female mate choice preferences regarding:
   - **Habitat quality:** Females expected to prefer rock substrate over sand
   - **Male size:** Females predicted to prefer larger males; larger males expected to outcompete smaller males
   - **Competitive ability:** Females may assess male quality through observing male-male contests
   - **Female copying:** Females may be attracted to males observed consorting with other females

6. **Hybridization Mechanisms (Chapter 4):** Investigate whether hybridization results from:
   - Pre-existing female preferences for certain traits that override conspecific preferences
   - Habitat asymmetries affecting encounter rates and mating opportunities
   - Size differences between species affecting competitive ability
   - Male choice or lack thereof for conspecific versus heterospecific females

7. **Null Hypotheses for Hybridization:**
   - Females of both species mate randomly with males of either species
   - Males do not discriminate between conspecific and heterospecific females

8. **Alternative Hypotheses:**
   - Females prefer heterospecific males
   - Females prefer conspecifics but mate with heterospecifics when no choice available
   - Males prefer conspecific females but mate with heterospecifics when necessary

9. **Methodological Approaches:**
   - Field observations using focal sampling (20-minute observations on 30 territorial males per locality)
   - Population censuses using 5m transects to estimate operational sex ratios and size-class distributions
   - Laboratory choice experiments using divided aquaria with clear/opaque partitions
   - No-choice mating trials measuring latency to spawn
   - Male-male competition trials to assess fighting ability

10. **Expected Contributions:** This research will provide insights into how sexual selection operates in closely related species, the role of habitat in mate choice, mechanisms maintaining or breaking down reproductive isolation, and whether sexual selection acts as a diversifying or congealing force in speciation processes.
