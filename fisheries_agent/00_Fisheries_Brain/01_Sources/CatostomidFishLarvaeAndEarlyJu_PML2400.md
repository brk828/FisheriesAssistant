# Morphological Descriptions, Comparisons, and Computer-Interactive Key to Catostomid Larvae and Early Juveniles of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife Technical Publication (No. 42, September 2004), by Darrel E. Snyder and Robert T. Muth with illustrations by G. Lynn Bjork, is an expanded and retitled update of the 1990 guide to larval and early juvenile suckers of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It provides developmental descriptions, morphometric and meristic data, pigmentation and skeletal characters for seven catostomid species, a comparative summary, and a [[Computer-Interactive Key]] built in the DELTA Intkey program. The guide's purpose is to support identification of early life stages, which are difficult to assess and yet important for monitoring, protecting and managing native and endangered fishes, particularly [[Razorback Sucker]].

## Background & Description

The guide covers the native [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*C. discobolus*), and [[Mountain Sucker]] (*C. platyrhynchus*), along with the non-native [[White Sucker]] (*C. commersoni*), [[Longnose Sucker]] (*C. catostomus*) and [[Utah Sucker]] (*C. ardens*). Previously undescribed longnose sucker larvae were added, and Utah sucker larvae were described for the first time by these authors.

Development is divided into a combined interval terminology: larva, protolarva, mesolarva (preflexion, flexion, postflexion), metalarva and juvenile, with yolk-sac, yolk-bearing and yolk-free modifiers. Gut-coil phases 1 to 5 track internal development. Specimens were drawn from [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] culture, wild collections and museum material, with 553 specimens analyzed in detail for morphometrics and meristics. Clearing and staining (alcian blue for cartilage, alizarin red for bone) supported skeletal study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most UCRB catostomids are non-guarding, open-substrate lithophils. Eggs are demersal and initially adhesive, hatching in roughly 5 to 14 days depending on temperature. Larvae remain in gravel for one to two weeks before drifting downstream, usually at night, and occupy low-velocity shoreline and backwater areas. Razorback Sucker is an endangered species, and its recovery is an objective of multi-agency efforts in the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] and the [[San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]]. Hybridization among Colorado River catostomids is well documented (for example white × bluehead sucker hybrids as small as 25 mm SL), complicating identification. Distribution and status of several species are poorly documented.

## Key Findings

- **Species coverage:** Seven catostomids are covered, with full descriptive accounts, developmental size data, morphometric and meristic tables, and illustrations for each.
- **Specimen base:** 553 specimens were analyzed in detail; hundreds more were documented for size, developmental state, skeleton and pigmentation.
- **Egg size:** Egg diameters are 3.3–3.9 mm for flannelmouth sucker, 2.2–3.5 mm for others (bluehead 3.3–3.5), with razorback and mountain sucker eggs noted as relatively small (about 2.3–2.8 mm for razorback, which is 2.5–2.8 mm in the species account).
- **Developmental timing:** Flannelmouth larvae finish yolk absorption at about 15 mm SL, while razorback, mountain and some white and longnose suckers finish at much smaller sizes. Transition to the juvenile period ranges widely, with white and Utah suckers at the smallest sizes.
- **Meristics:** Lateral scale counts are usually fewer than 75 for white and Utah suckers, more than 85 for longnose, bluehead and flannelmouth suckers, and 75–85 for mountain and razorback suckers. Typical principal dorsal-fin ray counts are 14–15 for razorback sucker, and 10 for longnose and mountain suckers.
- **Myomeres and vertebrae:** Combined total counts are typically 47 or more for bluehead and flannelmouth suckers and 47 or fewer for Utah, longnose, white and mountain suckers; razorback typically has 46–48. Myomere counts alone are of limited diagnostic value because ranges overlap.
- **Morphometrics:** Protolarval yolk remaining distinguishes species: longnose, mountain and razorback suckers consume most or all yolk by the end of the protolarval phase, while flannelmouth retains about half.
- **Pigmentation:** Peritoneal pigmentation is the most diagnostic character for bluehead and mountain suckers. Razorback larvae rarely have more than six melanophores along the ventral midline, and razorback juveniles often show pigmented dorsal-to-caudal fin membranes.
- **Mouth and lips:** Subterminal mouths in bluehead metalarvae appear by 19 mm SL. Notches at lip corners are present in bluehead and mountain suckers and absent in the others.
- **Skeletal features:** The large, fan-shaped first interneural bone is diagnostic for razorback sucker; subgenus *Pantosteus* (bluehead, mountain) has smaller blocky interneurals and mandibles positioned posteriorly. Frontoparietal fontanelle width-to-length ratios decline in most species, but razorback retains a wider fontanelle, and mountain sucker's closes in the single yearling examined.
- **Computer-interactive key:** The key comprises 112 characters and 234 taxon items (species by developmental interval and size), runs in DELTA Intkey under Windows 95 or later, uses about 2.2 MB for the program and about 1 MB for the data set, and is available free for non-commercial use. It can be subset by species or river reach.
- **Identification guidance:** Specimens that cannot be resolved should be marked tentatively (e.g., *Xyrauchen texanus?*), left at family level (unidentified Catostomidae) or genus (*Catostomus* sp.). Hybrids are possible, and the key is an aid rather than a definitive authority.
- **Ancillary information:** Reproductive notes, spawning temperatures and drift behavior are provided in each species account, supporting field interpretation.
