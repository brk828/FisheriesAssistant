# Drainage map of Arizona showing perennial streams and some important wetlands

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae #hydrology #Arizona

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1978 USGS/Arizona Game and Fish Department compilation (Brown, Carmony, and Turner) presenting a **drainage map of Arizona** that delineates perennial, intermittent, ephemeral, and wastewater-fed streams alongside wetlands and reservoirs. Critically, the map ties elevation bands to fish thermal guild distribution, distinguishing **warm-water fish habitat** (lowland streams) from **cold-water fish habitat** (highland streams), naming [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Red Shiner]] as exclusive warm-water indicator species at the lowest elevation band (<3,500 ft), and [[Arizona Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] as exclusive cold-water indicator species at the highest elevation band (≥7,500 ft). The accompanying annotated bibliography, spanning 19th-century exploration journals through 1970s hydrogeologic surveys, documents a widespread historical decline of perennial flow, cienegas, and marshes across Arizona due to groundwater depletion, diversion, and arroyo cutting — representing a long-term contraction of native aquatic fish habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose**: The map and glossary classify Arizona's stream network by flow permanence (perennial, intermittent, ephemeral, interrupted), flow regulation status (regulated vs. unregulated), and water source (natural base flow vs. municipal/industrial/agricultural wastewater).
- **Elevation-based fish zonation** (used as the ecological classification key on the map):
  - **<3,500 ft**: Unregulated streams provide habitat *exclusively* for warm-water species — [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) and [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*, now *Cyprinella lutrensis*).
  - **3,500–5,500 ft**: Streams "mainly" populated by warm-water fish species.
  - **5,500–7,500 ft**: Streams "mainly" populated by cold-water fish species.
  - **≥7,500 ft**: Streams provide habitat *exclusively* for cold-water species — [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*, now *Oncorhynchus apache*) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*, now *Oncorhynchus mykiss*).
- **Stream taxonomy (Glossary)**:
  - *Perennial*: flows continuously.
  - *Intermittent*: flows seasonally, fed by springs or snowmelt.
  - *Ephemeral*: flows only in direct response to precipitation; channel remains above the water table.
  - *Continuous* vs. *Interrupted* (spatial classification): interrupted reaches alternate between perennial, intermittent, and ephemeral character.
  - *Wastewater streams*: channels that were formerly perennial but now flow mainly/entirely from municipal, industrial, or agricultural effluent.
- **Base flow**: defined as sustained dry-weather discharge from groundwater into a channel; historically stable year-to-year in unregulated systems.
- **Wetlands/Cienegas**: marshes, bogs, and shallow water bodies supporting emergent/submergent aquatic flora; many shown on the historical map are now "former wetlands" (dried).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The annotated bibliography (compiled by N.B. Carmony) provides over a century of historical observations documenting the shrinkage of aquatic habitat across Arizona, directly relevant to the historic range and decline of native fish assemblages:

- **Widespread loss of perennial flow and cienegas**: Multiple 19th-century travelers/surveyors (Bartlett 1852; Bell 1869; Leach 1858; Way 1858) documented perennial or marshy reaches — e.g., the [[Babocomari River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[Santa Cruz River]], [[San Simon Cienega]], [[Cienega Creek]], [[Sonoita Creek]] — that are now ephemeral, dry, or well-drained.
- **Groundwater decline and arroyo cutting**: Bryan (1925, 1928) linked arroyo cutting and vegetation change to declining groundwater tables across the arid Southwest, a major driver of stream drying.
- **Anthropogenic diversion**: Bartlett (1852) recorded the Gila River drying below the Pima villages in 1852 due to complete upstream diversion for Indigenous irrigation — an early documented case of flow dewatering impacting fish habitat.
- **Fish fauna as historical proxy**: Minckley (1973, *Fishes of Arizona*) inferred former perennial conditions in [[San Bernardino Creek]] based on historical fish species collected below Astin Spring, illustrating use of fish distribution records as indicators of paleohydrology.
- **Miller (1961)** explicitly addressed "Man and the changing fish fauna of the American Southwest," documenting anthropogenic impacts on native fish assemblages region-wide.
- **Regulated flow impacts**: The glossary explicitly notes that regulated streams (dams, diversions) alter discharge patterns, temperature regimes, and suspended sediment loads in ways "unfavorable to the native aquatic fauna."
- **Wastewater-dependent systems**: Streams sustained by treated effluent are flagged as potentially degraded habitats due to contaminating waste products.
- Notable formerly perennial/marshy systems now degraded or dry: [[Whitewater Draw]], [[Aravaipa Creek]] headwater marsh, [[Rillito Creek]], [[Kanab Creek]], [[Little Colorado River]] (dry reach reported 1879), [[San Simon River]].

## Key Findings

1. **Two-tier thermal fish guild system**: Arizona streams are ecologically stratified by elevation into warm-water (<5,500 ft) and cold-water (>5,500 ft) fish habitat zones, with strict exclusivity at the extremes (<3,500 ft and ≥7,500 ft).
2. **Indicator species identified**:
   - Warm-water exclusive: [[Longfin Dace]], [[Red Shiner]]
   - Cold-water exclusive: [[Arizona Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]]
3. **Flow regulation and wastewater inputs are identified as key stressors** capable of degrading conditions for native aquatic fauna, independent of natural flow classification.
4. **Historical record confirms substantial habitat contraction**: numerous perennial reaches, cienegas, and marshes documented from the 1840s–1930s (Babocomari, San Pedro, Santa Cruz, San Simon, Sonoita Creek, Cienega Creek, Aravaipa Creek, Rillito Creek) have since become ephemeral or dry, indicating long-term loss of native fish habitat connectivity across Arizona's drainage network.
5. **Fish distribution used as paleoecological/hydrological evidence**: Historical fish collection records (per Minckley 1973) were used to infer former stream permanence, linking ichthyological data to hydrogeological reconstruction.
6. **Methodological note**: The map itself (1:1,000,000 scale, plus 1:500,000 companion) was produced by compiling more than a century of survey, exploration, and agency records — establishing this as a synthesis/meta-analysis resource rather than primary field data.
