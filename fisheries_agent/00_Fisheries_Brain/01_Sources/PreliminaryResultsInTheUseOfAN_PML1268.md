# Preliminary Results in the Use of a Nursery Pond as a Tool in Fishery Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Esocidae #Percidae #Ictaluridae #Moronidae

## Executive Summary

This early report by William E. Keith of the [[Arkansas Game and Fish Commission]] evaluates the separate-structure [[Nursery Pond]] as a fishery management tool for large multi-purpose reservoirs. A nursery pond is a controlled, separately built pond connected to a [[Reservoir]] by a pipe, gate and canal, which allows fry to be reared to fingerling or catchable size before release. The author concludes that the technique succeeded in establishing [[Walleye]] populations in Norfork and Bull Shoals reservoirs and in producing a fishery for [[Striped Bass]] and [[Albino Channel Catfish]] in Lake Greeson, while noting unresolved problems with cost, reservoir water-level fluctuation, draining losses and predation.

## Background & Description

Historically, reservoir fishery management relied largely on regulations and limited interventions, and the author notes that exotic or non-native introductions were a recent approach to fully use ecological niches. Earlier nursery concepts included fencing shallow bays to protect spawning adults, improving spawning sites, and using pond overflow to supply streams. Natural analogues include shallow, marshy sloughs that are key to [[Northern Pike]] production. Renovated [[Oxbow Lake]]s in the Saline River, Arkansas (Mathis, 1964) were restocked with game fish that later emigrated into the river during high water.

The separate-structure design is fed by natural runoff. Guidelines include draining directly into the reservoir, a drainage-area-to-pond-area ratio of about 10:1 under typical Arkansas hydrology, and minimum construction cost per acre. Most Arkansas ponds were built in steep, mountainous terrain requiring high levees.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Watershed control:** Enough runoff is needed to fill the pond at least annually, but excessive watersheds flush fertility and admit unwanted fish during uncontrolled spillway flows.
- **Water-level fluctuation:** Reservoirs fluctuate widely, so the pond's lowest point is set near maximum power pool; a canal allows draining at lower reservoir levels.
- **Species behaviour during draining:** [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Striped Bass]] remain in the pond until the last water drains, whereas fingerling walleye drain rapidly with little loss.
- **Initial predation:** Released fish attract shad and then predatory fishes, which can cause losses of fingerlings.
- **Crop-size estimation:** Estimating numbers is difficult; the author relies on seine samples, growth rate, size uniformity and hatchery experience, and treats return to the fishery as the true measure of success.

## Key Findings

**Uses of nursery ponds**
- Introducing non-native or desirable species absent from reservoirs, especially when only eggs or fry are available.
- Maintenance stocking of species such as [[Walleye]] and [[Channel Catfish]] that no longer reproduce sufficiently.
- Rearing catchable [[Rainbow Trout]] and channel catfish on commercial feed for immediate fishing.
- Producing food fish (buffalo, common carp) in emergencies.

**Arkansas production (1964–1969)**
- Five major reservoirs had nursery ponds in operation.
- Norfork Nursery Pond (8 acres) produced 40,000 walleye fingerlings (1964) and 75,000 (1965), plus 40,000 channel catfish (1966) and small numbers of northern pike, striped bass and muskellunge.
- Bull Shoals (10 acres) produced 120,000 walleye (1966) and 300,000 walleye (1967).
- Beaver (27 acres) produced 500,000 walleye (1968) and 250,000 northern pike (1969).
- Greeson (20 acres) produced 23,000 albino channel catfish and 17,000 striped bass (1968) and 300,000 walleye (1969).
- Conway (80 acres) had no release as of the report; Norfork had a crop failure in 1968.

**Norfork walleye results**
- Native walleye disappeared after impoundment; over 1.3 million eggs (per the source's figure) and fry were stocked by Arkansas and Missouri.
- Netting of 116,090 two- to three-inch walleye from the nursery pond in 1964 and 1965 was reported.
- Missouri reported improved walleye catches in 1968, with fish of eight to nine pounds taken and anglers' average weights (1960s) listed at about forty-six pounds per list entry in the Lake Norfork Recreational Association newsletter; the author considers this a small share of total catch but evidence of a successful fishery.
- Since 1963 no walleye were stocked in Lake Norfork other than from the nursery pond, suggesting a self-sustaining population had developed.

**Lake Greeson pilot (first crop, 1968)**
- The 20-acre pond was filled in January 1968 and stocked with 28,000 albino channel catfish and 80,000 striped bass.
- Catfish were fed commercial pellets; 570 pounds of minnows and about 50,000 threadfin shad were added as forage.
- Released crop: about 23,000 catfish (10–12 inches) and 17,000 striped bass (4–6 inches).
- Catfish appeared in creels before draining was complete and were caught up to nearly 10 miles upstream, with most harvest within three to five miles.
- Seine and rotenone collections (November 1968 to June 1969) recorded catfish of 8–24 inches and striped bass of 6–11 inches; anglers were targeting striped bass, most under three pounds.

**Lake Greeson second crop (1969)**
- The pond was limed with 30 tons of lime, fertilized with 2,000 pounds of 10-20-10 fertilizer, and given 200 bales of hay and hatchery zooplankton.
- 500,000 two- to three-day-old walleye fry were stocked on April 28, 1969.
- Release began June 3, 1969, with an estimated 250,000–300,000 two- to three-inch walleye.
- A June 1969 sample yielded 59 young walleye; the author anticipated a walleye fishery within two to three years.

**Main problems**
- High construction cost, especially in mountainous terrain.
- Fluctuating reservoir water levels, requiring draining canals.
- Differential draining losses of catfish and striped bass versus walleye.
- Predation on newly released fish.
- Difficulty estimating released crop size.

**Conclusion**
- The nursery pond is a practical intensive-management tool, with the Norfork walleye fishery and the Greeson pilot cited as demonstrations of success. Most problems are considered solvable as experience accumulates.
