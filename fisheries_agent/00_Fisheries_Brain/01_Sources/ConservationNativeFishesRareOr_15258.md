# Rare or Endangered Canadian Fishes and Related Native Fish Notes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation #acipenseridae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This compilation from the [[North American Native Fish Association]] (NANFA) newsletter *American Currents* combines four items. The lead article, by [[D. E. McAllister]] of the National Museum of Natural Sciences (Ottawa), presents a provisional list of rare or endangered Canadian fishes and the threats behind them. The other items are a report on fish diversity in urban Louisville streams, a NANFA speakers' directory and staff call, and an update on the endangered [[Blue Sucker]] in the Wabash River. The common theme is that restricted-range and habitat-sensitive native fishes are declining under human pressure, and that data for many of them are incomplete.

## Background & Description

The McAllister article (reprinted from *The Canadian Field-Naturalist*, 1979) states that each fish depends on specific habitat elements, from cool, fast, pebbly water to warm, still water with vegetation. Once those elements are lost, the fish will not persist for long.

The article lists four main classes of human-caused change:

1. Pollution, silting, biocide spraying, and heating of water.
2. Dams, river diversions, farming, dredging, mining, logging, and water depletion.
3. Introduction of exotic competitors, predators, or diseases.
4. Overfishing.

Gradual changes are described as the most insidious because they can go unnoticed. The article cites eutrophication of [[Lake Erie]], [[Lake Ontario]], and [[Lake Michigan]] within 50 to 60 years, and the decimation of lake trout in the upper [[Great Lakes]] after the introduction of the [[Sea Lamprey]].

The Louisville note is a short survey by [[Jim Pitts]] of urban fish collections, with a count of 26 species. The speakers' directory and staff notices contain no scientific data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Scope and criteria.** The McAllister list covers only species occurring in Canada, including those shared with the United States. A species is excluded if it has adequate breeding populations in any province. Fishes that only stray into Canadian waters are also excluded. Widespread common species are considered unlikely to become extinct, while species with small local ranges are considered highly vulnerable.

**Louisville urban streams.** The author compares a 26-species urban sample with the richer fauna of streams outside the city's waste influence:

- The [[Rainbow Darter]] (*Etheostoma caeruleum*) is abundant in clean riffles outside the city. Other riffle darters there include the Greenside Darter, Orangethroat Darter, Johnny Darter, and [[Logperch]].
- Only one darter, [[Etheostoma flabellare]], was found in the city. It is tolerant of siltation and replaces other darters in urban streams.
- Several shiners are absent from the city, including the Rosefin, Bigeye, and Mimic shiners and the Silverjaw Minnow.

**Blue Sucker range.** The [[Blue Sucker]] (*Cycleptus elongatus*) is native to the Missouri-Mississippi system. It prefers deep water, large rivers and reservoirs, low turbidity, and swift current. Few specimens have been reported.

## Key Findings

### Provisional list of rare or endangered Canadian fishes (Table 1, summarized)

The list has about 20 entries, and the article stresses that it is provisional and warrants investigation. Some of the most significant entries are:

- **[[Shortnose Sturgeon]]** (*Acipenser brevirostrum*): anadromous; known only in the lower Saint John River, New Brunswick, from the mouth to Gagetown. Pollution and a hydroelectric dam may affect survival. In the United States it is listed as endangered, yet its range extends south to Florida.
- **[[Blueback Herring]]** (*Alosa aestivalis*): anadromous; known from Bras d'Or Lake, the Shubenacadie and Stewiacke rivers (Nova Scotia), and the lower Saint John River.
- **[[Longjaw Cisco]]** (*Coregonus alpenae*): restricted to depths at the east end of Lake Erie, or possibly extinct.
- **[[Deepwater Cisco]]** (*Coregonus johannae*): known from Lake Huron; the last specimen was caught in 1951.
- **[[Atlantic Whitefish]]** (*Coregonus* sp.): restricted to the Tusket River system, Millipsigate Lake, and Yarmouth Harbour in southern Nova Scotia. Populations may be affected by power developments.
- **[[Brook Charr]]** (*Salvelinus fontinalis timagamiensis*): known only from Whirligig and Wilderness lakes, northwest of North Bay, Ontario.
- **[[Silvery Minnow]]** (*Hybognathus nuchalis nuchalis*): known only from the Milk River system, southern Alberta.
- **[[Gravel Chub]]** (*Hybopsis x-punctata*): known only from the Thames River, Ontario.
- **[[Pugnose Shiner]]** (*Notropis anogenus*): known from two areas on Lake Erie's Ontario shore and the outlet of Lake Ontario. Sensitive to turbidity and loss of aquatic vegetation.
- **[[Longnose Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys cataractae* subspp.): one population in the Nooksack River, southwestern British Columbia. The subspecies *smithi* from Cave and Basin Hot Spring, Banff, may already be extinct after the introduction of tropical fishes; none were found in two 1968 collections.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): three localities in the Kettle River, southern British Columbia.
- **[[Copper Redhorse]]** (*Moxostoma hubbsi*): St. Lawrence River, Quebec, from Lac St. Pierre to the Ottawa River mouth.
- **[[Blue Walleye]]** (*Sander vitreus glaucus*): now rare or perhaps extinct in Lakes Erie and Ontario, probably from pollution.
- **[[Shorthead Sculpin]]** (*Cottus confusus*): known only from the North Fork of the Flathead River, British Columbia.
- **[[Giant Threespine Stickleback]]** (*Gasterosteus* sp.): known only from freshwaters of the Queen Charlotte Islands.
- **[[Paddlefish]]** (*Polyodon spathula*): not recorded in Canada for over 50 years and probably extinct there.

### Species on the decline but not listed

- The last Blackfin Cisco from the Great Lakes was caught in 1955.
- The Grass Pickerel is seldom seen despite intense seining near Montreal.
- The [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*) is declining in Canada.
- The Deepwater Sculpin has not been seen in Lake Ontario for two to three years, but it is still common elsewhere.

### Conservation recommendations

The article proposes several remedial actions:

- Removing the cause of the threat.
- Setting aside refuges with adequate supervision.
- Enacting total or regional protection laws.
- Reducing or stopping fishing where overfishing occurs.
- Permitting exotic introductions only after exhaustive review by specialists.
- Maintaining species through planting elsewhere or in aquaria.

The article also calls for immediate study, citing [[Minckley and Deacon]] (1968) on freshwater fish losses in the southwestern United States and [[Smith]] (1968) on Great Lakes species changes, which show that faunal change is accelerating.

### Blue Sucker update (Tom Robb)

- In March 1978, an Environmental Impact Study group working on the Lafayette Railroad Relocation Project (Lafayette, Indiana) was concerned that construction would cut the food supply of the endangered Blue Sucker. The Blue Sucker was reported 1 to 14 miles downstream of the proposed South Street bridge site.
- The final plan omits the South Street bridge. However, a scuba survey by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service found no Blue Suckers near that area, and their location in the Wabash River remains unknown.
- Life-history data are sparse. The species probably matures in its second or third year and spawns from April to June at about 50 °F. Average lengths are 7 inches at age 1, 13 at age 2, 17 at age 3, 20 at age 4, and 23 at age 5. The largest Montana specimen was 28 inches and 7.7 pounds. Fish up to 40 inches and 16 pounds have been reported elsewhere.
