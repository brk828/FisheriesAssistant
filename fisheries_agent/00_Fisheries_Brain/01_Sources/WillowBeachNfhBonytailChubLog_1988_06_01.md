## Executive Summary
This document is a hatchery log and interagency memorandum from Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery (NFH) detailing the collection, transport, and captive holding of wild-caught [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) adults during May-June 1988. Fish were captured from [[Lake Mohave]] near [[Cottonwood Cove]] and [[Carp Cove]] by a collaborative team including [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] (referred to as Nevada Game & Fish), [[Arizona State University]] researchers ([[Paul Marsh]] and [[W.L. Minckley]]), and [[California Department of Fish and Game]]. The effort represents an early conservation broodstock collection initiative for this critically endangered cyprinid, with surviving fish slated for transfer to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] for captive propagation. The log documents significant mortality during the holding period (4 of 10 total fish died), alongside prophylactic disease treatment protocols.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native Range/Habitat**: [[Bonytail Chub]] is native to the [[Colorado River Basin]], historically inhabiting large, warm, turbid, fast-flowing river channels.
- **Collection Habitat**: Specimens in this log were collected from [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the Colorado River — an altered lentic habitat that now harbors remnant wild populations of this otherwise river-adapted species, highlighting the species' precarious refugial status post-dam construction.
- **Collection Method**: Fish were captured using nets deployed by [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] biologists, indicating active field surveillance and rescue operations targeting remnant wild adults.
- **Conservation Context**: This log reflects an early "Native Fish" recovery/refugia effort for the [[Bonytail Chub]], one of the rarest fish species in North America, listed under the Endangered Species Act. The transfer pathway (wild capture → Willow Beach NFH → [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]) illustrates the establishment of a captive broodstock/refuge population network for a species facing imminent extirpation from the wild.
- **Interagency Collaboration**: The niche of this species within a management context is defined by multi-agency cooperation, involving state wildlife agencies (Nevada, California), federal hatcheries (Willow Beach, Dexter), and academic researchers ([[Arizona State University]]) — indicative of the intensive, coordinated interventions required for this species' survival.

## Key Findings
- **Total fish handled**: 10 adult Bonytail Chub were collected/received between 05/19/88 and 06/01/88 from wild sources in [[Lake Mohave]].
- **Mortality rate**: 4 of 10 fish (40%) died during the ~2-week holding period at Willow Beach NFH, with deaths occurring 05/27, 05/28, and 05/30 (plus one earlier fish that died 4/28, referenced retroactively).
- **Disease/Stress Management Protocol**: Fish were treated prophylactically with:
  - **Furacin** (nitrofurazone) maintained at **10 ppm** in holding tanks — an antimicrobial treatment likely used to prevent secondary bacterial infection from handling/capture stress.
  - **Potassium permanganate** treatments applied to newly arrived fish, likely to address external parasites or as a disinfectant/oxidizing treatment.
- **Behavioral Indicator of Distress**: Multiple instances of fish observed swimming "upside down" preceded mortality events, suggesting this behavior may serve as a useful early clinical indicator of terminal stress or disease in captive Bonytail Chub.
- **Specimen Disposition**: Deceased specimens were preserved via freezing and subsequently shipped (packed in ice, express mail) to Dr. Paul Marsh at the [[Center for Environmental Studies]], [[Arizona State University]], likely for necropsy, genetic analysis, or scientific voucher specimen retention.
- **Surviving Stock**: As of 06/01/88, 6 of 10 fish remained alive and were reported as active and in good condition, with anticipated transfer to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] for continued captive management — a critical step in establishing an ex-situ refuge population.
- **Logistics Note**: The hatchery manager noted the fish transport method (via boat from field capture site to hatchery) "worked very well," suggesting this collection/transport protocol could serve as a model for future collection efforts.
