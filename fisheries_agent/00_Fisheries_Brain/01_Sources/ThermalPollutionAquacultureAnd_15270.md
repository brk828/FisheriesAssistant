# Thermal Pollution, Aquaculture and Pathobiology in Aquatic Systems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percichthyidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 conference paper, presented at the Tenth Biennial Veterinary Conference and Annual Conference of the Wildlife Disease Association, by J.A. Mihursky, A.J. McErlean and V.S. Kennedy of the [[University of Maryland]] Natural Resources Institute and [[Chesapeake Biological Laboratory]], argues that human-caused temperature increases, chiefly from [[Steam Electric Station]] (S.E.S.) cooling water, pose a growing threat to aquatic life. The authors link elevated temperatures to fish disease, shellfish mortality and reduced physiological condition, and they consider how waste heat and waste materials might be recycled into [[Aquaculture]]. The paper is a review and position statement rather than a primary study, and it reports no new experimental dataset.

## Background & Description

**Definition.** [[Thermal Pollution]] is defined as a human-produced, unnatural temperature change that alters an aquatic system to the extent that other legitimate uses are impaired. Sources include dams, irrigation practices and industrial waste heat, but the authors identify S.E.S. operations as the greatest threat, because they have the largest non-consumptive industrial demand for water as a heat-transfer medium.

**Growth of the problem.**
- Water requirements of the steam-electric industry have grown much faster than population.
- U.S. electricity demand shows a doubling time of 6 to 10 years. Projecting from a 1960 base, the authors estimate an increase of 30 to possibly 256 times by 2010.
- Present S.E.S. discharges are generally 5 to 15 °C above ambient. New engineering designs produce increases of 5 to 8 °C in an attempt to meet new water quality standards.
- Individual plants currently need generally less than 1 million gallons per minute.
- Most existing plants use [[Once-Through Cooling]] (open-system, once-pass design), in which water is pumped from a river, lake or marine source, passed through heat exchangers and returned to the source.
- Plants under construction or just starting operation are 1,000 to 2,000 megawatts (MW). Future single installations may reach 4,000 to 8,000 MW. The authors state that a 4,000 to 8,000 MW plant using once-through design could require, daily, over 60 square miles of water one foot deep for cooling.
- The move to less efficient nuclear designs increases cooling needs. The rule of thumb is two kilowatts of waste heat to be dissipated for every kilowatt of electricity generated.

**Role of temperature.** Temperature is called the "master factor" in aquatic habitats (Fry, 1967). It has three effects:
1. **Lethal:** extreme high or low levels can directly cause mortality.
2. **Directive:** it influences daily and seasonal behavior.
3. **Controlling:** it affects biochemical reaction rates and therefore metabolic rates.

Brett (1960) noted that life-history stages have different temperature requirements, which also vary with season. The paper's Figure 2 (after Brett, 1960) shows this for [[Sockeye Salmon]]: reproduction is more temperature-restricted than growth, and growth is more restricted than survival. Early life stages are generally more conservative in their requirements than adults, even at spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal damage in U.S. waters.** Temperature tolerances of aquatic organisms have been exceeded by thermal discharges in the Delaware River (Trembley, 1965; Coutant, 1962), the Green River, Kentucky (Churchill and Wojtalik, 1969), and the Patuxent Estuary, Maryland (Mihursky, 1969).

**Shifting waste-heat patterns.** Industry is changing site locations, engineering designs and dilution methods to avoid direct damage from excessive heating, especially in restricted circulation areas. The authors predict that future waste heat will produce extensive areas of low-level temperature increase rather than small zones of high temperature. They pose an open question for aquatic scientists: "What are the ecological consequences of extensive low-level, unseasonal temperature elevations?"

**Temperature stress and pathobiology.** A sub-optimal physiological state, produced by temperature stress, gives pathogenic organisms the opportunity to overcome their hosts. Quoting René Dubos (1955), the authors note that many microbes are constant environmental components and cause disease only when another factor weakens the host.

**Disease and temperature.** Several publications link temperature changes with higher fish disease incidence:
- Sinderman (1966) discussed a myxosporidian (*Kudoa clupeidae*) infection in [[Atlantic Herring]], with distribution attributed largely to summer seawater temperatures, though other factors may also be involved.
- De Sylva (1969) summarized disease links to high temperature, including [[Columnaris Disease]] in Sockeye salmon in the [[Columbia River]] (Brett, 1956), and kidney disease, furunculosis, Vibrio disease and Columnaris in salmonids (Ordal and Pacha, 1967).

**Operational stressors besides temperature.**
- **[[Chlorine Biocide]]** is used to keep heat-exchange surfaces clean. It damages phytoplankton and zooplankton entrained in cooling water (Heinle, 1969; Morgan and Stross, 1969).
- **Heavy metals** from corrosion or erosion of heat-exchange surfaces have been linked to copper accumulation and greening in shellfish, reported for the [[American Oyster]] (Roosenburg, 1969).
- **Mechanical effects** from intake screening and pumping also damage organisms.

## Key Findings

### 1. Summer white perch epizootic, Chesapeake system (1963)

- A massive kill of [[White Perch]] (*Roccus americanus*) occurred in the [[Chesapeake Bay]] system in summer 1963.
- Research indicated that a [[Pasteurella-Like Organism]] was probably the responsible agent (Snieszko et al., 1964; Janssen and Surgalla, 1968).
- Allen and Pelczar (1967) isolated bacteria from internal organs and identified seven major groups: *Pasteurella*, *Bacillus*, *Achromobacter*, *Pseudomonas*, *Aeromonas*, *Enterobacter* and *Micrococcus*. Several isolates, including the *Pasteurella*-like species, caused infection and death when injected intraperitoneally.
- The authors conclude that opportunistic organisms present in healthy fish can infect and kill fish under stress when present in large numbers.
- The epizootic occurred during a period of high natural temperatures. Figure 3 (after Mihursky and Cory, 1965) shows temperature, epifaunal weight and fish numbers from channel trawling. White perch dominated the trawl catches, and the post-summer drop in total fish numbers reflected fewer white perch. Summer trawl catches were generally lower across the record, which the authors attribute to fish being unavailable to the gear.

### 2. Soft-shelled clam summer mortality

- Summer mortalities of the [[Soft-Shelled Clam]] (*Mya arenaria*) have occurred recently in the Bay region. The Chesapeake population is near the southern limit of the species' U.S. East Coast range.
- Laboratory data show the species is quite intolerant of temperature increases above normal summer surface-water levels in Chesapeake Bay.
- Cursory screening did not identify the cause. The authors suspect a temperature-related microbial infection and recommend further study.

### 3. Thermal-biotic predictive models

The laboratory has produced data on temperature effects on mortality, metabolism and activity across many estuarine organisms. The authors developed [[Thermal-Biotic Predictive Model]]s that classify temperature effects into two categories (Figures 4 and 5):
- **Optimal:** temperatures that support optimal energy conversion efficiency, as determined from respiration and activity data.
- **Sub-optimal:** temperatures that would not kill immediately but would reduce energy conversion, degrade physiological condition, and alter predator-host, host-parasite and host-disease relationships.

Figure 5 introduces a *maximum allowable temperature elevation* (M.A.T.E.). It is a dotted-line extrapolation that is more conservative than the LD50 minimum line and is proposed as an ecologically safe level of temperature change across seasons.

### 4. Host-disease research gaps at heated sites

- Little host-disease work has been done at heated discharge sites.
- The main exceptions are Columnaris research on Sockeye salmon in the Columbia River around Hanford nuclear reactor discharges (Fujihara and Tramel, 1969).
- De Sylva (1969) reported a high incidence of fungus disease in a marine fish species at a Florida S.E.S., but noted that no formal investigation has examined the relationship.

### 5. Waste heat recycling and aquaculture

- The authors propose a closed cycle in which waste heat accelerates bacterial decomposition of sewage, enhances algal and zooplankton growth to feed shellfish or fish, and may heat homes (Figure 6). The resulting biomass would be cycled back for human use. See [[Waste Heat Recycling]].
- Mattoni et al. (1965) grew algae on treated sewage and used the dried product as chicken feed.
- The authors say that more substantial attempts to use waste materials, including waste heat, in beneficial ways are coming, especially in aquaculture.
- Nash (1970) cautioned that aquaculture needs strain selection, nutrition, hybridization and lineage work, and more information on disease control and medication. These needs are especially critical in intensive culture. Most current knowledge comes from traditional hatchery operations with salmonids. Many new species will be used in future recycling systems, and many new host-disease relationships will need study.

### 6. Overall conclusions

- Sub-optimal environmental conditions are recognized, but they have been hard to define and document ecologically.
- Hard-to-measure sub-optimal conditions are likely contributing to population extirpation and extinction.
- Human-caused environmental modification is increasing in both natural open systems and artificial closed aquaculture systems.
- The authors argue that pathobiologists will be needed to understand the limits of these environments and to manage them, since the field has strong potential for explaining the ecological problems of environmental modification.

### 7. Reference base

The paper cites 31 references, mostly 1960s literature on thermal discharges, fish pathology, estuarine productivity and waste heat use. Key sources include Brett (1956, 1960), Fry (1967), Mihursky and Kennedy (1967), Sinderman (1966) and Snieszko et al. (1964).
