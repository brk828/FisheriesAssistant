# Thermal pollution, aquaculture and pathobiology in aquatic systems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 conference paper by [[J. A. Mihursky]], [[A. J. McErlean]] and [[V. S. Kennedy]] of the University of Maryland's [[Chesapeake Biological Laboratory]] argues that [[Thermal Pollution]] from [[Steam Electric Station]] (S.E.S.) cooling is a growing threat to aquatic life. It links elevated water temperatures to disease outbreaks and mortality in estuarine fish and shellfish, and presents [[Thermal-Biotic Predictive Model]] concepts for defining ecologically safe temperature increases. The paper also argues for recycling waste heat and waste materials into [[Aquaculture]] systems, while warning that disease control in intensive culture is poorly understood. The authors conclude that sub-optimal conditions, which are hard to detect, may be driving population extirpation and extinction, and that [[Pathobiology]] will need a larger role in understanding environmental modification.

## Background & Description

**Definition and scope.** The authors define thermal pollution as a man-produced, unnatural temperature change that alters an aquatic system enough to impair other legitimate uses. Causes include dams, irrigation practices and industrial waste heat. The authors single out the S.E.S. industry as the greatest threat, because it has the largest non-consumptive industrial demand for water as a heat transfer medium (quoting [[Mihursky and Kennedy 1967]]).

**Growth of demand.** Water requirements of the S.E.S. industry have risen much faster than population. Electricity demand in parts of the USA shows a doubling time of 6 to 10 years. Projecting from a 1960 base, the authors estimate an increase of 30 to possibly 256 times by 2010.

**Discharge temperatures and design.** Current S.E.S. discharges are generally 5 to 15 °C above ambient. New engineering designs aim for increases of 5 to 8 °C to meet new water quality standards. Most plants use an [[Open-Cycle Cooling]] ("once-through") system, which pumps river, lake or marine water through heat exchangers and returns it to the source. Individual plants generally need less than 1 million gallons per minute, but the trend is toward larger plants with greater cooling demands. The authors note a rule of thumb that each kilowatt of electricity produces about two kilowatts of waste heat, and that nuclear designs, being less efficient, need more cooling water per kilowatt.

**Plant scale.** Most operating S.E.S. have capacities below 1,000 megawatts (MW). Plants under construction or just starting up are 1,000 to 2,000 MW, and future single installations may reach 4,000 to 8,000 MW. A 4,000 to 8,000 MW open-cycle plant could need over 60 square miles of water, one foot deep, per day for cooling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature as the "master factor."** Citing [[Fry 1967]], the authors describe temperature as the "master factor" in aquatic habitats. It is lethal (certain high or low levels cause death), directive (it shapes daily and seasonal behaviour), and controlling (it alters biochemical reaction rates and therefore metabolism). [[Brett 1960]] stressed that life history stages differ in temperature requirements, and that these requirements vary with season. Figure 2 illustrates this for [[Sockeye Salmon]]: reproduction is the most temperature-restrictive, followed by growth, with survival the least restrictive. Early life stages are generally more conservative than adults, even at spawning.

**Documented thermal impacts.** The authors cite studies in which thermal discharges exceeded aquatic temperature tolerances in the [[Delaware River]] (Trembley 1965; Coutant 1962), the [[Green River]] in Kentucky (Churchill and Wojtalik 1969) and the [[Patuxent Estuary]] (Mihursky 1969). In response, the S.E.S. industry is changing site selection and engineering design and trying to achieve faster dilution of waste heat. The authors expect future waste heat to produce wide areas of low-level temperature increase rather than small areas of high increase. They pose an open question: "What are the ecological consequences of extensive low level, unseasonal temperature elevations?"

**Temperature and disease.** A sub-optimal physiological state gives pathogens an opportunity to overcome their hosts. The authors quote [[Rene Dubos]] (1955): a microbe may be a constant environmental component yet cause disease only when another factor weakens the host. Several reviews link temperature shifts to higher fish disease incidence. [[Sinderman 1966]] associated the myxosporidian *Kudoa clupeidae* in [[Atlantic Herring]] with summer sea temperatures, though other factors may be involved. de Sylva (1969) summarised cases including [[Columnaris Disease]] and other bacterial infections in salmonids, with kidney disease, furunculosis, vibriosis and columnaris reported.

**Chesapeake white perch kill (1963).** A massive kill of [[White Perch]] occurred in the Chesapeake Bay system in summer 1963. Research implicated a [[Pasteurella]]-like organism (Snieszko et al. 1964; Janssen and Surgalla 1968). [[Allen and Pelczar 1967]] isolated seven major bacterial groups from internal organs: *Pasteurella*, *Bacillus*, *Achromobacter*, *Pseudomonas*, *Aeromonas*, *Enterobacter* and *Micrococcus*. Several isolates, including the Pasteurella-like species, caused infection and death when injected into white perch. Allen and Pelczar concluded that opportunist organisms present in healthy fish may infect stressed fish. The epizootic occurred during a period of high natural temperatures. Figure 3 shows that temperature, epifaunal weight and fish numbers coincide with the kill, suggesting a possible role for temperature.

**Soft-shelled clam mortality.** High summer mortalities of [[Soft-Shelled Clam]] (*Mya arenaria*) have been reported in the Bay region in recent years. The Chesapeake population lies near the southern limit of the species' east coast range. The authors' data show that this shallow-shelf species is quite intolerant of temperature increases above normal summer surface temperatures. The cause is not yet identified, but a temperature-related microbial infection is suspected.

**Other S.E.S. stressors.** Beyond temperature, chlorine used as a biocide to clean heat exchange surfaces damages entrained phytoplankton and zooplankton (Heinle 1969; Morgan and Stross 1969). Corrosion and erosion of heat exchange surfaces release heavy metals; this has been linked to copper accumulation and "greening" in [[American Oyster]] (Roosenburg 1969). Intake screens and pumping also cause mechanical injury.

## Key Findings

- **Thermal-biotic predictive models.** The authors developed models that separate temperatures within the optimal range for an estuarine species (energy conversion efficiency determined from respiration and activity data) from sub-optimal ones. Sub-optimal temperatures do not kill immediately but reduce energy conversion efficiency and physiological condition, and alter predator–host, host–parasite and host–disease relationships (Figures 4 and 5).
- **Maximum allowable temperature elevation (M.A.T.E.).** Figure 5 proposes a dotted-line criterion, an extrapolation more conservative than the LD minimum line. It is suggested as an ecologically safe level of temperature change across seasons.
- **Limited host–disease research at heated sites.** Apart from work on columnaris in sockeye salmon around the [[Hanford Nuclear Reactors]] discharges (Fujihara and Tramel 1969), little host–disease research has been done at heated discharge sites. de Sylva reported a high incidence of fungal disease in a marine fish at a Florida S.E.S., with no formal investigation yet.
- **Waste recycling.** Mattoni et al. (1965) grew algae on treated sewage, then used them as chicken feed. Mihursky (1967; 1969) proposed combining domestic wastes and waste heat with aquaculture (Figure 6). The proposed closed cycle uses waste heat to speed sewage decomposition, boost algal and zooplankton growth for shellfish or fish, and possibly heat homes.
- **Aquaculture caveats.** Nash (1970) cautioned that aquaculture needs more than strain selection, nutrition and hybridisation. It also needs far more knowledge of disease control and medication, which is especially critical in crowded, intensive culture. Most current knowledge comes from salmonid hatcheries, and many new species and host–disease relationships will need study.
- **Core conclusion.** Sub-optimal conditions are recognised but hard to define and measure ecologically. The authors suspect these conditions are causing population extirpation and extinction. Growing human modification of both natural and artificial systems will require pathobiologists to play a larger role in understanding its ecological effects.
