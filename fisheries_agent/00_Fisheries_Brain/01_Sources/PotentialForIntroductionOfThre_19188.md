# Potential for Introduction of Three Species of Nonnative Fishes into Central Arizona via the Central Arizona Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percichthyidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation literature review (REC-ERC-84-7, June 1984) assesses whether three nonnative fishes, [[Striped Bass]], [[White Bass]] and [[Blue Tilapia]], could be transferred from the [[Colorado River]] into central Arizona during operation of the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), whether they would establish there, and what impacts they could have. No field sampling was conducted; the analysis compiles existing literature, agency reports, stocking records and personal communications.

Fish would be pumped from [[Lake Havasu]] at the [[Havasu Pumping Plant]], moved along the [[Granite Reef Aqueduct]], and stored in enlarged [[Lake Pleasant]] or released to the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] through the Salt River Project interconnection. The report concludes that impacts cannot be predicted with certainty, but that establishment and reproduction are likely to differ markedly among the three species. The study also considers the [[Southern Bald Eagle]], whose fish food base along these rivers could be affected.

## Background & Description

The [[Central Arizona Project]] was authorized September 30, 1968, by the Colorado River Basin Project Act (Public Law 90-537). It is designed to deliver up to 2.2 x 10^6 acre-feet per year of allocated Colorado River water to central and southern Arizona for agricultural, municipal and industrial use, supplementing or replacing surface and groundwater supplies in Maricopa, Pinal and Pima Counties.

Key infrastructure described:
- Water is lifted about 244 m at the Havasu Pumping Plant and conveyed through a 10.5 km tunnel into the Granite Reef Aqueduct, which runs about 304 km to the Salt River.
- The Granite Reef Aqueduct is an open, concrete-lined canal with three inline pumping plants (Bouse Hills, Little Harquahala, Hassayampa) and a design capacity of 85 m³/s.
- Lake Pleasant, about 48 km northwest of Phoenix, would be enlarged by New Waddell Dam, with capacity about eight times its present size. Water would be diverted via a 6.4 km reversible canal.

Species of concern: striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*), white bass (*Morone chrysops*), and blue tilapia (*Tilapia aurea*). Neither striped nor white bass is native to Arizona. Blue tilapia is an exotic cichlid from Africa that is already established in the lower Colorado River basin.

The six study objectives, formulated by USBR with USFWS and AGFD biologists, cover water chemistry, existing fisheries, pump passage survival, biological requirements, establishment and impact predictions, and effects on bald eagles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Striped bass.** Native to Atlantic and Gulf coast waters; established on the Pacific coast and in landlocked reservoirs, including [[Lake Powell]], [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. Temperature is considered the most important factor affecting adults; they avoid water above about 25–27 °C and need well-oxygenated cool refugia. Eutrophic lakes pose a "temperature–dissolved oxygen squeeze". Adults depend heavily on [[Threadfin Shad]] as forage, and food limitation has caused starvation and die-offs in Lakes Powell and Mead.

**White bass.** Native to the Mississippi River system and Great Lakes drainages; introduced across the Missouri River basin and southwestern states. It prefers relatively clear water and firm sandy or rocky substrates, spawns in shoal areas at 13–23 °C, and does well in warm, slightly alkaline reservoirs.

**Blue tilapia.** Thermophilic and cold-sensitive; distribution is largely governed by winter lows. Reported lower lethal temperatures for *T. aurea* range from about 5 to 6.5 °C, and it is more cold-tolerant than *T. mossambica*. It tolerates very low dissolved oxygen (around 1.0 mg/L) and wide salinity ranges, and thrives in eutrophic waters. It is a mouthbrooding planktivore/omnivore with high fecundity relative to its brooding strategy.

**Existing fisheries.** The report summarizes fisheries of Lake Havasu, Lake Pleasant, Alamo Lake, and the Salt and Verde Rivers, including creel and electrofishing data, and water chemistry for these systems (temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, turbidity, TDS). Lake Pleasant's white bass fishery depends on spring inflows from the Agua Fria River.

**Pumping plant effects.** The report reviews effects of shear force, pressure, temperature, and velocity on eggs, larvae and adults passing through pumps.

**Southern bald eagle.** Prey remains beneath nest sites from 1979–1981 were analyzed to assess the fish food base along the Salt and Verde Rivers.

## Key Findings

- **Lake Pleasant, blue tilapia:** if they enter, they are likely to survive and reproduce successfully.
- **Lake Pleasant, striped bass:** much less likely to reproduce and establish populations.
- **Salt and Verde Rivers, ranked establishment by reproduction:** blue tilapia most likely, followed by white bass, then striped bass least likely. Recruitment by repeated introduction would continue regardless.
- **Impacts:** the degree of impact on Lake Pleasant and on the Salt and Verde Rivers cannot be predicted with certainty. If the species become established and are not controlled by existing predators or environmental pressures, they could reduce the fish food base of nesting southern bald eagles.
- **Striped bass in the Colorado system:** successful establishment in multiple reservoirs, with reproduction documented in Lake Powell, Lake Mead and the Arkansas River, but competition with shad-dependent sport fisheries and starvation of adults noted in several impoundments.
- **White bass in Lake Pleasant:** introduced in 1959 and 1961, with successful reproduction first documented in 1965, and year-class strength linked to high spring inflows.
- **Tilapia in Arizona:** first introduced from Hawaii in 1961, with more than 60 documented stockings and wide transplanting across central and southwestern counties. About eight tilapia species and hybrids are present or suspected in the lower Colorado system, and the true identity of Arizona "aurea" stocks is uncertain because of possible hybridization with *T. nilotica*.
- **Data gaps:** the authors identified significant gaps, including limited information on juvenile and larval striped bass food habits in the lower Colorado River and the unresolved taxonomy of Arizona tilapia.
- **Method note:** the search produced 641 striped bass, 147 white bass and 855 tilapia citations; much of the grey literature and many personal communications are non-peer-reviewed, which the authors acknowledge.
