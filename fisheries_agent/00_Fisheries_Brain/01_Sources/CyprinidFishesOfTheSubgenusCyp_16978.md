# Spotfin Shiner (*Notropis spilopterus*): Redescription, Subspecies, and Distribution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper by Robert H. Gibbs Jr. (Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, 1957, published in *Lloydia*) is Part II of a revision of the subgenus *Cyprinella* of [[Notropis]]. It redescribes the [[Notropis Spilopterus|Spotfin Shiner]], which the author describes as abundant across much of the northeastern United States, and recognizes two subspecies. The new subspecies, the [[Western Spotfin Shiner]] (*N. s. hypsisomatus*), is described from the Illinois River system. The original subspecies, the [[Eastern Spotfin Shiner]] (*N. s. spilopterus*), is retained for the eastern populations. Subspecies are separated mainly by [[Lateral-Line Scale Count]] and body depth, and the paper proposes a glacial-era origin for their divergence.

## Background & Description

**Scope and methods.** Specimens came from the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia (ANSP), Cornell University (CU), the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, and the United States National Museum. The author analyzed seven meristic and nine proportional characters, following Hubbs and Lagler (1947), with two exceptions: postdorsal length and the distance from dorsal origin to lateral line. To limit geographic bias, no more than ten specimens were counted from any one collection. Proportional measurements were taken only on fish 50–60 mm standard length, a range that covers much of the sampled populations and reduces allometric effects without regression analysis.

**Morphology (species level).**
- Pharyngeal teeth 1,4-4,1, hooked.
- Anal rays 8; pectoral rays usually 13–15.
- Lateral-line scales usually 35–39, variable between subspecies.
- Predorsal scales usually 13-2-11 (often 15-2-11); caudal peduncle scales 7-2-5.
- Head length usually 24–27% of standard length; orbit 7–8% of standard length.
- Mouth terminal or subterminal and oblique; body slender to deep, varying between and within subspecies.
- Breeding males develop nuptial tubercles on the head, snout (with a hiatus between snout and head tubercles), nape, and mandibular rami. Their unpaired fins turn yellowish.

**Coloration and diagnosis.** Scales above the lateral line are narrowly edged in black, giving a diamond-shaped pattern. A plumbeous lateral stripe is present, most prominent in breeding fish. The species has no caudal spot. Its diagnostic feature is dark pigment on the last two interradial membranes of the dorsal fin. Dorsal pigment was quantified on a 0–5 scale per membrane across seven membranes, giving a maximum index of 35. Tables 1 and 2 give these indices for [[Notropis Spilopterus|N. spilopterus]] (Wheeling Creek, West Virginia) and [[Notropis Whipplei]] (Elk River, Tennessee, and Caston Creek, Oklahoma). Dorsal pigment separates the species from [[Notropis Analostanus]] and [[Notropis Whipplei]], and the index is shown in Figure 1.

**Nomenclature and types.**
- The species was first described by Cope (1866) as *Photogenis spilopterus*. The synonymy is long, and the species was widely misidentified as *N. whipplei* or *N. analostanus* in earlier literature.
- The only extant type is a poorly preserved specimen, ANSP 22901, from the St. Joseph's River in southwestern Michigan. It is retained for the eastern subspecies, and the author recommends separate type series for species and subspecies in such cases.
- The holotype of *N. s. hypsisomatus* is CU 28541, an adult male of 68.8 mm standard length in breeding condition. It was collected at Wonder Lake Bay, McHenry County, Illinois, on July 2, 1950, by James N. Layne and Lois V. Linderoth. Paratypes came from the Illinois River system, including the Nippersink and Des Plaines Rivers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The Eastern Spotfin Shiner prefers small to moderately large streams. It avoids extremely small headwater creeks and large mainstream rivers, and it is most common in running water, where it gathers in pools along stream banks. The author describes it as an upland form. The [[Mississippi River]] below the Ohio junction appears to be a strong barrier that keeps the subspecies apart. The Western Spotfin Shiner apparently occurs in warmer and more turbid water than the eastern subspecies. Its isolated Ozark populations, however, suggest a preference for cooler, clearer water as well. Habitat information for this subspecies is limited.

**Geographic distribution.**
- *N. s. spilopterus* occurs in Ohio River tributaries from the Tennessee and Wabash to the Allegheny, the Great Lakes Basin from Lake Huron to the St. Lawrence River and Lake Champlain, and Atlantic coastal systems from the Mohawk-Hudson south to the Potomac.
- *N. s. hypsisomatus* occurs in the Illinois and Spring Rivers (Arkansas system), tributaries to the upper Mississippi north of the Ohio junction, and the Gasconade in the Missouri system. Iowa lake populations draining to the Little Sioux are presumed to be introductions.
- Intergrades occur in the Lake Michigan drainages of Michigan (16 collections) and Wisconsin (10 collections).
- Collection effort was greatest in the Lake Erie drainage (63 collections) and the Tennessee system (61). Other well-sampled systems include the Kanawha (37) and Susquehanna (28).

**Competitors and relatives.** The author compares the species with [[Notropis Analostanus]], [[Notropis Whipplei]], [[Notropis Galacturus]], [[Notropis Lutrensis]], and [[Notropis Camurus]]. These are the other *Cyprinella* species found within its range. The Western Spotfin Shiner is deep-bodied and superficially resembles [[Notropis Lutrensis]], and early reports of intergrades between the two may reflect extreme development of lutrensis-like characters.

## Key Findings

1. **Subspecies recognition.** Two subspecies are recognized: the eastern *N. s. spilopterus* and the western [[Western Spotfin Shiner|N. s. hypsisomatus]]. The author notes that the subspecies differences are "more consistent than appears at a glance."

2. **Lateral-line scales separate the subspecies.** The author's cutoff between 37 and 38 scales separates 86.8% of the western subspecies from 80.3% of the eastern subspecies, with an average index of divergence of 83.6 (Ginsburg, 1954).
   - Of 184 tabulated eastern collections, only 17 (9.2%) contained specimens with 36 scales. Four others had only 37-scale specimens. The remaining 163 (88.6%) had no 36-scale specimens and many with 38 or more. The mode is 38.
   - Of 65 western collections, 6 (9.2%) lacked a 36-scale specimen. Only 29 collections (44.6%) included any specimen with 38 or more scales.

3. **Body depth differs.** The western subspecies is deeper-bodied, with a modal body depth of 26% of standard length compared with 24% in the eastern form. The eastern subspecies is more slender, with body depth usually 22–25% of standard length.

4. **Dorsal pigmentation is diagnostic.** The species is identified by darkly pigmented last two dorsal interradial membranes. Development of this pigment starts around 45 mm standard length, and it is most extensive in breeding males.

5. **Synonymy and misidentification.** The species has had a complicated taxonomic history. Its ruling synonymy lists over 100 citations under *Photogenis*, *Notropis whipplei*, and *Notropis analostanus*. Many earlier records are considered misidentifications.

6. **Intergrades in Lake Michigan.** Lake Michigan drainage populations are treated as intergrades because their lateral-line counts are intermediate. Their modal count is 37. The author proposes that the two subspecies came into contact there at some point in the past.

7. **Arkansas populations may reflect hybridization.** Populations in the Illinois and Spring Rivers have lower scale counts (35 or less in many specimens). The author suggests introgression with [[Notropis Lutrensis]] as a possible cause but considers this unproven and notes that the two species have different ecological preferences.

8. **Proposed origin and dispersal.** The paper reconstructs biogeographic history:
   - The species may have been widespread in the preglacial [[Teays River]].
   - Subspecific differentiation may have begun during an early glacial stage, when the range was split into eastern and western parts.
   - The western population may have survived in one or both refugia, the Ozarks or the driftless area of Wisconsin. The author favors the Ozarks as the origin of *N. s. hypsisomatus*, based on relict populations in the Spring, Illinois, Gasconade, and Meramec Rivers.
   - Postglacial routes into the Great Lakes and Atlantic drainages followed glacial lake outlets, including the Maumee, Finger Lakes, Mohawk-Hudson, and Champlain Sea routes.
   - The Potomac population most likely arose through stream capture of part of the Youghiogheny (Monongahela system).

9. **Lack of gene flow.** The Ohio Valley population is significantly different from the Atlantic and Great Lakes populations in body depth and caudal peduncle depth. The author treats this as evidence of limited gene flow between the subspecies in their present ranges.

10. **Taxonomic recommendation.** The author recommends naming a type for the species and separate types for each subspecies where intergradation occurs, rather than relying on a single type from an intergrade area.
