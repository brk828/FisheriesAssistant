# Systematics and Ecology of Chubs (Gila: Cyprinidae) of the Alvord Basin, Oregon and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 Oregon State University doctoral thesis by Jack Edward Williams treats the [[Gila]] chubs of the endorheic [[Alvord Basin]] in southeastern Oregon and northwestern Nevada. Seventeen localities were surveyed and the populations were found to occupy warm springs, cool springs, creeks, reservoirs and a thermal lake. The study concludes that the [[Borax Lake Chub]] is a dwarf, specifically distinct species, described as *Gila boraxobius*, while all other Alvord Basin populations are referred to *Gila alvordensis* in the subgenus *Siphateles*. The thesis also documents the life history, diet and reproduction of the Borax Lake population and argues for its conservation under [[Endangered Species Act]] listing.

## Background & Description

The Alvord Basin is a north-south oriented endorheic basin of about 6066 km², flanked by the [[Steens Mountain]] and [[Pueblo Mountains]] to the west and the [[Trout Creek Mountains]] to the east. Pluvial [[Lake Alvord]] once covered much of the floor, reaching 112 km in length and 1272 km² at maximum extent. Its recession isolated fish populations in remnant permanent waters an estimated 7,000 to 10,000 years ago.

The genus *Gila* (Baird and Girard) is a cyprinid group of roughly 17 valid species, placed in three subgenera (*Gila*, *Siphateles*, *Snyderichthys*). Uyeno's (1961) osteological work provided the diagnostic characters used here. The thesis is a compilation of several studies, some previously published with co-authors Cynthia D. Williams and [[Carl E. Bond]].

Taxonomic work used 42 characters, including morphometrics, fin rays, scales, lateral-line pores, gill rakers, pharyngeal teeth, vertebrae and skull osteology, following [[Hubbs and Lagler]] (1958) methods. Sample sizes for the populations examined ranged from roughly 22 to 200 specimens per locality (for example, 182 specimens of OS 4138 from Borax Lake and 114 specimens of UMMZ 203330).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate:** Semi-arid, with hot summers and cold winters. Average monthly temperatures run from about −6°C in January and December to about 20°C in summer. Annual precipitation is near 200 mm, mostly in late spring and winter. Annual evapotranspiration near Malheur Lake is about 1016 mm.
- **Vegetation:** The basin floor lies in the Desert Shrub Zone (about 1350–1600 m), dominated by greasewood (*Sarcobatus vermiculatus*) and budsage (*Artemisia spinescens*). Alkaline soils around Borax Lake support saltgrass (*Distichlis spicata* var. *stricta*) and other salt-tolerant plants.
- **Borax Lake:** A 4.1 ha, very clear, slightly alkaline (pH about 7.3, specific conductance 2410) natural lake fed by 35–40 °C thermal springs. Lake temperatures typically run 30–32 °C, with chubs avoiding water above about 34 °C. A critical thermal maximum near 34.5 °C was observed in aquaria. The lake sits roughly 10 m above surrounding land because of salt deposition.
- **Threats:** Geothermal development of aquifers feeding Borax Lake, surface disturbance, and diversion of outflows by a local rancher, which dried Lower Borax Lake and lowered water levels by about 7–10 cm.
- **Other taxa:** Diatoms (identified by [[Stanley V. Gregory]]), chironomid larvae, copepods, ostracods, cladocerans, gastropods, and a hydrobiid snail endemic to West Spring. Introduced [[Guppy]] (*Poecilia reticulata*) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) affect native fishes, and the native [[Alvord Cutthroat Trout]] has been largely eliminated by hybridization.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and habitats**
- Gila are recorded from 17 localities (5 in Oregon, 12 in Nevada). Chubs have been extirpated from Lower Borax Lake and Thousand Creek Spring.
- Two new locality records were added for Bog Hot Reservoir, the unnamed spring near Flowing Well, Dufurrena Ponds #22 and #19, West Spring and West Creek.
- The Trout Creek population is the only Alvord Basin Gila sympatric with another fish species (rainbow trout).

**Taxonomy and systematics**
- The Borax Lake population is specifically distinct and described as *Gila boraxobius* (holotype male, 50.6 mm SL). All other populations are referred to *G. alvordensis* (type locality Trout Creek).
- Borax Lake chubs are dwarf (maturity near 30–35 mm SL; typical adults 33–50 mm SL) with a large head, long snout, long predorsal length and narrow caudal peduncle.
- Pectoral fin rays are modal at 13 in Borax Lake, compared with 14–15 elsewhere; gill rakers average 15.9 (versus 17.2 at Virgin Creek and 19.0–20.8 elsewhere).
- Borax Lake shows the most reduced cephalic and body lateral-line systems (mean 30.2 cephalic pores; 13.2 body pores), and a modal vertebra count of 37.
- Alvord Basin Gila are placed in subgenus *Siphateles* because they have a dark peritoneum, a single row of pharyngeal teeth, a narrow supraethmoid and a non-silvery body, with the exception of some body colour variation.

**Diet and feeding ecology**
- Alvord Basin Gila are opportunistic omnivores that often feed highly exploitively: 89% of Serrano Pond fish with food contained one item exceeding 50% of intestinal volume.
- Borax Lake chubs ate large amounts of terrestrial insects in summer (about 31% mean volume, versus 21% in spring) and microcrustaceans in winter (about 35% mean volume).
- Chironomid larvae were the most consistently important aquatic food. Diatoms made up 70–80% of volume in some spring fish.
- A daily ration of 11.14% of body weight was calculated for June 1979 chubs, far above the 2–5% typical of other species, possibly because of the high proportion of undigestible material and thermal-spring conditions.

**Reproduction and life history**
- Females are asynchronous with individual spawning periodicity and typically spawn twice per year. Major spawning occurs in March–April, with larvae observed in shallow vegetated shoreline areas.
- Fecundity increased steeply with length: 75–82 ova at 32.7–34.5 mm SL, rising to 6924 ova in a 93.0 mm SL female.
- Borax Lake chubs usually live one year, with few age II and III fish (almost all of them female). Males apparently die at age I. The sex ratio across the year was 1:1.3 (male to female).
- The reproductive pattern parallels thermal-spring fishes such as [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]] and the [[White River Springfish]], suggesting photoperiod may matter more than temperature.

**Conservation status**
- The American Fisheries Society listed the Borax Lake chub as threatened in 1979. A federal notice of 28 May 1980 declared it an Endangered Species after emergency listing was initiated.
- The author argues that the species' unique qualities and the Borax Lake ecosystem justify protection over short-term economic gains.
