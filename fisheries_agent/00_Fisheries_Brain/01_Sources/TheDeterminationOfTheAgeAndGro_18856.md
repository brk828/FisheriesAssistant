# Age and Growth of Pike from Scales and Opercular Bones

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This methodological study by [[Winifred E. Frost]] and [[Charlotte Kipling]] (Freshwater Biological Association, Ambleside) evaluates [[Scale Ageing]] and [[Opercular Bone Ageing]] for [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) from [[Windermere]], England, using material collected 1944–1957. The authors describe the use of opercular bones for pike age and growth determination as a first, and conclude that opercular bones are valid and preferable to scales. Scales remain acceptable for age determination but are not recommended for growth back-calculation. Key technical problems addressed include missing early annuli on opercular bones, false annuli on scales, and allometric growth of the scale. The paper provides correction equations and a theoretical growth table approach using [[Walford Plot]] analysis, based on the [[Ford-Walford Method]].

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), a freshwater predator. Age groups are recorded so that the first year of life is age-group 0, with completed first years recorded as age 1.
- **Structures examined:** Scales (sampled from above the lateral line, midway along the body) and left opercular bones, both read as annual "rings", "checks" or "annuli".
- **Prior work:** Pike scales had been used by European and American workers (e.g. [[Williams 1955]], [[Miller and Kennedy 1948]], [[Healy 1956]], [[Oliva 1956]]). Opercular bones had been used for perch by [[Le Cren 1947]], and this paper claims the first use of opercular bones for pike.
- **Scope:** The paper concerns technique and considers growth in length only, not weight.

**Materials**

- Roughly 5,000 pike from gill nets (1944–1957), about 2,000 from perch traps (scales only, plus about 250 operculars), about 300 scales and 150 operculars from monthly seines, and about 250 young-of-year specimens.
- Over 1,500 pike tagged from 1949; by December 1957 more than a third had been recaptured.
- Only one fish of known age (reared, 1+) was available.

**Measurement**

- Fork length was the standard; a total-to-fork length ratio of 1:1.06 was found for 157 fish.
- Gill-net fish from 1944–1952 were measured to the centimetre with a +0.5 cm correction; later fish were measured to the millimetre.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Early life:** Scales first form at about 3.5 cm, usually reached in June in Windermere. Smaller young-of-year (3–4 cm and less) had no scales. Young pike were found in weed beds and shallow bays.
- **Birthday convention:** Because many recently hatched pike were found in early and mid-May, the conventional birthday is set at 1 May.
- **Annulus timing:** Scale annuli form mainly in the first half of May; opercular annuli form in early May, at about the same time.
- **Diet shift:** [[Williams 1955]] suggested a false first annulus may relate to a change from insect to fish diet; the authors note this could also apply to Windermere (citing [[Frost 1954]] on pike food).
- **Comparative timing:** In East Anglia (Hartley 1947), check formation occurs in late summer. In Michigan, scale annulus formation ranges from early March to 1 June (southern) and late March to late June (northern).
- **Environment-specific tables:** The ultimate lengths and theoretical growth tables are based on Windermere pike in the last fifteen years and "must not be assumed to apply to other environments".

## Key Findings

1. **Opercular bones are preferred.** Opercular bones are annual, show fewer false annuli than scales, and give clearer boundaries. The authors recommend opercular bones and use scales only when opercular readings are difficult to interpret.
2. **Scale growth is allometric on the anterior part and isometric on the posterior part.** Anterior back-calculations need correction, using the equation log₁₀F = 0.836 + 0.749 log₁₀S (77 fish, 10–106 cm), where F is fish length and S is the projected anterior scale length. Posterior annuli were rejected for growth determination.
3. **Scales are laid down at about 3.5 cm** total length, and this correction is required for all scale back-calculations.
4. **Opercular growth is isometric.** Measurements plotted against fish length (10–98 cm) lie on a straight line through the origin, so no correction is needed. The origin is placed at the midpoint of a line between two outline indentations.
5. **Validation against tagged fish.** Mean back-calculated length at tagging was 47.0 cm (observed), 47.8 cm (scales, corrected) and 46.2 cm (operculars) for 42 fish; differences were not significant. Mean absolute individual differences were 1.5 cm (scales) and 1.0 cm (operculars).
6. **Scale age agreement.** For 85 fish, anterior and posterior counts agreed up to about five years; older fish showed discrepancies of up to three years, usually with the anterior count higher.
7. **Missing early annuli.** The thickened, discoloured base of the bone often obscures the first one or two annuli, mainly in larger fish (50–110 cm). Few fish under age 4 have missing annuli; over age 7 almost all have at least one. The maximum found was two.
8. **Missing-annuli method.** Fish with visible early annuli grow faster than those with missing annuli (Tables 4 and 5), so omitting them would bias growth estimates. The authors developed a method using [[Walford Plot]] analysis and theoretical growth tables (Table 3) for slow, medium, fast and very fast growers. Ultimate lengths were taken as 75 cm for males and 100 cm for females.
9. **First-year length.** Three methods (direct observation, scale back-calculation of 271 fish with mean 21.6 cm, and tagged-fish Walford plots giving 20 cm males and 21 cm females) produced similar results, with a range of about 11–35 cm and a mean of about 21 cm.
10. **Age overlap.** Age groups I and II overlap between about 25 and 35 cm, sometimes down to 20 cm. Of 18 fish of 24–30 cm, 12 were aged 1 and 6 aged 2.
11. **Gill-net selection.** Mean back-calculated lengths in [[Table 6]] show some evidence of [[Lee Phenomenon]], probably due to gill-net selection.
12. **Conclusion.** Opercular bones give a valid method for determining age and growth of pike. Scales are satisfactory for age but not recommended for growth.
