# Razorback Sucker Stocking and Genetic Integrity in Lake Mojave: Correspondence to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is a letter of November 1982 (the OCR date is partly garbled) from a researcher writing on behalf of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] to Dr. James E. Johnson of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. It responds to a meeting on the [[Razorback Sucker]] stocking program for [[Lake Mojave]]. The core dispute is whether the Mojave population contains pure razorbacks or hybrids produced by introgression with a related sucker. The author argues that the genetic question is unresolved, that the adult population is probably not about to collapse, and that stocking fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] should be delayed until the question is settled. The letter also reflects on the broader conservation practice of fish introductions.

## Background & Description

The letter opens by summarizing a meeting held with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] staff and their colleagues. The author writes that they had spent two days on the problem and that the discussion centered on the Mojave razorback stock.

The author lists the points of agreement and disagreement:

- **Agreed:** Razorback suckers occur in the Mojave population, and the adult population there is numerous.
- **Disputed:** Whether introgression or backcrossing with another sucker is occurring in Lake Mojave. The author notes that the extent of recruitment is not known and that no evidence yet shows that the stocking program is aimed at determining the presence or absence of hybrid fish.

An [[Electrophoretic Analysis]] of protein banding patterns in razorback and related suckers was in development at the time. The author treats it as a possible means of resolving the question, though not a certain one.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Genetic evaluation.** The author proposes a test. If the protein patterns of pure razorbacks and pure related suckers differ distinctly, then hybrids should show intermediate patterns. Pure razorback patterns from Mojave fish would support the view that only pure razorbacks are present. If extensive introgression had occurred, a graded series of protein patterns would be expected. A blind test is suggested: colleagues (named as "Mink" and "Gene" in the letter) would select and mark razorbacks and related fish, and the experimenter would identify them without knowing their origin. The author also suggests that a "backcrossed" specimen set be added to the test.

**Recruitment and population status.** The author observed considerable variability in size and condition among the several hundred razorbacks seen in spring. Length-frequency analysis did not show a highly uniform or strongly dominant size class at the upper end of the adult range. Most samples were taken during the spawning season, when only spawning adults are present. The author therefore suspects that the adult population is unlikely to crash within a year or two. However, if comparable observations continue over several years, declining numbers should become visible before any catastrophe. The author recommends that recruitment be studied and that the stocked population be monitored and tagged for several years.

**Timing of stocking.** The decision depends on how long the Mojave population is likely to persist:

- If the population is likely to disappear within a few years, a delay could be costly, because a viable stocking program with a genetically variable broodstock may be critical.
- If the population is likely to last 10 or more years, one more year of delay would give much greater confidence that the program is using the "right" animals.

**Introductions and conservation practice.** The letter also reflects on fish introductions in the Southwest. The author notes that one of the most frequent reasons for introductions in the region is to establish populations for conservation or preservation. Such transplants are carried out by federal, state, and academic personnel, and some are made outside native ranges. The author observes that the biologists involved often did not consider other ecological consequences. The author adds that many biologists still believe that finding a suitable habitat is sufficient justification for a transplant.

## Key Findings

- **Points of agreement:** Razorback suckers occur in the Lake Mojave population, and the adult population there is numerous.
- **Unresolved question:** Whether introgression or backcrossing with a related sucker has occurred. The author states that this question requires testing before any decision.
- **Proposed test:** A blind protein-electrophoresis comparison using coded samples, including backcrossed specimens, to distinguish pure razorbacks from hybrids and introgressed fish.
- **Population status:** The adult population is probably not at imminent risk of collapse in one or two years, but its long-term trend should be monitored.
- **Recruitment:** Length-frequency data did not show a dominant size class. Recruitment was a key uncertainty and should be studied.
- **Stocking recommendation:** Delay stocking until the genetic question is resolved, unless the population is expected to disappear soon. Monitor and tag the existing population for several years.
- **Dexter stock:** Some participants argued that the Dexter razorbacks, which are present in quantity, should be planted because they are available. The author disagrees. Fish should be stocked into a habitat because they are native to it and can reestablish a reproducing population, not merely because they are available. Planting now could jeopardize the genetic integrity of the Mojave stock.
- **Conservation philosophy:** The author notes that the Desert Fishes Council has been conservative about transplanting native fishes and critical of exotic introductions. The author argues that denying the planting of razorbacks in this case is more environmentally responsible, and that the delay reflects appropriate caution.
