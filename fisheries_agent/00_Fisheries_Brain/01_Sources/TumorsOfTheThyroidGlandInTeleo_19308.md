# Tumors of the Thyroid Gland in Teleost Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Pamela A. Mac Intyre]] from the [[New York Aquarium]] Genetics Laboratory describes atypical thyroid growths in two poeciliid fishes, *Girardinus falcatus* and *Xiphophorus variatus variatus*, and reviews all known thyroid tumors in teleosts. Thyroid hyperplasia in these fishes was linked to low iodine in aquarium water, and the Xiphophorus case showed heterotopic thyroid tissue in the spleen and body cavity. The paper concludes that heredity strongly influences susceptibility, while the physiological mechanisms remain unresolved. Work was supported by a [[National Cancer Institute]] grant.

## Background & Description

Thyroid tumors in teleosts have been studied more thoroughly than most other fish tumor types, partly because many affected fishes are economically valuable and because the lesions resemble goitre in humans. Since 1948, thyroid tumors had been studied in poeciliids held at the [[New York Aquarium]] Genetics Laboratory. Teleost thyroid tissue is generally not encapsulated, which makes goitre and malignant growth difficult to separate histologically. The paper also notes that the normal function of the fish thyroid is still poorly understood.

Tissue was fixed in Bouin's fluid, decalcified in formic acid, paraffin-embedded and sectioned at 10 microns. Staining used [[Masson's Trichrome]] for *Girardinus* and Harris' hematoxylin and eosin for *Xiphophorus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Normal *Girardinus* thyroid:** isolated follicles (10 to 80 microns) clustered around the ventral aorta, with no more than ten follicles per cross-section and none in the gills.
- **Hyperplastic *Girardinus* thyroid:** follicles up to 300 microns, several hundred per pharyngeal cross-section, spreading into the tongue, lower jaw and gill filaments, with impaired respiration likely. No heterotopic tissue was found in the kidney or elsewhere.
- **Iodine response:** swellings disappeared within a month of adding potassium iodide to the water. Offspring reared in iodine-enriched water had normal thyroids.
- **Iodine-poor water:** the second generation, reared in iodine-poor water (distilled water replacing evaporation loss), showed normal thyroids at 20 mm. Girardinins therefore appear to have a lower iodine requirement than [[Platyfish]], and the Shedd Aquarium water was probably very low in iodine.
- **Natural records:** thyroid tumors are rare in nature. They have been reported in only 12 species (Table I), most represented by a single individual. The [[Scioto River]] had 0.21 µg/kg iodine versus 0.83 µg/kg in the [[Mississippi River]].
- **Captivity:** most tumorous fishes came from hatcheries, public aquariums and research laboratories. Proposed inducers include iodine deficiency, low oxygen tension, altered light, decreased salinity, calcium-magnesium imbalance, crowding by tankmates and infectious agents.

## Key Findings

1. **Girardinus falcatus** showed hyperplastic thyroid tissue invading the gills. Iodine supplementation reversed the external swelling, and the iodine requirement appears low relative to platyfish.
2. **Xiphophorus variatus variatus** (one adult male, 35 mm standard length, from 8 tank-mates, the only one affected) showed a markedly hyperplastic pharyngeal thyroid, plus thyroid tissue in the spleen and body cavity. No kidney involvement was found. This distribution differs from [[Xiphophorus Maculatus]], where heterotopic tissue is most abundant in the kidney and has not been found in the body cavity.
3. **Tumor reports:** over 40 teleost species have reported thyroid tumors. The first report was by Bonnet in 1883 and the last comprehensive review by Schlumberger & Lucké in 1948.
4. **Iodine:** most thyroid tumors can be prevented or made to regress with iodine. [[Iodine Deficiency]] is the factor with the greatest emphasis, but it does not explain all cases; two marine specimens (a cutlass fish and a bogue) suggest other factors are operative.
5. **Heredity:** strong evidence for genetic susceptibility. In [[Salmonidae]] hatcheries, some strains became resistant over 25 years, and hybrids of *Oncorhynchus* showed more susceptibility than either parent. Among [[Xiphophorus]] species and strains, tumor frequency varied widely; *X. maculatus* strain "30", inbred for 29 generations, lived in low-iodine water without difficulty.
6. **Genetic models:** Baker (1958a) concluded from crosses that multiple genetic factors with some dominance promote tumor development. Berg, Edgar & Gordon (1953) attributed enhanced tumor development in inbred strains to increasing homozygosity of multiple factors. The author states that crosses are ongoing to study the genetics of susceptibility.
7. **Iodine thresholds:** [[Berg, Gordon & Gorbman]] (1954) found 2.3 g/liter iodine prevented tumors in *X. montezumae cortezi*, while rainbow trout required at least 5 g/liter (Robertson & Chaney, 1953).
8. **Physiological hypotheses:** iodine deficiency may lower blood thyroxine, raising thyrotropin (TSH) from the pituitary and causing hyperplasia (Pickford, 1957). Active, high-oxygen species appear more susceptible, and Duerst (1941) found trout developed hyperplasia at low oxygen where carp did not. Tumorous *X. montezumae cortezi* produced thyroxine more slowly than normal fish.
9. **Ecology and reproduction:** thyroid hyperplasia occurs at spawning in some Great Lakes landlocked species (alewife, rainbow trout) but not their marine counterparts. The landlocked smelt was unaffected. Pink and chum salmon fry held in fresh water beyond their migration developed hyperplastic thyroids, prevented by an iodine-rich diet (Hoar & Bell, 1950).
10. **Summary of captive surveys:** 400 small tropical freshwater fishes at the New York Aquarium (1931 to 1936) had only two affected species, *Rasbora lateristriata* and *Heterandria formosa*. No thyroid tumors appeared in marine or freshwater fishes at the Fairmount Park Aquarium during 12 years, apart from shark suckers.
