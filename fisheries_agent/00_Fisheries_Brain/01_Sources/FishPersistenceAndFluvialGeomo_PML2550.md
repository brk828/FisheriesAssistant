# Fish Persistence and Fluvial Geomorphology in Central Australia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Terapontidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Peter Unmack]] (2001, *Journal of Arid Environments*) documents the composition and distribution of fishes in the Alice Springs region of central Australia and evaluates the physical constraints on fish persistence in this arid landscape. Nine native and six exotic species were recorded, and most exotics no longer persist. Outside the [[Finke River]], only one or no native species were found. Fish survival is aided by broad environmental tolerances and migratory abilities, but ultimately depends on [[Geology]], [[Fluvial Geomorphology]], and variable [[Sediment Transport]] patterns that control how long surface water persists.

## Background & Description

Deserts are often assumed to hold little permanent water, yet arid zones can support surprisingly diverse fish faunas. The [[Lake Eyre]], Bulloo-Bancannia, and Western Plateau watersheds of central Australia contain some of the world's last large, essentially unregulated arid-zone drainages. Thirty-three indigenous fishes, about half of them endemic, are known from the region, and eight non-indigenous fishes have been reported. Most native species are ecological generalists with high fecundity, broad salinity tolerance (up to 1.5 ppt), temperature tolerance (about 5 to 36 °C), and, in some cases, extensive migratory ability.

The study area covers the drainages of the [[Finke River]], [[Todd River]], [[Hale River]], [[Plenty River]], and [[Hay River]], which drain north of [[Alice Springs]] into the [[Simpson Desert]]. The area measures about 400 km north to south and 625 km east to west. Based on prehistoric water levels of Lake Eyre and climate, these catchments have probably been isolated for 50,000–90,000 years. The paper's stated aims were to document fish composition and distribution and to discuss geomorphic constraints on surface-water persistence, and therefore fish survival.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Waterhole classification.** The author classified [[Waterholes]] into six types based on morphology and landscape setting:
- **Confined gorge pools:** deep (> 3 m) pools bounded by steep bedrock, formed by scour between confining walls.
- **Broad gorge pools:** pools in bedrock-enclosed riparian zones, with more variable substrate and less constriction.
- **[[Plunge Pools]]:** (> 1 m) below waterfalls; three of the four found were in confined gorges.
- **Eddy pools:** scoured alongside instream or bank structures in alluvial valleys.
- **Deep depression pools:** long (> 200 m) and deep (> 2 m) pools in broad alluvial valleys, of uncertain origin.
- **Shallow depression pools:** short (< 100 m) and shallow (< 1 m); they persist only briefly after flows.

**Climate.** Median rainfall at Alice Springs is about 200 mm/year (range 82–783 mm). Estimated open-water evaporation is about 2600 mm/year. Rainfall falls to about 110 mm/year nearer the Simpson Desert. A single event of 154 mm fell in 11 hours. Air temperatures range from 21–36 °C in January to 4–20 °C in July.

**Drainage characteristics.**
- **Finke River:** rises at 1339 m on Mt Giles and runs about 730 km to the dunefields. Waterholes are mostly restricted to gorges, and downstream plain waterholes are likely non-permanent and saline when cut off from flow.
- **Todd River:** about 315 km long. Maximum recorded surface flow at Alice Springs rose from 5 days before 1973 to 67 days in 1974 and more than 160 days in 1976.
- **Hale River:** about 403 km long, with no major tributaries and a single bedrock valley (Ruby Gap Gorge). Surface flow ends near 180 m elevation.
- **Plenty and Hay rivers:** treated together because they converge above Jervois. Both lack bedrock valleys or significant constrictions.

**Sampling methods.** Fieldwork ran 12–21 May 1991. Methods included a 7 × 1 m seine (2 mm mesh), angling, and daytime and spotlight observation at night. Water pH was measured with bromthymol blue and temperature with a mercury thermometer. Records were also compiled from [[Museum Collections]] and local testimony. Vouchers were deposited in Australian, Victorian, Northern Territory, and South Australian museums.

## Key Findings

**Fish records and distribution**
- Nine native species of eight families were recorded from the Finke River. Todd, Plenty, and Hay rivers yielded only [[Leiopotherapon Unicolor]]. No fish were caught in the Hale River.
- A total of 113 museum collections from the Finke drainage were made between 1891 and 1997 in 33 different years. Most collections (79%) were upstream of the Running Water gorge; only 22 were downstream.
- There are apparently no historical fish records for the Hale and Hay rivers or [[Illogwa Creek]].
- The first fish recorded from the Todd River (1973 and 1977) was an exotic livebearer, probably released by aquarists.

**Native species notes**
- *[[Nematalosa Erebi]]* (bony bream) was the most abundant species at site 3, and was rare in the sampling record, probably due to sampling technique.
- *[[Neosilurus Hyrtlii]]* was collected at night in the Finke River (52 specimens at site 1). It was caught only after sunset at site 1, which may explain its low representation in daytime collections.
- *Craterocephalus centralis* is an endemic hardyhead described by Crowley and Ivantsoff (1990). It was widespread and abundant in the Finke River.
- *[[Mogurnda Larapintae]]* is endemic to the Finke system and was common at sites 1 and 2. It was taken only in shallows (< 15 cm) near dense vegetation.
- *[[Chlamydogobius Japalpa]]* is endemic to the Finke River. Six were collected at sites 1 and 2, in the same habitat as *M. larapintae*.
- *Leiopotherapon unicolor* was the most numerous and widespread species in the region. It was the only fish taken at the last 11 sites, sometimes in numbers of thousands.

**Non-native species**
- Six exotic species were recorded according to the abstract, but the introduction refers to eight non-indigenous fishes. The non-native taxa in Table 2 are *[[Gambusia Holbrooki]]*, *Gambusia dominicensis*, *Phalloceros caudimaculatus*, *Xiphophorus helleri*, *[[Macquaria Ambigua]]*, *Maccullochella peelii*, and *Bidyanus bidyanus*.
- *G. holbrooki* was recently found in Ilparpa Swamp (May 2000), sustained by sewage discharge, and eradication efforts were under way.
- *Macquaria ambigua* was apparently stocked illegally. About 1800 kg were found dead in Jervois Mine Dam after the 1990 rains.
- Most exotics were not confirmed or no longer persist. *Xiphophorus helleri* was presumably extirpated.

**Persistence mechanisms**
- Permanence does not guarantee continuous fish presence. Waterholes may become saline through groundwater inputs or evapotranspiration, and habitat diversity declines as pools shrink. Populations therefore behave as [[Metapopulations]].
- Waterholes in the arid zone usually dry because they fill with sand, not because water is lacking. Floods of high volume and long duration tend to straighten, fill, and homogenize channels, while moderate local floods create heterogeneity.
- Gorges cut through impervious bedrock ridges act as refugia. Bedrock also forces [[Hyporheic Flow]] to the surface and reduces evaporation through shading.
- In the Finke River, most waterholes contained 80–100% of the known fish fauna. Elsewhere, all waterholes except those above obvious barriers in the Todd and Hay/Plenty rivers contained fish.

**Two requirements for persistence (after Minckley 1991)**
1. Not all wetted reaches dry at the same time, so fish can survive to recolonize.
2. Fish maintain wide geographic ranges by dispersing when conditions allow.

**Dispersal and recolonization**
- *Leiopotherapon unicolor* has an extraordinary capacity to disperse, high fecundity, and the ability to breed in small ponds. Its only habitat requirement is the presence of water. Local residents reported "rain of fish," which the author attributes to overland flow rather than aestivation. Shipway (1947) recorded hundreds of young swimming 16 km in 6 hours along a wheel rut.
- The Simpsons Gap waterhole and Dead Horse soak dried at least once this century but still supported fish in 1991. Trephina Gorge had no fish before the record floods of 1974, indicating that rare, intense floods allow recolonization, while severe drought or sand accumulation can extirpate populations.

**Drainage structure and species richness**
- The Finke River, with nine species, has seven substantially larger permanent waterholes and greater habitat heterogeneity than other systems.
- The three drainages with fishes each have waterholes on at least five tributaries. This distribution reduces the chance that one catastrophic event will eliminate the fauna and improves recolonization prospects.

**Absence of fish in the Hale River**
- The Hale River has the fewest waterholes of any regional watershed, no large tributary refugia, and is the only major drainage without fish. The author suggests that the Todd and Hale floods rarely coincide in timing or duration, which limits recolonization from the Todd River.

**Conclusion**
- Geomorphology plays a major role in determining where surface water persists in arid regions, and therefore where fish occur. Although specific causes vary between systems, survival of arid-zone fishes depends on persistent waterholes.
