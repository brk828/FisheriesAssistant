# Hormonal Control of Reproductive Behavior in Some Cichlid Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

Blum and Fiedler (1965) examined how pituitary hormones regulate reproductive behavior and skin physiology in several [[Cichlidae]] species kept in laboratory aquaria. Injections of [[Prolactin]] (LtH) produced two kinds of effect: a histotropic effect (increased [[Mucus Cell Production]] in the skin) and behavioral effects (parental [[Fanning Behavior]] directed at a fixed point, and suppressed fighting). [[Follicle-Stimulating Hormone]] (FSH), [[Luteinizing Hormone]] (LH) and [[Chorionic Gonadotropin]] antagonized the LtH effects, while FSH and LH promoted sexual-phase traits such as blue coloration, courtship and aggression. The authors interpret these results as evidence that prolactin controls the parental phase of the breeding cycle and gonadotropins control the sexual phase.

## Background & Description

The paper distinguishes two- or three-phase breeding cycles in fish. In [[Three-Spined Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*), the cycle moves from nest-building through a courtship (sexual) phase to a parental phase marked by pectoral-fin fanning. In the labrid [[Crenilabrus Ocellatus]], the sexual phase includes nest-building, courtship, fighting and fertilization, whereas the parental phase is marked by near-complete inhibition of fighting, building and sexual behavior.

Earlier work by the authors and colleagues had shown that prolactin elicits fanning even without external cues such as eggs, nests or CO₂, and that fanning intensity depends on dose. The present study extends these observations to several cichlid genera besides *Symphysodon*. In *Symphysodon*, prolactin also induces mucus secretion on the body surface, which in this species normally nourishes the young.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The experiments were conducted under constant laboratory conditions with isolated, half-grown fish. The authors note that the parental phase is shaped by external factors as well as internal hormonal state, and that substrate-breeding species depend on the feeding inhibition produced by prolactin to avoid eating their eggs and young. This matters most in mouth-brooders, where it is considered a prerequisite for that specialized form of parental care.

The authors also discuss seasonal timing. Baggerman (1957) showed that rising day-length and temperature trigger spawning in *Gasterosteus aculeatus*, and that an 8-hour photoperiod acts like physiological hypophysectomy in that species (Hoar, 1962, 1963). The hypothalamus and brain are proposed to trigger the behavioral sequence and adapt it to external stimuli.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sample sizes**
- Fish were narcotized with MS 222 Sandoz. Scales were removed as controls, and hormones were injected into the dorsal musculature.
- Mucus cells were counted microscopically on stained scales. Fighting was scored as snaps and pushes per minute against an untreated test fish. Fanning was scored as minutes per 30-minute period.
- Totals: 191 injections, 67 fanning measurements, 431 fighting experiments with 21 fish, and about 1500 scales evaluated.

**Prolactin (LtH) effects**
- Mucus cell production responded across a broad dose range (0.02–3 IU LtH per gram body weight). Fanning responded only within a narrow range (0.02–0.12 IU per gram). Higher doses suddenly inhibited fanning.
- *Pterophyllum scalare* (5 fish) and *Symphysodon aequifasciata axelrodi* (2 fish) were injected weekly with 0.03–0.08 IU per gram for 5 weeks. Fanning occurred in all animals. Cumulative doses up to 0.28 IU per gram reduced fanning to 0–45 seconds per 30 minutes.
- Fighting was depressed in all animals. Three previously aggressive *Pterophyllum* stopped fighting.
- In *Symphysodon*, skin mucus cells rose to a maximum of 150–170% of control, reached at a cumulative dose of 0.08–0.13 IU per gram.
- At high doses (0.3–0.4 IU per gram weekly, totaling 1.5–2.0 IU over 10 weeks; 3 *Symphysodon*), fanning was not elicited. Mucus cells increased by 60–100%, and fighting dipped briefly before rising again.
- In *Aequidens latifrons* (5 fish, 0.1–0.4 IU per gram weekly for 6 weeks), mucus cells rose by 40–60%, and fighting was progressively depressed.

**FSH, LH and chorionic gonadotropin effects**
- In *Symphysodon* (Di N), LtH had raised mucus cells to 86% above control, but this fell to 8% within 2 weeks.
- FSH (0.01–0.02 mg per gram) induced blue body and fin coloration, and at 0.07 mg per gram produced courtship-like fin-flickering. Subsequent LH produced a fighting rise to 21 snaps and pushes per minute and protrusion of the genital papilla (2–3 mm).
- In *Cichlasoma severum*, FSH and LH produced no early mucus increase. Mucus cells rose by 45% only after cumulative doses of 0.15 mg FSH plus 0.09 mg LH, which the authors attribute to LtH contamination. Fighting rose gradually under both hormones.
- In *Astronotus ocellatus* (3 fish), FSH and LH did not increase mucus cells, while LtH raised them by 60–80%. No fighting was observed in any animal.
- A dose of 0.0025 mg FSH or LH reduced mucus cells in *Symphysodon* from about 150% to 60% or 37% within one week.
- LH protruded the genital papilla in *Symphysodon* and *Pterophyllum* only.

**Interpretation**
- The authors describe an apparent antagonism: LtH's histotropic and behavioral effects are inhibited by FSH and LH.
- Prolactin's histotropic effect on skin mucus is considered homologous to crop-milk secretion in pigeons and milk production in mammals.
- Gonadotropins are associated with the sexual phase (color, courtship, aggression, genital protrusion), and prolactin with the parental phase (fanning, calmness, reduced feeding).
- The authors suggest that prolactin acts directly, since progesterone did not elicit fanning in *Pterophyllum* or *Symphysodon*.
- Breeding experience was not required for parental behavior, since none of the prolactin-treated fish had previously matured.
- The inhibition of fanning at high doses is unexplained. Possibilities include antibodies to ovine prolactin or central nervous inhibition involving the formatio reticularis. The authors judged a simple antibody reaction unlikely because the mucus response persisted at high doses. Reserpine did not produce fanning, which the authors take as evidence that the fish were not making their own prolactin.
