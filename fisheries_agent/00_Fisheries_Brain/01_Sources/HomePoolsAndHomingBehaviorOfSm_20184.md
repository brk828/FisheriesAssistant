# Home Pools and Homing Behavior of Smallmouth Black Bass in Jordan Creek

#ichthyology #marine-biology #micropterus

## Executive Summary

This 1952 Illinois Natural History Survey Biological Notes report by R. Weldon Larimore tests whether [[Smallmouth Black Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) establish [[Home Pool]] territories in [[Jordan Creek]], a small stream in east-central Illinois. A [[Mark-Recapture]] census, followed by three transfer experiments, showed that most bass tended to remain in their sections of the stream and that many displaced fish returned to their home pools. Return rates were modest, and both low water and fish size affected the results. The study also found limited homing in [[Longear Sunfish]].

## Background & Description

Larimore frames the question as both a point of animal behavior and a matter for fish management. Earlier studies gave mixed results:

- [[Thompson]] (1933) found apparently random movement among Illinois river and stream fishes during drought years.
- Shetter (1937) reported that at least one-third of over-winter [[Brook Trout]] recoveries in Michigan had returned to their tagging sites after spawning.
- Schuck (1945) found that larger [[Brown Trout]] in a New York creek tended to return to their initial locations after spawning upstream.
- Scott (1949) found [[Rock Bass]] in an Indiana stream confined to a limited area.
- Gerking (1950) found that longear sunfish, green sunfish, and rock bass were more sedentary than smallmouth bass, spotted bass, or suckers.
- Rodeheffer (1941) moved 767 marked fish 0.6 mile across a bay of Douglas Lake, Michigan. Forty-two were retaken, 37 at their original capture site. Rodeheffer did not interpret this as homing.

The study was limited in scope. It focused on smallmouth bass, with a few minor observations on longear sunfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Jordan Creek is a tributary of the Salt Fork of the Vermilion River. It is composed of short, hard-bottomed pools and shallow riffles and is 15 to 35 feet wide. The initial census covered the lower 4 miles, which included all waters suitable for game fish. The stream was divided into 15 sections of 0.11 to 0.42 mile, following natural breaks between pools.

**Methods.** Fish were collected with a 21-foot [[Electric Seine]] powered by a 115-volt alternating current generator. Game and pan fish were marked by fin-clipping during the census, then released in their pools of capture. Repeat censuses used numbered [[Opercular Tagging]]. Efficiency of the electric seine for smallmouth bass ranged from 65 to 80 per cent among individual pools under average conditions.

**Water level.** Shallow riffles could impede movement between pools. In August 1951, low water covered many riffles with less than 2 inches of water, and this condition persisted for about 7 weeks (see [[Low Water Levels]]).

## Key Findings

### 1. Attachment to home pools in the census data

- The initial census ran from July 25 to September 5, 1950.
- Table 1 shows that 80 per cent of smallmouths recaptured in late summer and fall 1950 (45 in their marked sections, 11 outside) were in the sections where they were marked.
- In 1951, 67 per cent of recaptures (51 in sections, 25 outside) were in their original sections.
- Of 239 smallmouths tagged after the census and before October 1951, 25 were retaken in 1951. Fifteen (60 per cent) were in the pools where they had been tagged.
- Five of the 15 apparently had passed the winter of 1950–51 in their home pools. Six of the 10 that were elsewhere were a short distance away, averaging 0.12 mile.

### 2. Experiment A (upstream transfer)

- Five bass were moved 0.66 mile upstream from pool VI, 8 to pool IX, 3 on September 28, 1950. Seven fish were eventually moved in this experiment.
- The route included 14 shallow riffles and 13 pools.
- Of the seven fish, two never returned, two returned once, and three returned every time they were moved (one fish three times, two fish twice).
- Thirteen transfers were made, and nine (69 per cent) were followed by homing responses.
- No smallmouths under 10 inches were used in this experiment.

### 3. Experiment B (downstream transfer)

- Seven bass were moved from pool V, 11 to pool II, 2 on October 13, 1950, a distance of 0.81 mile by water, passing 20 riffles and 19 pools.
- Two weeks later, three of the seven were recaptured in the home pool, and four were not.
- A recensus in August–October 1951 found 16 smallmouths in the home pool, seven of which bore the home-pool fin clip and matched the sizes of the transferred fish.

### 4. Experiment C (bidirectional transfer)

- One fish was moved downstream and then upstream, returning both times (see [[Homing Behavior]]).
- Three fish moved downstream and did not return; two moved upstream and did not return.
- Four of the five fish that did not return were under 9.1 inches total length.

### 5. Overall homing rates

- Discussion reports that 12 of the 23 smallmouths used in Experiments A, B, and C (52 per cent) showed positive homing responses.
- The Summary and Table 2 report 17 returns from 31 transfers (55 per cent). Four of seven fish moved twice returned a second time. One fish moved three times returned three times.
- Direction of movement (upstream or downstream) did not appear to affect the result.

### 6. Effects of size and residency

- Large fish showed stronger homing tendencies than small ones.
- Seven of the 11 smallmouths that did not return had not previously been taken in the sections where their home pools lay. Five of these seven were 9.0 inches or less.
- Some smallmouths in any pool appear to be temporary residents, and fish of 9.0 inches or less tended to wander more.

### 7. Low-water experiment (1951)

- Seven smallmouths were moved 0.60 mile upstream from pool V, 5 to pool VI, 8 on August 10, 1951.
- None was recaptured in its home pool by August 24. A brief rise on August 29 did not restore movement.
- By September 28, apparently only one bass had returned home. Two of the seven were recaptured in the receiving pool.
- Larimore concludes that low water prevented return. Fish that were confined to a new area may have lost or reduced their drive to return.

### 8. Longear sunfish comparison

- Twenty-four longear sunfish were moved 0.66 mile upstream with the bass in Experiment A. Two were recaptured in their home pool two weeks later.
- Eleven were moved downstream in Experiment C, and three were back in their home pool four weeks later.
- Fifteen were moved upstream in Experiment C, and four were in their home pool two weeks later.
- Larimore suggests the longear's slower and less certain return reflects a weaker homing tendency or its more sedentary nature, while the smallmouth's swimming strength supports faster return.

### 9. Mechanism

- Homing could not be explained as random wandering. Most returning fish passed through many suitable pools before reaching their home pools.
- The response to water taste, odor, or physical conditions could not account for return in both directions.
- The mechanism of homing remains unexplained.

### 10. Limitations

- Recapture rates were likely underestimated because the electric seine did not catch all fish, and recheck operations were limited. Ice ended searching in November 1950.
- Some tags may have been lost, and mortality was unknown.
- Sample sizes were small because a single pool in such a small stream could support only a few large fish.
- All transfer experiments were conducted in fall, so seasonal effects were not measured.
