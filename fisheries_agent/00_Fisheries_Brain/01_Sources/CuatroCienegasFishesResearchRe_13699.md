# Cuatro Ciénegas Fishes: Research Review and a Local Test of Diversity Versus Habitat Size

#ichthyology #desert-fishes #cyprinodontidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This review (Minckley 1984) synthesizes decades of ichthyological research (1958–1981) on the highly endemic fish fauna of the **[[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]]**, an isolated endorheic desert basin in Coahuila, México. At least **16 native fish species** occupy the basin, **8 of which are endemic**, spanning both Nearctic (Cyprinidae, Ictaluridae, Centrarchidae, Percidae) and Neotropical (Characidae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Cichlidae) lineages — an unusual biogeographic mixture for a North American desert region. Using 462 collections across 46 localities, the study demonstrates that **species richness correlates positively with habitat size in stable spring and river systems**, but this relationship breaks down in unstable, fluctuating habitats (playas, marshes, ephemeral pools) and in artificial canals. Distinct faunal assemblages characterize stable versus fluctuating habitats: **ostariophysine and percoid fishes dominate stable springs/rivers**, while **cyprinodontoids dominate fluctuating terminal habitats**.

## Background & Description

The Cuatro Ciénegas basin is a small (<1,500 km²), low-relief (700–760 m elevation), endorheic desert basin supporting an unusually diverse and endemic aquatic fauna. Habitats studied include:

- **Headsprings** (lagunas and pozos): range from large (>20 ha, >5 m deep) to small (<10 m², <10 cm deep) thermal (25–38°C) limnocrenes with minimal current except near in/outflows.
- **Rivers and creeks** (rios/riachuelos): spring-fed, discharge ranging from >2 m³/sec to a few L/sec.
- **Marshes (ciénegas)**: relatively stable, typically associated with lagunas, pozos, and rivers.
- **Playa (barrial) lakes and ephemeral pools**: terminal/near-terminal habitats subject to extreme fluctuations in temperature and total dissolved solids — the most severe habitats in the system.
- **Canals**: man-dug channels draining springs or diverting river water; the major anthropogenic modification in the basin, causing lowered water levels and marsh reduction. Agricultural modification dates to the 16th century.

### Endemic Taxa
- **[[Notropis xanthicara]]** (Cyprinidae)
- **[[Cyprinodon atrorus]]** and **[[Cyprinodon bifasciatus]]** (Cyprinodontidae)
- **[[Lucania interioris]]** (Cyprinodontidae)
- **[[Gambusia longispinis]]** (Poeciliidae)
- **[[Xiphophorus gordoni]]** (Poeciliidae) — taxonomic status contested (species vs. subspecies of *X. couchianus*); author retains as full species
- **[[Cichlasoma minckleyi]]** (Cichlidae) — treated by the author as a **species flock** rather than a single polytypic species, contra Kornfield & Taylor (1983)
- An undescribed **Etheostoma sp.** (Percidae), living in interstices of travertine stream-bottoms

### Additional/Undifferentiated Taxa
- *Dionda episcopa*, *Ictalurus lupus*, *Lepomis megalotis*, *Micropterus salmoides* — thought differentiated at subspecific level, formally unnamed
- *Astyanax mexicanus*, *Pylodictis olivaris*, *Gambusia marshi*, *Cichlasoma cyanoguttatum*, and an unnamed *Cichlasoma* sp. — undifferentiated or little studied
- **[[Gambusia marshi]]** exhibits two distinct color morphs and chromosomal variation (2N = 42, 46, 48)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Stability and Faunal Structure
The basin functions as a natural laboratory due to its combination of extreme habitat stability (large springs) and extreme instability (playas/ephemeral pools) within a small, low-relief, endorheic area lacking latitudinal/altitudinal diversity gradients. Barriers to dispersal are minimal — most systems are or were interconnected — meaning species absence from a locality reflects **environmental aversion rather than physical exclusion**.

- **Stable habitats** (headsprings, rivers): dominated by *Astyanax mexicanus*, *Notropis xanthicara*, *Ictalurus lupus*, *Cyprinodon bifasciatus*, *Gambusia marshi*, *Micropterus salmoides*, and *Cichlasoma minckleyi*. Rivers additionally support *Etheostoma sp.*
- **Rheophilic species** (*N. xanthicara*, *Dionda episcopa*) are more abundant in riverine habitats; *D. episcopa* reaches peak abundance in canals.
- **Fluctuating terminal habitats** (playas, marshes, ephemeral pools): dominated by cyprinodontoids — *Cyprinodon atrorus*, *Lucania interioris*, *Gambusia longispinis* — species tolerant of high salinity and thermal extremes.
- **Canals**: dominated by hybrid *Cyprinodon atrorus × C. bifasciatus*, *Gambusia marshi*, and *Cichlasoma minckleyi*.
- **Gambusia marshi** is the only species that is truly ubiquitous, occurring across nearly all habitat types (near 100% occurrence).
- **Cyprinodon bifasciatus** is a notable exception among cyprinodontoids, being restricted to headsprings due to stenothermal preferences.

### Species Co-occurrence Patterns
- *Cyprinodon atrorus* occurred alone in 14 of 64 collections; most commonly associated with *Gambusia marshi*.
- *Lucania interioris* and *Gambusia longispinis* co-occurred in 64% of recorded collections in weedy ephemeral habitats.

### Anthropogenic Modification
Canalization is the dominant human impact, documented in 9 of 18 major headsprings by 1958–60 (Lagunas Escobeda, Ferrino, Santa Tecla, Tío Cándido, Tío Quintero, Anteojo, Orosco, San Marcos), expanding to include Poza la Becerra by 1981. This has caused lowered water levels and marshland reduction throughout the basin.

## Key Findings

1. **Habitat size vs. diversity — stable systems**: Strong positive correlation between habitat size (ranked 1–10) and species per collection in flowing systems (**r = 0.79**) and headsprings (**r = 0.63** unmodified; **r = 0.60** post-canalization).
2. **Diversity plateau**: In headsprings above rank ~3.0 (>200 m²), species richness plateaus at ~6.9 ± 0.33 species/collection despite a species pool of at least 14 — smaller habitats show fewer species than predicted by regression.
3. **No significant difference** (p < 0.05) between correlation coefficients for unmodified vs. canalized lagunas, suggesting **extinction is not detectably driven by artificial size reduction** within observed limits.
4. **Unstable systems show no size-diversity correlation**: Playas, marshes, ephemeral pools, and canals show little to no statistical relationship between habitat size and species richness — water quality (salinity, temperature) is the primary structuring filter, not size.
5. **Two distinct faunal assemblages**:
   - *Ostariophysine/percoid* assemblage → stable, large, high-volume habitats (springs, rivers)
   - *Cyprinodontoid* assemblage → fluctuating, terminal habitats (playas, marshes, ephemeral pools)
6. **Marshes as ecotone**: When deep and spring-associated, marshes hold intermediate/mixed faunas with the highest species values among fluctuating habitats; when shallow or distant from springs, they trend toward playa-like faunas.
7. **Predicted extinction sequence** under desiccation: rheophiles (*Astyanax mexicanus*, *Notropis xanthicara*, *Dionda episcopa*, *Etheostoma sp.*) would disappear first, followed by larger predators (*Ictalurus lupus*, *Pylodictis olivaris*, *Micropterus salmoides*, *Lepomis megalotis*, *Cichlasoma* spp.), leaving cyprinodontoids as the most resilient group — consistent with patterns documented in other American desert basins.
8. This basin-scale pattern (larger habitats support more species in stable systems) mirrors well-established regional/global patterns (**[[Island Biogeography]]**, species-area relationships; MacArthur & Wilson 1967; Preston 1960–62), providing a rare **local-scale confirmation** of these broader ecological theories.
9. Conservation concern: Many Cuatro Ciénegas endemics were already listed by Deacon et al. (1979) as endangered, threatened, or of special concern, reflecting broader vulnerability of obligate aquatic organisms in arid regions of western North America.
