# Management of the Owens Pupfish (Cyprinodon radiosus) in Mono County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

The [[Owens Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon radiosus* Miller, 1948) is a killifish endemic to the [[Owens River]] drainage of [[Owens Valley]], California. Thought extinct after its 1948 description, it was rediscovered in 1956 and again in 1964 at [[Fish Slough]]. Its decline was driven by habitat loss from water diversion through the [[Los Angeles Aqueduct]], introduced exotic fishes (notably [[Largemouth Bass]]), and competition with [[Mosquitofish]]. The authors describe the cooperative reestablishment of the species in the [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]], built by the City of Los Angeles and the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] and completed in October 1969. The paper frames this as part of growing scientific and public concern over threats to native fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described by Miller in 1948. Earlier, Snyder (1917) had assigned Owens Valley populations to the desert pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*), but that species is restricted to the Salton Sea basin in California (Miller, 1948).
- **Conservation status:** Classified as rare and endangered (U.S. Dept. Interior, 1968; Miller, 1969a).
- **Historical range:** From near Lone Pine (Inyo County) north to the source springs of Fish Slough, Mono County, about 10 to 12 miles north of Bishop.
- **Morphology (breeding males):** Dorsal fin pinkish tan to orange; anal fin pinkish tan to chalky blue or turquoise; bright blue to turquoise predorsal stripe; broad vertical side bars that intensify to metallic blue during breeding. Unlike other *Cyprinodon* males, the caudal fin lacks a conspicuous terminal black border in preserved specimens, though the border is present in live showy males.
- **Morphology (females):** Smaller fins, olivaceous brown to deep olive back, silvery or whitish venter, and narrower irregular brownish vertical bars.
- **Size:** Maximum length about 60 mm total length (50 mm standard length) at the sampled site.
- **Historical ecological note:** Kennedy (1916) suggested the species could help control mosquitoes in permanent freshwater marshes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and decline.** In the early twentieth century the species was abundant in springs, shallow sloughs, swamps, irrigation ditches and bog pastures along the Owens River. Its decline followed the introduction of exotic fishes and the drying of marshes after water was exported by the [[Los Angeles Aqueduct]] (Hinely, 1930s).

**Fish Slough population history (as documented):**
- **1934:** [[Carl L. Hubbs]] found only shallow, fishless pools along the Owens River near Bishop.
- **Mid-1930s:** The Mono-Inyo survey of the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries found the species abundant in Fish Slough, in shallow water 3 to 6 inches deep.
- **1937:** Carp and largemouth bass dominated the lower and middle slough; the species remained fairly common at the westernmost spring.
- **1938–1939:** The westernmost spring still supported an adequate population.
- **August–September 1942:** No pupfish were found in any of the three springs. Disappearance was attributed mainly to predation by largemouth bass, with declining minnow and sucker numbers also noted.

**Setting of the Redisovery Pool.** Pool about 100 yards long and 15 to 40 feet wide, very clear water, dense *Chara* on a firm mud bottom, scattered *Scirpus* stands, and an open marshy meadow shore. Water 92 °F and air 91 °F at 3 PM on 10 July 1964.

**Associated fishes.** Mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*), speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), juvenile largemouth bass, chubs, carp, suckers and small bullheads.

**Threats.** Exotic predators, competition from mosquitofish, and water-flow fluctuations. In August 1969 an unexplained decline in spring flow nearly eliminated the last individuals in Fish Slough.

## Key Findings

1. **Near-extinction and rediscovery.** No pupfish were found in 1942 surveys; the species was described as extinct in 1948. A population was rediscovered in June or July 1956 by W. M. Richardson and Ralph V. Beck of the California Department of Fish and Game, who caught about one pupfish per three seine hauls. A further survey in April 1963 failed to relocate that population.
2. **1964 rediscovery.** On 10 July 1964, Hubbs, Miller, Pister and colleagues rediscovered the population in an elongate pool fed by overflow from a spring-fed ditch. The estimated population was about 200 individuals, including fish in breeding colors.
3. **Refuge design.** Following a June 1968 meeting, [[Carl L. Hubbs]], R. R. Miller and E. P. Pister proposed a refuge in the two northwestern springs, which included the type locality. The refuge would also hold the other native Owens Basin fishes: a dace (*Rhinichthys*), a chub (*Gila*) and a sucker (*Catostomus*). An exotic-fish barrier was modeled on a design used by the Nevada Department of Fish and Game for White River springfish (*Crenichthys baileyi*) at Sunnyside, Nevada, in 1963.
4. **Sanctuary specifications.** Surface area 5.6 acres within a 21-acre enclosure fenced with five-strand barbed wire. Depth can be lowered by up to three feet using flashboards in the outlet structure; maximum depth about six feet. Construction was completed in October 1969 by inmate crews from the Inyo-Mono Conservation Camp under John N. Clark of the California Division of Forestry.
5. **Eradication of exotics.** In April 1970 the refuge was reduced to minimum volume and treated on 20 May and 3 June with calcium hypochlorite (HTH) in the shoals and liquid rotenone in deeper areas, to remove carp and bass eggs and fish before refilling for native stocking.
6. **Funding and cooperation.** Water and power engineering help was provided by the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, and the project was approved by the California Fish and Game Commission in April 1968.
7. **Behavior.** Observers recorded "leapfrogging," in which fish of both sexes leapt over a fallen *Scirpus* stem, a behavior also seen in mosquitofish.
8. **Policy outlook.** The authors present the sanctuary as a model for conserving native fishes. A second refuge was completed or under construction, and the three other native fishes of the valley were planned for one of these refuges.
