# Lahontan Cutthroat Trout Spawning and Downstream Migration of Juveniles into Summit Lake, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study monitored the spawning run of the only remaining secure native adfluvial population of [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] in [[Summit Lake]], Nevada, using a fish trap on [[Mahogany Creek]], the lake's sole spawning tributary, during 1993 and 1994. Adult upstream runs were similar in size (1290 fish in 1993; 1255 in 1994). Spawned-out adults returned downstream in different proportions (10.6% in 1993; 34.6% in 1994). Juveniles moved downstream in two distinct size-based groups: an early pulse of larger fish (mean fork length 90 mm), likely overwintered from 1993, and a later, smaller group (mean fork length 42 mm), likely young-of-the-year. The authors conclude that continued, year-round trap operation is needed to determine population demography.

## Background & Description

Lahontan cutthroat trout (*Oncorhynchus clarki henshawi*) were once widespread across the northwestern [[Great Basin]] of Nevada, California and Oregon, but their range declined sharply after the late 19th century. The species now occupies about 10% of its former fluvial habitat and less than 1% of its former lacustrine habitat (Coffin and Cowan 1994). The Summit Lake population is considered essential to recovery of the subspecies.

Summit Lake is a small, shallow basin (maximum depth about 12 m) with no outlet. It was formed by a landslide 8,000 to 19,000 years ago that reversed the drainage toward the [[Alvord Basin]]. Most of the lake lies within the [[Summit Lake Paiute Reservation]], established in 1913. Trout are an important cultural resource for the Summit Lake Paiute Tribe, which funded the work.

Because Mahogany Creek is the only tributary large enough to support reproduction, the entire lake population depends on it for spawning. Most suitable spawning gravel lies on [[Bureau of Land Management]] land. Intensive grazing destroyed much riparian habitat before 1970; a grazing exclosure established in 1974 has allowed substantial recovery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Historical management influenced the population. Egg collection from Mahogany Creek began in 1950, totalling about 18 million eggs between 1968 and 1984, with roughly 968,000 fingerlings stocked into the lake (about 5% of the egg take). Spawning runs were perceived to be declining through the 1980s, with only about 500 spawners observed in 1992 (J. Piccolo, unpublished data).

The study years followed one of the most significant drought periods recorded for northwestern Nevada, an 8-year drought according to the figure caption. Adults sampled were probably spawned during drought conditions. Mahogany Creek discharge data (1987 to 1995) showed that 1993 and 1994 differed substantially in total runoff.

Water temperature appeared to influence migration. Upstream movement was most prominent when temperatures at the trap exceeded 6 °C, and spawning temperatures reported for cutthroat trout range from 5.0 to 15.5 °C (Varley and Gresswell 1988). A [[Multiple Linear Regression]] of Julian day and temperature explained about 51% of variance in 1994 migrant numbers, but was not significant in 1993.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** A fish trap blocking the full stream flow was operated on Mahogany Creek. In 1993 it ran from 2 April to 6 June and from 27 July to 4 November. In 1994 it ran nearly continuously from 3 April to 31 October. Adults were sexed, weighed and measured. In 1994, 1223 upstream adults were tagged with [[Visible Implant Tags]], and downstream juveniles received coded wire microtags. Temperature was logged in both years.

**Upstream spawners.**
- 1993: 1290 fish (653 female, 637 male) moved upstream. Peaks occurred on 9 April (111 fish) and 4–5 May (437 fish), together accounting for over 42% of the run.
- 1994: 1255 fish (784 female, 469 male). The maximum daily count was 175 fish on 17 April. The 10 days from 14 April held 705 fish (56%).
- Mean fork length was 462 mm in 1994 and 463 mm in 1993 (mean weight 2.6 kg in 1993, range 0.1–7.2 kg). Size-frequency distributions did not differ significantly between years (Kolmogorov-Smirnov, P>0.999).
- The 1993 sex ratio was near 1:1 (1.02:1.0). The 1994 run was strongly female-biased (0.58:1.0).

**Downstream spent spawners.**
- 137 fish (10.6%) returned in 1993. In 1994, 434 fish (34.6%) returned, comprising 129 males and 303 females (sex ratio 0.43:1.0).
- Mean fork length of returning fish was 441 mm (range 227–610 mm) and mean weight 1.8 kg (range 0.1–4.5 kg). Both were smaller than upstream migrants.
- Condition (weight adjusted for length) was significantly lower in returning fish (F=7043, df=1, P<0.001, r²=0.83).
- Visible implant tags were recorded from 344 (80%) of returning 1994 spawners. The authors suggest tag loss, observation error or implantation problems, and note that other studies report higher retention.

**Residence time.**
- Most individually identified fish stayed upstream 12 to 28 days, though some returned in under 10 days and others stayed beyond 30 days.
- Females that moved upstream early tended to stay longer. Days upstream fell with Julian day for females (b=-0.40, r²=0.09, P<0.001), an effect not seen in males.
- Days upstream rose with fish length in both sexes (b=0.06, r²=0.05, P<0.001). Low r² values indicate other factors are important.

**Juvenile downstream migration (1994).**
- 2348 juveniles passed downstream between 27 April and 31 October, in two groups:
  - Early group: 1188 fish, mean fork length 90 mm (range 24–199 mm), mean weight 8.5 g. It passed from 27 April to 29 July, peaked around 15 June, and fell to zero by late July, when water temperatures were highest. Mean size declined from over 110 mm to under 90 mm.
  - Late group: 1160 fish, mean fork length 42 mm (range 22–161 mm), passing from August through October at about 13.5 fish per day, with no trend in daily numbers.
- The two groups differed significantly in fork length (t-test, T=18.89, df=91.6, P<0.001).
- Size and timing indicate the early group were probably fish from 1993 that overwintered in Mahogany Creek, and the late group were young-of-the-year from 1994.

**Projections and recommendations.**
- If the autumn passage rate continued for six months, roughly 2400 juveniles could enter the lake. A late-spring pulse could add a further 1000 to 2000.
- The authors recommend retaining the Mahogany Creek trap for as much of the year as access allows, to monitor fish moving in both directions, and tracking known-size cohorts to estimate age-specific growth and mortality.
