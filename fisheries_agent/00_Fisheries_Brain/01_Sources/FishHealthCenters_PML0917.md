# U.S. Fish Health Centers: Federal Disease Surveillance for Hatchery and Wild Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This magazine-style article describes the [[Fish Health Centers]] operated by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). The centers began as low-budget laboratories that monitored and certified the health of fish raised in federal hatcheries and advised states, tribes, and private aquaculturists. Their role has expanded to cover wild fish populations, disease research, and the testing of new drugs. The article is largely descriptive and quotes program staff. It reports funding figures for a national [[Wild Fish Health Survey]] and gives examples of disease threats and management practices.

## Background & Description

- **Origins:** The USFWS developed a fish health policy in 1959. Over the next decade, disease laboratories were established, and by the early 1980s they were consolidated into a centralized system of Federal Fish Health Centers.
- **Structure:** Each of the USFWS's seven regions has at least one center, and Alaska is included in the network.
- **Historical impact:** Fish diseases had decimated populations of bass, trout, salmon, and other species, virtually shutting down major fisheries.
- **Mandate shift:** The original purpose was to monitor, inspect, and certify the health of hatchery fish. Following the passage of the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1973, hatcheries were increasingly used to raise wild broodstock for threatened and endangered species. Centers now certify that hatchery fish are free of pathogens before they are stocked, so they do not infect wild fish.
- **Modern demands:** Advanced diagnostics, including viral detection and DNA analysis, are expensive. Because few state laboratories can afford them, demand for center services has grown.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Disease threats named in the article:**
- [[Whirling Disease]], first found in both federal hatchery fish and wild fish in Colorado and Montana in 1995. This discovery shifted the centers' focus toward wild populations.
- [[Infectious Hematopoietic Necrosis]] (IHN), which the article says caused the loss of about 90 percent of a steelhead stock in the 1980s. The virus was traced to the river water used in rearing.
- [[Furunculosis]], [[Enteric Redmouth Disease]], [[Columnaris]], [[Bacterial Gill Disease]], and [[Bacterial Kidney Disease]], which the centers can diagnose and, in some cases, treat in federal hatchery fish.
- Viral diseases that cannot be treated, for which centers test and block the shipment of infected fish.

**Habitat and water quality concerns:**
- Water source and quality are treated as major disease risk factors. Pathogens and toxins can enter ponds through runoff, including pesticides from crop spraying.
- [[Bait Fish]] are a concern because they are often imported, may include several species, and can carry diseases and parasites into new watersheds. Some bait ponds are near rivers and may spread pathogens from wild watersheds.

**Conservation-focused species work:**
- The [[Bonytail Chub]] population in the Southwest is being revived through a captive-breeding and recovery effort.
- Work also covers [[Gila Trout]] and [[Colorado Cutthroat Trout]] populations, with an emphasis on preventing the introduction of unknown pathogens into waters that hold endangered species.

## Key Findings

1. **Certification is central.** Centers certify hatchery fish as disease-free before release. This protects wild stocks, particularly endangered species.
2. **Funding for wild fish surveillance:** Congress provided $1.5 million in 1997 for a nationwide [[Wild Fish Health Survey]]. A second appropriation of $1.8 million followed, and $2 million was expected in 1999. Program lead Mary Ellen Mueller noted that before this work, most knowledge of whirling disease came from hatchery conditions.
3. **Drug approval is difficult.** Program director John Thoesen (Pinetop, Arizona) reported that it is hard to get new drugs registered, and the centers work with the [[Food and Drug Administration]] to approve treatments. He said most of the work is preventive. The Arizona center had not found whirling disease but was monitoring for it because neighboring regions had it.
4. **Hatchery practice matters.** Biologist Bill Miller (Wisconsin) said that releasing only healthy fish is important. The article reports that rearing practices and water sources can strongly influence survival and health.
5. **Emerging concerns:** Species diversity in aquaculture is growing, and more cold-water species are being raised. Bait fish imports and contaminated water sources are seen as underrecognized risks.
6. **Locations named:** Centers are listed in Pinetop, AZ; Bozeman, MT (Region VI, project leader Richard Hudson); Washington state; Wisconsin (Region III, project leader Richard Nelson); and Duluth, MN, the last of which specializes in [[Great Lakes]] and [[Mississippi River]] disease issues.

**Limitations:** The article is a feature piece rather than peer-reviewed research. Several percentage figures about disease losses are partly garbled in the scanned text and cannot be reliably reported.
