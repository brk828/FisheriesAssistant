# Use of Population Genetics Data in the Management of Fish and Wildlife Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #population-genetics #cervidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at the Forty-First North American Wildlife Conference (Washington, D.C., 1976), argues that [[Population Genetics]] data should be integrated into [[Fish and Wildlife Management]] decisions. The authors, working from the [[University of Georgia]] and the [[Savannah River Ecology Laboratory]], answer six questions that wildlife managers commonly ask: how genetic data can define management unit boundaries, how population characteristics relate to genetic composition, whether inbreeding and heterosis matter, how stocking should account for genetics, whether genetics can help manage endangered species, and what research is needed. The paper concludes that genetic information supplements but does not replace habitat and other ecological data. The authors recommend [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] as a practical, largely nondestructive tool for these questions. The transcribed discussion that follows records a debate over the value of heterozygosity as a management criterion.

## Background & Description

The authors note that the nature-nurture debate over population differences is partly shaped by political and cultural outlooks, but that both genetic and environmental factors clearly influence population attributes. Modern practices such as harvest regulation, habitat improvement, stocking, and predator control inadvertently alter the genetic structure of populations. Historically, managers treated genetics as an "unknown catch-all category," assuming genetic causes only when other explanations failed. Growing demand for hunting, fishing, and commercial harvest has pushed agencies to reconsider this approach.

Electrophoretic methods, which detect variation in proteins reflecting genetic polymorphisms, are the main tool discussed. The paper draws on work by [[Utter et al.]] (1973, 1974) on [[Pacific Salmon]] and related trout, and on [[Selander et al.]] (1971) on biochemical polymorphism in *Peromyscus*. The paper was supported by a contract between the U.S. Energy Research and Development Administration and the University of Georgia, and it acknowledges cooperation from the U.S. Forest Service, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Army, and several state wildlife agencies in Georgia, Florida, and South Carolina. Data collection was conducted by these agencies and by University of Georgia students and staff.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Management unit boundaries.** Management units are usually defined by political boundaries, ownership, habitat types, and species ecology such as distribution and movement. Genetic criteria can define functional population units, particularly for migratory species (for example, anadromous fish) whose breeding and wintering populations are hard to link through tagging. Genetic markers may be cheaper than tagging, though they are not always successful.

**Sedentary species.** For sedentary species, allele frequencies can differ significantly across small geographic distances. On the Savannah River Plant, white-tailed deer allele frequencies differed significantly over relatively small distances, and these differences were associated with differences in reproductive rate (Urbston 1967). The paper also cites allele differences in other taxa, including [[Rock Bass]], [[Kangaroo Rats]], [[Pocket Gophers]], Yellow Pine Chipmunks, [[Drosophila]] species, and [[Sparrows]].

**Environmental selection.** Several studies link genotype to environment. Powers and Powers (1975) modeled selection by environmental variability at the LDH-B locus in the mummichog. Hurzyk and Tsuyuki (1974) associated certain LDH-B genotypes with swimming endurance in rainbow trout. Yardley et al. (1974) found allele frequency differences between lake and river populations of three freshwater fish species, though no clear thermal effect. Schaffer and Johnson (1974) found correlations between allele frequencies and environmental variables in insects. Enzyme maintenance in salmonids is thought to increase flexibility in fluctuating thermal regimes.

**Population dynamics.** The paper identifies four characteristics that change population numbers: original numbers, natality, mortality, and net movement. Genetic structure is linked to each. Heterozygosity correlates positively with reproductive rate in old-field mice (Smith et al. 1975) and with genetic variability in *Drosophila*. Genotype influences mortality in pigeons, blue grouse, and the sand shiner (Koehn et al. 1971). Population density is associated with changes in mean heterozygosity in old-field mice, and gene frequency shifts track fluctuating microtine numbers.

**Inbreeding.** Public concern about inbreeding is common, but small deer with poor antler racks are often limited by overpopulation or habitat quality rather than inbreeding. Electrophoresis now allows the inbreeding coefficient to be estimated from the deviation between observed and expected heterozygotes (F, Wright 1969). Past inbreeding may have reduced variability even when a population is no longer inbreeding, and this can be detected by surveying variable loci and calculating average individual heterozygosity. Evidence of harmful inbreeding effects comes mainly from laboratory populations and livestock.

**Stocking and reintroduction.** Many fish and wildlife species were extirpated from parts of their range and reintroduced, often from few and young founders. Deer in Georgia were stocked from many states and from coastal islands, and raccoons were moved from coastal plain sources into Piedmont and mountain regions. Turkeys were restocked, and biologists sometimes selected young gobblers to keep superior birds in resident flocks. Most stocking ignored genetic information.

**Endangered species.** Low genetic variability appears in species isolated on islands or in refuges. The northern elephant seal probably lost most of its variability through a bottleneck caused by human exploitation. Extinction probability rises as variability falls.

## Key Findings

- **Genetic data can define functional management units.** Allele frequency differences over small distances can justify smaller management units than political or ownership boundaries would suggest. Genetic "tags" may be simpler than traditional tagging for migratory species.
- **Genetic variation relates to population traits.** Heterozygosity is linked to reproductive rate, mortality, dispersal, body size, and survival in multiple taxa, though causal links remain unclear and need further study.
- **Inbreeding is often misdiagnosed.** Small body size or poor trophy quality in deer more often reflects overpopulation or habitat quality. Electrophoresis can measure inbreeding and past genetic loss directly.
- **Stocking should maximize founder diversity.** Genetic drift and the Founder Principle mean that introduced populations inherit the genetic makeup of their founders. Mutation supplies negligible new variation on management timescales, so managers should introduce as many animals as possible, from several populations with differing gene frequencies and from similar habitats.
- **Endangered species need genetic surveys.** Electrophoresis can assess variability without killing animals. Survey data should inform reserve size (using effective population size and movement patterns), cross-stocking among areas with spatial heterogeneity, and monitoring of variability over time.
- **Genetics aids law enforcement and taxonomy.** Electrophoresis can identify the source of animals such as American alligators and separate closely related species, such as black ducks and mallards or eastern and New England cottontails.
- **Research priorities.** The authors call for rapid, nondestructive survey techniques (blood or muscle-plug sampling), standardized procedures and manuals, surveys across many fish and wildlife species, and experiments linking management practices to genetic and phenotypic outcomes. They note that electrophoretic techniques, using similar buffers and stains for fish and mammals, are currently the only practical option.
- **Genetics supplements, not replaces, other data.** The authors conclude that genetic information should be combined with habitat and other ecological data in management decisions.

**Discussion highlights.** In the question period, a participant asked whether it is worthwhile to spend money trying to manage endangered species with little genetic variability. Dr. Smith replied that the most promising candidates are species that still retain variability, because a species already inbred has little chance of recovery. A Michigan participant cautioned that heterozygosity may reflect stable environments rather than vulnerability, so habitat specialization should be considered alongside genetic data. Dr. Smith agreed that other factors must be weighed, and argued that managers should act on the best available data rather than wait for conclusive results.
