# Native Status of the Rio Grande Sucker in the Gila Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether the [[Rio Grande Sucker]] (*Pantosteus plebeius*) was recently introduced by humans into the [[Gila River Basin]] of New Mexico or is native there. Earlier surveys lacked the species, and it was first recorded in Sapillo Creek in 1949 and the San Francisco River in 1953, which led [[Koster]] (1957) and later authors to propose human-mediated introduction. The authors combined microsatellite and mitochondrial DNA (cyt b, ND4) data with [[Approximate Bayesian Computation]] (ABC) to compare introduction scenarios. They rejected introduction within the last 150 years and favored an older headwater stream capture from the [[Mimbres River]] into Sapillo Creek, roughly 4,000 years ago, followed by dispersal into the upper San Francisco River about 2,000 years ago. The authors conclude the species is native to the Gila Basin and that these populations hold unique genetic diversity relevant to conservation.

## Background & Description

*Pantosteus plebeius* is a catostomid sucker found in upland streams east of the [[Continental Divide]] in the southern Rocky Mountains. Its presence in the Gila Basin was unexplained by early surveys, including those by [[W. H. Emory]] in the 1850s, which did not record it. Prior accounts (Koster 1957; LaBounty and Minckley 1972; Minckley 1973) treated it as introduced. Alternative explanations, including stream capture across a low-relief divide near the head of Sapillo Creek (Crabtree and Buth 1987; McPhee et al. 2008), had been proposed but not formally tested.

The study framed its test around a genetic prediction. A recent introduction should show little differentiation between source and introduced populations, whereas an older natural colonization should show differentiation proportional to elapsed time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sampling:** About 30 fish were collected per site by single-pass electrofishing and dip netting across 12 site records in the Gila Basin (Sapillo Creek, San Francisco River and tributaries, Centerfire Creek, Saliz Canyon), the Mimbres River, and the Rio Grande Basin (Jemez River, Alamosa Creek) in New Mexico.
- **Hybridization:** [[Desert Sucker]] (*P. clarkii*) co-occurs in the Gila Basin. Of 206 fishes assayed in the Gila drainage, 18 were hybrids (morphologically Rio Grande Sucker with Desert Sucker mtDNA). Fewer than 9% of Gila River individuals showed mtDNA introgression.
- **Paleoclimate:** Late Holocene pluvial periods, including one beginning about 4,000 years before present, may have opened dispersal routes between the Mimbres drainage and Sapillo Creek.
- **Conservation status:** The species is declining in the upper Rio Grande and is under consideration for listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It is also perceived to be declining in Mexico.
- **Related taxa:** [[Zuni Bluehead Sucker]] (*P. discobolus yarrowi*) in the Little Colorado system, and [[Casas Grandes River]] populations in Mexico, share phylogeographic history with the study populations.

## Key Findings

- **Sample size:** 294 individuals were assayed at microsatellite and mtDNA loci. Of these, 23 were Desert Sucker or hybrids and were excluded from further analysis.
- **Phylogeography:** Gila Basin populations share recent common ancestry with the Mimbres population. Upper Rio Grande populations (Jemez River, Alamosa Creek) form a basal sister lineage that diverged earlier, probably in the late Pliocene or early Pleistocene. Headwater capture or human translocation from other proximal sources was ruled out for the Gila origin.
- **Genetic diversity:** Gila and Mimbres populations had higher diversity than Rio Grande populations. The Mimbres population had the highest microsatellite diversity (H_E = 0.67, H_O = 0.66, A_R = 7.55).
- **Differentiation:** Pairwise F_ST was high between the Rio Grande and Mimbres (maximum 0.619) and between the Rio Grande and Gila (maximum 0.430). It was lower between Mimbres and Gila catchments (0.075 with upper San Francisco; 0.061 with Sapillo Creek).
- **Population structure:** STRUCTURE supported K = 2 (Gila/Mimbres vs. Rio Grande) as most likely. K = 5 separated the upper San Francisco, Sapillo Creek, Mimbres, and Rio Grande units.
- **ABC scenario testing:** The best-supported scenario (posterior probability 0.963) placed Sapillo Creek as the most recent common ancestor of San Francisco Basin populations, with the Mimbres River as the source for all Gila Basin populations. Introduction within the last 150 years was rejected.
- **Divergence times:** Coalescence between San Francisco and Sapillo populations was estimated at 1,180 generations. The Sapillo–Mimbres coalescence was estimated at 2,040 generations (427–8,390), or about 4,080 years ago, assuming a two-year generation time.
- **Private alleles:** Private alleles at low average frequency (about 0.03) were found at Sapillo Creek (two loci) and the San Francisco River (five loci). Two ND4 haplotypes and one cyt b haplotype occurred only in Gila Basin populations.
- **Hybridization:** Introgression with Desert Sucker was below 9% of Gila individuals. The authors concluded that Rio Grande Sucker maintains species cohesion and has not formed a hybrid swarm.
- **Conclusion:** Rio Grande Sucker is native to the upper Gila River Basin, documenting a natural range expansion and previously unrecognized diversity. The authors recommend binational conservation efforts that include Mexican populations, and they note that contact with Desert Sucker may increase as the climate warms.
