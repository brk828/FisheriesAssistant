# Lake Mohave Razorback Sucker Rearing Program (1991–1995)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a faxed progress report (15 pages, Lower Colorado Region, [[Bureau Of Reclamation]]) from the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) describing efforts to rescue the aging adult population of [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]]. The group rears wild-collected larvae and juveniles in lakeside backwaters and releases them to replace adults lost to old age, with the aim of preserving the population's genetic diversity for future recovery efforts. Reported work from 1991 through 1995 covers larval collection, backwater stocking, survival estimates and the planned two-year rearing schedule. ## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large sucker of the family Catostomidae, is native to the [[Colorado River]]. Over 95% of the remaining wild adult population lives in Lake Mohave, a mainstem reservoir between Arizona and Nevada impounded in 1950. Studies over the previous ten years showed the adult population is dying off. Similar declines occurred in four other reservoirs (Lakes Mead, Havasu, Roosevelt and Saguaro), where populations collapsed soon after impoundment, alongside expanding exotic fish populations.

Key biological constraints identified in the document:
- Adults spawn each spring in shoreline gravels; eggs receive no parental defense and are heavily consumed.
- Larvae hatch within roughly five to ten days and are heavily preyed upon by [[Green Sunfish]] (OCR reads "young sunfish"), [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]].
- Young adults are rarely observed, and there is no significant recruitment to adulthood.
- Adult lifespan is under 50 years, and Lake Mohave's closure in 1950 means the population was projected to disappear by the turn of the century without intervention.
- A notable feature is the population's high [[Genetic Diversity]]. Studies at Arizona State University and the University of California, Davis reported a ratio of individuals sharing genetic traits, but the figure is illegible in the scan.

The NFWG is a multi-agency ad hoc team formed by mutual consent in 1989 (member agencies listed in Table 1, including the Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service, National Park Service, USGS Biological Resources Division, Nevada Division of Wildlife, Arizona State University and Arizona Game & Fish). Its primary method is on-site rearing of juveniles in lakeside backwaters, sourced from adults, lakeshore larvae or brood fish at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The stated program goal is to produce and stock 50,000 fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The program depends on the habitat dynamics of Lake Mohave. Lake levels fluctuate: in 1994 the team used [[Ephemeral Backwaters]] that filled during late winter and early spring as the reservoir rose and dried in fall as water levels declined. These isolated pools:
- provided enough water through the growing season for fish to grow;
- allowed fish harvest and removal during the fall drawdown;
- dried out in late fall, removing non-native fish and limiting access by visitors.

Natural lakeside backwaters were also used. In November 1991, the NFWG reinforced a natural sand-and-gravel berm across [[Yuma Cove]], isolating it from the main lake. Four additional backwaters were added in 1993, each formed by wave-built sand and gravel bars. Predation by exotic species is the central threat, and rearing fish to a larger size before release is the main tactic against it.

## Key Findings

**Program history and results by year**

- **1991–1992 (Yuma Cove):** Berm reinforced in November 1991. In January 1992, ripe adults were placed in the backwater, and 150 juveniles were reared and released to the lake in the fall. Juveniles grew from about one inch in March to as much as fourteen inches by November (average 12.8 inches). Mean size data are in Table 3.
- **1993:** Four new backwaters were added. Wild larvae were collected and held in lab tanks until they doubled in size (about three weeks) before stocking. One backwater received fertilized eggs, one wild larvae, and one larvae held for three weeks. The efforts produced over 500 young fish released to the lake (exact figure garbled in scan).
- **1994:** Nine ephemeral backwaters were used. Field crews captured wild larvae with submerged lights 2–4 evenings per week, with over 10,000 larvae collected. About 9,000 three-week-old larvae were stocked into the nine backwaters. The team reared fish with live [[Brine Shrimp Nauplii]], and the cycle was complete by mid-October with a reported total of about 7,200 razorback suckers reared and released (OCR-garbled).
- **1995:** Spring collections yielded 20,746 wild larvae according to the table total (text reads "ZA,L46", possibly 24,146; the scan is inconsistent). 10,801 were stocked into one of seven lakeside ponds, and 7,529 larvae were transferred to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], where they were held for at least three weeks. Estimated survival over this period was 91% (18,330 of 20,146 fish).

**Survival and growth data**

- Fish from 1992 Yuma Cove releases: seven 1992 fish and three 1993 fish were recaptured at spawning areas during the 1994 "Razorback Sucker Roundup," which the NFWG cited as encouraging.
- 1993 test: 57 of 69 fingerlings (82%) from Dexter NFH survived to over 300 mm by mid-August.
- June 1994 test: 420 advanced larvae (26–35 mm) stocked in Yuma Cove; 385 were recaptured in October, over 200 mm long, with a reported survival of 91.5%.
- August 1994 test: 272 juveniles were captured in [[Willow Cove]] and [[South Sidewinder Cove]], fin-clipped, and moved to Yuma Cove. In October, 268 were recovered (about 98.5% by calculation; the printed figure is garbled).

**Size distributions (Table 8)** for fish reared in lakeside backwaters in 1994 show that the proportion of fish over 200 mm ranged from 49% to 99% across backwaters, and over 250 mm from 12% to 92%.

**Planned direction**

- Goal: maintain the adult population's gene pool for recovery throughout the species' historical range, which requires rearing fish large enough to escape exotic predators.
- The NFWG plans a two-year rearing cycle to reach 300 mm. Wild larvae will be collected during the spawning season and transferred to Willow Beach NFH, then moved to lakeside backwaters the following January (expected over 100 mm). Harvest is planned for October of the second year.
- The NFWG expects that juveniles stocked as young adults will return each spring to spawn and be available for recovery over the next forty years.

**Overall outcome:** The document reports sustained survival of stocked fish and recaptures of released fish, but the authors describe these results as providing only "guarded optimism."
