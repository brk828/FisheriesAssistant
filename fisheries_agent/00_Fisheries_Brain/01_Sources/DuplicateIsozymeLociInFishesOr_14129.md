# Duplicate Isozyme Loci in Fishes: Origins, Distribution, Phyletic Consequences, and Locus Nomenclature

#ichthyology #marine-biology #polyploidy #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review by Donald G. Buth (UCLA, Department of Biology), published in *Isozymes: Current Topics in Biological and Medical Research* (Volume 10, 1983), examines duplicated isozyme loci in fishes. It addresses three questions: where duplicated genes originate, how widespread duplicate gene expression is (especially from [[Polyploidy]]), and what evolutionary fates these duplicates meet. It then proposes improvements to [[Isozyme Nomenclature]] for fish loci. The author argues that fishes, as a heterogeneous and very diverse group, are well suited to studying gene duplication, and that polyploidization has played a greater role than tandem duplication in fish evolution.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with a quotation from [[Susumu Ohno]] (1970) arguing that [[Gene Duplication]] is the major force of evolution, because a redundant locus is free to accumulate mutations and take on new functions. The controversy over gene duplication and polyploidy has continued for over a decade, and isozyme studies have revealed multilocus enzyme systems that support the hypothesis.

Nearly half of extant vertebrate species are fishes. Their lineages are heterogeneous, and some are only distantly related, which provides many alternative pathways for the origin and fate of duplications. Fishes are also phylogenetically linked to tetrapods, making them useful for understanding the evolutionary background of human genetics.

Two modes of duplication are distinguished:

- **[[Tandem Duplication]]**: duplication of a single gene or cistron within a chromosome, arising from mitotic or meiotic errors. Regulatory genes are probably not duplicated, which limits divergence.
- **[[Polyploidization]]**: duplication of the entire genome, producing unlinked duplicate loci under separate regulatory control. In fishes this has most commonly yielded even ploidies, usually tetraploids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a literature synthesis and does not report field sampling, habitat measurements, or water chemistry. Ecological relevance is indirect:

- Catostomid suckers are described as a tetraploid group that may have radiated from a single tetraploid ancestor. At least 60+ North American species are included.
- Diploid-tetraploid complexes occur among cobitid loaches (for example, intraspecific relationships within *Cobitis biwae* and *C. taenia*), which may represent polyploidization in action.
- Most known cyprinid polyploids are temperate Eurasian in distribution, and the very speciose East Asian cyprinid assemblage is largely unexamined.
- Siluriform research is sparse, partly because of a perceived difficulty in resolving isozymes on starch gels, and the paper calls for greater attention to tropical South American forms.

## Key Findings

**1. Tandem duplication is rare in fishes but detectable.** [[Ferris and Whitt]] (1977a) examined 20 enzyme systems in 26 tetraploid catostomid species and found only one-gene or two-gene control; no three-gene control indicating post-tetraploidization tandem duplication was observed. Some tandem duplicates persist across lineages (for example, three-gene control of mitochondrial aspartate aminotransferase in several advanced catostomids). A single-specimen tandem duplicate was reported in the characid *Cheirodon axelrodi* (1 of 132 individuals examined).

**2. Polyploidization has played the greater role in fish gene duplication.** The example genus *Hypentelium* (tetraploid catostomids) expresses five malate dehydrogenase (MDH) loci, producing at least seven MDH isozymes, reflecting at least two polyploidization events. Diploid cypriniforms express three MDH loci.

**3. Polyploid fishes are known from six orders.** These are Acipenseriformes, Salmoniformes, Cypriniformes, Siluriformes, Atheriniformes, and Perciformes.

- **Acipenseriformes:** Shovelnose sturgeon (112 chromosomes, suspected tetraploid); starred sturgeon (suspected from DNA content); North American paddlefish *Polyodon spathula* (120 chromosomes in 30 groups of four homologs).
- **Salmoniformes:** Salmonidae are considered autotetraploids, with tetraploidy as the only supported level. [[White]] (1978) disputed this, but the author argues White confused ancient and recent duplications.
- **Cypriniformes:** Tetraploid cyprinids (for example, *Carassius auratus*, *Cyprinus carpio*, *Barbus* species) and catostomids (2n ≈ 100) are reported. Only eight cyprinid species are identified as tetraploids among more than 1,600 species.
- **Siluriformes:** In four *Corydoras* species, duplicate isozyme loci ranged from 6.25% to 37.5%, paralleling haploid DNA content of 2.3–4.4 pg per cell.
- **Atheriniformes:** Triploid unisexual poeciliids (for example, *Poecilia formosa*) and allotriploids in *Poeciliopsis*. The goodeid *Skiffia* shows species-specific duplications despite being diploid, a notable exception to the duplication–DNA content correlation.
- **Perciformes:** Of roughly one-third of extant fish species, only *Lucioperca sandra* suggests polyploidy (2n = 24 in Sweden, 2n = 48 in Finland).

**4. Duplicates diverge in function and regulation.** Duplicates may differ in allelic composition, tissue expression, or regulatory control. The Ldh-C locus, expressed in eye or liver depending on lineage, is cited as a striking example of regulatory divergence.

**5. Functional diploidization silences duplicates.** Ohno (1970) proposed that many duplicated loci are silenced for lack of selective advantage. Ferris and Whitt (1978) argue that loss of duplicate expression permits identification of primitive and derived character states, a system applied to catostomid systematics.

**6. Retetraploidization is proposed as a hypothesis.** Ferris and Whitt (1978) considered silencing largely irreversible. Buth (1982) proposed that reactivation of silenced genes, possibly from a single regulatory mutation, could explain some catostomid data, termed "retetraploidization." The author argues this deserves further study and that duplicate expression data should be interpreted cautiously.

**7. Locus nomenclature needs an informative system.** Allendorf and Utter (1978) proposed the only formal system: capitalized enzyme abbreviations, italicized locus roots, hyphenated numeric locus suffixes ordered by anodal mobility, and allele values from 0 to 100. Buth argues this is insufficient as multilocus systems grow. He proposes that:

- Enzyme names follow International Union of Biochemistry recommendations and EC numbers.
- Mitochondrial (M-) and supernatant (S-) prefixes denote intracellular location, independent of polyploidization.
- Ancient duplications are recognized through homology established by substrate specificity, tissue expression, temperature optima, and immunology, not mobility alone.
- Polyploid duplicates use numbered superscripts (following Engel et al., 1973) and are origin-specific; homologies should not be inferred across Catostomidae and Salmonidae.
- Buffer conditions should be specified because mobilities vary with buffer.

**8. Research recommendations.** The author identifies priority questions, including:

- Whether sturgeons are entirely polyploid, requiring karyotype and isozyme analysis of 23 extant species and both paddlefish species.
- Whether holosteans (gars, bowfin) are polyploid, although karyotypic data suggest diploidy.
- Whether the catostomid polyploidization occurred in China before dispersal to North America, which could be addressed by studying *Myxocyprinus asiaticus*.
- Whether East Asian cyprinid and cobitid polyploids remain undiscovered, especially in *Barbus*.
- Whether Siluriformes, with about 2,000 species, hold the greatest number of undiscovered polyploids.
