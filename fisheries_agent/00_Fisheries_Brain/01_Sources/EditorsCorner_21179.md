# Arizona Section Society for Range Management Editor's Corner on Yaqui Chub Conservation Conflict

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #habitat-conservation-plan

## Executive Summary

This editorial from the Arizona Section of the Society for Range Management discusses the case of rancher Josiah T. Austin, recipient of SRM's prestigious Chapline Land Stewardship Award in 1998, who developed a Habitat Conservation Plan for the endangered [[Yaqui Chub]] (Gila purpurea) on his [[El Coronado Ranch]] and adjacent [[Coronado National Forest]] lands. Despite exemplary conservation efforts including fish reintroduction, non-native fish removal, and cooperative management, the Forest Service's 1998 Biological Assessment determined Austin's livestock grazing was "likely to adversely affect" the [[Yaqui Chub]], leading to litigation by the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]]. The editorial criticizes this determination as ignoring actual on-ground conditions and sending counterproductive messages about cooperation in endangered species management.

## Background & Description

The [[Yaqui Chub]] is an endangered native cyprinid fish species that was the subject of a voluntary Habitat Conservation Plan developed by rancher Josiah T. Austin. Austin allowed introduction of the chub into ponds on his private land and adjacent Forest Service land in the [[Turkey Creek]] allotment within the [[Chiricahua Mountains]]. 

Austin removed non-native fish from these ponds to benefit the [[Yaqui Chub]]. When sediment from a large wildfire on Forest Service land threatened the fish, Austin purchased an aerator to save them. His management also considered the [[Longfin Dace]] (Agosia chrysogaster), another native fish species in the area.

Additional conservation efforts on the property included cooperation with reintroduction of thick-billed parrots, financial support for [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] studies, and implementation of rangeland management practices including fencing, water developments, shrub control, native grass reseeding, prescribed fire, and loose-rock gabions for sediment control and water management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Turkey Creek]] allotment on the [[Coronado National Forest]] was determined by the Forest Service to show upward trend in range condition with almost all the allotment in satisfactory soil condition. Austin's land management practices, including livestock grazing management, were designed to be compatible with endangered species conservation.

The [[Yaqui Chub]] habitat in ponds was threatened by sediment resulting from wildfire on Forest Service lands, requiring active intervention (aeration) to maintain fish survival. The presence of non-native fish species required removal to protect the native [[Yaqui Chub]] population.

Austin's grazing management was conducted to benefit both the [[Yaqui Chub]] and [[Longfin Dace]], demonstrating an integrated approach to native fish conservation within a working ranch landscape.

## Key Findings

The Coronado National Forest's Biological Assessment of On-going and Long-term Grazing (December 1998) determined that Austin's livestock grazing was "likely to adversely affect" the continued existence of the [[Yaqui Chub]] on the [[Turkey Creek]] allotment, despite his exemplary conservation record and active Habitat Conservation Plan.

The Biological Assessment cited general possible effects of livestock grazing without apparent consideration of actual conditions on the allotment or Austin's demonstrated commitment to [[Yaqui Chub]] protection.

This determination resulted in Austin's allotment being named in a lawsuit by the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]] against the Forest Service alleging failure to comply with the Endangered Species Act, threatening removal of livestock grazing.

The editorial argues this case sends a counterproductive message to ranchers and land users about cooperation in endangered species protection, potentially discouraging voluntary conservation efforts and leading to increased litigation rather than collaborative solutions.

The author contends the Forest Service's approach lacked adequate consideration of actual on-ground conditions, good science, and common sense in evaluating threats to endangered species, and that greater involvement of range management professionals in such assessments would improve outcomes.
