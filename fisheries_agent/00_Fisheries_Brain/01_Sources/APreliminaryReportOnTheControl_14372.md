# Control of Pathogenic Fungi in Earthen Culture Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary report by Theophilus D. Inslee (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Fish Culture Development Center, Tishomingo, Oklahoma) evaluates whether repeated chemical treatments can control parasitic fungal infestations, chiefly [[Saprolegnia Parasitica]], in pond-reared [[Largemouth Bass]] and handled adult [[Striped Bass]]. The working hypothesis is that removing free-swimming [[Zoospores]] around the time of stocking protects fish while they recover from handling injuries.

[[Copper Sulfate]] at 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 ppm markedly improved survival of largemouth bass fingerlings, and no fungal infestation was seen in copper-treated ponds. [[2,4-D]] gave no significant survival benefit. In striped bass, copper sulfate sharply reduced losses. In vitro, [[Formalin]], [[Malachite Green]] and [[Potassium Permanganate]] did not control fungal growth, although they caused hyphal abnormalities.

## Background & Description

Parasitic fungal infestations are among the hardest fish diseases to control. Saprolegniaceae are widespread in natural fresh waters, and the chemical concentrations needed to kill established mycelium often exceed levels safe for use in ponds. Standard treatments for eggs and fish use high concentrations for short periods.

Fungal losses are common in largemouth bass broodstock at warm-water culture stations. Outbreaks are usually linked to stress or injury, and moving broodfish between ponds in cold weather can trigger them. Outbreaks can also follow infestations by protozoans or copepods. The report cites heavy losses in several other species: striped bass adults, [[Walleye]], [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]].

Two hatchery cases illustrate the problem:
- At one hatchery, 8,904 largemouth bass fingerlings (6–8 inches total length) were harvested in December for marking. Fungal outbreaks killed all but 134 fish despite control efforts.
- At [[Tishomingo National Fish Hatchery]], 550 largemouth bass broodstock were moved to another pond in February. A fungal outbreak followed 10 to 14 days later, and despite treatment a 60% loss occurred.

Because most parasitic fungi are transmitted by flagellated zoospores (Kanouse, 1932), the author proposed that removing viable zoospores before and for a period after stocking would protect susceptible fish until handling injuries healed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond conditions.** Ponds (0.1 surface acre each) were chosen by the amount and type of organic matter present:
- Four ponds had been dry for at least six months and had thick [[Bermuda Grass]] growth.
- Four ponds had little or no plant growth.
- Five ponds had been dry for about 40 days and held heavy growth of dead aquatic plants.

The report states that copper sulfate activity did not vary with the amount or type of organic material.

**Water temperature.** Fingerling feeding stopped at about 9 °C, roughly 30 days before the trial. Striped bass were tested at 13–19 °C. The author concludes that fungal control in largemouth bass is needed mainly when water temperatures are below 15 °C. At higher temperatures, fungal invasions caused lower mortality, probably because of greater host resistance. During the 48-day largemouth bass trial, the ponds were ice-covered for about 50% of the observation period.

**Water chemistry.** The toxicity of copper sulfate depends on [[Water Hardness]], so each culture station must run bioassays to find safe limits before routine use.

**Capture stress.** Striped bass were taken by [[Gill Net Capture]] in Lake Toxoma and transported to Tishomingo. Their stress was a confounding factor, and fish dying within five days of stocking were excluded from the fungus-control data.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- **Largemouth bass fingerlings:** Lot I came from a commercial fish farm and was fed a floating trout diet. Lot II came from San Marcos Fish Cultural Development Center, was raised in a raceway on sinking trout diet, and was reared in constant-temperature water. Lots were marked by clipping the caudal fin in different places.
- **Treatments:** Copper sulfate at 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 ppm, or 2,4-D at 2.0 ppm, were applied by the dipper method on the day before stocking and on days 1, 3, 6, 8, 10, 13 and 20 after stocking. Three control ponds were used. The 2.0 ppm copper sulfate treatment was tested in only one pond.
- **Striped bass:** Fish weighing 2–28 pounds were stocked in three 0.1-acre ponds (one control, two test). Test ponds received 1.0 ppm copper sulfate with an equal amount of citric acid, followed by five more treatments applied every other day. Adrenalin was injected near the eye in about 66% of fish in both control and test ponds to test whether it relieved capture stress.
- **In vitro:** Fungal culture was grown on hemp seed and cornmeal agar and then exposed to chemicals in petri dishes in triplicate, with one, two or three treatments.

**Largemouth bass results**
- Survival under copper sulfate was 96%, 90.7% and 100% at 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 ppm, respectively. Only 10.7% of controls survived.
- 2,4-D showed no significant survival difference from controls.
- Dead fish from control and 2,4-D ponds were heavily infested with fungi. Fish from copper sulfate ponds showed no infestation, even after more than 30 days, although bacterial decay was advanced.
- Lot II fish darkened, rested upright on the bottom, and all died within 18 days. Lot I was not affected in the same way. The author called Lot II's mortality unexpected and unexplained, possibly linked to extreme obesity. Lot I had shown a similar problem before its 30-day starvation period.

**Striped bass results**
- All 17 control fish were attacked by fungus and died.
- Only 2 of 39 fish in the two copper sulfate ponds contacted fungi.
- The author calls the difference highly significant and recommends further refinement of copper sulfate treatment.

**In vitro results**
- Negative controls were free of growth, and all positive controls became infested.
- Copper sulfate at 0.5 and 1.0 ppm greatly reduced colony size and density in most trials. Three treatments at 1.0 ppm left 100% of hemp seed free of growth, and three treatments at 0.5 ppm left 66.6% free.
- Formalin, potassium permanganate and malachite green gave no control at the tested levels, though they caused hyphal abnormalities.

**Conclusions and planned work**
- Copper sulfate is an effective control for secondary fungal infestations, but its safe use depends on water hardness.
- 2,4-D showed little or no effect on zoospores or fungal intensity.
- The author plans further work to find the minimum number of treatments needed for recovery from handling injuries, to refine copper sulfate use in striped bass, and to run field and intensive in vitro tests of formalin, malachite green and potassium permanganate.
