# Behavior and Morphology in the Glandulocaudine Fishes (Ostariophysi, Characidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #characidae

## Executive Summary

This monograph by [[Keith Nelson]], published in the *University of California Publications in Zoology* (1964, vol. 75, no. 2, pp. 59–152), combines aquarium behavioral studies, field ecology, preserved-specimen morphology and taxonomic review of the [[Glandulocaudini]], a group of South American characids. The author's central aim was to find clues about the origin of [[Internal Fertilization]] in fishes. The study covers six species in four genera studied alive, a survey of 284 collections from Trinidad, and preserved material of 17 characid genera. The author concludes that the group is monophyletic, that its basic adaptations are surface feeding and internal fertilization, and that much of its sexual dimorphism and courtship behavior evolved to ensure successful mating with unresponsive females.

## Background & Description

The Characidae are a large family of primitive teleosts from African and New World freshwaters. [[Eigenmann]] and Myers (1929) separated a subfamily, Glandulocaudinae, from the Characinae on the basis of marked male sexual dimorphism. Dimorphism appears in the opercle, scales, fins, lower caudal fulcra and a glandular pouch with modified scales on the caudal fin of males.

The work is organized around these main subjects:

- **Materials and methods:** Aquarium stocks were kept at 18–25 °C with pH mostly 6.0–7.0. Behavior was timed with a tape recorder and later an Esterline-Angus 20-channel recorder. Preserved and cleared-and-stained specimens of 17 genera were examined.
- **Taxonomy:** The author argues for retaining [[Coelurichthys]] as distinct from [[Mimagoniates]] and [[Glandulocauda]]. The three Brazilian forms are treated as *Glandulocauda inequalis*, *Coelurichthys microlepis* and *Coelurichthys tenuis*. Table 1 compares caudal glandular characters across genera.
- **Behavior:** Non-social locomotion, feeding, comfort movements and spawning are described. A detailed ethogram of social acts follows, including Approaching, Chasing, Biting, Lateral Display, Zigzagging, Hovering, Wobbling, Ovalling, Dusting, Pairing and Rolling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat, predation and food.** In Trinidad, *Corynopoma* was one of the three most common species. It was present in 178 of 284 collections and was nearly ubiquitous within the habitats sampled. It was absent from valleys steeper than about 50 ft/mile and from habitats likely to turn brackish in the dry season. Experiments showed that predators caught *Corynopoma* more readily than other prey species. Aquarium observations suggested that the fish eat floating and low-flying insects and small crustaceans. Water temperature in Trinidad probably did not fall below 20 °C, and both 23 °C and 33 °C were recorded with *Corynopoma* present.

**Distribution.** *Corynopoma* is treated as a monotypic genus within the Orinoco system, including Trinidad. In southeastern Brazil, *Coelurichthys tenuis* occupies quiet, clear, acid, brown-water coastal streams. *C. microlepis* occurs in larger, clear-water streams. *Glandulocauda inequalis* replaces it farther south. Several genera have disjunct distributions, such as [[Hysteronotus]] in Peru and the São Francisco system, which the author interprets as relict populations.

**Breeding season.** In aquaria, *Corynopoma* courted and spawned throughout the year. Field data suggest that spawning in most species occurs at the start of the rainy season, when flooded areas become available for rearing young. The subtropical *Pseudocorynopoma doriae* showed courtship mainly in spring.

## Key Findings

1. **Sexual dimorphism:** Males of the group show extreme dimorphism, with a caudal gland in all forms. In *Corynopoma riisei* the opercular paddle and spur formed by lower caudal fulcra are the most striking features.
2. **Monophyly:** The glandulocaudine genera share glandular areas at the base of the tail together with features of surface life. The author concludes they have a common ancestor that was internally fertilizing, surface-feeding and close to *Bryconamericus*.
3. **Internal fertilization:** Internal fertilization is inferred for *Corynopoma*, *Glandulocauda* and *Coelurichthys*. It is not confirmed for *Pseudocorynopoma*, where Rolling was never seen and spawning was not observed in the study.
4. **Mating and spawning are temporally separated:** Males court continuously across the year, while females spawn only at the start of the rainy season. Females are generally unresponsive, and male courtship must compensate.
5. **Caudal gland function:** The author proposes that the caudal gland secretes a water-borne substance directed toward the female by accessory tubes, pouches and courtship movements such as Dusting in *C. tenuis* and figure-of-eight Chasing in *Corynopoma*. This remains a hypothesis.
6. **Surface feeding and mating:** Nipping Surface is more frequent in the glandulocaudines than in generalized characids. Pairing above the water is interpreted as the route by which internal fertilization evolved.
7. **Aggregation and dominance:** Aggregation occurred in all species, often without external stimuli. Dominance was closely correlated with size. *C. microlepis* showed strong individual recognition and male mate constancy, whereas *Corynopoma* showed weaker recognition and unstable dominance.
8. **Courtship patterns:** *Corynopoma* courtship sequences had relatively stationary probabilities. *C. tenuis* and *Coelurichthys* showed sequences that were shorter at first and lengthened as dominance was established. *Pseudocorynopoma doriae* showed a relaxation-oscillation pattern of Wobbling.
9. **Taxonomic conclusions:** The author retains *Glandulocauda inequalis* and *Coelurichthys microlepis* and *C. tenuis* as distinct species, and argues against lumping them into [[Mimagoniates]]. The status of *C. lateralis* remains unresolved.
10. **Evolutionary hypothesis:** Adaptive radiation after internal fertilization produced a flowering of sexual dimorphism, including caudal glands, hooks, opercular extensions and croaking in *Glandulocauda*. The author describes this as possibly unrivalled in ingenuity among teleosts of similar age.
