# Collecting Fishes in an Urban Environment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #urban-ecology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation from the newsletter *American Currents* (NANFA). It has three parts: a short opening passage on aquarium husbandry, a reference list of 21+ mostly North American fish studies, and a feature article by Jim Pitts on collecting native fishes in urban Louisville, Kentucky. Pitts argues that urban native-fish collecting remains possible despite heavy industrial and residential pollution, and that urban fish assemblages are much less diverse than those of clean streams nearby. The issue closes with NANFA directory and staffing notices.

## Background & Description

**Aquarium husbandry (opening passage):** The text challenges the common assumption that captive fish must be kept under conditions identical to their native habitat. Experienced aquarists find that marine fishes do better when aquarium [[Salinity]] is kept somewhat below that of the coral reef. Freshwater fish collected from acidic water may simply tolerate low pH better than their competitors, and they may do better in neutral water if no other stressor is present. The author recommends more attention to biological needs and less reliance on physical parameters.

**Reference list:** Approximately 21 entries (the list is cut off at item 21 and continues on page 37). Topics include [[Acclimation]] rates of juvenile channel catfish to high temperature (Allen and Strawn 1971), microhabitat physical parameters for brown trout in a flume, longear sunfish repopulation after stream decimation, cyprinid life history in North Carolina, distribution of fishes in the Moapa (Muddy) River, Nevada, and fish respiration in an anaerobic Florida spring. Standard regional works include Eddy's *How to Know the Freshwater Fishes*, Pflieger's *The Fishes of Missouri*, Carlander's *Handbook of Freshwater Fishery Biology*, and Bailey et al.'s list of common and scientific names of North American fishes.

**Urban collecting article (Jim Pitts):** The Louisville metropolitan area has a population of roughly 800,000, with industrial plants including General Electric, Ford, DuPont, Union Carbide, and Phillip Morris. Treatment plants are described as insufficient to prevent damage to local streams. The article focuses on species with high tolerance for siltation and chemical pollutants.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**The Ohio River:** The [[Ohio River]] forms the northern and western borders of Louisville. It flows at up to half a million cubic feet per second and is pooled along most of its 981-mile course by dams. The McAlpine Dam sits above the [[Falls of the Ohio]], the site where [[Rafinesque]] described many of the 113 fishes in his *Ichthyologia Ohiensis* (1819). Today perhaps 130 fish species still occur along the river, though many are rare. Species collected by seining below the dam include gizzard shad, emerald shiner, American eel, channel catfish, blackstripe topminnow, bluegill, and freshwater drum. Hook-and-line catches listed as likely include longnose gar, carp, flathead catfish, black bullhead, white crappie, sauger, and walleye.

**Floodplain swamp:** A small swamp survives beneath expressway pillars about five minutes from downtown. It yielded the grass pickerel (*Esox americanus vermiculatus*), the subject of an earlier Pitts article, plus yellow bullhead, creek chub, steelcolor shiner, gizzard shad, and [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish).

**Beargrass Creek:** The creek has absorbed about 250 years of urban development. Its stretch through [[Cherokee Park]] is partly channelized but avoids concrete lining. Historically it held clean-water species such as the least darter (*Etheostoma microperca*) and banded sculpin (*Cottus carolinae*), both now gone. Pitts collected 10 species there, dominated by pollution-tolerant forms: goldfish, stoneroller, common shiner, bluntnose minnow, creek chub, smallmouth bass, largemouth bass, longear sunfish, and fantail darter. Longnose gar enter periodically from the Ohio.

**Suburban ditches:** Intermittent ditches often hold large numbers of mosquitofish, which are sometimes deliberately introduced to control mosquito larvae. A mottled variety, *Gambusia affinis holbrooki*, occurs in some southern localities. Brown bullhead and fantail darter were also noted in ditch habitats.

**Diversity loss:** Pitts's casual list of easily obtained urban fishes contains **26 species**. Streams just outside the city's waste influence host many more, including rainbow darter (large numbers), greenside darter, orangethroat darter, johnny darter, logperch, rosefin shiner, bigeye shiner, mimic shiner, silverjaw minnow, banded sculpin, brook silverside, and orangespotted sunfish. Within Louisville, only one darter, the fantail darter (*Etheostoma flabellare*), was found, and it appears to replace other darters because it tolerates siltation. Several shiners are absent from the city altogether.

## Key Findings

- **Aquarium principle:** Native habitat conditions are not always the ideal captive conditions. Marine fish may do better at slightly lower salinity than reef water, and acid-water fish may thrive in neutral water if biological needs are met.
- **Urban fish persistence:** Native fish collecting is still viable in Louisville. The species that persist are mostly tolerant of siltation and chemical pollution.
- **Loss of sensitive taxa:** Least darter and banded sculpin have disappeared from Beargrass Creek. Pollution-tolerant species such as goldfish and creek chub have replaced them.
- **Reduced diversity:** The urban list of 26 species is much smaller than the assemblages of nearby unpolluted streams. Only one darter occurs in the city, against several in clean riffles.
- **Biological monitoring:** Pitts recommends that local native-fish enthusiasts survey their own streams and record the ecological factors that shape urban fish communities.
- **Community notices:** NANFA members are invited to become slide-program speakers (Pitts's program covers about 65 species in 20 families and has no fee), to report on the Southeastern and Desert Fishes Councils, to join the *American Currents* staff, and to contribute "favorite native fish" articles.
