# Variability in the Macrofauna of a Single Riffle in Prosser Creek, California, as Indicated by the Surber Sampler

#entomology #freshwater-ecology #benthic-macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1956 study in *Hilgardia* (Needham and Usinger, University of California) measured the normal variability of benthic macroinvertebrates in one uniform riffle on [[Prosser Creek]], Nevada County, California. One hundred bottom samples were collected with the [[Surber Sampler]] in a [[Latin Square Design]] on July 1–2, 1953. The authors found that purely quantitative sampling is impractical: about 194 samples would be needed for significant total wet weight and 73 for total numbers at the 95 per cent confidence level. In contrast, only 2–3 samples were needed to capture at least one representative of the commonest insect groups. The authors therefore recommend [[Qualitative Sampling]] for [[Stream Pollution Monitoring]]. Organism distribution tracked depth and current speed strongly, but not bottom type.

## Background & Description

**Rationale.** The authors had designed a periodic macrofauna sampling program for the California State Water Pollution Control Board (1954). A literature review showed that no thorough study had quantified the normal variability within a single riffle. The Surber sampler was chosen because it is the most widely used quantitative stream sampler, although its statistical reliability had not been tested. It is used for estimating stream productivity for game-fish stocking, for studying trout food supply, and for detecting pollution.

**Sampler limitations.** The Surber sampler can only be used in running water and at depths within arm's reach. Organisms can escape in swift water, and rocks are disturbed while the frame is set. Alternatives mentioned include the [[Square Foot Box Sampler]], the [[Circular Square Foot Sampler]], the [[Drag-Type Riffle Sampler]], the [[Ekman Dredge]] and [[Peterson Dredge]], and the [[Basket Tray Sampler]].

**Study site.** The riffle was about 30 feet wide, with a test area of 100 feet of stream, about one-eighth mile upstream of the State Route 89 bridge near Truckee. The bottom was mostly small gravel and rubble, with fine gravel and sand, and no large rubble.

**Design.** A 10 × 10 grid of columns and rows was laid out. Five men sampled so that no one collected more than twice in any row or column. Sampling began at the downstream end, and each sampler took 10 samples per day over two days.

**Laboratory methods.** Samples were preserved in 70 per cent alcohol, then picked with forceps, weighed (wet weight after blotting and 1 minute of drying), and sorted to genus for insects. Each genus was placed in its own vial.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions.** Air temperature was 66–79 °F and water temperature 48–58 °F. Ph was 7.1 throughout. The riffle was fully sunlit with no bank vegetation, so shade was not a factor. Snowmelt at the head of the basin raised afternoon flows. Stream width increased 9–15 inches between morning and afternoon at rows 10 and 5.

**Depth and velocity.** On the gently sloping south bank (column 1), depths averaged 3.0 inches (range 2.5–8.0 inches). The maximum depth recorded was 17.0 inches. Current speed measured with a [[Gurley Current Meter]] ranged from 0.7 ft/s at the riffle edge to 5.27 ft/s in column 6. A float timing gave 5.5 ft/s in the morning and 5.3 ft/s in the afternoon.

**Cross-channel pattern.** Depth and velocity were lowest in column 1, rose gradually to a peak in columns 7–8, and fell sharply by column 10. The steep north bank dropped quickly to the deepest, fastest water.

**Known distribution theory.** The authors cited [[Needham 1928]], who found an 11.9 per cent weight decrease in bottom food from shoreline to midchannel in streams over 18 feet wide. [[Leger 1910]] reported a 50 per cent decrease in food organisms from shore to midchannel in southern French streams.

## Key Findings

**Sample-size requirements for quantitative data**
- Total wet weight required 194 samples at the 95 per cent confidence level.
- Total numbers required 73 samples.
- Coefficients of variation were 0.78 for weight and 0.56 for numbers, so weight was more variable.
- The authors concluded that routine quantitative sampling for weight and numbers is impractical.

**Raw sample statistics**
- Wet weights ranged from 0.015 g (sample 8) to 2.31 g (sample 95), averaging 0.575 g.
- Total numbers per sample ranged from 2 (sample 8) to 198 (sample 44), averaging 75.7.

**Sample-size requirements for group presence**
- The commonest genera from [[Plecoptera]], [[Ephemeroptera]], [[Trichoptera]], and [[Diptera]] were each represented in 2–3 samples at the 95 per cent level.
- At the ordinal level, 1–2 samples were enough.
- The authors used [[Poisson Distribution]] for [[Alloperla]] (mean 1.54 per sample; n = 2) and [[Binomial Distribution]] for [[Brachycentrus]] (n = 4 calculated).
- Caseless (free-living) trichopteran larvae needed 6–13 samples, while case-bearing forms needed 2.

**Observer bias**
- Four of five samplers gave statistically consistent results.
- The fifth sampler consistently covered the equivalent of 1.57 times the area, apparently by including rocks outside the frame. A correction factor was applied.
- The authors concluded that trained personnel can be substituted for one another, but training is essential.

**Spatial effects**
- Row effects were not significant.
- Column effects were highly significant, even after accounting for depth and velocity.
- No correlation was found between organism type and bottom type, which the authors attributed partly to the riffle's uniformity.

**Organism–habitat correlations**
- [[Rhithrogena]] preferred the deepest, fastest water, consistent with its streamlined body.
- Alloperla, Isoperla, Cinygmula, Ephemerella, Hydropsyche, Rhyacophila, Brachycentrus, and Lepidostoma were more abundant in shallower, slower water in columns 1–5 than in the swift columns 6–7.
- Most aquatic insect genera preferred shallower, slower water. Other genera showed no consistent trend.
- [[Glossosoma]] was also an exception to the shallow-water preference.

**Practical recommendations**
- Existing stream productivity data should be interpreted cautiously.
- Two to three square-foot samples are enough to capture the main taxa, which is what matters for pollution surveys because taxonomic spectrum is more informative than total weight or number ([[Patrick 1949]]).
- Qualitative sampling is recommended for pollution studies because it saves time and labor.
