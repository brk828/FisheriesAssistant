# Rusty Crayfish Invasion and Solar-Powered Artificial Reefs

#decapoda #cambaridae #marine-biology

## Executive Summary

This short magazine feature, published in *Audubon* (March–April, year garbled in scan), covers two aquatic conservation problems. The first is the spread of the invasive [[Rusty Crayfish]] (*Orconectes rusticus*) across North American lakes and streams, and the difficulty of controlling it. The second is a solar-powered technique for growing artificial coral reefs in Jamaica. Both are described through quotes from researchers and reef restoration practitioners. The article contains no formal study design; its figures come from field surveys and project reports as relayed by the interviewees.

## Background & Description

- **Rusty Crayfish (*Orconectes rusticus*):** Once native to a limited range, this species has spread widely through the upper Midwest and beyond. Its aggressive behavior and tolerance of disturbance make it a dominant invader.
- **Virile Crayfish (*Orconectes virilis*):** Has a large natural range and is not considered to be in immediate danger, in contrast to the rusty crayfish's impact on native species.
- **Shoup's Crayfish (*Orconectes shoupi*):** A small-range species in Tennessee that is at risk if the rusty crayfish spreads into its habitat. A Kentucky *Orconectes* species is also named but the name is illegible in the scan.
- **Coral Reef Systems:** Reefs and the marine life they support have been damaged by sewage runoff from coastal tourism development. The article also notes reef fish such as surgeonfish and squirrelfish present at the restoration site.
- **Key Sources Quoted:** [[David M. Lodge]] (University of Notre Dame, expert on crayfish), [[Jeff Gunderson]] (Minnesota Sea Grant), [[Thomas Goreau]] and [[Wolf Hilbertz]] (Global Coral Reef Alliance, Chappaqua, New York), and [[Harold Hudson]] (Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Crayfish invasion.** The rusty crayfish is aggressive and disturbs its surroundings. Densities can reach 20 adults per square meter. Lodge warned that if it moves into regions holding small populations of rare endemic crayfish, it "could cause global extinctions" of some of them. The article also notes spread into other Midwestern states, the Northeast, the Southeast, the Southwest, and West Virginia.

**Management constraints.** No pesticides selectively target the rusty crayfish. Trapping, which Gunderson described as "Catch-22," may spread the species further if commercial harvest is encouraged, since it could keep the crayfish showing up in new lakes. Illinois, Minnesota, and Wisconsin have tightened rules on using live crayfish as bait, which the scientists welcomed. Controlling established populations is harder. One proposed option is to increase predatory game fish by tightening size restrictions and lowering bag limits.

**Reef restoration.** Solar-powered reef technology uses solar panels to generate a mild electrical current through metal structures in seawater, which promotes mineral growth (calcareous algae, deposited minerals) that binds and strengthens the framework. Goreau notes the voltage is just six volts, so a swimmer touching the wires would barely feel the current. The work has the approval of Jamaica's Natural Resources Conservation Authority, the equivalent of the US Environmental Protection Agency. The reef structures also trap sand and sediment, helping to build up eroding beaches that have lost mangrove forests. The technique is still being evaluated for long-term success.

## Key Findings

- **Rusty crayfish spread:** Lodge surveyed 150 lakes and streams in northern Wisconsin and Upper Michigan that had been invaded by rusties. More than three-quarters of them had no native crayfish left.
- **Extinction risk:** The rusty crayfish's expansion threatens small populations of other crayfish, such as *Orconectes shoupi* in Tennessee, potentially causing global extinctions of some species.
- **Bait regulation:** Illinois, Minnesota, and Wisconsin have tightened rules on the use of live crayfish as bait, which is seen as the most practical immediate measure.
- **No selective control:** No pesticides target only the rusty crayfish, and trapping alone is considered inadequate.
- **Solar reef results:** Goreau and Hilbertz have built four "solar-powered reefs" in Jamaica. Transplanted coral has grown well, and the structures help build beaches and reduce erosion.
- **Caveat:** Hudson noted that a natural reef takes thousands of years to build, so the long-term success of the artificial technique depends on whether it can create large, structurally stable reefs.
