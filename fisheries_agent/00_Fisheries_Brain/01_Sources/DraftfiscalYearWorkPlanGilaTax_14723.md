# Gila Taxonomy Project: FY 1991 Work Plan and Solicitation Materials

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of project materials for the [[Gila Taxonomy Project]], a multiphase effort to resolve the taxonomy and distribution of endangered chubs of the genus *Gila* in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It includes a cover letter (27 May 1990) from Project Manager Wayne C. Starnes, a draft FY 1991 Work Plan (3 May 1990), and Sections C and M of a Bureau of Reclamation request for proposals (RFP). The project operates under a cooperative agreement between the [[U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service]] (Region 6) and the [[Smithsonian Institution]], with funding from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], USFWS, and Utah Division of Wildlife. Taxonomic uncertainty has hampered recovery planning for endangered forms, so the project aims to develop non-lethal sampling methods and document morphological and genetic variation across the basin.

## Background & Description

The genus *Gila* in the Colorado Basin is a cyprinid group that includes several endangered forms. Recovery efforts were complicated by unresolved taxonomy. Management had assumed three chub species (humpback, bonytail, and roundtail), but mainstem populations show variable morphology that does not fit those concepts. Open questions include whether this variation is natural infraspecific variation or the result of [[Hybridization]] and introgression, and what the status is of various tributary chub populations.

The project is split into phases:

- **Phase I (began July 1989):** Compiled existing taxonomic and distribution information, identified priority research needs, and produced a research plan and sampling strategy. Completed by early March 1990.
- **Phase II (research phase, about 2.5 to 3 years, plus 6 months for analysis and reporting):** Contracts researchers for morphological and genetic work, develops [[Non-Lethal Sampling]] procedures, and conducts field collections.

Research is organized into four schedules, and proposers may bid on any or all:

1. **Morphological investigations** (imagery techniques where mortality must be minimized)
2. **[[Protein Electrophoresis]]** (allozymes and isozymes; 30 to 50 loci anticipated)
3. **[[DNA Analysis]]** (range-wide study, including mitochondrial DNA)
4. **[[Chromosomal Analysis]]** (banding techniques requiring few cells)

Larval taxonomy ([[Larval Taxonomy]]) will be started one to two years into the project, under a separate RFP, once adult genetic data are sufficient.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Study areas span the Green and Gila river systems and disjunct pluvial tributaries throughout the Colorado Basin, plus possible drainages in northwestern Mexico with *Gila robusta*-like forms. Key reaches named in the plan include:

- **Upper basin large rivers:** [[Green River]] (including Cataract, Desolation, and Gray canyons), [[Yampa River]] (Yampa Canyon, Cross Mountain, Echo Park), Colorado River (Westwater Canyon, Palisades-Black Rocks, Debeque Canyon)
- **[[Grand Canyon]] and Little Colorado River**, with integration into Glen Canyon studies where possible
- **Smaller tributaries:** Little Snake, White, Dolores, Gunnison, San Juan, and several western Utah tributaries, plus headwater localities above Flaming Gorge and Cross Mountain
- **Lower basin:** 30 to 40 varied localities, with timing left to investigators

Humpback chub is the species specifically noted for sampling in upper-basin reaches. Captive stock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (e.g., Pahranagat roundtail and bonytail) is also to be sampled for the study.

Because all target forms are endangered, collection requires permits from federal and state agencies. Sampling protocols emphasize non-lethal tissue collection, and experiments at Ouray National Wildlife Refuge and Jones Hole National Hatchery (Utah) are used to test methods on hatchery-reared and some wild-caught fish. USFWS will supply ultracold freezers at strategic basin locations for temporary tissue storage.

## Key Findings

**Objectives (FY 1991)**
- Refine collection protocols and a specimen/tissue cross-referencing system.
- Sample selected large upper-basin rivers and the Grand Canyon–Little Colorado area using non-lethal methods.
- Sample selected smaller tributaries across the basin.
- Begin genetic and morphological research on new, previously collected, and hatchery material.
- Exchange preliminary results to inform FY 1992 sampling and decide whether to start larval studies.

**Project objectives (Section C.2)**
1. Develop non-lethal techniques for morphological and genetic data.
2. Document morphological and genetic variation within and among chub populations across the basin (and possibly Mexican drainages).
3. Determine whether mainstem canyon-reach populations comprise two or more taxa, and whether introgressive hybridization occurs.
4. Determine the taxonomic status of confluent and isolated tributary populations.
5. Improve knowledge of larval *Gila* taxonomy.
6. Support future genetic monitoring through non-lethal methods and a knowledge of genetic variability.

**Key schedule and deliverables**
- Finalized sampling protocol and cross-referencing procedure: 10 Oct. 1990 (Project Manager and Harold Tyus, USFWS)
- Fall field sampling (Oct. to early Nov. 1990): Cataract Canyon (7 days), Westwater Canyon (3 days), Palisades-Black Rocks (5 days)
- Spring-summer 1991 large-river sampling (April to June, about 45 days estimated), with additional upper-basin and Grand Canyon work
- Mid-year progress report: 1 April 1991
- FY 92 Scope of Work: 1 May 1991; first draft of FY 92 Work Plan: 10 May 1991
- Review Panel and researcher meeting: early June 1991
- Revised FY 92 Work Plan: about 30 June 1991
- Year-end progress report: 30 Sept. 1991

**Budget**
- FY 1991 overall budget: **$252,000**
  - Sampling: $59,000
  - Transfer of hatchery material to investigators: $500
  - Investigator contract contributions: $127,500 (added to $154,000 contributed in FY 90)
  - Smithsonian cooperative agreement (includes Review Panel travel): $65,000
- FY 1992 projection: **$252,000**
  - Sampling: $25,000
  - Investigator contracts (excluding larval studies): $127,000
  - Possible larval-juvenile researchers: $35,000
  - Smithsonian cooperative agreement: $65,000
- Individual sampling trip estimates range from about $500 to $9,000. Lower-basin costs are largely covered by investigators' field budgets.

**Proposal evaluation (Section M)**
- Technical factors: 70% of points (140 of 200)
  - Respondent qualifications (100 points): prior fish studies, cyprinid and *Gila* experience, data analysis, timely reporting, publication record, and field competence
  - Physical and technical resources (40 points): laboratory facilities and equipment (30); existing *Gila* samples or tissues (10)
- Cost/price factors: 30% of points (60 of 200), scored relative to the lowest offered price

**Participation requirements**
- Researchers must share and cross-reference material and exchange information freely. Failure to do so may result in dismissal from the project.
- Researchers must supply their own tributary-sampling equipment and laboratory equipment. Agency biologists supply boats and major collecting gear for large rivers.
- Researchers need not attend every trip once protocols are in place, but must budget travel for field involvement.
