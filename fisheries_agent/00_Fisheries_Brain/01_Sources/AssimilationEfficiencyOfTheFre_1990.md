# Assimilation Efficiency of the Freshwater Shrimp *Atyaephyra desmaresti* Fed *Microcystis aeruginosa*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Atyidae #Cyanobacteria #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1990 study by Maria Leonor Fidalgo (Instituto de Zoologia «Dr. Augusto Nobre», Universidade do Porto) quantifies the assimilation efficiency of the freshwater shrimp **[[Atyaephyra desmaresti]]** when fed exclusively on the toxic bloom-forming cyanobacterium **[[Microcystis aeruginosa]]** (strain NIVA-CyA 43). Using the Conover ash ratio method, the shrimp assimilated the cyanobacterium at a very high efficiency (~98.9%), comparable to assimilation of a green alga (*Scenedesmus* sp.) and higher than a commercial fish food (tetramin). Despite this high assimilation efficiency, prior survivorship data (Fidalgo, 1989) showed that shrimp fed *M. aeruginosa* alone survived no better than starved animals, indicating that high assimilation does not equate to nutritional adequacy or ecological suitability. The paper is significant for aquatic food-web studies because *A. desmaresti* is described as "an important fish food in many aquatic ecosystems," linking cyanobacterial bloom dynamics to zooplankton and, indirectly, to fish prey availability.

## Background & Description
- **Study organism (consumer):** *Atyaephyra desmaresti* Millet — a decapod shrimp (Natantia), collected across multiple size classes including males, females, and juveniles from the Crestuma/Lever reservoir (River [[Douro]], northern Portugal).
- **Food source (cyanobacterium):** *Microcystis aeruginosa* Kütz., strain NIVA-CyA 43 (non-toxic strain used in these specific assimilation trials, as contrasted with the toxic NIVA-CyA 57 strain referenced from companion work).
  - Growth form: single or paired spherical cells.
  - Cell diameter: ≈ 5 µm.
  - Cultured in chemostats (250 ml) using Z8 medium (Staus, 1961; modified by NIVA 1972/1976) at 20°C ± 1°C under a fixed photoperiod (6 a.m.–8 p.m.).
- No morphological description of the shrimp's digestive or feeding apparatus is given beyond its role as a filter/grazer capable of ingesting harvested algal cells retained on glass fiber filters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Trophic role:** *A. desmaresti* functions as a primary/secondary consumer grazing on phytoplankton, including toxic and non-toxic cyanobacteria, and serves as a critical prey item ("important fish food") within freshwater food webs — linking cyanobacterial bloom biomass to higher trophic levels via zooplankton/shrimp intermediaries.
- **Habitat context:** Collected from the Crestuma/Lever reservoir on the River Douro, a system subject to recorded *M. aeruginosa* blooms — indicative of eutrophic conditions.
- **Bloom dynamics:** The study is framed within the broader issue of increasing **[[Cyanobacterial Blooms]]** driven by eutrophication, and the variable/controversial effects of cyanobacteria on zooplankton grazers depending on species and strain toxicity (citing [[Lampert 1981]]; [[Infante & Riehl 1984]]).
- **Synergistic mortality hypotheses:** Referenced literature (Ayles et al., 1976; Calò et al., 1987) suggests that fish mortalities associated with algal blooms may result from synergistic effects of algal toxins, ammonia, temperature, and low oxygen rather than a single causal toxin — relevant to interpreting bloom impacts on fish populations such as rainbow trout ([[Salmo gairdneri]]) in eutrophic lakes.
- **Feeding ecology caveat:** In nature, grazers are never exposed to monocultures of cyanobacteria; the paper stresses that lab-based single-food assimilation experiments may not realistically predict field impacts of blooms on zooplankton or their consumers.

## Key Findings
- **Method:** Conover ash ratio method (Conover, 1966) applied to shrimp fed only *M. aeruginosa*; animals starved 24 h pre-trial to clear gut contents before feces analysis.
- **Formula used:**
  U′ = [(F′ − E′) / ((1 − E′) F′)] × 100
  where U′ = % assimilation, F′ = ash-free dry weight of ingested food, E′ = organic matter in egested feces.
- **Quantitative results (n = 20 determinations, 20°C ± 1°C):**
  - F′ (organic content of ingested food): 96.08% ± 2.58 (n = 27)
  - E′ (organic content of feces): 21.02% ± 5.59 (n = 20)
  - U′ (% assimilation efficiency): 98.89% ± 0.37 (n = 20)
- **Comparative assimilation efficiencies (from Fidalgo, 1985, cited here):**
  - *M. aeruginosa*: 98.89% ± 0.37
  - *Scenedesmus* sp. (green alga): 98.13% ± 1.07 — statistically comparable
  - Tetramin (commercial fish food): 92.28% ± 3.31 — somewhat lower
- **Disconnect between assimilation and fitness:** Despite very high assimilation of *M. aeruginosa*, survivorship of shrimp fed this alga exclusively was ~86%, statistically indistinguishable from starving controls (Fidalgo, 1989) — showing high digestibility does not guarantee nutritional adequacy for growth/reproduction.
- **Literature parallel:** Arnold (1971) and Lampert (1977a), cited in Holm et al. (1983), similarly found high assimilation but poor reproduction/survival in *Daphnia* fed certain cyanobacteria — reinforcing that assimilation efficiency alone is an incomplete proxy for food quality.
- **Toxin production caveat:** Berg & Soli (1985) suggest lab-cultured cyanobacteria may underproduce toxins relative to natural bloom populations, complicating extrapolation of lab results to field bloom risk.
- **Conclusion/Recommendation:** The author concludes *M. aeruginosa* is well-assimilated and could be considered a "suitable" food item nutritionally, but stresses that reproduction/growth data are needed, and future experiments should test cyanobacteria alongside alternate food sources to better reflect natural grazing conditions and realistically assess bloom risk to zooplankton and dependent fish populations.
