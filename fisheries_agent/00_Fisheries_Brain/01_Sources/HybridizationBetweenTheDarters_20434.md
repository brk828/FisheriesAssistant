# Hybridization Between *Percina crassa roanoka* and *Percina oxyrhyncha* in New River, Virginia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

Hocutt and Hambrick (1973) describe a natural hybrid between the [[Piedmont Darter]] (*Percina crassa roanoka*) and the [[Sharpnose Darter]] (*Percina oxyrhyncha*) from the upper [[Kanawha River]] ([[New River]]) system in Virginia. The single hybrid specimen was collected in 1971 at Radford. Its meristic counts were intermediate or closer to *P. c. roanoka*, and its morphometrics were intermediate or similar to *P. oxyrhyncha*. The authors argue that *P. c. roanoka* is not native to New River but was introduced recently, probably from the [[Roanoke River]] drainage, and that it behaves as an [[Opportunistic Species]]. The paper also maps the species' spread within New River and its tributaries.

## Background & Description

Hybrids were already documented across several darter genera, including *Percina caprodes* × *P. maculata* (Trautman, 1948), *P. caprodes* × *P. sciera* (Hubbs and Laritz, 1961a), and *P. notogramma* × *P. peltata* (Loos and Woolcott, 1969). An intergeneric hybrid, *Etheostoma spectabile* × *Percina sciera*, was reported by Hubbs and Laritz (1961b). Branson and Campbell (1969) reported the first case of massive hybridization in darters, between *Etheostoma radiosum cyanorum* and *E. spectabile*.

*Percina oxyrhyncha* (originally described as *Hadropterus oxyrhynchus* by Hubbs and Raney, 1939) is characteristic of the upper Kanawha (New) River system. *P. c. roanoka*, described by Jordan and Jenkins, was first collected in the New River drainage in 1963 by R. D. Ross near the Giles-Montgomery County line, Virginia. Its origin is uncertain. It may be native and previously overlooked, introduced by people, or a re-colonizing population after ancestral extirpation (Jenkins et al., 1972).

Habitat notes: *P. oxyrhyncha* occupies sandy riffles under large stones and boulders, and vegetated, swift water. *P. c. roanoka* prefers similar habitat in the Roanoke and New rivers. The hybrid was taken from a riffle-run with bedrock, boulders and rubble, with sand in shoreline eddies. Depths ranged from 4 inches in shallow riffles to 3.5 ft in pools, and the most heavily fished run was 2 ft deep. The shores were well shaded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and collection.** The hybrid and *P. c. roanoka* specimens were taken on 21 June 1971 from a riffle-run about 100 yards across New River at the Radford sewage treatment facility, 1/3 mile downstream of the Rt. 11 bridge in [[Radford, Virginia]]. Collection used seine and [[Rotenone Sampling]]. Specimens were deposited in the Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University (VPI) fish museum.

**Hybridization drivers.** The authors attribute hybridization to overlap in habitat preference (submergent vegetation, rubble, fast flow), overlapping spawning time, and behavior. They note that [[Introgression]] is unusual and generally requires extreme habitat alteration, and that genetic factors further reduce the chance of introgressed populations forming. The single hybrid nonetheless shows that *P. c. roanoka* uses *P. oxyrhyncha* habitat to some degree.

**Range and dispersal.** The authors describe *P. c. roanoka* as having extended its range in New River since 1963:
- 1963: first record (Ross; VPI 1395).
- 1968: juvenile and adult specimens collected by University of Richmond personnel from riffles and pools of the lowermost Spruce Run and adjacent New River over rubble.
- 1970: a single specimen (VPI 2435) from the mouth of the [[Greenbrier River]], the farthest downstream record.
- 1971: specimens from the mouth of East River at [[Glen Lyn]], Virginia (VPI 2040).
- 1971–1972: VPI collections showed the darter is more concentrated in the main channel near Radford than 30 miles downstream at Glen Lyn.
- No specimens have been recorded upstream of [[Claytor Lake Dam]], about 5 river miles above Radford, which acts as a physical barrier.

**Sympatric species.** Associates in New River include *Nocomis platyrhynchus*, *Campostoma anomalum*, *Hypentilium nigricans*, *Cottus carolinae* and *Etheostoma blennioides*. Common associates of *P. oxyrhyncha* in the Cheat River include *Rhinichthys cataractae*, *R. atratulus obtusus*, *Nocomis micropogon* and *H. nigricans*.

**Faunal context.** The New River fauna is depauperate, partly because the 24-ft [[Kanawha Falls]] blocks upstream fish dispersal (Jenkins et al., 1972). Similarities between the Tennessee and Roanoke drainages suggest New River was a major dispersal route for ancestral stocks now extirpated from its waters. Possible causes include climatic change and interspecific competition.

**Threat to *P. oxyrhyncha*.** *P. oxyrhyncha* has a wide but localized distribution in New River (Radford to Kanawha Falls) and in the tributaries Little River, Big Reed Island Creek, Cripple Creek, Reed Creek and Big Fox Creek. Changing water quality and further impoundment may lead to its extirpation from New River and some tributaries.

**Introduction hypothesis.** The authors propose that *P. c. roanoka* reached New River in minnow buckets. Darters were probably caught incidentally by bass fishermen searching for [[Madtoms]] (*Noturus* spp.), a common bait that lives in fast water over rubble, the same habitat the piedmont darter prefers. Bright spawning coloration may have made darters more likely to be kept as bait.

**Ecological theory.** Following [[MacArthur'S Opportunistic Species Concept]] (MacArthur, 1960), the authors argue that extirpations reduce diversity and interspecific interactions, which favors invasion. Stable, fully exploited communities resist invasion, but invasion can occur when geographic barriers are breached. They cite [[Mohave Chub]] (*Gila mohavensis*) as an example of a native fish endangered by competition and hybridization after a related chub (*G. orcutti*) was introduced to the Mohave River, California (Miller, 1961).

## Key Findings

1. **Hybrid identity.** The hybrid *P. c. roanoka* × *P. oxyrhyncha* is documented from one specimen (VPI 2610, 61 mm SL, male) collected at Radford in 1971.
2. **Meristic intermediacy.** Scale counts above the lateral line (4–5 in *P. c. roanoka*, 9–10 in *P. oxyrhyncha*), along the lateral line (44–52 vs. 73–80) and below it (6–7 vs. 14–16) were intermediate. The hybrid had 58 lateral-line scales. Dorsal spine counts were also intermediate. Vertebral count (40) and dorsal soft ray counts indicated more influence from *P. c. roanoka*.
3. **Meristic index.** Loos and Woolcott's (1969) index gave 30.9 for *P. c. roanoka*, 54.0 for the hybrid and 95.9 for *P. oxyrhyncha*. The hybrid is therefore intermediate.
4. **Morphometric patterns.** Standard length/head length and head length/snout length were intermediate. Head length/eye length and snout length/eye length resembled *P. oxyrhyncha*. Head length/upper jaw length exceeded the range of both parents, which the authors attribute to luxuriance or complementary gene action (Minckley and Krumholz, 1960).
5. **Qualitative traits.** The hybrid resembled *P. oxyrhyncha* in its pointed head and horizontal mouth. It shared pigmentation and squamation traits with *P. c. roanoka*, such as lateral blotches, caudal spots, a subocular bar, and a naked opercle and nape. Its cheeks were asymmetric, with the right partly scaled and the left naked, whereas *P. oxyrhyncha* cheeks are scaled and *P. c. roanoka* cheeks are naked. Caducous midventral belly scales were absent from the hybrid.
6. **Introduction thesis.** *P. c. roanoka* is considered introduced to New River, most likely from the Roanoke River drainage, based on the meristic similarity of Roanoke specimens and the unlikelihood that repeated surveys missed it.
7. **Taxonomic status.** The authors doubt that *P. c. roanoka* is a subspecies of the southern *P. c. crassa* and suggest species rank should be recognized.
8. **Invasion behavior.** *P. c. roanoka* exhibits traits of an opportunistic species and has expanded its range in New River since 1963, but its distribution is limited upstream by [[Claytor Lake Dam]].
9. **Future work.** The authors call for further sampling to define the range of *P. c. roanoka* in New River and its tributaries.
