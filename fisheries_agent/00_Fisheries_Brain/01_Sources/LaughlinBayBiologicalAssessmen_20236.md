# Laughlin Bay Biological Assessment, Spring 1988

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This fax from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to the Arizona State University Zoo Department reports a spring 1988 biological assessment of [[Laughlin Bay]], a man-made backwater on the [[Colorado River]] in Nevada. The assessment, prepared by consulting ecologist [[William Leibfried]], compares water chemistry, aquatic plant cover and fish populations with data from November 1987 and February 1988. It finds a large spring warming of the bay, a sharp decline in aquatic macrophytes, and a fish community dominated by the non-native [[Common Carp]], with only a small proportion of native suckers. The author argues that this backwater is critical habitat for native fishes in a heavily channelized river.

## Background & Description

The bay is a constructed lagoon-type backwater in the Colorado River below [[Davis Dam]]. The document describes it as a manmade backwater; natural backwaters are rare in this reach. Water chemistry was sampled at the east end of the lagoon and near the inlet to the river, and fish were sampled by night [[Electrofishing]] on May 17 and 18, 1988.

Six fish species were collected. Three were recorded for the first time in this study: [[Bluegill]], [[Channel Catfish]] and the native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. The other species were [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and the native [[Razorback Sucker]]. The flannelmouth and razorback suckers are both native to the Colorado River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Warm backwater habitat.** The author states that warm backwaters are critical to both native and introduced fishes, for physical and biological reasons. Backwaters in large river systems provide a stable environment and a food base that fish depend on. Citing Valdez et al. (1986) and Maddux et al. (1987), the document notes that in the channelized Colorado River below Davis Dam, natural backwaters are rare, which makes [[Laughlin Bay]] especially important for native fish.

**Water temperature.** The most striking change was a rise in water temperature during the spring sample. Before the rising mainstream river water mixed with the bay, the east end of the lagoon reached 25 °C at 11:00 hours. After mixing, the temperature fell to 19.1 °C.

**Water chemistry.** Data are given in tables comparing November 1987, February 1988 and May 1988 at the inlet and east end of the lagoon. Key values reported:
- Salinity was below 1 ppt (recorded as "<1 ppt") in all samples.
- Conductivity ranged from about 558 to 748 µmhos across sampling dates and sites.
- Dissolved oxygen ranged from about 9.5 to 11.2 mg/L in the readings given. Some values are illegible in the scan.
- Values for pH and temperature are partly unreadable in the scan and are not reported here.

**Aquatic plants ([[Aquatic Macrophytes]]).** A drastic decline in the plant community was seen in the February and May 1988 surveys. Although no quantitative collections were made, density observations supported the decline. Spiny naiad and pondweed cover fell from about 75% in November 1987 to less than 30%. The November 1987 macrophyte biomass was 321.5 g/m². Possible causes include:
- Light intensity
- Temperature change
- Nutrient limitation, particularly phosphates, sulfates and nitrates
- Day length, as suggested in personal communication with the [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] regional chief

The author notes that such plant declines can follow high biomass when nutrients are limited, and that the cycle usually repeats once terrestrial and atmospheric sources replenish nutrients.

## Key Findings

- **Warming with river mixing:** Bay temperature was 25 °C before mixing with river water and 19.1 °C after it.
- **Macrophyte collapse:** Spiny naiad and pondweed cover fell from about 75% (November 1987) to under 30% (spring 1988).
- **Fish community:** Six species were collected by night electrofishing on May 17–18, 1988, with [[Common Carp]] dominant at 79.4% of the catch.
- **Largemouth Bass** made up 13.6% of the catch; [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] 4.9%; and [[Bluegill]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] 3.7% each.
- **Native fish share:** Native suckers (flannelmouth and razorback) made up 8.6% of fish collected.
- **Size structure:** All native suckers collected were large adults. Flannelmouths measured about 525–628 mm and razorbacks 535–556 mm. The absence of smaller size classes suggests a lack of recent reproductive success and a general population decline.
- **Catch per unit effort (CPUE):** In May, 81 fish were caught in 204 minutes, a CPUE of about 0.40 fish per minute. In November 1987, 64 fish were caught in 178 minutes, about 0.36 fish per minute (calculated from the counts given).
- **Conservation significance:** The author treats [[Laughlin Bay]] as critical habitat for native Colorado River fishes, since natural backwaters are scarce in the channelized river below [[Davis Dam]].
