# Endangered Species Technical Bulletin, Vol. XIX No. 1 (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #multi-taxon-conservation

## Executive Summary

This January/February 1994 issue of the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] *Endangered Species Technical Bulletin* is a multi-article newsletter rather than a single research study. It covers the status of plants, birds, mammals, reptiles, fishes, insects and marine mammals under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Its main themes are recovery progress (reclassifications and delistings), new listing proposals, policy and legal protections, international trade control, and the human dimensions of conservation. Fishes are a minority of the content: two Georgia darters, the Oregon chub, and the four [[Colorado River Fishes]] receive the most attention.

## Background & Description

The issue has several sections:

- **Reclassification proposals:** [[Small Whorled Pogonia]] (*Isotria medeoloides*) and [[Loch Lomond Coyote Thistle]] (*Eryngium constancei*), both proposed for downlisting from Endangered to Threatened on November 29, 1993.
- **Regional news:** Whooping crane reintroduction and population status, with a directory of the seven FWS regions.
- **Feature essays:** Holly Dublin on African elephant conservation politics (an opinion piece, not an official FWS position); Mary Maruca on the [[African Elephant Conservation Fund]]; Espinoza and Mann on forensic identification of mammoth versus elephant ivory; Godbey and Biggins on [[Black-footed Ferret]] recovery; Ann Haas on the [[Nile Crocodile]] reclassification.
- **Listing proposals and final rules** (October to November 1993): eleven species proposed, comprising seven plants and four animals, plus four final listings.
- **Colorado River recovery plan, sea otter disease report, and an item on Chinese and Taiwanese illegal wildlife trade.**

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vernal lakes** (Loch Lomond) form in Mediterranean-climate depressions with impervious soils. Cyclic wetting and drying supports a specialized biota that cannot survive outside temporary pools.
- **Southern maritime chaparral** is a coastal plant community found only in southern California and northern Baja California. About 85 percent of it has been lost to agriculture and urbanization.
- **Prairie dogs** are the primary food (over 90 percent of diet) for black-footed ferrets. Their populations declined about 98 percent since the early 1900s through persecution and sylvatic plague, and about 2 percent of their original range remains. Roughly 130 other grassland species are associated with them.
- **The African elephant** functions as a [[Keystone Species]]: it converts woodland to grassland, disperses seeds, and creates waterholes. Competition for land with a growing human population is a major management challenge.
- **Nile crocodile** is a significant part of the riverine food web. It controls predatory catfish (*Clarias*), which otherwise prey on tilapia, and it also limits populations of large herbivores.
- **Etowah River system** fishes (the Etowah and Cherokee darters) require clean, free-flowing rocky streams and cannot survive in impoundments. Dams fragment populations and alter downstream temperatures.
- **Southern sea otter** health is tied to the nearshore ecosystem.

## Key Findings

**Reclassification proposals**
- **Small whorled pogonia:** Listed Endangered in 1982. About 60 percent of viable sites are now secure, many on public land. The FWS proposed Threatened status on November 29, 1993, but delisting is not appropriate until more sites are protected.
- **Loch Lomond coyote-thistle:** This perennial herb, in the parsley family, is found only on the floor of Loch Lomond, California. It was listed Endangered in 1985, after partial dredging and filling. The State of California purchased the lake and, with FWS help, installed a split-rail fence. Complete delisting is not yet appropriate because of vandalism, possible logging erosion, and the risk of losing the single population.

**Whooping crane (*Grus americana*)**
- Five surviving cranes released in Florida in February 1993 are the base of a nonmigratory flock. Five more cranes were added, and 14 isolation-reared young were planned for release.
- By December 20, 143 cranes, including 16 chicks, reached Aransas National Wildlife Refuge, Texas, which is about the total expected. Surveys in June found no chicks in six pairs at the Wood Buffalo National Park breeding grounds.
- A Captive Breeding Specialist Group viability assessment estimated that about 87 percent of genetic diversity survived the 1940s population bottleneck.
- At the 4.6 percent annual growth rate of the past 50 years, the Aransas wild population would reach about 500 birds by about 2020. Predicted extinction probability over 100 years is under 1 percent. The species has a long-term recruitment rate of 13.9 percent, the highest of any North American crane population.

**African elephant (*Loxodonta africana*)**
- The 1989 ivory import ban was called an oversimplified solution. Illegal killing is rising again in several range states, and human–elephant conflict is growing.
- The African Elephant Specialist Group (IUCN) was reconstituted to provide a technical forum. Its 1992 Zimbabwe meeting addressed survey techniques and data needs.
- The United States Congress passed the African Elephant Conservation Act in 1988. Elephant numbers had fallen from an estimated 1.2 million to fewer than 600,000 during the 1980s.
- Since 1990 the Conservation Fund has supported 33 projects in 11 of 34 range countries, generating more than $2 million in matching funds.
- A translocation of an estimated 1,000 elephants from Zimbabwe's Gonarezhou National Park was funded during the 1991–1992 drought. It may serve as a model for herd reduction elsewhere.

**Mammoth versus elephant ivory forensics**
- Imports of African elephant ivory were banned in 1989, after Asian elephant ivory had been banned for similar reasons. Shipments of "mammoth" ivory then began arriving.
- [[Schreger Pattern]] angle measurement distinguishes the two. Mammoth ivory angles averaged 73 degrees, compared with 124 degrees for elephant ivory.
- In the Hong Kong shipments, the first 500 carvings were all elephant ivory. A 400-piece shipment contained 200 elephant-derived items. A 600-piece shipment contained 597 mammoth and 3 elephant carvings.
- The method is simple and non-destructive and is described in a 1991 WWF booklet. Trade has since shifted toward bone, warthog tusks and hippopotamus teeth.

**Black-footed ferret (*Mustela nigripes*)**
- Listed in 1967, one of the first US endangered species. Canine distemper and plague devastated the Meeteetse, Wyoming population. Only 18 of 127 animals were captured for captive breeding in 1985–1987.
- The captive population exceeds 250 breeding adults. In 1991–1993, 187 surplus animals were released in Wyoming's Shirley Basin.
- Predation and dispersal caused heavy losses. In 1991, 86 percent of released animals were lost within 90 days, compared with 17 percent in the wild Meeteetse population. In 1992, 26 percent were lost in 18 days.
- Only 8 of the 18 original captured animals were considered genetic founders. Captive animals show abnormal physical features.
- Local success depended on the Wyoming Advisory Team, a management plan with a maximum of six field workers at once, and the "nonessential experimental population" designation, which eased rancher concerns about federal prosecution. Experimental populations have been proposed for South Dakota and Montana.
- The authors' main lessons are to avoid putting all eggs in one basket, avoid reinventing the wheel, and resist "preservation paralysis."

**Listing proposals (October–November 1993)**
- **Six California chaparral plants:** Four are proposed Endangered (Del Mar manzanita, Encinitas baccharis, Orcutt's spineflower, short-leaved dudleya). Two are proposed Threatened (Del Mar sand aster, big-leaved crown-beard).
- **Winkler cactus (*Pediocactus winkleri*):** Six populations totaling about 3,500 plants on 200 acres in Utah. About 80 percent of plants in one area were taken by collectors in 10 years. Proposed Endangered on October 6.
- **Flat-tailed horned lizard (*Phrynosoma mcallii*):** About 40 percent of California habitat is lost. An estimated 95 percent of remaining suitable California habitat and 35 percent in Arizona is threatened. Proposed Threatened on November 29.
- **[[Etowah Darter]] (*Etheostoma etowae*):** Proposed Endangered on October 18.
- **[[Cherokee Darter]] (*Etheostoma* sp.):** Proposed Threatened on October 18. Its formal description is in preparation.
- **[[Hine's Emerald Dragonfly]] (*Somatochlora hineana*):** Known from the Chicago area and Door County, Wisconsin. Proposed Endangered on October 4.

**Final listing rules**
- Star cactus (Endangered, October 18), reduced to two sites.
- Beach jacquemontia (Endangered, November 24), a Florida coastal vine.
- Giant garter snake (Threatened, October 20), extirpated from about two-thirds of its range.
- [[Oregon Chub]] (*Oregonichthys crameri*) (Endangered, October 18), eliminated from 98 percent of its former Willamette range. Remaining populations occupy an 18.5-mile (30 km) stretch.

**Colorado River fishes**
- The FWS completed a five-year Recovery Action Plan on October 15, 1993, for four Endangered fishes: Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub and razorback sucker.
- Measures include protecting in-stream flows, including trial "mimic historical" flow releases from Flaming Gorge and Blue Mesa dams, removing barriers to wetlands, building fish ladders (construction at Redlands Diversion Dam set for 1995), and evaluating hatchery stocking. Bonytails are nearly extinct in the wild.
- Hatchery-raised fish previously stocked below Lake Powell did not survive, so the stocking approach is uncertain.
- A [[Section 7 Consultation]] agreement lets the Recovery Action Plan serve as a "reasonable and prudent alternative" in jeopardy opinions for water projects.

**Sea otter disease report**
- Of 22 southern sea otters found dead in 1992, 10 deaths were due to infectious or parasitic disease. Other causes included emaciation, mating wounds or other causes (7), trauma (4), and intestinal perforation (1).
- Disease findings: coccidioidomycosis (3), aberrant acanthocephalan migration (5), and protozoal encephalitis (2, a newly identified problem).
- The population growth rate remains well below that of Washington, Alaska and Canadian populations.

**Other items**
- The FWS and US delegations are assisting China and Taiwan to end illegal wildlife trade before a March 1994 CITES Standing Committee review. Recommended measures include stronger penalties and an end to amnesty periods.
- Under the Act, [[Section 9 Prohibitions]] prohibit take, import, export and interstate commerce in listed animals without permits. Federal agencies must avoid actions that jeopardize listed species.
