# Descriptive Analysis of the Behaviour of the African Cichlid Fish *Pelmatochromis guentheri*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This behavioural study by [[Arthur A. Myrberg]] Jr. describes the complete life cycle of *Pelmatochromis guentheri* (Sauvage), a rarely imported West African [[Cichlidae|cichlid]], based on observations of 15 juveniles and their second and third generations kept in aquaria of 120, 700 and 6,000 litres. The fish show normal cichlid territoriality, but females can hold stable territories and still pair with males. Spawning resembles that of substrate-brooding cichlids, yet only the male orally incubates the eggs, releasing the young after 9 to 12 days. Both parents defend the brood for a further 30 to 40 days. The author uses these traits to compare nine characters across oral-brooding cichlids and argues that a single-dimension model of brooding evolution is inadequate.

## Background & Description

*Pelmatochromis* is held together mainly by a cushion-like papillose pad on the dorsal branchial arches. The species was first described by Sauvage (1882) as *Hemichromis guentheri*, later moved to *Pelmatochromis* by Boulenger (1898), and has remained there since Pellegrin (1903). Trewavas (1962) separated it from the closely related [[Pelmatochromis Kingsleyae]] and *P. loennbergi*. Early aquarium reports described males as mouth brooders, and the first importation to Germany, from the mouth of the [[Niger River]], occurred around 1913.

Adults reached an average total length of 115 mm in small to moderate aquaria. In the 6,000-litre tank, males approached 160 mm and females 145 mm. Voucher specimens are held at the British Museum (Natural History), catalogue Nos. 1962.12.17.1-11.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Husbandry conditions were water temperature 25 to 27 °C (occasionally 23 °C in the 6,000-litre tank), hardness 3 to 6 °dH, pH 6 to 7, and salinity 500 to 700 µS. Tanks held plants, overturned flowerpots and a gravel-sand substrate, under fluorescent light, with [[Strobe-light Photography]] used to supplement direct observation.

Space strongly shaped behaviour. Schooling under stress increased as tank size decreased. Territories were larger in the 6,000-litre tank, where males rarely fought or met their mates for long periods, which the author argues is closer to natural conditions. Aggression among non-territorial fish rose as territories took up space. The author also notes that territories may partly be laboratory artefacts.

Juveniles were shown to form small schools of 5 to 10 individuals and "mob" a larger predator ([[Haplochromis wingatii]]), an analogy to avian mobbing. Stress reliably turned juveniles patternless with black eyes.

## Key Findings

- **Territoriality:** Strong territoriality first appeared at the start of the fourth month in larger tanks. Females could hold territories and still pair with entering males, a departure from other cichlids.
- **Agonistic behaviour:** Mouth fighting occurred but was passive, with little pushing or pulling, unlike other cichlids. Fin-spreading and head-standing precede "explosive" swimming and act as intention movements.
- **Courtship:** Courtship begins at about 5 months, usually after a male establishes a territory. Females show a belly-display ("S" shape), and males show lateral-display, tail-beating, quivering and nipping. The sequence is not rigidly fixed. Pairs form a wedge-shaped substrate territory and dig a spawning depression.
- **Spawning:** Pair formation to spawning typically took 3 to 5 days in a large aquarium. Eggs are pale orange, about 2 mm in diameter, and each sits on a stalk. Counts reached 7 to 15 eggs within 4 minutes, about 60 within 6 to 7 minutes, and 80 to 100 after 20 minutes. Of approximately 25 spawnings, females never orally incubated the eggs, and only one abortive pickup of a few eggs occurred.
- **Oral incubation:** Only the male incubates. He inhales the eggs about 40 minutes after spawning begins, and females fan the eggs during spawning. The male also feeds on single worms while brooding, which is rare. Renesting occurred within 7 to 9 days if eggs were lost, with a minimum of 21 days between spawnings when young were present.
- **Young and release:** Young are released after 9 to 12 days, depending on temperature and tank population. Both parents use twitching (warning) and calling movements to manage the brood. Parents accept young into their mouths during stress and at dusk until about the 10th to 12th day. Independence came around the 15th day in smaller tanks, and parental care lasted 30 to 40 days, ending earlier in the 120-litre tank (30th to 33rd day) than in the 6,000-litre tank (around the 40th day).
- **Colour patterns:** Adults shift among patternless, striped, barred, spotted and two "lattice" patterns depending on motivation. Fear, not general activity, drives the striped pattern in juveniles, based on experiments with predator presence.
- **Comparative analysis:** Of nine oral-brooding characters, *P. guentheri* resembles substrate brooders and less specialised oral brooders in five (pair bond, spawning site, shared egg-site preparation, parental care duration, and post-release acceptance). It is intermediate in egg pickup timing and chromatic dimorphism. The author concludes that oral incubation probably evolved independently in several cichlid lineages, and that grades (adaptive levels) rather than a single linear trait sequence better describe the diversity.
