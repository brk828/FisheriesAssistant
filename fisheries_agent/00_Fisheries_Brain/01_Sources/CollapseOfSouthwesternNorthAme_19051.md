# Miocene Collapse of Southwestern North America and the Evolution of the San Andreas Fault System

#structural-geology #tectonics #plate-tectonics

## Executive Summary

This is a geology research article, not an ichthyological or aquatic-ecology study. It proposes that early Miocene transtension along the Pacific–North American transform triggered gravitational collapse of the North American plate's western edge toward the southwest. The authors link inboard extension in the [[Mojave Desert]], southeastern California and southern Arizona to the plate boundary. They also link this to clockwise rotation of the southern [[Sierra Nevada]], the [[Trans-Mojave-Sierran Shear Zone]], and the progressive truncation of coastal California by the [[San Andreas Fault System]]. The paper is a Geology letter (December 1995, v. 23, no. 12, pp. 1075–1078).

## Background & Description

The article opens with the Miocene reorganization of the southwestern Cordillera. Around 27 Ma, the Farallon–Pacific ridge interacted with the Farallon–North American subduction zone, creating the [[Mendocino Triple Junction]] and the [[Rivera Triple Junction]]. These migrated northwest and southeast, respectively, as subduction ceased. Right-slip faults of the San Andreas transform connected the two junctions. Over time the locus of transform shear moved into the North American plate, truncating continental slivers such as the [[Salinian Block]] and transferring them to the Pacific plate.

Interior parts of North America also experienced major pre-Basin-and-Range extension. This produced detachment faults and [[Metamorphic Core Complexes]] in the Great Basin, southeastern California, southern Arizona and the Mojave Desert. Earlier work had struggled to tie interior deformation to plate kinematics. Early Miocene extension in east-central Nevada occurred in a back-arc setting, while extension in southeastern California developed opposite the transform. The authors therefore favor gravitational collapse, triggered by mid-Tertiary subduction-related magmatism, over direct kinematic control. They argue that the early Miocene elements of the region can be tied to one progressive plate-kinematic evolution.

Regional extension is described as northeast–southwest oriented (N50°–60°E to S50°–60°W), with low-angle, mainly northeast-dipping brittle-ductile detachments rooted in the middle crust. Minimum extensional lengthening in southern Arizona–southeastern California is 50–70 km, and may exceed 100 km including the [[Salton Trough]]. Regional extension is dated to 25–12 Ma, with separate pulses peaking near 20 Ma and 18 Ma.

The Mojave Desert showed two temporally separated deformation phases, unlike the more continuous extension further east:
- Opening of the ~24–20 Ma [[Mojave Extensional Belt]], a north–south extension zone facilitated mainly by north-dipping detachments.
- Dextral shearing (~20–18 Ma) that produced regional oroclinal folding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not address aquatic ecology, fish, invertebrates, plankton, water chemistry or aquatic habitat. The closest environmental context is geothermal and mantle-related:

- The authors argue that the [[Slab Gap]] along the trailing edge of the subducted Farallon plate controlled where collapse occurred in the overlying North American plate. Thermal weakening from asthenospheric upwelling and/or magmatism is invoked.
- The close spatial correspondence of the collapse area with the slab gap and mid-Tertiary magmatism is cited as strong evidence for thermal control on the localization of deformation.
- Paleomagnetic data come mainly from early Miocene volcanic strata, and the southern Sierra Nevada record includes middle Miocene (~16 Ma) strata.

## Key Findings

**1. Transtensional plate boundary.** Early Miocene Pacific–North American motion was oblique, not purely strike-slip. This produced transtension across the transform and west-northwestward migration of the Pacific plate. Stock and Molnar (1988) suggest 340 ± 200 km of transform-normal migration since ~30 Ma, with as much as ~175 km between 20 and 16 Ma.

**2. Gravitational collapse of North America.** The area east of the transform extended laterally to maintain contact with the moving Pacific plate. Collapse began near 24 Ma and continued to ~16 Ma, moving the southern California–southern Arizona–northwestern Mexico region southwestward relative to the rest of the continent. The collapse direction (S50°–60°W, nearly normal to the plate boundary) indicates gravity as the driving force.

**3. Two-phase Mojave history.**
- *Mojave Extensional Belt (~24–20 Ma):* north–south extension, partly driven by northwestward translation of the block to the north (pre-20 Ma).
- *Trans-Mojave-Sierran Shear Zone (~20–16 Ma):* east–west dextral shear, which replaced extension along the northern boundary once northwestward motion of the northern block ceased.

**4. Vertical-axis rotation.**
- Rocks in an ~83–110 km-wide belt through the Mojave Desert rotated on average 51.1° ± 15.6° clockwise between 20 and 18 Ma (paleomagnetic data, Ross, 1995).
- The southern Sierra Nevada rotated ~40°–60° clockwise between ~21 and 16 Ma. The 80–89 Ma plutonic rocks are deflected, while overlying ~16 Ma strata are not. Plescia and Calderone (1986) report 40.0° ± 9.5° of clockwise rotation in 25–21 Ma basalts.
- Using N = w sin θ, the authors calculate 48–76 km of right slip.
- Resolved parallel to the collapse direction, the shear zone accounts for 41–66 km of 20–18 Ma collapse. This implies a collapse rate of ~21–33 km/m.y. between 20 and 18 Ma, which should match the extensional lengthening in southeastern California and southern Arizona if the two are kinematically linked.

**5. Truncation of coastal California.** As collapse proceeded, North America moved west past the locus of transform shear. A new fault propagated between the triple junctions. Slivers such as the Salinian block were progressively truncated and transferred to the Pacific plate, in a "meat slicer-like" fashion. The slivers are ~150 km wide in central California. The authors argue that the amount of extensional lengthening (175 ± 100 km) should equal the transform-normal divergence and the width of the transferred slivers.

**6. Secondary implication.** Northwest movement of fault slivers such as the Salinian block created extension north of the Rivera Triple Junction. The authors speculate this created room for the middle Miocene rotation of the western [[Transverse Ranges]].

**Conclusion.** The Pacific–North American boundary was transtensional, and the Pacific plate migrated west-northwest relative to North America. In response, the continental edge east of the transform collapsed southwestward. Extension and detachment faulting dominated from ~24 Ma. From ~20 Ma the northern boundary shifted to dextral shear, and the western continental edge was progressively transferred to the Pacific plate by the San Andreas system.
