# Coexistence of Desert Scorpions by Differential Habitat Preference

#arachnology #desert-ecology #vejovidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how four habitat types on Phoenix South Mountain, Arizona, shape the abundance, distribution and community structure of desert scorpions. Using standardized [[Pitfall Trapping]] over 104 consecutive weeks (1964–1966), the author recorded 645 scorpions from eight species in three families. Three species, [[Hadrurus Arizonensis]], [[Vejovis Confusus]] and [[Vejovis Spinigerus]], were abundant enough for analysis. Scorpion abundance differed significantly among habitats, and the three dominant species formed a distinct community structure in each. The author proposes that [[Habitat Specialization]] and differences in prey size allow these species to avoid [[Competitive Exclusion]]. Population totals were stable between years, though two species showed significant local fluctuations.

## Background & Description

Scorpion research before this study concentrated on systematics, behavior and venom, and population or community ecology was rare. Earlier work covered population periodicity, temperature-adaptive behavior, pectine function, desert adaptation and the habitat affinities of scorpions at the [[Nevada Test Site]]. The author states two aims: to determine how habitat type affects scorpion abundance, distribution and community structure, and to measure year-to-year changes in these populations.

Fieldwork took place from September 1964 to January 1967 at the eastern end of Phoenix South Mountain, Maricopa County, Arizona (30° 26′ N, 112° 01′ W). The study area was about three square kilometers and was chosen for relatively undisturbed ecology, easy access, close juxtaposition of distinct desert habitats, and abundant, diverse scorpion fauna. The project was supported by the National Science Foundation (grant GB 7679) and the National Institutes of Health (predoctoral fellowship 5-F1-GM-23,794-02).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The mountain is a rugged, rocky range in the Basin and Range Physiographic Province, with elevations from 382 m at its eastern foot to 758 m at its highest peak. Slope soils are shallow, whereas open-desert soils are deeper, stonier and underlain by a water-impervious mineral layer.

**Climate.** Records spanning 28 years give air temperatures from −13 °C to 46 °C. Rainfall is highly irregular; August is generally the wettest month, averaging 2.84 cm, while May and June are the driest. Daily temperature peaks coincide with the lowest relative humidity (about 4:00 p.m. MST in summer).

**Four habitats.** Each subarea, sampled with 20 traps, differed in topography, soil, slope, plant community and sun exposure:

- **[[Open Desert Basin]]:** flat, sandy-loam terrain dominated by [[Creosote Bush]] (*Larrea tridentata*) and bur-sage (*Franseria deltoidea*), part of a [[Larrea-Franseria Community]].
- **[[North-Facing Slope]]:** a canyon side at about 25° with the most abundant and largest vegetation, shaded and moister, with bur-sage, brittle bush and paloverde.
- **[[South-Facing Slope]]:** the opposite canyon side, also about 25°, with intense sun, shallow gravelly soils and sparse vegetation dominated by teddy bear cholla.
- **[[Canyon Bottom]]:** the most physically and biotically heterogeneous habitat, with rocky outcrops, alluvial soils, a deep sandy wash and a seasonal spring.

Ecotones, where habitat types intergrade, were noted in the canyon bottoms and at the transition from mountain to open desert.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** Sampling used unbaited pitfall traps made from No. 10 food cans, buried flush with the ground and covered by rocks. Twenty traps were placed in each of the four subareas (80 total), at intervals never closer than 10 m along line transects. Traps were checked every seven days. Catches were pooled into 52-week units, and an additional 100 traps tested the effects of scorpion removal and trap covers. Abundance comparisons used [[Chi-Square Analysis]], with Yates' correction where appropriate.

**Species composition.** Eight species were recorded from three families:

- Buthidae: *Centruroides sculpturatus* (2 specimens trapped).
- Vejovidae: *Hadrurus arizonensis*, *Vejovis confusus*, *Vejovis spinigerus*, *Vejovis stahnkei* (5 trapped, open desert), *Vejovis wupatkiensis* (1 trapped, southern exposure) and *Vejovis lapidicola* (1 found under a rock chip).
- Chactidae: one unidentified specimen, found in the stomach of a whip-tail lizard (*Cnemidophorus tigris*) and identified by its six eyes.

The three abundant species were the only ones analysed in detail. The other five were rare.

**Total captures and year-to-year stability.** A total of 645 scorpions was captured, with 320 in 1965 and 325 in 1966. The difference between years was not significant (χ²(1) = 0.024; P > 0.80).

**Abundance by habitat.** Total catches were 197 (open desert basin), 166 (canyon bottom), 158 (southern exposure) and 124 (northern exposure). These differed significantly (χ²(3) = 16.74; P < 0.01). Adjacent-pair comparisons showed that the desert basin, canyon bottom and southern exposure did not differ significantly from one another, but each supported significantly more scorpions than the northern exposure.

**Species-specific habitat patterns (Table 3, pooled 104-week data):**

- *[[Vejovis Confusus]]* was numerically dominant in the open desert (103 vs. 73 *H. arizonensis* and 21 *V. spinigerus*; χ² = 52.40; P < 0.01). It was absent from the northern exposure and rare in rocky habitats.
- *[[Vejovis Spinigerus]]* was dominant in all rocky habitats: 113 of 124 on the northern exposure (χ² = 82.26; P < 0.01), 128 of 166 in the canyon bottom (χ² = 154.39; P < 0.01), and 101 of 158 on the southern exposure (χ² = 85.70; P < 0.01).
- *[[Hadrurus Arizonensis]]* occurred in every habitat but was never numerically dominant. Its greatest abundance was in the open desert.

Three community types emerged: the open desert (dominated by *V. confusus*), the northern exposure (dominated by *V. spinigerus*, with *V. confusus* absent), and the canyon bottom and southern exposure (both dominated by *V. spinigerus*, with *H. arizonensis* common and *V. confusus* rare).

**Significant population changes (Table 4).** Most species remained stable between 1965 and 1966. Two changes were significant:

- *H. arizonensis* declined from 51 to 22 in the open desert (P < 0.01).
- *V. spinigerus* increased from 40 to 61 on the southern exposure (P < 0.05).

*V. confusus* may have declined in the canyon bottom and southern exposure, but counts were too low for statistical testing.

**Diversity in context.** The eight-species South Mountain fauna compares favorably with regional faunas: nine species at the Nevada Test Site (the North American record), five at Puerto Peñasco, Sonora, and five at Durango, Mexico. Scorpion faunas are far less diverse than other arachnid groups, with 14 spider species at Portal, Arizona and 28 solpugid species at the Nevada Test Site. The author attributes this to shared food, habitat needs and morphology among scorpions, which should intensify competition.

**Mechanisms of coexistence.** The author identifies two main mechanisms:

1. *Prey size:* *H. arizonensis* captures prey too large for the other species to use.
2. *Habitat specialization:* *V. confusus* and *V. spinigerus* feed on similar prey but occupy different habitats. Their ranges overlap at the ecotone where mountain and desert meet, but neither shares dominance in any single habitat.

**Conclusions.** Scorpion communities on South Mountain are structured by habitat, with species-specific preferences producing distinct dominance patterns. Coexistence depends on niche partitioning by prey size and habitat type. Population totals were stable, and the few significant fluctuations were confined to particular habitats.
