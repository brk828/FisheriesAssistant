# Harelip Sucker (Moxostoma lacerum): Taxonomy, Biology and Extinction

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

The harelip sucker, *Moxostoma lacerum* (originally described as *Lagochila lacera* by Jordan and Brayton), was a freshwater catostomid of eastern North America. It is known only from preserved specimens and historical records. The document reviews its systematics, morphology, feeding ecology, distribution, abundance, and the causes of its extinction. It is the first fish known to have become extinct in the United States in modern times. Its decline is attributed mainly to habitat alteration, including siltation from deforestation, and to its highly specialized feeding biology.

## Background & Description

**Systematics.** The species was described in 1877 as a new species, genus and subfamily (Lagochilinae) from Georgia and Tennessee. The subfamily was rarely recognized, and *Lagochila* held monotypic rank until recently. Its non-protractile upper jaw, distinctive lower lip and delicate pharyngeal teeth were early grounds for its distinction. Later studies by Jenkins and G. R. Smith documented extensive modification of skull bones. Phylogenetic placement has been difficult:

- Bailey (1959) linked *Lagochila* to the subgenus *Moxostoma* on the basis of a circumpeduncle scale count of 12. Jenkins (1970) regarded it as the terminal, advanced offshoot of the *Moxostoma* lineage.
- Smith (1992) placed the harelip sucker within a short-headed redhorse group that includes *M. duquesnei*, *M. macrolepidotum* and *M. poecilurum*.
- If *Lagochila* is kept as a separate genus, *Moxostoma* becomes paraphyletic. The author favors placing the species in *Moxostoma* and notes that the resurrection of [[Teretulus]] (Rafinesque 1820) is one nomenclatural option.

**Morphology.** Specimens were examined from 10 museums. Adults reach at least 313 mm SL. The upper jaw is non-protractile, the upper lip hood-like, and the lower lip is split into two lobes. The body is moderately deep and somewhat compressed. The eye is moderate to large and lateral, and the snout is well rounded in juveniles and more protruding in adults. Meristic features include:

- Lateral line complete, with 42 to 46 scales
- 31 to 34 circumbody scales
- 12 circumpeduncle scales
- Dorsal, anal and pelvic rays within typical redhorse ranges

**Coloration.** In preserved specimens the body is dusky dorsally and pale ventrally, with plain fins. In life the back was olive to steel-blue, with silvery sides and belly and creamy fins.

**Name.** *Lacerum* refers to the lacerated or "torn" lower lip.

**Related taxon.** The document also covers the finescale sucker genus [[Catostomus]], which comprises about 28 species (about five undescribed), most in western North America, with two species extending east.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Feeding biology.** Because the species is extinct, its biology was inferred from functional anatomy, from comparison with living redhorses, and from the gut contents of extant specimens. Nine of ten specimens contained food; the adult was the exception.

- All nine specimens with food contained snail opercula, which were identified to *Campeloma* and *Goniobasis*.
- Mollusks made up about 90% of the diet.
- Two specimens had eaten limpets. Midge larvae were found in four specimens.
- Minor items included copepods, cladocerans, a water mite, mayfly and caddisfly larvae, and fingernail clams.

The small, frail pharyngeal apparatus could not crush heavy shells as living redhorses do, so the species likely swallowed whole snails and digested the soft parts. The absence of shells in the guts is attributed to dissolution during preservation.

**Sight feeding.** Several features indicate a visual predator:

- A large eye and relatively large optic lobes of the brain, contrasting with other suckers
- A short intestine, typical of carnivores
- Few cephalic lateral line pores
- Few short gill rakers, indicating limited straining ability

Miller and Evans (1965) concluded that the fish located prey visually and manipulated it with the lips. Foraging was probably restricted to daylight.

**Habitat.** All records come from streams and rivers of about medium size, with moderate gradient, running through rolling upland or intermontane terrain. Substrates were mainly gravel and rock, and the water was usually clear and unvegetated. Six summer temperatures associated with captures ranged from 17.2 to about 25 °C, with a mean of about 21.7 °C.

- Small individuals were taken more often in smaller streams and adults in larger ones.
- The cerebellum is small, suggesting occupation of gentle current. Pool habitat with forays into slow runs is most likely.

**Reproduction and life history.** The species migrated and spawned in spring, as most suckers do. Klippart reported that it ascended the Scioto River in central Ohio in May, where fishermen called it the "May sucker." The only clearly mature specimen is a female of 401 mm TL (15.8 inches) and 528 g eviscerated weight from Tennessee. Based on scale reading, she was at least 6 and possibly 8 years old. Her ovaries were somewhat enlarged, and her eggs were small for ripe redhorse ova (about 1.5 mm). She was probably not spawning when captured.

**Distribution.** Based on 19 or 20 capture records, the species was historically widespread but localized. Records come from eight states, and at least two more states were probably occupied (Illinois, Missouri), possibly three more (Mississippi, Pennsylvania, West Virginia). Eleven sites were in the Cumberland and Tennessee drainages. Post-Pleistocene fossils have been found at three archaeological sites in the Tennessee drainage.

**Abundance.** Reports of former abundance are conflicting and were mostly made by fishermen, possibly reflecting localized spawning aggregations. Capture data suggest that populations were generally less dense than those of five sympatric redhorse species. Most ichthyological collections contain only one or a few specimens.

## Key Findings

1. **Taxonomic status:** The species is a distinctive, highly specialized catostomid whose placement within *Moxostoma* remains debated. Smith (1992) and the author favor its inclusion in *Moxostoma*.
2. **Feeding ecology:** Mollusks (about 90% of the diet) were consumed whole, and the species was a sight feeder with reduced straining ability. Its specialization made it poorly adapted to change.
3. **Habitat requirements:** The species occupied clear, gravelly, moderately graded streams with cool summer temperatures (mean about 21.7 °C), and probably avoided silted waters.
4. **Specimen base:** About 33 specimens are held by 10 institutions. Specimen records come from roughly 14 to 15 of the 19 or 20 known sites.
5. **Historical decline:** The species was reported as generally uncommon or rare by the 1880s. The last record was a juvenile in northwestern Ohio in 1893 (Kirsch 1895). Lachner (1956) judged it probably extinct, and the ichthyological community agreed.
6. **Primary causes of extinction:** Habitat alteration is considered the major factor. Increased turbidity and sedimentation from 1800s deforestation probably contributed substantially. Sedimentation may have impaired sight feeding and reduced prey. Dams fragmented populations, and industrial and municipal pollution affected some populations. Interspecific competition was unlikely to be a factor.
7. **Reproductive vulnerability:** Spawning requirements are poorly known. The species may have needed specific spawning beds and large spawning groups, which dwindling numbers could have disrupted.
8. **Broader lessons:** A widespread but strongly divergent lineage declined almost unnoticed. The species had likely been in precarious balance with its environment long before it was discovered.
9. **Data discrepancy:** The text gives the adult female's weight as "7.2 pounds," which conflicts with the stated 528 g (about 1.2 pounds). This note reports the gram value.
