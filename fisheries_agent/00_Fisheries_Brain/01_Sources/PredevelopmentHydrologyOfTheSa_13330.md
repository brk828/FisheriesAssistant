# Predevelopment Hydrology of the Salt River Indian Reservation, East Salt River Valley, Arizona

#hydrology #groundwater #surface-water #predevelopment #water-rights #geological-survey

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey report, prepared in cooperation with the U.S. Bureau of Indian Affairs, documents predevelopment hydrologic conditions in the [[Salt River Indian Reservation]] area prior to non-Indian settlement (pre-1870s). The study was conducted to support water rights adjudication in the [[Gila River]] watershed. Before development, the [[Salt River]] was perennial through the reservation, with average annual discharge estimated at 1,250,000 acre-feet and median discharge at 950,000 acre-feet. The ground-water reservoir was at or near capacity, sustained primarily by infiltration from the Salt River, with water levels 10-70 feet below land surface. A ground-water flow model simulated average predevelopment conditions, quantifying recharge from river infiltration (19,700 acre-ft/yr), mountain-front recharge and subsurface inflow (10,700 acre-ft/yr), discharge to the river (9,800 acre-ft/yr), evapotranspiration (13,300 acre-ft/yr), and subsurface outflow (7,300 acre-ft/yr).

## Background & Description

The [[Salt River Indian Reservation]] was established in 1879 in the eastern [[Salt River Valley]], south-central Arizona. The study area encompasses approximately 950 mi², including 77 mi² within the reservation. The region is characterized by broad desert plains dissected by arroyos and separated by rugged mountains, with elevations ranging from 1,200 to 5,000 feet. The area lies within the [[Basin and Range]] physiographic province.

Modern irrigation began in 1867 with the Swilling Ditch, followed by numerous canal systems in the 1870s-1880s. By 1889, approximately 35,212 acres were irrigated in [[Maricopa County]], with water use averaging 4.6 acre-ft/acre in 1895. Reservoir construction began with [[Roosevelt Dam]] (1911), followed by [[Mormon Flat Dam]] (1925), [[Horse Mesa Dam]] (1927), [[Stewart Mountain Dam]] (1930), [[Bartlett Dam]] (1939), and [[Horseshoe Dam]] (1946), creating combined storage capacity exceeding 2 million acre-feet.

The valley floor is underlain by sedimentary deposits several thousand feet thick, divided into four units: red unit (well-cemented breccia, conglomerate, sandstone), lower unit (clay, silt, mudstone with interbedded materials, 600-10,000 ft thick), middle unit (silt, siltstone, silty sand and gravel, 100-1,000 ft thick), and upper unit (gravel, sand, silt, mostly unconsolidated, 200-300+ ft thick). The upper unit exhibits excellent water-bearing characteristics, potentially yielding up to 4,500 gal/min to wells.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The climate is arid, with annual precipitation averaging 8 inches, insufficient to support vegetation without irrigation. Mean daily temperatures range from 33-70°F in winter to 64-105°F in summer. Precipitation occurs primarily from summer thunderstorms (July-August) and winter Pacific storms. Wind movement is relatively light, averaging 5-8 mi/h.

Native vegetation includes [[Mesquite]] and saltbush along washes, palo verde and cacti on hills, and creosote bush on the desert floor. Before settlement, mesquite, cottonwood, and willow trees grew along the river. The area of potential phreatophytic growth was estimated at 18,500 acres, though probably only half contained phreatophytes.

Evapotranspiration by phreatophytes was estimated at 15,000 acre-ft/yr based on an evapotranspiration rate of 1.6 ft/yr applied to half the potential growth area. Studies on the [[Gila River]] flood plain showed annual evapotranspiration averaged 3.7 ft, ranging from 4.7 ft for dense phreatophyte stands to 2.1 ft for areas without phreatophytes. Depth to ground water ranged from 5-20 feet on the flood plain.

The [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] each drain areas exceeding 6,000 mi² upstream from their confluence near the reservation's east boundary. Tree-ring reconstructions extending back to A.D. 1580 indicated that average discharge for five 80-year periods ranged from 83-99% of the recorded period average, with the 400-year average at 91% of recorded values.

## Key Findings

**Predevelopment Streamflow:**
- Natural (predevelopment) annual discharge of the Salt-Verde drainage basin averaged 1,250,000 acre-feet
- Median annual discharge estimated at 950,000 acre-feet
- Combined average discharge (1931-86 records) was 1,151,000 acre-ft/yr with median of 673,000 acre-ft/yr
- Upstream diversions and reservoir evaporation reduced natural discharge by approximately 150,000 acre-ft/yr
- The Salt River was perennial through the study area and the main source of recharge

**Ground-Water Conditions:**
- Water levels in 1900 were 10-70 feet below land surface in developed areas
- Ground-water flow direction: east to west along Salt River flood plain and south of river; north to south in [[Paradise Valley]]
- Ground-water reservoir was at or near capacity, sustained mainly by Salt River infiltration
- Hydraulic gradient averaged 0.001, ranging from 0.0006 to 0.004
- Transmissivity values ranged from 2,500 to 75,000 ft²/d, with greatest values south of Salt River in Mesa area
- Storage coefficients for sedimentary deposits averaged 15-20%

**Ground-Water Flow Model Results (Study Area):**
- Recharge from Salt River: 19,700 acre-ft/yr
- Mountain-front recharge and subsurface inflow: 10,700 acre-ft/yr (including 6,700 acre-ft/yr from [[Cave Creek]] and 4,000 acre-ft/yr from [[McDowell Mountains]])
- Discharge to Salt River: 9,800 acre-ft/yr
- Evapotranspiration from ground water: 13,300 acre-ft/yr
- Subsurface outflow: 7,300 acre-ft/yr (800 acre-ft/yr at [[Tempe Butte]], 6,500 acre-ft/yr at Gila River)
- Net flux from river to aquifer: 9,900 acre-ft/yr

**Salt River Indian Reservation Specific:**
- Ground-water inflow: 26,700 acre-ft/yr (19,700 from Salt River infiltration, 7,000 from underflow and mountain-front recharge)
- Subsurface outflow: 13,600 acre-ft/yr (51% of discharge)
- Evapotranspiration: 8,100 acre-ft/yr (30% of discharge)
- Discharge to Salt River: 5,000 acre-ft/yr (19% of discharge)
- Net flux from river: 14,700 acre-ft/yr

**Model Calibration:**
- 121 wells used for calibration
- Head residuals generally less than ±20 feet
- Average absolute residual: 10 feet; standard deviation: 15 feet
- Simulated transmissivities ranged from 2,000 to 40,000 ft²/d within reservation
- Model indicated Salt River was predominantly losing stream in eastern half, gaining stream in western half

**Sensitivity Analysis:**
- Heads most sensitive to transmissivity and boundary-flux changes
- Heads insensitive to evapotranspiration parameters
- Flow components most sensitive to transmissivity changes
- Net river flux insensitive to riverbed conductance but highly sensitive to boundary fluxes and evapotranspiration parameters
- Riverbed conductance values reduced by two orders of magnitude from initial estimates to produce realistic flux distributions

**Historical Context:**
- Prehistoric [[Hohokam Indians]] occupied and irrigated the valley from 300 B.C. to A.D. 1450, with at least 250,000 acres under irrigation
- Archaeological studies recorded 300+ miles of main canals and 1,000+ miles of smaller canals
- Ground-water withdrawals remained below 100,000 acre-ft/yr until early 1920s, exceeded 1 million acre-ft in 1942 and 2 million acre-ft in 1952
