# Bonytail Chub (Gila elegans) Five-Year Status Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game status report, prepared by Susan R. Ellis (Associate Fishery Biologist, Inland Fisheries Division, Endangered Species Project) and dated July 1, 1990, reviews the [[Bonytail Chub]], a [[Cyprinidae]] fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species is listed as endangered by both the State of California and the federal government. The report recommends maintaining its listing as Endangered in California.

The bonytail has declined more abruptly than any other native mainstem Colorado River fish. By 1980 no viable populations were known, except possibly in [[Lake Mohave]]. Hatchery propagation and stocking are the main recovery tools, and the report notes that stocking is now restricted and that no management alternatives remain for California.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and identity.** The bonytail is a cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River basin. Its taxonomy has been confused. Some past sightings may have been [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), and the status of bonytail versus related [[Gila]] species remains unresolved. Hybridization with roundtail and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is also considered a possible factor in its decline.

**Morphology.** The body form suits unstable bottoms, swift currents, and heavy sediment loads of the pre-dam river. Key features include:
- A short, flattened head with a broad snout and a terminal mouth.
- A nuchal hump in adults, an enlarged muscle mass that helps hold position in strong current.
- A long, slender caudal peduncle and a deeply forked tail.
- Large, sickle-shaped fins.
- Dorsal coloration grey or olive, with a silver or white belly.
- Breeding males with light orange slashes at the base of the pectoral and pelvic fins, and small head tubercles.
- Reduced or embedded scales.

**Meristics (as printed).** Pharyngeal teeth 2,5–4,2; gill rakers on the anterior row of the second arch usually 18 (range 15–21); dorsal fin usually 10 rays (9–11); anal fin 10 rays (9–12). Young bonytail are difficult to separate in the field from roundtail and humpback chub, though theoretically they can be distinguished by fin-ray counts, larger eyes, and the absence of an overhanging snout.

**Size and growth.** Typical length is 30–35 cm, with individuals exceeding 60 cm. Estimated lengths are about 5.5 cm at the end of the first season, 10.0 cm in the second, and 15.8 cm in the third.

**Diet.** Adults feed in schools at the water surface, mainly on terrestrial insects. Plant debris and filamentous algae occur in stomachs, likely incidentally. Young fish feed mostly on aquatic insect larvae.

**Reproduction.** Spawning occurs from late June to early July at water temperatures of about 18 °C (65 °F), over gravel riffles or rubble-bottomed eddies. Sexual maturity is reached late (5–7 years for females; the male figure is partly illegible in the scan, reading 5–9 years), likely prolonged by environmental change. A 1954 observation by Jonez and Sumner in Lake Mohave recorded 500 bonytail broadcast-spawning over a gravelly shelf, with 3–5 males per female, in water as deep as 9 m (30 ft). No young from that session were observed. In hatchery conditions:
- Mean fecundity is about 25,000 eggs per female (printed as "2S,OTO").
- Incubation takes 99–174 hours at 20–21 °C, and longer in cooler water.
- Egg survival is highest in warm water and falls at 12–13 °C.
- Swim-up fry survival was high at 20–21 °C and about 25% at 12–13 °C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic habitat.** The Colorado River historically alternated between swift canyon reaches with torrential rapids and slow, meandering, sand-bottomed stretches. Bonytail were most common in the large, low-gradient channels of the 19th and early 20th centuries. Collections have come from pools and eddies, rarely from strong current, over silt and clay or silt and boulders with few aquatic plants. Bonytail appear adapted to the muddy, colder waters now found below the dams.

**Habitat alteration.** The report identifies these main drivers of decline:
- **Dams and impoundments** reduced downstream water temperatures, altered flow timing (peak flows shifted from winter to summer as water was stored for irrigation), and fragmented habitat into reservoirs and cold tailwaters. Prior to impoundment, water temperatures below Hoover and Davis dams were constant, varying only between 10 and 30 °C.
- **Sedimentation and channel work.** Reduced, moderated flows allowed silt to fill pools and backwaters, the prime habitat of the bonytail. Dredging, channelization, and channel deflectors followed from flood concerns and filled pools further.
- **Salinity.** Draining of mineral-rich agricultural fields raised total dissolved solids and elevated salinity in the lower basin.
- **Biological interactions.** Competition and predation from introduced species better adapted to altered conditions suppressed bonytail abundance. Hybridization with related *Gila* species is also implicated in the upper basin.

**Parasitism.** Of 32 bonytail collected from Lake Mohave for culture (1974–1985), eight were heavily infected with the copepod *Lernaea*, and these were released.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and abundance**
- Bonytail were once distributed from Mexico to Wyoming in the main Colorado River and major tributaries.
- Bonytail were the most abundant fish in the lower Colorado River. Occasional specimens were reported at Needles–Yuba by 1944. They were common in the Green and Yampa rivers in the 1960s, very rare by 1973, and occasionally found in Desolation Canyon (Green River) in 1979. The last Gila River record is from 1926.
- As of 1973, the species persisted in extremely low numbers in only Lake Havasu and Lake Mohave.
- In 1980, no viable populations were found except in Lake Mohave, where elderly adults showed no evidence of reproduction.
- In 1988, a few large adults were found in Lake Mohave. These may have been fish stocked in 1981. Lake Mohave yielded ten bonytail that year, though some may be survivors of the 1981 stocking.

**Legal status**
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) declared the species endangered in 1980 (Federal Register, 1980). No critical habitat was proposed because no known area fulfilled reproduction requirements.
- Take, possession, and sale are prohibited by state law. The species is endangered under both state and federal law.

**Recovery and management**
- A Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team (CRFRT) was appointed to determine abundance and distribution and to recommend survival and replenishment measures. Its recovery plan was approved in 1984 and was being updated in 1988.
- The CRFRT outline calls for:
  1. Preventing extinction in the wild, including protecting refugia and high-priority recovery areas.
  2. Protecting populations and habitat by enforcing regulations.
  3. Reintroducing hatchery-reared fish, including artificial propagation, experimental stocking, and stocking in priority sites.
  4. Developing quantitative recovery goals and a long-term habitat protection strategy.
  5. Resolving taxonomic problems in the basin *Gila* and gathering life-history and habitat data.
- The report argues that channelization and other degradation of the river should cease.
- Hatchery stocking was carried out at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, Quinn Native Fish Ponds, and other sites. A 1983 release of 1,966 subfingerlings at Blythe Municipal Golf Course was followed by only 633 surviving fish in April 1985.
- USFWS attempted fry stocking in Lake Mohave in 1982, without success.
- In 1989, USFWS restricted hatchery-reared bonytail to Lake Mohave only. The final stocking of California-reared fish occurred in February 1990.
- The report concludes that the species is endemic to California, that no management alternatives remain for it there, and that California is not currently permitted to stock it in state waters.

**Information gaps**
- Habitat requirements are not adequately known.
- The true extent of the historic range and the taxonomic status of the species are unresolved.
