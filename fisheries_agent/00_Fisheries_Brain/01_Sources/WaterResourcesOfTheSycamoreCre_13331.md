# Water Resources of the Sycamore Creek Watershed, Maricopa County, Arizona

#hydrology #groundwater #phreatophytes

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Water-Supply Paper (1968) reports a 1961–65 hydrologic investigation of the [[Sycamore Creek Watershed]] in eastern Maricopa County, Arizona. The study was prepared with the Arizona State Land Department and the Salt River Valley Water Users' Association. The authors present Sycamore Creek as representative of many small Southwestern watersheds where streamflow originates in mountains and quickly infiltrates adjacent alluvium. Mountain runoff averaged 6,110 acre-feet per year, but only a small fraction reaches the [[Verde River]] as surface flow. Most of the water is stored in the [[Ground-Water Reservoir]] and returned as subsurface discharge, while [[Evapotranspiration]] consumes most of the precipitation. The document is a technical hydrologic report, not a fish or aquatic-biology study; no fish species, invertebrates, or aquatic biota are described.

## Background & Description

The watershed drains about 195 square miles of the Mazatzal Mountains and flows about 40 miles southwestward to the Verde River. Elevations range from about 1,400 feet near the Verde River to more than 7,100 feet in the mountains. The watershed is divided into two parts:

- **[[Upper Hard-Rock Area]]:** about 85 percent of the watershed (165 square miles, or 105,000 acres, per the abstract). It is rugged terrain of granite, crystalline and metamorphic rock, and basaltic lava. Mean channel gradient is about 120 feet per mile.
- **[[Lower Alluvial Area]]:** a 9-mile reach of low relief with a channel gradient of about 30 feet per mile. Its floodplain is underlain by as much as 105 feet of unconsolidated Quaternary sand, gravel, silt, and clay.

The climate is hot and dry at lower elevations. Annual pan evaporation at Bartlett Reservoir averaged 120.33 inches for 1961–65, against a 25-year average of 123.33 inches. Annual precipitation is about 11.6 inches at Bartlett Reservoir (1,650 feet) and 20.0 inches at [[Sunflower Station]] (3,360 feet). Winter storms are generally low-intensity, widespread frontal rain or snow. Summer storms are short, intense, convective thunderstorms of small areal extent.

The study had three objectives: (1) define the hydrologic system, (2) estimate the potential water yield, and (3) quantify the amount, location, and nature of water losses. Instrumentation included six streamflow gaging stations, four recording precipitation gages, 20 observation wells, and 26 streamflow-measurement sites. Investigators also drilled 17 test holes and ran three seismic-refraction profiles across the flood plain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report does not study fish or other aquatic fauna, but it describes the riparian and hydrologic conditions that shape the ecosystem:

- **Intermittent flow:** the [[Fort McDowell Gage]] channel was dry on average about 150 days per year, versus about 50 days per year of "no flow" (under 0.05 cfs) at the upstream Sunflower gage. Rock Creek had no flow for 360 days in the dry 1964 water year and 250 days in the wet 1965 water year. Alder Creek had no flow for 77 and 53 days in those years.
- **Phreatophytes:** [[Phreatophytes]], mainly [[Mesquite]] with smaller amounts of cottonwood and other trees, cover about 1,400 acres along the lower channel and flood plain. They are densest where depth to water is usually less than 20 feet.
- **Water-table control:** [[Evapotranspiration]] is controlled by depth to water. Diurnal water-level fluctuations in the lower reach indicate groundwater use by vegetation, and evaporation from groundwater is considered negligible where the water table lies more than a few feet below the surface.
- **Flood disturbance:** the largest recorded flood at the Fort McDowell gage was 2,860 cfs (August 16, 1963). A slope-area estimate gave a peak of 15,800 cfs in December 1959.

## Key Findings

**Precipitation and water input**
- Average annual precipitation at Sunflower is about 20 inches (18-year record), with 55 percent in winter and 45 percent in summer. During the 5-year study, the mean was 19.11 inches, about 60 percent in winter and 40 percent in summer.
- Precipitation increases with altitude. In water year 1963, recorded totals were 6.91 inches at Fort McDowell (1,440 feet), 20.00 inches at Sunflower, 23.31 inches at Alder Creek (4,100 feet), and 14.36 inches at Mount Ord (7,120 feet). The Mount Ord catch was judged likely deficient because of wind effects near the crest.
- The 5-year mean annual input to the upper hard-rock area was 168,000 acre-feet.
- Winter storms produce more precipitation, last longer, and are less intense. The largest winter storm total was 4.62 inches; the largest summer storm total was 1.80 inches. The longest winter storm lasted 64 hours and the longest summer storm 19.5 hours. The maximum 30-minute intensity was 0.84 inch per hour in winter and 2.30 inches per hour in summer.
- The 5-year record was statistically indistinguishable from the 18-year record in mean and variance at the 5 percent significance level, for yearly and seasonal data.

**Runoff and water yield**
- Winter precipitation (about 60 percent of the total) produces about 90 percent of runoff because evaporation demand is lower and soil moisture is higher.
- Average annual water yield from the upper hard-rock area was 6,110 acre-feet, or 0.61 inch. This is 3.6 percent of precipitation. The annual yield ranged from 167 acre-feet (1961) to 14,320 acre-feet (1965). The authors caution that five years is too short for a reliable average.
- About 162,000 acre-feet per year (96.4 percent of precipitation) is returned to the atmosphere by evapotranspiration from the upper hard-rock area.
- Flood potential: the mean annual flood for the 165-square-mile area is about 3,400 cfs. The 50-year flood is about 4.45 times this, or roughly 15,000 cfs. The watershed lies in hydrologic area 18 and flood-frequency region C (Patterson and Somers, 1966).

**Transmission losses in the lower alluvial area**
- Streamflow rapidly infiltrates the alluvial channel. On February 13, 1962, 20 cfs disappeared in the first 3 miles below the gage. On March 14, 1962, 40 cfs disappeared in 4 miles. On March 23, 1962, a steady 200 cfs disappeared within the 9-mile reach.
- A peak of about 200 cfs at the Fort McDowell gage is required before any flow reaches the Verde River. Only 0 to 10 percent of inflow to the lower area reaches the river as surface flow.
- Over five years, Sycamore Creek reached the Verde River on 18 occasions. The average surface discharge was about 500 acre-feet per year, under 10 percent of upper-area runoff.
- One measurement set showed a decrease from 200 cfs at Fort McDowell to 0.3 cfs at the Verde River.

**Aquifer properties and groundwater movement**
- Unconsolidated alluvium is the main water-bearing unit. It has a permeability of 5,200 gpd per square foot and a transmissibility of 264,000 gpd per foot (well 18, constant-discharge test). Its specific yield is 25 to 30 percent.
- Consolidated alluvium is much less permeable. Its average permeability was about 8.3 gpd per square foot, from three aquifer tests. The slug test at well 20 exceeded the method's upper limit and was not reliable.
- Calculated groundwater velocity was 18.5 feet per day (about 6,750 feet per year) in the unconsolidated alluvium and about 0.04 foot per day (about 15 feet per year) in the consolidated alluvium. Movement is roughly 450 times faster in the unconsolidated unit.
- Recharge is concentrated in the upper half of the lower alluvial area, where most infiltration occurs. Direct precipitation is considered an insignificant recharge source.

**Groundwater discharge and water budget**
- Subsurface discharge to the Verde River is about 4,000 acre-feet per year (5.6 cfs), consistent with the average gain measured in the Verde River below the confluence. Darcy's calculation gave 3.64 × 10⁶ gpd.
- In October 1964, a 0.80-foot drop in Verde River stage produced a 0.21-foot drop in the water level in well 18, showing hydraulic connection between the aquifer and the river.
- The five-year average water-budget analysis gives an evapotranspiration loss of about 1,500 acre-feet per year in the lower area. The authors estimate this at about 1.1 acre-feet per acre of phreatophyte cover. Assumptions of zero net change in storage add some uncertainty.
- Channel losses between the Sunflower and Fort McDowell gages were 120 acre-feet in 1962 and 820 acre-feet in 1963 (Hydrology section). The Summary gives a 120–320 acre-foot range for the five years. The two statements differ and are reported here as written.

**Main conclusion:** most of the water from the mountains never reaches the river as surface flow. Instead it recharges the alluvial aquifer, is stored temporarily, and is then either returned as steady groundwater discharge (about 4,000 acre-feet per year) or lost to evapotranspiration (about 1,500 acre-feet per year). The authors state that the methods may apply to other watersheds in the arid Southwest.
