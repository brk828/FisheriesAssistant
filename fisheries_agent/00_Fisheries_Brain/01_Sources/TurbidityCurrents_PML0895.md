# Turbidity Currents in Lake Mead

#limnology #sedimentology #turbidity-currents

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey paper by H.R. Gould (OCR reads "Gouro") is a chapter from the *Comprehensive Survey of Sedimentation in Lake Mead, 1948–49*. It defines turbidity currents as gravity flows of sediment-laden water that move because of density differences. It then describes the three forms of these currents seen in [[Lake Mead]]: [[Underflow]], [[Interflow]] and [[Overflow]]. Underflow is the dominant and most important type. The paper uses sediment-trap, density and deposit data to show how these flows delivered sediment from the [[Colorado River]] and [[Virgin River]] deep into the reservoir. It also discusses how [[Flocculation]] and channel slope affect flow behaviour.

## Background & Description

**Definition and classification.** A turbidity current is a density current in which suspended sediment accounts for part of the density difference. Temperature and salinity can also contribute, and sediment is not always the dominant factor. Gould adopts the broad definition of density current from Bell (1942b), which includes gravity flows over, under or through fluid of approximately equal density. The three types are:

- **Underflow:** turbid river water sinks beneath clear standing water and flows along the bottom.
- **Interflow:** turbid water enters a layer of intermediate density within a stratified lake.
- **Overflow:** turbid water is less dense than the lake surface (warmer or less saline) and spreads over it.

**Setting.** The Colorado River enters [[Lake Mead]] and transports large quantities of silt and clay into the deeper basins, including [[Boulder Basin]] near [[Hoover Dam]]. [[Virgin Basin]] acts as a large mixing basin that smooths seasonal salinity variations in the inflowing Colorado River water. Below Virgin Basin the water is nearly uniform in salinity.

**Why few currents are identified.** The author notes that density currents degenerate through mixing with surrounding water. This may explain why relatively few have been identified in the investigations that began in 1937, mainly in Boulder Basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This paper covers reservoir sedimentation and physical limnology. It does not study fish or other aquatic organisms, so no species data are reported.

- **Stratification.** Lake water is stratified in temperature and salinity, with colder, saltier and denser water at depth. Underflows and interflows depend on this layering.
- **Seasonal overflows.** Overflows occur mainly from late April to early July, when the Colorado River is warmer or less saline than the lake surface. Sand from the river is deposited in the delta foreset and topset beds during this period.
- **Overton Arm and the Virgin River.** Sediment from the Virgin River has accumulated mainly in the upper 14 miles of [[Overton Arm]]. The [[Lower Narrows]] gauging station, 9.3 miles upstream of the Virgin–Colorado confluence, recorded suspended sediment that came and went over time.
- **Debris as an indicator.** Logs carried by the Colorado River stay near the river mouth in most seasons. During overflow periods they are carried downlake and stranded on shorelines. Their distribution showed that overflows rarely extended west of Virgin Basin and never reached the dam.

## Key Findings

**1. Overflows are limited in extent.**
- In May 1948, about 10 miles below the Colorado River entrance (mile 287.5), the sediment proportion by weight rose from 0.013 percent at the surface to 0.110 percent near the bottom.
- Overflows deposit much of their sediment during the first few miles of travel.
- In May 1948 the westernmost boundary of the overflow was in Virgin Basin, where suspended sediment ranged from 0.007 percent at the surface to 0.009 percent at the bottom.

**2. Underflow is the dominant current.**
- Underflows move the main sediment load, mostly during periods of high-density river water.
- The data show that sediment rises in [[Boulder Basin]] generally follow peak water densities measured at the [[Grand Canyon]] station, with a lag between the two.
- Density of incoming river water is computed from temperature, salinity and suspended sediment. Gould notes that suspended sediment is not always the main source of density.
- The November 1948 underflow west of Iceberg Canyon was about 9 feet thick, with its density increasing toward the bottom.

**3. Sediment transport and deposition.**
- Gould estimates that about 60 percent of the sediment accumulated in Boulder Basin was carried in by underflows (the OCR reads "b0").
- About 80 percent of sediment carried by turbidity currents accumulated more than 43 miles from the head of the reservoir. About 23 percent accumulated more than 100 miles from the head.
- Delta deposits near Temple Bar, about 40 miles above Hoover Dam, reach a minimum thickness at mile 314.1. The OCR for this value is unclear.

**4. Channel changes.**
- Sediment deposition reduced the average slope of the former Colorado River channel. The greatest reduction occurred in Boulder Basin, where the sediment surface slopes less than 1 foot per mile. This happened largely before 1942.
- The channel also widened during the first years of reservoir operation. The authors suggest that the reduced slope and wider channel may partly explain the decline in turbid flow into the western lake since 1942. Infrequent high-density inflows may be the main reason for fewer major underflows after 1942.

**5. Particle size and flocculation.**
- Estimated median diameter of transported particles is 1.65 microns in the dispersed state. The reported composition is 67 percent clay (smaller than 4 microns). The silt figure is garbled in the scan, and the sand fraction is less than 1 percent.
- Sediment is horizontally graded: coarser particles settle near the river mouth and finer particles farther away.
- Sediment settles as flocculated masses 2 to 20 times larger than single grains.
- Gould argues that flocculation may help maintain turbid underflows. Flocs trap water and can flow on gentler slopes than unflocculated sediment, which contradicts earlier views (Johnson, 1939).

**6. Virgin River currents.**
- Virgin River currents are of minor quantitative importance. Most of their sediment settles in Overton Arm.
- Suspended sediment was recorded at Lower Narrows in early 1938 and mid-1938. It was absent from 1939 to early 1940, then reappeared in March 1940 and was partly deposited.
- By June 1940 a layer of silt and clay, a few tenths of a foot to about 2 feet thick, covered the sand in the inundated Virgin River channel. Some of this sediment likely came from the Colorado River through overflows.

**Summary.** Underflow dominates sediment transport in Lake Mead. Overflows are seasonal and short-ranged. Flocculation and channel geometry influence how far the currents travel. Most sediment accumulates in the lower reservoir, and the Virgin River contributes only a small share.
