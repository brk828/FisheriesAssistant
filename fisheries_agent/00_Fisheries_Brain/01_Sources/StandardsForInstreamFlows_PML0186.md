# Standards for Instream Flows

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #instream-flow

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Hal A. Beecher of the [[Washington Department of Wildlife]] (Habitat Management Division), appeared in the journal *Rivers* (Volume 1, Number 2, pages 97–109, 1990). It argues that [[Instream Flow Standards]] are vaguely defined in the laws of most western states, and that this ambiguity has fueled unresolved controversy over water allocation in Washington. The author proposes that a clear standard should function as a formula combining biological and hydrological information to set a range of instream flows for a given stream reach. Five elements are identified as necessary for an unambiguous standard, and a standard for [[Fish Habitat Protection]] is offered as a worked example.

## Background & Description

**Scope and purpose.** The paper addresses policy design rather than a field study. Its central claim is that many states have established programs for instream uses of water, but few have reached consensus on what those programs are meant to achieve.

**Terminology.** The term "minimum" is attached to instream flows in several states, including Colorado, Idaho, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska, Oregon, Washington, and Wyoming (McKinney and Taylor 1988). Oregon and Alaska place specific hydrologic upper limits on instream flows. Oregon law sets optimum levels for managing fish habitat and so indirectly governs instream flows for fish. Oregon's 1955 instream flow statute is also discussed, though the text is partly illegible.

**Legal context.** Although 15 states have laws allowing the establishment and protection of instream flows (McKinney and Taylor 1988; Reiser et al. 1989), the standards in those laws are vague. The author notes that a clearly defined standard would prescribe instream flows within relatively narrow limits, with specific information about the stream reach built into the formula.

**Requirements of a sound standard.** According to the paper, an instream flow standard should:
1. Define the goal of the instream use of water.
2. State the extent to which the goal is to be achieved.
3. Identify criteria for evaluating whether the goal has been achieved.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish habitat as the target use.** Fish habitat protection is the main instream use discussed, and the example standard is built around it.
- **Hydrological and biological inputs.** The proposed formula draws on both biological requirements of aquatic organisms and the hydrology of the reach.
- **Water allocation pressure.** Future water appropriation (withdrawals for other uses) competes with instream flow needs. The paper treats the choice of standard as a direct determinant of how much water remains in streams.
- **Regional scope.** The discussion spans Washington, Oregon, Alaska, and other western states, reflecting common legal and management problems across the region.

## Key Findings

1. **Ambiguity is the central problem.** Instream flow standards in most western states lack a clear, measurable goal. This vagueness has produced unresolved controversy over water allocation in Washington and leads to costly delays in water resource planning.

2. **Five elements define an unambiguous standard.** The author identifies:
   - the **goal** of the standard;
   - the **resources** to be considered;
   - the **unit of measurement**;
   - the **benchmark time period**; and
   - the **protection statistic**.

   Future water management options and the resulting instream resource levels depend on the choices made for each element.

3. **Proposed fish habitat standard.** An instream flow standard for fish habitat protection is offered as an example. Its implementation would restrict future water appropriation to large, high-gradient rivers. The paper presents this as a consequence of the recommended standards rather than as a tested outcome.

4. **Clear goals reduce controversy.** Programs that lack a clear, measurable goal are expected to produce more controversy and vague results. The statutory language of a program should therefore state a measurable goal at minimum.

5. **Widespread legal framework, weak definition.** Fifteen states have enabling laws for instream flows, yet few have precise standards, so the paper calls for better-defined formulas across these programs.
