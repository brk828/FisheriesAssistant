# Fishes and Aquatic Resources of the Death Valley System, California–Nevada: An Annotated Bibliography (1878–1976)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a comprehensive bibliography on the native fishes and aquatic resources of the [[Death Valley System]], a Pleistocene drainage network spanning eastern California and southwestern Nevada. Compiled by Robert Rush Miller (University of Michigan), David L. Soltz (California State University, Los Angeles), and Peter G. Sanchez (National Park Service, Death Valley), it was published in April 1977 by the [[National Park Service]], Western Region. It covers technical publications, reports, popular books, and magazine articles through 1976, with a few 1977 articles in press. Newspaper stories are excluded. The bibliography also includes references on associated aquatic organisms, geography, geology, and hydrology. Its stated purpose is to gather in one place the sources needed to understand the region's native fishes, their habitats, and the water supplies on which their survival depends.

## Background & Description

**Scope.** The bibliography addresses the Death Valley system, described as a formerly integrated Pleistocene drainage extending from the Mono Lake basin and Owens Valley, on the eastern flanks of the Sierra Nevada, through interbasin ranges around [[Death Valley]], to the [[Amargosa River]] and its former and present tributaries in southwestern Nevada. This drainage provided routes by which the region's fishes spread. Articles on closely related fishes outside the system are also included.

**Audience.** The preface states that the work is intended for researchers, students, writers, administrators, and interested laypeople. Desert fishes are described as valued for their adaptation to widely fluctuating temperature and salinity, and as part of a vanishing native fauna.

**Authorship and production.** The compilers are a university zoologist and curator of fishes, a university biology professor, and a National Park Service resources management specialist. Acknowledgments credit numerous contributors, including Francis H. Miller for literature searches and typing drafts, and Howard W. Wittlinger for the typescript. The cover and printing were arranged by the Western Region Chief Scientist, and distribution was handled by the superintendent of [[Death Valley National Monument]].

**Structure.** The main list is alphabetical by author and runs from about 1878 (Lyle, springs of southern Nevada) to 1977. An addendum lists further geological, paleontological, and mollusc references, and a supplementary set covers related Pleistocene and geologic material. Exact entry totals are not stated in the text, so this note reports the structure rather than a count.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish taxa treated.** The bibliography centers on the cyprinodontid pupfishes of the genus *Cyprinodon*, with the [[Amargosa Pupfish]] (*C. nevadensis*), [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*C. diabolis*), [[Desert Pupfish]] (*C. macularius*), [[Salt Creek Pupfish]] (*C. salinus*), and *C. milleri* as principal species. Related *Empetrichthys* (Warm Springs pupfish) and the *Apocope nevadensis* minnow also appear. Introduced fishes, including a tiger barb (*Barbus*) in a thermal stream and mosquitofish, are noted as threats to natives.

**Habitats.** Native fishes occupy springs, warm springs, marshes, and streams in arid basins. Key localities include [[Devils Hole]], [[Saratoga Springs]], [[Ash Meadows]], [[Salt Creek]], [[Quitobaquito Springs]] (Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument), and the [[Salton Sea]] region. Several entries address the [[Pleistocene Lakes]] that once connected these waters, including [[Lake Manly]] and lakes of the Owens and Mono basins.

**Physiological and ecological themes.** The literature covers thermal tolerance, salinity and oxygen effects on development, growth, and metabolism, daily movement, social behavior, feeding and predation (including predation on mosquito larvae), and population ecology. Evolutionary themes include speciation, hybridization, and genetic divergence among populations, studied through biochemical and morphological methods.

**Hydrology and geology.** Groundwater studies form a large share of the references. These cover aquifer systems, spring sources, water-level fluctuations, regional carbonate aquifers, and the effects of pumping and irrigation on spring pool levels, including at Devils Hole and Ash Meadows. Geological entries address basin history, faulting, and the origin of springs.

**Threats and management.** Many works address habitat loss from groundwater withdrawal, introduced species, and small population sizes. Management proposals, task force reports, recovery plans, and conservation notes are included.

## Key Findings

The bibliography's main contents and themes are as follows:

- **Scope and coverage:** It gathers technical, popular, and agency literature from roughly 1878 to 1976, with a few 1977 items in press. It is a reference work, not a research study, and it reports no new field data.
- **Core taxonomic literature:** Robert Rush Miller's work forms the backbone of the list. It includes a 1944 PhD thesis on the relict waters of the Pleistocene Death Valley stream system, a 1948 monograph on the cyprinodont fishes of the system, and a 1946 paper correlating fish distribution with Pleistocene hydrography. Miller's 1967 report on the status of native fish populations is also listed.
- **Devils Hole Pupfish conservation:** A large cluster of entries documents the species' decline and protection. These include Deacon and Bunnell (1970) on the species' decline as "a process of destruction," reports from a 1970 Task Force and a 1971 Task Force status report, and a 1976 entry titled "US Supreme Court rules for survival of pupfish," with a related Sanchez (1976) notice. Devils Hole entries also cover water-level and groundwater studies (Mehringer and Haynes, 1972; Larson, 1974; Walker and Eakin, 1963).
- **Physiology and development:** Studies on the Amargosa and Devils Hole pupfishes by Kinne and Kinne (1962a, 1962b), Shrode (1974, 1975), and Shrode and Gerking (1977) address effects of temperature, salinity, and oxygen on embryonic development and reproductive performance. Lowe and Heath (1969) examine behavioral and physiological responses to temperature.
- **Ecology and behavior:** Field studies cover daily movements (Barlow, 1958), high-salinity mortality, social behavior (Liu, Soltz), food habits (Cox, 1962; Naiman, 1975), and predation on mosquitoes and midges (Legner et al., 1975). Naiman and colleagues study primary productivity, thermal environment, and osmoregulation in the Death Valley pupfish.
- **Genetics and evolution:** Turner (1972–1977) and Turner and Liu (1976, 1977a, 1977b) examine genetic divergence among species, interspecific compatibility, and the identity of an introduced pupfish at Zzyzx Spring. Hubbs and Miller (1941, 1948) address hybridization and the biogeography of the group.
- **Geology and paleontology:** Entries on Pleistocene lakes, fossil cyprinodonts, and freshwater mollusks document the drainage history used to explain fish distribution. Key works include Blackwelder (1933), Snyder et al. (1964), and Miller's paleontological papers on fossil cyprinodonts (1945).
- **Management and status:** The compilers note that many species are endangered, and that native habitats depend on spring and groundwater supplies. Recovery planning, conservation notes, and public-interest articles (for example, Lyle, 1878; Nappe, 1972a; Miller, 1972) are included.
- **Forward-looking note:** The compilers expect some references to be missing and invite corrections for future editions. Soltz and Naiman (1977, in press) on the natural history of native fishes in the Death Valley system is listed as a forthcoming synthesis.
