# Fisheries Survey of Hopkins Lake, Shiawassee County

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This report, Report No. 815 by C. J. D. Brown of the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]] ([[Michigan Department of Conservation]]), is a small-scale fisheries survey of [[Hopkins Lake]], a 7.9-acre lake at the south edge of [[Owosso]] in [[Shiawassee County]], Michigan. It was reprinted in part from *Michigan Conservation* (November 1942). The report is presented as a miniature example of the historical, physical, chemical and biological inventories the Institute had already completed on hundreds of Michigan lakes.

The survey found physical, chemical and biological conditions that the author judged "not favorable for high fish production." The lake has a peat bottom, high color and turbidity, and oxygen-depleted deep water. Nine fish species were collected. The game species were mostly sub-legal in length and showed stunted growth in bluegills and pumpkinseeds. The main recommendations were to reclassify the lake, stock [[Largemouth Bass]] and forage fish, improve spawning areas, and consider a [[Peat Removal Experiment]] to pump out part of the accumulated peat.

## Background & Description

**Location and access.** Hopkins Lake, also called Hopkins Pond, lies at the south edge of the City of Owosso (T 7 N., R. 2 E., Sec. 25). It is reached from [[M-47]] by a one-quarter mile gravel road. The lake has no inlet. It is connected to the [[Shiawassee River]], about a mile away, by a small outlet.

**Survey history.** The lake was mapped by an Institute party in February 1941 ([[Winter Lake Mapping]]). Fish collections were made by another party in June, and the regular [[Limnological Survey]] was conducted in July of the same year.

**Use.** The lake has never had industrial use. Because it lies almost within the Owosso city limits and the area is nearly without other lakes, the report considers its recreational potential to be rather high. There is no resort, cottage development or boat livery. Most surrounding land belongs to the City of Owosso, which has developed a city park on the east side. The lake is reported to be heavily fished in summer and winter, despite reports of poor fishing.

**Physical characteristics.**
- Geological origin is unknown.
- The basin is roughly oval, with the long axis running almost due north-south. The shoreline has no embayments or pronounced irregularities.
- Shoreline development is 1.07, meaning the circumference is only very slightly greater than that of a circle of equal area.
- Maximum depth is 24 feet.
- Water comes from seeps, springs and surface runoff. The outlet is about 6 feet wide and 6 inches deep. The lake level fluctuates only slightly.
- The bottom is entirely peat: fibrous peat in shallow water and pulpy peat beyond the 15-foot contour. The peat is soft and probably deep.

**Mapping methods.** Mapping was done in winter on the ice, usually in the season before the fisheries survey. Methods included plane-table sighting, drilling through the ice, and sounding with a lead line on 100 feet of cable. The [[Ekman Dredge]] was used to sample bottom material for soil analysis and for plant and animal life.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature and oxygen (July 22).**
- Surface temperature was 74°F and bottom temperature 46°F.
- The [[Thermocline]] extended between the 8-foot and 20-foot levels.
- Dissolved oxygen was 7.6 ppm at 8 feet and absent at the bottom. No thermocline samples were taken, but the author assumed little or no oxygen below 15 feet. Decaying peat probably reduces oxygen below the tolerance of fishes.

**Water chemistry.**
- Water is hard (Methyl Orange alkalinity 165–264 ppm) and distinctly alkaline (pH given as 74–82 in the OCR, most likely 7.4–8.2).
- The author notes that alkaline, hard-water lakes are generally more productive than soft, acid lakes, though optimum alkalinity and hardness are not known.
- Surface temperatures suit warm-water fish, but low oxygen prevents use of water below 15 feet in late summer.
- No pollution has been reported.

**Light and turbidity.** A [[Secchi Disc]] was visible only to 5½ feet. The water is light brown in color and turbid, which limits light penetration and plant production.

**Aquatic vegetation.**
- Plant beds were dense from shore to about the 5–6 foot contour. No rooted plants were found deeper.
- Cattails and yellow water lilies were abundant near shore. Coontail was common in water 3–6 feet deep.
- The author judged the weed beds fair, but the species present, especially cattails and water lilies, are not the most desirable for shelter or fish food.

**Fish food organisms.**
- Plankton was abundant at the time of survey, mostly animal forms. One sample was considered insufficient to judge abundance, but plankton production is believed to be very good.
- Scuds, mayflies and dragonflies were the most abundant organisms on aquatic plants. Leeches, bloodworms, snails and midge larvae were also present.
- Overall food conditions were judged fair to good, but not excellent.

**Fish community.** Nine species were collected: three game fish, five coarse fish and one forage fish. The [[Golden Shiner]] was the only forage fish found.

**Parasites and predators.** None of the fish examined had parasites, and they were "surprisingly free" from common parasitic forms. No predators were reported. Black crappie is the only piscivorous species in the lake. Numerous stumps and deadheads provide adequate shelter.

## Key Findings

**Physical and chemical limits**
- The lake's physical factors are "not favorable for high fish production." The peat bottom, lack of solid substrate, high color, turbidity and lack of shallow bays limit fish food and fish.
- Oxygen is absent below about 15 feet in late summer, so the usable fish habitat is limited to the upper water column.

**Stocking and creel data**
- Records show no fish were planted in the last 8 years. A local resident, Mr. M. F. Blair of Owosso, claims perch were planted about four or five years earlier.
- No creel census data exist for this lake.

**Growth of game species**
- Nearly all game fish captured were sub-legal in length.
- Bluegills in their fourth summer reached 5.4 inches on average (given as "54 inches" in the OCR, corrected to match the growth table). Pumpkinseeds in their seventh summer averaged 4.7 inches (given as "47 inches" in the OCR, corrected to match the table).
- The author considers bluegills and pumpkinseeds stunted. Black crappies grow at about an average rate and reach legal length in their third summer.

**Management proposals**
1. **Classification:** Hopkins Lake is currently classed as a "pike lake." Because pike are absent and the lake suits [[Largemouth Bass]] and bluegills better, the report recommends reclassifying it as an "all other lakes" class.
2. **Stocking:** Stock largemouth bass, preferably 200 adults in the first year, or a liberal planting of large fingerlings. No other game species should be planted. Introduce black-nosed or black-chinned shiners and mudminnows to add forage. Make introductions in the fall.
3. **Water level:** If feasible and justified by cost, raise the level by two or three feet and hold it as constant as possible.
4. **Spawning areas:**
   - If water is raised, place gravel on the present shoreline so it is submerged later.
   - If not, build gravel-filled cribs or boxes and place them in 18 inches to 3 feet of water.
   - Current reproduction appears adequate for bluegills and pumpkinseeds despite poor spawning conditions.
5. **Peat removal experiment:** Given the lake's potential value, the report suggests pumping out at least part of the accumulated peat. Expected benefits include exposed spawning grounds, lower turbidity and color, more vegetation, and less risk of winter-kill. The other management steps should wait until the pumping outcome is known, and the Institute should conduct a thorough pre-pumping study and checks during and after.

**Conclusion.** The lake is poor for fish production due to its physical and chemical limits, but it has good recreational potential close to Owosso. Improvement depends on stocking, habitat work, and possibly the peat removal experiment.
