# A New Butterflyfish, *Chaetodon falcifer*, from Guadalupe Island, Baja California, with Notes on Related Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #chaetodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 taxonomic paper by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Andreas B. Rechnitzer]] (Scripps Institution of Oceanography) describes [[Chaetodon Falcifer]], a new butterflyfish known from a single adult specimen taken at about 100 ft off the east shore of [[Guadalupe Island]], [[Baja California]]. The species is distinguished by a long, sharply produced snout, small scales, and a distinctive scythe-shaped dark body mark. The authors regard it as probably endemic to Guadalupe Island, a tropical element in a mixed warm-temperate and tropical fauna, and possibly the northernmost chaetodontid in the eastern Pacific. Comparisons with Atlantic species ([[Chaetodon Aya]], [[Chaetodon Marcellae]]) and the West Indian [[Prognathodes Aculeatus]] lead to conclusions about convergent beak evolution in the family and the taxonomic weight of the [[Lateral Line]] character.

## Background & Description

**Type specimen.** The holotype is a large adult, 188 mm standard length, collected 16 November 1954 at 100 ft on the east shore of Guadalupe Island (Cat. No. 20734, Department of Fishes, California Academy of Sciences). The authors say the fish was speared by Rechnitzer during a deep-poisoning operation using derris-root paste, supplemented by Primacord detonation on 17 November. It is the only specimen known. The text uses 138 mm as the standard length for its proportional measurements, a figure that also appears as the maximum known size in the comparison table.

**Diagnostic characters.**
- Dorsal fin XIII, 21; anal fin III, 16; pectoral rays 15; principal caudal rays 9 + 8 = 17.
- Very long, sharply produced snout (the beak); lacks a suborbital bar, with the nuchal band extending through the eye along the snout.
- Small scales: 51–53 transverse rows from the upper gill opening to the caudal flexure; 64–66 scales in the first row above the lateral line; 44–46 lateral-line pores; about 30 scale rows around the caudal peduncle.
- High, strong dorsal and anal spines with deeply incised interspinal membranes.
- Vertebrae 10 + 14 (including the hypural plate); 12 nearly straight cross rows of fine inner teeth across the tip of the beak; gill rakers 5 + 11.
- Body square behind the head; body depth 554 per thousand standard length.
- Scythe-shaped mark on the head and body, deep brown-purple in life, which gives the species its name (*falcifer*, "scythe-bearer").

**Comparative morphology.** Table 1 contrasts *C. falcifer* with *C. aya* (17 specimens examined). Key contrasts include dorsal soft rays (21 versus 17–19), scales in the lateral-line row (64–66 versus 32–38), and the form of the posterior dark mark. The maximum known size is 138 mm for *C. falcifer* and 98.5 mm for *C. aya*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Collecting and habitat.** The collecting station (SLO 54-219) was a joint operation of two Scripps-led parties using the research ships *Paolina* and *Orca*, with thirteen collectors (six using aqualungs). The site was a cove on the generally calm eastern shore, about 0.5 mi north of Morro Sur, with a vertical rock wall penetrated by recesses and crevices. Shallower water held attached *Sargassum*, other algae, and eelgrass (*Zostera*). Water temperature was 65 °F from top to bottom, and the bottom at about 100 ft was rock with some sand and gravel.

**Associated fauna.** The collection included 33 associated species, of which the authors treat as approximate associates of the butterflyfish. These span Sternoptychidae to Balistidae, including serranids, labrids, embiotocids, pomacentrids, gobiids, and clinids. Several are marked as endemic to Guadalupe Island or as possibly differentiated, and some occur on the San Benito Islands or the Galápagos Islands.

**Distribution and endemism.** The authors consider it "highly probable" that *C. falcifer* is more common than one specimen suggests. The species might occur deeper than 100 ft, as its Atlantic relatives do, or on the poorly collected western shore, where surf limits collecting. They think it unlikely that it occurs on the mainland or the Revillagigedo Islands, which were extensively collected. It is therefore provisionally listed as endemic to Guadalupe Island.

**Biogeographic context.** Guadalupe is an oceanic island about 225 nautical miles south and slightly west of San Diego, separated by 133 miles from the nearest other land. The authors describe a high incidence of endemism in its littoral marine and terrestrial biota. *C. falcifer* is a tropical element in the intermixed warm-temperate and tropical fauna, and it may represent the northern limit of the Chaetodontidae in the eastern Pacific.

**Historical record.** Two *Chaetodon humeralis* specimens in the United States National Museum (catalog no. 3170) were recorded from San Diego, collected by Lt. W. P. Trowbridge on the Pacific Railroad Surveys, nearly a century ago. This suggests tropical fishes reached San Diego during a warm period. The authors note some doubt about the record but consider it probably valid.

## Key Findings

1. **New species described.** *Chaetodon falcifer* is a distinct butterflyfish, known only from the holotype (188 mm SL, 100 ft, Guadalupe Island). Its distinctiveness rests on snout form, scale size and alignment, lateral-line position, and color pattern.

2. **Not closely related to eastern Pacific congeners.** *C. humeralis* is retained in *Chaetodon*. *C. nigrirostris* is provisionally moved to [[Heniochus Nigrirostris]] because its lateral line runs to the caudal base. *Forcipiger longirostris* differs from *C. falcifer* in beak extent, lateral-line form, and coloration.

3. **Indo-Pacific origin ruled out.** Comparison with the many Indo-Pacific species found "nothing corresponding closely," so the authors conclude it is not a stray from the present Indo-Pacific fauna.

4. **Closest relatives are Atlantic.** *C. falcifer* most resembles [[Chaetodon Aya]] (western Atlantic) and [[Chaetodon Marcellae]] (tropical West Africa). Shared features include a produced muzzle, strong excised fin spines, regular scale alignment, and deep-water habitat. The authors consider the resemblances stronger than the differences, which are tabulated.

5. **Deep-water pattern.** Atlantic relatives were taken at depths of roughly 20–150 fathoms. *C. marcellae* was taken at 85 m on sandy brown mud. The authors treat this shared deep-water habitat as supporting relationship.

6. **Beak convergence.** [[Prognathodes Aculeatus]] (Cuba) appears derived from a *C. aya*-like ancestor, with a more extreme beak. The authors retain *Prognathodes* as a monotypic genus pending wider study.

7. **Lateral line as a key character.** The authors treat the lateral-line position as the most important phylogenetic character in the family. Beaked species with a complete lateral line (*Chelmon*, *Chelmonops*, *Forcipiger*) are grouped with *Heniochus*, while beaked species with an incomplete line (*C. falcifer*, *C. aya*, *C. marcellae*, *P. aculeatus*) form a separate phyletic line.

8. **Independent evolution of beaks.** The beak evolved independently in lineages with complete and incomplete lateral lines. The authors criticize earlier workers for classing all beaked chaetodontids together. The beak is a feeding adaptation for "plucking food out of crevices" on overgrazed reefs, and similar beaks occur in Siganidae and Labridae.

9. **New World relationships.** *Heniochus nigrirostris* and *Forcipiger longirostris* are the only New World members of the *Heniochus* series, and both are confined to the Pacific. They are interpreted as migrants from the Indo-Pacific, relatively old and recent respectively.

10. **Pomacanthinae.** The authors suggest the Pomacanthinae may be polyphyletic and not worthy of subfamily rank. They reject elevating it to family rank (Pomacanthidae) as proposed by Smith (1955), even though they consider it a natural assemblage.

11. **Zoogeographic patterns.** The authors cite two tendencies in critical taxonomic work: New World fishes show marked independence from Old World lineages, and New World types are sparingly represented in tropical West Africa.

12. **Provisional endemism.** *C. falcifer* is probably endemic to Guadalupe Island, but this status is provisional given limited sampling of deep and western-shore habitats.
