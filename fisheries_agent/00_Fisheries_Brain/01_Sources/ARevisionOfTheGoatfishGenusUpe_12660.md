# A Revision of the Goatfish Genus Upeneus (Mullidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mullidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 taxonomic revision by Ernest A. Lachner of the goatfish genus [[Upeneus]] (family [[Mullidae]]) resolves nomenclatural confusion among 24 nominal species. Based mainly on [[United States National Museum]] collections, with additional material from the [[Museum of Comparative Zoology]], the author recognizes **ten valid species**, two of them new: [[Upeneus Asymmetricus]] and [[Upeneus Oligospilus]]. The study sets out diagnostic characters, evaluates interspecific differentiation, and tests for population divergence across the Indo-Pacific. It finds little evidence of geographic differentiation within species, which the author interprets as differentiation having reached the species level followed by stability.

## Background & Description

Earlier faunal works by [[Herre and Montalban]] (1928), [[Weber and de Beaufort]] (1931) and [[Fowler]] (1933) treated the genus, but their species lists and characters were inconsistent. Herre and Montalban recognized six species in the Philippines, Weber and de Beaufort recognized the same six in the Indo-Australian Archipelago, and Fowler listed eleven for the Philippines, five of them extralimital. Specimens from the Philippines and the [[Persian Gulf]] matched none of the published accounts.

Methods follow these conventions:
- All lengths are standard length.
- Meristic counts are reported as mean: range (number of specimens).
- Dark caudal-fin lobe tip spots were counted as bars; small midbase spots were omitted.

**Genus characters (as given):**
- Complete dentition, with villiform teeth on the vomer, palatines and both jaws.
- Scales present on the soft dorsal, anal and caudal fins.
- Elongate, somewhat compressed body; dark oblique caudal bars in most species.
- Small size relative to other mullids.
- Common features include 16 scale rows around the caudal peduncle, ctenoid scales, a complete lateral line, and a barbel with less than one-third of its length rigid at the base.

**Diagnostic characters, in order of importance:**
1. Number of dorsal spines (7 or 8), linked to a minute first spine.
2. Pectoral fin rays.
3. Total gillrakers, with upper and lower limbs and rudiments counted separately.
4. Peritoneum color.
5. Vertical scale rows.
6. Color marks, especially caudal fin bars.
7. Barbel length.

Preorbital scales, body depth, head and snout proportions, eye size, intestinal loops and pyloric caeca were found unreliable or not taxonomically useful.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** The genus lives in tropical and subtropical, littoral, marine waters of the Indo-Pacific and the Western Atlantic. Life histories of these inshore fishes are poorly known.
- **Larvae:** Larval forms of some species are thought to be pelagic, and drift may explain minor connectivity among populations.
- **Distribution:** The East Indies and Philippines hold at least seven species; four are known from East Africa, four from Oceania, and one (arge) from Hawaii. Five extend north to southern Japan, and at least three reach eastern Australia. Only *U. parvus* occurs in the Western Atlantic.
- **Population structure:** Widely distributed species (vittatus, sulphureus, tragula) are presumed isolated among subfaunal areas yet remain morphologically homogeneous. The author treats these conclusions as tentative because specimen numbers are limited for several species.

## Key Findings

**Species status and nomenclature**
- Ten valid species are recognized, two new. Two nominal species are classed as doubtful: *U. sundaicus* (possibly a synonym of *tragula* or *luzonius*) and *U. taeniopterus* (known only from the type).
- *Mullus dubius* Temminck and Schlegel was re-examined and referred to the genus *Parupeneus*.
- *Upeneus phillipsi* Fowler was shown to be a juvenile of *Mullus auratus*.
- Fowler's counts for the Atlantic *U. parvus* (eight dorsal spines, toothed upper jaw, 16 pectoral rays) were found to be inaccurate. The author counted seven spines, no upper-jaw teeth, and 15 pectoral rays.

**Key diagnostic results**
- Dorsal spine count is 7 or 8 per species, with no variability in more than 300 specimens. Fowler's reported variability was judged erroneous.
- Pectoral ray counts (range never exceeding four per species) split the species into two groups, with modes at 13 or 14 versus 16.
- Indo-Pacific species with light peritoneum have fewer gillrakers than those with dark peritoneum. This relationship fails when *U. parvus* is included.
- Scale row counts vary by no more than five within any species, but the scales are deciduous, so counts may be impossible on large or poorly preserved specimens.
- Caudal fin bar numbers (on upper and lower lobes) are a key character. Bar number increases with growth in some species and stays constant in others.
- Preorbital scale presence was judged unreliable for identification because the scales are deciduous.

**Group relationships**
- Three methods split the species into two groups: the presence or absence of the minute first dorsal spine; peritoneum color combined with gillraker number; and pectoral ray count.
- The author suggests the minute first spine may reflect an ontogenetic change producing two phyletic lines, but declines to propose subgeneric names.

**Key species distinctions**
- [[Upeneus Oligospilus]] (Persian Gulf) differs from [[Upeneus Tragula]] in having fewer caudal bars, fewer and larger diffuse body spots absent on the belly, a deeper body and shorter pectoral and pelvic fins, and a higher modal pectoral ray count.
- [[Upeneus Luzonius]] and [[Upeneus Tragula]] are closely related and easily confused when small. Their key distinctions are pectoral ray count, gillraker number, barbel length, body spotting and saddle pattern (Table 11).
- Misidentification between *tragula* and *luzonius* in the literature led to the uncertain status of *sundaicus*.

**Population differentiation**
- No notable differentiation was found among subfaunal areas for meristic counts, proportions, or color, even across localities about 12,000 miles apart (*U. vittatus*).
- The author regards these results as tentative given small sample sizes.

**Specimen base**
- *U. bensasi*: 115 specimens (37–148 mm).
- *U. sulphureus*: 217 specimens (48–132 mm).
- *U. vittatus*: 150 specimens (56–226 mm).
- *U. tragula*: 188 specimens (29–227 mm).
- *U. moluccensis*: 21 specimens (49–125 mm).
- *U. luzonius*: 16 specimens (25–119 mm).
- *U. arge*: 11 specimens (164–250 mm).
- *U. parvus*: 6 specimens (68–119 mm).
- *U. asymmetricus*: holotype (76 mm) and three paratypes.
- *U. oligospilus*: holotype (115 mm) and 11 paratypes (70–160 mm).

**Species-specific highlights**
- [[Upeneus Bensasi]]: 7 dorsal spines, pectoral rays 13.8, 25.0 gillrakers (23–27), no caudal lower-lobe bars.
- [[Upeneus Asymmetricus]] (new): holotype from Pandanon Island, Philippines. Named for the reduced upper-lobe caudal bars (3 bars vs 6–7 on the lower lobe).
- [[Upeneus Sulphureus]]: pectoral rays 15.8, 35.4 scale rows, 29.4 gillrakers (26–32), barbel 58–82% of head length in large specimens.
- [[Upeneus Moluccensis]]: pectoral rays 16.0, 28.9 gillrakers (27–31), barbel 48–64% of head length.
- [[Upeneus Vittatus]]: pectoral rays 16.1, 28.2 gillrakers (26–31), 35.0 scale rows; widespread from the Red Sea to the Low Archipelago, and not reported from Hawaii.
- [[Upeneus Arge]]: small scales (36–38 scale rows), peritoneum transparent, 22.3 gillrakers (21–24); Hawaiian and Phoenix Islands.
- [[Upeneus Luzonius]]: 14.1 pectoral rays, 20.4 gillrakers (19–22), barbel 62–72% of head length; Philippines and East Indies.
- [[Upeneus Tragula]]: 13.0 pectoral rays, 22.8 gillrakers (21–25), barbel 52–68%; Philippines, East Africa to Australia, rare in Oceania.
- [[Upeneus Oligospilus]] (new): holotype from Tarut Bay, Persian Gulf; 14 pectoral rays, 23 gillrakers, barbel 50–64% of head length.
- [[Upeneus Parvus]]: the only Western Atlantic species, known from Cuba, Tobago, Puerto Rico and the Tortugas; apparently rare.
