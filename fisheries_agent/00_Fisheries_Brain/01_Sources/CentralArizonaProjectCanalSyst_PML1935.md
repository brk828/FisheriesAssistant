# Central Arizona Project Canal Fishery Investigations: 1988 Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation progress report presents preliminary results of the 1988 fishery investigations in the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) canal system. The study monitors fish losses from [[Lake Havasu]], documents the fish community establishing itself in the canal as it becomes operational, and evaluates fish entrainment at the [[Lake Havasu Pumping Plant]]. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] leads the work with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and Central Arizona Water Conservation District. Sampling continued quarterly, and a final report analyzing 1986–1989 data was planned for spring 1990.

In 1988 the canal diverted 499,800 acre-feet (af) of water, roughly half of Arizona's 1.1 million af Colorado River allocation and a 29 percent increase over 1987. About 4,800 fish were collected. The community is dominated by [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Striped Bass]], and evidence indicates that several species are reproducing in the canal. Striped bass and largemouth bass show signs of a prey-base collapse in Reach 1.

## Background & Description

The CAP conveys Colorado River water from Lake Havasu roughly 250 miles to the Phoenix area, with the Tucson extension due by about 1991. Water is lifted 824 feet (251 m) at the Lake Havasu Pumping Plant and conveyed through three major segments: the [[Granite Reef Aqueduct]], the [[Salt-Gila Aqueduct]], and the Tucson Aqueduct.

The Granite Reef Aqueduct is the first segment and is divided into 12 reaches. Reach 1 is wider to store about 4,000 acre-feet. Its typical channel has a 24-foot bottom width, about 18-foot depth, and a maximum water surface width near 75 feet. Full-operation discharge is 3,000 cubic feet per second (85 m³/s), with mean velocities up to 3 feet per second (0.9 m/s). Pumping plants with triangular forebays (maximum depth 35 feet) are spaced along the system.

Sampling methods included:

- **Standardized gill nets:** five-panel experimental nets (1½ to 4 inch bar mesh) set in pairs, fished 12 hours overnight, quarterly.
- **[[Electrofishing]]:** used in forebays and a ½-mile canal section at each pumping plant, with blocking gill nets.
- **Angling:** standard recreational gear, recording effort, success, and creel composition.
- **Egg and larval studies:** [[Bongo Net]] plankton tows (330 μm mesh) at pumping plant discharges, conducted at two sites in 1987 and in 1988 at three times of day (dawn, noon, dusk).
- **Other data:** length, weight, K condition factors, diet from stomach analysis, water quality, and [[SCUBA Surveys]] of forebays.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Canal water was generally well mixed. Temperatures ranged from 11.2 to 29.9 °C, dissolved oxygen from 7.9 mg/L upward, pH from about 7.3 to 10, and conductivity from 840 to 894 μmhos/cm (see [[Water Quality]] appendix).

**Hydraulics and fish behavior.** Increased diversion rates in 1988, roughly double 1987 flows, increased suspended algae and debris, which matted gill nets within minutes and reduced their efficiency. Higher velocities may also reduce [[Electrofishing]] efficiency and make fish harder to locate. Forebays and canal depths (averaging 16 feet) allowed fish to avoid electrotaxis.

**Habitat.** SCUBA divers observed aquatic plants, including Asiatic clam, sago pondweed, and Eurasian watermilfoil (*Myriophyllum* sp.), in forebays. Sediment accumulated in forebays and along canal banks, with depths ranging from a few centimeters in the canal to 3 meters in the [[Hassayampa Pumping Plant]] forebay.

**Predator-prey dynamics.** The investigators described a simplified food chain in Reach 1. Abundant prey (threadfin shad, green sunfish, bluegill, and snails) and the lack of protective cover supported high predator numbers, primarily striped and largemouth bass. After the prey base collapsed, these predators declined, and smaller fish such as shad and sunfish were reduced. Downstream, the absence of predators allowed large populations of shad and sunfish to persist.

## Key Findings

### Species Composition and Collections

- **Species list:** 16 species are documented in Table 2 (1986–1988), including game fish, forage fish, catfish, suckers, mosquitofish, and [[Common Carp]]. A [[Goldfish]] and a juvenile [[Flathead Catfish]] were observed but not collected.
- **1988 catch:** approximately 4,800 fish were collected: electrofishing 3,698, gill netting 594, and angling 512.
- **Notable first records:** threadfin shad were collected in small numbers for the first time.

### Relative Abundance

- Threadfin shad (~40 percent of the 1988 catch by one estimate), bluegill, and carp were the dominant species, and striped bass were in third or fourth place.
- Gill-net catches in Reach 1 showed declines in bluegill, threadfin shad, black crappie, and green sunfish between 1986 and 1988.
- Electrofishing comparisons (1987 versus 1988) showed gradual declines in relative abundance for most species, with exceptions for striped bass, which were spreading downstream.
- Anglers averaged 3.26 fish per hour in 1987 and 3.81 fish per hour in 1988. Striped bass made up 344 of 512 fish caught (67.2 percent) in 1988.

### Striped Bass

- The striped bass population in Reach 1 was the most abundant predator. Mean length in Reach 1 rose from 257 mm in 1986 to 375 mm in 1987.
- Striped bass were concentrated near the Buckskin Mountain Tunnel outfall and appeared to be scavenging after the prey collapse.
- Stomach analysis found feeding on large numbers of carp eggs and small fish. Photos document a striped bass stomach full of carp eggs.
- Condition factors for striped bass (1.6 to 1.1) and largemouth bass (1.4 to 1.2) declined in 1987. Tagged striped bass gained 1.0 percent body weight per month, while tagged largemouth bass lost 1.7 percent per month.
- Few juvenile striped bass were captured, and no striped bass eggs were collected in 1988. Five eggs (0.02 percent of the egg sample) were found in 1987.

### Fish Entrainment and Passage

- Lake Havasu larval fish entrainment averaged 1.13 fish per cubic meter in 1987 and 0.39 in 1988.
- Larval composition was primarily threadfin shad (72 to 86 percent), sunfish (10 to 17 percent), and carp (4 to 10 percent).
- Sampling at the [[Hassayampa Pumping Plant]] passage system found about 2 fish per hour (0.00023 fish per cubic meter), with cover-oriented species most susceptible to entrainment.
- Tagging (Floy tags, fish 150 mm and larger) produced recaptures, including a largemouth bass tagged at [[Bouse Hills Pumping Plant]] and found about 17 miles downstream near the Buckskin Mountain Tunnel. Striped bass recaptures suggested downstream movement.

### Evidence of Reproduction

- Larval and egg data indicated successful spawning by threadfin shad, carp, and possibly sunfish in the canal.
- SCUBA observations recorded bass spawning on forebay banks at Bouse Hills and bluegill nest-building, but no eggs or larvae were found in the associated redds.
- Egg densities are described as very low, and the investigators concluded that many striped bass eggs and larvae may have gone unsampled.

### Management Changes and Conclusions

- Because of the high diversion rates, the investigators planned to drop summer sampling and shift to a winter and fall schedule, with [[Electrofishing]] and gill netting as primary methods.
- The investigators suggested that increased velocities and predator-prey interactions are reshaping the fish community, drawing parallels to the [[Coachella Canal]] and its shift to riverine species.
- The final report will incorporate 1989 findings and analyze the 1986–1989 data for recommendations on mitigation.
