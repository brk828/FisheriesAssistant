# Razorback Sucker Recovery Potential and Hydropower Relicensing: Fossil Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This biological report, prepared by [[EnviroNet Inc]] for [[Arizona Public Service]] (APS) in October 1998, supports the [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] (FERC) relicensing of the [[Childs and Irving Hydroelectric Plants]] (Project No. 2069-003) on [[Fossil Creek]], Arizona. It addresses potential impacts to the federally endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]], as requested by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS).

The razorback sucker was accidentally introduced into Fossil Springs and Fossil Creek by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) in October 1988, when about 10,000 young-of-year fish were stocked near the Irving plant. The report concludes that the project is anticipated to have **no effect** on the species, that stocked fish no longer persist, and that Fossil Creek has essentially no potential for razorback sucker recovery under any flow regime.

## Background & Description

**Legal status.** The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was listed as endangered by the federal government in 1991 and is classed as "Wildlife of Special Concern in Arizona." Critical habitat (designated 1994) includes the [[Verde River]] and its 100-year floodplain from the Prescott National Forest to Horseshoe Dam, including the Childs reach and the Fossil Creek mouth. Fossil Creek itself is not critical habitat.

**Species biology.** The razorback sucker is one of the largest catostomids in western North America, reaching nearly a meter and 5 to 6 kg. Historically it inhabited large mainstem rivers, backwaters, sloughs, oxbows and floodplains of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Spawning occurs in broad, flat runs over gravel and cobble bars, triggered by rising spring flows and water temperatures near 14°C (range 9–17°C). Larvae drift downstream into floodplain nursery habitat, and adults feed mainly on benthic invertebrates, detritus and zooplankton.

**Historical setting.** The species was once found in the Verde River upstream to Perkinsville, Arizona, but was extirpated from the Verde system before 1960; the last Verde collection was from Pecks Lake in 1954. It has never been recorded historically from Fossil Creek or Fossil Springs, and earlier surveys (Chamberlain 1904; Neve 1976) did not record it.

**Recovery criteria.** The Draft Recovery Plan (Tyus 1997) calls for a recovery area of at least 30 to 50 river miles with unrestricted movement, and a self-sustaining population for at least 10 years through natural recruitment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fossil Creek fish community.** A 1994–1996 AGFD survey documented five native species below the diversion dam: [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Longfin Dace]]. Nonnative species included common carp, yellow bullhead, flathead catfish, smallmouth bass and green sunfish. Crayfish are abundant in Fossil Creek and the Verde River and are considered predators of fish eggs.

**Hydrology and geomorphology.** Fossil Creek is a steep, flashy, travertine-depositing stream. Existing baseflow is very low (about 2.2 cfs cited by one expert), and the proposed minimum flows are 10 cfs in the Irving reach and 5 cfs in the Childs reach. The full-flow alternative (decommissioning) restores about 43 cfs baseflow. Spring flows are generally 21°C year-round, and the creek is scoured to bedrock roughly every two years by peak flows.

**Critical habitat at Childs and the Verde.** Along the Verde near Childs, the habitat is limited to a few isolated deep eddies and undercut banks. The Childs tailrace (about 41 cfs) provides a concentrated food source and some cover, but no nursery habitat. Riparian vegetation in the tailrace would decline under decommissioning.

**Fossil Creek mouth.** Field surveys on April 16 (Verde flow ~2,200 cfs; Fossil Creek ~100–200 cfs; creek temperature 13°C) and June 6, 1998 (Verde ~100 cfs; Fossil Creek 2–5 cfs; creek temperature 24°C) showed that pools at low flow become runs or riffles at high flow. Green sunfish and smallmouth bass occupy the lower-velocity habitat there.

## Key Findings

### Stocking and persistence
- About 10,000 young-of-year razorbacks (mean TL 76.2 mm) were stocked near the Irving plant in October 1988, and some were planted above the diversion dam.
- Barrett (1992) collected 13 sub-adults (120–190 mm TL) in Fossil Springs in 1990 and found no larvae or juveniles.
- CH2M Hill (August 1991) and AGFD (1994–1996) surveys found no razorbacks. Experts agree the stocked fish no longer persist.
- Verde River stocking (1981–1983) involved 20,650 young-of-year fish; in 1997 AGFD recaptured 29 razorbacks, and all lower Verde fish were heavily parasitized by *Lernaea* and 80% had ulcerated sores.

### Expert recovery-potential study
Six experts were selected through a value-based (Delphi-style) method. Their opinions were gathered from questionnaires and field visits. Responses were largely consistent:
- Recovery potential is **essentially zero** at all flow regimes (Question 13: four of five experts said zero; one gave no answer).
- Adult survival potential is very low (four of five experts), with one expert saying it is possible under varied conditions.
- Spawning and larval recruitment are very low (four of five experts). Limits include constant spring temperatures, marginal substrate, steep gradient, flashy peak flows, predation, and no record of spawning in small creeks.
- Nonnative predators are a major threat; experts agree they preclude juvenile survival.
- The food base is inadequate, with low algal and invertebrate productivity and travertine precipitation removing nutrients.
- Stehr Lake has no potential with nonnatives present, but high potential if nonnatives were removed.

### Limiting factors
The experts identified these limits: lack of required habitat (channel morphology), inadequate food, lack of cover, peak-flow hydrograph and steep gradient, lack of spawning substrate, lack of recruitment potential, not historical habitat, nonnative predators, competition with native suckers, small creek size, lack of persistence of stocked fish, and lack of recovery success in the Verde and Salt Rivers.

### Flow alternatives
- **Minimum flows (10 cfs Irving, 5 cfs Childs):** no change in recovery potential; fish habitat gains are small and flow is not the primary limiting factor.
- **Full flow (43 cfs):** would increase velocities in existing pools, build travertine dams over about two miles near Irving, and possibly increase nonnative habitat and invasion from the Verde River. Experts disagreed on the net habitat effect; most expected no recovery improvement.
- **Decommissioning:** would move tailrace water to Fossil Creek, reduce flows and temperature buffering on the Verde at Childs, and may aid nonnative movement into Fossil Creek.

### Impact conclusions (APS)
- **Fossil Creek:** no effect on continued survival, recovery potential, or habitat for the razorback sucker.
- **Verde River confluence:** no adverse modification of critical habitat; the 3 cfs shift between Childs and Fossil Creek is not expected to have a detectable effect.
- **Overall:** the proposed action is anticipated to have no effect on the species.

### Dissenting views
A minority of respondents, including [[John Rinne]] and [[Paul Holden]], argued that restoring full flow could greatly improve the native fish community and ecosystem even though it would not recover razorbacks. Rinne called the single-species framing "meaningless" for ecosystem management. [[Dean Blinn]] judged that Fossil Creek, with full flow and removal of nonnatives, could be an important native-fish refuge.
