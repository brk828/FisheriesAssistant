# Fish and Grazing Relationships in the Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service review (edited by Jamison, Raish and Finch, Rocky Mountain Research Station) examines what is known about the relationships between domestic livestock grazing, riparian habitat and fishes in the southwestern United States, with emphasis on USFS Region 3 (R-3). The author concludes that direct effects of grazing on vegetation and streambanks are well documented, whereas indirect effects on fishes are poorly studied, rarely demonstrated with sound design, and dominated by salmonid research. Native southwestern fishes are mostly non-salmonid [[Cypriniform Fishes]] and [[Catostomid Suckers]], which are largely absent from the grazing literature. The paper argues that managers need replicated, pre-treatment, statistically analysed monitoring and research, combined with interagency cooperation and adaptive management, and that natural factors such as geology, floods and drought can override grazing effects.

## Background & Description

- **Grazing history:** Livestock have grazed southwestern landscapes for centuries; control efforts began with the 1897 Organic Act, followed by the Taylor Grazing Act (1934), the Multiple Use and Sustained Yield Act (1960), the National Forest Management Act (1976) and the [[National Forest and Rangelands Renewable Resources Research Act]] (1978).
- **Fish diversity:** Fewer than 60 fish species are native to the southwestern United States, a low diversity compared with eastern streams. About 100 non-native species have been introduced to Arizona waters, and almost 50 have become established.
- **Conservation status:** Native fish fauna is largely federal- or state-listed, and all native species have declined in numbers since non-native introductions, habitat alteration, and water diversion and mining.
- **Scope:** The paper addresses fish, fish habitat and grazing in the Southwest, identifies knowledge gaps and recommends research directions.
- **Key sites:** Rio de las Vacas (Santa Fe National Forest, northern New Mexico), West Fork allotment (Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest, [[White Mountains]], Arizona), and the [[Verde River]] (Prescott National Forest, Arizona).
- **Principal author's research:** Conducted by J. N. Rinne at these sites from 1983 to 1997.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Direct vs. indirect effects:** Grazing directly removes vegetation through herbivory and compacts or damages banks by trampling. Indirect effects on fishes operate through changes to channel morphology, streambank stability, instream cover, sediment and water temperature. Cause and effect are harder to demonstrate for the indirect pathway.
- **Riparian-fish linkage:** Riparian areas and their watersheds are closely linked; in the Southwest, riparian-stream areas often serve as fish habitat, and riparian condition can indicate watershed condition.
- **Salmonid bias:** Most published grazing-fish work concerns cold-water [[Salmonid Fishes]] in upper montane streams. Lower-elevation desert streams with cypriniform and catostomid fishes are almost unstudied.
- **Natural controls:** Geology (for example, [[Glacial Geology of the White Mountains]] and basalt versus alluvium), floods, drought and the natural hydrograph can strongly shape fish populations and may mask or exceed livestock effects.
- **Elk:** The large wild ungulate [[Elk]] is increasingly recognised as a significant grazer of riparian areas in R-3, sometimes altering the "fence line" contrast used to demonstrate livestock effects.
- **Predation:** Non-native predators such as [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Yellow Bullhead]] reduced [[Red Shiner]] prey in laboratory raceways (smallmouth bass mean loss 80% per 24 hours with 7 replicates; yellow bullhead mean loss 21% per 24 hours with 12 replicates).

## Key Findings

**Literature review (Platts 1991 and the author's review):**
- Early authors widely considered livestock grazing a serious threat to western trout streams (Behnke and Zarn 1976; Gallizioli 1977; Davis 1977; Saltzman 1977; Meehan and Platts 1978).
- Platts (1991) found 15 of 21 studies indicated grazing negatively affected fish populations, but noted that in most cases the affected variables were stream attributes that provide fish habitat rather than fish themselves.
- Platts (1991) found all 21 grazing-riparian studies indicated negative impacts on riparian habitat.
- The author reviewed about 200 references: 66 were fish-related; 47 of these (72%) concerned salmonids and 19 (28%) addressed other species; 13 were classified as "fish general". Of the 66, 11 were opinion, summary or review papers without new data. Only 8 studies were in the Southwest and 3 were data-based.
- Of 30 papers examined on fish and grazing, only 3 (10%) collected data on fish populations and riparian/instream habitat together.

**Study design deficiencies (Table 3, 30 studies):**
- No pre-treatment data: 27
- No controls: 9
- Controls positioned linearly on the stream: 17
- No replication in time: 14
- No replication in space: 5
- No statistical analysis: 17 (only about one-third contained any statistics)
- Only 17 of 30 (57%) studied salmonids exclusively; only one study addressed non-salmonids.
- Data were quantitative in most studies; the author stresses that qualitative categories (substrate, bank damage) reduce repeatability.

**Case studies:**
- **Rio de las Vacas:** Over five years of sampling, grazed and ungrazed reaches differed significantly in fish density in most years. Rio Grande sucker and Rio Grande chub were often more numerous in grazed reaches, while trout densities and biomass were consistently greater in ungrazed reaches (except one year). Conclusions depend heavily on which species or metric is chosen.
- **Verde River:** Native fishes were greater in number and biomass in C-type channels within a grazed reach, while nonnative predators dominated E-type channels with undercut banks. Native fishes were overall less abundant where predators were present; the upper Verde had about six native and six nonnative species, and nearly 17,000 fish were collected over five years at seven sites.
- **West Fork allotment:** Three of four years of sampling were affected by stream drying in the upper reach within an elk exclosure, which negated apparent positive effects of exclusion. Trout density was lower in some grazed reaches, but the author suggests grazing strategy cannot be separated from habitat and flow conditions.

**Geology and substrate (White Mountains):**
- Fine sediment (<2 mm) in Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest and Fort Apache Reservation streams was nearly identical, yet optimum spawning substrate (4–32 mm) was significantly greater on the Reservation, correlating with Apache trout density (P = 0.000) and biomass (P = 0.02).
- Few young-of-year fish were found on the Forest streams (Boggy and Centerfire creeks, four years), suggesting a limited spawning substrate.

**Management and policy:**
- Nonnative fish stocking and predation are major threats; coordinated management of fish habitat and fishery resources by the USFS and the Arizona Game and Fish Department is recommended.
- Monitoring is underfunded; there is no budget line item for monitoring, though research and monitoring can be combined. The author calls for a shift from "talking" to collecting well-designed data.

**Seven Species Evaluation (1997):**
- Of almost 200 ongoing Forest Service projects evaluated in R-3, 50 were determined to have a potential "direct effect" on four threatened native, non-salmonid cypriniform fishes. The author notes this addresses only "the tip of the iceberg," since many more fish species occur on National Forest lands.
- Nine fish species are subject to biological opinions on Forest plans, including spikedace in the upper Verde River.
- Over three dozen native and nonnative fish species are monitored at some level in R-3.

**Recommendations for future research:**
1. Study grazing-vegetation relationships.
2. Define riparian thresholds (reference conditions).
3. Establish fish-habitat relationships and extrapolations to non-salmonid species.
4. Foster cooperative management and research among agencies, permittees and the public.
5. Replace qualitative and opinion-based data with scientific data.

Overall, the author concludes that grazing effects on fishes remain controversial, and that without replicated, pre-treatment, statistically analysed data, "one is just another person with an opinion."
