# Summary of North American Blancan Nonmarine Mollusks

#marine-biology #malacology #paleontology #freshwater-mollusks

## Executive Summary

This 1966 monograph by D. W. Taylor (U.S. Geological Survey) in *Malacologia* 4(1) compiles all known North American nonmarine mollusks of Blancan age, the late Pliocene and early Pleistocene interval, roughly 2 to 5 million years ago. About 50 to 55 Blancan assemblages are known; together with 10 to 15 older or younger faunas they are summarized under 57 local geographic headings. Each locality is dated using associated mammals, stratigraphy and potassium-argon dates, and the paper includes a catalogue of 140 nominal species described from Blancan-age types. The work is primarily a key to previous literature and a framework for future review, and it proposes a few new taxa (one family, three subgenera of *Juga*, one new genus, and several species and new names).

## Background & Description

The Blancan age is defined by restricted ranges and first and last appearances of mammalian genera and is calibrated by radiogenic dates. Taylor notes that, unlike mammals, mollusks show no continent-wide faunal change; freshwater mollusk faunas of Florida, Kansas and California are each more like their living faunas than like one another. Criteria for recognizing Blancan mollusk faunas therefore vary between faunal provinces and sedimentary basins.

The locality summary records, for each site, location (USGS quadrangle and 15-minute area), previous mollusk references, stratigraphic unit, species count, associated fossils, age, repository and recent topographic maps. Institutional abbreviations include ANSP, CAS, MCZ, SU, UCMP, UMMZ and USGS, among others.

Key taxonomic innovations include the new family Pliopholygidae (Viviparacea), new subgenera Calibasis, Oreobasis and Idabasis of *Juga* (Pleuroceridae), the new genus [[Savaginius]] (Hydrobiidae), and new names such as *Juga chrysopylica* and *Radix intermontana*. [[Juga]] is recognized as a valid genus, and *Lithoglyphus* absorbs several former *Fluminicola* species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeographic contrasts.** Blancan faunas of the Great Plains (Nebraska, Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas) and Arizona are similar and dominated by widespread living species. Western U.S. faunas (California, Oregon, Idaho, Nevada, Utah, Wyoming) differ from one another and from the living fauna, with many extinct species, genera and families, and high local endemism. Post-Blancan change in the West reflects extinction linked to draining or filling of lake basins, volcanism, drainage reorganization and climate change.

**Tectonism and endemism.** Taylor proposes two generalizations applicable to freshwater mollusks broadly: (a) species of widespread habitats have wide geographic and geologic ranges; (b) tectonic activity promotes taxonomic differentiation. The eastern limit of living local endemism in Utah and western Wyoming corresponds approximately to the eastern limit of Blancan deposits, and both track areas of tectonic activity.

**The "fishhook" pattern.** A crudely fishhook-shaped belt in and near the northern and western Great Basin contains more living and Blancan species than any other western pattern. Taylor argues it formed from Blancan or immediately pre-Blancan drainage rearrangement, while an older Idaho–California affinity reflects an earlier river connection of early to middle Pliocene age.

**Glaciation and age.** Taylor argues that the "Nebraskan" and "Aftonian" faunas of the Plains are probably pre-Nebraskan, and that glaciation evidence in southwestern Kansas represents alpine or limited continental glaciation. The Blancan/Irvingtonian faunal change is placed about 2 million years ago.

**Lacustrine versus stream habitats.** Western Blancan faunas are often lacustrine and endemic, whereas the living fauna of the Southern Appalachians is the nearest modern analogue for rich local stream faunas. Glenns Ferry Formation mollusks show a sharp separation between lake-endemic faunas and more widespread tributary faunas, paralleled in Lake Ohrid, Lake Tanganyika and Lake Titicaca.

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** 57 local headings; about 50–55 Blancan assemblages plus 10–15 older or younger faunas; 140 nominal species catalogued with type data.
- **Largest western lake faunas:** Glenns Ferry Formation (Idaho–Oregon), with a lacustrine fauna of many endemic species and a flood-plain fauna of widespread taxa. Cache Valley Formation (Utah–Idaho) and Tulare Formation (Kettleman Hills, California) are also rich.
- **Key Great Plains localities:** Rexroad, Bender, Sanders, Spring Creek, Dixon, Blanco and Sand Draw faunas; the *Biomphalaria kansasensis* record from the Ballard Formation is the northernmost occurrence of the genus.
- **Age constraints:** Hagerman horse quarry mammals (latest Pliocene) with a 3.5 million-year date; Coso Mountain fauna at 2.3 million years; a 1.4 million-year minimum for later Glenns Ferry deposition; Danforth Formation mollusks bracketed by a 6.4 million-year rhyolite as a minimum age.
- **Anodonta wahlamatensis** is disjunct across four structural basins in central California, and its fossil record indicates drainage connections (San Benito–Santa Clara valleys) older than the lower Coyote Creek course.
- ***Pisidium ultramontanum*** has the longest geologic range of any living western freshwater mollusk, known from the early Pliocene.
- **Arizona–High Plains similarity:** Benson local fauna mollusks closely resemble Pleistocene faunas of Kansas, Oklahoma and Texas, suggesting similar faunas across much of the Southwest.
- **Florida "Unit A":** A distinctive late Pliocene to early Pleistocene nonmarine fauna of extinct and locally extinct species, including new species *Planorbella wilsoni* and several extinct taxa.
- **Recognized new taxa:** Family Pliopholygidae; *Juga* subgenera *Calibasis*, *Oreobasis*, *Idabasis*; genus [[Savaginius]]; *Juga chrysopylica*; *Radix intermontana*; *Planorbella wilsoni*.
- **Scope limits:** The paper focuses on mollusks; other groups (fishes, mammals, birds, reptiles, crustaceans, plants) are cited mainly for dating and association, and the author notes that no comparable continental summaries exist for ostracodes, crayfish or diatoms.
