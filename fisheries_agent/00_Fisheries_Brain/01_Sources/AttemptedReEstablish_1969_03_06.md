# Attempted Re-establishment of the Gila Topminnow Within Its Former Range

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #freshwater-ecology #endangered-species #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary
This 1969 short communication by W. L. Minckley documents an early conservation translocation effort for the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), a livebearing fish historically described as "one of the commonest fishes" in the southern [[Colorado River Drainage]] (Hubbs and Miller, 1941). By the time of writing, the species had been extirpated from all but one small stream system in its native range. Beginning in 1964, researchers attempted to re-establish populations at multiple semi-isolated sites throughout the [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona, using a single source population from a pond fed by Monkey Spring near Patagonia, Santa Cruz County. The note catalogs the outcomes—successes, failures, and uncertain status—across seven introduction sites in four counties, highlighting the fragility of reintroduction efforts against flooding events and invasive species competition, particularly from [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).

## Background & Description
The source text does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of *Poeciliopsis occidentalis*, as it is a conservation status report rather than a taxonomic or anatomical study. The species is classified within the order Cyprinodontiformes per Hubbs and Miller (1941), and is a member of the livebearing topminnow group (family [[Poeciliidae]]), related taxonomically to the [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), with which it appears to compete/hybridize unfavorably in shared habitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range**: Historically widespread across the southern [[Colorado River Drainage]], now restricted to a single small stream system by 1969.
- **Habitat type**: Utilizes small, semi-isolated freshwater habitats including spring-fed ponds, golf course ponds, scoured river-bed pools, headsprings, and creek-adjacent marshes.
- **Source population**: All reintroduced stocks derived from a single population seined from a pond fed by [[Monkey Spring]], located seven miles northeast of Patagonia, Santa Cruz County, Arizona.
- **Interspecific competition**: Populations were repeatedly eliminated following introduction and establishment of the invasive [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), indicating strong competitive exclusion or predation pressure from this congener-analog species.
- **Environmental vulnerability**: Populations were highly susceptible to elimination via flooding events (1965–1967) and possible pesticide runoff, underscoring the fragility of small, isolated founder populations in variable desert stream systems.

## Key Findings

### Introduction Site Outcomes (by County)

**Maricopa County:**
- Papago Golf Course & Papago Park ponds (north Tempe): Eliminated by suspected pesticide blowover (1965) and Mosquitofish establishment (1966).
- Salt River bed pools (Tempe): Reproduced in 1966, eliminated by Mosquitofish by autumn 1966.
- Cave Creek (10 mi N, 6 mi E of Cave Creek town): Probably eliminated by flooding 1965–66.
- Camp Creek (5 mi N, 7 mi E of Cave Creek town): Established 1966, probably eliminated by flooding 1966–67.
- Fish Creek (11 mi S, 9 mi E of Roosevelt): Stock immediately eliminated by flooding in 1965.

**Gila County:**
- Headspring of East Verde River (~12 mi N, 4 mi E of Payson): Reproducing as of 1965; not subsequently monitored.

**Gila and Yavapai Counties:**
- Fossil Creek (2 mi N, 2 mi W of Pine): Reproducing in 1968, but population small and localized.

**Pinal County:**
- Aravaipa Creek marsh (7 mi S, 12 mi E of Winkleman): Established in 1968 with a large population; a few individuals also found in the main creek channel.

**Graham County:**
- Aravaipa Creek (4 mi N, 4 mi W of Klondyke): Reproducing (possibly established) in 1968.

### Conservation Implications
- Reintroduction success was highly variable and largely undermined by two dominant factors: **flash flooding** in desert stream systems and **competitive displacement by invasive Mosquitofish**.
- The most durable successes by 1968 were at [[Aravaipa Creek]] sites (both Pinal and Graham County populations) and [[Fossil Creek]], suggesting spring-fed, less flood-prone marsh/channel habitats may offer better refugia.
- The author explicitly frames these efforts as insurance against total extinction of the species, and calls for continued monitoring and identification of additional isolated waters suitable for establishment.
- This work represents an early documented case of active species translocation as a management tool for an imperiled freshwater fish, predating much of the modern endangered species reintroduction literature framework (see [[Minckley and Deacon 1968]] on the "enigma of endangered species").
