# Eight Eastside Rivers Wild and Scenic River Study Report

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Salmonidae #threatened-species #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document is an excerpt from a Wild and Scenic River Suitability Study and Draft Environmental Impact Statement for eight eastside rivers in the [[Tahoe National Forest]] and [[Lake Tahoe Basin Management Unit]]. The study evaluates ecological values including meadow associations, alpine communities, old-growth forests, and threatened, endangered, and sensitive (TES) species. The [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] is identified as a federally threatened species within the study area. The document emphasizes the importance of riparian corridors, meadow complexes, and old-growth communities for biodiversity, wildlife habitat, and aquatic ecosystem function. River designation is expected to be primarily beneficial for most TES species.

## Background & Description

The study area encompasses eight rivers on the eastside of the [[Sierra Nevada]], managed by the [[Tahoe National Forest]] (TNF) and [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM). Key waterways mentioned include [[Independence Creek]], [[Perazzo Creek]], [[Alder Creek]], [[Little Truckee River]], and [[Sagehen Creek]].

**Meadow Associations**: Meadows comprise only 10 percent of total Sierra Nevada land area but provide critical habitat. The study area contains extensive meadow complexes including both dry and wet meadows. [[Perazzo Meadow]] is approximately 1.5 miles long and exhibits high botanical diversity with plants common to both the [[Great Basin]] and Sierra Nevada. Meadows along [[Independence Creek]] are relatively undisturbed from management activities such as grazing.

**Old-Growth Areas**: Old-growth forests provide habitat for diverse animal and plant species, maintain biodiversity and genetic resources, and contain long-term biological records of climate. The current extent of old-growth on TNF and LTBMU is unknown but substantially reduced from historical levels. Older forests along rivers and streams provide recruitment of large woody debris (LWD) to stream environments. LWD provides nutrients, shapes stream channels, traps sediments, creates structural complexity, and provides rearing habitat for fish. Known old-growth communities exist along [[Independence Creek]] and [[Perazzo Creek]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic Habitat**: The riparian corridors and stream environments are shaped by large woody debris recruitment from old-growth forests. This structural complexity is essential for fish rearing habitat and sediment dynamics.

**Elevation Gradients**: The study area spans multiple elevation zones from approximately 3,000 to 9,000 feet, supporting diverse plant communities from mixed conifer forests to alpine areas. Alpine communities occur at high elevations on steep slopes with scattered vegetation among rocks and gravel.

**Botanical Diversity**: The eastside Sierra Nevada location creates unique conditions where Great Basin and Sierra Nevada flora overlap, particularly evident in meadow complexes like [[Perazzo Meadow]] and [[Independence Creek]] meadows.

## Key Findings

**Threatened and Endangered Fish Species**:
- [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarkii henshawi*) - federally threatened species, also listed by California
- [[Sagehen Creek]] goeracean caddisfly - Category 1 (sufficient information for federal listing determination)

**Other Threatened/Endangered Wildlife**:
- [[American Bald Eagle]] - federally endangered
- [[American Peregrine Falcon]] - federally endangered

**Category 2 Species of Special Interest** (insufficient information for listing determination):
- [[Mt. Lyell Salamander]]
- Yellow legged mountain frog
- Wolverine
- Mono Basin mountain beaver
- Sierra Nevada snowshoe hare
- Cold Spring caddisfly
- Confusion caddisfly
- Kings Canyon cryptochian caddisfly

**Forest Service/California Sensitive Species**:
- Sierra Nevada red fox
- [[Spotted Owl]]
- [[Goshawk]]
- [[Willow Flycatcher]]
- Pine marten
- Pacific fisher
- Great grey owl

**Sensitive Plant Species**: Eight sensitive plant species have potential habitat in the study area, including *Ivesia sericoluca* (Plumas Valley Ivesia) and *Silene invisa* (Hidden-petal campion) with known occurrences. Habitat ranges from 3,000-9,000 feet elevation in various environments including alkaline flats, meadows, sphagnum bogs, forest openings, and drainage areas.

**Watch-List Plants**: Additional rare plant species and communities identified include pitcher plant (*Darlingtonia californica*), round-leaved sundew (*Drosera rotundifolia*), English sundew (*Drosera anglica*), and specialized wetland habitats (bogs, fens, vernal pools). Known occurrences of both sundew species and specialized wetlands have been documented.

**Management Implications**: River designation under Wild and Scenic River status is expected to be primarily beneficial for most TES species, though most species would not have habitat directly affected by the designation action itself. The study emphasizes the importance of maintaining riparian corridors, old-growth recruitment, and meadow complexes for overall ecosystem function and biodiversity conservation.
