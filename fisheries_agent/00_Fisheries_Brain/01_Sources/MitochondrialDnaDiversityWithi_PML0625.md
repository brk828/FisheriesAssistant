# Mitochondrial DNA Diversity within and among Populations of Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variation in the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) to test whether its populations are geographically structured. Restriction-fragment analysis of 143 specimens, using eight restriction endonucleases, identified 38 composite mtDNA haplotypes. Genetic diversity was highest in [[Lake Mohave]] and declined progressively upstream, while genetic divergence among populations was very low. The authors interpret this as a historical pattern of gene exchange rather than local differentiation, and they recommend managing the species as a single breeding unit across its range.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large, long-lived catostomid fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Adults exceed 500 mm standard length and can live more than 50 years. Once widespread, the species has been extirpated from much of its former range, and remaining populations are small. The largest remaining populations were in the upper [[Green River Basin]] (estimated at fewer than 1,000 adults) and in Lake Mohave, where estimates were reported as fewer than 2,000. Natural populations in the [[Gila River Basin]] were either extirpated or too small for reliable size estimates. The species was listed as endangered by the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1991.

Several factors could have promoted local isolation. The species apparently favored wide, low-gradient, non-canyon reaches, and some studies reported fidelity to specific spawning sites. Conversely, its large body size, longevity, and potential for dispersal could have allowed gene flow throughout the basin. Molecular markers, including mtDNA, are widely used to address population structure and conservation questions in fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling localities.** Tissue samples came from five main areas: Lake Mohave (AZ-NV), Lake Mead (AZ-NV), Lake Powell (UT), the Green/Yampa rivers (CO-UT), and the upper Colorado River near Grand Junction (CO). Twelve putative hybrids between razorback and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) were collected near the confluence of the Little Colorado and Colorado rivers, AZ. Most tissues were gametes or muscle plugs from nonlethally sampled adults. Populations from the [[Salton Sea]], California, were not included.

**Hybridization.** Hybrids were excluded from the main diversity estimates because their inclusion would inflate diversity and divergence values. Hybridization appeared more common in the lower Little Colorado River, where several individuals carried flannelmouth sucker mtDNA haplotypes, suggesting introgression.

**Climate history.** The last glacial period peaked about 18 thousand years ago (ka) and ended around 10 ka. Warm-season air temperatures on the [[Colorado Plateau]] were about 6.3 °C cooler than today. Spawning, hatching, and growth of razorback sucker and other big-river fishes are negatively affected when water temperatures fall well below 20 °C. Glacial-era cold conditions would likely have excluded self-sustaining populations from the upper basin while leaving the lower basin relatively warm.

**Dams and reservoirs.** Mainstem reservoirs on the Colorado River were built between 1935 and 1962. Cold hypolimnetic releases from these reservoirs limit reproduction and recruitment downstream, and upper-basin populations remain temperature-limited.

## Key Findings

**Methods and dataset**
- Restriction analysis of 143 specimens with eight restriction endonucleases (four- and six-base-pair recognition sites) produced 182 fragments and 38 composite haplotypes.
- Haplotypes were named with an eight-character code, with enzyme patterns assigned by order of discovery rather than similarity.
- Two analysis sets were run: one using only individuals with complete haplotypes for all enzymes, and one using enzymes recognizing six base pairs.

**Within-population diversity**
- Haplotype diversity was highest in Lake Mohave (h = 0.98).
- Diversity was lowest in the upper Colorado River (h = 0.00), where a single haplotype (ABADA'AAA) was fixed.
- Diversity declined progressively with distance upstream from Lake Mohave, a clinal pattern.

**Among-population structure**
- Most genetic variation lay within populations rather than among them.
- Estimates of sequence divergence among populations were very small (on the order of 0.0004 to 0.0035 in the reported ranges), and there were no clear geographic clusters in haplotype networks or phenograms.
- Monte Carlo tests found significant heterogeneity in haplotype frequencies, but the authors judged the biological significance of these differences to be small.
- Haplotype networks and phylogenetic analyses showed that shared or closely related haplotypes occurred across localities. Lake Mohave haplotypes were the most distinct, but the overall pattern was not one of isolation.

**Interpretation**
- Lake Mohave fish were mostly older than 40 years and likely descended from a population occupying the whole Colorado River basin 40 to 80 years ago.
- Upstream fish from the Little Colorado River and Grand Junction area were younger (under 15 years, by otolith age), reflecting more recent conditions.
- Two hypotheses explain the upstream decline in diversity. The Lake Mohave "sink" hypothesis was judged unlikely because it would require divergent upstream populations that the data do not show. The authors favored a recolonization scenario, in which lower-basin populations served as a stable source and serial bottlenecks occurred as fish colonized upstream after glaciation.
- Smaller effective population sizes at the range periphery, consistent with the cline in population size, may also contribute to the diversity gradient.
- Gene exchange among distant regions is clearly documented by shared haplotypes.

**Management recommendation**
- The authors argue that locally adapted stocks cannot be ruled out but that the species shows no demonstrated local genetic differentiation.
- Maintaining separate discrete stocks is impractical for most populations because they lack enough brood fish.
- They recommend conserving genetic variation by treating the razorback sucker as a single breeding unit throughout its range.
- Citing [[Hedrick 1995]], they note that local adaptation can persist under moderate gene flow, which weakens the argument for strict stock separation.
