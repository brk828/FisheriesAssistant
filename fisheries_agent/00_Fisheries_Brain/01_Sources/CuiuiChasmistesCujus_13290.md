# Cui-ui Recovery Plan (1977)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sucker

## Executive Summary

This December 1977 recovery plan, prepared by the Cui-ui Recovery Team and published by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (approved January 23, 1978), addresses the endangered [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*), a sucker endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. The stated prime objective is to restore the species to non-endangered status, reclassifying it from endangered to threatened. The plan identifies the main causes of decline as reduced [[Truckee River]] inflow, an impassable delta, a blocked route above [[Derby Dam]], and rising total dissolved solids (TDS) in the lake. It sets out a prioritized action outline covering lake and river habitat, predator control, hatchery augmentation, transplants and public education, along with a schedule of priorities, responsibilities and costs. The document also contains essential-habitat designations and formal comment letters from state, federal and tribal agencies.

## Background & Description

- **Species identity and cultural importance:** The cui-ui is intertwined with the [[Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe]], whose ancestral name (*Kuyuidokado*, "sucker eaters") reflects its role as a dependable food source alongside [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] spawning runs.
- **Life history knowledge:** The text states that very little is known about cui-ui life history. The first published information came from Snyder (1917). Later work by Trelease, Jonez, Johnson and [[David L. Koch]] (1971–1972) added feeding, aging and reproductive data.
- **Taxonomy and distribution:** Three *Chasmistes* species are recognized, each restricted to a separate drainage: the short-nose sucker (Klamath Lake, California and Oregon), the June sucker (Utah Lake), and the cui-ui. In Nevada the cui-ui was confined to Pyramid Lake and Winnemucca Lake, remnants of [[Pleistocene Lake Lahontan]] (which once covered up to 8,000 square miles).
- **Anadromous tendencies:** The cui-ui, like the now-extinct [[Pyramid Lake Cutthroat Trout]], made annual spring spawning migrations up the Truckee River. Historically these runs were harvested by tribes and later by non-Indian settlers, with many tons of trout removed for sale.
- **Pronunciation note:** Commonly "kwee-wee"; the Paiute pronunciation is closest to "kooee-ooee."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and water balance**
- The Truckee River, rising in the Sierra Nevada and flowing through Lake Tahoe, is the only permanent inflow to Pyramid Lake. Lake level depends almost entirely on this inflow to offset evaporation and seepage.
- Diversions for domestic, industrial and agricultural use, including the 1905 Derby Dam and canal feeding the [[Newlands Project]], lowered the lake by nearly 80 feet this century. [[Winnemucca Lake]] was desiccated around 1938.
- Estimated evaporation from the lake is approximately 400,000 acre-feet per year.

**Water chemistry**
- Recorded TDS rose from 3486.1 ppm (1882) to 3980 ppm (1933), a 14 percent increase, then from 3980 ppm to 5420 ppm (1933–1968), a 36 percent increase. The plan states overall TDS increased by nearly 50 percent during the period of increased Truckee water use.
- Snail species have become extinct; shells on basin beaches are the only evidence of earlier species. One species survives in a stunted form (Robertson, 1976).

**Stream habitat**
- Diminished flows allow silt to settle into spawning gravels. A study of the lower Truckee (McBrayer and Ringo) found sediment impeded subsurface water flow, reducing habitat for cutthroat trout eggs and fry; similar effects on cui-ui beds remain unverified.

**Predators and competitors**
- Identified or suspected threats include Sacramento perch and lake chub (juveniles), other fish (eggs), pelicans and herons (all ages), and "rough" fish competition.

## Key Findings

**Status and history**
- Cui-ui were listed as endangered in the Federal Register (Vol. 32, No. 48, March 11, 1967).
- The Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe first imposed a daily bag limit and in 1969 prohibited non-Indian fishing for cui-ui.
- The Pyramid Lake cutthroat trout became extinct around 1938.

**Completed accomplishments**
- A fishway for cui-ui and Lahontan cutthroat was completed in 1976 (the Pyramid Lake Fishway, also called the Marble Bluff Fishway in the text).
- Fish and Wildlife Service personnel distributed nearly 7.5 million cui-ui fry into the lower Truckee River and lake during 1973–1976. Success cannot yet be assessed because the fish must mature.
- Nevada Department of Fish and Game reared larvae to adulthood, demonstrating culture in waters other than Pyramid Lake.
- Tribal members have been trained in netting, transport and culture techniques.

**Setbacks**
- Cui-ui failed to ascend the Marble Bluff Fishway in spring 1976 and 1977.

**Recovery objectives and actions (Part III outline)**
- Maintain a self-sustaining population at optimum levels in Pyramid Lake.
- Restore and maintain essential habitat in the lake and inflowing streams, including fishways, diversion screens, flow management, barrier removal, stream canopy restoration and bank protection.
- Study chemical, biological and physical factors, age and growth, reproduction, natural recruitment and behavior.
- Augment the population with hatchery-reared fish until natural reproduction is established: produce two million fry annually, release a minimum of two million hatchery-reared cui-ui into Pyramid Lake each year, and train tribal members.
- Establish viable subpopulations at suitable alternative sites if needed.
- Educate the public through leaflets and news media.
- Protect all age classes in Pyramid Lake and stream-spawning fish in the Truckee River, with no taking of fish except for hatchery propagation.

**Priority and planning elements**
- Acquire the [[Peigh Ranch]] to protect the water supply for the [[Hardscrabble Hatchery]] on [[Hardscrabble Creek]], and secure upstream water rights in public ownership.
- Conduct a three-year study of factors affecting the cui-ui life cycle, and a two-person, four-year evaluation of hatchery-reared survival.
- Determine critical habitat under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), with essential habitat proposed as:
  - the Truckee River main channel from Derby Dam about 35 miles to Pyramid Lake;
  - Pyramid Lake below the 3795-foot elevation;
  - Hardscrabble Creek, about 7 miles from its headwaters to the lake.

**Agency comments and concerns**
- **Nevada Department of Fish and Game** objected to designating critical habitat along the entire historic Truckee range, proposing instead that only the river within the Pyramid Indian Reservation be considered.
- **Nevada Division of Environmental Protection** called fencing of the Truckee below Derby Dam premature, requested itemized costs, questioned the Fish and Wildlife Service's enforcement role, and asked for an assessment of impacts on Truckee water users. It recommended a full socio-economic impact statement before any critical habitat designation.
- **U.S. Environmental Protection Agency** will support the plan through pollution abatement under the Federal Water Pollution Control Act, but noted that habitat criteria will not be ready in time for its wastewater and water quality planning schedules and requested interim criteria.
- **Desert Research Institute** stated that the plan omits past and ongoing work, notably Koch's life history research, and that the cui-ui probably did not migrate far above Wadsworth.
- **Bureau of Reclamation** questioned its authority to fund fishery work beyond fiscal year 1978, requested a funding and authority section and an environmental assessment, and asked for a target population for reclassification.
- **Bureau of Land Management** reported only about 4 miles of public-land stream in the Hardscrabble drainage and noted potential uranium mining in the Virginia Mountains.
- **U.S. Army Corps of Engineers** is studying flood protection alternatives for Reno, Sparks and Truckee Meadows and pledged to consider cui-ui in project formulation.
- **Bureau of Outdoor Recreation** has no implementation role but suggested compatible recreation at Peigh Ranch.

**Unresolved questions raised across comments**
- How far up the Truckee the cui-ui historically migrated, which determines the number of fishways and screens needed.
- Whether the Truckee or Hardscrabble flows are meant in the flow objectives.
- Feasibility and cost-effectiveness of rough fish reduction.
- Whether transplants to other watersheds (e.g., Klamath or Utah Lake systems) should be considered.
