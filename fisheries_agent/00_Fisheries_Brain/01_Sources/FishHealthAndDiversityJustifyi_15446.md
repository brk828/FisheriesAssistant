# Fish Health and Diversity: Justifying Flows for a California Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #instream-flow #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This feature article describes how a citizen group, the [[Putah Creek Council]] (PCC), won a 1996 state court trial to increase flows in Putah Creek, California, below the [[Putah Diversion Dam]]. The case turned on the meaning of "good condition" for fish under [[Section 5937]] of the California Fish and Game Code. The authors (Moyle, Marchetti, Baldrige, and Taylor) define good condition at three levels: individual, population, and community. The trial judge ordered a 50% increase in minimum releases and resident native fish spawning flows, but denied anadromous fish flows and habitat maintenance flows. The decision was under appeal at publication (July 1998).

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Putah Creek drains the Mayacamas Mountains (the source text reads "Macaymas") in Napa and Lake counties and flows through Solano and Yolo counties into the [[Yolo Bypass]] and the [[Sacramento River]].
- **Native fish history:** The creek once supported all native resident fishes of the Sacramento Valley in elevation-linked assemblages. Anadromous fishes present in low numbers included steelhead, fall-run chinook salmon, and Pacific lamprey.
- **Nonnative species:** By the late nineteenth century, [[White Catfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Common Carp]] dominated the lower creek fishery.
- **Water development:** The [[Solano Project]], built by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and completed in 1957, centers on [[Monticello Dam]], which impounds [[Berryessa Reservoir]] (capacity 1.6 million acre-feet). The reservoir flooded about 14 miles of creek channel and drastically altered the downstream flow regime.
- **Flow history:** Before the project, summer flows were usually below 20 cfs and lower reaches were often intermittent. Average annual flow was 374,725 acre-feet. Under the 1970 [[State Water Resources Control Board]] schedule, releases could drop very low in dry years, and large sections below the diversion dam were dewatered.
- **Trial background:** Drought from 1987 to 1992 caused long reaches to dry and major fish die-offs from 1989. The PCC sued in August 1990. The trial ran five weeks in March and April 1996 in Sacramento County Superior Court.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Ecosystem framing:** The authors argue that aquatic biodiversity is declining faster than terrestrial biodiversity. Single-species instream flow models, such as [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM), have limited value for ecosystem-based management.
- **Flow regime:** Restoring natural seasonal flow patterns is presented as more important than simply increasing flows.
- **Remnant native assemblage:** A native fish assemblage persisted in the permanent reach below the diversion dam, which the authors describe as a relict "valley floor transitional assemblage." Its distribution was limited, and its persistence was considered doubtful.
- **Nonnative assemblages:** Warm, slow pools in the lower creek supported nonnative game fishes, which the authors judged to be in no danger because similar species dominate the Sacramento Valley floor.
- **Hydrology and sampling constraints:** Water was turbid, and channel geometry was complex. The Bureau of Reclamation abandoned an IFIM study, partly because of difficulty building [[Habitat Suitability Index]] (PHABSIM) curves.
- **Legal context:** The State Water Resources Control Board denied all instream flow water-right applications for fish, according to the authors. Other legal tools mentioned include the [[Public Trust Doctrine]] and Article 10, Section 2 of the California Constitution.

## Key Findings

**1. Three-tiered definition of "good condition" (Section 5937)**
- *Individual level:* Healthy fish have robust body condition, few diseases or lesions, typical regional growth rates, and appropriate responses to stimuli. Most fish in the flowing creek met this standard, and fish killed by drying were not in good condition.
- *Population level:* Populations need multiple age classes, viable size, and healthy individuals. Viable size was approximated by two surrogates: extensive habitat for all life stages, and broad distribution sufficient for indefinite persistence. Most native fishes failed this test. Sacramento blackfish were mostly found in small numbers in one or two size classes.
- *Community level:* Equivalent to Karr's biotic integrity concept. A healthy community is dominated by co-evolved species, has predictable structure, is resilient to extreme events, persists in membership over time, and is replicated geographically. The native assemblage below the diversion dam did not meet this standard.

**2. Four-component PCC flow proposal**
- Living-space flows: continuous flow to the Yolo Bypass year-round.
- Resident native fish spawning and rearing flows, enhanced in February and March.
- Habitat maintenance pulse flows every three to five years.
- Anadromous fish flows from November through April.

**3. Court ruling**
- Judge Richard K. Park ordered a 50% increase in the minimum release schedule, about 10,000 additional acre-feet per year, plus spawning and rearing flows for native fishes.
- He denied anadromous fish flows, reasoning that the historic anadromous run was small and the water costs too high.
- He denied habitat maintenance flows for similar cost reasons and because natural high-flow events occur periodically.
- The legal basis was Section 5937, the Public Trust Doctrine, and the California Constitution. The decision was appealed by SCWA and SID. PCC and the City of Davis filed a cross-appeal arguing that Section 5937 protects all fish, including salmon.

**4. Restoration versus improvement**
- Water agencies argued the project had already improved conditions and created the native assemblage below the dam, so fish were already in good condition there.
- PCC countered that the project caused cumulative ecosystem damage, that a short fixed release could not offset it, and that the agencies bore responsibility for restoration.

**5. Broader implications**
- The authors consider the good-condition definition transferable to other regulated streams.
- Qualitative models were accepted as a basis for flow recommendations.
- Wet years after the trial partly validated the model, including successful chinook salmon spawning in the creek.
- Local institutions and public amenity values (aesthetics, recreation, education, research) were key to the outcome.

**Conclusion:** The authors conclude that Section 5937 can serve as a legal tool for ecosystem-based flow allocation. The Putah Creek case shows that protecting native fish communities can be justified even without endangered species present, provided the good-condition criterion is applied at the individual, population, and community levels.
