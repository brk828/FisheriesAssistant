# Ecological Studies of Native Green River Fishes Below Flaming Gorge Dam, 1964-1966

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 Utah State University dissertation by Charles David Vanicek documents the post-impoundment fish fauna, habitat use, growth, food habits, parasites, reproduction and movements of native fishes in the Green River from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] to Ouray, Utah, from May 1964 to October 1966. The study followed the closure of the dam in November 1962 and a [[Rotenone Eradication Project|rotenone eradication program]] in 1962 undertaken to remove non-game fishes before reservoir stocking. Four native species were studied intensively: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Colorado Chub]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Humpback Sucker]]. The dam altered seasonal flow and water temperature, and native fish populations were largely replaced by introduced trout in the first 26 miles below the dam. Below the [[Yampa River]] confluence, fish populations were similar to pre-impoundment conditions.

## Background & Description

The study was part of a broader federal program assigned to the Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit to evaluate environmental and faunal effects of Flaming Gorge Dam and the Green River Fish Control Project. Its stated objectives were to describe species composition and distribution, compare them with pre-impoundment years, describe growth, reproduction, survival and food habits, identify limiting factors, and determine whether environmental requirements of selected species were being met.

The four selected species are all endemic to the Colorado River drainage:

- **Colorado squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): the largest native cyprinid in North America, locally called the "Colorado salmon."
- **Colorado chub** (*Gila robusta*): a large cyprinid with two forms in the Green River, the roundtail (*G. r. robusta*) and bonytail (*G. r. elegans*), whose taxonomic status is debated.
- **Bluehead sucker** (*Pantosteus delphinus*): a catostomid showing variation in caudal peduncle depth.
- **Humpback sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): a monotypic catostomid that hybridizes with the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]].

Methods included electrofishing, gill nets, seines and fry gear (food strainers, tow nets, modified scoop shovel). The study recorded 539 collections (the abstract states 639; the body text gives 539) containing 23,735 fish. Scales were used for age and growth, with validation by length-frequency analysis. Water chemistry, temperature and discharge were compared with USGS and state records.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Green River flows from the western slope of the Wind River Range through Red Canyon, Browns Park, Lodore Canyon, Echo Park (confluence with the Yampa River), Whirlpool Canyon, Island Park, Split Mountain Canyon and Uintah Basin to Ouray, Utah. Four longitudinal zones were defined by gradient: a fast zone from the dam to Swallow Canyon (26 miles); a reduced-gradient zone to Lodore Canyon (21 miles); a steep canyon zone in Dinosaur National Monument (47 miles); and a slow, silt-bottomed zone to Ouray (66 miles). Vegetation is sparse except in the Little Hole-Bridgeport area.

**Flow.** Before impoundment, the Green and Yampa rivers had similar seasonal patterns with a peak in May or June. After closure, the spring peak was replaced by a more stable seasonal pattern. At Jensen, the pattern resembled pre-impoundment years, though peak run-off shifted to May and late fall and winter flows were higher.

**Temperature.** Before impoundment, temperatures rose from the mid-30s °F in March to the mid-70s °F in July. After closure, temperatures at Greendale stayed largely between 35 and 55 °F. In high-discharge summers (1964 and 1966), temperatures were well below pre-impoundment values as far downstream as Island Park or Jensen. Maximum summer temperatures at Little Hole were 53 °F and 55 °F in 1964 and 1966, against 63 °F and 59 °F in the low-discharge years 1963 and 1965.

**Water chemistry.** No permanent change was found in annual bicarbonate, total dissolved solids, conductance or pH after closure. Dissolved oxygen ranged from 6.3 to 13.0 ppm, mostly 6.5 to 8.5 ppm. Total alkalinity during April to November was 135 to 160 ppm. pH ranged from 7.6 to 8.6. Hydrogen sulfide from the reservoir monimolimnion reached the tailwater in March and April 1965, killing some trout.

**Turbidity.** Mean suspended sediment at Jensen fell from 1529 ppm (12 years before impoundment) to 870 ppm (first 4 years after).

**Fish distribution.** Twenty-one species were collected (9 indigenous, 11 exotic). [[Rainbow Trout]] was the most abundant fish to at least 26 miles below the dam. The [[Redside Shiner]] and speckled dace dominated Dinosaur National Monument, and carp dominated Wonsits Valley.

## Key Findings

- **Dam effects on natives:** Native fishes were largely eliminated in the first 7 miles below the dam and reduced in number as far as the Yampa mouth. Colorado squawfish, humpback sucker and Colorado chub disappeared from much of the upper reach. Below the Yampa, fish populations were not significantly affected.
- **Reproduction:** Colorado squawfish, Colorado chub and bluehead sucker reproduced successfully below the Yampa mouth in all four years after impoundment. In the high-discharge years 1964 and 1966, no native reproduction was found above the Yampa mouth. Chubs reproduced at Lodore in 1965, when temperatures resembled those downstream. No juvenile humpback suckers were collected.
- **Spawning threshold:** Colorado chub and bluehead sucker spawned when water temperature reached about 65 °F. Squawfish spawned about a month later. Spawning in 1966 occurred roughly a month earlier than in 1964 and 1965.
- **Growth:** Colorado squawfish and Colorado chub grew more slowly after closure (1963-1965) than before (1957-1962), with significance at the 0.01 level (sign test). Growth was linked to altered temperature patterns. Squawfish percentage deviation from mean annual increment moved from +19% in 1958 to -27% in 1965.
- **Roundtail vs. bonytail:** The bonytail grew faster after the third year. The roundtail became heavier than the bonytail at larger lengths. Their length-weight relationships differed significantly. The author suggested they may be sibling species rather than subspecies.
- **Squawfish diet:** Squawfish under about 50 mm fed on cladocerans, copepods and chironomid larvae. Above 200 mm they were entirely piscivorous, and 39% of stomachs from fish over 200 mm were empty.
- **Chub diet:** Young chubs fed mainly on chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs. Adults over 200 mm fed mainly on terrestrial insects. Bonytails ate terrestrial insects, plant debris and filamentous algae.
- **Parasites:** Bass tapeworm (*Proteocephalus ambloplites*) occurred in 65% of squawfish over 200 mm. Anchor worm (*Lernea* sp.) was common on chubs.
- **Movements:** Of 386 flannelmouth suckers marked, 31 were recaptured, with a significant downstream movement. Of 216 bluehead suckers, 12 were recaptured, and movements were slight. Of 13 humpback suckers, four were recaptured, with slight downstream movement.
- **Management conclusion:** Environmental requirements of the three species are apparently met below the Yampa confluence. Between the dam and Echo Park, requirements, particularly water temperature, are not met.
- **Large fish decline:** Very large squawfish reported in the past were no longer found. The author noted possible causes, including reduced growth, heavy fishing pressure and parasite loads. Local fishermen blamed competition with introduced [[Channel Catfish]].
