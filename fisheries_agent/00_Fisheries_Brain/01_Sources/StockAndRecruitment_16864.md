# Stock and Recruitment: Density-Dependent Reproduction Curves and Population Regulation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stock-recruitment

## Executive Summary

Ricker (1954) synthesizes the theory of how population size is regulated by density-dependent mortality, and how the resulting stock-recruitment (reproduction) curves shape abundance over time. Plotting filial egg production against parental egg production typically gives a domed curve crossing the [[45-Degree Replacement Line]]. Steep descending limbs generate oscillations, damped or permanent depending on slope and generational mixing. Removing mature stock can stabilize or reduce such cycles, and the paper argues that most observed reproduction curves for fishes and invertebrates have a downward-sloping right limb. A cannibalism model yields an asymmetrical dome shape. The paper concludes that no universal theory of recruitment exists, but that stock-recruitment relationships must be considered in predicting sustainable exploitation.

## Background & Description

The paper is a 1954 article in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada* (vol. 11, no. 5), written by W. E. Ricker of the [[Pacific Biological Station]] in Nanaimo, B.C. Portions were earlier presented in the *Journal of Wildlife Management* as part of a symposium on cycles in animal populations.

The author argues that fishery yield models ("the modern theory of fishing") predict catch from recruits but neglect how adult stock size affects future recruitment. The work therefore focuses on the mechanisms that limit population size.

Key conceptual terms:
- **Reproduction curve:** a plot of progeny (filial) egg production against parental egg production, with axes in egg units.
- **Compensatory (density-dependent) mortality:** mortality whose effect increases with stock density; it is the main regulating force, and the paper restricts it mainly to young stages (eggs, larvae, fry, fingerlings).
- **Non-compensatory mortality:** density-independent mortality, such as extreme temperatures, drought, and floods.
- **Equilibrium:** the stock density where the reproduction curve crosses the 45-degree line.

The paper builds on work by [[Nicholson and Bailey Host-Parasite Model]], [[Verhulst Logistic Equation]], and [[Cole Random Population Cycles]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mechanisms of compensatory mortality** (eight listed): prevention of breeding when sites are occupied; limited good breeding areas; competition for space among young; starvation; greater predation on slow-growing, small individuals; cannibalism by adults; parasitism more frequent in large broods; and accumulation of waste materials in confined media.

**Predation situations** are classified as:
- Type A: predators eat a fixed number regardless of prey abundance (short-lived).
- Type B: predators take a fixed fraction of prey (produces the domed reproduction curve).
- Type C: predators take all prey above a minimum set by habitat limits (produces a flat-topped curve).

**Random environmental variation:** simulations using normal-distribution random multipliers show that single-age spawners eventually fluctuate toward extinction, while multiple-age spawners tend to increase without limit under purely density-independent effects.

**Exploitation:** harvesting mature fish at a constant rate shifts equilibrium abundance and can suppress oscillations. Juvenile harvest during the compensatory phase may increase recruitment, as the author suggests for the [[Quoddy Herring Fishery]].

## Key Findings

**Theoretical results**
1. Density-dependent mortality limits population size; reproduction curves must cut the 45-degree line and end below it.
2. For single-age spawners, a slope between −1 and −∞ at the replacement point produces unstable oscillation; slopes between 0 and −1 produce damped cycles (Figs. 5, 7, 8).
3. Oscillation period is approximately twice the mean egg-to-egg generation interval, and independent of curve shape (Figs. 9-10).
4. Random environmental fluctuation alone causes extinction in single-age spawners and unlimited increase in multiple-age spawners (Figs. 11A, 12A).
5. Moderate exploitation of mature stock can raise average abundance; intensive exploitation lowers it. Light to moderate exploitation can maintain but reduce oscillations (Fig. 16, Table III).
6. In a cannibalism model, reproduction is proportional to the expression w·e^(−w) (eq. 10), with maximum reproduction at a survival rate of 1/e (≈0.3679) of the low-density value.

**Empirical examples**
- **Pacific herring (*Clupea pallasi*), west Vancouver Island:** Tester's index gave r = −0.80 (1931-48), not significantly different from zero; with extended data (Stevenson), r = −0.16 for 1931-49. The largest broods came from smaller spawnings. A 1943-47 stanza of abundant year-classes is noted.
- **Pink salmon (*Oncorhynchus gorbuscha*), McClinton Creek, B.C.:** fry production declines with increasing parental stock, suggesting freshwater compensation. The curve slopes downward from about 15,000-20,000 spawners.
- **Pink salmon, Karluk River, Alaska:** a broad-range curve; the 1924 escapement was "over 4,000,000" and possibly reduced by fungus.
- **Coho salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*), Minter Creek, Washington:** smolt output is flat at high spawner numbers, with a productive capacity of about 25,000-85,000 smolts depending on flow.
- **Sockeye salmon (*Oncorhynchus nerka*), Karluk Lake:** data from 1921-29 year-classes; peak production at 1-1.5 million spawners, but the lake stock has since declined.
- **Late Shuswap sockeye (Fraser River):** a steep ascending limb; a possible maximum at 1-1.5 million spawners.
- **Haddock (*Melanogrammus aeglefinus*), George's Bank (1912-29):** the best natural example of a steep descending limb; two oscillations with trough-to-trough periods of 9 and 8 years and trough:peak amplitude of about 1:2. Intensified trawl fishing after about 1930 appears to have eliminated the oscillation.
- **Fruit fly (*Drosophila melanogaster*):** Pearl and Parker (1920s) and Chiang and Hodson (1950) show decreasing fecundity with larval density.
- **Water flea (*Daphnia magna*), Pratt (1943):** reproduction curves at 18°C and 25°C are steeper than any fish curve; at 25°C, observed and computed oscillations are similar in period.
- **Starfish (*Asterias forbesi*), New England:** a 14-year oscillation with a peak-to-trough ratio of about 20:1 (Burkenroad 1946) is speculatively attributed to a steep curve, with no reproduction curve available.
- **Azuki bean weevil (*Callosobruchus chinensis*):** damped oscillations observed under experimental conditions.

**Management implications**
- Most fish reproduction curves examined have a downward-sloping right limb.
- Juvenile harvest is not inherently unsound; the optimal age of exploitation depends on each stock.
- Attempts to reduce nuisance fish (e.g., squawfish at Cultus Lake; ciscoes at Lesser Slave Lake) often fail once the initial removal of old stock ends.
