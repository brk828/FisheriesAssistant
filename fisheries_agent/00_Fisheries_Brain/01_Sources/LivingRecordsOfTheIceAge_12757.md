# Living Records of the Ice Age

#ichthyology #marine-biology #biogeography #pleistocene-biogeography

## Executive Summary

This 1949 *Scientific American* article by [[Edward S. Deevey Jr.]] argues that the present distribution of animals and plants can reveal [[Pleistocene Epoch]] geography that geologists cannot always recover. Using examples from the British Isles, the Philippines, the Great Basin of the western United States, Africa and eastern North America, the author shows how the absence of species (such as snakes in Ireland), the isolation of populations, and the spread of aquatic taxa can be read as records of land bridges, lake connections and pluvial climates. The article also cautions that biogeographic reconstructions are easily mistaken, using the [[Nunatak Hypothesis]] as a cautionary case. Fishes are discussed mainly in the Great Basin context. The piece is a review essay rather than a primary study, and it reports no new sample data.

## Background & Description

- **Format:** Reprint from *Scientific American* (May 1949), with a note on the author and a short bibliography.
- **Author:** [[Edward S. Deevey Jr.]], professor of biology and director of the Geochronometric Laboratory at Yale University. His research centered on paleoecology, and an NSF award supported lake-sediment coring in the Auvergne and Jura Mountains.
- **Core theme:** [[Biogeography]] as a tool for the geologist. The author states that the existing range of a species reflects both past and present geography, and that biogeography can sometimes correct or confirm geological interpretations.
- **Chronological framework:** Much of the British sequence rests on [[Pollen Analysis]], a time scale devised by biologists, which dates postglacial events such as the [[Boreal Phase]].
- **Cited works:** Stanley A. Cain, *Foundations of Plant Geography* (1944); Richard Foster Flint, *Glacial Geology and the Pleistocene Epoch* (1947).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**British Isles and the English Channel.** Ireland has no land snakes, although [[Great Britain]] has at least three native species. Ireland does have a salamander, two tailless amphibians and a lizard, all shared with Britain. The mammal fauna is also incomplete: Ireland lacks voles, the brown hare, the common shrew, the mole, the weasel, the dormouse and the yellow-necked field mouse. It does have the frog, hedgehog, pygmy shrew and stoat. The author argues that these patterns point to a few early postglacial immigrants crossing a land bridge via Britain before the rising sea cut Ireland off. Some Irish forms, such as the Irish hare and Irish stoat, have diverged from their European relatives. Peat dredged from the North Sea at depths down to 162 feet contains fossil beetles and Boreal-age pollen, showing a former land surface that was later drowned.

**Glacial sea-level change.** At the glacial climax, sea level is estimated to have been lowered by about 300 feet. Most estimates for the worldwide fall range from 230 to 335 feet. After the glacial retreat, the sea rose about 150 feet by the end of the Boreal phase, forming the [[English Channel]]. The land was also rebounding from ice loading, so the two processes were partly opposed.

**Visayan Islands, Philippines.** [[Visayan Islands]] (Negros, Panay and Masbate) sit on a shelf less than 160 feet deep and are separated from Cebu by a 320-foot strait. The author reports 32 kinds of nonmigratory birds restricted to the Visayan group and 12 restricted to Cebu. The 32 endemic subspecies are shared among the three joined islands but absent from Cebu. This pattern implies a sea-level lowering greater than 160 feet but less than 320 feet, which the author considers consistent with published estimates.

**Great Basin fishes.** The article's fish-focused section concerns the [[Great Basin]] and its pluvial lakes. [[Lake Bonneville]] and [[Lake Lahontan]] were large Pleistocene lakes, of which [[Great Salt Lake]] and [[Pyramid Lake]] are remnants. Fishes of the same species found in separate basins indicate past connections. Dry or nearly dry basins such as Big Washoe Lake and Carson Sink still hold Lahontan-type fishes in streams and springs. Pyramid Lake's endemic salmon and sucker are Lahontan types, implying the lake's fauna dates back at least 15,000 years. This conflicts with a geological estimate that the lake's freshness dates only to about 4,000 years. The author notes that the conflict was resolved when geologists accepted an earlier outlet, with salts later deposited in the Black Rock Desert.

**Africa.** Biogeographic data suggest that the Sahara and other deserts had pluvial episodes. The waterbug genus [[Corixa]], native to temperate Europe, has an outlying population at [[Lake Naivasha]] in the Rift Valley. A salamander in French Guinea appears to have migrated from Europe and is the only salamander below the Tropic of Cancer in the Old World. The bird distribution between Abyssinia and East African mountains suggests a former wider forest cover on the plateau.

**Eastern North America.** Many plants in the Gulf of St. Lawrence highlands have disjunct populations in the Rocky Mountains, sometimes about 2,000 miles away. The author considers the St. Lawrence flora to have survived the last glaciation in refuges south of the ice and then migrated north, while the disjunctions are explained by the breaking up of a formerly continuous range.

## Key Findings

- The absence of snakes and several mammals from Ireland, despite their presence in Britain, is interpreted as the result of postglacial sea-level rise cutting off a land connection with the Continent, with the Irish list reflecting early immigration.
- The English Channel formed around the end of the Boreal phase, after the sea rose about 150 feet. Drowned peat with fossil beetles and Boreal pollen supplies the dating evidence.
- Visayan bird distribution constrains the last glacial sea-level lowering to between 160 and 320 feet, consistent with global estimates of 230 to 335 feet.
- Shared fish species across Great Basin basins, including the endemic Pyramid Lake salmon and sucker, reveal past connections among Lake Lahontan waters and suggest a lake fauna at least 15,000 years old.
- Disjunct distributions of aquatic and terrestrial organisms across today's deserts (Corixa in East Africa, the salamander in French Guinea) support past pluvial episodes.
- Biogeographic reconstruction is prone to error, especially when pushed far back in geologic time. The nunatak hypothesis, proposed by [[Merritt L. Fernald]] in 1925, is cited as an overreach because warmth-loving plants such as irises and lady's-slippers could not have survived on nunataks.
- Geologists who reexamined the nunatak question found that frost action destroys glacial evidence on mountain peaks, and that foreign boulders were decisive. Active ice had moved over some supposed nunataks.
- The author's overall conclusion is that biogeographic and geological evidence should be combined, because strictly geological data may be inadequate for restoring Pleistocene land and stream connections.
