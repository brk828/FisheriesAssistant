# Pollen Analysis of Pluvial Lake Cochise Shore Deposits, Southern Arizona

#palynology #paleoecology #pleistocene #lake-cochise

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by [[Richard H. Hevly]] and [[Paul S. Martin]] applies [[Pollen Analysis]] to mound deposits at Croton Springs on the shore of the [[Willcox Playa]] in [[Cochise County]], Arizona. The authors test [[Meinzer's Hypothesis]], which held that the mounds formed by aeolian trapping of dust after the desiccation of [[Pluvial Lake Cochise]]. The pollen record contradicts this view: the fossil spectra are dominated by tree pollen, indicating that the mounds are erosional remnants of the pluvial period, when the climate was more mesic and the lake flooded the shore. Comparison with modern soils from the [[Catalina Mountains]] suggests a vertical displacement of vegetation belts of about 900 to 1200 m during the pluvial period.

## Background & Description

The study area lies in desert grassland dominated by *Aristida*, *Hilaria*, *Bouteloua*, and *Sporobolus*, with shrubs such as *Yucca*, *Opuntia*, *Gutierrezia*, *Ephedra*, and mesquite (*Prosopis*). The mounds were sampled at about 4150 ft (1245 m) elevation, 11.6 km southwest of Willcox.

Sampling and methods:
- Soil cores were collected at 10 cm intervals to depths of 1 to 2 m from trenches at three locations in each of three knolls, on March 6 and June 21, 1960.
- Modern pollen rain was sampled from nine stock tank and soil surface samples, plus sediment from six metal-rim stock tanks and an artificial pond.
- Three Catalina Mountain soil samples (2160 to 2370 m) served as modern analogues.
- Extraction used a silica depressant preconcentration method ([[Arms Method]], 1960), flotation, hydrofluoric acid digestion, and acetolysis. At least 400 pollen grains were counted per level.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water table and hydrology.** Water tables in the knolls lay 1.0 to 1.5 m lower than Meinzer reported, even after 9.3 cm of winter rain recorded at Willcox from December through February. Seepage was absent except at an artificial goldfish pond. Hydrophytic conditions persisted at mound tops, marked by colonies of [[Juncus Mexicanus]] (rush).

**Salinity.** The surrounding flat is hard and salt-crusted, with [[Suaeda Torreyana]] (seep weed) abundant. The barren flat covers about 50 square miles, and at nine stations total soluble salt exceeds 3 percent, mainly chlorides with some black alkali (Na₂CO₃). The saline crust protects the flat from wind deflation.

**Pollen rain.** Modern tank samples show pine at 1 to 10 percent. Pine sources lie in the [[Pinaleno Mountains]], [[Chiricahua Mountains]], and [[Dragoon Mountains]]. Fossil levels below 20 cm show tree pollen exceeding 50 percent and sometimes 90 percent.

**Vegetation history.** The fossil flora is interpreted as a pluvial yellow pine parkland or forest, with occasional spruce, fir, and Douglas fir on sheltered slopes and near streams. The desert grassland was displaced to lower elevations within the present [[Sonoran Desert]].

## Key Findings

- **Origin of mounds:** Pollen content indicates the mounds are pluvial in age. Meinzer's postpluvial aeolian hypothesis is not supported. The high pine frequency in uneroded Cochise Overpass shore clay (96 percent pine, about 10 m above the playa) reinforces this.
- **Stratigraphic record:** Mound B yielded a continuous pollen record. The upper 10 cm is a rooted soil zone, 10 to 50 cm is gray sand with salt grass roots, and 60 to 110 cm is sandy blue clay. Mounds A and C yielded only sparse or surface pollen, possibly due to post-depositional washout.
- **Dominant taxa:** Pine dominates, with more than 90 percent at lower levels. Abies, Picea, and Pseudotsuga together total 2 to 4 percent.
- **Pine pollen size:** Smaller mean pine grains, probably including pinyon, predominate in upper levels. The largest grains occur at 50 to 80 percent pine with maximum sagebrush, suggesting shifts in forest composition.
- **Artemisia peak:** Artemisia reached 10 to 20 percent between 50 and 80 cm, indicating a drier episode and possibly a dense sagebrush ring on fresh beach deposits at slightly lower lake level.
- **Sedges and unknowns:** Sedge pollen in upper levels suggests sedges grew on the mounds as recently as Meinzer's visit. An unidentified Umbelliferae-type pollen reached 23 percent at 20 cm.
- **Modern analogues:** Catalina soil spectra closely match fossil horizons (for example, level 80 with Bear Wallow, level 110 with Summerhaven, level 50 with Rose Canyon). Fossil levels 30, 40, 90, 100, and 110 indicate a vertical displacement of at least 900 to 1200 m during the pluvial period.
- **Biogeographic implication:** Pluvial conditions allowed coniferous flora and fauna from the southern Rocky Mountains and [[Colorado Plateau]] to spread south into the Chiricahua and Huachuca mountains and the [[Sierra Madre Occidental]].
- **Methodological recommendation:** Reasonable estimates of pluvial conditions can be obtained from shallow deposits near the playa margin, without costly playa coring. Similar sediments may be sought at margins of other pluvial lake basins in arid America.
