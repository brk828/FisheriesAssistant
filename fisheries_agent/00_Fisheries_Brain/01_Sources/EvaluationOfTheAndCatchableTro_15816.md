# Salt River Catchable Rainbow Trout Stocking Evaluation (1974–1975)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department supplement evaluates the [[Catchable Trout Program]] in the [[Salt River]] below [[Stewart Mountain Dam]] during 1974 and 1975. About 30,000 [[Rainbow Trout]] were stocked and 100 percent were tagged with [[Flag Tags]] to estimate harvest, angler usage and catch rates. Harvest was estimated at roughly 50 to 65 percent of planted fish, and the fishery produced about two angling hours per trout planted. The authors conclude the program is successful and recommend making it a permanent part of the state trout program, with changes to stocking numbers, frequency and a rotenone renovation.

## Background & Description

The Salt River below Stewart Mountain Dam was first stocked with trout in 1946. A fingerling program ran from 1966 until it was discontinued after the 1969 plants, due to declining angling success and unmanageable flow variation. Renewed interest after the 1973 energy crisis led to planting catchable trout near the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]], so anglers could fish without long trips to northern waters.

The study area is a regulated reach of about 23 km between Stewart Mountain Dam and [[Granite Reef Diversion Dam]]. It runs through [[Sonoran Desert Riparian Habitat]] dominated by mesquite, cottonwood and palo verde, with arrowweed, cattail and giant reed on sand bars. Flows are set by power demand daily and by season: winter flows average 25 cubic feet per second (cfs) or less (October–March), while summer releases average about 1,600 cfs (April–September). Annual rainfall is under 25.4 cm. Recreational use includes [[Tubers]], fishing, camping and motorcycling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water conditions.** Summer water temperatures ranged from 17°C in May to 22°C in September. PH and temperature were monitored in Areas 1, 4 and 5 and were within acceptable ranges for rainbow trout. Temperatures warmed insignificantly from upper to lower reaches. The warmer 1974 temperatures were linked to extended pump-back operation at [[Mormon Flat Dam]] on [[Saguaro Lake]] (over 260 days in 1974 versus fewer than 100 in 1975), which warmed hypolimnetic water released downstream. The authors warn that continued pump-generation could raise release temperatures to levels that prevent future stocking.

**Limited persistence.** Suitable trout water is usually available only from May through August, averaging 130 days over the previous decade. Almost no tagged 1974 trout were recovered in 1975, indicating they did not overwinter in the small pools left after releases ended.

**Fish community.** Gila mountain suckers (*Pantosteus clarki*) filled gill nets in 1974, reducing sampling effectiveness. The authors suggest suckers may increase stress and disease spread, particularly ich, in overwintering trout, though they doubt competition for food.

**Movement.** Trout showed minimal downstream movement, possibly restricted by a 1–2 °C warming over the 16 km reach to Coon Bluff. The farthest recorded downstream movement was 2 km.

## Key Findings

### Stocking and Tagging
- About 30,000 catchable rainbow trout (mean fork length 22 cm) were stocked over two years, 15,000 per year, in five plants of 3,000 each per year.
- Plant intervals varied from 13 to 30 days; the mean interval was 15 days in 1974 and 29 days in 1975.
- All fish were tagged with flag tags, and 200 fish per 1975 plant were weighed, measured and individually numbered for growth assessment.
- Tag boxes supplied most returns (73.8 percent of 6,485 tags). Creel returns made up 15.7 percent, and mail, telephone and hand delivery supplied the rest.

### Harvest and Returns
- Harvest estimates ranged from 50 to 65 percent of planted fish. Tag data gave 2,400 fish (16 percent) for 1974 and 7,590 fish (50 percent) for 1975.
- [[Angler Interviews]] produced return values of 60 percent (1974) and 65 percent (1975).
- A 40 percent [[Nonreporting Rate]] was applied, based on a 100 percent check station at [[Canyon Lake]].
- The authors judged the actual harvest to be within 50–65 percent.

### Catch Rate and Angler Effort
- Catch rates were 0.51 fish per hour in 1974 and 0.35 in 1975 (the Results section reports 0.32 for 1975).
- The Arizona standard for an acceptable catchable program is 0.50 fish per hour.
- Angler numbers rose about 58 percent in 1975 without more trout stocked. Weekday anglers out-caught weekend anglers, and weekends had 44 percent more anglers.
- About 10,000 anglers spent about 30,000 hours fishing from May to October 1975.
- Harvest was reduced after the 1975 interviews, with about two angling hours per trout planted. By comparison, [[Oak Creek]] produced 0.9 hours per trout planted.

### Exploitation Timing
- The [[Exploitation Curve]] followed the classic catchable pattern, with most harvest soon after planting.
- In 1975, 57 percent of catch came in the first 10 days and 77 percent within 20 days.
- In 1974, 74 percent was caught only after 40 days.
- The authors recommend planning on 75 percent removal within 20 days.

### Growth
- Only six of 1,000 recovered preweighed trout had been in the river long enough (at least three weeks) to show measurable growth.
- Based on the sample and an additional 41 trout, catchables gained about 30 g and 3.5 cm by four weeks.
- One tagged fish at large for 25 weeks gained 184 g.
- Earlier fingerlings (11 cm in May 1967) reached 23 cm by August.

### Catch Composition
- Rainbow trout made up 88 percent of the combined catch in both years (1,234 of 1,400 fish).
- Other species included [[Largemouth Bass]], bluegill, green sunfish, channel catfish, bullhead, carp and a few brown trout, mostly incidental catches.

### Management Recommendations
1. Conduct a complete [[Rotenone Renovation]] from Stewart Mountain Dam to below Blue Point Bridge in 1976, immediately after flow termination, to estimate remaining trout and sucker numbers.
2. Stock fingerlings with catchables in 1977, beginning when continuous releases start in March or April. Stop catchable plants once fingerlings reach creel size (about 23 cm).
3. Reduce stocking to 2,000 trout every 15 days in 1977 to maintain acceptable returns and reduce catch-rate fluctuations.

### Overall Conclusion
The authors judge the Salt River fishery feasible and a permanent part of Arizona's trout program. Still, they note that rising angler numbers may push catch rates below 0.50 fish per hour without changes to stocking, and they flag thermal loading from pump-storage operations as a long-term risk.
