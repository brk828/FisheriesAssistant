# Decline in Abundance, Diversity, and Distribution of Native Freshwater Fishes of Western North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This December 1995 review by Gary Schiffmiller (University of New Mexico) documents a rapid, unprecedented decline in the abundance, diversity, and distribution of native freshwater fishes in western North America. Western species account for a disproportionate share of threatened and extinct North American fishes. The decline results from several interacting causes, chiefly [[Habitat Fragmentation]] and degradation and the introduction of [[Nonnative Species]]. Pollution, [[Overharvesting]], and fisheries management policies are contributing factors. The author concludes that recovery efforts have had mixed success and that a more vigorous conservation strategy is required.

## Background & Description

- **Global context:** More than half of all living vertebrates are fishes. About 40.5% of fishes are freshwater species, although fresh water makes up less than 0.01% of the earth's water. Freshwater fishes are among the most threatened vertebrate groups, probably second only to amphibians.
- **Listing trends:** The [[American Fisheries Society]] endangered list grew 45% from 1979 to 1989. Twenty-six taxa were removed during this period: 16 because of new information or taxonomic changes, and 10 because they went extinct. No delistings resulted from recovery. Worldwide, the number of threatened fish species reported by the [[International Union for Conservation of Nature]] rose 27% from 1990 to 1994, reflecting both better knowledge and increasing threats.
- **Western share:** About 170 of roughly 910 native North American freshwater fish species (about 19%) occur west of the continental divide. Of 224 listed or candidate taxa in North America, 105 (47%) are western. Of 40 species known to have gone extinct in historic times, 31 (78%) came from western states, provinces, and Mexico.
- **Endemism:** Western fauna is highly endemic because of a geologically disruptive history that isolated populations in scarce, unstable habitats. Although the southeastern United States has the most endemic species, the West has the highest proportion. More than 70% of species in the [[Colorado River Basin]] are endemic.
- **Regional tallies:** New Mexico has lost 12 native species to extinction or extirpation and has 20 of 66 native taxa listed as endangered, threatened, or of special concern. Other regional figures are 42 of 58 species for California, 22 of 26 for Arizona, and "43 taxa out of 39 species" for Nevada. The Nevada figure is internally inconsistent as printed in the source.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline.** Citing Miller et al. (1989), the author reports that more than one factor was implicated in 82% of North American fish extinctions in the past century. The most frequently cited factors were:

| Factor | Share of extinctions |
|---|---|
| Physical habitat alteration (impoundments, channelization, diversion, drainage) | 73% |
| Introduction of nonnative species | 68% |
| Chemical habitat alteration | 38% |
| Hybridization | 38% |
| Overharvesting | 15% |

**Habitat loss and degradation.**
- **Dams:** More than 50% of the [[Columbia River Basin]] is blocked. Dams slow current, which increases juvenile salmon (smolt) migration time two to three times and raises mortality. Impoundments trap nutrients, slowing [[White Sturgeon]] growth, and warm water, which favors disease and warm-water predators. Cold, steady releases below dams favor nonnative species over native fishes adapted to warm, seasonally fluctuating flows. On the [[White River]] in Colorado, nonnatives made up 90% of species in the reservoir and 80% downstream after impoundment.
- **Water diversion and groundwater pumping:** Diversions contributed to the decline of the endangered [[Woundfin]] in the Virgin River. Groundwater withdrawal near [[Ash Meadows]] lowered spring levels, threatening at least four endemic taxa, including [[Devils Hole Pupfish]].
- **Channelization:** In a study of 45 Idaho streams, undisturbed streams averaged eight times the weight of game fish found in channelized streams.
- **Acid precipitation:** Acid effects are most severe in New England, the Adirondacks, and southeastern Canada. Rainbow trout are the least acid-tolerant salmonid tested, followed by Atlantic salmon, brown trout, and brook trout. Negative effects appear at pH 6.5, and pH 4.0 to 3.5 is lethal to most fish over extended periods.
- **Livestock and logging:** Livestock grazing increased fine sediment in streams used by [[Paiute Cutthroat Trout]] in the White Mountains of California. The U.S. Forest Service rejected a no-grazing alternative, despite spending $260,000 to repair livestock-related watershed damage. Logging-related sediment affected coho salmon in the Pacific Northwest.
- **Contaminants:** Irrigation drainage contaminated habitat at [[Kesterson National Wildlife Refuge]]. In the Green River, Utah, inorganic contaminants including vanadium, zinc, selenium, lithium, uranium, and boron threaten [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail Chub]].
- **Ultraviolet-B radiation:** Simulated mid-latitude summer irradiance caused sunburn, fungal infection, and death in [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]. Lahontan cutthroat trout showed sunburn within two days, fungal infection within three, and death within six. [[Rainbow Trout]] showed similar sunburn and infection but survived. Few [[Apache Trout]] developed sunburn, and none developed fungal infection.

**Nonnative species.**
- **Introductions:** Common goldfish, the first nonnative fish recorded in North America (around 1690), now occur in every state except Alaska. [[Common Carp]] were first introduced in 1831 (Hudson River, New York) and in 1871 in California. [[Brown Trout]] were first introduced in April 1883 in Michigan and are now in 41 states, where they are implicated in cutthroat trout declines. An illegal tropical fish farm contributed to the extinction of the Ash Meadows killifish.
- **Predation:** [[Largemouth Bass]] introduced into Lago de Pátzcuaro, Mexico, caused a drastic decline of the Pátzcuaro whitefish. [[Mosquitofish]], introduced for mosquito control, drove declines of the Yaqui topminnow and eliminated Gila topminnow unless refugia were present. Rainbow trout strongly preyed on Little Colorado spinedace, which show almost no predator avoidance.
- **Competition:** Nonnative guppies caused declines of White River springfish. [[Red Shiner]] introduction in the Gila River basin led [[Spikedace]] to shift into swifter, less favorable water. The mechanism is hypothesized, not demonstrated.
- **Hybridization:** Introduced rainbow trout hybridize with Apache, cutthroat, and Gila trout. In Arizona, Carmichael et al. (1993) analyzed 31 trout populations and found only 11 potentially unhybridized. Nineteen contained rainbow-Apache hybrids, and four contained cutthroat-Apache hybrids. Brook trout have displaced native [[Bull Trout]] genetically in the Columbia and Klamath drainages.
- **Parasites:** Introduced guppies spread the trematode *Gyrodactylus turnbulli* to Poeciliopsis in Sonora. A clonal lineage susceptible to the parasite illustrates how low genetic diversity increases extinction risk.
- **Ecosystem effects:** Common carp uproot vegetation, increasing turbidity and releasing phosphate, which can push systems toward [[Eutrophication]].

**Overharvesting and management.**
- In the [[Columbia River Basin]], the chinook summer run was depleted by 1889. By 1975, total chinook catch had fallen eight-fold from its historical peak. Coho catch fell ten-fold, and steelhead and chum catches fell 100-fold. Sockeye could no longer support a commercial fishery. Dams contributed, but overharvesting "has certainly played a role."
- Following impoundment behind Flaming Gorge Dam in September 1962, managers treated 445 miles of the Green River and its tributaries with rotenone to eliminate nongame fish. This caused the near-extirpation of several endemic fishes.
- Large-scale hatchery stocking reduces genetic diversity because broodstock come from few parents. An Idaho model predicted native steelhead extinction under some long-term stocking scenarios.

**Conservation genetics.** Protecting distinct populations matters because many species vary genetically more between populations than within them. Dexter National Fish Hatchery work on [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]], and [[Pecos Gambusia]] showed that captive stocks founded with 30 to 50 wild fish and bred for 6 to 8 years retained heterozygosity similar to their source populations, though some rare alleles were lost. The author recommends founding captive stocks with hundreds of wild fish and periodically introducing wild genetic material.

## Key Findings

1. **Western fishes are disproportionately imperiled.** About 47% of threatened and 78% of extinct North American fish taxa come from the West. Between 1979 and 1989, the endangered list grew 45%, with no delistings due to recovery.
2. **Decline is multicausal.** More than one factor was involved in 82% of North American extinctions. Habitat alteration (73%) and nonnative species (68%) were the leading causes.
3. **Aridity correlates with risk.** The author reports a positive correlation between aridity and the proportion of native fish species at risk.
4. **Dams and diversions reshape communities.** Impoundments favor nonnative species, while diversions and groundwater pumping lower spring and river levels that endemic species depend on.
5. **Nonnatives act through predation, competition, hybridization, and disease.** Mosquitofish, red shiner, rainbow trout, and brook trout have each contributed to native declines.
6. **Genetic erosion is a hidden threat.** Hybridization, hatchery broodstock bottlenecks, and low diversity within clonal lineages all reduce resilience.
7. **Recovery has mixed results.** The [[Gambusia Amistadensis]] was lost despite captive efforts because of accidental hybridization and misidentification. [[Gila Trout]] recovery was nearly undone by fire, flood, and drought in the 1980s, which showed that entire sub-drainages must be restored to permit gene flow. [[Yaqui Chub]] recovery succeeded after transplantation to Leslie Creek in 1969, protection of the native habitat, and reintroduction of captive-bred fish.
8. **Conservation priorities:** The author calls for public awareness (since birds and mammals receive more attention), more monitoring, genetic and ecological research, stronger enforcement of the [[Endangered Species Act]] and related laws, protection of intact habitat and native fish communities, ecosystem-level restoration, and stabilization of human population growth and consumption.
