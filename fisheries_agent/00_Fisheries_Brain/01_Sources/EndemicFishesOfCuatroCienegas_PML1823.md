# Ichthyofauna of the Cuatro Ciénegas Basin, Coahuila, Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This section of a faunal review, attributed to Minckley and covering pages approximately 396 to 404, examines the fish fauna of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in Coahuila, Mexico. The fauna is small but highly differentiated. Of 20 known fish kinds, 15 are differentiated to at least the subspecific level and 11 are endemic. The author concludes that the endemic fishes have affinities weighted toward the south coastal fauna of Mexico, while a smaller group shows northern (Rio Grande) connections. The author also argues that several species represent multiple, layered invasions, and that the basin's invertebrates show a similar pattern of ancient, endemic lineages. The text also documents a continuing problem: hybridization between the local [[Herichthys Cichlids]] and an invading canal-connected form.

## Background & Description

The basin's fishes include a cichlid species flock, pupfishes, gambusiid livebearers, sunfishes, minnows, and catfishes. The native cichlids belong to two subgenera. The subgenus *Parapetenia* comprises two pairs of sibling species. Within each pair the species are externally indistinguishable but differ clearly when the pairs are compared. The two kinds of [[Herichthys Cichlids]] show the same pattern.

Two diagnostic characters separate the species:

- **Pharyngeal dentition** is diagnostic at the species level.
- **Oral dentition** distinguishes the two subgenera.

The author notes that this sibling-species condition parallels many situations in African cichlid groups, citing [[Fryer and Iles 1972]] and [[Greenwood 1974]].

Ecologically, the *Parapetenia* kinds occupy different feeding roles. The detritivore is the most widely distributed, the molluscivore is less so, and two piscivores are rare and restricted to headspring situations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Zoogeographic relations.** The author places the fauna in a biogeographic framework, dividing the endemic species by their apparent source:

- **North and east (Rio Grande affinity):** Only two of the 11 endemic species, *Notropis xanthicara* and an undescribed *Etheostoma*, link to the middle [[Rio Grande Fauna]]. That fauna is derived largely from the Mississippi River biota.
- **South and east (Mexican affinity):** *Xiphophorus gordoni* and the four-species *Parapetenia* flock show strong affinities to the south and east. This is supported by the presence of [[Gambusia Marshi]], which is endemic to the [[Rio Salado System]] and is the northernmost member of the subgenus *Heterophallina*.
- **Coastal derivatives:** [[Lucania Interioris]] and the two *Cyprinodon* species may be coastal derivatives. *Lucania interioris* is highly differentiated and retains many primitive traits.
- **Mexican Plateau relatives:** [[Cyprinodon Atrorus]] resembles species related to *C. eximius*, which occur on the [[Mexican Plateau]].

**Gambusia relationships.** The secretive [[Gambusia Longispinis]] is closely related to the [[Gambusia Nobilis Group]], especially *G. gaigei*, which occurs in [[Big Bend National Park]]. The author rejects a proposed link between *G. longispinis* and *G. atrora* despite similar male gonopodia, because the pectoral fin of *G. atrora* places it closer to the subgenus *Heterophallina*.

**Sunfishes.** [[Lepomis Megalotis]] and [[Micropterus Salmoides]] are distinctive compared with their counterparts elsewhere in the Rio Salado system but are not differentiated to the species level. Recent change is reported: since 1968, some introduction of *M. salmoides* may have occurred. The basin's blackened, elongate form with an extremely produced lower jaw has been replaced, in less isolated habitats, by a more typical heavy-bodied, differently pigmented form.

**Invasion and hybridization.** The [[Herichthys Cichlids]] present a major problem. [[LaBounty 1974]] offered three alternative explanations for the mixed population, which includes both *C. (H.) c. cyanoguttatum* and hybridizing or introgressed forms. The alternatives are:

1. An undescribed *Herichthys* derived from a Texas form, in secondary contact with a Rio Grande cichlid that entered through a canal.
2. Rio Grande cichlids hybridizing with caracole cichlids (*Parapetenia* sp. "B") to produce a hybrid swarm.
3. A combination of primary hybridization and introgression among Texas, Rio Grande, and caracole cichlids.

Distribution data as of 1973 show that *C. (H.) c. cyanoguttatum* is invading the basin through canal connections. The author finds LaBounty's third alternative most acceptable, and the problem remained under study.

**Invertebrate parallels.** The author argues that the invertebrates show a similar historical pattern. Holsinger and Minckley (1971) reported endemism percentages for crustaceans and mollusks. Crustaceans have 7 families, 8 genera (50% endemic), and 10 species (60% endemic). Mollusks have 7 families, 22 genera (27% endemic), and 34 species (50% endemic). The widespread amphipod [[Hyalella Azteca]] occurs across much of North and Middle America, while [[Mexistenasellus Coahuila]] was the first record of the family [[Stenasellidae]] in the Western Hemisphere. A "tertiary gastropod" living in Texas, reported by Taylor, adds to the basin's evidence of antiquity. Some molluscan genera are most closely related to peri-Mediterranean forms.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna size and endemism:** The basin supports 20 known fish kinds. Of these, 15 are differentiated to at least the subspecific level, and 11 are endemic.
- **Dominant affinity:** Nine of the 15 endemic forms, together with *Gambusia marshi*, dominate the fauna. All nine are differentiated to the species level, and their affinities lie to the south and east, toward the south coastal fauna of Mexico.
- **Northern derivation:** Only two full endemic species (*Notropis xanthicara* and the undescribed *Etheostoma*) show northern Rio Grande affinities.
- **Multiple invasions:** The author concludes that several separate invasions have occurred, consistent with earlier work by Minckley (1969).
- **Ancient components:** *Cyprinodon bifasciatus*, *Gambusia marshi*, and the likely autochthonous cichlid flock may represent an ancient part of the fauna. *Xiphophorus gordoni* may also belong here, depending on its true relationships.
- **Secondary invasions:** Species such as *Notropis xanthicara*, *Ictalurus lupus*, *Gambusia longispinis*, and the undescribed *Etheostoma* may be secondary invaders from the Rio Grande fauna.
- **Recent arrivals:** The sunfishes *Micropterus* and *Lepomis* are thought to have arrived relatively late. *Cichlasoma c. cyanoguttatum* is a recent invader.
- **Uncertain origins:** The times of origin of the local *Astyanax f. mexicanus* and *Pilodictis olivaris* populations cannot be determined at present.
- **Caution on invasion inference:** The author warns that inferring invasion or reliction times from degrees of taxonomic difference is risky, and argues that a population need not differentiate morphologically if selective pressures remain stable.
- **Hybridization problem:** The *Herichthys* hybridization remains unresolved. The author favors LaBounty's view that primary hybridization and introgression are combined, and the question remained under intensive study.
- **Invertebrate congruence:** Crustacean and mollusk endemism percentages (for example, 60% of crustacean species and 50% of mollusk species) support the view that the basin's biota contains old, endemic lineages.
