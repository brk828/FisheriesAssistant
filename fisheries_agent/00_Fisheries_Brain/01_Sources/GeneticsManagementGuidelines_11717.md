# Genetics Management Guidelines for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

These guidelines, prepared by J. Holt Williamson and Richard S. Wydoski for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Denver, May 25, 1994), provide a conceptual framework for [[Genetics Management]] of four endangered endemic fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The stated mission of the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] is to maintain the genetic integrity of wild and captive-reared stocks and to prevent irreversible losses of genetic diversity from management actions or inaction.

The guidelines define a [[Genetics Management Plan]] (stock identification, status and trends, and priorities) and [[Stocking Plans]] (justification, species, size, timing, location, genetic risk and evaluation) that require Program approval, a Federal Fish and Wildlife Permit, and possibly [[Section 7 Consultation]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Stocking is treated as a temporary tool, with [[Adaptive Management]] applied throughout; the guidelines are reviewed annually by the [[Biology Committee]].

## Background & Description

The four species are endemic large-river fishes of the upper basin, listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended. Listing followed water development that created fish-passage barriers and altered flows, temperatures and low-velocity habitats needed for recruitment, along with established nonnative fishes suspected of competition and predation (Miller et al. 1982).

The Program has five elements: in-stream flows, habitat development and maintenance, native fish stocking, management of nonnative species and sport fishing, and research, monitoring and data management. Genetics management is integrated into all of them. The document is organized into sections on philosophy and goals, stock criteria, wild-stock management, captive-stock management, disposition of captive-reared fish, references and a glossary (Appendix 1). Its conceptual approach is compared to a medical model of diagnosis, prognosis, treatment and monitoring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stock concept:** A stock is a randomly breeding group with spatial, temporal or behavioral integrity from other such groups (Kutkuhn 1981). Stocks are treated as the operational unit, and limited-interchange stocks are kept separate under a conservative approach.
- **Distribution and movement:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] is the most mobile species, migrating long distances to spawn. [[Razorback Sucker]] shows moderate migration. Some [[Humpback Chub]] stocks, such as those in Black Rocks and Westwater Canyons, do not migrate for spawning. [[Bonytail]] movements are unstudied in the upper basin because the species became extremely rare. Dams have fragmented habitat and produced much of the geographic separation among stocks.
- **Recruitment limits:** Low survival in early life stages is the main limiting factor. Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub stocks are considered low but stable; razorback sucker recruitment is low or nonexistent although spawning has been documented. Razorback sucker spawning runs from April through mid-June at roughly 14–15 °C (Tyus and Karp 1991).
- **Hybridization:** No hybridization has been documented in Colorado Squawfish. Razorback sucker hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] is low (0–5% toward flannelmouth suckers; Buth et al. 1987) but could rise if habitat is further altered. Introgression has also occurred among Colorado River Gila species in the past (Dowling and DeMarais 1993).
- **Program production strategies (Riggs 1990):** All Natural, Supplementation, and All Captive-Reared. Supplementation is used for razorback sucker and bonytail; All Natural is preferred for stable Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub.

## Key Findings

- **Program goals (Box 1):** (1) prevent immediate extinction of any endangered Colorado River fish stock; (2) conserve genetic diversity of wild stocks by removing or reducing limiting factors; (3) maintain captive broodstock diversity similar to the wild founder stock.
- **Stock identification criteria:** geographic distribution and abundance, spawning sites, migrations and interchange, and genetic markers (electrophoresis, restriction enzyme analysis of DNA, mtDNA). Genetic analyses of Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker were scheduled for 1994; interim Gila analysis for 1995 and the final Gila report, including mtDNA, for 1997.
- **Status criteria:** monitoring distribution and relative abundance through the Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program, documenting spawning and recruitment, assessing catastrophic risk, and estimating imminent extinction probability.
- **Wild stock management:** all presumptive stocks are protected. Stocking, including experimental, augmentation and restoration, is to be run as thoroughly evaluated experiments. Captive-reared fish should not substitute for habitat protection (Lichatowich and Watson 1993).
- **Larval protocol:** naturally spawned razorback sucker larvae may be stocked into predator-free protected habitats. Juveniles at 250–300 mm total length are PIT-tagged and sampled for muscle plugs. Release of up to 10% of the wild spawning stock is allowed only if genetic characterization matches the wild stock; otherwise fish are not released to prevent genetic swamping.
- **Broodstock targets:** the ideal is 50 adults (25 males, 25 females) in 25 pedigreed paired matings, giving an effective population size of 50 and an inbreeding rate of 1%. A minimum 5 × 5 di-allele cross gives an effective population size of 10 and a 5% inbreeding rate. If sexes are unequal, factorial matings capture the contribution of the least numerous sex. Additional wild adults should be added when available.
- **Founder-number estimates:** to ensure about 30 adults per family lot in primary and backup refuges, required fish numbers at the end of each growing season are 500 (year 1), 250, 200, 160, 152 and 144 (year 6) for razorback sucker and Colorado Squawfish. Bonytail and Humpback Chub mature by the fourth growing season; about 300 fish per family lot in year 1 are needed to yield 60 adults.
- **Maturation times:** about five years for bonytail and humpback chub, and seven years for razorback sucker and Colorado Squawfish (propagation planning section).
- **Genetic risks (Box 4):** extinction, loss of diversity within and among stocks, increased drift and inbreeding, and inadvertent artificial selection. Excessive wild removals, low parent numbers, unrelated broodstock sources, and genetic swamping are key hatchery-activity risks.
- **Precautions (Box 8, in priority order):** prevent escapement; stock only where no significant harm to indigenous fish is shown; restrict supplementation to suitable or historic habitat; mark and release equal pedigreed family lots; limit large-scale stocking until life history is understood; require an approved stocking plan and permit.
- **Disposition of excess fish:** unfit fish (hybrids, deformed, untreatable contagious disease) are euthanized. Surplus fish are used by priority: Program needs (refugia, broodstock, experiments, augmentation/restoration), other research, education, agencies in the Lower Colorado River Basin, maintenance if feasible, museum reference collections, and euthanasia for the rest, following fisheries professional guidelines.
- **Transmittal memorandum (May 21, 1994):** the Biology Committee reached consensus on May 11, 1994 to adopt these guidelines. The companion Coordinated Hatchery Facility Plan had general agreement but no complete consensus and is a working document.
