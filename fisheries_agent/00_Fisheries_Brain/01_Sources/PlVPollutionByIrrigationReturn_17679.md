# Pollution by Irrigation Return Flow under PL 92-500

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-ecosystems

## Executive Summary

This 1977 *Journal of the Irrigation and Drainage Division* paper, written by three EPA National Enforcement Investigations Center staff, examines how the [[National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System]] (NPDES) created under [[Public Law 92-500]] (the Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments of 1972) can be applied to [[Irrigation Return Flow]] (IRF). The authors argue that the municipal and industrial permit model fits agriculture poorly, that the first-generation monitoring-only permits have produced little useful data, and that a flexible, area-based permitting program built on optimum water application is needed. The paper does not report original field data; it is a policy and regulatory analysis supported by cited literature.

## Background & Description

**Legal framework.** PL 92-500 requires point-source discharges into "waters of the United States" to meet best practicable control technology (BPT) by July 1, 1977, and best available technology economically achievable (BAT) by July 1, 1983. Section 306 sets new-source performance standards, and the EPA Administrator published a minimum of 27 source categories in the January 16, 1973 *Federal Register*. The authors note that IRF is covered only when it constitutes a point-source discharge into navigable water.

**Irrigation practice.** More than 48,000,000 acres of US land are irrigated, all but 5,000,000 acres of which lie in 17 western states. Irrigation uses 35% of water withdrawn from US streams and aquifers and accounts for 84% of freshwater consumed. Common methods include [[Flood Irrigation]], [[Sprinkler Irrigation]], and [[Trickle Irrigation]]. Roughly half of applied water is consumed by transpiration and evaporation or lost to percolation; the remainder returns as tailwater, base flow, seepage, tile drainage, or spills.

**Scope.** The paper is concerned with chemical, thermal, and sediment impacts on receiving waters, rather than with specific aquatic species. No fish or other taxa are surveyed, so species fields in this note are recorded as N/A.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salinity.** Salt loading comes from leaching of soil minerals, pumped groundwater, and displacement of saline groundwater, while transpiration and evaporation concentrate salts. Annual salt yields in the Colorado River Basin range from 8.5 tons/acre in the Price River Valley to under 0.1 ton/acre in the Green River Valley upstream of the New Fork River. Lower Colorado River TDS already approaches and sometimes exceeds 1,000 mg/l. The USDA salinity hazard at Imperial Dam (1973 data) is C3-S1 ("high"), and projected TDS of 1,300 mg/l by 2000 would make it "very high." Boron has approached the 0.33 ppm limit for sensitive crops such as citrus.

**Nutrients.** Tailwater and percolation carry phosphorus and nitrogen to streams, where they drive nuisance growth, eutrophication of lakes and reservoirs, oxygen depletion, and impaired fisheries. Notable overenriched waters include the Snake River impoundments and the San Joaquin River.

**Pesticides.** Pesticides enter waters through spray drift, runoff, subsurface drainage, equipment cleaning, and direct aquatic applications. Organochlorines persist, bioconcentrate through food chains, and accumulate in fat. Organophosphates are more acutely toxic and less persistent. In 1971, 10% of reported fish kills in the five western EPA Regions were attributed to pesticides, versus 30% in Region IX (Arizona, California, Nevada).

**Temperature and solids.** Thermal effects are poorly defined; surface tailwater may warm, while subsurface return flow usually does not. Suspended and settleable solids, mainly from unlined canals, poorly controlled conveyances, tailwater, and silty soils, blanket stream and reservoir bottoms, damage bottom life, and raise treatment costs.

**Health.** Sodium in drinking water is a hazard for people with hypertension, congestive heart failure edema, or pregnancy toxemia, and the 20 mg/l sodium diet limit is routinely exceeded in IRF-impacted streams.

## Key Findings

- **The NPDES model is poorly suited to IRF.** The authors describe a conflict between a 1972 water-quality law and 19th-century western water-rights laws that prioritize allocation and "beneficial use." Enforcement is complicated by Federal versus state jurisdiction.
- **Control is mainly "housekeeping."** Lining conveyances, optimizing water and agrochemical application, pump-back systems, scheduling, subsurface and trickle delivery, and tile drainage are the main tools. End-of-pipe treatment has had only marginal success and is often prohibitive. Settling ponds are not suited to water-short or saline areas, and pond-held pesticides can be remobilized under anaerobic conditions.
- **Over-application is the central issue.** Applying only the water needed for leaching and plant requirements would reduce salt leaching, leave more water to dilute loads, retain solids and agrochemicals on fields, and cut treatment and disposal costs. Irrigators often view full use of their appropriated rights as necessary to keep them, which the authors say conflicts with optimum practice. Several courts have ruled that water beyond plant needs is not beneficial use.
- **Responsibility is hard to assign.** Drainage from one farm may be affected by neighbors' practices, districts often lack police powers, and some drains are federally owned or of unknown ownership. Some operators could be held responsible for discharges they cannot control.
- **Permit program failures.** EPA's 1973 policy exempted operations under 3,000 acres and required only monitoring in the "first generation" of permits. The *NRDC v. Train* ruling (June 10, 1975) voided the 3,000-acre exemption. Issued permits monitor only temperature, pH, specific conductance, and occasionally suspended solids, often quarterly. The authors judge this data insufficient to reach BPT by 1977 or BAT by 1983.
- **Proposed 1976 regulation.** EPA's February 23, 1976 proposal excludes natural precipitation runoff from agricultural land. Controlled irrigation discharges from discernible, confined conveyances into navigable waters are point sources requiring an NPDES permit, which may be a general permit or an individual permit.
- **Likely outcomes if nothing changes.** The authors warn that continued monitoring or a "do-nothing" approach would let salinity and nutrient problems worsen in western and southern waters, raise domestic water costs, reduce irrigated crop acreage, and eventually bring pressure for federally funded treatment works and federal water-use laws.
- **Recommended program (six points).**
  1. Adopt optimum water application (which may be less than the appropriated right), supported by education and district-level guidance.
  2. Allow flexible permittee designation: one permit to the owner of a common drain serving many farms (areal control), and individual permits for small drains discharging directly to waters.
  3. Create EPA grants and contracts for optimally managed demonstration plots, with site-specific limits derived from their results.
  4. Use the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] to lead optimum practices on new projects, and give new districts police powers to act as permittees.
  5. Provide technical assistance to unorganized operators and set reasonable, economically feasible requirements.
  6. Have state agencies press for legislation requiring districts to meet BPT and BAT and monitor compliance.

The paper states that its views do not represent official EPA policy.
