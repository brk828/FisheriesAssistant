# Survey of a Life's Writing — Paul L. Errington's Bibliography

#wildlife-biology #mammalogy #ornithology #population-ecology #muskrat #Ondatra-zibethicus

> **Note on scope:** This source is a biographical bibliography of wildlife ecologist Dr. Paul L. Errington and contains **no direct fish or ichthyological data**. Errington's scientific career centered on terrestrial and semi-aquatic vertebrate population ecology — predation dynamics, muskrat population regulation, and gamebird (quail) survival — within freshwater marsh and prairie ecosystems. This note is therefore synthesized as an aquatic/wetland ecosystem and vertebrate population ecology record rather than a fish-specific one, per the source's actual content.

## Executive Summary

This paper (Carlander & Weller, 1964) reviews the scientific, popular, literary, and professional writings of Dr. Paul L. Errington (1902–1962), a pioneering wildlife ecologist at Iowa State University. Errington's research established foundational concepts in **predator-prey dynamics** and **automatic population regulation** in vertebrates, using long-term field studies of the [[Northern Bobwhite]] (*Colinus virginianus*) in the 1930s–40s and the [[Muskrat]] (*Ondatra zibethicus*) in freshwater marsh habitats through the 1940s–1960s. Although not an ichthyologist, his work on marsh ecosystems, wetland management, and semi-aquatic mammal population cycles is highly relevant to aquatic ecosystem science, particularly regarding habitat carrying capacity, disease epizootiology, and predation pressure (notably by [[Mink]], *Mustela vison*) within marshland environments. His culminating monograph, *[[Muskrat Populations]]* (1963), synthesized decades of marsh ecosystem observation and received the Iowa State University Press Faculty Award. He was honored posthumously and during his life with the Aldo Leopold Award (1962) from the Wildlife Society.

## Background & Description

No morphological or anatomical fish data are present in this source. Species-level biological notes extracted concern non-fish taxa relevant to Errington's marsh/wetland studies:

- **[[Muskrat]]** (*Ondatra zibethicus*) — semi-aquatic rodent; studies addressed sex/age tagging, drowning mortality, fungal skin disease, and population fluctuation dynamics in marsh habitats (refs. 84, 86, 106, 115, 122, 138).
- **[[Northern Bobwhite]]** (*Colinus virginianus*) — gallinaceous upland gamebird; studied for winter survival, cold/starvation resistance, and nesting/life-table equations (refs. 28, 33, 41, 56, 101).
- **[[Mink]]** (*Mustela vison*) — key mammalian predator of muskrats in north-central U.S. marsh systems; predation intensity linked to muskrat epizootic disease cycles (refs. 94, 118, 147).
- **[[Great Horned Owl]]** (*Bubo virginianus*) and various *Buteo*/*Falco* raptors — studied as indicators of prey population vulnerability via pellet-analysis food habits methodology (refs. 4, 14, 19, 27, 98, 108).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Errington's body of work collectively describes the **trophic structure of prairie marsh and upland ecosystems** in the north-central United States (Wisconsin, Iowa, Minnesota):

- **Habitat type:** Freshwater emergent marshes, stream-margin wetlands, and adjacent agricultural/prairie uplands.
- **Trophic interactions:** Raptors (owls, hawks, falcons) and mammalian carnivores (mink, red fox, house cats) as predators of gamebirds and muskrats; predation treated not as a limiting factor per se but as a **compensatory mechanism** operating on already-doomed surplus individuals ("[[Doomed Surplus]]" concept, foundational to his predation theory).
- **Population regulation:** Errington's central ecological thesis — articulated in "Predation and vertebrate populations" (1946, refs. 132–133) and "The phenomenon of predation" (1963, ref. 173) — proposed that vertebrate populations are regulated primarily by **intrinsic factors** (habitat carrying capacity, social behavior, disease) with predation acting as an **automatic, density-dependent** rather than primary limiting mechanism.
- **Wetland management applications:** Direct habitat management recommendations for muskrat production (refs. 135, 165) and marsh conservation more broadly, culminating in *[[Of Men and Marshes]]* (1957) and *[[Muskrats and Marsh Management]]* (1961).
- **Disease ecology:** Documented fungal skin disease and epizootic-driven mink predation feedback loops in muskrat populations (refs. 115, 147, 170), an early example of linking pathogen dynamics to predator-prey population cycles in a semi-aquatic system.

## Key Findings

- Errington's bibliography comprises **175 articles/books plus 35 reviews**, spanning gallinaceous birds (4 species), waterfowl (3 species), predaceous birds (9 species), and mammals (8 species) — no fish taxa were subjects of his primary research.
- Two publications ("The great horned owl and its prey in north-central United States," 1940; "Predation and vertebrate populations," 1946) were designated outstanding wildlife/terrestrial ecology publications by the Wildlife Society.
- His monograph *[[Muskrat Populations]]* (1963, Iowa State University Press, 665 pp.) represents the definitive synthesis of decades of marsh ecosystem population data and won the Press's Faculty Award for outstanding manuscript (1961–62).
- Errington pioneered the "**pellet analysis method**" for raptor food-habits study (1930, ref. 4), a foundational field technique still relevant to predator diet studies in wetland ecosystems.
- He developed the concept of **predation as compensatory rather than additive mortality** — a paradigm shift countering the prevailing view of predators as vermin, instead framing them as regulators operating on a population's "doomed surplus."
- His later career (1950s–60s) integrated Scandinavian wildlife literature into American wetland ecology after self-teaching Swedish, Danish, and Norwegian, producing comparative transatlantic marsh/waterfowl management studies (refs. 143, 156, 157, 160, 162).
- Awarded the **Aldo Leopold Award** (Wildlife Society, 1962) for combined scientific and philosophical contributions to wildlife/wetland ecology.
