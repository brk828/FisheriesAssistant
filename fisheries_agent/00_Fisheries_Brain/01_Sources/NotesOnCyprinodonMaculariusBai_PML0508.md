# Ichthyological Notes: Courtship Coloration, Range Extension of Pacific Mackerel, and Mutilated Sting-Ray Tails

#ichthyology #marine-biology #scombridae #dasyatidae

## Executive Summary

This *Copeia* "Ichthyological Notes" section contains three short, independent contributions. The first describes courtship colour change, egg laying, and early growth in an aquarium-reared fish whose species name is not legible in the extracted text. The second reports a [[Pacific Mackerel]] far north of its previously recorded range in [[Prince William Sound]], Alaska. The third discusses the frequent South Carolina fishing practice of cutting off sting-ray tails or spines, and questions a published claim that shark bites explain such injuries. The scan is partly degraded, so some words are reconstructed from context.

## Background & Description

**Courtship coloration (first note, unnamed species).** The male keeps its brilliant blue nuptial colouring during courtship, with slightly darker tones. Dark bands or crossbars that normally appear when the fish is frightened also appear during courtship. At peak nuptial colour the male is bright blue, its dorsal fin turns black, the posterior edge of the anal fin is tipped with black, and the caudal fin and part of the caudal peduncle are pale lemon yellow. The female becomes slightly less crossbarred and somewhat lighter during courtship.

**Pacific mackerel in Alaska (second note).** A mackerel, *Pneumatophorus diego*, was taken from herring catches in Prince William Sound in July 1932. The authors note this is about a thousand miles north of its reported northern range limit in [[Puget Sound]]. A September 1932 report in the *Pacific Fisherman* describes a catch of mackerel in early August from a trap in southeastern Alaska.

**Mutilated sting-ray tails (third note).** The author responds to an article by E. W. Gudger on malformed tails and spines in sting rays. Gudger argued that shark bites could explain all the defects he listed. The author accepts Gudger's conclusions for *Dasyatis hastata*, but reports that South Carolina fishermen commonly cut off the tail above the spine or remove the spine, then release the ray. The practice is not limited to *D. say* or *D. americana*: a butterfly ray, *Pteroplatea micrura*, which has no spine, was also reported with a cut tail.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The notes concern coastal and fishery settings. The mackerel record shows a species moving well outside its known range, which may reflect changing distribution or sampling in fishery catches. The sting-ray note links mutilation directly to angling practice: fishermen cut the tail because a hooked ray, especially one that has swallowed the hook, is hard and sometimes dangerous to free. Killing the ray is slower than cutting, so mutilation is a practical shortcut. The tail-cutting habit may also make it hard to tell fishing injuries apart from natural injuries such as shark bites.

## Key Findings

- **Nuptial colouration:** Courtship males are bright blue with a black dorsal fin, black-tipped anal fin, and pale lemon-yellow caudal fin. Females become lighter and less crossbarred.
- **Egg laying and hatching:** Eggs were laid singly on algae covering stones. The first hatched on April 5, when the young were 5 mm long and light tan grey. By April 7, 12 eggs had hatched and the young were moved to another aquarium.
- **Early growth:** By June 6 the 12 young averaged 20 mm, with the largest at 22 mm. The author suggests the schools seen in January were young from the previous year, while adults may have sheltered in deeper water or in small groups.
- **Northern range record:** *Pneumatophorus diego* was recorded in July 1932 in Prince William Sound, about a thousand miles beyond its reported northern limit in Puget Sound. A second, anecdotal report from southeastern Alaska in August 1932 supports occasional northern occurrence.
- **Tail mutilation:** Cutting off the tail or spine of sting rays is common among [[South Carolina Coast]] fishermen and has been observed repeatedly. It also occurs in *Pteroplatea micrura*, which has no spine.
- **Challenge to shark-bite explanation:** The author accepts that shark bites may explain some defects, but argues that fishing practice accounts for many of the mutilations seen in rays.
