# Diel Periodicity of Mate Attraction and Flight Activity in Three Caddisfly Species (Trichoptera)

#aquatic-entomology #freshwater-ecology #trichoptera

## Executive Summary

This study examines the daily timing of mate attraction and flight in three caddisfly species that use female-released [[Sex Pheromone]]s: [[Dicosmoecus Gilvipes]] (Limnephilidae), [[Gumaga Nigricula]], and [[Gumaga Griseola]] (both Sericostomatidae). Combining field trapping at two northern California streams with controlled [[Laboratory Flight Chamber]] experiments, the authors found that each species attracts males at a distinct, brief period of the day: nocturnal, diurnal, or crepuscular. Flight patterns were governed by [[Circadian Rhythm]]s in most taxa and sexes, and were modulated by light and air temperature. The authors interpret the coordinated, short activity windows as a means of conserving metabolic resources, improving reproductive success, and maintaining [[Reproductive Isolation]] between the two closely related Gumaga species.

## Background & Description

Adult caddisflies are short-lived and cryptic. They spend daylight hours resting in crevices or on foliage, and mating and dispersal are presumed to occur at night, when direct observation is difficult. [[Light Trapping]] has been used to infer nocturnal flight, but the authors note that light-trap timing patterns can be inaccurate. Detailed information on caddisfly mating periodicity is limited, with pheromone-timing data available for only one other caddisfly, *Rhyacophila nubila* (Solem 1985).

The study addressed three questions:
- When do males respond to females, and is this timing the same for live females and pheromone extracts?
- How do flight patterns differ between sexes, and are they driven by endogenous rhythms?
- How do light and air temperature influence flight and mate attraction?

**Study organisms**
- *Dicosmoecus gilvipes* is a large, conspicuous limnephilid found in pool and slow-run habitats across western North America. It has a one-year life cycle in Big Sulphur Creek, emerges in autumn, and has adults that live about two weeks.
- *Gumaga nigricula* and *G. griseola* are found in streams and springs in western North America and have one-year life cycles. Adults live roughly 4–8 days. *G. griseola* has smaller adults and a longer emergence season that occurs later in the year.

**Study sites**
- Big Sulphur Creek (38°48'03"N, 122°48'36"W; 415 m a.s.l.): a third-order stream in the Mayacmas Mountains, with a narrow, discontinuous riparian zone dominated by white alder. Used for *D. gilvipes* and *G. nigricula*.
- Las Trampas Creek (37°51'43"N, 122°06'01"W; 152 m a.s.l.): a second-order stream in a narrow, deep arroyo with a closed riparian canopy. Used for *G. griseola*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Field methods**
- Virgin females were caged inside sticky traps (Pherocon LC traps). Control traps lacked females and were interspersed among treatment traps. Traps hung 1.5–2 m above ground, 5–20 m apart, along transects of roughly 400 m (Big Sulphur Creek) and 200 m (Las Trampas Creek).
- Pheromone tests used foam stoppers soaked in dichloromethane extracts of 4–5 females, with the same trap design.
- Captures were recorded every 2 h over 24-h sample dates, with air temperature and light intensity logged at the same intervals.

**Laboratory methods**
- Flight chambers held 20 (*D. gilvipes*) or 30 (*Gumaga* spp.) adults of a single sex. Five copper-wired baffles registered each landing or flight as a "hit" on a continuous recorder.
- Light-dark (LD) cycles matched field sunrise and sunset, with lights on at 58 lux and off at 0 lux. Constant dark (DD) or constant light (LL) tests assessed endogenous control. Temperatures were held constant at 10 °C (*D. gilvipes*) or 14 °C (*Gumaga* spp.).

**Environmental influences**
- Light intensity and air temperature altered the amount of flight but did not shift its timing.
- Low temperature delayed mate attraction in *G. nigricula* until air temperature exceeded 13 °C, a threshold that was reached between 0200 and 0730 h on most sample dates.
- Even very dim light (4 lux) suppressed flight in some taxa.

## Key Findings

**Timing of pheromone-induced mate attraction**
- ***Dicosmoecus gilvipes* (nocturnal):** Males were attracted over roughly a 10-h period beginning at dark. Attraction peaked in the first hour after sunset, when 58% of males were captured. Field sample size was 33 live females pooled over five dates.
- ***Gumaga nigricula* (diurnal):** 85% of males were attracted between morning hours, 2–4 h after sunrise. Field sample size was 50 females pooled over five dates.
- ***Gumaga griseola* (crepuscular):** 97% of males were attracted during the hour before sunrise. Observations at 15–30 min intervals showed attraction began around 0345 h and ended about 45 min later. Field sample size was 79 females pooled over seven dates.
- Control trap captures were rare (0, 0.02, and 0.5 males per period for *D. gilvipes*, *G. griseola*, and *G. nigricula*, respectively).
- Attraction to pheromone extracts matched attraction to live females in timing for all three species, indicating that male response to pheromone controls the timing of mate attraction.

**Reproductive isolation**
- Attraction in *G. griseola* ended at least 2 h before attraction in *G. nigricula* began. This temporal separation likely prevents interspecific mating between the two species, which have similar microhabitat preferences and pheromone chemistry (Wood and Resh 1984; Resh and Wood 1985).

**Flight periodicity and sex differences**
- In the laboratory, *D. gilvipes* males and females flew mainly during the first 1–2 h after lights-off. Male flight declined gradually through the dark phase, while female flight was brief.
- *G. nigricula* males flew for more than 8 h after lights-on, while females flew only 1–2 h. Sex differences were significant (χ² = 69.6, p < 0.0001).
- *G. griseola* males flew for about 1 h after lights-off and resumed flight a few hours before lights-on. Females showed a single flight period after lights-off that declined over 4–5 h. Sex differences were significant (χ² = 27.9, p < 0.0001).
- For *D. gilvipes*, sex differences were also significant (χ² = 15.3, p < 0.001).
- Field observations were consistent with the laboratory: flight was never observed during the day in *D. gilvipes* and *G. griseola*. *G. nigricula* males flew often in morning and early afternoon, when females were rarely active.

**Endogenous (circadian) control**
- *D. gilvipes* males showed a unimodal endogenous flight period in DD, beginning about 23 h after DD started and lasting roughly 11 h. Female *D. gilvipes* flight was arrhythmic over 72 h of DD.
- *G. nigricula* males and females showed unimodal rhythms in LL, with flight periods beginning 23–24 h after LL started and lasting 7–8 h.
- *G. griseola* males had a bimodal rhythm in DD, with peaks at about 10 h and 23 h. Females had a unimodal rhythm beginning about 26 h after DD started.
- The authors concluded that circadian rhythms govern flight in all three species, except female *D. gilvipes*.
- The small flight peaks immediately after lights-on (*D. gilvipes* males) and lights-off (*G. nigricula*) were absent in darkness or constant light, so the authors classed them as startle responses to light change.

**Temperature effects**
- In *D. gilvipes* and *G. griseola*, air temperature in the field did not affect mate attraction timing. Males were attracted even at low temperatures (10.4 °C and 12.4 °C).
- In the laboratory, *D. gilvipes* and *G. griseola* males flew at 8 °C and 6 °C but not at 4 °C.
- *G. nigricula* males were much less tolerant of cold. Flight declined between 14 °C and 12 °C and was fully suppressed at 8 °C, matching the 13 °C field threshold for mate attraction.
- Low temperature did not alter flight periodicity in any species, but it suppressed flight activity during mating periods.

**Adult behavior and life history**
- Copulation lasted about 4 h (*G. nigricula*), 8 h (*G. griseola*), and 16 h (*D. gilvipes*). Pairs remained together through the attraction period, so mating was effectively monogamous within a single diel period.
- *G. nigricula* and *G. griseola* oviposited the evening after copulation. *D. gilvipes* oviposition was not observed.
- Total adults captured in the field were 381 (*D. gilvipes*), 93 (*G. nigricula*), and 89 (*G. griseola*), across the sampling days reported.

**Conclusions and ecological interpretation**
- The three species show different and non-overlapping timing of mate attraction, consistent with temporal partitioning as a premating isolation mechanism.
- Active male flight during mate attraction (*Gumaga* males and *D. gilvipes*) should increase the chance of intercepting pheromone plumes. Sedentary females may leave more persistent pheromone trails.
- Long periods of inactivity likely reduce predation risk and conserve metabolic reserves in these short-lived adults.
- Because activity timing is set by circadian clocks and modulated by temperature and light, the authors argue that studies of aquatic insect behavior should test for endogenous mechanisms alongside exogenous ones.
