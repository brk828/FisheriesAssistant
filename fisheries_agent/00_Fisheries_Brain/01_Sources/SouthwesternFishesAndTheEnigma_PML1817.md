# Southwestern Fishes and the Enigma of "Endangered Species"

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 *Science* paper by W. L. Minckley and James E. Deacon reviews the status of native fishes of the American Southwest and questions how the term "endangered species" is applied to them. The authors argue that the region's fishes are disappearing through habitat alteration (dams, groundwater pumping, diversion, and introduced species) faster than they can be studied. They focus on [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Gila Spinedace]], [[Pahrump Poolfish]], [[Moapa Dace]], [[Woundfin]] and [[Gila Topminnow]]. They propose a four-category scheme for ranking aquatic fishes by vulnerability and call for habitat-based conservation before more species are lost.

## Background & Description

The Southwest's fauna is largely a desert and arid-land assemblage. Most of its native fishes are endemic, and many are restricted to small or isolated waters. The authors note that springs and desert streams are often more isolated than oceanic islands and therefore contain relict organisms, which makes them especially susceptible to faunal change. Much surface water is directly used by people, and most remaining natural waters are highly modified. Many of the region's fishes are poorly known, and basic data on their physiological, ecological and reproductive requirements are scarce.

The paper relies on fish collections, field surveys, museum records and reports from state and federal agencies, including the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the Nevada Game and Fish Commission and the [[U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]]. A regional map (Fig. 1) shows the Colorado River system and localities discussed in the text, including Glen Canyon, Yuma, Roosevelt Lake, Lake Mohave and Pahrump Valley.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Large-river fishes of the Colorado system.** Squawfish and humpback suckers were once abundant from the upper Colorado to the lower Colorado. Both were largely eliminated from roughly 250 miles of mainstream above the Flaming Gorge Dam site after the dam's closure in 1961. Both persisted in the Green River between Echo Park and Ouray, Utah, and a squawfish was reported from the San Juan River in 1959. Recent data for the lower river show that squawfish became increasingly uncommon after 1950. Only two were reported from the lower Colorado between 1962 and 1967. Humpback suckers were still seen in Lake Mohave, where spawning aggregations were recorded several times, most recently in March 1967.

**Gila and Salt river systems.** The authors describe the Gila basin as the site of "virtual, and perhaps actual, extinction" of both squawfish and humpback sucker. The headwaters of the Gila were blocked by Coolidge Dam in 1919. The Verde and Salt rivers are effectively impounded. Surveys from 1963 onward failed to find either species in the Salt River Canyon, and no adult squawfish had been taken near Roosevelt since 1939 (as printed in the source).

**Introduced and competing species.** Introduced species are a recurring threat. The aggressive [[Red Shiner]] and [[Mosquitofish]] spread into native habitats. Largemouth bass and mollies are cited as examples of introductions that altered native communities. Goldfish invaded the last spring holding Pahrump poolfish. Table 1 lists the fishes recorded from the Salt River at Tempe from 1890 to 1967. The list includes native and introduced species, and the authors indicate which species were probably present in different periods.

**Groundwater depletion.** Desert springs depend on groundwater. Pumping for irrigation in Pahrump Valley lowered the water table, and one spring failed in 1958 after nearby pumping. Groundwater pumping and surface diversion are the main threats to these habitats.

## Key Findings

- **Colorado River squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):** Abundant at Yuma in the early 1900s and persisted in the lower Colorado mainstream until the 1940s. Only two specimens were reported from the lower Colorado for 1962–1967. Historically present in the Gila, San Pedro and Verde systems.
- **Humpback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** Less commonly reported than squawfish, possibly because it is harder to capture with conventional methods. Some large specimens were taken from Salt River impoundments, including fish heavier than 14 pounds. No specimen shorter than about 15 inches has been caught in recent years in the lower Colorado lakes. The authors warn that large adults do not indicate a healthy population, since reproduction may already be failing.
- **Hybridization:** A hybrid of humpback sucker and *Catostomus latipinnis* was taken below Glen Canyon Dam in 1965.
- **Pahrump poolfish (*Empetrichthys latos*):** Three known stocks existed. Two were extinct by 1967. The survivor at Manse Spring was estimated at about 1,300 fish in July 1967, and goldfish competition was reducing it. Water use nearly doubled between 1945 and 1959. The estimated annual recharge is 22,100 acre-feet, while pumping was nearly 41,000 acre-feet annually, so the authors expected the spring to fail within 10 to 11 years.
- **Moapa dace (*Moapa coriacea*):** Abundant in the Moapa River headwaters in 1933 and apparently still common into the early 1950s, but rare by 1964. Its population later rose suddenly after 3 years, with no detectable physical habitat change. The authors attribute the decline mainly to changes in the biotic community, including the introduction of the shortfin molly (*Poecilia mexicana*).
- **Woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*):** A monotypic genus whose last reproducing population lives in the lower Virgin River of southwestern Utah, northwestern Arizona and southeastern Nevada. A proposed dam 8 miles above St. George, Utah, would affect about 40 of the approximately 90 miles of suitable habitat. Downstream flow would become less variable and lower in each month. The authors state that any change in river conditions is likely to be detrimental until habitat requirements are defined.
- **Gila spinedace (*Meda fulgida*):** Endemic to the Gila basin, preferring moderately swift currents over gravel. It had not been taken in the Verde drainage in recent years, although habitat there appeared suitable.
- **Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*):** Fig. 3 maps its past and present distribution in the Gila River system. The authors note its restriction to a few isolated habitats.
- **Classification of endangered status:** The authors propose a four-category system for aquatic fishes. Category 4 includes species in small, isolated, specialized habitats, such as desert springs, and is the most vulnerable. Fishes in category 4 are the hardest to define because they are narrowly distributed and their populations are small and unstable.
- **Causes of decline:** Principal causes include dams, diversion and dewatering, groundwater pumping, and introduced predators and competitors. Populations of species in small springs can be eliminated by a single catastrophe.
- **Conservation conclusions:** The authors state that the status of most species cannot be assessed without basic life-history data. They recommend avoiding further habitat change until requirements are known, and they call for coordinated research, better communication among workers, and protection of remaining habitat. They also stress that the Southwest's native fishes provide a natural experiment in evolution and extinction, which should be documented before the fauna is lost.
