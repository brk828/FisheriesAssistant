# Bighead Pupfish (Cyprinodon pachycephalus): A New Thermal-Spring Pupfish from Chihuahua, Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by W. L. Minckley and C. O. Minckley formally describes [[Cyprinodon Pachycephalus]], a new pupfish from the thermal springs of [[Baños de San Diego]] in Chihuahua, Mexico. The species is distinguished by an exaggerated head, an elongated predorsal region, and a specialized pharyngeal apparatus with both raptorial and submolariform teeth. It lives in water up to about 44 °C, eats mainly hydrobiid snails in adulthood, and appears to hybridize with [[Cyprinodon Eximius]] where the two meet. The authors also discuss how isolation in the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] basin and range landscape may have produced this and other pupfish species.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The [[Chihuahuan Desert]] of northern Mexico supports about 110 fish species (figure partly garbled in the scan) and at least 15 species of *Cyprinodon*. Aquatic habitats there are mostly small springs and seeps in closed basins, with only a few through-flowing rivers.
- **Collection:** The authors collected at Baños de San Diego, roughly 1,140 m elevation, where they found a small assemblage of fishes and molluscs in warm water, including a "grotesque" pupfish in water as warm as 44 °C.
- **Name:** The trivial name *pachycephalus* comes from Greek words for "thick" and "head," referring to the enlarged cephalic region.
- **Type material:** The holotype is a nuptial male of 40.5 mm SL, deposited at the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (UMMZ). The description is based on the holotype and 19 adult paratopotypes; additional paratypes are at Arizona State University (ASU) and UMMZ.
- **Prior listings:** Earlier authors had noted this form under various provisional names. [[Deacon]] et al. (1979) listed it as an endangered "bigheaded pupfish."

### Diagnosis and Morphology

- **Head:** Head length exceeds one-third of SL (32.1–38.4% SL) and head width exceeds one-fourth (23.2–31.8% SL). The head is almost as deep as long (27.6–36.9% SL), flattened ventrally, and steeply sloped from nape to snout.
- **Mouth and jaws:** The mouth is broad (12.3–20.4% SL), with jaws of roughly equal length.
- **Scales and rays:** Lateral scales number 24–29 (usually 25 or 26). Dorsal rays are 9 or 10 and pelvic rays 6–7.
- **Predorsal region:** The predorsal length is 59.8–70.1% SL and the predorsal area is markedly arched. The dorsal fin sits far back on the body.
- **Fins:** All fins are small. Pectoral fins are 17.5–23.3% SL and pelvic fins 8.5–11.9% SL. Females have longer dorsal fins than males (24.9% vs. 21.6% SL), which the authors note is generally reversed in pupfishes.
- **Cleithral process:** Enlarged, extending posteriorly to about the second scale in the lateral series.
- **Pores:** Sensory pores are variable; mandibular pores are absent in all 40 specimens counted.

### Pharyngeal Apparatus

The expanded buccopharyngeal region holds specialized pharyngeal bones. The thickened, paired infrapharyngeal bones are bound by ligaments into a subtriangular "pharyngeal mill" resembling that of some perciform fishes. The suprapharyngeal bones are mobile and bear enlarged teeth. Mouth teeth are deeply tricuspid and firmly attached.

### Coloration

- **Nuptial males:** Pale yellow dorsal fins, a faint yellow wash on the head, grayish-brown sides, and sometimes a sky-blue wash over the body. The gular area, breast, and belly are white. The terminal caudal bar is variable.
- **Females and juveniles:** White below, with a strongly developed dorsal ocellus that is often larger than the eye. Anal fins are immaculate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Thermal Habitat

- **Springs:** Spring sources were about 20 cm in diameter. They merged into an unshaded brook about 2.0 m wide and less than 3.0 cm deep, over a bottom of travertine fragments and fine calcareous copropel. Saltgrass (*Distichlis spicata*) lined undercut banks.
- **Temperatures:** The warmest springheads were uniformly 43.8 °C (44.0 °C by the authors' instrument in 1971). Water cooled downflow to 38–42.5 °C. Downstream impoundment water was about 25 °C.
- **Oxygen:** Water at 44 °C could hold about 5.3 mg/l dissolved oxygen at this elevation. [[Soltz]] and [[Feldmuth]] (pers. comm.) recorded less than 3.0 but not less than 2.0 mg/l in the headsprings.
- **Tolerance:** Juveniles and adults occupied the warmest water. The authors state that these temperatures represent the highest long-term tolerances known for a reproducing teleost population.

### Associated Fauna

- **Isopod:** *Thermosphaeroma smithi* (Sphaeromatidae), one of few known inland isopods of this family in North America.
- **Snail:** An undescribed hydrobiid snail of the genus *Tryonia*.
- **Fish:** [[Gambusia Senilis]] (*G.* cf. *senilis*) and, historically, [[Cyprinodon Eximius]] and a few [[Lepomis Megalotis]].

### Diet

- **Juveniles (n = 5, 15–20 mm SL):** Mostly detritus and fine calcareous copropel, with bits of travertine and broken shell. One ate two snails (about 0.5 mm) and another ate portions of an ant.
- **Adults (n = 5, 28.5–39.0 mm SL):** All had eaten [[Hydrobiid Snails]]. Guts held 3–14 snails each (mean 7.4), with shells 0.7–2.6 mm. Of 37 shells, 28 were intact and 9 partly crushed. All adults also contained detritus and shell fragments.
- **Interpretation:** Consumption of relatively large snails is unusual for pupfishes, which are mostly opportunistic detritivores. The authors suggest the pharyngeal mill crushes shells, though the intact shells in the hindgut suggest crushing may not always be necessary.

### Hybridization and Interactions

Before the spring system was altered, [[Cyprinodon Pachycephalus]] and *C. eximius* were largely allopatric at Baños de San Diego in 1971. The authors collected one male *C. eximius*, three possible hybrids, and a few gambusias. Putative hybrids show intermediacy in head shape and size, a lesser body depth, smaller male fins, and pigmentation resembling *C. eximius*. Smith and Chernoff (1981) found the new species abundant in 1980 but did not record *C. eximius* or hybrids. By 1980 the outflow had been diverted into an irrigation canal, and in 1982 putative hybrids were again found in the impoundment.

### Laboratory Maintenance

The authors kept a laboratory population through two generations at room temperature (about 20 °C). The stock was lost to equipment failure in 1973. First- and second-generation progeny were morphologically indistinguishable from wild-caught juveniles and adults of similar size.

### Thermal Endemism

The authors argue that high temperatures are not required for survival. Related pupfishes such as [[Cyprinodon Bifasciatus]] reproduce poorly below 30.2 °C in the laboratory, whereas [[Cyprinodon Macrolepis]] was maintained through four generations at room temperature (unpublished data). The spring population could have dispersed into the [[Rio Chuviscar]], but other fishes were probably excluded by high temperatures.

### Geological and Biogeographic Context

- **Basin and range history:** [[Rio Grande Rift]] opening began about 22 million years ago. Basin and range topography developed progressively from east to west over the next 16–17 million years.
- **Basin:** The closed basin containing the Baños de San Diego springs and the [[Julimes Population]] is large, up to 50 km wide, and contains the usually dry Laguna del Cuervo. It is drained by the Rio Chuviscar, which flows into the [[Rio Conchos]].
- **Origin:** The authors consider the species' origin conjectural, possibly since the Oligocene or earliest Miocene. Relict isopods and hydrobiid snails suggest the habitats are old, although the springs themselves may be relatively recent.

## Key Findings

1. **New species:** *Cyprinodon pachycephalus* is separable from all but one other Chihuahuan pupfish by its enlarged head, massive jaws, elongated predorsal region, and specialized pharyngeal mill.
2. **Diagnostic counts:** Lateral scales 24–29 (usually 25 or 26), dorsal rays 9–10, pelvic rays 6–7, no mandibular pores, and an enlarged cleithral process.
3. **Thermal tolerance:** The species lives at up to 43.8–44 °C, which the authors describe as the highest long-term tolerance known for a reproducing teleost population.
4. **Diet shift:** Juveniles eat mainly detritus, while adults eat hydrobiid snails (3–14 per fish, mean 7.4).
5. **Hybridization:** Putative hybrids with *C. eximius* show intermediate morphology, but the authors note interfertility among pupfishes does not by itself prove that allopatric taxa are not distinct species.
6. **Habitat change:** Human modification, including a diverted outflow and canal, altered the spring system by 1980.
7. **Adaptive hypotheses:** The enlarged head may reflect feeding adaptations, or expanded gills for low dissolved oxygen. The thermal, low-oxygen, food-rich springs may have promoted rapid divergence.
8. **Conservation status:** Listed as endangered by [[Deacon]] et al. (1979).
9. **Further work:** The authors plan to describe the putative hybrids elsewhere and note that the [[Julimes Population]] may prove distinct, which would make parapatric evolution plausible.
