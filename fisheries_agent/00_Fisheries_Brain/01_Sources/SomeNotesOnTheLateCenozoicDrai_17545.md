# Late Cenozoic Drainage Patterns in Southeastern Arizona and Southwestern New Mexico

#geology #hydrology #drainage-evolution

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey report presents a working hypothesis for how the drainage of southeastern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico developed during late Cenozoic time, mainly the [[Pliocene]] and [[Quaternary]]. Drainage evolution was repeatedly controlled by large-scale faulting, epeirogenic upwarping or subsidence, and volcanism. The hypothesis is based on regional trends in sediment size, the association of sediments with volcanic rocks and structure, and about 1,100 measurements of pebble imbrication and crossbed dip directions. The document is a geological and hydrogeological paper. It contains no fish, invertebrate, or other aquatic biological data. Its only biological content is a brief mention of vertebrate fossils in valley-fill deposits.

## Background & Description

The paper builds on earlier work on the [[Basin and Range Province]], beginning with the early surveys of [[Gilbert]] (1875). Later studies cited include those by Lee (1905), McKee (1951), Heindl (1958; 1962), Lance (1960), Melton (1960), Cooley and Davidson (1963), Kottlowski and others (1965), and Cooley and others (1967). The author notes that the report's main contribution is a synthesis of sediment-size trends and structural relations, combined with new directional field measurements.

The [[Gila River]] and [[Salt River]] are the two main streams of the area. Their evolution is tied to the regional geologic history described below. The report's stated sources of field evidence are:

- About 1,100 measurements of pebble imbrication
- Dip directions of crossbeds
- Regional sediment-size trends
- Associations between sedimentary deposits, volcanic rocks, and geologic structure

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Initial geologic setting.** Large-scale differential structural movements in the middle Cenozoic, mainly the [[Miocene]], formed the mountain chains and valleys south of the [[Mogollon Rim]]. Pre-late-Cenozoic rocks, such as the Helmet Fanglomerate near Tucson, were affected by normal faulting and some compressional folding and thrusting. Late Cenozoic deposits were not involved in thrusting. Normal faulting continued through late Cenozoic time at decreasing intensity and was minor by the start of the Quaternary.

**Volcanic transition.** Pre-late-Cenozoic volcanic rocks included rhyolite, light-colored andesite, latite, dark andesite, andesitic basalt, and dacite. Late Cenozoic volcanism was mainly basalt, reflecting a rough shift from silicic to more mafic compositions. Earlier drainage directions were similar to present streams in some places. In parts of the [[Santa Cruz Valley]] and [[San Pedro Valley]], however, they were opposite to present flow.

**Valley-fill deposits.** Late Cenozoic alluvium is more than 1,500 feet thick in many large valleys, notably the [[Phoenix Basin]] and [[Safford Valley]]. It forms two main depositional periods, termed the [[Older Valley Fill]] and the [[Younger Valley Fill]]. Many investigators have called the bulk of these deposits the [[Gila Conglomerate]] or the [[Gila Group]] (Heindl, 1963). Both fills consist of mixed clay, silt, sand, and gravel. The younger fill is usually reddish-brown and more weakly cemented, with more silt in its gravel. The older fill is buff or gray. Fine-grained deposits, including gypsum in the [[Tonto Basin]] and San Pedro Valley, are widespread in the older fill. Limestone is abundant in the younger fill of Safford Valley.

**Fossil evidence.** Only a few vertebrate fossils have been found in valley-fill units. Fossils in the older fill are early to middle Pliocene in age. Those in the younger fill are late Pliocene to early Quaternary and early post-[[Blancan Stage]] in age. The report does not describe any fish or aquatic fauna.

## Key Findings

**1. Drainage was mainly internal in early to middle Pliocene time.**
During early late Cenozoic time, internal drainage was more widespread than at any other period. This was followed by gradual integration into through-drainage and the establishment of the Gila River system.

**2. Pre-integration drainage configuration.**
- The upper Gila drainage may have flowed to the San Francisco River and been impounded in a basin near the New Mexico–Arizona state line.
- Santa Cruz Valley water likely flowed into the southern Phoenix basin and was impounded there.
- The San Pedro and Safford valleys were large areas of internal drainage.
- The Salt River was through-flowing as far downstream as the Tonto Basin. Southwest of the Tonto Basin, the ancestral Salt River was impounded near Phoenix. Its tributary, the [[Verde River]], was not yet through-flowing.

**3. Younger valley fill drainage.**
During deposition of the younger fill, the Salt, Verde, and possibly San Pedro rivers drained into the Phoenix basin. Some water may have continued westward across the basin. The upper Gila remained impounded. The San Francisco River flowed into the Gila system, as suggested by rounded gravel of different lithologies east of Safford. The Gila may have been impounded in pluvial lakes in the southern Duncan basin until late Pleistocene time.

**4. Upwarping ended deposition and integrated drainage.**
A pulse of regional upwarping ended deposition of the younger fill and established through drainage in early post-Blancan time across many valleys in southeastern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico. Upwarping appears to continue today, shown by downward and headward stream cutting.

**5. Remaining internal basins.**
Only the Sulphur Spring Valley in Arizona and a broad area near Lordsburg and Silver City, New Mexico, remain internally drained.

**6. Quantified valley deepening.**
Late Quaternary upwarping led to large-scale alluvial removal and valley deepening:
- Safford Valley: about 900 feet
- Santa Cruz Valley near Tucson and much of San Pedro Valley: about 500 feet
- Phoenix basin: downcutting was slight. Post-younger-fill flood-plain alluvium was deposited to depths of 200 to 300 feet, suggesting some subsidence. The Salt and Gila rivers stayed through-flowing.
