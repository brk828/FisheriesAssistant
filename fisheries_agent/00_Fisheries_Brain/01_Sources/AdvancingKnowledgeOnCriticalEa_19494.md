# Advancing Knowledge on Critical Early Life History and Survival of the Endangered Razorback Sucker

## Executive Summary
This 1996 research study plan (National Biological Service, Midcontinent Ecological Science Center) outlines a multi-year effort to close critical knowledge gaps in the early life history of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Upper Colorado River System. Building on prior NPS/NBS work (1992–1995), the study aims to document spawning, larval distribution, growth, and survival patterns, evaluate predation pressure from nonnative [[Red Shiner]], and assess the role of floodplain/backwater nursery habitat connectivity — driven by reservoir release patterns from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] — in recruitment success. The work is directly tied to an upcoming Biological Opinion on Flaming Gorge Dam re-operation (due 1997) and broader floodplain restoration efforts in the Green River's Jensen and Ouray reaches.

## Background & Description
- **Larval size range observed in field (1994):** 11–16 mm total length (TL), indicating survival into the second or third month post-spawning.
- **Developmental threshold (captive growth data):** A minimum of 45–90 days is required to reach 20–26 mm TL.
- **Fin and mouth development:** At 20–26 mm TL, full fin development occurs, and mouth position migrates from terminal (forward-facing) to subterminal.
- **Functional significance of subterminal mouth (~26 mm):** Enables benthic feeding on food items such as [[Chironomid Larvae]], a common prey item in riverine backwater substrates.
- **Predator escape threshold:** At ~20 mm TL, larvae grow beyond the mouth-gap size of the dominant nonnative predator, [[Red Shiner]], and achieve faster swim speeds potentially aiding predator avoidance.
- **Juvenile telemetry size class:** Fish reaching ~30–35 cm are considered appropriate for radio/sonic transmitter implantation (achieved in approximately 2 years under hatchery rearing; held 4–5 years for sexual maturity studies).
- **Hatchery survival rate:** Approximately 25% survival from larvae to stocked juvenile size (30 cm) under improved aquaculture techniques at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range:** Historically common in large mainstem rivers of the [[Colorado River System]]; now largely restricted to the Green and Yampa Rivers (Utah/Colorado), believed to hold the largest/only naturally reproducing river population.
- **Spawning habitat:** Adult spawning aggregations identified on the Green and Yampa Rivers in and adjacent to [[Dinosaur National Monument]]; spawning season spans late April through June at the lower end of Cataract Canyon near the Colorado River's inflow to [[Lake Powell]].
- **Nursery habitat:** Floodplain and tributary-mouth backwaters (e.g., [[Millard Canyon]], Anderson Bottom–Bonita Bend reach in [[Canyonlands National Park]]) serve as critical larval rearing zones; habitat is ephemeral, dependent on spring flow magnitude and duration of inundation (documented example: 30-day inundation period at Millard Canyon).
- **Reservoir population:** A relict, aging population persists in [[Lake Mohave]] (Lake Mead National Recreation Area), sustained via light-trap larval collection and hatchery augmentation (Native Fish Work Group program), also serving as [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] brood stock source.
- **Threats:**
  - Altered flow regimes from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (Green River) and Gunnison River CURE Units reduce spring peak flows, shorten flow duration, and disconnect river-floodplain habitats, limiting nursery habitat availability.
  - Predation by nonnative [[Red Shiner]] on small larvae (<20 mm TL).
  - Larval dispersal/flushing downstream during cold, high-runoff years (documented in 1995), potentially reducing local recruitment.
  - Competition/predation from other nonnative fishes colonizing backwater nursery habitats.
- **Downstream larval transport:** Larvae documented traveling considerable distances downstream — from Green River spawning areas through [[Canyonlands National Park]] to the Colorado River inflow of [[Lake Powell]] (15 larvae collected at inflow in 1993; 122 larvae collected at Anderson Bottom same year).

## Key Findings
- **Effective non-lethal sampling method:** Floating, low-light-intensity, quatrefoil-style [[Light Traps]] were determined most effective for sampling razorback sucker larvae in riverine conditions (developed via NPS/Larval Fish Laboratory testing, 1992).
- **Interannual variability in larval production:**
  - 1993: 122 larvae collected at Anderson Bottom (Canyonlands); 15 larvae at Lake Powell inflow.
  - 1994: 47 larvae from Canyonlands backwaters; larval size range 11–16 mm TL suggesting extended survival.
  - 1995: Only 47 larvae collected on Green River (vs. 1,217 the previous year); zero larvae collected in Canyonlands nursery areas — attributed to cold water temperatures and high runoff delaying/dispersing spawning.
- **Lake Mohave larval collection program growth:** 12,000 larvae (1993) → 21,000 (1995) → 68,000 (1996), collected via light traps and dip-netting by volunteer agencies, reared at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].
- **Flow timing hypothesis:** Green River flows historically peaked 10–14 days after Yampa River flows, extending shoreline nursery habitat duration; longer inundation (6–7 weeks) is hypothesized to improve larval growth and survival, informing planned floodplain restoration in the Jensen and Ouray reaches (RIP, Fall 1996).
- **Study objectives (1996–1997):**
  1. Document spatial/temporal distribution and abundance of larvae via continued light-trap surveys (Lake Powell inflow, Canyonlands, Green River Jensen-Ouray reach).
  2. Evaluate size/developmental-dependent survival and growth of native sucker larvae (razorback, [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]]) relative to red shiner predation and habitat/food conditions, using field enclosure experiments (1 m² x 1.3 m deep, 500-micron nitex mesh; tetracycline-marked larvae; 7-day trials).
  3. Assess feasibility of block nets (2-mm mesh) to exclude nonnative fish from native sucker nursery habitats.
  4. Rear captured larvae for genetic analysis, potential brood stock selection, and future telemetry studies (radio/sonic tagging of 30–35 cm juveniles) to track riverine and reservoir habitat use.
- **Methodological challenges:** Existing telemetry programs show signal loss when tagged suckers move into deep reservoir waters (Lake Powell) or shallow riverine habitats, prompting calls for coordinated multi-agency tracking approaches.
- **Management relevance:** Findings directly support the 1997 Biological Opinion on [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] re-operation and the Recovery Implementation Program's (RIP) broader augmentation and floodplain restoration strategy for the endangered razorback sucker.
