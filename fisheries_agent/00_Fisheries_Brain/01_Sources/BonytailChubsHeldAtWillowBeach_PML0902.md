# Bonytail Chub Holding and Treatment Log at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery, May 1993

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bonytail-chub

## Executive Summary

This document is a station log and management memorandum covering the capture, delivery, holding and treatment of [[Bonytail Chub]] (BTC) at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] between 11 and 26 May 1993. Fourteen BTC were collected by trammel net in [[Carp Cove]], [[Lake Mohave]], and delivered in three batches. Nearly all arrived with net abrasions, skin loss, split fins and, in one case, a suspected cataract. Fungal infection by [[Saprolegnia]] became the dominant problem. Staff applied repeated [[Formalin Treatment]] baths, a [[Potassium Permanganate]] paint that caused at least one fatality, and extended [[Salt Bath Treatment]] regimes, with advice from outside fish health specialists. Two deaths are recorded. On 26 May 1993 the surviving fish were transferred to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]. The log concludes that the injuries and stress were incurred before delivery, and it recommends less destructive capture methods and faster shipping to the final destination.

## Background & Description

- **Subject:** Bonytail chub, an endangered fish. Fish were collected while spawning, which the author notes left them with a high stress threshold.
- **Delivery sources:** [[Charles Minckley]] (Parker Fishery Resources Office) and [[Paul Marsh]] (Arizona State University) supplied the fish.
- **Delivery schedule:**
  - 11 May 1993: 9 BTC delivered (arrival water at 76°F). Acclimation was started at 10:00 a.m., reaching 58°F by 10:49 a.m.
  - 12 May 1993: 2 additional BTC delivered, already tempered to 58°F.
  - 13 May 1993: 3 additional BTC delivered, including one large gravid female (arrival at 72°F), bringing the total to 14.
- **Holding facility:** A 24 ft × 1.5 ft × 2.5 ft covered hatchery tank, maintained by station biologist [[Glenn Green]].
- **Injury profile at delivery:** Net marks encircling the head at the opercle, just in front of the dorsal hump; raw skin abrasions on the lateral body, most marked at the caudal peduncle; split and bloodied fins, most often the caudal fin; one fish with a discolored eye, probably a cataract.
- **Consultation:** On 11 May, staff phoned [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (Roger Hamman). Advice included a one-hour formalin flow-through treatment. Spawning the fish at the hatchery was judged unlikely to be feasible, as it would add stress and damage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Holding conditions and stressors**

- Flow-through systems ran at about 20 GPM. Standing (static) salt baths were used between flow-through treatments.
- Standpipe seal leaks repeatedly lowered water levels in the holding tank (noted on 20 May and 21 May), requiring resealing and renewed treatment.
- Static baths were a concern because metabolite toxins accumulated. Advice from [[Gary Carmichael]], based on his literature on treating stressed and injured fish, was to flush the tank with fresh water every 8–12 hours.
- Indications of fungal infection and tissue damage were progressive. By 18 May, eight of the 14 fish had severe [[Saprolegnia]] infections. By 21 May, three infected fish showed secondary infection with severe edema.

**Treatment regimes recorded**

| Treatment | Details as recorded |
|---|---|
| Formalin flow-through | 1/6,000 for one hour at 20 GPM, repeated over many days |
| Potassium permanganate | 2 g/L painted on wounds (18 May); 4 mg/L flow-through for one hour (21 May) |
| Standing salt bath | NaCl at 5.9 g/L; MgSO4 dose is garbled in the scan. Later batches used 28 lb salt and 0.66 lb magnesium sulfate. |
| Salt dip | 1% saline bath for 30 seconds (advised 21 May); later 1% bath on 22 May |
| Oxygen | Industrial-grade oxygen via diffuser during the 18 May salt treatment |

**Consultation network:** [[Pinetop Fish Health Center]] (Carol Shanks), [[Ron Majors]] (Mescalero National Fish Hatchery and Pinetop), [[Jim Brooks]] and Caryl Williams (Dexter Fishery Resources Office), and the Endangered Species regional office (George Divine). Advice from Pinetop advised against further formalin use and against antibiotic injection.

## Key Findings

**Chronology of events**

- **11–13 May 1993:** All 14 BTC arrived. All had some degree of injury, and one was noted as bent and stiff.
- **14 May:** Formalin treatment was applied again.
- **18 May:** Eight of 14 fish had severe saprolegnia infections. Potassium permanganate was painted on wounds, beginning with the worst-affected fish. After seven fish were treated, the first treated fish rolled over and lost equilibrium. All treatment was stopped and the tank covered for recovery. Advice from Gary Carmichael prompted an emergency standing salt bath with oxygen.
- **19–23 May:** Standing salt baths and formalin flow-through treatments were alternated. By 20 May, most fish had "drastically improved," but six still had fungus and three had large open sores on the caudal peduncle. One fish that never regained equilibrium after the permanganate treatment died on 20 May. It was frozen and held for age analysis by Chuck Minckley.
- **24 May:** Decision made to transfer the BTC to Dexter on 26 May. Pinetop advised against another formalin treatment.
- **25 May:** Another fish rolled over, and one fish lay on its side. The final salt flush was performed.
- **26 May:** One BTC was found dead. Roger Hamman and Phillip Soza loaded the remaining fish and departed for Dexter at 7:25 a.m.

**Conclusions and recommendations (Station Biologist, Glenn S. Green)**

- Future captures should use less destructive methods. Electroshocking, large hoop nets, or fyke nets are recommended over trammel nets.
- All holding problems at Willow Beach stem from stress and wounds inflicted before delivery.
- Permission should be obtained for chemicals such as NaCl and MgSO4, used with [[Nitrofurazone]], to reduce stress and infection during transport to holding facilities.
- Collecting BTC outside the pre-spawning period was suggested as a possible, though unlikely, alternative.
- Shipping fish directly from capture to their final destination is probably preferable to holding them at Willow Beach.

**Hatchery Manager's observation ([[Lynne R. Miller]])**

- The treatment that probably did the most good was a chemical not named in the log. It was a prohibited drug not listed on any [[Investigational New Animal Drug]] (INAD) application.
- A regional memo asked staff to slow the use of chemicals and drugs in fish culture. The manager stated an intention to comply.
- Endangered fish are not destined for human consumption. The concern is chemicals entering the environment.
- The manager questioned why BTC were subjected to such trauma and suggested leaving the fish in Lake Mohave.

**Mortality record:** Two BTC deaths are recorded in the log, one on 20 May (after permanganate exposure) and one on 26 May, before transfer.
