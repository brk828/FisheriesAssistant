# Management Plan for the Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game management plan, prepared by Linda Ulmer under the supervision of James St. Amant and Keith R. Anderson (Region 5, Inland Fisheries, October 1985), covers the [[Razorback Sucker]], a State-listed endangered catostomid once abundant throughout the [[Colorado River]] drainage. The plan sets a 10-year management period (1986–1995) aimed at securing existing populations and reestablishing a self-sustaining population in the lower Colorado River. Its central strategy combines biological monitoring, legal protection and mitigation, and a long-term reintroduction program using hatchery-reared fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was first described as *Catostomus texanus* from the Colorado and New rivers of California (Abbott, 1861). It is the fourth large endemic "big river" fish of the Colorado system to be listed, following the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]] (named in the document as *Gila elegans*). The plan notes the species' unique body form and physiology, its status as a monotypic genus, and its potential as a forage fish and contributor to sport-fishery diversity.

Historically, the species was among the most abundant large fishes of the basin. It was an important food for Indian tribes and was commercially harvested in southern Arizona; it was collected "by the wagon-load" from canals and the Salt River near Phoenix. Concentrations in the lower basin were reported until the early 1950s, after which they declined precipitously.

Listing history: the species was listed as endangered by the State of California in January 1974, protected in Nevada, and given "Group 3" status in Arizona. Federal listing was deferred in favor of an active recovery and reintroduction program; it was designated a federal candidate species in December 1982.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reasons for decline.** The document attributes decline to:
- Dam construction (1930s–1950s), which altered seasonal flow and temperature regimes (for example, 104 km of the Green River below Flaming Gorge Dam).
- Channelization and dredging, which created deeper, uniform channels and eliminated the backwaters and oxbows needed for spawning, nursery, and refuge habitat.
- Introductions of exotic fishes, which prey on eggs, larvae, and fingerlings. Green sunfish were singled out as a principal predator of recruits in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Senator Wash Reservoir]].

**Distribution.** In the upper basin (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming), numbers are declining and populations are dominated by large, presumably old individuals. Collections were scarce in the Green River and Gunnison River, and no fish have been taken in Wyoming since Flaming Gorge Dam closed in 1962. In the lower basin, the species persists in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Mead]] (Arizona–Nevada), with no documented recruitment in either. Remaining California populations occur in Senator Wash Reservoir, West Pond, and possibly the mainstream at undetectably low numbers. The species has not been recorded in New Mexico.

**Habitat linkages.** Ponds and canal systems fed by the All-American Canal, including the East Highline Canal and Coachella Canal, have yielded young razorback suckers, linking these artificial habitats to the lower river.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** Razorback sucker is severely reduced and shows no evidence of recruitment in most of its range. Remaining adults are senescent, and the plan concludes the species will go extinct without a recovery program.
- **Senator Wash Reservoir:** A mark-recapture estimate in August 1980 gave 54 ± 22 adults (95% confidence limits). Larvae were collected over 1980–83, but no young were taken in later sampling. Ultrasonic telemetry studies documented seasonal movement, depth, and habitat preferences.
- **Lower river survey:** A 1981 CDFG netting survey of the lower Colorado River (Parker Dam to the Mexican border) caught no razorback suckers in 2,500 hours of effort, despite gear selective for the species.
- **Management objectives:** Stock 25 to 30 cm (10 to 12 inch) fish into Senator Wash Reservoir; establish West Pond as a native fishes refugium; stock 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 inch) fish annually into the Topock, Parker, and Imperial divisions; grow fry at [[Niland Endangered Species Ponds]] and the Blythe Municipal Golf Course pond; and cease stocking exotic fishes in target reaches.
- **Timeline:** A minimum of 5 years of monitoring is needed to determine whether a viable population has reestablished. A review in 1991 will inform revision of the second five-year period.
- **Arizona program:** The AGFD–USFWS memorandum of understanding (1981) set a 10-year reintroduction goal. More than 10 million fry and fingerlings reared at Dexter NFH had been stocked by the time of the report, mostly as fry because rearing space was limited.
- **Federal status:** The Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team recommended listing as threatened, at least in the upper basin.
- **Priority:** The plan argues that immediate action is needed to prevent extirpation from the lower Colorado River, citing the Public Trust responsibility of CDFG under the California Endangered Species Act and Fish and Game Code section 1700.
