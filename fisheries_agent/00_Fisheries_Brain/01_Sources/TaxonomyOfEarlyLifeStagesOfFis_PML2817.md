# Taxonomy of the Early Life Stages of Fishes of the Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary and Moss Landing Harbor-Elkhorn Slough, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #early-life-stages #larval-taxonomy

## Executive Summary

This 1981 technical report, prepared by [[Johnson C.S. Wang]] for [[Pacific Gas and Electric Company]] and produced by [[Ecological Analysts]] (Concord, California), provides identification keys and descriptions for the eggs, larvae and juveniles of fishes from the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary]] and [[Moss Landing Harbor-Elkhorn Slough]]. The excerpt covers seven taxa from five or more families. Each species account describes spawning season, temperature and salinity range, egg morphology, larval development (myomere counts, fin completion, pigmentation) and juvenile meristics, with a major source of information given for each. The material is intended to support identification of early life stages in power-plant entrainment and environmental monitoring work.

## Background & Description

The report opens with a stage-by-stage key (larval and juvenile) and then gives taxonomic accounts organized by family. The author notes that the Sacramento-San Joaquin system contains a diverse centrarchid fauna, of which only one species was collected in the study area.

Species accounts in the excerpt follow a consistent template:

- **Spawning:** location, season, temperature, salinity and substrate
- **Eggs:** shape, diameter, yolk, oil globule, chorion, egg mass, adhesiveness, buoyancy
- **Larvae:** length at hatching, snout-to-anus length, yolk-sac and gut form, air bladder, myomere counts, fin completion, pigmentation, distribution
- **Juveniles:** fin ray counts, mouth position, vertebral count, distribution

Key species treated:

- [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*, Centrarchidae), the only centrarchid collected in the study area. The source lists 11 centrarchid species known from the river system, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]].
- [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*, Percichthyidae), an introduced species in California, with spawning in the lower Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers.
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*, Poeciliidae), a livebearer that spawns year-round in warm months.
- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*, Ictaluridae), found throughout the system, especially in shallow Delta waters.
- [[Sacramento Sucker]] (*Catostomus occidentalis*, Catostomidae), one of at least 12 catostomid species, most of them native.
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*, Cyprinidae), one of at least 17 minnow and carp species recorded in the system. Carp are introduced from Asia.
- [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*, Clupeidae), spawning from April through August in sluggish waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Spawning habitats and conditions vary by species:

- **Black crappie:** shallow weedy areas of ponds, reservoirs, sloughs and the Delta; spawning April through July; temperature 14 °C or greater; freshwater.
- **Striped bass:** lower reaches of the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers, spawning April through June, in fresh to slightly brackish water over a substrate of none (free-floating eggs).
- **Mosquitofish:** sluggish lower elevation waters of the Sacramento-San Joaquin system, including landlocked ponds and irrigation ditches; spawning throughout the year in warm months.
- **Channel catfish:** spawning from May through August; temperature about 23.9–28.5 °C (Scott and Crossman 1973); fresh water; nests in cavities such as undercut banks, rock crevices and burrows.
- **Sacramento sucker:** tributaries of the river system, spawning in spring; temperature about 10.0–15.0 °C; freshwater; gravel and sand substrate.
- **Common carp:** shallow weedy areas of tidal and nontidal fresh waters, including Montezuma Slough, New York Slough, Lindsey Slough and Millerton Lake; spawning March through July at 15–29 °C.
- **Threadfin shad:** sluggish waters of the Delta, reservoirs and sloughs; spawning April through August at roughly 20–25 °C on floating and submerged vegetation, logs and other floating substrates.

Larval distribution follows similar patterns. Larvae of several species remain in shallow, weedy inshore waters, while juveniles of some (for example Sacramento sucker) disperse into nontidal tributaries, the upper San Joaquin River and tidal oligohaline waters.

## Key Findings

- **Black crappie:** spherical eggs averaging 0.93 mm in diameter, single oil globule, adhesive, demersal. Larvae are 2.3–3.0 mm TL at hatching, with 10–14 preanal and 19–21 postanal myomeres (values as given in the source). Fecundity is reported from Scott and Crossman (1973) and Moyle (1976), though the OCR digits are uncertain.
- **Striped bass:** spherical, non-adhesive, demersal-free eggs, 3.2 mm in diameter (Murawski 1969). Larvae hatch at about 3.0–4.0 mm TL. Juveniles have 25 vertebrae in most cases (Merriman 1940) and a terminal, large mouth.
- **Mosquitofish:** eggs 2.38–2.76 mm, adhesive to aquatic vegetation. Larvae 4.0–5.5 mm TL at hatching, with 16–20 preanal and 16–20 postanal myomeres (source values as printed). Juveniles have 1 dorsal fin and an inferior mouth.
- **Channel catfish:** eggs 3.5–4.0 mm, deposited in large adhesive clusters in cavities. Larvae hatch at about 6.4 mm TL and complete yolk-sac stage at about 14–15 mm TL. Myomeres total 42–46.
- **Sacramento sucker:** ripe eggs 2.4–2.7 mm, fertilized eggs 2.4–3.2 mm; demersal and adhesive. Larvae have 45–49 total myomeres and 35–40 preanal myomeres (this study).
- **Carp:** eggs 1.5–2.1 mm, adhesive. Larvae are 4.0–5.0 mm TL at hatching (this study; Jones et al. 1978 give 3.0–6.69 mm). Total myomeres 34–36; juveniles have 36–39 vertebrae (this study).
- **Threadfin shad:** eggs are demersal and adhesive. Larvae are 3.2–4.4 mm TL at hatching and complete yolk-sac stage at about 4–5.5 mm TL. Juveniles have 17–27 anal fin rays and a terminal, oblique mouth.

Several key identification features (pigmentation patterns, gut shape, air bladder position and fin completion sequence) are described per species. Where the source mixes its own observations ("this study") with literature values, both are kept in the species accounts. The excerpt lacks the full key and the species checklists in their entirety, so this note should be checked against the original report for complete keys.
