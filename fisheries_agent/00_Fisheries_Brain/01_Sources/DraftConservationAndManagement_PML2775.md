# Draft Conservation and Management Plan for Three Fish Species in Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft plan from the [[Utah Division Of Wildlife Resources]] (Division) addresses documented population declines in three large-river cypriniform fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*). The plan is a state-level response to a charge from the [[Colorado River Fish And Wildlife Council]], which concluded in February 2001 that a basin-wide conservation agreement was premature because of limited information. Roundtail chub is listed as a Utah state threatened species; the two suckers are state species of special concern. The Division adopted an ecosystem-oriented approach, organizing distribution data by hydrologic unit and proposing baseline establishment, database consolidation, additional surveys, genetic and life-history research, and interagency cooperation. Funding came from the [[Endangered Species Mitigation Fund]], and future work depends on securing additional funds.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Utah has 66 recorded fish species, 27 of them native. The Colorado River Basin supports at least 35 species, more than 26 of them endemic, some persisting for over 10 million years. Geologic history, including the flooding of [[Lake Bonneville]] and shifts of the Bear River, shaped the distribution of native fishes. The Bonneville Basin has no outflow streams, and the upper Snake River's fish communities were separated from Columbia Basin fishes by Shoshone Falls.

**Species.** All three species are in the order Cypriniformes. Roundtail chub belongs to the minnow family Cyprinidae; the two suckers belong to Catostomidae. Shared traits of endemic large-river fishes include fusiform bodies, humped or keeled dorsal surfaces, leathery skin with embedded scales, and large falcate fins, likely adaptations to the harsh, unpredictable Colorado Basin. Taxonomy is unsettled: roundtail chub in the Gila River drainage was recently split into three species, and the [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*) was elevated to species status in 1993 and is federally listed under the Endangered Species Act.

**Life history highlights.**
- *Roundtail chub:* carnivorous, opportunistic feeder; mature at 5 years or 254–305 mm; spawn from June to early July at 18.3 °C; a 305 mm female may produce about 10,000 eggs. Relatively sedentary.
- *Bluehead sucker:* facultative herbivore; prefers large, cool streams with rocky substrates; mature at 2 years or 127–179 mm; a 39–44 cm female may produce over 20,000 eggs.
- *Flannelmouth sucker:* benthic omnivore; mature at 4–5 years; spawns for 2–5 weeks over gravel; a female produces 9,000–23,000 adhesive eggs; makes spring spawning migrations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** All three species are endemic to the Colorado River Basin, including the Green and San Juan basins in Utah. Bluehead sucker also occurred historically in the Bonneville and Snake drainages, and flannelmouth sucker occurs in the Virgin River system. Historical records suggest all three were common across the basin until the 1960s. Roundtail chub also occurs in the Gila River Basin and northern Mexico. Utah is near the geographic centre of the upper basin range and may hold a genetically distinct bluehead sucker population in the Bonneville Basin.

**Threats.** The plan estimates that about 22% of historic lotic habitat in the Colorado Basin has been converted to reservoirs by 140 dams and diversions. Dam-caused changes include:
- Cold hypolimnial tailwaters that impair reproduction and larval growth.
- Altered flow timing and magnitude that disrupt migration and spawning cues.
- Fragmentation that blocks access to spawning, rearing and overwintering habitat.
- Reduced fine sediment transport, which alters spawning and rearing habitat.

Introductions of at least 60 non-native fish species cause predation, competition, pathogen introduction and hybridization. Agriculture, infrastructure and urban development have also distorted the river environment. Preliminary investigations point to water withdrawals, hydrograph alterations and non-native interactions as current drivers.

**Sympatric species.** The Plan may also benefit boreal toad, canyon treefrog, several spadefoot and leopard frog species, speckled dace, virgin spinedace, mountain sucker, woundfin and the four federally listed Colorado Basin endangered fishes.

**Related programs.** Existing efforts include the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fishes Recovery Implementation Program]], the [[San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program]], the [[Virgin River Resource Management And Recovery Program]] and the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]]. Other states are also active: Arizona (game-species designation, PIT-tag studies, native fish refugia), Colorado and New Mexico (roundtail chub brood stock at the Mumma Fish Hatchery), Nevada (monitoring in the lower Virgin River) and Wyoming (University of Wyoming ecology studies).

## Key Findings

- **Status:** The three species have declined over much of their historic range. Utah's database holds 418 recorded sites: 60 historic (1984 or earlier) and 358 recent (1985 or later). Roundtail chub was recorded at 19 sites on or before 1984. Flannelmouth sucker was recorded at 11 sites on or before 1984, and Flaming Gorge Dam impacts were noted at seven sites in 1965.
- **Data gaps:** Range-wide assessments were not conducted before Bezzerides and Bestgen (2002). Many records come from incidental observations, and absence records are not systematically reported.
- **Approach:** The plan emphasises ecosystem management, baseline establishment at existing conditions, and a goal of preventing the need to federally list the species.
- **Goals:** Increase population sizes and distributions of the three species, insure their long-term persistence, and identify indicator reaches for long-term monitoring.
- **Management actions:** Consolidate databases (linked to the National Hydrologic Dataset), conduct additional surveys, pursue genetic structure research (null hypothesis: no structuring among basins), study life history and threats, and evaluate augmentation. Roundtail chub may not naturally recolonize historic habitat, making it the first candidate for augmentation.
- **Coordination:** Core Division managers meet twice yearly; cooperating agencies include the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Forest Service, Ute Tribe and National Park Service. Participation in the Memorandum of Understanding is voluntary, with withdrawal on 30 days' notice.
- **Evaluation:** The Plan will be reviewed at least every three years. Evaluation questions include whether populations and habitats are stable or improving and whether threats are identified and addressed.

**Note on source status:** This is a draft (Draft 4, 26 June 2002) and several funding and management commitments remain contingent on future support.
