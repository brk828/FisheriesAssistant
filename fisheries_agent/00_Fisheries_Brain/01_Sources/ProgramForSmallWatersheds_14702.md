# Program for small watersheds

#hydrology #watershed-management #flood-prevention

## Executive Summary

This 1956 extension article by Irving B. Stafford, State Conservationist of the [[U.S. Department of Agriculture]], reviews progress on the nationwide small-watershed program aimed at controlling erosion, floods, and sedimentation. It covers the [[Pilot Watersheds]] experiment, with four New York sites, and introduces the [[Watershed Protection and Flood Prevention Act]] of 1954. The article reports completed flood-control works on [[Dean Creek]], a severe 1955 storm test, and the status of six watershed applications under the new law. It also lists the conditions needed for successful projects and stresses that land-treatment measures must complement structural works. The article contains no fish, aquatic-invertebrate, or water-chemistry data.

## Background & Description

**Pilot Watershed experiment.** About two years before the article, a nationwide experiment began with 60 so-called Pilot Watersheds. Four were chosen in New York:

- [[Great Brook]] (Chenango County)
- [[Dean Creek]] (Tioga County)
- [[Little Hoosick]] (Rensselaer County)
- [[Ball Creek]] (Chautauqua County)

Pilot Watersheds are experimental. No further Federal funds could be used on them after June 30, 1958.

**Watershed Protection and Flood Prevention Act (1954).** Knowledge from the Pilots informed this newer law, which provides for truly local watershed projects with added Federal help. It is meant to bridge the gap between soil and water conservation programs and major river-valley development and flood-prevention programs. The [[Secretary of Agriculture]] may provide technical and financial aid to local organizations for:

1. Flood prevention
2. Agricultural phases of the conservation, development, use, and disposal of water

Primary responsibility rests with the [[Soil Conservation Service]]. Other Federal and state agencies may take part at any stage.

**Application process.** A project begins when an organization or group of landowners defines the problem and assesses local interest and resources. The original application and one copy go to the State Department of Agriculture and Markets, which represents the Governor and the State and consults the State Soil Conservation Committee. A second copy goes to the State Conservationist of the Soil Conservation Service at Syracuse. Applications must thoroughly analyze the problem, possible solutions, economics, local interest, and the resources needed to build and maintain the works.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Structural works.** Flood-control structures include reservoirs, debris dams, debris basins, riprap, and channel and stream-bank work. Debris basins must be cleaned after each storm-flood, and local sponsors furnish this maintenance.

**Land treatment.** The article stresses that structures alone do not prevent floods. Soil-conserving, water-holding practices are equally important, including:

- [[Contour Strip Cropping]]
- Reforestation
- Pasture improvement
- Cover cropping

Most of these practices, applied by cooperators with [[Soil Conservation Districts]], are especially valuable on stream headwaters. Big dams and extensive channel work are described as necessary in many downstream areas. Farm ponds and wildlife marshes may be included, but their contribution to flood control is seldom significant.

**Flood context.** The article notes that many small, local floods occur almost every year and can cause as much damage in small areas as large floods do over wide areas, though they receive little attention. Prevention is described as far cheaper than rehabilitation, and no flood can be stopped completely, but damage can be reduced.

## Key Findings

**Dean Creek (Tioga County) is the most complete Pilot project described.**
- Two detention dams were finished in summer 1955: one on Dean Creek and one on its tributary, Burheight Creek. They were built under Federal contract let by the Soil Conservation Service.
- [[Easements]] covering the dam sites and the areas that would flood in severe storms were secured by the Tioga County Soil Conservation District.
- Eleven timber crib debris-catching structures were built across the stream through local cooperation, involving the Soil Conservation District, the Town of Spencer, the County Highway Department, and the Lehigh Valley Railroad.

**The October 14–17, 1955 storm tested the Dean Creek works.**
- Total rainfall was 6.41 inches, with 4.02 inches on October 16.
- The two reservoirs impounded water to heights of 714 feet and 534 feet above normal, respectively, which was far below their capacity.
- Outflow held the stream within its banks, and the debris-catchers kept channels clear of sediment, trash, and boulders.

**Other Pilot projects are at varying stages.**
- [[Little Hoosick]]: Channel improvement, stream-bank protection, and debris-catchers are fully planned. One debris dam was built by local people in the fall before the article, and more construction was underway that summer.
- [[Great Brook]]: Interest remains high. Planned construction is limited and was expected to start in spring, but easement difficulties delayed it. The sponsors' annual field day near Norwich has become a major conservation event.
- [[Ball Creek]]: Construction is delayed by a lack of easements.

**Six New York and Pennsylvania watersheds submitted requests under the new Act.**
1. [[Cowaselon]] (Madison County)
2. [[Conewango]] (Cattaraugus and Chautauqua counties)
3. [[Upper Otselic]] (Madison County)
4. [[Genegantslet]] (Chenango County)
5. [[Canasawacta]] (Chenango County)
6. [[Cowanesque]] (Steuben County, N.Y., and Potter and Tioga counties, Pa.)

Field examinations led to Washington authority to plan four of them. Planning was well advanced on the Cowaselon and Cowanesque, had just started on the Conewango and Upper Otselic, and the Canasawacta and Genegantslet were combined at the request of their residents.

**Ten essentials for successful watershed projects:**
1. A significant problem that existing programs cannot solve in a timely and adequate manner
2. Local desire to act
3. Acceptable local sponsorship under the law
4. Capable local leadership
5. Cooperation and participation by all agencies and groups
6. Local provision of lands, easements, and rights-of-way
7. A practical, feasible, and economic plan
8. Financial ability and willingness to construct, operate, and maintain works
9. Financial ability and willingness to install conservation measures on schedule
10. Adherence to standards

**Conclusion.** The article presents the Watershed Protection and Flood Prevention Act as the best opportunity yet to reduce flood damage in New York. It calls for thorough applications and continued local preparedness, and it notes that watershed associations help keep the public informed.
