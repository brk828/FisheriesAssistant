# Bonytail Broodstock Collection Report: Lake Mohave, AZ-NV

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 2003 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared by C.O. Minckley and Mitch Thorson for the [[National Fish And Wildlife Foundation]] and the [[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]], documents a 2002 effort to collect wild [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) from [[Lake Mohave]] for [[Hatchery Broodstock]] production. Wild bonytail are nearly extinct in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Over five trammel-net sampling periods (15 April to 14 June 2002), crews fished 110 twelve-hour net sets and handled 2,470 fish of ten species. Only one bonytail was captured, a large adult that died after transport. The authors attribute the poor catch to low wild recruitment, low survival of stocked fish, and catchability factors, and they recommend changes in stocking size and future collection sites.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail]] is a Colorado River basin cyprinid. It was listed as Endangered in 1980, a [[Recovery Plan]] was completed in 1991, and [[Critical Habitat]] was designated in 1993. Earlier records came from [[Grand Canyon]], [[Lake Mead]], and the Colorado River below [[Parker Dam]], largely from angler reports. Bonytail collected after 1980 were taken by researchers seeking wild broodstock. Between 1980 and 2002 only about 112 wild bonytail were captured in Lake Mohave.

The broodstock base is genetically narrow. In 1991 the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] produced progeny from a small number of wild fish (10 or 16 individuals, per the text). These F1 progeny were transferred to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (DNFHTC), where 205 fish remained in September 2002. The authors state that these fish carry genetic representation of each original cross and are the best available genetic stock. The report notes that a single new wild fish could raise genetic diversity by about 20%, citing [[Hedrick et al. 2000]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Mohave conditions:** Surface elevation varied by slightly less than 2 m (194.65 to 196.52 m MSL). Hoover Dam discharge ranged from roughly 14,000 to 20,000 CFS, and Davis Dam releases averaged about 462 and 458 m³/s (approximately 16,300 CFS).

**Study areas:** Collections in April and May were made in the Cottonwood East area, about 2 km south of Cottonwood Bay Marina. The June collection was made in Arrowhead Cove, about 5 km north of Katherine's Landing. These were the two locations where bonytail had been collected in the previous five years. Most waters fished were under 5 m deep.

**Community composition:** Of ten species captured, only bonytail and razorback sucker are native. Non-native fishes dominated the catch.

**Transport water chemistry:** A bonytail transported to WBNFH and later to DNFHTC arrived with a water temperature of 10°C, dissolved oxygen above 20 mg/L (about 360% saturation), and 2 ppt salinity. Submerged aerators are known to produce lethal dissolved gas concentrations, which the authors consider the most likely cause of death.

**Telemetry habitat use:** Tagged bonytail were found near shore 76% of the time and in open water 24% of the time. Wild-caught fish were 70% along shore and the remainder pelagic. Fish were distributed across five reservoir zones: Black Canyon, Interface, Cottonwood Basin, Cottonwood Cove, and Reservoir Canyon.

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort and catch**
- Five sampling periods were conducted, with 110 net sets totaling 3,300 m of net and 1,320 hours of netting.
- A total of 2,470 fish of ten species were handled. Catch per net ranged from 14.1 to 32.1 fish/net, and catch per 30 m of net ranged from 4.7 to 10.7.
- Common Carp dominated the catch (50.9%), followed by Striped Bass (33.7%).

**Native fish captures**
- Fifteen [[Razorback Sucker]] were taken, 422–624 mm total length and 533–2,524 g. Eleven were recaptures, and four appeared to be repatriated fish.
- One large adult bonytail (520 mm, 995 g) was captured on 30 May 2002. It was held for three weeks at WBNFH and then transported to DNFHTC, where it died en route. Genetic analysis confirmed it was produced at DNFHTC.

**Stocking history and survival**
- Since 1981, 180,129 bonytail have been stocked in Lake Mohave. Between 1981 and 1995, 155,005 fish (86%) were stocked at 72–102 mm, plus 24 fish at 167–243 mm. The remaining 25,124 fish (14%) averaged 25 cm and were stocked between 1996 and 2002.
- Between 1980 and 1996, 91 bonytail were taken from Lake Mohave, an average of 4.8 per year.
- The first release of more than 25,000 larger bonytail (over 25 cm, N=15,557) occurred in 1999. An additional 8,682 were released between 2000 and 2002.
- The authors estimate that about 8,477 adult razorback suckers remained in Lake Mohave in March 2001. Of 46,610 juvenile razorbacks stocked between 1992 and 2001, about 4,523 (9.7%) remained, and fish stocked above 35 cm were recaptured at nearly ten times the rate of those under 30 cm.

**Interpretation and recommendations**
- The authors attribute the small catch to a combination of factors: collection variability, lack of wild recruitment, low survival of stocked fish, and low catchability of bonytail relative to razorbacks.
- They recommend testing whether releasing bonytail at 30 cm or larger improves survival compared with the current 25 cm standard, using returns of fish released over the next five years.
- Future wild-fish collection should target shallow points and gravel bars along the Arizona shoreline in the lower Reservoir Canyon area. Telemetry should continue because it can locate tagged fish.
- Monitoring must continue long after the possibility of contacting wild fish ends, because it is the only way to evaluate management actions.

**Telemetry and capture findings**
- Two wild-caught bonytail were located by telemetry near Arrowhead Cove, and a set of two shore-based nets captured four unmarked adult bonytail. The authors describe this as a significant result suggesting a method for future broodstock acquisition.
