# Razorback Sucker Embryonic Incubation and Temperature Thresholds in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines whether the water temperatures found in [[Lake Mohave]] allow successful embryonic development and larval emergence in the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*), an endangered catostomid of the [[Colorado River]]. The authors combined field collections of larvae from known spawning areas (1982–1983) with laboratory incubation experiments at controlled temperatures (8–20 °C), measuring [[Hatching Success]], [[Embryonic Development]] rates, and [[Oxygen Consumption]]. The central conclusion is that razorback sucker embryos hatch successfully between about 10 and 20 °C, with optimal hatching at roughly 12–20 °C, and that the minimum viable incubation temperature is close to that of the [[White Sucker]]. The authors argue that cold [[Hypolimnetic Discharge]] from dams may be a factor in poor recruitment, although Lake Mohave's temperature regime and long spawning season may buffer this effect there. Slower development at low temperatures is identified as a potential indirect source of mortality.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large, long-lived catostomid native to the upper and lower Colorado River basin. Lake Mohave holds the largest known population of the species, while it is rare in other lower-river locations and in the upper Colorado River. Its decline has been linked to altered thermal regimes below dams, which increase winter water temperatures and decrease summer temperatures (Paulson et al. 1980a). Although spawning is widespread in Lake Mohave and successful emergence of larvae has been documented, no evidence of recruitment into the adult population had been found (McCarthy & Minckley 1987).

Previous work by [[Marsh 1985]] identified 20 °C as the optimal incubation temperature, with lower hatching at 15 °C and complete mortality at 5 and 10 °C. The present study tests whether these conditions match the temperatures actually experienced in the field. Reported spawning temperatures range from 6 to 22 °C across the species' range (Douglas 1952; McAda & Wydoski 1980; Bozek 1984; Tyus 1987).

The study was conducted by researchers at the [[Lake Mead Limnological Research Center]], University of Nevada–Las Vegas, and was funded by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Office of Endangered Species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime and reproduction.** Hypolimnetic releases from reservoirs alter the seasonal temperature cycles under which native fishes evolved. Because embryonic development is strongly temperature dependent, reduced incubation temperatures may cause direct egg mortality or prolong exposure of embryos and larvae to other threats, including [[Water Level Fluctuation]], predation, and wave action.

**Lake Mohave conditions.** Spawning in Lake Mohave occurs over a protracted season of about 7 months, from November through May. Basin-area incubation temperatures (about 11–22 °C) were judged compatible with successful incubation in the laboratory and with field evidence of hatching.

**Spawning versus optimal incubation temperature.** Fishes commonly begin spawning below their optimal incubation temperatures. Both razorback suckers and white suckers initiate spawning at low temperatures, which may be a general pattern in freshwater fish. The authors suggest that the spawning window of about 6 °C for razorback suckers could approximate historical spawning temperatures.

**Growth and habitat.** Razorback sucker larvae grow much more slowly at 10 °C than at warmer temperatures. Based on white sucker behavior, the authors expect larvae to move to warmer shallow water to optimize growth. Similar size-dependent survival has been documented for [[Colorado River Squawfish]] and other fishes, where minimum overwintering size affects first-winter survival.

**Potential mortality sources.** Water level fluctuations and wind-wave activity can affect larvae still in gravel, while food availability and predation are important once larvae reach the swim-up stage. The authors note that the effects of slow development on these mortality sources remain unknown.

## Key Findings

### Field collections (1982–1983)
- Larvae were collected at night using dive lights in known spawning areas, with 15-minute netting samples.
- Larval collections were made at water temperatures of 10–15 °C.
- When a site yielded more than 300 individuals, only 50% were processed for developmental staging.
- Larvae collected in February 1983 were generally developmentally older than those from January.
- Collections in the Six Mile Coves area in February 1983 sampled three water depths.
- Field results were consistent with laboratory findings that lower temperatures did not preclude successful spawning.

### Laboratory breeding protocol
- Gametes came from 6 adult pairs in 1982 and 5 adult pairs in 1983.
- Sperm from multiple males was pooled before stripping females to maximize genetic variability.
- No hormones were used to induce egg maturation; all fish were naturally ripe.
- Eggs were acclimated at 1 °C per hour to experimental temperatures of 8, 10, 12, 15, and 20 °C, held within ±1.5 °C.
- Dechlorinated municipal water from Lake Mead was used.

### Hatching success
- No viable embryos hatched at 8 °C.
- Hatching success ranged from 22 to 57% at 10 °C, 32 to 65% at 15 °C, and 34 to 65% at 20 °C.
- Hatching success was similar between 12 and 20 °C within experiments. Optimal hatching appears to occur at 12–20 °C.
- The highest incubation mortality occurred at 10 °C, before the morula stage. In Experiment D, pre-morula mortality at 10 °C was almost 30% higher than at 15 or 20 °C.
- Hatching at 10 °C was never less than 50% of the warmer-temperature hatching rates in the discussion, although one experiment showed markedly reduced success.

### Development rates
- Development rate was positively related to temperature.
- Time to 50% hatch was about 420–556 h at 10 °C, 256–298 h at 15 °C, and shorter at 20 °C.
- Swim-up for 50% of larvae took about three times longer at 10 °C than at 20 °C.
- Development through yolk depletion was also faster at higher temperatures, but experiments at 10 °C were often ended before full yolk depletion.

### Oxygen consumption
- Oxygen consumption increased with temperature at all developmental stages.
- Consumption also increased with development at each temperature.
- The largest increase occurred at the swim-up stages, which also showed the highest variability among replicates.

### Egg characteristics
- Naturally ripened eggs averaged 3.1 ± 0.4 mm (SD) in diameter.
- Earlier work by [[Minckley & Gustafson]] reported 1.8 mm for hormonally induced ova and 2.9 mm for naturally ripened eggs.

### Comparison with previous studies
- The authors' development rates were slower than those reported by Minckley & Gustafson (1981) and Marsh (1985) at corresponding temperatures. This may reflect lower initial spawning and pre-acclimation temperatures in this study.
- Marsh (1985) and Toney (1974) reported total egg mortality at 10 °C, which the present study did not observe.
- Differences may stem from methods: previous studies used higher acclimation temperatures, formaldehyde to control fungus, and hormonal induction. The present study used naturally ripened fish, lower acclimation temperatures, and avoided formaldehyde. Fungal control was achieved by rocking eggs on netting in 1983.

### Razorback sucker and white sucker comparison
- White sucker (*[[Catostomus Commersoni]]*) incubation succeeded from 9.0 to 20.8 °C, with high normal hatching from 9.0 to 17.2 °C.
- Razorback sucker incubation succeeded from 10.0 to 20.0 °C. Marsh (1985) reported an apparent upper limit of 25–30 °C.
- White suckers begin spawning at 7.2–11.8 °C. Razorback suckers have been recorded spawning as low as 6 °C.
- Minimum viable incubation temperatures for both species are similar. The maximum is warmer for razorback suckers.
- White sucker growth is maximized at 26.9 °C, while larval growth is extremely slow at 10 °C.

### Synthesis and conclusions
- Optimal temperatures differ among life stages. In white suckers, the optimal larval growth temperature is about 11.7 °C higher than the optimal embryonic incubation temperature (McCormick et al. 1977).
- Faster embryonic development leads to earlier hatching, allowing larvae to forage sooner, grow larger, and compete more effectively, which can improve recruitment.
- Reduced growth from low temperatures may limit razorback sucker survival in the upper Colorado River.
- In Lake Mohave, the protracted spawning season may reduce the impact of cold early-season temperatures on incubation.
- The authors conclude that more ecological research is needed to quantify causes of mortality and identify factors that limit recruitment into the adult stock.

### Limitations noted by the authors
- Fluctuating temperatures in experimental chambers (8.5–11.5 °C) may have increased mortality at 10 °C.
- Experiments were not combined across years because the rocker-arm system changed water flow and oxygen diffusion.
- The combined effects of formaldehyde and low temperature are unknown.
