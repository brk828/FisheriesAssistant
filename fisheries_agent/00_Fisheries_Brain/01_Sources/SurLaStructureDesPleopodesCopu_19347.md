# Copulatory Pleopods of Aselloidea (Crustacea Isopoda Asellota): Comparative Morphology of Asellidae and Stenasellidae

#isopoda #aselloidea #crustacea

## Executive Summary

This 1970 Note to the *Comptes Rendus de l'Académie des Sciences* (Série D), by Guy Magniez and Jean-Paul Henry, compares the male copulatory organ, the distal endopodite of pleopod II, across three species each of the two freshwater isopod families of the superfamily [[Aselloidea]]: [[Asellidae]] and [[Stenasellidae]]. Building on earlier work using [[Scanning Electron Microscopy]] on *Proasellus*, the authors describe a characteristic distal architecture within each genus and argue that the two families represent independent lineages that converged functionally on a similar copulatory structure.

## Background & Description

The aselloid isopods are freshwater or groundwater crustaceans in the suborder [[Asellota]]. The authors report that fewer than 200 aselloid species were described at the time. The note works within a framework in which the male copulatory organ is the key taxonomic character. Its general form is an ovoid, vesicular organ with a proximal transverse muscle, formed by the fusion of the two original endopodite articles. Its distal end receives sperm from the genital papilla and transmits it under pressure to the female genital opening.

**Asellidae** are described as epigean (surface) or non-troglobitic forms with eyes in the cited examples. **Stenasellidae** are anophthalmous (eyeless) inhabitants of subterranean continental waters, fresh, thermal or sometimes mineralized. Stenasellids show a large development of free pleonites I and II and the presence of blood pigments, features that separate them from other Asellotes. The authors nonetheless keep them in the Aselloidea with the Asellidae, noting some affinity with the [[Stenetrioidea]] and [[Parastenetrioidea]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Freshwater colonization:** The authors suggest that the increasing complexity of the copulatory organ in aselloids relative to marine forms is more closely tied to repeated attempts by Asellotes to colonize continental waters than to a criterion of zoological homogeneity. Each family is thought to have colonized inland waters at different places and geological times.
- **Subterranean habitats:** Stenasellids are largely confined to hypogean (underground) waters, with the Mediterranean region to the equator as their main range. Tropical coverage is described as insufficient to judge their diversity. Conasellus, a North American genus of more than 40 species, is "for the most part hypogean."
- **Biogeography:** *Asellus aquaticus* is described as systematically isolated in Europe, since most *Asellus* species occur in the boreal Far East, especially Japan. Conasellus is a Nearctic genus that appears to have evolved independently of Palearctic asellids. Lirceus is a North American genus of about fifteen eyed forms.
- **Convergence:** Asellidae and Stenasellidae are regarded as two independent lineages with functional, not phylogenetic, convergence in copulatory structures. The authors conclude that the more primitive stenasellid pleopod II does not indicate that stenasellids are the ancestral stock of the Asellidae.

## Key Findings

**1. Asellidae (three genera examined)**

- ***Asellus aquaticus*** (Linnaeus, 1758): A male specimen of 11 mm from the Ouche at Dijon. It is "very common" in northeastern France. The distal endopodite shows a sinuous sternal cleft, a bent process inserted in a depression at the base of the inner lip, and an outer lip ending in a spiral hook with small denticles. Internal structure was previously studied by serial sections (Maercks, 1930).
- ***Lirceus fontinalis*** Rafinesque, 1820 (synonym *Mancasellus* Harger, 1876): A male of 17 mm from Cold Stream Farm, Kentucky (Holsinger and Norton leg.). Notable traits for an aselloid include double dactylar claws and small, ankylosed coxopodites. The sternal cleft lips are well developed, bear spiny areas, and largely enclose an internal-insertion duct.
- ***Conasellus antricolus*** (Creaser, 1931): A male of 14 mm from Tumbling Creek Cave, Missouri (Holsinger leg.). The sternal invagination is wide open and continues into a very fine duct (canula) formed by rolled lips. This duct is functionally equivalent to the neck of *Proasellus*. In *C. antricolus* only the caudal process is well developed, whereas *C. hobbsi* (Maloney) and *C. kenki* (Bowman) show the primitive typical structure of the genus.

The authors note that these three genera, together with the earlier *Proasellus* study, cover four major groups of Asellidae and clarify the general architecture of the family's copulatory organ.

**2. Stenasellidae (three species examined)**

- ***Stenasellus virei hussoni*** Magniez, 1968: A male of 8.5 mm from Goueil di Her, Haute-Garonne. A polytypic species of caves and groundwater in southwestern France. The distal article forms a funnel with a wide oblique proximal opening. Its sternal and tergal margins fold medially, meeting without fusing in a helical line that turns more than 90° distally. This shifts the latero-distal orifice to the tergal face, surrounded by fine denticles.
- ***Stenasellus buili*** Rémy, 1949: A male of 11 mm from the Giraudasso cave, Soulatgé, Aude. Described as a large form restricted to the flooded zone of karst areas in Hérault and Corbières. The margins close more completely, to about two-thirds of the funnel height, so the organ appears functionally more perfect than that of *S. virei*. Denticles are more developed than in *S. virei*.
- ***Stenasellus breuili*** Racovitza, 1924: A male of 8 mm from the Cueva Akelar, Spanish Basque Country. The two membranous articulation areas give the endopodite high mobility (about 100° in the plane of the appendage). The organ is only slightly twisted, and a terminal axial protuberance bears a fan of simple setae. The inner face of the funnel bears distally oriented setules. Denticles are hypertrophied in this species.

Across all three stenasellids, the endopodite has the same basic plan, which the authors expect in other stenasellid genera. The first article forms a manubrium and the second is a mobile copulatory organ, an imperfect funnel that can connect proximally with the genital papilla and distally with the female genital opening.

**3. Overall conclusions**

- Fewer than 200 aselloid species were described at the time, yet the group shows considerable diversity and complexity in the copulatory pleopod.
- The structure is remarkably fixed within a species but fairly uniform within a genus.
- The two families have clearly different architectures.
- The primitive stenasellid condition does not make them ancestors of Asellidae.
- Aselloidea, like terrestrial isopods, are regarded as an essentially synthetic group.
