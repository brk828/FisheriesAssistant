# Options for Selectively Controlling Non-Indigenous Fish in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #esocidae #centrarchidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This draft report (June 1995), prepared by the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] Colorado River Fishery Project with [[Colorado State University]] and [[Utah State University]] researchers, reviews the life histories and control options for non-indigenous, non-salmonid fishes in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] (UCRB). Introduced species are blamed for contributing to the decline of native fishes, and the authors argue that selective control is needed to protect and possibly recover endangered fishes. The review drew on more than 10,000 references and used more than 200 sources for the species accounts. Each of the 34 non-native, non-salmonid species is evaluated for distribution, habitat, environmental tolerances, reproduction, interactions with native fishes, and control options, which are grouped as mechanical, chemical, biological, and physicochemical.

## Background & Description

Non-native fish introductions in the UCRB began more than 100 years ago, mainly for food and sport, and most introductions after 1900 were made for anglers. Water development converted a variable river into a more stable system with flow and temperature patterns that favored non-native fishes ([[Behnke and Benson 1983]]). Tyus et al. (1982) reported that 42 of 55 fish species (76%) in the upper basin were introduced, and 34 of these are non-salmonid. These introduced species account for over 90% of fish sampled in standardized efforts (McAda et al. 1994).

[[Hawkins and Nesler 1991]] surveyed Colorado River Basin researchers, who ranked 28 non-native species by their adverse impact on native fishes. The top-ranked species included [[Channel Catfish]] (first), [[Northern Pike]] (third), [[Common Carp]] (fourth), and [[Red Shiner]] (second). [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Green Sunfish]] were tied for fifth. The four cyprinids and channel catfish are abundant or common in the upper basin and sympatric with native fishes.

The authors frame control under four categories, with physicochemical control added to the three traditional categories of [[Wiley and Wydoski 1993]] (mechanical, chemical, and biological control). ## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mechanical control.** Removal by seines, traps, gill nets, barriers, electrofishing, and harvest. Published results are mixed. [[Rose and Moen 1953]] and [[Crawford 1957]] reported increases in game fish after non-game fish removal. [[Huish 1959]] removed 357,947 pounds of fish from Lake Reedy, Florida, with no effect on the sport catch. [[Johnson 1977]] removed 85% of the adult white sucker standing crop in a Minnesota lake, and recovery by recruitment was not evident until 1972.

**Chemical control.** Rotenone and antimycin kill indiscriminately, while selective toxicants such as TFM and Squoxin target certain species. Success depends on water chemistry, toxicant, formulation, differential toxicity, and application thoroughness ([[Lennon et al. 1971]]). Stream eradications often required at least two consecutive years of treatment. [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure was preceded by a 1962 treatment of 445 miles of the [[Green River]] with 81,681 liters of 5% rotenone, but flannelmouth suckers, redside shiners, fathead minnows, and speckled dace recovered to pretreatment distribution by 1964. The [[Strawberry Reservoir]] reclamation in 1990 used 878,000 lbs of powdered rotenone in a 12,040-acre reservoir and 2,500 gal of liquid rotenone in 161 miles of streams. It met its objectives and was judged a success.

**Biological control.** Includes predation, pathogens, biomanipulation, and prevention of stocking. The [[Channel Catfish Virus Disease]] affects only channel catfish, and young fish can die 50 to 95% of the time ([[Plumb et al. 1989]]).

**Physicochemical control.** Involves manipulating water level, temperature, flow, and turbidity. Historic flow regimes in the West were modified in ways that favor non-native species. [[Minckley and Meffe 1987]] found that non-native fishes declined or disappeared after major floods in unregulated Arizona streams, while native fishes were rarely affected. Water-level drawdowns have reduced spawning of many fishes, but they are harder to apply in arid regions where water is scarce.

**Study scale.** The UCRB drains over 284,000 square kilometers ([[Tyus and Karp 1991]]). Earlier control efforts were confined to isolated reservoirs, lakes, and small drainages, with none at the basin scale.

## Key Findings

**Overall framing**
- The introduction of non-indigenous fishes is a major cause of native fish decline in the UCRB. Control measures must be combined with habitat changes that favor natives.
- The authors recommend that control be approached carefully and evaluated fully, because targeting one species can create new interactions (Wiley and Wydoski 1993).

**Species-level conclusions**
- **[[Threadfin Shad]]:** Restricted to [[Lake Powell]] and not considered a threat. Winter temperatures in other parts of the basin are too cold for it.
- **[[Red Shiner]]:** Among the most abundant fishes in the upper basin. Its ability to re-colonize and its high reproductive potential make control difficult. Chemical treatment is not viable. Its abundance is negatively correlated with high discharge and lower water temperatures.
- **[[Common Carp]]:** Tolerant of a wide range of conditions. Eradication is "virtually impossible" except in small, isolated lakes. Commercial and sport harvest is the most promising approach, but it is impractical in the upper basin. Spawning aggregations, peaking in early June to early July, could be targeted.
- **[[Utah Chub]]:** Rare in most of the UCRB but abundant in [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]]. Chemical eradication has been tried in [[Strawberry Reservoir]]. Water-level drawdown during spawning and predation by [[Lake Trout]] were suggested as controls.
- **[[Sand Shiner]]:** Locally common and sympatric with native fishes in nursery habitats. Its abundance rises when spring peak flows decline.
- **[[Fathead Minnow]]:** Widespread and highly adaptable, with high reproductive potential. Laboratory and field evidence suggest it competes with and possibly preys on larval [[Colorado Squawfish]].
- **[[Channel Catfish]]:** Considered by Hawkins and Nesler (1991) the greatest threat to native fishes. Mechanical removal and reduction of stocking are the main options. Channel catfish are rare to incidental across most of the upper basin, so removal would take a great deal of effort. Predation on larval and juvenile native fishes has been documented.
- **[[Black Bullhead]]:** Rare or incidental. Control efforts would be similar to those for channel catfish.
- **[[Northern Pike]]:** Rare or incidental but piscivorous, increasing in range and abundance, and sympatric with endangered fishes. Stocking should stop. Mechanical options include increased fishing pressure, downstream barriers, and netting or electrofishing of spawning aggregations.
- **[[Green Sunfish]]:** The most abundant centrarchid in the upper basin and considered a concern for predation on young squawfish. Isolating off-channel habitats and ending stocking in them are suggested.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]]:** Piscivorous and increasingly moving into endangered-fish habitat. Barriers, harvest, and removal of spawning aggregations are recommended.
- **[[Walleye]]:** Reproducing populations were established in Duchesne River reservoirs in the 1960s and 1970s. Stocking has stopped in Colorado and Utah, and barriers and harvest are recommended.
- **[[White Sucker]]:** Widespread, tolerant, and hybridizing with flannelmouth and bluehead suckers. Mechanical removal at spawning aggregations and chemical treatment in reservoirs are the main options.
- **Minor species:** Leatherside chub, brassy minnow, plains minnow, longnose dace, creek chub, Utah sucker, longnose sucker, yellow bullhead, plains topminnow, plains killifish, western mosquitofish, white bass, striped bass (restricted to Lake Powell by [[Cataract Canyon]]), bluegill, white crappie, black crappie, Iowa darter, johnny darter, and yellow perch are rare or incidental and currently pose no important threat to endangered fishes, though several warrant monitoring.

**Recommendations (summary)**
1. Use a temporally and spatially sequenced approach that includes evaluation of all control attempts, beginning in high-priority reaches. Document abundance before and after removal.
2. Evaluate system-wide food-web and bioenergetics effects before selective control, because competition for limited food may be the true bottleneck.
3. Establish monitoring and evaluation programs. Responses to predator-prey management may take one to ten years to appear.
4. Avoid creating habitats that favor non-natives, such as low-velocity, structurally complex floodplain habitats.
5. Maintain existing barriers on the Gunnison and Colorado rivers.
6. Address angler concerns by developing alternative fishing opportunities outside the 100-year floodplain.
7. Apply mechanical control (liberalized regulations, traps, seines, gill nets, electrofishing, downstream barriers), targeted chemical treatment in reservoirs and isolated areas with few endangered fishes, biological control (stocking piscivorous natives, ending non-native stocking), and physicochemical control (flow regimes that approximate natural hydrographs with above-average spring and summer peaks, and drawdowns to expose spawning areas).

**Key uncertainty:** The authors repeatedly note that cause-and-effect relationships between discharge and non-native abundance remain to be determined. Flow manipulations, such as releases from Flaming Gorge Dam, should be tested with before-and-after enumeration.
