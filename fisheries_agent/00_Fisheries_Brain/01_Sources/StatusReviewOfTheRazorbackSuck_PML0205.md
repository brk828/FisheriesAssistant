# Status Review of the Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 status review, prepared by Kevin R. Bestgen of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], compiles historic and present status, life history, causes of decline, and current research and management for the [[Razorback Sucker]], an endemic catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species was once widespread from Wyoming to Mexico but is now greatly reduced. The largest remaining concentration is in [[Lake Mohave]] (estimated about 60,000 fish, range 15,000 to 120,000, in spring 1989), with smaller populations in the upper [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]]. The report was produced under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) for endangered fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin, which aims to delist three species and manage the razorback sucker so it no longer needs Endangered Species Act protection. The species had been proposed for endangered listing in 1989.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Xyrauchen* is one of three monotypic genera in the family Catostomidae. The species was first described in 1860 as a mounted specimen from the Colorado and Gila rivers, Arizona. The [[Uncompahgre Sucker]] was later judged a hybrid of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and razorback sucker.
- **Morphology:** Identified by an abruptly rising, bony dorsal keel behind the head, which gives the common name. Dorsal fin rays usually 14-15, 45-47 total vertebrae, 68-87 lateral-line scales, and 44-50 gill rakers. Dark brown to olive dorsally, yellow to white ventrally.
- **Size:** Adults can reach 1 m TL and 5-6 kg, but are more commonly 400-700 mm TL and under 3 kg.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Breeding males are dark dorsally and bright yellow-orange laterally and ventrally, with more pronounced tubercles. Females are larger and heavier.
- **Feeding anatomy:** Pharyngeal teeth are comb-like, consistent with benthic feeding. The heavily ossified caudal skeleton is thought to be an adaptation to strong currents.
- **Intraspecific variation:** Not studied; a qualitative comparison suggested Upper Colorado and Gila specimens are similar.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and status**

- **Lower Colorado River Basin (LCRB):** Common only in [[Lake Mohave]], which probably holds the largest remaining concentration. Smaller numbers occur in [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Havasu]], [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] and below [[Hoover Dam]]. Records from the mainstream and canals are scattered.
- **Upper Colorado River Basin (UCRB):** Regularly found only in the upper Green River, Utah, and lower Yampa River, Colorado. Small concentrations occur in Lake Powell arms (Dirty Devil, San Juan, Colorado). The [[San Juan River]] sub-basin is largely unsurveyed and records are rare.
- **Gila River drainage:** Historically common in warmwater reaches. The last substantiated collection of young fish was in 1926, and the only later captures were hatchery fish stocked after 1981.
- **Historic losses:** The Salton Sea population disappeared as salinity rose after 1929. Declines in the Colorado River sub-basin were evident by the early 1940s.

**Life history**

- **Habitat:** Mainstream rivers, backwaters, sloughs and oxbow lakes. Adults use nearshore runs and off-channel habitats, and congregate in warm, low-velocity backwaters in spring. Larvae are found in shallow shoreline areas.
- **Movement:** The Green River population is mostly sedentary, with a migratory component that shows strong fidelity to spawning areas. Migrations of 53 to 190 km were recorded.
- **Reproduction:** In Lake Mohave, spawning runs from late November to May, peaking January-March at 10-12 °C. Males outnumber females about 1.2 to 3.6:1. Spawning occurs over gravel and cobble in shallow water. Fecundity in Lake Mohave averaged about 100,000 ova.
- **Hybridization:** Hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] are common throughout the UCRB. Some hybrid identifications may be errors, since low-keeled females can be mistaken for hybrids.
- **Age and growth:** Otolith analysis gave ages of 24 to 44 years for Lake Mohave fish. Wild adults in the UCRB grow slowly (about 2-3 mm/yr). Hatchery fish grow rapidly, reaching over 300 mm TL within a year in predator-free ponds.
- **Diet:** Larvae feed mainly on phytoplankton and zooplankton. Lacustrine adults are planktivorous, while riverine adults feed mostly on benthos.
- **Disease:** Blindness in one or both eyes was common in Lake Mohave (about 14% and 6.3%). Bacterial infections and external lesions were more common in females during the spawning season.

**Decline drivers**

Decline is attributed to habitat loss from dams, flow regulation, channelization and loss of off-channel habitat, water quality change, introduced non-native fishes, and interactions among these factors. Predation on eggs and larvae by non-native [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Green Sunfish]] is considered the main constraint on recruitment. Cold water temperatures have also been implicated. Non-native riparian plants such as [[Saltcedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) have altered riverine habitat.

**Native predators** include [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Bonytail]]. Birds are a significant source of mortality in hatcheries.

## Key Findings

- The razorback sucker has been greatly reduced across its range. Lake Mohave is the largest remaining population, and wild populations in the UCRB are small and mostly old adults.
- Recruitment failure is the central problem. Young fish disappear soon after emergence, and juveniles have been extremely rare in collections since the early 1990s. Predation by non-native fishes is identified as the most important cause in the LCRB.
- Experimental results support predation as a major limit on recruitment. Larvae caged away from predators grew to 30 mm TL or more, whereas those in the Lake Mohave reservoir disappeared at 10-12 mm TL.
- Stocking of hatchery fish (over 12 million in the Gila River, with only 19 recaptured) has had very low recovery rates, mainly because of predation and dispersal.
- Brood stock is being collected from the Colorado River, the San Juan and Dirty Devil arms of Lake Powell, and the Green River, and is held at the Colorado River Fishes Propagation and Experiment Station at Ouray, Utah. About 1,635 larvae were produced from 1987 to 1991, and about 2,000 juveniles were stocked in the Green River in 1989 and about 5,000 in 1990.
- Recommendations include: resolving genetic diversity; identifying causes of recruitment failure through controlled experiments; defining movements and habitat use; establishing refugia populations; identifying predator-free reintroduction sites; and continuing monitoring.
- The report notes that the razorback sucker has high intrinsic value as an endemic of the Colorado River fish assemblage and as a scientifically interesting species because of its unique morphology and variable life history.
