# Public Comments on the Recovery Implementation Program for Rare and Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a critical public review of a draft [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for four rare and endangered native fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The reviewer concludes that the draft is not a recovery program in substance. It provides a mechanism for [[Water Development]] to continue despite the presence of listed species, lacks concrete, data-based recovery actions, and rests on undocumented and largely unverifiable information. The reviewer also judges that the program's process was selective, excluding lower-basin states, conservation groups, and other interested parties from meaningful participation. The reviewer urges that recovery be defined as securing self-sustaining wild populations under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA), rather than as delisting or conflict resolution.

## Background & Description

The draft program addresses four species of concern:

- [[Colorado Squawfish]]
- [[Humpback Chub]]
- [[Bonytail Chub]]
- [[Razorback Sucker]]

The three species federally listed under the ESA are named in the comments. The razorback sucker was not federally listed at the time. The reviewer notes that the program's stated intent is to delist the three listed species and manage the razorback sucker so that it does not require protection. The reviewer also notes that the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (the Service) is charged with administering the ESA for the benefit of the species, and that the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] is a participant whose projects are considered a major source of impact.

The review is organized as a series of comments keyed to page numbers of the draft. It covers the cover letter, cooperative agreement, participant list, geographic scope, funding, supporting resolution, recovery elements, monitoring, research and hatchery needs, and appendices.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Status of the species (as reviewed):**

- **Bonytail:** Rare. Only three individuals were collected from [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada) in 1986. Some stocks are held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico), with others at Buenos Aires NWR, Dexter, and ASU Research Park (Imperial Valley). The reviewer argues against declaring the species extinct and notes that apparent upper-basin bonytail may be artificially inflated by misidentification within the genus *Gila*.
- **Razorback sucker:** Relative abundance of adults in some places masks a lack of natural recruitment. Knowledgeable scientists predict wild adults will disappear within the next 15 years. A long-term recovery program in Arizona begun in 1980 had not produced convincing evidence of recovery.
- **Humpback chub:** Maintains a self-sustaining population at the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]] (Arizona, lower basin). Other populations are in upper-basin waters.
- **Colorado squawfish:** Extirpated from much of the lower basin but persists in the upper basin. Its true status relative to pre-development conditions is hard to assess because historic data are largely lacking.

**Water and habitat pressures:**

- Water development, including [[Water Depletion]] of instream flows, is the central concern. The reviewer states that no documented case exists in the basin where water development and endangered species recovery were compatible.
- The [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations are noted as having minimally impacted Colorado squawfish spawning and recruitment, though the reviewer says this needs better documentation.
- [[Non-Native Fish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] (a domesticated species used to illustrate concerns about genetic change) are treated as ecological threats and genetic risks.
- Mapping issues include the omission of populations in the [[White River]] (upstream of [[Taylor Draw Dam]]) and the [[Gunnison River]], and the claim that the historic range of all four species was similar.

## Key Findings

**1. Overall assessment of the draft program (summary points):**

- It does not present a mechanism for recovery of native fishes, but for accommodating water development.
- It fails to involve conservation groups, lower basin states, and other interested parties outside the upper basin, and the mailing list is "highly selective."
- Biological documentation is not made available for review, and the basis of the program is "information of questionable (but unknown) credibility."
- It appears to disregard the intent of the ESA, makes no recommendations for the fishes, and lacks measurable criteria for success.
- It delegates decisions to an implementation committee dominated by water-development and state interests, and it proposes a strategy that prioritizes conflict resolution over recovery.

**2. Funding and governance:**

- The reviewer questions why Wyoming's contribution equals those of Colorado and Utah, given unequal benefit and Wyoming's limited historic range of the species.
- It is unclear which government agency will receive and manage the funds, and whether the program will involve several independent agencies.
- The "$10 per acre-foot" figure, its derivation, and the share of depletion attributable to Reclamation projects that are exempt from the depletion charge are unexplained.

**3. Recovery goals and criteria:**

- Equating recovery with delisting is "ridiculous and distasteful." Recovery means securing the species in nature in perpetuity.
- Recovery goals must apply throughout each species' range, including the lower basin and the [[San Juan River]], which the draft omits without justification.
- The statement that habitat for all four species must be maintained, and that the goal is merely that populations be "self-sustaining," is considered vague and needs to be defined as a goal at the outset.
- Measurable criteria to assess progress were not proposed. Lifespans of the species likely exceed the program's timeframe.

**4. Instream flow and habitat:**

- The reviewer rejects "instream flow" as the sole habitat measure. Flow reductions, temperature change, and turbidity changes are likely to jeopardize the fishes.
- Specific figures are questioned: why 5,000 acre-feet is withheld, how the cited demand of 40,000 acre-feet compares with actual project demand, and why a 3-month, 5,000 acre-feet release (cfs equivalent given) reflects habitat needs.
- The program does not show how habitat suitability would be determined to support all life-history requirements, which "would likely demand a period of time greatly exceeding the term of the present program."

**5. Research, propagation, and stocking:**

- Recovery plans are under revision and cannot be evaluated. Stocks of at least razorback sucker have been augmented since 1984.
- Mass production techniques for all four species were reportedly developed over the past 15 years at Dexter and [[Willow Beach Hatchery]], so the need for additional research is unclear.
- Genetic integrity is not defined, and no mechanism to protect it is proposed. The reviewer notes that the genetic diversity of present captive stocks is unknown.
- A conservative estimate for completing the proposed research is perhaps 15 to 20 years (partly illegible in the scan). The reviewer says the program's timeframe is unrealistic and contingencies must be planned.

**6. Legal and procedural concerns:**

- The "experimental population regulation" provision needs explanation, and the reviewer questions whether the committee is effectively amending the ESA.
- The program's language on [[Condemnation]] rights is unacceptable. The federal government must retain its authority to manage endangered species.
- The use of "100 biological opinions" lacks any account of the outcomes, including how many were jeopardy opinions and why.

**7. Recommendations from the reviewer:**

- Prioritize bonytail first, followed by razorback sucker, humpback chub, and Colorado squawfish. The reviewer argues that the most critical problems should be addressed first.
- Define recovery as self-sustaining wild populations across each species' historic range.
- Make all data sources public and subject them to peer review before the program is finalized.
- Separate water management for fish from water management for development.
- Reject the draft in its current form and redraft it to include concrete, data-based actions, measurable criteria, and broader participation.
