# Summer Foods of the Cyprinid Fish *Semotilus atromaculatus* (Creek Chub)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Barber and Minckley (Department of Zoology, Arizona State University) examines the summer diet of the [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*) in the headwaters of the [[Mississippi River]] at [[Lake Itasca]], Clearwater County, Minnesota, in 1965. A total of 469 chubs was collected on five dates. Aquatic larvae and pupae, fishes, and molluscs together made up 95% of stomach contents. Diet shifted from small [[Ephemeroptera]] nymphs and dipterans in small fish to larger caddisfly and dipteran larvae and then to fishes in the largest chubs. Feeding peaked in early evening and was lowest in the morning, and mean stomach fullness declined over the summer.

## Background & Description

The [[Creek Chub]] is one of the most common and characteristic fishes of clear streams in the midwestern United States and Canada. It is valued as bait, as forage for other species, and as a sport fish for children. It occurs in many habitats but is most frequent in small creeks and upper parts of larger streams, where well-defined riffles separate deeper pools. Before this study, feeding had been described only in observational publications, with no detailed report.

Study fish were taken at two stations: about half at 0.5 km downstream of the [[Lake Itasca]] outflow and half at 8.5 km downstream. The stream averaged about 5 m wide (3–15 m), 0.25–1 m deep, with clear water and a mean discharge of 0.38 m³/s. Vegetation included *Elodea canadensis*, *Potamogeton pectinatus*, *P. richardsonii*, and *Cladophora* sp.

**Methods:** Fish were collected with a bag seine (9.2 m, 6.4 mm mesh) and a smaller seine (3.1 m, 2.7 mm mesh) and preserved in 10% [[Formalin]] immediately. In the laboratory, the anterior third of each digestive tract was examined, food volume was measured by displacement in 40% [[Isopropyl Alcohol]], and items were sorted to taxon under a dissecting microscope. Fish were grouped into four standard-length classes: 20–40 mm, 41–60 mm, 61–80 mm, and over 80 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water temperature:** Surface water at Lake Itasca averaged 22 °C (range 16–24 °C) in summer 1965. Origin of the river in a lake likely dampened daily and seasonal temperature fluctuations downstream.
- **Habitat change:** Submergent vegetation was sparse in early summer but grew into dense stands by August, nearly choking the channel. Chubs became more concentrated in deeper pools as the season progressed.
- **Community:** Of more than twenty fish species captured, three were as abundant as or more abundant than the Creek Chub: [[Common Shiner]] (*Notropis cornutus*), [[Blacknose Dace]] (*Rhinichthys atratulus*), and [[Horny-Headed Chub]] (*Nocomis biguttata*).
- **Invertebrates:** Large numbers of the amphipod *Hyalella azteca* and dragonfly nymphs of the genus *Gomphus* were present in the stream but rarely appeared in stomachs.

## Key Findings

**Diet by size class (percent frequency of occurrence, % F.O.; percent total volume, % T.V.; Table 1):**

- **20–40 mm (n = 61):** Dipterans (70.5% F.O., 53.4% T.V.) and mayflies (50.8% F.O., 21.7% T.V.) dominated. Baetis and Pseudocloeon nymphs were the most abundant mayflies. Hydropsychidae were the most frequent caddisflies. Terrestrial insects were substantial.
- **41–60 mm (n = 104):** Dipterans (52% F.O., 32% T.V.) and mayflies (43% F.O., 18.3% T.V.) persisted. Gomphid dragonfly nymphs were most common among odonates. Plecoptera were most frequent in this group.
- **61–80 mm (n = 86):** Dipterans and mayflies declined sharply in volume (16.8% and 0.58% T.V.). Fishes rose to 29.1% F.O. and 53.7% T.V. Almost all fish eaten were common shiners.
- **81+ mm (n = 70):** Fishes were the primary food (71% F.O., 70.1% T.V.). Invertebrates were still eaten, but common shiners were nearly the only fish taken.

**Feeding periodicity:**
- Feeding intensity peaked in early evening, around sundown, and was lowest in the morning.
- Mean stomach fullness peaked near midnight (about 30% of total volume) and was lowest at about 5:00 a.m. on 6–7 July (16%) and 8:00 a.m. on 10–11 August (10%).
- Feeding resumed in late morning and increased through the day.

**Seasonal trend in feeding intensity:**

| Date | Mean fullness | Empty stomachs | Sample size |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 June | 50% | 5% | 27 |
| 6–7 July | 21% | 26% | 225 |
| August (later collections) | 13% and 10% | 29% and 45% | 47 and 70 |

Feeding intensity declined over the summer. Other freshwater fishes showed similar reductions.

**Other findings:**
- Plant debris, detritus, and algae were only traces in the first two collections, but about 9% of total volume in the last two. The authors suggest this reflects accidental ingestion, since few algal cells were plasmolyzed and no fish ate the leaves or algae that fell on the water.
- Chubs were highly insectivorous and fed at all depths. The authors judged that they showed little food selectivity and fed roughly in proportion to availability, especially on abundant ephemeropteran nymphs.
- Creek chubs elsewhere are known to eat blacknose dace and to be cannibalistic. Here, only three stomachs contained fish other than common shiners.

**Proposed causes of late-summer feeding decline:**
1. Temperature effects on consumption rate (Markus, 1933; Pentelow, 1939; Krumholz, 1956). Stream temperatures here were relatively stable, which likely reduced this effect.
2. Schooling and local food depletion, with larger fish aggregations observed in late summer (Lachner, 1950; Hynes, 1950).
3. Insect emergence, which occurred throughout summer, but was not quantified.
4. Increasing competition as young and older fish overlapped in size and food, combined with vegetation-related crowding and possible interspecific competition.
