## Executive Summary

This 1945 study by William E. Ricker, part of the [[Indiana Lake and Stream Survey]], investigates the relative contributions of **fishing mortality** versus **natural mortality** in populations of Indiana's small-lake game fishes. Using fin-clip marking and creel census methodology, the study quantifies instantaneous mortality rates for six species/populations across two study lakes ([[Muskellunge Lake]] and [[Shoe Lake]]). The central finding is that natural mortality — not angling pressure — accounts for the majority of fish deaths in most populations studied, with total annual mortality frequently exceeding 55-95%. Critically, the paper argues against the popular hypothesis that predation drives this mortality, instead attributing most natural death to **senility**, occurring across a surprisingly wide age range rather than concentrated near maximum lifespan. The paper concludes with management recommendations regarding size limits, closed seasons, and catch limits.

## Background & Description

This paper is primarily a population-dynamics and mortality study rather than a morphological analysis; however, it notes species-specific ecological traits relevant to predation vulnerability:

- **Large [[Black Crappie]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], and other large-bodied sunfish** are noted to be difficult for predatory fish and birds to capture and swallow due to their size, undermining the "predator control" hypothesis commonly proposed by anglers and the public.
- No detailed morphometric, meristic, or anatomical data are presented in this source; focus is entirely on age-class survivorship and mortality rate as a function of age.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Small inland lakes (<50 acres) in Indiana, subject to recreational angling pressure.
- **Community structure**: Study lakes contained low abundances of large predatory fish — [[Gar]], [[Bowfin]], [[Pike]], and large [[Largemouth Bass]] — insufficient in number to meaningfully impact legal-sized game fish populations via predation.
- **Trophic interactions**: The paper explicitly rules out predation as a major driver of adult mortality in target game species, based on rough abundance estimates of predators relative to prey biomass.
- **Life history variation between lakes**:
  - In **[[Shoe Lake]]**, [[Bluegill]] populations were numerous but exhibited exceptionally slow growth, suggesting potential for increased exploitation without population risk.
  - In **[[Shoe Lake]]**, [[Black Crappie]] populations were comparatively scarce, with at least one year-class nearly absent — indicating vulnerability to overharvest.
  - In **[[Muskellunge Lake]]**, [[Bluegill]] were less abundant than in Shoe Lake but grew more rapidly, with substantial year-class variability (one cohort ~2x more numerous than another), which drove significant fluctuations in fishery availability (by weight) over subsequent years.
- **Disease**: No epidemic or conspicuous disease outbreaks were observed in any study lake, though the author notes disease effects may be functionally indistinguishable from senility-driven mortality.

### Core Conclusions

1. **Mortality is uniformly heavy**: No population studied showed less than 55% annual mortality; several exceeded 90%.
2. **Fishing is a minority cause of death**: In most populations, angling accounted for less than half of total mortality — sometimes far less. [[Pumpkinseed]] and [[Yellow Perch]] had the highest fishing-attributable mortality; [[Black Crappie]] had the lowest.
3. **Natural mortality increases sharply with age**: This age-related increase occurs almost entirely within the natural mortality component, not fishing mortality. Age III mortality for [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Yellow Bullhead]] approached 100%, as almost no Age IV individuals were caught after June 15.
4. **Predation is ruled out as primary cause**: Predator abundance (gar, bowfin, pike, large bass) was too low to explain observed mortality, and large prey fish are physically resistant to predation (size/swallowability constraints).
5. **Senility is proposed as the dominant natural mortality cause**: Because deaths occurred well below known maximum size/age for these species, the author concludes senility operates across a much broader age range in fish than in humans — notably in [[Bluegill]], where natural mortality rate remains roughly constant up to age VI–VII.
6. **Disease could not be ruled out** but is difficult to disentangle from senility-based mortality; no epidemic disease was observed in study lakes.
7. **Management implications**:
   - Abolishing/lowering the 5-inch minimum size limit (applicable to [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]]) would likely have minimal effect on total yield, since catch weight changes slowly near the optimum size limit ([[Ricker's Yield Model]]).
   - Relaxing closed seasons (currently May 1–June 15) or catch limits, or permitting additional gear (e.g., traps), could increase exploitation rates and potentially recover more of the "wasted" natural mortality crop.
   - Recommendations should be piloted on a small number of lakes first, particularly those with **slow-growing game fish populations** (e.g., Shoe Lake bluegills), due to insufficient understanding of the spawner-recruitment relationship.
   - Fluctuating year-class strength (seen in Muskellunge Lake bluegills) suggests recruitment variability may average out over long time periods, consistent with general fish pond production theory, but this remains unconfirmed for natural lake systems.

### Methodological Notes
- Mortality estimation method: fin-clip marking + near-complete creel census + repeated census in subsequent year(s) to calculate **annual total mortality rate**; cross-validated via age-frequency analysis of catch.
- Rates converted to **instantaneous mortality** format per [[Ricker 1944]] for analytical comparability.
- Fishing rates reported (excluding 1941) are estimated at ~2/3 of pre-WWII fishing intensity, meaning even a doubling of fishing effort would generally remain below natural mortality rates in most cases.
