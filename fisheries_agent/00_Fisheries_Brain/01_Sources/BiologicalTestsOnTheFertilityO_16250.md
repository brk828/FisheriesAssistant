# Biological Tests on the Fertility of an English Reservoir Water (Stocks Reservoir, Bowland Forest)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bacillariophyceae

*Note: The source concerns freshwater algae and water chemistry rather than fish; the taxon tag above reflects the diatom focus, and no fish species are described.*

## Executive Summary

This 1959 report by [[J. W. G. Lund]] of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]], reprinted from the Journal of the Institution of Water Engineers, assesses whether applying [[Basic Slag]] and compound fertilizers to farmland in the catchment of [[Stocks Reservoir]] would raise algal growth in the reservoir. The main nuisance organism is the diatom [[Asterionella Formosa]], which clogs water filters. Biological bioassays using Asterionella showed that moorland drainage is infertile, that fertility rises as streams pass through limestone bands, and that phosphate is the key nutrient. The joint recommendation was to permit basic slag on 200 acres (81 ha) per year, excluding the Asterionella growth period.

## Background & Description

- **Reservoir:** Stocks Reservoir holds 3,059 million gallons (9.64 million cu. m.), covers 344 acres (139 ha), has a maximum depth of 103 ft (31.3 m) and a mean depth of 32 ft (9.72 m).
- **Catchment:** 9,259 acres (3,760 ha). The [[River Hodder]] is the largest inflow, draining 3,980 acres (1,611 ha), about 43 per cent of the catchment.
- **Request:** The [[Fylde Water Board]] was asked by farmers to permit basic slag and possibly compound fertilizers in addition to the lime already allowed.
- **Water chemistry:** Stocks water is richer in total inorganic ions than any [[English Lake District]] lake, owing to [[Carboniferous Limestone]] bands in the basin (see Table I comparing Stocks with [[Windermere]] South Basin, March 1955). Stocks had Ca 10.1 vs 6.3 mg/l, HCO3 as CaCO3 10.1 vs 8.6 mg/l, SiO2 3.5 vs 2.2 mg/l, and PO4-P 0.003 vs 0.002 mg/l.
- **Organism:** Asterionella formosa is a diatom whose dry weight is over 40 per cent silica. It is a well-known waterworks pest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Algal contrast:** Lakes such as Windermere, Bassenthwaite and Esthwaite have large algal crops, while Thirlmere, Haweswater, Ennerdale and Wastwater show no marked chemical difference yet remain unfiltered for algae.
- **Land use:** The author links algal differences to [[Agriculture]], sewage disposal, deep fertile soils and lowland position. High rainfall in the north and west increases leaching.
- **Stream fertility:** Moorland headwaters on [[Millstone Grit]] were infertile. Passage through [[Limestone Belt]] waters made them fertile for most of the year. Fertility was higher in winter and early spring than in late spring and summer. The October upper reaches were more fertile after the first autumn rains.
- **Afforestation:** Planting started in 1949 on nearly one-fifth of the gathering ground. Filter clogging began in 1950 and was marked in 1951. The author notes a correlation but no proven causation.
- **Soil extracts:** An alkaline extract of [[Windermere Mud]] stimulated Asterionella growth strongly. Peat-derived extracts were less effective.

## Key Findings

- **Phosphorus sensitivity:** Asterionella growth in lake water needs only about 1 µg PO4-P/l, while culture solution needs more than 1 mg/l. The author therefore warns that additional phosphorus could cause much heavier growths and other algal proliferation.
- **Bioassay method:** Direct culture was not feasible because sterilization harmed growth. Filtration reduced fertility. The chosen method used unfiltered water, heating to 40–45 °C for one hour to kill competing algae, Pyrex bottles, and inoculation below 50 cells/ml.
- **Moorland plots (Greet):** Lime and basic slag raised drainage fertility briefly. The compound fertilizer plot stayed acid and infertile. Lime and slag effects were transient, and the plot looked the same as others after one year.
- **Neglected grassland (Hollins):** Before fertilization, mixtures of one part drainage water to four parts Hodder water were infertile. Basic slag increased dilution tolerance more than lime or compound fertilizer. The effects were unclear and variable.
- **Chemical additions:** Calcium carbonate plus potassium dihydrogen phosphate produced the largest growths. Potassium was not limiting, and nitrogen (calcium nitrate) was unimportant. The main risk from compound fertilizers was superphosphate.
- **Conclusions:** Liming is unlikely to have detectable effects in the Limestone Belt. Basic slag is the main danger, especially if applied frequently or in large quantity. Its effects on the soil are expected to be small and short-lived.
- **Recommendation:** Permit basic slag on 200 acres (81 ha) per year, about 2 per cent of the catchment and 16 per cent of farmland concerned. Do not apply it during the Asterionella multiplication period. The Freshwater Biological Association will continue routine observations.
- **Addendum:** After the first basic slag additions, no large algal crop developed, and the filter clogging index was as low as in the years before 1950.
