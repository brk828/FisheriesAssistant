# Native Fish Conservation Project at Phoenix Zoo

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Poeciliidae #conservation #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

The Native Fish Project at [[Phoenix Zoo]] established a dual-purpose conservation facility in summer 1996 to provide long-term refugium and head-start capabilities for endangered southwestern native fish species. Through multi-agency collaboration, zoo staff successfully converted existing lake habitat to house approximately 5,000 [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), 200 [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and 1,000 [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*). The project involved complete removal of exotic game fish, installation of a fish excluder device, and establishment of public education stations, creating a functional conservation breeding facility that allows fish to grow to reintroduction size while maintaining natural predator avoidance behaviors.

## Background & Description

The [[Native Fish Project]] represented a collaborative conservation effort involving multiple organizations: [[Phoenix Zoo]] living collections staff, [[AZA Freshwater Fishes Advisory Group]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS), [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[Arizona State University]] scientists, Phoenix Parks Department managers, and [[Salt River Project]].

The three target species are all endangered native fish of the southwestern United States:
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) - Family Cyprinidae
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) - Family Catostomidae  
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*) - Family Poeciliidae

The project utilized existing zoo infrastructure, converting one of several lakes and an adjoining pond into suitable habitat for these endangered species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The project addressed the critical need for refugia and head-start facilities for endangered southwestern native fish species threatened by exotic game fish predation in their natural habitats. The facility design incorporated several ecological considerations:

**Habitat Preparation**: The lake underwent complete drainage in early June 1996, followed by a 30-day drying period. Remaining pools were treated with toxins three times to eliminate all exotic fish life stages (adults, juveniles, larvae, eggs). Exotic species removed included [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], sunfish, and catfish, captured using hook and line, electroshock, and drift nets.

**Water Source**: The lake was refilled using filtered irrigation water from mountain watershed runoff northeast of Phoenix. Refilling required approximately two weeks.

**Fish Excluder System**: Engineers from [[Salt River Project]] and biologists from [[Arizona State University]] collaborated with zoo personnel to design a concrete trough filled with crushed rock installed in the supply canal. This suspended gravel bed physically impedes fish, larvae, crayfish, and debris from entering zoo waters. Daily maintenance requires only 5 minutes to remove trapped debris and exotic organisms. Local [[Grackle]] populations exploit this system as an opportunistic feeding site.

**Natural Predator Retention**: Natural predators including ducks, herons, and turtles were intentionally maintained in the lake to ensure captive fish retain predator avoidance behaviors necessary for survival upon reintroduction to wild habitats.

**Water Conditioning**: Lake water was "cured" for several weeks before fish introduction to allow residual toxin dissipation and [[Zooplankton]] reestablishment.

## Key Findings

**Stocking Success**: On 13 November 1996, the facility was successfully stocked with:
- Approximately 5,000 razorback suckers in the main lake
- 200 bonytail chub in the main lake
- 1,000 gila topminnows in the adjoining pond

**Survival Rates**: Mortality has been minimal following introduction.

**Dual Conservation Function**: The facility serves both as:
1. Long-term refugium for endangered species populations
2. Head-start facility allowing fish to grow to sufficient size for reintroduction without exotic predator pressure

**Reintroduction Potential**: Upon reaching sufficient size, fish will be reintroduced into native lake and stream habitats throughout the southwestern United States.

**Public Education Component**: Permanent interpretive stations were installed around the lake featuring:
- Natural history information on endangered southwestern fish
- Life-size cement sculptures of adult fish for tactile and visual education
- Explanations of conservation challenges

**Expanded Conservation Portfolio**: Combined with [[Desert Pupfish]] maintained in other zoo ponds, [[Phoenix Zoo]] now provides refugia for four endangered fish species.

**Public Reception**: Temporary interpretive signage during the lake drainage phase proved critical for visitor understanding. Despite odors from decaying organic debris, zoo visitors demonstrated strong understanding and support for the conservation project.
