# Evaluating the Use of Sloped Gravel-Pit Ponds by Listed and Non-Listed Native Fishes and Removal of Nonnative Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Near Grand Junction, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 2002 final report by [[Bob Burdick]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Colorado River Fishery Project) describes a pilot study, funded by the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin, of whether sloped gravel-pit ponds on the floodplain can provide seasonal habitat for [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and whether nonnative fish can be removed from them during runoff. Three off-channel ponds were studied: [[Gardner Pond]], [[Pickup Pond]] and [[Jarvis Pond]]. Nonnative fishes dominated all catches, and the native endangered fish use of these ponds was limited. Mechanical removal did not produce a measurable, lasting depletion of nonnative populations, and nonnative numbers rebounded, particularly at Gardner Pond. The report concludes that restoration of these sites may chiefly benefit nonnative fishes unless the ponds can be designed to drain fully after runoff.

## Background & Description

The upper Colorado River floodplain has lost much of its natural inundation because of dikes, rip-rap, transmountain and irrigation diversions, and mainstem dams. Flooded bottomlands are believed to have served as nursery and spawning habitat for [[Razorback Sucker]] and as feeding habitat for adult [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and their loss is thought to contribute to poor recruitment. Gravel-pit ponds are artificial, typically isolated water bodies that make up a large share of floodplain pond habitat in the corridor. Mitchell (1995) identified 243 ponds within one mile of the river between Palisade and Loma, totaling about 713 surface acres (289 ha).

The study had five objectives: (1) determine abundance and age composition of sub-adult and adult Colorado Pikeminnow and other natives in the ponds; (2) determine species composition and abundance of nonnative fishes using connected ponds; (3) determine whether nonnative removal during runoff is effective and feasible, and whether it can scale to a river reach; (4) document species, numbers and biomass removed; and (5) recommend long-term strategies. Objective 3b could not be determined because no reach-scale assessment was made, and biomass estimates were not provided because individual weights were not consistently recorded in the field.

The study sites lie in the 15-mile reach, which is designated critical habitat for both [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]. Gardner Pond (about 3 ha, mean depth about 1.7 m, maximum 4.6 m) is owned by the Colorado Department of Natural Resources. Pickup Pond (1.6 ha, mean depth about 1.5 m) is owned by Mesa County. Jarvis Pond, on the Jarvis Restoration Site owned by the City of Grand Junction, has a potential wetted area of about 4 ha.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nonnative fish pressure.** At least 67 nonnative fishes have been introduced into the Colorado River system in the last 100 years, and more than 50 were actively introduced by 1980. Warmwater gamefish such as [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], bluegill, black crappie, channel catfish and northern pike are thought to have the greatest adverse effects on endangered natives through predation, competition and habitat displacement. Recruitment failure of [[Razorback Sucker]] is attributed largely to predation on early life stages, and juveniles are nearly absent from the upper basin.

**Hydrology.** Peak flows during 1998-2000 did not exceed 15,000 cfs in the 15-mile reach. Surface connection between the river and Jarvis Pond began at about 5,500 cfs. The mean daily flow of 15,000 cfs was equalled or exceeded on 16.7% of days from 1970-2000 during the runoff period, and 20,000 cfs on 7.4% of days. Flows of at least 15,000 cfs for 21 consecutive days occurred on average in 7 of 31 years, and 20,000 cfs for 21 days in 3 of 31 years. Under present management, flows that equal or exceed 20,000 cfs occur less often than before water development, and the mean peak flow fell from roughly 29,000 cfs to about 16,500 cfs.

**Pond hydrology.** Gardner Pond held water year-round and did not drain naturally. Its eastern depression (about 0.8 ha) stayed wet most of the year because of a high water table and groundwater seepage from irrigation. Jarvis Pond's bottom sits perched well above the river bed, so its use depends on high runoff.

**Sampling methods.** Gear included trap nets (small and large), trammel nets (2.54- and 3.8-cm bar mesh), boat-boom and barge electrofishing, and seines (3.2-mm mesh). Trap nets were the dominant gear. Colorado Pikeminnow were PIT-tagged on first capture.

## Key Findings

**Study chronology and effort**
- 1996: Gardner Pond had been connected to the river but left as a depression; a temporary earthen berm was placed in the connection channel in September 1996.
- 1997: The pond was drained (March) and fish were removed; it was drained again in late 1997, back-filled, sloped toward the river, and reconnected in March 1998.
- 1998: Sampling at Jarvis was not conducted because the contract and on-site work were delayed, so Pickup Pond substituted. Trap nets fished 2,617.8 h during runoff at Gardner and Pickup ponds combined.
- 1999: Trap nets fished 4,333.8 h at Gardner and 491 h at Jarvis. Trammel and boat-boom electrofishing were not used because water was too shallow.
- 2000: Gardner Pond was sampled over 10 weeks (10 May to 18 July), and trap nets fished 4,697.5 h; Jarvis was sampled only three days with seines and barge electrofishing.

**Colorado Pikeminnow**
- 1998: Eleven Colorado Pikeminnow were captured in Gardner Pond in 1999 (1 sub-adult, 10 adults; mean TL 628 mm, range 434-872 mm), all previously captured. One adult documented movement between the Green and Colorado subbasins.
- 2000: Three adult Colorado Pikeminnow (mean TL 670 mm, range 616-765 mm) were captured, all at Gardner Pond.
- No endangered fish were captured at Jarvis Pond in 1999 or 2000.

**Razorback Sucker**
- 2000: Two hatchery-produced razorback suckers were captured in Gardner Pond in early June. One had been stocked in the Gunnison River near Delta and had moved about 57 miles downstream to the Colorado confluence before entering the pond. The other, stocked near Parachute, traveled about 47 miles and passed three dams.

**Nonnative composition**
- Nonnative fish made up 69.3% of catches in Gardner Pond in 1998 and 93% in 1999 (in 2000, 98.2%). Nonnatives were 85.3% in Jarvis Pond in 1999 and 99.5% in 2000.
- Native fish captured in Gardner Pond fell from 218 in 1998 to 413 in 1999, but their relative share dropped (30.8% to 6.5%). Only three natives were caught at Jarvis in 2000.
- In 1998, common carp was most abundant at Pickup Pond (22.5%), and white sucker was most abundant at Gardner Pond (18.3%).
- Green sunfish increased sharply at Gardner Pond: about 60 times the 1998 catch in 1999 and about 47 times in 2000 (3,015 fish in 2000, 47.7% of catch).
- Largemouth bass produced a strong young-of-year cohort in 2000 (340 measured; 93% of the bass collected that year were YOY), indicating successful spawning.

**Mechanical removal**
- A total of 12,760 nonnative fish were removed from Gardner Pond in 1998-2000 (green sunfish 54.6%, black bullhead 18.2%, common carp 4.6%, largemouth bass 3.7%, white sucker 3.3%). Jarvis Pond yielded 1,504 nonnatives in 1999-2000, and Pickup Pond 878 in 1998.
- Catch rates of green sunfish in trap nets rose from 0.13 fish/h (1996) to 0.9 (1999) and 0.61 (2000). Electrofishing catch rates for largemouth bass rose from 6.9 fish/h in 1996 to 247.2 in 2000.
- The authors concluded that removal did not produce a measurable, lasting depletion; treated areas were rapidly recolonized, a pattern also found in other Upper Colorado projects.
- Martinez (2001) rotenone-treated 54 ponds in 1997-2001; 31 (57%) were reinvaded, with green sunfish in 22 of them.

**Jarvis Pond use and flow**
- Hydrologic surface connection with the river lasted 29 days in 1999 and 38 days in 2000, and flows were insufficient to draw endangered fish into the site.

**Conclusions**
1. Gardner Pond continues to supply suitable year-round habitat for [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]], which persist and reproduce.
2. Sloped pond design did not eliminate year-round nonnative habitat. Gardner Pond still does not drain properly because it is not correctly sloped.
3. Jarvis Pond may be used by endangered and non-listed native fish only in above-average or wet years with flows of at least 20,000 cfs lasting 7 to 14 days.
4. It remains uncertain whether sloped gravel-pit ponds benefit native endangered fish more than nonnative fish. The net benefit to listed natives may be negative.
5. Recovery of some listed natives may have to occur alongside high densities of nonnative fish.

**Recommendations**
- Gardner Pond (in order of feasibility): create an active river flow-through to fill depressions; use controlled flow-through; or erect a set-back berm to maintain part of the pond with reduced surface area. Backfilling was estimated at $230,000-$520,000 and was rejected by the Management Committee. A sediment-culvert proposal estimated at $40,000 was not funded.
- Jarvis Pond: evaluate fish use during the next spring high-flow runoff (greater than 15,000 cfs) before exploring other options. Alternatives are lowering the pond bottom (which would require draining and re-excavation) or providing spring flows of about 20,000 cfs for 7 days.
- Restoration sites should be designed to drain fully after runoff, because connected sites that cannot drain may chiefly benefit nonnatives.
