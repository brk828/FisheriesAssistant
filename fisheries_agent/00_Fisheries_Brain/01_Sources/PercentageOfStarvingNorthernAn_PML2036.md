# Percentage of Starving Northern Anchovy Engraulis mordax Larvae in the Sea as Estimated by Histological Methods

#ichthyology #marine-biology #engraulidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 *Fishery Bulletin* study by C. P. O'Connell (NOAA, Southwest Fisheries Center, La Jolla) estimates the proportion of starving [[Northern Anchovy]] larvae in the Southern California Bight using [[Histological Analysis]] of larvae collected in March 1977. Of 318 larvae sectioned from 64 net tows, 26 were classified as severely or moderately emaciated and 11 as incipiently emaciated. The overall estimate was 8% emaciated across the whole cruise. The author proposes that the proportion of starving larvae could serve as an independent, direct index of mortality and, potentially, of [[Year Class Strength]] for this pelagic fishery species.

## Background & Description

Predicting year-class strength in pelagic fish is a central goal of fishery science. Most approaches estimate larval mortality from egg and larval abundance surveys, which are costly and slow to process. The study explores an alternative: measuring the proportion of larvae showing symptoms of starvation.

Earlier starvation criteria (condition factor, chemical indices, morphometrics) were mostly developed under laboratory conditions. The histological approach differs because it relies on qualitative changes in cell and tissue character rather than quantitative measurements. Criteria developed earlier by O'Connell (1976) for laboratory-starved [[Northern Anchovy]] larvae served as the main guide for classifying ocean-caught specimens.

Key sampling and analytical methods included:
- **Sampling:** 64 net tows over 12 days from the NOAA ship *David Starr Jordan*, using a 1 m plankton net with a Plexiglas cod-end bucket and 0.505 mm mesh sieve, fixed in [[Bouin's Fluid]]. Mean tow duration was 3 min (SD 40 s), with the net fished to 20 m.
- **Subsampling:** 318 larvae were selected at random for serial sagittal sectioning, stained with [[Hematoxylin and Eosin]]-phloxine B, and examined blind.
- **Classification:** Larvae were scored as healthy, incipient, moderate, or severe emaciation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tissue indicators of starvation.** Starvation in laboratory larvae causes cellular dissociation in the pancreas, separation and hyalinization of trunk muscle fibers, and notochord shrinkage. In ocean-caught larvae (2.5–10 mm SL), similar trunk-muscle and notochord anomalies occurred, along with more striking irregularities in the foregut and midgut (thinning or collapse of midgut lumen, necrotic debris, fragmenting mucosa). Midgut anomalies were interpreted as indicative of starvation rather than disease, since no parasites or pathogens were found and the larvae contained supranuclear inclusion bodies showing prior feeding.

**Alternative causes considered.** Capture myopathy was judged an unlikely cause because capture time was minimized. Parasitic nematodes, protozoans, and viral diseases were considered but rejected. Sewage outfalls near Newport Beach were noted as another possible stressor, though discharges were described as rapidly diluted and likely harmless.

**Spatial and environmental patterns.**
- More than half of the tows covered a large offshore area with low larval abundance; six tows there each contained a single emaciated larva.
- The remaining tows fell within a few hundred square miles off Newport Beach, where larval abundance was high. Four tows there each contained several emaciated larvae, indicating a patchy distribution.
- Surface temperatures were variable (approximately 12–15 °C) and plankton volumes were generally lower in the high-emaciation tows. Short-term temperature shifts (e.g., tows 15 and 20 differing by 1.5 °C over 24 h) suggested water mass instability.
- Plankton volume increased from about 21 March onward, potentially replacing water with low food levels.

## Key Findings

- **Starvation estimate:** About 8% of northern anchovy larvae were starving during the March 1977 survey (26 moderately or severely emaciated of 318 sectioned; 11 more incipient).
- **Patchiness:** Emaciated larvae were clustered in four tows off Newport Beach, where 60% of larvae in those tows were emaciated. The rate fell to 12% across a few dozen tows over roughly 200 mi² and to 10% with inshore tows included. Offshore pooled samples showed 3%, and the whole-cruise pool 8%.
- **Size distribution:** All emaciated larvae were under 10 mm SL. The absence of emaciated larvae above 10 mm may partly reflect increasing lipid reserves with growth.
- **Day–night differences:** Night catches of the inshore set showed higher emaciation rates than day catches, but the author judged these differences probably reflected sampling variation given the contagious distribution.
- **Visual screening:** Emaciated larvae in patches could be recognized by eye (lumpy trunks, angular body bends, misshapen heads), suggesting that stereomicroscope scanning of tow catches could identify patches before histological verification.
- **Mortality implications:** Using [[Zweifel and Smith]] mortality estimates, the author calculated that if all larvae with symptoms die of starvation, starvation could account for about 8% daily mortality, roughly 40% of the average total daily mortality (27%) for 7.5 mm larvae in the San Pedro Channel area.
- **Recruitment forecast:** Mild winter and early spring conditions and above-average larval growth suggested good recruitment for 1977, but the 1977 year class was estimated as moderate in size. The author stressed that the starvation index needs several years of correlation with recruitment before it can be validated.
- **Recommended survey strategy:** Future work should stratify sampling by population distribution, sample multiple times and areas of high abundance (especially larvae under 10 mm SL), and use stereomicroscope screening to locate patches, reserving histology for verification.
