# The Status of Legalized Gigging and Snagging in Kentucky

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #fisheries-management #rough-fish #centrarchidae #percidae #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1950s-era fisheries management study by Bernard T. Carter (Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources) evaluated the impact of legalizing **gigging** (spearfishing) and **snagging** (snag-line fishing) as harvest methods for "rough fish" in Kentucky streams. An experimental study on [[Stoner Creek]] (Bourbon County, KY) in spring 1949 demonstrated that rough fish comprised 95.9% of the harvest by weight, prompting the state to legalize an annual open gigging/snagging season (March–May). Subsequent statewide creel censuses (1951–1953) sampling 9,278 fishermen and 29,203.6 lbs of fish confirmed that rough fish consistently made up 95.9–99.2% of harvest weight, with game and pan fish never exceeding 1.6% by weight in any census year. The study concluded that gigging/snagging is a low-impact, supplementary management tool for exerting harvest pressure on under-fished rough fish populations, though select high-value game fish streams (e.g., muskie and smallmouth bass streams) required closure to protect vulnerable populations.

## Background & Description

This paper is a fisheries management/harvest-method study rather than a morphological or taxonomic description. No individual morphometric data (length, weight-at-age, meristics) are provided for specific fish species. However, the paper establishes a critical **functional taxonomic classification** used throughout the harvest data:

- **Game and Pan Fish** — Defined as members of four families:
  - [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes, includes bass)
  - [[Percidae]] (perches, includes darters/walleye)
  - [[Serranidae]] (temperate basses, e.g., white bass)
  - [[Esocidae]] (pikes, includes muskellunge)
- **Rough Fish** — Defined operationally as *all other fish species* not belonging to the above four families (includes suckers and other non-game taxa).

Average individual fish weight across census years ranged from **0.98–1.13 lbs** (1949: 0.98 lb; 1951: 1.05 lb; 1952: 1.13 lb; 1953: 1.09 lb), reflecting the size class of fish vulnerable to gigging/snagging gear rather than species-specific biology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study Habitat**: [[Stoner Creek]], a stream near Paris, Kentucky, characterized as a productive smallmouth bass stream with a substantial co-occurring population of suckers and other rough fish — selected specifically to test harvest selectivity in a stream with high game fish density.
- **Regulatory Habitat Context**: Kentucky law prohibited commercial rough fish harvest (via traps, nets, seines) in all streams not under lock and dam, restricting commercial gear to major navigable waters: the [[Ohio River]], [[Mississippi River]], [[Kentucky River]], and lower reaches of the [[Barren River]], [[Green River]], and [[Cumberland River]]. This regulatory gap left most of Kentucky's smaller streams with essentially unexploited rough fish populations, creating the management rationale for legalized gigging/snagging.
- **Seasonal Timing**: Gigging/snagging season ran March–May, timed to close just prior to bass spawning season, and coincided with rough fish spawning runs and shoaling behavior (particularly relevant to snagging technique, which targets fish in swift water during these periods).
- **Protected/Closed Streams** (due to high game fish value):
  - [[Dix River]] — critical spawning run habitat for white bass migrating from [[Herrington Lake]]
  - [[Tygart Creek]], [[Kinniconick Creek]], [[Tripplett Creek]], [[Red River (Kentucky)]] — considered the state's best muskellunge (Esocidae) streams
  - [[Elkhorn Creek]] — one of Kentucky's best smallmouth bass streams
  - [[Harrod's Creek]] — near metropolitan Louisville, likely reflecting both ecological and public-use sensitivity

## Key Findings

1. **1949 Experimental Study (Stoner Creek)**: Of 862.4 lbs of fish gigged over a 10-mile study section (March 15–May 10), 827.0 lbs (95.9%) were rough fish and only 35.4 lbs (4.1%) were game/pan fish — even in a stream known for strong bass populations.

2. **Statewide Census Validation (1951–1953)**: Across 9,278 fishermen contacted and 29,203.6 lbs harvested, rough fish averaged **98.9%** of total harvest weight; game and pan fish never exceeded **1.6%** of total weight in any single census year (1951: 0.8%; 1952: 4.1%; 1953: 1.6%).

3. **Gear-Specific Catch Rates**: Gigging consistently outperformed snagging in fish-per-hour (e.g., 1953: 1.3 vs. 0.5 fish/hr) and fish-per-man (1953: 3.1 vs. 2.5), though snagging required longer average time fished per outing.

4. **Comparative Stream Production Study**: A comparison across seven Kentucky streams (Fleming, Drakes, Whippoorwill, North Fork, Fishing, Beaver, and Slate Creeks) found that **rough fish comprised 80.6%** of total stream biomass production by weight on average (range 61.2–93.3%), while gigging/snagging harvest was even more skewed toward rough fish (98.9% rough fish vs. 1.1% game/pan) — demonstrating that the method selectively targets rough fish beyond their already-dominant natural biomass proportion.

5. **Management Conclusion**: Gigging and snagging, while less efficient than standard commercial gear (traps/nets/seines), was validated as a **low-risk harvest method** for exerting fishing pressure on previously unexploited rough fish populations in Kentucky's smaller streams, providing additional recreational opportunity and food resources without materially harming game and pan fish stocks — except in specific high-value streams requiring targeted closures.

6. **Water Conditions as Confounding Variable**: High water conditions in 1952 substantially reduced the number of fishermen contacted (2,117 giggers vs. 3,422 in 1951) without altering the fundamental rough-fish-dominant harvest ratios, suggesting the biological pattern is robust to sampling/environmental variation.
