## Executive Summary
This report documents a rapid-response, multi-agency investigation into a reported sighting of non-native fish in [[Quitobaquito Pond]], a critically important, isolated desert spring habitat within [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]] that supports an endangered endemic subspecies, the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus*). Following a visitor report of 40–50 unidentified fish (4.5"–6" long) on April 12, 1995, National Park Service (NPS) staff, in coordination with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) and [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), conducted a series of escalating surveys between April 25 and May 2, 1995. Despite repeated visual observations suggestive of a benthic, catfish-like non-native fish, no such fish were ever captured. Intensive sampling using hoop nets, gill nets, angling, seining, and snorkeling confirmed the continued abundance and apparent health of the native desert pupfish population, with no recorded mortality attributable to survey activities. The monument adopted a precautionary, phased monitoring strategy pending further sampling and potential Endangered Species Act Section 7 consultation.

## Background & Description
**Suspected Non-Native Fish (Unconfirmed Species):**
- Estimated total length: 4.5" to 6" (approx. 11–15 cm)
- Body coloration: dark-bodied
- Body form: described on one occasion as having a "catfish-like head and body"
- Behavior: secretive, benthic (bottom-dwelling), observed deep in the pond partially obscured by aquatic vegetation
- No specimens were captured; identification remains unconfirmed at species or even family level, though field biologists strongly suspected [[Ictaluridae]] (catfish/bullhead) based on morphology and behavior.

**Native Desert Pupfish (Confirmed Present):**
- *Cyprinodon macularius eremus*, a federally listed subspecies endemic to the Quitobaquito/Rio Sonoyta system
- Observed in large numbers ("abundant," "numerous," "several hundred" captured in a single fine-meshed hoop net during one sampling event)
- No morphological description beyond confirming species identity; population appeared robust and unaffected by survey disturbance

**Co-occurring Native Species:**
- Sonoran mud turtle (*Kinosternon sonoriensis*), 8–10 individuals observed during initial inspections, with none captured but observed near dawn (~0500) on May 2.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
[[Quitobaquito Pond]] is a spring-fed, isolated desert aquatic oasis within the Sonoran Desert, forming part of the greater [[Rio Sonoyta]] watershed. It functions as critical habitat for the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] and the [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]], both adapted to a small, thermally and hydrologically unique refugial habitat with dense aquatic vegetation and a "Turtle Island" feature separated from the mainland by a moat/channel.

The pond's small size and isolation make it highly vulnerable to non-native fish introductions, which could pose severe predation and competition pressure on the pupfish population. Species raised as potential threats included [[Tilapia]], [[Black Bass]], [[Sunfish]] (Centrarchidae), and [[Catfish]]/Bullhead (Ictaluridae) — all known invasive threats to desert fish refugia in the American Southwest. The suspected intruder's secretive, benthic behavior and morphology were most consistent with an ictalurid catfish or bullhead, a group known to prey on small fish and eggs, posing a direct threat to pupfish recruitment and survival.

The dense aquatic vegetation in the pond provided cover that complicated visual confirmation and capture efforts, highlighting the structural complexity of the habitat and the challenges of monitoring cryptic non-native species in small refugial systems.

## Key Findings
- **Initial Report:** A visitor (Dr. Robin Silver) observed 40–50 unidentified fish (4.5"–6") in Quitobaquito Pond on April 12, 1995, but the significance was not recognized until relayed to AGFD and USFWS personnel.
- **Early Inspections (April 25):** Foot survey of pond perimeter, channel, and springs found no non-native fish; desert pupfish abundant; 8–10 Sonoran mud turtles observed.
- **Boat Survey (April 26 AM):** Three brief visual observations of a single, likely non-native, dark-bodied fish (4"–6") deep in the pond; one sighting suggested a catfish-like head/body. No fish captured via dipnet.
- **Evening Survey (April 26 PM):** Combined methods (boat patrol, night lighting, angling with ham/sardines, snorkeling, seining) yielded no non-native fish observations; no pupfish mortality.
- **Multi-Agency Coordination (April 27–30):** NPS, USFWS, and AGFD coordinated an intensive sampling response; informal Section 7 consultation under the [[Endangered Species Act]] was initiated; National Biological Service (Pete Bennett) consulted for advice.
- **Intensive Sampling Event (May 1–2):** Conducted overnight (~1700–0930) using four baited hoop-net traps (sardines/pineapple), two ~20-ft gill nets, angling with nightcrawlers, and high-powered lighting.
  - **Result:** No non-native fish caught or observed.
  - Several hundred desert pupfish captured in a fine-mesh hoop net; all released successfully with no mortality.
  - No mud turtles captured; none observed until ~0500 on May 2.
- **Management Strategy Adopted:**
  - Continue periodic re-sampling of Quitobaquito Pond using similar methods plus modified minnow traps designed to admit catfish.
  - All desert pupfish mortality during monitoring to be reported to USFWS and AGFD; sampling to cease if incidental take limits are approached.
  - If non-native fish are confirmed, a formal multi-agency biologist meeting (NPS, USFWS, AGFD, NBS) will be convened to determine further management action.
- **Overall Conclusion:** Despite multiple credible visual sightings suggestive of an introduced catfish or bullhead, no physical specimen was ever captured or confirmed during this survey period. The desert pupfish population remained abundant and apparently unharmed throughout all survey activities.
