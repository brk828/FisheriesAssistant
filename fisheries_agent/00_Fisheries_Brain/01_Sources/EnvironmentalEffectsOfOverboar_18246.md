# Environmental Effects of Overboard Spoil Disposal

#marine-biology #estuarine-sediments #dredging

## Executive Summary

This 1968 interim report by Robert B. Biggs, of the [[Chesapeake Biological Laboratory]] (University of Maryland), studies the physical, chemical and sediment effects of disposing of dredged spoil overboard in the upper [[Chesapeake Bay]]. The work supports the enlargement of the [[Chesapeake and Delaware Canal]] approaches. The document addresses water physics, sediments and nutrients, not fish or other organisms. No species are named, so the species fields are N/A.

The study was a phase 2 test of shallow-water disposal of about 1.1 x 10^6 cu m of silt and clay. The main results were:

- Turbidity plumes covered roughly 4 to 5 sq km around the discharge point.
- Spoil spread across the bottom to cover an area at least five times the designated disposal area.
- Total phosphate and total nitrogen rose by a factor of 50 to 100 over ambient levels near the discharge.

The author concludes that the spoil is likely to be resuspended and transported, creating possible maintenance dredging problems.

## Background & Description

**Project and disposal plan.** The [[Philadelphia District]] of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers favoured overboard shallow-water disposal (about 3 m to 6 m depth) as the most economical option. The State of Maryland argued that land or deep-water disposal would better protect regional natural resources. Negotiations between Maryland, the Philadelphia District, the Corps' Committee on Tidal Hydraulics and the U.S. Department of the Interior produced a three-part plan:

1. Deep-water disposal of about 3 x 10^6 cu m from the lower leg of the work.
2. Shallow-water disposal of about 1.1 x 10^6 cu m from the upper leg, with geological and biological studies of effects.
3. Disposal of about 3.3 x 10^6 cu m from the middle leg, using a method based on the phase 2 observations.

The area to be dredged is estimated at about 7.4 x 10^6 cu m of silt and clay. The abstract gives a different figure, about 4.5 x 10^6 m^3 across a 14 km reach, and both figures are reported here as published.

**Study aims.** The study measured the distribution and fate of spoil, the physical and chemical effects of overboard disposal, and the natural environmental framework needed to interpret biological studies.

**Sampling design.** Stations were set on five transects between Turkey Point and Tolchester, from the Aberdeen Proving Grounds restricted zone to the 3 m depth contour. Additional stations were placed off Havre de Grace, off Betterton in the Sassafras River and at Love Point. Each station was sampled every 3 m from surface to bottom, on a bi-weekly schedule to capture seasonal and short-term variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**[[Salinity]] and [[Water Temperature]].** The [[Susquehanna River]] supplies over 90% of the freshwater entering the upper Bay and strongly controls salinity. Salinity was lowest from mid-May to mid-June and highest from September through January. Water temperature ranged from 1°C (January to early February 1966) to a maximum of 28°C (6 July 1966). A stable high of 26°C to 27°C followed in early September, then autumn cooling set in.

**[[Seston]] and [[Suspended Sediment]].** Seston was defined as the weight per unit volume of particles between 0.2 µm and 60 mm, including mineral grains, organic detritus and living plankton and bacteria. Key points:

- The Susquehanna's supply of new material was usually less than suspended loads in the upper Bay. Its total 1966 contribution was 0.61 x 10^6 metric tons of sediment.
- Most suspended material was judged to come from the bottom, stirred by [[Wind-Wave Resuspension]] and tidal currents. Strong, persistent winds produced high suspended loads across the transects.
- Seston showed a general but inconsistent rise with higher tidal current velocities.
- Shoal material is moved to deeper, lower-energy areas by wind-waves and currents.

**[[Bottom Sediments]].** Six 6-ft cores were taken along the dredge channel and a similar number along the proposed disposal area before dredging. Sediment in the channel was more than 90% silt and clay. The disposal area was about 75% silt and clay. This indicates that wind-wave and current transport, or non-deposition, selectively removed fine material from the disposal area.

## Key Findings

**Dredging and disposal operations.** A 68 cm suction dredge deepened and widened about 3050 m of channel between 7 October and 11 November 1966. It pumped about 1.1 x 10^6 cu m of sediment to create a channel 137 m wide, cut to a plane 10.5 m below the Corps' Chesapeake and Delaware Canal datum. Spoil was released over a designated disposal area west of the channel. The discharge pipe was an elbow configuration, with its outlet 1 m below the surface pointing downward.

**Turbidity plume.** Two cruises tracked the plume using transmissometers and radar reference buoys at 600 m intervals.

- Surface turbidity was low except very close to the discharge.
- Measurable suspended sediment extended as far as 4 km from the disposal point (abstract).
- The turbid area at any one time was about 6% of the surface area between Transects I and III.
- The increase in turbidity was temporary. Natural suspended-sediment concentrations in February through April reached levels as high as or higher than those seen during disposal.

**Spoil spread and burial.** A depth survey was made before disposal and 4 days after.

- The disposal area covered 0.6 sq km, and the affected area was about 3.0 sq km.
- Spoil spread as a semi-liquid across the relatively flat bottom, so the deposit did not stay within the disposal area's limits.
- Maximum measured slopes were 1:100 (a 1 m pile spreads at least 100 m horizontally), and average slopes were 1:500.
- About 3 sq km of bottom was covered to a depth of at least 30 cm.
- If similar methods were used for the rest of the project, burial would affect roughly 10% to 15% (about 14 sq km) of the upper Bay bottom between Transects I and III.

**Nutrient release.** The [[Chesapeake Bay Institute]] analysed spoil samples while pumping, after four hours of settling.

- Total phosphate in pore water was about 2,000 µg-atoms per litre, and total nitrogen about 3,000 µg-atoms per litre.
- Ambient Bay phosphate normally ranges from 1 to 3 µg-atoms per litre, and nitrogen was about 50 µg-atoms per litre in October and November.
- Total phosphate and nitrogen rose by a factor of 50 to 100 over ambient levels in the immediate vicinity of the discharge. The author concludes there was "obvious release of nutrients" to the water. Nutrient enrichment was confined to the immediate disposal vicinity.

**Fate and long-term concerns.** The spoil is finer than the natural shoal sediment and has a lower bulk density (1.2 compared with about 2.0 for the original material), so it is more easily transported. The author judges it unlikely to stay in shallow water. Possible future movement back to the channel could create a chronic maintenance dredging problem, and the amount and timing of this cannot be predicted. Deep-water disposal would significantly reduce the area covered and the likelihood of future movement. Hauling dredged material more than a mile would substantially raise costs, so economics bear on the choice of method.

**Conclusions.** The author concludes that:

1. Overboard disposal increased turbidity over an area of 4 to 5 sq km around the discharge site.
2. Deposited spoil covered an area at least five times the defined disposal area.
3. Total phosphate and nitrogen increased 50- to 100-fold over ambient levels near the discharge point.

Any addition of material at any time of year will raise suspended concentrations above natural levels.
