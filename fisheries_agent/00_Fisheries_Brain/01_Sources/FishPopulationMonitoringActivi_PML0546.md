# Fish Population Monitoring of the Virgin River, Utah, 1985

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report by James E. Deacon (Environmental Consultants, Inc., Las Vegas, Nevada; January 1986) presents fish population monitoring by the [[Woundfin Recovery Team]] in the mainstream [[Virgin River]] below [[La Verkin Springs]] on 18 April and 9 October 1985. It compares 1985 results with data collected from 1977 to 1984 at six standard stations. The data are described as preliminary, with some values missing and most requiring verification.

The central conclusion is that water quality degradation, driven by reduced flow and increased spring discharge, has had an adverse impact on the entire fish fauna of the Utah segment of the Virgin River in 1985. Non-native and generalist species, particularly the [[Red Shiner]], expanded in the lower river, while the endangered [[Woundfin]] declined in the Power Plant/Ash Creek segment.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The Virgin River rises in mountains in and near Zion National Park, Utah, and flows about 200 km to Lake Mead. Much of the river below St. George, Utah, and below Riverside, Nevada, has no surface flow for much of the year. Gradients are steep in the upper river, low between the Washington Diversion and the upper end of the Virgin Narrows, steep again to the [[Mesquite Diversion]], and low below it.

**Sampling network.** The Recovery Team established six stations below La Verkin Springs:
- **Disturbed segments (four stations):** Power Plant/Ash Creek, [[Twin Bridges]], upper Mesquite Diversion, and [[Riverside]]. Disturbance is mainly the removal of large volumes of water for irrigation.
- **Undisturbed segments (two stations):** the canyon about 2 miles below [[Berry Springs]], and near the mouth of [[Beaver Dam Wash]].

**Historical context.** Before 1941, when Lake Mead filled, the Virgin River was a small tributary of the [[Colorado River]]. Agricultural diversion and groundwater use since 1854 have reduced flows throughout the system, and further projects are expected to reduce flows more. Reduced flows and related habitat changes have affected the six fish species native to the Virgin River.

**Sampling method** (from the Woundfin Recovery Plan, 1984):
- Target at least one run, one pool and one riffle per station.
- Sample a roughly uniform 10 m x 3 m habitat segment with a 4.5 m x 1.8 m x 6 m nylon seine.
- Haul repeatedly until catch falls to 10% or less of the most successful haul, with at least three hauls.
- Record depth, velocity and substrate at nine points per segment.
- Measure all fish and release them; stop measuring a species at a station after at least 100 individuals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and physical conditions.** Flow is highly variable, with higher flows generally in winter than in summer, except in very dry years. Daily and seasonal fluctuations in temperature, flow and chemistry are large. The sand bottom is nearly always in motion, and the streambed can shift as flows change. These conditions are typical of desert streams (Cook, 1960; Deacon and Minckley, 1974; Naiman, 1981).

**Sink hole and salinity event (summer 1985).** A diversion structure was installed near the old Hurricane Diversion in 1985 to supply the new [[Quail Creek Reservoir]]. A sink hole then developed in the riverbed above La Verkin Springs. During the lowest summer flows of 1985, the entire river disappeared into the sink hole (per Terry Hickman, personal communication). Spring flow increased, raising salinity throughout the Utah segment. Conductivity (µmhos/cm) changed as follows:

| Location | Pre-sink hole | Post-sink hole |
|---|---|---|
| Above La Verkin/Ash Creek confluence | 5900–6600 | 9400–10500 |
| Below La Verkin/Ash Creek confluence | 1500–1800 | 7800–8000 |
| Below St. George | 2400–? (upper value illegible in scan) | 4500–4800 |

Water temperature also rose, especially between La Verkin Springs and the Ash and La Verkin Creek inflows. On at least one occasion it reached 37 °C.

**Diversions.** Decreased streamflow from the Hurricane and new Quail Creek diversions worsened water quality and low-flow problems, particularly in the Power Plant/Ash Creek segment.

## Key Findings

**1. Power Plant/Ash Creek segment declined sharply in 1985.**
- Average catch per repetitive seine set in fall 1985 was about 5 fish, compared with about 25–100 fish elsewhere in the river.
- Woundfin made up about 20–30% of the population in 1977–78, dropped to about 2–10% in 1979–81, and rose again in 1982–85.
- Reproduction and recruitment were most significant in 1978 and least significant in 1980 and 1985.

**2. Roundtail chub** comprised less than 1% of the population in most years at this station. Its peak was 13.8% in 1978. It was absent here in 1980, 1981 and 1985.

**3. Berry Springs (undisturbed) remained woundfin-dominated.**
- Woundfin exceeded 80% of catches in 1982, 1983 and 1984. The lowest share, 45%, occurred in 1977.
- The best recruitment was in 1983 (average size about 37 mm in late September). The worst was in 1980.
- In October 1985, woundfin averaged about 56 mm and made up about 64% of the catch.
- Roundtail chub were recorded only in 1983 (about 5%) and 1984.

**4. Twin Bridges.** Woundfin were the most abundant species in nearly all collections. Good recruitment occurred in 1978, 1980 and 1983. Red shiner first appeared in spring 1984, was absent in fall 1984 and spring 1985, and made up 25% of the population in fall 1985.

**5. Beaver Dam Wash.**
- Woundfin made up 60–80% of the population until spring 1984, when it fell to 30%. It was only 9% in fall 1985.
- Red shiner became dominant in 1985, reaching 64% in fall 1985 (previous maximum 14% in spring 1984 and 1985).

**6. Mesquite.** Red shiners were always prominent and dominated in 1979, 1980, 1982 and 1984. They reached about 96% of the population in 1985. Woundfin were least evident in 1985, while the best woundfin reproduction occurred in 1978, 1979, 1980 and 1983.

**7. Riverside.** Woundfin dominated in 1977, 1978 and 1981, with best reproduction in 1978, 1983 and 1984. Red shiners made up 90–100% of the population in 1979, 1980, 1983, 1984 and 1985.

**8. Flow and woundfin recruitment.** Figure 3 plots fall woundfin mean size against spring mean flow. All low-flow years (1977, 1981, 1984) were associated with poor recruitment. Only 1978 had intermediate flows, and recruitment that year was excellent throughout the river. Higher flows showed considerable variability. The authors conclude that spring flow is not the only factor; year-round flow and water quality also matter. Mean spring flow data for 1977–85 are in Table 17.

**9. Red shiner expansion.** Red shiners reached their greatest abundance throughout the lower river in 1985 and became firmly established around St. George.

**10. Overall conclusion.** Water quality degradation, linked to low flow and the 1985 sink hole event, appears to have adversely affected all fish habitats in the Utah segment in 1985. The authors note that the poor condition of the lower river fauna could not be attributed to flow alone, and that a cause other than flow was not yet evident.

**Data status.** Tables 1–16 report relative abundance and mean size for 1977–1985. Several values are missing or illegible, and the authors describe all results as preliminary.
