# Striped Bass In-Reservoir Spawning in Lake Powell and Enlarger Photography of Black Crappie Otoliths

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #Centrarchidae #Clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This source contains two papers from the *North American Journal of Fisheries Management*. The main paper (pp. 540–546, 1984) presents evidence that [[Striped Bass]] reproduced inside [[Lake Powell]] (Utah-Arizona) without relying on turbulent river current to keep eggs suspended. Earlier spawning was attributed to fish moving up the [[Colorado River]]. The second paper (pp. 547–551, 1984) is a methods note showing that enlarger-produced photographs of [[Black Crappie]] otoliths can be used for measurement in place of microscope ocular measurements. The second paper is truncated in the source, so its results are incomplete.

## Background & Description

**Striped Bass (*Morone saxatilis*)** is an anadromous species widely introduced into inland waters. About 38 states had established striped bass fisheries, most supported by stocking. Natural reproduction has been reported in several reservoirs, including [[Santee Cooper Reservoir]], [[Kerr Reservoir]], [[Millerton Lake]], [[Keystone Reservoir]], [[Texoma Reservoir]], [[Lake Havasu]], and [[Lake Mead]].

Key reproductive traits summarized from the literature:
- Spawning occurs at 10–24 °C and usually peaks at 15–20 °C. In the [[Tar River]], North Carolina, most spawning occurred at 17.8–20.0 °C.
- Spawning typically takes place in silt-laden rivers with rapids, boulders, and strong currents.
- Fish show homing tendency, returning to the same river area each year.
- Semi-buoyant eggs are broadcast near the surface and stay suspended in current. Incubation lasts 48–71 hours at 15.6–17.8 °C.
- Larvae need turbulence to avoid smothering and can hold position over minor currents by about 4 days of age.

**Lake Powell** is a 300-km-long, warm, monomictic reservoir impounded by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the Colorado River, 9.7 km south of the Utah-Arizona border. It fills a deep, narrow canyon. Key physical features:
- Shoreline: 2,930 km, mostly sandstone cliffs and talus slopes
- Average depth: 50.9 m
- Maximum capacity: 3.32 × 10⁹ m³ (exponent reconstructed from OCR)
- Stratification: April to December
- Surface temperature range: 6.7–29.4 °C
- Visibility: typically 3–9 m; Secchi readings up to 13 m recorded

**Objectives of the main paper:**
1. Describe evidence of in-reservoir spawning, probable spawning habitat, and water physical characteristics at spawning sites.
2. Discuss abundance and distribution of young-of-the-year (y-o-y) striped bass from natural reproduction.
3. Assess management implications of in-reservoir reproduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking and reproduction history.** Striped bass fingerlings were stocked into Lake Powell annually from 1974 through 1979, originally in [[Wahweap Bay]] near the dam. Natural reproduction was first detected in 1979. Fish spawned in the [[Colorado River]] above the reservoir, mainly in [[Cataract Canyon]], a 19-km gorge with 23 major rapids. Fish used less than 20 km of river above the reservoir for spawning.

**Pre-spawn aggregation.** Each spring from 1979 to 1983, mature striped bass gathered near the dam in March, April, and early May. Attraction may have come from the strong current drawn through the dam penstocks. Most fish left the staging area together in early May, when surface water reached 16–19 °C.

**Spawning sites.**
- *Canyon mouth (May 1979):* Floating dead eggs were found at a canyon mouth 8 river km above the dam, on a rocky shoal. This attempt was considered unsuccessful because no young-of-the-year were found near the dam.
- *[[Warm Creek Bay]] (1981–1983):* Ripe adults were collected 14 May 1981 at 19 km above the dam, in 3–6 m of water over sand, cobble, and boulder substrate with 2–3 mm of fine silt. Spawning was directly observed on the night of 22 May 1982 in 3–10 m of water, from about 2230 hours until dawn.

**Water chemistry.** Surface values from [[Hite]] and [[Wahweap]] (May 1981–1982) included temperature 15.5–17.5 °C, pH 8.2–8.6, total dissolved solids 340–518 mg/L, and conductance 570–835 µmhos/cm. Water hardness during the spawning season was about 250 mg/L. A dissolved oxygen profile at Warm Creek Bay on 31 May 1983 found 8.4 mg/L at the surface and 13.2 mg/L at the substrate in 9 m of water. Temperature at the substrate was 16.0 °C.

**Egg settling.** In a no-flow laboratory test (water hardness 120 mg/L), eggs descended 0.3 m in 27.3 s immediately after fertilization. The time increased to 69.0 s at 24 hours. Eggs at Warm Creek were expected to settle onto the substrate in about 10 m of water.

**Overflow density current.** [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] data using total dissolved solids showed that weak 1981 spring flood water moved the density current about 190 km upstream from the dam. Summer interflow then mixed it into the lake, so transport was effectively eliminated at that point. Eggs and larvae from river spawning would not be expected to reach the dam area by this mechanism.

**Ecological interactions.** The strong 1981 year class was followed by a decline in [[Threadfin Shad]] abundance and a decline in striped bass condition factor in 1982 (Gustaveson et al. 1983).

**Crappie otolith methods.** [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) otoliths were examined for ageing and back-calculation. Microprojection was not suitable because the otoliths were too thick. Ocular micrometer measurements were imprecise because centres and annulus margins were hard to relocate and values were rounded to the nearest unit.

## Key Findings

**Main paper (striped bass, Lake Powell):**

1. **Natural reproduction in the reservoir.** Striped bass reproduced in Lake Powell after 1974 stocking. In 1981, y-o-y fish were collected near [[Glen Canyon Dam]], and the authors concluded they were spawned inside the reservoir. Relative abundance near the dam was higher than up-reservoir in a low-inflow year. Egg observations, high substrate oxygen, and spawning-condition adults supported this conclusion.

2. **Distribution of y-o-y.** In 1979 and 1980, when riverine spawning was thought to dominate, larvae were confined to the upper half of the reservoir. In 1981, low inflow and weak density-current transport meant y-o-y were distributed through the reservoir, with higher relative abundance at both ends and lower numbers at midlake. The authors expected midlake numbers to be higher if fish had dispersed from river spawning by current-aided swimming.

3. **Larval capture.** Two larval striped bass (18 mm and 22 mm TL) were captured on 24 and 26 June 1981, 19 river km above the dam and within 3 km of the 1981 and 1982 spawning area. Beach seining caught y-o-y in most major canyons and bays in the first 25 river km above the dam.

4. **Catch rates (1981).** Table 4 reports catch rates by technique. July beach seining ranged from 3.5 to 7.0 fish per haul (for example, 3.5 at Good Hope Bay, 4.5 at Rincon, 7.0 at Wahweap Bay). Other data were partly garbled in the source.

5. **Relative importance of recruitment sources.** The authors state that the relative contributions of lacustrine (in-reservoir) and riverine recruitment remain undetermined.

6. **Management implications.**
   - In-reservoir recruitment may be as important as river-spawned recruitment.
   - Lake Powell's steep, deep, clear shorelines with little littoral zone, plus high substrate oxygen linked to low organic content of desert soils and low nutrients, may favour spawning success. The exact mechanism remains unknown.
   - Other reservoirs with similar water quality and morphology, including some lower Colorado River reservoirs and pump-storage reservoirs, might support natural reproduction even without spawning tributaries.
   - Striped bass populations that are not recruitment-limited may reach prey carrying capacity. Such populations are less likely to meet trophy-fishery criteria, which matters when stocking is used to create trophy fisheries.

**Methods note (black crappie otoliths):**

7. **Enlarger photography.** The abstract states that photographs of black crappie otoliths provide an attractive alternative to ocular-micrometer measurement. Photos are easy to mark, interpret, and store, and give greater precision than microscope measurements.

8. **Sample.** Measurements were taken from 112 black crappie collected in Florida. Total lengths (TL) were recorded to the nearest millimetre. Otoliths were photographed on a dark-field base with an enlarger-based camera setup. Radii and annuli were measured twice on the photos. Measurements from the source's microscope comparison were not fully recovered because the text is truncated.

**Source note:** The crappie paper is truncated, so its final results, statistics, and conclusions are not available. The Lake Powell paper is complete.
