## Executive Summary

This 1957 *Tellus* paper by Eville Gorham (Freshwater Biological Association, Ambleside) presents a chemical survey of eight lowland meres near Ellesmere, Shropshire, England, conducted in November 1954 and June 1955. These meres are glacial kettle-hole lakes situated on Triassic sandstones, waterstones, and marls overlain by glacial drift (boulder clay or sand/gravel). The meres are noted locally for periodically "breaking" — a term for sudden, dense blooms of [[Blue-Green Algae]] (cyanobacteria). The study measured pH, major cations (Na, K, Ca, Mg), major anions (HCO₃, Cl, SO₄), and nutrients (NO₃, PO₄, SiO₂) to characterize water chemistry gradients across the meres and relate them to local geology, hydrology, and the algal bloom phenomenon. No fish populations, fish morphology, or fish-specific ecological data are addressed in this paper; the ecological focus is entirely on water chemistry and [[Phytoplankton]] dynamics.

## Background & Description

Not applicable — this paper contains no fish morphological data. The only biological "morphological" identifications concern phytoplankton taxa observed during sampling:

- **[[Sphaerocystis schroeteri]]** agg. — identified in Newton Mere (June 1955)
- **[[Coelosphaerium naegelianum]]** — identified in Blake Mere (June 1955)
- **[[Oscillatoria redekei]]** — identified in Kettle Mere (June 1955), associated with especially abundant bloom growth

These are colonial/filamentous blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) responsible for the dense "water-bloom" or "breaking" events described for these meres.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Eight lowland meres (Newton, Blake, Kettle, White, Ellesmere, Cole, Crose, Sweat Meres) in the Midland plain of central England, <100 m above sea level, occupying glacial kettle-hole depressions.
- **Substrate/geology:** Triassic Bunter and Keuper sandstones, Keuper waterstones, and Keuper marl, overlain by glacial boulder clay/till (favoring deep, steep-sided basins) or glacial sand and gravel (associated with shallower, marshy-shored meres — Cole, Crose, Sweat).
- **Soils:** Brown earths of low base status (Baschurch series) and gley soils (Salop series, near Ellesmere Mere); peats near Kettle, Cole, Crose, and Sweat Meres. Upper soil horizons are largely non-calcareous and near-neutral to slightly acidic.
- **Hydrology:** Drainage is sluggish; only Cole, Crose, and Sweat Meres have appreciable outflow streams, suggesting more continuous groundwater feeding via the more pervious glacial sand/gravel deposits. Newton, Blake, and Kettle Meres form a compact group with restricted drainage areas, dominated by surface run-off rather than deep percolation.
- **Climate:** Annual rainfall ~70 cm, evaporation ~50 cm (concentration factor ~3.5×); mean monthly temperatures range ~4.5°C (winter) to ~16°C (summer) at nearby Shrewsbury.
- **Trophic/bloom ecology:** All meres are subject to dense [[Cyanobacteria]] blooms ("breaking"), though bloom frequency (documented historically by Phillips 1883 and Griffiths 1925) does not correlate clearly with measured water chemistry. Newton and Kettle Meres (dilute waters) broke frequently; Blake Mere (also dilute) rarely or never broke; Cole Mere (rich in salts) broke annually — indicating that local biological/ecological factors beyond bulk ionic chemistry govern bloom occurrence.
- **Nutrient status:** Phosphate-P is generally low (0.002–0.11 ppm P) except in Newton and Ellesmere Meres (0.3–0.5 ppm P, especially in summer); nitrate-N is low in dilute meres (0.02–0.05 ppm N) but higher in the most concentrated meres (0.14–0.29 ppm N).

## Key Findings

- **Total ionic concentration gradient:** Ranged from 1.06 to 4.77 milliequivalents/L across the eight meres, with Newton, Blake, and Kettle Meres being dilute and Crose and Sweat Meres the most concentrated.
- **Dominant ions:** In all meres except Newton, [[Calcium]] and [[Bicarbonate]] dominate the ionic composition, increasing sharply with total salt concentration (Ca: 0.38–3.68 meq/L; HCO₃: 0.25–3.04 meq/L). In Newton Mere, sodium approximately equals calcium, and chloride and sulfate exceed bicarbonate.
- **Potassium anomaly:** Unlike calcium and bicarbonate, potassium *declines* with increasing total salt concentration (from ~0.2 to ~0.1 meq/L) and is proportionally far more abundant in dilute waters (up to 19% of total cations) compared to average world freshwater (3.4%) or Lake District waters (<4%, per Mackereth 1957). The author hypothesizes this reflects glauconite-derived potassium in Keuper clay deposits, released preferentially where carbonate buffering is low.
- **Seasonal magnesium variation:** Magnesium was the only major ion to vary strongly with season — June values averaged only ~70% of November values, possibly reflecting differential groundwater level/soil horizon drainage and dolomite crystal dissolution in Keuper deposits.
- **Rainwater-derived signature:** Comparison with Lake District rain chemistry shows that dilute meres (especially Newton Mere) retain ionic proportions closer to rainwater (high Na, Cl exceeding SO₄), while richer meres show substantial enrichment via rock/soil weathering — calcium increased up to 227-fold and magnesium up to 44-fold over rainwater levels in Crose Mere.
- **Sodium/chloride ratio:** Closely matches seawater ratio (0.84–0.85), supporting an atmospheric (marine aerosol) origin for Na and Cl in these inland meres, concentrated ~3.5× by evaporation.
- **Geological control on chemistry:** Meres on/near glacial sand and gravel with outflow streams (Cole, Crose, Sweat) show higher mineral enrichment, likely due to greater groundwater influence and longer soil percolation, whereas meres on boulder clay with restricted drainage (Newton, Blake, Kettle) remain dilute due to surface-runoff dominance.
- **No direct link between chemistry and bloom frequency:** Despite general nutrient richness correlating with propensity for blooms, specific historical "breaking" patterns (per Phillips 1883, Griffiths 1925) do not align cleanly with measured chemical gradients, indicating unresolved local ecological drivers of cyanobacterial bloom dynamics — a topic flagged for further [[Limnological Investigation]].
