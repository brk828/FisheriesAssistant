# Hybrid Finfish in Aquaculture and the Challenge of FAO Reporting

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hybrid-fish

## Executive Summary

This article argues that hybrid finfish now make up a substantial and growing share of aquaculture output, although they are poorly captured in [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] (FAO) statistics. It reviews commercially important hybrids, the traits hybridization can deliver, and the unintentional hybridization that occurs in hatcheries and ponds. It then explains why the FAO coding system and questionnaires cannot represent hybrids, and sets out recommendations for more consistent reporting. The authors state that hybridization is not offered as the only route to genetic improvement, but as one method with potential for near-term gains.

## Background & Description

Early hybridization work on [[Salmonid]] species produced few commercially useful hybrids, because growth gains were small and survival was low. This left an impression that hybrids held little interest for aquaculturists. The expansion of farmed species has changed this view. Several hybrids now account for significant production, and more are emerging.

Reproductive barriers that prevent hybridization in nature can be overcome with:

- Induced spawning, including [[Hypophysation]] (pituitary extract to induce ovulation) and synthetic hormones
- In-vitro fertilisation
- Better knowledge of reproductive biology

Hybridization is used to increase growth rate, manipulate sex ratios, produce sterile animals, improve flesh quality, increase disease resistance and improve environmental tolerance. Examples include:

- **[[Tilapia]]:** The cross [[Nile Tilapia]] (*Oreochromis niloticus*) × [[Blue Tilapia]] (*O. aureus*) produces mainly male offspring and limits unwanted reproduction in grow-out ponds. Most tilapia production in Israel is based on this hybrid, but it is reported to FAO as *Oreochromis* spp.
- **[[African Catfish]] × [[Thai Catfish]]:** The Thai National Inland Fisheries Institute states that nearly 80% of Thai catfish aquaculture uses this hybrid, combining the flesh quality of the Thai catfish with the fast growth of the African catfish. Official statistics list it only as *Clarias* spp.
- **[[Striped Bass]] × [[White Bass]]:** In the USA this is the sixth most cultured "fish species," accounting for 1.4% of production. It was only recently added to FAO statistics.
- **[[Tambaqui]] and [[Pacu]]:** Official Venezuelan data attribute 29% of total aquaculture to *Colossoma macropomum*, but government experts believe most of this is a hybrid with *Piaractus brachypoma*.
- **[[Bester Sturgeon]]:** A cross of [[Beluga Sturgeon]] (*Huso huso*) and sterlet (*Acipenser ruthenus*), fully fertile and with good culture characteristics.
- **[[Channel Catfish]] × [[Blue Catfish]]:** Superior growth in ponds and high-density culture, plus disease resistance and low oxygen tolerance.

Hybrids can also exploit degraded habitats. Lakes affected by [[Acid Rain]] may not suit native salmonids, but [[Splake]] (*Salvelinus namaycush* × *S. fontinalis*) tolerates low pH and is fertile and fast-growing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Chromosome-set manipulation can be combined with hybridization to improve viability and developmental stability. The authors define viability as the ability to survive and grow, which does not imply fertility. Key examples include:

- Triploid [[Atlantic Salmon]] × [[Brown Trout]] hybrids are sterile. Their survival and growth matched diploid Atlantic salmon.
- Triploid [[Pacific Salmon]] hybrids show earlier seawater acclimation.
- Rainbow trout × char triploids and rainbow trout × coho salmon triploids showed increased resistance to [[Infectious Hematopoietic Necrosis Virus]], but grew more slowly.
- Most diploid Pacific salmon hybrids are not useful in culture, but gain disease resistance, sterility and early seawater tolerance when made triploid.

Unintentional hybridization occurs through:

- Mixed spawning of different species in one hatchery
- Misidentification of species by hatchery staff
- Contamination of facilities with wild fish

[[Indian Major Carp]] seed is often produced by inducing several species to spawn in a common pond, creating opportunities for accidental crosses. Tilapia ponds connected to natural water bodies with indigenous or feral tilapia are especially prone to hybridization with wild fish. This can degrade cultured stocks and make contaminated broodstock unreliable.

Hybrids in the wider literature include common carp × rohu (sterile), grass carp × bighead carp, silver carp × bighead carp, common carp × mrigal, and common carp × catla. Some are sterile, some fertile, and some show heterosis for growth. Reproductive isolation can also be overcome in sciaenids (black drum × red drum), centrarchids (black × white crappie, green sunfish × bluegill) and esocids (muskellunge × northern pike, walleye × sauger). The Kuwait Institute of Scientific Research has produced a hybrid bream from *Acanthopagrus latus* × *Sparidentex hasta*, which shows good growth and appears fertile.

## Key Findings

**1. Hybrids are commercially important and under-reported.**
- Hybrids are a growing part of aquaculture production, but many are hidden in genus-level or "nei" categories.
- Unclassified groupings (production not reported to species level) made up about 16% of global aquaculture production over the last seven years. In 1995 this was 4,669,000 tonnes.
- Genus-level reporting accounted for 3.6% of total aquaculture production in 1995. Some of this is likely to be hybrids, such as *Clarias* spp. from Thailand.
- Tilapia production in Indonesia is reported as *O. niloticus* and *O. mossambicus*, yet some of it is known to be hybrid.

**2. The FAO system cannot represent hybrids.**
- The FAO taxonomic code has no provision for hybrid nomenclature. Its structure runs from main grouping to order or sub-order, family, genus and species.
- The FAO aquaculture questionnaire asks for species-level data. Hybrids are therefore often reported as genus only, or as "nei."
- The authors note the problem is not unique to FAO. Most international monitoring organisations need more precise data.

**3. Value data also miss hybrid premiums.**
- Hybrids may sell for more where quality traits such as flesh colour or texture are superior, so price should be recorded separately.
- Example: hybrid striped bass × white bass sells for $3/lb ($6.6/kg) in the USA, compared with $2.44/lb for freshwater white bass.

**4. Reporting is inconsistent over time.**
- Some countries, such as Israel, do report hybrids. Reporting is often inconsistent, however.
- Israeli tilapia hybrids were first reported as the full cross (*O. niloticus* × *O. aureus*). After two years this changed to "tilapia hybrids," and by 1995 to "tilapia nei" (*Oreochromis* spp.).
- FIDI (Fisheries Information, Data and Statistics Unit) sends a fisheries questionnaire and an aquaculture questionnaire to each country. Its annual [[Aquaculture Production Statistics]] publication (Fisheries Circular No. 815) is based on species or groupings.

**5. Recommendations for data standardisation.**
- Report hybrid data consistently and standardised, following the parental order convention: female first for known crosses, and sex in parentheses for random crosses.
- Establish that a hybrid will be part of the long-term production system before it is given a separate category.
- Report hybrids consistently, year after year, even at low production levels, so FAO can record them as separate entries.
- Avoid aggregating regional and national data, which reduces clarity for assessing production needs and biodiversity.
- Give data collectors training in technologies that capture hybrid information.
- Where hybrid levels are unknown, countries can attach estimated percentages of production for each hybrid to their questionnaires.

**6. Hybridization is one tool among several.**
- Desirable traits can often be passed to a hybrid in one generation, but outcomes can be unpredictable.
- Fertile hybrids may be backcrossed to parental lines, or crossed with each other, then selected, combining hybridization with [[Selective Breeding]].
- Hybridization needs less data management than long-term selective breeding, but still requires careful [[Broodstock Management]]. This includes documenting and maintaining correct parental lines, avoiding inbreeding, and choosing the appropriate sex for matings.
- The authors state that documenting hybrid use is essential for evaluating the technique correctly.
