# Conference Abstracts on Blind Bullheads, Razorback Sucker Predator Training, Bonytail Chub Stress, and Utah Culvert Swimming Performance

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of conference abstracts (a poster session program) covering four freshwater fish topics. The lead item examines a blind-phenotype bullhead population in the [[Modoc Triangle]]. The remaining three posters address hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] predator avoidance, stress physiology in the [[Bonytail Chub]], and swimming performance of native Utah fishes for [[Culvert Design]]. The text is an abstract booklet, not a research report, so results are summarized as reported.

## Background & Description

**Blind bullhead catfishes (Modoc Triangle).** Blind bullheads in [[Dog Lake]] and the surrounding region were first recognized in the 1960s as a significant portion of the local population. The specimens are thought to derive from the non-native [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus nebulosus*), introduced to California in 1874 and since widely dispersed across the West. Later collections found high percentages of the blind phenotype in the [[Goose Lake]] and [[Pit River Basin]]. Their coloration differs from that of sympatric sighted brown bullheads. No native bullheads are known from the Rocky Mountain region, but the text notes that the Pliocene Lake Idaho fish fauna and other western localities contained similar fishes. Pre-1900 tribal vocabularies include words for "bullhead," though it is unclear whether these refer to sculpin (*Cottus* spp.) or to a catfish. Native myths also feature a "bullhead character" with behaviors that may resemble the blind form. The origin and identity of the phenotype are being studied through paleontological, ethological, osteological, biogeographic, historical, and genetic evidence.

**Razorback sucker and predator training (poster).** The study used hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and large [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), a native predator. Experiments tested whether exercise and prior predator exposure improve swimming performance and survival.

**Bonytail chub stress (poster).** The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) is nearly extirpated from the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Hatchery propagation and release are used to prevent extirpation. Handling during culture and release is unavoidable and causes stress.

**Utah native fish swimming (poster).** Historically, fish-passage design in culverts has focused on migratory salmonids, leaving non-game, poorly studied native species with barriers to movement. The study was conducted by researchers at [[Brigham Young University]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hybrid-like or phenotypic divergence:** The blind form in the Modoc Triangle is a notable case of phenotypic divergence within an introduced species, and its origin remains unresolved.
- **Hatchery conditioning:** Raceway conditioning and predator exposure change hatchery-reared fish behavior and survival, bearing on reintroduction success.
- **Stream connectivity:** Culverts can block movement of native non-game fishes, and maintaining connectivity depends on knowing their swimming abilities at different body sizes and flows.
- **Handling stress:** Crowding, netting, anesthesia, and tagging are key stressors in conservation culture of endangered fishes.

## Key Findings

**Razorback sucker exercise and predator training**
- Exercised fish were raised in raceways with current velocities increasing from 7.5 cm/s to 30 cm/s over 10 weeks.
- In a swim test, 50% more exercised fish than control fish reached critical flow velocities of 25 body lengths/s or higher.
- In a 7 m tank with 5 large flathead catfish (57–84 cm TL), exercised fish had 33% ± 6.2 SE mortality, significantly lower than the 51% ± 7.3 SE for unexercised fish.
- Fish exposed to a predation event, followed by unexercised fish, showed reduced mortality (reported as approximately 31% ± 4.4 SE, OCR-uncertain). Fish that were initially naive to flathead catfish did not avoid predator zones; after the first predation event, use of the predator-free zone nearly doubled.
- Conclusion: hatchery-reared razorback suckers are naive predators-wise, and exercise plus predator exposure improves swimming performance and survival.

**Bonytail chub stress**
- Cortisol is the most common stress indicator measured in this study; levels are being assessed using an EIA kit (Cayman Chemical).
- Planned work will test whether stress levels fall more rapidly during one season than another.

**Utah native fishes and culvert design**
- Swimming performance was measured for multiple native species (including widespread and restricted-range taxa) in terms of prolonged (sustained) and burst swimming.
- Prolonged swimming velocity was measured with a step-endurance test; burst velocity with a simulated predator attack.
- Swimming performance increases with body size and is related to morphological shape variation.
- Unique behavioral responses were observed under simulated high flows in a laboratory setting; further work is ongoing.
- These data support culvert design and mitigation for non-game species.

**Blind bullhead origin**
- The blind phenotype is an open question. The ongoing multidisciplinary investigation is intended to resolve its identity and origin.
