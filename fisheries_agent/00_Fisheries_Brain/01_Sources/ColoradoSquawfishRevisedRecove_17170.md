# Colorado Squawfish Revised Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is the revised recovery plan for the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), prepared by the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team for U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Region 6. The species was listed as endangered on March 11, 1967, and the original plan was approved on March 16, 1978. The document is dated August 6, 1991 (the scan reads "1971", but the references and context indicate 1991). Its population has declined from historic levels, likely due to human-caused physical and biological changes in the [[Colorado River]] system.

Natural populations are restricted to the [[Upper Basin]] (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming). Hatchery-reared fish have been reintroduced to the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] in Arizona. The plan sets delisting as the recovery objective, with downlisting and delisting evaluated separately for each Recovery Area.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and size.** The Colorado Squawfish is the largest of the four living species of *Ptychocheilus* and the largest [[Cyprinidae]] (minnow family) in North America. Despite the name *lucius* ("pike like"), it is a minnow. It is a voracious predator and the top native carnivore of the Colorado River system. Recorded maxima are over 36 kg (79 lbs) and nearly 1.8 m (71 in.), but specimens above 7 kg (15 lbs) have been rare in recent times. Early literature called it "white salmon," "Colorado salmon," or "salmon."

**Evolutionary history.** Populations have experienced repeated shifts between pluvial and arid habitats during the Miocene, Pliocene, and early Pleistocene. The species was adapted to swift water by the mid-Pliocene and may have used lakes as well as rivers. Large size, mobility, and spawning migrations were adaptations to drier conditions. The plan notes these same traits may now contribute to its decline.

**Related species.** The other living congeners are the [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (California), the Northern squawfish (Columbia River Basin), and the Umpqua squawfish (Oregon). Unlike the Colorado Squawfish, these remain common.

**Legal status.** Listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service on March 11, 1967. Full protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] began January 4, 1974. California, Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, and Colorado each protect it under state law.

**Morphology.** The fish is elongated and pike-like, with a large mouth and long, slender pharyngeal teeth (tooth formula 2,5-4,2) suited to grasping prey. The anal and dorsal fins each have nine principal rays. The dorsal fin originates slightly posterior to the pelvic fin insertion. Scales are small and embedded on the belly, breast, and nape, with 80-95 in the lateral line. Adults are countershaded (dark olive back, lighter sides, white belly). Young are silvery with a dark, wedge-shaped spot at the base of the caudal fin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic distribution.** The species is endemic to the Colorado River basin and was abundant in the mainstem, most major tributaries, and the Colorado River delta in Mexico. Historic records include:
- **Arizona:** Gila River and tributaries, San Pedro, Salt, and Verde Rivers, the mainstem from the Mexico border to the Utah line, and the lowermost Little Colorado River.
- **California:** Colorado River mainstem to the Nevada line and the Salton Sea (sporadically filled).
- **Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming:** Colorado, Green, Duchesne, San Juan, White, Dolores, Animas, and Little Snake Rivers, plus probably smaller streams.

Early settlers valued it as a food and game fish, and it was used for food and fertilizer, which indicates its prior abundance.

**Present distribution (Upper Basin).** The species is most abundant in:
- the [[Green River]] below the [[Yampa River]] confluence,
- the Yampa River from near Hayden, Colorado, to the Green River,
- the [[White River]] from Taylor Draw Dam near Rangely, Colorado, to the Green River,
- the mainstem [[Colorado River]] from Palisade, Colorado, to [[Lake Powell]].

Catches of young, juvenile, and adult fish in the Green River subbasin are reported to be an order of magnitude higher than elsewhere. Adults and larvae have been documented in the Yampa River and Lodore Canyon. Two radio-tagged adults ascended the Little Snake River in 1988, and a single adult was found there in 1990. Adult and young fish still inhabit Lake Powell. Juveniles and adults in Cataract Canyon indicate reproduction in or above that reach.

**San Juan River.** A small reproducing population exists here. Platania et al. (1991) recorded eight adults and 19 young-of-the-year captured from 1987 to 1989, with two more adults observed but not captured. Most adults were taken between River Mile (RM) 89 near Bluff, Utah, and RM 163 near Shiprock, New Mexico.

**Lower Basin status.** Natural populations were extirpated from the [[Lower Basin]]. No wild fish, other than stocked individuals, have been taken from the Gila River basin since 1950. A 1958 Salt River record was based on misidentified [[Roundtail Chub]]. The last adult from the mainstem lower Colorado River was taken in [[Lake Mohave]] in 1967.

**Limiting factors.** Declines are attributed to:
- stream alteration and habitat fragmentation from [[Dam Construction]], irrigation, dewatering, and channelization;
- competition with and predation by introduced [[Nonnative Fish]].

**Monitoring methods named in the plan.** Electrofishing, handling, tagging, larval sampling, and experimental trammel nets are cited. The plan calls for evaluating their effects on squawfish populations.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective and criteria**
- The objective is delisting, applied separately to each [[Recovery Area]].
- Downlisting to threatened requires naturally self-sustaining populations in three areas: (a) the Green River subbasin, including the lower 220 km (137 mi) of the Yampa River and the lower 240 km (150 mi) of the White River; (b) the Colorado River from Palisade to Lake Powell; and (c) the San Juan River from Lake Powell upstream to the Animas River confluence near Farmington, New Mexico.
- Delisting additionally requires: downlisting criteria met; a reestablished population in either the Salt or Verde River (to be reevaluated after the 1995 Lower Basin Agreement); removal of significant fragmentation threats; legal protection of essential habitats, migration routes, streamflows, and water quality; and removal of other significant threats.

**Five major actions**
1. Monitor population status and define life history requirements.
2. Implement management plans to protect populations and habitat.
3. Reintroduce the species into its historic range.
4. Promote communication and information dissemination.
5. Determine biological criteria for downlisting and delisting.

**Recovery Program and timeline**
- The species is recovered in correlation with the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- The [[Recovery Program]] for the Upper Basin (excluding the San Juan) aims to recover these fishes in 15 years at an estimated cost of $53 million. It serves as the stepdown mechanism for this plan.
- A separate San Juan River program (with the razorback sucker) and a Lower Basin Recovery Action Plan were under development.

**Monitoring tasks (Action 1)**
- Compile population data, identify index monitoring sites, and establish baseline trends.
- Develop standardized monitoring procedures across the basin.
- Conduct intensive monitoring to determine age classes, hatching and rearing success, and relative abundance.
- All fish mortalities go to the Fort Collins Service facility, [[Arizona State University]], or another designated facility.

**Stocking and production (Actions 2 and 3)**
- In 1985, more than 96,000 fingerlings and 442 larger fish (355-405 mm TL) were stocked at six sites on the Salt and Verde Rivers. Seven larger fish were recaptured within 10 days, and five more about 5 months later.
- In 1987, 31,750 fingerlings from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] were stocked in the Salt River drainage, including 6,750 in Canyon Creek. An additional 100 fish went to the Verde River. Three were recaptured in Canyon Creek and one in the Salt River.
- In 1988, Dexter stocked 20,000 fingerlings in the Salt River, 18,000 in Canyon Creek, and 89,303 in the Verde River. Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery stocked 120,604 in the Verde River and 1,194 in Sycamore Creek. Recaptures were 57 in the Verde River and six in the Salt River.
- Stocking in the Salt and Verde Rivers is planned to continue through 1995.
- Upper Basin augmentation will depend on whether stocking contributes to self-sustaining populations. Upper Basin fish are to be marked before release.
- Hatchery broodstock must maintain [[Genetic Diversity]] over at least 20 generations, supplemented with wild fish or gametes if needed.
- The Salt and Verde Rivers stocked fish are designated as nonessential experimental populations.

**Funding mechanism**
- Entities proposing water projects are to provide a one-time contribution of $10 per acre-foot of the average annual depletion of the project.

**Public outreach (Action 4)**
- The plan calls for a national campaign, articles in the Service's Endangered Species Technical Bulletin, and local leaflets for anglers and river users.
- Measures to reduce incidental take include license-purchase education, enforcement contact, and signage at high-use angler areas.
- A sportfishery potential assessment is proposed to build support for recovery.
