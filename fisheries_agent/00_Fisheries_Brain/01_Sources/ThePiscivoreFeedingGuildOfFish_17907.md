# Piscivore Feeding Guild of Fishes in Small Freshwater Ecosystems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Esocidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Allen Keast]] examines the piscivore feeding guild in two small eastern Ontario freshwater systems: [[Lake Opinicon]] (a lake) and [[Jones Creek]] (a stream). Three patterns of fish predation co-occur: (1) specialist piscivores that begin eating fish within weeks of hatching, such as [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*); (2) "secondary" piscivores that eat fish only later in life, such as [[Yellow Perch]], [[Rock Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]]; and (3) species that take only some fish larvae, such as [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*). Specialists compress the usual dietary sequence of zooplankton, then small invertebrates, then large invertebrates, then fish, into a few months. Prey size increases with predator growth, and no piscivore specialized on a single prey species. Specialist piscivores also spawn several weeks before their main prey, so their young can feed on prey larvae. The paper also draws implications for fisheries stocking.

## Background & Description

Piscivory, meaning fish-eating, is a major feature of freshwater ecosystems and has evolved repeatedly across many families. In Nearctica, at least eight families (Centrarchidae, Percidae, Cyprinidae, Amiidae, Salmonidae, Cottidae, Anguillidae, Ictaluridae) have produced piscivores, in addition to the specialized Esocidae and Lepisosteidae. The cichlids of [[Lake Victoria Cichlids|Lake Victoria]], which include more than 35 piscivorous species, represent an extreme case (Greenwood 1974).

Fish prey is large, energetically rewarding and usually abundant. Piscivores are structurally specialized for this diet, with large mouths and, in *Esox*, recurved teeth. These traits limit their efficiency on smaller prey, a constraint typical of specialists.

The author poses four questions: how co-occurring piscivore patterns develop over evolutionary time; whether piscivores specialize on different prey; whether diet changes with age; and whether predator and prey have coevolved.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites**
- **Lake Opinicon:** a 2200 ha lake, 10 m deep, in Leeds County, Ontario, with 18 fish species.
- **Jones Creek:** a 5 km stream entering the St. Lawrence River near Brockville, Ontario, with 12 fish species.

**Methods**
Data were collected at four levels: (a) the age at which each species became piscivorous; (b) the prey species eaten; (c) changes in prey size with predator age; and (d) spawning times of piscivores relative to prey. Diets were grouped into zooplankton, small invertebrates (<10 mm), large invertebrates (>10 mm) and fish. Fish were collected monthly from May to October at three sites and grouped into year classes. Diet data for the first two levels were synthesized from earlier publications, while the prey-size and spawning data are new.

## Key Findings

**1. Diet shifts with age**
- *Micropterus salmoides*: zooplankton and small invertebrate feeding is confined to the first summer, and large invertebrates (crayfish) make up about 15% of volume throughout life. Fish made up 50% of stomach volume in Year I, 70% in Year II, and 85% or more thereafter. The smallest piscivorous individuals were 23 mm long, eating prey of 4.5–6.0 mm.
- *Pomoxis nigromaculatus*: fish were under 10% of diet until Year IV and stabilized at about 30% in older fish.
- *Ambloplites rupestris*: zooplankton and small invertebrates were of little consequence. Fish became a regular diet item in Year IV, stabilizing at 20% of volume.
- *Ictalurus natalis* (yellow bullhead): zooplankton was insignificant. Fish reached 45% of volume.
- *Perca flavescens*: fish became a major item from Year V, at 25% of volume.
- *Semotilus atromaculatus*: fish reached 70% of stomach contents by Year IV.
- *Esox americanus vermiculatus*: fish made up 50% of diet in Year I and 80% from Year IV.
- *Esox lucius*: piscivory begins in the first summer. In Windermere, Great Britain, it began at 35 mm (Frost 1954).

Species that became piscivorous only in Year II, III or IV were never more than partly piscivorous. Their fish consumption ranged from 20% to 45% of volume in Lake Opinicon, and reached 70% in *Semotilus* in Jones Creek.

**2. Prey species**
- Data on prey were compiled for six piscivores in Lake Opinicon, based on 1,000+ prey individuals in total. The counts were: *Esox lucius* 109, *Micropterus salmoides* 407, *Pomoxis nigromaculatus* 139, *Ambloplites rupestris* 288, *Perca flavescens* 284, *Ictalurus natalis* 127 and *Lepomis macrochirus* 53.
- *Lepomis* was the most important prey type. Seven piscivores ate it, compared with six taking *Micropterus*, five taking *Perca* and five taking *Notemigonus*. *Lepomis* made up 14% of the diet of *Pomoxis* and 60% of *Ictalurus natalis*.
- Every fish species in the lake appeared in piscivore diets, and *Esox*, *Micropterus*, *Ambloplites* and *Perca* each took nine species.
- No piscivore specialized on one prey species. Consumption depended on body size, abundance, habitat and habits. For example, *Ictalurus natalis* preyed on *Lepomis*, which rest near the bottom where they are vulnerable to nocturnal, tactile-feeding catfish.
- In Jones Creek, *Esox americanus vermiculatus* mainly took *Umbra limi*, small *Lepomis*, *Notemigonus* and *Esox*. *Semotilus* mainly took *Chrosomus eos*, *Eucalia inconstans* and small *Catostomus commersonii*.

**3. Prey size selection**
- Prey size increased significantly with predator age in all series, though there was much variation in prey size.
- *Esox* took prey up to 150 mm. *Micropterus* commonly took prey up to 100 mm, and rarely up to 120 mm. *Ambloplites*, *Pomoxis* and *Perca* took no prey larger than 40–50 mm.
- Mean prey lengths at the end of the first summer were: *Pomoxis* 43 mm, *Ambloplites* 45 mm, *Lepomis macrochirus* 42 mm, *L. gibbosus* 48 mm, *Micropterus* 62 mm and *Perca* 54 mm.
- Year 0 fish made up 64% of prey individuals eaten by *Pomoxis*, 66% by *Ambloplites* and 67% by *Perca*.

**4. Spawning synchrony**
- *Esox lucius* spawns in mid-April, *Perca flavescens* in late April to early May, and *Micropterus* in May, the first centrarchid to spawn.
- The most important prey, *Lepomis macrochirus*, produces young from late June to mid-July, about three weeks after *Micropterus*. Most major prey are late spring spawners with protracted seasons.
- In Jones Creek, *Esox americanus vermiculatus* spawns in late April to early May. Its prey *Umbra limi* spawns early, so its year 0 young escape much predation.
- Secondary piscivores, whose year 0 fish do not eat fish, are generally later spawners.

**5. Interpretation**
- Specialist piscivores shorten the dietary sequence. Their transition to fish feeding must be rapid because fast growth and large body size depend on maximizing energy gain.
- Secondary piscivores are slow-moving, nocturnal and not structurally specialized. They mainly exploit first-summer prey, which the author considers superabundant in late summer. This may explain why many secondary piscivores can coexist.
- The early spawning of specialists allows their young to feed on prey young. The author argues that avoiding competition with zooplankton-feeding larvae is not the main selective force, because the zooplankton phase is very short in *Micropterus* and *Esox lucius*.
- Prey probably do not spawn earlier because of trade-offs, including slower development in cold early-season water, lower prey abundance and a lack of protective vegetation for young.
- The predator-prey timing pattern resembles systems such as mink and muskrat, lynx and snowshoe hare, and falcons and their prey.

**6. Implications for fisheries management**
Ignoring predator-prey size structure may explain some failures of fish stocking. *Micropterus* and *Lepomis macrochirus* were once considered an ideal pond combination (Swingle 1950), but their limited success may reflect an absence of the full range of prey sizes in the right proportions. The author argues that a better understanding of prey size relationships could move stocking from trial and error to a more exact procedure.
