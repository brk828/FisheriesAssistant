# Hot Springs Canyon Fish Barrier Reconnaissance Trip Report (February 2, 2000)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

A February 2, 2000 field reconnaissance by [[Sally Stefferud]], [[Rob Clarkson]] (Bureau of Reclamation), and [[Paul Marsh]] (Arizona State University) assessed [[Hot Springs Canyon]] as a possible site for a fish barrier. The team hiked from the lower canyon upstream to a turnaround point near the [[State-BLM Boundary]]. They found live fish (longfin dace and small suckers, apparently young-of-the-year) in a small pool just upstream of the canyon mouth, and identified a narrow rock neck in the canyon bottom as the most promising barrier site.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Evaluate the potential for a fish barrier in Hot Springs Canyon, a tributary setting near the [[San Pedro River]].
- **Access:** A road leaves the San Pedro road near the Cascabel airport and climbs the south rim, paralleling the canyon for about a mile before crossing the creek. It ends at a windmill in the SE 1/4 of Section 12, T13S, R19E (USGS topo). A [[Sahuaro-Juniper Association]] parking area and trailhead sign are located there.
- **Landowners:** The Sahuaro-Juniper Association is a cooperative ranching group of about 60 landowners. Its covenants ban new powerlines and new roads and allow hiker and horse access. The other landowner at the road end is the [[Cascabel Hermitage]], a meditation group. Land ownership in the canyon is a mix of private and State parcels.
- **Trip members:** Three participants, as listed above.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream morphology and flow**
- Near the trailhead the stream bottom is wide, sandy, braided, and mostly dry, with little to no riparian vegetation. Vegetation is dominated by desert broom, burro brush, and mesquite.
- The canyon narrows upstream, but the channel stays wide, braided, and seldom flowing until the canyon mouth (SE corner of Section 6, T13S, R20E).
- Flowing water was first encountered about 1/4 mile below the canyon mouth (the OCR reads "114 mile," interpreted here as 1/4 mile).
- Just upstream of the mouth, the channel is about 300 yards wide, with abrupt, sub-vertical rock walls and a flow of about 1–2 cfs. The stream is silt-sand-gravel bottomed and mostly run/riffle, with algae present.

**Riparian vegetation**
- [[Sycamore]] appear just upstream of the mouth. Moving upstream, there are a few mature [[Cottonwood]], then numbers of small to moderate cottonwood and various sizes of [[Willow]] near the State-BLM boundary.
- [[Deergrass]], seepwillow, [[Equisetum]], and watercress become prominent farther upstream.

**Land use**
- Moderate cattle use occurs downstream of a fence across the canyon mouth. There is none upstream of the fence.
- Resident Daniel Baker (Sahuaro-Juniper Association; [[Nature Conservancy]] employee) reported that grazing was removed from the canyon about 3–4 years earlier.

**Upper reach near the State-BLM boundary**
- The canyon is narrow, flow is about half again greater than at the mouth, and riparian cover is thick with mostly stable banks.
- The stream is relatively narrow, mostly single-channel, with good habitat diversity. Substrate is mostly gravel/cobble.
- Large pools, 2 to 4 times average channel width and at least 3 feet deep, are formed by lateral scour along rock walls or by backwater/eddy scour around large rocks.
- Woody debris in the channel itself is scarce despite abundant bankside vegetation.

## Key Findings

1. **Fish presence:** Fish were observed in a small pool about 1/4 mile upstream of the canyon mouth. These included several sizes of [[Longfin Dace]] and small suckers, apparently young-of-the-year, which suggests recent reproduction in the reach.
2. **Initial barrier candidate:** The first place a barrier would be feasible is just upstream of the canyon mouth (GPS 562432E, 3576973N), where the channel is about 300 yards wide between sub-vertical rock walls.
3. **Most promising barrier site:** A narrow neck in the NW corner of Section 5 (T13S, R20E), where the USGS 15-minute quadrangle shows the end of perennial flow. The neck is about 150 feet wide with rock outcrops on both sides (GPS 563049E, 3577542N). The stream still appears seasonally intermittent here.
4. **Turnaround point:** The team turned back just before the State-BLM boundary (GPS 563635E, 3578150N).
5. **Habitat quality:** The upper reach offers good habitat diversity, deep pools, and thick riparian cover, though woody debris is limited.
6. **Access and land-use constraints:** Barrier construction and access depend on coordination among the Sahuaro-Juniper Association (covenant restrictions on roads and powerlines), the Cascabel Hermitage, and mixed private and State landowners.
7. **Management note:** Removal of cattle grazing about 3–4 years ago may have benefited the upstream riparian zone, which is now more vegetated than the downstream reach.
