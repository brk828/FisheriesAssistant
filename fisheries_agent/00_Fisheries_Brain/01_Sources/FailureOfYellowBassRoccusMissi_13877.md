# Failure of Yellow Bass to Form Annuli

#ichthyology #marine-biology #roccus

## Executive Summary

This 1963 article in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* by Marvin M. Buchholz and Kenneth D. Carlander, from [[Iowa State University]], reports that [[Yellow Bass]] in [[Clear Lake Iowa]] sometimes failed to form [[Annulus Formation|annuli]] on their scales during years of slow growth (1952–1959 data). Inconsistencies in [[Scale Age Determination|scale-based age data]] were resolved using [[Year Class Analysis]] and [[Length-Frequency Distribution|length-frequency distributions]], showing that the abundant 1949 year class did not add annuli in 1955–1958. The authors conclude that annulus failure in slow-growing fish is probably more common than reported but hard to detect, and that a yellow bass with five annuli at age IX extended the species' known lifespan.

## Background & Description

Yellow bass in Clear Lake had shown declining growth since the late 1930s. [[Carlander et al. (1953)]] documented decreased growth from 1937 to 1949, and growth and average size fell further in the following decade. Fish caught in 1950–1955 were typically 8 to 10 inches long, whereas from 1955 to 1960 most were 6 to 7 inches total length. The authors attributed this decline to dropping water levels and increased fish population density, a topic reserved for a separate paper.

**Methods**

- Fish were collected by [[Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]] biologists from 1952 through 1959, mainly with experimental [[Gill Netting|gill nets]] of five 25-foot sections (mesh sizes 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5, and 2 inches, bar measure). These nets catch yellow bass over 4.5 inches. A 150-volt electric shocker was also used in 1958 and 1959.
- Gill-net effort in 1952–1954 ranged from 527 to 678 gill-net hours.
- A [[Body-Scale Regression]] was derived from 107 pairs of data: total length (inches) = 1.1 + 0.569 × scale radius (inches) at 49 magnifications. It followed the method of [[Whitney and Carlander (1956)]].
- Past growth was back-calculated with a nomograph ([[Carlander and Smith (1944)]]). Scale impressions were made on plastic and read with a scale projector.

**Description of Annuli**

Yellow bass annuli are usually distinct, especially in the anterior field, where new circuli begin and end abruptly. In years of little growth, the first new circulus may not cross over incomplete lateral circuli, though the annulus can still be detected anteriorly. The authors noted a similar situation in [[Largemouth Bass]] (Bennett 1948).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Clear Lake population dynamics**

- The 1949 year class dominated samples, making up 73%, 68%, and 64% of yellow bass collected in 1952, 1953, and 1954, respectively.
- The 1950 year class was believed to be eliminated before summer 1951, probably by predation. No yearlings were taken in 1951, and gill netting from 1952 to 1954 caught none of this group.
- Only two fish of the 1951 year class were collected in routine netting from 1952 to 1955, both as yearlings in 1952.
- The 1953 year class was unsuccessful (27 fish in 1954 and 11 in 1955) and was considered lost after 1955.

**Scale resorption and food**

Scales from 1949 year-class fish showed lateral resorption in 1956, 1957, and 1958. The entire scale growth for 1952–1954 and half the growth for 1951 was resorbed along one lateral margin in one example. Resorption typically began at a postero-lateral position, while the anterior edge was relatively resistant. Citing [[Johnels (1952)]], [[Eschmeyer (1937)]], and [[Burress (1949)]], the authors concluded that resorption in Clear Lake yellow bass was most likely caused by insufficient food, a sign of [[Starvation and Scale Resorption|starvation]].

**Comparative cases in other species**

The authors reviewed similar annulus failures reported elsewhere:

- [[White Bass]] (Kegonsa Lake, Wisconsin; Spirit Lake, Iowa): Frey and Vike (1941) and Sigler (1949).
- [[White Crappie]] (Lake Decatur, Illinois): Hansen (1951).
- [[Brook Trout]] (Bunny Lake, California): Reimers (1958). Average total length rose from 4.7 inches in August of year 2 to 5.2, 5.9, 6.0, and 6.4 inches in years 3–6, and annuli stopped after the second year.
- [[Arctic Grayling]] (Grebe Lake, Wyoming): Kruse (1959), with resorption involved in some cases.
- Other reports include [[Bluegill]] (Regier 1962), European perch (Alm 1946), carp (Frey 1942), and trout species (Alvord 1954).

## Key Findings

1. **Missing annuli in the 1949 year class.** Many fish collected in 1955 had five annuli and were first assigned to the 1950 year class. That assignment was rejected because the 1950 year class was gone before summer 1951. Abundance of five-annulus fish (65% of the 1955 catch, 20% of the 1956 catch) and matching growth histories pointed to the 1949 year class. Fish of this class with only five annuli were also found in 1957 and 1958, and four 1958 fish with a sixth annulus were also assigned to it.

2. **Other year classes affected.** Six-annulus fish in 1955 and 1956 were assigned to the 1948 year class. Seven-annulus fish in 1954 and 1955 were assigned to the 1946 year class. The 1952 year class also failed: six fish collected in 1956 with four annuli had formed their fourth annulus very late in summer 1956, and no further annuli formed through 1958.

3. **Separating year classes.** In 1956, 117 fish with three annuli (compared with 11 two-annulus fish in 1955) were split by length-frequency distributions. A minor mode at 5.6 inches (the 1953 year-class average) was judged insufficient to isolate the 1953 year class. In 1958, 63 fish with four annuli were separated into two groups: 35 fish matching the 1954 year class and 28 matching the 1952 year class.

4. **Reliability of age determinations.** Age and growth estimates were considered reliable in almost all cases. Year-class strength was consistent across collections; growth patterns differed enough between year classes to separate them; and length-frequency data for the first three years agreed with scale results.

5. **Extended longevity.** Yellow bass with five annuli at age IX (from the 1949 class in 1958 and the 1946 class in 1955) extend the known lifespan of the species to 9 years. Previous records stopped at age VII (Carlander et al. 1953; Lopinot 1958), and age V was the usual maximum.

6. **General conclusion.** Failure to form annuli occurs when there is little or no growth, and it is probably more common in nature than reported. It is hard to detect without known-age fish or several consecutive years of collections. The authors recommend considering it in any study of stunted or slow-growing populations.
