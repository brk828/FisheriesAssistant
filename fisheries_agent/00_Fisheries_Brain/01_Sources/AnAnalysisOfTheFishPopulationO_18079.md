# Analysis of the Fish Population of Big Bear Lake, Otsego County, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 paper by [[Walter R. Crowe]], published by the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]] of the Michigan Department of Conservation, estimates the fish populations of [[Big Bear Lake]], a small, landlocked lake in Otsego County, Michigan. Using [[Mark-And-Recapture]] sampling with [[Trap Net]] gear over eight netting periods (1940–1946), the author estimated abundances for six species and reviewed the assumptions behind the estimators. The study also compares the statistical methods available at the time, and it concludes that the sucker population crashed after a 1943 removal, that bluegill recruitment had nearly failed since 1938, and that perch were under-sampled by the gear.

## Background & Description

The paper argues that reliable population estimates are a prerequisite for evaluating fisheries management, including population control efforts such as fish removal, corrective stocking, environmental alteration and movement restriction. The author notes that complete eradication by toxicants (e.g., [[Rotenone]]), draining or winterkill rarely succeeds on large waters, and that many Michigan lakes poisoned earlier were repopulated by undesirable species.

The historical framework covers the [[Petersen Method]] (first applied to fish by C. G. J. Petersen in 1896, and known as the [[Lincoln Index]] for land animals), the [[DeLury Method]] (based on catch per unit effort against cumulative catch), and direct counts after draining or winterkill. The author attributes early development of the marking approach to [[Laplace]] (1783) and the theoretical error analysis to [[Karl Pearson]] (1928).

Estimators applied at Big Bear Lake:
- [[Schnabel Method]]: maximum-likelihood estimate from daily recaptures, suited to marking spread over weeks.
- [[Schumacher And Eschmeyer Method]]: least-squares estimate with a sampling variance and standard error.
- [[Chapman Method]]: hypergeometric-based estimate with confidence limits.

Ricker (1948) listed six conditions for a valid Petersen estimate: equal mortality of marked and unmarked fish; retention of marks; equal vulnerability to gear; random mixing or proportional sampling; complete reporting of recaptures; and a closed population with negligible recruitment. The author examined each for Big Bear Lake:
- **Marking mortality:** Only instantaneous mortality matters, and fin clipping has little immediate lethal effect. Losses from rough handling and trap-net abrasion were judged minor.
- **Mark permanency:** Clipped fins regenerated little, and many marks remained recognizable two to three years later.
- **Availability:** No reason was found to suspect that marked fish were more or less catchable.
- **Mixing:** Random sampling across the lake was easier to achieve than random mixing of marked fish, so random sampling was emphasized.
- **Reporting:** All fish were handled by the author, so reporting error did not arise.
- **Recruitment:** Judged negligible, since most sampled fish were older than the size range that younger cohorts would reach during netting.
- **Closure:** The lake is landlocked and records of stocking were available, so it was treated as closed.

### Study Site

Big Bear Lake lies midway between Lewiston and Johannesburg in Otsego County (T. 29–30 N., R. 1 W.). Its basin is roughly triangular, with sides of about 6,800 feet and maximum diameters of about one mile. The lake is of glacial origin, lying where a morainic region meets a sandy outwash plain. Its drainage area is very small.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical setting:**
- Surface area: about 350 acres
- Maximum depth: 35 feet
- Mean depth: 15.8 feet
- Bottom: sand, gravel and fibrous peat in shallows; pulpy peat and clay in deeper water
- Water: clear and colorless; Secchi reading 12.8 feet (August 1940)
- Chemistry: alkaline and moderately hard, pH 8.0–8.2, methyl orange alkalinity 45–98 ppm
- Thermal regime: a temperate lake that usually does not stratify
- Habitat: wind- and wave-swept shoals, protected bays with dense aquatic vegetation, and sandy or gravelly beaches; the basin slope is moderate and habitat is fairly uniform

**Fish community:** The author lists 15 resident species, unchanged throughout the study. They include White Sucker, [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[Northern Common Shiner]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], Northern Brown Bullhead, [[Western Mudminnow]], [[Western Banded Killifish]], Yellow Perch, [[Iowa Darter]], Northern Smallmouth Bass, Largemouth Bass, Pumpkinseed, Common Bluegill, Northern Rock Bass and [[Northern Muddler]]. Other records were:
- Two [[Black Crappie]] captured
- One [[Fine-Scale Dace]] taken in 1949
- [[Emerald Shiner]] found in perch stomachs, interpreted as bait minnows
- A walleye reported in the 1940 creel census, judged a probable misidentification because walleye never became established despite earlier plantings

## Key Findings

**Sampling design**
- Eight netting periods: 1940 (July 6–Aug. 31), 1941 (July 1–Aug. 24), 1942 (Aug. 19–Sept. 17), 1943 spring (May 18–June 21), 1943 fall (Sept. 25–Oct. 15), 1944 (May 17–June 20), 1946 spring (May 22–June 28) and 1946 fall (Sept. 5–28).
- Gear: double-pot trap nets, each trap 20 feet long, with 300-foot leaders and graded mesh. Four or five nets were fished at once and lifted every 24 hours. Fish were measured, weighed and marked before release.
- Trap nets rarely caught perch or small fish, which is a major limitation.

**Population estimates (Table II, selected values)**

| Species | Key estimates (number of fish) |
|---|---|
| White Sucker | 1940: 9,699 (Schnabel); 1941: 15,423; 1942: 10,480; 1944: 1,412 (probably high); 1946: about 235 (spring) and 390 (fall) |
| Largemouth Bass | 1940: 2,987; 1941: 2,383; 1942: 1,194 (low point) |

Sucker estimates for other periods came from the same formulas and are in Table II; the largemouth bass population stayed at a roughly constant level after 1942.

**Sucker collapse**
- In spring 1943 the author removed 5,778 suckers of catchable size.
- The DeLury regression intercepted the abscissa at about 5,600, consistent with an available stock of roughly 6,000 and not the 10,000–11,000 implied by the 1942 estimate.
- Catches fell sharply after removal, and fall 1943 estimates were about 350.
- By 1949 the 1943 stock had not recovered, and reproduction was thought negligible. Suckers were fairly numerous again in 1950, mostly yearlings and two-year-olds.

**Game and panfish**
- **Largemouth Bass:** The most abundant game fish, reproducing successfully with numerous year classes.
- **Smallmouth Bass:** Less abundant than largemouth but well established. Its population rose after the 1943 low point.
- **Bluegill:** Nearly all fish since 1941 belonged to the 1938 year class. Survival from reproduction after 1938 was almost negligible, so the species' ability to maintain its numbers is questionable.
- **Pumpkinseed:** The most important panfish apart from perch, well established, with numerous year classes from 1940–1946.
- **Rock Bass:** Estimates are probably unrealistic, and likely minimal, because the fish concentrated around brush piles and were poorly sampled except in fall 1943 and fall 1946.
- **Yellow Perch:** Not estimated, because trap nets did not capture them. Perch were nonetheless the most numerous panfish and made up most of the angler catch, especially after 1944.

**Bullheads and hybrids**
- Bullhead estimates were 11 (1940), 17 (1941), 9 (1942), 6 and 13 (1943), and 209 (fall 1946). The 1946 jump reflected a previously unmarked population, mostly small brown bullheads (97 of 102 caught in September 1946 were brown bullheads).
- [[Bluegill X Pumpkinseed Hybrid]] estimates were 35 (1941), 31 (1942), 110 (fall 1943), 50 (spring 1946) and 110 (fall 1946).

**Methodological conclusions**
- Schnabel and Chapman estimates were computed consistently. The Schumacher and Eschmeyer estimate was used when B/P exceeded 0.25. [[Ricker]] found that Schnabel is most efficient when B/P is near 0 and Schumacher–Eschmeyer when B/P is near 5, with equal efficiency at 0.25.
- Recaptures were few, so confidence limits were wide, and systematic error from net-shyness or gear preference could not be readily detected.
- Separate estimates for each species were recommended, along with careful gear planning and sampling of all habitats.
