# Camp Four Multi-Agency Fish Sampling at Arrowhead Cove, Lake Mohave (March 1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This memorandum, dated May 31, 1989, from the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Phoenix, Arizona) to participants of "Camp Four," reports results of a second multi-agency sampling effort at [[Arrowhead Cove]], [[Lake Mohave]], held March 7–12, 1989. The effort aimed to capture [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and to tag [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Eight species were captured, totaling 100 individuals. No bonytail chub were taken. Most razorback suckers were recaptures from previous years. Data were sent to [[Arizona State University]] (ASU), which coordinated the survey and will tabulate final results, and to the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AGFD) and the Service.

## Background & Description

The survey was a cooperative effort involving the [[University Of Arizona]], the [[Bureau Of Land Management]] (BLM) Kingman Resource Area, Northern Arizona University, the Service, and individual volunteers. Drs. R.L. Minckley and Paul Marsh of ASU coordinated the work. The memo includes a participant list of about 15 people from these agencies and institutions, all based in Arizona (Tempe, Tucson, Kingman, Flagstaff, and Phoenix).

Target species:
- **Bonytail chub** (*Gila elegans*): a native Colorado River fish that was the primary capture target. None were caught.
- **Razorback sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): a native catostomid that was tagged and tracked.

Methods:
- [[Trammel Net]] sets and [[Electrofishing]] were used. Table 2 (not legible in the scanned copy) gave set locations and razorback sucker counts by site.
- Scales from largemouth bass were sent to the AGFD Regional Office in Kingman.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The sampling took place in a reservoir cove on the lower Colorado River system. The memo notes that the group's camp was at Arrowhead Cove, with two other camps set up upstream. Communication and safety were supported by portable radios supplied by the BLM Kingman Resource Area.

The newspaper fragment in the attachment reports that a BLM wildlife biologist said trammel nets had been set several miles north of Katherine Landing. It also reports a comment that some fish may be caught more readily at night than during the day. The fragment is partly illegible, so the exact wording cannot be verified.

Bonytail management was discussed in the same press material. A quoted biologist (Boucom) noted that bonytail were being spawned in fish hatcheries and that fish of about six inches or more could be introduced to the Colorado River. The largemouth bass, a non-native species, is also mentioned in this context.

## Key Findings

- **Total catch:** 8 species and 100 individuals were captured during March 7–12, 1989.
- **Razorback sucker:** Most captured razorback suckers were recaptures from earlier years. The exact total is hard to read in the scan; it appears to be about 47. The number of recaptures is illegible.
- **Bonytail chub:** None were captured, so the primary objective was not met.
- **Species composition:** The eight species were razorback sucker, largemouth bass, common carp, rainbow trout, yellow bullhead, green sunfish, channel catfish, and bluegill. Native species were few. Most others are non-native to this reach.
- **Table 1 counts:** The per-species and per-method counts in the scanned Table 1 are too garbled to report reliably. Only the total of 100 is stated clearly in the text.
- **Data distribution:** Results went to ASU (final tabulation), AGFD, and the Service. Largemouth bass scales went to AGFD Kingman.
- **Logistics:** Radios from the BLM Kingman Resource Area supported safety and communication. Media coverage appeared in the *Mohave Daily Miner* on March 9, 1989.
- **Geographic note:** The text places the survey in Clark County, California. The Table 1 header lists "Arizona-Nevada," so the state reference is inconsistent in the source.
