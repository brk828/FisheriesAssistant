# Growth and Longevity of Two Brook Trout Populations Under Light Exploitation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 paper by Edwin L. Cooper (The Pennsylvania State University), reprinted from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 96, No. 4, pp. 383-386), compares the growth, longevity and age composition of two natural [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) populations in Pennsylvania streams that experience only light angling exploitation. The fertile [[Big Spring Creek]] population grew faster and had a far higher standing crop than the infertile [[Larry's Creek]] population, yet both had similar age structures, with few fish surviving beyond 6 years. [[Lee's Phenomenon]] was absent in both populations, which the author interprets as consistent with selective angling mortality of faster-growing fish being its underlying cause.

## Background & Description

The brook trout is a popular sport fish. Large populations are typically heavily angled, which radically alters natural age composition. The two Pennsylvania populations studied were considered unusual because they were under very light exploitation, making them more representative of populations in equilibrium with their natural environment. The author notes that growth and longevity data from disturbed populations are less meaningful for understanding a species' adaptation to environmental change.

Growth was estimated by [[Scale Analysis]] (back-calculation from scale measurements) using samples collected by [[Electrofishing]] from September 1961 to November 1962. Three to five scales per fish were mounted in glycerine-gelatin and examined at 95 diameters. Age was determined by counting completed annual marks, and the anterior scale radius was used as the scale measurement. A curvilinear body-scale relationship of the form log (L-1.6) = log a + b log S was fitted by least squares, and [[Hile Nomograph]] methods were used to back-calculate growth. Scales were first formed at a total length of 1.6 inches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Big Spring Creek:** a fertile, rheocrene, limestone stream producing about 268 pounds/acre of brook trout annually (standing crop 206 pounds/acre as reported in the study).
- **Larry's Creek:** an infertile, helocrene, sandstone stream producing about 52 pounds/acre annually (standing crop 39 pounds/acre).
- Exploitation was inferred to be very light from the size and structure of the standing crop, since no catch data were available. Unusually large numbers of fish in age-groups III, IV and V were present in both streams.
- The contrasting study by McFadden (1961) found brook trout older than age-group II were rarely encountered because of heavy exploitation.
- Year-to-year variation in growth within each stream was minimal compared with the difference between streams, which is attributed to environmental (fertility) differences.

## Key Findings

- **Body-scale relationships were nearly identical:**
  - Larry's Creek: log (L-1.6) = 1.96 + 1.57 log S (n = 353; 1.6 to 11.9 inches)
  - Big Spring: log (L-1.6) = 2.03 + 1.58 log S (n = 501; 1.6 to 15.5 inches)
  - Separate nomographs were still prepared, as combining the populations would introduce slight bias (e.g., a 10-inch age-group III fish back-calculated to 8.50 vs. 8.45 inches at the 3rd annulus).
- **Growth differed markedly:** At the end of 3 growing seasons, Big Spring trout were about 10 inches long versus about 6.5 inches in Larry's Creek.
- **Mean back-calculated length at end of year 1:** 3.14 inches in Larry's Creek (range 2.97-3.19 across 5 years) versus 4.28 inches in Big Spring (range 4.00-4.37).
- **Age composition (October 1962, mark-and-recapture estimate):**
  - Larry's Creek: 704 age-group-0 fish per acre, declining about 35% per year to about 10 trout per acre in age-group V.
  - Big Spring: 1,186 age-group-0 fish per acre, declining about 41% per year to about 24 fish per acre in age-group V.
  - No fish older than age-group V were collected from either population.
- **Longevity and age structure were similar** in both populations, with few fish living as long as 6 years.
- **Lee's phenomenon was not observed** in either population. Its absence in Larry's Creek is consistent with very low exploitation. Slight evidence of size-selective mortality in Big Spring (fewer age-group IV fish than Larry's Creek) was judged insufficient to disrupt year-class size distributions.
- **Conclusion:** Selective angling mortality of faster-growing fish is a reasonable explanation for Lee's phenomenon, because the phenomenon disappears where exploitation is minimal. The author also notes that the Big Spring deviation at the end of the 3rd season may reflect non-random mortality of faster-growing fish.
- **Methodological validation:** Back-calculated lengths for the first 3 years closely matched observed mean lengths of corresponding age-groups, supporting the scale-fitting approach.
