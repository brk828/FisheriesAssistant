# Gila River Fishery Reconnaissance Survey (May 22–24, 1968)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #fishery-survey

## Executive Summary

This report presents a general fishery reconnaissance survey of the [[Gila River]] in southwestern [[New Mexico]], conducted May 22–24, 1968. The survey was requested by the [[U.S. Forest Service]] because the proposed [[Hooker Dam]] and Reservoir would inundate the river, and the downstream area near the alternate [[Conner Site]] could also be affected. Seven stations were electrofished over roughly 3,550 feet of stream, yielding a catch of 316 fish. The community was dominated by small cyprinids and suckers, with [[Longfin Dace]] (40%) the most abundant species. Survey conditions limited sampling of deep, fast-flowing sections, so the large-fish data are likely underestimated. The authors also flagged a possible decline of [[Gila Spikedace]] and the presence of several native species of conservation concern.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Evaluate the fish populations, including rare or endangered species, and document the status of native species in relation to the Hooker Dam project.
- **Study team:**
  - Douglas B. Jester, Assistant Professor, [[New Mexico State University]]
  - Harold Olson, Fisheries Biologist, [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]]
  - Henry J. McKirdy, Regional Fisheries Biologist, U.S. Forest Service
- **Study area:** Seven sampling points on the Gila River, from the confluence of Turkey Creek near the Wilderness Boundary downstream to the proposed alternate Conner Site, a few miles upstream from Redrock, New Mexico.
- **Photographic record:** The report includes photos of the wilderness terrain near the Hooker Dam site, the river looking downstream near Gila/Cliff, and the river bottom near Station 7. The photos note sycamore-cottonwood riparian habitat that supports songbirds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods**
- Seven stations were chosen at various intervals where the stream was accessible to heavy [[Electrofishing]] gear.
- About 500 feet of stream was fished at each station (250 feet upstream and 250 feet downstream of the generator).
- Collected fish were preserved in 10% [[Formalin]] solution and later identified and weighed at New Mexico State University.

**Water quality**
- Field measurements were made at Stations 3, 6, and 7 using a field kit ([[Hach Water Test Kit]]).
- Water temperatures ranged from a low of 60°F at Station 1 to a high of 66°F at Station 3.
- [[Total Dissolved Solids]] increased from about 50 ppm at Station 3 to 150 ppm at Station 7.

**Aquatic invertebrates**
- [[Mayfly Larvae]] were the predominant aquatic insect. They occurred to some degree at all sampling points and were most abundant between Stations 5 and 6.

**Habitat constraints**
- Portions of the main channel were swift and deep. These areas could not be sampled effectively, and the survey mainly sampled the shallower, slower margins.

## Key Findings

**1. Catch and effort**
- Approximately 3,550 feet of stream was electrofished across seven stations.
- The total catch was 316 fish.

**2. Species composition (percent of total population)**

| Species | Percent |
|---|---|
| Longfin Dace | 40% |
| Fathead Minnow | 24.5% |
| Sonora Sucker | 19.9% |
| Black Bullhead | 6.6% |
| Green Sunfish | 3.3% |
| Gila Sucker | 3.2% |
| Smallmouth Bass | 2.8% |
| Channel Catfish | 2.0% |
| Loach Minnow | 0.6% |
| Bonytail | 0.3% |

- Ten species were recorded. Small fishes, especially [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Fathead Minnow]], dominated the catch.
- Native species of conservation concern included [[Gila Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Loach Minnow]], and Sonora Sucker.
- The authors noted the survey was not designed for a full population census.

**3. Gila Spikedace**
- According to earlier reports and personal contact with Dr. Minckley, [[Gila Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) was believed to occur in the Gila River between the towns of Gila and Cliff.
- The species is also considered by some to be endangered.
- It was not found at Station 4 during this survey.
- The authors suggested that the reach may need resurvey at a different time of year, since the fish may also no longer exist there.

**4. Sampling limitations**
- Deep, fast water prevented sampling in portions of the main channel.
- This could bias population estimates, especially for larger fishes such as bass and catfish.
- The authors also noted the possible presence of trout in the upper portion of the river, which the survey could not adequately assess.

**5. Water quality trend**
- Total dissolved solids rose from about 50 ppm at Station 3 to 150 ppm at Station 7.
- The authors expected this increase because of agricultural uses and the towns of Gila and Cliff.

**6. Aquatic invertebrates**
- Mayfly larvae were the most common aquatic insect and were found at all stations.
- Abundance peaked between Stations 5 and 6.

**7. Management implications**
- The survey provided baseline data on native and possibly endangered fishes before the proposed Hooker Dam project.
- The authors recommended caution in interpreting results and suggested repeat surveys at other seasons, particularly for the Gila Spikedace reach.
