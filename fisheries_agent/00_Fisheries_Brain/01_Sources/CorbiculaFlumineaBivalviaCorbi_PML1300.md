# Corbicula fluminea (Asian Clam) as a Potential Commercial Fishery in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #corbiculidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 final report to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, prepared by Dennis M. Kubly and J. Jerry Landye for the Arizona Game and Fish Department, assesses whether the introduced Asian clam, [[Corbicula Fluminea]], could support a commercial harvest in Arizona. Field and laboratory work in 1982–83 sampled the [[Lower Colorado River]], [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]] for densities, biomass, size structure, reproduction, and tissue safety. Harvest potential was found to be limited to canals, the Parker and lower Havasu divisions, and a few reservoirs, with most reaches judged too sparse to support commercial harvest.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Corbicula Fluminea]] (also called *C. manilensis* by some authors), an introduced freshwater bivalve.
- **Early records:** First documented in the lower Colorado River in 1953 near Yuma, at the origin of the [[All-American Canal]]. Reported in Lakes Mead and Havasu in 1958 and in the Parker canal system in 1964. In the Gila basin, first recorded from Phoenix specimens accessioned in 1954.
- **Objectives:** Synthesize distribution literature; build a density, biomass, size-class and reproductive database; measure habitat and environmental variables; test tissue suitability for human consumption (bacteria, heavy metals, pesticides, herbicides); and assess impacts on benthic fauna and fishes from commercial operations.
- **Study design:** Samples were taken with a Ponar dredge sampling about 0.023 m² per grab. Transects ran perpendicular to shore at roughly 3.2 km (2 mile) intervals on river reaches and 4.15 km (2.5 mile) intervals on reservoirs, with five samples per transect from 0.5 m to 30 m depth. Density estimates were expressed per square meter; the report notes the factor of 43× needed to convert to per hectare was questionable given the clumped distribution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lower Colorado River.** The river runs from Morelos Dam (River Mile 22.1) to Separation Rapids (RM 450) in the Grand Canyon. It is heavily regulated by a series of dams (Table I lists nine, including [[Davis Dam]], [[Parker Dam]], [[Headgate Rock Dam]] and [[Hoover Dam]]). Daily water-level fluctuations of up to 2.0 m are reported, and the river supports four habitat types: reservoirs, flowing reaches, backwaters and marshes, and canals. Reservoirs include [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]].

**Gila, Salt and Verde rivers.** The Gila watershed is the largest drainage in the lower Colorado basin (about 149,900 km² in Arizona and New Mexico) and is largely ephemeral. The Salt River is regulated by four dams forming [[Saguaro Lake]], [[Canyon Lake]], [[Apache Lake]] and [[Roosevelt Lake]]. The Verde River has [[Horseshoe Reservoir]] and [[Bartlett Reservoir]] on its lower reach.

**Climate and vegetation.** The area has bimodal precipitation and high evaporation, with summer air temperatures near 40 °C. Vegetation is Mohave and Sonoran Desertscrub, with much riparian cover replaced by introduced species such as [[Tamarisk]].

## Key Findings

**Distribution and density (Lower Colorado River)**
- **Yuma Division:** Only 11 clams in 40 Ponar samples; mean density 11.54/m². This was far below 147.46/m² recorded in 1974–75.
- **Laguna Division:** No clams in four natural-channel samples. The Gila Gravity Canal yielded a mean of about 2115/m².
- **Imperial Division:** Mean density 8.39/m², compared with 69.63/m² in 1974–75. The authors judged numbers to be declining.
- **Cibola Division:** Mean 6.88/m² across 25 mainstream samples, with higher values in backwaters (Cibola Lake 25.80/m²).
- **Palo Verde Division:** Mean 5.73/m² (four clams), versus 30.33/m² in 1974–75.
- **Parker Division:** The highest densities on the river, with a range of 0 to about 344/m² upstream. Mean tissue biomass was 77.70 g/m² upstream and 9.96 g/m² downstream. The Parker Main Canal sample gave 2494/m² (single sample).
- **Havasu Division:** Grand mean 24.48/m² in Lake Havasu; the Parker Dam–Headgate Rock reach was higher.
- **Topock Gorge and Mohave Valley:** Low to moderate densities. Clams were larger in Mohave Valley, decreasing sharply 33 km below Davis Dam.
- **Lake Mohave:** Absent from the first 16 km above Davis Dam and from the flowing reach below Hoover Dam; mean 12.9/m² in the intermediate reservoir reach.
- **Lake Mead:** Of 177 samples, 70% contained no clams; mean density 38.7/m² and biomass 16.42 g/m². Wagon Trail Bay in Gregg's Basin reached up to 1,888/m².
- **Lower Grand Canyon:** None in 50 Ponar samples from Pierce Ferry to Separation Canyon.

**Gila, Salt and Verde rivers**
- **Gila River mainstream:** No live clams; shells only.
- **Wellton-Mohawk Canal:** Mean density about 850.30/m² across ten samples, with biomass averaging 64.09 g/m². Clams occurred in every sample.
- **Florence-Casa Grande Canal:** No clams in about 20 grabs.
- **Salt River reservoirs:** Mean densities were 40.85/m² (Saguaro), 21.16 g/m² biomass; other reservoirs were lower. Canal mean was about 349.77/m², with the highest transect mean of 1,114.60/m².
- **Verde River:** No clams in Horseshoe Reservoir. Bartlett Reservoir produced four clams in 25 samples (6.88/m²).

**Reproduction**
- Gonads were largely ready for spawning year-round. Larvae in the ctenidia varied seasonally, with the greatest abundance in Lake Havasu in spring and summer.
- The expected autumn spawning peak was not observed, possibly because sampling coincided with high flows.

**Size structure**
- Canal populations showed bimodal length distributions (modes at about 3–4 mm and 13–14 mm). Lake Mead clams were predominantly 1–4 mm.

**Shell morphology**
- Two shell forms (large, thick, white-nacre; small, thin, purple-nacre) co-occurred. The authors suspected one highly variable species rather than two.

**Tissue safety**
- **Heavy metals:** Arsenic 0.03–1.73 ppm, chromium 0.08–1.17 ppm, cadmium <0.20–5.61 ppm, lead 0.20–2.76 ppm, selenium 0.65–8.28 ppm, mercury <0.01–0.15 ppm, copper 37.95–100.24 ppm.
- **Pesticides:** Of seven compounds tested, only DDD and DDE were detected, attributed to past DDT use.
- **Bacteria:** Fecal coliform exceeded the 230/100 g standard at five of ten sites, and total aerobic plate counts exceeded the 500,000/g standard at three sites.

**Commercial harvest recommendations**
- Harvest is unsuitable in most mainstream reaches of the lower Colorado, Gila, Salt and Verde rivers.
- Canals (Parker, Gila Gravity, Wellton-Mohawk, Salt River canals) had the highest densities, and were close to markets in [[Los Angeles]] and [[Phoenix]].
- Marginal areas include the three major lower Colorado reservoirs, the upper Mohave Valley, and possibly the Salt River reservoirs.
- Harvest in river reaches with fish, wildlife and recreational value should be limited to SCUBA and hand collection, and suction dredges should be prohibited.
- Canal harvest could follow a 3–4 year rotation, and caution is advised on culturing near nuclear plants and golf courses.

**Impacts and trends**
- Corbicula constituted a substantial part of the macrobenthic biomass and may be a valuable food source for [[Predatory Fishes]] and shorebirds.
- Densities appear to have declined between 1974–75 and 1982–83 across most divisions. The authors noted that the change is difficult to assess because of differing methods.
- Reservoir level fluctuations at Lake Havasu may strand clams and harm [[Largemouth Bass]] populations at Lake Mead.
