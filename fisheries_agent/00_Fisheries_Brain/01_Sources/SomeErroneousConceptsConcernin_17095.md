# Some Erroneous Concepts Concerning Artemia Habitats

#invertebrate-zoology #crustacea #anostraca #limnology #artemiidae

## Executive Summary
This short communication by Gerald A. Cole (Arizona State University / University of Toronto) challenges several widely-held assumptions about the physiological tolerances of the brine shrimp *[[Artemia salina]]*. Using field data from newly discovered and previously documented populations across North America, Tibet, and the Middle East, the author demonstrates that *Artemia* survives in habitats with carbonate, sulfate, magnesium, and potassium concentrations far exceeding values previously deemed lethal based on laboratory experiments with pure salt solutions. The paper argues that discrepancies between experimental toxicity thresholds and field observations may stem from inadequate taxonomic resolution — suggesting *Artemia salina* may represent a complex of ecological-physiological races or sibling species rather than a single uniform taxon.

## Background & Description
- *Artemia salina* is classified as a **phyllopod** (branchiopod crustacean), commonly known as the [[Brine Shrimp]].
- No detailed morphological description is provided in this text; the focus is exclusively on **habitat chemistry and physiological tolerance**.
- The author raises the possibility that what is taxonomically lumped as *Artemia salina* may actually constitute multiple **sibling species** or **ecological-physiological races**, implying cryptic morphological/physiological diversity not captured by classical taxonomy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Primarily [[Hypersaline Lakes]] and ponds; most *Artemia* habitats are **thalassohaline** (chemically resembling concentrated seawater — high chloride, low sulfate, low carbonate).
- **Exception habitats (athalassohaline):** Some populations occupy **sulfate-dominated** and **carbonate/soda lake** waters that do not mirror seawater ionic composition.
- **Documented extreme habitats:**
  - Arizona ponds: carbonate 15.8–46.2 g/liter (site of first known Arizona occurrence).
  - Mono Lake, California, and two Arizona salt ponds: thriving populations despite high carbonate.
  - Nebraska ponds (Sheridan & Garden counties, per D.B. McCanaher, pers. comm.): carbonate ranging up to double-digit g/liter; pH values up to **10.0** commonly recorded.
  - Soap Lake, Washington (now inundated due to Columbia Basin Irrigation Project activities): contained *Artemia* despite saturation with **MgCO₃** at times (M. Biondiea, pers. comm.).
  - Tso Kar, Tibet; Kalia potash works, Dead Sea; and McCanaher's Nebraska potash ponds: potassium-rich habitats where *Artemia* nonetheless persists.
- **Ionic tolerance ranges observed in the field:**
  - Carbonate: up to ~46.2 g/liter (vs. previously cited lethal thresholds of 0.79–4.27 g/liter in evaporating ponds).
  - pH: up to 10.0.
  - Potassium: up to 16 g/liter.
  - Na:K ratio: as low as 0.9 (previous experimental work suggested sodium's "antagonistic" protective effect required Na:K ≥ 10).
- **Biogeographic distribution:** Populations documented in North America (Arizona, California, Nebraska, Washington), Central Asia (Tibet), and the Middle East (Dead Sea region) — indicating broad global tolerance of extreme, chemically diverse hypersaline environments.

## Key Findings
1. **Carbonate/pH tolerance underestimated:** Contrary to prior claims that *Artemia* cannot survive in soda lakes or high-carbonate, high-pH waters, field populations thrive at carbonate levels up to ~46 g/liter and pH up to 10.0 — roughly an order of magnitude beyond previously assumed lethal limits.
2. **Sulfate tolerance underestimated:** Some populations occur in athalassohaline sulfate waters exceeding experimentally-derived toxic thresholds (based on pure BaSO₄ solutions).
3. **Magnesium tolerance underestimated:** Populations persist in magnesium concentrations previously considered lethal based on pure MgCl₂ solution experiments (e.g., Soap Lake, WA, saturated with MgCO₃ at times).
4. **Potassium tolerance underestimated:** Despite experimental evidence that high potassium is lethal (mitigated only by high Na:K ratios ≥10), field populations occur at K concentrations up to 16 g/liter with Na:K ratios as low as 0.9 — directly contradicting the sodium-antagonism model.
5. **Taxonomic ambiguity as root cause:** The author proposes that conflicting experimental vs. field results likely reflect **inadequate taxonomic resolution** within *Artemia salina* — the species name may obscure multiple physiologically distinct races or sibling species, each with different ionic tolerance profiles. This has significant implications for how [[Artemia]] toxicology and ecology should be studied going forward.
6. **Methodological caution:** The paper serves as a caution against extrapolating pure-solution laboratory toxicity thresholds to complex, multi-ionic natural hypersaline habitats.
