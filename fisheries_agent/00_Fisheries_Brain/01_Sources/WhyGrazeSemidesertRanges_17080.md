# Why Graze Semidesert Ranges?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #rangeland-ecology

## Executive Summary

S. Clark Martin, a range scientist with the [[Rocky Mountain Forest And Range Experiment Station]], argues that the 80-million-acre [[Southwestern Semidesert]] region should continue to be grazed. The region supports about one million animal units yearlong, serves livestock, wildlife, hunting, and recreation, and is ecologically fragile. The paper weighs [[Grazing Management]] against shrub-to-grassland conversion, population pressure, and the energy efficiency of [[Range Beef Production]]. The author concludes that ranges will remain grazed and that vegetation should be managed in mosaics rather than cleared uniformly.

## Background & Description

The semidesert region lies across Arizona, New Mexico, and Texas. Mild winters, infrequent rainfall, low humidity, and open space have driven recent population growth, especially near cities, and most arid land in Arizona and New Mexico is publicly owned.

Vegetation comprises three range types: [[Southwestern Shrubsteppe]], [[Desert Grassland]], and [[Texas Savanna]]. Grasses, forbs, and palatable shrubs feed herbivores. Larger trees such as mesquite, catclaw, oak, and desert willow provide summer shade and escape cover for big game. Thornier shrubs and cacti offer nesting sites for birds. Vegetation also limits wind and water erosion, although much of the soil surface stays bare.

The desert is fragile. Mature shrubs accumulate through short growth spurts over many years, injuries heal slowly, and litter such as tin cans, bottles, and tires persists because decomposition is slow. Wheel tracks and foot paths often become gullies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rangeland attributes.** The [[U.S. Forest Service]] rated range types from 0 to 5 on several attributes. The three southwestern types ranked well below the national average for soil stability and slightly below for air quality. Desert grassland had better soil stability than the other two types but lower beauty and recreation ratings. All three ranked above the national average for hunting, with shrubsteppe highest (4.20). The conclusion was that converting shrubland to grassland raises livestock output and cuts erosion, but costs scenic beauty and recreation.

**Shrub replacement and grazing.** Many southwestern ranges produce below their potential because past improper grazing allowed shrubs to replace perennial grasses. This reduced rainfall effectiveness, increased erosion, and lowered carrying capacity. Continuous cattle grazing suppresses perennial grasses, and planned rest periods help plants recover. Moderately grazed black grama stands recovered from drought faster than ungrazed stands, according to [[Paulsen And Ares]].

**Population pressure.** Growth is expected to concentrate near existing cities. Remote subdivisions often lack shopping, medical care, schools, and reliable water and sewage, and rising vehicle costs may reduce the appeal of distant living.

**Energy efficiency.** Range beef uses far less fossil fuel per unit of food energy than feedlot beef, which requires 10 or more times as much. [[Cardon]] reported that about two-thirds of the energy needed to produce a 1,000-pound slaughter steer was spent on the 400-pound feeder calf, and that this portion came from range forage requiring no fossil fuel.

## Key Findings

- **Carrying capacity:** the three range types together support about one million animal units yearlong.
- **Calf and beef projection:** assuming 15% of animal units are replacement stock and an 80% calf crop, the ranges would yield 680,000 calves per year. After holding back 15% for replacements, 578,000 could go to market. At 1,000 pounds live weight and 600 pounds dressed, this equals 346,800,000 pounds of beef, enough for 2.89 million Americans at 120 pounds per capita per year. These figures exclude cull cows and bulls.
- **Labor productivity:** farm output per man-hour rose fourfold from before World War I to 1960, but range livestock lags tilled agriculture in productivity gains.
- **Grazing as mosaic management:** the author recommends alternating grassland and shrub patches with scattered mesquite (about a few trees per acre) instead of clearing large areas. This preserves scenic and wildlife value, lets visitors see livestock and game, and sustains forage production.
- **Rancher motivation:** many buyers value ranching as a way of life and will pay a premium, which the author expects will keep ranges grazed despite weak investment returns.
- **Conflicts:** recreation and grazing can conflict, especially where trail bikes disrupt solitude. Criticism of livestock producers is most likely over vegetation management.
- **Conclusion:** grazing will continue on southwestern semidesert ranges, and feeding livestock from cropland is not a wise substitute for range forage.
