# Laboratory hybrid combinations among etheostomatine fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Etheostomatinae #Percidae #Darters #Hybridization #FreshwaterFishes

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by Clark Hubbs presents extensive laboratory data on artificial hybridization among darters (subfamily [[Etheostomatinae]]), building on prior work by [[C. Hubbs and Strawn 1957]] and [[Lindler 1958]]. Gametes were manually stripped from wild-caught and laboratory-reared adults (following [[Strawn and Hubbs 1956]] methodology) and cross-fertilized across numerous species combinations. The paper reports 25 newly documented hybrid combinations plus 7 reciprocal crosses, supplementing the 20 combinations previously reported. The central finding is that **virtually any hybrid combination can be produced between darter species**, regardless of phylogenetic distance, though survival to later ontogenetic stages shows an "all-or-none" pattern correlated with phylogenetic relationship. Notably, interfamilial crosses between darters and sunfishes ([[Lepomis]], family Centrarchidae) failed to survive past yolk-sac absorption, despite some successful fertilization and hatching, establishing a clear ontogenetic barrier at the family level distinct from the permissive pattern seen within Etheostomatinae.

## Background & Description

This paper is a hybridization/experimental physiology study rather than a morphological description; morphological data is referenced only in the context of taxonomic groupings used to interpret phylogenetic relationships:

- **[[Hadropterus sciera]]** (Swain) and **[[Percina caprodes]]** (Rafinesque): Considered closely related by all recent taxonomists; morphological similarity led [[Bailey, Winn and Smith 1954]] to propose combining their genera.
- **[[Etheostoma fonticola]]** and **[[Etheostoma proeliare]]**: Considered closely related based on morphological grounds; both exhibit notably low fertilization percentages in this study, potentially reflecting shared ancestry.
- **[[Etheostoma lepidum]]**, **[[Etheostoma spectabile]]**, and **[[Etheostoma caeruleum]]**: Morphologically similar species showing roughly equal survival rates under laboratory conditions in homospecific matings.
- **[[Ammocrypta vivax]]** (Hay): Notable as the first addition of the genus *Ammocrypta* to the array of darters tested in cross-hybridization experiments.
- Species referenced span multiple darter genera: *Hadropterus*, *Percina*, *Ammocrypta*, and numerous *Etheostoma* species (*E. stigmaeum, E. chlorosomum, E. juliae, E. zonale, E. euzonum, E. radiosum, E. grahami, E. lepidum, E. spectabile, E. caeruleum, E. proeliare, E. fonticola*), plus outgroup controls using *Lepomis microlophus* and *L. punctatus* (Centrarchidae).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- All study species are **bottom-inhabiting darters** (Etheostomatinae), small benthic percid fishes native to North American freshwater systems.
- Source populations were drawn from geographically distinct systems with contrasting water chemistry: hard water (Austin, Texas) versus soft water (eastern Louisiana) environments.
- **[[Etheostoma radiosum]]** specimens were sourced from near Nacogdoches, Texas.
- Water chemistry mismatch is proposed as a confounding ecological factor: the author attributes the poor survival of *Hadropterus nigrofasciatus* × *Etheostoma stigmaeum* hybrids not to phylogenetic distance but to the adverse physiological effects of hard Austin water on fish lineages adapted to soft, eastern Louisiana water.
- Interfamilial ecological separation is evidenced by the reproductive incompatibility between [[Etheostomatinae]] darters and [[Lepomis]] sunfishes (Centrarchidae), which, despite co-occurring in similar freshwater habitats, show a hard ontogenetic barrier to hybrid viability.
- Dietary deficiencies in maternal parents (carotenoid deficiency; see [[C. Hubbs and Stavenhagen 1958]]) and dissolved solid concentrations were noted as environmental/nutritional variables affecting egg and larval survival, underscoring the sensitivity of darter reproduction to habitat water quality.

## Key Findings

- **Near-universal hybridization capacity**: Virtually any two darter species can be experimentally hybridized to produce viable larvae, regardless of phylogenetic distance — supporting and extending the conclusions of [[C. Hubbs and Strawn 1957a]].
- **Data scope**: This paper adds 25 previously unreported hybrid combinations, 7 new reciprocal crosses, and supplementary data on most previously reported crosses, plus fertilization data on 3 additional controls.
- **Phylogeny vs. survival rate — no simple correlation**: Despite the close taxonomic relationship between *[[Hadropterus sciera]]* and *[[Percina caprodes]]* (proposed generic merger by Bailey, Winn & Smith 1954), hybrids using their eggs survived far better when fertilized by *[[Etheostoma spectabile]]* sperm than by each other's sperm. This result is attributed to the poor performance of both parental species under laboratory conditions (post-hatching mortality from inadequate feeding technique) rather than true phylogenetic proximity.
- **Reciprocal cross caveat**: When *Etheostoma* species served as egg donors instead, crosses with *P. caprodes* or *H. sciera* sperm were not particularly successful — survival tends to approximate that of the most successful parental form under the specific experimental conditions, not a phylogenetic signal.
- **Low fertilization as phylogenetic marker**: *[[Etheostoma fonticola]]* and *[[Etheostoma proeliare]]*, considered closely related, both show notably low fertilization percentages, possibly reflecting shared ancestry.
- **Ontogenetic "all-or-none" barrier**: While fertilization/hatching can occur even between highly divergent taxa (including interfamilial darter × sunfish crosses — 8 hybrids hatched across 5 experiments), all interfamilial (*Lepomis* × darter) hybrids died before yolk-sac absorption. This suggests capacity to reach a given developmental stage is governed by a threshold ("all or none") mechanism tied to phylogenetic relationship, even though hybrid frequency/survival rate is not.
- **Confounding environmental factors**: Water hardness mismatches (e.g., Austin hard water vs. native soft water for Louisiana-adapted *H. nigrofasciatus* and *E. stigmaeum*) can produce spuriously low survival unrelated to phylogeny.
- **Conclusion on using hybrid frequency as phylogenetic index**: The author cautions that hybrid frequency in lab or field should not be used as a direct index of phylogenetic closeness without controlling for non-phylogenetic factors such as gamete viability duration ([[C. Hubbs 1957]]), nutrition, and water chemistry.
- **Taxonomic implication**: Results indirectly challenge simplistic morphology-based generic classifications (e.g., merging *Hadropterus* and *Percina*) when hybridization survival data are taken at face value, though the author argues this apparent contradiction is an artifact of experimental conditions rather than true phylogenetic signal.
