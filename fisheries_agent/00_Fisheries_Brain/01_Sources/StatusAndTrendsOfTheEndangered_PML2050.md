# Status and Trends of Endangered Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ptychocheilus-lucius

## Executive Summary

This study assessed the status of the upper Colorado River population of the federally endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, recently renamed [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]) by estimating adult numbers, measuring reproduction and recruitment, examining size-structure trends, and comparing results with historical accounts. Adults and subadults were captured across 279 km of river from 1991 to 1994, and larvae and age-0 fish were sampled from 1986 to 1994. The upper 98 km held an estimated 253 adults on average, with an annual adult survival rate of 0.86. Recruitment was highly variable: a strong pulse of fish from the 1985–1987 year-classes dominated the lower reach, and no comparable year-class followed. The authors conclude that the population is vulnerable, that recruitment is the most important demographic factor for persistence, and that abundance is far lower than historical accounts suggest.

## Background & Description

The Colorado Squawfish was historically distributed through the warmwater reaches of the [[Colorado River Basin]], from Wyoming, Utah, and Colorado south to the Gulf of California. By the 1970s it had been extirpated from the lower basin below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and from parts of the upper basin, owing to major alterations of the riverine environment. The species lost a large share of its former range and was federally listed as endangered in 1967. The source text gives the percentage lost, but the figure is garbled by OCR, so it is not reported here.

Three populations persist. The [[Green River]] system, including the White and Yampa rivers, supports the largest and perhaps most viable population. The [[San Juan River]] population is the smallest. The upper Colorado River population has relatively low catch rates of adults and young, suggesting limited viability. The authors argue that assessing these smaller populations is central to species-level viability.

Adults and subadults were defined as subadults 250–500 mm long and adults above that size range. The study area ran from the [[Green River]] confluence (river km 0.0) upstream to river km 298.1 at Palisade, Colorado, where two diversion dams block upstream movement. It also included the lower 3.5 km of the [[Gunnison River]]. The study area was divided into an upper reach (98 km) and a lower reach (181 km), separated by the 19-km Westwater Canyon. The upper reach was further split into lower, middle, and upper subreaches based on discharge, gradient, and landform.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Colorado Squawfish aggregate in low-velocity [[Backwater Habitat]] during spring runoff, when main-channel flows increase sharply. Because these backwaters concentrate fish, they are the primary sampling targets. The authors describe backwaters and embayments as likely nursery habitat for young fish.

Recruitment in the upper reach appears to depend largely on colonization from the lower reach, since very few individuals under 450 mm were found there in the preceding 15 years. The text attributes the population's vulnerability to low adult numbers combined with sporadic pulses of recruitment. Threats discussed include habitat alteration, dam-related changes to flow, floodplain constriction from levees and dikes, the stabilizing effects of exotic riparian vegetation, and the potential introduction of more nonnative species, which may compete with or prey on young squawfish. The authors also raise the role of genetic problems in small, isolated populations and cite [[Metapopulation Dynamics]] as a framework for understanding population persistence.

## Key Findings

**Adult abundance and survival**
- Estimated adult abundance in the upper 98 km averaged **253 individuals**.
- In the lower 181 km, subadults and adults combined averaged **344 individuals**.
- The best [[Cormack-Jolly-Seber Model]] estimates of annual adult survival were **0.845** and **0.860**. These two models differed by only 0.034 AIC units, so both were considered plausible.
- The authors judge adult survival to be fairly constant, in contrast to recruitment.

**Recruitment and year-classes**
- A sizable pulse of subadults 300–400 mm long was found in the lower reach in 1991. These fish came from three year-classes (1985–1987) and had spread throughout the river by 1992.
- Scale analysis of fish captured in 1991 (N = 15, 259–383 mm) and 1992 (N = 22, 307–479 mm) assigned hatch years as follows: 22% in 1985, 46% in 1986, 21% in 1987, and 5% in 1988.
- The 1985–1987 cohorts were the largest produced since before 1977. No similarly strong year-class was produced in the following years.
- Years of origin for these fish coincided with years of higher-than-average larval and age-0 catch rates in the upper reach. The authors note that 1986 and perhaps 1987 were comparatively good reproductive years there.

**Catch rates and trends**
- Catch rates of adults in the upper reach did not increase significantly during 1991–1994.
- Catch rates of smaller fish increased fivefold over the same period. The source's abstract gives the length cutoff, but OCR garbled the number, so it is not reported here.
- Upper-subreach fish shifted toward smaller sizes between 1991–1992 and 1993–1994 (K-S test, P = 0.03). The proportion of fish under 550 mm also rose significantly (P = 0.01), which the authors read as an influx of young fish from the lower reach.

**Size-structure comparisons**
- Size distributions of upper- and middle-subreach fish (P = 0.003), and of upper- and lower-subreach fish (P = 0.0002), differed significantly. Middle- and lower-subreach distributions did not differ (P = 0.11).
- Middle-subreach fish from 1974–1976 were significantly smaller than those from 1991–1995 (P = 0.00001). Of fish from 1974–1976, 35% were under 450 mm, and the proportion under 550 mm was significantly higher than in recent years.

**Historical comparison and conclusions**
- Abundance appears much lower than historical accounts suggest.
- Low adult numbers and sporadic recruitment pulses may make the upper Colorado River population vulnerable to extirpation.
- Recruitment is highly variable and is likely the most important demographic factor for persistence.
- The authors recommend that managers consider how stochastic recruitment failure interacts with habitat condition and nonnative species, and that efforts to maintain early life-stage survival (such as backwater nursery protection) be prioritized. The work was supported by the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]].

**Methods used**
- Adults and subadults were captured with trammel nets using a "[[Scare and Snare Sampling]]" technique in backwaters and supplemented with [[Electrofishing]]. Each spring, three passes were made through the upper reach and two through the lower reach, in 1991–1994.
- Fish were marked with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging]] (PIT) tags for [[Capture-Recapture Analysis]]. Models were compared with [[Akaike's Information Criterion]]. Abundance was estimated with closed-model capture-recapture (program CAPTURE).
- Larvae were collected weekly with a hand seine from 18 sections (3.2 km each) in the middle and upper subreaches. Age-0 fish were sampled once a year with a beach seine from 8-km sections.
- Length-frequency comparisons used the [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]], and CPUE comparisons used [[Kruskal-Wallis Test]] methods.
