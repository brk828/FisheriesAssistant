# Nonnative Fish Are Homogenizing U.S. Freshwater Fish Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This document is a forwarded email (June 19, 2000) containing a reprinted [[Los Angeles Times]] article by Usha Lee McFarling on the spread of nonnative fish in U.S. ponds, lakes, rivers and streams. The article reports, citing a study by [[Frank Rahel]] (University of Wyoming, published in *Science*), that America's native fish fauna is becoming "homogenized" as a small set of widespread, hardy species replaces regionally distinct native assemblages. The main driver is widespread introduction of nonnative species, not extinction. Habitat alteration such as dam building and marsh draining further disadvantages native fish. The article also covers the legacy of historical stocking, the costs of removal, illegal introductions, and emerging policy and management responses.

## Background & Description

The email was forwarded by Rob Bettaso of the Arizona Game and Fish Department to a list of state, federal and university fisheries contacts, with the note that the summary table at the end was worth sharing. The original article draws on:

- Rahel's analysis comparing states' historical and current fish species, using state-by-state species sharing as the metric.
- Quotes from [[Peter B. Moyle]] (UC Davis, California native fishes), [[Michael J. Mac]] (USGS Biological Resources Division, fisheries program), [[Norville Prosser]] (American Sportfishing Association), and [[Chuck Knutson]] (California Department of Fish and Game).
- A summary table of "successful invaders" showing native range and the number of states (out of about 48 surveyed; [[Alaska]] and [[Hawaii]] were excluded) where each species is now found.

The table lists 12 introduced species, with state counts ranging from 28 to 48. The most widespread were [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]] (each 48 states), and [[Goldfish]] (42), [[Rainbow Trout]] (41), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (45), and [[Green Sunfish]] (45). Native ranges were mostly Eastern U.S., with carp and goldfish from Eurasia and rainbow trout from the Western U.S. [[Bluegill]] was the least widespread at 31 states.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Homogenization:** Pairs of states now share more species on average than before European settlement. [[Arizona]] and [[Montana]] historically shared no fish species; today they share 33.
- **Mechanism:** Extinction was long expected to drive biodiversity loss, but the article reports that widespread introductions are the main cause of blending. Habitat changes such as dam building and marsh draining harm vulnerable natives and favor hardy exotics.
- **Warm-water tolerance:** Most Western fish cannot tolerate warmer Eastern waters, but Eastern species thrive in Western reservoirs, which are warmer than natural mountain streams and lakes. Rainbow trout is an exception among Western natives in tolerating warm conditions.
- **Sources of introductions:** Aquarium escapes (swordtails, guppies, piranhas), discarded bait (e.g., [[Red Shiner]] in [[Los Angeles Basin]] streams), and historical stocking by fisheries agencies.
- **Historical legacy:** Late-19th-century officials stocked Western lakes with [[Northern Pike]] and [[Walleye]], and in 1882 alone more than a quarter million [[Common Carp]] were distributed nationally. Some managers introduced [[Brook Trout]] into remote streams holding [[Cutthroat Trout]]. Rahel describes this as "a legacy of fisheries management of the past 120 to 150 years."
- **Taxonomic scope:** The article notes similar homogenization in mammals, birds and insects.

Key data points:

- Of 17 widely introduced species, 12 are game fish.
- Nevada has 85 fish species, 24 of which come from the East.
- Kentucky has 212 species (rendered "2L2" in the OCR text), only one of which, rainbow trout, is a native Westerner.
- In California, two-thirds of the state's 116 native fish require special protection.

Specific threats documented:

- [[Striped Bass]] prey on downstream-migrating [[Chinook Salmon]] in the Central Valley.
- [[Sacramento Perch]] compete for spawning sites with transplanted [[Bluegill]] and [[Green Sunfish]].
- Illegal introduction of [[Lake Trout]] into [[Yellowstone Lake]] threatens the native cutthroat trout fishery, and removal efforts have had limited success.

## Key Findings

1. **Biodiversity is blending, not just declining.** Fish faunas across the U.S. are becoming more similar because of nonnative introductions beyond native ranges. Three fish (largemouth bass, black crappie, common carp) are now found in each of the lower 48 states.
2. **Introductions drive homogenization more than extinction.** Rahel's state-by-state comparison shows increasing species sharing over time.
3. **Habitat change amplifies the problem.** Dams and marsh drainage weaken native fish and favor exotics. Moyle: "You change the habitat, then you send in the exotics, and the poor native fish don't stand a chance."
4. **Eradication is difficult and costly.** Once established, exotics are "almost impossible to remove." The removal of [[White Bass]] from [[Lake Kaweah]] (Tulare County) cost taxpayers $7.5 million. An attempt to poison [[Lake Davis]] near Portola to eliminate northern pike failed.
5. **Stocking history shapes the present.** Early and ongoing introductions for angling created the current pattern; Rahel views it as a long-term legacy of past management.
6. **Policy is changing.** Recent laws address the problem. In California, importing nonnative aquatic species into state waters carries a fine and up to one year in prison (the fine amount is garbled in the OCR text, appearing as "9501000"). Knutson, the California biologist cited, also pointed to an anonymous illegal-transplant reporting hotline.
7. **Management options.** Moyle showed that altering water flow near dams increases native [[Tule Perch]], [[Sacramento Pikeminnow]] and rainbow trout downstream. He advocates rapid eradication of new introductions and proposed "SWAT teams for exotic species" in California.
8. **Responsible-release guidance.** Unwanted aquarium fish should be given away or euthanized, and baitfish should be returned to the shop or killed, not released.
9. **Angler and stocking debate.** Prosser of the American Sportfishing Association supports stocking only when "scientifically based" and accepts habitat protection; Moyle criticized anglers who "believe themselves to be amateur biologists."
