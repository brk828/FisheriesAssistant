# Salmon Spawning Ground Surveys, Salmon River Drainage (1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #anadromous-fish #population-assessment

## Executive Summary

This annual progress report documents the 1966 [[Chinook Salmon]] spawning ground surveys conducted in the [[Salmon River]] drainage, Idaho, from August 1 to September 30, 1966. Spring and summer chinook salmon spawning runs into Idaho in 1966 were larger than those of 1965, though the summer-run chinook trend line continues downward. A total of 7,311 redds were counted in 1966, representing a 73 percent increase over the 4,235 redds counted in 1965. Redd counts for each run paralleled their respective counts at [[Ice Harbor Dam]], continuing a four-year correlation pattern. The surveys employed aerial counts, ground counts, and experimental underwater diving observations to assess spawning escapement across major tributaries of the Salmon River system.

## Background & Description

The [[Idaho Fish and Game Department]] has conducted annual chinook salmon redd trend counts in the [[Salmon River]] drainage since 1951. The surveys target major spawning areas to obtain an index of spawning escapement abundance and to determine sex ratio and age-class composition. The majority of counts are made from low-flying aircraft, with ground counts conducted on streams where redds are difficult to observe due to redd concentrations, overhanging brush, or other conditions. Some streams with suitable conditions are surveyed from small rafts.

[[Chinook Salmon]] in this system are classified as spring-run or summer-run based on arrival time, spawning time, and redd condition. Spring-run fish utilize streams including [[Bear Valley Creek]], [[Elk Creek]], [[Marsh Creek]], [[Sulphur Creek]], [[Lemhi River]], upper [[East Fork]], upper [[Yankee Fork]], upper [[Valley Creek]], [[Alturas Lake Creek]], and upper [[Salmon River]]. Summer-run fish spawn primarily in [[Johnson Creek]], [[Lake Creek]] (Secesh River), [[South Fork Salmon River]], [[Loon Creek]], [[Pahsimeroi River]], lower East Fork, and lower Salmon River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The chinook salmon runs are monitored at three downstream dams—[[Bonneville Dam]], [[McNary Dam]], and [[Ice Harbor Dam]]—which provide indices of fish numbers entering Idaho streams. In 1966, counts at all three dams were larger than in 1965. At Bonneville Dam, combined spring and summer runs totaled 184,666 fish (+15% from 1965). At McNary Dam, the combined total was 148,022 fish (+199% from 1965). At Ice Harbor Dam, the combined count reached 60,864 fish (+126% from 1965).

The correlation between dam counts and redd counts in spawning areas has been consistent over the past four years. Spring chinook redd counts in designated spawning areas totaled 4,226 in 1966 compared to 2,498 in 1965. Summer chinook redd counts totaled 1,928 in 1966 compared to 906 in 1965.

Age-class composition was determined using fork length measurements: for spring chinook, age class 3₂ = <24 inches, age class 4₂ = 24-31 inches, age class 5₂ = >31 inches; for summer chinook, age class 3₂ = <25 inches, age class 4₂ = 25-34 inches, age class 5₂ = ≥34 inches. Age class 5₂ fish comprised the majority of the spring chinook spawning escapement in 1966, with [[Lemhi River]] being the only spring chinook stream containing predominantly 4₂ fish. Summer-run chinook in the [[South Fork Salmon River]] drainage contained more 4₂ fish than other year classes.

## Key Findings

**Redd Count Results by Major Streams (1966 vs 1965):**
- [[South Fork]] drainage: 1,230 redds (906 in 1965)
- [[Middle Fork]] drainage: 2,208 redds (1,286 in 1965)
- [[North Fork]]: 70 redds (5 in 1965)
- [[Lemhi River]]: 819 redds (454 in 1965)
- [[Pahsimeroi River]]: 113 redds (16 in 1965)
- [[East Fork]] drainage: 806 redds (300 in 1965)
- [[Yankee Fork]]: 454 redds (233 in 1965)
- [[Valley Creek]]: 403 redds (261 in 1965)
- [[Alturas Lake Creek]]: 119 redds (101 in 1965)
- Upper [[Salmon River]]: 1,089 redds (673 in 1965)

**Experimental Live Fish Counts:** On July 21, 1966, two observers using underwater diving gear (wet suits, facemasks, snorkels, hand tallies) floated a four-mile section of [[Bear Valley Creek]] from Stanley bridge to the upper weir. One observer counted 442 chinook and the other counted 428 chinook. The observers found that floating directly opposite one another near opposite stream banks allowed accurate counts even in large resting pools containing over 100 fish. Fish moved away from divers to pool ends, then darted single file upstream between observers. This method proved superior to single-observer center-stream counts where fish swam by on both sides, making accurate counts impossible.

**Fish Transfer Experiment:** A total of 112 summer chinook salmon were transferred by [[Idaho Power Company]] trucks from the fish trap below [[Hells Canyon Dam]] on the [[Snake River]] to [[Summit Creek]], a tributary of the [[Secesh River]]. Wire mesh was placed across a log jam at Summit Creek mouth to prevent emigration. Surveys revealed fish moved primarily upstream, with seven fish reaching an impassable cascade approximately one mile above the release site, passing through four log jams judged impassable. By September 4, seven redds were counted above the release site and three redds below. Dead females examined had immature ovaries, suggesting premature mortality. Most observed movement was upstream; no fish were detected at the screened log jam, though emigration through openings could not be ruled out.

**Sex Ratios:** Percentage of females in spring chinook spawning areas ranged from 44% ([[Bear Valley Creek]]) to 65% ([[Lemhi River]]), with combined streams averaging 51%. Summer chinook showed 32% females in [[Johnson Creek]], 41% in [[Secesh River]] drainage, and 38% in [[South Fork Salmon River]], with combined average of 38%.

**Management Recommendations:** The report emphasizes that redd count, age-class composition, and sex ratio data are essential for effective management of chinook salmon runs. No further changes in counting procedures should be made, as trend data becomes increasingly difficult to interpret with methodology changes. New ground redd counts should supplement, not substitute for, aerial counts. Live chinook counts using underwater diving gear should be continued and evaluated as they may provide valuable trend data or serve as additional means of evaluating aerial redd counts. Counts should be made just prior to spawning activity onset.
