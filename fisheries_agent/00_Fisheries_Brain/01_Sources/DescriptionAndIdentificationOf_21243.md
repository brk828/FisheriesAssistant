# Description and Identification of June, Utah, and Mountain Sucker Larvae and Early Juveniles

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Utah Division of Wildlife Resources publication (Publication No. 88-8, Snyder and Muth, Larval Fish Laboratory, Colorado State University, July 1988) documents the morphological development and diagnostic characters of the larvae and early juveniles of three catostomids in [[Utah Lake]] and its tributaries: the endangered [[June Sucker]] (*Chasmistes liorus*), the [[Utah Sucker]] (*Catostomus ardens*), and the [[Mountain Sucker]] (*Catostomus platyrhynchus*). The work provides a combined larval terminology, measurement and counting protocols, a set of identification keys, species accounts, and detailed comparative data drawn from reared and field-collected specimens. Its stated purpose is to make accurate identification possible so that June sucker reproduction, spawning, and nursery habitat can be evaluated for recovery.

## Background & Description

The June sucker is endemic to Utah Lake and is federally and state protected as endangered. The authors argue that the present Utah Lake population is the product of introgressive hybridization with Utah sucker during severe environmental stress in the 1930s, and that the pure form (*Chasmistes liorus liorus*) has probably been extinct since then; the current form is treated as *C. liorus mictus*. The "webug sucker" (*Catostomus fecundus*) is considered by some authors to be a localized hybrid rather than a distinct species.

Adult June suckers may reach 60 cm total length, Utah suckers similarly, while adult mountain suckers seldom exceed 20 cm. The three species share the subfamily Catostominae and tribe Catostomini. Earlier published descriptions of June sucker metalarvae and juveniles (Shirley 1983) were based largely on misidentified mountain sucker specimens, which the authors identify as a substantive correction to the literature.

The study uses a combined developmental terminology that integrates [[Hardy Larval Terminology]], [[Ahlstrom Flexion Terminology]] (preflexion, flexion, postflexion), and the protolarva/mesolarva/metalarva phases of Snyder (1981). Fin-ray appearance is used as the transition criterion between phases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat contrasts:** Utah suckers occupy a wide range of habitats, from deep cold lakes to small warm streams. Young are usually found in tributaries or near shore. Mountain suckers are typically found in cold, clear rivers and mountain streams and are rarely collected in lakes except possibly as larvae. June suckers are lakesuckers; adults enter tributaries mainly during spawning.
- **Spawning:** All three are non-guarding, open-substrate lithophils. June sucker spawns in late spring and early summer at daily mean water temperatures of 12–13 °C, over coarse gravel to small cobble at depths of about 0.3–0.8 m with bottom velocities well under 0.3 m/s. Utah sucker spawns mid to late spring, and mountain sucker late spring to early summer between 11 and 19 °C over gravel riffles.
- **Incubation:** June sucker eggs hatch in 4 days at 21 °C and 10 days at 11 °C. Utah sucker hatches in 8–9 days at 17 °C. Mountain sucker hatches in 7–8 days at about 15 °C.
- **Hybridization:** Hybrids of June and Utah suckers are documented and may be hard to identify; hybrids of either with mountain sucker are considered possible under unusual conditions.
- **Preservation:** Specimens were fixed in 10% formalin and stored in 3% buffered formalin. Alcohol-preserved specimens showed excessive shrinkage and were excluded from morphometric analysis.

## Key Findings

**Scope and sample base**
- 151 specimens were intensively analyzed for morphometrics and meristics; several hundred more were documented for size, developmental state, and pigmentation.
- Reared series came from Utah and Bear Lake parentage (June sucker from the Provo River; Utah sucker from Bear Lake; mountain sucker from Willow Creek and Ways Gulch, Colorado). Wild mountain sucker material came from Montana, Nevada, Colorado, and the Provo and Spanish Fork Rivers, Utah.

**Identification characters**
- **Mountain sucker:** Protolarvae and mesolarvae typically show a nearly continuous ventral midline pigment line from heart to vent (at least a couple of melanin spots always present). Dorsal midline pigment is usually nearly continuous. Metalarvae show peritoneal pigmentation. Juveniles have notched lip corners, a "scraper" ridge on the lower jaw by about 30–40 mm SL, smaller scales appearing later (over 30 mm SL), and a distinct fontanelle narrowing to absence.
- **June and Utah suckers:** Separation is often not possible. June sucker larvae are slightly smaller relative to developmental state. Utah suckers transform to the juvenile state before the other species, except some June suckers. The fontanelle width differs: 20–30% of length in mountain sucker, 35–45% in Utah sucker, and 45–65% in June sucker in the earliest juveniles, with actual widths of less than 0.7 mm, 0.9–1.2 mm, and 1.1–1.4 mm respectively.
- **Mouth and lips:** Adult June sucker lips have well-separated, nearly smooth lobes. Adult Utah sucker has a medially divided lower lip with adjacent lobes. Adult mountain sucker has a shallowly divided, well-connected lower lip with prominent papillae.

**Development timing**
- Pigment first appears at about 8 mm SL in mountain sucker, 9–10 mm in June sucker, and 11–12 mm (rarely 10) in Utah sucker.
- Yolk was assimilated earliest in mountain sucker (protolarvae), while June and Utah suckers retained notable yolk into the mesolarval phase.
- At the juvenile stage, full lateral scale series appear earlier in June and Utah suckers (below 30 mm SL) than in mountain sucker (above 30 mm SL).

**Meristic summaries (means, from Table 2)**
- Principal dorsal fin rays: June sucker 12 (range 11–14), Utah sucker 12 (10–14), mountain sucker 10 (8–13).
- Lateral series scales: June sucker 55–61, Utah sucker 62, mountain sucker 75–92.
- Gill rakers (adults): June sucker 38, Utah sucker 28–34, mountain sucker 23–37 (from species tables).
- Vertebrae (adults): June sucker 45–47, Utah sucker 47–48, mountain sucker 46 (42–48).

**Keys**
- Separate keys were built for protolarvae, flexion mesolarvae, postflexion mesolarvae, metalarvae, and young-of-the-year juveniles (under 50 mm TL). They rely on standard length, myomere counts to the dorsal and preanal finfold origins (counted using Siefert's 1969 method), yolk length, melanin dot counts on the ventral and dorsal midlines, fin-ray counts, gut phase, eye diameter, and fin lengths. When characters overlap, the keys give two or three possible species, with an asterisk marking the most probable one.

**Recommendations for practitioners**
- Verify identifications with multiple diagnostic characters because intra- and inter-regional variation is substantial.
- Treat unresolvable specimens as tentative, marking them with "?" or leaving them as "unidentified Catostomidae".
- Use dilute buffered formalin rather than alcohol for storage.
- Use collection date and location only cautiously when delimiting species, because misidentified larvae have already distorted earlier spawning and nursery conclusions for June sucker.

**Ecological implications**
- Earlier conclusions that young June suckers remained in the Provo River from summer 1982 to January 1983 were probably based largely on mountain sucker material and should be reconsidered.
- Accurate larval identification is needed to evaluate June sucker spawning, nursery, and recovery requirements.
