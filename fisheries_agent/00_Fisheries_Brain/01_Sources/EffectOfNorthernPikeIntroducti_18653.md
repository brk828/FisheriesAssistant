# Effect of Northern Pike Introductions on an Overabundant Crappie Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife Special Report (No. 31, April 1973, by T. G. Powell) evaluates whether [[Northern Pike]] stocking can control an overabundant [[White Crappie]] population in [[Carbody Reservoir]], a small [[Irrigation Reservoir]] on the Colorado high plains. The crappie population showed a cyclic abundance pattern. After a viable pike population was established by stocking in 1969, crappie numbers were reduced and average size of age group III fish increased, though the cycle still appeared to persist at study's end. The report closes with stocking recommendations for pike sizes and densities. It was conducted under [[Federal Aid In Sport Fish Restoration]] Project F-34-R.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Many U.S. regions report high panfish densities, causing slow growth, small average size and reduced angler acceptance. Predator stocking has had limited success, though northern pike were considered the most promising predator (Beard 1971; Doxtater 1967; McCarraher 1959).
- **Study setting:** Colorado small warmwater irrigation reservoirs (under 500 acres) lie at 3,400 to 5,000 ft elevation, receive 12 to 15 inches of precipitation annually, and have a 140 to 165 day nominal growing season. Effective growing season for warmwater species is usually under 100 days, because surface temperatures seldom reach 85 °F and typically average 70 to 75 °F in summer.
- **Life-history constraint:** In Colorado most predators do not mature until their third summer, while most panfish spawn in their second summer, giving panfish a head start and keeping predator numbers low.
- **Carbody Reservoir:** Located in Weld County at 4,711 ft, with 49.4 surface acres, 361.9 acre-feet capacity, maximum depth 16 ft, average depth 7.3 ft and average annual drawdown of 2 ft. The bottom is fine sand, clay and silt. Its name derives from old car bodies dumped along the dam.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** Phenolphthalein (ph-th) alkalinity averaged 9 ppm and total alkalinity 150 ppm, indicating reasonable productivity.
- **Vegetation:** Rooted aquatic vegetation was essentially absent.
- **Zooplankton:** Averaged 7,338 organisms per liter (4,081 Cladocera, 3,257 Copepoda).
- **Benthos:** Very limited, averaging 17.4 individuals per square foot. Composition was 86.2% *Chironomus* spp., 13.6% Planorbidae snails and 0.2% damselfly larvae (Zygoptera).
- **Oxygen:** Bottom waters became oxygen-depleted each June after thermal stratification until fall overturn. Benthic organisms were restricted above about six feet. Carbon dioxide was present in bottom waters, but pH never fell below 7.3 and averaged 8.0 during depletion.
- **Fish community:** Ten species were recorded, including white and black crappie, carp (the most abundant species in 1965), black bullhead, channel catfish, orangespotted sunfish, white sucker, bluegill, green sunfish, yellow perch and northern pike.

## Key Findings

- **Sampling method:** Standardized graduated-mesh gillnets (125 ft long, 6 ft deep) were set in the same week, duration and location each year, following [[Carlander 1953]] and [[Moyle 1950]]. The nets were biased against young-of-the-year and age group I crappie, so this bias had to be considered when interpreting results.
- **Pre-introduction decline:** Netting from 1965 to 1969 (excluding 1967) showed a steady, appreciable decline in white crappie numbers with no significant increase in average size. Catch per 100 ft of net per hour fell from 0.69 (1965) to 0.21 (1969). Pike predation could not explain the decline.
- **Abundance cycle hypothesis:** Following [[Swingle And Swingle 1967]], the crappie population was judged to follow a 3 to 5 year dominant-year-class cycle. The large 1967 year class was later linked to intraspecific competition and slowed growth.
- **Diet:** Stomach contents were 70% zooplankton, 18% aquatic insects and 12% terrestrial insects. Crappie larger than about 2 years could no longer filter-feed and had to take individual plankters, so growth approached maintenance levels.
- **Pike stocking history:** In 1969, 8,240 fingerlings (50 mm) and 2,000 subadults (228 to 305 mm) were stocked. Subadults were stocked again in 1970 and 1971 (4,000 each year). A 20-inch length limit took effect in January 1970. An earlier pike-fry program (1,000 fry per acre annually for three years, 147,000 fry in total) yielded only one surviving adult.
- **Crappie response:** Crappie catch rose to 0.80 per 100 ft per hour in 1970, the cycle's peak year, then fell to 0.41 in 1971 and 0.17 in 1972. Pike catches rose from 0.07 (1970) to 0.14 (1971 and 1972). No age group I fish were captured in 1971 or 1972.
- **Growth:** Age group III crappie grew significantly larger after pike introduction, surpassing the pre-introduction age group IV in length. Overall average size did not increase enough to create a widely acceptable fishery.
- **Cycle status:** The cycle was reduced to about 3 years but still appeared present at study's end. The 1972 catch matched the 1969 low point. Termination in 1972 was caused by time and landowner restrictions.
- **Conclusions:** Once established, [[Northern Pike]] effectively reduce crappie numbers. Stocking large predators cannot correct inadequate forage fish and invertebrate supplies, so some form of [[Food Chain Management]] is needed to accelerate growth. Most Colorado crappie reservoirs have little littoral habitat, causing loss of forage fish to predators before adequate reproduction.
- **Stocking recommendations:** Stock a minimum of 50 two-inch pike or 25 six-inch pike per surface acre, for at least two successive years. Protect pike with a 20-inch size limit. Higher stocking rates act more quickly but can slow pike growth (200 pike of 8 to 12 inches per acre slowed first-year growth to 5 inches). Pike of 12 to 16 inches (5 to 8 oz) are just becoming effective predators and should be retained as long as possible.
