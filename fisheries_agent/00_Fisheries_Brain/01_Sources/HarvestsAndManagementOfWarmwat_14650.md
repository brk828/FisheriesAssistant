# Angler Harvest and Management of Warm-Water Fish Populations in Massachusetts Lakes, Ponds, and Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #esocidae #ictaluridae #moronidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Richard H. Stroud]] and [[Harold Bitzer]] (Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game, published in 1955) measured angler harvest of six warm-water game and pan fishes using [[Fish Tagging]] across 107 Massachusetts waters from September 1950 to October 1953. Of 12,978 tagged fish, 8,038 were game species and 4,940 were pan fish. Anglers harvested an estimated 23% of game fish but only 4% of pan fish, so game fish were taken roughly six times as readily. The authors concluded that outlet screening was unnecessary, that [[Salvage Stocking]] for maintenance stocking was not cost-justified (over $11 per pound reaching the creel), and that management should favor [[Chain Pickerel]] and [[Yellow Perch]] while eliminating [[White Perch]] and, where possible, [[Largemouth Bass]] in pickerel waters.

## Background & Description

The study addressed a gap in management data: before 1950, virtually no angler-harvest information existed for Northeastern states, and tagging of species other than black basses was rarely recorded. Harvest rates and angler behavior were considered essential for explaining poor fishing and identifying solutions alongside population studies.

Six species were tagged, all at legal and desirable sizes (black basses 9.5 inches and up, pickerel 13.5 inches and up, panfishes 8.0 inches and up):

- **Game fish:** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Chain Pickerel]] were jaw-tagged with No. 3 strap tags.
- **Pan fish:** [[Brown Bullhead]], [[White Perch]], and [[Yellow Perch]] were cheek-tagged.

Fish were drawn from two sources:

- **Native fish (4,829):** captured in fyke nets, tagged, and released in their home ponds.
- **Salvaged fish (8,149):** taken from closed water supplies by drainage or netting, tagged, and transferred to open public waters.

Tagged fish covered about 18,750 acres of surface water, an average of roughly 2/3 fish per acre.

Recoveries relied mainly on voluntary angler mailings, stimulated by newspaper publicity, up to 35 posters per pond, and a reward system with partial creel census at two ponds. Tag reliability was tested in year-long trials in drainable ponds, where tags appeared to stay secure, though their effect on growth was uncertain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population turnover and natural mortality.** Chain pickerel showed rapid natural mortality. Fish not harvested in the season they were present rarely survived to contribute to later fishing. The authors noted that large pickerel (18 to 24 inches) in the sport fishery depended on a small surviving fraction. They suggested that environmental factors, including competition and predation by pan fish on young pickerel, limited juvenile survival more than angler harvest did.

**Pan fish and game fish balance.** Pan fish were far more abundant than game fish, yet most were lightly exploited. The authors argued that thinning of pan fish by netting, chemicals, and drainage was compensating for anglers' failure to harvest them. They also noted that bass were poorly adapted to Massachusetts waters, partly because of short growing seasons.

**Pond accessibility and popularity.** Harvest varied widely between waters, which the authors attributed mainly to pond popularity, itself governed largely by accessibility.

**Seasonal access.** Winter ice fishing strongly shaped harvest. A law banning artificial jiggers for ice fishing was in effect during the study and sharply reduced yellow perch harvest, with only 25% of perch recoveries made in winter. The law was repealed in 1953.

**Fish movement.** Only 9 of 1,283 known recaptures (0.7%) were found outside their waters of release, all salvaged fish. Seven stayed in the same township, and one smallmouth was gaffed in Plymouth Harbor after moving to sea.

## Key Findings

**1. Tagging and recovery scale**
- 12,978 fish were tagged and released across 107 ponds, September 1950 to October 1953.
- Known angler recoveries were 19% for game fish and 3% for pan fish. Of these, 91% came in the first year, 8% in the second, and 1% in the third.
- After correcting for an estimated 75% reporting efficiency (an expansion factor of 1-1/3), estimated harvest was 23% for game fish and 4% for pan fish.

**2. Species-specific harvest**
- **Largemouth Bass:** overall average harvest of 9% (known); too few fish per pond for separate analysis. Two ponds reached about 23% and 28% after correction.
- **Smallmouth Bass:** highest native harvest was 20% of 39 fish at Chauncey Pond. The highest salvaged harvest was about 61%, at [[Stockbridge Bowl]].
- **Chain Pickerel:** the most heavily exploited species. [[Cheshire Reservoir]] averaged 28% known recovery across four tagging lots, and Stockbridge Bowl reached 54%. Maximum annual harvest was estimated at 55 to 62%.
- **Brown Bullhead:** 10% known harvest at Massapoag Pond, twice the next highest pond. Usage was otherwise low.
- **Yellow Perch:** variable, with greatest use at Bare Hill Pond and Stockbridge Bowl (at least 8%). Some salvaged lots reached an estimated 35 to 36%.
- **White Perch:** only 5% recovered at one pond (Chauncey) out of 58 fish tagged. Harvest never exceeded 1%.

**3. Catchability**
- Game fish appeared roughly six times as catchable as pan fish. Part of this reflects angler habit: most anglers fish shallow cover where game fish concentrate, while locating deep pan fish schools takes more effort.
- A 1954 survey of angling effort ranked fishing for trout at 24.0%, black bass at 23.9%, pickerel at 19.4%, yellow perch at 11.0%, bullheads at 10.5%, and white perch at 4.0%.
- Bass were the least readily recovered game fish. Pickerel were nearly three times as easy to take as bass.

**4. Seasonal patterns in pickerel harvest**
- Winter fishing accounted for 78% of known pickerel recaptures.
- Fall-tagged pickerel were harvested at least 37% of the time, against only 12% for spring-tagged fish.
- At Cheshire Reservoir, fall 1950 tagging yielded 59% harvest the first winter, while fall 1951 tagging yielded only 13% because ice was unsafe. The authors concluded that unharvested pickerel did not improve summer fishing.

**5. Selective mortality of salvaged fish**
- Collectively, selective mortality affected 30 to 50% of salvaged fish. The authors estimated at least one-third mortality for largemouths, smallmouths, white perch, and yellow perch, and about one-half for fall-stocked pickerel.
- Direct observation supported this. At one pond, 64 of 89 salvaged smallmouths (72%) were found dead within days, while a second plant of 112 smallmouths at the same pond showed no dead fish.
- Bullheads were extremely hardy and probably suffered little selective mortality.
- A Massapoag Pond comparison suggested about one-third of salvaged smallmouths died soon after release.

**6. Salvaged fish and angler vulnerability**
- Salvaged fish may have been more catchable than native fish, possibly because native fish had developed "hook-wiseness" under continuous fishing. The authors stated this remained to be demonstrated.

**7. Economics of salvage**
- Salvage of about 5,700 pounds of catchable game and pan fish cost more than $10,000.
- At least one-third died after release, leaving no more than 3,800 pounds available. Estimated creel yield was about 875 pounds (773 pounds of game fish, 29%; 102 pounds of pan fish, 9%).
- License buyers paid well over $11 per pound. The authors called this prohibitive, though less costly than hatchery maintenance stocking.

**8. Screening and migration**
- Only 0.7% of known recaptures were outside waters of release, so the authors concluded that outlet screens are unnecessary and earlier recommendations ([[McCabe 1948]]) were invalid.

**9. Comparative studies**
- Lake Ontario smallmouths: 21.5% of 2,853 fish recovered after lower-jaw tagging with rounded strap tags. Maxillary tagging produced 9.1% of 1,039 fish, indicating tag-method effects on return rates.
- TVA reservoirs: about 40 to 42% harvest of 1,909 maxillary-tagged largemouth and smallmouth basses, with no evidence of later fishery injury ([[Chance]], in press).

**10. Management recommendations**
- Harvest rates for game fish were not considered excessive. Pickerel can sustain their heavy exploitation.
- Winter fishing regulations for yellow perch were liberalized (1953). Pan fish harvest restrictions were largely relaxed.
- Bass stocking was judged ill advised. Where feasible, bass should be eliminated from potentially good pickerel waters through drainage or chemical treatment, followed by restocking with pickerel and a forage species such as yellow perch.
- White perch should be eliminated wherever possible.
- Salvage for maintenance stocking was judged indefensible on cost grounds. The authors argued that the same money would yield far more fishing if invested in reclaiming unbalanced waters and providing permanent public access.
- Anglers need education on pan-fish methods and opportunities.
