# Mayflies and Trout Food in Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-entomology #stream-ecology #trout-diet

## Executive Summary

This document provides a comprehensive overview of aquatic insects as food sources for [[Trout]] in New York State streams, with particular emphasis on [[Mayflies]] (Ephemeroptera). Written by Dwight A. Webster of the Department of Conservation, Cornell, the article describes the life cycles, habitats, and ecological importance of seven major insect groups found in stream environments. The document explains seasonal feeding patterns of trout, the vulnerability of insects during emergence periods, and discusses stream management practices that affect aquatic insect populations. The work serves as both an educational resource for anglers and a guide to understanding stream food web dynamics.

## Background & Description

The document identifies seven major groups of aquatic insects in stream ecosystems: [[Mayflies]] (Ephemeroptera), [[Stoneflies]] (Plecoptera), dragonflies and damselflies (Odonata), [[Caddisflies]] (Trichoptera), fish-flies and alders (Neuroptera), two-winged flies (Diptera), beetles (Coleoptera), and true bugs (Hemiptera). Only the first four groups are entirely aquatic in habit.

[[Mayfly]] nymphs are categorized into three morphological groups: burrowing forms with feathery gills and prominent "tusks" (abundant in slow-moving streams with soft mud bottoms), flattened forms living in riffles (adapted to swift water by pressing closely to rocks), and streamlined swimmers (inhabiting eddies, backwaters, or broken water). Most species spend one year as nymphs, though larger species require two years and smaller ones may complete several generations annually. The adult aerial existence lasts only three to four days.

[[Stonefly]] nymphs predominantly inhabit running waters and reach greatest abundance in swift water. The nymphal stage lasts one year in most species, two years in larger species. Unlike mayflies, stonefly nymphs crawl out onto shore or protruding rocks to emerge as winged adults.

[[Caddisfly]] larvae exhibit diverse case-building behaviors, constructing portable houses from sand grains, pebbles, twigs, leaves, algal threads, or snail shells cemented with silken secretions. Some species construct stationary nets to strain food from currents. Case builders are herbivorous, browsing on algal slime, while net builders and naked caddis are carnivorous. All caddis pass through a pupal stage between larva and adult.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Seasonal feeding patterns show distinct temporal dynamics. During winter months when water temperatures approach freezing, trout feed little while aquatic insects prosper and grow. Some insects browse on plant growth covering rocks; others are predatory "wolves" of the aquatic underworld. By March or April, warming waters stimulate trout appetite when the riffle storehouse is near capacity with insect life. The great bulk of water insects transform into adult flies during spring months (April through June), enabling trout to make a large share of their annual growth when food is plentiful and easily secured.

By summer, stream larders are comparatively empty as the next insect generation is very tiny or undergoing delayed egg development. Summer and early fall months find trout turning to terrestrial food sources including beetles, ants, leaf-hoppers, and plant lice. Heavy winds and rain increase this land-born food availability.

[[Mayfly]] emergence represents a critical vulnerability period in the life cycle. Nymphs congregate on favorable bottom rock sites, then cast loose and swim toward the surface where they are exposed and unprotected. During hatches, trout and other fish engage in intensive feeding on floating nymphs. The hatching operation involves the nymph riding the surface until the winged "dun" form emerges from the shuck. A second transformation produces the "spinner" stage with glistening body, transparent wings, and elongated appendages. After mating flights, females return to deposit eggs, providing another feeding opportunity.

The mayfly emergence period in New York State extends from late March to October stragglers, with May being the primary month. Adding late April and part of June brackets the main hatches under most stream conditions.

Environmental factors affecting insect populations include pollution (both chemical and silt/erosion), ice damage during spring breakup, and competition from non-trout fish species including [[Fallfish]], large suckers, [[Smallmouth Bass]], horned dace, and various minnows. Ice going out of streams causes tremendous loss in insect life by grinding and scrubbing bottom substrates.

## Key Findings

**Seasonal Food Dynamics**: The document establishes that trout make a large share of annual growth during spring months (April-June) when aquatic insect emergence peaks and food is most plentiful. Winter months support insect growth while trout feed minimally. Summer and fall require trout to shift to terrestrial food sources as aquatic insect populations are depleted.

**Mayfly Dominance**: For anglers using floating flies, [[Mayflies]] stand far above other groups in importance. Day-to-day surface activity of trout is primarily driven by hatching mayflies. For submerged food, underwater forms of mayflies, caddis, and stoneflies usually predominate, possibly in that order of importance.

**Emergence Vulnerability**: The mechanics of mayfly hatching create extended exposure periods. Nymphs cast off from bottom protection, struggle or swim to the surface, ride the current during emergence, and may make hesitating flights—all while exposed, active, conspicuous, and vulnerable to predation.

**Caddisfly Diversity**: [[Caddisfly]] larvae exhibit remarkable architectural diversity in case construction using varied materials (sand grains, pebbles, twigs, leaves, algal threads, snail shells). Net-building species strain food from currents. Most species are nocturnal, transforming at dusk or night, though the "Grannon" hatches in mid-day swarms around mid-May on [[Catskill]] streams.

**Stonefly Limitations**: Despite abundant nymphs in swift water, [[Stoneflies]] are of limited importance to fly fishermen because adults emerge by crawling onto shore rather than at the water surface, and they do not return in mating swarms. Exceptions include pale greenish or yellowish "willow-flies" and early brown stoneflies appearing in March-April.

**Stream Management Implications**: The document identifies several management considerations: (1) artificial enrichment with basic nutrients may theoretically increase food production but practicability is unestablished; (2) slight pollution of the right kind may be beneficial, but most pollution is detrimental; (3) silt and excessive erosion are devastating "natural" pollutants requiring land-use practices, reforestation, and stream bank replanting; (4) willow and shrub plantings check erosion while providing shade, bank stability, and terrestrial food sources; (5) ice damage during spring breakup causes tremendous insect loss, making ice-minimizing improvements worthwhile; (6) competitor fish species (fallfish, large suckers, smallmouth bass, minnows) consume food unavailable to trout, with evidence suggesting trout perform better alone.

**Fundamental Principle**: The amount of life a stream can support relates fundamentally to soils and rocks forming the drainage area, which supply basic inorganic nutrients. The generalization offered is that if the stream is kept healthy, insects and other aquatic life take care of themselves.
