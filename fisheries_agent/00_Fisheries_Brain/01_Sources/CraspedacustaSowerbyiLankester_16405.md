# Craspedacusta sowerbyi in New Zealand Lakes

#limnomedusae #cnidaria #freshwater-biology

## Executive Summary

This paper reports a sudden infestation of medusae of [[Craspedacusta Sowerbyi]], a freshwater hydrozoan of the [[Limnomedusae]], in [[Lake Maraetai]] on the [[Waikato River]] hydro-electric chain, North Island, New Zealand, in late January 1970. Earlier New Zealand records of immature medusae, and a laboratory culture of the hydroid (polyp) stage, probably refer to the same species. Seven specimens were examined, and the author concluded that unusually high water temperatures favoured medusa development, while the ecological effect on the lake was probably negligible.

## Background & Description

Medusae were first recorded in New Zealand plankton by Dr V. H. Jolly in 1956 (Lakes Taupo and Tarawera), with further collections in 1957 (Lakes Rotorua and Tarawera), 1964 (Lakes Ngapouri and Rotoehu) and 1969 (Lakes Rotorua and Rotoiti). All of these earlier medusae were immature, with the largest only 2 mm in diameter, so a firm identification was not possible. Supporting evidence came from a hydroid culture first established in 1962 at Dunedin Teachers' College, whose polyps reached more than 2 mm in length. In that culture, medusae were produced once but were very small and survived no more than a week.

The Lake Maraetai medusae were reported by anglers in summer 1970 and collected by a wildlife field officer and a [[New Zealand Forest Products]] scientist. Most living medusae measured 13 to 15 mm in maximum diameter, with one individual reaching 20 mm. Gonads were well developed, hung from the radial canals and extended below the manubrium. Identification rested on gonad position, the large perradial marginal tentacles and body size, consistent with the description by [[Russell 1953]]. The author noted that the Maraetai gonads were larger and longer than those described by Russell, but judged this insufficient to serve as a specific character, since gonads enlarge before maturity. All specimens appeared male on smear preparations. Preserved material is held in the Dominion Museum, Wellington.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water conditions in Lake Maraetai at the time were exceptional:

- **Upper stratum:** well oxygenated, 25.5 °C at the surface falling to 23.0 °C at 17 m.
- **Lower stratum (20 m to bottom at 23 m):** 21.0 to 20.5 °C, with dissolved oxygen of only 0.95 to 0.70 ppm.

The low oxygen indicated high oxygen demand and correspondingly high biological production. The author linked the medusa outbreak to this warm, productive, impounded system. Hydroid polyps are thought to develop mainly in flowing water, while medusae favour more static areas (Lytle 1960), which may explain frequent reports from artificially impounded waters such as [[Lake Maraetai]]. Temperatures across the [[Rotorua]] district in summer 1969–70 were higher than for at least the previous 10, and probably 30, years.

The species has a wide but sporadic distribution in North America, was reported from Hawaii in 1940 and South Australia in 1950, and was first recorded at Kew Gardens in 1880. Because the medusae are sporadic and the polyps inconspicuous, the species' true range is probably much wider than medusa records suggest.

Medusae are unlikely to harm the [[Trout Fisheries]] of New Zealand. They have attacked guppies and goldfish only under extremely artificial conditions, and their natural diet consists wholly of planktonic crustaceans and rotifers.

## Key Findings

- **Identification:** Medusae from Lake Maraetai were identified as *Craspedacusta sowerbyi* on the basis of gonad position, large perradial tentacles and size, with the author treating gonad size differences as insufficient evidence for a separate species.
- **Sample size:** 7 specimens in total (5 collected 26 January 1970, 2 collected 29 January 1970). This was too few to establish sex distribution in the population, though all examined appeared male.
- **Size:** Living medusae measured mostly 13–15 mm in diameter, with one individual of 20 mm.
- **Timing and temperature:** The outbreak occurred in the last week of January 1970, during exceptionally high surface water temperatures (25.5 °C at the surface).
- **Oxygen:** Lower-stratum dissolved oxygen was very low (0.95–0.70 ppm), indicating high biological oxygen demand.
- **Earlier records:** Immature medusae were found in multiple North Island lakes from 1956 to 1969, and a polyp culture has been maintained since 1962.
- **Temperature link:** Warm conditions probably favoured medusa maturity, since only the Maraetai animals matured while earlier sightings were of small, immature specimens.
- **Ecological impact:** The effect on the lake ecosystem was probably negligible, and large numbers are unlikely to affect trout fisheries.
- **Origin unresolved:** It is unclear whether *C. sowerbyi* is a long-established or recently introduced New Zealand species. The first record in 1956 suggests recent introduction, while slightly different gonad size could reflect genetic variation after long isolation. The author stressed that much larger samples are needed before the species' status can be settled.
