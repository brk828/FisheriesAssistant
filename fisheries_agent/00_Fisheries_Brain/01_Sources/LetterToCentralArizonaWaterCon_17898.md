# U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Response to CAWCD Report on CAP Biological Opinion

#ichthyology #endangered-species #conservation-policy #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a preliminary review by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) of a May 1995 report submitted by the [[Central Arizona Water Conservation District]] (CAWCD) challenging the Service's April 20, 1994 biological opinion on the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The biological opinion found that CAP water transport and delivery to the [[Gila River Basin]] would jeopardize four listed fish species—[[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)—primarily through introduction and spread of nonnative fish species. The Service declines to withdraw the biological opinion, maintaining that the jeopardy and adverse modification findings are sound and based on best available scientific information. The document addresses CAWCD's procedural, analytical, and cost objections while defending the consultation process and reasonable and prudent alternatives.

## Background & Description

The biological opinion in question was issued May 30, 1991 as a draft jeopardy opinion for two threatened fish ([[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]), one endangered fish ([[Gila Topminnow]]), and one proposed endangered fish ([[Razorback Sucker]]). The opinion also found adverse modification of critical habitat for spikedace, loach minnow, and razorback sucker. Despite extensive negotiations over three years with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BR), the Service maintained its jeopardy/adverse modification conclusion, though the reasonable and prudent alternative underwent substantial modifications.

The consultation scope covered potential for CAP to introduce and spread nonnative species throughout the [[Gila River Basin]], excluding the [[Santa Cruz River Basin]]. Key occupied habitats include [[Eagle Creek]], [[Blue River]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Tularosa River]], [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Upper Gila River]], and [[Middle Gila River]]. Critical habitat designations exist for multiple stream reaches supporting these species.

CAWCD's challenge rested on six main issues: (1) the consultation should never have occurred, (2) alleged procedural errors, (3) alleged analytical errors, (4) alleged errors in conclusions, (5) alleged errors in reasonable and prudent alternatives, and (6) justification for reinitiation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The four listed fish species face multiple threats beyond CAP, including habitat degradation from livestock grazing, timber harvest, groundwater pumping, and existing nonnative fish populations. The Service emphasizes that presence of other threats does not negate the need to protect species from CAP effects; rather, existing threats increase the risk from additional impacts.

Nonnative fish introduction occurs through multiple pathways: direct escape from CAP canals and turnouts, connection of previously isolated water bodies, sport fish stocking (both Federal and State programs), unauthorized "bait bucket" transfers, aquarium releases, and aquaculture escapes. The CAP system creates a major connecting waterway linking fragmented portions of the basin, elevating threat levels from all nonnative sources.

Fish movement in southwestern streams often includes passage through temporarily "dry" reaches. Winter floods particularly enable fish dispersal during extended trailing flow declines lasting days to months. Colonizing species with appropriate life histories move readily through temporarily watered channels and persist in isolated perennial pools, backwaters, and seepage areas scattered along channels. This creates an inexorable spread process over years or decades.

Electric barriers on [[Salt River Project]] canals provide some protection but have experienced operational failures allowing nonnative passage. No comprehensive effectiveness studies have been conducted on existing barriers.

## Key Findings

**Service Position on Biological Opinion Validity:**
- The Service declines to withdraw the biological opinion, maintaining jeopardy and adverse modification findings are sound
- All literature cited in CAWCD's five-page bibliography was known to and considered by the Service during consultation
- The biological opinion conforms to the July 1, 1994 policy on information standards under the [[Endangered Species Act]]
- An independent scientific review of the biological opinion is being explored

**Consultation Scope and Process:**
- Formal consultation initiated February 12, 1991 by BR on "possible impacts to federally-listed endangered species for the Central Arizona Project due to the transfer of nonnative fish"
- Scope agreed upon as CAP effects in [[Gila River Basin]] excluding [[Santa Cruz River Basin]]
- CAWCD was not granted applicant status for this consultation, though BR later granted such status for the separate Santa Cruz consultation
- Section 7 regulations require analysis of direct effects, indirect effects, cumulative effects, and interrelated/interdependent actions

**Nonnative Species Threat Analysis:**
- Analysis must consider not only species currently present in CAP but potential future introductions over the 100-year project lifespan
- Low probability at individual CAP points accumulates to substantial probability across the entire system over time and space
- "Dry" river channels in Arizona flow periodically, enabling fish movement during flood recession periods
- Physical barriers alone cannot prevent upstream movement; human transport ("bait bucket transfer") remains a significant pathway requiring education and management

**Cost and Feasibility:**
- BR estimates reasonable and prudent alternative cost at $11,800,000 (0.3% of total CAP cost)
- CAWCD estimates cost at $152,056,000 (3.8% of total CAP cost)
- Either estimate supports economic feasibility argument
- Costs spread over 100-year project lifespan: $118,000/year (BR estimate) or $1,520,056/year (CAWCD estimate)
- Cost differences reflect BR construction standards, monitoring requirements, and implementation approaches versus Service recovery plan estimates

**Species-Specific Considerations:**
- [[Spikedace]] occupied habitat includes [[Eagle Creek]] and [[Middle Gila River]] (omitted in CAWCD report)
- [[Loach Minnow]] occupied habitat includes [[Eagle Creek]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]], and [[Tularosa River]]
- [[Razorback Sucker]] occupied habitat includes [[Bonita Creek]] and [[Eagle Creek]]
- [[Gila Topminnow]] populations established after CAP construction began receive equal protection under the Act
- CAP water deliveries to [[San Carlos Irrigation Project]] may benefit razorback sucker through maintaining [[San Carlos Lake]] levels, but beneficial effects do not cancel adverse effects

**Jeopardy Determination:**
- Jeopardy conclusion based on cumulative effects to all populations and habitats, not individual populations
- Loss of or damage to individual populations may not constitute jeopardy, but combined effects across multiple populations may
- Environmental baseline includes past Federal actions that underwent consultation; unconsulted Federal actions are essentially nonexistent for analysis purposes
- Recovery habitats must be considered even if not designated critical habitat, as jeopardy definition includes "reduce appreciably the likelihood of both survival and recovery"

**Other Federal Actions:**
- Service stocking programs and Federal Aid to State programs have contributed to nonnative fish introduction and spread
- Consultation completed on some Service stocking programs; remainder in formal or informal consultation
- [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]] [[San Carlos Irrigation Project]] may require additional section 7 analysis
- Presence of other responsible parties does not negate BR's responsibility for CAP effects

**Reinitiation Criteria:**
- Action agency (BR, not Service) must reinitiate consultation based on: (1) incidental take exceeded, (2) new information on impacts, (3) project modification, or (4) newly listed species/critical habitat
- CAWCD report presents no new information; all issues were extensively discussed during 3-year consultation
- Restatement of existing information does not reveal effects in manner or extent not previously considered

**Barrier Effectiveness:**
- Large mainstem dams ([[Roosevelt Dam]], [[Horseshoe Dam]]) considered 100% effective against swimming fish
- Smaller barriers may be less than 100% effective individually
- Biological opinion calls for paired barriers with monitoring and removal between them
- At least two electric barriers experienced shutdowns due to maintenance/operation difficulties, allowing nonnative passage

**Recovery and Management:**
- Recovery plans for all four species identify nonnative species as significant threat
- Barriers on [[Aravaipa Creek]] identified as high priority recovery need since 1988
- Service policy encourages using section 7 consultation to achieve recovery by incorporating identified recovery measures as reasonable and prudent alternatives
- Funding transfer from BR to Service utilizes existing expertise, infrastructure, and process rather than creating duplicative capabilities
