## Executive Summary
The behaviorally richest and most relevant section of this document describes **feeding aggregation behavior in *Bufo marinus* tadpoles** observed over a 10-day dry-season period (March 1970) in a small pond along the Rio Rincón, Osa Peninsula, Costa Rica. Researchers documented large, amoeboid-like mobile tadpole swarms (up to ~2,500 of an estimated 6,000 individuals in the pond) that moved cohesively via "pseudopod" extensions, exhibited thermotactic behavior (seeking cooler water, detecting gradients as fine as 0.4°C), and engaged in coordinated tail-flicking presumed to stir and expose algal food. A secondary note documents the third Mexican record of the massasauga snake (*Sistrurus catenatus edwardsi*) from a wetland/marsh margin habitat in Coahuila, useful as an aquatic-margin herpetofaunal record. No fish taxa, fish morphology, or fish ecology are discussed in this source.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Shallow (5–25 mm to ~15–25 cm) pond margins within [[Tropical Wet Forest]] life zone (Holdridge classification), Osa Peninsula, Puntarenas Province, Costa Rica. Tadpole aggregations were closely tied to shoreline zones with floating mats of green algae, which served as both refuge structure and food substrate.
- **Thermal microhabitat selection**: Tadpoles actively tracked cooler water pockets, moving between shallow (rapidly warming) and deeper (cooler) water across the diel cycle; behavioral response to temperature differentials as small as 0.4°C was inferred from field data (see temperature/position table, 0545–1445 hr).
- **Trophic ecology**: Aggregations functioned as **feeding swarms**, congregating around partially submerged twigs that trapped floating algae; agitation via synchronized tail-flicking dispersed algae into suspension, increasing its availability as food. Intraspecific competitive feeding behavior was also noted (tadpoles competing over a discarded fish head), indicating **facultative scavenging/omnivory** in this life stage.
- **Group dynamics / collective behavior**: Aggregations exhibited fission-fusion dynamics — exchange of individuals between large and small groups was common when groups were within ~20 cm of each other. Directional movement was polarized, with pseudopod-like extensions forming only at the leading edge, never at the rear, implying non-random, coordinated directional cohesion rather than simple diffusion.
- **Sensory ecology**: A controlled sub-experiment (isolating a dozen tadpoles in a submerged petri dish) suggested that passing aggregations were attracted toward the visually distinguishable captive group, supporting a hypothesis that **visual cues** mediate aggregation formation and maintenance in *B. marinus* tadpoles.
- **Secondary habitat note** — [[Sistrurus catenatus edwardsi]]: captured in salt grass (*Distichlis* spp.) bordering a marsh (Rio Puente Colorado terminal marsh system), within 2 feet of open water, indicating reliance on wetland-margin vegetation as refuge habitat within an otherwise arid basin (Cuatro Ciénegas de Carranza, Coahuila, México).

## Key Findings
- Large-scale *Bufo marinus* tadpole aggregations (into the thousands) display **coordinated, amoeboid-like collective locomotion**, with directional "pseudopods" forming preferentially at the leading edge of the swarm.
- Swimming speed differed markedly between the advancing pseudopod front and the trailing bulk of the aggregation (e.g., 45 cm covered in 139 sec by the pseudopod vs. 17 sec by following tadpoles), suggesting differential individual effort or leader-follower dynamics within the group.
- Aggregative behavior appears to serve a **dual thermoregulatory and trophic function**: tadpoles preferentially occupy cooler water and exploit algal mats stirred up by collective tail-flicking, contrasting with the depression-forming, evaporation-reducing tail-flicking function previously described for *Scaphiopus hurterii* (Bragg 1946) — no equivalent depressions were observed in this *B. marinus* population.
- Feeding activity was time-restricted, peaking in early morning and declining sharply after ~3 hours, with aggregations dispersing into smaller, dispersed groups by mid-morning (~1000 hr).
- Preliminary evidence supports a **visual cue hypothesis** for aggregation cohesion, based on attraction of a passing pseudopod toward visually isolated captive tadpoles.
- [[Sistrurus catenatus edwardsi]]: This record represents the third documented Mexican specimen, with all Mexican massasauga records confined to a single basin (Cuatro Ciénegas de Carranza, Coahuila), underscoring the extreme habitat restriction of this population at the species' range margin.
