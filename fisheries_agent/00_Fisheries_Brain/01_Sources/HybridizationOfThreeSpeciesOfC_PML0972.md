# Hybridization of Three Species of Chub in a Hatchery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) can hybridize with the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), three cyprinid species of the [[Gila]] complex in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. Using hatchery-held brood stock and hormone-induced spawning, the author produced two hybrid crosses: bonytail x roundtail and bonytail x humpback. Both crosses developed and hatched under warm conditions (about 20-21°C), while embryos held at 12-13°C died. Hybrid fry were reared to swim-up and later transferred to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The work also suggests that natural hybridization may contribute to the decline of genetically pure stocks.

## Background & Description

The three chubs coexist in the [[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]]. Their taxonomy within the *Gila* complex has long been difficult to resolve. [[Deacon et al.]] (1979) listed the bonytail chub as endangered in Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming, and the humpback chub as endangered in Arizona, Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming. The U.S. Department of the Interior (1980) also listed both species as endangered.

[[Holden and Stalnaker]] (1970) reported intergrades between roundtail and bonytail chubs and between bonytail and humpback chubs in the upper basin, with bonytail x humpback intergrades being more numerous. [[Smith et al.]] (1979) argued that many specimens classed as intergrades were actually pure species. The present study asked whether bonytail chubs could hybridize with the other two species under controlled conditions.

**Brood stock:**
- 5 bonytail female, 8 roundtail male, and 5 humpback male.
- Bonytail collected from [[Lake Mohave]] near Cottonwood Cove (30 April to 1 May 1979; 29 April to 3 May 1980).
- Roundtail and humpback collected from the [[Colorado River]] near Black Rocks (5 November 1979).
- Total length and weight: bonytail 457-559 mm and 850-1,300 g; roundtail 298-352 mm and 240-420 g; humpback 304-330 mm and 275-385 g.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Spawning temperatures for these chubs are reported at 18-21°C ([[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] 1979). Incoming hatchery water at 12-13°C was therefore heated with a propane water heater to 20-21°C. The system included:

- Two recirculating concrete tanks (6 x 0.75 x 0.75 m) for settling, aeration, and recirculation. Adults were held in the first tank, which contained a cobble layer intended as an artificial spawning substrate. The fish did not use it.
- Three aluminum egg incubator troughs. Two received heated water and one received river water (12-13°C).
- Hatching trays (53 x 53 cm) with 1.4 mm mesh, set at a 30° slope into the incoming water.
- Fry rearing in an outdoor recirculating raceway, using a [[Cage Culture]] enclosure with 0.5 mm mesh. Fry were fed a starter trout diet and natural zooplankton, mainly cladocerans.

The author notes that low water temperature in the upper Colorado River basin may limit egg survival and be one cause of the decline of these endangered species. Alterations in the upper basin may also increase the potential for hybridization.

## Key Findings

**Spawning method**
- Ripening was induced by intraperitoneal injection of 4 mg/kg body weight of [[Carp Pituitary]] extract (bonytail x roundtail cross) or [[Oxytetracycline]] solution (bonytail x humpback cross), repeated at 24-h intervals until ripe.
- Fish were anesthetized with [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] at 1:20,000 to 1:10,000.
- All roundtail males produced low-viscosity milt after two injections. One bonytail female was ripe at capture, and two became ripe 24 h after the first injection.
- Bonytail x humpback males also produced milt after two injections; females became ripe after one.
- Eggs were 1.5-2.0 mm in diameter after water hardening, at about 103 eggs/mL.

**Fecundity**
- Bonytail x roundtail: 15,862 and 20,806 eggs in the two injected females. An uninjected female produced 7,216 eggs, which were infertile.
- Bonytail x humpback: 22,660 eggs from the one female stripped.

**Bonytail x roundtail hybrids**
- At 13°C, embryos formed by 96 h but died after 110 h, probably due to low temperature.
- At 20°C, the first cleavage occurred at 3.6 h, embryos moved vigorously at 60 h, hatching occurred at 86-95 h, and the yolk sac was absorbed by 72 h.
- Newly hatched fry measured 5.5-6.5 mm, and swimming fry 6.5-7.5 mm. Hybrids reached 12-13 mm total length in 16 days.

**Bonytail x humpback hybrids**
- At 12°C, embryos formed by 96 h but all died after 110 h, and no eggs hatched.
- At 21°C, development was slower than the roundtail cross: first cleavage at 4.8 h, vigorous movement at 72 h, hatching at 101-172 h.
- Newly hatched fry measured 5.5-6.5 mm, and swimming fry 6.5-7.5 mm. Hybrids reached 13-14 mm in 15 days.

**Hatchery transfer and survival**
- 12,000 bonytail x roundtail and 8,000 bonytail x humpback hybrids were transferred to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- Seven months after hatching, 500-1,000 hybrids survived from each cross.

**Natural spawning and hybridization**
- Neither hybrid combination spawned naturally, even though the parents were sexually mature and suitable substrate was present in the brood tank.
- The author nonetheless concludes that natural hybridization probably occurs, citing observed hybrids and intergrades in the upper Colorado River basin ([[Holden and Stalnaker]] 1970).

**Conservation implications**
- Parent fish from successful matings were retained at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] to validate species designations.
- Preserved developmental series and progeny could help resolve taxonomic questions in the *Gila* complex.
- The spawning techniques may also be applied to culture genetically pure bonytail, humpback, and roundtail chubs.
