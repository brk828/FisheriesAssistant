# Environmental Changes in a Polluted Stream During Winter

#aquatic-invertebrates #freshwater-biology #macroinvertebrates #stream-pollution

## Executive Summary

Gaufin and Tarzwell (1955) report a year-round study of [[Lytle Creek]], a small stream in Ohio polluted by primary-treated sewage from Wilmington. The study compares [[Summer Septic Zone|summer]] and [[Winter Conditions|winter]] conditions and their effects on [[Macro-Invertebrates]] assemblages. In winter, high flows and dissolved oxygen (DO) near saturation allowed the pollution "carpet" of bacteria, protozoa and organic matter to extend downstream, eliminating much of the former clean-water zone. Clean-water insects that drifted in from upstream and tributaries could not persist there. The authors conclude that seasonal life histories and drift must be considered when using macro-invertebrates as indicators of pollution. The document contains no fish data.

## Background & Description

- **Study site:** [[Lytle Creek]] is about 11 miles long and drains to the [[Little Miami River]], which enters the [[Ohio River]] at Cincinnati. Normal summer mean width and depth are 15 ft and 1 ft, with a maximum depth of 6 ft.
- **Pollution source:** The primary sewage treatment plant at Wilmington supplies 80–90% of total summer stream flow. Summer flow is largely effluent, so a definite septic zone forms below the outfall.
- **Aims:** Relate environmental conditions to the qualitative and quantitative composition of the aquatic fauna, observe seasonal changes, and develop a biological method usable year-round in most streams for indicating past organic pollution, reconnaissance surveys, and stream recovery.
- **Sampling:** Seven stations were sampled monthly for DO, pH, CO₂, alkalinity and temperature. Hourly 24-hour sampling was carried out each season. Flow was recorded with a weir and float recorder about 3 miles above the mouth. Benthic samples used a [[Surber Sampler]] in riffles and Peterson or Ekman dredges elsewhere. Marginal samples and Needham handscreen samples were also taken.
- **Period:** Intensive surveys ran from May 1951 through February 1952, following earlier spring and summer work (Gaufin and Tarzwell 1952).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and oxygen**
- Winter minimum flows exceeded 6 cfs, with frequent floods of 40 cfs or more. Travel time from the sewage plant to the mouth was generally under 36 hours.
- Late summer and fall flows averaged about 1 cfs, with travel times of 7–8 days.
- Summer septic conditions extended about 2 miles below the outfall, followed by a 2-mile recovery zone and a 2-mile clean-water zone.
- Winter DO stayed well above the minimum needs of most aquatic organisms. The lowest winter value was 7.8 ppm (57% saturation) on 25 February 1952 at midnight, 0.7 miles below the outfall. Winter water temperatures ranged from 32° to 50 °F.

**Pollutional zones and biota**
- By January, the [[Sphaerotilus Growth|Sphaerotilus]] "carpet" (with bacteria and protozoa) extended three miles downstream into the former clean-water zone.
- Tributaries contributed winter drift fauna during floods. Street drainage and a sewer-line break added wastes to the upper main stream.
- Coleoptera and Odonata were thought to have burrowed deeper into the bed or banks during winter, so they were probably under-sampled.

**Life histories**
- Summer-abundant [[Culex Pipiens]] and [[Eristalis Bastardi]] disappeared or nearly disappeared in winter. The authors treat this as a normal developmental pattern, not a pollution signal.
- Beetle, dragonfly and damselfly declines in winter were considered normal hibernation responses.

## Key Findings

1. **Downstream extension of pollution in winter.** Higher flows, lower temperatures and slower bacterial action moved the septic carpet downstream, eliminating the former recovery and clean-water zones. Biological zones became difficult or impossible to delineate during floods.

2. **Reduced clean-water fauna despite adequate oxygen.** The number of macro-invertebrate species in the lower clean-water section fell sharply. Organic pollution and the blanket, not oxygen, appear to be the limiting factor.

3. **Species counts (macro-invertebrates):**
   - October 1951: 43 species from the clean upstream section, 8 from the septic zone, 9 from the recovery zone and 70 from the lower clean-water section.
   - January–February 1952: 52 species from the upstream section, 18 from the septic zone (10 of them drift forms from tributaries), 5 from the recovery zone and 25 from the lower section.
   - In the 2-mile section below the outfall, only one-fifth as many species occurred as in the upper two miles during both seasons.

4. **Tubificid and midge dominance.** Tubificid worms and [[Chironomus Decorus]] larvae dominated winter riffles, reaching as many as 3,000 tubificids per square foot in a formerly clean-water area. Some worms showed incomplete adaptation, shown by a beaded condition.

5. **Mayfly tolerance.** [[Stenonema Femoratum]] tolerated pollution when dissolved oxygen was ample, and nymphs survived in riffles where other invertebrates had been eliminated. Nymphs were also found heavily covered with Sphaerotilus, which the authors describe as a formidable competitor.

6. **Transient clean-water taxa.** [[Rhyacophila Lobifera]] and [[Allocapnia Viviparia]] appeared in polluted areas only briefly after floods, clinging to leaf drift from intermittent tributaries. They did not persist once flow stabilized.

7. **Indicator value of Diptera.** Only 7 of 23 Diptera species collected in winter survived the pollutional bottom blanket. Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera and Plecoptera were restricted to clean-water areas in October, with only one or two species per order in formerly septic zones in winter.

8. **Methodological implications.** The authors argue that using macro-invertebrates as indicators requires knowledge of species composition, drift, tributary inputs, life histories and seasonal habits. The absence of long-life-cycle clean-water taxa is more significant than the presence of short-cycle tolerant taxa. Annual-cycle organisms are especially valuable for reconstructing past conditions.

9. **Summer contrast.** In summer, about 70% of biochemical oxygen demand was satisfied within 2 miles, with severe diurnal DO depletion and clearly defined life zones.
