# Survey of Fish Populations in Streams of Eastern Kansas Following Four Years of Drought

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-community #Ictaluridae #Centrarchidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957–1958 progress report, authored by James E. Deacon in cooperation with the [[Kansas Forestry, Fish, and Game Commission]] and the [[University of Kansas]], documents the recovery of fish populations in eastern Kansas prairie streams following a severe four-year drought (1952–1956). Six primary sampling stations were established along the mainstems of the [[Marais des Cygnes River]] and [[Neosho River]], stratified into upper, middle, and lower reaches. A total of 3,901 fish were collected from the Marais des Cygnes and 4,412 from the Neosho. Supplementary data were obtained from a concurrent [[Big Blue River]] preimpoundment survey (conducted with W. L. Minckley) and from opportunistic collections on the [[Caney River]]. The study found that most game and forage fish groups increased in abundance in downstream, larger-river reaches, while [[Bullhead]] species, [[Sunfish]], and [[Crappie]] were more abundant in smaller, upstream, drought-affected reaches. Successful reproduction of [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] in 1957 indicated a strong recovery response following the resumption of normal stream-flow in spring 1957, potentially aided by reduced competition from riffle-dwelling minnow and darter species that had been eliminated during the drought.

## Background & Description

While this report is primarily a population/community-level survey rather than a morphological study, several size and life-stage metrics were recorded for key species:

- **[[Channel Catfish]]**: Young-of-the-year (YOY) individuals collected via rotenone at Station II (13 September 1957) ranged 2–6 inches total length (average 3.75 in.); spine examination and length-frequency analysis confirmed these were 1957-year-class fish. YOY seined at Station XIII (2 November 1957) ranged 2.0–3.7 inches (average 3.0 in.), consistent with a slightly earlier or slower growth cohort downstream.
- **[[White Crappie]]**: Size classes varied by station — adults 6–10 inches at Stations I and III; large adults up to 13.5 inches at Stations XII and XIII, indicating an age/size gradient correlating with river size and habitat stability.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]] / [[Spotted Bass]]** (treated jointly as "bass"): No YOY collected, but a gravid adult female was stripped of eggs on 11 July 1957 at Station XII, confirming active spawning condition. Bass at Stations XII and XIII were noted to be in "excellent condition."
- **[[Flathead Catfish]]**: YOY individuals were collected specifically from riffle habitats via electroshocking, suggesting a habitat preference distinct from adult flathead catfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat structure**: Stations were characterized by large pools connected by riffles, with substrates of mud, gravel, and bedrock/rock, and river widths ranging from 20–125 feet and depths up to 10 feet. Several stations (I, II, and the upper Neosho at Station XI) experienced intermittent flow or complete drying during the 1952–1956 drought, while lower stations (III, XIII) maintained more continuous flow.

**Community structure and fish groupings**: Fish were classified into three functional/economic categories:
- **Game fish**: treated individually (e.g., [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]])
- **Forage fish**: minnows ([[Cyprinidae]]), topminnows, silversides, and darters, treated as a unit
- **Rough fish**: [[Gizzard Shad]], redhorse, carpsuckers, buffalo, suckers, and [[Common Carp]], treated as a unit

**Longitudinal gradient in abundance**: Gar, rough fish, forage fish, channel catfish, flathead catfish, and freshwater drum increased in abundance moving downstream (larger river reaches), while bullhead, sunfish, and crappie were more abundant in smaller, upstream reaches — a classic river-continuum pattern likely reinforced by differential drought impacts on habitat volume and permanence.

**Drought legacy effects on competition**: Riffle-dwelling minnow and darter species documented by [[Frank B. Cross]] prior to and during early drought years were notably absent or rare in 1957 collections. The report hypothesizes that this depletion of competitors reduced pressure on food resources and hiding niches, potentially facilitating high survival of channel catfish fry in the drought's aftermath — an example of competitive release following disturbance.

**Cross-basin comparison**: The [[Big Blue River]] experienced less severe drought impact than the Neosho or Marais des Cygnes, and maintained relatively robust rough fish and forage fish populations (dominated by [[Red Shiner]], [[Sand Shiner]], and [[Gizzard Shad]]). The [[Caney River]], by contrast, showed evidence of channel catfish population collapse during the drought (rare in 1956) followed by apparent recolonization via upstream migration during 1957 high-water events, restoring recreational fishing success.

## Key Findings

- **Relative abundance by river (Table 1)**: Nearly all groups (gar, forage fish, channel catfish, flathead catfish, sunfish, crappie, bass) were more abundant per unit effort in the **Neosho River** than in the **Marais des Cygnes River**; only rough fish, bullhead, and freshwater drum were more abundant (or comparable) in the Marais des Cygnes.
- **Station-level gradient (Table 2)**: Gar, rough fish, forage fish, channel catfish, flathead catfish, and freshwater drum abundance increased with downstream station position (larger river size); bullhead, sunfish, and crappie showed the inverse pattern, peaking in smaller upstream reaches.
- **Channel catfish reproduction**: Strong evidence of successful 1957 spawning and fry survival at Stations II, XI, XII, and XIII. Rotenone sampling at Station XI yielded 786 fish, of which 30% were YOY channel catfish and 11% were bullheads.
- **Flathead catfish reproduction**: High reproductive success documented at Stations II, III, XII, and XIII, with YOY collected specifically from riffle habitat via shocking; a single sub-adult flathead was found at each of Stations I and XI.
- **Bullhead dominance at Station I**: Rotenone treatment of a small intermittent tributary at Station I yielded 1,526 bullheads from just 0.01 acre-feet of water, with only one white crappie and one green sunfish as incidental catches — indicating near-total dominance of the bullhead guild in this marginal, drought-stressed habitat.
- **Species inventory**: A comprehensive species list (Table 3) documents presence/absence across the six stations, including [[Paddlefish]], [[Longnose Gar]], [[Shortnose Gar]], [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Golden Redhorse]], [[Northern Redhorse]], [[River Carpsucker]], [[Buffalo (fish)]], [[Blue Sucker]], [[Common Carp]], multiple [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]] species (red shiner, redfin shiner, ghost shiner, sand shiner, bluntface minnow, stoneroller, creek chub, silver chub, mountain minnow, bluntnose minnow, parrot minnow, fathead minnow, suckermouth minnow), [[Stonecat]], [[Slender Madtom]], [[Tadpole Madtom]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Brook Silverside]], [[Blackstripe Topminnow]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Longear Sunfish]], [[Orangespotted Sunfish]], [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Spotted Bass]], [[Slenderhead Darter]], [[Orangethroat Darter]], [[Logperch]], and [[Freshwater Drum]].
- **Regional comparison of game fish diversity**: The Neosho River supported the greatest variety of game species, followed by the Marais des Cygnes and Big Blue Rivers; however, the Big Blue held the largest number of adult flathead catfish.
- **Basin-wide reproduction pattern**: Channel catfish reproduction was successful in 1957 across all sampled rivers except the upper Marais des Cygnes, upper Black Vermillion River, and the Caney River. Flathead catfish reproduction was successful specifically in the middle/lower Marais des Cygnes, Neosho, and Big Blue Rivers.
- **Caney River recovery narrative**: Channel catfish were rare in 1956 collections but fishing success reportedly increased greatly by 1957, with the report proposing upstream migration during high-water events as the mechanism of recolonization, alongside confirmed successful 1957 spawning of bass, other sunfish, and bullheads.
- **Methodological note**: Sampling combined boat-based boom electroshocking (600W, 115V AC), a 125-ft experimental gill net, variable-length haul seines, hoop and fyke nets, wire traps, and 5% wettable powdered rotenone (applied at 4–6 lbs/acre-foot), standardized into "units of effort" (1 hour shocking = 20 seine hauls = 5 trap-days) for cross-station comparison.
