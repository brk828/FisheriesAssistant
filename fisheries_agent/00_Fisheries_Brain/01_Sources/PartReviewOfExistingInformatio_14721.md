# Colorado River Basin Gila Taxonomy Project: Phase I Part 1 Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Final Draft report (12 December 1989), prepared by [[Wayne C. Starnes]] of the Smithsonian Institution for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], is Part 1 of Phase I of the [[Colorado River Gila Taxonomy Project]]. It reviews existing taxonomic and distributional literature on the cyprinid genus [[Gila]] in the Colorado River basin, summarizes the activities of researchers, and surveys museum collection resources. Of roughly one thousand titles examined, about two hundred were judged moderately to very relevant. The report is the groundwork for a Research Work Plan (Part 2) and data-collection protocols (Part 3) that will precede a research phase (Phase II).

## Background & Description

The report has three sections:

- **Section 1, Annotated bibliography:** taxonomic references are arranged chronologically and distributional references by drainage. It includes an alphabetical reference list (about 300 entries) and species indexes.
- **Section 2, Investigators:** brief accounts of about 20 researchers active on Colorado basin Gila taxonomy over the past two decades.
- **Section 3, Collections resources:** a survey of Gila holdings at 26 institutions, seven of them visited in person.

The genus has a complicated nomenclatural history. [[Baird and Girard]] (1853) described [[Gila robusta]] (type species) and [[Gila elegans]] from the "Zuni River", but the type locality is now thought to be the lower Colorado River near Yuma, Arizona. Several synonyms were proposed and later reassigned: [[Gila intermedia]] was placed in other genera, resurrected, and treated as valid species; [[Gila cypha]] was described by [[Robert R. Miller]] in 1946 from Grand Canyon. Subspecies such as [[Gila robusta seminuda]] (Virgin River) and [[Gila robusta jordani]] (Pluvial White River, Nevada) have been recognized by some authors and not others. Common-name confusion between bonytail and roundtail chubs has further complicated records.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes steep declines across the basin:

- **Bonytail ([[Gila elegans]]):** largely extirpated. Lower-basin populations disappeared by about 1960 except in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]; upper Green River numbers fell sharply after [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure. Only five bonytails were captured in recent years in the upper basin by 1987.
- **Humpback chub ([[Gila cypha]]):** main populations are in the Grand Canyon area ([[Little Colorado River]] mouth), [[Black Rocks]] and [[Westwater Canyon]], [[Cataract Canyon]], and [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]] on the Green River.
- **Roundtail chub ([[Gila robusta]]):** widespread but declining, with the notable exception of the [[Yampa River]].
- **Habitat alteration** (dams, reservoirs, poisoning programs, and temperature changes) is repeatedly proposed as a driver of hybridization among forms in disturbed reaches.

Sampling problems are also significant. Many specimens from canyon reaches are intermediate and difficult to identify, and early records are often ambiguous.

## Key Findings

- **Literature:** about 1,000 titles examined; about 200 relevant; the bibliography is presented in chronological (taxonomy) and drainage (distribution) order.
- **Taxonomic controversy:** the status of [[Gila cypha]] relative to [[Gila elegans]] and [[Gila robusta]] remains unresolved. Early work by [[Holden and Stalnaker]] (1970) and [[Smith et al.]] (1979) provided competing views. [[Smith et al.]] proposed a "[[Gila robusta superspecies]]" concept.
- **Hybridization debate:** Smith (1983) and Valdez et al. (1982) argued for extensive hybridization in disturbed habitats. Douglas et al. (1989) and Tyus and Karp (1989) found little hybridization in the relatively unaltered Yampa River. [[Rosenfeld and Wilkinson]] (1989) reported allozyme results that conflict with chromosomal findings from [[Moretti et al.]] (1989).
- **Yampa River:** Douglas et al. (1989) achieved complete separation of cypha and robusta with qualitative characters and no hybrids indicated. Karp and Tyus (unpublished) identified only three intermediates among more than 1,200 chubs.
- **Collections:** about 1,600 lots were located at 26 institutions; about 1,500 were personally examined. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] facility at Fort Collins (BS/FC, about 473 lots categorized) and [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] (UMMZ, 251 lots) hold the largest holdings. The [[National Museum of Natural History]] has 125 lots, including many type specimens.
- **Gaps:** very little [[Gila robusta]] material exists from the Grand Canyon region (one specimen from Glen Canyon), and from upper Little Colorado, upper Colorado and Green tributaries. Old preimpoundment material of [[Gila elegans]] is critical and is largely housed at BS/FC and on loan to Tulane University.
- **Hatchery stocks:** [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] holds live stock of Virgin River roundtails (162 wild adults captured 1988) and Pahranagat roundtails (28 surviving juveniles), which are important for genetic studies.
- **Investigators:** key workers include [[Donald G. Buth]], [[Michael E. Douglas]], [[Thomas A. Dowling]], [[Paul B. Holden]], [[Lynn R. Kaeding]], [[Robert R. Miller]], [[W.L. Minckley]], [[Mark J. Rosenfeld]], [[Gerald R. Smith]], [[Royal D. Suttkus]], [[Harold M. Tyus]], and [[Richard A. Valdez]]. Several projects were shelved or uncertain as of 1989.
- **Next phases:** Part 2 will set priority taxonomic research needs and a work plan; Part 3 will give collection protocols; Phase II is the research phase.
