# Quitobaquito Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius eremus* n. subsp.): Description, Ecology, and Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This paper formally describes a new subspecies of desert pupfish, *Cyprinodon macularius eremus*, endemic to the spring-fed pond and outflow springs at [[Quitobaquito Springs]] in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Arizona. Morphometric comparison of 26 measurements from 12 localities across the species' range, analysed with [[Principal Components Analysis]], showed the Quitobaquito fish to be distinct from all other populations studied, with the closest affinity to fish from the [[Rio Sonoyta]] basin. The authors judged the divergence too small to justify full species status because of overlap with other populations. The paper also reviews the biology, habitat history, and threats to the species, and concludes that the Quitobaquito population is the only surviving natural stock of the species in Arizona and is therefore critical to conserve.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic placement.** The Quitobaquito fish is assigned to the desert pupfish, *Cyprinodon macularius* (Baird and Girard), a member of the Cyprinodontidae. Earlier literature variously applied the names *C. macularius californiensis* and *C. macularius* subspecies designations; the authors found no compelling evidence to recognise *C. m. californiensis* as a valid subspecies.

**Type series.** The holotype (UMMZ 162551) is a nuptial male, 40.0 mm standard length (SL), collected on 14 April 1950 from the spring-fed pond at Quitobaquito, at an elevation of 332 m. Paratypes include adults from several UMMZ, FMNH, USNM, and related collections, plus juveniles to adults at 17–33 mm SL.

**Morphological diagnosis.** Adult males have a deeper and more elongate head and a broader, deeper body than other populations. Females have a slightly deeper head and body, a longer dorsal-fin base, and a shorter anal fin. The authors verified that none of these differences resulted from allometric growth. The dorsal fin in males is positioned more posteriorly than typical for the species, and pelvic fins are reduced compared with most populations.

**Coloration.** Nuptial males are brilliant dark metallic blue to turquoise over the body (except the abdomen), with deep chalky-blue dorsal and anal fins and yellow-to-olive caudal fins. Females are golden yellow to brownish olive, with silvery lower sides and a white abdomen. Females show dark lateral blotches that may be continuous, broken, or intermediate in pattern.

**Meristics.** Meristic counts were taken from 15 males and 15 females, with holotype values noted separately. Counts included dorsal, anal, pectoral, pelvic, and caudal fin rays, lateral scale series, gill rakers, and vertebrae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** The Quitobaquito system comprises two warm springs (about 26–27 °C at the source in 1950 accounts) that feed a constructed pond. The pond, enlarged and deepened in 1962 from an irrigation reservoir dug about a century earlier, is roughly 20 × 70 m, with a thick silt-and-detritus bottom and shallow water (about 2.5 cm in places). Aquatic vegetation includes *Chara* and *Ruppia*-type plants, and cottonwoods line the margins. Spring temperature is near-constant, and the pond warms seasonally. Water temperature in the pond was 24 °C on 21 April 1983, with salinity 0.7 ppt.

**Physiological tolerances.** The subspecies tolerates very low dissolved oxygen (reported as 0.13 mg/L O₂ under laboratory conditions). Laboratory tests showed tolerance of water temperatures up to 44.6 °C (range 44.2–45.4 °C), while the highest natural temperature recorded for the genus was 43.8 °C, in a related population at San Diego, Chihuahua.

**Diet and reproduction.** Food habits match those of pupfishes generally: omnivorous and largely unselective, with guts full of detritus, plus insect larvae and nymphs, water mites, ostracods, and eggs. Breeding begins in April or May when water exceeds 20 °C, peaks in early June, and tapers off by August. Males prefer water less than 15 cm deep over solid substrates. A nematode parasite was found in 13 of 58 fish (22%).

**Population.** Estimates were 7,986 (22 October 1975), roughly 3,159 (10 June 1976, as printed), roughly 4,158 (10 November 1976, as printed), and 3,143 (fall 1980). The maximum life span appears to be about three years.

**Origin.** The authors reject the assumption that the Quitobaquito and Rio Sonoyta fish share recent water connection. Fossil spring deposits suggest that floods, possibly in the Pleistocene, created a connection. The population is interpreted as having arisen by natural selection in a small, isolated population after a founder event. The name *eremus* ("solitary" or "lonely") reflects its isolation.

**Human history.** The [[Papago Indians]] used the oasis, probably seasonally, long before European exploration. Later channelling, grazing, woodcutting, agriculture, and introduction of exotic plants altered the site. Papago residence continued after the monument was established in 1937, until the National Park Service acquired the site in the 1950s.

**Range of the species.** Historically, *C. macularius* occurred in the Gila River basin, lower Colorado River and delta, the Salton Sea and Laguna Salada basins, and the Rio Sonoyta basin. It is now extirpated from most of this range. Within Arizona, Quitobaquito is the only surviving natural population; artificial refugia hold other stocks.

## Key Findings

- **Subspecies description.** *Cyprinodon macularius eremus* n. subsp. is distinguished by a deeper, broader head and body in males and by a deeper head, longer dorsal-fin base, and shorter anal fin in females. The taxon is recognised as a distinct form, though not a full species, because of overlap with other populations.
- **Morphometric analysis.** Principal components analysis of 26 measurements from 12 localities (about 10 males and 10 females per site in Group 1; 15 males and 15 females per site in Group 2, with exceptions) showed Quitobaquito to be the most distinct population. Principal components 1 and 2 explained 93.3% of variance among Group 1 males, 94.9% among Group 2 males, and 94.22% among Group 2 females.
- **Nearest relative.** Fish from the Rio Sonoyta are the most morphologically similar and geographically closest to Quitobaquito.
- **Allometry ruled out.** Multivariate allometric coefficients did not differ between Quitobaquito and Rio Sonoyta, so the observed shape differences are not due to size.
- **Taxonomic conclusion.** *C. m. californiensis* is not accepted as a valid subspecies. Repeated mixing of stocks through Colorado River overflows into the Salton Sink argues against separate taxonomic recognition there.
- **Threats.** Introduction of the [[Golden Shiner]] (eliminated December 1969), habitat modification and water diversion in the Mexican portion of the Rio Sonoyta basin, and airborne agricultural pesticides all threatened the population.
- **Pesticide die-off.** A fish kill during summer 1976 reduced the population to fewer than 1,000 individuals, and below 1,800 the next spring. Fish tissue contained lethal levels of m-parathion.
- **Refugia.** Artificial refugia were established at Bates Well and Quitobaquito-linked sites, several later abandoned. Allozyme analysis (Turner 1984) found polymorphism and heterozygosity essentially identical between natural and cultured stocks, supporting refugia as a temporary conservation tool only.
- **Salton Sea decline.** The species, once abundant in the [[Salton Sea]], is now scarce there. Exotic fish introductions are considered the main cause. It survives in Salt Creek and San Felipe Creek, with 70 individuals captured in upper Salt Creek in 1986.
- **Colorado Delta.** Populations in Baja California are uncertain after high Colorado River flows from 1983 flooded the delta and [[Laguna Salada]]. Exotic species, including tilapia, now threaten the remaining fish.
- **Rio Sonoyta flooding.** Major floods in 1981 and 1982 scoured channels and reduced the desert pupfish to isolated individuals. Repopulation was observed by May 1986, with more than 100 fish caught in a short haul.
- **Conservation priority.** Large, healthy natural populations able to persist long-term may now be restricted to Quitobaquito alone. The authors recommend deepening part of the pond to around 15 cm, providing shallow refuges from temperature extremes, and preventing the pond from being used as a bird-scaring site.
- **Status.** The species is listed as endangered (Federal Register, 30 April 1986), and the authors state that its survival in Arizona depends on the Quitobaquito population.
