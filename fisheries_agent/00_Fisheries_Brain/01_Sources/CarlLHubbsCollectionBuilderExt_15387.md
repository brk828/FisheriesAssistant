# Carl L. Hubbs (1894–1979): Collection Builder Extraordinaire

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-collections

## Executive Summary

This biographical article, reprinted from *Collection Building in Ichthyology and Herpetology* (1997), profiles [[Carl L. Hubbs]], a 20th-century ichthyologist and complete naturalist whose lasting legacy is the large fish collections he built at the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] and at [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]]. The authors, Robert Rush Miller and Elizabeth N. Shor, draw on Hubbs's own records, correspondence and the recollections of colleagues and students. The piece traces his training at Stanford under [[David Starr Jordan]], his methods for cataloging and storing material, his expeditions across North America, Asia and Central America, and his attitude that completeness and accessibility of specimens were the core criteria of a good collection.

## Background & Description

- **Early life:** Born in Arizona and raised in California. He developed an interest in natural history through field exploration and his grandmother's shell collection. [[George Bliss Culver]] gave him a minnow from Malibu Creek, which turned him toward ichthyology and led him to Stanford.
- **Education:** B.A. (1916) and M.A. (1917) from Stanford. His Ph.D. from the University of Michigan (1927) was awarded on the basis of his publication record. Mentors included [[Charles Henry Gilbert]] and David Starr Jordan.
- **Career positions:**
  - Assistant curator, Field Museum of Natural History, Chicago (1917–1919).
  - Curator, Division of Fishes, University of Michigan Museum of Zoology (from 1920; full professor in 1940).
  - First director (1930–1935) of the Institute for Fisheries Research, Michigan Conservation Department; also Ichthyological Editor of *Copeia*.
  - Professor, Scripps Institution of Oceanography, La Jolla (from 1944 until his death).
- **Research interests:** Hybridization of fishes in nature, effects of water temperature on morphological variation, systematics of freshwater cyprinodontiforms, relict desert fishes of the Great Basin, marine fishes, past climates and paleoecology of Baja California, aboriginal archaeology, marine mammals, and hagfish taxonomy.
- **Personal:** Married Laura Clark in 1918; she was a trained mathematician who co-authored more than a dozen papers and managed his daily life. Their three children were Frances, Clark and Earl.
- **Publications:** Total of 707 titles cited in the article, and a personal library of roughly 40,000 books and reprints in 1944, later cataloged past 80,000 entries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The collections reflect a close link between field ecology and specimen-based taxonomy. Hubbs's field notes recorded life colors, sexual dimorphism, niche requirements, abundance, habitat, behavior and other ecological parameters. His desert-fish work followed the isolated aquatic habitats of the [[Great Basin]] and [[Bonneville Basin]] that survived after the wetter [[Pleistocene Epoch]]. Temperature monitoring at 61 locations along 225 miles of Baja California coast tied his work to oceanographic change. His [[Marine Life Research Program]] sampling covered 670,000 square miles of the [[California Current]] region, and the [[Isaacs-Kidd Midwater Trawl]] opened deep-water habitats to systematic collecting.

## Key Findings

**Collection growth at Michigan (1920–1944)**
- Hubbs collected 530,599 specimens in 19,046 lots during his Michigan years, with family, students and colleagues.
- The UMMZ fish collection grew from about 5,000 specimens to 1,705,197 specimens in 90,100 lots by August 1944, approaching two million including the backlog.
- The university holds the world's largest collection of natural fish hybrids: 19,750 specimens in 1,470 lots, representing 157 crosses in 17 families.
- A cooperative agreement with the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] (1936) added nearly 50,000 specimens from the Tennessee River basin.

**Cataloging and curation methods**
- He devised a cataloging system with 65 phylogenetically arranged group numbers, species index cards, labeled jars with tin tags, and cross-referenced geographic indexes.
- Specimens were stored in about 70% ethanol, and he insisted on reliable containers.

**Key expeditions**
- 1915: Survey of the Bonneville Basin, Utah, with [[John Otterbein Snyder]] (70 days, 3,500 miles).
- 1928: Two months at the Museum of Comparative Zoology, Harvard; gift of 3,856 specimens including types of 60 species.
- 1929: Java and Japan trip after the Fifth Pacific Science Congress; about 45,000 fishes in 8,000 lots collected over five months.
- 1935: Three months in Petén, Guatemala; nearly 30,000 specimens from 138 stations.
- 1934, 1938, 1942: Great Basin collecting; over 118,000 specimens from 305 stations.

**Scripps Institution collections**
- Hubbs began collecting at Scripps in 1944 and reported 107 species of fishes collected in about six months, several rare and a few new.
- By the time of his death, the Scripps fish collection held well over 1,200,000 specimens, with Hubbs estimated to have collected about 350,000 of them.
- Storage shortages persisted; Hubbs's memoranda pleaded for space, and in 1958 [[Richard H. Rosenblatt]] was appointed curator of the Vertebrate Collection. A new wing of Ritter Hall was completed in 1960.

**Other collections**
- Aboriginal archaeological material from southern California and Baja California was donated in 1972 to the Museum of Man in San Diego.
- His personal library was given to Scripps as a special collection.

**Collecting philosophy**
- Hubbs held that specimens should be abundant enough to define species clearly. He preserved even edible fishes and kept large series, which strained storage capacity.
- Ken Norris recalled a 1952 dispute over discarding duplicates of [[Topsmelt]] (*Atherinops affinis*). Norris later measured 48 running meters of shelving devoted to that species.

**Legacy**
- Hubbs was buried at sea off La Jolla on 6 July 1979.
- The article concludes that his collections, methods and mentorship continued to shape ichthyology.
