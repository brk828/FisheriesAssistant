# Appraisal of the Twenty-Second North American Wildlife Conference

#wildlife-management #conservation-policy #waterfowl #angling

## Executive Summary

This document is a closing appraisal of the Twenty-Second [[North American Wildlife Conference]], held March 4–6, 1957, and published in the Transactions by the [[Wildlife Management Institute]]. The author, Justin W. Leonard, Assistant Deputy Director of the Michigan Department of Conservation, acted as summarizer. He describes the conference as one of the most fruitful he had attended and reviews selected papers across deer nutrition, disease, herbicides, wetlands, beaver management, shooting preserves, angling, waterfowl, conservation education, and the state of the profession. The appraisal is primarily a commentary on conservation policy and the wildlife workforce rather than a fisheries study. Fish appear only in passing, through one paper on [[California Surf Fishes]] and remarks on angler training.

## Background & Description

The conference program rested on three interdependent lines, which Leonard identifies as:

1. **Research**, which supplies the factual basis for progress.
2. **Management and administration**, which supplies the action needed to achieve progress.
3. **Laymen**, non-agency people devoted enough to the cause to offer constructive thinking, suggestions, questions, and criticism.

Leonard notes that closing summarizers had apologized for repetition since the second conference. He rejects that practice and states that he intends to repeat, paraphrase, and borrow from earlier summaries. He records that this was the sixteenth time the conference was closed by a summarizer.

The author is a former fisheries-oriented scientist who "came up through the scientific ranks," and he writes from an administrative viewpoint. His remarks combine policy critique with humor and historical perspective.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several papers and remarks address habitat and ecosystem change:

- **Deer nutrition and antler growth.** Research by [[McEwen]] and associates on controlled [[White-Tailed Deer]] nutrition was described as relevant across the species' range.
- **Disease and wildlife reservoirs.** Work by [[Baker]], [[Longhurst]], and [[Douglas]] found that nematodes of the [[Black-Tailed Deer]] are not a significant menace to sheep on shared range. Leonard argues that scare stories about wildlife pathogens are overstated.
- **Botulism.** An outbreak of [[Botulism]] in New York [[Game Farm Pheasants]] had wide-ranging effects and was reported by Dr. Cheatum and colleagues.
- **Herbicides.** Herbicides are described as controversial. They can improve deer range by encouraging sprouting and maintain openings for waterfowl and upland birds, but indiscriminate cover destruction harms songbirds and small mammals. Leonard questions whether traffic mortality along attractive highway rights-of-way may offset gains in production.
- **Flood control.** [[Gordon Gunter]] examined effects of flood control on the [[Lower Mississippi River]] on wildlife and other values, which Leonard cites as a warning against narrow engineering solutions.
- **Watershed programs.** O'Donnell described a cooperative [[Wisconsin Watershed Program]] that reduced damaging forces while increasing collateral values.
- **Beavers.** Yeager's [[Beaver Management]] work was praised as a basis for rational management. Leonard notes that beavers can alter habitat nearly as quickly as a bulldozer.
- **Lions of Gir.** A paper on the [[Asiatic Lion]] showed that in India the main threat is habitat destruction rather than overhunting.

## Key Findings

**Research results and their policy implications**

- In McEwen's experiments, two-and-a-half-year-old bucks moved from a low-quality to a high-quality diet gained an average of **87 per cent** in live weight over seven months, and antler weight was **300 per cent** above the preceding year's. Leonard uses this to challenge sportsmen who discount nutrition's effect on antler size and body condition.
- Baker, Longhurst, and Douglas found no significant nematode threat to sheep from black-tailed deer on shared range.
- Leonard states that research into wildlife pathogens is needed to counter sensational press coverage.

**Shooting preserves and public policy**

- Dickey's discussion of privately owned, commercially operated shooting preserves was the most anticipated upland topic. Leonard argues that the main questions are sociological and political rather than biological, and that more facts are needed, especially on the percentage of escapement and survival. He suggests arms and ammunition manufacturers might fund such research.

**Angling and fish-related remarks**

- Stephens and associates studied the food preference of California surf fishes, with emphasis on bait preference. Leonard considers this kind of work worth replicating in freshwater systems.
- Leonard observes that many new anglers take up fishing as adults without instruction and expect the state to guarantee catches. He notes that fishing requires increasing skill and that sportsmen's clubs could take on youth instruction. He is unconvinced that agencies should formally research angling methods and writes that fishing should remain partly "emotional" and folkloric.

**Management and public participation**

- Erickson's bear-trapping report led Leonard to conclude that "guts and a sense of fatalism" matter more than the trap's bill of materials.
- Hunter's description of methods for distributing hunters raises the problem of areas and quotas. Leonard proposes adding "people biologists" to agency staffs.
- Weaver's survey of conservation education found that eclecticism drives its vigor. Leonard observes that public argument over education methods is a sign of vitality.

**Waterfowl**

- Waterfowl are described as a resource requiring interstate and international cooperation, since birds spend only weeks or months at any one location.
- Each [[Flyway Council]] has been developing long-range plans. Leonard reviewed the early draft of the Mississippi Flyway Council plan and expressed high hopes for it.
- Most waterfowl are produced on private lands in sparsely populated areas, so Leonard argues that farmers bearing crop damage should be compensated in some way.
- Leonard states that concentrations of birds that attract nonhunting sightseers are paid for almost entirely by hunters.
- Specific needs include faster, more localized analysis of the banding program run by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and state and provincial agencies. Leonard foresees management of ducks by species, citing the [[Redhead]] as an example of a Lake States population of great local importance.

**Professional status and research capacity**

- Leonard notes a decline in able young scientists entering wildlife research, which he attributes to the greater material rewards and appeal of the physical sciences.
- He welcomes Leffler's promise of better status for technical workers in the Fish and Wildlife Service and urges the states to follow. He endorses the call by Leffler and Selke for fundamental research as the field's greatest need.
- Leonard's Law, based on nearly 20 years of editorial experience, holds that there is an inverse relationship between the quality of a manuscript and the number of academic degrees its author holds.

**Public understanding**

- Leonard credits the press, especially a new generation of well-informed outdoor writers, with advancing public understanding.
- He cites the warning that the public is "a parade," not a fixed audience, so education must be continuous.
- He notes that the human population in the United States may exceed **200 million** by 1975, which he cites as a reason conservation technicians need both research skills and articulateness.
