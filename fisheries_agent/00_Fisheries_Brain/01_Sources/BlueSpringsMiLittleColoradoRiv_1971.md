# Blue Springs as a Barrier to Distribution of Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #Rhinichthys-osculus

## Executive Summary

This preliminary report (Carothers & Aitchison, 1971, Museum of Northern Arizona) investigates whether [[Blue Springs]] (river mile 13, [[Little Colorado River]] Gorge, Arizona) acts as a physicochemical barrier limiting gene flow between two geographically separated populations of [[Speckled Dace]] (*[[Rhinichthys osculus]]*, family [[Cyprinidae]]). Population A occurs upstream of Blue Springs, and Population B occurs downstream. Initial observations by Dr. V. A. Taylor (1968) found that upstream fish (Population A) become rapidly incapacitated and often die upon contact with Blue Springs water, while downstream fish (Population B) show tolerance to it. The study is organized into three phases: (I) identifying the physicochemical cause of incapacitation, (II) quantifying the degree of gene flow inhibition, and (III) assessing morphological, physiological, and genetic divergence between the two populations. The 1972 request seeks additional helicopter and field-assistant funding to complete data collection, particularly missing carbon dioxide measurements above river mile 7.5.

## Background & Description

- Formal morphological analysis (Phase III) had **not yet been undertaken** at time of writing but was planned, including:
  - Standard body measurements
  - Scale counts
  - Color variation assessment
- Authors note these standard taxonomic/morphometric parameters are "tedious to obtain and often of doubtful value" alone, and stress that **isoenzyme-based genetic variance analysis** would likely provide more definitive evidence of population divergence than gross morphology.
- No quantitative morphometric data are presented in this preliminary report; species identification and population distinctions are behavioral/physiological rather than morphological to date.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Speckled dace occur throughout the [[Little Colorado River]] Gorge wherever permanent running water exists, including:
  - [[Salt Trail Canyon Spring]] (mi. 7.0)
  - [[Big Canyon Spring]] (mi. 7.5)
  - [[Blue Springs]] (mi. 13) — the focal barrier site
- Fish occur abundantly in both running water and standing/quiet pools with emergent and floating vegetation at spring sites away from Blue Springs.
- **Water chemistry tolerance ranges** (Salt Trail Canyon Spring & Big Canyon Spring, representative of "normal" habitat for Population A/B outside Blue Springs influence):

| Parameter | Range |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 20.5–21°C (springs, stable); up to 29.8°C in main channel |
| Dissolved Oxygen | 6–16 ppm |
| Carbon Dioxide | 45–60 ppm |
| pH | ~7.0 (near neutral), up to 8.7 measured upstream |
| Total Hardness | 633–873 ppm |
| Alkalinity | 32–581 ppm |
| Total Acidity | 5.64–91.1 ppm |

- **Blue Springs water** (the barrier zone) is anomalous:
  - Dissolved oxygen: **<1.0 ppm** (severely hypoxic) at 20.7°C — far below the general 3–5 ppm minimum tolerance cited for fish generally (Ellis et al. 1946), though some desert cyprinodontids (*Crenichthys baileyi*; Hubbs & Hettler 1964) tolerate 0.7–2.7 ppm.
  - Temperature drop of **9.1°C** compared to conditions 1000 m upstream (29.8°C → 20.7°C).
  - Suspected elevated CO₂ (unmeasured directly at Blue Springs due to equipment/flood limitations), cited as potentially more physiologically critical than oxygen depletion (Shelford & Allee 1912).
- This combination of hypoxia, thermal shock, and likely CO₂ toxicity appears to function as a **physiological barrier** rather than a purely physical/geographic one, differentially affecting the two populations depending on their acclimation history.
- Interacts with [[Population Genetics]] and [[Gene Flow]] concepts: the barrier is described as permitting only rare, unidirectional gene flow (upstream → downstream) during flood events, when Population A individuals may be swept past Blue Springs and survive.

## Key Findings

1. **Directional incapacitation response**: Population A fish (from above Blue Springs) placed in Blue Springs water at 20.7°C exhibited immediate distress (erratic swimming, rapid gill movement, surfacing/gulping) and were fully incapacitated within ~3 minutes (16 Sept 1968 experiment). Population B fish (from below Blue Springs) placed in the same water showed **no adverse reaction**.
2. **Partial tolerance divergence confirmed but complicated**: 1971 replication reproduced the Population A response, but Population B fish showed an unexpected initial negative reaction (with recovery after ~6 hours) — possibly because the tested individuals were recent, non-acclimated immigrants from Population A trapped in a flood-isolated pool below the spring.
3. **Hypothesized mechanism**: Low dissolved oxygen (<1 ppm), a sharp temperature drop (~9°C), and likely high CO₂ at Blue Springs act synergistically to anesthetize/kill unadapted fish; CO₂ toxicity is flagged as an especially important unmeasured variable requiring further study.
4. **Gene flow inference (Phase II)**: Using parallel 15-liter tank experiments with water from above, below, and at Blue Springs, the authors conclude any gene flow between Population A and B is likely **rare and unidirectional** (only from A → B, during flood conditions), since both populations otherwise actively avoid Blue Springs water.
5. **Survival pathway for gene flow**: During floods, Population A individuals may be carried downstream through/past Blue Springs; individuals trapped in spring eddies typically die within an hour, but some may escape and, if they survive to the next spawning season, could contribute genetic material to Population B — establishing a plausible but infrequent gene flow mechanism.
6. **Research design**: Three-phase framework established — (I) physicochemical characterization of the barrier, (II) behavioral/experimental quantification of gene flow inhibition, (III) planned morphological, physiological (metabolic/tolerance), and isoenzyme-based genetic comparison of Populations A and B.
7. **Logistical constraints**: Data collection was limited by floods and equipment shortages, notably preventing full water chemistry characterization (e.g., CO₂) directly at Blue Springs and above river mile 7.5; the 1972 funding request ($960.00) sought helicopter transport and field assistance to complete this sampling.
