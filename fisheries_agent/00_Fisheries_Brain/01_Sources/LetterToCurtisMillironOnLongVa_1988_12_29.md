## Executive Summary
This 1988 letter from the Smithsonian Institution's Division of Mollusks to a California Department of Fish and Game biologist addresses the taxonomic identity, distribution, and conservation status of a hydrobiid snail, **[[Tryonia protea]]**, found in the Hot Creek area of Long Valley, California. Although the correspondence does not pertain to fish, it documents critical ecological and biogeographic data on a narrowly distributed thermal-spring gastropod, warns of the vulnerability of its few remaining populations, and recommends formal conservation listing due to threats from geothermal development in the Long Valley region — an area also relevant to aquatic habitat management for co-occurring fish species such as those in the **[[Owens River]]** drainage.

## Background & Description
- No detailed shell or soft-tissue morphological description is provided in this letter; the taxonomic identity is based on prior work by Gould (1855) and Taylor (1981).
- The species concept for **[[Tryonia protea]]** is described as "somewhat vague" due to an imprecise original type locality ("Colorado Desert," generally attributed to Riverside County).
- The author (Associate Curator, Division of Mollusks) was conducting an active taxonomic revision at the time of writing to determine whether the **Long Valley** population is conspecific with other disjunct populations, or represents a distinct form/species.
- Pending completion of this taxonomic study, all known populations were provisionally treated as a single species (one composite "group of demes").

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Thermal and warm springs, spring-fed seepages, and creeks with submerged macrophyte vegetation.
- **Thermal tolerance/specialization:**
  - Salt Creek drainage populations (Riverside Co.) occupied water temperatures of **22–29°C**.
  - Long Valley (Hot Creek) populations occupied notably warmer water, approximately **32°C**, associated with submerged macrophytes — suggesting a degree of **thermal specialization** in this population relative to conspecifics elsewhere.
- **Known extant populations (as of 1988):**
  1. **El Doktor Spring**, Sonora, Mexico (reported disturbed by dynamiting; status uncertain).
  2. **Salt Creek drainage**, Riverside County, California — snails absent from the original type-collection site (Dos Palmas Spring) but present at three other disturbed springs: Hunter Spring, an unnamed seepage, and Oasis Spring, where populations were "moderately common to abundant."
  3. **Owens River drainage, Long Valley**, California — including **Hot Creek**, **Whitmore Hot Spring**, and a small spring near **Little Alkali Lakes**.
- **Historical range** (per Taylor, 1981): "Western Utah to southeastern California, adjacent Baja California, and southwestern Arizona" — indicating the species was once far more widespread than its current fragmented distribution suggests.
- **Disturbance association:** Most surviving populations occur at anthropogenically disturbed sites, indicating resilience to some habitat alteration but overall rarity of intact, "natural" occurrences.
- **Threats identified:**
  - Physical destruction of spring habitat (e.g., dynamiting at El Doktor Spring).
  - Proposed **geothermal energy development** in the Long Valley area, which the author identifies as a significant existential threat to the thermally specialized Long Valley populations.

## Key Findings
- **Taxonomic status:** *Tryonia protea* (Gould, 1855) is provisionally treated as a single wide-ranging species encompassing geographically disjunct thermal-spring populations from Utah to Sonora, Mexico, pending an ongoing taxonomic revision.
- **Rarity:** Only **three known extant population centers** existed as of 1988 — a very restricted geographic distribution for a species with a historically broad range.
- **Population loss:** The snail was found to be **extirpated from its original type-collection locality** at Dos Palmas Spring (Salt Creek drainage), despite persisting at nearby disturbed springs.
- **Thermal ecology:** Long Valley (Hot Creek) populations tolerate/require unusually warm water (~32°C) compared to Salt Creek populations (22–29°C), suggesting local thermal adaptation relevant to any future taxonomic split.
- **Conservation recommendation:** The author formally recommended listing *T. protea* as **threatened or endangered** at both the **federal** and **California state** level, citing population scarcity, habitat disturbance, and specific vulnerability of Long Valley demes to proposed geothermal development.
- **Management implication:** This letter functioned as an early conservation advisory directly informing California Department of Fish and Game policy regarding geothermal development permitting in the **[[Long Valley]]** caldera region, with implications for broader aquatic ecosystem management in the **[[Owens River]]** watershed.
