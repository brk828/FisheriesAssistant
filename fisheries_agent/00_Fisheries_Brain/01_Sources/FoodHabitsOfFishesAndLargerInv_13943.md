# Food Habits of Fishes and Larger Invertebrates of Lake Pontchartrain, Louisiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae #estuarine-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1958 report by [[Rexford M. Darnell]] (Marquette University) presents stomach and intestine analyses of fishes and larger invertebrates of [[Lake Pontchartrain]], a turbid, brackish estuary in southeastern Louisiana. The study is a first step toward quantifying energy transfer through the estuarine community. Analyses were made on 1,399 quantitative and about 100 qualitative stomach examinations of 35 important species, mostly collected between July 1953 and August 1954 by trawl and seine. Species for which data were most complete include [[Southern Bay Anchovy]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Silverside]], [[Sand Trout]], [[Speckled Trout]], [[Spot]], [[Atlantic Croaker]], [[Pinfish]] and [[Blue Crab]]. The author concludes that most species are omnivorous, that ontogenetic diet shifts are widespread, and that distinct trophic levels could not be recognized, with organic detritus prominent in most diets.

## Background & Description

The report motivates food-habit study as a prerequisite for energy-flow calculations. Citing [[Lindeman]] (1942), [[Ivlev]] (1945) and others, the author argues that "consumer levels" oversimplify exchange pathways, and that secondary-production estimates should rest on adequate food analyses.

**Methods.** Specimens were collected by trawl and seine and preserved promptly. Attempts to neutralize stomach contents with alkaline formalin were abandoned as ineffective. Each stomach was assessed for percent fullness and for percentage volume of each food type, by volumetric displacement or grid estimation. The author notes that "percent frequency of occurrence" alone can mislead, and that average percent of total stomach volume is more informative. Intestines were examined qualitatively and were found rewarding enough to recommend as standard procedure. Results were plotted by size group, and where data were complete, ontogenetic changes were shown as frequency polygons on semilog paper.

**Study area.** Lake Pontchartrain is a remnant Gulf arm impounded by [[Mississippi River]] deltaic deposits about 2,000 years ago. It covers 635 square miles with about 100 miles of shoreline, an average depth near 11 feet, and a maximum of 18 feet in the main basin (over 30 feet only in artificial dredge holes). Temperatures ranged from 9°C (December) to 34°C (June and August). Salinity averaged under 6 per mille for most months, with extremes of 1.2 and 18.6 per mille. Mean summer Secchi visibility was about 6 feet, and the overall survey mean was about 4 feet, indicating persistent turbidity. Most fishes and invertebrates are transients, with marine species mainly immature and freshwater species mainly mature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Turbidity and wind.** Wind-driven resuspension of bottom sediments dominates the turbidity regime, and the shallow south shore can read under one inch Secchi during storms.
- **Salinity and stratification.** Summer intrusions of cool Gulf salt water beneath fresher lake water may aid inward migration of Gulf-spawned larvae. Spawning does not occur within the lake (per [[Suttkus]], 1956).
- **Vegetation and bottom.** Shallow areas below 6 feet are confined largely to the western sector. Weed beds are sparse, which the author links to the scarcity of caridean shrimp and pinfish-dependent diets seen in Florida populations.
- **Food webs.** The author identifies two partial pathways: a planktonic route (copepods, such as Acartia, to small fishes to predators) and a benthic route (small benthos to larger invertebrates and bottom fishes to the same predators). Detritus supports copepods and benthos.
- **Mullet and menhaden.** [[Striped Mullet]] and [[Gulf Menhaden]] are major detritus and plankton consumers. Surface blooms of [[Anabaena]] in some years are noted as a potentially important food source linked to rainfall and freshening.

## Key Findings

**Trophic structure**
- Distinct trophic levels (sensu Lindeman) were not recognizable. Most species are classed as omnivorous.
- Detritus was prominent in the diet of most species, and the author suggests it may be a key nutritional source for copepods, benthos and sciaenids.
- Two partial food chains (planktonic and benthonic) support the top predators.

**Ontogenetic feeding stages (examples)**
- [[Atlantic Croaker]]: four overlapping stages, successively zooplankton, micro-bottom animals, detritus and larger animals. Calanoid copepods (especially *Acartia tonsa*) made up almost 70 percent of the diet of the smallest class.
- [[Blue Catfish]]: three stages, zooplankton (below 100 mm), bottom invertebrates (100-240 mm), and macro-mobile prey (over 200 mm).
- [[Speckled Trout]]: copepods, then schizopod-amphipod, then fishes (fishes made up about half the diet below 100 mm and about three-quarters in adults). Shift to adult diet occurred near 100 mm, earlier than the Florida population's 150 mm.
- [[Anchoa mitchilli diaphana]]: two stages, planktonic straining in the young (detritus and micro-zooplankton) and selective predation on shrimp and fishes in larger fish.

**Key predators and dietary highlights**
- [[Bull Shark]]: 3 specimens examined; 2 had food, and fishes made up over 90 percent of the food.
- [[Alligator Gar]]: 5 examined, 3 with food; blue crabs (65 percent) and striped mullet (35 percent). Lambou (1952, 1956) reported blue crabs at about 87 percent of 30 gars from nearby marshes.
- [[Spotted Gar]]: crabs (70 percent) and fishes (24 percent) in the 7 stomachs with food.
- [[Red Drum]]: crabs predominated (62 percent blue and mud crab combined) among 12 stomachs with food.
- [[Black Drum]]: clams (*Rangia cuneata*) were the staple for individuals above 100 mm; mollusks made up over 65 percent of the food of small drums.

**Specific quantitative results**
- [[Gulf Menhaden]] (17 specimens): the smallest (38-48 mm) fed on phytoplankton (82 percent), mostly Anabaena (77 percent of total food). Larger fish (85-103 mm) consumed mainly ground organic matter and silt.
- [[Threadfin Shad]] (5 specimens, 3 with food): plankton 76 percent, detritus 14 percent, sand 10 percent.
- [[Common Rangia]] (3 specimens): detritus about 70 percent, sand 10 percent, suggesting a nonselective straining feeder.
- [[White Shrimp]] (10 specimens): detritus and ground organic matter 58 percent; mollusks 12 percent.
- [[Blue Crab]] (124 specimens in stomach analysis): a detritivore, bottom predator and general scavenger. Mollusks rose from about one-third of food in small crabs to nearly two-thirds in the largest.

**Feeding periodicity**
- Young blue crabs fed mainly at night or early morning; adults fed mainly in mid-afternoon.
- Speckled trout fed heavily in early to mid-morning, and sand trout early morning and mid-afternoon.
- Atlantic croakers showed bimodal feeding in large adults.

**Methodological recommendations**
- Intestinal analysis should be standard procedure.
- Keysort or IBM cards are recommended for data handling.

**Limitations**
- Analyses were incomplete for many species due to limited specimens and time. The author calls this "a regrettable shortcoming," especially for the abundant Gulf menhaden.
- No stomach specimens were examined for the [[Stingaree]], so its diet was taken from the literature.
