# Toxicity Bioassay of Oil Refinery Effluents in Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 report, reprinted from the Second Seminar on Biological Problems in Water Pollution, summarizes a toxicity bioassay program on oil refinery effluents in Oklahoma. The program was funded by the Oklahoma Oil Refiners' Waste Control Council and run by the [[Zoology Department, Oklahoma State University]] from February 1957. It covers 42 series of bioassays at 14 refineries and more than 400 samples, using [[Fat-Head Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) as the test organism. The authors conclude that toxicity, rather than oxygen depletion, is the principal pollutional effect of refinery effluents on receiving streams. Untreated final effluents had a mean [[Median Tolerance Limit]] (TLm48) of about 15 percent.

## Background & Description

The study was motivated by the main aim of most antipollution legislation: preventing injury to aquatic life. Toxicity bioassay was therefore chosen as the evaluation tool, and the authors note that treatment that reduces toxicity also improves oil content, oxygen demand, chemical composition, turbidity and odor.

**Methods**

- Procedures followed [[Hart et al. 1945]], [[Doudoroff et al. 1951]], [[Turnbull et al. 1954]] and [[Henderson and Tarzwell 1956]].
- A trailer was equipped as a traveling laboratory and used at each refinery; samples were also tested on campus. Chemical analyses were performed by refinery personnel.
- Three test series were completed per refinery. A maximum of 33 bioassays ran in one series at one refinery; an average of eight bioassays ran per testing period, each series taking about one week.
- Minnows were mostly pond-raised by university staff. Test solutions were acceptable at 1 p.p.m. dissolved oxygen or higher; low-oxygen solutions were aerated with pure oxygen.
- Early tests diluted effluents with receiving-stream water, which varied too much for comparable results. The team therefore used a synthetic dilution water based on Stillwater tap water, adjusted to approximate the upper limits of US surface-water constituents (Table 1). Duplicate tests with receiving-stream dilution were run for some final effluents; heavy sewage loads made those samples more toxic.
- Toxicity endpoint: TLm48, the concentration at which 50 percent of fish survive 48 hours of exposure, either observed directly or derived by graphical interpolation. If no toxicity appeared at the maximum concentration, the result was recorded as NT (no measurable toxicity).

**Toxicity characteristics by source**

- *Catalytic cracker effluents* (Table 2): phenolic compounds arise from naphthenic acid decomposition; the "foul condensate" stream carries high phenol, sulfide and ammonia and is the only process stream where cyanide was reported. Its stripper effluent is among the most toxic streams in the refinery.
- *Cooling tower blowdown* (Table 4): chromates up to 24 p.p.m. were non-toxic, while chlorine combined with ammonia as chloramines was toxic at low concentrations.
- *Untreated final effluents* (Table 5): 13 samples describing a typical, unprocessed final effluent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Final effluent characteristics (untreated)**

- Mean TLm48 about 15 percent (range about 3 to 22 percent).
- Mean pH 9.1; ammonia about 22 p.p.m.; phenol about 7 p.p.m.; sulfide about 5 p.p.m.
- Mean chemical oxygen demand about 300 p.p.m.; 5-day BOD mean about 100 p.p.m. (limited data).
- No dissolved oxygen in any effluent; mean dissolved solids about 2900 p.p.m.; mean oil about 40 p.p.m.

**Treated effluents**

Effluents rendered nontoxic by pond retention or open-ditch passage (Table 6) showed pH reduced by about two units to near neutrality, total alkalinity reduced by more than 50 percent, phenols and sulfides reduced about 90 percent, COD reduced about 50 percent, BOD reduced about 89 percent, and oil reduced about 80 percent. Dissolved oxygen rose to 4 p.p.m. from none. Dissolved solids were unchanged. Ammonia was not significantly reduced but likely existed as nontoxic salts at lower pH.

**Holding ponds** ([[Holding Pond Treatment]]): one refinery ran a 60-day design-capacity pond series (Table 7). Ammonia, phenol, sulfide and oil declined progressively in all seasons, though ammonia and phenol were removed less completely in winter. Summer algal growth (mainly blue-green *Chroococcus*) raised pH and alkalinity in the later ponds.

**Open-ditch passage** ([[Open Ditch Effluent Passage]]): passage usually produces rapid improvement. One refinery's effluent (about 2,500,000 gallons per day) traveled a 3.7-mile ditch in 16 to 20 hours. Other cases include a 7.5-mile intermittent stream receiving sewage and a 2-mile ditch carrying about 2,000,000 gallons per day. Typical changes include lower pH, alkalinity, sulfites, oxygen demand, sulfides and phenols, and higher dissolved oxygen. Ammonia totals changed inconsistently.

**Effects on streams**: the authors compare earlier views that oxygen depletion dominates refinery impacts ([[Mau 1954]], [[Coogan and Paille 1954]], [[Ludzack et al. 1957]]) with their own finding that toxicity is the main concern. Earlier studies apparently did not consider toxic effects.

## Key Findings

- **Toxicity dominates**: all untreated final effluents and most process effluents were toxic, with mean TLm48 near 15 percent. The only non-toxic process streams were cooling tower blowdowns not treated with chlorine.
- **Oxygen is not the whole story**: some effluents reached dissolved oxygen of 4 to 5 p.p.m. after satisfying immediate oxygen demand and were still toxic.
- **Foul condensate stripper matters most**: when the stripper was offline, TLm48 at the oil separator fell to 2 percent. Using foul condensate as desalter make-up water improved the stripper effluent substantially (mean change about 58 percent in phenol and 51 percent in sulfide; Table 3).
- **Treatment reduces toxicity and other pollutants together**: ponds and ditches reduced toxicity to non-measurable levels while also lowering oil, phenols, sulfides and oxygen demand.
- **Dilution helps but is not the main driver**: ditch improvement largely resulted from non-dilution factors, and a tributary raised TLm48 from 24 to 48 percent. Reduction appears tied to initial toxicity level rather than season.
- **Receiving-stream water can increase toxicity**: in one February test, the end-of-ditch effluent had TLm48 of 92 percent with standard water but 70 percent with harder, more alkaline receiving-stream water, suggesting possible synergism.
- **Monitoring recommendation** (implicit): bioassay should accompany chemical analysis in effluent control, since chemical data alone did not indicate toxic potential.
