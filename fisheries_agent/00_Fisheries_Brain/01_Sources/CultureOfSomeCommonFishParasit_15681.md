# Culture of Some Common Fish Parasites for Experimental Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ichthyoparasitology

## Executive Summary

This Auburn University Agricultural Experiment Station bulletin (Zoology-Entomology Department Series, Fisheries No. 5, May 1967) by Heino Beckert gives laboratory culture protocols for three common freshwater fish parasites: [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]], a ciliated protozoan causing white spot disease; [[Cleidodiscus Pricei]], a monogenean gill fluke of channel catfish; and [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]], a parasitic copepod known as the anchor worm. The aim is to supply parasites in sufficient numbers for studies of host range, life cycle, and environmental tolerance, which are needed for effective control. The paper reviews earlier in vitro and host-based culture efforts, sets out general husbandry conditions, and gives step-by-step methods for maintaining, collecting, and re-exposing hosts for each parasite.

## Background & Description

**Rationale.** Parasite control depends on basic information: host range, life cycle, and responses to temperature, oxygen, pH, and salinity. Wild fish usually carry low parasite incidence unless an epizootic occurs, and they typically harbour several parasite species at once. Pure laboratory cultures therefore remove the variable conditions of natural settings.

**Prior culture work cited.** The introduction summarizes earlier efforts, including:
- [[Uzmann And Hayduk]] (1963): in vitro culture of *Hexamita salmonis*, an intestinal flagellate of young trout and salmon.
- [[Williams Hopkins And Wyllie]] (1961): in vitro culture of the strigeid trematode *Diplostomum phoxini*.
- [[Jensen Stirewalt And Walters]] (1965): in vitro culture of *Schistosoma mansoni* cercariae for as long as 112 days.
- [[Meerovitch]] (1965): rearing *Trichinella spiralis* from larvae to immature adults in vitro.
- [[Hoffman]] (1958) and [[Hoffman And Putz]] (1964): rearing *Posthodiplostomum minimum* to infertile adults, and maintaining *Gyrodactylus macrochiri* on 6-month-old bluegills in running water.
- [[Hlond]] (1966): an in vitro culture of *I. multifiliis* on carp slime medium. Personal communication showed only the tomite stage was reared and the life cycle was not completed in vitro.
- [[Tidd]] (1963, 1965): culture of *L. cyprinacea* and infection of goldfish and of tadpoles and young adults of *Rana pipiens*.
- [[Lahav And Sarig]] (1964): culture and detailed life-cycle description of *L. cyprinacea*.

**Host-screen method.** Earlier workers kept parasites by dividing an aquarium with a screen, placing infected fish on one side and non-immune fish on the other. When infected hosts died, they were replaced with fresh non-immune hosts.

**General laboratory conditions.** The paper lists six conditions for culture:
1. Use host fish from the same population or parent stock, or fish spawned together in the same pond.
2. Move prospective hosts to an isolated environment early. Storage ponds (0.05 acre, average depth 2.5 feet at Auburn) or running-water troughs are used; troughs allow observation and pre-exposure treatment.
3. Keep host fish healthy and parasite-free, with periodic examinations.
4. Use manageable water volumes: 40-liter aquaria and 100-liter feeding troughs.
5. Hold cultures at relatively constant temperature, because life cycles are temperature-dependent.
6. Use dechlorinated water at pH 7 or 8.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature regimes.** Culture temperatures were chosen per parasite:
- *I. multifiliis* exposures were kept between 70 and 80°F; the parasite matures in a few days to several weeks, depending on temperature.
- *C. pricei* work used 55°F in the baffle comparison.
- *L. cyprinacea* was optimal at about 80°F and was held at 75 to 80°F during exposure.

Abrupt temperature differences between parasite dishes and infection aquaria should be avoided.

**Host suitability.** *I. multifiliis* was cultured on carp, goldfish, bluegills, tilapia, fathead minnows, golden shiners, channel catfish, and white catfish. Scaly fish were unsuitable because heavy infections cause mucus secretion and scale loss, which interferes with recovery of mature parasites. *L. cyprinacea* infects cyprinids and catostomids readily, with goldfish or carp preferred as hosts because they are hardy.

**Environmental effects on parasite retention.** Water flow strongly affects parasite numbers in culture. In gill fluke work, unimpeded flow removed eggs and larvae. Baffles reduced this loss.

## Key Findings

### 1. Culture of *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*
- The parasite is a one-celled ciliated protozoan affecting skin and gills. Mature trophozoites are 0.5 to 1.0 mm in diameter and whitish or grey. The cyst divides into as many as 1,000 daughter cells (tomites).
- Exposure: 10 channel or white catfish fingerlings per 40-liter aquarium, with one heavily infected fingerling killed and added. White spots appeared in 4 or 5 days. A minimum of 200 trophozoites per 10 fish in 40 liters was recommended. Mild, uniform infections could not be produced consistently; uniformly heavy infections resulted only from large exposures.
- Recovery: parasites should be collected just before they leave the host to encyst. Premature scraping yields few daughter organisms. Pithing a heavily infected fish in a petri dish releases trophozoites. The pithed fish is moved to a fresh dish about every hour to avoid low oxygen.
- Counting: trophozoites are counted on a dark background, ideally just after they settle and adhere to the glass. For larger batches, eight wedge-shaped sections are marked with a grease pencil. For example, 300 trophozoites can be counted and marked, then washed gently into an aquarium with a wash bottle.

### 2. Culture of *Cleidodiscus pricei*
- This monogenetic trematode has no intermediate host and no polyembryony. Adults lay adhesive three-lobed eggs that cling to gill mucus or fall into the water.
- Maintenance: 10 to 15 channel catfish fingerlings are held in a 40-liter aquarium without water changes, so the population builds up to a heavy infection over several weeks. Feeding is not possible, so hosts become thin and may die.
- Egg collection: eggs are siphoned from aquarium debris into a urine sedimentation glass and checked microscopically. In one Auburn trial, debris-derived eggs maintained the culture for several months. Gills removed and placed in water released one egg per adult worm within 30 minutes, and most collected eggs yielded infective larvae.
- Feeding troughs: 100-liter stainless steel troughs with 50 fingerlings fed about 1% of body weight daily were used. In the first trial without baffles, no significant increase was found over 4 weeks, and eggs and larvae were presumed washed out.
- Baffle comparison (65 days, 55°F, 50 fish per trough, five fish sampled per trough at about 9-day intervals):
  - Pre-stocking mean: 6.3 gill flukes per outer gill.
  - Control trough (no baffles): the parasite population decreased.
  - Baffled trough (Styrofoam baffles): the parasite population increased.
  - The author concluded that a baffled set-up would suit large-scale laboratory culture. Figures 17 and 18 plot the sample means and ranges.
- Quantification: gill arches are rinsed, frozen for 24 hours to relax the flukes, then thawed, vortex-shaken to remove mucus, and counted under a dissecting microscope on a grid-bottomed dish over a dark background.

### 3. Culture of *Lernaea cyprinacea*
- The anchor worm is a parasitic copepod whose anterior end forms an anchor under the host's skin. Eggs pass through up to seven molts (naupliar and metanaupliar stages) before the first copepodid, which infects fish. Five more molts follow. Males do not develop further after mating. Females burrow into the skin and produce several pairs of egg sacs under good conditions.
- Egg sacs are removed when they turn from greenish to whitish, using forceps under strong light, with the fish handled in a wire basket and held in paper towel to avoid injury.
- Incubation at 80°F: most eggs hatch within 24 hours, and infective first copepodids develop in about 72 hours. Only the first copepodid stage is introduced, because earlier stages may be eaten by fish.
- Exposure: three 6-inch goldfish in a 40-liter aquarium were exposed to the larvae from one egg sac, giving fairly satisfactory but not uniformly light infections. Fish were lightly fed to prevent them from eating protruding worms on other fish.
- Symptoms: lethargy and fin folding 2 or 3 days after exposure, small bloody lesions at fin bases and body sides, and jerking or rubbing. Small anchor worms are visible about 10 days after exposure.

### 4. Overall Conclusions
- Each parasite requires a tailored protocol, including host choice, exposure dose, water handling, and temperature.
- Crowded, unfed aquaria and fed, flow-controlled troughs are alternative ways to maintain gill flukes, each with trade-offs in host condition and parasite retention.
- The paper presents these as methods reported from Auburn trials rather than as a comparative study with formal statistical testing.
