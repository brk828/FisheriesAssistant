# Early Life History of Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 final report, prepared by Daniel R. Langhorst and Paul C. Marsh at the [[Center for Environmental Studies]], Arizona State University, for the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Lower Colorado Region), examines why [[Razorback Sucker]] larvae disappear from [[Lake Mohave]] each spring despite successful spawning. The study tested predation by introduced fishes, larval food habits relative to zooplankton availability, larval dispersal, and survival in a predator-free backwater. Larvae were abundant from early February to mid-April, averaged about 10.6 mm total length, and showed no measurable growth in the lake, yet grew to 20.3 and 34 mm in the backwater and in cages. The authors attribute the disappearance to predation and/or downreservoir transport, and propose that larvae move offshore into deeper water, where they are lost.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a large catostomid endemic to the Colorado River system of southwestern North America. Its range and numbers have declined sharply. The only substantial remaining population is in Lake Mohave, which consists of large, old adults with no evidence of recruitment since the 1950s. Larvae appear each winter, but their causes of mortality were unknown at the time of the study. The stated objectives were to assess (1) predation impacts, (2) relationships between larval food habits and food availability, (3) larval dispersal from spawning areas, and (4) larval survival in predator-free habitats.

Study areas included [[Arizona Bay]], [[Hammerhead Cove]], and the Six Mile Area, plus the [[Arizona Bay Backwater]], a protected habitat separated from the lake by a wave-formed gravel spit. The backwater has maximum depths of 1.7 to 3.7 m and surface areas of 0.85 to 2.1 ha. It was stocked in January 1985 with 30 female and 150 male adults from Arizona Bay after its fish community was removed by ichthyocide in autumn 1984.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning:** Spawning was first observed on 9 January 1985 along Arizona Bay shorelines at a water temperature of 11.5 °C. More than 1,000 individuals were seen on days of optimum viewing. Spawning ceased when surface waves exceeded about 25 cm, which the authors considered a more important control than time of day. Adults were largely gone from Hammerhead Cove by late January, and by May only a few adults remained in Arizona Bay.
- **Zooplankton:** Lake Mohave plankton was dominated by cladocerans (*Bosmina*, *Daphnia*) and adult copepods. Densities ranged from under 100 to over 7,500 individuals per m³. The backwater was dominated by rotifers, with densities up to about 1,000 per liter.
- **Diet:** Larvae under 12 mm fed mainly on zooplankton, but most guts were empty, likely because mouths were not yet fully functional. Larger larvae (over 12 mm) had more diverse diets, including chironomid larvae and other benthic taxa. *Daphnia* in larval guts averaged 0.66 to 0.96 mm, about half the size available in the plankton (1.42 to 1.60 mm).
- **Predators:** Green sunfish were the only fish found to have eaten sucker larvae in gill-net samples, with 2.7% occurrence in Lake Mohave. Digestion experiments showed larvae were undetectable in sunfish stomachs after 8 hours.

## Key Findings

1. **Spawning timing:** Adults spawned from early January to late April. Larvae were abundant from early February to mid-April.
2. **Larval size in the lake:** Mean total length averaged 10.6 mm from early February through early April and did not increase, indicating no measurable growth. The largest specimen was 12.2 mm.
3. **Dispersal:** Most larvae were collected from shoreline areas, and only one came from open water. Dispersal patterns from major spawning areas could not be determined. A conical larval trawl (1 m mouth diameter, 1.0 mm mesh) caught one larva in 22.0 net-hours, with a result of 0.045 larvae per net-hour.
4. **Growth in protected habitats:** Larvae in the Arizona Bay backwater first appeared on 7 March and reached 20.3 mm TL by 28 April. In cages, larvae reached about 34 mm TL, the largest seen outside hatchery conditions. Growth in the lake is therefore not precluded.
5. **Predation:** In experimental digestion trials, sunfish consumed 10 larvae each in under 15 minutes. Larvae were digested beyond identification at 4 hours and undetectable after 8 hours. Five separate green sunfish attacks on larvae were observed during netting after the June-equivalent backwater breach. Green sunfish had 37% occurrence of larvae in stomachs in the backwater, averaging 3.9 larvae per fish.
6. **Backwater breach and loss:** A breach on 4 April 1985 connected the backwater to the lake. Introduced fishes (green sunfish, bluegill, threadfin shad, channel catfish, largemouth bass, common carp) then invaded. Larvae were no longer collectable by 5 May, when the lake dropped and the backwater became disconnected.
7. **Hypothesis:** Larvae move offshore into deeper, benthic habitats after reaching about 12 mm. They are then either eaten by threadfin shad or carried downreservoir by currents. Estimated currents of 1 to 25 cm/s could transport larvae toward Davis Dam.
8. **Recommendations:** Future work includes sampling below [[Davis Dam]] for dispersal, measuring deep currents, repeating digestion experiments with other predators, monitoring the restocked backwater in 1986, and refining cage experiments.

**Notable statistics:** More than 2,000 razorback suckers were taken in Lake Mohave over the past decade, and none smaller than about 400 mm TL (exclusive of larvae). Adults were at least 20 years old in the smallest sampled specimens (McCarthy 1986). The last juveniles were collected in 1950, when 6,100 specimens (12 to 42 mm) were seined near Cottonwood Landing. Since the mid-1970s, at least a dozen small razorback suckers have been collected in canals fed from Davis Dam.
