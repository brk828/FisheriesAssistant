# Effectiveness of Restricted Areas in Reducing Incidental Catches of Game Fish in a Gill-Net Fishery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 study, published in the *North American Journal of Fisheries Management*, evaluates whether limiting a small-scale [[Gill-Net Fishery]] for suckers to carefully chosen areas can reduce the incidental capture of [[Game Fish]] in [[Lake Seminole]], Georgia. Conflict existed between commercial netters seeking expanded areas and season, and [[Sport Fishing]] interests who opposed netting as a threat to game-fish populations. Using [[Test Netting]] from November 1983 through January 1984, the author compared catch composition inside and outside the legal netting areas. Game-fish catches were significantly lower in the legal areas, while sucker and rough-fish proportions were higher. The author concluded that restricting gillnetting to selected areas is a valuable way to limit [[Incidental Catch]] and its biological and social problems.

## Background & Description

Commercial fishing for nongame species in reservoirs can control rough fish, provide food, and create jobs, but anglers often oppose it, assuming many game fish are taken and then illegally kept or released dead. In Georgia, gill-netters may use up to 300 ft of 2-in-bar-mesh net per license in designated areas of Lake Seminole, where nets may be set in water less than 8 ft deep but not in creek channels. The license costs US$10.00. Target species are suckers, including the [[Spotted Sucker]] and [[Grayfin Redhorse]]. All game fish must be released, and five commercial catfish species may be taken by trotline or wire basket. Fishermen sell some suckers locally but many are used for personal consumption and community gatherings, so the fishery has both commercial and recreational components.

Before 1983, netting was restricted to weekdays in December and January in the upper Spring Creek and Flint River arms. The 1983–1984 regulations extended the season to include November and expanded the legal area in the Flint River arm about 2.5 mi upstream. Seminole County residents also sought netting in the Chattahoochee River arm and upper [[Fish Pond Drain]]. The study had three aims: (1) assess the gill-net catch in the legal areas, (2) determine the effects of the expanded season and area, and (3) evaluate other areas, particularly in Seminole County, that could open to netting without harming game-fish populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Seminole is a 37,500-acre impoundment created by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers in southwestern Georgia and northwestern Florida. Mean depth is 9.8 ft and maximum depth is 35 ft. Aquatic macrophytes cover about 70% of the reservoir, notably [[Hydrilla]] (*Hydrilla verticillata*), Eurasian water milfoil (*Myriophyllum spicatum*), giant cutgrass (*Zizaniopsis miliacea*) and water hyacinth (*Eichornia crassipes*).

The fishery operates under a "traditional" framework: netting predates the 1957 reservoir construction, and Lake Seminole is the only Georgia water where entanglement gear is allowed. The 2-in bar mesh is effective for adult suckers but also captures hybrid striped bass, crappies, catfish and largemouth bass of about 1–2 lb. Gill-net mortality is a concern, with the extent of initial or delayed mortality in Lake Seminole unknown but expected to be lowest in winter.

## Key Findings

**Study design**
- Test netting was conducted twice a month from November 1983 through January 1984.
- A total of 230 gill-net sets used 16,600 ft of net. Nets were 6 ft deep, 50 or 100 ft long, and strung between driven pipes, the same method used by local netters.
- Zones were divided across the Flint River arm (14 zones), the upper 11.2 mi of Spring Creek (14 zones), a 10.5-mi section of the Chattahoochee River arm (10 zones) and Fish Pond Drain (11 zones). Effort was roughly proportional to fishable water area and zones were randomly assigned each trip.
- An analysis of variance with randomized block design tested differences in catch composition among locations and months.

**Catch composition**
- Total catch was 1,403 fish of 28 species, weighing 2,519 lb.
- Catch composition of game fish, suckers, rough fish and commercial catfish varied significantly among areas (P < 0.05).
- Flint River arm: suckers made up 59.3% of the catch in the legal area versus 33.6% outside it. Game fish were significantly lower in the legal area. Rough fish plus suckers were 81.5% in the legal area versus 65.6% outside (P < 0.01). The newly opened 2.5-mi section did not differ significantly from the traditional legal area.
- Spring Creek arm: suckers were 37.0% in the legal area versus 44.4% outside, not significant. Game-fish catches were small and nearly equal. Inside the legal area, [[Black Crappie]] and [[Chain Pickerel]] made up 54.5% of game fish by number. Outside it, [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]] made up 55.6%. The illegal area had 25.0% commercial catfish, mainly spotted bullhead, significantly more than the legal area (P < 0.05).
- Chattahoochee River arm: game fish were 38.6% of the catch, significantly higher than any other area. Suckers were significantly lower than elsewhere (P < 0.01). This arm was judged unsuitable for sucker netting.
- Fish Pond Drain: game fish were only 7.0% of the catch, the lowest of any area, while suckers were 54.0%. White catfish and spotted bullheads made up 82.5% of the commercial catfish group.
- No significant trends in species composition or catch rates occurred across the three months (P > 0.05). Extending the season into November raised fishing pressure and total catch by about one-third.

**Catch rates (per 300 ft net-night, legal areas)**
- Game fish: 1.64 fish and 2.56 lb, from 0.06 (white bass and flathead catfish) to 0.42 (black crappie) fish per net-night.
- Spotted sucker: 8.12 fish and 15.97 lb, the highest rate of any species.
- Commercial catfish: 2.12 fish and 2.32 lb, mostly [[White Catfish]].
- Total catch: 18.97 fish and 34.90 lb per net-night.

**Fishing pressure and total catch**
- Questionnaires were mailed to 433 licensed sucker netters, with 149 returned (34%). Of these, 58 reported no sucker netting, 66 fished only the Flint River arm, 14 only Spring Creek and 8 both.
- Reported catch rates for game fish and commercial catfish were close to study rates. Reported sucker catch rates were two to three times higher than study rates, possibly reflecting differences in netting efficiency.
- Estimated total catch for 1983–1984 was 85,144 fish (2.27 per acre). This comprised about 43,147 suckers, 27,310 rough fish, 7,573 commercial catfish and 7,114 game fish (0.19 per acre). Incidental game-fish catch was small relative to total reservoir biomass estimated from [[Cove-Rotenone Sampling]] (Keefer 1981).
- The author noted that questionnaire returns tend to overestimate catch, so the true catch is probably even less important relative to biomass.

**Management outcomes and recommendations**
- Expanding the legal area into Spring Creek and Fish Pond Drain was recommended and adopted for the 1984–1985 season. No adverse effects were reported.
- The Chattahoochee River arm and downstream Flint River expansion were not recommended because they would significantly increase game-fish bycatch.
- The restriction on weekend netting was judged to unduly limit participation and was recommended for removal. Weekend netting was permitted in 1984–1985, with nets required to bear the owner's name and address.
- Seasonal restriction to cold months is expected to reduce game-fish mortality and user conflict, consistent with [[Seasonal Restriction]] results from Kentucky Lake (crappie mortality of 45% in winter versus as high as 100% in summer elsewhere).
- Central conclusion: careful selection of legal areas is a useful tool for limiting incidental game-fish catch and its associated biological and sociological problems.
