# Density Currents in Impounded Waters — Significance for Fisheries Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #reservoir-ecology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary
This 1941 paper by A. H. Wiebe (Tennessee Valley Authority) presents field evidence for **density currents** — sub-surface gravity flows of water within reservoirs caused by differences in temperature, electrolyte concentration, and silt content — as the mechanism driving an atypical four-layer vertical dissolved oxygen (DO) structure in deep, long, narrow impoundments. Unlike natural lakes, which show the classic three-zone stratification (epilimnion, thermocline, hypolimnion), reservoirs such as [[Norris Reservoir]], [[Hiwassee Reservoir]], [[Lake Herrington]], and [[Wilson Reservoir]] exhibited a **second oxygen-rich stratum below a zone of stagnant water**, indicating that oxygen-depleted water originating near the reservoir head was being transported at depth to points many miles downstream. The paper links this physical limnological phenomenon directly to fisheries outcomes: game fish orientation, surface fishing success, fish kills, and potential reservoir productivity.

## Background & Description
This paper is limnological/physical rather than morphological; no anatomical descriptions of fish are provided. Fish taxa are referenced only functionally as "game fishes":
- **Bass** (likely *Micropterus* spp., [[Largemouth Bass]])
- **Pike** ([[Esocidae]])
- **Crappie** ([[Pomoxis]] spp.)

These species are described behaviorally rather than morphologically — noted for their tendency to lose equilibrium and float to the surface after invading oxygen-depleted (stagnant) strata, implying vulnerability to low-DO conditions despite otherwise "apparently healthy" adult condition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat structure:** Reservoirs with a **long, deep, narrow morphometry** (e.g., Norris, Hiwassee, Lake Herrington, Wilson) are prone to density current formation, in contrast to natural lakes and more open/wide impoundments.
- **Four-zone DO stratification** (vs. natural lake's three-zone model per [[Birge And Juday 1911]]):
  1. Well-aerated surface stratum (epilimnion-like)
  2. Zone of stagnant, oxygen-depleted water (generally within thermocline)
  3. Second oxygen-rich stratum below the thermocline
  4. Bottom stagnant layer
- **Cause of density currents:** Differences between reservoir water and tributary inflow in:
  1. Temperature
  2. Electrolyte concentration (especially carbonates)
  3. Silt/turbidity content
- **Fish vertical distribution:** Game fishes (bass, pike, crappie) actively orient to the stagnant stratum boundary rather than random depth distribution.
  - During wet years with reservoir-wide stagnation below 30 ft (e.g., 1938), fish concentrated in surface waters.
  - During years with partial-length stagnant strata (e.g., 1939), fish in upper reservoir sections were forced into warm, well-aerated surface waters, while fish in lower sections were caught consistently at 35–45 ft depth, favoring cooler temperatures where oxygen remained available at all depths.
- **Fish mortality mechanism:** Fish invading the stagnant, low-oxygen stratum (attracted by preferred cooler temperature) lose equilibrium due to oxygen deficiency and float to the surface — surface DO is sufficient to sustain life but insufficient to revive fish already impaired. Most severe fish loss in [[Norris Reservoir]] occurred in **summer 1937**, when the stagnant stratum persisted reservoir-wide from July through October.
- **Productivity implications (hypothesized, not directly demonstrated):** If nutrient-rich turbid inflow travels as a density current *below* the photosynthetic zone (documented as deep as 60–80 ft in Norris Reservoir, well below the transparency-defined euphotic depth), nutrients bypass the productive zone and do not contribute to phytoplankton/zooplankton production, potentially limiting fish population support/growth rates.

## Key Findings
1. **Density currents confirmed** in four separate impoundments: [[Norris Reservoir]] (TN), [[Hiwassee Reservoir]] (NC), [[Wilson Reservoir]] (AL), and [[Lake Herrington]] (KY) — supporting the prediction that reservoirs sharing Norris's long/deep/narrow morphometry will develop density currents.
2. **Dissolved oxygen and temperature data** (Tables 1–4) document abrupt DO/temperature breaks and multiple thermoclines (e.g., Hiwassee showed thermoclines at 20–25 ft, 40–45 ft, and 55–60 ft near the dam), consistent with sub-surface density flow rather than simple thermal stratification.
3. **Lake Herrington** data showed DO dropping to a minimum of 0.26 ppm at 30 ft, then recovering to 6.76 ppm by 80 ft and remaining stable to 160 ft — direct evidence of the four-zone structure.
4. **Wilson Reservoir** exhibited a temporary turbid stratum (50–70 ft depth) traced to a cool, turbid discharge from [[Wheeler Reservoir]], demonstrating that density current persistence depends on both density differential and flow volume/velocity.
5. **Density currents likely more widespread** than previously recognized among TVA-type impoundments.
6. **Fisheries management implications:**
   - Sub-surface stagnant strata influence vertical distribution of game fish and contribute to fish mortality via equilibrium loss/anoxia.
   - Density currents may reduce reservoir productivity by transporting nutrient-laden inflow below the photosynthetic zone, preventing its use in the food web.
7. Alternative explanation (in-situ plankton decay within the thermocline, per [[Birge And Juday 1911]]) was ruled out because biological oxygen demand and plankton abundance data showed localized stagnation near the reservoir head insufficient to explain oxygen depletion detected 40 miles downstream — necessitating the density current mechanism.
