# Desert Sucker (*Catostomus clarki*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #freshwater-ecology #Pantosteus

## Executive Summary

The **Desert Sucker** (*Catostomus clarki*, syn. *Pantosteus clarki*) is a small-to-medium bodied catostomid fish endemic to desert stream systems of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico, including the [[Gila River Basin]], [[Virgin River Basin]], [[Bill Williams River]] drainage, and the [[White River (Nevada)]]/[[Meadow Valley Wash]] systems. Taxonomically contentious, the species is alternately classified under the genus *Catostomus* (subgenus *Pantosteus*) or retained as the distinct genus *Pantosteus*, with ongoing debate over whether it represents a single polytypic species or a "desert mountain-sucker complex" of several cryptic species. The species occupies rapids, riffles, and runs with rocky substrates, feeding primarily on algae and diatoms scraped from rock surfaces using specialized cartilage-sheathed jaws. Populations face threats from impoundment, water diversion, and habitat degradation, with two Nevada populations formally reviewed for federal listing in 1985.

## Background & Description

- **Body form**: Terete (cylindrical), slightly ventrally depressed, with a broadly rounded snout.
- **Size**: Adults 100–280 mm standard length (SL); maximum recorded 380 mm SL.
- **Fin ray counts**: Dorsal rays 10–11; pectoral rays 15; pelvic rays 9–10; anal rays 7.
- **Scales**: Larger predorsally, smaller postdorsally; 61–104 lateral line scales overall (65–80 in the [[Gila River Basin]] populations).
- **Mouth**: Ventrally located, laterally flared, with a distinct notch at each mouth corner; large lower lips with evenly dispersed papillae (absent from anterior face of upper lip).
- **Jaws**: Hard, cartilaginous sheath along inner jaw edges — an adaptation for algae-scraping.
- **Pharyngeal teeth**: 31–41 teeth in a single row per arch.
- **Coloration**: Silvery tan to dark greenish dorsally, silvery to yellowish ventrally. Breeding individuals may show an orange-red lateral stripe; in Sonoita Creek, AZ, ripe fish instead showed intense black dorsolateral coloration, a jet-black lateral band, and milky-white venter (Minckley 1969).
- **Larvae**: Have external chromatophores below the anterior horizontal myoseptum (diagnostic for subgenus *Pantosteus* vs. *Catostomus*); terminal mouth in early life stages.

### Distinguishing from Sympatric [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)

| Trait | Desert Sucker (*Pantosteus*) | Sonora Sucker (*Catostomus*) |
|---|---|---|
| Mouth | Laterally flared, notched corners | Not flared, no notches |
| Jaw sheath | Hard cartilaginous sheath present | Absent |
| Lateral line scales (Gila basin) | >60 | <60 |
| Intestine length | 5–8x SL | 1.5–5x SL |
| Diet | More plant material | More animal material |
| Swim bladder | Relatively smaller (swift water adaptation) | Relatively larger |

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat**: Found in flowing pools, behind boulders, and in rapids/runs; uses rapids for both spawning and feeding. Larvae and juveniles occupy stream margins and quiet pools. Altitudinal range approximately 600–2450 m. Documented tolerance of canal habitats in the Phoenix metropolitan area.

**Microhabitat use** ([[Aravaipa Creek]], Hardy et al. 1990):
- Adults: depth 0.18–0.46 m (mode 0.24 m), velocity 0–1.1 m/s (mode 0.61 m/s)
- Fry/juveniles: depth 0.12–0.46 m (mode 0.24 m), velocity 0–1.3 m/s (mode 0.85 m/s)
- Recommended instream flows: 30–40 cfs (adults), 20–30 cfs (fry/juveniles)

**Virgin River habitat data** (Cross 1975, n=123 collections):
- 60% from tributary creeks; 40% from mid/upper mainstem; rarely from lower mainstem
- 60% in runs, 30% pools, 10% riffles
- 66% over rubble/cobble substrate
- Mean current velocity at capture: 0.42 m/s (SD 0.27, range 0.08–1.10 m/s)
- Mean capture depth: 56 cm (SD 46 cm)
- >70% associated with cover (boulders, deadfalls, undercut banks)

**Thermal tolerance**: Critical thermal maximum 32.4°C (10°C acclimation) and 37.2°C (25°C acclimation) in Virgin River populations (Deacon et al. 1987). Showed lowest survivorship under reduced oxygen relative to co-occurring [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], and [[Desert Pupfish]] (Lowe et al. 1967).

**Diet**: Shifts from carnivorous (dipteran larvae) as larvae to herbivorous (algae, diatoms) post-metamorphosis. Adult diet ~97.4% plant material, 2.4% animal material, 0.2% detritus (Kobetich 1969). Feeds diurnally and nocturnally with peaks at dusk/dawn — contrasting with diurnal-only [[Longfin Dace]] (Fisher et al. 1981). Diet varies seasonally, with filamentous algae/diatoms dominant but chironomid larvae, turbellarians, and baetid nymphs seasonally important (Schreiber & Minckley 1981; Greger & Deacon 1988).

**Community associations**: Native fish assemblage includes [[Longfin Dace]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Gila Chub]], and [[Sonora Sucker]]. Historically co-occurred with now-extirpated (from Gila basin) [[Woundfin]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]].

**Parasites**: Copepod *Lernaea* sp., unidentified leech (Virgin River); protozoans *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* and *Myxobolus oblongus*; trematodes *Ornithodiplostomum ptychocheilus* and *Clinostomum marginatum*; cestode *Isoglaridacris bulbocirrus*; nematode *Rhabdochona decaturensis* (Aravaipa, Sycamore, Oak Creeks, Verde River).

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomic uncertainty**: Smith (1966) reduced *Pantosteus* to a subgenus of *Catostomus*; Minckley (1973, 1985) argued for retaining *Pantosteus* as a distinct genus based on morphological and hybridization criteria. A "desert mountain-sucker complex" may comprise multiple distinct forms/species (southern Gila River, central Gila River, Bill Williams River, two Virgin River forms, White River form).
- **Hybridization**: Occasional hybridization with sympatric [[Sonora Sucker]] documented; hybrids show intermediate but Sonora-sucker-skewed morphology and intermediate diet composition (Clarkson & Minckley 1988).
- **Reproduction**: Maturity at ~100 mm SL (range 85–120 mm). Spawning occurs late winter–early spring (documented January–July depending on population/location) on riffles/rapids/runs over gravel-cobble-boulder substrate in water <50 cm deep. Mating system and spawning periodicity (annual vs. non-annual) remain unknown.
- **Growth**: Most growth occurs June–August; females grow slightly larger than males at same age; fish >200 mm SL are 3–5 years old.
- **Movement**: Aravaipa Creek population shows seasonal movement (into cooler canyon reaches in summer, out in winter) (Siebert 1980), contrasting with Gila/San Francisco River populations showing high site fidelity (Bestgen et al. 1987a) — likely reflecting differing thermal regimes between smaller vs. larger streams.
- **Larval drift**: Peaks in early April and early May; primarily diurnal drifters (79% collected at noon/dusk); larvae shift from strongly nearshore-biased distribution at noon to even nearshore/midstream distribution at dawn (Bestgen et al. 1987b).
- **Conservation concerns**: Threatened by impoundment, water diversion, groundwater pumping, channelization, flow stabilization, sewage pollution, and pesticides. Intolerant of reservoir conditions and non-native fish assemblages. White River and Meadow Valley Wash (Nevada) populations placed under USFWS "notice of review" in 1985; southern Gila drainage populations flagged as increasingly rare.
- **Ecological role of natural flow regime**: Periodic flooding suppresses non-native fish populations and may serve as a spawning cue for desert sucker (Meffe & Minckley 1987; Minckley & Meffe 1987).
- **Research gaps**: Systematics/phylogenetics of the *Pantosteus*–*Catostomus* complex, spawning behavior and triggers, fecundity, spawning frequency, age at first reproduction, dispersal/barrier impacts, larval ecology, age/growth studies, and interactions with introduced species.
