# Intraspecific Variation in Diet Within and Between Trophic Morphs in Larval Tiger Salamanders

#herpetology #freshwater-ecology #ambystomatidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Collins and Holomuzki]] (1984, *Canadian Journal of Zoology* 62: 168–174) examines diet in larval [[Ambystoma Tigrinum Nebulosum]] from Arizona. Larvae show a feeding polymorphism: a "broad-headed" [[Cannibalistic Morph]] with a wider head and enlarged vomerine teeth, and a "typical" morph. The authors quantified diet across habitats, between morphs, and with age in typical larvae. Cannibalistic larvae ate mostly other salamanders (84%), while typical larvae rarely did (1 of 170 larvae analyzed). Both morphs also ate invertebrates. The authors conclude that "cannibalistic" and "typical" describe a dietary predilection rather than an absolute categorization.

## Background & Description

Diet and trophic morphology often covary within species. The authors distinguish three patterns of intraspecific variation: differences tied to sex, differences confined to one sex, and differences independent of sex. The third pattern, which includes the tiger salamander case, is considered especially relevant to speciation because trophic polymorphisms independent of sex may represent early ecological divergence within one species.

[[Powers]] (1903, 1907) first described the feeding polymorphism in larval and gilled adult *A. t. mavortium* in Nebraska. Powers characterized broad-headed morphs as cannibals and described typical morphs as having a "mixed diet" that included younger conspecifics. The polymorphism has since been documented in Oklahoma, Texas, Colorado, and Arizona. Earlier work by [[Rose and Armentrout]] (1976) on *A. t. mavortium* reported that all 30 broad-headed larvae examined from one population contained salamander larvae.

The study had two aims:
1. Quantify dietary differences between typical and cannibalistic morphs, both within and between habitats.
2. Describe how diet changes with ontogeny (age) in typical larvae from a single habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.** Larvae were seined from five populations in Arizona. Stomach analyses were restricted to four of these sites:

- **Baker Lake** (Coconino County, elevation 2274 m): a natural limestone sinkhole on the Mogollon Rim, with a maximum depth of about 1 m, surrounded by ponderosa pine (*Pinus ponderosa*) forest and dense emergent macrophytes. Sample: 167 typical larvae and 5 cannibals.
- **Snow Tank** (Navajo County, elevation 2134 m): a natural marsh deepened for cattle watering, with dense *Polygonum* and sedges. Sample: 435 typical and 11 cannibalistic larvae.
- **Big Meadows Tank No. 1** (Apache County, elevation 2774 m): an artificially deepened subalpine marsh with *Sparganium*, *Glyceria*, and *Ranunculus*. Sampled June–September 1981 (n = 66, 79, 28, and 106 by month; one cannibal in total).
- **Limestone Tank** (Coconino County, elevation 2219 m): an artificial meadow tank with no emergent macrophytes. Sample: 315 typical and 15 cannibalistic larvae.
- **Lower Cottonwood Tank** (Navajo County, elevation 1860 m): an artificial tank with *Potamogeton* near shore. Sample: 97 typical and 8 cannibals; none was used for stomach analysis.

**Diet categories.** Prey were grouped into six categories: *A. tigrinum* larvae, Crustacea, Mollusca, aquatic insects, terrestrial insects, and plant matter (including seeds). Plant matter was excluded from comparisons because it was presumed incidental. Volume was estimated visually using the [[Hynes Point System]].

**Diet of typical morphs.** Typical larvae consumed mainly aquatic insects, planktonic crustaceans (especially Cladocera), and some molluscs. Aquatic Diptera (notably Chaoboridae and Chironomidae) were the most frequently eaten insects among aquatic taxa.

**Trophic shifts with size and age.** Smaller larvae (under about 20 mm SVL) are primarily plankton feeders on cladocerans and copepods. Larger larvae shift toward benthic macroinvertebrates. Typical larvae may consume anywhere from 10% to 90% plankton, with the remainder being macroinvertebrates, primarily benthic insects.

## Key Findings

### 1. Cannibalistic morphs eat mostly salamanders
- Salamanders made up 84% of the diet of cannibalistic morphs (the figure appears as "f = 84%" in the abstract).
- At Snow Tank and Limestone Tank, cannibals ate a large percentage of typical larvae and no other salamander-free prey beyond invertebrates.
- Cannibals also ate a diversity of invertebrates, especially aquatic insects, so the morphs are not purely cannibalistic.

### 2. Typical morphs rarely eat salamanders
- Typical morphs ate conspecific larvae in only 1 of 170 instances.
- At all four sites, typical morphs ate aquatic insects, planktonic crustaceans, and molluscs.

### 3. Dietary overlap between morphs was low
- At Snow Tank, typical larvae and cannibals shared only two of five animal groups in their diets. Mean aquatic-insect use was 58% for typical larvae and 15% for cannibals.
- At Limestone Tank, both morphs ate aquatic insects, but they showed no overlap in the other four animal categories. The average typical morph ate about twice the proportion of aquatic insects consumed by a cannibal (34% versus 17%).
- Cannibals at Limestone ate five insect taxa, while cannibals at Snow Tank ate three; two taxa were shared.
- Differences in cannibal diet between sites were mainly quantitative. Qualitative differences were limited to aquatic insects. Cannibals at Snow Tank were larger, which may explain their higher proportion of salamanders.

### 4. Sex of cannibalistic morphs
- Powers (1907) had reported that cannibalistic individuals were almost exclusively male.
- At Lower Cottonwood Tank, the authors collected eight broad-headed branchiate larvae: six females, one male, and one juvenile. Both sexes can therefore develop into the broad-headed morph.

### 5. Ontogenetic diet shifts in typical larvae (Big Meadows No. 1)
- Two age classes (1st-year and 2nd-year larvae, approximately 13–14 and 1–2 months old in June and July) were distinguishable in the field.
- Over four months, only one instance of a typical larva eating another larva was recorded.
- Mollusc use was minimal in both age classes. Terrestrial insects were eaten infrequently, except in July, when 2nd-year larvae consumed winged reproductive ants from the tank surface.
- Crustaceans and aquatic insects formed a high proportion of both age classes' diets.
- In June, 1st-year larvae ate significantly more Cladocera than 2nd-year larvae (t = 5.09, 48 df, p < 0.001). This difference disappeared by July.
- In June, older larvae ate a significantly higher proportion of aquatic insects (t = 4.01, p << 0.001). They also ate twice as many aquatic insect taxa (14 versus 7). No significant difference in aquatic-insect proportion was found in July.
- Rank concordance of taxa eaten by the two age classes was not significant overall (Friedman's T = 4.55, Kendall's W = 0.39, p = 0.85), although concordance was greater in July (Kendall's W = 0.84, p = 0.50).

### 6. Ecological implications
- Both morphs eat invertebrates, so "cannibalistic" versus "typical" describes a predilection and is not an absolute categorization.
- Cannibal density may strongly influence the rate at which typical larvae develop into cannibals. Density of conspecifics likely affects this development, and cannibal numbers in turn affect the density of larvae in a population.
- Size-selective predation by typical larvae can affect pond community structure, including benthic macroinvertebrate abundances and pelagic crustacean composition.

### 7. Caveats
- Stomach samples were small for some groups (for example, 1–5 cannibals at some sites). Several differences between sites may reflect sampling error, prey availability, or cannibal size.
- Empty stomachs were excluded from the final statistics.
- Some habitat comparisons are based on only two sites.
