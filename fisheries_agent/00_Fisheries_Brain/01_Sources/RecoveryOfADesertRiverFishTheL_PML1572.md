# Razorback Sucker Recovery and the Proposed Black Wash Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from the *Arizona Conservation Voter* (Summer 1995) covers two conservation topics. The first is the recovery effort for the [[Razorback Sucker]], an endemic Colorado River fish that is close to extinction in the wild. The second is a critique of the proposed [[Black Wash Reservoir]] near Tucson, which the author argues would harm desert wildlife and waste public money. The razorback article reports that hatchery-reared fish are being released back into [[Lake Mohave]], but that natural recruitment has failed for decades. The reservoir article argues that a cheaper pipeline-based alternative would meet the same water-delivery need.

## Background & Description

The newsletter is a Tucson-based advocacy publication. Its issue includes a quotation from [[U.S. Forest Service]] Chief Jack Ward Thomas about keeping public lands in public hands. The razorback article was written by Paul C. Marsh of the [[Center for Environmental Studies]] at Arizona State University, with a co-author from the Department of Zoology at Arizona State University in Tempe. The reservoir article is by G. Nabors.

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**
- Grows to about 3 feet long and up to 13 lbs.
- Endemic to the [[Colorado River]] drainage. It occurred across much of the basin, from the Mexican delta north to the Rocky Mountain headwaters in Wyoming and Colorado, including the [[Salton Sea]], [[Gila River]] and [[San Juan River]] basins.
- Long-lived (over 50 years). A 24-inch female can carry up to 120,000 eggs, at about 5,000 eggs per inch of body length.
- Adults are bottom-oriented. In rivers they hold in slow, strong current over silt, sand and gravel. In [[Lake Mohave]] they feed among rocks along wave-washed shorelines and take plankton in midwater.
- Males develop breeding colors (dark backs, yellow bellies, white ventral fins, often with reddish bands) and tubercles on the tails and anal fins that help them grip females during spawning.
- Spawning runs from January to early May. Adhesive eggs stick to stones.

**Black Wash Reservoir (proposed)**
- Would sit on the fringes of the Santa Cruz River Basin, south of Tucson, on the edge of the [[Pascua Yaqui Reservation]].
- The article states it would store up to 15,000 acre-feet of [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water for emergencies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article states that most native Western North American fishes have declined because of habitat alteration by humans. Rivers are dammed, diverted and depleted for drinking water, agriculture, industry, waste disposal, recreation and stocking of game fish.

The razorback's decline is linked to the 1954 filling of Lake Mohave. The article notes that Native Americans and early settlers used the fish as food, and that a commercial fisherman was still selling razorbacks from [[Saguaro Lake]] on the [[Salt River]] as recently as 1949. The last large wild population in the Colorado basin is reported in Lake Mohave.

**Reservoir impacts**
- The proposed Black Wash site would affect the federally listed endangered [[Pima Pineapple Cactus]] and nearly 30 other plant species, according to the article.
- The article notes that federal administrators had suggested stocking the reservoir with non-native game fish. The author argues this could eliminate native fish in the Santa Cruz River Basin, and warns of the risk if a reservoir failure released its water and alien fish downstream.
- The author criticizes the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] for not discussing that risk in the [[Draft Environmental Impact Statement]] (DEIS).

## Key Findings

**Razorback recruitment failure**
- Hundreds of thousands of razorback larvae hatch each year in Lake Mohave, but there is no evidence that any survive to maturity. The oldest adults hatched around the time the lake was filled in 1954, so they are over 40 years old.
- Larvae hatch in about a week, begin feeding less than a week later at roughly 4/10 inch, and disappear from the lake before reaching 5/10 to 6/10 inch.
- Three explanations were tested: (1) turbine mortality at the dam, which found no supporting evidence; (2) starvation from missing food in the reservoir, which had some support; and (3) predation by non-native fish, which the article says accounts for most of the losses.

**Recovery program**
- Because predatory non-native fish (carp, catfish, sunfish and basses) cannot be removed from Lake Mohave, the program captures larvae instead. Larvae are phototactic, moving toward light at night, so they can be collected with lights from a boat or with light traps.
- Larvae are first reared in large aquaria for a few weeks, then placed in isolated bays separated from the lake, from which non-native predators have been removed.
- Fish are released at about one foot long, after being given an electronic tag for individual identification. The whole process takes about six months.
- More than 20,000 larvae were captured in Lake Mohave in 1995. The goal for the following year was 60,000 fish.
- Some tagged fish from earlier efforts have entered the breeding aggregations, which the article cites as evidence that the program is working.
- The program has involved volunteers, university professors and state and federal fisheries biologists since 1973.
- The authors conclude that the effort is "not yet a success story," but that limited success is better than none.

**Black Wash Reservoir critique**
- The DEIS comment period closed on July 14.
- The article argues the reservoir is unnecessary and harmful. It lists three other DEIS alternatives: recharging groundwater with CAP flow, building shallow holding basins, and doing nothing.
- It endorses **Alternative C**, which would add a second discharge line to the seven existing pumping plants between the Gila River and Tucson's CAP water treatment plant. If one line failed, the other could still deliver water to CAP customers during outages.
- According to the Bureau of Reclamation, dropping the reservoir would save about $35 million.
