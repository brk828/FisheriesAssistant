# Bilateral Asymmetry and Bilateral Variation in Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pleuronectidae

## Executive Summary

This monograph by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and Laura C. Hubbs examines left-right differences in paired and median structures of fishes. Its main focus is the flatfishes (order Heterosomata), the most asymmetric vertebrates, but it also surveys essentially symmetrical fishes such as [[Leptocottus Armatus]], [[Cottus Bairdii]] and several cyprinodonts. The authors argue that bilateral asymmetry is widespread among fishes and appears in many organs. They propose separate measures for the amount and direction of asymmetry, replacing [[Duncker Index of Asymmetry]], and interpret the findings for [[Handedness in Man]] and for systematics.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with a general account of vertebrate asymmetry, drawing on [[Ludwig 1932]] as the principal summary of earlier work. The authors state that most earlier descriptions were inadequate or poorly explained, and they set out to combine new data with old data, analysed by modern statistics.

**Statistical approach.** The authors reject Duncker's index (α) because it confounds the amount of asymmetry with its direction. They instead report:
- Percentage of asymmetry: 100(L + R)/N
- Percentage of dextrality: 100R/(L + R)
- Reliability: the [[Chi-Square Test]] (with Yates correction when expected values are below 25), giving P values. They caution against rigid thresholds such as P = 0.05.

**Scope.** Visceral asymmetries in the digestive and circulatory systems are treated only briefly, since Ludwig (1932) covers them. Numerical (meristic) differences receive the most detailed analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors place flatfish asymmetry in its adaptive setting. A flounder lying flat on one side can simulate the bottom and avoid predators. Eye migration and blind-side pigment loss follow from that life habit, but the side that becomes eyed is not explained by adaptation.

Geographic patterns are notable:
- In [[Platichthys Stellatus]], reversal (percentage of sinistral fish) is 49–60% on western North America, 68% near Kodiak Island and the Alaska Peninsula, and 100% in Japan.
- [[Platichthys Flesus]] in Europe usually shows reversal below 50%.
- Indiscriminate laterality is concentrated in the northeastern Pacific, a pattern the authors could not fully explain.

Environmental conditions such as light or the earth's rotation are judged unlikely to determine bilateral asymmetry in free-swimming animals.

## Key Findings

**Flatfish laterality and optic chiasma**
- Psettodidae ([[Psettodes Erumei]]) is the only flatfish family that is indiscriminately dextral or sinistral on a primordial basis. Museum counts were 10 sinistral and 13 dextral.
- Bothidae are normally sinistral; Pleuronectidae are normally dextral. Soles are normally dextral; tongue soles are normally sinistral.
- Optic chiasma type (monomorphic in Bothidae and Pleuronectidae) follows the eyed side, a relation first shown by [[Parker 1903]].

**Reversal**
- In [[Platichthys Stellatus]], all 476 Japanese specimens were sinistral. Hybrids with *Kareius bicoloratus* were 14 sinistral and 18 dextral, which suggests genetic control of laterality.
- Some reversal was also found in [[Paralichthys Californicus]] (93 sinistral, 57 dextral; P = 0.003) and [[Xystreurys Liolepis]] (9 sinistral, 18 dextral).
- A completely reversed [[Tanakius Kitaharae]] (220 mm standard length) was the only known full mirror-image flatfish. Its optic nerves crossed once, not twice.

**Viability**
- Duncker's Baltic data showed the proportion of sinistral flounders was higher among young fish (87:157) than among adults (53:172), χ² = 8.18, P = 0.004.
- The Puget Sound data were less conclusive (Table II), so the authors did not accept the decreased-viability hypothesis as established.

**Incomplete mirror imaging and secondary asymmetries**
- The optic nerve, visceral arrangement and branchiostegal membrane asymmetries do not reverse with eye position.
- Ambicoloration is regarded as a teratological return toward symmetry, found in only a few specimens.
- The eyed side in most flatfishes has more pectoral rays. In *Platichthys stellatus rugosus* (225 specimens), pectoral ray differences were measured for total and branched rays.

**Leptocottus armatus (handedness in pectoral rays)**
- Among 2,206 specimens from 12 localities, 142 had more left rays and 316 had more right rays (69% dextral among asymmetric fish; P < 0.00005).
- The right pectoral more often has a longer lowermost ray, and the lowest-ray length is inversely related to total ray count.

**Other fishes**
- [[Cottus Bairdii]]: 296 asymmetric fish, 223 right-higher and 73 left-higher; P is well below 0.00001.
- [[Clinocottus Analis]]: 58 left and 45 right asymmetric counts, which is sinistral and contradicts the cottoid pattern.
- Cyprinodontids ([[Cyprinodon Nevadensis]], [[Oryzias Latipes]]) show slight sinistrality. Pelvic fins in *Leptocottus* show a mild right bias (44 sinistral vs. 72 dextral).
- In the hagfish [[Eptatretus Stouti]] (cited as *Bdellostoma stouti*), gill openings are more often higher on the left.
- In cyprinid pharyngeal teeth, the left arch usually has the larger tooth count.
- In [[Salvelinus Fontinalis]] (4,642 specimens) the right optic nerve is dorsal more often (56%, P < 0.00001).

**Sex and sexual rights and lefts**
- Results do not show a clear link between sex and asymmetry overall.
- In [[Anableps]], [[Garman 1895]] claimed that males and females show opposite sidedness. The authors' added counts (for three species combined) show the deviation from equality is probable but not dependable.
- Several poeciliids have a gonopodium folded to one side, and the Phallostethidae and [[Horaichthys Setnai]] show associated asymmetries.

**Conclusions**
- Asymmetry is common among fishes and appears in many structures.
- A multitude of partly independent factors appears to control asymmetry.
- Reversal of sides is generally rare but is common in a few Pacific species.
- Handedness in fishes shows that human handedness is not an isolated phenomenon.
- Suggested further research includes large meristic surveys, metabolic studies of the pectoral fin during ray formation, and tests of the idea that available space controls meristic counts.
