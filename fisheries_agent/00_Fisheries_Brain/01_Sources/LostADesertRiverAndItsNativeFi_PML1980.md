# Seasonal Movement of Colorado Pikeminnow and the Loss of Native Fishes in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This note combines two abstracts from the Proceedings of the [[Desert Fishes Council]] (Volume XXXIII, 2001 Symposium, published December 2002). The first, in Spanish, examines seasonal movement of the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in controlled tributaries of the [[Green River]] subbasin, using telemetry and trap data. The second, in English with a Spanish summary, gives a historical perspective on the lower [[Colorado River]], which has lost most of its floodplain and [[Colorado River Delta]] habitat and now supports an entirely introduced fish fauna. Together they show that native fish habitat is both limited and disrupted, and that conventional reintroduction approaches are likely to fail.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] is described as a large native cyprinid of the Colorado River basin with a generally familiar, sedentary lifestyle. The document reports that adults can travel long distances (the abstract gives "360", with the unit garbled in the scan, presumably km). The first study focuses on how these fish use tributaries and spawning areas in the Green River subbasin.

The second abstract references three native species historically associated with the lower basin floodplain: the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and the Colorado pikeminnow. W. L. Minckley had suggested that core populations of these fishes originated in the broad floodplain and oxbow habitats that were once flooded across the lower river.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area (Green River subbasin):**
- [[Duchesne River]], a tributary used as a controlled habitat.
- [[Lodore Canyon]] of the Green River, a second controlled reach.
- Floodplain inundation zones along the middle Green River, sampled for movement patterns.

**Historical lower Colorado River:**
- Before high dams and storage reservoirs, the river periodically flooded hundreds of square kilometers of floodplain and desert playa.
- Upstream water diversion and agriculture have removed most of the delta and the lowermost 200 km of river.
- The remaining river is described as physically and biologically resembling the upper Mississippi or Missouri rivers.
- The lower Colorado River is noted as one of the few major rivers in the world with an entirely introduced fish fauna.
- Federal and state agencies are reintroducing native fishes into these altered waters.

**Methods mentioned:** [[Radio Telemetry]] (surgically implanted transmitters), [[Trap Sampling]] (described as "tipo *6,ke*", garbled in the scan), and historical analysis of old photographs and records.

## Key Findings

**Colorado pikeminnow movement (Green River subbasin)**
- Catch and telemetry data from 1997 to 2001 indicate that many adults are highly flexible in their use of habitat.
- Transmitters were implanted in 27 fish in the Duchesne River and 14 fish in Lodore Canyon between 1999 and 2001.
- Floodplain movement patterns were additionally studied in middle Green River inundation habitats from 1996 to 1999.
- At the start of the high-flow season, many fish in the main channel moved into the inundated floodplain and the controlled tributaries (Duchesne River and Lodore Canyon).
- Fish in the Duchesne River stayed there until the flood flows declined, then moved to [[Spawning Migration|spawning sites]]. Few returned to the Duchesne River after spawning.
- The following spring, many fish returned to the Duchesne River, and the same migration pattern was repeated.
- Fish from Lodore Canyon did not overwinter there, although they returned to the canyon after spawning in 2001. Fish that moved into Lodore Canyon travelled to sites more than 70 miles from the tributary.
- The authors conclude that Colorado pikeminnow are not establishing permanently in the controlled tributaries. Instead, they use these areas opportunistically, much as they use floodplain inundation zones in spring.

**Lower Colorado River historical perspective**
- Loss of the delta and lower 200 km of river has eliminated most of the ecological conditions in which native fishes evolved.
- Native fish populations were likely sustained by floodplain and oxbow habitats that no longer exist.
- The river now hosts a fully introduced fish community, which is a major management challenge.
- The authors conclude that current reintroduction methods, which are the traditional approaches used by agencies, are proving ineffective.
