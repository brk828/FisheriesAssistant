# Distribution and Abundance of Pelagic Fish in Lake Powell, Utah, and Lake Mead, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This study used hydroacoustics and vertical gill netting to measure the abundance and distribution of pelagic fish in two large [[Colorado River]] reservoirs, [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Mead]], across quarterly surveys from November 1995 through January 1999. Pelagic fish biomass was higher and more variable in Lake Powell, which reached 10,852,738 kg in August 1996, while Lake Mead ranged from 296,736 kg to 1,926,687 kg over the same period. Both reservoirs showed a marked decline in fish numbers between August 1996 and 1997. The authors conclude that hydroacoustics is the only practical method for measuring fish biomass in large reservoirs, but that schooling fish make estimates highly variable, and they recommend night surveys and more transects in patchy areas.

## Background & Description

The paper reports a monitoring program on two reservoirs of the regulated [[Colorado River]]. The system drains roughly 630,000 km² across Arizona, California, Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming. The reservoirs are dominated by pelagic fishes, including [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), a planktivore, and [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), a predator that is also the subject of creel management. The paper names other species only in its reference list, so the species focus is primarily the pelagic community as a whole.

Lake Mead has four large basins (Boulder, Virgin, Temple, and Gregg). Its main inflows are the Colorado River (98%), the [[Virgin River]] (1%), and [[Las Vegas Wash]] (1%). Lake Powell is fed mainly by the Colorado River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Both reservoirs are phosphorus limited and classed as [[Oligotrophy]] to mildly mesotrophic. Primary production is highest near nutrient inputs (rivers and treated sewage effluent) and declines with distance from those sources. [[Chlorophyll-a]] often falls below 2 µg L⁻¹, a level the authors describe as inadequate to support planktivores such as threadfin shad and age-0 fish. Water clarity often exceeds 15 m.

Nutrient loading from the Colorado River into Lake Mead has declined since [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in 1964. The authors argue that pelagic fish communities are highly vulnerable to shifts in [[Nutrient Loading]] and predator-prey interactions.

Regulatory and management context is also relevant. State agencies liberalized creel limits on striped bass to reduce the frequency and severity of emaciated fish and fish die-offs, which the paper describes as a problem that had made fishing economically nonviable.

## Key Findings

### Study design and methods

- **Sites:** 25 monitoring sites were selected (14 in Lake Powell, 11 in Lake Mead) to represent basins, main-stem canyons, side canyons, and inflows.
- **Schedule:** Surveys were conducted quarterly, beginning at Lake Powell (5-7 days) and ending at Lake Mead (3-5 days).
- **Gill netting:** Six vertical gill nets (2.5 m × 100 m), each with a different mesh size (12, 25, 31, 42, 50, and 64 mm), were suspended from floating spools to depths >30 m and fished overnight at eight sites in total, weather permitting.
- **Hydroacoustics:** A single-beam [[Echosounder]] (BioSonics DT-4000, upgraded to a dual-beam DT-5000 in September 1996) operated at 420 kHz, with data thresholded at −60 dB. Each site had three transects, with coordinates programmed into a GPS navigation system. Transects at dams and narrow canyons were 1-2 km long and zigzagged between shorelines.

### Abundance and biomass

- **Densities:** In August 1996 and 1997, average fish densities in Lake Powell were 26,188 and 2,605 fish ha⁻¹, compared with 2,394 and 1,840 fish ha⁻¹ in Lake Mead.
- **Total biomass:** Lake Powell ranged from 453,097 kg (May 1996) to 10,852,738 kg (August 1996). Lake Mead ranged from 296,736 kg to 1,926,687 kg over the same period.
- **Spatial pattern:** Biomass generally decreased with distance downstream. Biomass was below 10 kg ha⁻¹ at 13% of Lake Powell stations (n = 108) and 12% of Lake Mead stations (n = 87).
- **Exceptions:** In August 1996, biomass above 20 kg ha⁻¹ extended throughout Lake Powell, averaging 197 kg ha⁻¹ against 28 kg ha⁻¹ in Lake Mead. In November, shallow-inflow communities appeared to move to deeper water for winter.
- **Seasonal comparison:** In May, Lake Mead's biomass was 65% of Lake Powell's (296,736 vs. 453,097 kg) and 62% for the second reported pair (101,016 vs. 162,262 kg).

### Interannual decline

Between August 1996 and 1997, estimated fish numbers fell from 276 million to 90 million in Lake Powell and from 108 million to 66 million in Lake Mead.

### Carrying capacity and productivity

Data suggest that by late summer, Lake Powell can support nearly six times the biomass of Lake Mead. Carrying capacity during early spring appears lower and possibly less variable.

### Sampling variability and limitations

- Fish detections in sparsely populated areas were rare and, when they occurred, generally came from large schools. The authors attribute this to schooling fish in large water bodies, most evident in daytime surveys.
- Sample variance in more densely populated areas ranged from 25% to 30%.
- The authors note that hydroacoustics is the only practical method for measuring biomass in large reservoirs, but that schooling species produce highly variable estimates.

### Recommendations

- Conduct more surveys at night.
- Increase the number of transects where fish distribution is suspected to be patchy.
