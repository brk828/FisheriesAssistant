# Scripps Institution of Oceanography, University of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #oceanography

## Executive Summary

This 1963 institutional profile, published in the *American Zoologist*, describes the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]] at the [[University of California]] in La Jolla, California. The document covers the institution's history, organization, research programs, facilities and regional fauna. It is an overview rather than a research study, so it reports no original field or laboratory data on any species. Fish and other animals appear only as examples of the regional fauna and of research interests.

## Background & Description

**History.** The institution began in 1892 as a field investigation of coastal animals started by [[William Emerson Ritter]], head of Zoology at the University of California. It moved to San Diego in 1903 under the Marine Biological Association of San Diego, a private body supported by [[Ellen Browning Scripps]] and E. W. Scripps. In 1912 it was integrated into the university as the Scripps Institution for Biological Research. It was renamed Scripps Institution of Oceanography in 1925, after its scope widened to other marine sciences. Growth accelerated from 1936 to 1948 under [[Harald U. Sverdrup]], largely because of the wartime recognition that oceanographic proficiency is essential to national security. Later leaders were [[Carl Eckart]] (1948–1951) and [[Roger Revelle]] (1951–), with [[Thomas Wayland Vaughan]] having led the institution in the interim years.

**Growth, 1946–1962.** The document gives these figures:

- Faculty: 12 to 25
- Total personnel: 50 to about 800, including 110 ranking from Assistant Professor or Assistant Research Scientist upward
- State support: $64,000 to $1,500,000
- Total financial support (contracts and grants included): $150,000 to $7,900,000
- Gross square footage of permanent buildings: 42,854 to 220,788 (excluding extensive quarters at the [[Navy Electronics Laboratory]])
- Research fleet: from one schooner to eight ocean-going vessels, 65 to 180 feet long and 99 to 2,079 gross tons

**Organization.** The instructional departments are graduate-only: Oceanography (including biological oceanography, ecology, fisheries and environmental biology) and Marine Biology. Research units include the Oceanic Research Division, Earth Sciences Division, Marine Biology Division, Marine Physical Laboratory, Visibility Laboratory, Marine Life Research Group and Applied Oceanography Group. Organized research programs are Marine Life Research, [[Scripps Tuna Oceanography Research]] and the Indian Ocean Investigation. The institution is run by a Director who reports to the University President through the Chancellor of the San Diego Campus.

**Affiliations.** Closely linked units include the Institute of Marine Resources, the Institute of Geophysics and Planetary Physics, and the School of Science and Engineering. Two U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries laboratories and the [[Inter-American Tropical Tuna Commission]] are also nearby. The institution maintains close ties with the San Diego Zoological Gardens, the San Diego Natural History Museum and the California Cooperative Oceanic Fisheries Investigations.

**Research interests.** The document lists a broad set of zoological topics: comparative animal physiology, animal pigments, particulate matter in marine nutrition, productivity and grazing, trace-element accumulation, vision, bioluminescence, population dynamics and ecological analysis. Systematic, life-history, zoogeographical and oceanographic work covers Radiolaria, Foraminifera, Chaetognatha, Copepoda, Euphausiidae, other Crustacea, and pelagic molluscs and tunicates. Staff also study the systematics, distribution, life history, population dynamics and economics of fishes, with some attention to other marine vertebrates. A special topic is comparative cardiology, focusing on the aneural heartbeat of cyclostomes. Paleoecological work addresses prehistoric humans and changing environments.

**Facilities.** The main facility is the research fleet, which the institution's own Marine Facilities operates. Two vessels can dredge to the greatest depths. The [[Floating Instrument Platform]] (FLIP), which draws 300 feet when placed in its vertical stable position, had recently been launched. Other facilities include a radio station (WWD) for daily fleet contact, a 1000-foot pier at La Jolla, the Experimental Aquarium, the Thomas Wayland Vaughan Museum-Aquarium, a coastal invertebrate and algae preserve, and a fenced marsh preserve in Mission Bay. The Oceanographic Data Archives hold more than 300,000 processed bathythermograph records. Collections include marine vertebrates (emphasis on bathypelagic and Pacific Coast fishes), predominantly bathypelagic invertebrates, refrigerated marine-geological bottom cores and seawater samples from various depths. The institution also has a radiocarbon laboratory, mass spectrometers and electron microscopes. Funding from the [[National Science Foundation]] was secured for a large pool with towing equipment and a new comparative physiology building.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

San Diego sits in the warm-temperate zone of the rich North Pacific fauna. Cool-temperate fauna, which is richer intertidally, lies within reach in central California, and tropical forms occur in the [[Gulf of California]], where the cooperating San Diego Society of Natural History maintains a field station.

- **Marine mammals:** [[Gray Whale]] (*Eschrichtius gibbosus*) migrate alongshore each year and breed in Baja California lagoons. Other cetaceans occur. [[Elephant Seal]] (*Mirounga angustirostris*) and [[California Sea Lion]] (*Zalophus californianus*) are common on adjacent islands.
- **Reptiles and birds:** Sea turtles (Cheloniidae) are abundant to the south. The marine bird fauna is described as rich.
- **Fishes:** The shore-fish fauna includes many ovoviviparous and viviparous teleosts, notably many *Sebastodes* species and the unique Embiotocidae. The [[Blind Goby]] (*Typhlogobius californiensis*) lives intertidally. Several hagfish species (Myxinidae) are easily collected.
- **Invertebrates:** Sea urchins, sand dollars and other echinoderms are abundant on the shore, with good representation of other phyla.
- **Deep water:** The [[Bathypelagic Zone]] is especially rich and can be reached in about two hours from the institution, making it widely accessible to the fleet.

The document notes that no inclusive local faunal list has been published. Local checklists exist for birds, reptiles, fishes and algae, and monographs cover various pelagic invertebrates.

Funding history includes a million-dollar grant from the [[Rockefeller Foundation]] in the 1950s, which markedly advanced marine biology at Scripps.

## Key Findings

- Scripps Institution of Oceanography is described as a major, year-round centre for research and graduate training in marine sciences, with the sea as its chief laboratory.
- The institution grew strongly between 1946 and 1962 in faculty, personnel, funding, floor space and fleet size (figures listed above).
- Research spans physiology, ecology, systematics, population dynamics and oceanography across fishes, invertebrates and other marine groups.
- Access to the bathypelagic zone within two hours and a fleet of eight ocean-going vessels, including FLIP, are the main operational strengths.
- Visiting scientists are welcomed year-round, although there is no regular summer session. Housing on campus is limited, though accommodation is available in nearby communities.
- The document ends with the institution's location and address. It is in La Jolla, roughly two miles north of the village centre and within the northern city limits of San Diego.
