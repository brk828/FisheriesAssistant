# Survival, Growth and Recruitment of Larval and Juvenile Razorback Suckers in Green River Floodplain Depressions, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 2004 final report from the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, funded by the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], tested whether floodplain depressions containing abundant [[Nonnative Fish]] can still serve as rearing habitat for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). It followed the [[Levee Removal Study]], which had found too few wild razorbacks in the Green River to assess floodplain use. Age-1 fish and larvae were stocked into three depressions on the [[Green River]] in 1999 and 2000 and monitored for survival, growth, and movement.

Larval survival was never detected. Age-1 survival after the first growing season ranged from 37% to 57%. Survival fell sharply in 2000 after below-average spring flows and summer drought produced poor water quality, with fish kills at [[The Stirrup]] and [[Baeser Bend]]. Stocked fish grew well, and 666 floodplain-origin razorbacks were judged to have entered the river population. The authors conclude that stocked age-1 fish can supplement the river population, and that floodplains can support razorbacks when flows allow adequate connection.

## Background & Description

Razorback sucker is an endemic, endangered catostomid of the Colorado River Basin. The report treats floodplain wetlands as presumed critical rearing habitat. Flaming Gorge Dam has reduced the magnitude and duration of spring peak flows, which in turn reduces river-floodplain connection (Muth et al. 2000).

Floodplain wetlands are productive. Zooplankton densities (organisms/L) were 0–1.3 in the main channel, 0–13.1 in backwaters, and 4.2–81.5 in floodplain habitats (Wydoski and Wick 1998). Earlier work captured 73 juvenile razorbacks in the nonnative-dominated Old Charley Wash wetland (Modde 1996, 1997), but survival to adulthood was never shown.

The study addressed three open questions from the Levee Removal Study:
1. Can larvae survive in nonnative-dominated floodplains?
2. Do surviving fish grow, and how long do they stay?
3. Do fish leave the floodplain and recruit to the river, and what triggers this movement?

Study sites were [[Floodplain Depressions]] on the Middle Green River, about 27 km south of Vernal, in a low-gradient reach invaded by [[Tamarisk]]. Each site was connected to the river by breaches cut into natural levees:

- **The Stirrup** (Rm 273.0): one downstream breach, 146 m × 6 m; about 7.8 ha inundated at 13,000 cfs; connects near 15,000 cfs.
- **Baeser Bend** (Rm 272.6): one central breach, 61 m × 6 m; about 15.5 ha at 13,000 cfs; connects near 14,000 cfs.
- **[[Above Brennan]]** (Rm 268.0): five connection points (one 30.5 m × 12 m downstream breach, three upstream breaches, one natural connection); about 16.5 ha at 13,000 cfs; connects near 11,000 cfs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Each site held large nonnative populations at stocking, built up after several years of above-average flows. Fall 1999 removal estimates were:

- Baeser Bend: 5,357.4 kg (458,474 fish)
- The Stirrup: 2,208.2 kg (310,565 fish)
- Above Brennan: 711.4 kg (86,122 fish)

Four months after larval stocking, The Stirrup's nonnative community was dominated by fathead minnow (53.2%) and black bullhead (35.6%), with green sunfish and carp also present.

Other predators and competitors include [[Odonata Nymphs]], which destroyed significant numbers of larvae in laboratory trials (Horn et al. 1994). Larvae are considered predator-naive (Johnson et al. 1993). Water quality was a major concern in 2000. Dissolved oxygen fell to lethal levels, and [[Dissolved Oxygen]] depletion linked to decomposing organic matter was the suspected cause of the Stirrup loss. Late-summer water quality data (Appendix B) include temperatures of about 22–25 °C, pH near 7.6–8.4 at Stirrup and Brennan, and dissolved oxygen near 0 mg/L at some overnight readings.

## Key Findings

**Stocking**
- Each site received 1,985 age-1 razorbacks in spring 1999 and 2,511 in spring 2000, all coded-wire tagged.
- 1999 age-1 densities: 254 fish/ha (The Stirrup), 128 fish/ha (Baeser Bend), 120 fish/ha (Above Brennan). 2000 densities: 322, 162 and 152 fish/ha.
- Larval stocking: 56,907 larvae into The Stirrup in 1999 (about 7,290 fish/ha, in four batches between 18 May and 2 June); 58,240 larvae into Baeser Bend in 2001 (3,757 fish/ha, 8 and 11 May).
- In 2000, 204 age-1 fish were held at the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] as a stocking-stress control, with no mortality. They were stocked into Baeser Bend on 28 July 1999.

**Survival**
- Age-1 population estimates after the first growing season, by [[Mark-Recapture Estimation]]:
  - The Stirrup: 783 fish, 37% survival
  - Baeser Bend: 1,136 fish, 52% survival
  - Above Brennan: 1,129 fish, 57% survival
- The conclusions report first-year survival of 56–75%, which does not match the 37–57% in the results. The source text is inconsistent here.
- Baeser Bend had the narrowest confidence intervals because its open habitat made [[Fyke Net Sampling]] more efficient. Dead terrestrial vegetation at The Stirrup and Above Brennan reduced sampling efficiency.
- Larval survival was never detected at either site. The authors could not rule out ineffective sampling.
- Survival collapsed in 2000. The Stirrup showed no razorbacks in late-summer sampling, with mortality probably between 4 June and July. Several hundred dead razorbacks were found at Baeser Bend on 14 August after a fish kill over 12–14 August.
- At Baeser Bend, 520 fish were removed and 514 released to the river (6 died in handling). Depletion analysis estimated 749 fish present, leaving about 229 to overwinter. None survived the winter.
- Above Brennan had no fish kill. In September 2000, 52 razorbacks were captured, and 49 were captured in spring 2001. Above Brennan was not sampled successfully after 2001 and no razorbacks were caught in late summer 2001.
- Above Brennan's 2000 cohort vanished, and dead fish were seen against breach-trap leads during connection.

**Growth**
- First-season growth was similar at The Stirrup and Above Brennan. Fish grew from about 110 mm (The Stirrup) and 96 mm (Above Brennan) at stocking to 317 mm and 310 mm by September/October. Weight gains were 336 g and 347 g.
- Baeser Bend fish (1999 April stock) grew from 111 mm to 341 mm, with weight gain of 442 g, at 1.4 mm/day and 2.7 g/day.
- Hatchery-held fish grew at 0.9 mm/day and 0.3 g/day, compared with 1.5 mm/day and 1.4 g/day after stocking at Baeser Bend. The conclusions describe the hatchery rate as only about 50% of the floodplain rate.
- Second-season growth at Above Brennan averaged 0.5 mm/day and 2.6 g/day. Fish reached 410 mm and 863 g by fall 2000.
- Length growth slowed in the second season, but weight gain stayed roughly constant.

**Movement and Recruitment**
- Connection durations: 1999 (12 May–25 June): 52 days at Above Brennan, 33 at Baeser Bend, 31 at The Stirrup. 2000 (24 May–7 June): 15 days at Above Brennan, 7 at Baeser Bend, 3 at The Stirrup. Water depth at The Stirrup was only 5 cm during the 2000 connection.
- Only two age-1 fish left the sites in 1999, both at Baeser Bend. This suggests age-1 fish preferred to stay.
- In 2000, 42 age-2 fish left: 31 from Baeser Bend, 10 from Above Brennan, and 1 from The Stirrup.
- [[Breach Traps]] were not effective. Block nets failed, and high water velocities and beaver and muskrat damage opened holes.
- In 2000 river sampling, 41 razorbacks were caught, 23 of them (56%) of floodplain origin.
- In 2001 river sampling, 91 of the 149 razorbacks caught (61%) were of floodplain origin (the Executive Summary gives 148 total).
- The authors estimate between 1,000 and 2,000 floodplain fish entered the river population. Of 666 definite contributors, 44 were caught leaving the sites, 108 were caught later in the river, and 514 were moved from Baeser Bend by trammel net.
- Fish dispersed a mean of 20.3 river miles (2000) and 21.5 river miles (2001).

**Conclusions and Recommendations**
- Stocked age-1 fish grew well and survived at 56–75% (or 37–57%, see above) in the first year.
- Survival dropped sharply in year two because of low flows, poor connection and poor water quality. Long, high-magnitude flows are needed to maintain water quality. Mechanical support of sites (pumping) can help.
- Larval survival remains undetermined. The authors recommend continued study of larval survival to recruitment, including entrainment, nonnative reset, larval density, and food and water quality.
- Monitor the spawning contribution of floodplain-reared fish.
- Use floodplain depressions as grow-out ponds in average or above-average flow years.
- Conduct additional water quality monitoring at stocking sites.
