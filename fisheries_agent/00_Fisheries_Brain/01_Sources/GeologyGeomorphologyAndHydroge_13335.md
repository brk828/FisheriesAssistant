# South Australia's Mound Springs: Geology, Geomorphology and Hydrogeology of the Lake Eyre South Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #groundwater-springs

## Executive Summary

This section of a 1985 Nature Conservation Society of South Australia report, edited by John Greenslade, Leo Joseph and Anne Reeves, examines the geology, geomorphology and hydrogeology of the mound springs near [[Lake Eyre]] South. The mound springs are natural outlets of the [[Great Artesian Basin]], where groundwater discharges after very long subsurface travel. The authors describe five geomorphic units, the Cainozoic evolution of the landscape, the basin's water yields and salinity, and a classification of mound springs by activity. They conclude that man-made disturbance is degrading this fragile landform and recommend three areas for preservation.

## Background & Description

The chapter is authored by R. Thomson and S. Barnett. The study area lies within the Curdimurka Sheet (1:250,000, H53-8), at the extreme southwest of the Great Artesian Basin. Earlier geological work is attributed to Woodward (1885), Brown (1894), Jack (1930), and Firman (1967 to 1971), among others. The Cainozoic stratigraphy and geomorphology were treated by Jessup (1961), Jessup & Norris (1971) and Wopfner & Twidale (1967).

The region is structured by the Great Artesian Basin, described as one of the largest artesian basins in the world. It covers roughly 1,700,000 km², of which about 350,000 km² lie in South Australia. Mound springs are defined as spring outlets with an accretion of sediment built up around the vent. Their mound material is derived mainly from precipitated solutes and colloids, with calcium carbonate as the principal lithifying agent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geomorphic units.** Five units are recognised:
1. **Silcrete surface**: dissected plateaux, mesas and buttes 30–50 m above the plain (New Twin Hill at 110 m a.s.l.), with silcrete 0.5–4 m thick over kaolinitic and montmorillonitic clays up to 25 m deep.
2. **Pediment surface**: gently sloping from 60–25 m a.s.l., cut into Cretaceous shales and Pleistocene gypsum, with pronounced gilgai.
3. **Gypsum surface**: approximately 3–15 m a.s.l., with gibber lag, dunes, claypans and kopi pans.
4. **Post-gypsum surface and floodplains**: the lowest, youngest unit, subject to flash flooding; Lake Eyre South lies 13.5 m below sea level.
5. **Mound springs**: scattered in a line from the upper eastern border to the northwest of the study area.

**Groundwater and water quality.** Before European settlement, the basin was recharged mainly on its eastern margin near the [[Great Dividing Range]] and discharged by natural vertical leakage through confining beds and by springs at the margins. Leakage was believed to be the majority of loss. Artesian water quality deteriorates southwestward toward the mound springs. Northeastern waters of 450–600 mg/L are good quality, while western waters are sulphate ("corrosive") types, which attack steel casings. Corrosive water commonly destroys well casings within about two years, forming uncontrolled flows and craters.

**Human impact.** Cattle, rabbits, settlers and tourists have damaged the mounds directly through contact and indirectly through artesian water extraction. Rabbit burrows undermine slopes at Strangways.

**Geomorphic evolution.** Landscape history reflects minor tectonism, Pleistocene faulting of basin margins, and alternating wet and arid phases. [[Table 1 Geomorphic History (Jessup 1961)]] and [[Table 2 Geomorphic History (Wopfner and Twidale 1967)]] summarise competing sequences. Wopfner & Twidale place mound spring and travertine formation at about 80,000 years ago, during back-filling of gypsite sediments in swamps flanking the pediments.

## Key Findings

**Discharge and withdrawal figures (South Australia):**
- Total artesian well flow estimated at 77 × 10⁶ kL/year, from approximately 150 flowing wells averaging 1,500 kL/day.
- Mound spring flow estimated at about 30 × 10⁶ kL/year.
- Natural vertical leakage estimated at 91 × 10⁶ kL/year.
- Man-made losses are approximately 40% of total South Australian discharge.
- Basin-wide flow peaked at 2.0 × 10⁶ kL/day around 1918 (from about 1,500 bores), falling to about 1.5 × 10⁶ kL/day (Habermayl, 1980).

**Mound spring classification:**
- **Extinct Pleistocene springs** (e.g., [[Hamilton Hill]], Beresford Hill): 28–43 m high, caprock of spring limestone, no recent activity.
- **Active Recent springs**: mounds 1–4 m high, 5–12 m across typically; Blanche Cup is the widest at 25.5 m and holds the largest pool (15.2 m). Blanche Cup, the Bubbler, the Mini Bubbler and Strangways springs are among those active.
- **Waning springs**: closed vents, seepage two-thirds down slope, mounds 3–4 m high and 15–18 m wide.
- **Non-active springs**: vents infilled, slopes 20–25° with slumping, mounds 2–3 m high, considered geologically short-lived.

**Mound growth example:** a spring discharging 200 L/hour at 4,000 mg/L solute would deposit about 170 tonnes of calcium carbonate in 1,000 years, enough to build a hemispherical mound 3 m high.

**Conservation recommendation:** the authors recommend [[Blanche Cup]], Strangways and Hermit Hill for geomorphic preservation.

**Monitoring methods:** Williams & Holmes (1978) propose estimating mound spring discharge from the area of swamp vegetation, which avoids difficult stream gauging. A [[Bureau of Mineral Resources]] computer model of the basin (Habermayl, 1980) is used to predict the effects of withdrawals. Isotope dating and drilling aim to reconstruct the basin's discharge history.

**Terminology:** the authors reject the term "dormant" (borrowed from vulcanology) for mound springs, because a spring's mound growth eventually lowers pressure below what is needed for flow.
