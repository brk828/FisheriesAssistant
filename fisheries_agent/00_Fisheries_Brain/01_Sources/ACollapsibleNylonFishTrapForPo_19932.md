# A Collapsible Nylon Fish Trap for Pond Sampling

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-sampling-gear

## Executive Summary

This fisheries technical note by [[Alfred Houser]] of the [[Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory]] describes the development of a collapsible nylon-net fish trap to replace the rigid [[Wire Trap]] used for sampling ponds and small lakes in Oklahoma. The wire trap was effective but heavy, hard to transport, prone to netting damage, and linked to injury and higher mortality in fish used in [[Mark-And-Recapture]] population estimates. Field tests in 1959 and 1960 led to a redesigned [[Nylon Trap]] with 5/8-inch knotted nylon webbing, spring-steel hoops, and throat fingers. In a 1960 comparison, the nylon design caught more fish per lift than the wire trap, and the author concluded that the nylon trap should become the standard sampling gear.

## Background & Description

The wire trap was the standard gear used by Oklahoma fishery workers for about the previous decade, particularly on ponds and small lakes. It was cylindrical, measured 6 feet long and 3 feet in diameter, had conical throats at each end, and was built on a welded steel frame of four longitudinal rods and three circular hoops. Its cover was galvanized 1-inch-mesh poultry netting, with a side door for removing fish and a rope and wooden float for setting and retrieving it. Its rigid form meant it could be set simply by lowering it into the water.

Workers raised several criticisms:

- **Weight:** The trap weighed 30.5 pounds, which was considered excessive, and it was difficult to transport.
- **Maintenance:** The netting normally had to be replaced every 3 months, and bent netting distorted the trap's shape.
- **Worker injury:** Cut and broken wire ends injured hands and damaged clothing.
- **Fish injury:** Wire netting injured fish, and injured fish in mark-and-recapture estimates suffered higher mortality than normal.

Advances in synthetic fibers offered a way to address these problems. The laboratory built and tested several models that kept the general wire-trap configuration but used knotted nylon webbing for all webbing and lines. The new design used five spring-steel hoops in place of the steel framework, which made the trap collapsible. Detachable wooden brails held the net in a rigid form when fished, and the finished net was treated with a preservative for synthetic-fiber nets. The work was published as Contribution No. 75 of the Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory, a cooperative unit of the [[Oklahoma Department Of Wildlife Conservation]] and the [[University Of Oklahoma Biological Survey]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The trap was designed for sampling fish populations in ponds and small lakes. Sampling efficiency depended on how well the gear retained fish across a range of sizes, so the study examined both catch rates and [[Mesh Selectivity]].

Two aspects of the design bear on sampling quality:

- **Escapement:** Early nylon models caught far fewer fish than the wire traps. The author attributed the difference mainly to fish escaping through the throats, so throat fingers were added to correct this.
- **Length range:** The smaller 5/8-inch mesh retained smaller fish and extended the length range of fish caught relative to the 1-inch wire mesh. Part of the nylon trap's higher efficiency was attributed to this selectivity, although for some species it appeared to operate more effectively across the whole length range.

Fish-handling effects were also considered. Wire netting caused injury to fish, which mattered because injured fish in mark-and-recapture work had higher mortality. The nylon trap was reported to eliminate this injury, and the preservative was said to protect the net from sunlight and stiffen the twine, which practically eliminated [[Gilling]] of fish.

## Key Findings

**Field tests and design evolution**

- **June–July 1959, [[Lake Louis Burtschi]]** (160-acre lake, southwest Oklahoma): During a [[Population Estimate]], the first nylon model (30-inch diameter, 6 feet long, throat openings about 4.5 inches) caught 3.9 fish per lift in 265 lifts, while the wire traps caught 16.5 fish per lift in 379 lifts. This prompted design changes, and fingers were added to the throats to correct likely escapement.
- **January 1960, [[Crystal Lake]]** (27-acre lake near the laboratory): Three traps of each of four types were compared.

| Trap type | Catch per lift (fish) | Fish caught | Lifts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wire trap, 1-inch mesh | 11.4 | 170 | 15 |
| 35.5-inch nylon, fingered throats, 5/8-inch mesh | 14.4 | 216 | 15 |
| 30-inch nylon, fingered throats, 5/8-inch mesh | 12.2 | 171 | 14 |
| 30-inch nylon, no throat fingers, 5/8-inch mesh | 4.3 | 87 | 13 |

**Comparative results**

- Adding throat fingers raised the 30-inch trap's catch from 4.3 to 12.2 fish per lift, a 180-percent improvement.
- Increasing diameter from 30 to 35.5 inches raised the fingered nylon trap's catch from 12.2 to 14.4 fish per lift, an 18-percent gain in effectiveness.
- Both fingered nylon sizes outperformed the wire trap. The 35.5-inch nylon trap's 14.4 fish per lift was a 26-percent improvement over the wire trap's 11.4.
- Species recorded in the comparison included [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]], green sunfish, warmouth, hybrid sunfish, golden shiner, black bullhead, and [[Channel Catfish]].
- The length-frequency table reports average total lengths between 4.6 and 5.7 inches across its columns.

**Physical and economic advantages**

- **Weight:** The new trap weighs 18 pounds, about 40 percent less than the wire trap's 30.5 pounds.
- **Transport:** The collapsible design allows 30 or more traps to fit in a station wagon, whereas wire traps could be carried only in a truck or pickup.
- **Handling safety:** Nylon webbing removed the hand injuries and torn clothing caused by wire ends.
- **Cost:** Materials for 13 traps averaged $20.90 each. Construction took a minimum of 4 hours, with labor averaging $8 per trap, for a total of $28.90 per trap.
- **Durability:** Trials were too short to fully assess wear. Based on recent experience with treated nylon in trap nets, the author expected no visible damage for at least 2 years, and repairs can be made with nylon twine.

**Construction specifications**

- **Dimensions:** 6 feet long, 35.5 inches in diameter.
- **Webbing:** 5/8-inch square-mesh knotted No. 9 nylon, 127.5 meshes deep and 123 meshes long, about 2 pounds 12 ounces.
- **Other materials:** Twine (No. 9, No. 12, No. 60, No. 120), 20 feet of 1/4-inch nylon rope, a 2×10×10-inch wooden marker float, two yellow-pine brails, 13 metal rings, and 5 spring-steel hoops with pipe couplings.
- **Assembly:** The procedure is 15 steps covering webbing preparation, forming the cylinder, installing hoops, attaching brails and bridles, adding throat fingers, making the door and metal closure pin, and treating the net with preservative. Figure 2 gives mesh dimensions for cutting the webbing.

**Conclusion**

The author concluded that the collapsible nylon trap has definite advantages over the wire trap for pond sampling. These advantages were judged sufficient to replace the wire trap as the laboratory's standard gear for sampling ponds and small lakes.
