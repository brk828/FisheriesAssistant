# Virgin River Habitat Conservation and Management Plan (VRHMP), Draft 1995

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft plan, prepared by the [[Washington County Water Conservancy District]] (WCWCD) and dated January 10, 1995, seeks to reconcile rapid population growth and water development in Washington County, Utah, with protection of threatened and endangered aquatic and riparian species. Washington County's population was 48,560 in 1990 and 55,477 in 1992, and projections run to between 203,937 and 552,872 people by 2040. The Virgin River floodplain is described as the "lifeblood" of the county's ecosystem.

The plan would acquire or protect riparian, wetland and riverine habitat within the 100-year floodplains of the Virgin River, Santa Clara River and Ash Creek. In exchange, it provides water for county growth and supports a [[Section 10 Permit]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (TESA) for incidental take. Its central measure is reducing winter flows at the [[Washington Fields Diversion]] from 86 cfs to 50 cfs (November–April), with the saved water stored in [[Sand Hollow Reservoir]] and Quail Creek Reservoir.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Washington County lies in arid southwestern Utah, adjacent to Nevada and Arizona. Most residents live in a narrow corridor along the Virgin River at about 2,800 ft elevation. The county spans roughly 2,500 ft to 10,000 ft, and 86 percent of its land is state or federal.

**History.** Irrigation diversions began with [[Mormon Settlement]] of the basin, starting in 1854–1857. By 1903 at least 15 diversions irrigated 6,548 acres (1902 figure). The river was already dry in sections during dry months. Table 3 lists the diversions on the Virgin River, Santa Clara River and Ash Creek, and Table 5 lists surface water rights, but both are too detailed to reproduce here.

**Species of concern.** The federally listed and proposed species in the corridor include:
- [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): listed endangered in 1970; a small, silvery, scaleless minnow adapted to swift, sand-bottomed streams. Present range runs from LaVerkin Springs to Lake Mead.
- [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*): listed endangered in 1990; averages about 20 cm. Its present range reaches only to the Mesquite Diversion.
- [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*): proposed for threatened listing (Federal Register, May 18, 1994).
- [[Bald Eagle]]: listed endangered; winters along the rivers and reservoirs.
- [[Southwest Willow Flycatcher]]: proposed endangered; not documented as breeding in the county.
- Other federally listed species in Table 18 include the Mojave desert tortoise, peregrine falcon, Mexican spotted owl, dwarf bear-claw poppy and Siler pincushion cactus.

Appendix A lists roughly 100 bird, 25 mammal, 30 reptile and 8 amphibian species that may use the floodplain. Over 40 candidate species occur in the county.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats.** Diversions and dams, livestock grazing, road construction, channel changes, non-native game and bait fish, invasive plants, recreation, and urban development have reduced habitat for native fish, mammals, birds and reptiles. The plan notes that the [[Red Shiner]] now dominates much of the lower mainstem.

**Hydrology.** The river is not free-flowing and has been dewatered below several diversions. Below the [[LaVerkin Springs]] (Pah Tempe) hot springs, the springs add about 12 cfs of highly mineralized, warm water. This makes the water unsuitable for culinary use and contributes to poor water quality above the Washington Fields Diversion.

**Biological study.** The flow analysis by [[Thomas B. Hardy]] (March 3, 1994) used the UDWR daily flow simulation model, PHABSIM hydraulic routines and multispectral videography. Its results indicate that long-term median and average habitat changes from the 50 cfs winter scenario are mostly positive (about +1 to +8 percent). The exceptions are the two largest size classes of [[Roundtail Chub]] and the largest [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] size class, which show winter reductions of about -8 to -12 percent.

**Demand pressure.** Projected municipal and industrial demand rises from 19,040 acre-feet per year (AF/yr) in 1990 to between 79,964 and 216,781 AF/yr by 2040, depending on growth scenario. Agricultural irrigation is projected to decline as land converts to other uses (87,740 AF/yr falling to between 10,520 and 63,750 AF/yr by 2040).

## Key Findings

**Planned projects and cost estimates** (Chapter 2; the cost figures are as given in the draft):
- **LaVerkin Springs (Pah Tempe):** collect spring flow at source, pipe about 12 miles below the Washington Fields Diversion, cool it, and return it to the river. The alternative of drying the springs with well points was rejected because it would reduce their cultural and historical value.
- **Winter flow reduction (WFD):** reduce flow from 86 to 50 cfs from November 1 to April 30. Provide minimum flows of 2 cfs below the Quail Creek Diversion and 5 cfs below the WFD, year-round.
- **[[Sand Hollow Reservoir]]:** capacity 30,000 acre-feet, estimated yield 9,200 AF/yr, estimated groundwater recharge 4,500–11,000 AF/yr, cost approximately $1,782 per acre-foot of yield.
- **[[Gunlock-Ivins Pipeline]]:** 24-inch pipeline to stop seepage losses, estimated to save up to 3,000 AF (the plan also cites a yield of 4,800 AF and a cost of $598 per AF). Roughly 3 cfs would be restored to the Santa Clara River below Gunlock Reservoir.
- **[[Ash Creek Project]]:** pipe water from Ash Creek, Leap Creek, South Ash Creek and Wet Sandy to Sandstone Mountain Reservoir (3,000 AF active storage). Annual yield is 6,600 AF at an estimated $3,931 per AF, with hydropower potential from an 814-foot drop.
- **Fish barriers:** construct barriers at Fort Pierce Wash and Seegmiller Drain to isolate side drainages during red shiner treatment.

**Plan mechanics and governance:**
- **Land acquisition:** about 80 percent of the floodplain is private. Acquisition is on a willing-seller basis at fair market value, supplemented by easements. Paiute Indian land will not be acquired. Public floodplain land is to be designated an [[Area of Critical Environmental Concern]].
- **Administration:** the WCWCD administers the plan, the planning period is 50 years, and review is every five years. A coordinating committee, a technical advisory committee and a private monitoring consultant are included.
- **Implementation schedule:** Table 19 runs from 1995 (2 cfs restoration below Quail Lake, first priority) to 2006 (Sandstone Mountain Reservoir).
- **Funding:** Table 20 gives total needs and sources. Several line items are in the millions of dollars; the table's OCR is unreliable, so exact figures should be checked against the source.
- **Permitted floodplain uses:** grazing under management plans, existing-road vehicle use, and agriculture that does not harm riparian values. Home or industrial construction and off-road vehicle use are prohibited.

**Conclusions on take:** the draft argues that the winter flow reduction is the only element likely to require a Section 10 incidental take permit, and that habitat gains are generally positive. It also states that whether a permit is required is "questionable," and that the full plan must still be evaluated for take. The plan describes itself as preserving existing ecological values rather than recreating historic conditions.

**Status:** this is a draft dated January 10, 1995. Chapter 6 (Economic Impacts) is marked "being written."
