## Executive Summary

This 1999 correspondence from a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Colorado River Fish Project biologist to Dr. W.L. Minckley addresses floodplain hydrology and its role in [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment in the Green River subbasin. The letter discusses channel geomorphology changes following [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] regulation, debates over floodplain management strategy (temporary inundation vs. permanent depression habitats), the role of nonnative fishes in off-channel habitats, and the unique recruitment challenges facing razorback sucker populations at the northern extent of their range. The discussion synthesizes findings relevant to the Recovery Implementation Program (RIP) for endangered Colorado River fishes.

## Background & Description

No direct morphological measurements are provided in this correspondence; however, growth-related data are discussed:

- **Growth rate disparity**: [[Razorback Sucker]] in the Green River (northern range limit) require **two to three years** in nursery/floodplain habitats to reach a body size equivalent to that achieved after **one year** in lower Colorado River basin populations.
- This slower growth necessitates overwintering in off-channel depression habitats, extending the duration of vulnerability to predation during early life stages.
- Juvenile size thresholds are implicated as a key factor in recruitment success — fish must reach a "suitable size" (approximately two growing seasons) before they can successfully re-enter and persist in riverine (main channel) habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Floodplain Hydrology and Habitat Connectivity**
- The Biological Opinion governing [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations recommended discharges of **18,500 cfs** (measured at the Jensen, UT gage) to achieve floodplain inundation.
- A forthcoming data synthesis report (RIP project, led by Bob Muth) was expected to recommend substantially higher discharges — up to **30,000 cfs** in high-flow years — to more effectively inundate floodplain habitat.
- Channel surveys and hydraulic modeling (Jim O'Brien) indicate the Green River channel has **narrowed** but has **not substantially degraded** in depth since dam construction, except at one site near Ouray. Increased conveyance capacity (reduced channel roughness) means **more discharge is now required to achieve overbank flooding** than under pre-dam unregulated conditions — implying prior inundation estimates under unregulated scenarios were likely conservative.

**Floodplain Management Debate: Temporary Inundation vs. Depression Habitats**
- Two competing management philosophies are described:
  1. **Temporary floodplain inundation**: Avoids creating permanent nonnative fish reservoirs that could invade the main channel and prey upon/compete with juvenile [[Colorado Pikeminnow]].
  2. **Permanent depression habitats**: Supported by the author, based on the premise that razorback sucker require floodplain access for **at least one to two full growing seasons** — a duration of ~3 weeks of terrace inundation is deemed **inadequate** for recruitment.
- A limited **levee removal experiment** (3 years of data, UDWR/FWS) found:
  - Nonnative fish abundance **does increase** in off-channel impoundments.
  - However, this increase does **not appear to significantly affect** nonnative fish composition in the **main channel**.
  - Dominant nonnative species in off-channel depressions were lentic-adapted: **[[Green Sunfish]]**, **[[Black Bullhead]]**, and **[[Fathead Minnow]]**.
  - **Black bullhead** emerged as the dominant nonnative species in permanent off-channel depressions, with populations sometimes increasing greatly during the year but declining in several sites during winter months.
- Conclusion drawn: nonnative fish produced in floodplain depressions pose minimal threat to [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] in the main channel, but represent a significant predation/competition "gauntlet" for early life stages of [[Razorback Sucker]].

**Recruitment Bottleneck and Egress**
- In the lower Colorado River basin, **summer/fall spate flows** naturally reconnect off-channel impoundments to the main channel, allowing juvenile fish egress back to riverine habitat.
- The Green River subbasin **lacks this natural flushing/egress mechanism**, complicating recovery efforts.
- The Green River is characterized as ecologically suboptimal for recovery due to its position at the **northern range margin** of razorback sucker, resulting in slower growth and extended nursery-habitat dependency.
- Positive attributes of the Green River system: large areas of intact inundated floodplain persist, and **fewer nonnative fish species** are present compared to lower basin reaches.

## Key Findings

- Post-dam channel narrowing in the Green River has occurred without significant degradation (bed lowering), altering the discharge thresholds needed for floodplain inundation.
- Existing regulatory discharge targets (18,500 cfs) may be insufficient; higher flows (up to 30,000 cfs) may be needed to replicate historical floodplain inundation patterns.
- Floodplain depression habitats, despite harboring nonnative fish populations (notably [[Black Bullhead]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Fathead Minnow]]), do not appear to compromise main-channel fish community composition based on 3-year levee removal study data.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment in the Green River appears to require prolonged (1–2+ growing season) floodplain habitat access, contrasting with brief (~3 week) inundation events, which are considered inadequate.
- Slow growth rates at the species' northern range limit necessitate extended overwintering in nursery habitats, increasing cumulative predation exposure — a central recruitment bottleneck.
- The author reports observing and handling **young razorback sucker** during annual broodstock collection efforts, suggesting some level of natural recruitment is occurring, albeit possibly insufficient to sustain the population without management intervention.
- The primary recovery challenge identified is developing a mechanism ("vehicle") to return floodplain-reared juvenile razorback sucker to riverine habitat once they reach a viable recruitment size — a natural process absent in the Green River subbasin unlike the lower basin.
- Broader context: findings are intended to inform the Recovery Implementation Program (RIP) Biological Opinion and were slated for further discussion at a nonnative fish symposium in Las Vegas (July 1999).
