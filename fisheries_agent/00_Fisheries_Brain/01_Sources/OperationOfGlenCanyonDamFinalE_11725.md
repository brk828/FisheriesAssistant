# Glen Canyon Dam Operations Final Environmental Impact Statement Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation summary covers the Final Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) on the operation of [[Glen Canyon Dam]], which analyzes nine alternative operating regimes for the dam on the [[Colorado River]] through [[Glen Canyon]] and [[Grand Canyon]]. The EIS was commissioned in 1989 to minimize adverse impacts on downstream environmental, cultural, and Native American resources while still producing [[Hydropower]], as required by the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] of 1992. The preferred alternative is the [[Modified Low Fluctuating Flow Alternative]], which substantially reduces daily flow fluctuations and includes habitat maintenance flows. The document is a letter-plus-summary package from the Upper Colorado Region, and it does not itself report new field data beyond the figures cited from the studies it summarizes.

## Background & Description

Glen Canyon Dam was completed in 1963 as a multipurpose feature of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] (CRSP). Its purposes, set by the Colorado River Storage Project Act of 1956, include river flow regulation, water storage, reclamation of arid lands, flood control, and hydroelectric generation. The dam was built before the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] took effect, so no EIS was filed for its construction or early operation.

Daily peaking-power releases drove the concerns that prompted this reevaluation. Maximum releases have been capped at 31,500 cubic feet per second (cfs), although the uprated powerplant can pass 33,200 cfs. Lake Powell has an active storage of about 24.3 million acre-feet (maf). The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program (GCES, begun 1982) supplied much of the scientific basis for the assessment, and [[Interim Operations]] began in August and November 1991 to protect resources pending the final decision.

The affected area runs about 15 miles through Glen Canyon National Recreation Area and 278 miles through Grand Canyon National Park. Several tribes hold land and resource interests, including the Navajo Nation, Hopi Tribe, Havasupai Tribe, Hualapai Tribe, Southern Paiute Tribe, and Zuni Pueblo.

The public process was extensive. Scoping drew more than 17,000 comments, and the draft EIS (filed January 1994) drew over 33,000 written comments, with more than 2,300 distinct issues extracted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] remains the main driver of the canyon ecosystem. Before the dam, flows ranged from spring peaks above 100,000 cfs to winter lows of 1,000 to 3,000 cfs, and water temperatures ranged from about 80 °F to near freezing. Since closure, release temperature has averaged about 46 °F year-round, the river is clear except when tributaries add sediment, and sediment supply to the canyon has been sharply reduced. The [[Paria River]], [[Little Colorado River]], and Kanab Creek are now the main sources of sand.

The postdam aquatic food chain rests on the green alga [[Cladophora Glomerata]], which feeds diatoms, insect larvae, and the non-native amphipod *Gammarus lacustris*. This chain supports a [[Trout Fishery]] in the Glen Canyon reach. Native fish have declined: of eight native species present before the dam, three have been extirpated, two are listed as endangered (including [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]), and one is a candidate for listing ([[Flannelmouth Sucker]]).

Riparian vegetation now includes a "new high water zone" providing over 1,000 acres of wildlife habitat. Birds, bats, lizards, and [[Peregrine Falcon]] all depend on linked aquatic and terrestrial insect production. Sandbar and terrace erosion threatens [[Archeological Sites]] and traditional cultural properties; of 475 prehistoric and historic sites identified, 323 are eligible for the [[National Register of Historic Places]].

## Key Findings

**Alternatives.** Nine alternatives were evaluated: two unrestricted fluctuating options (No Action and Maximum Powerplant Capacity), four restricted fluctuating options (High, Moderate, Modified Low, and Interim Low), and three steady flow options (Existing Monthly Volume, Seasonally Adjusted, and Year-Round). The preferred alternative, Modified Low, was revised after draft comments and consultation with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

**Modified Low Fluctuating Flow (preferred).** Minimum flows are 8,000 cfs from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. and 5,000 cfs at night. Maximum fluctuating flow is 25,000 cfs. Daily fluctuation is limited to 5,000, 6,000, or 8,000 cfs depending on monthly volume. Ramp rates are 4,000 cfs/hour up and 1,500 cfs/hour down. Habitat maintenance flows of up to 33,200 cfs for 1 to 2 weeks are scheduled, typically in March.

**Flood control.** Unscheduled floodflows above 45,000 cfs would be reduced from about once in 40 years to once in 100 years. The preferred method raises the four spillway gates 4.5 feet, adding 0.75 maf of flood storage at a cost of about $3 million.

**Endangered fish.** The Fish and Wildlife Service issued a jeopardy opinion for humpback chub and razorback sucker, with a reasonable and prudent alternative that includes endangered fish research flows (8,000 to 20,000 cfs), possibly over up to 5 low-release years. Other findings include no jeopardy for bald eagle, peregrine falcon, and Kanab ambersnail.

**Selective withdrawal.** Multilevel intake structures could raise downstream temperature 5 to 18 °F, still 7 to 16 °F cooler than predam summers. Estimated cost is $60 million, and research is needed before a decision.

**Hydropower and economics.** Glen Canyon Powerplant has eight units with 1,356 MW capacity and about $55 million in revenue in a 8.23-maf year. Restricted and steady flows reduce peaking capacity. Small-system household bills would rise $5 to $58 per year under restricted flows and $80 to $106 under steady flows. National economic impacts range from a $1.5 million gain (Maximum Powerplant Capacity) to a $123.5 million cost (Seasonally Adjusted Steady Flow).

**Recreation.** Steady flows give the largest angler-safety gain and the most recreation benefit (Seasonally Adjusted: +$4.76 million per year in net economic value). Modified Low adds $3.74 million per year.

**Sediment.** Modified Low has a higher probability of net riverbed sand gain than No Action, Maximum Powerplant Capacity, and High Fluctuating options, but less than steady options.

**Air quality.** Restricted and steady flows slightly reduce regional emissions. Modeling projects SO₂ reductions of about 100,000 tons and NOₓ reductions of nearly 80,000 tons over 20 years.

**Non-use value.** A pilot study (June 1994) found a positive, significant public value for affected resources; the full-scale study was due in early 1995.

**Conclusions.** No alternative restores predam conditions. No immediate permits are needed, though future Clean Water Act permits may be required. No adverse impacts to Indian Trust Assets are expected under the preferred alternative. A record of decision can be issued 30 days after the final EIS appears in the Federal Register.
