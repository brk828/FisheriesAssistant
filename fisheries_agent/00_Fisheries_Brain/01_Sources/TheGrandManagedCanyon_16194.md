# The Grand Managed Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This National Geographic feature (July 1997), written by Michael E. Long with photographs by Peter Essick, describes how [[Glen Canyon Dam]], completed in 1963 on the [[Colorado River]], transformed the river below it and the surrounding [[Grand Canyon]] ecosystem. The article combines a dory trip through the canyon, led in part by boatman [[Martin Litton]], with a review of the scientific debate over how the river should be managed. Its central question is whether the canyon's competing interests (recreation, hydroelectric power, endangered species, cultural resources and ecological restoration) can all be satisfied. The article concludes that no final answers yet exist, and that the policy choice rests with the American people.

## Background & Description

- **Dam:** Glen Canyon Dam was authorized by Congress in 1956 as a water-storage facility to help fulfill the [[Colorado River Compact]] and to generate hydroelectric power. It is about 710 feet high and roughly 300 feet thick at the base, and holds back [[Lake Powell]].
- **Pre-dam river:** Muddy and seasonal, carrying millions of tons of sediment. Spring floods reached about 300,000 cfs (1884 flood) and scoured streamside vegetation while building large sandbars. Flows exceeded 120,000 cfs through rapids.
- **Post-dam river:** Flows are regulated for power demand. Daily fluctuations reached up to 13 feet and the water is clear and about 49°F year-round, drawn from the reservoir's depths.
- **Rapids:** The article cites 95 major rapids in the canyon. [[Crystal Rapids]] was formed by a 1966 debris flow and has killed five people, more than any other Grand Canyon rapid. A 25-foot wave there capsized the author's boat during the trip.
- **Visitation:** About 22,000 to 26,000 people run the river each year (26,000 in 1996, per the article). Rainbow Bridge draws more than 300,000 visitors annually by boat from Lake Powell.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Food chain:** Clear water fosters [[Cladophora]] algae, which attract diatoms and the introduced amphipod [[Gammarus]]. Rainbow trout feed on these amphipods, and bald eagles feed on the trout.
- **Native fish:** The cold water has eliminated three native fish species. The [[Humpback Chub]] survives but remains endangered. Rainbow trout now outnumber it, according to the illustrated feature.
- **Vegetation:** Exotic [[Tamarisk]] and native willows colonized beaches no longer scoured by floods. Tamarisk supports the highest insect biomass of any woody shrub in the area, according to ecologist Larry Stevens.
- **Birds:** Tamarisk and insect populations support migrant birds, including the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], an endangered species that nests in tamarisk. The dam-driven food web also supports the largest population of nesting [[Peregrine Falcon]]s in the lower 48 states.
- **Threats:** Predatory [[Striped Bass]] from Lake Mead may move upriver into warmer flows and prey on the chub and other native fish.
- **Sediment and beaches:** Most sediment is now trapped by the dam. Tributary debris flows are building new rapids and reshaping the river's hydraulics.

## Key Findings

- **Managed flows:** Secretary of the Interior [[Bruce Babbitt]] selected a preferred alternative that sets maximum flows at 25,000 cfs, with occasional floods to rebuild beaches and habitat. The decision launched adaptive management and long-term monitoring through the new [[Grand Canyon Science and Monitoring Center]].
- **Flood experiment:** A flood in March 1996 sustained 45,000 cfs for seven days. It created more than 50 new beaches, though Litton predicted they would erode within a year, and the article notes that the 1996 runoff from a record snowpack threatened them.
- **Legal framework:** The [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] of 1992 mandated that the dam be operated to "protect ... and improve" downstream resources. The [[Endangered Species Act]] gives the humpback chub significant influence over operations, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service can invoke a "hammer clause" to force steady flows.
- **Temperature management:** Managers are studying a multilevel intake structure costing up to $100 million to warm the water to aid chub spawning.
- **Competing positions:** Scientists and stakeholders divide into three camps: predam restorers (e.g., [[Jack Schmidt]]), postdam ecosystem supporters (e.g., [[Larry Stevens]]), and dambusters favoring draining Lake Powell (e.g., [[David Brower]], Sierra Club). Hydrologist [[Bob Webb]] argues that managing for the whole canyon matters more than managing only for the chub.
- **Draining Lake Powell:** The Sierra Club board voted to recommend draining the reservoir. Officials including Babbitt called this unrealistic, citing water storage for upper-basin states and risks from sediment contaminated with heavy metals, pesticides and radioactive material.
- **Long-term outlook:** Hydrologist Ted Melis warns that tributary sediment could overwhelm the river, and estimates place the eventual failure of the dam at up to 700 years.
