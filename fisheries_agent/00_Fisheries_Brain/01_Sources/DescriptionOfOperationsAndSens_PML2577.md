# Lower Colorado River Operations and Maintenance: Biological Assessment of Listed and Sensitive Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This document is a biological assessment (BA) prepared by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Reclamation) with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) for routine operation and maintenance (O&M) of the lower Colorado River over a five-year consultation period under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It evaluates terrestrial, marsh and aquatic species, plus designated critical habitat for the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]]. Its effect determinations range from "no effect" to "may affect, but not likely to adversely affect" and "may affect" for the big-river fishes. The appendices cover the Multi-Species Conservation Program (MSCP), the consultation schedule, river maintenance history, and major facilities.

## Background & Description

The assessment covers the mainstem from Lake Mead to the Southerly International Boundary (SIB) with Mexico. Species are grouped by habitat requirement: terrestrial, marsh, and aquatic. Table 16 summarizes effect determinations for roughly 45 species, including:

- **Endangered:** Peregrine Falcon, Southwestern Willow Flycatcher, Brown Pelican, Yuma Clapper Rail, Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, Bonytail, Desert Pupfish, Vaquita, Totoaba.
- **Threatened:** Bald Eagle, Desert Tortoise (Mojave population).
- **Proposed threatened:** Flat-tailed Horned Lizard.
- **Sensitive:** Ten bat species, Arizona Toad, Rosy Boa, Chuckwalla, several snails, insects, plants, and birds (for example, [[California Black Rail]], [[Least Bittern]], [[Crissal Thrasher]]).

The bald eagle was reclassified from endangered to threatened on August 11, 1995. The southwestern willow flycatcher was listed as endangered on February 27, 1995. The [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] is a planned 50-year partnership to address Section 7 and Section 10 needs for more than 100 listed or sensitive species.

Appendices reproduce the MSCP fact sheet and funding agreement, the Section 7 consultation schedule, a river maintenance history (Appendix C), and descriptions of major facilities (Appendix D), including [[Hoover Dam]], [[Davis Dam]], [[Parker Dam]], [[Imperial Dam]], and [[Laguna Dam]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian and marsh change.** Cottonwood-willow cover on the lower river fell from about 8,100 acres in 1976 to about 3,400 acres in 1994 (Table 12). Saltcedar (*Tamarix* sp.) became dominant after dam construction eliminated the natural flood regime. Cited causes of the flycatcher decline include river regulation, agricultural and urban development, cowbird parasitism, and saltcedar invasion.

**Marsh.** In 1986, more than 400 backwater marshes were cataloged between Davis Dam and Laguna Dam. Roughly 4,200 acres of cattail/bulrush marsh occur on the U.S. side, plus more than 10,600 acres in the Cienega de Santa Clara in Mexico.

**Water and sediment.** Mainstream dams trapped sediment, leading to channel armoring. Salinity and agricultural return flows are rising, and selenium and pesticides have been detected, though no data show levels harmful to native fish reproduction.

**Non-native predators.** Predation by non-native fishes (carp, catfish, largemouth bass, sunfish) is identified as the main limit on recruitment of razorback sucker and bonytail.

**Water use.** The apportionment described in the glossary allots 7.5 maf per year to the Lower Basin in a normal year (Arizona 2.8 maf, California 4.4 maf, Nevada 0.3 maf). Table 11 shows 1993 entitlements. The largest users include the Central Arizona Project (384,425 acre-feet), Imperial Valley (2,677,597 acre-feet), and the Southern Nevada Water Project (295,120 acre-feet).

## Key Findings

**Overall determinations**
- Routine O&M is expected to have no effect on most species, including the peregrine falcon, desert tortoise (Mojave), Colorado squawfish, Arizona toad, and desert pupfish.
- For the bald eagle, Reclamation concluded that river O&M "will not affect" the species over five years, and it continues funding the Arizona Nest Watch Program at $50,000 per year.
- For the southwestern willow flycatcher, operations "may affect" the species through unquantifiable disturbance and habitat modification. Known breeding now includes three active nests at Lake Mead delta willows in 1996, possible breeding in saltcedar, and an estimated 230–500 nesting pairs throughout the range in 1993.

**Big-river fishes**
- The razorback sucker and bonytail are assessed as "may affect." Reservoir operation is not likely to adversely affect razorback suckers. Riverine operation may affect them through temporary dewatering of spawning bars and entrainment at pumping plants.
- Lake Mohave held an estimated 60,000 razorback suckers in 1987, falling to fewer than 25,000 adults by 1994. Reclamation's goal is 50,000 new adults in Lake Mohave and 25,000 in Lake Havasu by 2000 (razorback) and 25,000 each by 2005 (bonytail).
- Critical habitat is "may adversely modify" for the bonytail and razorback sucker, but effects are judged short-term and immeasurable.

**Mexico and the delta**
- Reclamation concluded that totoaba and the vaquita need no Section 7 consultation because the U.S. has no discretion over treaty water sent to Mexico. Fishing and illegal gillnetting are identified as the main threats, with 95 vaquitas killed in gillnets from 1985 to 1990.
- Yuma Desalting Plant operation at one-third capacity could reduce the Cienega by about 2,300 acres of cattail-bulrush habitat unless replacement water is provided. Desert pupfish are judged unaffected.

**Consultation timeline (Appendix B)**
- Draft BA: March 31, 1996. Public comment: 30 days.
- Draft Biological Opinion: estimated November 15, 1996; public comment through January 15, 1997.
- Final Biological Opinion: estimated spring 1997.

**Program cost (Appendix A)**
- The MSCP is estimated at about $4.5 million over three years for planning and interim measures. For fiscal 1996, Reclamation reserved $300,000 and FWS $100,000, for a combined $800,000 including non-federal matching funds.
