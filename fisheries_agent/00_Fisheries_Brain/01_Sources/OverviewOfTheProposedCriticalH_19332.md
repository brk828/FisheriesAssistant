# Overview of the Proposed Critical Habitat Designation for the Four Colorado River Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service draft overview (November 4, 1993) explains the proposed critical habitat designation for four endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]]. The Service proposed 2,094 miles of combined critical habitat across Colorado, Utah, New Mexico, Arizona, Nevada, and California, published January 29, 1993 (58 FR 6578). The document covers six parts: a general overview, a biological overview, an economic analysis, the relationship to other Endangered Species Act (ESA) provisions, the exclusion process, and a summary of public comments.

The economic analysis projects a positive present-value output impact for the Basin as a whole ($167.20 million at 3% discount), while impacts are uneven among states, with Utah and Colorado bearing net losses.

## Background & Description

The four species are endemic to the Colorado River Basin, which drains about 242,000 square miles in the United States plus about 2,000 square miles in Mexico. The Upper Basin comprises Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming; the Lower Basin comprises Arizona, California, and Nevada. All four were historically dominated by cyprinid and catostomid fauna and are listed as endangered under the ESA of 1973. Declines are attributed to habitat loss from flow regulation, altered temperature and sediment regimes, and proliferation of nonnative fishes.

Critical habitat is defined by primary constituent elements: water (quality and quantity), physical habitat (spawning, nursery, feeding, and movement corridors, including 100-year floodplain areas), and the biological environment (food supply, predation, competition).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Razorback Sucker:** Largest extant riverine population is in the upper Green River Basin, about 1,000 fish. Lower Basin populations are virtually extirpated from riverine habitat, and recruitment is nearly absent. Spawning occurs over mixed substrates from silt to cobble at 50–70°F.
- **Colorado Squawfish:** Survives only in the Upper Basin. Spawning in the Yampa and Green canyons is associated with declining June–August flows and water temperatures of 72–77°F. Larvae drift about 6 days, averaging 100 miles, to nursery areas.
- **Humpback Chub:** Largest populations occur in the Little Colorado and Colorado rivers in Grand Canyon and in the Black Rocks/Westwater Canyon area. Spawning follows peak spring flows at water temperatures near 68°F.
- **Bonytail:** The rarest native Basin fish, represented mainly by old adults (40+ years) in Lake Mohave. Spawning was reported in June–July at about 64°F.

Dams and diversions have fragmented habitat, blocked genetic exchange, and suppressed the floods that once supplied nutrients to floodplain habitats. The document states that no unaltered habitat remains in the Basin for these species.

Study region and modeling: the economic analysis covers seven states over 1989–2020, using input-output models for each state, a regional model, and a computable general equilibrium (CGE) model, aggregated to 20 sectors from 528 IMPLAN sectors.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed designation:** 2,094 miles total, with heavy overlap among species. Razorback Sucker reaches cover 1,824 miles; Colorado Squawfish 1,148; Humpback Chub 379; Bonytail 344.
- **Regional economics:** Basin economic output is about $1.3 trillion annually. The Basin-wide present value (3%) of incremental output impact is +$167.20 million, with California at +$262.60 million and Utah at −$63.39 million. Employment impacts range from about +29 jobs in 1995 to about +878 jobs by 2020 for the Basin.
- **National efficiency (CGE):** Scenario A1 yields +$4.40 million in national output; B1 yields −$3.90 million. The document judges the annualized value of $3.77 million (Scenario A1) the most plausible.
- **Section 7 implications:** Federal agencies must avoid destruction or adverse modification of critical habitat in addition to jeopardy. Table 18 outlines four consultation outcomes, and only some produce incremental economic costs from designation.
- **Exclusion process:** Exclusion is allowed when costs outweigh benefits, but not where it would cause species extinction. Regional Directors of Regions 1, 2, and 6 would evaluate exclusions using economic thresholds and biological extinction criteria.
- **Public comment:** 686 responses were received, 79.1% from Arizona and 67.6% from the Safford area. About 61% were form letters, 97% of which opposed designation. The comment period was reopened on September 15, 1993 (58 FR 4835).
- **Principal comment themes:** Need for more review time and an EIS under NEPA; economic impacts on agriculture, hydropower, and recreation; and disputes over razorback sucker origin in the Gila Basin, where the document notes that some populations result from stocking.
