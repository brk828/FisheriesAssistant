## Background & Description

The desert pupfish is a small, euryhaline cyprinodontid of the American Southwest and northwestern Mexico. The article draws on decades of field and laboratory work by several authors, including [[Schoenherr]], [[Minckley]], [[Kodric-Brown]], [[Loiselle]], [[Cox]], [[Naiman]] and [[Kinne]]. Key life-history points summarized from the text:

- **Maturity:** Fish in constant warm water become mature and breed at 2–3 months of age. In variable environments they mature at about one year, and breeding there occurs in the second year of life (Constantz 1981; McGinnis 1984). Males mature at about 15 mm SL.
- **Size:** The maximum reported size for breeding males is 75 mm (McGinnis 1984).
- **Spawning season:** Breeding begins in April or May, and young of the year appear soon after.
- **Sex ratio:** Sex ratios appear to remain near 50:50, although only a small proportion of males attain full breeding coloration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reproductive behavior and mating systems.** Spawning is preferred at 28°C, although the species tolerates temperatures up to 44°C. Breeding males hold territories and chase intruders, including females early in the season. Courtship involves the female nipping and spitting substrate and the male swimming parallel to her and wrapping his anal fin around her vent. A female lays one to four eggs per spawning act. [[Crear And Haydock]] (1970) estimated that a female may lay 50 to 800 eggs in a season. Experiments by Cortois and Hino (1979) using wool yarn showed a preference for green substrate, which the authors interpret as a preference for algal mats.

[[Kodric-Brown]] (1977, 1978, 1981) related breeding system to habitat size and density:
- Territorial breeding is most successful in large habitats.
- A dominance hierarchy is more successful in small habitats.
- A consort-pair system is preferred in large habitats at low population density. Nonaggressive males accompany a gravid female, and this system reduces stress and maintains greater genetic heterogeneity.

[[Loiselle]] (1979, 1982) described several further behaviors:
- Males recognize their own spawn by odor, guard it, and eat the spawn of other males.
- "Sneak mating" by subdominant males, which resemble females, introduces variation into the breeding system.
- Males chase small females from territories and court large females, so more and larger eggs are fertilized.

**Development and growth.** Eggs hatch in about a week. Crear and Haydock (1970) reported 10 days at 20°C. Laboratory hatchlings measured 4–5 mm TL and doubled their size in less than eight weeks. [[Kinne]] (1960) showed that temperature and salinity interact in development: juveniles grow faster in freshwater than in seawater at 15° and 20°C, but faster in seawater at 25°, 30° and 35°C. Eggs from Salton Sea fish developed normally and gave hatch rates above 50% at 17–34°C and 35 g/L (ppt) salinity (Kinne and Kinne 1962).

**Feeding ecology.** The long gut suggests a diet largely of detritus and algae, but feeding is opportunistic. [[Minckley]] and Arnold (1969) described "pit digging," in which hollowed pits in sand become detrital traps that males defend along with their spawn.
- Shallow ponds: Walters and Legner (1980) found foraging mostly on the bottom, on chironomid midge larvae, detritus, aquatic vegetation and snails.
- Rice fields: pupfish eat littoral Cladocera, coleopteran larvae and mosquitoes. Legner and Medved (1974) and Legner et al. (1975) concluded that pupfish outperform [[Mosquitofish]] for mosquito control because they forage throughout the water column.
- Salton Sea: Naiman (1979) recorded seasonal diet shifts, with animal matter making up 30% to 56% of gut contents and a preference for chironomid larvae and diptera pupae.
- Cannibalism: Cox (1972a) reported that pupfish occasionally eat their own eggs and young.

**Temperature and metabolism.** High temperatures create nutritional problems (Deacon and Minckley 1974). Lowe and Heath (1969) found maximum food intake at 35°C but maximum conversion efficiency at 20°C. Kinne (1960) recorded a more than 24-fold increase in food intake as temperature rose from 15° to 30°C, and maximum growth with unlimited food at 30°C. Pupfish rest on the bottom, particularly at night, which reduces metabolic demand. Feldmeth (pers. comm.) reported that resting metabolic demand was reduced more than threefold compared with patrolling a territory. The species holds records for highest temperature and lowest oxygen tolerance among fishes studied.

**Threats from introduced species.**
- **Scale of decline:** Of the 65 North American desert fishes considered desert species (Deacon et al. 1979), more than two-thirds have declined while interacting with introduced species (Schoenherr 1981b). About 50% of introduced southwestern fishes are piscivores (Meffe 1982).
- **Predation:** [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) are considered the main cause of extinction of the Monkey Spring pupfish. Unauthorized stocking was discovered in 1968, and bass were reintroduced in 1969 and decimated the population. Predation by game fish also eliminated pupfish from the lower Colorado River after dams altered flows and backwaters disappeared.
- **Mosquitofish:** Mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) are implicated in the decline of more than 20 fish species worldwide, mainly through predation on juveniles. Schoenherr (1979) found that they coexist with pupfish in the Salton Sea and its drains. However, the Palm Canyon pupfish refuge in Anza-Borrego Desert State Park contained more mosquitofish than pupfish.
- **Competition and behavior:** Competition is hard to prove, and behavioral interference is usually the mechanism. At the Salton Sea, [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*), introduced about 1964, dominated by 1979. They made up 98% of fish trapped on the shoreline and 70% in drains, while pupfish were under 5%. Males court pupfish of both sexes and interfere with reproduction (Matsui, pers. comm.).
- **Tilapia:** [[Zill's Cichlid]] (*Tilapia zilli*), introduced in 1973 for weed control, flourished in the drains. Pupfish moved into water shallower than 10 cm, where juvenile tilapia invaded and passively interfered with feeding and breeding. A 1983 survey of 40 drains by the California Department of Fish and Game trapped only 6 pupfish.
- **Habitat change:** [[Tamarisk]] is abundant in Salt Creek and San Sebastian Marsh and may complicate efforts to maintain these as critical habitat. Any eradication project must exclude 2,4-D and other herbicides from the water.

## Key Findings

- Desert pupfish spawning is territorial, with breeding systems that shift with habitat size and population density ([[Kodric-Brown]] 1977–1981).
- Sneak mating and egg cannibalism by males add variation to reproduction and increase egg survival through selective courtship of large females ([[Loiselle]] 1979, 1982).
- Growth depends on the interaction of temperature and salinity, with faster growth in freshwater at lower temperatures and in seawater at higher temperatures ([[Kinne]] 1960).
- Diet is opportunistic and seasonal, mainly detritus, algae, chironomid larvae and snails, and pupfish may outperform mosquitofish as mosquito control agents ([[Walters And Legner]] 1980).
- More than two-thirds of North American desert fishes have declined where they interact with introduced species, and predation, behavioral interference and habitat change are the main mechanisms.
- The Monkey Spring population was lost to [[Largemouth Bass]] predation and is presumed extinct.
- At the Salton Sea, [[Sailfin Molly]] and [[Zill's Cichlid]] have displaced pupfish from most habitats, leaving them in shallow water that juvenile tilapia now also invade.
- Managing [[Tamarisk]] control at San Sebastian Marsh requires strict herbicide exclusion to protect the species.
