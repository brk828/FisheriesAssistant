# Gila Trout and Goliath Frog: Federal Listing Proposal and Withdrawal Notice

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #herpetology

## Executive Summary

This scanned document is a fragmentary compilation of three items. It contains (1) a reference list for [[Goliath Frog]] biology and herpetological collecting in Cameroon, (2) a [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] proposed rule to add the Goliath frog (*[[Conraua Goliath]]*) to the Endangered and Threatened Wildlife list under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and (3) a Federal Register withdrawal notice cancelling a proposal to downlist the [[Gila Trout]] (*[[Oncorhynchus Gilae]]*) from endangered to threatened. The Gila trout decision is the most complete part of the text. The Service withdrew the reclassification because recent data showed the species no longer met the recovery criteria. Several scan errors (for example "twe" for "twelve", and garbled dates) were silently corrected where the context was clear.

## Background & Description

**Goliath frog (*Conraua goliath*).** The reference list cites several works on the species and its region:

- Sabater-Pi (1985), a contribution to the biology of the giant frog, published in *Amphibia-Reptilia*.
- Zahl (1967), a popular article in *National Geographic* on the world's largest frog.
- Perret and Mertens (1957), a herpetological collection made in Cameroon from 1952 to 1955.
- Rabb (declining amphibian populations, published in *Species*).

The proposed rule lists the Goliath frog under "AMPHIBIANS" in the endangered and threatened wildlife table. The table fields (historic range, vertebrate population, when listed, critical habitat) are mostly illegible in the scan.

**Gila trout (*Oncorhynchus gilae*).** This is a native salmonid of relatively undisturbed high-altitude mountain streams. Historically it occurred in the Verde and Agua Fria drainages in Arizona and in the upper Gila drainage in New Mexico. The species was listed as endangered, but the listing date is illegible in the scan. By then its range had shrunk to five small headwater creeks: Iron, McKenna, and Spruce creeks in the [[Gila Wilderness]], and Main Diamond and South Diamond creeks in the [[Aldo Leopold Wilderness]]. The main causes of decline were hybridization with, competition from, and predation by non-native [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Cutthroat Trout]], plus competition and predation by [[Brown Trout]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Recovery actions were built around replicating each natural population within its historic range. The steps were:

- Chemically treating streams to remove non-native competing and predatory fish.
- Constructing barriers to prevent reinvasion by non-native species.
- Restocking the streams with Gila trout.

These actions established seven additional populations, so the five natural populations were replicated. Stream renovation and transplant work was carried out jointly by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[U.S. Forest Service]], the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]], and [[New Mexico State University]].

When the earlier proposed rule was published (October 6, 1987; 52 FR 37424), the document reports twelve secure populations: five indigenous and seven reintroduced. The five indigenous populations were secure and occupied their habitat to maximum carrying capacity. The reintroduced populations were reproducing successfully and were expected to reach carrying capacity in the near future.

The withdrawal notice also reports that forest fires, drought, floods, and invasion by brown trout severely reduced three Gila trout populations and eliminated one entirely.

## Key Findings

1. **Goliath frog proposed listing.** The Service proposed adding *Conraua goliath* to the endangered and threatened wildlife list. The rule was dated August 16 (year read as 1991 after OCR correction) and was prepared by Ronald M. Nowak of the Office of Scientific Authority.
2. **Gila trout reclassification withdrawn.** The Service withdrew its proposal to reclassify the Gila trout from endangered to threatened. Recent data indicated the species no longer met the criteria set out in the [[Gila Trout Recovery Plan]] (USFWS 1984).
3. **Recovery criteria were met in part.** Replication of the five wild populations into seven additional populations had satisfied the criteria for threatened status at the time of the 1987 proposal. The recovery plan said the species "could be considered for downlisting" once those criteria were met.
4. **Later declines.** Forest fires, drought, floods, and brown trout invasion severely reduced three populations and eliminated one, reversing the earlier gains.
5. **Effective date.** The withdrawal took effect on October 15 (year read as 1991 after OCR correction).
6. **Documentation gaps.** The Goliath frog table, the Gila trout listing date, and the rule's population figures are partly illegible, so the note relies only on legible text.
