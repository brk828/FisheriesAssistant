# Distribution and Abundance of Benthic Invertebrates in a Sonoran Desert Stream

#aquatic-ecology #benthic-invertebrates #desert-stream #community-dynamics #Ephemeroptera #Diptera #Trichoptera

## Executive Summary

This Master's thesis investigated the seasonal and longitudinal distribution and abundance of dominant benthic invertebrates in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a [[Sonoran Desert]] stream in Arizona, from April 1975 to April 1976. Eight sampling stations were established representing habitats above, within, and below a canyon area. The study documented 63 invertebrate taxa, with [[Chironomidae]] (28% of annual fauna) and [[Baetis]] spp. (26%) being most abundant numerically. [[Ephemeroptera]] as a group dominated at 46% by number and 48% by weight. Occurrence and abundance of filamentous algae (primarily [[Cladophora]]) were most important to invertebrate standing crops and densities during modal flow periods. Flash flooding was identified as a principal feature in the seasonal cycle, severely impacting invertebrate populations but followed by rapid recovery. The study provides baseline data on a rare, relatively undisturbed desert stream ecosystem supporting seven native fish species.

## Background & Description

[[Aravaipa Creek]] arises northwest of Klondyke, Arizona at approximately 1,310 m elevation in Graham County, flows through Pinal County, and after 35-45 km of surface flow typically returns underground before reaching the [[San Pedro River]] at 660 m elevation. The creek passes through a steep-walled canyon cut in Tertiary andesites and Pre-Cambrian rocks, with canyon walls often exceeding 250 m in height. The stream is one of the rare low desert streams to have escaped channelization, impoundment, and introduction of exotic species, serving as a refuge for seven native fishes.

The riparian community consists of broadleaf deciduous woodland including [[Populus fremontii]] (cottonwood), [[Salix]] spp. (willow), [[Platanus wrightii]] (sycamore), [[Fraxinus velutina]] (ash), and other species. Mean annual precipitation at Klondyke (1952-1972) was 34.7 cm, with highest rainfall in July and August and lowest in April and May. Air temperatures range from less than -5°C to more than 38°C.

Eight sampling stations were selected: Station I at the source with sandy substrate in shallow pools, and Stations II-VIII on comparable riffles with gravel, pebble, and cobble substrates. Stations varied in exposure to sunlight, with Stations IV, V, and VI deeply shaded by vegetation and canyon walls, while Stations VII and VIII received more incident light. Stations III-VI were within the [[Aravaipa Canyon Primitive Area]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and Chemical Factors:**
Water temperatures ranged from 8°C to 28°C at most stations. Discharge patterns were highly variable, with mean discharge in 1975 of 0.33 m³/sec, considerably lower than previous years. Flash floods were frequent in July-September 1975, with one observed flood increasing discharge from 1.16 m³/sec to 8.5 m³/sec within 15 minutes. pH ranged from 7.0-7.1 at Station I to 8.0-9.0 at downstream stations. Dissolved oxygen was generally near or above 100% saturation, reaching 150% at Station VIII during peak photosynthesis. Nitrate-nitrogen decreased progressively downstream from 0.17 mg/l at Station I to 0.02 mg/l at Station VIII. Ortho-phosphates similarly decreased from 0.26 mg/l to 0.08 mg/l.

**Biological Context:**
The fish community comprises seven native species: [[Pantosteus clarki]] (Gila mountain-sucker), [[Catostomus insignis]] (Gila sucker), [[Gila robusta grahami]] (Colorado River chub), [[Tiaroga cobitis]] (loach minnow), [[Rhinichthys osculus]] (speckled dace), [[Meda fulgida]] (spikedace), and [[Agosia chrysogaster]] (longfin dace). All except P. clarki feed on benthic invertebrates. One non-native species, [[Chaenobryttus cyanellus]] (green sunfish), exists in limited numbers in isolated pools.

[[Rorippa nasturtium aquaticum]] (watercress) was the dominant emergent macrophyte, seasonally abundant in dense beds along stream margins from Stations I-III. Benthic algae followed seasonal trends, with encrusting forms (principally [[Bacillariophyceae]]) common throughout, and filamentous algae ([[Cladophora]] sp.) most abundant at Stations VII and VIII in areas of high insolation. Dense algal mats were achieved in late January-early February, diminishing through March and generally absent by April.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic Composition:**
Sixty-three taxa of invertebrates were collected, with 49 taxa from quantitative riffle sampling. [[Chironomidae]] was most abundant numerically (28% of annual fauna in riffles, 17% at Station I), followed closely by [[Baetis]] spp. (26% in riffles). As groups, [[Ephemeroptera]] comprised 46% by number and 48% by weight, while [[Diptera]] accounted for 35% by number and 15% by weight in riffles. Other important groups in decreasing abundance: [[Oligochaeta]], [[Trichoptera]], [[Acarina]], [[Plecoptera]], [[Ostracoda]], and [[Hydrozoa]].

Eight genera of mayflies were present. [[Baetis]] spp. (possibly eight species) was most ubiquitous, with highest mean density of 7,470 individuals/m² at Station VII. [[Choroterpes inornata]] was strongly rheophilic, accounting for 9% of annual fauna on riffles, most abundant at Station II (1,481 individuals/m²). [[Tricorythodes]] spp. (three species: T. minutus, T. dimorphus, T. econdylus) preferred sandy areas, with greatest abundance at Station I (8,745 individuals/m²). [[Capnia frisoni]] was the only stonefly collected, seasonal from October to April, with highest densities at Stations VI and VII (300 and 543 individuals/m²).

**Longitudinal Patterns:**
Station I (sandy substrate at source) supported significantly higher densities and biomass than downstream riffles: annual mean density of 63,189 individuals/m² and biomass of 29.9 gm/m² versus highest downstream values of 20,448 individuals/m² and 10.3 gm/m². Among riffle stations (II-VIII), standing crops and numbers were lowest at Stations IV, V, and VI within the canyon (shaded areas) and highest at Stations II, III, VII, and VIII in more open areas. Differences between most exposed stations (VII, VIII) and most sheltered (IV, V, VI) were statistically significant at 95% confidence levels for both biomass and numbers.

**Seasonal Patterns:**
Summer floods (July-September) severely reduced populations of [[Baetis]] spp., [[Choroterpes inornata]], [[Tricorythodes]] spp., [[Ochrotrichia]] spp., and [[Chironomidae]]. Recovery was fairly rapid for Baetis, C. inornata, and chironomids during October-November, achieving peak densities by December. [[Simuliidae]] uniquely increased in numbers shortly after flash flooding. Overall invertebrate numbers increased disproportionate to biomass from October to December (hatching and recruitment of early instars), then numbers decreased December to March while biomass increased (maturation to larger instars). By April, considerable emergence had occurred, indicated by sharp decrease in biomass.

**Ecological Relationships:**
Filamentous algae were of singular importance to benthic invertebrates during modal flow periods. Distribution of invertebrates was relatively uniform on riffle habitats, but densities and standing crops were directly associated with occurrence and abundance of filamentous algae, which correlated with incident solar radiation. This pattern was apparent on both longitudinal and seasonal bases. Life history data suggest dependence by invertebrates upon algae for food, since large numbers of early instars were present when algal standing crops were greatest, allowing growth when food resources were most abundant and avoiding summer flood hazards.

**Detrital Inputs:**
Major inputs of allochthonous organic matter occurred with discharge phenomena. Flash floods deposited large quantities of fine detritus within inorganic substrates after subsidence. Organic materials from high in the watershed (Santa Teresa, Galiuro, and Pinaleno mountains) were processed by terrestrial arthropods, bacteria, fungi, and molar action during runoff in periodically dry sediments above the source. At Station I, steady quantities of suspended organic matter were elutriated from sand and released to the stream. This unique pathway accounted for abundance of detritus and detritivorous invertebrates at the source. Autumn leaf fall contributed additional organic material, though less than in temperate streams.

**Flood Impacts and Recovery:**
Flash flooding was the principal physical factor limiting abundance of benthic invertebrates. The observed flood on 25 July 1975 increased discharge from 1.16 m³/sec to 8.5 m³/sec within 15 minutes, disrupting stream bed and removing all algae and aquatic vegetation. Lower portions of the stream experienced more frequent flooding than upper reaches. Despite frequency and severity of flooding, the invertebrate fauna demonstrated resilient adaptability, maintaining diversities, densities (far exceeding most other streams), and standing crops comparable to systems with less frequent spates. At least 60 insect species were found on riffle habitats alone, an alpha-diversity comparable to streams from wide geographic ranges.

**Comparative Analysis:**
When compared with other streams, biomass in [[Aravaipa Creek]] was in lower ranges while numbers per unit area far exceeded most systems. This discrepancy partly resulted from finer mesh sampling (30 threads/cm versus typical 9-16 threads/cm), but also indicated relatively small-sized invertebrates. Flooding may be a strong selective factor for smaller-sized invertebrates in desert streams, as smaller individuals survive spates more successfully than larger specimens. Annual mean density at Station I was 63,189 individuals/m², while riffle stations ranged from 9,000 to 20,448 individuals/m². Biomass ranged from 5.9 to 10.3 gm/m² at riffle stations.
