# Stock Assessment and Fisheries Monitoring of Humpback Chub in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, 2001

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report, prepared by the [[US Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Arizona Fishery Resources Office, Flagstaff) for the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring And Research Center]], describes four fish monitoring trips to the lower 14.2 km of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) in 2001. The primary goal was stock assessment of the federally endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*, HBC). A secondary goal was to characterize long-term trends in the LCR fish community.

Two [[Mark-Recapture Analysis]] experiments (spring and fall) estimated HBC abundance. The spring estimate was 3,527 HBC > 100 mm total length (SE = 284). The fall estimate was 2,528 HBC > 100 mm (SE = 162), per the Discussion and Table 11; the Executive Summary gives 2,484 (SE = 150), a discrepancy the report does not resolve. The authors conclude that the Grand Canyon HBC population has declined significantly since the early 1990s.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] is a federally listed endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Under the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] (1992), the [[Adaptive Management Work Group]] (AMWG) of the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] Adaptive Management Program defines objectives for downstream resources. GCMRC shifted fisheries work in 2000 toward stock assessment methods because earlier research had focused on life history rather than population status and trends.

Earlier stock assessments in the LCR (Coggins and Van Haverbeke 2001) tested a two-pass mark-recapture design, which GCMRC then contracted USFWS to repeat in 2001. The study area was divided into three reaches (Salt, Coyote, Boulders) and nine sub-reaches, with base camps at 10.7, 5.5 and 2.0 rkm above the confluence.

**Manuscript review.** The attached reviewer form rates the report as technically sound and its conclusions consistent with the data. It recommends publication with major revisions. Reviewer concerns: the text is verbose and needs copy editing; the apparent decline is missing from the Executive Summary; the report offers no management or conservation recommendations; and the authors are one of several agencies responsible for HBC conservation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling gear and methods.** The main gear was [[Hoopnet Sampling]]: 60 nets (45 in the May trip), 0.5–0.6 m diameter, 6 mm mesh, baited with about 80–100 g of commercial fish food, fished for four days and checked daily. Nets were placed about 80–150 m apart in habitat suspected of supporting HBC. [[Trammel Net Sampling]] was used at the confluence during spring trips, and [[Fykenet Sampling]] was tested once in fall 2001. Large non-native fish were sacrificed for stomach analysis. Native fish were scanned for, or injected with, [[Pit Tagging]] (PIT) tags.

**Water quality.** Measured parameters included temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, conductivity and turbidity. Spring flows included two small spates in early May that raised turbidity to about 7,000 NTU. The LCR ran clear and blue in June, with turbidity of 8–14 NTU (mean 11.3 NTU). Water temperature ranged from 16.7 to 22.3 °C (mean 21.3 °C) in June. Fall spates on 9–10 October raised turbidity to 4,000 NTU, and November flows were at base level (22.2–29.9 NTU).

**Fish community.** Catches were dominated by native HBC and [[Speckled Dace]] alongside non-native [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]]. Native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] were also monitored. Non-native ictalurids, carp, green sunfish and rainbow trout were also present. Exotic species made up 18% and 25% of hoopnet catches in May and June, and 10% and 16% in October and November.

**Parasites and predation.** The external copepod [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] was found on HBC at low rates. The [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) was seen protruding from two HBC in November; the authors note that external inspection cannot detect internal infections. Non-native bullheads and catfish preyed on HBC and other small fish.

## Key Findings

**Trip effort and catch**
- Spring (May and June): 1,256 hoopnet sets, 28,882 net-hours, 5,311 fish caught, 2,253 of them HBC.
- Fall (October and November): 1,393 hoopnet sets, 32,449 net-hours, 8,235 fish caught, 5,246 of them HBC.
- HBC CPUE: 0.044 fish/net-hr in May and 0.102 in June (spring); 0.181 in October and 0.142 in November (fall). The authors attribute the June rise to low turbidity and more effort.

**Spring 2001 abundance**
- Spring estimate: 3,527 HBC > 100 mm (SE = 284). This is a Petersen-based sum, and the report prefers it because it did not differ significantly from the Danoch-based sum (3,866).
- Spring HBC > 150 mm: 2,090 (SE = 244).
- The authors caution that the estimate is biased. Large adults emigrated between the May marking and June recapture trips, violating the closure assumption. Mark rates differed significantly among reaches (χ² = 24.10, df = 2, p = 5.82E-06), and the estimate is germane to the May marking event.
- Length-stratified analysis placed the optimal break at 240 mm.

**Fall 2001 abundance**
- Fall estimate: 2,528 HBC > 100 mm (SE = 162); the Executive Summary lists 2,484 (SE = 150).
- Fall HBC > 150 mm: 1,106 (SE = 172), not significantly different from fall 2000 (1,590).
- Mark rates did not differ among reaches (χ² = 3.09, df = 2, p = 0.214), so pooling was justified.
- About 82% of the fall population was in the Salt and Coyote reaches, compared with 81% in Salt and Boulders in fall 2000.
- About 81% of the fall population was under 200 mm; only 3% was above 290 mm.

**Size structure and recruitment**
- Spring HBC were mostly 75–225 mm. Hoopnet catches of HBC > 300 mm fell from 165 in May to 40 in June.
- Young-of-year (YOY) HBC (< 50 mm) were almost absent in spring (zero in May, eleven in June). Strong YOY cohorts appeared in October and November, with most HBC in the 50–100 mm class.
- Flannelmouth and bluehead sucker YOY were scarce in fall, suggesting weak 2001 recruitment for these species.

**Sexual condition and spawning**
- Ripe female HBC in May had mean TL = 296 mm (SE = 44.5), range 140–386 mm. Ripe male HBC had mean TL = 268 mm (SE = 9.3) across both spring trips.
- Spawning concentrations of bluehead sucker were found in October (one net at 12.4 rkm held three ripe females and 23 ripe males). The authors infer late-season spawning in catostomids.

**Predation and parasites**
- Spring: three direct predation events on HBC by non-native fish; fall: four events.
- Lernaea infection on HBC was 4.3% in May (25 fish) and 6.4% in June (106 fish), averaging 1.5 and 1.3 parasites per infected fish. In fall it was 4.4% in October (129 fish) and 3.9% in November (90 fish).

**Population trend**
- Comparing spring 2001 (2,090 HBC > 150 mm) with Douglas and Marsh (1996) April–May 1992 average (4,959) suggests a sharp decline.
- Fall 2001 (1,106) and fall 2000 (1,590) averaged 1,348. The 1991–92 fall average was 1,636. The authors note the reach extent differed (0–14.9 km vs. 0–14.2 km).
- Mainstem HBC > 200 mm fell from 3,482 (SE = 408; Valdez and Ryel 1995, 1990–93) to 1,044 (SE = 300; SWCA 2002, 2001). The authors interpret this as a recruitment or survivorship decline from the LCR.
- The report concludes that the decline is real and significant, and that it is concentrated in the mainstem portion of the population.

**Methodological recommendations (2002)**
- Conduct the spring 2002 trip earlier, because spawning peaks in mid-April (Gorman and Stone 1999), but not so early that immigration and emigration bias results.
- PIT tag only fish > 150 mm; use fin clips for 100–150 mm fish, because recapture rates for small fish were about half those for 150–200 mm fish, suggesting tag loss or mortality.
- Apply a secondary mark to newly tagged fish to test tag loss.
- Do not adopt fykenets as an alternative gear.

**Limitations noted by the authors**
- Spring estimate affected by emigration and reach-level bias.
- Only one spring point estimate since 1992, so the trend rests on limited data.
- The estimated minimum viable population was not met; ripe-fish lengths suggest the effective population may be below the proposed goal (Valdez et al. 2001).
