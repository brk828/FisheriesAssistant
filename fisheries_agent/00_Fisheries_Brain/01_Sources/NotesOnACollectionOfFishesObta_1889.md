# Notes on a Collection of Fishes from the Gila River, Fort Thomas, Arizona (1889)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #Xyrauchen #GilaRiver

## Executive Summary

This 1889 paper by Philip H. Kirsch documents a small but taxonomically significant collection of seven fish species obtained by Lieut. W. L. Carpenter from the [[Gila River]] at Fort Thomas, Arizona Territory, and sent to the University of Indiana museum in 1887. The collection is historically notable for the description of a new species, **[[Catostomus Gila]]**, and — most importantly — the erection of a new monotypic genus, **[[Xyrauchen]]** (Eigenmann & Kirsch), to accommodate the morphologically bizarre hump-backed sucker previously known as *Catostomus cypho* Lockington. This genus remains recognized today as containing the endangered **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*, syn. *cypho*). The paper also provides early natural history notes on diet, coloration, seasonal variation, and life history for the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*, described here as *Gila emorii*) and the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychochilus lucius*), based on field observations by Carpenter.

## Background & Description

The collection was made by Lieutenant W. L. Carpenter of the U.S. Army at **Fort Thomas**, on the **[[Gila River]]**, Arizona Territory, and forwarded to the museum of Indiana University on June 4, 1887. Carpenter, in addition to specimens, supplied field notes on habits and local vernacular names. He opined that only seven species inhabited the Gila River at this locality, and that some previously described species from the stream were likely growth-stage variants of *[[Gila Emorii]]* and *[[Ptychochilus Lucius]]*, rather than distinct taxa — an early observation of ontogenetic morphological variation later validated in Southwestern cyprinids.

### Species Accounts

**1. [[Catostomus Latipinnis]]** (Flannelmouth Sucker)
- One specimen, 19 inches long.
- Matches Jordan & Gilbert's description except for a longer preorbital bone (>2x as long as deep) and a dorsal fin not deeply incised, with height less than head length.

**2. [[Catostomus Gila]]** Kirsch, sp. nov. (new species)
- Three specimens, 12–14 inches long.
- Closely allied to *C. ardens* but distinguished by: more compressed body, less elevated antedorsal region, shorter/more compressed caudal peduncle, shorter and higher dorsal fin, and longer, more pointed ventral fins.
- Meristics: Head 4⅓ in body; depth 4¼; Dorsal rays 11–12; Anal rays 7; Scales 11—58 to 60—10.
- Coloration (alcoholic): dark brown dorsally and laterally to pectoral axils with dark-based, punctulate scales; yellow venter; dark fin membranes.
- Holotype material deposited with the [[U.S. National Museum]].

**3. [[Catostomus Insignis]]** (Sonora Sucker)
- One specimen, 12¾ inches.
- Lateral scales of moderate size, larger anteriorly than posteriorly (though visually obscured); dorsal scales very large, belly scales roughly half that size.

**4. [[Catostomus Clarki]]** (Desert Sucker)
- One specimen, 12 inches.
- Back scales slightly smaller than *C. insignis* but otherwise similar; 68 scales on lateral line.

**5. [[Xyrauchen Cypho]]** (Lockington) — "Buffalo Fish" [new genus: *Xyrauchen* Eigenmann & Kirsch]
- Recognized as the type species of a new genus, distinguished from *Catostomus* by a large, sharp-edged nuchal hump formed by highly modified anterior interneural bones.
- Detailed osteological description of the hump's support structure, including a central pillar with anterior/posterior wings articulating with the supraoccipital, and a series of specialized neural arch processes on the first 8–9 vertebrae.
- External morphology: stout, elongated body compressed posteriorly; small conical head; inferior mouth; falcate ventral fins; broad, strongly forked caudal fin; scales cycloid and variable in size, largest on caudal peduncle; dorsal hump ridge scaleless.
- Coloration (December): dark brown with brassy reflection, yellow venter; reddish-tinted brown iris.
- Anatomy: pharyngeal teeth small, numerous (>35), single row; peritoneum black; intestine simple, 5.5x body length.
- Meristics: Head 4 in body; depth 4; Dorsal rays 13; Anal rays 7; Scales 17-77-12.
- Taxonomic note: This genus corresponds to the modern **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a species now federally endangered.

**6. [[Ptychochilus Lucius]]** Girard — "Gila Salmon" (Colorado Pikeminnow)
- One specimen, 15 inches.
- Agrees generally with Jordan & Gilbert's synopsis description.
- Carpenter's notes: peritoneum silvery; vertebrae 45–46.

**7. [[Gila Emorii]]** Baird & Girard — "Gila Trout; Bony Tail" (Bonytail Chub, *Gila elegans*)
- Six specimens, 8–14 inches.
- Differences from Jordan & Gilbert's description: maxillary usually not reaching front of orbit; pectorals not quite reaching pubic bone front; dorsal fin origin roughly equidistant between snout tip and caudal base; caudal peduncle least depth = ¼ its length.
- Coloration (October): dark back, light sides, bright silvery belly; darker dorsal fin, brown caudal fin; pink-tinted iris; dusky peritoneum.
- Dentition: pharyngeal bone falciform with several foramina; teeth falcate, compressed, with grinding surfaces (formula 2,5-4,3 or 2,2,5-4,2); grinding surfaces heavily worn in most specimens.
- Vertebrae: 42–45. Scales: 26-87 to 90-21.
- Ontogenetic note: young differ markedly from adults; cranial depression develops by the second year (a 4-inch specimen showed no cranial depression, eye 1/5 head length).
- Seasonal note: iris reddish in spring; grinding surface on pharyngeal teeth nearly obliterated in winter due to reduced feeding, potentially causing taxonomic confusion (misidentification as toothless morphs).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: All species were collected from a single locality — the [[Gila River]] at Fort Thomas, Arizona Territory — representing the historic native fish assemblage of this reach of the **Colorado River Basin** drainage prior to major anthropogenic alteration (damming, water diversion, introduction of non-native species).
- **Diet**: *[[Gila Emorii]]* fed almost entirely on **gastropods** and **caddis-worms** (Trichoptera larvae), crushed using powerful pharyngeal teeth — evidence of a durophagous molluscivore/insectivore feeding strategy.
- **Seasonal feeding behavior**: Reduced winter feeding activity in *G. emorii* led to near-obliteration of pharyngeal tooth grinding surfaces, a seasonal physiological/morphological plasticity noted by Carpenter as a potential source of taxonomic misidentification.
- **Resilience**: *G. emorii* was noted as extremely hardy, capable of reviving after several hours desiccated out of water — an adaptation likely suited to the intermittent, flashy hydrology of Southwestern desert streams.
- **Community structure**: Carpenter's assertion that only seven species occupied this river reach, combined with his recognition that some nominal species were simply ontogenetic variants of *Gila emorii* and *Ptychochilus lucius*, reflects an early (pre-modern) understanding of intraspecific morphological plasticity in desert fish taxa — a theme highly relevant to later revisions of Southwestern Cyprinidae and Catostomidae taxonomy.

## Key Findings

- Description of new species **[[Catostomus Gila]]** Kirsch, based on morphometric distinctions from *C. ardens* (body compression, fin proportions, caudal peduncle shape).
- Erection of new genus **[[Xyrauchen]]** Eigenmann & Kirsch to house *Xyrauchen cypho* (originally *Catostomus cypho* Lockington), based on unique cranial/vertebral osteology producing a pronounced nuchal hump — this genus persists in modern taxonomy as containing the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]].
- Detailed osteological description of the hump-supporting interneural and vertebral modifications in *Xyrauchen*, an early contribution to catostomid comparative anatomy.
- Documentation of ontogenetic cranial depression and dietary shifts in *[[Gila Emorii]]* (Bonytail), including seasonal variability in pharyngeal tooth wear tied to feeding activity.
- Field-based ecological data (diet, coloration by season, hardiness/desiccation tolerance) supplied by Lieut. Carpenter represent some of the earliest natural history observations for these Gila River basin endemics.
- Collection underscores the historically diverse endemic fish fauna of the Gila River, several members of which (*Xyrauchen texanus*, *Gila elegans*, *Ptychochilus lucius*) are now listed as threatened or endangered under modern conservation status due to habitat alteration and invasive species pressure.
