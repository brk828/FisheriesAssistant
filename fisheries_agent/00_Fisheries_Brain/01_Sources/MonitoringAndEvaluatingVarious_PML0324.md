# Monitoring and Evaluating Various Sizes of Domestic-Reared Razorback Sucker Stocked in the Upper Colorado and Gunnison Rivers, 1995–2001

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Recovery Program final report (Project Number 50, February 2003) by [[Bob Burdick]] of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Colorado River Fishery Project evaluates whether stocking can restore [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered catostomid, in the Upper Colorado River Basin. The experimental study compared survival and performance of three nominal size classes (100, 200 and 300 mm total length) released in the [[Upper Colorado River]] and [[Gunnison River]] from April 1994 through October 2001.

Of 49,954 fish stocked, 235 were recaptured (226 alive) or found dead, about 0.5%; 84 of these had been at large six months or more. Recapture rates rose with release size: fish under 200 mm showed very low return (about 0.03%), while larger fish were recaptured far more often. Dispersal was predominantly downstream, with mean downstream displacement of 50.9 miles for fish at large six months or more. Backwaters yielded the most recaptures, and electrofishing was the main sampling gear. The report recommends stocking fish over 200 mm and outlines related monitoring, genetics and handling measures.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was once common and widespread in warm-water reaches of the Colorado River Basin but has declined sharply since human alteration of the basin began near the turn of the 20th century. It was listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act in 1991. Recruitment failure, linked to altered flows, reduced floodplain inundation, water quality changes and nonnative predation, is believed to limit wild populations.

Wild adults are now nearly extinct in the Upper Colorado and Gunnison rivers, so the Recovery Program placed a high priority on stocking domestically produced fish. Broodstock came from a "blended stock" of wild fish from [[Lake Powell]] arms, the Upper Colorado River and [[Etter Pond]], which contained potential siblings and hybridized with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. Fish were reared at several hatcheries and grow-out ponds, including [[Wahweap State Fish Hatchery]] and the [[24-Road Hatchery]] in Grand Junction. Pit tags were applied to track family lot, stocking date and location.

Stocking plans from Colorado, Utah and an integrated 2002 plan set targets (for example, a minimum viable population of 5,800 adults from ages 5 to 7), with the Colorado plan originally calling for 300 mm fish by age 2+.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Released fish were not site-acclimated; they went into shallow, low-velocity side channels or backwaters after transport in aerated tanks. Post-stocking movement was largely downstream drift, consistent with other reintroductions in the [[Green River]] and [[San Juan River]]. Downstream displacement was significantly greater in the Gunnison River than in the Upper Colorado River.

Recaptured fish were found in multiple habitats, most often in backwaters (65% of 84 observations at six months or more). Fish were also recaptured in the [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] fish trap, showing that adults can ascend a fish passage structure. Larval razorback suckers collected in the Gunnison River in 2002 indicated that stocked fish were spawning.

Nonnative fishes such as centrarchids and catfishes were identified as predation risks, particularly for small fish. The report also discusses [[Domestication Selection]] as a possible cause of poor survival among older broodstock.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking totals:** 49,954 fish stocked (April 1994–October 2001); mean total length 187 mm (SD 60.3); 235 recaptured or dead (~0.5%); 84 at large six months or more.
- **Family lots:** 81 family lots stocked; 40 represented among fish recaptured alive. Family lot 1992-2A (reared at Wahweap) accounted for 13.1% of the 84 long-term recaptures and had a 12.4% recapture rate from its October 1996 stocking. Mean stocking length of this lot was about 343 mm.
- **Size and survival:** Fish under 200 mm had a very low return rate (about 0.03%), and only 4.8% of long-term recaptures were under 200 mm. Fish in the 200–249 mm class made up 38.6% of all recaptures, and the 250–299 mm class accounted for 14.2%. For fish at large six months or more, the 250–299 mm class was 64.2% of recaptures from 1997–2001. Comparatively, the San Juan River recapture rate (~1.4%) was about seven times higher than the Upper Colorado and Gunnison rate (~0.2%).
- **Dispersal:** Mean downstream displacement for fish at large six months or more was 50.9 miles (n=73); upstream mean was 1.3 miles. Gunnison River fish moved 3.3 times farther downstream (mean 73.8 miles) than Upper Colorado fish (mean 22.1 miles; P < 0.0001).
- **Notable movement:** One fish stocked in the Gunnison River at RM 57 in October 1995 was recaptured in Labyrinth Canyon of the Green River in May 2000, a total movement of about 317 river miles.
- **Habitat use:** Of 84 habitat observations, 64 were from the Upper Colorado and 20 from the Gunnison. Backwaters accounted for 65%.
- **Gear:** Electrofishing was the main gear, and seines, trammel nets and trap nets were used less often. Gear efficiency was not formally compared; the authors suggest trap nets may suit shallow backwaters.
- **Spring vs. fall stocking:** Spring return rate (about 0.2%) was roughly half that of fall-stocked fish (about 0.5%), but sample sizes were small.
- **Reproduction:** Eight larval razorback suckers were collected in 2002 in the Gunnison River, indicating that stocked fish spawned there.
- **Recommendations:** (1) Continue stocking fish over 200 mm, ideally about 300 mm, in the Upper Colorado and Gunnison rivers; (2) monitor survival, dispersal and reproduction; (3) continue PIT tagging and genetic monitoring; (4) use established handling and transport protocols, including 0.5% salt in transport water; (5) test site acclimation in floodplain habitats or backwaters held up to one week.
- **Uncertainties:** Authors question whether low returns for sub-200 mm fish are representative, whether survival in the wild will be higher than observed, and whether progeny will recruit to the adult population.
