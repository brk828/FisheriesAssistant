# Recovery Program for Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin: Spring/Summer 1997 Newsletter

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1997 newsletter from the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] reports on habitat acquisition, wetland restoration, hatchery operations, stocking, water-rights agreements, and local partnerships. The program aims to re-establish self-sustaining populations of four endangered fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. It also seeks to allow continued water development. Most items are project updates, with a few personnel and policy notes and a feature on a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] biologist.

## Background & Description

The newsletter is a program bulletin rather than a research paper. Its contents include:

- **Program scope:** A 15-year, multi-agency effort begun in 1988 under [[John Hamill]], who left the director post in late January 1997. [[Angela Kantola]] served as acting director. Participants include the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the Bureau of Reclamation, the Western Area Power Administration, the states of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, and several environmental, water-user and energy organizations.
- **Species background:** Razorback suckers are believed to have evolved about 4 million years ago. In the last 50 years they have nearly disappeared, largely due to dam construction and non-native fish introductions. Bonytails are the rarest of the four endangered Colorado River fishes and occur in the Colorado River drainage and nowhere else on Earth.
- **Feature articles:** A land purchase near Grand Junction, a Yampa Basin agreement, a water-rights settlement, a biologist profile, and a planned aquarium exhibit.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat value:** A 153-acre parcel on the south bank of the Colorado River between Grand Junction and Palisade includes riffles, pools and backwaters. About 24 acres of wetlands flood during spring runoff. Colorado squawfish move into these warm, slow, food-rich wetlands, and razorback suckers are expected to use them as populations recover. The site also provides nesting habitat for perhaps as many as 75 bird species and hosts mule deer, rabbits, raccoons, squirrels, muskrats and beavers.
- **Flood plain restoration:** Levees were breached at three sites totaling 123 acres along the Green River downstream from Jensen, Utah. Fish can now access these areas when flows at Jensen exceed 13,000 cubic feet per second. Before [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] was built, these sites flooded in two of every three years.
- **Dry-season flows:** Parts of the Colorado River near Grand Junction occasionally dried up in summer as recently as the mid-1980s. Enhanced late-summer and fall flows are a stated priority.
- **Water quality:** A water-rights settlement is expected to deliver cooler, lower-mineral, lower-sediment "excess" water from [[Green Mountain Reservoir]] to irrigators and municipalities.
- **Contaminants:** [[Selenium Contamination]], a naturally occurring trace element, is being studied as a possible cause of reduced reproductive success in razorback suckers.
- **Non-native fish:** Competition and predation from non-native species are a recognized threat, addressed through stocking rules and pond removal.

## Key Findings

**Land and wetland acquisition**
- The federal Bureau of Reclamation and a private landowner preserved the 153-acre parcel. The [[Mesa County Land Conservancy]] and government agencies will manage it.
- The purchase price was $569,000. Half came from the Recovery Program, and the rest came from mitigation money tied to wetland losses from the [[Grand Valley Salinity Control Program]].
- Up to 1,200 acres of wetlands along the Green and Colorado rivers may be restored. For fiscal year 1997, $1.9 million was targeted to these efforts.
- About $550,000 was set aside for [[Conservation Easements]] on a goal of 1,000 acres of Green River flood plain downstream of Dinosaur National Monument. Another $200,000 was available for easements on about 200 acres of Colorado River wetlands between Rifle, Colo., and the Gunnison River confluence.

**Fish passage and hatchery work (FY1997)**
- The Bureau of Reclamation allocated $600,000 for a fish passageway at the [[Grand Valley Irrigation Company Diversion Dam]].
- The [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] was allocated about $1 million to build 10 additional half-acre ponds and manage the facility.
- The Grand Valley Fish Facility received $220,000, and the Utah Division of Wildlife's Wahweap hatchery received $150,000.
- The [[Redlands Fish Ladder]] has enabled 8,000 native fish to migrate around the diversion dam to upstream habitat in the Gunnison River.

**Stocking**
- The Utah Division of Wildlife stocked 2,000 six-inch bonytails into the Colorado River near Moab last fall, and planned a similar number for 1997. A $35,000 allocation covered bonytail stocking at Professor Valley.
- Nearly 300 razorback suckers, 12 to 18 inches long, were stocked in the Gunnison River near Delta, Colo., to restore the species to its historic range.
- The Fish and Wildlife Service will continue stocking the Gunnison and Colorado rivers with 4-, 8- and 12-inch razorbacks to determine which size has the greatest survival.

**Water rights and flows**
- A Glenwood Springs water court settlement, signed by all 42 parties after five years of negotiation, is expected to make about 30,000 more acre-feet of water per year available.
- Colorado's Division 6 water court approved adding in-stream flow enhancement to the [[Steamboat Lake]] water right. This allows about 2,000 acre-feet to enhance late-summer Yampa River flows.

**Yampa River planning**
- The [[Yampa River Basin Partnership]] signed a memorandum of understanding with the Fish and Wildlife Service to establish local involvement in Yampa water and endangered fish decisions.
- Plan objectives include determining late-summer and early-fall water needs of Colorado squawfish and humpback chub, evaluating fish ladders, updating 1990 human water-need estimates, reducing non-native fish impacts, and evaluating Elkhead Reservoir expansion under the National Environmental Policy Act.

**Non-native fish policy**
- The Colorado Wildlife Commission removed bag limits for several non-native species in designated critical habitat.
- Stocking of nine non-native species is prohibited in upper basin critical habitat: black bullhead, yellow bullhead, common carp, flathead catfish, green sunfish, northern pike, red shiner, white crappie and wiper.
- Widespread trout stocking is allowed. The Fish and Wildlife Service will provide 40,000 catchable trout per year for public ponds unsuited to warmwater fish.
- Colorado Division of Wildlife and landowners drained two Western Slope ponds totaling 13 surface acres that were sources of non-native fish.

**Historical records**
- [[Harold Tyus]] located seven humpback chubs (6 to 12 inches) in a University of Colorado museum collection. The 1948 Yampa River specimens are the earliest humpback records on file for the upper basin; previous collections date from 1970. Former student H.A. Fehimann reported humpbacks were common in the Yampa in the 1940s.

**Research and education**
- [[Kathy Holley]], a Bureau of Reclamation biologist, leads a study of selenium effects on razorback sucker reproduction. Larval fish are held in lab jars, and 36 adult razorbacks were placed in a managed side channel for 18 months at a cooperating landowner's site.
- The [[River Watch Program]], run by the Colorado Division of Wildlife, has students measure dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature, hardness, alkalinity and heavy metals. Its data has been used by state and federal water agencies.
- [[Colorado's Ocean Journey]], a Denver aquarium slated to open in spring 1999, will feature live endangered Colorado River fishes.
