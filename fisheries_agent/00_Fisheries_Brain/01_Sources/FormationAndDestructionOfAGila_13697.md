# Formation and Destruction of a Gila River Mesquite Bosque Community

#botany #riparian-ecology #prosopis

## Executive Summary

This study documents the repeated formation and destruction of a [[Mesquite Bosque]] (a woodland of [[Honey Mesquite]] and [[Velvet Mesquite]]) on a terrace of the [[Gila River]] in eastern Arizona. The terrace originated behind a coarse [[Alluvial Fan]] (a boulder cone) deposited by flash floods from the ephemeral [[Dry Canyon]] tributary. Vegetation then colonized the surface, culminating in a mesquite-dominated bosque. Sustained flooding in the mainstream river later undercut and removed much of the terrace. The authors use the sequence to illustrate the dynamics of desert rivers and riparian biota, and they note that mesquite and [[Saltcedar]] respond differently to the same terrace conditions.

## Background & Description

Mesquite bosques once covered many subtropical Southwestern floodplains. The largest stands, including those at San Xavier, Komatke and Texas Hill, have been destroyed. Surviving stands face pressure from water diversion and pumping, agricultural clearing, and firewood cutting. Introduced saltcedar (*Tamarix chinensis*) now dominates thousands of hectares that formerly supported bosques and other native riparian forests. Floods in 1978–79 removed much of one mature community east of Safford, which prompted the study. The authors report historical documentation of its age and origin.

The study area is a terrace along the north bank of the Gila River, where the river is incised 3.5–5.0 m below the terrace surface, about 945 m above sea level in 1976. The bosque was about 1 km long and 10.0 ha in area in 1976. Its width ranged from 115 m at the upstream end to 275 m at its widest point. The stand was dominated by mesquite, with [[Seep-Willow]], [[Fremont Cottonwood]] and [[Goodding Willow]] also present. The authors state that mesquite made up 98.8% of all perennial plants in the community.

Size structure was reported as 19% of the stand in trees under 2 m tall, 80.7% in trees 2–9 m tall, and 1.3% in trees over 9 m. Young mesquite colonized cleared areas such as a woodcut and a corral site. The authors noted that the figures as reported total slightly over 100%.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical extent and regional decline.** Lacey et al. (1975) mapped riparian terraces from near the mouth of Bonita Creek upstream to Guthrie. They found that 56% of about 657 ha of terraces was covered by mesquite, with 60% of stands having more than 50% ground cover (35% over 75%). Historical records from 1876 describe an "almost unbroken mesquite forest that stretched for miles" along the river.

**Climate and flood history.** The period 1870–89 was one of the driest in almost 250 years. Floods followed in 1891–96, then a drought in 1896–1904. Heavy livestock grazing in the same period is thought to have damaged ground cover. Massive erosion occurred after high runoff in 1905. Flooding was widespread from the late 1880s through 1916. Between 1875 and 1914, the average channel width of the Gila River about 10 km downstream increased from 48.7 m to 588.3 m. In a 24.6-km reach, scoured channel area grew from 89 ha to 1,207 ha, and vegetative cover fell from 1,118 ha to none. The authors attribute most of this clearing to the 1905–06 floods.

**Terrace formation.** Flash floods from Dry Canyon deposited boulders greater than 1.0 m in diameter at the confluence with the Gila River. This created a partial dam that deflected the river against a cliff and formed a rapid. Later floods, including a flash flood of more than 10 m³/s in 1977, added to the cone. Successive layers of finer alluvium then accumulated on its downstream side, and mesquite stabilized and increased silt deposition. By 1978, mature mesquite had trunks buried more than 2.0 m above the major roots.

**Age of the bosque.** The authors estimate the bosque was no more than 60–70 years old in 1976. Larger trees along the cliffs are considered remnants of earlier bosques. Gavin (1973) estimated that mesquite grows to about 25 cm in diameter in 60 years, which the authors consider consistent with sizes observed along the Gila River.

**Destruction.** The bosque was largely removed in 1978–79 by undercutting and collapse, and the channel was abruptly straightened over about 1.0 km. About 1.5 ha disappeared in March 1978, and another 3.5 ha in December 1978–January 1979. The authors estimate that earlier floods in 1904–16 removed about 8 ha of a 10-ha terrace. Winter floods deposited up to 1 m of sediment on the south side of the river and about 0.5 m within the remaining bosque. Seedling mesquite was established on the landward side of the aggrading surface by summer 1982.

**Water relations.** Mesquite cannot grow in waterlogged soil but roots deeply to reach water. Fine alluvium over coarser bedload stays moist through capillarity. Soil moisture above the water table is used by mesquite more than groundwater is (McQueen and Miller, 1972). Seeds are buried by wind, water and rodents, and germinate readily in moist, well-aerated soil in sunny openings.

**Why saltcedar is excluded.** The authors hypothesize that saltcedar does not invade canyon-bound terraces for three reasons. First, the surface 5–10 cm of these terraces is very dry. Second, continuing accumulation of alluvium can raise terraces 3–5 m or more above river level, far above the water table. Third, saltcedar requires long periods of saturated soil for germination and a high water table to establish (Warren and Turner, 1975). The authors also attribute the absence of saltcedar from canyon-bound reaches to the erosive nature of these habitats (Minckley and Clark, 1981).

## Key Findings

- **Repeated cycle of formation and destruction.** Terrace and bosque formation were triggered by an alluvial cone from Dry Canyon. Destruction was caused by sustained mainstream flooding. The authors interpret this as a recurring process in canyon-bound desert rivers.
- **Age and origin.** The bosque was no more than 60–70 years old in 1976, and its origin is linked to the widespread scouring of 1905–16. Older trees at the cliff edges are interpreted as remnants of earlier stands.
- **Mesquite dominance and recovery.** The 1976 bosque was 98.8% mesquite by perennial plant share. Seedlings had re-established on the aggrading surface by 1982.
- **Quasi-climax character.** Where alluvial cones protect a site, the authors suggest that a mesquite bosque may be a quasi-climax community. Its origin and destruction may correspond to flooding at 50- to 100-year recurrence intervals.
- **Regional context.** Mesquite bosques have been largely lost or degraded along Southwestern rivers because of diversion, pumping, clearing and saltcedar invasion. The authors' terrace model suggests that canyon-bound reaches may remain less vulnerable to saltcedar invasion.
- **Management implication.** The authors frame the study as evidence for the dynamic, flood-driven nature of desert riparian systems. This suggests that conservation of bosque habitat depends on maintaining the alluvial and hydrologic processes that create and renew terraces.
