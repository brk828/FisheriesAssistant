# Genetic Differences in Male Development Time Among Populations of the Endangered Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1998 *Journal of Heredity* research communication compares the timing of male sexual maturation among four surviving natural populations of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] ([[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]]) in Arizona. Fish from each population were reared in a common laboratory environment. [[Monkey Spring]] males reached sexual maturity about 50% later than males from [[Bylas Spring]], [[Cienega Creek]], and [[Sharp Spring]]. The authors interpret this as a heritable, fitness-related difference, most likely shaped by the year-round warm conditions at Monkey Spring. They recommend that Gila topminnow from different watersheds be managed and conserved separately.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow is a small livebearing fish of the family Poeciliidae. It is endangered and now survives only in a small fraction of its former range, which once extended through southern Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico. Earlier molecular genetic surveys of the few remaining populations found little or no variation, both among and within populations. For example, of 25 [[Allozyme Analysis]] loci, only a few were variable, and only one population, Sharp Spring, was polymorphic.

Fish stocks were established in 1994 from 20 pregnant females collected at four locations: Bylas Spring, Cienega Creek, Sharp Spring, and Monkey Spring. These sites represent the four watersheds where natural populations still persist. Stocks of about 1000 adults from each site were maintained in large circular raceways under constant conditions.

Male sexual development was measured using two stages of gonopodium development:
- **Initiation age:** elongation of anterior anal fin rays 3–5.
- **Completion age:** the gonopodial tip is fully fused and clear, which marks sexual maturity.

Male development was observed daily until initiation, then every four days until completion. Broods of four or more were reared in sibling groups of four to nine individuals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The four populations occupy different thermal settings:
- **Bylas Spring, Cienega Creek, and Sharp Spring:** subject to large summer–winter temperature fluctuations.
- **Monkey Spring:** a warm spring with a constant temperature of 27.4 ± 0.6 °C that remains year-round. It is separated from other drainages by a 10 m natural travertine dam and by a modern irrigation diversion.

Monkey Spring is the only one of the four sites that supports year-round reproduction. Southern Arizona is the northernmost edge of the species' range, and the authors note that year-round breeding is a tropical trait in this lineage. Seasonal reproduction at the other sites ends from late fall to spring, so male development timing there is under selection pressure. Males that mature late in the year may be larger and more competitive in the next spring. Overwintering at cold temperatures, however, carries survival risks.

## Key Findings

- **Population differences in male maturation:** Overall differences among populations in both initiation and completion age were highly significant (p < .001). Monkey Spring males differed significantly from the other three populations (p < .001 for each comparison). Bylas Spring, Cienega Creek, and Sharp Spring did not differ significantly from one another.
- **Magnitude of delay:** Mean initiation age, mean completion age, and duration of development were 53%, 48%, and 45% longer, respectively, in Monkey Spring than the average of the other three populations. All five Monkey Spring tanks had the four latest initiation and completion ages and the four longest durations of development.
- **Extremes across tanks:** Of the 22 tanks, the first to initiate development did so at 19.5 days (Cienega Creek tank D), and the first to complete did so at 42 days (Cienega Creek tanks D and E).
- **Within-population variation:** Variation among tanks within populations was not significant for initiation of maturation but was significant for completion ages (p < .05).
- **Regression:** Duration of development was significantly related to initiation age (p < .01), explaining 29% of the variance.
- **Consistency with earlier work:** Sheffer et al. (1997) reported that 12-week-old, laboratory-reared Monkey Spring males were significantly larger than males from the other populations. Because male growth stops at maturity, the later maturation of Monkey Spring males is consistent with their larger size.
- **Interpretation:** The authors conclude that the difference is genetic. It fits a life-history model in which constant warm conditions and year-round breeding reduce selection against late, slower-developing, and larger males. Environmental influences such as temperature, day length, and social interactions are well documented in fish, but temperature, day length, and water quality were controlled in this experiment, and the authors judged the effect of tank density and sibling number to be non-significant.
- **Conservation implication:** The results support the recommendation of Simons et al. (1989) that at least one representative lineage be preserved from each of the four Arizona geographic areas. They also support the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1993) recommendation that Gila topminnow from different watersheds be managed and conserved separately.
