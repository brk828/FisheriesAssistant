# Distribution, Biology, and Conservation Status of the Carolina Madtom (*Noturus furiosus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study reviews the distribution, life history, and conservation status of the [[Carolina Madtom]] (*Noturus furiosus*), a small catfish endemic to the [[Tar River]] and [[Neuse River]] drainages of North Carolina. The species was a candidate for pre-listing studies by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. Field surveys from 1982 to 1985, combined with analysis of 326 preserved museum specimens, show that the species is still reproducing and recruiting at several localities, although it has declined and is threatened by reservoirs, pollution, and proposed channelization and dredging projects. The authors recommend protecting spawning sites and discouraging indiscriminate use of [[Rotenone]] in stream surveys.

## Background & Description

*Noturus furiosus* is a moderate-sized, boldly patterned madtom first described by [[David Starr Jordan]] and Meek (1889). Its status has long been uncertain. Early reports described it as relatively numerous, but later collections were sparse. [[Cooper and Braswell (1982)]] judged the species to have suffered a serious decline and to be vulnerable to extinction. It is placed in the subgenus *Rabida* and the *furiosus* species group (Taylor 1969). Its taxonomic status was questioned because Taylor suggested it might be a geographic subspecies of *N. stigmosus*, but allozyme analysis by James M. Grady showed several fixed alleles that distinguish it from its relatives.

The historical range covered the Piedmont and Coastal Plain of all major tributary systems of the Tar and Neuse rivers. Since 1963, however, the species has been recorded from fewer than 12 localities. Ichthyocide, electrofishing, and trapping surveys often yielded [[Notorus Insignis]] and [[Noturus Gyrinus]] instead.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** All records come from free-flowing streams. The species is rarely found in lentic habitat. Summer stream temperatures exceed 20°C, and water is generally clear to coffee-colored. Adults occupy swift current in riffles and runs at depths of about 0.3 to 1.0 m. Juveniles favor shallower, slower water. Most substrates are sand, gravel, leaf litter, and small rubble, and the species is often taken from debris piles in sandy areas. Macrophytes are generally absent.

**Nesting habitat.** During the May to July nesting season, adults use areas of moderate to slow flow with abundant cover. Human litter such as [[Beer Cans]], bottles, and [[Mussel Shells]] (mainly *Elliptio complanata*) serves as nesting cover. The authors note that artificial sites far outnumber adequate natural sites in the streams sampled.

**Threats.** Reservoir construction (for example [[Falls Lake]]), cold-water releases below dams, pollution around Raleigh, and a toxic spill on 10 July 1980 have reduced habitat in the upper Neuse. Federal and state projects involving stream cover removal, bank denuding, dredging, channelization, and reservoir construction threaten known localities. Drought-related habitat reduction and predation by fish-eating birds, snakes, and water snakes (*Nerodia*) are natural risks. Predation of larvae by fishes and crayfishes is likely important once guardian males are removed.

**Community.** The species shares its habitat with [[Necturus Lewisi]] (Neuse River waterdog), [[Anguilla Rostrata]], and other madtoms. Competition for nest and hiding sites with crayfishes and juvenile madtoms is possible.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- Extant populations are known with certainty from 17 localities. Of 36 total historical localities (24 backed by extant voucher material, plus literature records), the authors have revisited all but ten since 1982.
- Seven new populations were discovered in 1984 and 1985, indicating recent successful reproduction.
- The largest populations are in the Tar River proper (three extant populations), the Little River system (the largest extant Neuse population, up to 20 individuals at one time), Swift Creek, and possibly Fishing Creek, where a 1959 sample of 67 individuals was the largest ever taken.
- The Neuse River near Raleigh (the type locality) and the Eno River were not found occupied in recent surveys.

**Life history**
- Individuals live at least 4 years. The largest specimens were 101 mm SL (male) and 98 mm SL (female).
- Females mature at 2 to 3 years, most commonly at 3 years (mean 3.1 years). Mature females averaged 74.8 mm SL (N = 16).
- Fecundity averaged 126.3 vitellogenic oocytes per female (range 79 to 298; N = 17).
- Nesting occurs from about mid-May to the third week of July at water temperatures of 20 to 25°C. Nests are guarded solely by males aged 3 to 4 years (mean SL 89.8 mm).
- Complete clutches contained 139, 147, and 171 embryos (mean 152). Yolk diameter averaged 3.2 mm.
- Larvae averaged 9.5 mm TL one day after hatching.
- The sex ratio did not differ significantly from 1:1 (126 males:114 females).

**Diet**
- Of 200 stomachs examined, 94 contained food. Dipteran, ephemeropteran, trichopteran, coleopteran, and odonate larvae or nymphs made up more than 95% of food organisms.
- Chironomid larvae were the most important prey group, and stomachs of guardian males were mostly empty.
- The species is a nocturnal benthic insectivore that feeds mainly at dusk and dawn.

**Conservation recommendations**
- The authors recommend protecting known spawning sites and discouraging collectors from sampling prime nesting areas.
- They advise discouraging indiscriminate rotenone use, since most historical rotenone collections were made in June and July during the breeding season.
- Key knowledge gaps remain, including spawning behavior, female parental care, number of clutches per year, winter habitat, and movement. Propagation techniques for madtoms have not been developed.
