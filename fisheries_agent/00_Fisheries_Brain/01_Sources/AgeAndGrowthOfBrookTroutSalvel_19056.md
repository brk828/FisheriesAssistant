# Age and Growth of Brook Trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) in Montana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Domrose (1963) estimated the age and growth of [[Brook Trout]] across Montana using scale analysis of fish collected from 13 streams and 8 lakes by state fisheries personnel (1950–1959), plus scales from seven [[Beartooth Mountain Lakes]] sampled in summer 1959. Growth was back-calculated from scales using a nomograph under a straight-line scale-to-body-length assumption. Brook trout were found to be widespread and well established across most Montana river drainages, having been introduced from 1894 onward. Growth was generally slower than in earlier Montana studies, and the Beartooth lakes grew only slightly below the statewide average.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), introduced to Montana as early as 1894 (Glenwood Lake, Buffalo and Travis Creeks), with further plantings in the Yellowstone and Madison Rivers in 1889–1890 (as reported in the source).
- **Ecological impact noted:** Introduction of brook and [[Rainbow Trout]] around 1900 into the Centennial Valley is believed to have contributed to the decline of native [[Cutthroat Trout]] and [[American Grayling]]. Exotic trout also replaced cutthroat trout across the lower parts of their original range.
- **Prior research:** Age and growth studies existed for Montana (Gallatin River, Prickly Pear Creek) and for Utah, Alberta, California, New York, and Michigan.
- **Study scope:** Collections from 21 named waters across nine river drainages, including the Missouri, Clark Fork, Kootenai, Flathead, and Jefferson systems (the source lists these across Figure 1 and Table V).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Beartooth Mountain lakes (Table I):**
- Elevation 8,070–10,480 ft; size 15–160 acres; maximum depth 30–65 ft.
- Surface temperatures 42–62 °F (July 9–September 10).
- Methyl orange alkalinity low, 15–28 ppm.
- Fed by small snowmelt streams (3–4 ft wide) that probably dry in winter; shoreline ranges from steep escarpments to meadows.
- Vegetation: sub-alpine forest or alpine tundra.
- Food was not quantified, but Diptera, Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, and Plecoptera were common. Associated fish included [[Cutthroat Trout]] (Glacier and Round Lakes) and lake chub, *Hybopsis plumbea* (Kersey Lake).

**Drainage setting:** Montana streams originate at 5,000–8,000 ft, descend through steep canyons to cultivated valleys and rangeland, and most samples came from secondary and tertiary tributaries.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Gear: experimental gill nets, fine-mesh gill nets, fish toxicants, electric shocking, seining, and angling.
- Ageing: scale impressions or gum arabic–glycerin mounts, annuli read with a [[Microprojector]], and back-calculated lengths from a nomograph.
- Condition coefficient: C = W × 10⁵ / L³ (W in pounds, L in inches), calculated only for Beartooth fish over 6 inches.

**Age-Specific Growth and Scale Anomalies**
- In about 40% of Round Lake and 16% of Timberline Lake scales, the first annulus was absent. These fish had 8–12 circuli before the first annulus versus the usual 4–7. The author attributes this to a failure to form scales in the first year, a pattern also reported for cutthroat and golden trout.
- Beartooth grand average calculated lengths at annuli I–IV: 2.5, 5.3, 7.6, and 9.4 inches (n = 655, 543, 408, 230). Round Lake was largest at annulus IV (12.0 in); Timberline was smallest (7.9 in).
- Growth increments were greatest in year two and declined in years three and four.
- Beartooth actual lengths ranged 2.1–17.4 in, weights 0.91–2.29 lb (as reported). Glacier Lake averaged 11.3 in and 0.74 lb in gill-net catches; Timberline averaged 0.17 lb.
- Bald Knob Lake adults (15.5–17.7 in, 2.0–2.8 lb, all five annuli) were attributed to poor spawning conditions limiting overpopulation.
- No significant growth difference between sexes was found.

**Density and Condition**
- Average weight was lowest in lakes with the highest catch per 12-hour net set (Figure 3). Timberline had the greatest catch (41.0) and Round the least (13.7).
- Average condition factor ranged from 32.0 (Ship Lake) to 45.5 (Glacier Lake). Condition declined with fish size in most lakes, except Glacier and Kersey.

**Comparison with Other Waters (fourth annulus)**
- Fish Lake, Utah: 12.9 in. Maligne and Beaver Lakes, Alberta: 11.2 and 11.7 in. Bunny Lake, California: 5.9 in. In each case the Beartooth values were lower than Utah and Alberta and higher than Bunny Lake.

**Statewide Drainage Results**
- Grand average calculated lengths at annuli I–V: 3.2, 5.8, 8.4, 13.6, and 16.9 in (n = 1678, 888, 217, 31, 9). Maximum growth was in Culver Pond (14.4 in at III); minimum in Bloody Dick Creek (5.8 in at III, per the drainage listing).
- Flathead drainage had the largest third-annulus average (9.5 in). Crow Creek (Tizer Lakes) had the smallest (6.9 in).
- Culver Pond (Jefferson drainage) produced the largest fish, with actual lengths up to 21.9 in in that drainage.
- Growth was greatest during year one in most drainages.
- Previous Montana studies reported faster growth: Bridger and Spring Creeks, 10.9 in at annulus III (Purkett, 1951); Prickly Pear Creek, 9.8 in (Bishop, 1955).

**Interpretation**
- Montana fish grew larger than New York (Hazzard, 1932) and Michigan (Shetter and Leonard, 1943) collections at annuli II and III, though New York fish were larger at annulus I.
