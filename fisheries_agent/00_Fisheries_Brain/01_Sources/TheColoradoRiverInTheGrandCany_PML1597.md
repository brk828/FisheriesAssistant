# The Colorado River in the Grand Canyon: The Case for Heterotrophy Before Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[G. Richard Marzolf]], [[Edmund D. Andrews]] and [[J. Dungan Smith]] (U.S. Geological Survey) reconstructs the biotic structure and function of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]] before the construction of [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Because biological records from the pre-dam period are scarce, the authors combine hydrology, sediment transport, geomorphology, historical photographs and written accounts to argue that the river was heterotrophic: turbidity and unstable substrates limited in-stream primary production and benthic macroinvertebrate populations. They propose that the main carbon source was woody debris and terrestrial organic material delivered by tributary floods and the annual snowmelt flood, and retained in eddies. The authors frame this as a testable hypothesis to inform management under the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] of 1992.

## Background & Description

- **Management context:** The [[National Park Service]] must preserve the riparian corridor in a natural or pristine condition while balancing societal interests. Restoring pristine biotic elements requires knowledge of the pre-dam system, which is largely lacking, although flow and sediment records (1922–1966) are among the longest available.
- **Native fish fauna:** The pre-dam fauna presumably included at least eight endemic species. Of these, only three remain in Grand Canyon (the text also states that five are no longer present). The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), described from a specimen collected near Bright Angel Creek, was listed as endangered in 1967.
- **Geomorphic setting:** The canyon began forming 6–8 million years ago as the river incised the [[Colorado Plateau]]. Coarse talus and bedrock, not fluvial sediment, dominate bank material. The river has a minimal floodplain.
- **Tributaries:** Short, steep tributary channels deliver debris flows and floods that create constrictions, backwater pools and recirculation zones (eddies). Flow velocities in eddies are 10–20% of main-channel velocities.
- **Eddy bars:** Deposits beneath recirculation zones can cover up to 4 ha and reach 15 m thick.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and sediment (pre-dam, 1947–1957 and 1925–1957 records):**
- Mean annual flood approximately 2,600 m³/s; mean annual runoff approximately 490 m³/s (as given in the source).
- Mean annual suspended load 1925–1957: 180 × 10⁶ tons, about 60% silt and clay.
- Suspended sediment concentration exceeded 300 mg/L 95% of the time.
- Concentrations above 100 mg/L suppress light enough to preclude photosynthesis deeper than a few centimeters.
- Estimated eddy deposition rate at 1,415 m³/s and 1,500 mg/L suspended sediment: about 0.5 t/m²/day.

**Light and productivity:** The authors cite Yard et al. (1994) for present-day turbidity, which is 60% of the time below the Paria confluence, rising to 80% below the Little Colorado River and higher still below Kanab Creek. Pre-dam turbidity was higher still, with additional sediment from upstream tributaries (Gunnison, Dolores, Green, San Rafael, Price, Dirty Devil, Escalante, San Juan).

**Wood and terrestrial carbon:**
- Near-field sources: tributary flash floods deliver leaves, twigs, tree boles and terrestrial and aquatic invertebrates.
- Far-field sources: coarse woody debris from the upper basin, moving in annual flood pulses. Ferguson (1971) reported wood accumulating over more than 1,000 years.
- Dry wood in the arid canyon decomposes slowly; repeat photography showed logs lasting 100 years, and later dating gave 400–600 years.
- Nitrate averaged about 3 mg N/L at Lees Ferry (pre-dam weighted average).

**Post-dam changes:** Cold, clear regulated flows since 1963 support a Cladophora-based epibenthic flora and a trout fishery. [[Tamarisk]] (*Tamarix*) has invaded the riparian corridor, and several exotic fish families have established successfully. Wood now accumulates at the heads of Lake Powell tributary embayments, and the amount of wood in Grand Canyon is far lower than before the dam.

## Key Findings

1. **Light limitation:** Suspended sediment levels were high enough to severely limit in-stream photosynthesis, so the river was heterotrophic.
2. **Physical constraints:** Scouring floods, abrasive sand and unstable cobble/gravel substrates prevented establishment of algal floras and stable benthic macroinvertebrate communities.
3. **Woody debris as carbon base:** Large, predictable annual loads of wood from upstream, retained in eddies, likely supported riparian heterotrophic production. Decomposition by fungi, bacteria and lignivores (beetles, termites, ants) generated secondary production.
4. **Wetting by stage fluctuations:** Records (e.g., 1924 hydrograph at Phantom Ranch) show stage fluctuations of up to 5 m within a week, and a 1929 record shows 20 days of daily fluctuations of 1–3 m. These wet wood and mobilize invertebrates.
5. **Fish food web:** Endemic fishes likely depended heavily on terrestrial food imported by tributary floods and retained in eddies. Humpback chub stomachs contained Mormon crickets and ants, and Valdez and Ryel (1995) found ants more often than drift frequencies predicted.
6. **Adaptive traits:** The authors argue endemic fishes were opportunistic feeders, could withstand food shortage, could store energy (possibly the hump as a fat store), moved among tributary mouths, and gorged when food arrived.
7. **Loss of carbon:** Dam construction reduced the wood supply and therefore the carbon available to the Grand Canyon reach.
8. **Predictions and research gaps:** The authors predict a bimodal distribution of stomach fullness in fish populations and recommend museum collection analysis, captive studies and comparisons with unregulated reaches (Cataract Canyon, lower Yampa River).
9. **Management implications:** Future flow regimes should be guided by conceptual models of both pre- and post-dam systems, grounded in precise knowledge of flow and sediment processes, and supported by monitoring that accounts for uncertainty.
