## Executive Summary
This 1989 field study by Marsh and Brooks investigated the fate of hatchery-reared juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) reintroduced into the Gila River, Arizona. The research demonstrated that predation by two non-native ictalurids, [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), was intense enough to severely limit short-term survival of stocked fish. Estimated losses reached up to 900 individuals/km within a 2-day post-stocking window during autumn releases. Predation was reduced when stocked fish were larger and when water temperatures were colder (winter), suggesting that stocking larger, cold-acclimated razorback suckers may improve reintroduction success. The authors conclude that self-sustaining re-establishment of this endangered species is unlikely in stream reaches where non-native piscivores are abundant, absent active predator control or stream renovation.

## Background & Description
**Razorback Sucker (Stocked Juveniles):**
- 1984 cohort: 82–168 mm SL (mean 113 ± 19 mm, n=25); ~5,000 fish stocked
- 1985 cohort: 45–142 mm SL (mean 68 ± 17 mm, n=100); ~25,875 fish stocked
- 1986 cohort: 73–156 mm SL (mean 92 ± 19 mm, n=83); 4,600 fish stocked
- All reared at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (NM); 1986 fish were grown from fry at AZGFD Page Springs Hatchery in raceways for 16 days prior to stocking (no lotic acclimation observed)

**Channel Catfish (Predators, >150 mm SL):**
- Size range across years: 150–423 mm SL; means 212–267 mm SL (NS between years)
- Estimated 1985 population density: 281 fish/ha (99% CI: 99–330); biomass 56.5 kg/ha

**Flathead Catfish (Predators, >100 mm SL):**
- Size range across years: 100–640 mm SL; means 287–376 mm SL (NS between years)
- Estimated 1985 population density: 41 fish/ha (95% CI: 29–71); biomass 20.7 kg/ha
- Rarely exceed 750 mm SL in the upper Gila River; specimens >500 mm documented consuming carp over half their own body length

**Predator-Prey Size Relationship:**
- Positive correlation between flathead catfish SL and length of ingested razorback sucker prey: y = 0.16x + 22.88 (r² = 0.76, n = 14, P < 0.10)
- A 750 mm flathead catfish could consume razorback suckers up to ~130 mm SL
- Razorback suckers ~300 mm SL predicted to be largely immune to predation except by the largest flathead catfish

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**Study Site:** 10-km reach of the [[Gila River]] (Colorado River drainage) near Clifton, Greenlee Co., Arizona
- Stream width: 12–22 m; mean depth 0.5–0.9 m; discharge 1.1–4.0 m³/s
- Current velocity: ~0.2 m/s
- Substrate: predominantly sand and gravel with coarser material in shallow runs/short riffles
- Pools rare; sparse aquatic and riparian vegetation

**Fish Community Composition:**
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*, non-native): >80% of total fishes captured
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*, native): up to 10%
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) and [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*, natives): ~0.1% each
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*, non-native): ~1.0%
- Channel and flathead catfish were the only large piscivores present, despite being a minor proportion of overall fish abundance

**Trophic Role of Ictalurids:**
- Both catfish species are established non-native piscivores across western North America
- Flathead catfish diet dominated by fish prey (including red shiner and channel catfish) even prior to razorback sucker stockings
- Channel catfish diet more omnivorous, including aquatic insects (Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, Megaloptera, Coleoptera, Diptera), plant material, algae, and detritus
- Post-stocking, both species showed sharply elevated piscivory: 55% (channel catfish) and 90% (flathead catfish) of stomachs contained razorback suckers in 1985 — far exceeding typical regional frequencies of fish prey in catfish diets (usually <40%)

## Key Findings
- **Extreme, opportunistic predation surge:** Sudden mass availability of naive, disoriented, hatchery-stocked razorback suckers triggered unusually intense feeding by resident ictalurids, comparable to documented predation surges on stocked walleye, striped bass, and Colorado squawfish elsewhere.
- **Seasonal effect on predation intensity:** Winter (January) predation was ~1/6 that of autumn, attributed to reduced channel catfish feeding activity at colder temperatures; flathead catfish remained comparatively active feeders year-round.
- **Size-refuge effect:** Larger stocked razorback suckers (113 mm mean in 1984) experienced somewhat lower predation than smaller fish (68 mm mean in 1985), supporting a size-based predation refuge.
- **Catch composition:** Channel catfish stomachs (1985): 55.2% contained *Xyrauchen texanus* (mean 3.8 ± 3.7 fish/stomach, 84.9% of biomass); flathead catfish stomachs: 90.0% contained *Xyrauchen texanus* (mean 2.2 ± 1.4 fish/stomach, 95.3% of biomass).
- **Population-level loss estimates:** Extrapolated catfish consumption could have eliminated the entire 1984 stocking (5,000 fish) within 6 km of river, the 1985 stocking (25,875 fish) within ~20 km, and the 1986 winter stocking (4,600 fish) only after >37 km — still an unfavorable outcome for reintroduction efficacy.
- **Long-term reintroduction outcome:** Of >700,000 juvenile razorback suckers stocked into the Gila River system since 1981, only 16 were recovered by April 1988 more than a few days post-stocking, strongly implicating predation as the principal driver of poor survival.
- **Management recommendations:** (1) Stock larger juveniles (~300 mm SL) to exceed the size vulnerable to most catfish predators; (2) stock in late winter to exploit reduced catfish feeding activity while allowing acclimation before spring growth resumption; (3) recognize that self-sustaining razorback sucker populations are unlikely to establish in reaches with abundant non-native piscivores, absent stream renovation (removal of non-native predatory fishes) as the most robust long-term solution.
- **Corroborating evidence:** Prior work (Marsh and Langhorst, 1988) showed razorback sucker larvae are also highly vulnerable to centrarchid predation, and that the species exhibits high survival to adulthood and reproduction in predator-free habitats, reinforcing predation (not habitat suitability) as the primary bottleneck to reintroduction success.
