# Management Plan for the Aquatic Habitats of San Bernardino Ranch, Douglas, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinidae #conservation

## Executive Summary

This document is the second draft (October 1980) of a management plan for the aquatic habitats of the [[San Bernardino Ranch]], a 2,432.5-acre desert property in Cochise County, Arizona, on the international border with Mexico. The ranch sits at the headwaters of the [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage. Its springs, ponds, cienegas (marshy wetlands) and [[Black Draw]] stream support a group of native Rio Yaqui fishes that are now rare or extirpated in the United States. The plan sets three objectives: protect habitat for native fish, halt the erosion of Black Draw, and restore cienega habitat. It also lists prioritized tasks with cost estimates. The draft was sent to Dr. W.L. Minckley for comment before finalization. The historical and archaeological sections were removed because a separate draft covered them.

## Background & Description

**Location and history.** The ranch lies 18 miles east of Douglas, Arizona. Jesuit priests named the site in 1763 as a watering place for Apache Indians. Ignacio Perez received a 73,240-acre land grant in 1822, but the ranch was abandoned because of Apache raids. The [[Gadsden Purchase]] of 1854 split the ranch between Arizona and Sonora, leaving roughly one fourth of the property in the United States. "Texas" John Slaughter bought it in 1884. The [[The Nature Conservancy]] (TNC) acquired it in 1979 from landowner Paul Ramsower under an agreement with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS).

**Water resources.** Water was the resource that drew settlers to the site. An 1887 earthquake is credited with forming additional sources, and Slaughter's early-1890s wells created most of the present artesian flows. The ranch has at least six springs and ten artesian wells, with water rights dating to 1926. Water quality is excellent and total dissolved solids are low. Groundwater depth varies from under 30 feet at the border to 600 feet or more in the north. Groundwater moves toward the valley center and then south into Mexico.

**Climate and hydrology.** Average precipitation on the ranch is 12.4 inches per year. Almost half the annual total falls in July and August, and only 7% falls in April through June. The Black Draw watershed covers 421 square miles and averages about 6,700 acre-feet of runoff annually. Black Draw is the only stream on the ranch with permanent flow. None of the U.S. streams are classed as perennial.

**Vegetation and wildlife.** Chihuahuan desert scrub covers about 1,875 acres. Other types include irrigated pasture (250 acres), mesquite bosque (250 acres) and riparian/aquatic habitat (125 acres). Mule deer and javelina are unusually abundant, based on track counts rather than population surveys. A compiled list by Douglas Danforth records 124 species from eight one-day trips between October 1973 and July 1978.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fish fauna.** The original aquatic fauna included six native Rio Yaqui fishes: [[Yaqui Topminnow]], [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Yaqui Sucker]], [[Yaqui Shiner]], [[Yaqui Catfish]] and Mexican stoneroller. Longfin dace and roundtail chub, which are native to the Colorado River system, were also found in Black Draw. Of the Rio Yaqui species, three remain in the United States and only two are on the ranch. The sucker, catfish and shiner have been extirpated from the United States. The topminnow is federally listed as endangered. The chub, catfish and shiner are proposed for listing. Arizona protects the six Rio Yaqui species.

**Molluscs.** Two endemic molluscs occur in the San Bernardino waters: a springsnail (*Fontelicella* sp.), which is doing well in several western springs, and a clam (*Anodonta dejecta*), which may now be extinct on the ranch.

**Exotic species.** [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) are the main threat. Minckley (1973) identifies them as the principal cause of topminnow decline. Mosquitofish were first reported from Black Draw in 1980. They have gained access to most western springs but not to eastern springs. Other exotics found in [[House Pond]] in 1980 were largemouth bass, green sunfish, bluegill, black crappie and black bullhead.

**Habitat degradation.** Grazing removed riparian vegetation, which accelerated streambed downcutting and produced intermittent flow. Cienegas were "grazed down and plowed up" during more than 100 years of ranching. Historic bosques (cottonwood-willow marshes fed by springs) are described as among the most rapidly disappearing habitats in the Southwest. The plan notes that no undisturbed [[Mesquite Bosque]] habitat is known to remain in the southwestern United States.

## Key Findings

**Objectives.** The plan has three goals:
1. Protect and manage aquatic habitats for the perpetuation of native fish.
2. Halt the erosional cycle of Black Draw and restore it to a stable stream with permanent flow.
3. Restore cienega habitats for native flora and fauna.

**East and west spring systems.** The plan divides the springs into two systems separated by Black Draw. This separation is a deliberate safety measure to keep exotics out of the eastern springs.

- *East system:* Pipe Spring, Middle Well and Cottonwood Well together give an estimated combined flow of 90 gpm. Pipe Spring flowed about 50 gpm in February 1980. Middle Well reached about 25 gpm in September 1980. Cottonwood Well flows at about 15 gpm. The plan recommends canals at least 3 m wide and 2 m deep to channel these flows, along with periodic dredging. North Pond, a diked 38 × 30 m pond with a 1.5 m maximum depth, was stocked with Yaqui topminnows in March 1980 and is now abundant. Border Springs flow at about 20 gpm and currently hold topminnows. No change is planned there because of possible water commitments in Mexico.
- *West system:* House Pond (about 2.5 acres, 2 m maximum depth, about 50 gpm spring flow) was partially drained in February 1980 to eradicate exotics. Mosquitofish persisted, and a September 1980 report documented enormous numbers. The plan recommends a second drawdown and ichthyocide treatment, followed by stocking of five native species. Tule Spring now flows at less than 1 gpm into a sedge-choked pool. Twin Ponds dried after a pumped well was removed in January 1980.

**Black Draw.** Downcutting began for unknown reasons, and Austin Spring north of the ranch went dry in 1968. Permanent flow has returned to the lower 2 km of Black Draw over the past two years. This flow occurs because the cut reached a semipermeable organic layer, but lateral erosion continues. Flow was about 1 cfs in February 1980 and extended into Mexico. Three fish species live there: topminnow, mosquitofish and longfin dace. The authors predict that topminnows could be fully replaced by mosquitofish within one year.

**Cienega construction.** The plan identifies five former cienega fields and gives four reasons for recreating them: stable fish habitat, groundwater recharge that blocks exotic fish from off-ranch, suppression of mesquite encroachment, and preservation of unique habitat. Two cienegas are recommended in the initial phase: House Pond (using terraces) and Pipe (using spreaders with 24-inch dikes). Twin Ponds, Tule and North cienegas depend on water availability.

**Water use.** Total valley water use is under 1,300 acre-feet per year. About 160 acres were irrigated in 1977, all adjacent to Black Draw within two miles of the border. The plan states no major increase in use is foreseen.

**Priority tasks and costs (Table 1).** Costs are estimates. A caretaker, estimated by TNC at about $10,000 per year, is not included. Tasks marked "1st" are those in the first year. The first-priority tasks are:

| Priority | Task | Estimated Cost | Lead Agency |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-A | Secure stocks of Rio Yaqui fish for reintroduction | $25,000 | FWS |
| 1-B | Eliminate exotic fish and prevent reestablishment | $5,000 | FWS, Ariz. G&F |
| 2-A | Black Draw erosion plan | $5,000 | SCS, TNC |
| 2-B | Ranch map (elevations, wells, springs, vegetation) | $5,000 | FWS, TNC |
| 2-C | Hydrologic survey (aquifer and spring flows) | $10,000 | FWS |

Later tasks include implementing the Black Draw plan, developing pool and dike construction plans ($10,000 and $30,000), restocking native fish, and surveying upland vegetation and animals ($20,000). Several tasks are marked as partially completed. The "Lead Agency" designations are suggestions and do not commit any agency to action.
