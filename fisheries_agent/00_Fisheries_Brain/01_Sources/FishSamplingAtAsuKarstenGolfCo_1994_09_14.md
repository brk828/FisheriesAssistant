# Fish Sampling at ASU Karsten Golf Course, September 1994

## Executive Summary
In September 1994, Arizona State University's Center for Environmental Studies conducted a fish survey at the ASU Karsten Golf Course (ASUGC) lakes to evaluate the status of three federally significant native fish species — [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] — previously stocked into the lakes. Using trammel and experimental gill nets set overnight in North and South Lakes, researchers captured 42 individuals representing six **non-native** species. No native stocked species were captured, though researchers believe this reflects reduced catchability due to high water temperatures rather than absence of the species from the lakes.

## Background & Description
- **Study site:** ASU Karsten Golf Course (ASUGC), Tempe, Arizona — consists of constructed North Lake and South Lake water hazards used as holding/refuge habitat for endangered native fish.
- **Stocking history:**
  - September–October 1988: [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] were all stocked into ASUGC lakes.
  - April 1991: [[Bonytail]] were re-stocked.
  - All fish originated from the **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Research Center**, Dexter, New Mexico — a key captive propagation facility for endangered Colorado River basin fishes.
- **Sampling methods:**
  - Nets set overnight on 8 September 1994, retrieved ~11 hours later on 9 September.
  - **North Lake:** two 125-ft experimental gill nets + one 300-ft trammel net.
  - **South Lake:** one 125-ft experimental gill net + one 150-ft trammel net.
- **Purpose:** Assess persistence/status of the three endangered native species stocked in prior years using standard entanglement gear (gill and trammel nets).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Water temperatures were not directly measured, but regional daytime air temperatures in Tempe were reported between **100–105°F**, suggesting correspondingly elevated water temperatures in the shallow golf course lakes.
- High water temperatures likely reduced fish activity levels, decreasing the probability of capture in passive entanglement gear (gill/trammel nets), which depend on fish movement to become ensnared.
- This thermal stress is offered as the primary explanation for the notably **lower total catch** compared to previous ASUGC surveys, rather than an indication of population decline or extirpation.
- The lakes support a community dominated by non-native, warmwater-tolerant species typical of Southwestern reservoir and pond habitats, including [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Common Carp]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Yellow Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Green Sunfish]] — species that may compete with or prey upon young native fish.
- Presence of a diverse non-native assemblage alongside historically stocked endangered natives highlights the challenge of maintaining refuge populations of imperiled Colorado River basin fishes in non-natural, human-made habitats.

## Key Findings
- **Total catch:** 42 individual fish, representing **6 non-native species**; **zero native species** ([[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]]) were captured.

- **Catch magnitude:** Considerably lower than catches recorded in past ASUGC surveys.
- **Interpretation:** Researchers concluded that native species (bonytail, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker) likely still occupy the ASUGC lakes despite zero captures, attributing the absence in catch data to suppressed fish activity from high water temperatures rather than population loss.
- **Follow-up:** A subsequent survey was scheduled for winter 1994, presumably to re-assess native species status under cooler, more favorable sampling conditions.
