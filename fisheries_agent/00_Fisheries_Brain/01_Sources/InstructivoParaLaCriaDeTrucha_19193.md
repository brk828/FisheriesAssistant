# Trout Culture in Mexico: A 1962 Instructional Guide to Rearing Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 instructional manual, issued by the [[Instituto Nacional de Investigaciones Biológico Pesquera]] and the [[Dirección General de Pesca]], explains how to raise trout in Mexico. It covers site selection, water chemistry, the biology of the two cultivable trout species, hatchery design, spawning and egg handling, fry rearing, artificial feeding, disease control, fertilization of ponds, stocking density, transport, and legal protection of aquatic resources. Its main focus is [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), the species the Dirección General de Pesca distributed through its extension service. [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) is treated secondarily, and [[Lake Trout]] (*Cristivomer namaycush*) is mentioned as a species under study for introduction.

## Background & Description

- **Scope and definition.** The manual uses "trucha" only for cold-water fish of the family [[Salmonidae]], since the word is often applied to unrelated fish.
- **Rainbow Trout.** Naturally distributed from the Bering Sea to Mexico, in Durango, Sinaloa and Chihuahua, in rivers including the Casas Grandes, Verde, Sinaloa and Culiacán. Repopulation extended its range to Chiapas, Chihuahua, Durango, Hidalgo, Jalisco, México, Michoacán, Morelos, Nuevo León, Puebla, Querétaro, Veracruz, Tamaulipas, Tlaxcala and Guerrero. It usually measures about 40 cm; the largest reported fish was 1 m long and weighed 9.5 kg, caught at [[Presa Brockman]] in El Oro, Estado de México.
- **Brook Trout.** Native range runs from Saskatchewan and Labrador, Canada, to the headwaters of the Savannah River in Georgia, USA. It averages about 25 cm and is said to be less adaptable than rainbow trout in Mexico.
- **Sexual dimorphism.** Males are slimmer, with a slightly hooked lower jaw and an ovoid, pale genital pore. Females have a fuller abdomen and a round, reddish, turgid pore. Mature eggs are about 0.5 cm in diameter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and water quality**

- Trout need cold, clean, oxygenated water. Optimal temperatures are 5–13 °C. Rainbow trout tolerate water near 25 °C, but trout prefer cooler conditions.
- Dissolved oxygen: 10–11 ppm is ideal. Discomfort begins below 7 ppm, and fish can survive down to 5 ppm, especially in running water.
- pH: optimal range 6.7–8. Slightly alkaline waters support more fish than acidic waters.
- CO₂ below 2 ppm is considered normal. Higher values indicate pollution and oxygen deficiency.
- Bicarbonates range from 5 to 200 ppm. Waters with calcium above 65 ppm ("hard waters") are more productive, and trout grow faster in them. Zinc, copper and lead are lethal.
- Muddy or silty bottoms are unsuitable. Trout need sand and gravel to spawn. Eggs are lost when covered by mud or detritus. Basalt, tepetate and sand are less productive than silt or detritus-rich bottoms, according to work at Cornell University.

**Biotic environment**

- Natural diet includes aquatic and terrestrial insects, larvae, mollusks, crustaceans and small fish. Algae are also common in stomach contents.
- Riparian forests of oak, conifers and grasses stabilize slopes, limit erosion, and keep water clear. Clearing for crops causes turbidity during rains and reduces trout abundance.
- Predators and competitors include carp and suckers (which destroy nests), water beetles, turtles, herons, kingfishers, otters, bears and raccoons.

**Parasites and disease**

- Parasites and diseases are considered more severe in hatcheries than in the wild. No epizootics of economic importance had been recorded in Mexican hatcheries.
- Major pathogens listed include [[Chondrococcus columnaris]], [[Aeromonas salmonicida]] (furunculosis), [[Saprolegnia parasitica]], [[Dermocystidium salmonis]], [[Ceratomyxa shasta]] and the copepod [[Salmincola edwardsii]]. No human transmission was reported for trout diseases.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction and growth**

- Mexican rainbow trout spawn from 19 November to the end of February. At [[El Zarco Fish Station]], most fish spawned between 5 November and 5 January.
- Females of about 1.5 kg produced 1,500–2,000 eggs. At El Zarco in recent years, females produced 2,000–3,000 eggs, with a maximum of 5,000 and a minimum of 750.
- Graphs from El Zarco report N = 155 females with 172,768 eggs in 1959, and N = 118 females with 220,000 eggs in 1960.
- Growth is temperature-dependent. At 9 °C trout grew about 1 inch per month; at 14 °C they grew less than ¼ inch per month. Feeding becomes difficult above 15 °C.
- Brook trout mature at two years and spawn from September to late November in native areas, or October to mid-December in the Mexican variety. At El Zarco, females produced 135–600 eggs at weights of 50–150 g. US averages were 400–600 eggs, ranging from 150 to 2,600.

**Egg incubation**

- At El Zarco (8–9 °C), brook trout eggs hatched in 56 and 49 days (443 and 441 degree-days). At 10 °C hatching took about 50 days.
- Rainbow trout fry hatch in 30–35 days at 8–10 °C. Each 1 °C drop delays hatching by 10 days, so at 2 °C hatching takes 95–100 days.
- Incubation should occur in darkness. Fish incubated in direct light were more photonegative, more vulnerable to mortality, and showed convulsive, febrile-like activity.

**Hatchery operations**

- A typical station includes broodstock ponds, incubation troughs, and fry (alevin) ponds. El Zarco's incubation troughs are 4 m long, 30 cm wide and 20 cm deep, fed by gravity.
- Broodstock were selected for growth, health, body color, fecundity, reproductive timing and environmental adaptation. Females reach maturity at two years and males often at one year.
- Eggs are stripped by hand. The manual warns against excessive pressure and stresses that fertilization must occur before water is added.

**Stocking densities and rearing**

- Recommended fry densities in incubation and fry tanks: 10,000 per m² (1 per cm²), though up to 30,000 are sometimes used. After one month, numbers are reduced by one-third; at four months, when fish are 7 cm, density is halved; later the target is 1,000 per m².
- An outdoor rectangular pond of 1.20 × 12 m, 25–30 cm deep, with about 100 L/min flow, can hold up to 6,000 fry.
- Circular ponds can hold up to 20,000 trout (about 190 kg total, averaging 9–10 g each).
- El Zarco's outdoor circular tanks are 6 m in diameter. Rectangular tanks are 6 × 1.64 m and 50 cm deep, using about 21 L/min each.
- Weisner's commercial stocking guidelines range from 2–6 fry per m² in unfertilized, unfed ponds to 25–100 per m² in intensively fed artificial tanks.
- Huet's classes of water richness set stocking densities from 0.5–1.5 fry per m² in poor waters, to 2–3 in medium waters, and 3.5–5 in rich waters.

**Artificial feeding**

- Fry start on coagulated blood sieved through water and salted at 3 g/L. After two weeks, spleen or liver pulp is introduced. Daphnia can supplement the diet.
- Beef liver is the closest to an ideal food for fry. Mixed diets are preferred, with meals of 10–20% to start, up to 60% of the diet overall.
- A standard US mixture includes 20% each of alfalfa meal, wheat germ, tomato meal and brewer's yeast, plus 10% algae meal and 10% salt.
- Feeding rations decline with cold: Huet's table gives roughly 2% of body weight at the lowest temperature band, rising to 7–8% at 15–18 °C. Fry are fed four times daily, and adults one to four times daily.

**Disease control**

- Early steps: isolate infected fish and sick ponds, then either disinfect and drain the pond or call the Dirección General de Pesca's technical staff.
- Treatments listed include 3% salt baths for protozoan and fungal infections, copper sulfate (1:2,000) for 1–2 minutes for bacterial infections, and formalin (about 40 cc per 100 L) for parasites.
- Other recommended controls: periodic disinfection with chlorination or liming, independent water supplies, and isolating infected fish.

**Pond fertilization**

- Inorganic fertilization works best in standing water. Lime (CaCO₃) must be applied first if soil is acidic. The manual gives application rates: about 3,750–7,500 kg/ha at pH 1.0–4.0, falling to 875–1,000 kg/ha at pH 6.0–6.5.
- Common formulas include 125 kg of 6-8-4 with 12 kg potassium nitrate, 50 kg ammonium sulfate, 75 kg 16% superphosphate, 6 kg potassium chloride and 19 kg finely ground lime per hectare.
- Organic fertilizers (manure, meals, straw) are applied at 800–1,250 kg per hectare per season. One tonne of manure is equivalent to 50 kg of 10-5-10 fertilizer.

**Transport and stocking**

- Stocking should use fry 7–10 cm long, in warm seasons, where temperatures and predators are controlled.
- Fish population size can be estimated by mark-recapture: divide marked fish by recaptured marked fish, then multiply by total catch at recapture.
- For 1,000 trout of 5–7 cm, Huet recommends 200–400 L at 8–10 °C. For 1,000 fry with absorbed yolk sacs, 7–20 L is adequate. For 100 kg of market-size trout (200–800 g each), 1,000–1,500 L are needed.
- Oxygen-filled plastic bags allowed El Zarco to keep trout alive for up to eight days.
- Release should occur in clear, calm water at matching temperature. Narcotized fish require two or three water changes before release.

**Ecosystem management and conclusions**

- Undesirable fish can be removed with [[Rotenone]], as practiced in the United States.
- Excess aquatic vegetation is removed mechanically or chemically, or shaded out by fertilizer-driven plankton growth.
- The manual urges protection of watersheds through reforestation, grass cover, and farmer education. It also recommends fish passages for dams.
- Its closing perspective is that practically all high-elevation regions of Mexico's mountain systems are suitable for trout culture, and that a broad plan could rehabilitate degraded areas into sport-fishing and recreation centers.
