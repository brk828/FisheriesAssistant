## Executive Summary
This study by Marsh and Douglas (1997) quantifies predation by five introduced piscivorous fish species — [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Black Bullhead]], and [[Yellow Bullhead]] — on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and other native fishes in the Little Colorado River (LCR), Grand Canyon, Arizona. Across 408 examined digestive tracts collected monthly over four years, native species comprised 13.7% of prey items among stomachs containing food, with Humpback Chub identified in 3.2% of all stomachs examined. Diets overall were low in diversity, dominated by algae (*Cladophora*), aquatic insects, and fish. The authors project that predation, especially by [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], could substantially affect Humpback Chub population viability by both limiting juvenile recruitment and increasing adult mortality, given population-level extrapolations suggesting thousands of chub could be consumed annually.

## Background & Description
Data are presented as body-size (total length, TL) relationships between predators and prey rather than detailed anatomical description:

- **Rainbow Trout** (N=174 examined; 10 with fish prey): mean TL ~354 mm (range 210–491 mm); those with Humpback Chub prey averaged 332–398 mm TL.
- **Channel Catfish** (N=202 examined; 10 with fish prey groups reported): mean TL ~286 mm (range 37–796 mm); catfish that had eaten fish averaged ~500–554 mm TL, significantly larger than non-fish-eating conspecifics.
- **Brown Trout**: mean TL 397 mm (range 296–566 mm).
- **Black Bullhead**: mean TL 158 mm (range 70–228 mm).
- **Yellow Bullhead**: mean TL 166 mm (range 75–212 mm).

**Prey size data:**
- Humpback Chub found in predator stomachs: mean 102 mm TL (N=27); ranged from 40–45 mm (in trout/bullhead) up to 85–200 mm TL (in Channel Catfish).
- Other ingested native/nonnative fishes averaged 92 mm TL (N=74).
- No significant difference in mean length between ingested Humpback Chub and other prey fish (two-sample t-test).
- No larval or early post-larval fish were recovered from any stomach, attributed to rapid digestion of this fragile life stage.

Pharyngeal tooth counts were used to discriminate among cyprinid prey species when soft tissue was too digested for direct identification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The study characterizes the LCR as a naturally functioning tributary of the [[Colorado River]] within Grand Canyon, retaining hydrologic characteristics historically thought to favor native fishes over introduced taxa ([[Minckley and Meffe 1987]]). Key ecological findings:

- **Predator community**: [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Channel Catfish]] are common in the lower LCR; [[Brown Trout]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], and [[Striped Bass]] are uncommon.
- **Diet composition** (frequency of occurrence):
  - Rainbow Trout: algae/*Cladophora* dominant (47%), plus Simuliidae (19%), Chironomidae (14%), Gammarus (12%), and fish (~9% total across native taxa).
  - Channel Catfish: broadly omnivorous — detritus, vegetation, aquatic insects, algae (10%), and fish (~10% combined across species), consistent with prior characterizations as opportunistic omnivores.
  - Brown Trout: terrestrial invertebrates and fish (20% each), fish eggs (10%).
  - Black Bullhead: detritus (8%), aquatic insects (notably Chironomidae, 90%), and fish including Humpback Chub (17%).
  - Yellow Bullhead: detritus and Odonata naiads (10% each), fish (20%).
- **Empty stomach rates** ranged from 34% to 70% across predator species, indicating food limitation in the system.
- **Native prey species identified**: Humpback Chub, Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), Flannelmouth Sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*), Bluehead Sucker (*Pantosteus discobolus*); nonnative prey included Common Carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) and Fathead Minnow (*Pimephales promelas*).
- The LCR supports the largest extant population of Humpback Chub among the four Colorado River basin rivers where the species persists ([[Kaeding and Zimmerman 1983]]; [[Douglas and Marsh 1996]]).

## Key Findings
- **Humpback Chub predation incidence**: Identified in 13 of 408 stomachs (3.2%) overall; native fishes collectively comprised 13.7% frequency of occurrence among 219 stomachs containing food.
- **By predator species**:
  - Rainbow Trout: 3 of 174 stomachs contained Humpback Chub (40–45 mm prey); 22 total had eaten some fish.
  - Channel Catfish: 8 of 202 stomachs contained Humpback Chub, with individual catfish consuming 1–7 chub each (prey 85–200 mm TL) — the largest chub and largest prey sizes recorded among predators.
  - Black Bullhead: 2 of 12 stomachs contained Humpback Chub (49–55 mm TL).
  - Brown Trout and Yellow Bullhead: no Humpback Chub found, but other native (Speckled Dace) and unidentified fish remains present.
- **Predator size and piscivory link**: Channel Catfish that had eaten fish averaged significantly larger body size (~500 mm TL) than non-fish-eating conspecifics, corroborating findings from [[Tyus and Nikirk 1990]] in the Green and Yampa rivers.
- **Population-level risk estimate**: Assuming ~3% of Rainbow Trout/Channel Catfish consume an average of 2.3 Humpback Chub per meal weekly, a predator population of 1,000 individuals could consume an estimated 3,588 Humpback Chub annually — a potentially significant toll given adult Humpback Chub population estimates of only 4,508–10,444 in the LCR ([[Douglas and Marsh 1996]]).
- **Recruitment and mortality implications**: Predation targets primarily juveniles but also affects larger individuals (up to 200 mm TL), suggesting impacts on both recruitment (via juvenile removal) and total adult mortality.
- **Comparative diet consistency**: Findings align qualitatively with prior Colorado River basin studies ([[Minckley 1973]], [[Minckley 1982]], [[Marsh 1981]], [[AZGFD 1987]], [[Tyus and Nikirk 1990]]) confirming Channel Catfish as opportunistic omnivores with fish forming only a minor diet fraction overall.
- **Conservation implication**: Despite the LCR's relatively "natural" hydrologic regime — historically considered advantageous to native fish over introduced species — predation pressure from nonnative piscivores may still substantially constrain native fish populations, reinforcing the need for management intervention targeting introduced species in [[Humpback Chub]] recovery planning (USFWS 1990).
