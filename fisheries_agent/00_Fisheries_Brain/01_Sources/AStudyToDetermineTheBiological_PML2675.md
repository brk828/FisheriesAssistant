# Biological Feasibility of PIT Tags for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Colorado River Fishery Project report (February 1990) evaluates whether [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]]s (PIT tags) are biologically compatible with and practical for marking endangered fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. Studies ran from 1987 to 1989 across laboratory tanks and field ponds. Recovery of fish was very poor, so the sample sizes were too small for statistical analysis. Within those limits, tags were retained and verified in 100% of recovered fish, wounds healed, and no migration or swelling was observed. The report concludes that PIT tags appear biologically compatible with the species tested, but that pond sites were unsuitable and further controlled studies are needed.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Existing marks ([[Carlin Tag]]s, Floy anchor tags, coded-wire tags, fin clipping) all carry risks. Carlin tags may cause infections, and coded-wire tags require sacrificing the fish.
- **PIT tag:** A glass-encapsulated, passive transponder about 11 mm long and 2.1 mm in diameter (weight under 0.05 g), each with a unique code (about 34 billion possible). It is read in vivo by an external exciter/reader placed several centimeters from the fish, with no anesthesia needed.
- **Drawbacks noted:** Internal implantation carries risk, the reader costs about $1,750, and tags cost $6.00 each (later stated as $3.75 to $6.00 depending on lot size).
- **Prior use:** PIT tags had been used on [[Pacific Salmon]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] brood fish with promising results. The study generally followed the protocol developed for Pacific salmon.
- **Objectives:** Identify the best implantation sites and procedure, train workers, determine tag retention, tissue response, effects on growth, survival and wound healing, reader accuracy, and durability of the detector system in field use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites**

- **[[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]]** (Colorado): controlled tanks for roundtail chub (abandoned after losses to *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*) and juvenile Colorado squawfish.
- **Pond sites** (field portion): Gardner Pond and 13th-Hole Pond (Tiara Rado Golf Course, roundtail chub), West Pond (Colorado squawfish), and Humphrey Pond (razorback sucker). Gardner, Humphrey and 13th-Hole are gravel-pit or golf-course ponds near the Colorado River.
- **Grand Junction station:** recirculating tanks for juvenile Colorado squawfish. Abandoned after all fish died when water reached 27 °C.
- **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]** (New Mexico): principal endangered-fish brood facility, where 164 adult Colorado squawfish and 527 juvenile and adult razorback sucker were tagged in September 1989.

**Winter water quality**

- January 1989 dissolved oxygen was adequate in all ponds.
- February declined in two ponds: Gardner Pond reached 3 mg/L (9 Feb) and Humphrey Pond reached 2 mg/L (9 and 24 Feb).
- West Pond had a winterkill in 1988 (about 25 dead Colorado squawfish), linked to high hydrogen sulfide and a high incidence of Asian tapeworm. In 1989 no winterkill was found.
- Other fish in Gardner Pond included [[Common Carp]], green sunfish, black bullhead and fathead minnow, which appeared to overwinter well.

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- **Tagging sites:** body cavity (about 10 mm anterior to the pelvic girdle, 5 mm lateral to the mid-ventral line; for fish under 165 mm, just posterior to the pectoral fin) and dorsal musculature (5–10 mm below and posterior to the dorsal fin origin).
- Fish were anesthetized with MS-222 and tagged with a modified hypodermic needle. Tags and needles were disinfected with 70% isopropyl alcohol. Control fish received a pelvic-fin mark.
- Tag verification used a hand-held Identification Devices HS-0033 reader.

**Sample sizes and outcomes**

| Group | Initial numbers | Key outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Roundtail chub, Gardner Pond | 25 control and 25 per test group | No recaptures on day 133; 3 fish recaptured on day 430 |
| Roundtail chub, 13th-Hole Pond | 25 per group | 14 recaptured on day 114, 11 on day 360, none on day 437 |
| Colorado squawfish, West Pond | 20 per group (60 total) | 12 (20%) of the 60 recovered after rotenone on day 310 |
| Razorback sucker, Gardner Pond | 6 per group | 8 died before release; none of 10 recaptured in four samplings |
| Razorback sucker, Humphrey Pond | 3 tagged | Tags verified on day 163 |
| Colorado squawfish, Hotchkiss NFH | 38 control, 37 test | 34 tagged fish remained at day 70; 100% retention |

- **Mark-recapture check (West Pond):** 47 fin-clipped Colorado squawfish were angled, marked and released. Only 12 (26%) were recovered, suggesting high post-handling mortality.

**Results**

- **Tag retention and verification:** 100% for all recovered fish. The scanner usually read tags with one pass.
- **Wound healing:** Healing began by day 2. Body-cavity wounds were less visible than dorsal-muscle wounds. By the end, all wounds had healed to a barely noticeable scar.
- **Tissue response:** Tags stayed in place. Body-cavity tags were found near the pyloric caeca or adipose tissue; dorsal tags were no more than 5 mm from insertion. No swelling or edema was observed.
- **Hatchery result:** At Hotchkiss, 85% of tagged Colorado squawfish 120–165 mm (11–28 g) had only a small scar by day 70. The report concludes these fish can be successfully tagged.
- **Limitations:** Survival, growth and wound-healing comparisons were not statistically testable. Roundtail chub mortality was similar across control and test groups, so handling and capture likely caused much of the mortality.

**Conclusions**

- PIT tags appear biologically compatible with roundtail chub, razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish, and the scanner is reliable. Evidence is limited by poor recovery.
- Pond sites are not recommended for future PIT-tag evaluation. Controlled facilities, such as Dexter NFH, are needed.
- PIT tags are costly and may suit small lots (individual wild fish, brood fish for genetics management) better than batch marking. Tags from senescent brood fish could be reused.
- Carlin tags should continue in use, but they need controlled laboratory evaluation for retention and attachment materials.
- No single tag is best. Other systems (visible implant tags, acrylic dyes, Floy tags) should be compared at Dexter NFH from fall 1989 onward.
- Manufacturer improvements suggested: an optional longer power cord, a metal male connector, and a more compact hand-held scanner.
