# Southwestern Willow Flycatcher Final Listing Rule (Federal Register, 27 February 1995)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tyrannidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service issued a final rule listing the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. The rule takes effect 29 March 1995. The Service found that the subspecies is restricted to dense, mostly riparian woodland in the arid Southwest and has suffered extensive habitat loss and modification, compounded by [[Brown-Headed Cowbird]] brood parasitism and small, isolated populations. Designation of [[Critical Habitat]] was deferred for up to one year, pending further review. The document combines the rule itself with a long summary of public comments and the Service's responses.

## Background & Description

- **Morphology:** A small bird, about 15 cm long, with grayish-green back and wings, whitish throat, light olive breast, pale yellowish belly, and two visible wingbars. The eye ring is thin or absent.
- **Vocalization:** The song is a sneezy "fitz-bew" or "fitz-a-bew"; the call is a repeated "whit."
- **Taxonomy:** A subspecies of the willow flycatcher within the genus *Empidonax* (Tyrannidae). The willow and [[Alder Flycatcher]] were once treated as one species, the "Traill's flycatcher." The Service accepted *E. t. extimus* as a valid taxon, noting that the taxonomy of the willow flycatcher complex remains debated. Five subspecies are generally recognized.
- **Range:** Breeds in southern California, southern Nevada, southern Utah, Arizona, New Mexico and western Texas, with possible but unconfirmed breeding in southwestern Colorado and sparse records in northwestern Mexico.
- **Nesting:** Builds a compact cup nest in a fork or horizontal branch about 1.5 to 4.5 m above ground, in dense shrubs or small trees. Breeding territories are occupied by mid-May; nests are built and eggs laid from mid-May to early June; young fledge in early to mid-July.
- **Diet:** Insectivorous, foraging within and above dense riparian vegetation by hawking or gleaning. Specific prey species are unknown.
- **Wintering:** Winter grounds are believed to be Mexico, Central America and perhaps northern South America, but habitat use there is largely unknown.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Breeding habitat consists of dense thickets of [[Willow]] (*Salix* spp.), [[Buttonbush]], [[Tamarisk]] and [[Russian Olive]], often with scattered [[Cottonwood]] (*Populus* spp.). Patches are typically small and linear, separated by arid lands. The subspecies nests almost always near surface water or saturated soil, and the water table must be near enough to the surface to support riparian vegetation. Patches of 0.5 ha supported one or two pairs in one study, and the Service estimated a minimum patch size of 1 to 1.5 ha.
- **Habitat loss:** Dahl (1990) estimated wetland losses in the Southwest since the 1780s at 91% for California, 52% for Nevada, 30% for Utah, 36% for Arizona, 33% for New Mexico and 52% for Texas. The Service accepted that up to 90% of southwestern riparian ecosystems have been lost or modified, with near-total loss in major lowland desert systems.
- **Tamarisk:** The species nests in tamarisk, but at lower densities and apparently with lower success than in native vegetation. The Service regards tamarisk as an invasive exotic, not a successional stage.
- **Water development:** Dams, diversions and channelization have altered riparian systems. Glen Canyon Dam created tamarisk habitat in the Grand Canyon, but flycatchers there nest in low numbers with poor success, while Lake Powell inundated formerly superior habitat.
- **Livestock grazing:** Overuse by livestock is a major cause of riparian degradation. The Service recognized that some grazing regimes may be compatible with habitat, and that the effect depends on intensity, season and local conditions.
- **Brown-headed cowbird:** Cowbirds lay eggs in the nests of other songbirds. Parasitism can reach very high levels; the Service cited 50% in Grand Canyon averaged over 1982–1987 and 100% in 1993.
- **Predators and other threats:** Snakes, hawks, ravens, grackles and domestic cats are potential predators. Floods, fire, recreation, mosquito control, pesticides and urban development were also considered.

## Key Findings

- **Listing decision:** The Service determined the subspecies is endangered; the threatened classification was rejected because large historic habitat loss has already caused extirpation across a significant portion of its range, population numbers are extremely low, and threats are serious and imminent.
- **Five listing factors:** Habitat loss and modification (Factor A) is the principal threat. Overutilization (Factor B) was not identified. Disease (Factor C) was not significant, though predation may be increasing. Existing regulatory mechanisms (Factor D) were judged inadequate; the [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]] is the only current federal protection. Other factors (Factor E) include stochastic floods, cowbird parasitism and genetic isolation.
- **Population estimate:** The total population was estimated at about 300 to 500 pairs (Unitt 1987 suggested well under 1,000). California had about 70 pairs plus a single male; the Service's 1994 survey estimate in Arizona was about 70 to 80 breeding pairs at 12 locations, with cowbird parasitism at at least six (50%) of them.
- **Cowbird effects:** High parasitism rates were documented in California (50–80%) and the Grand Canyon (50–100%). Cowbird control was identified as a primary recovery need.
- **Public comment:** The Service received 3,102 written comments (294 supporting, 2,650 opposing, 188 neutral) and 62 oral or written comments at hearings. Six public hearings drew about 424 attendees.
- **Critical habitat deferred:** Critical habitat was proposed in the 1993 rule (about 600 miles of riparian zones in California, Arizona and New Mexico). It was deferred until 23 July 1995 because of substantial scientific disagreement and the lack of economic information. The Service reopened a 60-day comment period.
- **Section 9 and 7 guidance:** Activities such as dispersed recreation that do not disrupt breeding, and federally approved projects that avoid habitat destruction, would not constitute take. Livestock grazing causing direct destruction of riparian habitat or facilitating cowbird parasitism, and unauthorized fill or alteration of wetlands, would.
- **Recovery:** Recovery planning should address cowbird control, habitat protection and restoration, and the needs of single unmated birds. Federal agencies must consult under section 7 for actions that may affect the species.
- **Conclusion:** The Service determined that the southwestern willow flycatcher meets the definition of an endangered species and listed it as such, effective 29 March 1995.
