# Causes and Controls of Freshwater Drum Mortality During Transportation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aplodinotus

## Executive Summary

This 1982 study examines why live [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) die after being hauled from [[Lake Erie]] to pay-fishing ponds, and which measures reduce that loss. The species is abundant in Lake Erie but underused, with only about 15% of its 11.3-million-kilogram maximum sustainable yield harvested each year. Live hauling causes high delayed mortality, reported as 94% over 1 to 2 weeks after 6-hour transport, with only 4% dying during transport itself.

Of the treatments tested, only [[Sodium Chloride]] (5 g/liter) consistently reduced mortality. Lower [[Stocking Density]] (60 g fish/liter) and late-summer hauling also helped. Tagging and capture stress in the [[Commercial Shore Seine]] were more important sources of death than transport or pond stocking. The authors conclude that healthy, active fish selected from small seine catches, hauled in salt water at about 60 g/liter, should give economically acceptable survival.

## Background & Description

The freshwater drum is described as abundant but underutilized in Lake Erie. About 1% of Ohio's 1979 commercial shore-seine catch of drums was sold to [[Fish Transportation Companies]] for stocking fee-fishing lakes; most of the remainder went to animal food. The authors note that most live transport occurred in April because mortality was higher in later months. Reducing transport mortality was expected to improve the market for the species.

Mortality during the transport of live fish is a long-recognized problem in hatchery and stocking operations. Prior work points to several management tools:

- **Water quality control:** low fish densities, aeration or agitation, and control of oxygen, [[Carbon Dioxide]], and [[Ammonia]] accumulation (Brockway 1950; Randall 1970).
- **Temperature and anesthetics:** cooler water or anesthetics such as [[MS-222]] (tricaine methane sulfonate) can reduce excitability (Blahm 1961; Meehan and Revet 1962; Randall 1962; Lloyd and Orr 1969; Anderson and Lewis 1974).
- **Salts, buffers, and prophylactics:** physiologically active salts (especially NaCl), buffers, disease treatments, and tempering before stocking have improved survival (McFarland and Norris 1958; Collins and Hulsey 1963; Wedemeyer 1972).

The authors note that few of these methods work consistently across species, and none had been tested on hauled freshwater drums.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study fish:** Freshwater drums averaged 365 mm total length (range 305 to 435 mm). They were obtained from commercial shore-seine fishermen in [[Sandusky Bay]], southwestern Lake Erie.

**Holding and transport sites:** Fish were hauled 135 km to two Ohio Department of Natural Resources hatchery ponds in Akron, Ohio. Each pond covered about 0.25 hectares with a mean depth of 1.0 m (maximum 1.5 m). A separate 3-hectare pond 10 km from the seining beach, with a mean depth of 1.8 m, a hard clay bottom, groundwater supply, high turbidity, and no aquatic vegetation, was used in the stress-source experiment.

**Transport conditions:** Transport tanks were epoxy-sealed plywood units (61 × 91 × 69 cm) with a working capacity of 227 liters. Water was oxygenated by lid agitators or by bubbled oxygen through perforated latex tubing.

**Water quality:** Measured ranges were similar across sites, and none appeared detrimental to survival:

## Key Findings

### 1. Overall mortality under transport

- Freshwater drums transported 6 hours had **4% immediate mortality** and **94% delayed mortality** over 1 to 2 weeks.
- Across the 1976 and 1977 trials, [[Analysis of Variance]] on square-root-transformed data showed that main effects and two-way interactions explained only 37% (immediate) and 45% (delayed) of variability in 1976. In 1977 they explained 70% (immediate) and 80% (delayed).

### 2. Chemical and mechanical treatments (Table 1 summary)

- **1976 trials (six main treatments in a factorial design):** only NaCl (5 g/liter) significantly reduced both immediate and delayed mortality below control values. Delayed mortality was 80% with NaCl versus 100% in controls.
- **Interaction effects in 1976:** the combination of [[Malachite Green]] and [[Oxytetracycline]] significantly reduced delayed mortality (P < 0.049). Bubbled oxygen with NaCl came close to significance (P < 0.051).
- **1977 trials:** NaCl again reduced delayed mortality (82% versus 100% in controls). Bubbled oxygen combined with NaCl significantly reduced immediate mortality.
- **Adverse combination:** CaCl₂ plus Na₂HPO₄ significantly increased both immediate and delayed mortality.

### 3. Density and season

- **Stocking density (1977):** delayed mortality was 70% at 60 g/liter and 96% at 120 g/liter. Immediate mortality was 4% and 7%, respectively.
- **Season (1977):** delayed mortality was 94% in spring/June and 81% in summer/late July. Mortality was lower in late July and August than in June.

### 4. Tagging effects

- Jaw-tagged fish had significantly higher delayed mortality than untagged controls: **43%** (range 42 to 44%) versus **12%** (range 7 to 17%) across two replicates.

### 5. Sources of mortality

- Immediate mortality did not differ significantly among non-transported, 1-hour, and 6-hour groups (mean 1.4% ± 2.8 SD).
- Delayed mortality declined with transport time, and the treatments differed as follows:
  - Held in Sandusky Bay (no transport): **61.2% ± 31.9**
  - Transported 1 hour to the pond: **48.4% ± 20.7**
  - Transported 6 hours, returned to Sandusky Bay: **41.5% ± 25.9**
  - Transported 6 hours to the pond: **33.1% ± 20.7**
- Transported fish (1 or 6 hours) had significantly lower delayed mortality than non-transported fish (P < 0.01). Pond-held fish also fared better than fish placed back in source water (P < 0.01).

### 6. Physiological and behavioral observations

- Blood Na⁺ and Cl⁻ did not change within 2 hours of capture, but blood pH fell from about 7.0 to 6.6 when fish were crowded in seines (Harman et al. 1980).
- Crowded fish develop subcutaneous reddening, possibly from hemolysis linked to low pH. Salt in transport water reduced this reddening.
- The authors propose that external sodium absorbed through the gills exchanges with H⁺, buffering blood pH. This mechanism may matter more when blood pH is low, as in stressed drums.
- Drums prefer the lower strata of tanks. At 120 g/liter they were forced into less-preferred areas and stayed active, which may sustain [[Lactic Acid]] production and lower blood pH.
- Fish in shore-seine bags showed distress and reddening near the surface, while healthy fish stayed in deeper water. Oxygen in the seine bag may be low when catches are large.

### 7. Conclusions and recommendations

- **Capture and handling** were identified as the dominant causes of mortality. The low survival of fish held in Sandusky Bay without transport, and the lower survival of tagged fish, support this conclusion.
- Transport in 5 g/liter NaCl improved survival over immediate return to the water of origin.
- Successful hauling depends on small seine catches, with healthy, active fish selected over exhausted, reddened ones.
- Transport at about 60 g/liter in salt water should produce economically acceptable survival.
- A lower-profile tank with more bottom area might allow higher densities of drums to be moved safely.

### 8. Context from related species

- The authors compare their results with [[Crappie]] mortality in pound nets, where fish density in the net was important (Mitzner 1969).
- [[Muskellunge]] captured by seine showed a greater physiological response than fish that were finclipped and trucked (Miles et al. 1974).
