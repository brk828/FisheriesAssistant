# Cui-ui (Chasmistes cujus) Recovery Plan: Second Revision

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan (second revision, 1991) addresses the [[Cui-ui]], a large, long-lived lakesucker endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada, listed as endangered in 1967. The species was driven to decline mainly by reduced [[Truckee River]] inflow caused by upstream storage and diversion, with [[Derby Dam]] and the [[Newlands Project]] as the largest diverters. The revision replaces earlier goals with a quantifiable objective: a 0.95 probability of persisting 200 years, equivalent to about 100,000 acre-feet/year of added Truckee River inflow, for delisting, and a 0.85 probability (about 60,000 acre-feet/year) for reclassification to threatened. Recovery is projected as possible by 2012 if criteria are met. The plan relies on water acquisition, lower river and delta habitat rehabilitation, fish passage improvements, hatchery support, and monitoring, with estimated program costs of about $40.67 million (1992 to 2012).

## Background & Description

Cui-ui belong to the genus *Chasmistes*, distinguished within Catostomidae by thin, lobed lips and a large terminal, oblique mouth. Of the four recognized species, cui-ui is the only one suspected not to have hybridized extensively with syntopic catostomids. It is a large, robust sucker, weighing up to 3.5 kg, with females documented exceeding 700 mm and males reaching 662 mm. Breeding males develop tubercles on the anal and caudal fins.

The genus has a Miocene fossil record across the western United States, while cui-ui fossils are restricted to the [[Lahontan Basin]] and are Pleistocene in age. The species was historically present across ancient Lake Lahontan and in [[Winnemucca Lake]] until that lake dried in the 1930s. Today it is restricted to Pyramid Lake and the lower Truckee River.

Life history highlights:
- **Spawning:** Obligate stream spawners. Adults migrate from the lake into the lower Truckee River, usually April to May, and most migrate less than 9.7 km. Females broadcast eggs over gravel, and spawners return to the lake and do not re-enter the river that year.
- **Fecundity:** 25,000 to 186,000 eggs for 430 mm to 657 mm females.
- **Growth and longevity:** Both sexes live 40+ years; females generally live longer and grow faster after maturity (ages 6 to 12).
- **Diet:** Larvae feed mainly on zooplankton and chironomid larvae; adults mainly on zooplankton (cladocerans, copepods).
- **Population:** Tagging suggested 90,000 to 200,000 prespawning adults (1982 to 1986). Recent estimates put the population at about 300,000 adults (age 9+) plus one to several million juveniles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pyramid Lake** is a saline (>4.1 ppt), alkaline (pH 9.1 to 9.3), oligotrophic to mesotrophic, monomictic terminal lake. It stratifies from about May until December, with thermocline depth of 11 to 23 m. Adults and juveniles use the littoral zone at 18 to 31 m for much of the year. Lake elevation was 1159.3 m in November 1990, and lake TDS rose from about 3,500 mg/L to over 5,000 mg/L since 1905, reaching about 5,400 mg/L in 1991 according to the plan's appendices.

**Lower Truckee River** is a low-gradient, braided, unstable channel with a broad delta at the mouth. Marble Bluff Dam acts as a hydraulic control. Water quality is sensitive to nutrients, sediment, temperature and dissolved oxygen. Recorded ranges at Nixon (1968 to 1989) include temperature 0.5 to 29.6 °C (mean 13.2), dissolved oxygen 6.0 to 15.5 mg/L (mean 11.0), and total dissolved phosphate 0.002 to 0.45 mg/L (mean 0.13).

**Predators and threats:** Lahontan redside shiners eat eggs and emergent larvae; tui chub and Lahontan cutthroat trout prey on larvae and yearlings; white pelicans and cormorants attack adults in the river. Key threats include reduced flows, passage barriers, elevated temperatures, sediment loading, nutrient inputs, and toxic spill risk.

**Egg temperature:** Optimal egg hatch occurs at 14.4 to 17.2 °C, and survival falls sharply above 17.2 °C.

## Key Findings

**Reasons for listing**
- Reduced Truckee River inflow from upstream storage and diversion was the primary cause of decline. Extirpation of [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] from Pyramid Lake in the 1940s was seen as a warning sign.
- Only three year classes (1942, 1946, 1950) were recorded before 1966. In 1983, a single year class (1969) made up 97% of the spawning run.

**Recovery objectives (Section II)**
- **Delisting:** 0.95 probability of persistence for 200 years, equivalent to about 100,000 acre-feet/year of added inflow, acquired at a minimum of 5,000 acre-feet/year. Delisting could take 20 years if begun by 1994.
- **Reclassification:** 0.85 probability of persistence for 200 years, equivalent to about 60,000 acre-feet/year, at 5,000 acre-feet/year minimum. Reclassification could take 12 years if begun by 1994.
- Recovery could be possible by 2012 if all criteria are met.

**Population modeling (Appendices B and C)**
- A [[Cui-ui Model]] tracks females, the limiting sex, using a Leslie-type matrix driven by stochastic hydrology (200 traces from 89 years of data, 1901 to 1989).
- Best-estimate larval survival is 0.002 (range 0.001 to 0.003). Supplemental water needed for 95% persistence is 45,000 to 115,000 acre-feet, with about 70,000 acre-feet as the best estimate for delisting. For 85% persistence, about 40,000 acre-feet is needed for reclassification.
- The model tends to overestimate population numbers because it ignores density dependence and year-to-year variation.

**Conservation measures (Appendix D)**
- Water rights purchases in the Truckee Meadows, Sierra Valley, Truckee Division of the Newlands Project and lower basin, estimated at $75 to 195 million.
- Reducing Newlands Project demand by 40,000 to 100,000 acre-feet, with OCAP refinement.
- Lower river channel rehabilitation, which could reduce May/June flow needs by about 300 cfs, and delta passage improvement (lowering the passage threshold from 3,812 to 3,800 feet).
- Recoupment of about 800,000 acre-feet diverted above project entitlement from 1973 to 1985.

**Implementation**
- Total estimated cost is about $40.67 million (in thousands of dollars, 1992 to 2012, with about $29.25 million in Need 1, the water acquisition and flow component). The implementation schedule is organized by priority levels and two phases: reclassification by increasing spawning flows, then long-term habitat security for delisting.
- The Stampede Reservoir flow regime (minimum managed spawning flow of 1,000 cfs in May and June, with a 60,000 acre-foot attraction volume) is the current operational guide.
- Funding for the eight-year population dynamics research was not assured beyond FY 1992.

**Other key points**
- Translocation to other river systems is rejected. The plan states recovery can only occur within the Truckee River-Pyramid Lake system.
- A [[Hatchery Operations]] program at the David Koch Cui-ui Hatchery maintains a refuge stock. Earlier stocking added millions of larvae, but their recruitment is unknown.
- Genetic analysis (allozymes, 18 putative hybrids) found no evidence of hybridization with Tahoe suckers. Heterozygosity is very low, and the 1950 year class is predicted to hold the most variability.
- The [[Truckee-Carson-Pyramid Lake Settlement Act]] (P.L. 101-618, 1990) is a major future conservation vehicle.
