# Solving Sacramento River Salmon Problems with Radio-Tagged Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Outdoor California article, written by [[Dick Hallock]] of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (DFG), describes a radio-tagging study of adult [[Chinook Salmon]] migrating past [[Red Bluff Diversion Dam]] on the upper [[Sacramento River]]. The study asks whether the dam delays or injures migrating salmon, prevents successful spawning, or causes fish to fall back and skew counts. It is a cooperative effort with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (then the U.S. Water and Power Resources Service), which owns the dam and supplied the tags and detection equipment. The article covers the background problem, the tag and receiver technology, the salmon runs involved, and the tagging procedure. It does not report study results.

## Background & Description

**Study authors and context.** Hallock is a marine biologist with the DFG's [[Anadromous Fisheries Branch]], stationed at Red Bluff. Field personnel named in the photo captions include Bret Rohrer, Bill Holtz, Bob Marsh and Tom Vallier.

**Study history.** In 1975 the DFG launched a cooperative study with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to determine whether the dam harmed [[Fingerling Salmon]] moving downstream. The radio-tagging study, aimed at adult salmon passage, was initiated in 1979.

**Dam description.** The article describes Red Bluff Diversion Dam as having gates through which the river flows under, rather than over, the dam. During floods the gates are raised completely out of the water. Two [[Fishway]] entrances are present, one on the east side and one on the west side.

**Radio tags.** The tags are small battery-powered transmitters with the following characteristics:
- Signal frequency of approximately 40 MHz (range 40.600 to 40.690 MHz), the [[Federal Communications Commission]]-assigned frequency for wildlife tracking.
- Ten frequencies provide ten identifiable tag codes. Additional tags within each code are distinguished by different pulse rates.
- Minimum transmitting life of 60 days.
- A sealed plastic capsule about ¼ inch in diameter, with a 4-inch wire antenna at one end.
- Weight of about one ounce in water.
- Detection range depends strongly on [[Water Conductivity]]: higher conductivity shortens range. In the upper Sacramento, signals were detectable out to a fraction of a mile from shore or boat (the exact figure is partly illegible in the scan).

**Tracking equipment.** Two portable receiver types are used:
- A non-directional search receiver that scans all 10 tag channels at once.
- A directional receiver with digital frequency control, hand-tuned by the operator to each channel in turn.

Both run on 12-volt DC power and can be converted to 110-volt AC. A stationary [[Data Logger]] also scans all 10 channels and records tag signals, including pulse-rate variants, on paper together with the time of reception.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**The problem.** When Red Bluff Diversion Dam was completed in 1966, 96 percent of the salmon in the upper Sacramento River system spawned upstream of Red Bluff each fall. By 1979 that proportion had fallen to 48 percent. The article stresses that this was not just a case of total numbers holding steady while more fish spawned downstream. The increase downstream was too small to account for the loss above the dam. The spawning center had shifted downstream, and overall spawning and production had declined.

**Competing explanations.** The author acknowledges that pollution, water temperature, fluctuating flows and habitat deterioration could all contribute. Biologists focus on the dam, however, because the changes closely track its operation. If the dam is the cause, the problem could involve juveniles migrating seaward past the dam, adults moving upstream past the dam, or both.

**Salmon population.** About 139,000 salmon now spawn in the upper Sacramento River system each year, of which 97,000 spawn upstream of Red Bluff. A continuous stream of chinook migrates into the upper river, and spawning occurs in every month of the year. Four distinct [[Salmon Runs]] are recognized:
- [[Fall Run Chinook]]
- [[Late Fall Run Chinook]]
- [[Winter Run Chinook]]
- [[Spring Run Chinook]]

**Study sites.** Fish for tagging come from two sources: the trapping facility built into the east bank fishway at Red Bluff Diversion Dam, and a large wire-mesh [[Fyke Trap]] fished in the Sacramento River two miles below Red Bluff. All tagged fish are released at the fyke-trap site, regardless of capture location.

## Key Findings

The article is a program description and does not report data or outcomes. Its main points are:

1. **Core research questions.** Does Red Bluff Diversion Dam impede upstream salmon migration, cause injury or delay, prevent successful spawning, drive fish to spawn downstream, or cause fallback that produces inaccurate fish counts?

2. **Scale of the decline.** The share of upper Sacramento salmon spawning above Red Bluff fell from 96 percent (after dam completion in 1966) to 48 percent by 1979, with overall spawning and production also declining.

3. **Study design.** Radio tags are used to track adults during upstream migration, complementing an earlier DFG, Bureau of Reclamation and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service study of downstream fingerling passage that began in 1975.

4. **Proportional sampling.** Because the study covers all four runs (fall, late fall, winter and spring), the available tags were allocated to each run in proportion to its relative size.

5. **Tagging method.** Tags are placed in the stomach because salmon stop feeding after leaving the ocean. Fish are anesthetized, placed on their backs in a tagging cradle, and fitted with a glycerine-coated tag. The antenna protrudes from the esophagus and is attached to the roof of the mouth. Fish are out of water for about 30 seconds during tagging.

6. **Monitoring infrastructure.** Tracking combines handheld directional and non-directional receivers with a stationary data logger on the dam, which records tag signals with timestamps.

7. **Open questions.** The article notes that factors other than the dam (pollution, temperature, flows and habitat) may contribute to the decline, so the radio-tagging results are intended to isolate the dam's effect. No results are reported in this article.
