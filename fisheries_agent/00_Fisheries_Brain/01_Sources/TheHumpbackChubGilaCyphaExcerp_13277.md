# The Humpback Chub, *Gila cypha*

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha* Miller, 1946) is a highly distinctive, endemic cyprinid minnow restricted to the swift, turbulent canyon reaches of the [[Colorado River]] basin. Described from specimens collected in the Grand Canyon, it is notable for its bizarre morphology — including a pronounced nuchal hump, flattened head, and fleshy snout — which are hypothesized to be adaptations to high-velocity, turbulent river environments. Historically distributed throughout the larger canyon systems of the Colorado and Green rivers above Lake Mead, the species has suffered dramatic range contraction and population decline, primarily attributed to the construction and operation of large mainstem dams ([[Flaming Gorge Dam]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]], and possibly [[Hoover Dam]]), habitat alteration from cold tailwater releases, competition/predation from introduced exotic fishes, and hybridization with closely related congeners ([[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]]). By the time of this Recovery Plan excerpt, remaining populations were fragmented among five disjunct locales, with the [[Little Colorado River]] serving as a critical thermal refugium for the Grand Canyon population.

## Background & Description

- **Maximum length:** ~45 cm
- **Body form:** Streamlined body; skull concave along the dorsum
- **Nuchal hump:** Nape abruptly rises at the occiput into a truncate, prominent hump, sometimes overhanging the occiput in large adults; hump bears lateral grooves extending posteriorly
- **Head:** Flattened, concave posteriorly; long, fleshy, overhanging snout; small eyes
- **Caudal peduncle:** Thin, pencil-like but not greatly elongated; peduncle length/head length ratio < 1.0; head length/peduncle length ratio < 5.0 — intermediate in depth between the narrow-peduncled [[Bonytail Chub]] and the more robust [[Roundtail Chub]]
- **Squamation:** Often incomplete or scales deeply embedded, especially on the hump
- **Fins:** Large and falcate (sickle-shaped); dorsal fin origin roughly equidistant between snout tip and caudal fin base
- **Fin ray counts:** Dorsal rays typically 9; anal rays typically 10 (occasionally 11)
- **Pharyngeal arch:** Small, with a short lower ramus; teeth usually formula 2,5-4,2
- **Mouth:** Inferior, overhung by the snout — suggestive of bottom-feeding morphology
- **Coloration:** Olivaceous to brownish dorsally, silvery on sides and belly; breeding males show slight dorsal tubercles and orange splashes near paired fins (paralleling [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]])

### Diagnostic Features vs. Congeners
Distinguished from *G. elegans* (bonytail) and *G. robusta* (roundtail) by:
1. Prominent grooved nuchal hump
2. Flat head with fleshy snout and small eyes
3. Dorsal rays typically 9, anal rays typically 10
4. Intermediate caudal peduncle depth

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Associations**
- Historically occupied larger, swift-water canyons of the Colorado and Green rivers above [[Lake Mead]], including the [[Yampa River]] and [[White River]] tributaries
- Strongly associated with fast currents and/or deep channel habitats at the macrohabitat scale
- Microhabitat use (Holden 1977, Desolation/Gray Canyons) revealed more nuanced patterns:
  - **Young-of-year and juveniles:** prefer low-current areas, silt substrate, 0.3–1.0 m depth
  - **Adults:** use varied habitats, generally over sand substrate, with little preference for depth or velocity

**Reproduction**
- Inferred spawning temperature ~18°C, based on congeners ([[Roundtail Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]])
- Likely spawning period: May–June (Green River system) to June–July (Grand Canyon area)
- Breeding males develop slight dorsal tubercles and orange coloration near paired fins

**Growth**
- Young-of-year (September, Desolation Canyon): 30–70 mm
- Juveniles (age I–II): 70–150 mm
- Growth exceeds that of sympatric young [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] in comparable study areas, likely due to earlier spawning timing

**Feeding Ecology**
- Subterminal/inferior mouth suggests benthic (bottom) feeding, though untested via stomach content analysis in natural settings
- Field observations show surface feeding in Desolation Canyon and near-surface netting elsewhere, suggesting the species may function as a surface feeder, akin to the [[Bonytail Chub]]
- Specimens below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] fed primarily on planktonic crustaceans originating from [[Lake Powell]] — likely an artifact of reservoir influence rather than a natural diet

**Refugia**
- The [[Little Colorado River]] provides critical thermal refuge for the Grand Canyon population against cold, fluctuating mainstem conditions caused by Glen Canyon Dam tailwater releases

## Key Findings

**Distribution (Historical vs. Present)**
- Historical range: larger canyon reaches of the Colorado and Green rivers above Lake Mead, plus the Yampa and White rivers
- Present range (5 documented locales):
  1. Green River — Desolation and Gray Canyons (likely largest population)
  2. Green River — Dinosaur National Monument
  3. Yampa River — Dinosaur National Monument (rare, restricted reaches)
  4. Colorado River — Palisade, CO to Black Rocks (Colorado-Utah border)
  5. Colorado River — Marble/Grand Canyons (River Mile 27–108) and Little Colorado River (up to 13 km upstream)
- Distribution within all extant areas is sporadic, concentrated in small subareas
- Extirpated from the Green River above the Yampa confluence following [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure (1962)
- No longer collected in [[Lake Powell]] despite early post-closure abundance
- Not found in recent surveys of the Colorado River near Moab, Utah

**Reasons for Population Decline**
1. **Dam construction and operation** — Flaming Gorge and Glen Canyon dams (and possibly Hoover Dam) created cold tailwaters and impoundments that eliminated humpback chub from significant historical habitat
2. **Fish eradication programs** — Pre-Flaming Gorge Dam chemical treatment of the Green River likely affected Dinosaur National Monument populations (undocumented due to lack of pre-eradication baseline data)
3. **Flow alteration** — Reduced flows downstream of dams may degrade spawning/rearing habitat via altered river hydraulics
4. **Exotic species introductions** — Competition and/or predation from non-native fishes introduced throughout the Colorado basin
5. **Hybridization** — Frequent hybridization with [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] threatens genetic integrity, especially in small, isolated populations; debate exists over whether hybridization predates or follows major dam-driven habitat alteration

**Conservation Implications**
- Small, isolated, and fragmented populations face elevated risk from genetic swamping via hybridization
- The [[Little Colorado River]] refugium underscores the importance of tributary habitats in buffering mainstem dam impacts
- Life history data remain largely extrapolated from closely related [[Gila (genus)|Gila]] species due to absence of direct study on *G. cypha* reproduction and diet
