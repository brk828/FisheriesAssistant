# Maricopa Audubon Society Letter on San Pedro River Water Policy

#ichthyology #conservation #cyprinidae #catostomidae #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This December 1993 letter from the [[Maricopa Audubon Society]] to the [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]] (ADWR) addresses concerns about a water policy change permitting subdivision development in the upper [[San Pedro River]] basin. The letter documents the ecological significance of the [[San Pedro River]] as the last living river in the Southwest, supporting 489 species including rare and imperiled fish species ([[Gila Chub]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]]). The organization challenges ADWR's September 29, 1993 determination of water "adequacy" for future development, citing hydrological evidence that groundwater overdrafting already captures 20-40% of well water from the riparian area itself and threatens the river's survival.

## Background & Description

The [[San Pedro River]] is characterized as the most extensive surviving expanse of cottonwood/willow gallery or broadleaf riparian association forest in North America. Congress established the [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] in 1988 to protect this ecosystem. The river supports 489 species of birds, mammals, fish, amphibians, and reptiles, with 24 species requiring federal and/or state protection.

Fish species of particular conservation concern include:
- [[Gila Chub]]
- [[Desert Sucker]]
- [[Longfin Dace]]
- [[Spikedace]]
- [[Loach Minnow]]

Other rare species mentioned include [[Southwest Willow Flycatcher]], [[Yellow-billed Cuckoo]], [[Green Kingfisher]], [[Gray Hawk]], [[Northern Beardless Tyrannulet]], [[Sanborne's Long-nosed Bat]], and [[Mexican Long-tongued Bat]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The primary threat to the [[San Pedro River]] ecosystem is groundwater overdrafting from the San Pedro basin aquifer. Hydrological models from the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]], and [[University of Arizona]] indicate that 20-40% of well water from the [[Sierra Vista]] sub-basin watershed is already being captured from the riparian area of the river itself.

Historical warnings document the overdrafting problem:
- August 1977: [[U.S. Department of Agriculture]] and [[Arizona Water Commission]] warned that withdrawal exceeded recharge in the Sierra Vista-Fort Huachuca area
- 1981: [[Council on Environmental Quality]] warned that groundwater overdraft was the major desertification force and could exhaust the aquifer by 2020, potentially causing the river to run dry like the [[Santa Cruz River]]

Recent hydrographic evidence shows declining river flows and a cone of depression in the basin's aquifer. Wells near the river no longer show dropping water levels like those farther away, indicating water is being captured directly from the river rather than from the aquifer.

## Key Findings

At the October 28, 1993 meeting of the [[Upper San Pedro Technical Committee]] in Sierra Vista, three consensus points emerged from hydrological experts:

1. **Current Impact**: The [[San Pedro River]] is presently being significantly and negatively affected by ongoing groundwater pumping in the [[Sierra Vista]] sub-watershed. Even at current groundwater use levels, the river will not survive unless conservation measures are aggressively pursued. Any additional pumping will exacerbate the precarious situation.

2. **Legal-Hydrological Disconnect**: "Arizona water law does not reflect hydrological reality." ADWR officials acknowledged that groundwater in an aquifer forming stream base flow and surface water are hydrologically connected regardless of court rulings. The net effect on downstream users from groundwater pumping interception and surface water pumping are indistinguishable, resulting in less water in the stream with devastating effects to the riparian area.

3. **Modeling Studies Non-existent**: The "current groundwater modeling studies" cited as the basis for ADWR's September 29, 1993 water policy change on water "adequacy" do not exist.

The letter requests clarification and explanation for ADWR actions that threaten the survival of the upper [[San Pedro River]], challenging the claim that there is adequate groundwater for future subdivision development while protecting the river ecosystem and its rare fish species.
