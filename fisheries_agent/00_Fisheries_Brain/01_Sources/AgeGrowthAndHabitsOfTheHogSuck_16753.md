# Age, Growth, and Habits of the Hog Sucker (*Hypentelium nigricans*) in New York

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1946 study by Raney and Lachner examines the [[Hog Sucker]] (*Hypentelium nigricans*) in New York. It covers habitat and feeding, spawning behavior in May, and growth and maturity estimated from scale annuli. The authors aged 337 specimens from the [[Genesee River]] and [[Catatonk Creek]] systems. Growth is fastest in the first five years, and males slow more than females after that. The largest fish recorded was 14.4 inches (365 mm) from [[Cayuga Lake]].

## Background & Description

- **Range:** Minnesota and Lake of the Woods, the upper [[Mississippi River]] system, east to southern Ontario and New York, south to Georgia, the Gulf slope of Mississippi, southwestern Arkansas, and eastern Oklahoma (after Hubbs and Lagler, 1941).
- **Common names:** Hammerhead, hog molly, hog mullet, crawl-a-bottom, stone lugger, stone toter.
- **Use:** Sometimes caught on hook and line in spring but seldom eaten because of small size and many small bones. Large individuals are used as bait for [[Muskellunge]], [[Northern Pike]], and other large game fish.
- **Diet:** Mainly insect larvae, crustaceans, diatoms, and other minute vegetation. The fish scrapes rubble surfaces, overturns stones, and sucks up ooze.
- **Feeding association:** Reighard (1920) and Greeley (1935) describe [[Shiners]] (*Notropis*) and [[Northern Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu dolomieu*) feeding downstream of hog suckers on insects dislodged as they turn rocks. The hog sucker is also found in the stomachs of smallmouth bass.
- **Behavior:** Not gregarious. It usually rests on the bottom, where black saddles across the back provide disruptive coloration. When disturbed it darts off and swims fast for a short distance.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Both sexes have [[Pearl Organs]] (breeding tubercles). In males they cover all fins, the top of the head, opercle, and body scales. In females they are smaller and absent from the head, body sides, dorsal fin, and lower paired fins. The male anal fin is longer. Sexes are colored alike and do not undergo the breeding color change seen in the [[Common White Sucker]]. Males are more active and aggressive during breeding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat partitioning among three sucker species in the Genesee River** (with the [[Golden Redhorse]] and the Common White Sucker):

- Hog suckers of all ages were most common in riffles and shallower pool margins.
- Common white suckers preferred shallow pool areas.
- Golden redhorse were restricted to deeper pools and were gregarious, in schools of 5 to 8. Five large individuals were captured within seconds in turbid water after a storm.
- All three use riffles for spawning in spring and occasionally feed there.

**Spawning environment:**

- Usually in shallow riffles in May at the latitude of New York. Ripe males were collected as early as April 19 at 60°F.
- Males are sluggish below 50°F and nearly motionless at 41°F.
- Females enter riffles mainly to spawn and otherwise stay in nearby pools.
- Hog suckers sometimes spawn at the shallow edges of pools, as observed in the [[Ithaca]] region.

**Lake occurrence:** Occasionally found in lakes, usually near stream mouths. Lake fish grew much faster than stream fish of the same year class. The authors note that hog suckers are seldom found in lakes, unlike the common white sucker.

**Sampling methods:** Specimens were collected by [[Electrofishing]] (with a screen electrode and dip nets) and seining, as part of a New York State Conservation Department bass salvage operation. Electrofishing was judged to give a representative sample. Seining in pools missed many hog suckers, which hug the bottom near rocks.

## Key Findings

### Reproduction and Spawning

- Spawning occurs in May in New York. The authors describe spawning behavior that is strongly similar to earlier accounts.
- At [[Newfield Creek]] on May 27, 1938 (observed by Dr. W. J. Hamilton, Jr.), 35 to 40 hog suckers, 6 to 12 inches long, gathered in pool groups of 3, 5, 7, and 12 fish, each with one female.
- The female settled over a sand and gravel patch about three feet in diameter, in 3 to 5 inches of water. Two or three males crowded against her and beat the water with their tails.
- The breeding act lasted about two seconds and was repeated after rests of 4 to 7 minutes.
- Small blacknose dace and creek chub ate the eggs. Hog suckers ignored food, including a dangled earthworm, and showed little concern for an observer a few feet away.
- Eggs were found the next day in single layers (one group of four was the exception) and were collected by disturbing the bottom and drawing a fine wire screen through the roiled water.

### Age Determination

- Ages came from scales of **337** hog suckers: **181** from the Genesee River (August 11–16, 1940) and **154** from Cornell University collections (1928–1939) in the Catatonk Creek area. The text gives 154, but 181 + 154 = 335; the discrepancy is not explained.
- Scale annuli form in the first two weeks of May at mean midday water temperatures of about 56–60°F. By May 20, all fish sampled had formed annuli.
- The annulus cuts across circuli formed the previous summer and fall. It is best seen on the posterior lateral field. Dense fall circuli give a dark band and wide spring/summer circuli give a light band.
- Scale validity was checked with known young-of-year fish from Catatonk Creek.

### Growth

- Catatonk Creek (mean total length, inches) at the end of years 1–4: **2.5, 4.7, 7.0, 8.4**.
- Genesee River, first year: **1.7** inches.
- Genesee River males, ages 2–9 (inches): **4.1, 5.7, 7.4, 9.2, 9.6, 10.5, 10.7, 11.4**.
- Genesee River females, ages 2–9 (inches): **3.8, 5.4, 7.4, 9.4, 10.1, 11.0, 12.6, 12.8**.
- Both sexes grow at similar rates for the first five years. After that, growth slows, more in males than females.
- Catatonk Creek fish may grow slightly faster than Genesee River fish, but the authors say the Genesee fish lacked about 1.5 months of growing season, so the comparison is not strict.
- The growth pattern resembles that of the common white sucker in Muskellunge Lake, Wisconsin (Spoor, 1938). Hog suckers grow more slowly than white suckers from Skaneateles Lake Inlet, New York (Raney and Webster, 1942).

### Maturity and Sex Ratio

- Some males mature at 2 years (about 5.3 in); practically all males are mature at 3 years (7.1 in).
- Most females mature at 3 years (average 6.7 in). Slower-growing females mature at 4 years (8.4 in). These values come only from Catatonk Creek fish.
- Sex ratio in the Genesee River sample was about **1:1** (86 males, 92 females, 3 unsexed).

### Maximum Size and Age

- Oldest: an **11-year-old female** (350 mm) from the Genesee River. Five Genesee fish reached age 9.
- Largest: a female, **365 mm (14.4 in)**, **22 oz**, in its sixth summer, collected in Cayuga Lake near Flat Rock on August 21, 1945 by Dr. Dwight A. Webster.
- A female of **330 mm** from Goodyear Lake was in its seventh summer.
- Goode (1888) reported hog suckers reaching "about two feet," and Kuhne (1939) reported specimens of two pounds. The authors say more precise measurements of large fish are needed.

### Summary Conclusions

1. Spawning occurs in New York in May, usually in riffles, sometimes at pool margins.
2. Scale annuli are reliable age markers, formed in early May at 56–60°F.
3. Growth is rapid to year five, then slows, more in males.
4. Males mature earlier than females, and both sexes are mature by about age 3–4.
