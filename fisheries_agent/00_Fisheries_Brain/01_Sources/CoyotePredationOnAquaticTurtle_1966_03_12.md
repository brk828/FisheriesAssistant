## Executive Summary
This 1966 short communication documents a direct field observation of a coyote (*Canis latrans*) capturing and attacking an aquatic slider turtle (*Pseudemys scripta*, now *Trachemys scripta*) in a shallow gypsum-bottomed lake near [[Cuatro Ciénegas]], Coahuila, México. The account details predation behavior, prey handling, and shoreline evidence of repeated coyote predation on multiple turtle taxa, including [[Pseudemys scripta]] and softshell turtles (*Trionyx* sp.). Notably, the endemic Coahuilan box turtle (*Terrapene coahuila*) — despite being locally abundant and seemingly easy prey — showed no confirmed evidence of successful predation, suggesting morphological defenses (hinged plastron, high-domed carapace) may confer protection against mammalian predators. The observations occurred incidentally during unrelated NSF-funded research (Grant No. GB-2461).

## Background & Description
While the paper is not fish-focused, it provides comparative morphological detail on the aquatic turtle taxa involved in predation:

- **[[Pseudemys scripta]]** (slider turtle): ~1 foot in length in the observed capture; found swimming submerged, parallel to shore in ~25 yards from shoreline; captured in water only ~15 inches deep.
- **[[Trionyx sp.]]** (softshell turtle): Remains found along shoreline; soft, leathery carapace generally considered vulnerable to crushing bite predation.
- **[[Terrapene coahuila]]** (Coahuilan box turtle, endemic to the Cuatro Ciénegas basin): Distinguished by a **hinged plastron** capable of closing fully, and a **highly arched (domed) carapace** — traits hypothesized to impede predator access compared to the flatter-shelled *Pseudemys* and *Trionyx*.
- Predation damage pattern: turtle shell remains were "crushed at the anterior and posterior ends" with visible tooth marks, consistent with a predator biting and crushing the shell margins rather than penetrating the carapace dome.
- A second *T. coahuila* individual (25 Dec 1965) showed carapace/plastron scratching but no shell breakage, despite apparent coyote attack — supporting the shell-defense hypothesis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Shallow desert lake (Cuatro Ciénegas basin, an isolated endemism hotspot) with clear water over soft gypsum mud substrate; shoreline dominated by open white gypsum flats interspersed with sedge clumps.
- **Turtle microhabitat use**: *Pseudemys* observed swimming in open water near shore; *Terrapene coahuila* recorded both in the lake, along the shore, and briefly in open water — indicating a broader habitat tolerance among the three taxa present.
- **Predator behavior**: The coyote exhibited a stalking posture (erect ears, stiff legs) before an active aquatic pursuit, submerging its head/forequarters to seize prey underwater — an unusual foraging strategy for a terrestrial canid, indicating opportunistic exploitation of an aquatic prey base in this arid ecosystem.
- **Prey handling behavior**: Turtle carried to shore, "nudged and rolled" until it withdrew into its shell, then gnawed at the plastron — suggesting coyotes target the plastron (softer, flatter ventral shell) as the point of entry for consumption.
- **Predator sign**: Scats and tracks of coyote found consistently associated with turtle shell remains along the shoreline, and turbid coyote tracks found near an attacked (surviving) box turtle — reinforcing coyote as the primary identified turtle predator at this site.
- **Refuge association**: All turtle remains were found "invariably near clumps of sedges," suggesting sedge patches may serve as either ambush sites for coyotes or refugia where injured/killed turtles end up.

## Key Findings
- Direct observational evidence that coyotes actively hunt and consume aquatic turtles (*Pseudemys scripta*) via underwater pursuit and capture, an unusual foraging mode for a primarily terrestrial carnivore.
- At least 9 additional *Pseudemys* and 2 *Trionyx* sp. shell remains were found along the lakeshore, all bearing tooth marks and crush damage at the anterior/posterior shell margins, indicating recurrent coyote predation pressure on these species at this site.
- Despite apparent local abundance, *Terrapene coahuila* (a range-restricted endemic species) showed **no confirmed predation mortality**, only non-lethal shell scratching in one case — supporting the hypothesis that its hinged plastron and domed carapace are effective anti-predator adaptations against coyotes.
- This paper is an early natural history data point relevant to understanding predator-prey dynamics and possible selective pressures shaping shell morphology in the endemic turtle fauna of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] basin, a globally significant site of aquatic endemism in the Chihuahuan Desert.
