# Systematic Revision of the Hydrobiidae of the Cuatro Ciénegas Basin, Coahuila, Mexico

#malacology #marine-biology #hydrobiidae

*Note on scope: this source is a systematic malacology study of freshwater snails (Gastropoda: Rissoacea), not fish research. Fish are mentioned only as predators and biogeographic context.*

## Executive Summary

This revision by [[Robert Hershler]] describes 12 species in nine genera of hydrobiid snails from the isolated spring system of [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], Coahuila, México. Snails were collected from 103 localities. One new genus ([[Mexistiobia]]) and three new species are described. The six nominal species of [[Mexipyrgus]] are reduced to one variable species, *Mexipyrgus churinceanus*. Contrary to earlier views, no hydrobiid subfamilies are endemic to the basin; all taxa belong to the widely distributed Nymphophilinae or Littoridininae. Five genera and at least nine species are endemic. Phylogenetic analyses suggest a polyphyletic origin for the endemic snails, which may be more recent and local than previously assumed. Four genera are ovoviviparous, and the study interprets anatomical changes associated with this reproductive mode.

## Background & Description

The Cuatro Ciénegas valley (roughly 30 by 40 km) lies in the mideastern [[Chihuahuan Desert]] at about 740 m elevation, bounded by Sierra Madre Orientale peaks. Its endemic biota includes crustaceans, eight to ten endemic fishes ([[Cuatro Ciénegas Fishes]]), two turtles, and hydrobioid snails. The original description by [[Taylor (1966)]] recognized three subfamilies, five genera and 12 species of hydrobioid snails as endemic, but was based mainly on shell, operculum, penis and radula characters, which are known to converge among hydrobioids.

The snails are of interest because:
1. Several taxa have large, sculptured or color-banded shells, unlike most small, smooth hydrobioids.
2. Some taxa may have coevolved with snail-eating [[Cichlid Fishes]] (Vermeij & Covich, 1978).
3. They inhabit a diverse set of spring-fed environments.
4. Differentiated populations among springs offer a "natural laboratory" for evolution.

The author applied [[Soft-Part Anatomy]] (especially the female reproductive system), which had been shown by [[Davis (1979)]] to resolve convergence in the family.

### Environmental Setting
- Annual precipitation under 200 mm; mean annual temperature 23°C; midday summer temperatures above 40°C.
- Springs range from seeps to large springs with pools over 900 m² and depths to 7 m. Types include limnocrenes, rheocrenes, playa lakes, spring-fed pools and marshes.
- Most springs are thermal (to 34°C); cooler springs (14–25°C) also occur. Water is hard and high in sulphates.
- Water tables rise in winter and fall in summer.
- Example: North Spring (pool about 230 m², maximum depth 1.5 m) had headspring temperatures averaging 32.9°C (29.5–34.5°C) across 10 readings in 1981; the downstream hole varied 20.6–37.8°C over four days, against 31–34.5°C at the head.
- Five to seven drainage systems exist, with possible connections via underground flow or rainy-season surface water. Irrigation canals lower spring levels and can link previously isolated populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Microhabitats.** Large springs host aquatic vegetation (sedges, *Nymphaea*, *Chara*, *Utricularia*), copropel or algal-detritus sediments, travertine sand, and banks. Small springs have dark organic mud, fine travertine sand and occasional *Chara* mats.

**Sampling.** Collections used fine hand sieves, travertine picking, vegetation washing, and domestic mops left in springheads for 24 hours to capture groundwater-associated snails. Mops cannot separate true subterranean taxa from downstream washout, so the author notes that a net over groundwater outlets would be superior. Sampling dates ran from September 1978 to December 1981.

**Light and pigment.** Upstream snails in some taxa are pale or unpigmented, possibly because riparian cover mimics subterranean conditions. Downstream snails are darkly pigmented, probably protecting against strong insolation. Blind, unpigmented species (e.g., [[Coahuilix hubbsi]], [[Paludiscala caramba]]) are interpreted as groundwater-adapted.

**Predation.** [[Mexithauma quadripaludium]], [[Nymphophilus minckleyi]] and [[Mexipyrgus churinceanus]] may be nocturnal, avoiding predatory cichlids. Shell banding in *Mexipyrgus* may be cryptic against dark sediment, which matters where cichlids forage. The fragile, loosely coiled shell of [[Cochliopina milleri]] may reflect restriction to cichlid-free waters.

**Biogeography.** The southeastern lobe of the basin contains Rio Grande elements, notably [[Cochliopina riograndensis]] and the cichlid *Cichlasoma cyanoguttatum*. Earlier connections with the Rio Salado de Nadadores, and possibly the Rio Nazas-Aguanaval, are inferred. Fossil *Nymphophilus* occurs about 70 km east, along the Rio Monclova.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomy and species status**
- **Species count:** 12 species in nine genera were treated. One new genus and three new species were described.
- ***Mexipyrgus*:** Six nominal species were reduced to one, *M. churinceanus*. A multivariate analysis of 20 characters from 33 populations found trends of variation that only partly follow drainage. Three races or subspecies were tentatively suggested but not formally named.
- ***Nymphophilus acarinatus* n. sp.:** Smaller (length 4.20 mm), with 4.8 rounded whorls and no peripheral keel. Known from empty shells at Locality 98 and live specimens from Santa Tecla Laguna (Locality 101).
- ***Mexistiobia* n. gen. with *M. manantiali* n. sp.:** Minute (1.20 mm). Resembles *Stiobia nana* of Alabama in shell, but differs in 11 features. Known from the basin and one Durango specimen.
- ***Coahuilix landyei* n. sp.:** Differs from *C. hubbsi* in having one more whorl and a larger size (width to 1.31 mm vs. under 1.0 mm). Both species occur together at six localities.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Shells of female *Nymphophilus minckleyi* were significantly smaller than those of males at all three populations (p < .01), while female *Mexistiobia* were larger than males in two of three populations.

**Endemism and origin**
- No endemic hydrobiid subfamilies exist. Five genera and at least nine species are endemic.
- Three of five endemic genera (*Nymphophilus*, *Mexithauma*, *Mexipyrgus*) are more similar to non-endemic basin genera than to each other, suggesting at least four lineages.
- Pebas Formation (Pliocene, Peru) taxa resembling Cuatro Ciénegas endemics are not hydrobiids (they have a siphonal notch), so the resemblance is convergent.
- The author predicts that [[Paludiscala]] and [[Coahuilix]] will be found outside the basin. Relatives of *Coahuilix* (e.g., *Horatia micra*) occur in Texas artesian wells, and *Orygoceras* (?) sp. was previously known only from a Texas spring.
- The author argues that the low differentiation (12 species; no genus with more than two species) and recent desert origin (the Chihuahuan Desert is about 12,000 years old) favor a recent, local origin for the endemics.

**Ovoviviparity**
- Four genera (*Cochliopina*, *Mexithauma*, *Durangonella*, *Mexipyrgus*) are ovoviviparous. Changes include an enlarged pallial oviduct that brood-pouches embryos, reduced albumen gland, a muscularized anterior brood pouch, and small female gonads.
- Embryo brood size and range: *Cochliopina* (about 11.6 shelled embryos, shell width 0.317–0.475 mm) and *Mexithauma* (19.4 ± 4.5 embryos) brood similar-sized young. *Durangonella* and *Mexipyrgus* brood fewer young with wider size ranges.
- *Mexipyrgus* has the most complex pallial oviduct coiling, with brood-pouch loops in several planes.

**Revised subfamilial and generic placement**
- Littoridininae: *Coahuilix*, *Paludiscala*, *Mexithauma*, *Cochliopina*, *Durangonella*, *Mexipyrgus*.
- Nymphophilinae: *Nymphophilus*, *Mexistiobia*.
- Unplaced (anatomy pending): *Orygoceras* (?) sp.
- Taylor's (1966) subfamilies Mexithaumatinae, Paludiscalinae and Cochliopinae are not supported.

**Conclusion.** The author concludes that the endemic snail fauna is less ancient and more polyphyletic than previously believed. Its diversity reflects recent, local diversification in a dynamic spring system with past outside connections.

**Limitations.** Anatomy was studied for only one species per genus in several cases, and many generic descriptions are provisional. The phylogeny is tentative because female reproductive characters cannot always be confidently scored as primitive or derived. Mops cannot sort subterranean from downstream snails.
