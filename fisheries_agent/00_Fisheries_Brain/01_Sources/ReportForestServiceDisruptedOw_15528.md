## Executive Summary
An independent consultant, Laurence Jahn, produced a February report (commissioned by the U.S. Forest Service) concluding that Forest Service managers in Arizona and New Mexico obstructed scientific efforts to conserve the [[Mexican Spotted Owl]], a species listed as **threatened** under the Endangered Species Act. The report also substantiated claims that the agency retaliated against biologist [[Cole Crocker-Bedford]] for publishing research (1990, *Wildlife Society* magazine) warning that continued timber harvests could cause significant declines in nesting pairs of the [[Northern Goshawk]], an indicator species. The Forest Service heavily redacted the report before its release under a Freedom of Information Act request, fueling accusations of a cover-up by conservation groups, including the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Northern Goshawk** — a forest raptor considered an "indicator species," meaning its population trends are used by biologists as a proxy for overall forest ecosystem health; sensitive to reductions in old-growth nesting habitat caused by timber harvesting.
- Both species' conservation needs were reported to directly conflict with Forest Service timber quota mandates, illustrating a habitat-management vs. resource-extraction tension typical of contested public land ecosystems.

## Key Findings
- The Jahn report found that in 1991 the Forest Service disrupted development of a scientifically credible [[Mexican Spotted Owl]] conservation strategy by insisting a timber harvest quota be embedded in the plan, a demand deemed inconsistent with the agency's own forest management standards.
- The Forest Service allegedly terminated Mexican Spotted Owl research on the [[Gila National Forest]] by threatening researchers with arrest for lacking a research permit.
- Biologist [[Cole Crocker-Bedford]] was subjected to a two-day adversarial cross-examination by Justice Department and timber industry attorneys, faced public discrediting campaigns, and was ordered to remain silent under threat of reprisal after publishing goshawk research.
- A parallel, separate government investigation into Crocker-Bedford's professional history was reportedly intended to discredit him as "incompetent or unobjective," an objective Jahn concluded was "not fulfilled."
- Two months after the Jahn report was issued, the top Forest Service official for the Arizona/New Mexico region, [[Larry Henson]], unexpectedly retired amid accusations of being overly favorable to the timber industry.
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, under court order, proposed designating 4.8 million acres of federal land as critical habitat for the Mexican Spotted Owl; conservationists suspected federal agencies were attempting to circumvent this court order.
