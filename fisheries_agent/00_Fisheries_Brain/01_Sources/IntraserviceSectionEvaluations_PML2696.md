# Fish Stocking and Section 7 Consultations for Arizona and Lower Colorado River Native Fishes (1994–1995)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) correspondence, draft and final biological opinions (BOs), internal dissent memoranda, and one grazing consultation, all concerning the effects of non-native fish stocking and land management on federally listed fishes in Arizona and the lower [[Colorado River]]. Core topics are the stocking of sport fish by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) and the FWS [[Sport Fish Restoration Program]] under [[Federal Aid]] funding, the jeopardy determinations for [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] from rainbow trout stocking, and the incidental take conditions for [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] in Nelson, Blue Ridge and Knoll Lake reservoirs. A separate BO addresses livestock grazing on the Baseline and Horse Springs allotments affecting [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], Razorback Sucker and [[Bald Eagle]] in [[Eagle Creek]].

## Background & Description

- **Regulatory frame:** Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] requires federal agencies to ensure actions do not jeopardize listed species or adversely modify critical habitat. Consultation types include "no effect" intra-Service findings, informal and formal consultation, and biological opinions with reasonable and prudent alternatives (RPAs) and incidental take statements.
- **Scope of stocking:** In a 1995 memorandum, FWS reviewed 90 Arizona locations where fish stockings were anticipated for 1995–1999. A concurrent BO covered stocking of catchable rainbow trout into three Arizona impoundments (Nelson, Blue Ridge, Knoll Lake) from 1994 to 1999, with about 15,000 trout per year proposed for Blue Ridge, 20,000 for Knoll Lake, and Nelson stocked at a comparable rate.
- **Native fish status:** Of Arizona's 33 native fish species, most have been severely impacted by habitat loss and non-native introductions. The Colorado River Basin hosts 35 native fish species, of which 6 are not found outside the basin, and more than 70 species have been introduced.
- **Lower Colorado River stocking:** Rainbow trout (560,500 eight- to ten-inch fish proposed) from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] into Lake Mohave and the mainstem below Davis Dam, plus channel catfish into isolated waters, with a total of 145,000 trout proposed below Davis Dam, 13,000 to Colorado River Indian Reservation waters, and 8,500 to Fort Yuma Indian Reservation canals.
- **Grazing consultation:** Livestock grazing on the Baseline and Horse Springs allotments (Greenlee County, Arizona; Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests), under a biologically controlled (high-intensity, time-controlled) rotation, with a maximum of 300 cows and calves (4,818 animal unit months) proposed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predation and competition by non-natives.** Non-native fishes are repeatedly identified as a major cause of decline. In Little Colorado spinedace habitat, AGFD sampling from May 1990 to November 1991 recorded eight non-native species in East Clear Creek, including rainbow, brook and brown trout. Rainbow trout predation on spinedace is documented in aquaria and stream enclosures. Red shiner, common carp, bluegill and largemouth bass also co-occur with spinedace.

**Reservoir and river conditions.** Lake Mohave (108 km long, surface area 115.0 km², volume 2.3 × 10⁹ m³) is a riverine reservoir with a water retention time of 0.24 years and is fed by Hoover Dam releases of 11–12.5 °C. Lake Havasu is too warm to sustain trout over summer. Predators on larval razorback sucker include flathead catfish, and striped bass in Lake Mohave were found to have preyed on rainbow trout (952 percent of fish with food in stomach, as reported in a preliminary study).

**Spawning and habitat for listed species.** Razorback sucker spawns roughly January to April (Lake Mohave peak December–March); larvae use shallow coves at 21.7–24.4 °C. Loach minnow and spikedace are tied to shallow, swift water and water temperature cues for spawning. Eagle Creek is intermittent, with surface water only about 2–3 months per year above and below the project area.

**Grazing-related effects.** Vertisol soils (deteriorated clay-loam) cover a substantial portion of the allotments and are vulnerable to erosion. Riparian woody regeneration is low along Eagle Creek and its tributaries Bear and Bee Canyons. Livestock use is considered to have contributed to degradation of Eagle Creek over the past 100 years.

## Key Findings

- **No effect and "no jeopardy" for most Arizona stockings.** The FWS supported "no effect" findings for 90 Arizona stocking locations (Gila/Salt/Verde, Santa Cruz, Yaqui headwaters) on the basis that safeguards prevent adverse impact to [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Gila Topminnow]] and Apache trout. Bald eagle and southwestern willow flycatcher impacts were judged discountable or insignificant.
- **Jeopardy for Lake Mohave and Lower Colorado River fishes.** The June 16, 1994 draft BO concluded that rainbow trout stocking was likely to jeopardize [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] and adversely modify critical habitat. Channel catfish stocking in isolated waters was judged not likely to jeopardize.
- **Dissent within the Service.** The Fisheries biologist (Pinetop) argued that trout predation did not rise to a jeopardy level, that the Service had no evidence trout were a major predator, and that the RPAs were unsatisfactory and unfunded. Another biologist disputed the jeopardy call, arguing that stocking cessation would not restore recruitment because other predators and habitat loss were the real causes.
- **Final RPA.** The Assistant Regional Director accepted a modified RPA 2 that combined restricted stocking (suspension in Areas 1–5 of Lake Mohave during spawning, continued stocking in Areas 6–7) with augmentation of razorback sucker and bonytail. A five-year recovery program was committed. The Regional Director approved the BO on July 1, 1994.
- **Recruitment evidence.** Eleven of 39 bonytail captured in Lake Mohave (1981–1990) were stocked fish. Of roughly 2,000 razorback suckers stocked as 300 mm fingerlings, 15 were recaptured in 1993–1994 spring surveys. Razorback sucker recruitment in Lake Mohave has not been documented in the wild since the early 1950s, with the exception of one bonytail probably spawned in the 1970s.
- **Little Colorado spinedace incidental take.** The August 2, 1995 BO (consultation 2-21-92-F-403) found no jeopardy and no adverse modification of critical habitat for spinedace. Approximately 82 percent (36 of 44 miles) of designated critical habitat is affected. Take is considered exceeded if rainbow trout are found with spinedace in their stomachs, or if any of the three reservoirs spill during the stocking season.
- **Eagle Creek grazing BO.** The July 20, 1995 BO (consultation 2-21-95-F-020) found non-jeopardy for spikedace, loach minnow, razorback sucker and bald eagle. Incidental take of spikedace, loach minnow and razorback sucker is anticipated but not quantifiable. Take is considered exceeded if more than 20 dead fish of any species are observed within 300 yards downstream of the project area. Recommended monitoring includes biennial reviews and grazing of riparian zones only during the dormant season.
- **Policy issue.** The Regional Office and the Ecological Services field office disagreed on whether intra-Service "no effect" findings must be provided to Ecological Services; a revision was deferred pending the national Consultation Handbook revision, targeted for December 31, 1995.
