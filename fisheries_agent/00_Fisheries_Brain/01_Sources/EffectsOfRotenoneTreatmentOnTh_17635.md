# Effects of Rotenone Treatment on the Fauna of the Green River, Wyoming

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Wyoming Game and Fish Commission bulletin (Fisheries Research Bulletin No. 1, 1967) reports a study of the [[Green River]] fauna before and after a September 1962 [[Rotenone]] treatment. The treatment was intended to remove rotenone-resistant coarse fish ahead of the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Fontenelle Dam]] impoundments authorized under the [[Colorado River Storage Project]]. Fish and invertebrates were sampled from July 1962 to late 1964. The treatment nearly eliminated fish from the mainstream and devastated the invertebrate fauna. Recovery was driven mainly by downstream immigration from untreated reaches, with surviving or late-hatched populations in sloughs contributing as well. The fauna returned to near-pretreatment levels by 1963–1964, but composition changed. Some mayfly genera did not return, and midge larvae became dominant.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Green River rises in the Wind River Mountains and flows through southwestern Wyoming. The study focused on the river and its principal tributary, the [[New Fork River]], from above the treated area to Flaming Gorge Dam, about 117 miles downstream.
- **Rationale:** Managers faced a choice. They could establish a trout fishery after impoundment, competing with an expanding non-game fish population, or depress the non-game population before impoundment. The area had strong potential as a trout fishery, so the river was treated before dam closure. Treatment after closure was judged prohibitive in cost.
- **Treatment scale:** The report states that "about 445 miles" of the Green River and tributaries upstream of Flaming Gorge Dam were treated with Chem-Fish Regular, a five percent emulsified rotenone product. The figure is as printed and may be a transcription error, since the study reach is described as about 117 miles.
- **Dispersal:** Treatment used a series of introduction stations spaced about ten miles apart on the mainstream and tributaries. Each station used two 55-gallon barrels, and the toxicant was released over three to seven hours.
- **Target concentration:** The planned target was 5.0 ppm (the Methods text also gives 3.0 ppm as the target). Actual concentrations, after correcting for a flow drop that went undetected during treatment, ranged from 9.4 ppm at station 1 to 2.5 ppm at station 22. Potassium permanganate was used at a downstream bridge to detoxify the water.
- **Sampling methods:** Fish were sampled with gill nets, [[Dynamite]], minnow seines and traps, electrofishing, and rotenone. Invertebrates were collected with a one-square-foot bottom sampler and a [[Drift Net]]. Emergence traps were also used. Invertebrate counts and volumes were standardized per cubic foot per second (no./min./cfs).
- **Rotenone measurement:** [[Bioassay]] techniques were judged the most accurate. The [[Post Colorimetric Method]] was discarded as unreliable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and chemistry:** Flows ranged from about 30 cfs (New Fork, winter) to nearly 5,000 cfs (June runoff). Methyl-orange alkalinity was about 64–228 ppm CaCO3, and pH ranged from about 7.25 to 8.8 across sections. Water temperatures ranged from 31°F to 74°F.
- **Four river sections:** (I) New Fork River, (II) Upper Green River, (III) Middle Green River, (IV) Lower Green River. Section IV was strongly influenced by historical pollution from oil-field and domestic sources. Pollution declined after the late 1950s, but Bosley (1960) described its fauna as typical of polluted water.
- **Pre-treatment fish community:** The Mountain Whitefish dominated the New Fork and Upper Green sections, followed by Flannelmouth Suckers. Speckled Dace, Redside Shiners, and sculpins were numerous. The non-game fish dominated Sections III and IV. Carp migrated up the New Fork from the Green River during high water, threatening the upper trout fishery.
- **Rotenone toxicity literature:** The introduction reviews conflicting earlier findings. Many workers reported little effect on fish food organisms, while others documented high invertebrate mortality. Rotenone is a slow-acting contact and stomach poison. Its toxicity to insects appears variable, and detoxification is accelerated by vegetation, organic matter, and silt.
- **Recovery drivers:** Immigration from untreated areas was the most important factor. Whitefish reached about 114 miles downstream in one week after the 1961 test treatment, and Brown Trout moved in from tributaries near Big Piney. Scattered survivors in sloughs and backwaters, late-spawned eggs, and in-stream egg survival also contributed.

## Key Findings

**Fish**

- **Immediate kill:** Many fish fled downstream. Reactions included surfacing and splashing (whitefish), circling and shivering, and drifting as though stunned (carp). After about three hours of exposure, only dead fish remained in the main current. One dynamite blast in a New Fork hole killed 600–700 whitefish and trout.
- **Post-treatment mainstream:** Whitefish, Brown Trout, and redside shiners were found only in the upper sections in late 1962. No carp were found in the mainstream. Fish returned slowly through dispersal and sloughs.
- **Carp and suckers:** Carp reappeared in scattered treated-area sloughs and backwaters, and 1962 year-class fish were found. Flannelmouth Suckers recovered from widely scattered locations and from the 1962 year class. Growth was rapid: 1963 year-class suckers were 8–9 inches by late 1964, and one-year-old carp reached about seven inches.
- **Species that did not return:** [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Black Bullhead]] were not recorded by the close of study. Flaming Gorge Dam was believed to have blocked recruitment of the squawfish. [[Bonytail Chub]] reappeared in 1964, the first time since treatment. Specimens were under five inches and mostly smaller.
- **Restocking:** Rainbow Trout were planted from 1963 onward and supported a successful fishery. Predation on forage fish by the larger trout appeared weak, as their stomachs held mainly snails.
- **Final fish list:** Appendix 1 lists the fishes present as of November 1, 1964, and reports the disappearance of several pretreatment species. Lake Trout and Kokanee Salmon were newly recorded.

**Invertebrates**

- **Immediate devastation:** Most benthic invertebrates were killed. Survivors included dragonflies (Gomphidae) and snipe flies (Rhagionidae). At station B, water beetles dominated immediately after treatment. At station G, the number of taxonomic groups fell from 16 to two in one week.
- **Drift:** Drift catches increased briefly after treatment, largely as dead organisms. Drift counts were low until runoff in 1963.
- **Recovery:** At station D, the fauna recovered faster than at other stations because of proximity to untreated water and beds of moss and *Ranunculus*. The summer 1964 count reached 12,746 invertebrates in one square-foot sample, with Oligochaeta most numerous. Sugar-floatation tests showed that about 57 percent of organisms would have been missed without flotation. Some of the apparent increase may therefore reflect sorting method rather than true abundance.
- **Composition shift:** Tendipedidae (midges) became dominant at several stations. Pentagenia and Hexagenia had not returned by the end of study. [[Hydropsychidae]] (net-spinning caddisflies) was among the last groups to return. Five mayfly genera absent before treatment (including Leptophlebia, Traverella, and Isonychia) appeared during recovery.
- **Flash floods:** August and September 1963 floods reduced benthic populations at several stations and temporarily increased drift catches.
- **Station I:** Samples in the Flaming Gorge impoundment zone were dominated by Oligochaeta after treatment, with Tubificidae abundant. Later, midges became dominant.

**Ecological effects and management outcome**

- **Water clarity:** Water became clearer after removal of bottom-feeding fish. Algal growth, including a diatom bloom in fall 1962, increased. This is consistent with reports from earlier treatments.
- **Evaluation:** The report concludes that the project met its basic objectives. These were depressing coarse fish populations and establishing a sport fishery. Angler use exceeded preimpoundment estimates. Non-game populations were re-established but could become a problem again in the lower treated area.
- **Methods lesson:** Laboratory bioassay was judged the best available method for rotenone determination. A simple, accurate field method was still needed.
