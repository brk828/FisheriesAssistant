# Critical Habitat Designation for the Woundfin and Virgin River Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a final rule issued by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (U.S. Department of the Interior), published in the *Federal Register* on January 26, 2000 (Vol. 65, No. 17; RIN 1018-AD23; 50 CFR part 17). It designates [[Critical Habitat]] for two endangered cyprinid fishes, the [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and the [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*), in the mainstem [[Virgin River]] and its 100-year floodplain. The designation covers 140.1 km (87.5 mi) of river, which the rule states is about 12.5 percent of the woundfin's historical range and about 65.3 percent of the Virgin River chub's historical range. The designation was made under a federal court order and the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The rule also states that critical habitat produces no incremental economic impacts beyond those of listing, so no areas were excluded.

## Background & Description

**Species and listing status.** Both fishes were listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The woundfin was listed in October 1970 (35 FR 16047). The Virgin River chub was listed in August 1989 (54 FR 35305), but that listing specifically excluded the [[Muddy River]] population, which was then considered an undescribed subspecies. Later taxonomic work (DeMarais et al. 1992) concluded that the Virgin River chub merits full species status, likely arising through hybridization of *Gila robusta* and *G. elegans*. The Muddy River population was left out of this rule because it was not treated as listed when the proposal was prepared; it will be assessed separately.

**Range.** The Virgin River runs about 320 km (200 mi) from south-central Utah to northwestern Arizona and empties into [[Lake Mead]]. Before [[Boulder Dam]] was completed in 1935, the Muddy River joined the Virgin River before its confluence with the Colorado River. Woundfin were historically recorded from the Colorado River near Yuma, Arizona, and in tributaries across Nevada, Arizona and Utah. They are now largely extirpated from much of that range, surviving mainly in the mainstem Virgin River. The Virgin River chub was historically collected from [[Pah Tempe Springs]] downstream to the Colorado River, and now occurs from Pah Tempe Springs to at least the Mesquite Diversion near the Arizona–Nevada border.

**Habitat preferences.** Adult and juvenile woundfin use runs and quiet waters next to riffles with sand or sand/gravel substrates. Juveniles favor slower, deeper water, and larvae are found in backwaters and shoreline margins with filamentous algae. Reported velocities are 0.24–0.49 m/s (0.8–1.6 ft/s). Adult and juvenile Virgin River chub select deep runs or pools with slow to moderate velocities, boulders or other instream cover, and sand substrate. Chub are generally found at velocities up to 0.76 m/s (2.5 ft/s).

**Regulatory history.** The designation was delayed for several reasons:
- Critical habitat was proposed in 1978 and withdrawn in 1979 after the 1978 amendments to the Act.
- A March 18, 1994 court order required proposal and finalization of critical habitat. A proposal was published April 5, 1995 (60 FR 12256), covering the woundfin, Virgin River chub and [[Virgin Spinedace]].
- The Virgin Spinedace Conservation Agreement was signed in 1995. The spinedace listing and critical habitat proposal were withdrawn in 1996, so it is not included here.
- A congressional moratorium and budget disruptions delayed action until April 1996.
- On August 27, 1999, the U.S. District Court of Colorado ordered finalization by January 20, 2000.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Floodplain importance.** The rule states that channel preservation alone is not enough for recovery. The [[Floodplain Habitat]] is integral to the river system. Seasonally inundated floodplains supply [[Allochthonous Organic Matter]], such as detritus, silt and terrestrial plant and animal material, which the rule describes as a major food base for the river's fishes. Because the Virgin River has little aquatic vegetation, its fish fauna depends heavily on these inputs. Floodplain inundation during spring flows also supports spawning and provides warm, low-velocity refuge habitat. The rule cites loss of floodplain habitat in the Missouri River Basin reducing fish biomass production by as much as 98 percent (Kan and Schlosser 1978).

**Threats.** The Virgin River fishes have declined due to:
- Dewatering from numerous diversion projects, including the Quail Creek Diversion.
- Proliferation of [[Nonnative Fish Species]]. Fourteen introduced species, including the [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]], compete with or prey on the listed fishes. The red shiner is the most numerous and most problematic, competing for food and habitat and preying on eggs and early life stages.
- Altered natural flow, temperature and sediment regimes.
- Water quality decline. A decrease in water quality followed diversion of flows above Pah Tempe Springs, which had previously diluted saline input from the springs.

**Population trends.** Holden and Zucker (1996) analyzed 1976–1993 data and found a statistically significant negative trend (p < 0.05) in woundfin catch per seining effort at all long-term stations except one during the fall season. Deacon (1988) documented a substantial woundfin decline between 1976 and 1993 in the reaches above Quail Creek Reservoir and below Pah Tempe Springs.

**Constituent elements.** The rule identifies primary constituent elements (PCEs) that define critical habitat, grouped into:
1. *Water quality:* sufficient quantity and quality of water, including natural seasonal temperature, turbidity, conductivity and dissolved oxygen.
2. *Hydrologic regime:* flows that form and maintain channel and instream habitat, and floods that inundate the floodplain.
3. *Physical habitat:* river and side channels, backwaters and springs, plus adjacent areas of riffles, runs and pools with suitable substrate.
4. *Biological environment:* seasonal food supply and low levels of predation and competition from nonnative species.

## Key Findings

- **Designated area.** The final designation covers 140.1 km (87.5 mi) of mainstem Virgin River and its 100-year floodplain, from the confluence of La Verkin Creek, Utah, to Halfway Wash, Nevada. The Virgin River chub designation is identical to the woundfin designation.
- **Segment lengths (woundfin designation).** Utah 59.6 km (37.3 mi), Arizona 50.6 km (31.6 mi), Nevada 29.9 km (18.6 mi), totaling 140.1 km. The rule notes the Arizona share is only an estimate because historical data for Arizona are lacking.
- **Land ownership.** Of the designated shoreline, 57.7 percent is federal, 2.4 percent is state and 39.9 percent is private. Ownership was typically the same on both banks.
- **Floodplain boundary.** The 100-year floodplain was chosen because the regulations require defined boundaries referenced to standard maps. The designated portions are those containing at least one primary constituent element. Floodplain areas with developed land are excluded from the designation.
- **Economic conclusion.** The Service concluded that there are no incremental economic impacts from designation beyond those from listing. It found that the earlier analysis overstated potential impacts, and it withdrew the exclusion of areas based on economic grounds. Critical habitat boundaries were simplified into a single river section.
- **Adverse modification standard.** The rule acknowledges that its earlier assumption that the adverse-modification and jeopardy thresholds differ was incorrect, and it adopts a revised interpretation.
- **Public comment.** During the 1995 comment period the Service received 14 written letters and six oral testimonies at the public hearing in St. George, Utah. The 1999 draft environmental assessment drew 12 written letters, and a finding of no significant impact was published November 24, 1999 (64 FR 66192).
- **Issue on decline.** Commenters argued that numbers of fish might not have declined even though occupied range decreased. The Service disagreed, citing evidence of ongoing decline in both range and abundance.
- **Companion item.** The final pages of this issue include an unrelated listing for the plant [[Delphinium Luteum]] (yellow larkspur), which is endangered and found in coastal California. The text is largely garbled in the source and not analyzed further here.
