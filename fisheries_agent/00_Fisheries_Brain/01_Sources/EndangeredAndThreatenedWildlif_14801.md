# Critical Habitat Designation for the Threatened Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #conservation #regulatory

## Executive Summary

This final rule by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service designates approximately 257 kilometers (159 miles) of critical habitat for the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) under the Endangered Species Act. The loach minnow was listed as threatened on October 28, 1986, but critical habitat designation was postponed pending economic analysis. The designated areas include portions of the [[Gila River]] in Grant and Catron counties, New Mexico; the [[San Francisco River]] and [[Tularosa River]] and Dry Blue Creek in Catron County, New Mexico; the San Francisco and [[Blue River]] and Campbell Blue Creek in Greenlee County, Arizona; and [[Aravaipa Creek]] in Graham and Pinal counties, Arizona. Federal actions affecting these areas are now subject to consultation under section 7(a)(2) of the Act. The rule became effective April 7, 1994.

## Background & Description

The [[Loach Minnow]] is a small, slender, elongated fish less than 80 millimeters (3 inches) long. It is olivaceous in color with an oblique terminal mouth and eyes markedly upward-directed. First collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro in Arizona and described by Girard in 1856, the species was once locally common throughout much of the Verde, Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco, and upper Gila River systems, occupying suitable habitat in both mainstreams and perennial tributaries up to approximately 2,200 meters (7,200 feet) elevation.

The species' historic range encompassed approximately 2,600 km (1,600 mi) of river. Due to habitat destruction and competition and predation by non-native fish species, its range and abundance have been severely reduced to approximately 17 percent of historic range. Current distribution includes approximately 257 km of occupied habitat across seven stream systems in Arizona and New Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The loach minnow inhabits small to large perennial streams, using shallow, turbulent riffles with primarily cobble substrate and swift currents. Recurrent flooding is very important to loach minnow survival in keeping the substrate free of embedding sediments and helping maintain a competitive edge over invading non-native fish species.

**Primary Constituent Elements of Critical Habitat:**
- Permanent, flowing, unpolluted water
- Adult habitat: moderate to swift flow velocities (15-100 cm/second) in shallow water (3-40 cm deep) with gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates
- Juvenile habitat: moderate to swift flow velocities (15-100 cm/second) in shallow water (3-40 cm deep) with sand, gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates
- Larval habitat: slow to moderate flow velocities (0-30 cm/second) in shallow water (3-30 cm deep) with sand, gravel, and cobble substrates and abundant instream cover
- Spawning habitat: slow to swift flow velocities (3-85 cm/second) in shallow water (3-30 cm deep) with uncemented cobble and rubble substrate
- Low amounts of fine sediment and substrate embeddedness
- Riffle, run, and backwater components
- Low to moderate stream gradient (generally 0.5-1.5 percent)
- Water temperatures approximately 4-30°C (40-85°F) with natural diurnal and seasonal variation
- Abundant aquatic insect food base
- Periodic flooding
- Natural, unregulated hydrograph
- Few or no predatory or competitive non-native species present
- Healthy, intact riparian community
- Moderate to high bank stability

**Threats to Critical Habitat:**
Activities that may adversely modify critical habitat include groundwater pumping, impoundment, water diversions, vegetation manipulation, timber harvest, road construction, prescribed burning, livestock grazing, mining, urban/suburban development, channelization, destruction of riparian vegetation, excessive sedimentation, chemical or biological pollutant release, and introduction/spread of non-native fish species.

Genetic studies indicate that remnant populations are genetically distinctive. Maintenance of widely separated populations in the Gila, San Francisco, Tularosa, White and Blue rivers and Aravaipa Creek as independent entities is critical to buffer against threats to individual populations.

## Key Findings

**Designated Critical Habitat Areas (approximate distances):**

1. **Aravaipa Creek** (Graham and Pinal counties, Arizona): 24 km (15 mi)
   - Land ownership: 10 km BLM (Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness), 13 km The Nature Conservancy, ~1 km private inholdings

2. **Gila River** (Grant and Catron counties, New Mexico): 93 km (57 mi) total in four sections
   - Mainstem: 37 km (23 mi) from St. Peter's Rock upstream to Mogollon Creek confluence
   - West Fork: 11.5 km (7 mi)
   - Middle Fork: 18 km (11 mi)
   - East Fork: 26 km (16 mi)
   - Mixed ownership: 28 km private, 2 km TNC, 6 km New Mexico Game and Fish, 0.5 km State Land Office, 1 km National Park Service, 55.5 km U.S. Forest Service

3. **San Francisco River** (Catron County, New Mexico and Greenlee County, Arizona): 21 km (13 mi) in two sections
   - 15 km from U.S. Highway 180 bridge upstream to Kelly Flat
   - 6 km from Hickey Canyon confluence upstream to Blue River confluence

4. **Tularosa River** (Catron County, New Mexico): 24 km (15 mi)

5. **Blue River** (Greenlee County, Arizona and Catron County, New Mexico): 78 km (48 mi)

6. **Campbell Blue Creek** (Greenlee County, Arizona and Catron County, New Mexico): 14 km (9 mi)

7. **Dry Blue Creek** (Catron County, New Mexico): 3 km (2 mi)

**Public Comment Summary:**
- 111 letters received from 108 parties
- Three public hearings held (Silver City NM, Safford AZ, Phoenix AZ) with 107 attendees
- 77 letters in support, 21 in opposition, 13 neutral or providing economic information
- At hearings: 16 statements in support, 13 opposed, 3 neutral

**Economic Analysis:**
Maximum identifiable added costs estimated at $406,500 (of which $150,000 attributable to 84 km of river designated for both loach minnow and [[Spikedace]]). Expected impacts limited to:
- FEMA cost-shares for irrigation diversion reconstruction after floods
- Additional fencing and alternative water developments for cattle grazing management on National Forest
- Limited preventive measures at developed recreation sites
- With exception of $8,412 in local cost-share, added costs would be to Federal government

**Conservation Measures:**
Federal actions that may affect designated critical habitat are subject to section 7 consultation. No significant effects expected on most Federal activities. Potential impacts identified for:
- Bureau of Land Management recreational management (Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness)
- U.S. Forest Service land management activities (grazing, mining, timber harvest, recreation)
- Bureau of Reclamation water development (Upper Gila Water Supply Study, suspended until 2010)
- FEMA flood control projects
- Federal Highway Administration projects
- Clean Water Act section 402 and 404 permits

The Director determined no areas warranted exclusion from designation based on economic effects. The rule does not impose recordkeeping requirements and is not expected to have significant economic effect on a substantial number of small entities under the Regulatory Flexibility Act.
