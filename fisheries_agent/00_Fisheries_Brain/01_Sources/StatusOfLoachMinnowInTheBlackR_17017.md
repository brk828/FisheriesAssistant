# Status of Loach Minnow in the Black River Drainage, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (Phoenix Area Office), submitted by Paul C. Marsh of [[Arizona State University]], documents a 1996 fisheries survey of the [[Black River]] drainage on [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]] land. The survey was broadened after a new population of federally threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) was discovered, the first record of the species from the Black River system. Twelve fish species were captured at 47 locations on 24 streams: six native and six non-native. The report concludes that the river is important for southwestern native fish recovery and recommends further delineation of loach minnow habitat, control of non-native fish introductions, and genetic study of the White River population.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Black River forms from the East and West Forks, whose headwaters rise near 9,000 and 11,000 feet (2743 and 3353 m). It joins the White River to form the [[Salt River]]. The drainage crosses Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests, the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]], and private land.
- **Management history:** The river is managed as a trout fishery and has been stocked with non-native species. Stream renovation and surveys have usually centred on trout management. The North Fork of the East Fork was treated with [[Rotenone]] in 1958 to remove "rough fish," and this area was among the first stream renovations in Arizona.
- **Fish community (as reported):** Six native species: [[Apache Trout]] (federally threatened, historically present and reintroduced), [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Sonora Sucker]], and [[Desert Sucker]]. Six non-native species: [[Rainbow Trout]], Apache x rainbow hybrid, [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]].
- **Methods:** Four collecting trips were made in 1996 (10-14 June, 4-6 July, 22-26 July, 5-8 August). Gear included gill, trammel and dip nets, seines, backpack and gas electrofishers, and angling. Vouchers were fixed in 10% formalin and deposited at the ASU Museum. Twenty-five loach minnow were frozen for genetic analysis, under state and federal scientific collecting permits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Loach minnow range:** The species is known from about a dozen U.S. locations (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1988). The nearest Black River watershed population is more than 130 miles (208 km) downstream in the White River. Given that distance and the abundance of predatory non-native [[Smallmouth Bass]] and trout, the authors judged upstream migration from the White River unlikely in recent times.
- **Predation and displacement:** Trout and smallmouth bass are predatory or competitive threats. The report links the decline of roundtail chub in upper reaches to introduced trout and, further downstream, to smallmouth bass.
- **Hybridization:** Rainbow trout have direct access to streams holding pure Apache trout. The authors describe the future of these populations as "precarious" because of hybridization risk.
- **Habitat:** Loach minnow favour riffles with cobble and boulder substrates. Many intermittent tributaries (for example Coyote, Deer, Horton, Johns Canyon, Thomas and Willow creeks) were dry or nearly dry and yielded few or no fish.
- **Crayfish:** Crayfish were noted as common in several reaches, including the lower Black River and the East Fork.

## Key Findings

- **New loach minnow population:** Loach minnow were found in the North Fork of the East Fork of the Black River near Three Forks, common over at least 2.25 miles (3.6 km), from 0.25 mile (0.4 km) above Three Forks to at least 0.5 mile (0.8 km) above Open Draw. Field counts included 1 individual (1 June 1996 reach near FS rd 249) and 4 and 11 individuals taken on 12 and 13 June (15 total near FS rd 249). Genetic analysis of 25 individuals showed the population is distinct from all others sampled; the White River population has not been analysed.
- **Species richness:** Twelve fish species (six native, six non-native) were captured at 47 locations on 24 streams.
- **Roundtail chub:** Locally abundant near FS rd 25, where young-of-year and a reproducing population were found. The species was not found near Three Forks in 1996, despite an earlier 1938 record.
- **Apache trout:** Encountered at several sites (e.g., East Fork at Deer Creek, West Fork downstream of FS rd 116). Recovery efforts include hybrid removal and reintroduction of pure native stock.
- **Non-native fish:** Brown trout was the most frequently encountered non-native species. Rainbow trout occurred in six streams but was never abundant. Brook trout was locally abundant only in Boneyard Creek. Smallmouth bass occurred only in the lowest 2 miles (3.2 km) of the Black River, possibly extending to Buffalo Crossing.
- **Recommendations:**
  - Define the upper and lower limits of the loach minnow population. Unsampled reaches include about 3 miles above Three Forks and 1.5 miles near Open Draw.
  - Avoid trout stocking where it would threaten loach minnow habitat.
  - Curtail non-native fish introductions into loach minnow habitat. This and the genetic work are listed as priority 1 tasks in the [[Loach Minnow Recovery Plan]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1988).
  - Sample the White River population for genetic comparison.
  - Foster cooperation among the Fort Apache Indian Tribe, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and U.S. Forest Service.
