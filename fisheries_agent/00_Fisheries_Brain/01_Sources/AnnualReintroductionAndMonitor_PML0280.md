# Razorback Sucker Reintroduction and Monitoring in the Gila River Basin, Arizona, 1985

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report, prepared by [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] biologist Janes E. Brooks and submitted to the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] in March 1986, summarizes 1985 reintroductions of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) across the [[Gila River Basin]] of central Arizona and evaluates post-stocking survival. Monitoring showed that predation by introduced [[Catfish]] was a major constraint: channel and flathead catfish consumed stocked fingerlings at significant rates. Recaptures of stocked fish remained scarce, and the authors concluded that predator reduction, larger stocking sizes, and modified stocking timing are needed for recovery.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] reintroduction began in 1981 and, by the end of 1985, nearly 9.5 million fish had been stocked. Recaptures were rare, which the authors attributed to three factors: stocking was dominated by fry (92%), which normally suffer high attrition; heavy predation on stocked fish; and survey effort that was inadequate for the very large reintroduction area.

The study area covers three mainstem rivers and ten tributaries:

- **[[Gila River]]**: a shallow, wide, shifting channel with low summer flow and warm water. Tributaries include [[Bonita Creek]] and [[Eagle Creek]].
- **[[Salt River]]**: drains the White Mountains through the Salt River Canyon, with deep runs and gravel riffles. Coon, Cherry, Canyon, Carrizo and Cedar creeks enter from the north.
- **[[Verde River]]**: heads in Big Chino Valley and includes Oak, West Clear and Wet Beaver creeks. Lower reaches of Oak Creek and, to a lesser extent, West Clear and Wet Beaver creeks are heavily modified by diversions, gravel pits and housing.

Stocking used fry and fingerlings reared at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH) and fingerlings reared at [[Page Springs Hatchery]] (PSH). Surveys used seines, hoop, gill and trammel nets, and backpack, raft and boat electrofishing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat in the study area is degraded relative to historic conditions, but the authors judged this less important than predation and hatchery-related factors in determining recovery success. Native fishes were more abundant and diverse in tributaries than in mainstem rivers. Reservoir surveys were dominated by non-native species, and the Verde River, despite many introduced fish, still had native fishes making up nearly 38% of combined collections.

Downstream dispersal of stocked fish was documented, and hatchery rearing (including pond rearing) may reduce a fish's ability to adapt to flowing water. The report also notes that [[Predation]] by introduced fishes is expected to remain a persistent feature of southwestern rivers.

## Key Findings

**Stocking (1985)**
- More than 3 million razorback suckers were stocked at fifteen localities in the Gila drainage in 1985. Most (86.5%) were fry released in the Salt River drainage.
- Fry made up roughly 96.5% of the total stocked (2,619,475 fish); fingerlings and 1-year-olds were a small fraction.

**Recaptures and growth**
- Before 1985, recaptures were limited to two collections. Several recaptures occurred in 1985, including two large suckers from the Gila and Verde (unverified) rivers.
- Subfingerlings more than doubled in size and fingerlings in Bonita Creek were about 25% larger at capture.
- Growth differed among three isolated [[Bonita Creek]] backwaters, with the best growth where other catostomids were absent.
- Mainstem sampling (including Eagle Creek) found no razorback suckers other than those from post-stocking studies.

**Dispersal (September 1985, Gila River near Clifton)**
- 25,875 fingerlings (mean 90 mm TL) were stocked on 13 September 1985.
- Dispersal was predominantly downstream, with nighttime movement the norm. Only one fish was taken in the hoop net 0.5 km upstream.
- No razorback suckers were caught in daylight before the study ended by flooding on 15 September.

**Predation**
- Razorback suckers occurred in 46% of channel catfish stomachs and 79% of flathead catfish stomachs. Channel catfish consumed stocked fish at a higher rate.
- Predator size was strongly correlated with prey size (r² = 0.76). Channel catfish (216 ± 69 mm SL) ate smaller suckers than flathead catfish (287 ± 108 mm SL).
- Estimated predation in the study reach was 558 fingerlings per hectare per 24 hours, and about 50,000 fingerlings over two days for the Gila River from the study area downstream to Safford (ca. 35 km).

**Reservoir surveys (March 1985)**
- No razorback suckers were collected. Largemouth bass and common carp dominated catches; Sonora sucker was the only native species collected.

**Management recommendations**
- Reduce or remove predators, possibly by piscicide or mechanical means in discrete reaches (1–2 km) or larger sections such as about 90 km of the Salt River Canyon, without permanently affecting sportfish.
- Stock larger fish: the authors recommended at least 250 mm TL, requiring an extra year of hatchery rearing, because fry and small fingerlings are highly vulnerable.
- Consider upstream stocking sites to offset downstream dispersal, and raceway conditioning before release.
- Evaluate winter stocking in 1986, as cooler temperatures may reduce predator activity, and test dispersal and predation on one-year-old fish reared at PSH.
