# Survival Status of the Sandia and Potosí Valleys Endemic Pupfishes, Crayfishes and Associated Snails

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This manuscript, by [[Salvador Contreras-Balderas]] and [[María de Lourdes Lozano-Vilano]], documents the decline of a group of narrowly endemic pupfishes, crayfishes and valvatid snails inhabiting isolated springs in the arid southwest of Nuevo León, Mexico. Of the springs surveyed, four are extinct or nearly so, with the sites of [[Charco La Palma]], [[Ojo La Trinidad]], [[Ojo de Agua La Presa]] and [[Ojo de Agua Charco Azul]] losing their populations within roughly a decade of discovery. The authors attribute these losses to aquifer depletion from unregulated groundwater pumping for agriculture and call for regional, basin-scale management of geohydrological systems.

The paper also functions as a conservation case study. Editorial review in 1995 found it timely but structured as a series of loosely connected notes and in need of language editing.

## Background & Description

The arid lands of southwestern Nuevo León were long considered devoid of aquatic fauna. A cambarellid crayfish and a pupfish were first obtained from the large spring of [[El Potosí]] in 1948. The crayfish was described as *Cambarellus alvarezi* by Villalobos (1952) and the pupfish as *Cyprinodon alvarezi* by Miller (1976). A dwarf pupfish, *Megupsilon aporus*, was collected there in 1961 and described by Miller & Walters (1972). The genus *Megupsilon* is a dwarf endemic.

Subsequent exploration of a pluvial lake area in the uplands, begun in 1983 and continued in 1984 and 1988, revealed further spring-restricted endemics. Each spring generally held one pupfish and one crayfish, with the smallest spring holding only the pupfish. Three pupfishes were described by Lozano-Vilano & Contreras-Balderas (1993), and the crayfishes remain undescribed. Several snail species were also found, with *Valvata beltrani* described by Contreras-Arquieta (1993) and a second *Valvata* species in press.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site-level habitat notes**
- Charco La Palma: spring head of 4 to 4.5 m², 1.4 m deep, with a small outlet pool of about 6 m². Water was used for drinking, orchard irrigation and cattle.
- La Trinidad: originally about 1 ha, reduced to under 0.5 ha and then to less than 0.1 ha, with water more than 1 m below its original level.
- Charco Azul: extensive spring complex of about 1.5 ha with pools 2 to 4 m deep, which fed a small creek.
- La Presa: spring area of about 0.8 ha, with pools 1 to 2 m deep and an outlet creek.
- El Potosí: spring of about 1 ha with depths above 2.5 m, and vegetation including *Ceratophyllum*, *Najas*, *Lemna*, *Nasturtium*, *Utricularia*, *Typha* and *Scirpus*. Water flowed downslope through distributaries for 1 to 1.5 km.

**Threats**
- Introduced goldfish (*Carassius auratus*) were present at Potosí before 1961 but remained at low numbers.
- Largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*) were collected at Potosí in 1974, and their predation was linked to the loss of large individuals of the endemics.
- Groundwater overexploitation was the dominant driver across all sites, as described below.

**Regional water context**
The authors cite a 1981 national plan indicating that 33 of 36 aquifers were being depleted at an average of 1 m per year, that 35 of 36 were overexploited, and that 12 of 36 were saline. Surface water quality was poor in 24 of 37 regions. Most endangered Mexican fishes come from arid and semi-arid zones, with 115 of 135 cited in an unpublished analysis.

## Key Findings

- **Charco La Palma** (*Cyprinodon longidorsalis*): discovered in 1984 with a population of roughly 50 to 70 individuals. Water dropped steadily, and the spring was found dried to a shallow, filled basin with only mosquito larvae in 1994. The species persisted for about 10 years after discovery.
- **Ojo La Trinidad** (*C. inmemoriam*): described from a single mature male. The spring was almost dry by April 1984 and contained only two fungus-covered female crayfish in 1985. The species disappeared in less than 2 years.
- **Charco Azul** (*C. veronicae*, *Valvata beltrani*, *Valvata* sp.): populations of about 10,000 to 12,000 pupfish and crayfish were recorded at discovery. Water fell by more than 1 m after 1988, and in November 1994 only pupfish remained in 1 to 3 pools. The crayfish had disappeared. The snails were found dead as dry shells at discovery.
- **Ojo de Agua La Presa** (*C. ceciliae*): a water-level drop exceeding 1.5 m was recorded by June 1990, with no fish left. The spring dried completely in 1991, and the species was lost within about 2 years of discovery.
- **El Potosí** (*C. alvarezi*, *Megupsilon aporus*, *Cambarellus alvarezi*): the 1968 census recorded 10,000 to 12,000 individuals each of two endemics and 5,000 to 6,000 dwarf pupfish. In 1984 the pool surface was reduced by 90%, and by 1985 the pool had nearly disappeared. In November 1994 only a few specimens of the endemics remained.
- **Management and law**: the 1988 General Environmental Law did not protect these fish. The 1993 federal listing included only the Potosí forms. The 1994 Official Mexican Norm also recognized the Potosí endemics but not the Sandia forms, and it was poorly enforced.
- **Attempted conservation**: dwarf pupfish were moved to side springs in 1974, and a 1976 gillnetting campaign removed 286 bass before it was halted for lack of success.
- **Conclusion**: the authors state that regulation must be applied across geohydrological basins, and that the surviving springs should be maintained as indicators of agricultural sustainability.
- **Editorial review (1995)**: the reviewer described the paper as timely but a series of notes lacking integration. The reviewer also advised English language editing and suggested citing studies from Cuatro Ciénegas, Coahuila, which was recently designated a biological reserve. Related sources included [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] studies by [[Wendell L. Minckley]] and [[Ramamoorthy et al. 1993]].
