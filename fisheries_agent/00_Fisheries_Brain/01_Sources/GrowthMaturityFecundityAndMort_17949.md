# Growth, Maturity, Fecundity and Mortality in the Relatively Unexploited Lake Whitefish of Great Slave Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 study by W. A. Kennedy, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, examines the [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*) of [[Great Slave Lake]], the only large North American lake that was essentially unexploited until commercial fishing began in 1945. Ages were determined for 6,571 whitefish caught 1944–1949, using scale reading. Smaller samples supplied the length–weight relationship, maturity schedule, sex ratio, spawning frequency and fecundity estimates. The central result is an unexploited total annual mortality rate of 61 per cent for fish aged 17–22, which did not change measurably after four years of commercial gill-netting. The author concludes that a more intensive fishery would probably increase sustained yield.

## Background & Description

The lake is the fifth-largest in North America, with a total area of 11,070 square miles, of which 10,430 square miles are water. Commercial fishing began late in the 1945 season following recommendations by [[D. S. Rawson]] under the [[Fisheries Research Board of Canada]]. The author was instructed in 1946 to study the effect of the fishery. Rawson (1951) recorded at least 21 other fish species in the lake; the [[Lake Trout]] is nearly as important commercially as whitefish, and [[Inconnu]], [[Northern Pike]] and [[Walleye]] together make up about 5 per cent of the commercial catch.

The whitefish is described as a salmonid whose scales have well-defined annuli, with no false annuli formed, a feature attributed to the northern location. Two forms are discussed: the normal whitefish and a "black whitefish" in the northeast end of the lake, which is darker, longer, more compressed and has softer flesh. A "dwarf whitefish" form is also suspected.

**Methods (summarised):**
- Seven scale-collection stations (I–VII), each a five-mile radius, ordered from moderately to extremely oligotrophic water.
- Commercial catches sampled in 54-inch (stretched) gill-nets, with subsamples of about 100 fish.
- Standard gangs of graded gill-net mesh (1¼ to 5½ inches) used for additional samples.
- Scales mounted dry and read at about 30× magnification; at least two scales per fish.
- Ages assigned to 5,321 commercial and 1,250 standard-gang whitefish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Growth is restricted to the open-water season. Scale growth begins in mid-June, is rapid in July and August, and ceases by the end of September. The author links this to periods of high water temperature, with the fastest growth near the annual temperature maximum. Spawning occurs from September to January.

Net selection strongly influences the apparent size structure. Gill-nets preferentially catch the largest fish of younger age-classes, and the mesh size used determines which size range is captured. Standard-gang and commercial samples therefore show different apparent growth curves, which the author attributes to gear selectivity rather than true differences.

The northeast end of the lake (Station VII) appears to be a distinct, sparsely populated population. Its fish are larger, grow faster and have a flattened catch-curve dome, which the author attributes to immigration rather than local recruitment. The author cites tag-recapture evidence that most whitefish stay within a few miles of their point of capture.

## Key Findings

**Age, growth and size**
- Length–weight relationship: log y = 3.333 log x − 8.690 (weight in pounds, length in inches), equivalent to y = 0.000204x^2.88. No sex or maturity difference was found.
- Maturity: 50 per cent of fish are mature at about age 8.8 (13.5 in, 1.21 lb); 100 per cent at about age 13 or older (18 in or more, 2.78 lb or more). Fish from age 4 to 13 mature across a size range of about 7–18 inches and 0.3–2.8 pounds.
- Growth is slower than in most whitefish populations studied. Station VII fish grow faster, as do the black whitefish of the northeast end.
- Growth rate increases gradually from Station I to Station VII, a southwest-to-northeast gradient with no clear explanation.

**Reproduction**
- Sex ratio: approximately 1:1 (451 males to 467 females, P = 0.1).
- Only about half of mature females spawn in any given year (38 "large eggs" versus 38 "small eggs"), suggesting a roughly biennial spawning cycle, as is also suspected for lake trout, char and ciscoes elsewhere.
- Fecundity: egg counts were made on 21 mature females caught 14 August 1950. Fecundity rises with body weight, and the author estimates about 39,400 eggs for a 25-pound fish. Figure 2 is referenced but not reproduced in the text.

**Mortality**
- Total annual mortality for fish aged 17–22 is 0.61 (61 per cent), used as the unexploited baseline.
- Four years of commercial fishing produced no measurable change in mortality. Apparent year-to-year differences (1946 highest, 1949 lowest) are attributed to sampling variation and fishing depth.
- Station-specific rates ranged from 0.51 (Station V) to 0.63 (Station IV); Station VII showed 0.52 because of immigration.
- Year-classes appear to be of roughly equal strength.

**Population dynamics and management**
- Modelling (Table IX, three hypotheses) indicates that a moderate mortality rate acting at all ages can account for the enormous egg-to-adult loss. Thousands of eggs are produced for each surviving adult, so a low egg fertilisation rate or high early mortality need not be assumed.
- Most spawning is by fish too small to be fully vulnerable to the 54-inch gear, and about half of the spawn comes from fish too small to be caught at all. The fishery is therefore unlikely to impair spawning even if intensified.
- Each year-class reaching commercial size is losing more weight to natural mortality than it gains through growth, so harvesting it promptly would yield more.
- **Conclusion:** a more intensive fishery would probably increase the sustained annual yield of whitefish.

**Limitations noted by the author**
- Maturity, sex ratio, spawning frequency and egg-count data are based on small samples; the author argues that substantially different values would not change the main conclusions.
- Standard-gang samples are not random, and the Station VII population may be partly composed of a distinct form.
