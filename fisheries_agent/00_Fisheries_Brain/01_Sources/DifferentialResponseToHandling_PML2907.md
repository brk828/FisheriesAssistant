# Differential Response to Handling Stress by Florida, Northern, and Hybrid [[Largemouth Bass]]

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 study compares the physiological and survival responses of two electrophoretically identified [[Largemouth Bass]] subspecies, the [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides floridanus*) and the [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] (*M. s. salmoides*), and their reciprocal F1 hybrids, to handling and net-confinement stress. Fish were reared in 0.04 ha ponds and then subjected to hauling and confinement experiments in hatchery tanks. The authors used [[Plasma Glucose]], [[Plasma Chloride]] and mortality as indicators of stress. The main result is that the four strains did not differ in resting plasma values, but they did differ in their responses to confinement and survival, with northern fish generally performing best. The work was framed as a test of whether subspecific differences in physiology, suspected from differing latitudinal origins, affect culture and management decisions.

## Background & Description

[[Largemouth Bass]] is a centrarchid game fish that is widely cultured and stocked in the United States. Two subspecies are recognized. The northern form (*M. s. salmoides*) occurs north of the Gulf of Mexico grade belt, and the Florida form (*M. s. floridanus*) occurs in peninsular and subtropical waters. The two are morphologically similar and can be hard to tell apart, so the authors used [[Electrophoretic Identification]] of protein markers to assign individual fish to strain.

Earlier studies that compared growth and survival between subspecies gave inconsistent results. The authors attributed part of this ambiguity to misidentification of subspecies and to indiscriminate stocking. They also noted that the subspecies may differ in temperature tolerance and in their underlying physiology, which would matter for hatchery handling and stocking decisions.

The study had two main aims:
- To determine whether [[Florida Largemouth Bass]], [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] and their reciprocal [[Hybrid Largemouth Bass]] strains differ in their responses to confinement and handling stress.
- To use [[Plasma Glucose]] (an indicator of generalized stress) and [[Plasma Chloride]] (an indicator of osmoregulatory disturbance) as physiological measures, with survival as a practical and economically relevant endpoint.

The paper reports only the stress and survival components of a larger ongoing growth and survival study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rearing and stocking.** Experimental fish came from parental broods that were spawned in spring 1984 and identified by electrophoresis of fin tissue. Four experimental strains were produced: Florida (F × F), northern (N × N), and the two reciprocal crosses (N × F and F × N; female parent listed first). Fish were trained on pelleted [[Biodiet]] feed and stocked at about 40 days of age into 0.04 ha ponds.

- Strains were stocked separately into individual ponds, with a mean stocking weight of 1.3 g.
- A communal pond held equal numbers of all four strains, with a mean stocking weight of 2.0 g. Strains were marked with fin clips.
- Separately stocked fish were grown for 111 days, and communally stocked fish for 127 days, before seining.
- Separately stocked fish were 120 ± 5 g (northern) at harvest; Florida fish were lighter (the value is illegible in the scan); hybrids were intermediate in weight.
- Communal-pond fish weighed 82 ± 2 g (Florida) and 152 ± 3 g (northern), with hybrids again intermediate.
- Sample size for the blood and stress analyses was N = 24 fish per strain from separate ponds.

**Water quality during confinement.** Stress tests used [[Hatchery Culture]] tanks with constantly renewed well water:

- Temperature: 23 °C, within 1 °C of pond temperature
- Dissolved oxygen: 5–6 mg/L
- pH: 7.3
- Hardness: 320 mg/L
- Dissolved carbon dioxide: 35 mg/L

Pelleted feed was withheld for 48 hours before experiments.

**Stress protocols.** The primary stressor in all treatments was a 12–24 h [[Confinement Stress]] in nylon nets (76 × 76 cm, open at the top, submerged 30 cm). Fish could swim upright but were in close contact with each other and the net walls. As fish died or were sampled, the net volume was reduced to keep confinement intensity constant. Fish were then moved to 800 L recovery tanks with flowing well water.

- **Treatment S-1 (hauling plus confinement):** Fish were moved by a 15-minute [[Hauling Stress]] procedure at loading densities of 17.5–28.6 g fish/L, in water treated with saline, Furacin and an anesthetic. Six fish per strain were bled after hauling. The remaining fish were confined for 24 h, with six fish per group bled at 3, 6, 12 and 24 h of confinement and on recovery days 1, 3, 5, 8 and 14. Dead fish were counted in decreasing population size, while fish sampled for blood were counted as alive. A water-supply failure for 12 h after 24 h of recovery may have biased plasma chloride and mortality values for NxN fish from recovery day 3 onward.
- **Treatments S-2 and S-3 (mortality only):** No blood was taken. Fish were acclimatized in raceways for 14 days, and were fed Biodiet for the first 12 days. In S-2, all strains were confined for 24 h, except F × F fish, which were released after 12 h because they were losing the ability to remain upright. S-3 used a reduced-severity protocol (the excerpt indicates 12 h at full severity followed by 12 h at reduced severity for some groups).

**Statistics.** The authors used analysis of variance with Tukey's multiple-comparison test ([[Tukey Test]], 1949) on means, and significance was set at p < 0.05.

## Key Findings

- **Resting physiology was similar.** No differences in [[Plasma Glucose]] or [[Plasma Chloride]] concentrations were detected among the four strains in unstressed fish.

- **Confinement produced strain-specific responses.** After confinement, some strains, reported as Florida and hybrid fish, had significantly higher plasma glucose and chloride concentrations than northern fish. The OCR of the abstract is partly garbled, so the exact strain and time-point pattern should be checked against the original paper.

- **Northern fish had the best recovery and survival.** The abstract reports that northern fish showed high survival, given as 96%, compared with lower survival in the other strains. Some of the other survival percentages are illegible in the scan.

- **Florida fish were the most sensitive.** F × F fish had to be released after 12 h during the S-2 treatment because they lost their ability to stay upright. Overall, 8% of F × F fish died in these tests, but the scan of this figure is unclear.

- **Hybrids were intermediate.** For weight, and in some stress and survival comparisons, the hybrids fell between the two parental subspecies.

- **Management implications.** The authors concluded that subspecific differences in stress physiology and survival are real and matter for hatchery culture and stocking. Culturists and managers should consider which strain or hybrid is used, and which receiving waters it is placed in, because stocking is costly and survival depends on strain.

- **Limitations.** The study covers only one set of ponds, one water-quality regime (23 °C, pH 7.3), and a single confinement protocol. Some blood and mortality data for NxN fish may be biased by a water-supply failure. Several numerical values in the source are illegible after scanning.
