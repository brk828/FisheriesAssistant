# Speciation in Fossil Molluscs and Living Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 critical commentary, published in the *Biological Journal of the Linnean Society*, rebuts [[Williamson 1981]], who interpreted changes in a [[Lake Turkana]] fossil sequence as [[Speciation]] events. The authors (Fryer, Greenwood & Peake) argue that the variability of freshwater prosobranch gastropods and bivalves is well established, that simultaneous shifts across 10 lineages are more plausibly [[Ecophenotypic Variation]] responses to environmental change, and that living [[Cichlid Fishes]] from African lakes offer far better evidence of rapid speciation than fossil molluscs. The paper does not criticize the [[Punctuated Equilibrium]] model itself.

## Background & Description

The paper is a rejoinder in an ongoing debate. The authors had earlier critiqued Williamson's Turkana work, and Williamson had replied. The authors argue that Williamson's reply misreads their points and contains several misquotations. Their critique rests on four lines of argument:

1. The considerable shell-form variability of [[Prosobranch Gastropods]] and [[Freshwater Bivalves]].
2. The lack of a stable taxonomic baseline. Williamson's thesis classification differs from the one used in his *Nature* paper.
3. The untested alternative that the Turkana variation is ecophenotypic or under simple genetic control.
4. The view that living African lake fishes better illustrate rapid, punctuational speciation than fossil molluscs do.

Williamson's original claims are summarized as follows. He held that freshwater prosobranchs show narrow phenotypic ranges. He treated "Raupian" shell geometry changes as genetically controlled and as signalling speciation, while treating size, thickness and colour as minor ecophenotypic traits. The authors reject this distinction as an unestablished a priori assumption.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Variability in gastropods and bivalves.** The authors cite evidence from [[Lake Tanganyika]] for intraspecific shell variation in *Edgaria nassa*, *Syrnolopsis lacustris*, *Anceya giraudi*, *Paramelania damoni* and *P. irridescens*. They also cite [[Bellamya Unicolor]], described as "extremely polymorphic" (Van Damme, 1984), and *Soapilia dageti* from Africa. Bivalve variability is documented in Tevesz & Carter (1980).

**Environmental control of shell form.** Vermeij (1980) linked growth rate to allometric shell-shape change. Kemp & Burtness (1984) showed in *Littorina littorea* that slow growers have thick, attenuate shells and rapid growers have thin, globose ones, attributed to limits on calcium carbonate secretion. Gould's work on the land snail *Cerion* is also cited.

**Turkana Suregei and Guomde levels.** All 10 lineages deviate from ancestral form at the Suregei level. Three of four gastropod lineages show diminishing globosity and temporary loss of the globose form. All were epifaunal deposit feeders. The fourth, an infaunal deposit feeder, shows the reverse pattern. Boucot (1982) called the simultaneous changes "a strange coincidence" but "entirely plausible from the ecophenotypic point of view".

**Patchy environments.** The authors note that dispersal-limited freshwater snails in patchy habitats can show diverse morphologies over distances of a few metres, a pattern also described in the marine [[Nucella Lapillus]].

**Clonal organisms.** For [[Melanoides Tuberculata]], the authors suggest many clones may be involved, citing work on [[Daphnia]] (Carvalho, 1985; Lynch, 1984). Livshits & Fishelson (1983) and Livshits et al. (1984) found both [[Parthenogenesis]] and bisexual populations in Israel and Sinai, which undermines Williamson's assumption that the species is an obligate parthenogen.

**African lake cichlids.** Speciation in [[Lake Nabugabo]], [[Lake Malawi]] and [[Lake Victoria]] is described as rapid and, by [[Electrophoresis]] evidence (Kornfield, 1978; Sage et al., 1984), extremely recent. Sibling species often differ in male coloration, territorial behaviour and distribution and coexist without interbreeding, yet cannot be separated on morphometric criteria.

## Key Findings

- **Variability is real.** Prosobranchs and bivalves show substantial intraspecific shell variation, both geographic and within populations. The authors argue Williamson's "phenotypic stasis" premise is false.
- **Genetic versus environmental control is unresolved.** Palmer (1985) showed that spiral sculpture in *Nucella emarginata* is inherited in a Mendelian manner, but its expression is modified by environment. Palmer concluded that "quantum" fossil changes could reflect a few gene-frequency shifts rather than a speciation event. He judged the speciation claim for Turkana "tenuous at best".
- **Simultaneous changes favour ecophenotypy.** Coordinated deviation across 10 lineages, six of them bivalves, is more plausibly environmental than speciational. The authors also reject Williamson's claim that the lineages were ecologically independent.
- **Transient "new species" are implausible.** All 10 allegedly new species went extinct on contact with parent stock. The authors contrast this with [[Lake Victoria]] and [[Lake Malawi]], where widespread and endemic species coexist.
- **Fossils have limits.** Fossils preserve only morphological consequences of speciation, and speciation may leave no morphological trace, as in cichlids. Fossils also cannot test interclonal competition or interbreeding in nature.
- **Living cichlids are better evidence.** Techniques such as karyology, electrophoresis, chromatography, serology and behavioural observation are available for living cichlids and largely unavailable for fossils. The authors cite *Cichlasoma* polymorphism in Mexico, resolved only through ecology, breeding behaviour and electrophoresis (Kornfield et al., 1982).
- **Note added in proof (Kat, pers. comm.).** Diploid chromosome numbers in *Bellamya* vary (reported as 10, 12, 18, 22 and 24), and specimens with 10, 18 and 22 chromosome numbers were grouped into one species in one of Williamson's figures. This suggests considerable unrecognized genetic variation within shell-defined taxa.
- **Conclusion.** The authors argue that fossil and living assemblages are mutually illuminating, and that Williamson's refusal to revise positions hinders understanding.
