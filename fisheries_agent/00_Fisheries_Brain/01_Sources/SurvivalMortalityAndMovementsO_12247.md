# Survival, Mortality, and Movements of White Catfish and Brown Bullheads in Clear Lake, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game study, by George W. McCammon and Charles M. Seeley of the [[Inland Fisheries Branch]], tagged and released [[White Catfish]] (*Ictalurus catus*) and [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus nebulosus*) in [[Clear Lake]] to estimate survival, mortality, and movement. Two tagging studies were run: one in summer 1952 and one in winter 1954–55. The authors compared three tag types, found no statistically significant difference in total returns, but judged the [[Hydrostatic Tag]] unsuitable for multi-year work. Both catfish stocks were found to be lightly exploited, with apparent annual survival near 0.8 for white catfish. Movements were non-directional, and the authors concluded that large, underfished stocks exist in the lake.

## Background & Description

Clear Lake lies at an elevation of 1,325 feet in Lake County, northern California. It has an average surface area of about 40,000 acres and a maximum depth of about 50 feet. The lake is highly fertile and supports a large warmwater sport fishery. Earlier work included the fishery history by Murphy (1951), boat creel censuses by Pintler (1956, 1957a, 1957b), [[Largemouth Bass]] tagging by Kimsey (1957), and food studies of fingerling largemouth bass.

The two ictalurid species are the only catfish present in the lake and both are important to the game catch. Boat creel records show white catfish made up nearly 80 percent of the catch sample in 1948 and 1949, 12 percent in 1953 and 1954, and almost 40 percent in 1956. Brown bullheads contributed 0.4 percent (1954) to 9.7 percent (1955) of the boat catch. Creel data, however, under-represent bullheads, which are abundant at tributary mouths in late winter and spring and are consistently more abundant than white catfish in commercial carp and blackfish seine records.

The work formed part of the [[Dingell-Johnson Project]] (California F-2-R), "A Study of the Catfish Fishery of California," which began in 1951. Its objectives were to obtain reliable survival and mortality estimates and to define fish movements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat and capture site:** Fish were captured at [[The Narrows]], a constriction between the eastern and western parts of the lake, using unbaited [[Fyke Net]]s. The most productive sets were at 15 to 30 feet depth, left undisturbed for at least five days.
- **Release geography:** The 1952 study released all fish at The Narrows. The 1954–55 study used four stations: The Narrows, [[Clear Lake Oaks]], Nice (nine miles northwest), and [[Jago's Resort]] (seven miles southeast).
- **Hydrologic link:** Bullhead recaptures in [[Cache Creek]], below the Clear Lake Impoundment Dam (at least six fish), suggest that substantial numbers may pass the dam under high runoff. Runoff was well above average in winters 1955–56 and 1957–58.
- **Regional comparison:** The authors compared their results with [[Channel Catfish]] tagging in the lower Colorado River and Sacramento Valley, and with white catfish movement in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]].

## Key Findings

### 1952 Study: Tagging and Returns

- Between August 18 and September 24, 1952, 722 white catfish and 724 brown bullheads (1,446 total) were tagged and released at The Narrows.
- Tag allocation: [[Staple Tag]], 247 white catfish and 248 brown bullheads; [[Disk-Dangler Tag]], 248 white catfish and 247 brown bullheads; Hydrostatic Tag, 227 white catfish and 229 brown bullheads.
- Mean fork length was 10.4 inches for white catfish (range 6.3–18.5) and 9.8 inches for brown bullheads (range 6.6–13.9).
- Anglers returned 110 white catfish tags (15.2 percent) and 47 brown bullhead tags (6.5 percent) over 1952–1959.
- Annual white catfish returns (years 1–7): 13, 20, 23, 23, 19, 5, 7. Brown bullhead returns: 19, 8, 6, 6, 3, 4, 1.

### Tag Comparison

- Chi-square tests found no significant difference among tag types for white catfish (χ² = 3.29, 2 d.f., P > 0.10) or brown bullheads (χ² = 3.05, 2 d.f., P > 0.20).
- Of 20 hydrostatic tags returned from brown bullheads, 13 came in the first 12 months. The abrupt post-year-one decline is read as tag detachment. Three detached tags (one white catfish, two brown bullheads) were found near The Narrows within 14 months.
- Staple and disk-dangler tags returned at a steady rate for seven years. The authors concluded the hydrostatic tag is unsuitable for long-term mortality studies.

### 1952 Survival and Mortality

- The white catfish return pattern rose through year four, which ruled out simple survival ratios or log-decline methods.
- Fish of 10 inches or longer returned at significantly higher rates than smaller fish (χ² = 9.79, 1 d.f., P < 0.01), indicating that many smaller fish were not fully vulnerable to the fishery when tagged. Recruitment was estimated at about Age IV, though the authors flagged several confounding factors, including [[Type C Error]].
- Using recoveries of Age IV and older fish, the apparent mean annual survival of fully vulnerable white catfish was s = 0.81 (a = 0.19; instantaneous mortality i = 0.21).
- After correcting for an assumed 40 percent [[Angler Nonresponse]] (borrowed from a channel catfish study), the estimates were: u = 0.10, v = 0.09, p = 0.11, q = 0.10. The stock was judged "lightly exploited" in 1952–59.
- Brown bullheads also showed significantly higher returns from fish of 10 inches or longer (χ² = 16.79, 1 d.f., P < 0.0005). Survival and fishing rates appeared slightly lower, and natural mortality slightly higher, than for white catfish. A reliable survival estimate could not be derived.

### 1954–55 Study: Methods and Returns

- Tagging ran from November 10, 1954, to January 11, 1955, with the [[Disk-Dangler Tag]] only. Cold-season tagging tested whether summer tagging had caused the low 1952 returns; the authors rejected that hypothesis.
- The original plan was 250 fish of each species at each of four stations. White catfish trapping was difficult, so only 15 were tagged at Jago's Resort and none at Nice. Totals: 1,001 white catfish (484 at The Narrows, 502 at Clear Lake Oaks, 15 at Jago's Resort) and 999 brown bullheads.
- Mean white catfish fork lengths were 8.9 inches (Clear Lake Oaks), 8.8 (The Narrows), and 10.9 (Jago's Resort). Mean brown bullhead lengths were 11.5 (Clear Lake Oaks), 11.8 (Nice), 11.8 (Jago's Resort), and 12.1 (The Narrows).
- Over the first five years, anglers returned 96 white catfish tags (9.6 percent) and 122 brown bullhead tags (12.2 percent).
- Annual white catfish returns: 20, 18, 18, 27, 13. Brown bullhead returns: 37, 29, 36, 9, 11.
- Return rates by release site: white catfish, Clear Lake Oaks 48 (9.6 percent), The Narrows 44 (9.1 percent), Jago's Resort 4 of 15 (26.7 percent, too few for comparison). Brown bullheads, Jago's Resort 41 (16.4 percent), Clear Lake Oaks 34 (13.6 percent), The Narrows 25 (10 percent), Nice 22 (8.8 percent). Differences among bullhead sites were not significant at the 5 percent level.

### 1954–55 Rates and Movements

- Returns from white catfish of 10 inches or longer were significantly higher than from smaller fish (χ² = 4.79, 1 d.f., P < 0.05), but no survival estimate could be derived. The only obtainable parameter was the first-year exploitation rate, u = 0.05 (12/238 after the 40 percent nonresponse correction). This was about half the 1952 estimate, and the authors said both estimates were the same order of magnitude.
- Brown bullheads were essentially fully vulnerable at tagging (mean length 11.8 inches). Using the annual return ratio 85/111, the weighted mean annual survival was s = 0.77 (a = 0.23; i = 0.27). Corrected components were p = 0.08, q = 0.19, u = 0.07, v = 0.16.
- The higher 1954–55 bullhead return rate (12.2 percent versus 6.5 percent) was attributed to complete vulnerability of the tagged sample rather than a change in fishing rate.
- Of 65 white catfish recaptures located in 1954–55, distribution resembled the 1952 pattern: wide, non-directional movement, with concentrations in high-use angling areas such as Clear Lake Oaks, Soda Bay, and Glenhaven.
- Of 26 usable 1952 brown bullhead recovery locations and 47 usable 1954–55 locations, bullheads tagged in one of the three main arms tended to stay there, while those tagged at The Narrows dispersed into adjacent arms.

### Movement Summary

- Of 110 white catfish returns, 87 had usable location data. No regular migration appeared, and no discrete subpopulations were evident.

### Management Implications

- The authors judged both stocks "huge" and underexploited. Relaxing the bag limit and gear rules was considered the most direct route to higher yield.
- In 1959 the combined daily bag limit for white catfish and brown bullheads rose from 15 to 20. The authors doubted this would measurably change yield, given low catch per unit effort from 1947 to 1956.
- Better angling techniques were suggested because tourist anglers struggled to catch either species despite their abundance.
- Current gear rules allow one line with up to three hooks. Liberalizing them (for example, to allow trot lines) could create enforcement and [[Largemouth Bass]] conservation problems, so the benefits were judged questionable. A controlled commercial fishery was rejected for the same reasons, although the authors thought stocks could support one.
- The lake has no closed season or minimum size limit.

### Data Discrepancies

- The summary table lists white catfish total annual mortality as 0.91. This conflicts with the stated survival of 0.81 and the text's a = 0.19, so 0.19 is probably the intended value.
- Brown bullhead survival is given as 0.77 in the results and 0.76 in the summary.
