# Elimination of Fish Populations in Michigan Lakes with Rotenone, 1934-1942

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-population-management

## Executive Summary

This 1949 *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* paper by Robert C. Ball (Michigan State College) summarizes data from 32 Michigan lakes treated with [[Rotenone]] between 1934 and 1942 to remove or reduce unwanted fish populations. A complete or nearly complete kill with recovery of the population was obtained on 18 of these lakes. Most treated lakes were not supporting desirable fish populations before treatment. Productivity, measured as standing crop of recovered fish, ranged from 10.0 to 194.5 pounds per acre, averaging 58.5 pounds per acre, of which 18.2 pounds were legal-sized game fish. Hard-water lakes and warm-water lakes were generally more productive. The paper also stresses that removing fish is only the first step in [[Lake Management]]: many lakes were later repopulated with unwanted species.

## Background & Description

Rotenone and related compounds in [[Derris Root]] and cubé root were recognized as tools for removing fish. The first recorded application was made by the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]], Michigan Department of Conservation, on July 17 and 23, 1934, in two small ponds on the estate of W. O. Briggs at Birmingham, Michigan, to remove a heavy [[Carp Removal]] population. Those early treatments used 0.04 to 0.09 p.p.m., which were too weak. Some fish survived in both ponds.

Later work set the working standard. A concentration of at least 0.5 p.p.m. of derris or cubé root with 5 percent rotenone content is needed for a complete kill (Leonard, 1939). Brown and Ball (1942) showed this concentration is ineffective at temperatures below 48°F.

The 32 lakes were mostly small (all under 22 acres) and varied in water chemistry and depth. Most were treated so a different, presumably better, population could be introduced. Four were treated for other reasons:
- [[Walsh Lake]] was poisoned because its fish, though growing well, were heavily parasitized.
- [[Howe Lake]] had a normally growing population, but carp removal was considered desirable.
- [[Third Sister Lake]] and [[Deep Lake]] were treated as parts of long-range experimental programs.

Data came from several workers and were recorded non-uniformly. Nine lakes had no recovery effort or only partial recovery attempts. Results from formal reports are cross-referenced to Eschmeyer, Leonard, Greenbank, Beckman, Krumholz, and Brown and Ball.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and morphology of the 18 complete-kill lakes (Table 2):**
- Area: less than 1 acre to 22 acres (Table 1 gives Ford Lake as 10.7 acres; Table 2 OCR shows 40.7, likely a scan error).
- Depth: 9 to 90 feet.
- pH: acid to quite alkaline.
- [[Methyl-Orange Alkalinity]]: 5 to 240 p.p.m.
- Geographic spread: southern Michigan to the northern Upper Peninsula.
- Excluded: large lake trout-cisco lakes.

**Hardness classes (Table 3):**
- Soft (under 50 p.p.m.): grand average 28.0 pounds per acre.
- Intermediate (50 to 150 p.p.m.): grand average 53.6 pounds per acre.
- Hard (above 150 p.p.m.): grand average 74.2 pounds per acre.

The author reports a positive correlation between alkalinity and fish production, but with considerable variation among lakes.

**Lake-type comparison (Table 4):**

| Lake type | Total pounds per acre | Legal-sized pounds per acre |
|---|---|---|
| [[Trout Lakes]] | 47.7 | 13.2 (printed as "132" in OCR) |
| [[Warm-Water Lakes]] (bass) | 81.3 | 31.3 |

Bass lakes were considerably more productive than trout lakes. Third Sister Lake was classed as a bass lake, though it is a marginal trout lake.

**Counting limitations:** Recovery of young-of-the-year fish and minnows was incomplete. First-year fish decomposed quickly in some lakes. Total numbers therefore have little significance, though weights are less affected. Some fish may have sunk in deep water and never been recovered.

## Key Findings

- **Scale and outcome:** 32 lakes were treated; 18 had a complete or nearly complete kill with recovery attempted. Table 1 lists species counts and weights for these lakes. Table 2 summarizes limnological data for the 18 complete-kill lakes.
- **Overall productivity:** Lakes averaged 10.6 acres in size. Across the complete-pick-up lakes, the average was 58.5 pounds of fish per acre, 18.2 pounds of which were legal-sized game fish. Average legal game fish density was 68 per acre, considered a close approximation.
- **Productivity range:** 10.0 to 194.5 pounds per acre, with the highest value at [[Clear Lake]].
- **Water hardness:** Hard-water lakes were more productive than soft-water lakes, and warm-water lakes more than trout lakes. The author warns that these patterns are not conclusive.
- **Recovery limits:** Recovery of the full kill could not be verified. Shoreline and bottom conditions prevented collection in many lakes.
- **Post-treatment management:** Twelve complete-kill lakes were trout lakes and six were warm-water lakes. The 12 trout lakes were stocked with hatchery trout, and live minnow bait was prohibited. Six lakes were later repopulated with warm-water fish, three contain only trout, and records for three are incomplete.
- **Causes of repopulation:** Ineffective barrier dams on outlets, incomplete poisoning, and release of unused bait. Half the trout-managed lakes held only warm-water species, which the author attributes to repeated introductions.
- **Central conclusion:** Removing unwanted fish is only partly the job of [[Lake Rehabilitation]]. Preventing reintroduction of unwanted species, through barrier dams, bait control, and monitoring, is needed for removal programs to be worthwhile and economical.
- **Methodological note:** The author declines to draw definite comparisons or conclusions because the data were collected over a long period by several workers. Productivity differences among lakes are considerable, and no single index of productivity explains them.
