# Spatial Distribution and Temperature Selection of Fish Near the Thermal Outfall of a Power Plant During Fall, Winter and Spring

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This EPA Environmental Research Laboratory-Duluth report (EPA-600/3-80-009, January 1980; M. J. Ross and D. B. Siniff, University of Minnesota) uses radio telemetry to study how fish respond to the heated discharge of a coal-fired power plant on the upper [[Mississippi River]] at Cohasset, Minnesota. The primary focus is [[Yellow Perch]], whose reproduction is known from laboratory work to depend on a winter chill period, yet which prefers warmer water in the laboratory. Four species were tracked: yellow perch, [[Northern Pike]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Walleye]]. The authors conclude that temperature, acting together with many other variables, did not alter yellow perch distribution from what would be expected, and that little if any adverse effect on perch reproduction is expected at the site.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Laboratory data showed that yellow perch need a long winter chill (185 days at 4°C) for successful spring spawning (Jones et al. 1977), while adult perch preferred temperatures above 10°C after acclimation to 5°C (McCauley 1977). A thermal discharge could offer perch warm water in winter, raising the question of whether they would choose it.
- **Method rationale:** Conventional sampling could not capture cumulative thermal exposure, so continuous radio telemetry was used to follow individual fish movements and the temperatures they experienced.
- **Study system:** Clay Boswell Station units 1 and 2 (each 69 MW net, on-line 1958 and 1960) use once-through river cooling. The receiving bay is a shallow lentic environment connected to [[Jay Gould Lake]]; the power plant intake sits on [[Blackwater Lake]]. Discharge raises water temperature about 7.2°C above ambient in open-water seasons and about 15°C in winter design conditions. Normal river temperatures range from 0°C to 27°C. The reservoir supports 37 fish species, and perch, bullheads, suckers and burbot have been taken commercially since 1944.
- **Tags:** Transmitters operated at 53 MHz, weighed about 3.5 g in water (location tags) and 4.9 g (temperature tags), and were attached externally via a subdorsal mount. Estimated range was about 0.7 km and battery life averaged about 33 days (30 to 40 days in specifications).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal and chemical changes:** Besides heat, the discharge increases copper and sulfate concentrations, and copper and chlorine occur at elevated levels in the discharge canal. Discharge velocity is 1.1 m/s in open-water seasons and 0.5 m/s in winter, and the bay has a 0.003 to 0.05 m/s current.
- **Fish community:** Thermophilic centrarchids and ictalurids were especially prevalent in the immediate discharge, and the greatest density and number of species occurred there.
- **Vegetation:** Shallow areas support wild rice beds and aquatic vegetation, which the authors link to perch cover and spawning habitat.
- **Competing factors:** Ice-covered winter conditions, predation by northern pike, competition from thermophilic species, and loss of vegetation habitat downstream are proposed as influences beyond temperature.

## Key Findings

**Objectives:** (1) determine winter temperature selection of adult yellow perch near the effluent; (2) determine fish distribution and movement relative to the discharge in fall, winter and spring; (3) determine whether the discharge concentrated perch populations in the spawning season.

**Temperature selection (yellow perch)**
- Overall winter mean temperature selection was **5.4°C** (average of individual means, all data, 10 individuals).
- Within the thermally altered area only, the mean was **6.3°C** by averaged individual means (grouped data mean 7.0°C).
- Individual means ranged from **1.1°C to 9.2°C** (overall), and winter temperatures used by fish in the discharge ranged from **2.8 to 10.7°C**. Variation among individuals was high and significant (P < 0.05).
- Laboratory preferences for the same season (12 to 16°C, Barans and Tubb 1973) were much higher than the field values.
- No significant day–night difference was found in most individuals; 2 of 6 discharge perch were significantly warmer by day, but only by 0.6 to 0.7°C.

**Distribution and movement**
- Northern pike had the largest mean winter home range (18.7 ha), followed by yellow perch (10.6 ha), largemouth bass (3.7 ha) and walleye (2.2 ha).
- Mean daily movement was highest in northern pike and lowest in walleye and largemouth bass.
- Of 31 winter perch, 19 crossed between altered and unaltered waters, 18 dispersed from the discharge bay, and only 2 remained wholly within altered areas.
- All northern pike tracked more than 4 days crossed between waters; both largemouth bass remained entirely in the discharge bay.
- Of 26 perch that dispersed, 58% moved upstream, 35% downstream and 8% into Jay Gould Lake.
- Largemouth bass were in the warmest, shallowest areas: 95% of locations were under 1.5 m deep. Walleye favored deep, cooler center areas (58% of locations deeper than 3 m).

**Autumn behavior**
- In autumn 1975 and 1976, 35 of 53 perch tracked below the discharge moved upstream through the mixing zone; only 2 entered the thermal gradient into the discharge bay, and only briefly.
- None of 9 perch tracked above the confluence entered thermally altered water.
- Perch did not follow the autumn thermal gradient into the bay as predicted from laboratory preference data.

**Associated observations**
- Gonadosomatic indices were similar between altered and unaltered areas: females averaged 17.7% (discharge, n = 20) and 16.8% (unaltered, n = 27); males 2.5% and 1.9%.
- Length and weight did not differ significantly between sexes in discharge and upstream samples.
- Discharge area males were ripe over the whole spawning period; peak spawning occurred about April 30 to May 5, 1975.
- Recapture rate was 9.5% for 116 radio-tagged perch versus 7.7% for 903 Atkins-tagged perch.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Only a small portion of the perch population experienced a transitory, low-level increase in winter temperature; little if any adverse reproductive effect was expected.
- Laboratory single-variable data should be applied to natural systems with caution, and field validation is recommended.
- Yellow perch gonad development can be protected near a thermal effluent if fish can reach attractive, thermally unaltered habitat.

**Methodological caveats**
- Tags were attached to fish averaging 1 to 2% of body weight; in a pond study, dummy tags caused significantly higher mortality (only 2 of 28 dummy-tagged perch recovered versus 23 of 28 controls), but field recapture rates were comparable to standard tags.
- Depth was inferred from location rather than measured.
