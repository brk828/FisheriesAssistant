# Riparian Management: Little Colorado River Case Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #humpback-chub

## Executive Summary

This paper is a case study from the USDA Forest Service Technical Report RM-226 (1993), *Riparian Management: Common Threads and Shared Interests*, a Western Regional Conference held February 4-6, 1993, in Albuquerque, New Mexico. It examines the [[Little Colorado River]] in Arizona as a model for integrated riparian management. The authors argue that management must operate across spatial scales, from the regional [[Coconino Aquifer]] watershed to the reach-level habitat of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], and must also account for the organizational and cultural scales that shape decisions. Key concerns include groundwater pumping, climate-driven flow variability, invasive [[Tamarisk]], sediment loads, and water chemistry near [[Blue Springs]]. The paper also identifies seven "information flow pathologies" that limit how management agencies use ecological knowledge, and it offers a [[Navajo World View]] perspective on planning.

## Background & Description

The document is a conference chapter with five technical coordinators: Barbara Tellman, Hanna J. Cortner, Mary G. Wallace (Water Resource Research Center, University of Arizona), Leonard F. DeBano, and R. H. Hamre (Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station). The text is organized into sections on the watershed, historic change, tamarisk, the humpback chub, scale issues, information flow pathologies, and Navajo cultural perspectives. Its reference list cites hydrologic, geomorphic, climatological, and ecological sources, including Hereford (1982, 1983), Dahm and Molles (1992), Graf (1986), and Wiens (1989).

The river is described as a site where ecological, hydrologic, cultural, and institutional systems overlap. The [[Little Colorado River Gorge]] is a perennial reach that flows into [[Grand Canyon]] within [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. It is proposed as critical habitat by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) and as a candidate for wild and scenic river designation. The gorge is also culturally significant to the [[Navajo Nation]] and [[Hopi Tribe]], with traditional salt-gathering areas located there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Watershed and groundwater.** The [[Coconino Aquifer]] is the large-scale watershed system. It is recharged by the Defiance uplift on the Navajo Nation and the Mogollon Rim/Flagstaff area, and it discharges at Blue Springs, where water emerges from the Redwall Limestone into the gorge. The aquifer holds about 413 million acre-feet, and major withdrawals total roughly 100,000 acre-feet per year for power, forest industries, irrigation, municipalities, and coal. Heavy pumping could reduce the perennial flows the aquifer supports in the southern watershed.

**Surface flow and climate.** Surface flow in the Little Colorado River is ephemeral except where Blue Springs sustains perennial reaches. Gorge precipitation is less than 8 inches per year, while the San Francisco Peaks receive more than 35 inches. The river has summer and spring runoff. Summer runoff reaching the Navajo Nation largely originates from the [[Puerco River]], which carries high sediment loads from eroded, grazed land. Spring runoff is the most dependable continuous flow. Discharge is significantly correlated with precipitation and varies strongly with climate. Median and mean annual discharges are about 165,800 and 189,890 acre-feet (the OCR-degraded text is ambiguous on the exact median), with a large flow year in 1973 and very low flow in 1974. Erosional phases were linked to a 1 degree C rise in annual temperature, a 50 mm drop in precipitation, and large floods. Aggradation was linked to rising precipitation and discharge with declining temperature. Spring discharge from two New Mexico rivers was 6-7 times higher during [[El Nino]] years (Dahm and Molles 1992).

**Springs and water chemistry.** Blue Springs discharges about 211 cubic feet per second (Loughlin 1983). The Blue Springs fault strongly influences water chemistry. Springs west of the fault differ in temperature and dissolved solids from those east of it. Carbonate precipitation on diatoms and debris has created travertine dams, and pools form behind them. These dams can also act as barriers to upstream movement of the humpback chub. Dissolved and suspended material, including nutrients, metals, and radionuclides, may bind to sediment and move downstream.

**Vegetation and channel change.** Phreatophytes deplete water, and tamarisk (salt cedar) has spread widely since its ornamental introduction. Eliminating all cultural water uses south of the Navajo Nation was estimated to increase flow at Winslow by about 60,000 acre-feet. Tamarisk could cover more than 60% of about 5,700 acres of pasture and cropland on the floodplain. Vegetated bars alter fluvial geomorphology and may affect backwater habitat for fish.

## Key Findings

- **Historic change is documented.** Early observers described the river as lined with cottonwood groves and swamps. The Navajo named it "Big Timbers" because floods uprooted large cottonwoods. Beale's 1858 description of good grazing land foreshadowed a large population, which in practice consisted of sheep. During a drought in the 1900s, Navajos fed young cottonwoods to sheep.
- **Tamarisk invasion followed flood suppression.** Tamarisk was first cultivated as an ornamental in 1909 and was not established in the channel until after 1937. Large floods until 1941 had prevented invasion. After flooding declined, vegetation stabilized banks and trapped sediment. Channel width had decreased by about 54% by 1954 (Hereford 1982, as read from OCR).
- **Wetland management is unresolved.** The Navajo Natural Heritage Program was weighing options for wetlands near Winslow. Open questions include whether historical conditions can be restored, whether tamarisk should be eradicated, which native species could replace it, whether native species can survive current hydrology, and how groundwater drawdown and climate change will affect erosion and aggradation.
- **The humpback chub depends on the whole system.** Its life history depends on gorge habitat, seasonal flood-driven debris inputs, sediment supply, and temperature regimes, which vary from the Little Colorado River to Grand Canyon. Cold water from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] may limit spawning in the Grand Canyon, while the Little Colorado River remains an important spawning habitat.
- **Research and conservation are underway.** Arizona State University and the Navajo Natural Heritage Program tagged and tracked a substantial number of humpback chub (exact count unclear in OCR) to study their life history and ecology. The USFWS is mapping stream habitat. These studies contribute to conservation measures under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and to the environmental impact statement for the operation of Glen Canyon Dam.
- **Scale is a central issue.** The authors argue that management plans should not be developed from single-species studies and that questions must be addressed at multiple scales, from groundwater to reach-level habitat to decadal climate variability.
- **Information flow pathologies limit management.** The authors describe seven pathologies: (1) False Analogy, where one ecosystem's knowledge is applied to another; (2) Insufficient Detail, where models are overgeneralized; (3) Short Observational Series, where short funding cycles miss key life-history processes; (4) Managerial Detachment, where managers are separated from local people and culture; (5) Reactions Out of Phase, where action comes too late; (6) Someone Else's Problem, where agencies act only for short-term interests; and (7) Ideological Beliefs, where information is ignored because it conflicts with an ideology. The authors propose adding cultural clashes to this list.
- **Cultural perspectives matter.** Initially the Navajo, Hualapai, and Havasupai tribes were not cooperators in the Glen Canyon Dam planning process, though they were later included reluctantly. The authors note that differing cultural values and goals can impede communication. Navajo worldview emphasizes consensus, careful thought to avoid bad outcomes, and holistic understanding, which the authors contrast with the reductive, planning-based approach of many agencies.
- **Sediment and channel management.** The Little Colorado River supplies a significant share of the sediment that maintains beaches in the Colorado River and Grand Canyon. Historical erosion control on the Colorado River was criticized for taking a single-scale approach, a point drawn from Graf (1986).
