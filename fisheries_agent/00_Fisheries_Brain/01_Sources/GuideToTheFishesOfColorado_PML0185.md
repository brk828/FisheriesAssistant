# Guide to the Fishes of Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This mid-twentieth-century identification guide, prepared by W. Harry Brickley of the [[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] and sponsored by Colorado A&M College, the [[Colorado Game and Fish Department]], and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, catalogs the fishes found in Colorado waters. The guide states that 99 different fishes occur in the state at the time of writing: 64 are native, 33 have been introduced, and 2 are potential residents taken near the border in neighboring states. It is written for amateurs and beginning students, with line drawings rather than photographs, dichotomous keys, standardized measurement definitions, and short notes on distribution, life history and angling value for each species.

## Background & Description

The guide defines a fish as a backboned animal that lives in water, propels and balances itself with fins, and breathes oxygen through gills. Distribution is given in broad terms by drainage: the [[Mississippi River]] system (North Platte, South Platte, Republican and Arkansas rivers), the [[Colorado River]] system draining the western slope, and the [[Rio Grande]] draining the San Luis Valley.

Methods sections explain how to identify and preserve specimens. Small specimens (up to 5 inches) are fixed in 10% formalin and transferred to 70% ethyl or 40% isopropyl alcohol; larger specimens need a right-side belly slit so formalin reaches internal organs. Labels should record location, date and collector. Identification relies on counts and measurements, including total and standard length, body depth categories (very slender at 5.5 and above, to very deep at 2.4 and under), head length, eye diameter, fin ray and spine counts, lateral line scale counts, gill raker counts and pharyngeal tooth formulas. Scientific nomenclature follows the standard binomial rules, with genus capitalized and author names noted where relevant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guide is a taxonomic and distributional reference rather than a study of ecosystem dynamics, so environmental information appears only in species accounts. Recurring habitat associations include:

- **Cold, clear mountain streams:** [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Mountain Whitefish]], [[American Grayling]], [[Longnose Dace]], [[Mountain Sucker]] species.
- **Warm, turbid plains waters:** [[Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Plains Killifish]], [[Plains Topminnow]], [[Gizzard Shad]].
- **Large rivers:** [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Freshwater Drum]].
- **Reservoirs and lakes:** [[Lake Trout]], [[Kokanee Salmon]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[White Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]].

Several accounts note human-driven change. Irrigation withdrawals and increased silting reduced [[Channel Catfish]] in the Platte and Arkansas rivers. Stocking by the Game and Fish Department transplanted [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] into waters once occupied by other cutthroat subspecies. Introduced species such as the [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Carp]] and [[Bluegill]] now dominate many waters, and the guide warns that some introductions, such as the Bluegill, can overpopulate and stunt without predator control.

## Key Findings

- **Inventory:** 99 fish forms in Colorado at the time of writing (64 native, 33 introduced, 2 potential residents); an asterisk marks rare species. The tentative checklist at the end lists 46 families, 78 species and 89 forms including subspecies.
- **Taxonomic coverage:** Keyed families include Clupeidae, Coregonidae, Salmonidae, Thymallidae, Esocidae, Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Ictaluridae, Anguillidae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Serranidae, Centrarchidae, Percidae, Sciaenidae and Cottidae.
- **Rare native fishes:** The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is described as the largest minnow in the New World, reaching 6 feet and 80 pounds, and the [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Sucker]] are restricted to the Colorado system.
- **Notable introductions:** [[Kokanee Salmon]] (planted in Skaguay, Green Mountain and Granby reservoirs), [[Lake Atlantic Salmon]] (Twin Lakes, about 1885, probably extinct), [[Gizzard Shad]] as forage, and [[White Bass]], which showed promise in eastern plains reservoirs.
- **Life-history examples:** Brook Trout lay about 500 eggs at 8 inches; Rainbow Trout about 1,000 eggs at 10 inches; the Cutthroat Trout matures in 3 years and a 10-inch female lays about 800 eggs; the Carp female yields about 500,000 eggs; and the White Bass produces 650,000 to 970,000 eggs per female.
- **Angling and food value:** Rainbow Trout supplies most stocked trout; Carp is characterized as undervalued food and a fly-rod game fish; Smallmouth and Largemouth Basses are described as valued game fish, with Smallmouth limited by suitable habitat.
- **Conservation caution:** The guide advises against encouraging [[Brown Bullhead]] and notes that the [[Blue Catfish]] was recently introduced without confirmed establishment.
- **Reporting:** Unknown or rare specimens should be preserved and sent to the [[Colorado Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] at Colorado A&M College (Fort Collins) or the University of Colorado Museum (Boulder), as anglers may find what scientists miss.
