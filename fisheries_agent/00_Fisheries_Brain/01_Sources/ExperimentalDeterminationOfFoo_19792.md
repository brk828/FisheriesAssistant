# Experimental Determination of Food Consumption of Pike (*Esox lucius*) for Growth and Maintenance

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study measured the food requirements of pike (*Esox lucius*) held in outdoor tanks under conditions designed to simulate the field. It is the first of two papers on the food consumption of the [[Lake Windermere]] pike population. The authors separated food intake into maintenance (food needed to hold body weight constant) and growth (food consumed above maintenance). Maintenance requirements varied seasonally and appeared largely independent of [[Water Temperature]], peaking in June. The annual [[Maintenance Coefficient]] was 1.385 g per gram per year, with no apparent decline with body size. Under ad libitum feeding, 1 g of food produced 0.437 g of pike. Pike showed low maintenance costs and a high food-to-flesh conversion relative to the species compared in the paper.

## Background & Description

**Species and study system.** The work focuses on the northern pike, a predatory freshwater fish. The study was based at the [[Freshwater Biological Station]] on [[Lake Windermere]], the largest lake in England, located in the [[Lake District]]. Pike in Windermere prey mainly on [[European Perch]] (*Perca fluviatilis*). In winter, [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) and [[Arctic Char]] (*Salvelinus alpinus willughbii*) are also eaten in quantity. [[Three-Spined Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) and [[Minnow]] (*Phoxinus phoxinus*) are subsidiary prey, especially in summer. Minnows were the food supplied in the experiments.

**Theoretical framework.** The paper follows the [[Beverton And Holt Model]] for food consumption, production and yield in exploited fish populations:

- Maintenance consumption is modeled as a power function of body weight, with an exponent equal to or below 1. An exponent below 1 means maintenance per gram falls as fish grow.
- Short-term growth is expressed as specific growth rate (% per unit time), following Brown (1957).
- Long-term growth follows the [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Equation]], which relates length and weight to age through an asymptote and a catabolism constant *K*.
- Following [[Brody And Proctor]] and Kostitzin, the authors note that food is probably used more efficiently at some intake below maximum than at ad libitum feeding. Their experiment therefore covered both extremes and the range between them.

**Methods.**

- Pike were kept in ten outdoor galvanized iron tanks (1.25 × 0.75 × 0.75 m, water depth 50 cm). Tanks were supplied in series at ambient temperature, under beech trees for summer shade. Temperature was not otherwise controlled.
- Cover was provided by the aquatic moss *Fontinalis antipyretica*.
- Experimental fish were progeny of one male and one female, raised from eggs of mature wild fish taken in spring 1959. Fish were of known age.
- Fish and food were anaesthetised with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222, 1:15,000) before weighing (to 0.01 g) and measuring (total length, snout tip to tail fork).
- Mean weekly tank temperatures tracked the surface temperature of Lake Windermere.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Seasonal regulation.** Maintenance and growth both followed a seasonal cycle that the authors describe as largely independent of temperature. Maintenance peaked in late June, when the requirement nearly doubled. The authors suggest this is driven by increasing daylength, with the peak near the summer solstice. Activity stayed low throughout the experiment, and movement was mostly limited to feeding, which may explain the uniform results.
- **Temperature effects.** Temperature was not held constant. In February, when tank temperatures fell close to zero, some fish lost weight while maintenance-ration fish did not.
- **Comparison with other species.**
  - *Plaice* (*Pleuronectes platessa*): Dawes' data, as used by Beverton and Holt, give a maintenance coefficient of 20.48 g per gram per year, far above the pike value.
  - *Brown trout*: Brown reported that maintenance diminished with size, reaching about 60 mg per gram per week at 11.5 °C. This is roughly 3.12 g per gram per year, comparable to pike once temperature is considered.
  - *Scholz (1932)*: For young pike at 18–19 °C, Scholz estimated 1–2% of body weight per day, equivalent to 3.7–7.3 g per gram per year. The authors consider this consistent with their results given the higher temperature.
- **Growth in captivity.** Frost and Kipling's wild-fish data for Windermere give 20 cm at the end of year one and 36 cm at the end of year two for medium-paced growers. Experimental fish reached 21.0 cm at one year and 27.8–28.3 cm at 18 months, which the authors judge comparable. They conclude the tank environment did not impair growth.

## Key Findings

**1. Seasonal maintenance requirement**

- Peak requirement of about 45 mg of minnow per gram of pike per week occurred in late June.
- Requirement fell through July and August to a steady level of about 25 mg/g per week, held from October until about February.
- A gradual rise from February accelerated in May, reaching the June peak.
- The mean weekly maintenance ration was 26.7 mg/g.

**2. Annual maintenance coefficient**

- The annual maintenance coefficient (*c*) was **1.385 ± 0.053 g per gram per year**, derived from four fish of known age (21–107 g) held for 352 days.
- The exponent for body size (*n*) was taken as 1, since no decline in requirement per gram with size was observed. Even a 400 g fish in a preliminary trial showed no difference per gram.

**3. Growth and conversion efficiency**

- Growth followed the same seasonal pattern as maintenance: it rose from February, peaked in late June, fell through July, held steady, and declined in December, then ceased in midwinter.
- Under ad libitum feeding, 1 g of food produced **0.437 g of pike** (the overall efficiency of utilization, *e*, from Table II).
- Net conversion was 2.28 g food per gram of pike. Gross conversion was given as 3.41 in the discussion, while Table II reports a mean of 3.31.
- The **maximum coefficient of utilization** (*e₀*) was **0.485 ± 0.033**, found at feeding levels below maximum intake.

**4. Maintenance efficiency and energy coefficient**

- The efficiency of utilization at maintenance (*e_m*) averaged **0.476**, from Kostitzin's equation solved by successive approximation.
- The maintenance energy coefficient (*Q* = *e_m* × *c*) was **0.659**.

**5. Comparative position**

- The authors conclude that pike have relatively low maintenance requirements and a relatively high conversion of food to body substance, making them outstanding among the species compared.
- Comparisons cited include brown trout (gross conversion 2.3–7.1), plaice (net conversion about 5:1), and green sunfish (*Apomotis cyanellus*, net conversion 2.3–3.9, per Moore 1941).

**6. Implications for population food consumption**

- The authors argue that maintenance food, which depends strongly on fish size, has greater influence on total consumption than growth food. The second paper (Johnson, 1966) applies these results to the resident Windermere population.
- The low activity of tank-held fish may limit how far these figures apply to wild pike, which move more.
