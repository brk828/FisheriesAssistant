# Iron Salts in Ichthyopathology: A Histological and Histochemical Experimental Contribution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 paper by J. Amorim Machado Cruz, published by the [[Instituto de Zoologia Dr. Augusto Nobre]] as its No. 106, is an experimental study of how dissolved [[Iron Salts]] harm freshwater fish. Using [[Carassius Auratus]] (goldfish) exposed to four iron salts in water, the author combined autopsy, [[Histochemistry]] and [[Histology]] to examine the gills, digestive tract, liver, kidneys and other organs. The paper argues that iron salts act in three ways: a lesional action on the respiratory epithelium, physical blocking of gill filaments by mucus and ferruginous precipitate, and an internal toxic action after iron is absorbed through the digestive tract. The author concludes that the classic view of [[Carpenter 1927]], that iron acts only externally and mechanically, is incomplete.

## Background & Description

The author frames the work within the growing concern over industrial pollution of air, fresh water and seawater, including discharges from mines, paper, textile and chemical industries, detergents, insecticides and radioactive products. Early toxicology mainly measured lethal doses, survival times and lethality curves. Comparability improved after a 1951 meeting of the [[Federation of Sewage and Industrial Wastes Association]] in the United States and a 1952 European agreement on standard bioassay conditions. The author argues that these practical measures say little about the physiopathological action of toxicants, which is the gap this paper addresses.

Historically, [[Carpenter 1927]] proposed that heavy metal salts form an insoluble compound with mucus on the gills, causing death by suffocation. This view was widely accepted until the mid-1950s. [[Laroze]] questioned it in 1950 and 1955, proposing that metals cause true intoxication by acting on gill cell protoplasm and blocking enzymes. [[Kuhn and Korcke]] (1956) described a progressive gill lesion in fish exposed to ferric chloride, beginning with oedema and changes in branchial epithelial cells and ending, in severe cases, in near-total destruction of the epithelium.

The author lists guiding questions, including whether iron acts externally, internally, or both; the main route of absorption; the minimum mortal dose; the organs where iron accumulates; whether effects are reversible; and whether iron acts directly or through the pH drop it causes.

The author's list of other publications from the institute, covering isopods, amphipods, spiders, Lepidoptera and sardine biology, is not relevant to the study and is not summarised here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The experiments were run in controlled laboratory conditions, not in natural waters. Fish were acclimated for some hours in distilled water. Solutions were prepared from distilled water and iron salts at the start of each experiment. Water temperature was 15–20 °C. Measured pH ranged from 3.5 to 6, with 3.5 to 5.2 reported for the short-exposure results. The author notes that pH was measured with Merck indicator paper and so values are only estimates.

The paper links iron exposure to the acidification it causes. It reports that gill destruction correlates with falling pH and is most severe below pH 5. This suggests that water chemistry, not iron alone, shapes the outcome in polluted systems. Freshwater fish drinking behaviour is also central. Earlier authors held that freshwater fish do not drink, but the author cites evidence (Eddy 1937, Adler 1949, Allee and Frank, and Jochims 1969) that they do. This supports a plausible internal exposure route.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design**
- Species: [[Carassius Auratus]], individuals weighing 13–28 g.
- Four salts tested: [[Ferrous Chloride]], [[Ferric Chloride]], [[Ferrous Sulphate]] and [[Ferric Sulphate]].
- Concentrations: 100, 50, 10 and 5 mg Fe/L.
- Short-exposure trials: 3 fish per aquarium (2 L, aerated) at 100 and 50 mg Fe/L, sacrificed at 5 min, 30 min, 60 min and 3 h.
- Long-exposure trials: 10 and 5 mg Fe/L, sacrificed at 6 h, 24 h and 4 days, or at death.
- Renewed-solution trials: 3 fish each in ferrous sulphate at 5 mg Fe/L (renewed every 4 h, 8 h and 24 h) and at 10 mg Fe/L (renewed every 4 h until death).
- Organs examined: gills, digestive tract, liver, kidneys and brain, with heart and skeletal muscle occasionally.
- Fixation: 10% neutral formalin or [[Bouin Fixation]]. Stains included Azan, Weigert Haematoxylin–Van Gieson, Hotchkiss–MacManus and [[Turnbull Blue Staining]] (for iron).

**Behaviour**
- Early on, fish were excited, then slowed. They gasped at the surface with extensive opercular movement.
- Severe signs increased toward death, when fish lay motionless on the bottom.
- In the renewed-solution trials, signs rose after each renewal and then fell, while body and opercular movements became progressively slower.

**Autopsy**
- Gills and skin showed [[Mucus Hypersecretion]] and ferruginous precipitate.
- Gill congestion was frequent. Haemorrhages were never seen macroscopically.
- Liver congestion was frequent. Slight congestion of the digestive tract was sometimes seen.
- Precipitate was most marked in fish held for days in small, frequently renewed iron doses.

**Gill pathology (short exposures)**
- Generalised oedema of the respiratory epithelium and capillary congestion.
- Rare, small haemorrhages, usually without blood leaving the epithelium.
- Hyperactivity of mucus cells, sometimes covering most of the epithelium.
- Epithelial destruction with dead cells that readily take up iron.
- An iron-positive film covering filaments and penetrating between respiratory folds.
- Coaptation of folds and filaments, and inflammatory infiltration.
- Lesions were present after 5 minutes but milder. Most were well established by 30 minutes and tended to increase up to 3 hours.
- No clear difference was found between salts or between concentrations.

**Digestive tract, liver and kidney (short exposures)**
- Digestive tract: iron-positive masses in the lumen, intracellular granules in surface, goblet-like and corium cells, and iron-positive thrombi in mucosal and submucosal capillaries. No significant erosion.
- Liver: almost constant congestion and intracellular iron granules, occasional iron-positive thrombi in small veins and fibrin-like strands. Iron deposition in Kupffer cells was not seen. Thrombi appeared even after 5 minutes at 100 mg Fe/L.
- Kidney: iron-positive blue colour in many renal tubule lumens, iron granules and thrombi-like blockages, and intracellular granules. Intravascular granules appeared in glomeruli of one fish (100 mg Fe/L, 1 h).

**Long, low-concentration exposure**
- Gill lesions were milder and tended to diminish over time. Some epithelial destruction appeared after 3 days, but many areas were nearly normal. Rare cells with intracellular iron appeared, which were not seen in short trials.
- Digestive tract changes matched the short trials, with no grading difference. Surface iron granules were as clear on day 1 as on day 4.
- Liver tissue disaggregation and necrosis appeared after 24 hours, a new finding.
- Kidney changes were similar to the short trials, with iron-positive thrombi-like blockages and rare tubular necrosis.

**Renewed low-concentration exposure**
- Epithelial destruction was less important in fish given longer renewal intervals.
- Iron-positive film was greatly intensified in fish given short-interval renewals, in thickness, extent and depth of penetration.

**Conclusions**
1. Dissolved iron salts have a complex pathological action detectable in several organs.
2. Gill action includes oedema, mucus hypersecretion, inflammatory infiltration, capillary congestion, epithelial destruction, and blocking of filaments and folds by mucus and ferruginous precipitate. Intracellular iron was rare.
3. Gill destruction is aggravated by falling pH and is especially important below pH 5.
4. Iron is most harmful in the period just after the solution is made, when the ferruginous blocking forms.
5. Iron, in ionic or semicolloidal form, is especially active in forming the precipitate.
6. Iron can be rapidly absorbed through the digestive tract and accumulated in liver parenchyma cells.
7. Iron granules in renal tubules are common.
8. Gill blocking by mucus and iron precipitate is reversible.
9. Harm from iron salts works through three mechanisms: destruction of the respiratory epithelium, blocking of gill filaments and folds, and internal toxicity from large-scale absorption via the digestive tract.

**Discussion**
- The author concludes that the pathological action of iron should not be seen only as external and branchial, but as internal and complex. This disagrees with Carpenter's view and supports [[Laroze]]'s intoxication hypothesis.
- The digestive tract is probably the main absorption route. Gill iron is mainly extracellular, lying between filaments and folds. Intracellular gill iron is likely excretion rather than absorption.
- The author reproduced Laroze's staining technique and saw the same pattern. On sectioned tissue with the Turnbull method, however, the iron was not intracellular, so the two authors agree on the facts but differ in interpretation.
- The data suggest freshwater fish drink the surrounding water, which conflicts with the accepted view.
