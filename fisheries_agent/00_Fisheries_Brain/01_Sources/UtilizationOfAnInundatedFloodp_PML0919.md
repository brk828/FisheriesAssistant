# Utilization of an Inundated Floodplain by Mississippi River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-fishes

## Executive Summary

This paper examines how fishes from the [[Mississippi River]] main channel use an adjoining floodplain when river flooding inundates it. Fish were sampled in 1973 with [[Seine Sampling]], [[Gill Net Sampling]], and [[Trammel Net Sampling]] on a backswamp floodplain near St. Francisville, Louisiana. Of 62 species collected, 24 were characteristic of the main channel and 15 occurred equally in floodplain and main channel habitats. The author concludes that flooded [[Backwater Habitat]] serves as an important feeding and nursery area for riverine fishes, and that loss of floodplain connectivity through drainage likely contributed to the decline of the lower river's fishery.

## Background & Description

The lower Mississippi River has a seasonal flood pulse, and many of its fishes time spawning and feeding to rising water levels. During floods, fishes move from the main channel into inundated floodplain habitats. These movements are mainly lateral (toward and into the floodplain) rather than upriver or downriver.

Land management has altered this cycle. Drainage schemes for agricultural and urban development have removed many floodplains from the influence of the main channel. The author links this loss to the decline of the lower river's fishery, citing earlier work by [[Gunter]] (1952, 1956, 1957) and [[Viosca]] (1927).

The study tested the hypothesis that flooded backwaters serve as feeding and nursery areas for main channel fishes, using collections from the lower floodplain during the 1973 flood.

**Study area.** The site lay about 8 km south of St. Francisville in West Feliciana Parish. The western side of the river is leveed. On the eastern side, Pleistocene bluffs act as a protective levee, and a roughly 2 km wide natural floodplain lies between the river's natural levee and the bluffs. This backswamp contains cypress-tupelo gum forests or pastures, dense rooted aquatic vegetation, and mud substrate with organic debris. Poorly defined channels run through it and enter [[Thompson Creek]], a Mississippi tributary. Even at normal discharge, the shallow backswamp is often flooded with river water via lower Thompson Creek. The narrower western floodplain is mainly pasture with remnants of former borrow pits.

**Methods.** Sampling ran monthly from February to July 1973. All gear was nylon:

- Seine: 3.0 m × 1.8 m, 0.48 cm mesh. Twenty-one seine collections were made in shallow shoreline areas. Seining at each locality continued until no new species were taken.
- Gill net: 45.7 m × 1.8 m, mesh 2.5 to 7.6 cm. Set on the floodplain in open water parallel to shore, just before dark and retrieved the next morning. Effort was 5.5 net-nights.
- Trammel net: 45.7 m × 1.8 m, 5.1 cm webbing, 25.4 cm outside walls. Effort was 6 net-nights.

Four monthly seine collections were also taken in [[Alligator Bayou]], a small floodplain tributary, before and after the flood, for comparison. Fish were identified, counted, and measured in the field (nets) or fixed in 10% formalin and later preserved in 45% isopropanol (seine samples).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author contrasts the two habitat types. The main channel has high current velocities, high turbidity, no aquatic vegetation, and unstable sandy substrates. The floodplain has slackwaters, heavy aquatic vegetation, and fine, organically rich substrates. Reviews by Christenson and Smith (1965) and Schram and Lewis (1974) attribute the high nursery value of flooded backwaters to soil fertility, reduced current velocity, food availability, and water-level fluctuation.

Fish production tracks the extent of backwater. Richardson (1921) reported average yields of 199 kg/ha in an Illinois River section with about 90% backwater, 146 kg/ha where backwater covered about [sic, figure garbled in source] of the area, and 78 kg/ha in sections with 63% backwater. Barnickol and Starrett (1951) recorded a reduction in game fish where backwater areas were reduced in a Mississippi River section. Along the lower Colorado River, draining adjoining backwaters was reported to cause a 100% reduction in fishery values (Beland, 1953). Lambou (1963) found the most productive sport and commercial fisheries in the [[Atchafalaya Basin]] were in areas with the greatest backwater area.

Fish movement follows water-level change. Shapp (in Christenson and Smith, 1965) described fish entering a previously dry area during spring flooding and leaving as waters receded. Fish movements out of backwaters were also recorded in pools 8 and 9 of the upper Mississippi River. Johnson (1963) concluded that spring inundation of floodplains triggered spawning migrations of the [[Bigmouth Buffalo]]. Paddlefish have been observed moving into oxbow lakes as water levels rise (Stockard, 1907).

## Key Findings

**Species composition and catch**

- **Gill nets:** 9 species were reported, averaging 6.48 individuals and 5.09 kg per net-night. [[Gizzard Shad]] was most numerous (1.43 individuals per net-night). [[Carp]] and [[Shortnose Gar]] tied at 1.24 individuals per net-night, and all other species yielded under 0.38. Carp contributed the most biomass (1.84 kg per net-night). Shortnose gar, [[Bowfin]], and bigmouth buffalo each yielded 0.67 to 0.76 kg per net-night.
- **Trammel nets:** 5 species, totaling 2.70 individuals and 3.75 kg per net-night. Bowfin (1.06) and gizzard shad (1.00 individuals per net-night) were most frequent. Gizzard shad contributed the most biomass (2.95 kg per net-night), followed by bowfin (2.36 kg).
- **Seines:** 61 species were taken in 21 collections (the text also gives a total of 62 species across all gear). The most abundant were [[Threadfin Shad]] (1,275 individuals, 26.8%), [[Silvery Minnow]] (746, 15.7%), [[Mosquitofish]] (357, 7.5%), [[Blacktail Shiner]] (279, 5.9%), and [[Golden Shiner]] (214, 4.5%). Bluegill, yellow bullhead, pirate perch, and gizzard shad each yielded between 100 and 210 specimens.
- **Overall:** Of the 62 species, 39 inhabit the Mississippi River, and 23 are found mainly in main channel habitats. (The abstract gives 24 main-channel and 15 equally-distributed species, which differs slightly from this count.) In seine collections, 53.5% of individuals were normal river inhabitants and 52.9% belonged to species most abundant in the main channel (figures as reported; the source text is partly garbled).

**Floodplain use and migration**

- Juveniles and/or spawning adults of threadfin shad, gizzard shad, grass pickerel, and ribbon shiner were found on the floodplain. Shortnose gar, bowfin, carp, and bigmouth buffalo were collected in spawning condition during early gill net sampling, as floodwaters first reached the floodplain.
- Several species were absent from the floodplain before the flood and appeared during it, including skipjack herring, red shiner, silverband shiner, weed shiner, bigmouth buffalo, flathead catfish, channel catfish, white bass, and yellow bass.
- Characteristic river fishes stayed below 12 species on the floodplain until flooding. Their number then rose to a peak of 32 species in July. Their percent composition followed the same trend, and both fell as discharge dropped and floodwaters receded (Fig. 1).
- The author interprets the pattern as: (1) many river fishes use the floodplain as a nursery area; (2) juveniles and adults move back to the main channel as water recedes, contributing to main channel recruitment; and (3) rapidly falling water levels can trap river fishes in floodplain sloughs, which the continued presence of riverine fishes in late seine collections suggests.

**Conclusions and management implications**

- Floodplains play a major role in sustaining riverine fisheries. The author argues that little effort has gone into preserving floodplains along large rivers.
- Any physical destruction of floodplains, or any manipulation that restricts access to them, could adversely affect the fishery.
- Because the primary goal of fish management is the sustained yield of sport and commercial fisheries, the author argues that protecting or restoring floodplain habitats should be a top priority in large-river management programs.
