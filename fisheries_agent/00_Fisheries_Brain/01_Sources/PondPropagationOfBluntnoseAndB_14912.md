# Pond Propagation of Bluntnose and Blackhead Minnows

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pimephales

## Executive Summary

This Ohio Department of Natural Resources bulletin (Wildlife Conservation Bulletin No. 4, first issued March 1948, revised January 1951) describes how to raise bait minnows in managed ponds rather than collecting them from streams. The authors, [[Harold Wascko]] and [[Clarence F. Clark]], recommend [[Bluntnose Minnow]] and [[Blackhead Minnow]] as the preferred "chub" bait species. The bulletin covers pond design, water supply, breeding stock selection, spawning, feeding and fertilization, predators, diseases, vegetation control, harvesting and production yields from state fish farms.

## Background & Description

- **Problem addressed:** Bait supply was a major concern for anglers, dealers and wildlife managers. Collection from streams was controversial, and minnows from cool, clear streams often died when placed in warm, turbid lakes. Collection losses, transport losses and dealer possession limits also reduced profits and forced frequent resupply trips.
- **Scale of demand:** The document states that as many as 90% of anglers fishing certain western Ohio waters buy their bait, and that most waters in northwestern and western Ohio are [[Crappie]] waters.
- **Recommended species:** Anglers commonly ask for "chubs," a term covering several minnows. The authors chose bluntnose and blackhead minnows because they are active, hardy on the hook, live well in bait pails and holding tanks, and suit pond propagation across Ohio. Larger "big-mouth chubs" (up to 8 inches, favored as bass bait) need current to spawn and were not recommended for general propagation.
- **Breeding-stock note:** Males of both species grow larger than females. Selecting only the largest fish may therefore produce a brood stock of mostly old males.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond characteristics**
- Warm-water ponds with rich bottoms produce the best crops. Cold-water ponds with sterile bottoms are poor producers. Stone quarries are generally unproductive, but abandoned gravel pits have produced good crops.
- Ponds of about ¼ acre or more are preferred (the OCR reads "4%"). Smaller ponds raise labor and construction costs.
- Narrow ponds make vegetation control easier. Shape matters mainly for drainage.

**Water supply**
- A [[Chemical Analysis]] of the source should come first in hatchery planning.
- Warm, moderately soft water is most productive. [[Dissolved Oxygen]] of 4 to 12 ppm is required year-round.
- Shallow areas provide summer feeding grounds, and deep areas protect against winter losses. The document cites the [[St. Marys State Fish Farm]], where minnows were wintered in ponds with 4 feet of water in the deep end, though deeper ponds give greater security.
- Clear water is preferred, but turbid water has produced good crops. Spring water may need warming before it enters the hatchery. Inflow must be screened to keep out foreign fish.

**Site and levees**
- An engineer should examine the site before construction. Ponds must be protected from flooding, and levees should have at least a 2:1 slope.
- Earthen surfaces should be kept in heavy grass, with a woodland or pasture watershed where possible.

**Pond biology**
- Microscopic plants and animals are the base of the [[Food Chain]] that feeds young minnows. Fertilization aims to increase these organisms.
- Plant stems support large invertebrate populations. The document records as many as 1,442 tiny animals on 10 linear feet of plant stem.

## Key Findings

**Spawning and reproduction**
- Spawning can begin in the second summer. The season runs about four months, from May (water 65–70°F) into August.
- Both species lay eggs on the undersides of objects, not on the bottom. [[Spawning Devices]] (tiles, boards, stones, driven shingles at 2–3 feet, floating boards, leaf undersides) are needed in propagation ponds.
- Female egg counts range from 200 to 1,000. Nests of 2,000 to 3,000 eggs occur and suggest several females contributed.
- Hatching takes one to two weeks for bluntnose and four to six days for blackhead minnows, depending on temperature.

**Breeding stock**
- Stock should be healthy, medium-sized fish, with no more than about 2,000 per surface acre of water.
- Private propagators are a source of stock. Wild-caught fish must be checked for identity, and no other species should enter the ponds.

**Feeding and fertilization**
- Newly hatched fish are about ¼ inch long and eat microscopic organisms.
- Organic fertilizers (dry sheep and cow manure, sewage sludge, cottonseed, soybean and peanut meals) and commercial fertilizers are used. Recommended organic rates are 150 to 1,000 pounds per acre per season, applied about every ten days.
- [[Swingle and Smith]] recommend a 6-8-4 fertilizer, with 10 pounds of nitrate of soda (soft water) or ammonium sulphate (hard water) per 100 pounds, applied at one application per acre.
- Fertilization should start 2 to 3 weeks before spawning.
- Direct feeding uses [[Feeding Shelves]] anchored 8 to 10 inches below the surface. At the St. Marys farm, a 1:1:1 mix of soybean meal, middlings and commercial meat scraps was moistened into small balls and fed daily.

**Predators**
- [[Green Sunfish]] are the chief nuisance. They eat eggs and small fish, breed fast, and young fish can pass through screens.
- [[Crayfish]] destroy minnows during draining and handling. Complete annual drainage may reduce their numbers.
- Turtles compete with fish for food and eat some eggs and fry. Snapping turtles are considered the worst, though less important than once believed.
- Water snakes eat fish and should be excluded or destroyed. Water and shore birds are federally protected, so ponds may be screened or birds frightened away.
- [[Muskrats]] burrow through banks, mixing populations or draining ponds.

**Diseases and parasites**
- Crowding and high temperatures increase disease and parasitism. Effective pond-scale treatments were not yet established.
- [[Anchor Parasite]] (*Lernaea*) attaches to the body, often on the gills, and appears in crowded populations in hot summer months. No control is known. Stocking from uninfected ponds is recommended.
- [[Fin or Tail Rot]] appears as white blotches and fraying fins. Advanced cases cannot be cured. Affected fish should be removed, and early cases may be treated by dipping for 1 to 2 minutes in a 1:2,000 [[Copper Sulphate]] solution, repeated at 24-hour intervals.
- [[Fungus]] appears as fuzzy white growth on fish and eggs, often after injury. Early treatment uses a salt dip (1 teaspoon per gallon) or a 1:2,000 copper sulphate dip for 1 minute, or 30 minutes in 1:100,000 potassium permanganate. Prevention through careful handling is the best strategy.

**Vegetation control**
- Some vegetation is beneficial, but excess causes oxygen shortages, odors, matting over spawning devices, clogged screens and harvesting problems.
- Biological control: some crayfish roil the water, which suppresses plants. Fertilization can also shade out rooted plants.
- Chemical control (sodium arsenite, copper sulphate) is dangerous and may kill food organisms and fish. One gallon of concentrate treated about 65,000 gallons. The authors described chemical methods as still experimental.
- Mechanical control is more labor-intensive but safer. Methods include weed saws, rakes, scythes and chains. Lotus and cattails were controlled by cutting in July and August. Spatterdock was controlled by removing leaves five times per spring and summer, based on Alabama experiments.
- Removed vegetation should be taken out of the pond, because decomposition consumes oxygen.

**Harvesting**
- Trapping and dip nets give only partial harvests. Seining is more effective but can gill small minnows and disturb eggs if done during spawning.
- Complete drainage into a catch box or pit is the ideal method. It removes foreign fish and the whole crop, and allows inspection of levees and spawning devices.

**Production yields reported**
- Langlois (bluntnose, Ohio state fish farms): 201 per adult and 473,350 per acre, averaging 1¾ inches (as printed).
- Wascko (later Ohio state fish farm study): 138 per adult and 801,813 per acre.
- Cooper (Michigan): 104,800 per acre.
- St. Marys State Fish Farm, 1940: 33,000 breeders in a pond area printed as "4% acres." The crop was 1,084,984 minnows, or 291,266 per acre. Of these, 281,504 (25%) were saleable at 2 inches. Winter losses were 33.12% (93,229 saleable minnows). The remaining 188,275 were valued at a minimum of $5.00 per thousand, or $941.00 total ($221.67 per acre).
- St. Marys, 1944: 2,700 breeders in 1.20 acres produced 188,400 minnows after winter loss. About 54,000 were saleable, worth $263.00 ($165.00 per acre at $5.00 per thousand). A further 135,000 smaller minnows were ready for fall sale.

**Overall conclusion:** Pond propagation can supply bait reliably and profitably. The authors note that production varies with conditions, that fertilization research in Ohio was limited, and that further propagator experience is needed.
