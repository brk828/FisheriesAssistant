# Digestive Tract Contents of Adult Razorback Suckers in Lake Mohave, Arizona–Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 study by [[Paul C Marsh]] examined the digestive tract contents of 34 preserved adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) collected from [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona–Nevada. Contents were dominated by planktonic crustaceans, diatoms, filamentous algae, and detritus. The author concludes that the species is morphologically equipped for, and apparently pursues, a planktivorous diet, but that benthic material in the guts shows bottom feeding also occurs. The species is endangered throughout its native [[Colorado River Basin]] range, which made the sacrifice of specimens hard to justify; these fish had been preserved for other purposes.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] has received considerable attention because its numbers have dwindled and its status is imperiled across the Colorado River basin ([[McAda and Wydoski 1980]]; [[Minckley 1983]]). Many aspects of its life history, including diet and feeding mode, were poorly known. Because the species is so rare, dissecting individuals for diet analysis was scarcely justified, so the study relied on 34 specimens that became available as by-products of other work.

**Methods**

- **Collection:** Trammel net sampling of shoreline areas of Lake Mohave. Samples came from May 1980 (N = 8) and from January (N = 10), February (N = 3), and April (N = 13) 1984.
- **Preservation and measurement:** Fish were killed and preserved intact by formalin injection. Standard length (SL) was recorded to the nearest mm, and sex was determined by internal examination.
- **Gut sampling:** Digestive tracts were excised. In 1980 fish, 1.0 mL aliquots were taken from the anterior third only; in 1984 fish, aliquots came from the anterior, middle, and posterior thirds. Each aliquot was diluted in 5.0 mL water, and 1.0 mL was counted in a Sedgwick-Rafter chamber.
- **Identification:** Triplicate subsamples were examined by light microscopy at 35–400×.
- **Metrics:** Frequency of occurrence (% of non-empty tracts containing an item; N = 32), predominance (% of tracts where an item made up the greatest volume), and mean number per mL (1984 fish only; N = 24 with animal counts, from 26 specimens of which two were empty).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fish sampled were considered representative of the [[Lake Mohave]] population. Males averaged 431 mm SL (range 398–467 mm) and females 492 mm (range 411–530 mm). Gut contents varied among fore-, mid-, and hindgut samples within individuals, but not among individuals, and showed no evident differences between sexes or sampling dates, so samples were pooled.

Historical records support a mixed diet. Earlier workers reported:

- Feeding on planktonic crustaceans in May in Lake Mohave ([[Minckley 1973]]).
- Feeding on zooplankton swarming near artificial light in [[Lake Mead]] and Lake Mohave ([[Allan and Roden 1978]]).
- Guts packed with silt rich in diatoms and filamentous algae from the lower [[Colorado River]] near Parker, Arizona ([[Dill 1944]]).
- Algae, plant debris, and aquatic insect larvae from the [[Green River]], Colorado–Utah ([[Banks 1964]]), and mud, clay, chironomid larvae, and plant material in fish from the same river ([[Vanicek 1967]]).

Morphology supports planktivory. [[Hubbs and Miller 1953]] described the long, fine gill rakers, which resemble those of lake-dwelling planktivorous suckers of the genus *Chasmistes*, and found microplankton trapped between the rakers and pharyngeal pads.

## Key Findings

**Overall diet (Table 1; N = 32 non-empty tracts):**

| Item | Frequency (%) | Predominance (%) | Mean number/mL (SD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Bosmina* sp. (Cladocera) | 100 | 33 | 284 (314) |
| *Daphnia* sp. (Cladocera) | 72 | 3 | 58 (65) |
| *Chydorus* sp. | 16 | 0 | 32 |
| *Leptodora* sp. | 9 | 0 | 2 (0) |
| Rotifera | 53 | 0 | 42 (48) |
| Ostracoda | 53 | 0 | 12 (11) |
| Copepoda | 34 | 0 | 6 (5) |
| Chironomidae (larvae) | 3 | 0 | 3 (0) |
| Diatoms (mainly *Fragilaria crotonensis*) | 88 | 6 | N/A |
| Filamentous algae | 44 | 12 | N/A |
| Detritus and amorphous organic matter | 56 | 25 | N/A |
| Inorganics (e.g., sand) | 16 | 0 | N/A |

- Two of 34 tracts (6%) were empty and excluded.
- Diatoms, primarily *Fragilaria crotonensis*, occurred in nearly 90% of tracts, and planktonic crustaceans, rotifers, diatoms, detritus, and filamentous algae each occurred in at least 44% of tracts.
- *Bosmina* occurred in every tract and was the most abundant item. *Daphnia* was common (72%) but numerically minor.
- Rotifers, ostracods, and copepods each occurred in more than one-third of tracts but were predominant in none. The author therefore argues that frequency and predominance must be read together.
- Detritus was the most frequent predominant item after *Bosmina*, at 25%.

**Conclusions:**

1. Planktivory is the main feeding mode, supported by both gut contents and gill raker morphology.
2. A definite benthic component is present. Benthic material may have been selected, or it may have been incidental to burrowing and sediment excavation. The author states it is not clear which.
3. Zooplankton such as *Bosmina* and *Daphnia* may have been taken from the water column or from sediment where they accumulate after death.
4. Field observations describe fish "bouncing" along the bottom and excavating soft substrate. Fish sometimes burrowed headfirst to depths of 10 cm or more for several minutes, and moved up to about a meter, with sediment passed out through the opercles (original observations at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico).
