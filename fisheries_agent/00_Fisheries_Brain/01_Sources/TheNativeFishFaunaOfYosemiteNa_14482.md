# Native Fish Fauna of Yosemite National Park and Its Preservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #catostomidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This Yosemite Nature Notes article by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and Orthello L. Wallis (1948) revises the known native fish fauna of [[Yosemite National Park]]. It reports that the park's recognized native fishes double from three species to six, through the addition of the [[Sacramento Squawfish]], the [[Sierra Chub-Roach]] and the [[Riffle Muddler]]. The authors also review the conflict between preservation of native aquatic life and sport fishing, and they recommend halting stocking of the exotic [[Brown Trout]]. Their conclusion is that further native species are unlikely to be found in the park.

## Background & Description

The authors note that the park's fish fauna had appeared in only four prior publications: Hutchings (1886), [[Barton Warren Evermann]] (1921), Bryant (1929) and [[Willis A. Evans]] (1944). Earlier reports were brief or partly hypothetical, and the article describes the paucity of fish research as a contrast with the thorough study of terrestrial vertebrates.

The article also sets out a policy problem. Terrestrial native life in the parks has been strictly protected, while aquatic life has often been managed as a recreational asset. Hunting is prohibited, but fishing is generally allowed and sometimes encouraged. Exotic fishes, especially trouts, have been stocked for sport. The authors list the ecological risks of this practice: disease transmission, hybridization, competition, predation and depletion of native species.

The [[National Park Service]] compromise policy preserves native aquatic life where it still exists and allows stocking and sport fishing where the natural balance is already badly disturbed. The authors urge a more preservationist trend where practicable.

The six native species, as recognized in the article, are:
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii irideus*), the only trout
- [[Sacramento Sucker]] (*Catostomus occidentalis*)
- [[Hardhead]] (*Mylopharodon conocephalus*)
- [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus grandis*)
- [[Sierra Chub-Roach]] (*Hesperoleucus symmetricus*)
- [[Riffle Muddler]] (*Cottus bairdii gulosus*)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fauna is limited by steep stream gradients and torrential snowmelt flows from the Sierra Nevada. Most lowland [[Sacramento Valley]] species cannot ascend the [[Merced River]] or [[Tuolumne River]] to the western park boundary, which lies at roughly 2,200 ft on the Merced and 3,150 ft on the Tuolumne. The authors state that no native fish occur above the high plunging falls of Yosemite.

Key environmental and habitat points include:
- **[[Merced Canyon]] and [[Power Dam]]:** The power dam appears to block upstream movement of native fish into [[Yosemite Valley]], so species that once may have reached the valley could not re-enter it.
- **[[Brown Trout]] predation and competition:** Brown trout, a European exotic, are more piscivorous than other trouts and are resistant to depletion. They may have eliminated native fishes from the valley floor.
- **Slow sucker growth:** Sucker growth in the Merced is attributed to the purity of snow-fed water and to spring scouring of the bottom by high flows.
- **[[Hetch Hetchy Reservoir]]:** The Sierra chub-roach was first noted there in 1930, probably introduced through live bait dumping, though a native population in the pre-impoundment Tuolumne is also possible.
- **[[Indian Flats]]:** At about 1,550 ft, this site held every fish species collected in the park during extensive surveys, making it the richest known locality.

## Key Findings

**Revised native fauna.** The authors add three native species to the park list, doubling it from three to six: the squawfish, the chub-roach (previously considered introduced) and the riffle muddler. They conclude that additional native species are unlikely.

**Exotic stocking.** The authors recommend that the stocking of brown trout be stopped. Their proposed measures for restoring natural conditions were:
1. Cease stocking of brown trout.
2. Increase stocking of rainbow trout on the valley floor, using larger fingerlings, yearlings or adults.
3. Propagate rainbow trout from Yosemite or nearby Sierra Nevada waters.
4. Lessen or remove fishing restrictions on brown trout, or deplete them in the valley by other means.
5. Protect rainbow trout, particularly in the valley.
6. Remove the power dam to permit natural movement of native fishes into the valley.

The authors say the first two to four measures are the least objectionable and most practicable, and that any combination could be used.

**Rainbow trout.** Native rainbows were probably present in moderate-elevation waters of the Merced and Tuolumne systems. Hybridization and mixing by fish culturists have obscured the original stock. The authors favor the name *irideus* over *shasta*, since a re-examination of the type specimens by Hubbs and Follett found no valid basis for recognizing *shasta*. A 1924 letter from Colonel H. C. Benson indicates that no fish occurred above the falls before stocking. On 31 July 1948, 28 adult rainbows were counted in one pool below the power dam, and some natural reproduction was observed.

**Brown trout stocking.** A Fish and Game official reported planting about 100,000 brown trout fingerlings per year in the valley floor of the Merced. About the same number of rainbows were planted, mostly in the swifter water between the powerhouse and El Portal.

**Sacramento sucker.** On 31 July 1948, a sample of 82 young-of-the-year suckers ranged from 12 to 21 mm (mean 15.0 mm). A collection of 54 yearlings ranged from 33 to 64 mm (mean 47.3 mm). A two-year-old measured 107 mm and had a definite winter annulus. Adults are solitary and feed on rock surfaces in deeper pools. The species was not found to be native to [[Tenaya Lake]] (8,141 ft), where it is presumed introduced.

**Sacramento squawfish.** A half-grown specimen of 230 mm was found at the park boundary on 31 July 1948. A 1933 survey also caught a specimen 1.5 miles above El Portal, 0.2 mile inside the park. Since the specimen was a yearling, the authors infer that the species spawns within the park, probably at Indian Flats. The species is described as a voracious predator of trout and salmon, but the authors recommend protecting it because it is rare.

**Hardhead.** The inclusion of this species rests on a specimen collected on 26 July 1934 in the Merced River at about 2,800 ft and confirmed in 1948 (Yosemite Museum No. 139). The authors consider it uncommon in the park, confined to the lower Merced Canyon, and less predacious than the squawfish.

**Sierra chub-roach.** Six 9 mm postlarvae were recognized in the 1948 fry collection and identified as the native *Hesperoleucus symmetricus* from comparison with Fry's (1936) descriptions. This finding supports native status in the Merced within the park, at about 2,200 ft, though the species is scarce above 2,000 ft.

**Riffle muddler.** Four single-specimen collections from within the park are reported (including a 52 mm half-grown specimen taken in 1948), indicating the species is rare in the Merced. The authors note that the systematics of *Cottus* in western North America remains uncertain.

**Lowland species excluded.** The authors list lowland Central Valley fishes, such as the [[Sacramento Perch]], [[Valley Perch]], and Sacramento hitch, which are considered unable to reach the park by natural dispersal. The European brook lamprey is also listed among lowland forms.
