# Chironomid Tufa Discovery in the Meuse Region

#limnology #aquatic-ecology #chironomidae #tufa #freshwater

## Executive Summary

This short paper, published in French with a German summary, reports the discovery of **chironomid tufa** (calcareous deposits built around larval tubes of midges) in the **Ruisseau de Denée**, a spring-fed stream in the [[Meuse Valley]] of Belgium, found in 1952. The tufa forms in hard, calcium-rich water through the combined action of [[Chironomidae]] larvae, the cyanobacterium [[Phormidium Incrustatum]], and loss of dissolved carbon dioxide. Two midge taxa were identified: [[Lithotanytarsus Emarginatus]], recorded for the first time in Belgium, and a [[Rheotanytarsus]] species that may be new to science. The author also collates the known European distribution of *Lithotanytarsus* tufas, and a short discussion addresses the terminology of community, association and biocenosis.

## Background & Description

Calcareous tufa is a porous, limestone-like deposit. In some streams, the larval tubes of midges, mainly from the Tanytarsini tribe, become encrusted with calcium carbonate. In soft or temporary conditions these tubes only trap fine silt and form fragile, temporary deposits. In **hard water**, however, the encrusted tubes accumulate into durable structures called *tufs à Chironomides* (chironomid tufas). The genera most often involved are [[Rheotanytarsus]], [[Lithotanytarsus]] and, less commonly, [[Eukiefferiella]].

**Study site.** The author found tufas in 1952 in the Ruisseau de Denée, about 8 km west of [[Anhée]]. The stream arises from strong-flowing [[Rheocrene Spring]]s at about 220 m altitude, roughly 700 m east of the Denée church. After about 100 m it passes a farm, flows through meadows, and forms a shallow, silty pond. It then enters woodland and descends steeply through small cascades before joining the [[Molignée River]] at Maredsous (165 m altitude) after about 1,200 m. The mean gradient is about 45‰ (per mille), a steep slope for a lowland stream. The only fish recorded is the [[Brown Trout]], described simply as "la Truite".

**Water chemistry.** Analysis of the water (28 March 1953) shows very high calcium and magnesium, consistent with hard, basic water. Values reported are:

| Sampling point | Ca²⁺ (mg/l) | Mg²⁺ (mg/l) |
|---|---|---|
| Source | 120.8 | 15 |
| First tufas, under woodland | 117.1 | 7.2 |
| Near Denée-Maredsous station | 106.2 | 7.0 |

Alkalinity (S.B.V.) and total hardness (°fr.) were also measured, but the scanned table does not allow these values to be matched reliably to each site. Downstream, calcium, magnesium and alkalinity fall while pH rises. The author attributes this to loss of free CO₂ and then of semi-combined CO₂, which drives calcium carbonate precipitation.

**Tufa composition.** A partial analysis of a dried tufa sample (27 April 1952), per 100 g:

- Insoluble residue (cold HCl): 52.39 g, with SiO₂ 45.27 g
- Loss on ignition: 3.99 g
- Soluble fraction (cold HCl): 47.61 g, including CaO 24.98 g
- MgO: 0.67 g
- Fe₂O₃ + Al₂O₃ + P₂O₅: 0.97 g

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Algal role.** The calcifying alga is the cyanobacterium *Phormidium incrustatum*, which is typically responsible for travertine formation in streams. In the Denée tufas it is dominant. In the discussion, Symoens cites other calcicolous algae found with *Tanytarsus* larvae elsewhere, including *Lyngbya aeruginea-coerulea* and *Oocardium stratum* (Wallner). The author does not consider the algae–midge relationship close enough to be called [[Symbiosis]]. He regards them instead as members of one [[Biocenosis]] or association.

**Midge fauna.** Two chironomid taxa build the tufas at Denée:

1. *Lithotanytarsus emarginatus* (Goetghebuer), a first record for Belgium. The author treats it as a [[Periglacial Relict]] of southern origin, a species of cold-water refugia. Its presence in the Meuse area is attributed to the [[Stenothermy]] (stable, cool temperature regime) of the stream.
2. A *Rheotanytarsus* species, identified as a pupa that does not match any described species, and probably new to science. Professor A. Thienemann examined the larvae and confirmed the determination.

**Known distribution.** The author compiles records of *Lithotanytarsus* tufas, mainly from published sources (about 13 references):

- **Bavaria (Germany):** near Garmisch-Partenkirchen (Thienemann 1933–1936), and at several other sites (Wallner 1934–1935)
- **Franconia and Osnabrück:** near Bamberg, Nuremberg, Erlangen and Engter (Thienemann 1944, 1950)
- **Switzerland and Lake Constance region:** near Frauenfeld and Basel (Thienemann 1934; Grisches 1935)
- **French Alps:** Pleistocene tufas at Crémieu, Isère (Edwards 1936)
- **Eastern Alps:** the Meiergraben, a tributary of Lunz lake (Austria)
- **Pyrenees:** near Col d'Aubisque, Eaux-Chaudes and Barèges (Bertrand 1950)
- **Eastern Europe:** Novorossiysk, on the Black Sea coast (Thienemann 1944)

The Denée record extends the known range of this southern relict species considerably to the north-west.

## Key Findings

- **New national record:** *Lithotanytarsus emarginatus* is reported for Belgium for the first time, from the Ruisseau de Denée.
- **Probable new species:** The *Rheotanytarsus* pupae from Denée do not match any known species of the genus, and the author suspects that they represent an undescribed taxon.
- **Tufa mechanism:** Chironomid tubes in hard, basic water accumulate and become encrusted with calcium carbonate, forming durable chironomid tufas. Loss of CO₂ drives the precipitation, as shown by the downstream decline in calcium, magnesium and alkalinity and the rise in pH.
- **Chemistry of the tufa:** The dried sample is roughly half insoluble silica (52.39 g per 100 g) and half acid-soluble material (47.61 g, mostly CaO at 24.98 g).
- **Algal co-builder:** *Phormidium incrustatum* is the dominant calcifying cyanobacterium at the site, in line with typical travertine-forming streams.
- **Relict biogeography:** The author interprets *L. emarginatus* as a southern peri-glacial relict, whose persistence in the Meuse region reflects the stable, cool conditions of a spring-fed stream.
- **Biological terminology:** D'Ancona notes that "community" (*communità*, after Petersen) denotes organisms living together without known interactions, whereas "association" and "biocenosis" (treated as synonyms) denote groups linked by feeding relationships. Symoens reports that the algae and midges at Denée do not show the close dependence needed to call them symbionts.
- **Fish fauna:** The only fish recorded in the stream is the trout, and the paper notes no other fish species.
