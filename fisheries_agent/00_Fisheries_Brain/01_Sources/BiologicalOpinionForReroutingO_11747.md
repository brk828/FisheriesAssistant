# Biological Opinion for Rerouting of Existing Navopache Power Powerline on Upper Blue River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #conservation-biology #endangered-species-act

## Executive Summary

This biological opinion, issued March 24, 1997, evaluates the proposed rerouting of an existing [[Navopache Power]] powerline crossing the [[Blue River]] in [[Greenlee County]], [[Arizona]]. The [[U.S. Forest Service]] consulted under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] regarding potential impacts to three listed species: [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*, threatened), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, endangered), and [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*, threatened). The Service concluded that the project is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of any of these species or adversely modify critical habitat. Incidental take of loach minnow is anticipated but cannot be quantified; take limits are indexed to fish community mortality (>20 dead/dying fish within 500 yards downstream) or any toxic material spill.

## Background & Description

### Project Description

The proposed action involves rerouting a [[Navopache Power]] powerline that currently lies within the easement of Forest Road 281 on the [[Blue River]] in the [[Alpine Ranger District]]. The existing deteriorating powerline section would be removed after rerouting. Construction activities include:

1. Crossing the [[Blue River]] on foot (2-3 people, at least twice) to attach new powerline from existing pole to new pole above roadbed—expected completion in one work day
2. Stretching new powerline along existing road right-of-way with approximately 3-4 new poles
3. Trimming or removing (if necessary) one large cottonwood overhanging the river; if removed, tree would fall into floodplain
4. Leaving existing downed poles in place but removing wire from river and floodplain
5. No roadbed or bank material to be cast into river or floodplain
6. No machinery to enter river or floodplain

### Species Descriptions

**[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): Listed as threatened October 28, 1986. Small, slender, elongate fish with markedly upwardly-directed eyes. Historic range included basins of the [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[San Francisco River]], and [[Gila River]]. Competition and predation by non-native fish and habitat destruction have reduced range by approximately 85%. Life span approximately 2 years. Spawning occurs March-May (with some autumn spawning documented). Bottom-dwelling inhabitant of shallow, swift water over gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates. Feeds exclusively on aquatic insects. Uses interstitial spaces between larger substrate for resting and spawning. Rare or absent where fine sediments fill interstitial spaces. Males may guard nests during incubation.

Current distribution limited to portions of upper [[Gila River]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]], [[Black River]], [[Tularosa River]], [[White River]], and [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Eagle Creek]], [[Campbell Blue Creek]], and [[Dry Blue Creek]] in Arizona and New Mexico. In the [[Blue River]], loach minnow is found throughout areas of perennial flow in suitable habitat.

Critical habitat designated March 8, 1994, but set aside and enjoined from enforcement pending NEPA compliance following 1996 10th Circuit Court decision. The Service found the species warrants uplisting to endangered status; reclassification proposal is pending but precluded by higher priority listing actions.

Biochemical genetic work by Tibbets (1992) indicates substantial differences in genetic makeup between remnant populations, which occupy isolated fragments of the [[Gila Basin]]. Recommendation made to manage genetically distinctive units as separate units to preserve existing genetic variation.

**[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Listed as endangered October 23, 1991. Grows to over two feet in length with distinctive abrupt, sharp-edged dorsal ridge behind head. Once common throughout [[Colorado River Basin]], now rare, occurring sporadically in approximately 750 miles of upper basin. Substantial population exists only in [[Lake Mohave]] in lower basin. Habitat alteration/destruction and competition/predation from introduced nonnative fish responsible for decline.

Adults inhabit wide variety of riverine habitats including mainstream and backwater areas (slow runs, deep eddies, pools, sloughs) and reservoirs. Larval and juvenile habitat use poorly understood but thought to be shallow, slow-moving areas, backwaters and littoral zones. Spawning January-May, tied to water temperature, occurs in shallow water over large gravel, cobble, or coarse sand with little fine sediment. Lives up to approximately 50 years. Feeds on plankton, algae and detritus in reservoirs; riverine populations also consume benthic invertebrates.

No historic records exist from [[Blue River]], but 1887 type locality is [[Gila River]] at [[Fort Thomas]]. Local residents reported razorback sucker common in [[Gila River]] near [[Safford]] and [[Duncan]] in early 1900s. By 1904, fish fauna of lower [[San Francisco River]] and adjacent [[Gila River]] was seriously depleted due to human alterations. [[Blue River]] presumed to have historically supported razorback sucker due to historic presence in nearby [[Gila River]] and apparently suitable habitat.

Between 1986-1989, razorback sucker reintroduced into [[Blue River]] using hatchery stock from [[Lake Mohave]] via [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Stocking occurred prior to listing; equal protection given to stocked and natural populations upon 1991 listing. Few recaptures: one at [[Juan Miller Crossing]] (1986), two at [[Blue Box]] (1987). None found during extensive 1994-95 sampling by [[Arizona State University]] and [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]]. [[Blue River]] considered occupied by razorback sucker, though establishment of self-sustaining population unknown.

**[[Bald Eagle]]** (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*): Listed as endangered March 11, 1967; reclassified to threatened July 12, 1995. No critical habitat designated. Found throughout North America, nesting in trees or on cliffs near seacoasts, lakes and rivers. Primary food is fish (live or carrion). Chemical contamination (chiefly organochlorine pesticides) caused severe population declines through reproductive failure and direct toxicity. Banning of organochlorine pesticides plus habitat protection/restoration resulted in overall status improvement.

Southwestern bald eagles constitute distinct population, distinguishable by morphology, breeding chronology and geographic isolation. Frequently nest on cliffs (rare outside this region). Nest early (eggs laid January-February), behavioral adaptation to avoid extreme desert heat. Young remain near nest until June. Diet supplements fish with mammals, birds, reptiles (live or carrion). Approximately 38 occupied breeding areas identified in recent years in southwestern population, majority in Arizona along [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], [[Gila River]], [[Bill Williams River]] and major tributaries. Population on upward trend but remains small and threatened.

No bald eagle nests known on [[Blue River]]. Nearest nest site at [[Luna Lake]], approximately 20 air miles from project. Wintering bald eagles frequently use area; surveys conducted each winter by Forest Service personnel. Wintering habitat requirements less critical than nesting requirements. Primary needs: perching sites (large trees, living or dead, preferably near foraging areas) and food (primarily fish, also birds and small mammals). In northern Arizona, wintering bald eagles primarily forage on waterfowl and carrion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Blue River Ecosystem Status

The [[Blue River]] is a seriously degraded ecosystem. Aldo Leopold in 1922 called it "ruined" and cited it as an "extreme example" of human-caused erosion menace in the Southwest. Human uses of river and watershed resulted in extensive changes. When European American settlement occurred around 1885-1886, the canyon floor was "well sodded and covered with grama grass, hardwoods, and pine." By 1916, it had become a wide eroded wash. Olmstead (1919) noted that portions of few ranches totaling less than 200 acres (less than 8% of original arable area) escaped flood destruction. Bulk of flood destruction occurred 1900-1906.

Although proximate cause was flooding, flood destruction was enabled and exacerbated by human activities on watershed and streambanks:

- **Overgrazing** by cattle and goats depleted herbaceous cover, increasing sedimentation, increasing volume and decreasing duration of high flows, decreasing volume and increasing duration of low flows
- **Timber harvest, fuelwood, and railroad tie cutting** depleted vegetative cover, created eroding roads/tracks, damaged river channel when logs rafted downstream during high water
- **Field development** on river terraces removed stabilizing riparian vegetation; irrigation canals and headworks destabilized channel and funneled floodwaters onto terraces causing erosion
- **Roads and trails** along river destroyed riparian vegetation, eroded terraces, destabilized streambanks, channeled floodwaters into new areas eroding new channels or widening existing channel
- **Flood control measures** increased velocities, decreased habitat complexity, destabilized river through modification and constraint of natural channel geometry

Resulting stream channel characterized by: wide shallow profile, high sediment levels, eroding banks, braided shifting channels, depauperate riparian vegetation.

### Current Watershed Uses and Impacts

Present uses continue contributing to deteriorated condition, though at much reduced level from late 1800s:

- **Timber harvest, roads, grazing** within watershed continue contributing erosion, vegetation change, hydrologic regime alteration
- **Private lands** concentrated in upper 20-30 miles of river bottom; some cropping and irrigated agriculture continues on naturally protected terraces
- **Small diversion structures and irrigation canals** present
- **Private fish hatchery** on upper [[Blue River]] diverts substantial proportion of base flow, sometimes reportedly drying river for short distance
- **Subdividing of ranch lands** and construction of residences/summer homes at fairly low level
- **Forest Road 281** servicing private lands is major source of bank damage and erosion; numerous low-water ford crossings contribute to localized destabilization
- **Unauthorized off-road-vehicle use** in river bottom continues in lower Blue
- **Livestock grazing** continues on private lands in upper Blue valley bottom; river corridor excluded from authorized grazing on [[Clifton Ranger District]]; allotments on [[Alpine Ranger District]] included river grazing but recent decision to remove grazing is under administrative appeal

### Nonnative Species

Like all remaining [[Gila River Basin]] streams, [[Blue River]] has been subject to introduction of nonnative fish and aquatic species. Although nonnative species present are much lower than in most Gila basin streams, those present have adversely affected native fish community through competition and predation. Nonnative species reported during recent surveys:

- Rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*)
- Red shiner (*Cyprinella lutrensis*)
- Brown trout (*Salmo trutta*) - earlier surveys
- Channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*) - earlier surveys; local stories indicate sometimes quite abundant in lower [[Blue River]]

Scarcity of large pools, paucity of habitat structure, and flashiness of flooding may make nonnatives such as channel catfish susceptible to death or removal downstream during large flood events. Differential effects of flooding on native vs. nonnative fishes in Southwestern streams have been observed.

### Fish Fauna

For many years, fish fauna of [[Blue River]] was poorly known. Surveys were few and concentrated on tributary streams. Recent surveys by [[Arizona State University]] during 1995-1996 (funded by [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]]), combined with [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] data, indicate loach minnow still distributed throughout length of [[Blue River]] in suitable habitat. No razorback sucker found.

**Native fishes remaining** (in addition to loach minnow and razorback sucker):
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus [Pantosteus] clarki*)
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)

Historical records are few, but combined with records from [[San Francisco River]] and [[Gila River]] downstream, plus early river descriptions (much narrower with more distinct streambanks and riparian cover, more varied and complex aquatic habitat), support premise that **up to nine species of native fish have been extirpated** from [[Blue River]] in past century. Reintroduction of razorback sucker attempted with uncertain success.

### Previous Consultations

Three formal consultations and two informal concurrences on effects of Federal actions on loach minnow, razorback sucker, and/or bald eagle in [[Blue River Basin]]:

1. **May 1986**: Biological opinion on [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]] Land and Resources Management Plan—concluded implementation should provide net benefits to loach minnow and minimize adverse impacts to wintering bald eagles
2. **May 1993**: Biological opinion on [[Campbell and Isabelle Timber Sales]] on [[Campbell Blue Creek]]—no jeopardy to loach minnow, no adverse modification of proposed critical habitat; concerns included increased sedimentation, hydrologic regime alteration, cumulative watershed effects
3. **April 1995**: Biological opinion on maintenance/repair of low-water ford crossing of Forest Road 745 on middle [[Blue River]]—not likely to jeopardize loach minnow or adversely modify critical habitat; concerns were long-term effects of localized channel modification, sediment contribution, accumulative effects
4. **February 1996**: Concurrence with "not likely to adversely affect" for loach minnow on road repair work at several sites on Forest Road 281 along [[Blue River]]; concerns were sediment production, bank destabilization, overall accumulative effects
5. **August 1996**: Concurrence with "not likely to adversely affect" for loach minnow for repair work on Highway 191 crossing of [[Campbell Blue Creek]]; concerns were sediment production, channel alteration, accumulative effects

## Key Findings

### Effects Determination

**Non-jeopardy conclusion**: Implementation of proposed rerouting of [[Navopache Power]] powerline is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of [[Loach Minnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], or [[Bald Eagle]], or to destroy or adversely modify presently-enjoined critical habitat of loach minnow.

### Direct and Indirect Effects

Because of deteriorated state of [[Blue River]], accumulating effects of numerous small impacts are of serious concern. Proposed action expected to have only small, localized adverse effects on three listed species. However, Service believes long-term future of native fish and riparian communities depends upon improving aquatic habitat status through removal or amelioration of all impacts, including small, accumulative effects.

**Project design strengths**:
- Avoidance of machinery in river channel prevents fish mortality through crushing or harassment
- Provision that no roadbed or bank material placed into river or floodplain minimizes sediment input

**Small adverse impacts**:
- Bank disturbance and possible removal of riparian vegetation have potential to create erosion and sediment and destabilize streambank, but effect would be very localized and short-term
- Adverse effects of stream sedimentation to fish and fish habitat extensively documented; loach minnow and eggs particularly vulnerable to substrate sedimentation due to benthic habit
- Excess sediment can adversely affect razorback sucker by filling pools and reducing spawning habitat areas, but sediment amount in proposed action not sufficient to cause those effects

**Cottonwood removal**: If removed, proposal calls for dropping into floodplain. Service supports this concept. Although some potential for bank erosion due to presence of downed tree, [[Blue River]] lacks large downed woody material within channel. Important role of large woody material in stream ecosystems well documented.

**Bald eagle**: Removal of large cottonwood is adverse effect as it removes potential roosting habitat. Proposal calls for trimming tree if possible and removing only if trimming insufficient. Service supports tiered approach.

**Toxic substances**: Potential exists for introduction of toxic substances (e.g., petroleum products) into stream during repair work. Potential expected to be minimal for proposed project.

### Cumulative Effects

Most land within [[Blue River]] watershed under [[U.S. Forest Service]] jurisdiction; activities affecting loach minnow (grazing, timber harvest) would be Federal actions subject to section 7 consultation. Recreation light to moderate with minor impact on river in project area. Primary cumulative effects derive from private lands in valley bottom on upper [[Blue River]]:

- Livestock grazing, cropping, residential development on floodplain terraces remove water from river and add to river system instability
- Aquaculture operation feeds predatory nonnative fish species into [[Blue River]] and diverts water
- **Forest Road 281** located along river from confluence of [[Campbell Blue Creek]] and [[Dry Blue Creek]] downstream approximately 25 miles; substantial portion in floodplain with several low-water ford crossings; due to location in upper watershed and destabilizing effects on river channel, considered **major adverse impact** on [[Blue River]] aquatic and riparian ecosystems

### Incidental Take Statement

**Bald eagle**: No incidental take anticipated.

**Razorback sucker**: Probability of being in immediate area very low; no incidental take anticipated.

**Loach minnow**: Incidental take anticipated through direct mortality or indirect mortality due to habitat loss/alteration. Adult or larval loach minnow or eggs present in work area may be poisoned by accidental introduction of toxic substances or smothered by localized sediment input. Indirect take may potentially occur through destruction or alteration of habitat from bank and riparian modification.

**Take quantification**: Anticipated level cannot be directly quantified due to low level of population data and inability to predict
