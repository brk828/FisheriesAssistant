# Fish Migration in Backwater Areas on Wheeler Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-migration

## Executive Summary

This 1948 study by Lawrence F. Miller and Paul Bryan of the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] examines whether fish move extensively through the narrow channels that connect shallow [[Backwater Migration|backwaters]] to the main body of [[Wheeler Reservoir]]. The authors tested this with two-way fish traps at [[Railroad Ponds]] (1944) and [[Sweetwater Slough]] (1947). The premise was that if fish moved widely through narrow channels, commercial fishermen could use traps to remove [[Rough Fish]] effectively. The traps showed that movement was not extensive through either channel. The two backwater populations were largely independent of the main reservoir, and few commercially valuable fish were involved.

## Background & Description

Spring fishing on the middle part of Wheeler Reservoir was concentrated in extensive shallow backwaters. Most were broadly connected to the main reservoir, but a few were linked by narrow channels. An earlier migration study (Tarzwell, 1941) indicated that fish moved extensively into and out of broadly connected bays. It suggested that movement through narrow channels might also be significant, which would make traps a practical tool for commercial rough-fish removal.

**Trap design:** The trap, designed by the second author, consisted of a pair of simple V-type traps, one opening upstream and one downstream. It was built of wooden rounds spaced about 3/4 inch apart, so small fish passed through and only fish wider than about 3/4 inch were captured. The trap was 7 feet deep, had a padlocked hinged wood cover, and used dirt fill on both sides to block movement around it. In 1944, fish were removed with small seines and dip nets. In 1947, a false bottom could be raised to the surface for removal.

**Sampling schedule:** At Railroad Ponds the trap was checked six days a week from February to July 1944, then twice weekly. Sweetwater Slough followed the same schedule in 1947, except that operations began in April.

**Study areas:**
- **[[Railroad Ponds]]:** A channel 6 to 8 feet wide connects the ponds to the reservoir. At normal pool (elevation 556), the channel has a minimum depth of 4.4 feet. The pond covers about 172 acres and is isolated at low winter water levels. The area was cleared of woodland before impoundment, so stumps are numerous. It consists of three basins, two of which were studied in 1941 (Tarzwell, 1941, 1945).
- **[[Sweetwater Slough]]:** The connecting channel is also 6 to 8 feet wide, with a minimum depth of about 2.5 feet at normal pool. The slough covers 27.6 acres and consists of an upper basin (17.5 acres at normal pool) and a lower basin (10.1 acres). In prolonged dry seasons it may shrink to 3 or 4 acres. It has marginal vegetation and a few stumps.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population estimates (pre-trap studies):**
- Railroad Ponds, 1941: the upper basin supported 292 lb/acre (May) to 342 lb/acre (September). The middle pond supported an estimated 791 lb/acre in September. Rough fish predominated.
- Sweetwater Slough, 1941: a June 4 study, made after the water receded, estimated 188 lb/acre. A July migration study after a sudden water rise recorded 624 lb of fish moving into the slough in 48 hours.
- Sweetwater Slough, 1945: 651 lb/acre excluding small fish and 700 lb/acre including them (Hess and Keener, 1947).
- In 1941, 71.9 percent of all fish were pan fish, but rough fish made up 72.6 percent of total weight. A similar pan/rough distribution occurred in 1945.

**Derris treatment (1947):** In early March, when the water was 52°F, [[Derris]] was applied to remove fish beyond the trap. Large bass, catfish, and carp died within two hours. Later, some fish, especially carp, were found to have survived by staying near a spring. The authors estimated that 90 percent or more of the original population had been removed before the study began.

**Water conditions:** Current in the channels varied in intensity and changed direction at irregular intervals. When reservoir level rose, flow went into the backwater. When it fell, flow reversed. Except during heavy rain, flow rate depended mainly on the rate of reservoir water-level change. Water temperature also affected movement:
- Movement into Railroad Ponds was greatest at 50 to 60°F.
- Movement out of the backwaters was most rapid at 70 to 80°F.
- During the Sweetwater Slough study, temperatures exceeded 60°F except on two days in May.
- Water levels were too low in early 1947 for movement until April, when the water had already warmed considerably. The authors suggest this late rise partly explains the smaller number of fish migrating through that channel.

## Key Findings

**1. Fish movement through narrow channels was limited.**
- **Railroad Ponds (1944, February 12 to October 31):** 6,984 fish were trapped moving in and 12,837 moving out. Most were [[Gizzard Shad]]. Most movement occurred in spring (Table 1).
- **Sweetwater Slough (1947, April 8 to August 7):** 586 fish entered and 430 left. [[Common Carp]] and gizzard shad predominated.
- Of the 586 entering Sweetwater Slough (mostly in late April), 551 were rough fish: 6 longnose gar, 2 dogfish, 111 shad, 8 smallmouth buffalo, and 424 carp. Of the 430 leaving, 389 were rough fish: 10 longnose gar, 1 dogfish, 100 shad, and 278 carp.
- Food fish entering Sweetwater Slough were 6 drum and 3 channel catfish. Four drum left.
- Of the 63 [[Game and Pan Fish]] trapped, 26 moved in and 37 moved out.

**2. Few fish were commercially valuable.** Few fish at either site were large enough to interest commercial fishermen, and total numbers were small. Railroad Ponds, though well populated, contributed little to the main reservoir's fish population and gained little from it. Large specimens were few in number for most species. Average size differed little between fish entering and leaving the backwaters.

**3. Current drove movement.** Fish movement was greatest when current was rapid and least when there was no flow (Table 2). This held for both areas. Movement was driven by reservoir water-level changes.

**4. Temperature affected direction and timing.** Fish moved into Railroad Ponds most at 50 to 60°F and out of the backwaters most at 70 to 80°F.

**5. Backwater populations were largely independent.** The authors concluded that the fish populations of the two backwaters were more or less independent of the main body of Wheeler Reservoir. Few fish migrated back and forth through the narrow connecting channels. The premise that fish traps in narrow channels would be effective for commercial rough-fish removal was therefore not supported.

**6. Context from related work.** The authors cite [[Tarzwell (1941)]] on backwater fish populations and management, and [[Tarzwell (1945)]] on the commercial fishery potential of TVA impoundments. They also cite [[Eschmeyer et al. (1944)]] on shoal-area fish populations and [[Hess and Keener (1947)]] on the effects of airplane-distributed DDT aerosols on fish. Eschmeyer and Tarzwell (1941) analyzed fishing in TVA impoundments during 1939.
