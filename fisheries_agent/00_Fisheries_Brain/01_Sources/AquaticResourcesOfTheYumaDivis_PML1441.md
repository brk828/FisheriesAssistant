# Aquatic Resources of the Yuma Division, Lower Colorado River (Phase II Draft Final Report, 1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

This draft final report (Phase II) by Marsh and Minckley, prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Lower Colorado Region under Contract 2-07-30-X0214 and conducted at Arizona State University, documents fishery and habitat studies in the Yuma Division of the [[Lower Colorado River]] from May 1985 through November 1986. It extends the Phase I survey (November 1982 to August 1984) and examines population dynamics, growth, food habits, and habitat relationships of key species following high flows that began in 1983.

The fish community comprised 21 species during Phase II. Only [[Striped Mullet]] was native. [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Goldfish]], [[Threadfin Shad]], and [[Blue Tilapia]] were overall the most abundant. Backwaters supported most biological production, and feeding relationships confirmed their importance.

## Background & Description

The Yuma Division is a 32.5-km reach from Laguna Dam to Morelos Dam, extending from Arizona into California and the Mexican border. The Gila River is the only natural tributary. Lengths of channel had been altered by levees, dikes, channelization, and riprap; Minckley (1979) estimated 90% of the reach had been modified. Flows were relatively stable after upstream dam closure, but high flows in summer 1983 (peak near 750 m³/s) transformed habitat.

Six habitat types were sampled: main channel (I), beaches and bars (II), deep backwater (III), shallow backwater (IV), connecting channels (V), and isolated pools (VI). Sampling used electrofishing, seines, hoop nets, and gill nets, with effort standardized as catch per unit effort (CPE).

The fish community is dominated by introduced species; the report notes no self-sustaining populations of native freshwater fishes are known in the mainstem downstream of Lake Mead.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Physical and chemical conditions:** Temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, and conductivity were unremarkable, with predictable seasonal trends in temperature and dissolved oxygen similar across the four-year period. Water temperatures ranged from 12.9 °C (main channel, February 1986) to 38.4 °C (shallow backwater margin, August 1986).
- **Habitat change:** High flows created extensive shallow and deep backwaters that flooded terrestrial areas and supported most biological productivity. Connecting channels and isolated pools, both minor habitats, were destroyed and reformed as river level changed.
- **Invertebrates and plankton:** Benthic macroinvertebrates were depauperate, dominated by chironomids and oligochaetes. Zooplankton were low relative to other large lotic systems and dominated by copepods; phytoplankton were dominated by diatoms.
- **Feeding guilds:** Detritivores, benthic insectivores, and piscivores were prominent; backwaters sustained most fish productivity through detritus-based food webs.

## Key Findings

- **Community:** 21 species were collected in Phase II (native: striped mullet; introduced species included a clupeid, three cyprinids, three ictalurids, three poeciliids, a percichthyid, seven centrarchids, and two cichlids). [[Mexican Molly]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Redbelly Tilapia]] were absent in Phase I but present in Phase II.
- **Dominance:** Red shiner comprised more than two-thirds of all specimens (roughly double the Phase I proportion). Mosquitofish about 7%, goldfish 6%, and threadfin shad 5% of all fish; flathead catfish, sailfin molly, bluegill, largemouth bass, and blue tilapia each 1 to 3%.
- **Gear selectivity:** Nearly 95% of individuals were taken by seine. More than half of species were not taken by one or more gear types.
- **Stability:** Seasonal and annual fluctuations in composition were much less pronounced than in Phase I. Red shiner ranged 51.7% to 85.4% of monthly samples in Phase II versus 14.0% to 87.1% in Phase I.
- **Catch per unit effort:** Hoop-net CPE in main channel averaged 1.45 fish per 12-hr set (range 0.16 to 5.00). Electrofishing in main channel averaged 37.43 fish per hour. Seine CPE in deep backwater averaged about 34 fish per 100 m².
- **Food habits:** Threadfin shad fed mainly on detritus and diatoms, with plankton relatively unimportant in this reach. Goldfish were benthic detritivores with a planktonic component. Carp fed primarily on detritus and chironomid larvae. Red shiner was omnivorous. Channel catfish were opportunistic carnivores, shifting from benthic insects (small) to fish (large). Striped bass were piscivorous.
- **Growth:** Length-frequency and scale/spine analyses were used for ten species. Threadfin shad lived up to about three, possibly four years. Flathead catfish ages reached at least nine years in the sample, and growth in the Division was similar to other riverine populations. Largemouth bass grew slower than most other southwestern populations, with growth potentially affected by high flows in 1983.
- **Condition:** Condition factors varied annually. Blue tilapia and bluegill showed significant increases from 1982 to 1983, while blue tilapia dropped between 1983 and 1984.
- **Species-specific trends:** Sailfin molly declined sharply after the 1983 flood, with populations recovering only in shallow backwaters. Flathead catfish increased in abundance between 1982 and 1986, likely reflecting habitat expansion. Blue tilapia colonized new backwaters and expanded dramatically in summer 1983 and 1984.
- **Sport fishery:** Channel catfish, flathead catfish, striped bass, largemouth bass, and blue tilapia received the most angler use. Flathead catfish became a significant creel component in 1981 and comprised 56% of fish creeled in 1985.
- **Management implications:** Backwaters are the trophic base of the lower river; reduction of backwater habitat under lower flows would likely have negative effects on most species. Riprap shorelines appeared to support few fish in early colonization. Results form a substantial baseline against which future changes can be measured.
