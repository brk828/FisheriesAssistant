# Draft Biological Opinion: IHS Water Diversion Impact on Loach Minnow, White River, Arizona (1992)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #consultation #habitat-assessment

## Executive Summary

This draft biological opinion, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) in August 1992, evaluates the [[Indian Health Service]] (IHS) proposal to divert water from the [[North Fork White River]] on the [[White Mountain Apache Reservation]], [[Navajo County]], Arizona. The project aims to supply 480,000,000 gallons per year to meet projected water demand for the town of [[Whiteriver]] by 2022. The FWS concludes that the proposed action is **likely to jeopardize the continued existence** of the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). The opinion provides four reasonable and prudent alternatives (RPAs) designed to avoid jeopardy while meeting water supply needs. The consultation period began December 6, 1991, with multiple extensions through November 1, 1992.

## Background & Description

### Species Status and Distribution

The [[Loach Minnow]] was listed as threatened on October 28, 1986. Historic range included the basins of the [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[San Francisco River]], and [[Gila River]]. Competition and predation by non-native fish plus habitat destruction have reduced range by more than 85 percent. Five remaining populations exist in limited portions of the upper [[Gila River]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Blue River]], [[Tularosa River]], and [[White River]], plus [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Campbell Blue Creek]], and [[Dry Blue Creek]] in Arizona and New Mexico. The FWS Albuquerque Regional Office and [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] have recommended uplisting to endangered status.

The [[White River]] population represents the only remnant from the [[Salt River]] subbasin and is the most geographically isolated of remaining populations. Distribution in the White River is not well documented. Loach minnow were collected in the mainstem in the mid-1960s, not again until 1985, then regularly since 1985 in the [[East Fork White River]] and mainstem. Currently known to occupy approximately 14 miles from below [[Geronimo Cave]] on the East Fork downstream to below [[Canyon Day]] on the mainstem. The population likely extends further downstream but access limitations prevent adequate sampling. No loach minnow have been found in the [[North Fork White River]] despite sampling since 1988 between [[Alchesay National Fish Hatchery]] and Whiteriver.

### Species Biology and Habitat Requirements

[[Loach Minnow]] are bottom-dwelling inhabitants of shallow, swift water over sand, gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates. They use spaces between and in the lee of larger substrate for resting and spawning. Rare or absent where fine sediments fill interstitial spaces. Filamentous algae may be an important habitat component.

Life span ranges from 1.5 to 3 years, with most surviving about 2 years. Feed exclusively on aquatic insects, primarily [[Ephemeroptera]] and [[Diptera]] larvae. Spawning occurs primarily March through May, with recent reports confirming autumn spawning under certain circumstances. Eggs are attached to the underside of rocks forming small cavity roofs on the downstream side. Limited data indicate males may guard nests during incubation.

Maintenance of natural flow regime is considered critical for survival. Natural flood volume and timing are of major significance: flood flows determine channel morphology and substrate, rejuvenate habitat, and limit populations of most non-native fishes. The common factor among river stretches maintaining remnant populations is relative absence of flow regime alterations.

Genetic research indicates existing isolated populations are genetically quite distinct. The [[White River]] population, being most geographically separate, is expected to possess a distinct genetic component. Loss of any major component of remaining genetic diversity may substantially decrease probability of long-term species survival.

### Proposed Project Description

**Intake and Treatment Plant:** Water would be withdrawn via an intake structure placed into the channel side, located approximately 6 miles above the confluence of North and East Forks (T.6N., R.23E., NW1/4 of NW1/4 Sec. 6). The intake structure consists of a concrete vault (18 feet deep, 6 feet square) embedded in the streambank, with a 30-inch wide, 14-foot long concrete channel between two rock gabions projecting to the river's edge at low flow. A 3-inch on center steel bar grate excludes large debris; a gate valve controls flow into the 30-inch diameter screened entrance (0.15-inch mesh). Water enters by gravity flow.

No damming, diking, channelization, or stream channel alteration would occur. Construction during relatively low river flow with contractor minimizing in-channel activity. A small temporary berm or sheet-metal piling (about 3 feet tall) might be needed during construction to direct flow away from the work area, to be removed afterward.

Three 60-horsepower pumps would be placed above high-water mark to move water through a 12-inch pipeline to the treatment plant located on a bench 0.7 miles from the river. Grading of the bench (6,900 square meters) necessary to create flat area. The half-mile distance to river should prevent sediment from disturbance entering the river. At the treatment plant, water would be treated with coagulant, filtered, and treated with chlorine and fluoride before storage in existing facilities.

**Operation:** Two-phase implementation. Phase I: two pumps operating at 1.56 cfs each (700 gpm), total 3.1 cfs. Phase II: third pump added for total 4.7 cfs. Minimum 5 inches water depth at channel entrance required for operation; IHS estimates 6 inches at intake equates to approximately 10 cfs river discharge.

If withdrawn evenly throughout the year, Phase I pumps would operate approximately 5 hours per day, withdrawing 408,000 gallons per day (149,000,000 gpy). However, withdrawal would vary seasonally (Table 2), with highest withdrawals during May, June, and July, reaching maximum 11 hours per day in June.

Phase II: three pumps operated approximately 10 hours daily, withdrawing 1,260,000 gpd (460,000,000 gpy). Seasonal variation pattern same as Phase I, reaching maximum 22 hours per day in June.

IHS would construct the facility but turn it over to the Tribe for operation using Tribal funds or user fees. Once transferred, IHS would have no further authority. No technical barrier exists to 24-hour operation. Although no current demand exists for larger water amounts, the Tribe indicated (March 9, 1992 letter) they wish to reserve the right to use additional capacity in the future. Phase I 24-hour operation would withdraw up to 736,000,000 gpy; Phase II up to 1,103,000,000 gpy.

The existing spring (183,000,000 gpy) would continue as part of primary water supply. Nine existing alluvial wells would not be abandoned but operated during low river flow when intake draws insufficient water or during treatment plant repair. Two wells ([[Post Office Canyon Wells]]) would remain in continuous operation supplying 83 homes (35,000,000 gpy supply vs. 13,000,000 gpy current demand).

Municipal and industrial demand would increase from 319,000,000 gpy at Phase I start to 480,000,000 gpy during Phase II. Treatment plant filters require periodic backflushing using 13,800,000 gpy (Phase I) or 41,400,000 gpy (Phase II) of untreated and treated water. Backflushing effluent would discharge into two settling ponds (37-day retention), then release into a dry drainage flowing about one-half mile before entering the North Fork. An [[NPDES]] permit would be obtained from [[EPA]] for that discharge.

Long-term plans include possible extension of water distribution to communities on the East Fork. If extended, diversion of [[East Fork Spring]] (currently supplying many homes) might cease, returning approximately 120 gpm (0.3 cfs) to the East Fork White River near the upstream limit of loach minnow range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Environmental Baseline

The [[White River]] has undergone substantial modification in channel and flow during the past century, adversely affecting native aquatic fauna. Non-native fish species have been introduced, further affecting native fauna. The present fish fauna (natives and non-natives) of the lower North and East Forks and mainstem is depauperate. Although little historic data exist, there appears to have been reduction in numbers of native species and abundance of individuals within species. Abundance of non-native species is also less than expected. Causes of this depression are thought related to numerous existing diversions, point and non-point pollutant discharges, channel modifications, gravel operations, sedimentation from watershed disturbances, and other human activities.

**Existing Water Withdrawals:** At present, diversions and alluvial wells remove a large portion of discharge from the North and East Forks and mainstem (Table 3). According to IHS estimates, 27.6 cfs is presently diverted from the river or its forks, either upstream from or within loach minnow habitat. An additional 0.75 cfs is removed from the North Fork through spring diversion (part of existing Whiteriver water supply). Total existing diversion: 28.4 cfs.

Existing diversions and wells on the North Fork (6.9 cfs) remove 25% of mean median July discharge at the [[McNary gauge]]. Those on the East Fork (6.5 cfs) remove 37% of mean median July discharge at the East Fork gauge. Total existing diversion within loach minnow range (28.4 cfs) is 45% of mean median July discharge at the [[Whiteriver gauge]].

**Substrate Conditions:** Substrate in loach minnow habitat in the East Fork and mainstem contains large amounts of fine sediment. Sediment accumulation not quantified, but substantial substrate embeddedness noted by biologists. Sources include several past and present gravel mining operations in the streambed, agricultural operations along the river, timber harvest in the watershed, and roads. Propst et al. (1988) found loach minnow rare in areas of deeply embedded substrate.

**Non-Native Species:** Introduction of predatory and competitive non-native fish has adversely affected loach minnow status. Predatory [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) are a major component of the fish community within loach minnow habitat. Other predatory non-natives include [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*). The only non-predatory non-native known is [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*).

The environmental baseline consists of a small population of a highly fragmented species occupying seriously depleted and modified habitat. Combined effects of existing water diversions, excessive sedimentation, unbalanced predator load, and numerous other adverse impacts have resulted in a depauperate fish fauna with loach minnow the rarest species present.

### Hydrologic Analysis

Analysis uses data from the [[USGS]] discharge gauge on North Fork White River near McNary (No. 09491000), about 28 miles upstream from the proposed diversion site. Period of record: continuous 1958-1985 (discontinued), plus 1951-1954 and sporadic 1945-1950. This gauge is upstream from the project site and on the same fork as the proposed project, making it more representative than the mainstem gauge downstream below the confluence.

Use of North Fork gauge data may result in slight overestimates of diversion effects on mainstem flows due to tributary contributions between the gauge and the East Fork confluence. The FWS believes this is consistent with the responsibility to give benefit of the doubt to listed species conservation (*Sierra Club v. Marsh* 816 F.2d 1367 [9th Cir. 1987]).

## Key Findings

### Jeopardy Determination

**It is the FWS biological opinion that the proposed water diversion and water treatment plant on the North Fork White River, as described in the documents, is likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the loach minnow.**

The proposed diversion of up to 4.7 cfs from the North Fork is expected to have serious adverse effects on loach minnow in the White River system and may result in extirpation of the population. Effects may not be limited to the mainstem portion but may also result in serious losses to, or extirpation of, loach minnow in the East Fork.

### Direct Effects of the Proposed Action

**Flood Flows:** The proposed project is not expected to result in significant changes to flood flows. Maximum instantaneous withdrawal capacity (4.7 cfs) is small compared to the 2-year flood event (275 cfs at North Fork gauge), one of the primary channel-forming flows.

**"Normal" (Median) Flows:** Project-caused changes in median flow may be the determining factor in long-term survival. Reduced abundance, distribution, and overall population health due to moderate habitat losses and low-level stress may remove the resilience required to survive short-term, high-level impacts such as natural or human-induced drought.

Mean monthly median flows for the North Fork gauge period of record are given in Table 4. Post-project changes under Phase I would reduce flows by 3.1 cfs; Phase II by 4.7 cfs. For example, July mean median flow of 27.9 cfs would be reduced to 24.8 cfs (Phase I) or 23.2 cfs (Phase II). Maximum withdrawal scenario (24-hour operation) would reduce July flow to 23.2 cfs.

Expected effects from median flow reduction include:
- Reduction in wetted area and direct reduction in loach minnow habitat availability
- More extreme water temperatures
- Increased effects from non-native predaceous and competitive fish
- Reduction in water quality through changes in water chemistry
- Alteration of nutrient levels
- Reduction in food availability
- Reduction of sediment transport capability resulting in increased substrate embeddedness and tendency toward braided channel

Specific mechanisms and impact levels cannot be quantified due to lack of adequate life history, population, habitat, hydrologic, hydraulic, and water quality data.

**Habitat Loss:** For loach minnow, median flow reduction is particularly likely to result in habitat availability loss. Loach minnow are habitat specialists and obligate riffle-dwellers, tending to occupy the top and margins of riffles (lower and center portions occupied by [[Speckled Dace]] *Rhinichthys osculus*, a common native). Riffle habitat is most vulnerable to dewatering from flow reduction; riffle habitat quantity may decline rapidly with only small flow reductions.

Median flow reductions that do not dewater riffles still alter riffle habitat. As flow reduces, velocity over riffles reduces and sediment deposition increases. With lower velocity and increased sediment, riffle configuration changes with a tendency toward braiding. Sediment deposition occurs first at upstream end and edges of riffles—the most desirable portions for loach minnow.

**Larval Habitat:** Loss of shallow riffle habitat may be of even greater significance to larval loach minnow than adults. Larvae inhabit significantly shallower and slower water than adults. Larvae are present during early summer months when proposed withdrawals are highest. Larvae have much more limited thermal range than adults and exhibit subtle habitat use shifts for thermal regulation. Reduction of larval habitat and alteration of its thermal regime may result in disproportionate larval losses due to thermal shock or displacement. Displacement may cause larvae to be entrained into swifter currents (swept downstream) or into deeper water (more vulnerable to predation).

**Spawning Habitat:** Egg deposition sites appear limited to a rather narrow velocity range. Median flow reductions may alter availability and decrease quality of spawning sites. Propst and Bestgen (1991) reported finding fungus-infected eggs in shallow, slow-velocity water near shore. As flow reduces over riffles, the proportion of near-shore to channel habitat increases, potentially reducing egg hatching success.

**Native vs. Non-Native Fish Relationships:** Median flow changes may alter existing relationships between native and non-native fishes. Non-native fish (green sunfish, fathead minnow, smallmouth bass) tend to be habitat generalists tolerating wide variety of currents, substrates, depths, water temperatures, and water chemistries. Alteration of natural stream conditions usually causes changes unfavorable to loach minnow (habitat specialist) and favorable to non-native generalists. Non-natives are primarily introduced for sport fishing and are usually predatory. Streamflow reduction crowds fish into deeper and smaller areas where predation can be intense, favoring predatory non-natives over non-predatory natives.

**Low Flows:** Low flows are critical for fish survival and may be a limiting factor for some native fish populations. Lowest recorded flows in the North Fork are shown in Table 5 with post-project changes. For example, the lowest recorded July flow of 11 cfs (1959) would be reduced to 7.9 cfs (Phase I), 6.3 cfs (Phase II), or 6.3 cfs (maximum withdrawal).

The proposed project does not appear likely to result in total flow loss in the North Fork or mainstem. However, it would result in large reductions in low flows at all operation levels. Low-flow reductions are likely to dewater extensive areas of shallow riffle habitat where loach minnow reside. Loach minnow from dewatered areas would most likely die due to desiccation, decreased oxygen levels, increased water temperatures, and increased predation.

As a riffle-dwelling species, loach minnow require flowing, well-oxygenated water. They cannot survive more than a few hours of flow depletion forcing them into non-flowing water. Loach minnow are unlikely to retreat into pools as flow depletes. Although they may move into runs, they are most likely to seek pockets of water among riffle substrate and remain there until flow is restored or they die.

**San Francisco River Example:** The [[San Francisco River]] in New Mexico provides an example of effects on loach minnow from repeated severe flow reductions.
