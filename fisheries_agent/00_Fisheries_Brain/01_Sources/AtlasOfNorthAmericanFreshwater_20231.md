# Atlas of North American Freshwater Fishes: 1983 Supplement, Freshwater Fishes of the Greater Antilles

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae #rivulidae #bythitidae #lepisosteidae #synbranchidae #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 Supplement to the [[Atlas of North American Freshwater Fishes]] was compiled by D. S. Lee, S. P. Platania, and G. H. Burgess of the [[North Carolina Biological Survey]] and the [[North Carolina State Museum of Natural History]]. It supplies species accounts for the freshwater fishes of the [[Greater Antilles]] (Bahamas, Cuba, Cayman Islands, Jamaica, Hispaniola, and smaller islands), which had been deferred from the 1980 edition. The region holds at least 59 native freshwater species, 24 exotic species, and 50 marine invaders. Local endemism is high, and many species are restricted to single sites. The document explicitly describes itself as a first attempt to assemble scattered literature, and it notes that many species are threatened by habitat destruction.

## Background & Description

- **Origin and purpose:** Inclusion of the Antillean fauna was requested by Richard Franz. The authors note the fauna has never been categorically summarized, many species had never been illustrated, and the biology of many is unstudied. The fauna shows its greatest affinity with fish faunas of southern North America, especially Mexico and Central America.
- **Scope:** The accounts cover seven families: [[Lepisosteidae]] (gars), [[Bythitidae]] (cusk-eels), [[Poeciliidae]] (livebearers), [[Cyprinodontidae]] (killifishes), [[Rivulidae]] (rivulids), [[Synbranchidae]] (swamp-eels), and [[Cichlidae]] (cichlids). Each account gives type locality, systematics, distribution and habitat, adult size, and biology, usually with a specimen-voucher drawing or photograph credit to the [[North Carolina State Museum]] (NCSM).
- **Taxonomic conventions:** Following [[Parenti 1981]], the order Cyprinodontiformes replaces Atheriniformes for Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, and Rivulidae. Atractosteus is elevated to generic rank following [[Wiley 1976]].
- **Illustrations and production:** Illustrations were prepared largely by [[Renaldo Kuhler]]. Funding for this portion did not come from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], which the author nonetheless thanks for support.
- **Citation note:** The author states that 19 accounts of mainland North American species accompany the supplement and that the work is best cited as a separate publication.
- **Back matter:** The document ends with a list of 1982 contributions of the survey (bird, mammal, and invertebrate papers), a catalogue of special publications with prices, and a common and scientific name index.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat diversity:** Fishes occupy small and large rivers, coastal mangrove swamps, streams, mountain torrents, springs, freshwater swamps, ponds, lakes, subterranean waters, and hypersaline lakes and pools. The greatest number of species occurs on [[Cuba]] and [[Hispaniola]], which have the most diverse aquatic habitats. [[Puerto Rico]] has no native freshwater species.
- **Endemism:** The high rate is attributed to insularity and complex local topography and geology. Only [[Gambusia puncticulata]] is relatively widespread in the region.
- **Cave and sinkhole fauna:** Cuban and Bahamian [[Lucifuga]] species live in limestone sinkhole caves. Water surfaces in these caves are often covered by a crust of carbonate of lime, caused by evaporation and carbon dioxide discharge.
- **Salinity gradients:** Several species span wide salinity ranges. [[Cubanichthys cubensis]] is recorded from freshwater to nearly marine conditions. [[Cyprinodon laciniatus]] inhabits brackish water (10–13 ppt). Hispaniolan saline lakes differ markedly: [[Lago Enriquillo]] is hypersaline at 48.9 ppt, while [[Etang Saumatre]] is brackish at 7.4 ppt.
- **Marine invaders:** About 50 marine species, including snook, mullets, gobies, and tarpon-related forms, commonly enter freshwater and form an important part of the freshwater fauna. They were excluded from the accounts because of duplication with the 1980 Atlas and scant distributional records.
- **Exotic species:** Twenty-four established exotics were recorded, including tilapias, peacock bass ([[Cichla ocellaris]]), and largemouth bass ([[Micropterus salmoides]]). Most are reported from Puerto Rico, with several from Hispaniola and Jamaica.
- **Lesser Antilles:** Only five native freshwater species are known there (*Ophisternon aenigmaticum*, *Poecilia reticulata*, *P. vivipara*, *Rivulus harti*, *R. marmoratus*). Two of these are marine invaders. The two *Poecilia* species show South American affinities.
- **Conservation concern:** Widespread habitat destruction is occurring across the region, and many species are considered to be in jeopardy.

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** At least 59 native freshwater species, 24 exotics, and about 50 marine invaders occur in the Greater Antilles. Table 1 totals are listed by island (Bahamas, Cuba, Cayman Islands, Jamaica, Hispaniola).
- **Genus richness:** The largest genera in the accounts are [[Limia]] (about 20 species, most restricted to Hispaniola and Cuba), [[Gambusia]] (13 accounts, several endemic to single lakes or springs), and [[Girardinus]] (8 Cuban species). Poeciliids dominate the fauna.
- **Narrow endemics:**
  - *Gambusia beebei* and *Limia fuscomaculata*, *L. garnieri*, *L. grossidens*, *L. immaculata*, *L. miragoanensis*, *L. ornata* are restricted to Etang de Miragoane (Lake Miragoane), Haiti.
  - *Gambusia dominicensis* is known only from its type series, has not been recollected despite extensive collecting, and its origin on Hispaniola is uncertain.
  - *Rivulus garciai* is known only from its holotype, taken from a brackish pool at Varadero, Cuba.
  - *Limia rivasi* is known only from a tidal creek in a red mangrove swamp on La Gonave Island, Haiti.
- **Cuban gar:** *Atractosteus tristoechus* is known from western Cuba and the Isle of Pines, especially Ciénaga de Zapata, and is considered an elevation to genus level following Wiley (1976). The text records its maximum size as 2000 mm, which appears to be a scan or typographic error and should be verified.
- **Cave cusk-eels:** *Lucifuga dentatus* (Cuba), *L. spelaeotes* (New Providence, Bahamas; mean salinity one meter below surface 4.6 ppt), and *L. subterraneus* (Cuba) are viviparous or specialized cave fishes. The text reports *L. subterraneus* containing four 20 mm young in a 65 mm female.
- **Reproduction and feeding (selected):**
  - *Limia nigrofasciata* females produce 15–30 large young per delivery; adults are cannibalistic on young.
  - *Cubanichthys pengelleyi* eggs are 1.2–1.4 mm and hatch in about 9 days.
  - *Ophisternon aenigmaticum* nests in a horizontal mud tunnel, and the male guards eggs.
- **Water-quality data:**
  - *Cubanichthys pengelleyi* occurs in springs with pH 8.2 and 24–26 °C.
  - *Gambusia xanthosoma* was recorded from a Grand Cayman ditch with salinity 30‰; the text gives the water temperature as "82°C," which is physiologically implausible and likely a transcription error for a much lower value.
- **Taxonomic decisions:**
  - *Limia* is retained as a valid genus, following Rivas (1978), rather than as a subgenus of *Poecilia*.
  - *Poecilia dominicensis* retains its specific name under Article 59(c) of the Code, after the homonymy issue.
  - *Cubanichthys* is placed in its own subfamily, Cubanichthyinae, with *C. cubensis* and *C. pengelleyi*.
- **Range of [[Ophisternon aenigmaticum]]:** It ranges from northeastern South America through Guatemala and Mexico to Cuba, and occurs in habitats from muddy pools to lakes and streams.
- **Taxonomic gap:** Several Hispaniolan forms (*Cyprinodon bondi*, *Rivulus roloffi*) were recognized by earlier authors without discussion, and the *Cyprinodon variegatus* complex requires major revision.
- **Concluding statement:** The authors argue the accounts should support biogeographic hypotheses for the region and call for further study before many species are lost.
