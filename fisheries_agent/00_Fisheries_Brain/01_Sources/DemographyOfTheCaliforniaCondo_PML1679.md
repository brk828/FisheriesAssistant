# Demography of the California Condor: Implications for Reestablishment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cathartidae

## Executive Summary

This paper (Meretsky, Snyder, Beissinger, Clendenen and Wiley, *Conservation Biology* 14(4), August 2000) examines why reintroduced [[California Condor]] populations have failed to become self-sustaining. The authors build a simple female-based, age-structured deterministic model of the historic wild population to derive benchmark mortality rates, then evaluate the outcomes of captive-bred releases in California and Arizona. The wild population declined to about 30 birds by the late 1970s, and mortality in the early 1980s (average 26.6% per year) far exceeded the levels needed for stability. Releases since 1992 have shown annual mortality well above the roughly 10% sustainability threshold, mainly from [[Lead Poisoning]] and collisions with overhead wires. The authors conclude that future releases should use parent-reared birds, that puppet-reared birds should be removed from the wild, and that nontoxic ammunition should be adopted as the top near-term priority.

Note: the source is a research article, so the "Background & Description" section below summarizes its demographic background and methods.

## Background & Description

**Species and population history.** The California Condor (*Gymnogyps californianus*) ranged along the Pacific Coast from British Columbia to Baja California in the early 1800s. By the late 1970s the range had shrunk to a region around the southern San Joaquin Valley and the population numbered about 30 birds (Wilbur 1980). A captive flock was founded in 1982, and the last wild bird was captured in 1987, when the captive population stood at 27 individuals (14 females, 13 males). First captive reproduction occurred in 1988. By mid-1998 the total exceeded 150 birds. Reintroductions began in 1992, and by early 1999 88 birds had been released in 16 attempts.

**Reproductive parameters.** Age at first breeding was assumed to be 6 to 8 years, based on captive data (adult plumage at about 5 years; no documented subadult breeding). The oldest known captive breeder was 32 years old. Clutch size is one egg. Successful pairs breed in roughly 2 of 3 years; unsuccessful pairs breed annually. Replacement clutching was assumed at 25–75%. The sex ratio was 1:1 (135 males and 131 females recorded). Adult breeding was assumed at 50–80%, and nesting success at 40–50%.

**Modeling approach.** The model is female-based, age-structured, deterministic, with a prebreeding census and a one-year time step. It estimates the annual mortality rate that yields a stable population (λ = 1) across different reproductive scenarios: "most likely," "optimistic," "pessimistic," and "maximum conceivable" (100% nesting success, two young every three years, breeding at age 5). Maximum age was set at 100 years. The model excludes stochasticity and catastrophes.

**Historic mortality.** Between early 1982 and early 1986, when the population fell from 23 to 5 birds, mean annual mortality was 26.6% per bird-year. Immature birds (22.2%) died at a rate similar to adults (26.8%), suggesting the causes of death were not strongly age-dependent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats in the historic population.** Of 15 free-flying condors that died in the early 1980s, only four were recovered for necropsy. Three were victims of lead poisoning, and one died of cyanide poisoning from a poisoned coyote. Other factors included collisions with overhead wires and illegal shooting. Lead likely came from bullet fragments in carcasses shot by hunters. The switch to steel shot in the 1980s offered little benefit, because condors eat waterfowl and other animals shot with shotguns.

**Reproductive threats.** Nesting condors preferred areas with low [[Golden Eagle]] abundance, and eagle predation on nestlings is a concern where eagles are present. Common Ravens (*Corvus corax*) depredated eggs and were the most important cause of nesting failure in the historic population. Most current release regions have abundant raven populations.

**Comparison with other release programs.** [[Eurasian Griffon Vulture]] releases in France and [[Andean Condor]] releases in Peru and Colombia were far less problematic. Adult griffon mortality was about 12% per year, mainly from power-line electrocutions, and the wild population grew through natural reproduction. The authors attribute the Peru and Colombia success to integrating released birds into existing wild populations.

## Key Findings

- **Stability threshold.** Under the most likely scenario, adult mortality must average about 10% per year for a stable or increasing population. Adult mortality allowing stability was 5.3–6.7% (pessimistic), 7.5–9.9% (most likely), and 9.5–13.4% (optimistic). These values are only about 25–50% of the historic wild mean (26.6%). Even the maximum conceivable scenario permitted no more than about 10.4–17.8% mortality (see the study's Table 1), still well below historic rates. The authors conclude that the wild population suffered excessive mortality and that improving reproduction alone could not rescue it.
- **Release mortality.** Annual mortality in most releases greatly exceeded the roughly 10% sustainability level, despite clean food provisioning. The November 1997 Ventana Wilderness release of parent-reared birds was the only ongoing release with acceptable mortality for an appreciable period (no mortalities as of late 1999).
- **Lead resurgence.** Lead poisoning increased from 1997 onward, with six cases requiring chelation therapy and seven additional low-level exposures. Released birds increasingly fed on natural carcasses and abandoned food subsidies. The authors argue that emergency chelation is not a cost-effective long-term solution, so treated birds should be counted as mortalities. Counting them raised three release mortality rates to 18%, 17%, and 35%.
- **Collisions and behavior.** Collisions with overhead wires were frequent in early releases and declined after aversive conditioning on electrified dummy utility poles, though the authors note confounding factors. Human-oriented behavior, driven largely by puppet-reared birds, caused settled-area incursions, property damage, and retrapping. Of 21 parent-reared birds, 76% were alive in the wild, compared with 49% of 67 puppet-reared birds.
- **Recommendations.** The authors recommend (1) ending releases of puppet-reared birds and returning human-oriented birds to captivity; (2) restricting future releases to parent-reared birds and testing parent-rearing variants such as field enclosures; (3) designing future releases as true experiments with controls; (4) adopting nontoxic ammunition, such as tungsten-bismuth-tin (TTB) shot, in release areas as the overriding near-term goal; and (5) preferring large hunting-free reserves or single-site food subsidy only as secondary options, since the latter imposes perpetual costs.
- **Breeding constraints.** Only 7 of 19 nestlings from 15 fertile pairs were parent-reared in the 1999 season, largely because multiple clutching reduces parent-rearing opportunities. The authors argue that proliferating release sites before achieving self-sustaining populations at any one site is unhelpful.
- **Note added in proof (2000).** Three lead-poisoning deaths occurred in Arizona, a wild bird was released into the Sespe region, and the Ventana population expanded into Lion Canyon, where birds caused property damage. Parent-reared flock isolation was no longer possible after puppet-reared birds were added in spring 2000.
