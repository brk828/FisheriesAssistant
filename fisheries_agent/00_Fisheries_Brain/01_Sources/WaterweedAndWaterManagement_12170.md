# Water-Weed and Water Management

#limnology #macrophytes #freshwater-ecology #water-management

## Executive Summary

This 1960 paper by D. F. Westlake, a botanist with the Pollution Research Unit of the Freshwater Biological Association, was presented to the Institution of Public Health Engineers. It reviews the available quantitative data on macroscopic aquatic plants ("water-weed") and applies it to river pollution, water supply, land drainage, fisheries and sewage disposal. The author argues that water-weed is usually minor in lakes and reservoirs but can be very important in rivers, where it strongly affects dissolved oxygen, flow levels and silting. Because the effects depend heavily on local conditions, he concludes that control decisions must be made case by case, and that the timing and extent of cutting should follow from whether the critical concern is the minimum or the average oxygen level. The paper's main contribution is a set of biomass, oxygen-production and flow measurements from British and North American sites, which the author presents as an early basis for quantitative weed management.

## Background & Description

**Scope.** The paper covers macroscopic plants, mainly flowering plants (angiosperms), with some mosses and large algae such as [[Cladophora]]. Microscopic algae, including [[Phytoplankton]], are excluded, though the author notes they are important in reservoirs and sewage treatment.

**Plant physiology.** Growth depends on [[Photosynthesis]], which takes up carbon dioxide and releases an equal volume of oxygen. The rate of photosynthesis depends mainly on light intensity, carbon dioxide supply and, to a lesser extent, temperature. [[Respiration]] is mainly temperature-sensitive. Photosynthesis exceeds respiration during daylight, and only respiration occurs at night. Some free-floating plants take up all their mineral ions through their leaves.

**Biomass composition.** Weed biomass is about 90% water, 7.5% organic material and 2.5% mineral ash, though these proportions vary with species and conditions.

**Flow and volume.** Weed volume, rather than weight, is the more relevant measure for drainage. Internal air spaces make the fresh weight-to-volume relationship variable. For *Potamogeton pectinatus*, 1 kg fresh weight was equivalent to 1.6 litres.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lakes and reservoirs.** Light decreases with depth, so weed colonises only a small share of the bottom. In [[Lake Mendota]] (maximum depth 26 m), only one quarter of the total area supports plants. Plants were found to 7 m, and three-quarters of the total growth occurred in water shallower than 3 m. Generally, plants are not found deeper than 10 m in clear water or 2 m in turbid water, so dense growth is unlikely in steep-sided reservoirs. In most cases, water-weed is relatively unimportant for reservoir management compared with floating algae. In a poorly weeded lake, the standing crop of floating algae was estimated to exceed that of rooted plants by 45:17, and the annual production ratio was well over 100:1. In Lake Mendota the difference was smaller: the annual algal crop was probably only about three times the weed crop.

Where weed growth is substantial, its main effect is a gradual increase in reservoir fertility. Macrophytes sink and their nutrients accumulate in sediments, where release depends on decay rate, disturbance and anaerobic conditions. Weed can therefore favour the reservoir by shifting nutrient use away from algae.

**Standing crops.** Reported biomass values (fresh weight) are:
- [[Lake Mendota]] (eutrophic): 1.8 kg/m² in the colonised area; 0.47 kg/m² averaged over the whole lake (about 40 mg/l by volume).
- Densest reported shallow-bay population: 5.1 kg/m².
- [[Dubbs Reservoir]] (shallow, northern England): maximum 0.66 kg/m².
- Very infertile waters: average below 1 g/m² over the colonised area.

**Rivers.** Rivers are shallow and rarely deep enough for abundant floating algae, so the bottom is generally well lit. In fertile, not-too-swift waters, dense submerged growth develops. Emergent plants in marginal water mainly restrict flow and contribute little to river chemistry or fishery productivity.
- [[Maple Lodge Sewage Works]] effluent channel (turbid, polluted, about 1 m deep): 1.5 kg/m² at maximum growth.
- [[River Ivel]] (clear, unpolluted chalk stream, about 0.7 m deep): summer maximum 7.3 kg/m².

Expressed by volume, these river crops correspond to 2,000–15,000 mg/l, far above all but the shallowest lakes. Effects of weed are therefore usually much greater in rivers than in lakes, even at similar areal densities.

**Seasonality.** Temperate water plants grow rapidly in spring and summer, reaching peak density in midsummer or early autumn. Some die back in autumn, leaving buried perennating organs, while others keep green shoots through winter. The summer maximum occurred as early as July at Maple Lodge and as late as September on the Ivel. The figures above are all summer maxima.

**Pollution and oxygen.** Weed affects the oxygen balance more than any other factor. Without plants, oxygen is set by the balance of [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]], [[Benthic Oxygen Demand]] and [[Re-Aeration]]. With plants, photosynthesis adds oxygen by day, while respiration consumes it at night, reaching a maximum in darkness.

Three methods were used to quantify these effects:
1. **Oxygen change between stations**, assuming other factors are constant or predictable. On the River Ivel, midday production was of the order of 1.0 g/m² per hour.
2. **Calculation** from weed mass, photosynthetic characteristics and stream physics. For the Maple Lodge channel, the author estimated noon production of about 0.7 gO₂/m² per hour on a fine July day, about 0.5 g/m² per hour on an average day, and consumption of 0.05 g/m² per hour at noon on a very dull day and 0.18 g/m² per hour at night. The net daily figure for an average July day was about +1.8 g/m².
3. **Carbon content of the crop**, using 1 gC = 2.67 gO₂ from the photosynthetic equation.

## Key Findings

**1. Oxygen change on the River Ivel.** The observed change of about 3.6 ppm over 600 yd was close to the predicted range of 3.0–5.5 ppm, which depended on assumptions about river volume and flow.

**2. Oxygen change per kilometre.** For the Maple Lodge effluent, a predicted noon increase of 2.6 ppm after 1 km (4.2 ppm per mile) on a fine July day was estimated. Changes of up to 10 ppm/km were considered possible in ideal conditions. The River Ivel showed 7.3 ppm/km. These changes are comparable to those produced by BOD.

**3. Average net oxygen production.**
- Maple Lodge channel, March–July average: 0.82 g/m² per day.
- River Ivel, June–September average (from carbon fixation): 3.8 g/m² per day.

The author warns that averages can hide the single occasion of oxygen depletion that could be fatal to fish.

**4. Sag-curve effects.** Photosynthesis reduces the oxygen deficit and raises the minimum. Respiration at night deepens it. The author considers four scenarios, including constant pollution load, night-reduced loads, a weeded upstream reach, and critical points beyond twelve hours. In the last case, average sag curves stay above those without weed, provided oxygen losses are not excessive.

**5. Management rules for oxygen.**
- Leave healthy growing weed where daytime oxygen can reach the critical point.
- Remove weed where it is in overnight contact with water just upstream of the critical point. "Night" is taken as about ten hours in the peak growth months, so the night flow limit is the point ten hours upstream of the critical point.
- If average oxygen matters more than the minimum, avoid cutting healthy weed unless it becomes very dense. A tentative threshold of 3–5 kg/m² was suggested, depending on turbidity.

**6. Other chemical effects.**
- Each 2.0 ppm rise in oxygen corresponds to a 2.75 ppm fall in total [[Carbon Dioxide]]. This raises pH and lowers toxic free CO₂, so fish can tolerate lower oxygen levels.
- Higher pH increases the proportion of toxic [[Unionised Ammonia]], partly offset by nitrogen uptake by weed (about 0.1 ppm N per 2.0 ppm oxygen rise).
- Plants concentrate radionuclides 1,000 to over 100,000 times above water concentrations. This may confine contamination, but it can also be transferred through food chains. The author recommends considering this when setting limits for [[Radioactive Contamination]] from waste disposal.

**7. Weed volume and flow.** Dense weed populations occupy 1–2% of river volume. Hillebrand's study of the River Eder (70 m wide, average depth 0.8 m, discharge 20 m³/s) found:
- Water level rose by more than 75 cm due to weed alone, and maximum velocity fell to 75% of the weed-free value.
- Water rose by more than 1 cm per day at peak growth despite constant discharge.
- The weed index W (0.57 at maximum) was tentatively equated with 4% of river volume, though the relationship is far from linear. The index ranked growth well, with least weed in February (W = 0.19) and most in July.

**8. Flow measurement under weed.** Several approaches were reviewed in the [[River Gauging]] discussion:
- An artificial control such as a weir (may be impermissible in navigable waters).
- Removing weed below the gauge. On the Eder this needed about 300 m, which was uneconomically long.
- Level-discharge calibration curves for different weed conditions, which fail because weed varies within weeks.
- Friction-factor formulae, which proved impractical; the factor at maximum growth was about three times the weed-free value.
- Maximum surface velocity diagrams (accuracy ±8%).
- Submerged-rope tension diagrams (accuracy ±2.8%), considered better than many vane measurements.

**9. Silting.** Velocities within weed clumps can be as low as one-tenth of open-water flow, while channels between clumps run fast. Hillebrand's evidence showed that average and maximum velocities are lower in weed, contrary to the common view that weed reduces silting. In heavily abstracted rivers, weed can build silt banks while leaving gravel-bottomed channels, which may benefit fisheries.

**10. Fisheries.** The author stresses that weed is desirable for fisheries because it supplies organic matter and habitat. Needham's figures for animal biomass were:
- Rock and gravel: about 0.8 g/ft²
- Silt: 3–5 g/ft²
- Weed: 12 g/ft²

**11. Sewage and nutrient recovery.** Algal sewage treatment is considered unreliable in Britain because of weak and seasonal sunshine. Nutrients in effluent could in principle be recovered by growing fast-growing plants for fodder or protein, but the ideal plant had not been found. The water hyacinth ([[Water Hyacinth]]) was tested and found unsuitable because it cannot survive temperate winters.

**12. Sampling variability.** Coefficients of variation were 26% (five samples, Maple Lodge) and 20% (nine samples, Ivel), and up to 50% can occur. To estimate the mean within 5% in 95% of cases, the author gives N = 1.96σ/0.05x, which required 105 samples at Maple Lodge. Three workers can handle only four or five transects per day, so this accuracy was considered unattainable. An optical method using light readings at several depths was suggested as a possible cheaper alternative, though its accuracy was unknown. See [[Weed Sampling]].

**13. Control principles.**
- Chemical control is generally rejected: even non-toxic herbicides destroy habitat, spread downstream, and leave dead weed that decays and fouls water. The same applies to cutting unless the weed is removed.
- Cutting in late summer, after flooding has begun, is poor practice. The author suggests cutting when plants are only half-grown, which avoids flooding and reduces the volume handled.
- Cutting and removing weed just after the annual maximum secures the most oxygen gain, since decay would otherwise return oxygen consumption.
- The ideal is to set a target amount and distribution of weed suited to all interests, and maintain it through regular cutting. See [[Weed Control]].

**14. Discussion points.**
- Mr. Edwards reported a 7.3 ppm/km change on the Ivel, reflecting a very low current speed of 0.18 ft/s. The source gives 54 hours to travel 1 km, which looks like a transcription error, since the figure implies roughly 5 hours.
- A Water Pollution Research Laboratory study on the Ivel found that a sewage effluent (about 5% of dry-summer flow) raising inorganic nitrogen from about 8.0 to 10.0 ppm N and phosphate from 0.02 to 0.6 ppm P had little or no effect on weed growth.
- Mr. Westlake noted that detergents may reduce re-aeration and may kill older weed tissue, which could shift the seasonal maximum earlier.
- Selective herbicides were judged unsuitable for multi-purpose rivers.
- Weed-removal of metallic cations was considered a possible but unresolved benefit; radioisotope studies showed rapid initial uptake, with ions returned to water on decay unless the weed is removed.

**Overall conclusion.** Water-weed is generally of limited importance in lakes and reservoirs but very significant in rivers, where its effects on oxygen, flow and silting are often large. The author calls for management tailored to each situation, for quantitative data collected for each specific problem, and for further research. He notes that weed is usually desirable under normal conditions, but may need to be removed entirely in special cases.
