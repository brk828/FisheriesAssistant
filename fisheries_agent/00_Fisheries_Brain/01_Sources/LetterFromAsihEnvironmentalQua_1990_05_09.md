## Executive Summary

This document is a formal letter from the **American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists (ASIH) Environmental Quality Committee**, authored by Walter R. Courtenay, Jr., opposing a proposal by the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources to introduce **[[Rainbow Smelt]]** (*Osmerus mordax*) into **[[Lake Powell]]** as a forage/sportfish enhancement measure. The letter is not a primary research paper but a science-based policy critique, drawing on prior introduction case studies (notably **[[Striped Bass]]** in Lake Mead and Lake Powell) and published literature on rainbow smelt diet ecology to argue that the proposed introduction poses unacceptable risks to native and endangered fishes of the **[[Colorado River Basin]]**. The committee concludes the introduction should not proceed without a mandatory Section 7 consultation under the Endangered Species Act.

## Background & Description

No morphological data on rainbow smelt is presented in this letter; the document is ecological/policy-focused rather than descriptive of anatomy. Rainbow smelt is referenced only in terms of trophic ecology and life-history behavior relevant to introduction risk.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native range context**: Rainbow smelt is native to northeastern North America (e.g., the Great Lakes region, referenced via the Crystal Lake, Michigan case study); it is a non-native species relative to the American Southwest and the Colorado River drainage.
- **Feeding ecology**:
  - Generally described in the literature as **not preferentially piscivorous** — it favors invertebrates, particularly crustaceans, over other fish.
  - However, rainbow smelt is characterized as a **voracious, omnivorous predator** capable of **cannibalism**.
  - Under certain lake conditions (e.g., **Crystal Lake, Michigan**), rainbow smelt diet has been documented to consist of **>90% fish prey**, demonstrating strong dietary plasticity depending on food base availability.
  - The committee raises concern that in **[[Lake Powell]]**, given uncertainty over the availability of the smelt's "preferred" invertebrate food base, introduced rainbow smelt would likely shift diet toward available native fish populations — both within Lake Powell and in connected waters above and below **[[Glen Canyon Dam]]**.
- **Introduction risk model**: The letter emphasizes that fish introductions do not reliably reproduce trophic behavior or ecological outcomes across differing biogeographic contexts — food preferences observed in a species' native or previously introduced range are not necessarily replicated in novel southwestern reservoir ecosystems.
- **Comparative case — Striped Bass**: Cited as a precedent for introduction failure/risk. Striped bass introductions into **Lake Mead** and **Lake Powell** did not replicate the "successful" outcomes seen in Santee-Cooper (South Carolina) and other southeastern U.S. reservoirs. Instead, striped bass introduction **devastated a previously established introduced salmonid sportfishery** in these lakes and was, at the time of writing, threatening to do the same in **[[Lake Mohave]]**.
- **Endangered species context**: The proposed introduction area overlaps with habitat for **endangered fishes of the Colorado River Basin**, both within Lake Powell and in river reaches above and below it, extending impact potential across multiple U.S. states and three U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service administrative regions.

## Key Findings

- **No robust ecological justification**: The Utah proposal (authored by Gustaveson, Maddux, and Bonebrake) is characterized as sportfishery-driven rather than grounded in conservation of native or endangered Colorado River Basin fishes.
- **Precedent of introduction failure**: The striped bass case in Lake Mead/Lake Powell demonstrates that management-by-introduction in this specific reservoir system carries a documented history of unintended, severe negative impacts on existing (previously introduced) sportfisheries.
- **Dietary plasticity is the central risk factor**: Rainbow smelt's capacity to shift from an invertebrate-dominated diet to a >90% fish diet (as in Crystal Lake, Michigan) undermines claims in the Utah proposal that the species poses minimal risk to native/endangered fish populations.
- **Geographic non-transferability**: The committee explicitly rejects the reasoning that successful/benign introductions elsewhere (non-southwestern U.S.) predict similarly benign outcomes in the ecologically distinct Colorado River Basin/American Southwest.
- **Absence of causal analysis**: The proposal is criticized for not investigating *why* earlier introduced Lake Powell sportfisheries failed before proposing a new introduction as a solution — described as a pattern of "hit or miss" management.
- **Regulatory conclusion**: Because endangered fishes occupy the impacted drainage, the committee states that a **Section 7 consultation** (under the U.S. Endangered Species Act) is mandatory before proceeding.
- **Committee recommendation**: The ASIH Environmental Quality Committee formally recommends the rainbow smelt introduction **not proceed**, citing insufficient risk assessment, untested assumptions about diet and food-web impacts, and inadequate consideration of multi-state, multi-region ecological consequences.
