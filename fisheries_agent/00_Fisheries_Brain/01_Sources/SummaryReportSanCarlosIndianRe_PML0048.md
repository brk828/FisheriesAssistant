# Fishery Management Report: San Carlos Indian Reservation (1967)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 summary report from the [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]] (Division of Sport Fisheries, Albuquerque, New Mexico) reviews a recreational fishery program run on the [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] in Arizona. The program was carried out with the [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]] and the [[Bureau Of Indian Affairs]] over roughly five years. Angler use rose from about 11,000 angler-days in 1953 to 201,534 in 1966, then fell an estimated 19 percent to 169,569 in 1967. The report attributes the decline mainly to a copper-industry strike and to weather conditions. It identifies two major problems: unreliable reservoir water from low annual precipitation, and poor road access to many ponds. It also covers lake development, the status of new road access, and a set of recommendations for future construction and maintenance.

## Background & Description

The reservation's fishery program depends on a network of small, mostly man-made fishing waters, many built originally as livestock stock tanks. The report describes the reservation's life zones as ranging from Lower Sonoran to mixed conifer, which the author argues would allow fishery development in all seasons of the year. The reservation sits on two main highways leading anglers to Arizona's trout waters and is close to the state's population centers.

Fishing waters described in the report include:

- **Stock tanks and small ponds:** Many were originally built as stock tanks and become marginal for fish life at various times of the year.
- **[[Seneca Lake]]:** A constructed lake with a concession building and boat facilities, developed for fishing.
- **[[Point Of Pines Lake]]:** A new lake that did not fill during the year. Campground and other facilities were incomplete.
- **Upper [[Black River]] area:** Primarily a trout water, reachable by a newly opened road.
- **[[Black River]] and [[Salt River]]:** Supporting a growing bass fishery, with fish up to 21 pounds reported.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report stresses that the reservation's fishing waters are fragile and highly variable.

**Water supply and precipitation.** Low annual precipitation is the first major problem. Most small ponds were built as stock tanks, so they become marginal for fish at various times of the year. Earlier in the program, very little runoff reached 15 of the 50 tanks, and the state provided only a few fingerling trout to those waters. The report says that after summer rains, only 39 tanks could be stocked with fingerling trout. Several of these water tanks lost their fish populations and will need to be restocked the following year. In 1967, rainfall was sufficient to make roads difficult but insufficient to provide additional storage water for all the stock tanks.

**Access and isolation.** The second major problem is poor access. Many ponds are isolated, which makes it very difficult and often too costly to census individual tanks. Pressure estimates therefore rely on observations and on permits sold. Several attempts to use car counters at certain sites were abandoned because the counters were vulnerable to damage and theft.

**Introduced "trash fish."** The report notes that Seneca Lake has shown a decline in fishing pressure since trash fish were inadvertently introduced. The author recommends renovating the lake and returning it to a straight trout fishery, or a combination trout and channel catfish lake (the scanned text is partly illegible at this point).

**Effects of new roads.** The new all-weather highway is expected to increase accessibility and fishing pressure at lakes that are easy to reach. The report also notes that a road into the upper Black River area, if improved and maintained, could open a previously inaccessible trout water to several thousand angler-days of fishing (the exact figure is partly illegible in the scan).

## Key Findings

**1. Angler use trends**
- 1953: about 11,000 angler-days
- 1966: 201,534 angler-days (high point of the program)
- 1967: estimated 169,569 angler-days, a 19 percent decrease
- The author notes that a small program makes any fluctuation look large as a percentage.

**2. Causes of the 1967 decline**
- A strike in the copper industry, which reduced the usual flow of anglers from that sector. The decline was reported across Arizona.
- Weather that was less than optimal: road travel was difficult after rains, and rainfall was too low to refill all stock tanks.
- The 1967 estimate also excluded the late-season winter angler pressure that had been a significant part of the reservation's total.

**3. Two major management problems**
- Low and variable annual precipitation, which causes small stock-tank ponds to lose their fish populations.
- Poor access to isolated waters, which hampers creel surveys and pressure estimates.

**4. Lake and site status**
- [[Seneca Lake]]: Fishing pressure has fallen since trash fish were introduced. Renovation and restocking as a trout fishery were recommended. A concession building and boat facilities were built, but the report doubts the lake is large enough to support boat use.
- [[Point Of Pines Lake]]: Not filled during the year. Campground and facilities were incomplete. Opening was expected in spring 1968.
- Several new lake sites were surveyed in 1967. Construction of the largest and most desirable sites was recommended once funds become available, with good road access included in planning.

**5. Recommendations**
1. Increase effort by the San Carlos Apache Tribe and the Bureau of Indian Affairs to build new lakes as funds become available.
2. Increase effort by the San Carlos Apache Tribe and the Bureau of Indian Affairs to build and maintain access roads to existing ponds and new developments.
3. Complete the campground and facilities at Point Of Pines Lake and develop the site on a paying basis.
4. Further effort should be made to plan and develop a dam for the new highway crossing at Freezeout Canyon.
5. Install a pipe in the Tufa Stone dam and drain so that a higher water level can be maintained and a permanent fishery kept for the local children.

**6. Acknowledgments and distribution**
- The author thanks the Tribe and the Bureau of Indian Affairs for their interest and progressive attitude, and the staff of the Alchesay and Williams Creek national fish hatcheries.
- The report credits San Carlos Apache game rangers with keeping the area's popularity high.
- Copies were distributed to the Washington Office, Phoenix BIA Office, San Carlos Agency, Bylas and San Carlos Field Offices, the Williams Creek NFH, and the Regional Office. Distribution counts were 2, 2, 3, 3, 10, 2 and 1 copies respectively.
