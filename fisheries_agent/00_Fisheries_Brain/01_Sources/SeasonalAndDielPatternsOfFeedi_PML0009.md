# Seasonal and Diel Patterns of Feeding in Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the feeding ecology of the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), an endemic, benthic cyprinid of the [[Gila River Drainage]] in Arizona, New Mexico and Sonora. Stomach contents from 684 fish collected monthly from June 1966 to May 1967 at [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, were analysed by seasonal, size-class (ontogenetic) and sex categories. A supplementary 24-hour diel series (July–August 1987) tested feeding rhythm. Mayfly nymphs dominated the diet, and feeding was concentrated in daylight hours, with little food in stomachs at night. The authors conclude that habitat selection in this species is tightly linked to its feeding behaviour and its relatively restricted food preferences.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Tiaroga cobitis* Girard, the loach minnow, is a darter-like benthic minnow endemic to the Gila River drainage, also found in Sonora, Mexico.
- **Habitat preference:** It typically occupies gravel-cobble bottoms of shallow, turbulent [[Riffle Habitat]] where about 50% of the bottom is covered by macrophytes, in water less than 20 cm deep. It is generally absent from unvegetated riffles and rare in riffles choked with plants. Seine hauls containing loach minnow generally contained no other fish species.
- **Conservation status:** The species is becoming increasingly rare across most of its range, with substantial population reductions. Causes include stream modification by humans and interactions with introduced species, especially the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*).
- **Rationale:** Following [[Minckley and Deacon 1968]], the authors argue that basic ecological data on depleted species can help predict the impact of future habitat changes and invasive species.
- **Objectives:** To document the foods consumed and the seasonal, ontogenetic and diel patterns of feeding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site: Aravaipa Creek, Arizona**
- One of the few relatively unchanged aquatic habitats where a natural assemblage of southwestern fishes persists.
- Drains about 1,400 km² of grassland and desert.
- Average discharge is less than 0.57 m³/s for most of the time, but variation is very high because of drought and floods.
- Currents are relatively swift (mean gradient 8.3 m/km), ranging from turbulent over rocky areas to nearly laminar over smooth, sandy bottoms.
- Macrophytes are sparse, mainly watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*) and a filamentous alga (*Cladophora glomerata*).
- A riparian gallery forest of broad-leafed trees (*Populus fremontii*, *Platanus wrightii*, *Salix* spp.) lines the stream, and high canyon walls provide shade.

**Benthic invertebrate community**
- Mayflies dominate the riffle invertebrate fauna, followed by dipterans and then trichopterans ([[Bruns and Minckley 1980]]).
- These groups build high populations during periods of modal discharge but are reduced by summer [[Flash Floods]]. Reductions in benthic invertebrates after such floods regularly exceed 85%.
- Winter flooding also disrupts invertebrate communities, but is less violent and longer-lasting, so its effects are more widespread but less dramatic ([[Barber and Minckley 1983]]).

## Key Findings

### Methods

- **Field collection (1966–1967):** Specimens were seined monthly with nets of 1.2–3.5 m length and 3.2 mm mesh, then preserved in 10% formalin. Data were provided from the Arizona State University fish collection. Length changes after preservation were not corrected.
- **Diel series (July–August 1987):** Electrofishing (110 V, 1200 W, alternating current) collected at least 10 individuals every 2 or 4 hours over 24-hour periods. Additional diel collections were made in the Blue River (Arizona) and upper Gila River (New Mexico) in June 1987.
- **Stomach analysis:** Stomachs were excised, contents sorted to taxonomic category under a binocular microscope, and volume estimated using a modified [[Hynes 1950 Point System]] (Total Points, TP). Results were also analysed by [[Frequency of Occurrence]].

### Primary Foods

- Of 751 stomachs, 91% contained food (684 fish used: 264 males, 386 females, 34 undetermined).
- **Mayfly (Ephemeroptera) nymphs:** 56.4% TP.
- **Dipteran larvae:** 20.7% TP (mainly [[Chironomidae]] at 11.3%, and [[Simuliidae]]).
- **Trichopteran larvae:** 8.8% TP.
- **Unidentified animal material:** 5.4% TP.
- **Unidentified plant material:** about 1.1% TP.
- **Terrestrial invertebrates:** less than 0.1% TP.
- Most important single taxon: [[Baetis]] spp., at 30.3% TP, and the main constant component of the diet.
- Females ate chironomids (12.7%) and *Baetis* (34%). Males ate *Baetis* (28.7%) and preferred simuliids (11.4%) as their second-ranked food.
- Females also consumed adult ephemeropterans (10.9% by frequency), which were not recorded from males.
- Frequency-of-occurrence results broadly matched the TP pattern. Chironomids were always the most important food by frequency, followed by *Baetis*.
- Minor taxa (each below 0.1% TP) included *Heptagenia* nymphs, several dipteran families, *Cheumatopsyche*, *Ochrotrichia*, *Helicopsyche*, hemipterans, *Hydroporus*-type beetle larvae, lepidopteran larvae, and cladocerans.

### Seasonal Trends

- **June 1966:** Mayfly nymphs made up about 70% TP. *Baetis* was dominant at about 50%. Dipteran larvae and trichopterans were also present.
- **June–September:** The importance of these main groups declined sharply, and this period had the greatest diversity of food items. *Neocloeon* sp. was the dominant mayfly for both sexes. Large proportions of unidentified organic material were common. Females showed the highest food diversity from July to September. Unidentified plant material and earthworms together made up about 2% TP.
- **October–May:** Mayfly importance rose steadily, while other groups declined. The exception was chironomids (about 23%), which replaced simuliids in these months.
- **September 1966–January 1967:** Chironomids made up about 18% of stomach contents and were relatively more important for males. *Choroterpes*, *Neocloeon* and *Tricorythodes* together contributed about 30% TP. Females fed less on *Tricorythodes* (7.1%) than males (10.4%).
- **April–May 1967:** Another high-diversity period. Mayfly nymphs dominated again: *Baetis* (31%), *Tricorythodes* (11%), *Neocloeon* (7%), and *Choroterpes* (8%).

### Ontogenetic Changes

Fish were grouped into six standard-length classes: 5–15, 16–25, 26–35, 36–45, 46–55 and 56–65 mm.

- **General pattern:** Mayfly nymphs became more important as fish grew, while dipteran larvae declined. This was equal for both sexes, except that females consistently ate chironomids and simuliids.
- **5–15 mm:** Dipterans made up 77% TP, mostly chironomids (54%) and simuliids (23%).
- **16–25 mm:** Chironomids (30% TP) and *Neocloeon* (31% TP) were the main foods. *Baetis*, *Choroterpes* and glossosomatid caddisflies were also present. Females fed mainly on *Neocloeon* and *Baetis* (26% and 30% TP respectively).
- **26–35 mm and 36–45 mm:** The three dominant groups were mayfly nymphs (*Baetis*, *Choroterpes*, *Tricorythodes*) and dipteran larvae (chironomids and simuliids).
- **46–55 mm:** Mainly mayfly nymphs (*Baetis* 35%, *Choroterpes* 10%, *Tricorythodes* 7%). Simuliids, chironomids and *Hydropsyche* sp. were of nearly equal importance (about 8% TP each).
- **Above 56 mm:** Almost entirely trichopteran larvae, *Hydropsyche* sp. (83%), plus simuliids (17%). No females of this size class were found in the study.
- An abrupt decline in mayflies and dipterans was noted in the largest individuals, possibly because of the small sample size.

### Diel Periodicity

- Feeding peaked in the early morning and around midday (or, per the abstract, early morning and late afternoon).
- Stomachs at 1600 h held about 17% total fullness, and those at 1800 h about 12%.
- Little or no food was found in fish collected at night, and feeding activity fell sharply until midnight.
- The species feeds at moderate-to-high levels during the day, with reduced activity at night, and does not show strong visual orientation.

### Comparison with Previous Work

- [[Schreiber and Minckley 1981]] reported only 11 food items. This study found 40 different items, a difference the authors attribute mainly to sample size (47 vs. 684 individuals), the wider seasonal and size coverage, and possibly other factors.
- Earlier authors reported *Leptophlebiidae* nymphs, *Capniidae* nymphs and *Empididae* larvae, which were not found in this study.
- The high intake of mayfly nymphs and dipterans reflects the fish's occupation of riffles. However, the substantial proportion of *Tricorythodes*, a non-riffle invertebrate, suggests the fish is not exclusively restricted to riffles.
- The fish did not eat drifting invertebrates, unlike the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), which does ([[Barber and Minckley 1983]]).
- Food habits closely track the relative abundance and seasonal patterns of benthic invertebrates in Aravaipa Creek ([[Schreiber and Minckley 1981]]).

### Conclusions

- Mayfly nymphs, especially *Baetis* spp., are the core of the diet. Diet shifts with season, fish size and sex.
- Young fish (under 15 mm) feed on small dipterans in and on soft sediments at stream margins, and only later shift to mayflies.
- Feeding is diurnal, with little nocturnal activity.
- Habitat selection is strongly linked to feeding behaviour and relatively restricted food preferences, meaning the species may be vulnerable to riffle habitat change and to flood-driven reductions in its main prey.
