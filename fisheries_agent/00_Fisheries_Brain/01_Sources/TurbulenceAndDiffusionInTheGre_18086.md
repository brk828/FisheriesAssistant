# Turbulence and Diffusion in the Great Lakes

#limnology #aquatic-ecology #turbulent-diffusion

## Executive Summary

This 1964 Great Lakes Institute paper by [[G. T. Csanady]] reports continued field experiments on turbulent dispersion of fluorescent dye in [[Lake Huron]] and the [[Western Basin]] of [[Lake Erie]]. It has three aims: confirm earlier low vertical diffusivities, compare diffusion between the two lakes, and test a "steadily changing" (swinging) current model. The main results are that thermal stratification strongly suppresses vertical mixing, that Lake Erie diffusivities were roughly twice those of Lake Huron (attributed to a "bottom effect" in shallow water), and that swinging currents increase effective diffusion by a factor of about 2 to 5.

## Background & Description

The work continues a 1961 study at [[Douglas Point]], Lake Huron (earlier paper cited as Csanady 1963). The 1963 season added a site at [[Colchester]] on the Western Basin of Lake Erie. The paper frames vertical mixing through the classical hypothesis of [[Irving Langmuir]] (1938): helical wind-driven vortices, aligned with the wind, are the main agents of vertical mixing. These vortices show up as surface [[Windrows]] spaced about 2 to 5 m apart.

**Methods.** Fluorescent dye was released at a constant rate from a fixed continuous source. The plume was sampled with a [[Fluorometer]] in a 26-ft hydrographic vessel, the LOON, with a bow boom lowered to a maximum of 4.75 m. Known limitations included:
- Air bubbles from the gear pump raised background fluorescence, though this stayed steady.
- Pitching of the boat often prevented surface sampling; the closest approach to the surface was 25 cm, on one calm day.

Lateral plume standard deviation was taken as one quarter of the plume width between points where concentration fell to 10% of peak. Plume width was measured with the [[Fluorometer]] trace and converted to distance using boat speed.

**Experimental scope.** Table 1a lists 1963 Lake Erie test runs (drogue velocities at 2, 4, 6 and 10 m depths). Table 1b covers Lake Huron runs 9 to 17, with wind, temperature and cloud data. Table 2 summarizes 19 diffusivity estimates (10 Lake Erie, 9 Lake Huron). The paper also gives internal-report references for further data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vertical mixing and stratification.** Vertical mixing depended strongly on water column structure and wind:
- Where windrows were present, vertical mixing was good down to a deep "diffusion floor."
- Without windrows, a temperature gradient could form. During a calm, warm June spell in Lake Huron, a near-surface thermocline blocked dye from reaching 1 m.
- A "diffusion floor" was often as shallow as 2 m, and at one site a temperature drop from 22 to 21 °C at 2.5 m formed an impenetrable barrier, with the change occurring over no more than about 25 cm.

**Shallow versus deep water.** The difference between lakes was attributed to depth. Lake Erie experiments were in 8 to 9 m of water across a basin of similar depth. Lake Huron experiments were in 12 to 14 m at the edge of a much deeper basin. Higher turbulence in shallow water was suggested as the cause.

**Swinging currents.** Persistent directional change of 45 to 90° within a few hours was common. The paper models this as a current of constant angular velocity with depth-dependent speed and phase lag. Plumes take the form of circular arcs, and the paper estimates a plume radius of about 720 m for a typical 10 cm/s current and 0.5 rad/hr swing rate.

## Key Findings

- **Vertical eddy diffusivity (typical, 0.1 to 2 m depth):** about 7 cm²/s in Lake Erie and 3 cm²/s in Lake Huron.
- **Deeper layers:** diffusivity fell to perhaps one-third of these values at depths greater than 5 m.
- **Horizontal diffusivity:** the Douglas Point and Colchester values are given as "typical" but are inconsistent in the text. The Introduction and Table 2 discussion give 1000 cm²/s for Lake Huron and 500 cm²/s for Lake Erie, while the Abstract and Discussion say Lake Erie was about twice as high as Lake Huron. The source should be checked before these values are cited.
- **Swinging-current enhancement:** effective diffusivity was 2 to 5 times larger than in a steady current (Abstract). The Discussion describes a factor of 2 or 3 at moderate distances and a stronger effect farther out.
- **Experimental variability:** runs of 20 and 23 August 1963 in Lake Erie, with nearly identical conditions, gave a diffusivity ratio of about 1:4, which the author called unexplained.
- **Plume widening:** a 30 to 45° current swing was expected to widen the plume by about 100 m beyond horizontal diffusion alone.
- **Maximum concentration:** in a steady uniform current the maximum should fall as x⁻¹ once the asymptotic stage is reached. Diffusion-floor cases should fall as x^(-1/2). A Lake Erie axial profile in a swinging current (about 90° arc at 2000 m) fell faster than the steady-current model predicts.
- **Numerical modelling:** a full 3D finite-difference solution of the swinging-current diffusion equation was judged too computationally demanding and was not pursued.
- **Mechanism:** the paper provides subjective support for [[Langmuir Circulation]]: good vertical diffusion coincided with visible windrows. Dye often formed regularly spaced patches at depth, suggesting organized confluences whose origin was not identified.
- **Model constants (fitted from crude observations):** U₀ = 10 cm/s, b = 1/100 s⁻¹, h = 10 s/cm. The value for the swing rate "a" is garbled in the OCR text, but it is consistent with the 0.5 rad/hr figure cited elsewhere.
