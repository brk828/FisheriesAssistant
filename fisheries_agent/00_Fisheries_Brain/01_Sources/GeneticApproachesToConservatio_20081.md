# Genetic Approaches to Conservation of Rare Fishes: Examples from North American Desert Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae #conservation-genetics

## Executive Summary

This review by G. K. Meffe (1990, *Journal of Fish Biology* 37 Supplement A, 105–112) argues that the long-term persistence of rare fishes depends on maintaining genetic variation within populations, which provides the raw material for natural selection. The paper outlines six applications of genetic data in [[Fish Conservation Recovery]] programmes and illustrates them with examples from [[Desert Fishes]] of the American Southwest, a highly endemic and rapidly declining fauna. Genetic information is presented as a practical management tool, not only a conservation goal.

## Background & Description

The paper opens by criticising **typological thinking** that treats species as discrete units, arguing that conservation must address variation within species. Meffe draws on the [[Fundamental Theorem of Natural Selection]] (Fisher, 1930) to argue that short-term survival depends on demography, while persistence over evolutionary time depends on adaptive genetic variation.

Fishes of the region occupy limited aquatic systems fed by groundwater or occasional heavy rain. Geological uplift, faulting, volcanism and aridity produced high [[Endemism]] among fishes and aquatic invertebrates. The fauna has declined through habitat destruction and exotic fishes; at least 20 taxa have gone extinct in the region this century (Meffe, 1990). Recovery efforts include basic research, [[Captive Propagation]], refugia construction and habitat rehabilitation, with genetic approaches added more recently.

Two genetic parameters are central:
- **H_w**: mean heterozygosity within populations.
- **D_ac**: genetic divergence among colonies across the species range.
- **H_T** (total diversity) = H_w + D_ac.

Within-population loss is driven by genetic drift, bottlenecks and inbreeding, with inbreeding considered the more damaging because it expresses deleterious recessive alleles. Divergence arises from drift, founder effects and selection, and is reduced by [[Gene Flow]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Meffe describes two [[Zoogeographic Models]] of population structure (Meffe & Vrijenhoek, 1988):

- **Death Valley model:** complete natural isolation, no historical gene flow. Total diversity lies mainly in divergence among colonies, and colonies are small with low heterozygosity. Supported by limited data on Death Valley pupfishes (*Cyprinodon*; Turner, 1974). Artificial gene flow should be avoided; managers should maintain large populations at each site.
- **Stream hierarchy model:** populations in dendritic river systems with regular or periodic gene flow. Divergence is lower and local heterozygosity higher, with a hierarchical structure. Management must reconstruct historical gene flow, sometimes through fish transfers. Considered applicable to many native cyprinids and catostomids of the Southwest.

Habitat fragmentation by humans disrupts natural gene flow, and several previously connected populations are now isolated and expected to diverge through drift.

## Key Findings

**Six applications of genetic data in recovery:**

1. **Describing quantity and distribution of variation.** Allozyme electrophoresis gives this baseline. Echelle (1990) summarised genetic diversity for 27 taxa of threatened western North American fishes. Protection should preserve both within-population diversity and among-population uniqueness.

2. **Estimating historical isolation and gene flow.** In the Sonoran topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) found divergence among colonies within a river at 2.5–5% of total genetic variance, among rivers within drainages at 0–4%, and among three major subunits at 52–58%(as reported, OCR-garbled range "52:8%" read as 52–58%). Within-colony diversity accounted for 21–34% of variation (OCR range "21-3%"). Transfers among the three major genetic groups should be avoided.

3. **Identifying unique gene pools.** Echelle et al. (1989) studied 16 Pecos gambusia (*Gambusia nobilis*) populations from four regions. Toyah Creek populations showed the highest heterozygosity and allelic diversity and were the most divergent, marking them as a priority for protection. Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) also identified an unrecognised genetic grouping in the upper Rio Mayo, Mexico.

4. **Taxonomic clarification.** 52 of 182 North American desert fishes of special concern (29%) listed by Williams et al. (1985) are undescribed. Electrophoretic and breeding data showed that three putative *Cichlasoma* species in Cuatro Cienegas were better treated as one polymorphic species (*C. minckleyi*; Kornfield & Taylor, 1983). Turner & Grosse (1980) similarly reinterpreted *Ilyodon* goodeid morphs as polymorphisms of one species. Meffe also distinguishes environmentally induced plasticity from true local adaptation, which requires allozyme and quantitative genetic studies.

5. **Choosing stocks for release.** The Dexter National Fish Hatchery (New Mexico) is the only US facility devoted entirely to endangered fishes, rearing 10–20 species at a time; at least six have been restocked. Sonoran topminnow stock from Monkey Spring, Arizona, which showed no detectable genetic variation, was replaced by fish from Sharp Spring (3–7% heterozygosity) from 1986. Quattro & Vrijenhoek (1989) showed that Sharp Spring fish had higher growth, survivorship, fecundity and developmental stability than Monkey Spring (H = 0.0%) or intermediate (H = 1.5%) fish, supporting the [[Heterozygosity Consensus]].

6. **Monitoring hatchery populations.** Founder bottlenecks, drift and artificial selection can erode rare alleles and alter gene frequencies. Edds & Echelle (1989) found similar heterozygosity in captive and wild Leon Springs pupfish, Comanche Springs pupfish and Pecos gambusia (captive for 6–8 years), but rare alleles were lost in captivity. Minckley et al. (1989) found comparable heterozygosity in bonytail chub hatchery stock; second-generation stocks appeared genetically sound. Ammerman & Morizot (1989) found Colorado squawfish hatchery stocks representative of wild populations. Salmonid studies, by contrast, documented significant diversity loss in hatcheries.

**Conclusions:**
- Conservation must take an evolutionary perspective that preserves capacity for adaptive change.
- Genetic manipulation is not feasible for populations in extremely dire condition, which should be rescued by any means available.
- Populations in better condition should be managed with a long time-frame, since today's decisions will have genetic effects for millennia.
