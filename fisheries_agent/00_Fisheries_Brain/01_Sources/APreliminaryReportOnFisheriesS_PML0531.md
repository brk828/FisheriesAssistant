# A Preliminary Report on Fisheries Survey of Lake Mead and the Colorado River Below Boulder Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary report, prepared by R. S. Davis and P. R. Needham of the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], summarizes a brief reconnaissance of [[Lake Mead]] and the [[Colorado River]] below [[Boulder Dam]] from August 25 to 27. The authors state that all conclusions are tentative and subject to revision after further study.

The main conclusions are:

- Lake Mead is structurally low in food. Water-level fluctuations of roughly fifty feet a year are expected, which prevent rooted [[Aquatic Plants]] from establishing. Temporary food gains from flooding are expected to decline.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] are abundant, and the lake appears heavily overpopulated with bass. Many of the bass examined had nearly empty stomachs.
- [[Bluegill Sunfish]] are the principal forage fish for bass.
- Below the dam, [[Rainbow Trout]] are in excellent condition. Their diet relied heavily on [[Cladophora]] algae, which is unusual for the species.
- The authors do not recommend introducing forage fish or aquatic plants into the lake at this time. They favor introducing [[Gammarus]] shrimp and [[Crayfish]] below the dam and recommend heavier trout stocking. A fall survey was planned to develop a [[Stocking Policy]].

## Background & Description

The survey was a brief, superficial inspection by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], U.S. Department of the Interior. The authors were R. S. Davis, in charge of Aquicultural Investigations, and P. R. Needham, Aquatic Biologist. The report thanks G. E. Wardwell, Manager of the Lake Mead Refuge, and Guy Edwards, Superintendent of the Lake Mead Recreational Area, for information, boats, and other assistance.

The survey took place during an unusually high-water year. Water in Lake Mead reached a higher level than at any previous time. A large flow over the spillway was continuing until the lake surface dropped about sixteen feet from its maximum. The year of the survey is partly illegible in the scanned copy and is given here only as "August 25 to 27."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Mead.** Annual water-level fluctuations of about fifty feet are expected, and larger changes may follow as additional generators are installed. These fluctuations prevent rooted aquatic plants from becoming established. They also keep the richer shoal areas from contributing much to fish food. The authors judged that introducing aquatic plants would be futile and that the lake will always be relatively deficient in food. Flooding of new land this year increased fish food production, but the authors expected this gain to be temporary and food organisms to stabilize at a lower level.

**Colorado River below the dam.** Water discharged from the surface of the reservoir raised river temperatures to close to 70°F, compared with a normal 54°F when water came only from deep levels. Murky water prevented observation of both fish and bottom conditions.

## Key Findings

### 1. Lake Mead Fish Populations and Food

- High water in 1941-era conditions produced large numbers of naturally spawned largemouth bass. Thousands of fingerlings 3 to 4 inches long were seen around the shore in all parts of the lake, and a few yearlings were observed. The yearlings had grown well but were beginning to show signs of partial starvation.
- Stomach contents of **11 bass** and **6 sunfish** indicated a general scarcity of food.
  - One bass had eaten two spiny-rayed fish, evidently sunfish. The other bass stomachs were nearly empty.
  - One bass, 5-5/8 inches long, had eaten about 50 small water fleas. Another had an adult damselfly, and another had eaten four leaves.
  - Three sunfish had stomachs nearly filled with midges. The other sunfish had fed on damselflies, snails, and terrestrial insects.
- The authors concluded that the lake is heavily overpopulated with bass.
- Bluegill sunfish are the principal forage fish for bass, and young bass are also eaten in considerable numbers.
- Bass, bluegill, sunfish, and carp were the most abundant species. Carp, catfish, suckers, and minnows were also present, but only superficial information was collected on them because of the limited seine sampling.

### 2. Introduction of Forage Animals in Lake Mead

- The authors were not prepared to recommend introducing forage fish until a more complete study was made. They noted that enough forage may already be present to support the bass population.
- Native suckers and minnows occur in Lake Mead. Their abundance and extent of use as bass food were unknown, but the authors considered them likely to be available as bass food.
- An abundant organism in the lake (name garbled in the scan) is being extensively preyed upon.
- Introducing crayfish had been proposed, but the authors doubted it would benefit bass. Largemouth bass were not known to feed on crayfish, and crayfish had become a nuisance in hatchery ponds used for rearing bass.

### 3. Rainbow Trout Below Boulder Dam

- Conditions in the river were judged very favorable for rainbow trout. Planted trout had grown rapidly and were plump, deep-bodied, and highly colored.
- Stomachs of **9 trout**, 11 to 16 inches long, showed:
  - Six fish fed near the bottom, with stomachs crammed with filamentous Cladophora algae.
  - Two fish fed near the surface, with stomachs full of large grasshoppers, crickets, and other terrestrial and aquatic insects.
  - One fish was nearly empty.
  - Midge larvae were common in all stomachs. Other aquatic insects included stoneflies, mayflies, and additional aquatic forms (name garbled in the scan).
- The authors described trout feeding on algae to this extent as very exceptional. Algae normally make up 15 to 20 percent of rainbow trout food, with the rest mainly insects and similar organisms.
- The authors suspected an unusually heavy algal growth on the river bottom but could not confirm it because the water was cloudy.
- Trout were not judged to suffer from a food deficiency, since "no finer fish can be found anywhere."

### 4. Forage Animal Introductions Below the Dam

- On the recommendation of Fred J. Foster, shrimp (*Gammarus*) had recently been planted in the river. It was too early to judge success.
- The authors believed that introducing both shrimp and crayfish below the dam would be beneficial by increasing the animal food available to trout.
- Introducing insects was not advocated. Insects establish themselves wherever conditions are favorable, and no successful introduction of insects into waters where they were absent has been recorded, despite many attempts.

### 5. Stocking and Management Recommendations

- The information indicated that heavier trout stocking below the dam is desirable.
- Two principal objectives for the fall investigations were to develop a stocking policy for the river below the dam and to determine whether further plantings of forage animals are needed.

### 6. Planned Fall Survey

The planned survey of Lake Mead and the river below the dam was to give special attention to six problems:

1. Fish species present and their relative abundance.
2. Food of trout and bass.
3. Available foods in Lake Mead and the river below the dam.
4. The forage fish problem in Lake Mead.
5. Physical and chemical conditions in Lake Mead and the river.
6. Development of a stocking policy for the river below the dam.
