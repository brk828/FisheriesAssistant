# Ranid Frog Conservation and Management in Arizona

#herpetology #amphibians #ranidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report (Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program Technical Report 121, June 1997, edited by Michael J. Sredl) compiles three linked studies on Arizona's native ranid frogs, chiefly [[Lowland Leopard Frog]] (*Rana yavapaiensis*) and [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] (*Rana chiricahuensis*). The studies cover (1) six-year [[Mark-Recapture]] population studies, (2) validation of [[Visual Encounter Survey]] (VES) counts at stock tanks, and (3) a statewide status and distribution survey of all native Arizona ranids. The central conclusions are that leopard frog populations are highly dynamic and prone to local extinction, that nearly every native ranid has declined, and that small, isolated populations need active, coordinated management, with [[Stock Tanks]] now acting as critical refugia.

## Background & Description

The report addresses the taxonomically complex *Rana pipiens* group in the arid Southwest. Three new Arizona leopard frog species were described after 1979, and the Chiricahua leopard frog was expected to be split. Because of documented regional declines, baseline demographic data were treated as a high priority.

Native Arizona ranids covered in the report:

- **[[Plains Leopard Frog]]** (*R. blairi*): restricted in Arizona to the Sulphur Springs Valley, Cochise County, with one disjunct record from Ashurst Lake.
- **[[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]]** (*R. chiricahuensis*): split into a Rim form (White Mountains and Mogollon Rim) and a southeastern form.
- **[[Northern Leopard Frog]]** (*R. pipiens*): mainly on the Colorado Plateau.
- **[[Ramsey Canyon Leopard Frog]]** (*R. subaquavocalis*): the most recently described Arizona leopard frog, known only from the Huachuca Mountains.
- **[[Lowland Leopard Frog]]** (*R. yavapaiensis*): the most widespread native species in Arizona. Its Arizona habitats range from 146 to 1817 m elevation.
- **[[Tarahumara Frog]]** (*R. tarahumarae*): extirpated from Arizona.
- **[[Relict Leopard Frog]]** (*R. onca*): no confirmed Arizona records.

Amphibians are described as useful indicator species because of their biphasic life cycle, permeable skin and sensitivity to pollutants and UV radiation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mark-recapture study sites (1991–1996).** Seven sites were studied. Six were *R. yavapaiensis* sites and one was an *R. chiricahuensis* site:

- [[Alamo Canyon]]: tinajas (bedrock pools) in the Santa Catalina Mountains; sampled 1991–1992.
- [[Big Spring]]: a small spring-fed wash in Graham County, with a single maintained pool; the most intensively studied site.
- [[Barnhardt Mesa]]: three earthen cattle tanks in Gila County.
- [[Reed Spring]]: a perennial spring in Gila County; sampled 1991–1992.
- [[Thicket Spring]]: a pond in Yavapai County; sampled 1991, with few or no frogs in 1992.
- [[Tule Creek]]: the type locality of *R. yavapaiensis*, in Yavapai County; a perennial stretch of an intermittent tributary of the Agua Fria River, dropped after 1993 when fencing made sampling impossible.
- [[Horsefall Canyon]]: two cattle tanks in the Chiricahua Mountains; the *R. chiricahuensis* site, studied from May 1994.

**Climate and habitat.** Rainfall is biseasonal, with peaks in late summer and winter. Many sites are ephemeral or depend on surface runoff. Vegetation ranges from Sonoran desertscrub and semi-desert grassland to Madrean Evergreen Woodland.

**Statewide survey database.** The database holds 1623 locality records, dating from 1884 to the present. Of these, 595 have native leopard frog observations. Over 50% of native leopard frog localities are in Cochise, Gila, Santa Cruz and Yavapai counties, and over 75% are in the greater Gila River watershed. Sixty-six percent of native leopard frog localities are on state or federal land.

## Key Findings

### 1. Population dynamics (mark-recapture)

- Capture probabilities were low: 0.13–0.58 at Big Spring and 0.04–0.58 at Tule Creek. Recapture rates were insufficient at most sites, so only Big Spring and Tule Creek yielded Jolly-Seber estimates. Chapman-adjusted [[Lincoln-Peterson Estimator]] estimates were used for all sites, for individuals >55.0 mm SVL.
- Big Spring's Jolly-Seber population peaked at 632 animals in August 1992. It then fell to 80 between October 1992 and April 1993, after the December 1992 die-off. Numbers decreased overall for the rest of the study, although late-season peaks occurred in 1993, 1994 and 1995.
- Tule Creek's population ranged from 3283 frogs in October 1991 to 14 frogs in April 1993.
- Adult survivorship ranged from 0.06 to >1.00 and juvenile survivorship from 0.03 to >1.00. Survivorship was lowest in winter in most years, and recruitment was lowest in October. Recruitment at Big Spring fell to near zero in the last two years.
- Nearly half of the study populations went extinct during the study.

### 2. Growth, size and sex ratio

- Von Bertalanffy growth curves for Big Spring gave mean asymptotic SVL of 63.1 mm for males and 76.4 mm for females. Characteristic growth rates were 0.21 for males and 0.16 for females. Mean maximum length was reached at 22–24 months in both sexes.
- Median SVL was highest in April, lowest in June, and rose through October, reflecting a pulse of metamorphs each summer.
- Sex ratios did not differ from 1:1 at five of eight site-years tested. Female bias appeared at Alamo Canyon and Barnhardt Mesa in one year each, and at Big Spring in four of six years. The cause was not identified; incorrect sexing and differential capture probability were proposed.

### 3. Mortality events and ecological drivers

- **Red-leg disease** (*Aeromonas hydrophila*) followed the December 1992 cold snap and killed a large part of the Big Spring population. Big Spring had not recovered to its pre-epidemic size, which was attributed mainly to siltation and loss of pool habitat.
- **Desiccation:** Barnhardt Mesa's Mesa Tank dried completely in summer 1994. Frogs dispersed upstream and downstream, and a red-leg epidemic reduced the population to a few individuals in April 1995.
- **Hydrogen sulfide toxicity:** a die-off at Horsefall Canyon in June 1994 reduced 60–80 adults to fewer than 10. Detritus load, low water level, high temperature and low dissolved oxygen were implicated.
- **Floods:** high flows in summer 1992 reduced Big Spring pool area from about 200 m² to 35 m², yet adult and juvenile survivorship did not appear affected. A scouring flood at Tule Creek in January 1993 increased open water.
- The authors concluded that extinction risk is high because mortality can be density-independent (floods, toxicity) or density-dependent (disease), and both can destabilize [[Metapopulation Dynamics]].

### 4. Visual encounter survey (VES) validation

- Ten aquatic sites were surveyed in the Tonto Basin (nine stock tanks plus a 400 m reach of Rye Creek) from April 1995 to March 1997.
- In April–October 1995, frogs were detected in 93 of 100 daytime VES at five sites. Zero counts were uncommon during the activity season, supporting the reliability of negative results. Zero counts became common in winter (83 of 110 surveys, 75.5%, from November 1995 to March 1996).
- Counts were inconsistent between two observers. Of 73 paired counts, observer #1 recorded the higher count 34 times, observer #2 recorded it 16 times, and 23 counts were equal. A Wilcoxon signed-ranks test showed the difference was significant (Z = −2.528). Training and experience were proposed as important factors.
- Counts at most sites fell to zero by late 1995, and no frogs were found in spring 1997. The authors suspected local extinction caused by drying during the drought (1995 rainfall 415 mm versus a 30-year average of 478 mm; 1996 rainfall 208 mm).
- Mass metamorphosis at Rye Creek (80 newly metamorphosed frogs) and Unnamed Tank (18 frogs) in August 1995 followed drying. The authors cautioned that high metamorph counts alone do not indicate a healthy population.

### 5. Statewide status and distribution

Survey effort between September 1990 and April 1997 totaled 2089 surveys at 1274 localities, yielding 3208 riparian herpetofauna observations, 777 of them native leopard frogs.

- **Lowland leopard frog:** observed at 43 of 115 historical localities surveyed. It is extirpated from the lower Gila and Colorado rivers and adjacent California, and has declined in southeastern Arizona. Its central Arizona status is considered good, and it is "certainly the most stable native ranid in Arizona." There are 104 extant localities.
- **Rim form Chiricahua leopard frog:** frogs confirmed at 4 of 25 historical localities surveyed. The species is absent from 84% of historical sites surveyed, and has declined sharply, especially in the White Mountains.
- **Southeastern form Chiricahua leopard frog:** found at 17 of 84 historical sites surveyed, with 61 extant sites. Most extant sites are on Coronado National Forest, which makes it perhaps the second-most manageable leopard frog after the lowland leopard frog.
- **Northern leopard frog:** confirmed at 12 of 42 historical localities surveyed. 28 extant sites remain, and the overall status is "rather poor." Most known populations are federally managed.
- **Plains leopard frog:** frogs found at 2 of 18 historical localities; 6 extant populations remain, four of them on private land. The authors state it "may be the most endangered leopard frog in Arizona."
- **Ramsey Canyon leopard frog:** nine known localities, all in Cochise County and all small. A multi-party Conservation Agreement has been completed.
- **Tarahumara frog:** absent from all six historical sites surveyed and considered extirpated since 1983.
- **Relict leopard frog:** no Arizona records; the identity of frogs at Littlefield remains uncertain.

### 6. Causes of decline and management

- **Non-native species** are a major factor, including [[Bullfrog]]s (*R. catesbeiana*), non-native fishes and crayfish. The report notes a strong negative association between native ranids and non-native fishes.
- Other factors include acid deposition, habitat destruction, heavy metals, UV radiation from ozone thinning, pesticides and possible global warming, with possible synergistic interactions.
- **Habitat fragmentation** is considered a key cause. Surviving populations occur in small, clustered, isolated sites that are probably remnants of former metapopulations.
- Proposed strategies include [[Conservation And Management Zones]] (CMZs), ranked by criteria such as status, geographic context, evolutionary distinctness, manageability and cost. Other actions include [[Captive Breeding]] and rearing (Phoenix Zoo and Grand Canyon University), translocations, non-native species removal (for example, bullfrog eradication at a Gentry Creek pond in Tonto National Forest), habitat renovation, and stewardship agreements with ranchers for stock tank management.

### 7. Information needs

- Taxonomic clarification, especially for the Littlefield population, *R. onca*, *R. subaquavocalis*, and the two Chiricahua forms.
- Genetic structure, heterozygosity and gene flow within and among metapopulations.
- Dispersal capability and the characteristics of necessary dispersal corridors.
