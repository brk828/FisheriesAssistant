# The Biology and Taxonomy of the Burbot, *Lota lota leptura*, in Interior Alaska

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gadidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 monograph (Biological Papers of the University of Alaska, No. 11) by Lo-Chai Chen examines the [[Burbot]] (*Lota lota*) of interior Alaska. It argues for recognizing the Alaskan form as the subspecies *Lota lota leptura* on the basis of a high [[Peduncle Ratio]], geographic variation, and high vertebral and pyloric caecal counts. The study also covers age and growth (using [[Otolith Analysis]]), the length–weight relationship, seasonal liver and gonad cycles, reproduction, and summer food habits. Samples came from the upper [[Yukon River]] and [[Tanana River]] drainages, collected 1961–1965.

## Background & Description

- **Identity and naming:** The burbot is the only freshwater member of the cod family Gadidae. It is nocturnal, bottom-dwelling, and circumpolar in cold northern fresh and brackish waters. In Alaska it is called lingcod and known to Native peoples as lush or mud shark.
- **Peripheral freshwater fish:** Following Darlington (1957), the burbot is treated as a peripheral freshwater fish, and its marine-type parasites support a past marine connection (Manter, 1955).
- **Economic value:** The burbot is highly valued as food in Europe and Russia, but is generally considered unpalatable in America. Its liver is rich in vitamins A and D, several times more potent than medicinal cod-liver oil (Nelson et al., 1932).
- **Taxonomic framework:** Hubbs and Schultz (1941) separated *L. l. leptura* (Alaska and northeastern Siberia) from *L. l. lota* (Europe and part of Siberia) and *L. l. lacustris* (eastern North America). Nikol'skii (1954) and Berg (1949) offered alternative arrangements, and Lindsey (1956) and Lawler (1963) questioned whether the subspecies are valid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** The upper Yukon River (Circle to Eagle), the Fortymile River, and the Tanana River (Fairbanks to Tetlin Junction). The Yukon and Tanana are silty, run-off rivers fed by rain and snowmelt. Many tributaries are clear, and a sharp silt line usually separates clear tributary water from silty mainstem water. Ice forms in October; under-ice temperatures sit near 4 °C, and summer water temperatures in the main rivers rarely exceed 15 °C.

**Fish fauna (upper Yukon, summarized from a checklist of 15 species):** Notable members include [[Arctic Grayling]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Longnose Sucker]], [[Lake Chub]], [[Slimy Sculpin]], [[Chinook Salmon]], and [[Least Cisco]]. Longnose suckers, lake chubs, and slimy sculpins were abundant. The round whitefish was rare in the upper Yukon but common in the Tanana. Troutperch were absent from the Tanana sample.

**Habitat preference:** Burbot move freely into clearwater tributaries but are much more abundant in the silty main river. They ascend tributaries mainly during high water, when those tributaries also turn silty. Burbot are paler in silty water than in clear water.

**Climate:** The author links slow growth in interior Alaska to the severe local climate.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomy**
- The Alaskan burbot is considered subspecifically distinct, based on the peduncle ratio, its geographic pattern, and high vertebral and pyloric caecal counts.
- Peduncle ratios are about 1.95 in interior Alaska, southwestern Alaska, and Siberia, and 1.3–1.4 from Labrador to Heming Lake, Manitoba. The joint non-overlap between the Alaskan and Heming Lake samples was 97%, well above the 90% threshold for subspecies (Mayr et al., 1953).
- Pyloric caecal number in Alaska ranged from 67 to 168 (mean 115.9). Vertebral number ranged from 62 to 67 (mean 65.2), against 53–63 (mean 59.9) in Heming Lake burbot. Joint non-overlap for vertebral counts was 98.5%.
- Peduncle ratio showed little correlation with sex, size, or vertebral number, which supports its use as a key character.

**Age and growth**
- The scales are unsuitable for ageing. Otoliths were the most reliable structure; 378 pairs were read (271 Yukon, 107 Tanana).
- Opaque margins form from early June in Tanana fish and are universal by the end of July. Hyaline margins form later and are generally absent in young-of-year fish before July.
- The largest female was 99.5 cm, 5,020 g, and 16 years old. The oldest female was 24 years old (97.2 cm), and the oldest male was 21 years old (80.3 cm). Males have a shorter lifespan and slower growth after about age 10.
- Mean calculated lengths were 11.0 cm at age I and 65.9 cm at age XII. Growth slows to about 3–5 cm/year after age 6.
- Compared with other areas, burbot in interior Alaska have the greatest recorded longevity and maximum size, yet grow more slowly.

**Length–weight relationship**
- The summer total-sample equation is log W = −2.18341 + 2.95527 log L. The winter prespawning sample gives log W = −1.81087 + 2.75944 log L.
- A 60 cm burbot weighs 1,179 g in summer and 1,247 g in winter, a 5.4% difference.

**Reproduction**
- Interior Alaskan burbot mature at age 6 or 7, at 40–50 cm total length.
- Spawning occurs in early February under ice. The Tanana River was the main site; Chena River spawning is inferred from young-of-year captures.
- A 1,230 g, 57.8 cm, 10-year-old female produced an estimated 738,485 eggs. A comparable Heming Lake burbot produced 578,203.
- Eggs are demersal, non-adhesive, and oil-globuled, with a mean diameter of 0.71 mm, which is small relative to other populations.
- Liver weight peaks at 7.58% of body weight in August and falls to 2.82% by February. Gonad weight rises to 14.82% by January and drops to 1.87% after spawning, so about 13% of body weight is lost in spawning.

**Growth in early life**
- Young-of-year burbot reached about 89 mm in a pond by October, and an additional 25–74 mm group was taken in the Yukon.

**Food habits (284 summer stomachs, May–November 1964)**
- Young burbot feed mainly on invertebrates, dominated by Plecoptera (chiefly *Isoperla*), with Ephemeroptera and Diptera also important. Fish become dominant by age 4 and invertebrates disappear from the diet by age 9.
- Sculpins, burbot, and lampreys dominate the fish portion of the diet. Suckers were abundant but rarely eaten. Grayling and chinook fry appeared only in hoopnet catches.
- Small burbot had fuller stomachs than large ones, indicating higher relative feeding intensity during rapid growth.
- Summer feeding was reduced in adults, which were rarely caught on trotlines. Winter diet was not sampled.

**Methodological notes**
- The point-allotment method (Thompson, 1959, modified) assigns points by stomach fullness and item share. The author suggests volumetric measurement of pooled contents as a future improvement.
- Stomach and digestive tract weight can reach 25% of body weight when gorged, so future condition analyses should exclude it.
