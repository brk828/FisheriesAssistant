# Response to Representative Griffin's Concern of Proposed Renovations

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae #Cyprinodontidae #Salmonidae #native-fish-management

## Executive Summary

This 1999 internal memo from the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Region V Fisheries Program addresses concerns raised by a state representative regarding proposed fish renovation (removal/eradication) projects in southeastern Arizona. The memo outlines the agency's rationale for using piscicides (**Rotenone** and **Antimycin A**) to remove non-native, invasive fish species in order to protect and restore populations of federally sensitive and endangered native fish, including the [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Gila Chub]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Spikedace]], and [[Loach Minnow]]. The document emphasizes collaborative partnerships with federal agencies (USFS, BLM, USFWS), private landholders (El Coronado Ranch), and conservation organizations (The Nature Conservancy), framing native fish restoration as a proactive strategy to prevent Endangered Species Act (ESA) listings and preserve recreational fishing opportunities.

## Background & Description

While this document is primarily a policy/management memo rather than a morphological study, it references several species by taxonomic identity relevant to differentiation in the field:

- **[[Gila Chub]]** (*Gila intermedia*): A candidate species under the ESA; found in refugium habitats such as O'Donnell Creek.
- **[[Yaqui Chub]]** (*Gila purpurea*): Federally endangered; isolated population in West Turkey Creek.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*): Small-bodied livebearer, vulnerable to predation and competitive exclusion by invasive centrarchids and cyprinids.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon maculatus macularius*): Small cyprinodontid adapted to harsh desert aquatic conditions.
- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*) and **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): Small benthic cyprinids native to Gila River Basin drainages, both highly sensitive to habitat alteration and invasive competitors.
- **[[Apache Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus apache*): A salmonid native to the White Mountains of Arizona, the target of active native fishery rehabilitation using Antimycin A.

No detailed morphometric, meristic, or coloration data are provided in the source text; species are referenced by scientific/common name association only.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat Types**: Native fish populations discussed occupy small, often isolated desert stream systems — West Turkey Creek, O'Donnell Creek, and the Rog Hole Wildlife Area — characteristic of relict [[Desert Fish Refugium]] habitats in the Gila and Yaqui River drainages of southeastern Arizona.

- **Invasive Species Threats**: The memo identifies four principal non-native fish species responsible for extirpating native populations through **predation** and **competition for limited resources**:
  - [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*)
  - [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
  - [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*)
  - [[Western Mosquitafish]] (*Gambusia affinis*)

- **Case Study — Sabino Canyon**: A documented local example shows [[Gila Chub]] populations, once abundant throughout Sabino Canyon, now restricted to areas above "Bridge 9" — precisely where invasive [[Green Sunfish]] have not yet colonized. This illustrates a direct spatial correlation between invasive sunfish presence and native chub decline.

- **Conservation Partnerships**: Native fish refugia are managed through Habitat Conservation Plans (HCPs) and multi-agency collaboration:
  - El Coronado Ranch (private) + USFWS: HCP for [[Yaqui Chub]] in West Turkey Creek.
  - Coronado National Forest (CNF) + AGFD: managing West Turkey Creek watershed as a native fishery.
  - The Nature Conservancy (TNC): managing O'Donnell Creek as a [[Gila Chub]] refugium.
  - AGFD: managing Rog Hole Wildlife Area as a native fish refugium (closed to fishing since 1993), slated for renovation and native fish reintroduction.

## Key Findings

- **No public fishing impact**: None of the water bodies proposed for fish removal operations support public fishing, due to private access restrictions, insufficient water, or undesirable existing fish populations — mitigating recreational angling concerns raised by Rep. Griffin.

- **Piscicide Use Protocol**: Two primary piscicides are used for non-native fish removal:
  - **[[Rotenone]]**: Naturally derived from legume roots; discovered via use by indigenous South American peoples for fish harvesting; breaks down within a few days.
  - **[[Antimycin A]]**: An antibiotic derived from *Streptomyces* cultures; breaks down within a few hours.
  - Both compounds are selectively toxic to **gill-breathing organisms only** and are not toxic to terrestrial vertebrates at treatment concentrations.
  - Both are EPA-registered under the Federal Environmental Pesticide Control Act for fisheries use.
  - Toxicity can be neutralized with a **1-ppm potassium permanganate** solution; sufficient detoxifying agent must be on-site to neutralize the full piscicide stock in case of spills.
  - Application is legally required to be supervised by a certified pesticide applicator.

- **Regulatory Compliance**: Piscicide-based fish removal is the "most intrusive" management action and is only pursued after exhausting alternatives, requiring extensive documentation and multi-agency compliance review under NEPA, CWA, and ESA.

- **Successful Precedent — Apache Trout**: Antimycin A use in the White Mountains to replace non-native fish with native [[Apache Trout]] has significantly advanced delisting efforts for this species, serving as a proof-of-concept for the agency's broader native fish rehabilitation strategy.

- **Strategic Rationale**: The AGFD frames proactive native species protection and non-native removal as a mechanism to prevent future ESA listings, thereby preserving long-term flexibility for managing aquatic recreational resources (i.e., avoiding the regulatory constraints that come with formal endangered species listing).
