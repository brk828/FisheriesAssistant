# Bonytail Reintroduction on the Green River, Utah (1988–1989)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report documents a two-year study of [[Hatchery-Reared Bonytail]] reintroduction into the [[Green River]] of Utah, conducted by the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] and the [[Utah Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit]] with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. Between March and October 1989 (and in 1988), 86 adult bonytails from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] were implanted with radio transmitters and released in the upper portion of Island Park or immediately downstream of Split Mountain Canyon in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Mortality was high (confirmed mortality 74.42%, suspected mortality 90–95%). Released fish moved predominantly downstream, showed diel shifts in habitat use, and ultimately concluded that hatchery-reared adults were poorly suited to direct release without extended conditioning.

## Background & Description

The bonytail (*Gila elegans*) is considered the rarest endemic fish of the upper Colorado River Basin and was listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1980. Its common name has varied historically, and field identification of sympatric *Gila* forms (roundtail chub, humpback chub) is complicated by unresolved taxonomy and apparent hybridization.

Bonytails were once common in large river habitats of the Colorado system but are now virtually extirpated from the lower basin, with only remnant populations in reservoirs such as [[Lake Mohave]] (34 specimens collected 1980s–1990s, with no recent reproduction recorded). The last evidence of natural reproduction in the upper basin came from the 1959–1961 year classes in the Green River. Brood stock for the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] originated from wild Lake Mohave adults collected 1979–1981.

Hatchery fish used in the study were 7- and 8-year-old adults. Females were larger than males; in 1988 released females averaged 405 mm and 824 g (males averaged 4.5 mm and 1 kg as reported). Fish did not feed readily in holding facilities and were reared on pelleted food, with spawning occurring in ponds from May.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area comprised roughly 85 river miles of the Green River divided into three channel types:
1. Extensive meander with braided channels (Island and Rainbow Parks, and the foothills stretch below Split Mountain Canyon), dominated by cobble and gravel.
2. Deeply incised canyon with a single channel (Split Mountain Canyon, about 9 river miles, steep gradient).
3. Broad floodplain with extensive braiding (Uintah Basin, below Split Mountain Canyon), depositional with shifting fine substrates.

Green River discharge was low in both study years. In 1988 the hydrograph peaked at 14,160 cfs in early May; in 1989 the peak average daily flow (5,600 cfs) occurred in early June. Radio tracking used habitat classes including main channel, side channel, eddy, pool, backwater, run, riffle, rapid and shoreline (see [[Habitat Classification]]).

Study objectives addressed (1) survival, (2) dispersal and general behavior, (3) movement patterns and diel activity, (4) physical habitat, and (5) identification of suitable future release sites.

## Key Findings

**Survival**
- Of 86 adults released (1988–1989), 39 fish were released in 1988 and 47 in 1989 (Appendix Tables 1.2–1.3). Confirmed mortality was 74.42%, with the suspected mortality rate 90–95%.
- Six fish outlived their transmitters (four in 1988, two in 1989).
- Survival was negatively correlated with river temperature at release (r² = 0.44). Survival was best when fish were released at river temperatures of 10–15 °C; survival was poor below 5 °C and above 15 °C.
- Fish receiving post-operative injections of oxytetracycline and vitamin C survived longer than untreated fish, although the authors noted this was difficult to evaluate in an uncontrolled setting.
- A March 1988 release that was transported by fixed-wing aircraft and placed in an ephemeral backwater (subsequently converted to a side channel) suffered near-complete mortality within two days; those data were excluded from further analysis.

**Movements and Dispersal**
- Movement was directed downstream (Bernoulli trials, p < 0.01 for both years). Only one fish (#4132) was tracked to an ultimate location upstream of its release site.
- Movement magnitude was weakly correlated with discharge (r² = 0.29).
- The longest documented movement was fish #4185 (released Island Park, May 30 1989), located 82.9 river miles downstream; fish #4134 traveled 75.3 river miles.
- Five of six surviving bonytails eventually settled into localized areas and became active within those areas on a diel basis.
- No radio contacts were made in Split Mountain Canyon, suggesting active avoidance or inability to hold position there.

**Diel Activity and Habitat Use**
- Activity peaked during crepuscular and early nocturnal periods; activity was lowest from about 1000 to 1300 h.
- Diurnal habitats were deeper (3.86–6.30 ft) with moderate velocities (0.99–1.12 ft/s); nocturnal habitats were shallower (2.92–5.48 ft) and low-velocity (0.08–0.95 ft/s).
- During cool-water conditions (October–November 1989), overall activity decreased and diel habitat segregation was weaker; fish selected deeper, moderate-flow habitats near the main current.
- Eddies and runs were the most frequently used habitats in 1988; backwater habitat at River Mile 333.1–2 was heavily used by fish released there in 1989.

**Conclusions and Recommendations**
- Hatchery-reared adults should not be released into the wild without a lengthy conditioning period (possibly one year), because cumulative stressors (starvation, transport, flow and temperature changes, anesthesia, surgery and transmitter implantation) produced high mortality.
- Future efforts should prioritize young-of-year fish, which are more economical, and should evaluate predator populations and fish stock size before large-scale release.
- Multiple release sites should be used to account for downstream dispersal.
- Suitable release sites are likely areas with stable eddy habitats, identified via aerial and bathymetric inventories in a multi-agency effort that would feed into a Bonytail Reintroduction Plan.
- Nocturnal electrofishing in summer, when bonytails became sedentary in shallow, low-flow habitats, may improve detection of remnant wild populations.
- Taxonomic questions about *Gila* species should be resolved before any large-scale reintroduction.
