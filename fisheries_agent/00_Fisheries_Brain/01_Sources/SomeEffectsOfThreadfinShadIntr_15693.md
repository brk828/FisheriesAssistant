## Executive Summary
This 1966 study by Beers and McConnell examines the effects of introducing [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) as a forage fish on the diet and body condition of [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) in Roosevelt Lake, Arizona. Threadfin shad were introduced in fall 1957. By 1964–1965, threadfin shad had become the dominant prey item in crappie diets (averaging 53% relative abundance across seasons), and crappie condition (K factor) improved substantially and progressively from 1959 to 1964 compared to historically "wafer-thin," stunted populations of the early 1950s. The study also documents a persistent size-selective predation pattern, with crappie preferentially consuming the smallest available shad, and raises concern that dense adult shad populations may be suppressing recruitment of young black crappie, potentially through competition or "psychological harassment" (sensu Ivlev 1961).

## Background & Description
- **Threadfin Shad standard length back-calculation**: Since digested shad could not always be measured directly, standard length (L) was estimated from vertebral column measurements using the regression: 
  **L = 0.479 + 0.034V**, where L = standard length and V = length of the five terminal vertebrae preceding the hypural plate (calibrated on 10 intact specimens).
- **Threadfin Shad size structure**: Highly variable, multi-cohort population consistent with continuous spawning from spring through early fall. Fish <8 cm total length constituted at least 40% of the population at various points in winter/spring 1964–1965 (Fig. 1), confirmed independently by 1961–1962 electrofishing data (Fig. 2), despite electrofishing's known bias toward larger individuals.
- **Black Crappie body size range sampled**: Total length range of 24–37 cm for diet analysis (n=47); standard length range for condition analysis spanned 160–298 mm across 1006 fish (1959–1965).
- **Condition factor (K)**: Calculated per [[Lagler 1956]] method. Roosevelt Lake crappie K values (Table 3) ranged from ~3.08 (poor condition, 1959, smallest size class) to as high as 4.79, with population averages rising from 3.90 (1959) to 4.24 (1964).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Study System**: Roosevelt Lake, Arizona — largest reservoir in the Salt River Project system; located at confluence of Tonto Creek and Salt River, Gila County, AZ. Elevation 1,900 ft; max surface area 17,335 acres; max depth 236 ft; max volume 1,398,430 acre-feet.
- **Thermal regime**: Max water temp ~88°F (July–August); min ~56°F (December–January); surface temps >65°F typically March–October.
- **Community composition**: All dominant fish species in Roosevelt Lake are introduced, including [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), Black Crappie, [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus cyprinellus*), [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*), [[Red Plains Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), and Threadfin Shad.
- **Trophic relationship**: Threadfin shad functions as the primary forage base for black crappie, dominating diet by both frequency of occurrence and volumetric contribution (estimated >100x greater volume than chironomids despite similar numeric ingestion rates).
- **Size-selective predation**: Length-frequency comparison (Fig. 3) between gill-netted shad populations and shad recovered from crappie stomachs shows crappie disproportionately consume the smallest size classes available, suggesting active size selection or a sampling bias difference between crappie foraging and gillnet capture.
- **Potential recruitment bottleneck**: The near-total absence of young (<20 cm) black crappie in creels and gill nets, despite improved adult condition, raises the hypothesis that intensive threadfin shad populations may suppress crappie recruitment via competitive exclusion from zooplankton resources or indirect behavioral interference (harassment), a pattern also noted with threadfin shad in [[Pena Blanca Lake]] (McConnell and Gerdes 1964).

## Key Findings
1. **Diet composition (Table 1)**: Threadfin shad was the dominant food item in black crappie stomachs across all sampled months (Nov 1964–May 1965), with relative abundance ranging 31–100% (average 53%) and frequency of occurrence up to 100%. Chironomidae was the secondary food source (average 41% relative abundance, 47% frequency), while Red Plains Shiner and crappie scales were minor/occasional components.
2. **Historical condition improvement**: Prior to shad introduction (early-mid 1950s), Roosevelt Lake crappie were reportedly scarce beyond 25 cm TL and described as "knife-bellied"/"wafer-thin" by regional fishery biologists (J. Hemphill, R. Gruenewald, M. Conrad, pers. comm.).
3. **Post-introduction condition trend (Table 3)**: Average K factor rose from 3.90 (1959) → 3.98 (1961) → 4.17 (1962) → 4.24 (1964), with slight decline to 4.15 in 1965, across 1006 total fish sampled. Improvement occurred across nearly all size classes, including fish <20 cm.
4. **Comparative condition (Table 2)**: By 1959 (only 2 years post-introduction), Roosevelt Lake crappie K (3.90) already exceeded average K values reported for black crappie populations in California (Clear Lake, K=3.44), Iowa (K=3.39/3.25), Tennessee (K=3.38/3.02), Florida (K=3.28), Indiana (K=3.09), and Minnesota (K=3.08).
5. **Comparable forage failure pattern elsewhere**: Findings align with prior reports (McConnell & Gerdes 1964; Menn 1965) that threadfin shad can fail to sustain adequate small-size forage for young crappie/bass due to concentrated spawning and rapid growth, evidenced by near-total absence of shad <8 cm in Pena Blanca Lake from September–June.
6. **Analogous bass response**: Melander (1961) reported similar condition/weight improvements in [[Largemouth Bass]] following threadfin shad introduction in Lake Mead, supporting the broader inference that threadfin shad drove the crappie condition improvement in Roosevelt Lake.
7. **Growth rate limitation**: Direct evaluation of black crappie growth rate changes was not possible because Roosevelt Lake crappie scales did not exhibit unambiguous annual growth marks (annuli).
