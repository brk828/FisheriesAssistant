# Winter Feeding Habits of Brook Trout Fingerlings in Relation to Natural Food Organisms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1941 study by [[Justin W. Leonard]] of the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]] examines the winter feeding of young [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) in relation to the benthic invertebrates available to them. The work was conducted in Section I-B, a newly excavated 186-foot diversion channel at the [[Hunt Creek Fisheries Experiment Station]]. Monitoring of colonization showed that the bottom fauna recolonized the channel rapidly through drift from the upstream channel, and stomach analyses of 22 trout showed that the diet differed markedly from the available bottom fauna, with aquatic Diptera dominating both diet and benthos but mayflies and stoneflies being underrepresented in the trout diet. The author also observed that trout digestion was very slow at winter water temperatures.

## Background & Description

- **Study site:** Section I-B, one of three stream-channel diversions excavated in fall 1940 near the Hunt Creek Fisheries Experiment Station, operated by the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]] of the Michigan Department of Conservation. The channel is 186 feet long, averages 6.1 feet wide at the waterline, has a mean flow of 1.5 feet per second (measured with a [[Bentzel Velocity Tube]]), and an average depth of 8 inches.
- **Substrate and banks:** A mixture of sand and medium gravel with no submerged vegetation at the time of study; low, marshy banks dense with [[Carex]], and a higher morainal bank with aspen and balm-of-Gilead about 20 feet away. The stream was shaded only in early morning and late afternoon.
- **Study species:** [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*). On January 18, 1941, 34 trout were recovered when the section was screened and drained; 22 were retained for stomach analysis. Standard length ranged 49 to 103 mm (average 66.8 mm); total length 59 to 132 mm (average 80.3 mm); weight 1 to 19 g (average 5.0 g). The average coefficient of condition was 1.66.
- **Sampling methods:** Square-foot bottom samples, stomach content analysis by volume and number, and comparison with bottom fauna. Preference was discussed using the [[Hess and Rainwater Preference Ratio]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Colonization of the new channel.** Water first flowed into Section I-B on October 16, 1940, after adult aquatic insects had almost entirely disappeared. Blackfly larvae (*Simulium venustum*) were first noticed on October 23. The author concluded that all invaders were drift-borne from the upstream channel, since adult insects had already oviposited and died before the channel opened. Only a few late-emerging adult caddisflies (*Neophylax autumnus*) were seen.

**Faunal increase.** Sample totals rose through the winter:

| Sample date | Species | Individuals | Volume (cc) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dec 20, 1940 | 8 | 187 | 0.20 |
| Jan 24, 1941 | 19 | 701 | 0.925 |
| Jan 27, 1941 | 19 | 999 | 1.325 |
| Feb 4, 1941 | 21 | 1,535 | 1.575 |

The author compared this rapid influx with recovery studies by [[Moffett 1936]] in South Willow Creek, Utah, and [[Surber 1937]] in West Virginia. The influx began more than two months later in the season than recovery in those streams, which largely ruled out direct oviposition as the source and pointed to the swift current of Hunt Creek as the main cause.

**Dominant food organisms.** Key taxa in the bottom fauna included:
- Aquatic Diptera: [[Chironomus Modestus]], Chironomidae larvae, and [[Simulium venustum]] (blackflies)
- Ephemeroptera: [[Baetis vagans]] (the most numerous mayfly), *Ephemerella invaria*
- Plecoptera: *Allocapnia torontoensis*, *Leuctra tenuis*, *Isoperla* sp., *Nemoura* sp.
- Trichoptera: *Rhyacophila* sp., *Hydropsyche sparna*

**Diet versus benthos.** Diptera made up approximately 85% of stomach contents by volume but only 57% of bottom fauna volume. Mayflies were about 25% of bottom volume but only 7.5% of diet volume. Stoneflies were 6.5% of bottom volume but 0.3% of diet volume, although they ranked third numerically in the stream.

**Digestion at low temperature.** Three hours after capture, many midge and blackfly larvae, plus one large aquatic annelid, were still alive and crawling out of trout stomachs. Digestion appeared to have nearly stopped in winter water, though the stomachs were full. Soft-bodied organisms were found intact throughout the intestine and near the vent in winter-caught trout.

## Key Findings

1. **Rapid recolonization by drift:** The new channel was colonized within one week of water admission, with numbers rising from 187 organisms (8 species) on December 20 to 1,535 organisms (21 species) on February 4, 1941. The author attributes this to drift from upstream rather than oviposition.
2. **Diet selectivity:** Trout diet was dominated by aquatic Diptera (84.7% by volume; 80% by number), while bottom fauna were 56.9% Diptera by volume. Mayflies (7.5% of diet versus 24.8% of benthos) and stoneflies (0.3% versus 6.5%) were strongly underrepresented in the diet.
3. **Stomach contents:** The 22 stomachs held 5,541 specimens from 32 species, averaging 252 organisms and 0.425 cc per stomach. An average square foot of bottom held about three times the volume and slightly over four times the number of organisms of an average stomach.
4. **Availability versus selection:** The author argues that the preference-ratio method of Hess and Rainwater measures availability as much as preference, and that the gap between diet and benthos may reflect differences in availability. Small, weakly chitinized mayfly nymphs (*Baetis*) were not considered significantly more digestible than large midge larvae or blackfly larvae.
5. **Winter digestion and nutrition:** Digestion was very slow at low winter temperatures, and it is questionable how much nutritional benefit fish gain from winter feeding. The author suggests that food supply may therefore be less critical to trout survival during the coldest part of the year, and recommends further study.
6. **Stonefly size and predation:** Stoneflies in Hunt Creek are small (about 0.25 to 1 inch), unlike the large western *Pteronarcys californica* eaten heavily by trout in Yellowstone, which may explain their low importance in the diet.
