## Executive Summary
This 1989 field memorandum from Arizona State University's Center for Environmental Studies (P.C. Marsh & W.L. Minckley) reports on a reintroduction and grow-out monitoring program for two endangered Colorado River Basin fishes — the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) — stocked into artificial stock tanks ("tanks") at [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] (BANWR), Arizona, between 1986 and 1989. The survey found that severe drought, high summer temperatures, and degraded water-retention infrastructure had caused widespread habitat desiccation, eliminating all stocked bonytail refuge-wide and reducing viable razorback sucker populations to only 3 of 11 originally stocked sites. Despite these losses, several tanks (notably Carrizo Dam, Rock, and Tequila Tanks) demonstrated strong growth performance, suggesting stock tanks can serve as effective grow-out habitats for future reintroduction efforts if hydrological stability is ensured.

## Background & Description
Data collected consist primarily of growth metrics (total length, TL, and weight) tracked longitudinally per site:

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*):**
- **Carrizo Dam Tank** (stocked 16 Mar 1989, larval fish ~1.0 cm TL):
  - 9 April: 3.0 cm TL (n=10)
  - 12 June: 11.1 cm TL (n=13)
  - 01 October: 33.2 cm TL (n=17)
  - Mean weight (October): ~429 g (~1.0 lb)
  - Parent stock: cross of slow-growing adult male and slow-growing female razorback sucker produced at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] — an experimental stocking.

- **Rock Tank** (stocked 31 Mar 1987, 30,000 larvae, ~1.0 cm TL):
  - May 1988: 20.6 cm TL (n=14)
  - 07 December 1988: 25.7 cm TL (n=44)
  - 01 October 1989: 24.9 cm TL (n=28) — a decline attributed to differential mortality of larger fish under low-water/high-temperature summer stress.
  - At the December 1988 harvest point, mean weight was ~226 g (0.5 lb), comparable to hatchery-reared fish of similar age at Arizona Game and Fish Department's [[Page Springs Hatchery]].

- **Tequila Tank** (stocked 31 Mar 1987, 30,000 larvae, ~1.0 cm TL):
  - May 1988: 21.1 cm TL (n=44)
  - 07 December 1988: 27.7 cm TL (n=20)
  - 01 October 1989: 13 specimens collected and frozen (unmeasured on site) for genetic/electrophoretic analysis; fish were noted as generally smaller than the December 1988 cohort, paralleling the pattern seen at Rock Tank.

**Bonytail (*Gila elegans*):**
- Stocked at three sites (Big Bertha, Kings, Road Camp) on 04 Nov 1986 at ~7.0–7.6 cm TL (~2,500–3,000 individuals each).
- No growth follow-up data reported — all bonytail populations were lost refuge-wide due to habitat desiccation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type:** Artificial earthen impoundments ("stock tanks") originally built for livestock watering, repurposed as isolated grow-out/refuge ponds for endangered native Sonoran/Colorado River fishes.
- **Habitat stability as a limiting factor:** Survival and growth were strongly linked to water permanence. Tanks near mountain bajadas (especially along the refuge's western margin) retained water more reliably than those in gently rolling central-refuge terrain, because bajada-adjacent tanks receive runoff earlier in precipitation events before overland flow is lost to infiltration/evaporation.
- **Vegetation and food resources:** Carrizo Dam Tank supported dense aquatic vegetation in warm months, which was linked to abundant food resources and the tank's superior razorback sucker growth rates, despite complicating sampling.
- **Anthropogenic infrastructure effects:** Many tanks fell into disrepair during the private-to-federal land ownership transition, with damaged water seals, dikes, and berms reducing water retention. Refuge staff had begun remediating some of these structures.
- **Threats:**
  - Drought — January–June 1989 precipitation was the lowest in a 10-year record (Table 2), compounded by extreme summer heat.
  - Predation and competition — sites retaining water but harboring **non-native fish** or high predator densities were flagged as unsuitable and recommended for renovation.
  - Differential mortality of larger fish under thermal/hydrological stress, suggesting a size-selective bottleneck during summer drawdown.
- **Species interactions:** No native fish co-occurred with predatory non-natives in viable tanks; management recommended removing or renovating such sites before further stocking.

## Key Findings
- Of **11 sites** stocked with razorback sucker (1986–1989), only **3 remained viable** (Carrizo Dam, Rock, Tequila) by October 1989; the rest were dry or fishless (Table 1).
- **All bonytail stockings (3 sites) were lost** due to habitat desiccation — a complete reintroduction failure for this species at BANWR during the study period.
- Growth rates of razorback sucker varied substantially by site; **Carrizo Dam Tank showed the best growth performance**, linked to hydrological stability and food abundance.
- **Optimal harvest window** for grow-out to reintroduction size was determined to be **two summers** post-stocking; extending grow-out further risked size-selective mortality under current climate conditions (1987–1989 drought regime).
- Precipitation data (Table 2, 1978–1989) confirm 1988–89 as an anomalously dry year across all four regional weather stations (Arivaca, Sasabe, Sasabe 7NW, Sasabe 6NNE), with summer and winter totals well below the 10-year means.
- Authors recommend:
  1. Continued stocking of suitable tanks with hatchery-produced young fish each spring.
  2. Renovation of hydrologically stable tanks currently dominated by non-native fish/predators.
  3. Implementation of **mark-recapture population estimation** to quantify survival rates and refine stocking density for maximizing harvestable biomass.
  4. Prioritizing infrastructure repair (dikes, berms, water seals) at refuge tanks to restore habitat suitability.
- This report functions as a baseline dataset for adaptive management of the **razorback sucker and bonytail recovery program**, part of broader [[Endangered Colorado River Fish Recovery]] efforts in Arizona.
