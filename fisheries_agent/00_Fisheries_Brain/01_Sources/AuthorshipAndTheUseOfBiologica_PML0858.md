# Authorship and the Use of Biological Information in Endangered Species Recovery Plans

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Leah R. Gerber]] and [[Cheryl B. Schultz]] (National Center for Ecological Analysis and Synthesis, University of California, Santa Barbara) examines how the authorship of [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) [[Recovery Plan]]s relates to the use of biological information in them. Using a national database of 133 recovery plans compiled by the [[Society for Conservation Biology]] with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the authors test three hypotheses derived from [[Clark et al. 1994]]. Authorship by people from diverse affiliations and by academics was associated with greater use of biological information, while federal-only authorship was not associated with stronger use. The authors conclude that choosing who writes recovery plans is one of the few levers agencies control, and that changing participation could improve recovery outcomes.

## Background & Description

- **Legal context:** The ESA (1973) protects listed plant and animal species, requires designation of critical habitat, and requires recovery plans where agencies determine they will promote recovery. Recovery plans are not legally required where agencies judge them unhelpful.
- **Responsible agencies:** The USFWS covers terrestrial species and a few marine species. The [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS) covers most marine species.
- **Scale of listing:** As of December 1999, 1,763 species were listed as threatened or endangered, and draft or final recovery plans existed for 924 of them (USFWS 2000).
- **Criticism of plans:** Scientists have repeatedly questioned whether recovery plans are effective in implementation (Tear et al. 1993; Wilcove et al. 1993; Easter-Pilcher 1996; [[National Research Council]] 1995).
- **Research gap:** The authors note that selecting authors is one of the few recovery-planning decisions within agency control, making it a practical target for scientists assisting ESA implementation.

### Hypotheses tested
1. Participation by authors with diverse affiliations enhances the use of biological information in recovery plans.
2. Plans written exclusively by federal employees are less well grounded in biological information than plans including nonfederal participation.
3. Participation by individuals with university appointments improves the use of biological information.

Academic affiliation was used as a surrogate for state-of-the-art professional training, because plans do not record authors' educational credentials. The authors acknowledge this is an imperfect proxy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Database origin:** In September 1998, the SCB and USFWS launched a national review of recovery plans. The resulting database involved over 300 participants at 19 universities, who answered a questionnaire of more than 2,000 questions on plan content, construction and potential implementation (Hoekstra et al. 2001).
- **Sample construction:** The database covered a number of species across 135 recovery plans. Where several species were sampled from one plan, one species was selected at random per plan. Two plans lacked authorship data, leaving analyses based on one species in each of 133 plans.
- **Species examples:** The paper uses two fish-related and plant-related examples to illustrate scoring:
  - The [[Okaloosa Darter]] (*Etheostoma okaloosae*) persists in six Florida stream systems. Its plan included population-trend criteria tied to stable or increasing populations across all six systems, with standard-deviation metrics suggested to handle variability. This plan was scored *very clear* for use of biological information.
  - A rare Florida shrub, limited in range to two pastures, had a plan requiring 20 protected populations at five or more properties, but with definitive goals awaiting minimum viable population estimates. It was scored *unclear* on all metrics.
- **Management tasks examined:** Habitat management, population management, exotic species management, and incentive programs (for example, USDA Conservation Reserve Program, [[Habitat Conservation Plan]]s, and mitigation banks).
- **Other taxa in the record:** Reference citations include gray whale delisting and bowhead whale population studies, indicating the wider marine-mammal context of the database.

## Key Findings

- **Authorship patterns:** About one-third of plans were written solely by federal government employees. About one-third included authors with university affiliations. The number of plans written solely by federal staff increased significantly over time, while the share including academic authors stayed unchanged.
- **Hypothesis 1 (diverse groups):** Larger and more diverse author groups were associated with more use of biological information in the analyzed plan features, supporting the first hypothesis in the authors' framing of the results.
- **Hypothesis 2 (federal-only plans):** Plans written exclusively by federal employees were not found to be better grounded in biology than those including nonfederal participation. The abstract reports that plans lacking nonfederal participation suffered from inadequate attention to species biology.
- **Hypothesis 3 (academic affiliation):** Academic affiliation was strongly associated with the use of focal-species biology in recovery plans.
- **Animal versus plant plans:** Plans for animals were more likely to have nonfederal authors. Reported mean author numbers differed between plan groups (5.3 vs. 2.7, t = 3.7, p < 0.001).
- **Management and monitoring:** Authorship size and diversity were analyzed against recommended management tasks, recovery-criteria clarity, and monitoring design (population trends and demographic attributes), with results for each reported in the original tables.
- **Policy implication:** Modifying the choice of participants in recovery planning may increase the use of biological information in recommended measures, which could influence the eventual success of recovery efforts.
- **Recommendation to agencies:** Scientists can help agencies select appropriately diverse groups of professionals to write plans and to serve on recovery teams.
