# Temporal and Spatial Relations Between the Spawning of Two Sympatric Gila (Pisces: Cyprinidae) of the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #reproductive-biology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This study investigated reproductive isolating mechanisms between [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*) in the [[Upper Colorado River]] at [[Black Rocks]], Colorado, during 1983-1985. Using gonadosomatic indices, gamete expression, and radiotelemetry, researchers found that spawning of both species temporally overlapped (late June-July during high-flow years), but spatial segregation could not be definitively demonstrated. [[Humpback Chub]] showed strong site fidelity to the 1.8-km Black Rocks reach (mean maximum displacement 1.4 km), while [[Roundtail Chub]] ranged widely throughout the river system (mean maximum displacement 33.9 km). The study suggests behavioral mechanisms, particularly olfactory cues for mate recognition in turbid waters, may be the primary reproductive isolating mechanism. The unique morphology of [[Humpback Chub]] appears adapted to turbulent habitats with large boulders and rock outcrops, and the scarcity of such habitats may limit population size of this endangered species.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*) are endemic cyprinids of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. In the upper basin, the widespread and abundant [[Roundtail Chub]] shares habitat with the rare, disjunct populations of [[Humpback Chub]]. The [[Humpback Chub]] is federally listed as endangered (Federal Register 45(99):33768-33781).

The study site at [[Black Rocks]] is a narrow, turbulent, deepwater reach formed by passage of the river through metamorphic rock. This 1.8-km reach has mean depth of approximately 5 m with maximum recorded depths of 17 m, several times greater than adjacent reaches. The area is characterized by large, angular boulders and shoreline rock outcrops.

Morphological distinctions between species were assessed using discriminant analysis of nine characters. The most important taxonomic characters were depth of nuchal depression, number of anal-fin rays, and length of anal-fin base. Of 582 mature *Gila* (≥250 mm TL) examined, 75 fish (12.9%) could not be classified at the 95% probability level and were excluded as unclassified *Gila*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The aquatic ecosystem of the [[Colorado River]] has been significantly altered by water-development projects, introduction of non-native fishes, and other anthropogenic activities. These alterations have raised concerns about potential breakdown of reproductive isolating mechanisms between [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]], particularly given the occurrence of individuals with apparently intermediate morphologies.

During the study years (1983-1984), the [[Colorado River]] experienced two of the largest seasonal run-off flows on record, delaying seasonal warming. Spawning occurred when discharge was near seasonal high or receding, at water temperatures of 14-24°C. In years with below-average or near-average discharge, [[Humpback Chub]] spawned in May or June, respectively.

The [[Black Rocks]] habitat contains cobble and gravel bars similar to those found elsewhere in the river, but is distinguished by its unique combination of deep water, turbulent flow, and large physical structures. The dynamic flow vectors resulting from water moving rapidly among large, angular boulders and shoreline rock outcrops appear to be the defining characteristic of [[Humpback Chub]] habitat, rather than depth per se.

## Key Findings

**Temporal Overlap in Spawning:**
- Spawning of both species was concurrent, eliminating temporal isolation as a reproductive barrier
- In 1983, female [[Roundtail Chub]] GSI increased during May-June and decreased with spawning in late June-July
- Female [[Humpback Chub]] showed similar patterns, with spawning occurring in July of both study years
- Male fish of both species expressed milt earlier and for longer duration than females expressed ova
- One female [[Humpback Chub]] captured 28 June 1985 had flowing ova

**Movement Patterns and Spatial Distribution:**
- 33 [[Humpback Chub]] and 17 [[Roundtail Chub]] were implanted with radio transmitters
- [[Humpback Chub]] exhibited limited movement: mean maximum displacement 1.4 km from release site
- [[Roundtail Chub]] showed widespread movement: mean maximum displacement 33.9 km
- During estimated spawning period (20 June-30 July), 88% of radio contacts with [[Humpback Chub]] were within [[Black Rocks]] in 1983-1984
- Only 22% of radio contacts with [[Roundtail Chub]] were within [[Black Rocks]] during the same period in 1984
- However, male and female [[Roundtail Chub]] with expressable gametes were captured in [[Black Rocks]] during spawning season
- Both species were sometimes captured in the same net with expressable gametes

**Tag-Recapture Data:**
- 57 Carlin-tagged [[Humpback Chub]] were recaptured, including fish tagged 1-56 months earlier
- Recaptured fish averaged 1.1 km from release locations; most were within ±0.2 km of original [[Black Rocks]] location
- Two [[Humpback Chub]] showed long-distance movement (22 km) between [[Westwater Canyon]] and [[Black Rocks]]
- Radio-tagged [[Humpback Chub]] released 0.3 km from capture sites returned within several weeks
- One Carlin-tagged fish returned to original capture location within one day after displacement

**Reproductive Isolating Mechanisms:**
- Temporal isolation is unimportant (spawning concurrent)
- Spatial isolation unclear: while most [[Humpback Chub]] remained in [[Black Rocks]] and most [[Roundtail Chub]] ranged widely, some [[Roundtail Chub]] with expressable gametes occurred in [[Black Rocks]] during spawning
- Microhabitat selection at spawning sites could provide spatial segregation undetectable by radiotelemetry
- Behavioral mechanisms likely provide major barriers to interbreeding
- Olfactory cues may be important for intraspecific mate recognition in highly turbid spawning-season waters
- Visual cues (e.g., nuchal hump) unlikely to be important due to turbidity

**Hybrid Viability:**
- Artificial crosses of bonytail × [[Humpback Chub]] and bonytail × [[Roundtail Chub]] produced viable hybrids
- These hybrids matured at 2 years and were successfully self-crossed and introgressively crossed
- [[Humpback Chub]] × [[Roundtail Chub]] cross not tested but likely viable based on related crosses
- Postmating mechanisms appear incomplete among these *Gila* species
- Genetic nature of presumed intergrades in wild populations remains unknown

**Habitat Associations:**
- [[Humpback Chub]] show strong affinity for turbulent habitats with large, angular boulders and rock outcrops
- Unusual morphology may be adaptation to dynamic flow vectors in such habitats
- Narrow caudal peduncle may provide advantage, though concavity on leading edge of hump could be deleterious to flow
- [[Humpback Chub]] found associated with man-made riprap in railroad and highway construction
- Limited availability of preferred habitat may control distribution and abundance
- Species exhibits strong homing ability to specific locations within [[Black Rocks]]

**Conservation Implications:**
- Behavioral reproductive isolating mechanisms are probably strongest barrier to interbreeding
- Effects of subtle habitat alterations on [[Humpback Chub]] reproduction poorly understood
- Need for comprehensive monitoring program of populations and genomes at [[Black Rocks]] and other occupied reaches
- Monitoring genomes of age-0 *Gila* using electrophoresis or related techniques recommended
- Negative effects of hypolimnetic discharges from dams on reproduction well understood, but effects of other habitat modifications need study
- Scarcity of suitable turbulent boulder habitat may limit population size in [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]
