# Big Spring Spinedace Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Plagopterini #conservation #recovery-planning

## Executive Summary

The [[Big Spring Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis pratensis*) is a threatened subspecies of cyprinid fish endemic to [[Meadow Valley Wash]] in [[Lincoln County]], Nevada. The only extant population occupies a 5-mile section of stream through [[Condor Canyon]], making it extremely vulnerable to catastrophic events, habitat modification, and nonnative species introductions. The subspecies was extirpated from its type locality at [[Big Spring]] due to water diversion and introduction of nonnative species including [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). This Technical/Agency Review Draft recovery plan, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in January 1993, outlines actions needed to delist the species by establishing three self-sustaining populations secured from threats for 5 consecutive years. Recovery tasks include securing [[Condor Canyon]] essential habitat, identifying ecological parameters through research, establishing two additional populations (preferably one within the [[Meadow Valley Wash]] drainage), monitoring all populations, and maintaining public information programs. Total estimated recovery cost is $168,000 over 14 years, with delisting targeted for 2006.

## Background & Description

The [[Big Spring Spinedace]] belongs to the Plagopterini tribe of [[Cyprinidae]], which includes highly specialized desert fishes: the monotypic genera *Meda* ([[Spikedace]]) and *Plagopterus* ([[Woundfin]]), and the polytypic genus *Lepidomeda* (spinedace). Plagopterin fishes are distinguished by spinelike pelvic, pectoral, and anterior dorsal fin rays; membranous connection between innermost pelvic fin rays and belly; bright silver coloration; and absent or diminutive body scales. These fishes are among the few North American cyprinids that do not hybridize with other genera.

[[Big Spring Spinedace]] were described by Miller and Hubbs (1960) as a subspecies of [[Middle Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*), differentiated from [[Virgin River Spinedace]] (*L. m. mollispinis*) by having a higher, more pointed dorsal fin; longer pelvic fins; and a smaller, more oblique mouth. The subspecific epithet *pratensis* (Latin for "pertaining to meadows") reflects the species' apparent preference for vegetated habitats. Specimens range from 48 to over 110 millimeters total length. Coloration is bright silver with some individuals exhibiting yellow-to-orange pigmentation at fin axils, anal fin base, shoulder girdle, and above the mouth. Nuptial tubercles have been observed on spawning individuals.

The species possesses a pharyngeal tooth formula of 5-4 in the main row, relatively weak second dorsal spine, 9 anal rays, typically less than 90 lateral line scales, and melanophores confined to upper portions of opercle and preopercle. Chromosome morphology more closely resembles *Gila* or similar genera than other plagopterins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Big Spring Spinedace]] are restricted to the [[Pluvial Lake Carpenter]] system remnants. Pluvial Lake Carpenter existed 10,000 to 40,000 years ago in present-day [[Lake Valley]], Nevada, drained by the pluvial Carpenter River (now [[Meadow Valley Wash]]) which joined the pluvial [[White River]] to flow into the [[Colorado River]]. As the lake system dried, fish species became restricted to remnant habitats with permanent water.

The [[Condor Canyon]] section of [[Meadow Valley Wash]] currently supports three native fish species: [[Big Spring Spinedace]], [[Meadow Valley Wash Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki* ssp.), and [[Meadow Valley Wash Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus* ssp.). The latter two species are widespread throughout the approximately 120-mile drainage but are Category 2 candidates for federal listing. [[Big Spring Spinedace]] are restricted to the 5-mile Condor Canyon section.

**Habitat Characteristics:**
- Stream averages 9 feet wide, 0.6 feet deep, 1.6% gradient
- Base flow approximately 200 gallons per minute from [[Delmue Springs]]
- Flow measurements: 2.2 to 6.9 cubic feet per second (980-3,100 gpm)
- Water temperature: 84°F recorded in meadow habitat (air temp 94°F)
- Substrate: firm to soft clay with some gravel
- Vegetation: watercress (*Rorippa*), pondweed (*Potamageton*), rushes (*Scirpus*)
- Preferred microhabitat: 1-3 feet deep, moderate to slow current, undercut banks, floating aquatic vegetation

**Life History (poorly understood):**
- Spawning: Likely spring to early summer based on observations of tuberculate adults in late May and young-of-year in July-August
- Young-of-year (15 mm total length) observed September 1980
- Young-of-year (average 37 mm) common in early August 1990
- Food habits: Unknown, but closely related [[Virgin River Spinedace]] are opportunistic feeders consuming aquatic insect larvae, algae, and plant material
- Vegetation (especially watercress) likely important for providing invertebrate prey habitat

**Threats:**
1. **Nonnative species**: Unidentified crayfish firmly established; limited numbers of [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*) collected
2. **Habitat modification**: Livestock grazing impacts, vehicle use off railroad bed, tree cutting, bulldozer channel modification
3. **Water diversion**: Three irrigation water rights below canyon; potential groundwater depletion from well development
4. **Catastrophic events**: Single population vulnerable to floods, drought, toxic spills (empty pesticide containers found in stream)
5. **Mining claims**: Three valid claims overlap critical habitat
6. **Access**: Former [[Union Pacific Railroad]] right-of-way creates road access, increasing human impacts and introduction risk

## Key Findings

**Recovery Objective:** Delist [[Big Spring Spinedace]] when three self-sustaining populations exist for 5 consecutive years in habitats secured from all threats. Two additional populations must be established beyond the existing [[Condor Canyon]] population.

**Priority Recovery Actions:**

**Need 1: Secure Condor Canyon Essential Habitat ($29,000)**
- Obtain private landowner cooperation through conservation agreements or fee title acquisition
- Acquire additional instream flow water rights (current 0.31 cfs may be insufficient)
- Implement [[Bureau of Land Management]] [[Condor Canyon Habitat Management Plan]] (exclude livestock March 15-November 15, limit vehicle use to railroad bed, file for spring water rights, install flow gauging stations)
- Control nonnative aquatic species through removal/control methods; prevent immigration from upstream reservoirs

**Need 2: Identify Ecological Parameters ($68,000)**
- Determine life history and habitat requirements (reproductive behavior, feeding habits, demographic parameters, age structure, population growth rates)
- Determine species interactions with [[Meadow Valley Wash Desert Sucker]] and [[Meadow Valley Wash Speckled Dace]] to avoid management conflicts
- Evaluate genetic status of populations above and below [[Condor Canyon]] waterfall
- Conduct minimum viable population analysis to determine: habitat size for self-sustaining population, number needed to establish new population, safe removal numbers, population parameters

**Need 3: Establish Additional Populations ($29,000)**
- Select introduction habitats based on research results; prioritize [[Big Spring]] (type locality) for reestablishment
- Secure selected habitats through landowner cooperation, conservation agreements, or acquisition
- Prepare and implement habitat management plans for Federal lands
- Rehabilitate introduction habitats (remove nonnative species, prevent invasions)
- Establish populations through multiple releases of disease/parasite-free fish; time releases to maximize reproduction potential

**Need 4: Monitor All Populations and Habitats ($22,000)**
- Conduct annual monitoring of population size, age-class structure, distribution
- Schedule monitoring spring and autumn to evaluate over-winter survival and recruitment
- Monitor coexisting native and nonnative species occurrence and abundance
- Evaluate habitat quality and quantity regularly
- Use data to identify problems, guide management, analyze recovery program effectiveness

**Need 5: Public Information Program ($20,000)**
- Develop program to inform local residents of protected status and recovery efforts
- Continuously involve [[Lincoln County Board of Commissioners]], [[Bureau of Land Management]] Nevada State Office, [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]]
- Provide information for release via newspapers, television, radio

**Conservation Efforts to Date:**
- Federal listing as threatened species with critical habitat designation (March 1985)
- [[The Nature Conservancy]] purchased 40 acres (0.16 stream miles) in December 1981
- Instream flow water right (0.31 cfs) granted through entire canyon length
- Emergency refugium established at [[Shoshone Pond Resource Area]], White Pine County (July 1987, 75 fish released; success uncertain)
- [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] manual removal of nonnative fish
- [[Bureau of Land Management]] aquatic habitat inventory (1987): stream surveys, riparian transects, aerial infrared photography, water quality monitoring, botanical inventory
- [[Condor Canyon Habitat Management Plan]] published 1990

**Historical Context:**
- First collected 1938 from [[Big Spring]] outflow (only 7 specimens despite prolonged seining)
- Considered extinct before formal description (Miller and Hubbs 1960)
- Rediscovered September 1977 in [[Condor Canyon]] waterfall plunge pool
- 1980 transplant of larvae from waterfall pool to upstream pools (1 mile above waterfall)
- Extirpation from [[Big Spring]] attributed to water diversion, occasional desiccation, and nonnative species ([[Mosquitofish]], [[Bullfrog]] *Rana catesbeiana*)

**Population Data:**
- May 1986: 204 individuals collected from 11 of 15 sites (4.5-mile section); 97 from two transplant site pools
- November 1990: 546 individuals captured from 13 sites; total population estimate unavailable
- Population size never formally determined

**Implementation Schedule:**
- Total estimated cost: $168,000 (1993-2006)
- FY 1993: $28,000
- FY 1994: $31,000  
- FY 1995: $39,000
- FY 1996-2006: $6,000-$10,000 annually
- Target delisting date: 2006 if recovery criteria met

**Critical Habitat:** 4 stream miles of [[Meadow Valley Wash]] through [[Condor Canyon]] with 50-foot riparian zones (T. 1 S., R. 68 E., sections 13, 23, 24, 26, 27, 28; Mount Diablo Meridian). Primary constituent elements: clean, permanent, flowing, spring-fed stream with deep pools and shallow marshy areas; absence of exotic fishes.

**Essential Habitat:** Extends 0.75 miles upstream and 0.25 miles downstream of critical habitat (total 5 miles), from where flow surfaces north of canyon to current diversion point; includes sustaining springs and transplant pools.
