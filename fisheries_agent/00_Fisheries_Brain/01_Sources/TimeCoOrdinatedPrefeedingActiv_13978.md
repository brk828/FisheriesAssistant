# Time-Coordinated Prefeeding Activity in Fish

## Executive Summary
Davis & Bardach (1965) investigated whether the "pre-dawn"/pre-feeding locomotor activity previously documented in [[Bluegill]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] is an innate circadian phenomenon or a learned response to regular daily feeding. Using automated activity recorders and direct behavioral observation on several marine and freshwater fish species — most extensively the [[Killifish]] (*Fundulus heteroclitus*) — the authors demonstrated that daily anticipatory ("pre-feeding") activity emerges from repeated, regularly-timed feeding rather than solely from light-cycle entrainment. Critically, when feeding times were shifted under constant light (LL), the pre-feeding activity peak shifted correspondingly within 1–3 days, indicating the behavior is timed by an internal ("biological clock") mechanism that is itself entrainable by the act of feeding, independent of any light cue. The study concludes that pre-feeding activity represents a **learned, conditioned response** coupling an endogenous circadian cue to the act of eating — distinct from a true innate circadian rhythm.

## Background & Description
- Builds on prior work (Davis 1963) showing that bluegill and largemouth bass under alternating 12:12 bright/dim light and fed at light onset developed increased locomotion 1–3 hours *before* light/feeding onset after 10–20 days of training ("pre-light–pre-feeding activity" or "predawn peak").
- This paper tests whether this activity is (a) driven by the light–dark (LD) cycle itself, or (b) a consequence of regular daily feeding timing, using species with more flexible feeding schedules and constant-light (LL) conditions to decouple light cues from feeding cues.
- **Species studied:**
  - *Fundulus heteroclitus* ([[Killifish]]) — primary subject; readily tolerated confinement, tested extensively across four experiments.
  - *Microgadus tomcod* ([[Tomcod]]) — established pre-light–pre-feeding activity but frequently became ill in captivity.
  - *Stenotomus versicolor* ([[Scup]]) — established the response but became excitable after ~1 month; response inconsistent after initial establishment.
  - *Tautogolabrus adspersus* ([[Cunner]]) — did **not** establish the response; fed poorly, intolerant of tankmates.
  - *Pseudopleuronectes americanus* ([[Winter Flounder]]) — did **not** establish pre-light or pre-feeding activity; very low overall activity.
  - *Lepomis macrochirus* ([[Bluegill]]) — 32 individuals directly observed under constant light (LL) with two feeding-time groups (1030 vs 1700), confirming pre-feeding activity via behavioral scoring.

### Methods Summary
- **Locomotion detector:** rubber-band/wire-frame contact switches wired to thyratron circuits feeding an Esterline-Angus chart recorder; continuous automated activity records at 1.9 cm/hr chart speed.
- **Light regimes:** notated as LD (e.g., LD 12-12 = 12h bright light/12h dim "darkness," the latter provided by a dim NE-2 red-orange glow) or LL (constant light, 50–100 lux).
- **Water conditions:** 16–17°C storage; experimental tanks held at 19–23°C with no detectable daily thermal cycle.
- **Feeding:** chopped fish, clam, squid (marine species); earthworms and liver-supplemented trout pellets (bluegill); delivered via feeding holes/tubes to minimize disturbance.
- **Direct observation (bluegill):** 6-level activity scoring (0 = stationary to 5 = highly active), transcribed independently by two observers, analyzed via two-way ANOVA with least-significant-difference tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Daily behavioral rhythms tied to natural light cycles are broadly documented across fish taxa (Barlow 1958 in desert pupfish; Carlander & Cleary 1949; Harder & Hempel 1954; Jones 1956 in minnows; Kawamoto & Konishi 1955; Welsh & Osborn 1937 in catfish retina) — situating this study within a wider literature on fish [[Circadian Rhythm]] and photoperiodic behavior.
- The [[Biological Clock]] concept is invoked by analogy to the sun-orientation rhythm in fish (Hasler & Schwassmann 1960) and to circadian rhythms broadly observed across animal taxa (Pittendrigh 1960).
- Ecologically, this pre-feeding anticipatory behavior likely reflects adaptive foraging timing mechanisms — fish that can anticipate a temporally predictable food source (e.g., tidal, diel prey availability) may gain a competitive/energetic advantage by mobilizing toward foraging areas just prior to food availability.
- Species-specific tolerance of captivity and social confinement (e.g., cunner's aggression, scup's excitability, tomcod's susceptibility to illness) illustrate how experimental tractability under artificial husbandry conditions varies markedly among coastal marine fishes, a relevant consideration for aquaculture and laboratory aquatic research.
- The work highlights the interaction between exogenous light entrainment (LD cycle) and an endogenous, learned feeding-associated clock — relevant to understanding how fish integrate multiple environmental timegivers ("zeitgebers") in variable light/food regimes (e.g., estuarine turbidity, seasonal photoperiod shifts).

## Key Findings
1. **Establishment of pre-feeding activity:** Tomcod, scup, and killifish (but not flounder or cunner) developed a distinct peak of locomotor activity in the hours preceding scheduled feeding when fed daily at light onset (LD 12-12).
2. **Feeding regularity, not light cycle alone, drives the response:** Killifish fed at irregular times for ~24 days showed no anticipatory peak; once switched to daily feeding at light onset, a pre-feeding peak emerged within a single day — though possible confounding via incidental feeding-related noise cues was noted and discussed.
3. **Pre-feeding activity occurs even in constant light (LL):** Groups of killifish fed daily at a fixed clock time (1200) under LL developed pre-feeding activity peaks within 5–10 days, demonstrating that the response does not require a light–dark cycle, only regular feeding timing.
4. **Phase-shift experiments confirm endogenous timing:** When feeding time was shifted 6 hours (either advanced or delayed) under LL, the pre-feeding activity peak shifted by a corresponding ~6 hours within 1–3 days, consistent with resetting of an internal, clock-based mechanism rather than a direct environmental trigger.
5. **Interaction between feeding-time phase and light period:** When fed near the middle or end (rather than onset) of the light period, killifish showed a pre-feeding peak timed to feeding but not to light transitions; however, extended pairing (months) of feeding with a fixed light period eventually produced a secondary **pre-light** activity peak as well — suggesting conditioning of the feeding response to light cues over long-term exposure.
6. **Bluegill confirmation:** Under constant light, two groups of bluegill fed at different fixed times (1030 vs. 1700) each showed significantly greater activity (P < .01) in the hour preceding their own feeding time compared to other observation periods, corroborating the killifish findings in a freshwater species.
7. **Extinction and disruption effects:** Prolonged mismatch between light and feeding cues (e.g., extended pairing then dissociation) led to gradual disappearance of the pre-feeding peak in the light period, and fish became startled/refused food when feeding timing was substantially altered — indicating the response is a learned/conditioned association subject to extinction, not a fixed innate rhythm.
8. **Distinction from true circadian rhythms:** The authors emphasize that, unlike innate circadian rhythms (per Pittendrigh 1960), the pre-feeding activity rhythm is **learned** via conditioning of the feeding act to an endogenous, entrainable cue — comparable to time-associated learning documented in bees (Beling 1929), birds (Wagner 1956), and rats (Reid & Finger 1955).
9. **No unequivocal fish circadian rhythm demonstrated:** The paper notes that despite clear evidence of an internal "clock" mechanism (via phase-shift experiments), no study up to that point had unequivocally demonstrated a true innate circadian rhythm in fish; learned rhythms like pre-feeding activity are proposed as a useful experimental tool for studying time-coordinated behavior in fishes.
