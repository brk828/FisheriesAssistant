# Observations on the Biology of Tilapia variabilis in Northern Lake Victoria

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by Geoffrey Fryer, of the [[East African Fisheries Research Organisation]] at Jinja, gives a preliminary account of the biology of *Tilapia variabilis* (a cichlid, often called [[Tilapia]]) in the northern waters of [[Lake Victoria]]. Most observations were made near Jinja. The species is mouth-brooding, uses rocky shore nurseries, disperses over modest distances, and shows homing to brooding grounds. It is ecologically separated from *T. esculenta* but faces potential competition from the introduced *T. zillii*. Growth is very slow, and commercial fishing with finer gill-net mesh has depleted the accumulated stock.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and context:** Two *Tilapia* species are native to Lake Victoria: *T. esculenta* and *T. variabilis*. *T. esculenta* has received most study because of its economic importance (Graham 1929; Lowe [McConnell] 1956; Garrod 1959). *T. variabilis* has gained economic importance since relaxation of net-size regulations.
- **Life cycle and habitat by size:** Habitat use changes through development. Juveniles of 15 to 30 mm occur in very shallow water over rock. Fish of 45 to 80 mm frequent littoral habitats. Fish of 80 to 170 mm form shoals. Adults of 200 to 280+ mm use varied habitats, with sandy substrates for spawning and rocky substrates for brooding.
- **Breeding:** Males build sandscrape nests on sandy substrate. Females take up fertilised eggs into the mouth, then move to rocky brooding grounds.
- **Diet:** Adults are essentially deposit feeders, taking algae from the bottom with sand grains in the gut. Newly released young browse rock-surface algae, with some chironomid larvae, rotifers and chydorid cladocerans. Planktonic food is considered negligible.
- **Colour polymorphism:** The "maradadi" orange-and-black pattern, mostly in females, occurs at different frequencies in different localities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Brooding grounds and nurseries:** Brooding grounds are sheltered inshore areas with gently lakeward-tilting flat rock slabs, often about 15 m of shoreline. Exposed beaches were unsuitable. Nurseries are the extreme shallow lake margin, where water is often under 2 cm deep. Predators such as the [[Pied Kingfisher]] cannot hunt effectively there, which gives the young near-freedom from predation. Steep rocky shores lack this shallow margin and were unused.
- **Dispersal and homing:** Fish left brooding grounds and travelled more than 60 miles in some cases, but most recaptures were within 15 miles and often under 5 miles, suggesting relatively localised populations. Evidence of [[Homing Behaviour]] to brooding grounds was recorded, with recaptures after 73 to 805 days. The author notes this may be the first recorded homing in the family Cichlidae.
- **Interspecific relationships:** *T. variabilis* and *T. esculenta* are ecologically separated during breeding. *T. esculenta* predominates in soft-bottomed, phytoplankton-rich gulfs and bays, while *T. variabilis* favours exposed shores. [[Tilapia zillii]], introduced into the lake, now colonises the nurseries. Juveniles of both species share food and habitat. Larger *T. zillii* dominate food in aquarium tests, and *T. zillii* has higher fecundity and faster growth.
- **Predators and parasites:** Man is the main predator of adults. Predation on young is low. Common parasites include *Trichodina* (protozoan), microsporidians, *Dactylosoma mariae* (in over 70% of fish examined), and a strigeid trematode metacercarial infection, which peaks at about 30 to 50 mm and may indicate mortality of heavily infected fish. Crustacean parasites include *Argulus africanus* and *Lernaea cyprinacea*.

## Key Findings

- **Breeding and brood size:** Breeding occurs throughout the year, with varying intensity. At least three broods can be produced within about eight months, and the author estimates about 200 young reared to release per brood. One brood contained 513 eggs in a 25.1 cm female.
- **Maturity:** Breeding females are usually over 20 cm, with a peak among fish of 23 to 24 cm. More than 63% of brooding females measured 23.0 to 25.4 cm.
- **Sex ratio:** Males preponderate among fish above about 22 cm. In gill-net samples, males made up 64% of the catch at 4 in mesh, 75% at 4½ in, and 79% at 5 in mesh (Table 1, 1956–58).
- **Growth:** Growth is remarkably slow for a tropical fish. Fish reach about 20 cm in around 3 years, and a 26 cm fish is about 8 years old. A 23.3 cm female recaptured after 805 days had not grown.
- **Fishery:** Gill nets of 4½ in mesh, introduced in 1956, initially raised catches. Catch per net then declined sharply in 1959 and 1960, with the 1960 catch less than half that of 1958. The author recommends discouraging gill nets below 4½ in mesh. The sustainable yield is expected to be very low, at no more than about 0.3 fish per net at Jinja.
- **Competition:** Competition with *T. zillii* is considered probable, and could threaten the species' survival. *T. zillii* made up as much as 34% of *T. variabilis* numbers in some months at the Masese landing, and in one habitat 77 *T. zillii* were caught against 52 *T. variabilis* (January–February 1960).
- **Conservation message:** The author cautions that the introduction of *T. zillii* was made without foreseeing the competition now observed.
