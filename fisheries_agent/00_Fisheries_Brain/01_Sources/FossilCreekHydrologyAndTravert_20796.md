# Fossil Creek Hydrology and Travertine Geomorphology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-hydrology #travertine-geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This technical report, prepared by W.L. Bouchard & Associates for [[Arizona Public Service Company]] (APS), supports the relicensing of the [[Childs and Irving Hydroelectric Project]] (FERC Project No. 2069-003) on [[Fossil Creek]], a tributary of the [[Verde River]]. The core question is how changes in flow, driven by hydropower withdrawals and minimum flow proposals, affect [[Travertine Deposition]] and, through it, stream morphology and aquatic habitat. The report synthesizes hydrology, flood history, water chemistry, and travertine science, and concludes that travertine deposition will increase under any increased-flow alternative, but its rate and distribution cannot be reliably predicted. The authors recommend monitoring over several years as the only way to resolve whether travertine benefits or harms aquatic resources.

## Background & Description

Fossil Creek is a "flashy" mountain stream draining the Mogollon Rim area, ranging from roughly 7,260 ft at its headwaters to 2,550 ft at the Verde River confluence. Its base flow comes from [[Fossil Springs]], which discharge a constant 43 cubic feet per second (cfs) at 71.6 °F year-round from Naco Formation limestone. Springs are supersaturated with calcium carbonate and carbon dioxide, which drives travertine formation.

The hydropower system, owned and operated by APS, diverts the base flow about 0.2 miles below the springs. The conveyance includes:

- A diversion at [[Irving Reach]] feeding a penstock of about 3,278 ft and a concrete-lined flume of roughly 10,300 ft.
- A Childs flume and inverted siphon, totaling about 23,190 ft from the Irving intake to [[Stehr Lake]].
- Stehr Lake, originally 27.5 acres and now holding about 100 acre-feet after 89 years of sediment accumulation.
- The Childs penstock (about 4,800 ft), with tailraces discharging to the Verde River roughly 3 miles upstream of the Fossil Creek confluence.

The project's infrastructure is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and recognized as a National Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark.

Climate is hot-summer, mild-winter, with average annual precipitation of 18.1 in (Childs, records since 1915) and 19.9 in (Irving, since 1951). About half falls November through March, with much of the rest in July and August thunderstorms. Because of high evapotranspiration, less than 2% of annual runoff occurs in August, the wettest month.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime.** No gauging records exist above Fossil Springs. FERC estimated flow duration using neighboring watersheds (Wet Beaver, West Clear, Wet Bottom, Red Tank Draw, and Dry Beaver creeks). Springs supply about 77% of watershed yield above Irving, and runoff about 23%. Flows exceed the 43 cfs base flow roughly 23% of the time, mainly during snowmelt and thunderstorms. Reports from plant staff indicate continuous flow throughout the year, sustained by precipitation, a 2 cfs return at Irving, and about 0.2 cfs underflow at the springs dam. This flow supports a diverse riparian and aquatic community.

**Geomorphology.** The channel gradient is steep (given as "210 feet per meter" in the source, likely a transcription error), and the floodplain is confined by cliffs. The streambed is mainly large cobble and boulder with some bedrock. The USFS General Ecosystem Survey rates erosion hazard as severe. Shading is limited except where riparian broadleaf forest borders the channel, and mature trees are largely absent.

**Flood history.** Fossil Creek floods overtop low-flow banks about every other year. Four floods exceeding the 30-year recurrence interval are preserved in the geologic record over the past 100 years, and at least one exceeded the 100-year flow. The most recent 100-year event occurred in January and February 1993. In March 1991, a rainstorm of 5.35 to 5.87 inches over three days produced a peak near 7,000 cfs.

**Water chemistry.** ADEQ's 1996 Arizona Water Quality Assessment rated Fossil Creek (reach 15060203-024) as "full support" for aquatic and wildlife use (warm-water fishery), full-body contact, and agricultural uses, with no significant contaminant findings. APS sampling in November 1989 and May 1990 recorded:

- Dissolved oxygen of about 6.6 to 10.4 ppm, with lower values at Fossil Creek and the Childs effluent.
- pH of 6.0 to 8.19. Fossil Springs was 7.53, Stehr Lake inlet 7.73, outlet 7.84, and the Childs outfall 8.19 (June 1990).
- Conductivity of 62 to 76 µmhos/cm and temperatures of 51 to 67 °F in November 1989.
- Trace parameters above detection limits: barium (~0.15 mg/l), sulfides, nitrate (0.09 to 0.14 mg/l), Kjeldahl nitrogen, coliform (>16 colonies), and turbidity (0.23 to 0.59 NTU).

**Fish and aquatic habitat.** Fish are discussed mainly through habitat modeling and agency concern. The Arizona Game and Fish Department worried that travertine could block fish movement, cement the substrate (reducing invertebrate habitat), and reduce clean spawning gravel. FERC's Instream Flow Incremental Methodology (IFIM) analysis found that 43 cfs gave the least weighted usable area (WUA) for all life stages of all species studied, except adult and juvenile desert sucker. Pool-dwelling species' WUA generally declined as flows rose above existing conditions. Wetted-perimeter analysis indicated about 16 cfs maximized macroinvertebrate habitat.

## Key Findings

**1. Flow allocation and minimum flow proposal**
- APS proposed (1992) minimum releases of 10 cfs in the Irving Reach and 5 cfs in the [[Childs Reach]] as part of relicensing. FERC staff recommended accepting this with an interdisciplinary team (USFWS, AZGFD, USFS). Releases would begin within one year of license issuance and continue for the 30-year license term.
- The "no action" baseline, continuing current operations, is the environmental reference against which other alternatives are judged.
- Full decommissioning would return the 43 cfs base flow to the creek. Estimated plant decommissioning cost is about $19 million. A partial decommissioning option (43 cfs Irving, 5 cfs Childs) was judged infeasible for economic operation.

**2. Flood power and channel stability**
- Floods exceeding the 5-year interval may reach 8 to 10 fps. The 100-year velocity exceeds 15 fps.
- [[Loomis Flood Analysis]] (1994) estimated peak flows (cfs) for overgrazed, current, and natural watershed conditions:

| Recurrence | Overgrazed | Current | Natural |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5-year | 2,403 | 2,257 | 2,089 |
| 10-year | 3,951 | 3,737 | 3,499 |
| 25-year | 6,360 | 6,034 | 5,668 |
| 50-year | 9,438 | 8,998 | 8,510 |
| 100-year | 14,099 | 13,531 | 12,867 |

- Largest bed particles moved were about 10.5 to 11.0 inches for a 10-year flood and 15.8 to 16.4 inches for a 100-year flood. Erosion potential was high under all three watershed conditions.

**3. Travertine history**
- Historical accounts from [[Charles F. Lummis]] (1891) and [[F.W. Chamberlain]] (1904) describe large travertine dams and deep pools in the Irving Reach. These were destroyed by floods made worse by overgrazing before the project was built.
- [[Overby and Neary]] (1996) counted a minimum of 81 sets of relict travertine dams, mostly near channel nick points. The report notes this count is unverified.
- Today's deposition is reduced by diversion, flooding, and grazing, but is still occurring on project flumes and in the creek below Irving.
- The historical record suggests little or no travertine in the Childs Reach, because calcium carbonate deposits mainly in the upper reaches.

**4. Competing deposition estimates**
- APS (1992) estimated about 175 lb/day per cfs precipitating between the springs and the Stehr Lake inlet (about 32 tons per year per cfs for a 7.53 to 7.73 pH change). At 43 cfs this equals more than 1,375 tons/year (7,534 lb/day). Spread evenly over 4 miles, this would produce about 0.29 inch/year of accumulation.
- [[Malusa Thesis]] (1997, NAU) estimated 11,951 kg/day, or 26,347 lb/day, for the Irving Reach at full base flow, based on a March 1996 month with all 43 cfs flowing through the reach. That event produced travertine accumulation of 1 foot in one month, incorporating woody debris.
- The report stresses both estimates are gross, based on limited data, and unlikely to match actual deposition. The difference is about 3.5 times.

**5. FERC's conclusions**
- Travertine deposition could increase under any increased-flow alternative, including decommissioning. Its amount and development cannot be predicted effectively, so monitoring is required.
- Decommissioning would restart the pre-project cycle of travertine formation and destruction in the Irving Reach. The Irving Reach would likely shift from riffle/run to step/pool, improving low-flow cover and high-flow refuge.
- Potential benefits include more habitat complexity, more macroinvertebrate habitat, and higher visual and recreational value. Potential harms include cementing the substrate, creating barriers to fish movement, and reducing spawning gravel. Higher flows could also increase recreational damage to riparian zones, vandalism of historic facilities, and disturbance of archaeological sites.

**6. Bouchard's conclusions**
- Travertine deposition will increase under any increased-flow alternative. Flood erosion will strongly alter deposits, so deposition will vary in quantity and location through the Irving Reach.
- Whether more travertine benefits or harms aquatic resources can only be answered by releasing higher flows and monitoring for several years.
- The report is intended as background for the licensing process, not as a final prediction.
