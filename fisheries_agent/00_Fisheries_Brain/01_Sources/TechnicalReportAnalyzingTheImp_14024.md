# Technical report analyzing the impact of the Allen- Warner Valley energy system on the native fishes of the Virgin River

## Executive Summary

This 1977 technical report, prepared for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service by Dr. James E. Deacon and Dr. Paul B. Holden, assesses the anticipated ecological impacts of the proposed **Allen-Warner Valley Energy System (A.W.V.E.S.)** on three rare, endemic fishes of the [[Virgin River]]: the [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), the [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*), and the [[Virgin River Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*). The authors conclude that the project — primarily through **reduced winter streamflow, increased sedimentation, elevated salinity, and facilitation of exotic fish introductions** — will significantly and adversely impact these species, particularly the Woundfin and Roundtail Chub, increasing their risk of extinction. The report recommends mitigation measures but notes that under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, the project as proposed likely could not be approved due to unavoidable adverse effects on an endangered species and its essential habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Study purpose:** Environmental impact analysis submitted under Contract No. 14-16-0002-77-901 with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, evaluating a proposed energy diversion/reservoir system (including the **Warner Valley Reservoir**) affecting the Virgin River mainstream from the Hurricane Diversion downstream to Lake Mead.
- **Focal species:**
  - **[[Woundfin]]** — listed as *Endangered* by the U.S. Department of the Interior (1972); protected in Arizona, Utah, and Nevada. Restricted entirely to the Virgin River system. The river from La Verkin Springs to Lake Mead is proposed **Essential/Critical Habitat**.
  - **[[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]]** — pending addition to the federal Endangered Species list at time of writing; protected in Arizona only. Considered by Cross (1975) the **most threatened** of the three species.
  - **[[Virgin River Spinedace]]** — protected in Arizona, Utah, and Nevada; primarily occurs outside the impact zone (above La Verkin Springs to Zion Canyon Narrows).
- **Reference ecological study:** Cross, J.N. (1975) — unpublished M.S. thesis, University of Nevada, Las Vegas, describing ecological distribution of Virgin River fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat & Physical Preferences

| [[Woundfin]] | Runs | 43 cm | 0.42 m/s | Sand | Tolerant to 12,000 µmhos/cm | LT50 = 34.5°C (acclimated 21°C); >30°C likely unsuitable long-term |
| [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] | Pools & deep runs, with cover | 67 cm | 0.34 m/s | Sand + cover, near riffles | Tolerant to 12,000 µmhos/cm but prefers <4,000 µmhos/cm | Stress observed at 34°C; requires <30°C |

### Feeding Ecology
- **Woundfin & Roundtail Chub:** Omnivorous, opportunistic feeders — consume both aquatic insects and algae.
- **Virgin River Spinedace:** Largely insectivorous, feeding on both aquatic and terrestrial insects.

### Stream Zonation (Impact Areas)
The affected river was divided into three management-relevant zones:

- **Area I** (Hurricane Diversion → Washington Diversion at Virgin anticline):
  - Upper section: narrow gorge, low summer flows from irrigation/power diversions, few fish.
  - Middle section (La Verkin Springs–power plant): highly saline (12,000 µmhos/cm), hot (spring source 41.5°C), oxygen-poor (<1 ppm), mineral-cemented substrate; fish restricted to lower 300 m during low flow.
  - Lower section: relatively unaltered, most typical Virgin River habitat.
- **Area II** (Washington Diversion → Virgin River Narrows): Intermittent in summer; fish persist in isolated pools (>30°C possible); heavily impacted by irrigation withdrawal; considered marginal native habitat vulnerable to exotics (e.g., [[Red Shiner]], [[Green Sunfish]]).
- **Area III** (Narrows → Lake Mead): Upper part (Narrows–Mesquite Diversion) relatively unaltered with good native populations but more exotics present (proximity to Lake Mead/farm ponds). Below Mesquite Diversion, flows are minimal/intermittent and fish assemblages are **dominated by introduced [[Red Shiner]]**.

### Exotic Species Threats
- **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Notropis lutrensis*) — already dominant below Mesquite Diversion; likely to spread via bait-bucket release; demonstrated to displace young/juvenile native fishes in the Escalante River (Holden 1977) under marginal habitat conditions.
- **[[Green Sunfish]]** (*Lepomis cyanellus*) — already present near Washington, Utah; expected to expand with reservoir construction.
- **[[Golden Shiner]]** and **[[Redside Shiner]]** — cited as precedent examples of bait-bucket introductions following Gunlock Reservoir construction on the Santa Clara River.

## Key Findings

### Projected Hydrological Changes
- **Area I:** Winter flows reduced by ~50%, approaching present summer flow levels; summer flows largely unchanged; peak scouring flows expected to persist (critical for maintaining roundtail chub pool habitat).
- **Area II:** Summer flow remains intermittent; winter flow becomes intermittent (previously perennial), especially in low-water years.
- **Area III:** Summer flows ~60–80 c.f.s. (similar to present); winter flows reduced from 150–200 c.f.s. to 70–100 c.f.s.

### Predicted Impacts by Area

**Area I**
1. Reduced winter flow → minor habitat loss (fish populations tied more to summer habitat availability).
2. **Increased sediment load** (only 25–35% of suspended solids removed at desilting works) → channel aggradation, reduced riffle quality/food production → **major impact**, especially on Roundtail Chub.
3. **Increased winter salinity** in the La Verkin Springs–power plant reach (year-round 9,000–12,000 µmhos/cm) → **major impact**, effectively excluding fish from this reach.
4. Elevated exotic fish introduction risk from Warner Valley Reservoir → potential major impact on all three rare species.

**Area II**
1. Loss of winter flow → fish confined to intermittent pools → **increased predation, disease, and crowding-related mortality** (major impact on Woundfin & Roundtail Chub, most severe near Bloomington, UT).
2. **Winter ice formation** on isolated pools → risk of oxygen depletion → major impact.
3. Elevated risk of [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Red Shiner]] establishment due to proximity to Warner Valley Reservoir → potentially major impact, mirroring displacement seen in Area III below Mesquite Diversion.

**Area III**
1. Loss of winter flow/habitat → minor direct impact.
2. Reduced winter flows may enhance competitive advantage of Red Shiner over native young-of-year fish → potential major impact (precedent: Escalante River displacement, Holden 1977).

### Physiological Notes
- Woundfin osmotic/respiration studies indicate **no significant physiological stress** below ~4,000 µmhos/cm (expected salinity below the power plant post-project).
- Roundtail Chub distributional data suggest a **preference threshold of <4,000 µmhos/cm** despite higher tolerance limits.

### Mitigation Measures Proposed
1. Removal of sediment accumulated at control structures (addresses Area I sedimentation impact).
2. **Desalinization of La Verkin Springs** to restore ~4,000 µmhos/cm conductivity in the middle section except during peak flows.
3. Ongoing monitoring of physical habitat parameters (substrate particle size, pool depth/frequency, riffle extent, turbidity, TDS).
4. Monitoring of biological habitat components (food availability, species associations).
5. Delay in early-year water diversion to postpone adverse impacts until municipal demand increases.

### Regulatory/Legal Conclusion
- Authors interpret the **Endangered Species Act of 1973** (Section 7) as precluding project approval given unavoidable adverse effects on the Woundfin (an already-listed Endangered species) and its essential habitat.
- Adverse impacts on winter flows and exotic species competition are deemed **unavoidable** under the proposed action across all three areas.
- Population declines are projected to occur fastest in **Area II** (within 1–2 years), more gradually in Areas I and III.

### Alternatives Considered
- **No Action Alternative:** Eliminates direct project-related impacts but does not eliminate background risk of exotic species introduction (though at substantially lower probability than under the proposed action).
