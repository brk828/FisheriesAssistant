# Calculating growth of channel catfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Kermit E. Sneed]] (Fisheries Research Laboratory, Norman, Oklahoma) tests whether annual rings on the left pectoral spines of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus lacustris punctatus*) can be used to age fish and calculate their growth in length. Spines were collected from 204 fish taken by [[Rotenone Sampling]] in [[Grand Lake]], Oklahoma, in 1949. Spine-radius measurements correlated strongly with body length (r = 0.969 after removing the 17 largest fish). Spine-based ages were supported by consistency with empirical length data and by 50 fish of known age. The method is presented as a simpler alternative to [[Vertebrae Aging]], which is tedious to prepare.

## Background & Description

- **Organism:** Channel catfish, a member of the family [[Ictaluridae]] (North American catfishes).
- **Structure used:** The left pectoral spine, removed with minimal tissue and needing no preservation (formalin was avoided because it cracks bone and obscures year marks).
- **Preparation:** Spines were cut in cross-section near the base (articulating portion) at a groove end point, then ground and polished, or cut as thin wafers on a sliding-platform saw that can be read in water without grinding.
- **Measurement:** A micrometer with a movable cross-hair on a binocular microscope measured spine radius from the lumen center to each annulus. Values were multiplied by 100 for computation.
- **Background literature:** The paper reviews age determination from [[Scales]], otoliths, spines and vertebrae, including work on [[Sand Flounder]], [[Sturgeon]], [[Madtom]], [[Northern Black Bullhead]] and [[Yellow Stonecat]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Growth patterns were compared with reservoir dynamics in Oklahoma impoundments. Early-impoundment growth was typically highest and then declined, as seen in [[White Bass]] in Lake Duncan, [[River Carpsucker]] in the Great Salt Plains reservoir, carp and bass in Grand Lake, and [[White Crappie]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] in [[Lake Texoma]].
- The author links this to [[Lee's Phenomenon]], which describes declining growth with later year classes in scaled fish. The catfish results ran opposite to that pattern, with early year classes growing faster.
- Growth rate showed no significant variation in annual increment up to the eighth year of life, contrasting with many scaled fishes that slow after sexual maturity.

## Key Findings

- **Sample:** 204 channel catfish from Grand Lake (1949), treated as a random sample of the catch.
- **Body length–spine radius regression:**
  - All 204 fish: slope 1.41, intercept −23.10 mm, r = 0.951.
  - 187 fish (17 largest omitted, 8.3% of total): slope 1.27, intercept −3.24 mm, r = 0.969.
  - The near-zero intercept of the 187-fish line justified growth calculation by direct proportion. The large-fish deviation may reflect poor sampling of large fish, sex-related spine differences, or a curvilinear relationship.
- **Validation:**
  - Calculated length at age II slightly exceeded the measured length of age-group I at capture; calculated length at age III slightly exceeded measured length of age-group II captured in August. This held for every age group except age-group V, which had an inadequate sample.
  - Average length increased with spine ring count, supporting systematic ring addition.
  - Spines from 50 fish of known age (25 aged 1 year, 25 aged 2 years, from the [[Holdenville Hatchery]]) matched the Grand Lake markings. Formalin caused cracking, but the year marks remained readable.
- **Growth:** Grand average calculated lengths at annulus formation were derived from 188 fish, with year classes from 1941 to 1949. The 1945 year class (age-group IV) grew faster than others. Calculated growth at ages II–IV was greater in older fish.
- **Comparison with other structures:** Correlation between length and scale radius was 0.858 for 967 [[White Crappie]] and 0.870 for 182 black bass in Oklahoma. The author suggests vertebrae may give higher correlation but are less practical for management studies.
- **Conclusion:** Pectoral spines provide an accurate, practical method for aging channel catfish and calculating growth in length, suitable for fisheries management.
