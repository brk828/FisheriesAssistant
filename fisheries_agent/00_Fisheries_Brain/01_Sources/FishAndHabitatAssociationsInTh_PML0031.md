# Habitat Availability and Use by Fish in the Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared for the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], analyzes fish and macrohabitat data collected from 1995 to 1999 on the [[Salt River]], [[Gila River]], and [[San Pedro River]] in the [[Gila River Basin]], Arizona. The work was motivated by the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), whose canal network could transport non-native fishes into the basin. It has three goals: (1) describe macrohabitat availability and its variability, (2) describe how common fish species use particular habitats, and (3) examine associations among fish species. The report also recommends how monitoring could be stratified by habitat and how future 5-year analyses could test for links between habitat change and fish abundance.

## Background & Description

The CAP was completed in 1993. A 1994 [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion concluded that CAP water transport would jeopardize four threatened or endangered fishes: [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Reasonable and Prudent Alternative No. 2 required the Bureau of Reclamation to develop a monitoring plan to determine baseline community composition, monitor non-native impacts in the Gila and San Pedro Rivers, and describe movement of non-natives from the CAP canals.

A [[Power Analysis]] of the draft monitoring protocol (Allison 2000) indicated that counts were too variable. The author proposed stratifying counts by macrohabitat to reduce between-habitat variability, on the reasoning that species with non-random habitat use will respond to changes in habitat availability.

The protocol uses single-pass [[Electrofishing]] at three 200 m stations per reach, with macrohabitats (pools, riffles, runs, and isolated pools) as sampling units. Eight reaches were sampled: three on the San Pedro (reaches 1–3), four on the Gila (reaches 4–7), and one on the Salt (reach 8). Fish were identified to species and age class. Table 1 of the report lists 24 species or taxa and their acronyms; the 12 species analyzed for habitat use were those found in at least 5% of macrohabitats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime.** [[Flow Discharge]] was estimated from four [[U.S. Geological Survey]] gages (Charleston on the San Pedro; below Coolidge Dam and at Kelvin on the Gila; below Stewart Mountain Dam on the Salt). Sampling took place from October through December, centered near the end of the first week of November. On the Salt and Gila, this period coincides with interrupted flows from upstream reservoirs. Collections coincided with the end of each river's annual high-flow period.

**Basin modifications.** The author notes that dam regulation, water diversion, groundwater pumping, and land management can alter flows. Groundwater pumping and diversion could reduce flows over time.

**Sampling caveats.** Station length was a source of uncertainty: [[Lyons]] (1992) recommended a length of 35 times stream width, while [[Paller]] (1995) recommended 100 to 450 times width. In 1995, 400 m stations were tested on the San Pedro, but this was not continued. Macrohabitat measurements were not made on the Salt River in 1995 and 1996, and no macrohabitat measurements exist for the Gila in 1996 beyond a single station.

**Substrate.** Substrate was classified into seven size categories, from clay to bedrock, and was analyzed as an approximately multiplicative variable (Table 2).

## Key Findings

**Macrohabitat availability**

- A total of 379 macrohabitats were visited, and 364 were sampled. Of these, 37 had no fish. Data from 367 macrohabitats with measured length comprised 108 pools, 119 riffles, 133 runs, and 7 isolated pools.
- Macrohabitat frequency differed among rivers (χ² test, p < 0.0005). The Salt River had relatively fewer runs and many more pools, while the San Pedro had fewer pools.
- Within rivers, the relative frequency of macrohabitat types was consistent across years (p = 0.287 for San Pedro, 0.466 for Gila, and 1.000 for Salt).
- The Friedman's tests for reach consistency had limited power, with only 1, 3, or 4 reaches per river and five years of data. Within years, reaches were largely consistent, with one of ten year-by-river tests below p = 0.15.

**Macrohabitat dimensions**

- Pools were wider and deeper than riffles or runs. Riffles were shorter than pools and runs and shallower than runs.
- Salt River pools were longer, deeper, and wider than those on the other rivers. Salt River riffles were wider than Gila or San Pedro riffles but much shorter.
- Depth patterns across years differed among rivers. For example, 1999 was the shallowest year for Gila visits but moderate for the Salt and San Pedro, indicating significant river-by-year interactions.

**Effects of flow discharge**

- A tenfold increase in discharge was associated with a 56% increase in pool depth and a substrate diameter about one-fifth as large.
- Riffles increased in depth by 27% and in width by almost 3 m, regardless of river.
- Runs increased in length by 88%. Run width and substrate responses varied by river.
- The number of pools decreased with higher flows, and the number of riffles increased.

**Habitat use by fish (logistic regression)**

- Species occurrence was modeled for the 12 common species using river, macrohabitat type, length, depth, width, and substrate.
- Three native species (AGCH, CAIN, PACL) had reduced odds of occurring in pools. None of the non-natives had higher odds of occurring in non-pool habitats, and none had detectably increased odds in riffles.
- Longfin dace (AGCH) were associated with larger substrates. Sonora sucker (CAIN) and desert sucker (PACL) were associated with deeper macrohabitats, and CAIN was also associated with wider macrohabitats.
- Largemouth bass (MISA) and other non-natives were the main species that did not show these patterns. The report notes that conclusions from logistic models sometimes contrast with raw use data because the models account for available but unused macrohabitats.
- Some relationships were modeled as linear, and the author acknowledged that six of 48 relationships may be curvilinear.

**Canonical correspondence analysis (CCA)**

- The CCA used 232 macrohabitats with complete measurements. The full model explained 18.8% of total variance on Axis 1 but had a species-environment correlation of 0.90.
- Axis 1 was driven mainly by Reach 2 (middle San Pedro), with shallow, narrow, non-pool samples. Axis 2 was driven mainly by Reach 1 (upper San Pedro). Axis 3 was associated with Reach 8 (Salt River), pools, and wide, deep habitats.
- The CCA indicated that reach information explained much of the assemblage pattern, and macrohabitat measurements contributed little beyond reach and habitat type.
- Longfin dace occurred in 7 of 8 reaches, but samples with high numbers of AGCH had distinct, shallow, narrow characteristics typical of Reach 2.

**Associations between fish species**

- Of six statistically significant pairwise correlations involving AGCH, five were negative, more than any other species. AMME and PIPR also had many negative associations, though most were not significant.
- Most other significant correlations were positive, such as CAIN with AMNA (0.45) and CYCA with PACL (0.46).
- Relative risk analyses indicated that common species predicted the presence of some uncommon species, such as PYOL and PONI, but small sample sizes limited the strength of these inferences.

**Recommendations for stratified monitoring**

- Seven species showed significant associations with particular habitat types. Stratifying counts by habitat reduced the coefficient of variation by at least 11% for many species.
- Because the species with improved variance estimates were not those for which trends had been judged difficult to detect, the author concludes that stratification is unlikely to improve trend estimates.

**Proposed future analyses**

- Analyses should be conducted at the river level as well as the reach level, because flow history operates at both scales.
- Separate models are proposed for species counts, discharge, and macrohabitat type and dimensions, all log-transformed and expressed as percentage change over time.
- Flow data should be analyzed for the full year and for seasons, not only the low-flow fall period, to capture the timing of habitat changes that might matter to fish.
- The report acknowledges that habitat availability in the sampled season may not reflect habitat during other seasons or hydro-periods, and that observed correlations cannot establish causation.
