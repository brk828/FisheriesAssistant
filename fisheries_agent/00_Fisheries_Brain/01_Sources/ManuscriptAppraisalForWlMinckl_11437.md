# Battle Against Extinction: Native Fish Management in the American West (Manuscript Review and Editorial Correspondence)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This bundle of documents concerns the forthcoming volume *Battle Against Extinction: Native Fish Management in the American West*, edited by [[W.L. Minckley]] and [[James E. Deacon]] for the [[University of Arizona Press]]. The volume is a symposium-derived compilation on declines and extinctions of western North American fishes and on the management tools and policies meant to reverse them. The material includes two confidential manuscript appraisals (dated 29 October 1989), a cover letter to the acquiring editor, chapter-level review and acceptance letters, a progress memo, and chapter-development correspondence from 1987 to 1989.

Both reviewers recommend publication. The first calls the work the most comprehensive treatment of its subject yet attempted and says it should be used in advanced conservation and management courses. The second, on a structured review form, recommends publication as a good work, rates documentation as excellent, and notes that some conclusions are not fully supported by data and that the book takes an advocacy position. Only minor editing is required. The correspondence shows the book was delivered for technical review on 28 September 1989 and slipped to a 1990 publication date.

## Background & Description

The volume is organized into seven sections, as summarized by the first reviewer:

- **Section I:** Largely historical background.
- **Section II:** Human impacts on the western ichthyofauna and how organization, conservation efforts, and litigation can alter these trends.
- **Section III:** Ethics, political approaches, and ways to save these fishes. It includes Chapter 8, a philosophical argument for protecting resources that most people rarely see.
- **Section IV:** Genetics and management options and techniques.
- **Sections V and VI:** Contrast of life history strategies and conservation management for short-lived and long-lived western fishes, including past mistakes and open questions.
- **Section VII:** Editors' synthesis, which links fish conservation to wider biodiversity loss and explains why conservation has not progressed fast enough.

The second reviewer's form describes the manuscript's thesis as the history of actions and problems in preserving native species, the decline of native species as a result of development of western water basins, and the need for a "new land ethic." It also includes a species-by-species appraisal of status and recovery potential. The reviewer identified the work as the only existing compilation of the logic, rationale, and assumptions guiding endangered southwestern fish conservation over several decades. Most earlier treatment of the subject was unpublished, poorly documented, or not critically evaluated.

Contributing chapters and authors named in the correspondence include:

- A design-of-preserves-and-refuges chapter by [[Peter B. Moyle]] and Georgina M. Sato, accepted in 1989 after review by two external scientists and the editors.
- A chapter on refugia (including a [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] refugia analysis) by [[Gary E. Williams]], revised in 1988 and 1989 to include [[Baugh and Deacon]] (1988), [[Hendrickson and Varela Romero]] (1989) on [[Desert Pupfish]] conservation status, and [[Simons et al.]] (1989) on [[Gila Topminnow]] recovery.
- A chapter on the [[Colorado River Fisheries Project]] by Richard S. Wydoski with John Hamill, planned at 15 to 20 manuscript pages.
- An outline from 1987 for a refugia chapter with three case-study refugia and an appendix listing western fish refugia.

The volume is intended for fishery scientists, resource managers, historians, conservation organizations, and policymakers, especially in the American West. The first reviewer also argues that its lessons apply outside the region.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The documents identify the following threats and themes:

- **Habitat alteration:** Western water development, with habitat modification and alteration as the central driver of fish imperilment. The correspondence refers to fishes in deserts as a "paradox" that has received too little attention.
- **Nonnative species:** Introduced fishes are a major concern. The first reviewer notes that [[Spencer Fullerton Baird]], as U.S. Fish Commissioner, was largely responsible for many importations and introductions of nonnative fishes, some of which now prey on or otherwise harm native species. The reviewer suggests that this should be stated in Chapter 1, alongside Baird's taxonomic contributions.
- **Refugia and range fragmentation:** Refuge size, habitat fragmentation, and maintenance needs are treated as core conservation concepts. The Devils Hole pupfish, desert pupfish, and Gila topminnow are used as examples of refugia-dependent species.
- **Gray literature:** Much of the relevant information sits in agency files and unpublished reports. The volume's literature review is valued for pulling this material together.

## Key Findings

**Assessment of the manuscript (first appraisal):**
- The work is "the most comprehensive treatment on this topic ever attempted," and the editors are commended for choosing topics and authors.
- Coverage, organization, coherence, and logic are excellent. Literary style varies because of multiple authors, and some chapters need tightening.
- Documentation, use of primary and secondary sources, original research, and illustrations and tables are rated excellent. Typographical errors remain.
- Editorial guidance includes avoiding sentence-initial "However" and hyphens after adverbs.
- Removing or adding chapters would undermine the book's purpose, so the reviewer sees no need for structural change.
- The reviewer expects the conclusions to remain valid, so no update edition is needed.
- The reviewer would add the book to a personal library and recommends publication.

**Assessment of the manuscript (second appraisal, structured form):**
- Recommendation: "I recommend publication" (the good-work option; the "outstanding work" option was not selected).
- Completeness of coverage: Good. Organization and style: vary by chapter (some excellent, others fair to good).
- Accuracy: Some conclusions are unsupported by data, though the work is generally good.
- Documentation: Excellent. Original research: limited new data, but good evaluation of published and unpublished material.
- Weaknesses: advocacy position, redundancy between chapters (described as difficult to extract), and assumptions accepted as fact that underpin recommendations.
- Suggested improvement: a more balanced treatment that highlights the difficult decisions in endangered species management. This would require substantial effort.
- Title: appropriate. Author credentials: sufficient. Revision needed: not required.

**Suggested additions and revisions:**
- Add to Chapter 1 and elsewhere a note on Baird's role in nonnative introductions and on the contribution of [[Rosa Smith Eigenmann]], who trained in ichthyology under [[David Starr Jordan]], became the first woman president of the [[American Association for the Advancement of Science]], and should be recognized for that.
- Add to Chapter 8 a call for molecular biologists and geneticists to join studies of these fishes. The reviewer notes that funding sources outside management agencies could support work on evolutionary processes.
- A marginal note quoted in the review reads: "If nongame is correct, then so is nonnative," a remark on how these categories are used in management.

**Publication and logistics:**
- The complete manuscript was delivered to the University of Arizona Press on 28 September 1989 for technical review.
- The book was expected in 1990, not 1989, because a June 1989 target was missed.
- Literature-cited queries were issued to all chapter authors, as the references are shared across the book and were a major task in themselves.
- Work was to be presented at the [[Desert Fishes Council]] 20th Anniversary Symposium, which the correspondence places in Death Valley, California (November 1989), with a later DFC meeting in Albuquerque also mentioned.
