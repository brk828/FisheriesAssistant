# Recent Collections of Less Common Fishes in Southern Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 paper by [[Gerald Gunning]] and William M. Lewis of Southern Illinois University, Carbondale, reports recent collections of 18 uncommon fish species from 38 localities in the 16 southernmost counties of [[Illinois]], gathered over the preceding five years. The authors document each species' geographic range, numerical abundance in [[Southern Illinois]], and distribution across the state. Several records are new for the area, and the paper concludes that most of these species are rare in the region. Southern Illinois lies within the range of ten of them, and marks the northern range limit for six southern species and the southern limit for one northern species, the [[Burbot]].

## Background & Description

The study covers fishes collected from streams, lakes, springs, strip-mine ponds, and swamps, so it represents a wide range of habitats. Collecting methods were:

- [[Electrofishing]] apparatus
- A 14-inch-mesh seine and a common minnow seine
- Wire basket traps and hand dipnets
- One sample taken by poisoning with emulsified [[Rotenone Sampling]]

The paper is limited to three characteristics of each species: geographic range, numerical abundance in southern Illinois, and distribution within Illinois as a whole. The authors also discuss how "range" should be understood. Following Allee and Schmidt (1951), they treat it as the area enclosed by the outermost localities where a species occurs, noting that species often occupy only special habitats within that area.

The paper accounts for 18 species, summarized below.

**Centrarchids (sunfishes and basses)**
- [[Rock Bass]]: rare in the south, rather abundant in the north; established population in [[Big Creek]] near Rosiclare.
- [[Round Sunfish]]: collected in [[Pine Hills Swamp]] and Brownville Creek near Thebes.
- [[Smallmouth Bass]]: common in the north, rare in the south; Big Creek near Rosiclare is the only known southern Illinois locality.
- [[Small Sunfish]]: collected at [[Wolf Lake]] and Pine Hills Swamp.
- [[Pygmy Sunfish]]: collected at Pine Hills Swamp and one spring-fed tributary.

**Catfishes, minnows, and others**
- [[Brown Bullhead]]: found at only two localities, Wolf Lake and Pine Hills Swamp.
- [[Spring Cavefish]]: relatively abundant in the Pine Hills area, but difficult to collect by day; it is active and easily collected at night.
- [[Brook Silverside]]: collected at Wolf Lake, Clear Creek, Fulton Creek, and [[Horseshoe Lake]].
- [[Lake Chubsucker]]: absent from large lakes; found at one strip-mine pond in the Pyatt area south of Pinckneyville.
- [[Silverjaw Minnow]]: known only from Big Kinkaid Creek near Ava.
- [[Suckermouth Minnow]]: collected in the [[Big Muddy River]] and Big Kinkaid Creek.
- [[Burbot]]: a single specimen from the Big Muddy River.

**Percids (perches, saugers, walleyes)**
- [[Sauger]]: two specimens from the Big Muddy River.
- [[Walleye]]: collected in the Big Muddy River.

**Darters**
- [[Bluntnose Darter]]: collected at the Big Muddy River and Worthen Bayou.
- [[Spot-tailed Darter]]: from Roaring Springs Creek near Anna and the Big Creek drainage.
- [[Stripe-tailed Darter]]: only from the Big Creek drainage.
- [[Swamp Darter]]: from Pine Hills Swamp, Sugar Creek, and Worthen Bayou; not abundant at any site.

Identifications of the spot-tailed and stripe-tailed darters were confirmed by Dr. Reeve M. Bailey of the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Southern Illinois sits at a biogeographic transition. Six of the species are southern forms whose northern range limits fall in or near the region: round sunfish, small sunfish, pygmy sunfish, spot-tailed darter, stripe-tailed darter, and swamp darter. None is abundant there. The burbot is a northern species whose southern limit lies in the area, and it is very rare. Ten species, including the brown bullhead, brook silverside, rock bass, and sauger, are within their ranges in the south, yet none is abundant there.

The sampled habitats include swamps, springs, strip-mine ponds, and large rivers and lakes. Spring cavefish use subterranean waters during the day, which affects how they can be sampled. The [[Pine Hills Swamp]] area supports several species, including spring cavefish, round sunfish, small sunfish, pygmy sunfish, and brown bullhead, and therefore appears to be an important refuge for uncommon fishes in the region.

Changes in abundance are also noted. [[Round Sunfish]] were formerly abundant in Horseshoe Lake (Thompson and Bennett, 1938) but had disappeared by the time of the study (Gunning, 1953). The bluntnose darter was reported as very abundant in southern Illinois by O'Donnell (1935), but recent collections did not show that abundance.

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** 18 uncommon fishes recorded from 38 localities in the 16 southernmost counties of Illinois over five years.
- **New records:** The brook silverside was recorded from four localities (Wolf Lake, Clear Creek, Fulton Creek, Horseshoe Lake) that earlier surveys by Forbes and Richardson (1920) and O'Donnell (1935) had not reported. The burbot (Big Muddy River) and walleye (Big Muddy River) were also new for those localities.
- **Rarity:** Most species are rare or uncommon. The lake chubsucker is known from one locality, the silverjaw minnow from one stream, and the burbot from one specimen.
- **Range boundaries:** Southern Illinois is near the northern limit of six southern species and the southern limit of the northern burbot.
- **Established populations:** Rock bass and smallmouth bass persist in Big Creek near Rosiclare, which is the main southern Illinois locality for both.
- **Declines:** Round sunfish have disappeared from Horseshoe Lake, and bluntnose darter abundance is lower than O'Donnell (1935) reported.
- **Taxonomy:** The pygmy sunfish was placed in Elassomidae by Jordan and Evermann (1896) but in Centrarchidae by Berg (1947). The swamp darter has been treated under several names, and its complex was revised by Hubbs and Cannon (1935).
- **Sampling note:** Spring cavefish are best collected at night because most of the population retreats to subterranean water during the day.
