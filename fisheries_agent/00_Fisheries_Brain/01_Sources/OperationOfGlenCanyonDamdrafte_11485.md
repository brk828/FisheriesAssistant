# Operation of Glen Canyon Dam: Draft Environmental Impact Statement Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #aquatic-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This draft environmental impact statement (EIS), prepared by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] as lead agency with eleven cooperating agencies and tribes, evaluates the downstream effects of operating [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on the Colorado River corridor from [[Lake Powell]] through [[Grand Canyon National Park]] to [[Lake Mead]]. It compares a No Action baseline (1963 to 1990 operations) with nine alternatives: two unrestricted fluctuating-flow options, four restricted fluctuating-flow options, and three steady-flow options. The preferred option is the Modified Low Fluctuating Flow Alternative. The EIS was prepared under [[National Environmental Policy Act]] requirements, with comments due April 11, 1994. The Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1992 required a final EIS by October 30, 1994.

The core finding is that dam operations, through daily fluctuations and cold, clear releases, have reshaped the downstream ecosystem. Restricting daily fluctuations and adding habitat maintenance flows are presented as the main means to protect sediment, vegetation, wildlife, recreation, and cultural resources, while accepting trade-offs in hydropower.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and authority.** The Secretary of the Interior called for a reevaluation of dam operations to minimize, consistent with law, adverse impacts on downstream environmental and cultural resources and Native American interests. Authority derives from the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] Act of 1956, the Colorado River Basin Project Act of 1968, and the Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1992. The dam was completed in 1963, before NEPA, so alternatives to the dam itself were excluded from scope.

**Physical setting.** Glen Canyon Dam stores water in Lake Powell (active capacity almost 25 million acre-feet, maf). The powerplant has eight generators with 1,356,000 kilowatts (kW) capacity and a turbine discharge of about 33,200 cubic feet per second (cfs), with releases during fluctuations limited to 31,500 cfs. The river outlet works carry 15,000 cfs, and the spillways approximately 208,000 cfs. The river falls about 1,900 feet over roughly 278 miles of Grand Canyon, with more than 100 rapids.

**Historic operations.** Annual releases ranged from 8.23 maf (legal minimum) to 20.4 maf (1984). Postdam daily fluctuations have had medians of about 12,000 cfs (October) to 16,000 cfs (January and August). Water temperature averages a cool 46 °F year-round, compared with a predam range from near freezing to above 80 °F.

**Alternatives.**
- *Unrestricted fluctuating:* No Action (historic operations) and [[Maximum Powerplant Capacity Alternative]].
- *Restricted fluctuating:* High, Moderate, [[Modified Low Fluctuating Flow Alternative]] (preferred), and Interim Low.
- *Steady flow:* Existing Monthly Volume, Seasonally Adjusted, and Year-Round.

Common elements include [[Adaptive Management]], monitoring of cultural resources, flood frequency reduction, beach/habitat-building flows, a new humpback chub population, further study of selective withdrawal, and emergency exception criteria.

**Eliminated concepts.** The [[Run-of-the-River Alternative]], [[Historic Pattern Alternative]], and [[Reregulated Flow Alternative]] were dropped from detailed study, chiefly for sediment-augmentation needs, compact and treaty conflicts, or significant Glen Canyon reach impacts. Sand pumping, beach protection, dam removal, and shifting peaking to Hoover Dam were also eliminated.

**Consultation.** The Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) ran in two phases: Phase I (1982 to 1988) and Phase II (1988 to present), including special research flows from June 1990 to July 1991. Scoping drew more than 17,000 comments. Interim flows were implemented on November 1, 1991.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Linked resources.** The river's water and sediment regimes drive a "naturalized" system. Lake Powell traps over 97 percent of incoming phosphorus and most sediment. The clear, cold releases favor [[Cladophora]] (a non-native filamentous alga), its diatom epiphytes, and the non-native amphipod [[Gammarus lacustris]]. This food base supports a blue-ribbon [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery in the 15-mile Glen Canyon reach and provides prey for [[Bald Eagle]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], and insectivorous birds and bats.

**Sediment.** Annual suspended sediment load fell from about 85.9 million tons per year (1941 to 1957) to an estimated 11 million tons per year after dam construction, roughly 70 percent of it from the [[Paria River]] and [[Little Colorado River]]. Sandbars, high terraces, debris fans, and deltas are all affected, and high terraces will continue to erode under any alternative.

**Vegetation and wildlife.** The [[Old High Water Zone]] (about 1,870 acres) and the [[New High Water Zone]] (about 1,320 acres) support riparian plants. [[Tamarisk]] dominates the new zone. More than half of river-corridor bird species nest in riparian vegetation.

**Native fish.** Of eight native fish species, three are extirpated from Grand Canyon, two are federally endangered ([[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]), one is a candidate species, and the remaining two are relatively common. Cold water limits mainstem reproduction, so native fish depend on tributaries and warm backwaters. The Grand Canyon humpback chub population relies on the [[Little Colorado River]] for spawning.

**Cultural resources.** A total of 475 prehistoric and historic sites were recorded in the affected area, with 313 eligible for the National Register as contributing elements to the Grand Canyon River Corridor Historic District. Six tribes hold ancestral ties: Havasupai, Hopi, Hualapai, Navajo, Southern Paiute, and Zuni.

## Key Findings

- **Preferred alternative:** The Modified Low Fluctuating Flow Alternative limits minimum flows to 8,000 cfs (7 a.m. to 7 p.m.) and 5,000 cfs at night, caps fluctuating-flow maximum at 20,000 cfs, and includes habitat maintenance flows and endangered fish research.
- **Fluctuation reduction:** Daily fluctuations exceeded 6,000 cfs on 97 percent of days under No Action, versus 54 percent under the Modified Low and Interim Low alternatives and 0 percent under steady-flow options.
- **Sediment:** The probability of net gain in riverbed sand after 50 years is higher under steady and restricted-fluctuation options than under No Action; the Moderate, Modified Low, and Seasonally Adjusted alternatives best maintain sandbars at higher elevations.
- **Vegetation:** Tamarisk, coyote willow, arrowweed, and camelthorn dominate the new high water zone under all alternatives; the Modified Low and Interim alternatives show a 30 to 47 percent increase in woody plant area, while Year-Round Steady Flow shows a 63 to 94 percent increase.
- **Fish:** Steady-flow options and the Modified Low alternative are expected to increase trout food base and growth potential; none of the alternatives clearly increases mainstem spawning habitat for native fish.
- **Endangered species:** Peregrine falcon and Kanab ambersnail show no effect under most options. Humpback chub is stable under all alternatives but relies on the Little Colorado River, so a second spawning population is a stated objective.
- **Cultural resources:** The Moderate, Modified Low, Interim Low, and steady-flow options most effectively preserve sites in place. Fewer than 157 archeological sites are affected under most alternatives, versus 263 under High Fluctuating Flow.
- **Hydropower:** Median retail rate is 62.17 mills/kWh under No Action. Wholesale rates rise 26.0 percent (+23.4 percent for Interim Low and +50.8 percent for Seasonally Adjusted Steady Flow) (1991 mills/kWh figures). Annual economic costs range from roughly $36 million to $119 million under restricted and steady options.
- **Recreation:** Steady flows give the most immediate recreation benefits. Equivalent annual net recreation benefits are about $3.74 million under Modified Low, versus $3.94 million under Interim Low and $4.76 million under Existing Monthly Volume (1991 dollars), while Year-Round Steady Flow yields $2.93 million.
- **Management objectives:** The Moderate, Modified Low, and Seasonally Adjusted Steady Flow alternatives best meet the combined objectives of the National Park Service, Hualapai and Navajo tribes, and the Fish and Wildlife Service, though dam operations alone cannot meet all endangered-fish objectives.
- **Unresolved issues:** Fish–non-native interactions, selective withdrawal feasibility (estimated cost $60 million), and sediment augmentation all require further study.
