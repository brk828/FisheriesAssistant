# Hybridization of Pallid and Shovelnose Sturgeon in the Missouri and Mississippi Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #acipenseridae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 study by Carlson, Pflieger, Trial and Haverland (Missouri Department of Conservation) examined the distribution, life history, and threats to survival of [[Pallid Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus albus*) in Missouri, using comparative data on [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*S. platorynchus*). Sampling in 1978–1979 at 12 stations yielded 4,355 sturgeons, of which 12 were field-identified as hybrids between the two species. This was the first report of hybridization in the genus *Scaphirhynchus*. Morphometric, meristic, and multivariate analyses supported the hybrid identifications for most specimens, although electrophoresis could not distinguish the species. The authors concluded that hybridization is likely a recent phenomenon driven by human alteration of the big-river environment and may threaten the already endangered pallid sturgeon.

## Background & Description

The two river sturgeons are endemic to large rivers of the central United States, mainly the [[Mississippi River Basin]]. Shovelnose sturgeon were historically abundant and supported a substantial commercial fishery early in the twentieth century, which has since declined but remains locally important. Pallid sturgeon were first described in 1905 (Forbes & Richardson), were not separately reported in commercial fishery records, and are among the least known freshwater fishes in North America. The most recent authenticated Missouri capture before this study was in 1948, and the species was classified as endangered in Missouri (Nordstrom et al. 1977).

Sturgeons were collected with [[Trotline Fishing]], [[Trammel Net]]s, and dip nets, with gear fished for a total of 95 days in spring and fall of 1978 and 1979. Fork lengths were recorded, and a representative series of 5–15 fish per station was retained for dissection. Analyses included:

- **Age determination:** annuli counted on sectioned [[Pectoral Fin Ray]]s.
- **Diet analysis:** stomach contents sorted and estimated by volume, with worms from baited hooks excluded.
- **Morphometrics and meristics:** four fin-ray counts, 10 measurements (expressed as thousandths of standard length), and a four-state belly-scutellation scale.
- **Multivariate statistics:** cluster analysis and [[Principal Components Analysis]] using SAS.
- **Genetics:** [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] of frozen tissue from 7 shovelnose, 10 pallid, and 6 presumed hybrid sturgeons, analysed in a separate study (Phelps & Allendorf 1983).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Both species occurred in the main channels of the rivers, on sandbars at the inside of river bends, and behind wing dikes with deeply scoured trenches. Pallid sturgeon were usually taken in gear sets that also contained shovelnose sturgeon, but four of the 11 pallid specimens came from swift-current areas where shovelnose were less numerous. Presumed hybrids were more closely associated with shovelnose: each gear set that caught a hybrid contained an average of 14 shovelnose, compared with an average of two shovelnose per gear set that caught a pallid sturgeon.

Diet was dominated by aquatic insect larvae (chiefly immature insects) in all groups. Fish, mostly cyprinids, made up a much larger share of the diet of pallid sturgeon and hybrids than of shovelnose sturgeon. Sand and plant material were frequently present, probably ingested incidentally.

The authors linked hybridization to human changes in the river system:

- The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers' lower Missouri River projects reduced water surface area by 50% and largely eliminated islands and side channels (Funk & Robinson 1974).
- Six large mainstem reservoirs on the upper river altered natural seasonal flood patterns and reduced turbidity all the way to the mouth (Neel et al. 1963; Whitley & Campbell 1973).
- Similar changes were evident in the [[Mississippi River]] downstream of the Missouri confluence.

Citing Schmulbach (1974), the authors described the Missouri River as a "hybridized habitat," a setting where altered conditions promote hybridization between species.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and abundance**

- Of 4,355 sturgeons captured, field identifications were 4,332 shovelnose (99.5%), 11 pallid (0.2%), and 12 hybrids (0.3%).
- Shovelnose sturgeon were collected at all 12 stations and made up 73% of all fishes in the collections, although sampling was intentionally selective for sturgeons.
- Pallid sturgeon were recorded at 6 of 12 stations. The largest share was at Cairo on the Mississippi River (5 specimens, 2.5% of sturgeons there). No pallid sturgeon were found on the Mississippi upstream of the Missouri mouth.
- Hybrids were collected at 4 stations, with 7 from Chain of Rocks (0.4% of sturgeons there).

**Morphology and multivariate analysis**

- Using a 30-specimen comparison set (447–816 mm SL: 10 shovelnose, 8 pallid, 12 presumed hybrids), hybrid means were intermediate for most characters, usually closer to pallid sturgeon. Orbital length and inner barbel length were greater in hybrids than in both parent groups.
- Cluster analysis agreed with field identifications for 9 of 10 shovelnose (90%), 9 of 12 hybrids (75%), and 6 of 8 pallid sturgeon (75%). Overall, 6 of 30 specimens (20%) were grouped differently.
- The first two principal components explained 63% of the total variance. The full-character analysis separated shovelnose from pallid sturgeon, with most hybrids between them. Two field-identified hybrids fell within the pallid group, and one shovelnose fell within the hybrid group.
- Morphometric-only analysis showed greater overlap among groups. Reanalysis of Bailey & Cross (1954) data showed two distinct groups. The authors suggested that the separation in Bailey & Cross's data may have been greater if larger specimens had been available.

**Genetics**

- Shovelnose and pallid sturgeon were indistinguishable at all 37 loci examined. Morph frequencies at three polymorphic loci did not differ significantly, including among geographically distant shovelnose populations.
- Electrophoresis therefore neither supported nor refuted the hybrid identifications, though the authors noted the genetic similarity was surprising given the phenotypic differences.

**Growth, age, and sex**

- Pallid sturgeon were significantly longer than shovelnose sturgeon at each comparable age (T = 4.77, df = 6). Hybrids were generally intermediate. Average length-at-age was based on 172 specimens.
- Females predominated in both species, with males making up 27% of shovelnose and 33% of pallid sturgeon. Three percent of shovelnose were hermaphrodites. All 12 presumed hybrids were female.

**Diet**

- Fish made up 1.6% of the shovelnose diet by volume (N = 234), 31.3% of the hybrid diet (N = 9), and 37.7% of the pallid diet (N = 9). Differences in fish consumption were significant for both volume and frequency of occurrence.

**Interpretation and conservation implications**

- Hybridization appears to be recent, since earlier workers (Bailey & Cross 1954) did not report hybrids. The authors attribute this to human-driven ecological change.
- The authors considered hybrids as common as pallid sturgeon in the samples, which suggests hybridization may occur frequently.
- The main threats identified are [[Genetic Swamping]], if hybrids are fertile, and competition for limited habitat. Fertility and the extent of backcrossing were identified as research priorities.
