# The Mosquitofish (Gambusia affinis)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Poeciliidae #mosquito-control #biological-control

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service fishery leaflet documents [[Gambusia affinis]], a small livebearing fish of the family [[Poeciliidae]] that has been widely introduced globally for mosquito control. The species includes two subspecies: [[Gambusia affinis affinis]] and [[Gambusia affinis holbrooki]]. These fish consume aquatic mosquito larvae, including species that transmit malaria and yellow fever. First demonstrated effective at Camp Hancock, Georgia in 1918, mosquitofish have since been introduced to nearly all warm regions worldwide. The document covers taxonomy, distribution, life history, reproduction, culture methods, and effectiveness in mosquito control programs.

## Background & Description

[[Gambusia affinis]] belongs to the order Cyprinodontes and family [[Poeciliidae]]. Two subspecies exist with subtle morphological differences:

**[[Gambusia affinis affinis]]**: 7 dorsal fin rays; third ray of gonopodium lacks deep split; hardier subspecies tolerating northern winters. Natural range extends along Gulf of Mexico coast from Rio Grande mouth to Bay St. Louis, Mississippi, and through Mississippi River tributaries north to central Illinois.

**[[Gambusia affinis holbrooki]]**: 8 dorsal fin rays; third ray of gonopodium shows deep split under microscope; less cold-tolerant. Natural range from southern New Jersey along Atlantic coast to Florida Keys and Gulf coast to Apalachicola, Florida.

**Physical characteristics**: Color usually olive or dull silvery, darkest on head, lightest on belly; varies with habitat. Dark-water swamp individuals are dark green with distinct purple bar below eye. Fine dark line often present along sides. Females have dark purplish blotch above vent. Dorsal fin has 2-3 transverse rows of fine black spots. Scales large in proportion to body size, outlined by dark pigment.

**Sexual dimorphism**: Females have deeper bodies than males and grow throughout life, reaching maximum 2.5 inches. Males grow little after gonopodium formation, not exceeding 1.5 inches. Females possess small, rounded anal fin; males have third, fourth, and fifth anal fin rays united and lengthened to form gonopodium (modified sexual organ).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat preferences**: Mosquitofish inhabit diverse aquatic environments with temperatures ranging 40° to 100°F. They flourish in sluggish and standing water—fresh, brackish, or marine; clear or muddy; deep or shallow; open or overgrown—but prefer clear shallow water. Shallow habitats provide escape from large predacious fish like [[Largemouth Bass]] and access to abundant mosquito larvae.

Specific habitats include: ditches and pools with hard or brackish water (high mineral content as dissolved solids), artesian well discharges, cisterns, water tanks, potholes, rain barrels, and even open municipal septic tanks. In sewage environments, fish remain at surface respiring at the air-water interface.

**Predators**: Birds, frogs, large fish, and water snakes consume [[Gambusia]]. The species is relatively free from disease.

**Lifespan**: Most mosquitofish die during the summer in which they mature. Some females mature in first summer; others not until second summer, dying at approximately 15 months old. Males, less hardy than females, die at earlier ages.

**Food habits**: Adults eat principally larvae and nymphs of aquatic insects, plus plant food including diatoms, desmids, and filamentous algae. Young feed mostly on plankton (passively floating or weakly swimming organisms).

**Feeding capacity**: One female (2.33 inches long) consumed 225 large mosquito larvae within 1 hour; one male (1.25 inches long) ate 28 larvae in same period. Newborn fish immediately commence feeding on newly hatched mosquito larvae (threadlike, nearly microscopic animals).

## Key Findings

**Mosquito control effectiveness**: Experiments over two summers in Michigan demonstrated [[Gambusia]] reduced mosquito production 80-95% in ponds simulating natural conditions (Krumholz, 1948b). When introduced into fish-free pond at height of mosquito breeding season, mosquitofish achieved 80% reduction in mosquito reproduction within one week. Control effectiveness requires absence of aquatic plants or debris (which provide hiding places for larvae) and predators.

**Reproduction biology** (based on 30,093 specimens of [[Gambusia affinis affinis]] from eight Illinois ponds, 1938-1940):

- **Ovoviviparous**: Females produce eggs that hatch within body. Male fertilizes eggs internally using gonopodium to deposit sperm. Single fertilization enables female to produce several broods.
- **Maturation timing**: Males mature at smaller sizes and slightly earlier ages than females. Some females matured first time in second summer (8-10 months old, 1-2 inches long), reproducing 10-15 weeks and bearing 3-4 broods. Their offspring matured during first summer (4-6 weeks old, ~1 inch long), reproducing 4-10 weeks and bearing 1-2 broods.
- **Sex ratios**: First brood showed nearly equal male:female ratio; succeeding broods showed females gradually outnumbering males.
- **Brood size**: Average number of young per brood decreased as mother's length increased. Metabolic factors coincident with approaching end of reproductive season may control brood size.
- **Development**: Fertilized eggs hatch in 21-28 days. Female expels few to several hundred live young within several hours. Young are 3/8 inch long at birth, well-developed, completely formed with prominent black eyes, often uniformly yellowish. They swim immediately upon birth.

**Growth**: Rapid, depending primarily on food supply and secondarily on water temperature.

**Global distribution**: From original introductions around 1920, [[Gambusia]] spread worldwide. International Red Cross requested brood stocks from U.S. Bureau of Fisheries for Italy and Spain. [[Gambusia affinis holbrooki]] shipment to Italy failed; Spain shipment succeeded. Italy obtained stock from Spain but fish couldn't withstand northern Italian winters. In 1927, hardier [[Gambusia affinis affinis]] shipped from Carbondale, Illinois to Trieste, Italy; these survived and multiplied.

From Spain and Italy, mosquitofish distributed to: nearly all southern European countries, Germany, Austria, Palestine, Philippine Islands (thence to China and Japan), Russia, Thailand, Union of South Africa, Hawaiian Islands, West Indies, and Argentina. Species became nearly cosmopolitan in warmer world regions.

**U.S. introductions**: Successfully introduced into Arizona, California, northern Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Massachusetts, Michigan, Missouri, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Utah, Washington, Wisconsin, and probably other states. Range extended northward into Canada.

**Culture**: Easily bred in aquariums at ~75°F water temperature. Accept live and prepared foods. Should not be mixed with other species as [[Gambusia]] attack other fish by nipping fins and tails. In nature, females seldom eat young due to abundant natural food; in aquariums, young require hiding places (dense plant growths) or physical separation from adults via partitions.

**Commercial availability**: Most tropical fish dealers stock or can order [[Gambusia]].
