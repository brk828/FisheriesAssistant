# GCES Backwater Meeting Summary (December 16, 1992)

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #riverine-habitat

## Executive Summary

This memorandum, authored by Larry Riley (Arizona Game and Fish Department), summarizes a technical meeting held at Bio/West's offices in Logan, Utah, convened to inform the [[Glen Canyon Dam Environmental Impact Statement]] (EIS). The meeting brought together federal, state, and academic researchers (GCES/Bureau of Reclamation, USFWS, AGFD, USGS, NPS, Bio/West, SWCA, and Utah State University) to define "backwater" habitats in the Grand Canyon reach of the [[Colorado River]], describe their formation and maintenance processes relative to [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations, and assess their biological importance—particularly for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and other native fishes, in contrast to their use by nonnative warmwater fishes. Key outcomes include a formal typology of backwater habitats, a life-history-stage framework for chub habitat use, a geomorphic model of backwater creation/filling tied to dam discharge patterns, and management implications regarding flow regimes that favor native versus nonnative fish assemblages.

## Background & Description

While this document is primarily a habitat and management summary rather than a morphological study, size/age-class data for [[Humpback Chub]] were discussed as proxies for habitat association:

- **Age 0 chub** (up to 25–50 mm total length): Utilize return current backwaters.
- **Age I chub** (one calendar year): Primarily use shoreline cavities, with transient movement into/out of return current backwaters.
- **Age II chub**: Physiologically/behaviorally competent to survive in mainchannel habitats alongside adults.
- **20–60 mm humpback chub**: Documented present in the Colorado River mainstem during July, August, and September, indicating backwater habitat importance for this size class in mid-to-late summer.

No detailed anatomical or meristic data are provided in this source; morphological references are limited to size-class thresholds tied to ontogenetic habitat shifts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Backwater Habitat Typology

Two backwater types were defined as relevant to Grand Canyon:

- **Type I** — Backwaters associated with [[Eddy Return Current Channels]] and reattachment bars. Identified as the area of prime concern given cold mainstem temperatures.
- **Type II** — Shoreline cavities or pocket waters associated with bank irregularities or channel roughness; individually small but collectively provide more low-velocity habitat than Type I, and persist across a wider range of discharges.

Additional backwater-like features discussed: tributary mouths (during high mainstem discharge), isolated backwaters/pools (fully cut off from the mainstem, rare), and small low-velocity pockets from channel roughness (lacking the thermal warming characteristic of true backwaters).

### Native Fish Habitat Use

- In the upper Colorado River basin, [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (squawfish) depend heavily on very-low-velocity backwaters for up to two years, while upper-basin chub use the periphery of these same areas rather than the core.
- In Grand Canyon, squawfish are extirpated, and mainstem spawning by native fish is considered unlikely under current thermal conditions.
- The extant native fish community comprises [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Speckled Dace]].
- The bulk of documented humpback chub spawning occurs in the [[Little Colorado River]]; larvae subsequently disperse (actively or passively) into the cold, high-velocity Colorado River mainstem, which is a harsh rearing environment.
- Four mainstem habitat types used by dispersing chub: (1) return current backwaters, (2) shoreline cavities, (3) open mainstem, (4) tributary mouths.
- Dr. Valdez noted a strong association between humpback chub distribution and canyon-bound river reaches throughout their known range.
- Otolith microchemistry analysis (Dr. Hendrickson, University of Texas) was proposed to reconstruct thermal histories experienced by rearing chub.
- Management caution: if warmed dam releases are used to encourage mainstem spawning, adequate mainstem rearing habitat for larval chub must be assured.

### Nonnative Fish Interactions

Primary nonnative warmwater fish of concern: [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Plains Killifish]]. Characteristics:
- Obligate dependence on slackwater conditions throughout their life cycle (non-riverine).
- Capacity for multiple spawning events per year in warm, low-velocity environments.
- Relatively short lifespans (~2 years) compared to native "big river" fishes.
- Fathead minnow observed to potentially prey on larval native fishes (noted by Gordon Mueller).

Additional nonnative concerns: [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]] (Dr. Carothers noted cold mainstem temperatures have suppressed their numbers post-dam); [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Black Bass]] (centrarchids) flagged by Dr. Holden as a greater potential threat if established, though upper-basin experience shows natives can coexist with nonnatives under some equilibria.

### Geomorphic Processes Governing Backwater Formation

- Backwaters form via flow separation at a debris/alluvial fan and reattachment further downstream, creating a [[Return Current Channel]].
- Steep-faced alluvial fans cause separation across a wider range of river stages than low-profile fans.
- Grand Canyon eddies are shorter than modeled elsewhere due to abrupt channel morphology forcing rapid reattachment.
- Return channels are excavated/maintained when return flow transports sediment out of the channel faster than sediment migrates in across the reattachment bar face.
- Backwater value to fish depends on reattachment bar crest elevation exceeding return channel bed elevation.
- Large flood spills can "blow out" eddies and strip reattachment bar sand; moderate high flows are needed to rebuild bars, but flows that overtop the fan/separation point can also remove stored sand.
- Sediment-rich systems favor rapid backwater filling/shoreward migration of sediment; sediment-starved systems favor open, persistent return channels.

## Key Findings

1. **Backwater definitions**: Two operative types established for Grand Canyon EIS purposes — Type I (eddy return current channel backwaters) and Type II (shoreline cavity/pocket water backwaters).
2. **Thermal dependence**: The importance of Type I backwaters to native fish is largely a function of the Colorado River's cold mainstem temperature (a legacy of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] hypolimnetic releases); warmer mainstem temperatures would reduce backwater dependency.
3. **Ontogenetic habitat shift in humpback chub**: Age 0 → return current backwaters; Age I → shoreline cavities (with transience); Age II+ → mainchannel with adults.
4. **Unknown population contribution**: The relative contribution of mainstem-reared young chub to the overall population remains unquantified but is suspected to be small.
5. **Nonnative advantage under stability**: Stable, pond-like, year-round backwaters disproportionately favor nonnative warmwater fishes (red shiner, fathead minnow, plains killifish), which have poor survival under flow fluctuation or inundation. Seasonally variable backwaters suppress nonnative proliferation.
6. **Flow regime implications**: A more natural hydrograph with high-water years benefits natives and disadvantages nonnatives; drought-driven reduction of flood flows favors nonnatives. Thermal modification of releases (e.g., via a multi-level intake structure, "MLIS") could increase nonnative abundance but may be an acceptable tradeoff based on Upper Basin experience.
7. **Historical backwater dynamics** (aerial photo record):
   - 1965: high degree of backwater development.
   - 1973: low degree of backwater development (aging/filling).
   - 1980: reattachment bars overgrown with riparian vegetation; a 9-day, ~45,000 cfs bypass release failed to clear vegetation.
   - 1983: a ~100,000 cfs flood removed vegetation and deeply incised return current channels.
   - 1984–1986: suggests maximum backwater formation follows a high steady flow (~30 days) followed by a precipitous decline to low flow (analogous to October 1984).
8. **Backwater aging and rejuvenation**: Backwaters fill over time via sediment deposition (accentuated by low-flow silt/clay deposition and plant succession); a long-duration (≥2 weeks) spike flow of yet-undetermined magnitude is proposed as a mechanism to reopen aged backwaters.
9. **Management tension**: Any dam operation strategy must balance backwater creation/maintenance (benefiting native fish rearing) against the risk of stabilizing conditions that favor nonnative warmwater fish establishment.
