# Quinaldine as a Fish Anesthetic: Efficacy, Toxicity, and Safety

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This document compiles three reports from the U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, *Investigations in Fish Control* numbers 22 to 24 (January 1969). Together they evaluate [[Quinaldine]] (2-methylquinoline), a coal-tar derived chemical, as a lower-cost alternative to [[MS-222]] for anesthetizing hatchery and wild fish. The studies cover anesthetic efficacy, acute toxicity, water chemistry effects, recovery, safety indexes, toxicity to trout eggs, and a Maine field-style trial on coldwater salmonids. The principal conclusion is that quinaldine is an effective, rapid-acting anesthetic at about 15 to 16 ppm for most trout, but its efficacy collapses under acidic conditions (pH 5.0), and it is more acutely toxic than MS-222.

The document also contains an errata sheet correcting the citations and several numerical entries, and a title and contents page for each report.

## Background & Description

Quinaldine is manufactured from aniline and paraldehyde or acetaldehyde with hydrochloric acid. It boils at 246 to 247 °C, is insoluble in water, and is soluble in alcohol, ether, chloroform, and acetone. Its anesthetic effect on fish was first reported by [[Muench]] (1958), who found that 2.5 to 20 ppm narcotized several warmwater species within 0.5 to 4 minutes.

Several factors made the work necessary:

- **Regulation:** Amendments to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act require chemicals used on fish to be cleared and labeled for specific uses. Clearance requires data on fish toxicity, efficacy, tissue residues, and safety to other animals.
- **Use in hatcheries:** A survey of national fish hatcheries found quinaldine in use on sockeye, chinook, and coho salmon, rainbow, brown, and brook trout, northern pike, goldfish, channel catfish, smallmouth and largemouth bass, and walleye.
- **Cost:** Locke notes that quinaldine was sold at $14.35 per 500-gram bottle, and that MS-222 was the costly standard.

Mammalian toxicity data cited from Spector (1956) give an oral LD50 in rats of 1.23 g/kg and a cutaneous LD50 in rabbits of 1.87 g/kg, classifying quinaldine as slightly toxic to mammals. A personal communication from the National Cancer Institute (November 10, 1966) reported no evidence of carcinogenicity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mode of action and behavior.** The mode of action is unknown. [[Bell]] (1964) suggested it acts like barbiturates, depressing the central nervous system and especially the respiratory center. Treated fish show initial irritation that increases with concentration, then rapid loss of equilibrium without a pronounced sedation stage. At [[Loss Of Equilibrium]] (stage II), fish lie motionless but react strongly to striking the container or squeezing the caudal peduncle. Opercular rates in rainbow trout were about 60 per minute in controls and more than doubled after 5 minutes of exposure, with weak, gasping movements. Locke noted that anesthetized fish retained dark coloration, and that recovery began with shivering.

**Water chemistry.**
- *pH:* Efficacy was reduced or absent at pH 5.0. Smallmouth bass recovered within 20 to 25 minutes when pH was lowered to 5.0. At pH 5.7, fish did not recover within 2 hours. Efficacy was restored by raising pH; channel catfish exposed to 50 or 60 ppm at pH 5.0 were narcotized within 3 to 10 minutes when pH was raised to 7.0 or 10.3. The authors suggest that acidic conditions shift the molecule from an ionic to a less active non-ionic form. A pH of about 6.0 or above does not deactivate quinaldine.
- *Hardness:* Efficacy on rainbow trout was similar in soft (10 ppm total hardness) and hard (180 ppm) water. Locke's tests in 10 to 40 ppm hardness were also satisfactory.

**Temperature.** Channel catfish, bluegills, and largemouth bass were more resistant at 17 °C and below. Trout efficacy was unaffected by temperature or size in the Schoettger and Julin study, but Marking found that rainbow trout were more sensitive at higher temperatures in 24- to 96-hour exposures.

**Eggs.** Fertilized [[Rainbow Trout]] eggs tolerated 15- to 30-minute exposures to concentrations as high as 120 ppm. Two-hour exposures at 60 and 120 ppm killed about 40 percent and 100 percent of eggs, respectively. Concentrations of 15 to 30 ppm, typical in spawning, posed little risk, especially since eggs are placed in flowing water afterwards.

**Metabolism.** Metabolic fate in fish is unresolved. Studies in other animals (Kusui 1931, Takahashi 1930/1931, Tsunoo et al. 1965) showed conversion to quinaldic acid or alpha-quinolinic acid, with only trace quantities of 2-methylquinoline changed in chickens.

## Key Findings

### Report 22: Efficacy of Quinaldine as an Anesthetic for Seven Species of Fish (Schoettger and Julin)

- **Species tested:** Rainbow, brown, brook, and lake trout; channel catfish; bluegill; and largemouth bass. Smallmouth bass were tested in pH trials.
- **Core efficacy:** Concentrations of 15 or 16 ppm were at least 90 percent effective for inducing loss of equilibrium within 2 minutes in the four trout species. Mean tolerated exposures ranged from 45 minutes to more than 6 hours, with most fish recovering within several minutes.
- **Warmwater species:** Catfish required 70 ppm at 7 to 17 °C; bluegill 15 to 60 ppm; bass 20 to 70 ppm. At 22 or 27 °C, about 15 ppm was effective on bluegill and bass, and 30 ppm on catfish. Bass 7 to 12 inches long were "much more resistant" than smaller fish.
- **Mortality:** As many as 25 percent of test fish died in some trials. The authors attribute this to overexposure of the most susceptible fish, since fish could not be watched constantly, and say mortality should be minimal when anesthesia is monitored more closely.
- **Hardness:** Soft-water trials (4) and hard-water trials (2) all anesthetized fish within 2 minutes.
- **Repeated exposure:** Daily treatment of ten 3.6-inch rainbow trout with 12 ppm for six days did not change their sensitivity; anesthesia occurred within 1 to 3.5 minutes.
- **Solution aging:** Solutions at 15 ppm aged 50 days at 12 °C anesthetized rainbow trout within 2 minutes. At 27 °C, bluegill efficacy declined after 21 days. Aeration for 24 hours did not reduce efficacy.
- **Capacity:** A 15 ppm solution treated 3,542 g of rainbow trout (0.037 mL of quinaldine), about 94.5 kg of fish per mL, before exposure times exceeded 2 minutes. Meister and Ritzi (1958) reported about 14 kg of brook trout and 42 kg of lake trout per gram of MS-222.
- **Caveats:** Quinaldine anesthetized fish retain reflex responsiveness, which can interfere with stripping, delicate surgery, and blood collection.

### Report 23: Toxicity of Quinaldine to Selected Fishes (Marking)

- **Scope:** Nine species (adding northern pike and walleye) in 15-, 30-, and 60-minute and 3-, 6-, 24-, 48-, and 96-hour static bioassays, with ten fish per concentration.
- **96-hour LC50s:** Range from 2.0 to 24.9 ppm at 12 °C. Channel catfish were the most resistant. Northern pike fell from 20 ppm (24 h) to 2 ppm (96 h), attributed partly to starvation.
- **Size effects:** Small fish were more sensitive. For 2-inch rainbow trout the 96-hour LC50 was 5.0 ppm, versus 15.3 ppm for 6-inch fish. For brown trout the values were 3.5 ppm (2-inch) and 14.0 ppm (6-inch).
- **Temperature:** Rainbow trout 96-hour LC50s ranged from 13.3 ppm at 7 °C to 1.9 ppm at 17 °C. Bluegill was most sensitive at 22 °C (about 6 ppm at 96 hours) and LC50 was 10.1 ppm at 12 °C.
- **Water quality:** Quinaldine was significantly less toxic to rainbow trout in soft water over 1- to 6-hour exposures. Marking suggests toxicity is probably linked to pH.
- **Recovery:** Survivors of partial-kill concentrations generally recovered (about 95 percent after 96 hours). Recovery times ranged from 2 to 120 minutes; lake trout and largemouth bass occasionally needed longer.
- **Comparison with MS-222:** Quinaldine LC50s (2.0 to 24.9 ppm) are lower than MS-222 LC50s (32.0 to 62.1 ppm) at 12 °C over 96 hours, yet quinaldine is effective at lower anesthetic concentrations, so the safety indexes of the two drugs are similar.
- **Safety indexes (2-inch rainbow trout, 12 °C):** The safety index was 1.3 for 15-minute exposures, 0.9 for 30-minute, and 1.0 for 60-minute exposures. Values below 1.0 imply mortality if 99 percent of fish are anesthetized, so the 30- and 60-minute exposures are marginal.
- **Conclusion:** Brief exposures are safer and minimize mortality.

### Report 24: Quinaldine as an Anesthetic for Brook Trout, Lake Trout, and Atlantic Salmon (Locke)

- **Setting:** Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Game, testing yearling Atlantic and landlocked salmon, brook trout, and lake trout in soft water (10 to 40 ppm hardness) at 36 to 40 °F and 47 to 59 °F.
- **Design:** Five concentrations (5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 ppm) in duplicate; six fish per 5-gallon pail; exposures ended at 15 minutes; fish were fin-clipped and held for 2 weeks in raceways.
- **Results:** Anesthetization and recovery were directly proportional to concentration. Fish were anesthetized faster and recovered sooner at the higher temperature. Brook trout took 5 to 7 times as long at 5 ppm (36 °F) than at 10 or 15 ppm. No delayed mortality was observed.
- **Recommendations:** Lake trout were the most sensitive; 10 ppm was generally satisfactory. Salmon and brook trout were more tolerant; 15 ppm was suggested.
- **Cost:** Locke reports quinaldine at 15 ppm is 24 times cheaper than MS-222 at 1:12,000 and "warrants wider use as a fish anesthetic."
- **Preparation:** 37.85 mL of quinaldine with 40 mL of acetone, made up to 1 L with distilled water. Adding 1 mL of stock to 1 gallon yields 10 ppm.

### Overall Conclusions

- Quinaldine is an effective, rapid anesthetic at 15 to 16 ppm for most trout, and at 15 to 30 ppm for warmwater species at 22 to 27 °C.
- Its efficacy is lost under acidic conditions (pH 5.0) and is restored by raising pH.
- Water hardness, solution age (within limits), and repeated exposure have little influence on efficacy.
- It is more acutely toxic than MS-222, so short exposures and careful monitoring are essential.
- The primary assets are rapid action, prolonged anesthesia, and low cost. Drawbacks include residual reflexes and possible interference with surgery and blood collection.
