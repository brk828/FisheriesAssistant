# The Role of Competition in the Replacement of Native Fishes by Introduced Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter, by Allan A. Schoenherr (Fullerton College), examines whether competition actually drives the replacement of native desert fishes by introduced species. The classic case, the near extirpation of the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) by [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), is widely attributed to competition. Life-history data, however, failed to demonstrate competition for a common resource. The author proposes that replacement proceeds mainly through predation on young by mosquitofish, together with behavioral aggression that reduces female survival. The same pattern is examined in the [[Salton Sea]] replacement of the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) by [[Sailfin Molly]] and [[Zill's Cichlid]]. The author concludes that every native–introduced interaction must be assessed individually, and that niche partitioning and character displacement are indirect evidence of competitive exclusion.

## Background & Description

The chapter is part of the volume *Fishes in North American Deserts* (ed. R. J. Naiman and D. L. Soltz; Wiley, 1981). Deacon et al. (1979) listed 251 North American fish taxa as endangered, threatened, or of special concern. Of these, 26 percent (65 taxa) are desert species, and more than two-thirds of the desert species have declined while interacting with introduced forms.

Native fish populations can be reduced by hybridization, predation, or competition. Hybridization and predation are usually obvious, whereas competition is "nearly impossible to prove." Workers often attribute replacement to competition by default when neither of the other two is evident.

Desert fishes affected by introductions include:
- The [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), apparently replaced by the red shiner (*Notropis lutrensis*).
- The [[Pahrump Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys latos*) and Ash Meadows pupfish (*Empetrichthys merriami*), affected by goldfish, shortfin mollies, and mosquitofish.
- The Gila topminnow, declining as mosquitofish expanded.
- The desert pupfish, reduced in Arizona by mosquitofish and in California by sailfin mollies and Zill's cichlid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mosquitofish as competitor.** Mosquitofish have been introduced worldwide as biological control agents for mosquitoes, often where native larvivorous fishes already existed. Myers (1965) documented impacts on many fish populations. Table 1 lists about 25 fish taxa reported as reduced by mosquitofish, from Cyprinidae, Cyprinodontidae, Neostethidae, Gasterosteidae, Centrarchidae, and Poeciliidae, across Thailand, the Philippines, Egypt, the USA, and Mexico. Hurlbert et al. (1972) showed that mosquitofish predation alters trophic relationships across whole ecosystems, increasing phytoplankton as small invertebrates decline.

**Speciation and the Gambusia nobilis group.** Hubbs and Springer (1957) proposed that drought and mosquitofish invasion isolated populations in clear, spring-fed Texas and northern Mexican habitats, promoting speciation. Hubbs (1957, 1971) also linked mosquitofish competition to the origin of *Gambusia heterochir*. *Gambusia gaigei* in Big Bend National Park was eliminated several times by mosquitofish and re-established from laboratory stocks (Hubbs and Brodrick 1963).

**Arizona context.** Native topminnows and a now-extinct pupfish form once controlled mosquitoes effectively. Human activities (diversion, drying of creeks, drainage of marshes) began the decline before mosquitofish arrived. Mosquitofish were introduced to California in 1922 and reached Arizona soon after; by 1926 they were in the Gila River near its mouth and in the Salt River near Tempe.

**Salton Sea.** The King Street irrigation drain (Riverside County, California) provides a detailed habitat study. Shallow, warm water (around 39°C) supported *Cyprinodon macularius*, and each species separated into near-pure populations by habitat in March 1977. Reduced habitat diversity after flooding and reconstruction (March 1978) led to a mixed-species pond, and by March 1980 only four adult pupfish were captured in two hours of seining.

## Key Findings

**1. Competition for a common resource was not demonstrated.** Life-history comparisons of *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* and *Gambusia affinis* during cohabitation showed similar size, morphology, and habitat preferences, which suggests competition, but food did not appear limiting. Indirect evidence included abundant phytoplankton, food in stomachs, and sex ratios near 1:1.

**2. Replacement mechanisms identified:**
- **Predation on fry:** Gambusia guts contained young fish, and aquarium observations confirmed predation on topminnow fry.
- **Reduced female survival:** Topminnow females died at high rates during cohabitation, while Gambusia did not show this trend. Mortality appeared only after a divider separating the species was removed.
- **Behavioral dominance:** In aquaria, Gambusia females dominated all others, and Poeciliopsis females were chased constantly.

**3. Reproductive comparison.** Gambusia has a higher reproductive potential. Its brood sizes are larger, and Poeciliopsis brood sizes fell during peak interaction. Poeciliopsis recruitment ceased after Gambusia appeared in the experimental pond.

**4. Dietary and morphological differences.** Poeciliopsis is largely detritivorous (gut length 1.5–2.0 times standard length). Gambusia is carnivorous (gut length 69 percent of standard length, with conical teeth and open cephalic canals).

**5. Four documented replacements of Gila topminnow.**
- Aravaipa Creek: topminnows gone by March 30, 1959, less than two years after mosquitofish were first recorded there.
- Safford artesian system: mosquitofish alone found by 1966.
- Tempe-area reintroduction attempts (1965–1966): mosquitofish replaced the stocked topminnows.
- Deer Valley pond (1969–1970): one male and one female mosquitofish were found among 143 topminnows; by December 1970 only a few old female topminnows remained.

**6. Springhead refuge.** Mosquitofish are selected against in clear, carbonate-rich spring-fed waters occupied by poeciliids, so topminnows persist in springheads. Mosquitofish readily replace native species in other waters.

**7. Desert pupfish decline.** Quarterly surveys since March 1978 found desert pupfish made up less than 5 percent of the Salton Sea fauna. At least 22 introduced species are now present in its freshwater tributaries. Sailfin mollies court pupfish of both sexes, reducing reproductive success. Juvenile Zill's cichlid cause pupfish males to spend time patrolling territories instead of courting. Shallow water under 10 cm deep, the pupfish's last refuge, is being invaded by juvenile Zill's cichlid.

**8. Niche partitioning as indirect evidence.** Competitive exclusion may be inferred from niche partitioning and character displacement, even though competition is rarely observed directly. Aggression of short duration appears to divide cohabiting fish into separate niches. If no refuge is available, extirpation follows.

**9. Desert fishes as island species.** Pristine desert waters are "ecologic islands" with few species, well adapted to the physical environment but poorly equipped against generalist intruders such as carp, goldfish, mosquitofish, and sailfin mollies. Surviving native species typically occupy extreme refugia: carbonate- or CO₂-rich headsprings, or waters too shallow, hot, alkaline, or oxygen-poor for generalists.

**10. Conservation recommendation.** The author argues that continued introductions of nonnative fish, such as mosquitofish for mosquito control where native species could do the job, should be discouraged, and that life-history and behavioral studies should be expanded to inform this.
