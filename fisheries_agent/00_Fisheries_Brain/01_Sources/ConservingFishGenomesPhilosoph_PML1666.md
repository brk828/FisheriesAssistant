# Conserving Fish Genomes: Philosophies and Practices

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-genetics

## Executive Summary

This invited editorial by [[Gary K. Meffe]] (Savannah River Ecology Laboratory, University of Georgia) argues that the erosion of genetic diversity in fishes is a largely invisible but serious conservation problem, and that current knowledge of conserving fish gene pools is limited. The author outlines the genetics of small populations, states a "conservative approach" to conservation genetics, debates the taxonomic level at which conservation should be targeted, and identifies practical gaps. These gaps are framed around the Stockholm Resolution of the Fifth Congress of European Ichthyologists on [[Lake Victoria]] fishes. The essay concludes that genetics must be routinely integrated into conservation programs and that the field is in its infancy.

## Background & Description

The essay is a Vol. 18 (No. 1, 1987) editorial in *Environmental Biology of Fishes*, Dr W. Junk Publishers. Its keywords are biotic diversity, conservation, gene banks, genetic diversity, philosophy, selection, and stock-holm resolution. The author states that he does not repeat the details of his earlier paper (Meffe 1986) on genetic principles for endangered fishes. Instead, it expands on philosophical and practical issues.

The central premise is that species loss is obvious and quantifiable, whereas loss of [[Genetic Diversity]] is inconspicuous and easily overlooked. Key concepts include:

- **[[Effective Population Size]] (Ne):** Usually smaller than the absolute population size (N). It is determined by sex ratio, distribution of progeny among females, and population fluctuations.
- **Bottlenecks and [[Genetic Drift]]:** Random (bottlenecks and drift) effects, which erode genetic variance.
- **[[Inbreeding Depression]]:** Directional effects that increase homozygosity and reduce fecundity, fertility, clutch size and growth rate.
- **Rule of thumb:** Minimum effective population sizes of 50 for the short term and 500 for the long term, adjustable for species biology such as schooling behavior.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author identifies habitat loss as the main driver of small population size. Several contextual points are made:

- **Freshwater isolation:** Nearly all threatened and endangered fishes spend at least part of their lives in fresh water. Their populations behave like "island" situations, with low natural migration and increased genetic divergence. Examples include desert fishes restricted to isolated springs, and locally differentiated fishes in peninsular Florida.
- **Range position:** Edge-of-range populations may naturally have lower genetic variance than central populations. The author regards this as a reason for broader geographic analysis, not alarm.
- **Green turtle example:** Green turtles (*Chelonia mydas*) form genetically isolated breeding populations that mix as non-breeders in the Atlantic Ocean. A species can appear to be thriving while individual gene pools disappear.
- **Life history factors:** Mating patterns, assortative mating, dispersal and behavior all affect effective size and gene flow among demes.
- **Non-game species:** Representative minnows, darters, sculpins and killifishes are suggested as candidates for hatchery experience before recovery is urgent.

## Key Findings

**Guiding assumptions (the "conservative approach")**
- Allelic variance (heterozygosity) is assumed to be desirable and fitness-enhancing (a selectionist position), rather than neutral.
- Genetic variance is treated as a limited, non-renewable resource. Genetic diversity cannot be separated from biotic diversity, so species-level conservation alone may be too simple.
- Halting erosion of local and global genetic diversity is a desirable goal. The author argues that minimizing potential harm is the wisest course under uncertainty, citing Soulé (1985) on conservation biology as a "crisis discipline."

**Levels of conservation**
- Current policy, such as the [[Endangered Species Act]], focuses on species. The author argues this overlooks subspecies and populations.
- Preserving the full range of intraspecific diversity is ideal but is considered politically impractical at present.

**Practical gaps identified (seven in total)**
1. **Genetic information:** Basic data on how variance is distributed within and among populations is urgently needed. The author cites Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) on the endangered Sonoran topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*).
2. **Gene banks:** Only one hatchery worldwide was described as devoted exclusively to endangered fishes, the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, operating since 1974. The author knows of no cryopreservation efforts for fish gene pools.
3. **Heterozygosity and fitness:** A "heterozygosity consensus" exists, but experiments are needed. Fitness traits have low heritability, so preserving heterozygosity at fitness-related loci may matter more than overall heterozygosity.
4. **Life histories:** Mating success, assortative mating and dispersal need case-by-case consideration.
5. **Biogeography:** Isolation of freshwater populations increases the need to preserve differentiation among populations.
6. **Education:** Native fishes need value recognition in management regardless of their harvestability. Progress includes voluntary tax programs funding non-game branches of state agencies.
7. **The genomic "black box":** Knowledge of split genes, pleiotropy, epistasis and co-adapted gene complexes is limited. Outbreeding depression is a possible concern.

**Stockholm Resolution (Balon & Bruton 1986)**
- The Fifth Congress of European Ichthyologists in Stockholm recognized serious risks to many vertebrate species, especially Lake Victoria fishes.
- It recommended placing as many threatened species as possible in gene banks (cryopreservation and aquaria) and reintroducing them once the introduced [[Nile Perch]] stock declines to levels that permit native populations to persist.

**Conclusions**
- The essay "raised many questions and answered essentially none."
- Input is needed from molecular geneticists, political scientists, philosophers, evolutionary biologists and resource managers.
- Genetics must be routinely incorporated into all conservation programs for fishes.
- Retaining maximum global genetic diversity is the best hedge against the mistakes that a young field will inevitably make.
