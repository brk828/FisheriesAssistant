## Executive Summary

This paper by Harold M. Tyus (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) presents a conceptual and policy argument regarding instream flow management for four endangered "big-river" fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]]. The central thesis is that prevailing "survival flow" philosophies—which set flow standards based on current, already-degraded habitat use—are inadequate and potentially counterproductive for species recovery. Tyus argues that because remnant fish stocks likely occupy suboptimal or marginal habitats due to a century of flow regulation, damming, and nonnative species introductions, flow management must instead be grounded in a "recovery philosophy." This approach would combine empirical field studies, simulation modeling, and expert assessment to actively restore ecological conditions rather than merely maintaining the degraded status quo. The paper critiques widely used instream flow methodologies (IFIM/PHABSIM) as poorly suited to complex, species-rich warmwater river systems, and calls for experimental, adaptive management strategies to achieve genuine, self-sustaining recovery.

## Background & Description

### Target Species
Four native "big-river" fishes of the Colorado River Basin, all federally listed as endangered:
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) — a cyprinid; largest minnow native to North America; extirpated from the lower basin.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) — a cyprinid; reproduction in the lower basin now restricted to the Little Colorado River (Grand Canyon); reproducing successfully in the Yampa and upper Colorado rivers in the upper basin.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — a catostomid; most recently listed (1991) after deteriorating from "threatened" proposal status (1980) to only relict, nonrecruiting stocks.
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*) — a cyprinid; extremely rare, persisting mainly in hatchery or relict reservoir stocks; virtually absent from the wild upper basin.

### Taxonomic & Faunal Context
- These species belong to a historically unique and insular fish fauna shaped by long geographic isolation and extreme hydrologic/climatic conditions (citing Miller 1959, 1961; Molles 1980).
- The Colorado River fish fauna is classified into three groups:
  1. High/intermediate elevation fishes shared with adjacent drainages.
  2. Small-stream endemics at low-to-intermediate elevations.
  3. **Big-river fishes** (the focus of this paper) — mainstream endemics comprising cyprinids (minnows) and catostomids (suckers).
- The upper basin retains ~2,000 km of occupied habitat and includes six endemic large-river cyprinids/catostomids plus six headwater forms.

### Historical Habitat Alteration
- Major dam construction began with the **Colorado River Compact and Boulder Canyon Project Act (1928–1929)**.
- By the 1960s, most of the mainstream Colorado River was converted into a system of dams and diversions.
- Key impacts: altered flow timing/duration/magnitude, temperature regime shifts, channel morphology change, habitat inundation, and fragmentation via stream blockage.
- Example: **Flaming Gorge and Fontenelle Dams** on the upper Green River (1960s) eliminated most native fishes in 128 km of river above Dinosaur National Monument.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Nonnative Species Interactions
The paper documents substantial evidence of adverse interactions between native endangered fishes and introduced nonnative fishes:
- **Northern pike** (*Esox lucius*) and **channel catfish** (*Ictalurus punctatus*) occupy habitats used by adult [[Colorado Squawfish]].
- Habitats used by young Colorado squawfish are dominated by introduced fishes, with documented aggressive behavioral interactions.
- Juvenile Colorado squawfish have been consumed by introduced fishes.
- [[Humpback Chub]] populations may be adversely affected by channel catfish in the Green River basin.
- Lack of recruitment in [[Razorback Sucker]] has been linked to predation by **common carp** (*Cyprinus carpio*) and other nonnative fishes.
- In the lower Colorado River basin, native fishes have been largely replaced by approximately 44 introduced forms (20 abundant); in the upper basin, 42 introduced fishes are reported (fewer than 10 abundant).

### Habitat Refugia
- Standing crops of endangered fishes in the Green River below its confluence with the Yampa River are presumably sustained by the more natural flow/temperature regime of the Yampa — a relatively unregulated tributary.
- Seasonally flooded bottomlands are highlighted as ecologically important but increasingly lost to river regulation (reduced spring flows) and impoundment (loss of fish access), citing global floodplain-river literature (Welcomme 1979).

### Current Distribution (Upper Basin)
- **Colorado squawfish**: Yampa River, Green River below Yampa confluence, upper Colorado River mainstream, lower San Juan River.
- **Humpback chub**: reproducing in Yampa and upper Colorado rivers.
- **Razorback sucker**: lower Yampa and Green rivers, mainstream Colorado River, lower San Juan River — but no evidence of recent recruitment.
- **Bonytail**: extremely rare; wild upper-basin presence nearly absent.

## Key Findings

1. **Extinction crisis context**: Over the past 100 years, 3 genera, 27 species, and 13 subspecies of North American fishes have gone extinct due to habitat alteration and nonnative introductions (Miller et al. 1989); an additional 364 taxa are threatened (Williams et al. 1989), with zero species recovered/delisted in a 10-year review period.

2. **Two contrasting instream flow philosophies** are identified and contrasted:
   - **Survival flows**: aim to maintain current population levels using existing (degraded) habitat-use data; goal is preventing extinction, treated as "status quo."
   - **Recovery flows**: aim to increase abundance/distribution/reproduction sufficiently to allow delisting; requires improving, not just maintaining, habitat conditions.
   - Most prior upper-basin efforts have implicitly pursued survival flows, even when not explicitly stated.

3. **Critique of prevailing methodologies**:
   - **IFIM** (Instream Flow Incremental Methodology) and **PHABSIM** (Physical Habitat Simulation) are the dominant tools but are criticized as inadequate for complex warmwater systems.
   - PHABSIM's "weighted usable area" metric has established relationships mainly in simple coldwater streams (1–10 species) but shows little correlation with standing fish crops in large warmwater systems (30+ species).
   - Applications of PHABSIM to Colorado River endangered fishes have produced conflicting flow recommendations across species/life stages at the same location.
   - Microhabitat availability is not the sole limiting factor for fish populations (Orth 1987); methods cannot be validated without deeper ecosystem understanding.

4. **"Minimum flow is a myth"** (Stalnaker 1990): Once a minimum flow is legally established, it tends to become the *de facto* management target/average flow, which is insufficient — natural variability (wet/average/dry years) is necessary to meet fish life-history requirements.

5. **Barriers to recovery under survival-flow philosophy**:
   - Habitats are presumed marginal/suboptimal.
   - Insufficient life-history information exists for the target species.
   - Water management institutions and legal frameworks (e.g., Colorado water law) tend to favor only minimum flow protections.

6. **Recommended recovery philosophy** requires:
   - A conservation ethic distinct from commercial/sport fisheries management (citing Soulé and Wilcox 1980; Frankel 1983).
   - Determining ecological requirements ideally from least-altered reference systems (though such systems are increasingly rare/altered in the basin).
   - Comprehensive evaluation of all fish-community inhabitants (native and nonnative), not single-species flow targets.
   - Monitoring of compliance with agreed-upon instream flows, since project compliance is often lax (Hubert et al. 1990).
   - Integration of empirical studies, analytical/population models, computer simulations, and expert/subjective assessment in decision-making (Maguire 1986; Soulé 1987).
   - Experimental approaches: laboratory studies, stream section reclamation, or constructed test stream sections to test fish community responses to flow/species-composition manipulations.

7. **Broader ecosystem risk**: The "fundamentally insular" nature of the Colorado River fish fauna (Molles 1980) suggests additional native species beyond the four currently listed may become endangered or extinct as ecosystem perturbations accumulate, making future recovery efforts progressively more difficult.

8. **Institutional framework**: The interagency **Recovery Implementation Program** in the upper Colorado River basin was established to reconcile endangered species flow needs with existing state/federal water rights and Interstate Compact obligations, following more than 100 Section 7 Biological Opinions issued by the FWS.
