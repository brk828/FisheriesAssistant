# Native Fish in Troubled Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Lepisosteidae #Acipenseridae #Polyodontidae #Percidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This essay by George Reiger, illustrated with paintings by George Luther Schelling, argues that North America's native freshwater fishes are declining rapidly because of dams, habitat loss, pollution, overexploitation, nonnative species, hybridization, and public indifference. It opens with the author's boyhood encounter with a large [[Florida Gar]] in the St. Lucie River, then surveys the decline of [[Gar]], [[Sturgeon]], [[Paddlefish]], [[Colorado River Fishes]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Blue Pike]], [[Whitefish]], and [[Atlantic Salmon]]. The central message is that fish are "captive species" whose decline is hard to reverse, and that public concern lags because few people ever see them in the wild.

## Background & Description

**Opening anecdote.** Reiger, as a youth, heard a large predator feeding on mullet near his father's Florida dock. After days of searching, he speared it: an unusually large Florida gar (*Lepisosteus platyrhincus*), a close relative of the spotted gar (*L. oculatus*). Local people had been told the gar was a snook or possibly a gar; the identification was settled only after the spearing. He skinned the fish, failed to preserve the hide, and later wrote that he wished he had instead treated it as a subject for observation.

**Gars.** The alligator gar (*L. spatula*) is the largest gar. The largest reported was taken from a Louisiana lake: 302 pounds and ten feet long. The article also discusses the spotted and longnose gars (*L. osseus*) and the shortnose gar (*L. platostomus*).

**Sturgeons.** The white sturgeon (*Acipenser transmontanus*) and the Russian sturgeon (*Huso huso*) are described as the largest freshwater fishes on Earth. The only measured giant white sturgeon was taken from the Columbia River in 1912: about 12½ feet long, 1,285 pounds. Other species discussed include the lake sturgeon (*A. fulvescens*), Atlantic sturgeon (*A. oxyrinchus*), shortnose sturgeon (*A. brevirostrum*), and shovelnose sturgeon (*Scaphirhynchus platorynchus*).

**Paddlefish.** The paddlefish (*Polyodon spathula*) is a plankton-feeding species of the Mississippi drainage. Its populations are now concentrated in Missouri's Osage River.

**Darters and other small fishes.** The [[Snail Darter]] (*Percina tanasi*) was discovered in the Little Tennessee River in 1973. The Iowa darter (*Etheostoma exile*) is a smaller, regionally rare species.

**Colorado River fishes.** The Colorado River system holds seven indigenous species, including the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the largest North American minnow, and the humpback chub (*Gila cypha*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline.** The article attributes losses to:
- Dams and reservoirs. The [[Hoover Dam]] (1936) and [[Truman Dam]] are cited, along with the many Columbia River dams.
- Channelization and shoreline loss. The [[Missouri River]] was shortened by over 45 miles, its surface area reduced 50 percent, and its islands cut from 161 (1879) to 18 (about 75 years later).
- Overfishing, especially of sturgeon for isinglass, smoked flesh, and caviar.
- Poisons, explosives, and chemical controls, including the chemical "Sequoxin" used against squawfish.
- Nonnative species, such as introduced channel catfish that prey on squawfish and trout and bass stocked in place of natives.
- Hybridization, as with rainbow trout crossing with [[Gila Trout]] and mallard-black duck crosses.

**Gar ecology.** Toole's 1971 study in eastern Texas (by Joe E. Toole of the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department) found that alligator gar stomachs contained about 84 percent forage fish and 5 percent gamefish, with no waterbirds. Longnose gar contents were about 71 percent forage fish and 7 percent gamefish. Spotted gars ate nearly four times as many forage fish as gamefish. The author notes that the larvae of some freshwater mussels use gars as hosts, linking gar conservation to mussel survival.

**Sturgeon market history.** Sturgeon were harvested for isinglass, a clarifying agent for wine and beer, and for smoked flesh. Sandusky, Ohio, shipped nearly three million pounds of smoked sturgeon annually by 1866, and over five million pounds in 1885. By 1895 the catch had dropped to one million pounds, and about a decade later merchants could hardly find any. Shovelnose landings on the upper Mississippi fell from over 400,000 pounds in 1900 to 36,000 pounds in 1970.

**Missouri River.** Commercial netters landed over 1,300,000 pounds in Missouri's portion of the river in 1908. The catch is now under 300,000 pounds and declining. Blue catfish of 125 to 200 pounds were once common, but are now rare.

**Columbia River.** Dams have cut sturgeon off from lampreys, their historic prey. Donaldson describes upstream fish as "thin, even emaciated."

**Hybridization.** Hybridization is treated as a product of altered habitat. Black duck-mallard crosses have increased in response to the loss of Atlantic coastal salt marshes.

## Key Findings

- **Gars:** Gars are persecuted but ecologically valuable. Their stomach contents are dominated by forage fish, and they host mussel larvae. Alligator gar life cycles and spawning habitat are poorly known.
- **Sturgeon:** Sturgeon grow slowly and mature late. The article cites females that did not spawn until age 33 and a lake sturgeon that could live more than a century. Artificial propagation attempts since 1875 (Seth Green's four pans of Atlantic sturgeon eggs) have repeatedly failed to produce a reliable hatchery method. Early hatchery success was not repeated.
- **Regulation:** In 1970, a study of the lake sturgeon sport fishery on Wisconsin's Menominee River indicated declining quantity and quality. The state raised the minimum size limit from 42 to 50 inches. The author questions whether maximum length limits, such as those on the Columbia River, would better protect spawners.
- **Regional variation:** Sturgeon maturity varies by region. A 50-inch female lake sturgeon might be about 20 years old and pre-spawning in Lake Huron, but 36 years old and multiply spawned in Lake Nipigon, Ontario. A 20-year-old fish weighed 274 pounds in Lake Winnebago but only 8 pounds in Lake Nipigon.
- **Paddlefish:** Up to 20,000 fishermen fished the Osage River each spring (March 15 to May 15). As many as 5,000 fish are caught annually, averaging about 30 pounds. Males mature at 7 to 8 years, females at 12 to 13. Graham states that the [[Truman Dam]] blocked the spawning run. Missouri biologists have proposed removing the low Osceola dam, and 50,000 paddlefish fry were sent to the Soviet Union in an exchange.
- **Snail darter:** About 20,000 individuals are estimated to exist. Snails make up about 70 percent of their annual diet. The species was listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and the [[Tellico Dam]] was built on its last optimal habitat. About 700 fish were transplanted to the Hiwassee River. Federal spending on the dam exceeded $114 million, and research and transplantation spending was about one-third of $1 million. The author warns that Congress may be asked to exempt the project from Section 7 of the act.
- **Colorado River:** Four of the seven indigenous species are threatened. Squawfish, once up to six feet long and nearly 100 pounds, are now rare. The article describes how dams and introduced species eliminated the fish, and recommends that the Green, Gunnison, and Yampa rivers be designated as critical habitat under Section 7 of the act. The remaining viable populations are in these rivers.
- **Gila trout:** Rainbow trout were stocked in the Gila River "to improve the fishing." They hybridized with the native Gila trout (*Salmo gilae*). A pure strain was found in Ord Creek in Arizona, and fish were moved to Grant Creek.
- **Blue pike:** In 1922, U.S. netters in Lake Erie landed 12,937,796 pounds of blue pike worth $630,293, compared with 3,750 pounds of lake trout worth $333. Pollution, overfishing, and lamprey introduction caused the collapse.
- **Whitefish:** Canada is the largest seller and markets over 20 million pounds annually. Improved gill-net technology lets fishermen work through four feet of ice, and fisheries have pushed into northern Saskatchewan and the Northwest Territories.
- **Atlantic salmon:** Two-winter fish return at 9 to 12 pounds. The Penobscot Salmon Club's 1889 record was about 28 pounds. Restoration depends on water cleanup, and the author notes that salmon populations were recovering in the 1970s. A commercial catch of an 11¼-pound Atlantic salmon on the Connecticut River on June 3, 1975, was treated as unusual.
- **Public engagement:** The article concludes that fish-watching, through films and documentaries, can build public support for threatened species. The author argues that "the osprey is only an extension of the gar, and the bear an extension of the salmon," meaning that aquatic life is linked to terrestrial and avian life.
