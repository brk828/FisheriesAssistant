# Glen Canyon Dam Modifications to Control Downstream Temperatures: Plan and Draft Environmental Assessment (1999)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. [[Bureau of Reclamation]] draft environmental assessment (EA) and plan, dated January 1999, evaluates modifying the intakes of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] so that the release temperature of the Colorado River can be managed. Cold releases (about 8–10°C) from the dam's deep power intakes are considered a constraint on native and [[Endangered Species Act|endangered]] warmwater fishes, especially the [[Humpback Chub]]. The proposed action, a "tiered" cold/warm fishery, would allow summer releases of up to about 15°C, combined with a monitoring program and an [[Adaptive Management]] process. The estimated cost of the preferred design is $15 million, and the no-action alternative keeps the existing cold releases. Comments were due March 26, 1999.

## Background & Description

**Pre- and post-dam conditions.** Before [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was built, the Colorado River warmed from near freezing in winter to about 30°C (86°F) in late summer, according to the executive summary. Other sources give a monthly average high of about 26°C (78°F). The fish community was dominated by warmwater non-native species. Channel catfish made up 90% of the fish community in Glen Canyon (Woodbury 1959, as cited). Post-dam releases are clear and cold, varying little around 8°C (46°F). At closure in 1963 the river held eight native and eight non-native fish species. By 1968 non-natives outnumbered natives, and trout came to dominate the reach below the dam.

**The proposed design.** The preferred "Proposal 2A" removes the top of the existing [[Trashrack Structure]] and installs a gate at the penstock intake. It uses a fixed upper (warm) opening and a lower (cold) opening, and blends water across the eight penstocks to set outflow temperature. The report calls it innovative because it reuses existing structure. Warmwater releases are only possible when the reservoir is between 3,700 ft (full) and 3,670 ft, which is projected for about 85 of 100 years during the May–October season.

**Location.** The study area runs from [[Lake Powell]] through Glen and Marble Canyons and the [[Grand Canyon]] to [[Lake Mead]]. Lake Powell and the first 15 miles below the dam lie within the Glen Canyon National Recreation Area; the remaining 278 river miles are in Grand Canyon National Park.

**Regulatory context.** The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] biological opinion (1994) recommended that Reclamation evaluate and, if viable, implement temperature controls to remove jeopardy to humpback chub. Authority comes from Section 8 of the Colorado River Storage Project Act. Construction is funded by nonreimbursable appropriations, and monitoring is funded by power revenues under the Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1992.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Linked ecosystem.** The downstream food base is built on [[Cladophora Glomerata]], a filamentous green alga that grows because the water is clear. Together with diatoms, the amphipod [[Gammarus Lacustris]], and midges, it supports the aquatic food chain. Nutrient release from the dam is very low, and Lake Powell retains roughly 98% of inflowing total phosphorus.

**Thermal requirements.** Native spawning, egg incubation, and larval survival are reported to need 16–22°C for humpback chub, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and speckled dace. Razorback sucker spawns at 10–22°C. Current releases warm only to about 16°C (61°F) by mile 240, so mainstem spawning is rarely possible. Virtually all documented native spawning occurs in warm tributaries and springs, notably the [[Little Colorado River]].

**Thermal shock and survival.** Young fish descending from warm tributaries into the cold mainstem are thought to die from thermal shock or predation. The executive summary quotes Valdez and Carothers (1998) that swimming ability may be reduced by as much as 98% in cold mainstem water.

**Trout and temperature.** The [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery in the first 15 miles below the dam (Lees Ferry reach) is valued. Current releases sit at the bottom of the optimal range (8–10°C), and the optimum is about 15°C. Growth is reduced by about 8.25% for each degree below 15°C. Whirling disease, not yet found below the dam, is more severe at warmer temperatures, a risk the report flags.

**Competition and predation.** Warmwater releases may also favor non-native competitors, mainly [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Common Carp]]. Channel catfish have been documented eating humpback chub at the Little Colorado River mouth. Native fish live 20–30 years, while non-natives live 2–5 years, so infrequent warm releases (possibly once in five years) may favor natives.

**Lake and reservoir effects.** Surface water in [[Lake Powell]] is already cold enough to cause occasional winterkill of [[Threadfin Shad]], the main forage fish. Modeling for a 140-day release of 15°C predicts cooling of up to 1°C at the surface. Lake Mead inflow would warm and stay nearer the surface, possibly increasing algal productivity in the inflow zone.

**Other species.** The EA also assesses peregrine falcon, southwestern willow flycatcher, bald eagle, Kanab ambersnail, southwestern river otter, osprey, belted kingfisher, and northern leopard frog. Most are judged unaffected or potentially benefited.

## Key Findings

- **Preferred alternative:** Modify the existing intakes (Proposal 2A) at an estimated feasibility cost of $15 million, within Reclamation's spending authority. Release temperatures would be ramped to about 15°C in summer, with cold water blended in to protect the trout fishery.
- **Alternatives rejected on cost:** Proposal 2B (middle-level gate) at $35 million, Proposal 3 (controlled overdraw) at $44.5 million, Proposal 4 (external frame) at $148.5 million, and Proposal 5A (air-controlled curtain) at a life-cycle cost of $26 million. Proposal 5A was judged infeasible. The 4-intake option at $10 million was rejected for lacking flexibility.
- **Spillway option:** Proposal 1 (spillway releases) would have a high annual cost of about $13.5 million based on lost power revenue. The report also notes dissolved-gas risks.
- **Warming magnitude:** The "warm" release is only about 5°C warmer than existing conditions. It would still feel cold to rafters (59°F versus 46°F).
- **Adaptive management is central:** Because ecological interactions are complex, the plan treats operations as experiments, with annual testing, monitoring by the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring And Research Center]], and review by the Adaptive Management Work Group.
- **Two operating goals:** (1) a short, mid-to-late summer release to prevent thermal shock to young humpback chub entering the mainstem, and (2) a longer June–September release of about 15°C to create new spawning areas.
- **Reversibility:** Cold-water releases could be restored at any time. Monitoring is meant to detect harm to endangered fish, and the operation is described as reversible except for construction costs.
- **Costs and economics:** Annual operation and maintenance is estimated at under $100,000 (about $1.2 million annual cost is also cited for the proposed alternative). Power losses are estimated at about $37,000 per month if all eight intakes draw warm water, under 1% of monthly value. A physical model suggested headloss could reach 4 feet.
- **Recreation:** Warmer water would reduce hypothermia risk for rafters. Regional recreation supports about $25.7 million in spending and roughly 585 jobs.
- **Monitoring and schedule:** Intake modification would begin in fiscal year 2000, with temperature control possibly available by summer 2002. If significant unavoidable adverse impacts were identified, Reclamation could reconsult with the Fish and Wildlife Service and pursue the no-action alternative.
- **Key uncertainties:** The effects on competitors, predators, and nutrient cycling are not fully understood. The report acknowledges that "science cannot fully predict" impacts and that predictions are of limited usefulness in complex systems.
