# Central Australian Ichthyology: Historical Survey of Inland Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #Terapontidae

## Executive Summary

This historical review, by G. J. M. Glover and T. C. Sim (published in *Australian Zoologist* 19(3), 1978), traces the discovery and documentation of fishes in the arid interior of Australia. Early collecting was sparse: by the late nineteenth century only six species were recorded from the Centre, and the Horn Scientific Expedition (1896) raised the count to nine. Subsequent expeditions from 1900 onward, including reports of fish in hot bore overflows and on the Nullarbor Plain, extended the known distribution. The text also documents probable human introductions of exotic and poeciliid species.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with citations from the [[South Australian Museum]] Annual Report of 1886, which noted only three fish species from [[Lake Eyre]] and called for collection of small fishes from interior creeks and waterholes to improve knowledge of distribution. Lucas (1894) recorded two fishes near the [[MacDonnell Ranges]]: *Chatoessus erebi* (now *Nematolosa erebi*) and *Therapon fasciata* (now *Amniataba percoides*).

Pre-1896 records covered six species in five families: Clupeidae (1), Terapontidae (2), Atherinidae (1), Plectrochilidae (1) and Gobiidae (1). Only three were adequately described. The authors note that Central Australian localities were absent from [[Macleay]]'s (1881) catalogue and from checklists by Tenison-Woods (1883) and Ogilby (1886).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dispersal mechanisms:** The Horn Expedition report found no evidence for aestivation, proposed floodwaters as a dispersal agent, and suggested that eggs might be carried by birds on their feet. Northern origins were suggested because of no apparent affinity with [[Murray River]] forms.
- **Hot-water populations:** Basedow (1914), during the 1903 [[Government North-West Expedition]], reported "countless small fish" in pools along the overflow of the highly mineralised [[Oodnadatta Bore]], at water temperatures up to 45°C. A net placed over the bore outlet caught no fish, which led Basedow to favour bird-borne eggs over upwelling as the source.
- **Western Plateau drainage:** Fish were long unrecorded here. The Patterson Ranges record of *Leiopotherapon unicolor* (Basedow 1918) was for a time the only fish record from the division. An October survey, by a South Australian Museum expedition, yielded *Nematolosa erebi*, *Neosilurus argenteus*, *Melanotaenia tatei*, *Ambassis castelnaui* and *Leiopotherapon unicolor* from the Wiso and Barkly basins.
- **Introduced species:** *Carassius auratus* (goldfish) and *Gambusia affinis* (mosquitofish) were confirmed in a dam at Woomera in 1978 (Gairdner basin). The goldfish occurrence is almost certainly human-induced, and the poeciliids are considered probably introduced as well.
- **Subterranean and Nullarbor records:** Railway fettlers reported fish on the [[Nullarbor Plain]] (1917–22), and "blind fish" were sighted in a cave pool near the Nullarbor Homestead and Yalata Mission Station, a specimen set of which reached the South Australian Museum in 1968.

## Key Findings

- Only six fish species were recorded from Central Australia before 1896, three of them adequately described.
- The [[Horn Scientific Expedition]] (1896) recognised eight species in five genera, six of them new records for the Centre. After synonymy, the known fauna rose to nine species.
- [[Zietz]] (1896a, 1896b) provided the formal descriptions; [[Spencer and Gillen]] (1912) added ecological commentary.
- Basedow (1914) recorded fish surviving at around 45°C in mineralised bore overflow, and concluded that egg transport by waterfowl was more probable than upwelling.
- [[McCulloch and Waite]] (1917) described *Bidyanus welchi* and *Scortum barcoo* from a 1916 South Australian Museum expedition to the Strzelecki and Cooper Creeks.
- Western Plateau fish records were sparse until a 1970s South Australian Museum survey, after which records were added from the Wiso and Barkly basins.
- Goldfish and mosquitofish from the Woomera dam are interpreted as human introductions.
- The authors frame the historical record as incomplete and dependent on intermittent donor collections, which limits confidence in distribution patterns.
