# Distribution of Fishes in the Dolores and Yampa River Systems of the Upper Colorado Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1975 field study, published in *The Southwestern Naturalist* and produced by the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit]] at [[Utah State University]], documents fish distribution in the [[Dolores River]] (sampled 1971) and the [[Yampa River]] system (sampled 1968–1971) of northwestern Colorado. The work emphasized rare and endangered species. The Dolores yielded 11 species and no rare or endangered forms. The Yampa yielded 22 species, including four rare or endangered fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Sucker]]. The authors conclude that the Yampa is important for reproduction and preservation of the Colorado Squawfish, while the Dolores has little conservation value for native fishes.

## Background & Description

The native fish fauna of the [[Colorado River Basin]] is among the least understood of any major North American river basin. Contributing factors include rugged terrain, low economic and sport value of native species, and extensive habitat loss from dewatering and conversion to reservoirs over the previous 50 years. The native fauna divides into two groups: large-river forms of the main Colorado, Green and larger tributaries, and small-stream forms of the lower basin. Minckley and Deacon (1968) concluded that large forms are virtually extinct in the lower basin, making the upper basin the remaining refuge for these fishes.

Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub were listed as rare and endangered by the [[U.S. Department of the Interior]] (1968). Humpback Sucker and Bonytail Chub were considered very rare (Miller 1972).

**Study areas**
- **Dolores River:** A clear, cool trout stream above Dolores, Colorado, largely diverted for irrigation in summer. The middle reach near Cahone is slow, pooled and sometimes dries completely. The lower reach below the [[San Miguel River]] has long pools, short rapids and high salt load.
- **Yampa River:** Drains northwestern Colorado and a little of southwestern Wyoming, joining the [[Green River]] at Echo Park in [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. The [[Little Snake River]] is its major tributary. The Yampa is poor trout habitat below Craig because of warm summer temperatures and usually stays clear except during rainstorms and spring runoff.

**Methods**
- Dolores: seines (15 ft and 30 ft nets), sampled July 1971 and again late August–early September 1971. Electrofishing failed because of high salinity.
- Yampa: gill nets (1 to 2¼ in mesh), seines and electrofishing (lower one mile each year), with intensive June–August sampling of the lower 4–5 miles (1968–1969) and the lower 10 miles (1970). The entire study area was sampled in 1971.
- Continuous water temperature was recorded in summers 1968–1970 with a 7-day recording thermometer; turbidity was measured in 1968 with a Hach kit.
- Abundance categories: Abundant (A), Common (C), Scarce (S). Failure to collect a species did not prove absence but strongly suggested rarity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dolores River:** Fish were more diverse in the lower and upper sections than the drier middle reach. Chub, dace and flannelmouth sucker dominated the middle reach. Bluehead suckers were restricted to the upper and lower sections, favoring larger riffles. Irrigation use and a record of severe pollution have left the river far from its natural state.
- **Yampa River:** Lower Yampa Canyon, an intermediate zone between tributary and large-river habitat, held the greatest diversity. Native large-river species (Colorado Squawfish, Bonytail Chub) and tributary species (Roundtail Chub) co-occurred. Humpback Suckers favored backwaters and the ponded, silty mouth of the Yampa.
- **Temperature and turbidity:** Water temperatures rose to 68–70 °F by late July in the lower Yampa. The river is cold and turbid during spring runoff, then clears and warms through summer.
- **Trout:** Rainbow, brown and cutthroat trout and Mountain Whitefish occurred mainly in lower Yampa Canyon during fall, winter and early spring, likely moving in from the Green River as water cooled. Rainbow Trout in the Craig–Juniper Springs area were stocked by the Colorado Department of Game, Fish and Parks, and their numbers tracked time since stocking.
- **Introduced and wandering species:** Common Carp, Sand Shiner, Channel Catfish and Largemouth Bass occurred only in the lower, larger Dolores sections. Red Shiner was common in both the middle and lower Dolores. Fathead Minnow and Green Sunfish were most abundant in the lower and upper Dolores. Redside Shiner was abundant throughout the Yampa. Walleye, Largemouth Bass and Bluegill were likely wanderers from upper Green River reservoirs; four walleye, one largemouth bass and one bluegill were taken across the four years.

## Key Findings

**Species richness and rarity**
- Dolores River: 11 species collected (4 native); no rare or endangered forms. Roundtail Chub, Speckled Dace and Flannelmouth Sucker were most common.
- Yampa River system: 22 species collected. Flannelmouth Sucker, Bluehead Sucker, Speckled Dace, Roundtail Chub and Redside Shiner were most abundant. Four rare or endangered species were present, and none were common except Colorado Squawfish in July.

**Colorado Squawfish**
- Abundance in lower Yampa Canyon rose sharply in July when temperatures stabilized at 68–70 °F (Table 3). Fishing effort was similar within each summer, so the increase was considered real.
- Over 90% of squawfish collected during the July increase were ripe males. No ripe females were taken.
- The authors infer that squawfish move up from the Green River as waters clear and warm, probably to spawn. The origin and extent of this migration are unknown.
- Juvenile abundance declined sharply: readily seined in 1968, very few in 1969, none found in 1970. The cause, natural or human-influenced, is unclear.

**Other rare species**
- Humpback Chub: taken in very small numbers; its Yampa presence is considered important for the species' survival.
- Bonytail Chub: rare at Echo Park in this study, versus common there in 1964–1966 (Vanicek et al. 1970).
- Humpback Sucker: rare overall; spring and fall concentrations appear linked to the ponded, silty mouth of the Yampa.

**Range records**
- Sand Shiner (Dolores) is the first published record of this species in the upper Colorado basin; it was also common in the adjoining Colorado River.

**Conclusions**
- Dolores River: little importance for rare fish preservation, since the endangered upper-basin species are large-river forms. Its main value is potential flow contribution to the Colorado. It is currently neutral to possibly detrimental to native fishes.
- Yampa River: very important to preservation of rare and endangered fishes in the Colorado basin because all four rare forms are present, some in small numbers, and some are apparently reproducing.
- The Little Snake River is a typical small, shallow tributary without large-river rare species. Its unregulated flows help keep the Yampa a fairly natural large-river environment, possibly the most natural large-river environment left in the upper basin.
