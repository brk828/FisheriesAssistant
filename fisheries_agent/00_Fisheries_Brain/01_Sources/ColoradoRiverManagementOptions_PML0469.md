# Colorado River Management Options: Workshop Report and Policy Analysis

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #water-policy

## Executive Summary

This document reports on the Colorado River Working Symposium, "Management Options for the Future," held at Bishop's Lodge near Santa Fe, New Mexico, in May 1983. The symposium was convened by the [[Center For Natural Resource Studies]] and attended by about 70 participants, including legal authorities, hydrologists, water managers, interest-group representatives and public officials. Its central argument is that the river's institutions and allocation mechanisms, built for an era of plentiful supply, are being overtaxed by growth, because the river's water is fully (or arguably over-) appropriated. The text addresses the [[Colorado River Compact]], the [[Law Of The River]], [[Salinity Control]] obligations, the [[Mexico Water Treaty]], [[Indian Water Rights]], wilderness and instream-flow values, and proposed solutions, and it closes with seven major issues summarized by [[Gilbert White]].

The document also includes two unrelated magazine items: a piece on telephone industry cost allocation and bypass (including the [[Universal Service Act]] proposal), and a piece on the safety and energy implications of unvented kerosene heaters and electric spot heating. These are summarized briefly at the end.

## Background & Description

**Origins of the compact.** In November 1922, delegates meeting near Santa Fe agreed on the [[Colorado River Compact]], which divided the river's waters between the upper and lower basins. The upper basin states pushed for the compact in 1920 to protect their development from [[California]] growth. Arizona refused to join, so the compact became a six-state agreement. Arizona joined in 1944.

**Legal history.** Key milestones include:
- The compact's 10-year running-flow requirement of at least 7.5 million acre-feet per year at [[Lee's Ferry]].
- The 1952 suit *Arizona v. California*, decided in 1963, which awarded Arizona 2.8 million acre-feet plus its tributary water (about 1 million acre-feet).
- A 1968 authorization of the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), which deleted proposed dams and substituted a coal-fired plant at Page. Arizona guaranteed 4.4 million acre-feet per year to California in exchange.

**Hydrology.** Reconstructed streamflow from tree-ring analysis shows that the compact was drafted on wet-period estimates. More recent data suggest mean annual flow at Lee's Ferry is closer to 14 million acre-feet, not the 16.4 million acre-feet the compact assumed. The text notes that a long-term average flow of about 13.5 million acre-feet per year appears in the figures.

**Keynote and framing.** Governor [[Bruce Babbitt]] of Arizona argued that the [[Law Of The River]] (a set of compacts, court decisions and rules) "has served the past sixty years well" but does not provide adequate guidance for the next century. He stated, "The adversary model is no longer relevant or appropriate," and called for new forums, including possible congressional hearings in the West.

**Sidebar pieces.** The telephone article, drawing on economist Melody's research, argues that basic telephone rates should be based on independently measured local-service costs. It notes that the House had passed a Universal Preservation Act of 1983 proposing a universal service fund and a bypass fee, and that the Senate was considering a similar measure. The heater article, by a staff scientist at Oak Ridge Associated Universities, notes that one million electric spot heaters replacing kerosene heaters could add about 500 MW to the national winter peak demand. Neither topic is about the Colorado River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical changes.** The river between Lake Powell and Lake Mead is described as a different river from the one John Wesley Powell explored in 1869. Before [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was built, the mean flood was 3.5 times the present 800 cubic meters per second. The dam traps almost all fine sediment and debris from the upper basin. Consequently:
- Beaches below the dam were scoured away during the record flood of 1983, while new beaches formed in the lower canyon.
- Reduced flood flows move alluvial debris less often, so rapids in the [[Grand Canyon]] are growing.

**Flow fluctuations.** When air conditioners run in [[Phoenix]] and [[Tucson]], the Bureau of Reclamation increases turbine flow at Glen Canyon Dam. Downstream flows can rise as much as six feet in a few minutes. River runners call these changes "tides" and time their arrival at rapids to the optimum level.

**Salinity.** The text calls salinity the most serious water quality problem in the basin. Salts enter from natural springs and saline groundwater, irrigation return flows, and municipal and industrial discharges. Evaporation and transpiration from reservoirs, irrigated fields and [[Phreatophytes]] concentrate them. The Figure X source breakdown (OCR-garbled in the scan) appears to show roughly 47% natural sources, 37% irrigation and 12% reservoir evaporation. Projected damages to agricultural and urban users in the lower basin could reach $237 million by 2000 without control. The numeric criterion adopted by the [[Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Forum]] is 723 mg/l total dissolved solids at [[Hoover Dam]]. The optimistic projection assumes a flow of 14.0 million acre-feet per year at Lee's Ferry, and a 1981 Bureau of Reclamation study found that local use of saline water for industrial production is among the most cost-effective removal methods.

**Fish and habitat.** Fish are mentioned in several places:
- The [[Humpback Chub]] has been "all but extirpated" in the Grand Canyon, according to a cited report, and the potential ecological damage from reservoir operations has no bearing on current Glen Canyon Dam release criteria.
- The [[Woundfin Minnow]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] habitat in the Virgin River was threatened by the proposed Warner Valley reservoir. A U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biological opinion concluded the reservoir would violate the Endangered Species Act.
- Instream flow requirements for fish habitat figure in controversies over new projects. Only Colorado, among the basin states, recognizes an instream-flow water right.

**Recreation and wilderness.** Travel through the Grand Canyon grew exponentially. More than 16,000 people floated the canyon in 1972, prompting a Park Service permit system. Wilderness advocates fought dams at Echo Park and Glen Canyon in the 1950s, and at Bridge and Marble Canyons during the CAP debates.

## Key Findings

**1. The basin's institutions are strained.** With supply fully or arguably over-appropriated, the participants concluded that existing institutions will be seriously tested. The symposium's stated purpose was a neutral forum for "free-wheeling" discussion of the severity of the problems and possible solutions.

**2. Compact assumptions are optimistic.** The compact's assumed flow of 16.4 million acre-feet exceeds more recent estimates of about 14 million acre-feet. Historical evidence suggests a deep and prolonged drought is a significant possibility.

**3. Droughts are a recurring risk.** Droughts of 10-year duration with mean annual flows between 9.7 and 11.8 million acre-feet have occurred twice in the last 400 years. [[Kneese And Bonem]] concluded that if a ten-year drought starts 15 to 20 years from the time of writing, the upper basin is unlikely to meet its compact commitments even under favorable assumptions.

**4. Mexico's treaty adds pressure.** The 1944 treaty gave Mexico 1.5 million acre-feet per year. After decades of salinity damage in the Mexicali Valley, the 1961 completion of the Wellton-Mohawk drain and the 1974 Salinity Control Act authorized upstream salt-control projects and a desalinization plant scheduled for 1987. The text notes that cheaper alternatives existed but were not considered for institutional reasons. In January 1983, 1,168,000 acre-feet were spilled from Hoover Dam for flood control, about 737,000 acre-feet beyond January downstream requirements, which may be counted against the 1.7 million acre-feet annual treaty obligation.

**5. Indian water rights have a firm legal base but limited use.** The [[Winters Doctrine]] (1908) implied reserved rights for enough water to irrigate reservation lands. Rights claims are large: if only 500,000 acres of the Navajo reservation (about 4%) are practicably irrigable, Navajo entitlements would be about 2 million acre-feet per year and could reach 5 million. Combined Indian entitlements could approach half the river's long-run yield. In the late 1970s, about 60% of irrigable acreage on Indian lands along the river was under cultivation, but only 8% was cultivated by Indians.

**6. Conflict between timing and uses.** [[Getches And Meyers]] identified conflicts between release timing for hydroelectric power and for irrigation, and between agricultural and municipal or industrial uses. Municipal and industrial users can pay far more for water than irrigators, but institutional barriers prevent efficient transfers. Del Gardner argued for a free-market approach, while Ingram, Scaff and Silko argued that equity considerations are equally important.

**7. Proposed solutions.** Workshop participants offered several options:
- Importing new supplies (for example, from the Pacific Northwest), which Kneese and Bonem called the "last and Christmas tree" of traditional distributive politics.
- Conjunctive management of groundwater and surface water, and modern irrigation technology.
- Renegotiation of the compact. [[Paul Bloom]] proposed a congressional deadline after which failure to reach compromise would trigger mandated apportionment litigation. The symposium, however, showed little support for modifying the compact.
- Pricing reform and water markets, with public controls.

**8. Seven issues identified by White.** [[Gilbert White]] summarized seven major issues:
1. The optimum allocation of water among uses, and the meaning of "consumptive use" and "surplus."
2. Structural and nonstructural approaches to salinity, and how responsibility should be allocated.
3. The significance of unquantified federal reserve rights in inhibiting development.
4. The quantity, timing and transferability of Indian water rights.
5. The effect of resolving these questions on the flexibility of water allocation.
6. How much water is needed to sustain the life of the Indians and to protect ecological and recreational values.
7. How the river should be managed to satisfy both social goals and water conservation.

White recommended focusing on "cultivating understanding of where the nation thinks it is going" with the Colorado, rather than on any single institutional change. He concluded that "at such a point in the flow of talk about the Colorado," the basin must act.
