## Executive Summary

This 1980 field study examined resource partitioning between Age-0 [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and four co-occurring fish species — [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] — in the Green River, Utah, to evaluate whether habitat and dietary overlap could support hypotheses of competitive displacement of the endangered Colorado squawfish by introduced species. Using seine sampling, discriminant analysis, and Schoener's overlap index, researchers found that Age-0 Colorado squawfish were most abundant in shallow, low-velocity backwaters, with diel abundance peaks tied to water temperature. High spatial and dietary overlap was found between young Colorado squawfish and [[Red Shiner]] (and to a lesser extent [[Channel Catfish]]), while overlap with [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] was low. Despite the overlap, the authors caution that overlap alone does not demonstrate competition, since resource limitation was not directly measured.

## Background & Description

- **Species of primary concern**: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a large cyprinid historically distributed throughout the Colorado River Basin, now extirpated from the Lower Basin and rare in the Upper Basin. Federally listed as endangered (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service; American Fisheries Society).
- **Comparison species**:
  - [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) — introduced cyprinid, hypothesized competitor.
  - [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) — introduced cyprinid.
  - [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) — introduced ictalurid.
  - [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) — native catostomid.
  - [[Redside Shiner]] (*Richardsonius balteatus*) mentioned as another suspected competitor but not directly analyzed in this study.
- **Decline drivers for Colorado Squawfish**: conversion of free-flowing riverine habitat to lentic reservoir conditions via dam construction (e.g., Flaming Gorge Dam), altered streamflow/discharge/temperature regimes, water quality degradation, and introduction of exotic fish species (Holden 1979; Seethaler 1978).
- **Study location**: Green River, Utah, near km 424, within stretches identified in prior work (Tyus et al. 1982) as areas of relatively high Age-0 Colorado squawfish abundance (river km 50–145 and 400–470 from mouth).
- **Methods**:
  - Fish collected with 0.6-cm-mesh seines from discrete, predetermined microhabitats (backwater, shoreline, side channel, eddy, isolated pool, pool, riffle, run).
  - Environmental variables recorded: water depth, water velocity (Marsh-McBirney flow meter), substrate type (per Hynes 1970), water temperature, sample time, habitat type.
  - Colorado squawfish individually fin-clipped and released for mark-recapture of habitat fidelity/movement.
  - Multiple discriminant analysis (SPSS) used to characterize microhabitat separation among five species.
  - Habitat and dietary overlap quantified via the [[Schoener Index]] (Schoener 1970).
  - Diet composition determined from stomach content analysis (Jacobi and Jacobi 1982), summarized as mean weight percentages per food category.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Age-0 Colorado squawfish preferentially used **ephemeral backwaters** with **low water velocity**, **silt/sand substrate**, and **moderate depth** — consistent with earlier findings (Holden 1978).
- Of 1,124 marked small Colorado squawfish (30–60 mm TL), ~80% were initially captured in backwater habitat; 17% were subsequently recaptured, mostly in the same habitat type, though some moved between backwaters, eddies, and shorelines. Larger juveniles (40–60 mm TL) could traverse higher-velocity areas during rapidly changing water levels to relocate to quiet-water habitat.
- **Diel activity**: Backwater catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) of Colorado squawfish peaked between ~1200–0200 hours, correlated (r = 0.6) with rising water temperature; CPUE outside backwaters peaked 0400–1200 hours but never exceeded backwater CPUE.
- **Discriminant analysis**: A single discriminant function (DF1) explained 82% of among-species microhabitat variance. Contributing variables, in order of importance: water velocity > habitat type > substrate size > water depth > sample time > water temperature.
- **Habitat breadth**: Colorado squawfish, fathead minnow, and red shiner clustered near the center of DF1 (narrow habitat breadth/specialists of low-velocity habitats); channel catfish and flannelmouth sucker were more broadly dispersed along DF1 (wider habitat breadth/generalists).
- **Invertebrate prey base context**: Holden and Crist (1981) documented seasonal invertebrate fluctuations in the same river reach, with lowest densities during spring runoff and highest densities in early fall (when this study occurred), with dipterans as a dominant, abundant taxon — suggesting overlap in diet may reflect shared use of an abundant resource rather than true competition.

## Key Findings

### Spatial (Habitat) Overlap — Schoener Index values
| Species Pair | Overlap Index | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Colorado squawfish – Red shiner | 0.9 | High (biologically significant, >0.6 threshold per Matthews & Hill 1980) |
| Colorado squawfish – Fathead minnow | 0.8 | High |
| Colorado squawfish – Channel catfish | 0.5 | Moderate/low |
| Colorado squawfish – Flannelmouth sucker | 0.4 | Low |

### Dietary Composition & Overlap
- **Small Colorado squawfish (22–40 mm TL)**: Diet dominated by aquatic invertebrates, with some fish remains.
- **Large Colorado squawfish (41–59 mm TL)**: Diet shifted heavily toward fish, predominantly **red shiner** — indicating an ontogenetic shift toward piscivory as length increases.
- **Red shiner** (both size classes) and **channel catfish** (both size classes): Fed heavily on aquatic insect larvae, consuming a wider taxonomic variety than Colorado squawfish; no fish remains found in either introduced species' diet.
- **Fathead minnow** (both size classes): Diet dominated by seston/plant material — diatoms, green algae, and blue-green algae.
- **Dietary overlap findings**:
  - High overlap between small Colorado squawfish and small channel catfish (15–55 mm TL), and with both size classes of red shiner.
  - Overlap declined markedly as Colorado squawfish grew larger and shifted to piscivory.
  - **No significant dietary overlap** between Colorado squawfish (either size class) and fathead minnow.

### Interpretation of Competition
- High spatial and dietary overlap between small Colorado squawfish and red shiner is consistent with (but does not prove) a competition hypothesis.
- Per [[Li 1979]] competition framework, three conditions must be met: (1) resources must be limiting, (2) resources must be shared, (3) sharing must adversely affect one species' fitness (reproduction/survival). This study demonstrated resource **sharing** but did not measure resource **availability/limitation** or demonstrate an **adverse fitness effect** — therefore true competition was not established.
- Authors suggest that dietary overlap with red shiner and channel catfish may simply reflect mutual exploitation of a seasonally abundant invertebrate resource (e.g., high dipteran densities in early fall) rather than competitive interaction.
- No clear spatial partitioning among small fish species was observed, unlike patterns reported in lentic (Werner et al. 1977) or more structured stream systems (Baker and Ross 1981) — likely due to the low-gradient, low-cover nature of the Green River study reaches, more similar to desert stream systems (Matthews and Hill 1980).

### Study Limitations
- Resource availability (invertebrate/fish prey abundance, habitat availability) was not directly measured, limiting the ability to assess whether shared resources were actually limiting.
- Establishing causal competitive effects would require longer-term, more controlled experimental studies.
