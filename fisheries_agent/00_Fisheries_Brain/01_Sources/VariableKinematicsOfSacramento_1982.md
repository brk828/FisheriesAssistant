# Variable Kinematics of Sacramento Perch (*Archoplites interruptus*) Capturing Evasive and Nonevasive Prey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #freshwater-ecology #foraging-behavior

## Executive Summary
This study by Vinyard (1982) uses high-speed video kinematic analysis to quantify how [[Sacramento Perch]] (*Archoplites interruptus*) capture two functionally distinct zooplankton prey types: the nonevasive cladoceran *[[Daphnia magna]]* and the evasive cladoceran *[[Diaphanosoma brachyurum]]*. The fish deploy two discrete attack modes — a "leisurely," energy-conservative strike for nonevasive prey and a vigorous, energetically costly lunge for evasive prey. Naive fish rapidly learned to distinguish the two prey types and adopt the appropriate vigorous attack within 10–20 encounters, demonstrating fast associative learning of prey evasiveness. The vigorous attack mode required roughly four times more energy per attack, and was successful only 55% of the time (vs. 100% for the nonevasive prey), implying an effective per-capture energetic cost approximately eight times greater for evasive prey. In mixed-prey trials, fish preferentially targeted *Daphnia* first, switching to *Diaphanosoma* only once the preferred prey was depleted — supporting a link between capture-cost energetics and prey selectivity in planktivorous fish.

## Background & Description
- **Study specimens**: Progeny of wild-caught adults seined from Washoe Lake, Washoe County, Nevada (spring 1979); reared and spawned in laboratory.
- **Size range**: Ten fish averaged 26 mm TL (SD = 5.2) for the learning-rate observations; a separate set of 8 experienced fish averaged 29 mm TL and 0.33 g for detailed kinematic measurements.
- **Prey size**: Both *Daphnia* and *Diaphanosoma* individuals used ranged from 1.25–1.8 mm, kept approximately size-matched across trials.
- **Key kinematic/morphometric attack variables measured**:
  - Chord length of the body (pre-lunge)
  - Amplitude of body arch/curvature during final flexion before lunge
  - Lunge distance
  - Lunge duration
  - Derived: lunge speed, maximum speed, acceleration, and mechanical work/power output

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Sacramento perch are a centrarchid native to California–Nevada waters; this study's stock originated from Washoe Lake, Nevada — a lentic (lake) freshwater system.
- **Trophic role**: Larval/juvenile planktivore, foraging on cladoceran zooplankton (*Daphnia*, *Diaphanosoma*) and occasionally cyclopoid copepods and ostracods present as low-density contaminants in laboratory cultures.
- **Prey evasiveness spectrum**:
  - *Daphnia magna*: near-completely nonevasive; fish captured them on the first attempt essentially 100% of the time (consistent with [[Vinyard 1980]], [[Confer and Blades 1975]], [[Hall and Hyatt 1974]]).
  - *Diaphanosoma brachyurum*: rapid swimmer that detects and retreats several cm in response to hydrodynamic disturbance from an approaching predator or its mouth suction — a genuinely evasive prey type, and completely novel to these lab-reared fish prior to the experiment.
- **Foraging behavior/plasticity**: The existence of two discrete, rapidly switchable attack modes suggests an adaptive behavioral repertoire suited to patchy, spatiotemporally variable zooplankton resource distributions typical of lake ecosystems.
- **Comparative context**: Learning rate in Sacramento perch (10–20 encounters to develop vigorous attack) is notably faster than reported for another centrarchid, the [[Pumpkinseed]] (*Lepomis gibbosus*), which needed ~100 encounters over 3–5 days to reach 90% attack frequency on nonevasive prey ([[Vinyard 1980]]).

## Key Findings
1. **Two discrete attack modes**: Sacramento perch exhibit a conservative, leisurely approach/strike for nonevasive *Daphnia* and a vigorous, energetically demanding lunge for evasive *Diaphanosoma* — statistically distinct across all kinematic measures (P < 0.01, Student's t-test).
2. **Energetic cost differential**: Vigorous attacks on evasive prey required ~4× the energy per attack compared to nonevasive prey attacks; combined with a lower capture success rate (55% vs. 100%), the effective energetic cost per successful capture of evasive prey is estimated at ~8× that of nonevasive prey.
3. **Rapid learning of prey discrimination**: Naive fish shifted to the vigorous attack mode within 10–20 encounters with novel evasive prey, evidenced by increasing body curvature amplitude over successive encounters (regression: y = 4.53x^0.172, r = 0.51).
4. **Attack mode may be pre-existing, not novel**: The rapid, uniform adoption of the vigorous technique suggests it may already exist in the fish's behavioral repertoire (possibly primed by incidental exposure to evasive cyclopoid copepod contaminants in culture), rather than being newly invented — i.e., what is learned may be *prey identity/category recognition* rather than a new motor pattern.
5. **Mixed-prey trials confirm distinct techniques and preference**: When both prey types were presented together, amplitude measurements averaged 4.2% (Daphnia) vs. 9.5% (Diaphanosoma) of body length; chord lengths were 95.6% vs. 89.7%, respectively (P < 0.01). Capture success was 100% for Daphnia vs. 55% for Diaphanosoma in mixed trials.
6. **Prey selectivity linked to capture cost**: In mixed trials, 66% of the first 10 prey attacked were *Daphnia*; fish generally turned to *Diaphanosoma* only after *Daphnia* had been substantially depleted — supporting the hypothesis that [[Predator Selectivity]] is influenced not only by learned capture-probability information but also by the relative energetic cost of capture.
7. **Kinematic method validation**: Maximum speed (7.86 TL/s) and energy production (1.35×10⁻⁴ J·g⁻¹·s⁻¹) for perch attacking evasive prey closely matched published fast-start values for green sunfish (8.3 TL/s; 1.64×10⁻¹ J·g⁻¹·s⁻¹; Webb 1975a), indicating undisturbed video-based kinematic analysis yields data comparable to traditional electrically-stimulated fast-start protocols.
8. **Broader implication**: These findings support incorporating prey capture energetics — not just encounter/learning probabilities — into models of [[Planktivore Foraging Energetics]] and predator prey-selectivity theory.
