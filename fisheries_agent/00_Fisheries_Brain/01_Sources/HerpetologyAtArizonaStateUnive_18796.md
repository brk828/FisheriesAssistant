# Herpetology at Arizona State University

#herpetology #zoology #university-profile

## Executive Summary

This 1978 *Herp Review* profile describes the herpetological research and teaching strength of the [[Department of Zoology]] at [[Arizona State University]] (ASU) in [[Tempe]], Arizona. It traces the university's institutional history from its founding as a territorial normal school in 1885 to its 1958 renaming, outlines campus and facilities, and identifies faculty and graduate students working on amphibians and reptiles. It also notes that ASU would host the joint annual meetings of three herpetological and ichthyological societies in May–June 1978. The document contains no original field data, sample measurements or statistical results.

## Background & Description

**Institutional history.** ASU was established in 1885 as the Territorial Normal School in Tempe. The name changed several times: Tempe Normal School (by statehood in 1912), Tempe Teachers College (1925), Arizona State Teachers College at Tempe (1929) and Arizona State College at Tempe (1945). Enrollment was 882 at its prior peak, then rose sharply after World War II, exceeding 4,000 by 1949. The "Sun Devils" athletic name replaced "Bulldogs" in 1947. In 1954 the school was reorganized along university lines into four colleges, but kept its college name. A 1958 public referendum, with a 72% voter turnout, approved the name Arizona State University by a two-to-one margin (the source's "2 to J" is read as 2 to 1). In 1977–78 ASU was the largest university in Arizona, with registration well over 35,000.

**Campus and facilities.** The main campus covers 566 acres, and most buildings are less than 20 years old. Key facilities include:
- [[Hayden Library]], with over 1.5 million volumes, including a notable collection of classic and current herpetological literature.
- Gammage Auditorium, a 3,000-seat building designed by Frank Lloyd Wright.
- A 59,000-seat athletic stadium, then being enlarged.
- A PBS-affiliated television station.
- A 320-acre field laboratory (experimental farm) near campus.
- [[Camp Tontozona]], a continuing education facility near Payson, Arizona.

**Academic structure.** ASU had 11 colleges and special divisions. The [[Graduate College]] offered 13 Master's and three Doctoral degrees, including Master of Arts and Master of Science in 36 majors and Doctor of Philosophy in 17 majors, zoology among them. Over 9,000 students were enrolled in graduate programs.

**Zoology department.** The department is housed in the [[Life Sciences Center]] (over 150,000 sq ft), shared with Botany-Microbiology. Zoology has about 62,000 sq ft, including over 4,500 sq ft of systematic collections (herps, fishes, birds, mammals, insects, mollusc shells) and 4,000 sq ft in the Animal Resource Center. The new seven-level west wing contains an electron microscopy laboratory with transmission and scanning instruments. The department has:
- 32 faculty members, with research strongest in ecology.
- About 550 undergraduate majors.
- About 80 graduate students (50 M.S., 30 Ph.D.).
- The department chair, [[Ronald Alvarado]], who studies ionic regulation in amphibians.

**Endowed chair.** The [[Maytag Chair in Vertebrate Zoology]] brings an outstanding vertebrate zoologist each year to teach a course or seminar and strengthen the graduate program. Holders since 1971 include [[Ernst Mayr]], Donald Tinkle, E. Raymond Hall, Robert Briggs, John Emlen, Allen Stokes and Steve Fretwell. C. Ladd Prosser held the chair in Spring 1978, and James R. King was named for Spring 1979.

**Local setting.** [[Tempe]] (population about 100,000) adjoins [[Mesa]] and [[Scottsdale]] on the east side of [[Phoenix]] (population over 1,000,000), forming the main population center of Arizona. The cities lie in the [[Sonoran Desert]] of the [[Salt River Valley]] ("Valley of the Sun") at an elevation of about 500 m. Nearby destinations include the Phoenix Zoo (about 3 miles), Phoenix Sky Harbor airport (about 5 miles), [[Tucson]] (110 miles, with the University of Arizona and the Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum), Flagstaff (150 miles), the Grand Canyon (220 miles) and Nogales, Mexico (175 miles).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document places the department's herpetology within a desert and semi-arid setting. The [[Sonoran Desert]] and the Salt River Valley shape much of the research described. Faculty and students study desert ecophysiology, water loss through integuments, and desert-adapted species such as the [[Desert Tortoise]]. Montane and Mexican systems also feature: the Cuatro Cienegas region of Coahuila, and montane populations of *Cnemidophorus*. ## Key Findings

The document is a departmental profile, so its "findings" are the research programs, collections and notable outputs it reports:

- **Faculty research in herpetology:**
  - [[M.J. Fouquette]] (anuran systematics and ecology, bioacoustics) holds a National Science Foundation grant testing reproductive and ecological character displacement among anurans, especially spadefoot toads. He also studies South American treefrogs and southeastern U.S. chorus frogs, and is responsible for a herpetological collection of about 20,000 specimens.
  - [[James Collins]] studies the evolution of life-history patterns in anurans, including carnivorous morphs in *Scaphiopus* larvae and neoteny in *Ambystoma tigrinum*.
  - [[Neil Hadley]], an environmental physiologist, studies lizard bioenergetics in desert ecosystems and the role of lizard and anuran integuments in water loss.
  - [[Jerry Justus]], a developmental biologist, is funded by the American Heart Association to study heart development and developmental genetics in the [[Axolotl]], as well as developmental rates in *Scaphiopus* and metamorphosis in *Ambystoma tigrinum*.
  - [[Robert Ohmart]] directs the wildlife biology program and runs a current desert tortoise program with several graduate students.
  - [[Mink Minckley]] is primarily an ichthyologist, but has also worked on reptiles and amphibians, especially in Cuatro Cienegas.
  - [[Herbert Stahnke]] (Professor Emeritus) is known for pioneering cryotherapy for poisonous snakebite.

- **Selected graduate outputs:** The text lists recent completed theses and current projects, including:
  - [[Laurie Vitt]] (Ph.D., 1976), lizard reproductive effort, now on postdoctoral work in Brazil.
  - Justin Congdon (Ph.D., 1977), bioenergetics of *Sceloporus jarrovi*.
  - Marc Willner (M.S., 1977), numerical taxonomy of *Bufo*.
  - Current projects include Ph.D. work on *Hyla wrightorum* systematics, *Hyla arenicolor* thermoregulation, enzyme variation in *Cnemidophorus* and effects of *Rana pipiens* larvae on algal communities.
  - Roughly 20 further M.S. and Ph.D. projects are named across amphibians and reptiles, covering topics such as tapeworm infections in *Coleonyx*, biotelemetry of *Gopherus*, and axolotl heart muscle ultrastructure.

- **Institutional milestones:** Enrollment grew from 882 at the earlier peak to over 35,000 by 1977–78. ASU hosted the joint annual meetings of the [[Society for the Study of Amphibians and Reptiles]], the [[Herpetologists' League]] and the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]] from May 31 to June 7, 1978.
