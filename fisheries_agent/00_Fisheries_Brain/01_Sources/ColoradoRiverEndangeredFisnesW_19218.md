## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary scientific paper but an **interagency administrative memorandum** from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS), Region 6, dated October 1986, coordinating the **FY-86/FY-87 Colorado River Endangered Fishes Work Plan**. It outlines a planning meeting held October 30–31, 1986, in Denver, Colorado, intended to review prior-year research accomplishments and allocate discretionary funding for endangered fish recovery studies in the Upper Colorado River Basin. While it lacks direct morphological or physiological data, it provides valuable context on the **species of conservation concern**, the **institutional research priorities**, and the **threats and management actions** (e.g., broodstock development, reintroduction, non-native species interactions) being addressed for the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Chub]].

## Background & Description

No direct morphological, meristic, or anatomical data are presented in this source. The document references **taxonomic uncertainty within the genus *Gila*** (indicating studies to clarify Gila taxonomy and field techniques), suggesting that species-level identification among [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], and roundtail chub (*Gila robusta*) was an active scientific concern requiring dedicated field and laboratory taxonomic work (led by Rosenfeld/Minckley, coordinated by Tyus and Kaeding).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The work plan identifies several ecological and habitat-based research priorities relevant to the niche and life history of the target species:

- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** (referred to by its historic common name "Colorado squawfish"): Studied in relation to competitive/predatory **interactions with introduced [[Northern Pike]]** in the upper [[Yampa River]] — a key non-native species interaction study led by Tyus, addressing potential predation or competition pressure on native fish populations.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Focus on establishing and maintaining a **captive broodstock** (Kaeding/Hamill) and **intensive management** of wild razorback populations within the **Ouray National Wildlife Refuge (Ouray NWR)** management unit, indicating this species' reliance on refugial backwater habitats and artificial propagation support due to depressed wild recruitment.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Subject of **reintroduction and monitoring** efforts (Tyus/Recovery Team), reflecting the species' near-extirpation from the wild and reliance on hatchery-supported population augmentation.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]**: Addressed via a dedicated **Yampa River Humpback Study** (Tyus), suggesting localized population assessment in canyon-bound river reaches typical of this species' preferred swift-water habitat.
- **Instream Flow and Hydrology Studies** (Rose/Green): Tied to the Research and Monitoring Agreement, underscoring the importance of **flow regime management** (including releases from Flaming Gorge Dam, and studies at the "15-mile reach" near Grand Junction and Ruedi/Green Mountain Reservoirs) as a limiting ecological factor for endangered fish habitat.
- **Larval Fish Lab curation** (Muth/Kaeding): Supports systematic identification and archiving of larval specimens, critical for understanding early life-history stages and spawning/recruitment dynamics in the basin.

## Key Findings

- The **FY-86/87 work plan** targeted four federally listed or at-risk fish taxa: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Humpback Chub]], all endemic to the Colorado River Basin.
- Research was organized under two major interagency frameworks: the **Research and Monitoring Agreement** and the **Flaming Gorge Agreement**, reflecting coordinated management between FWS, the Bureau of Reclamation, and the Biology Subcommittee.
- A specific and notable ecological threat identified was the **interaction between non-native Northern Pike and native Colorado Pikeminnow** in the upper Yampa River, an early documented instance of concern over piscivorous non-native species impacting endangered native cyprinids.
- Conservation strategy at this stage relied heavily on **ex-situ propagation** (broodstock development for Razorback Sucker) and **reintroduction programs** (Bonytail Chub), indicating that wild populations of these species were already critically depressed by 1986.
- **Taxonomic clarification within *Gila*** was recognized as a foundational scientific need, underscoring persistent difficulty in distinguishing [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], and roundtail chub in field surveys — an issue with direct implications for accurate population monitoring and listing status.
- Habitat-based instream flow studies (e.g., the "15-mile reach" near Grand Junction, CO) were prioritized as critical to defining minimum flow requirements for endangered fish survival, an early application of instream flow science (via the National Ecology Center, Ft. Collins) to endangered species management.
- A San Juan River study component was also flagged as an emerging topic for future work planning, foreshadowing later expansion of recovery efforts to additional Colorado River tributaries.
