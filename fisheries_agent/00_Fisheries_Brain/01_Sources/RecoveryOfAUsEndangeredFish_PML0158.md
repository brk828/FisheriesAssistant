# Recovery of the Hudson River Shortnose Sturgeon Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #acipenseridae

## Executive Summary

This study argues that the [[Shortnose Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser brevirostrum*) population of the [[Hudson River Estuary]], New York, meets government and scientific criteria for recovery and merits delisting under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The authors combined field sampling from 1994 to 1997 with historical data from the 1970s and monitoring data from Hudson River electric utilities. Population estimates indicate about 60,000 fish in the late 1990s, a 407% increase since the late 1970s, with adults making up about 90% of the population. The authors conclude that combined species and habitat protection, with patience, can produce successful recovery even near one of the world's largest human population centers.

## Background & Description

- **Legal status:** The shortnose sturgeon was among the first species protected under US law (1966 Endangered Species Preservation Act) and was later listed as endangered under the 1973 Act. As of the paper, no fish species protected under the Act had been designated recovered and delisted. The species is also on the [[IUCN Red List]].
- **Threats:** Historical declines were attributed to overfishing, pollution and habitat loss from river damming.
- **Range:** Rivers along the North American Atlantic coast, from the Saint John River, New Brunswick, to the St. Johns River, Florida. The species is amphidromous: marine use is limited to natal river estuaries, and captures in coastal waters are rare.
- **Life history:** Maturity is reached at 8 to 10 years according to the Introduction, while the Discussion states maturity at age 6 or younger. Adults may live 60 years or more.
- **Diet:** Insects and crustaceans, with [[Mollusks]] a major component (reported as 25 to 50% of diet).
- **Seasonal cycle:** Adults congregate at a single [[Wintering Site]] near [[Sturgeon Point]] (river kilometer, rkm, 139) in late fall, migrate upriver to spawn in spring, and disperse through the estuary in summer. Spawning occurs from late April to early May below the [[Troy Dam]], over turbulent, relatively shallow river bottom. Eggs adhere to the substrate, as do newly hatched larvae.
- **Juveniles:** Yearlings grow to about 30 cm TL in their first year and disperse downriver, with winter use centered near Haverstraw Bay (rkm 55 to 63).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study system.** The Hudson River runs 246 km from The Battery (rkm 0) to the Troy Dam (rkm 246) and is a river-estuary gradient with freshwater, brackish and rock-confined reaches.
- Maximum width is 4.8 km (rkm 50) and maximum depth is 66 m (rkm 81). The navigation channel is maintained at 11 m or more.
- Mean ebb and flood current velocities are 0.4 m/s and 0.36 m/s. Tidal amplitude is 0.82 to 1.43 m, and tidal volume is 10 to 100 times river discharge.
- The [[Tidal Freshwater]] limnetic zone (<0.3 ppt) lies upriver of rkm 80. The oligohaline zone (0.3 to 5 ppt) spans rkm 40 to 80, and mesohaline water (5 to 18 ppt) lies farther downriver.
- Sediments grade from sand above rkm 164, to silty sand (rkm 164 to 148), to clayey silt below rkm 148.

**Habitat use.**
- Summer habitat is a deep (regularly 13 to 42 m) tidal freshwater channel. Downstream, the estuary is deeper (18 to 48 m) and brackish.
- The [[Spawning Reach]] is turbulent river habitat between the head of tide and the Troy Dam, surrounded by urban areas and immediately upstream of a port-modified section.
- Shoreline land use has been largely stable since early in the twentieth century, and the authors report that the main sturgeon habitats remain largely intact.

**Protection measures.** NOAA Fisheries reviewed more than 50 proposed actions since 2000 (e.g., dredging, shoreline stabilization, discharge permits) and often specified protective conditions. The approach has been "protect-and-wait," minimizing interference with natural processes, in contrast to hatchery-supplementation strategies.

## Key Findings

**1. Population size and trend**
- A [[Schnabel Method]] closed-population estimate based on nine targeted sampling periods gave 56,708 adults (95% CI 54,862 to 58,072).
- An estimate using all 12 sampling periods gave 61,057 fish (95% CI given as 52,890 to 72,191). The authors treat this as their best estimate of the whole population.
- [[Dovel Study]] estimates for 1979 and 1980 were 12,669 and 13,844 adults (95% CIs 9,080 to 17,735 and 10,014 to 19,224).
- Mean adult abundance increased 407% (95% CI 290 to 580%) from the late 1970s to the 1990s (P < 0.001 for non-overlap with the earlier range).
- Utility trawl survey catch rates (1985 to 1996, about 1,240 samples per year) rose more than fourfold in the second half of the survey. Catch ranged from 2 fish in 1990 to a maximum of 82 in 1993.

**2. Sampling effort and marking**
- 6,265 individual sturgeon were captured and 5,959 were marked: 3,836 at the wintering site, 1,937 at the spawning site and 492 in summer random sampling.
- Recaptures began in 1995 and totaled 269 by the end of the study.
- Gear was [[Gill Netting]] with 5, 10 and 15 cm stretch mesh. Utility monitoring used a 3.0 m beam trawl.

**3. Closed-population assumptions**
- A regression of marked fish in catches against known marked fish in the river gave R² = 0.96, supporting minimal bias from population change.
- Six [[Jolly-Seber Method]] open-population estimates averaged 59,545, with coefficients of variation of 27 to 30% in the initial and final estimates.
- Open and closed estimates did not differ significantly.

**4. Size and condition**
- Mean TL was 655 mm (1970s) versus 665 mm (1990s), a significant increase (P ≤ 0.0001).
- At the wintering site, mean FL was 645 mm (n = 1,220, 1970s) versus 663 mm (n = 4,310, 1990s), an 18 mm difference (P < 0.0001).
- [[Fulton's Condition Factor]] K was similar: 0.845 (1970s) and 0.835 (1990s), comparable to other populations.

**5. Growth of recaptured fish**
- Of 37 fish marked in 1979 and 1980 and recaptured in 1996 and 1997 after 17 to 18 years, the mean FL increase was reported as 28 mm (P = 0.038). The authors describe this as very little growth.
- The four fish that were juveniles at marking all grew, with a mean increase of 178 mm.
- The 33 fish that were adults at marking showed no significant length increase (P = 0.8243).

**6. Distribution**
- Summer random sampling (166 stations, 498 net sets) found sturgeon non-randomly distributed among seven strata (χ² = 16.87, P < 0.01).
- The middle estuary (rkm 108 to 189) held 63% of the catch in 71% of samples, and downstream brackish strata held 35% of the catch.
- Summer, wintering and spawning habitats together form a complete record of the habitat supporting most of the population.

**7. Recovery criteria**
- The [[Shortnose Sturgeon Recovery Plan]] names 19 distinct populations and three criteria: adequate size with a favorable abundance trend, sufficient habitat, and minimal mortality threats.
- NOAA treats 10,000 spawning adults as large enough to be at low extinction risk and adequate for delisting. The authors' total and spawning estimates exceed this threshold by more than 500%.
- Habitat is intact, the abundance trend is positive, and threats such as unregulated harvest, bycatch and pollution are managed through existing regulation. The authors conclude the population qualifies as recovered.

**8. Comparison with other populations**
- Maine's three-river estuary increased from 7,222 fish (1981) to 9,488 (2000).
- The [[Delaware River]] population was 6,408 to 14,880 (1981 to 1984), near 10,000 (2002) and 8,445 (2004).
- The Savannah River had about 97,000 fish (1984 to 1992) but about 3,000 in 1999.
- The Chesapeake Bay rivers had only 18 fish captured since 1996, despite having had sturgeon historically.
- The authors state that the Hudson River population is larger than the sum of all other estimated populations.

**9. Policy context**
- The authors note calls since the 1990s to revise the Endangered Species Act, and cite a claim that most endangered species (63%) will not reach recovery through habitat protection alone.
- The authors argue that more consistent status monitoring is needed, since few data are collected after recovery efforts.
- They propose that protect-and-wait strategies may suit slowly growing populations in the largest undammed rivers, while southern rivers such as the Savannah and Altamaha face bycatch in shad gillnet fisheries.
