# Snowmelt Runoff Efficiencies on Arizona Watersheds

#hydrology #watershed-science #snowmelt

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service and University of Arizona research report (Research Report 274, Summer 1975) documents **[[Snowmelt Runoff Efficiency]]** for 14 experimental watersheds across Arizona's snow zone. Efficiency is the share of on-site [[Snowpack Water Equivalent]] that leaves the watershed as surface runoff. The authors argue that water-yield improvement practices should target sites with high efficiency, because snowpack accumulation alone does not determine yield. Efficiency values were computed for 32 data-years. Exploratory [[Step-Wise Regression]] on 10 inventory-prediction variables, using 5 watersheds and 14 data-years, produced tentative equations. The authors describe these as preliminary.

## Background & Description

Snowpack water yield depends on two factors: (1) snowpack accumulation on site, and (2) snowmelt runoff efficiency. Earlier research had concentrated on accumulation. The report addresses the second factor, which the authors describe as necessary for prescribing land management practices for water-yield improvement.

Efficiency is calculated as surface runoff divided by precipitation input, adjusted for change in snowpack water equivalent, multiplied by 100. The equation can be applied to any time span, but only where snowpack was measured on a watershed basis. Measurements were timed to hydrologic events: accumulation and melt periods, peak accumulation, maximum daily snowmelt runoff, and cessation of runoff.

The study watersheds span mixed conifer forest, mountain grassland, and ponderosa pine forest. Most were gauged with 120° V-notch weirs, and snowpack was sampled with federal snow tubes. Some records were extended with nearby [[Snow Course]] data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation and land history**
- **[[Mixed Conifer Forest]]** (North and South Forks of Thomas Creek, uncut): overstory includes Engelmann spruce, blue spruce, Douglas-fir, white fir, corkbark fir, ponderosa pine, southwestern white pine, and quaking aspen.
- **[[Mountain Grassland]]** (West and East Forks of Seven Springs): dominated by Arizona fescue, forbs, and sedges, grazed in summer by livestock.
- **[[Ponderosa Pine Forest]]**: ranges from uncut to clearcut or selectively cut conditions. Examples include West and East Forks of Castle Creek, the Heber watersheds, and the Beaver Creek watersheds.
- **North Fork of Workman Creek**: converted in steps from mixed conifer overstory to perennial grass and shrubs.

**Physiography and climate**
- Elevations range from about 6,700 ft (Workman Creek) to 9,500 ft (Seven Springs).
- Soils are mostly basalt-derived. Others are alluvial, sandstone, and quartzite-derived.
- Annual precipitation ranges from roughly 14 to 61 inches, depending on the watershed.

**Measurement scope**
- Watershed sizes range from 28 acres (Heber HE-2) to 1,163 acres (East Fork of Castle Creek).

## Key Findings

**1. Efficiency varies widely among sites and years.** Seasonal totals ranged from 2.0% (South Fork of Thomas Creek, 1972) to 92.0% (Beaver Creek Watershed 9, 1973). Representative totals:

|---|---|

| South Fork Thomas Creek (mixed conifer) | 1966: 62.8%; 1969: 8.7%; 1972: 2.0% |
| North Fork Workman Creek (converted) | 1969: 40.9%; 1970: 23.2% |

**2. Peak-accumulation efficiencies are often higher than full-season values.** Several watersheds show a higher efficiency when the calculation starts at peak snowpack accumulation. For example, the North Fork of Thomas Creek in 1973 rose from 49.0% (full season) to 61.2% (peak to end of runoff).

**3. Tentative inventory-prediction relationships.** Ten variables were tested: overstory basal area, overstory stems, slope, aspect, elevation, drainage density, antecedent moisture, total seasonal precipitation, peak snowpack accumulation, and duration of surface runoff. Interaction terms were also evaluated. The regression equations (Table 1 of the report) are described as tentative.
- Total seasonal precipitation was **not** among the most significant variables for efficiency before peak accumulation. Peak snowpack accumulation did appear in some equations. The authors hypothesize that the timing of precipitation through the accumulation-melt period matters as much as the total.
- Overstory stems showed a **positive** relationship with efficiency after snowmelt runoff ended. This contrasts with many studies reporting inverse links between forest density and water yield. The authors suggest stems may be an index for another variable, such as elevation, which correlates positively with efficiency.

**4. Management implications.** Efficiency could help managers select sites for water-yield improvement, and could support predictions of snowpack yield if long-term values prove consistent. The authors state that the findings are only a starting point.

**5. Research needs.** The authors call for further study of differences among watersheds within a year, among years within a watershed, and across both. They also want better identification of variables with probable causal relationships to efficiency.

**Scope note:** The report concerns hydrology and forest management. It contains no fish, invertebrate, or aquatic-community data.
