# Deuterium Depletion in Great Basin Groundwater and the Potomac Horse Fever Agent

#hydrogeology #paleoclimatology #rickettsiology

## Executive Summary

This document combines two unrelated research papers from *Science* (1985). It contains **no fish data**. The first paper reconstructs a two-million-year deuterium (δD) record from fluid inclusions in dated calcite veins in the southern [[Great Basin]], interpreting a 40 per mil depletion as a consequence of Pleistocene uplift of the [[Sierra Nevada]] and [[Transverse Ranges]]. The second paper reports the isolation and characterization of a rickettsial agent of [[Potomac Horse Fever]], proposed as [[Equine Monocytic Ehrlichiosis]]. Both papers are of geoscience or veterinary-microbiology interest; neither addresses fish, fish habitat, or aquatic invertebrates.

## Background & Description

**Paper 1: Groundwater paleoclimate.** The study examined near-vertical calcitic veins in Pliocene and Pleistocene sediments at [[Ash Meadows]], Nevada, and [[Furnace Creek Wash]] in [[Death Valley]], California. The veins mark paleo-groundwater flow paths within a major regional flow system. Veins are 1 mm to 1 m thick, finely laminated, and traceable for tens of meters vertically and hundreds of meters horizontally. Vein ages, derived from uranium-series dating, range from about 64,000 years to 1.7 million years, with veins 10A and 10B possibly as old as 2.6 million years. Deuterium in [[Fluid Inclusions]] was measured by a modified extraction procedure (heating, vacuum crushing, uranium reduction to hydrogen) and expressed relative to [[Standard Mean Ocean Water]] on the V-SMOW/SLAP scale.

**Paper 2: Potomac horse fever.** The disease is characterized by fever, anorexia, leukopenia, and occasional diarrhea, and is fatal in about 30 percent of affected animals. It occurs seasonally (June to October), was first reported near the Potomac River in Maryland in 1979, and has since been found across the eastern United States and in Europe. The authors isolated a rickettsia from cultured blood monocytes of an experimentally infected pony and reproduced the disease in a healthy pony.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Groundwater setting (Paper 1):** Water-bearing fractures in the regional carbonate aquifer are coated with calcite or dolomite. The authors argue this coating limits water-clay exchange of hydrogen during flow from recharge to discharge areas. The Ash Meadows subbasin has a catchment of at least 12,000 km².
- **Modern reference waters:** Modern recharge δD is about −99 per mil. Late (?) Wisconsin discharge from major artesian springs is about −102 per mil. Modern recharge comes principally from winter-spring precipitation, which is 45 percent lighter in deuterium than summer rain.
- **Climatic drivers:** Pacific winter-spring storms are the main moisture source. Orographic uplift of the Sierra Nevada and Transverse Ranges is proposed to have progressively stripped deuterium from inland-bound storms.
- **Equine disease setting (Paper 2):** Transmission was experimentally achieved by blood transfusion, and the seasonal pattern points to vector-linked or environmental factors, though these are not tested in the excerpt.

## Key Findings

### Paper 1: Deuterium Depletion Record

- **Main result:** δD values of fluid inclusions decline progressively with decreasing age over the last 1 to 2 million years, a total reduction of about 40 per mil.
- **Late Pliocene values:** Laminae 10B-5 to 10B-7 and 10A-5 to 10A-8 average about −60 per mil, roughly 40 per mil heavier than modern recharge.
- **Alternative explanations tested and rejected:**
  - Water-mineral exchange, because fractures are calcite- or dolomite-coated.
  - Recrystallization, based on planar crystal boundaries, increasing crystal size within laminae, and absence of poikilotopic textures.
  - Diffusive loss of H₂O from inclusions, because the observed distribution is the reverse of what diffusion would produce.
- **Milankovitch cycles:** Rejected as the main driver because the overall change is unidirectional. Minor reversals may reflect climate cycles, but dating accuracy and secondary inclusions prevent firm conclusions.
- **Uplift estimate:** The central Sierra Nevada rose about 600 m in the past 2 million years. The Transverse Ranges rose to 2000–3000+ m in the last 1 million years. Using deuterium-altitude lapse rates of 25–50 per mil per 1000 m, the authors calculate a depletion of 15–30 per mil from a 600 m rise.
- **Alternative hypotheses:** Changing moisture-source contributions (polar, modified polar, tropical air), or greater late Pliocene summer precipitation. The authors note both require a mechanism for progressive depletion, which cyclic glacial-interglacial variation would not supply.
- **Comparison:** The 40 per mil depletion is several times larger than the change proposed for the entire Tertiary in the western United States.
- **Interpretation:** If the depletion reflects uplift, it serves as a proxy for increasing Pleistocene aridity in the southern Great Basin.

**Data quality caveats:**
- Water analyses reproduced to ±1 per mil.
- Vein analyses showed standard deviations of 2 to 11 per mil, with vein DH2 the least reproducible.
- Initial ²³⁴U/²³⁸U ratios were 2.76 ± 0.09 (Ash Meadows springs), 2.41 ± 0.22 (Furnace Creek springs), and 2.67 ± 0.11 (vein DH2 average). The authors state that errors of a few hundred thousand years would not change the 40 per mil finding.

### Paper 2: Potomac Horse Fever Agent

- **Isolation:** A rickettsia was isolated in cultured blood monocytes of an experimentally infected pony (50 ml whole citrated blood inoculated intravenously). The organism grew in primary canine blood monocyte cultures.
- **Transmission:** A healthy pony inoculated with infected monocytes developed the disease. The organism was reisolated, and necropsy showed typical pathology.
- **Serology (Table 1, IFA test):**
  - Potomac horse fever agent (PHFA) antisera from experimentally infected horses reached titers of 1:160 to 1:2560 against PHFA, and 1:20 to 1:320 against *E. sennetsu*.
  - Naturally infected horses (8 sera at the lowest group) reached up to 1:5120 against PHFA and 1:640 against *E. sennetsu*.
  - Cross-reactivity with *E. canis* and *E. equi* was low (≤1:20 for PHFA-positive sera).
  - Normal horse and canine sera were negative.
- **Conclusion:** The agent is related to members of the genus *Ehrlichia*, particularly [[Ehrlichia Sennetsu]]. The authors propose the name **equine monocytic ehrlichiosis** because the disease occurs beyond the Potomac region and in Europe.
