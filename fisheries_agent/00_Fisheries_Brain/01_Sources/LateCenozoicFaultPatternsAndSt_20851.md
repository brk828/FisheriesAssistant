# Late Cenozoic Fault Patterns and Stress Fields in the Great Basin and Westward Displacement of the Sierra Nevada Block

#structural-geology #tectonics #basin-and-range

## Executive Summary

This document is a geology research article, not an aquatic or ichthyological study. It contains no fish, invertebrate, plankton, or water-chemistry data, so the biological frontmatter fields are recorded as N/A. Its subject is the pattern of late Cenozoic (post-Oligocene) faulting in the [[Great Basin]] of the [[Basin and Range Province]] and what that pattern implies about crustal extension and stress. The author argues that the faults record two deformational fields with contrasting amounts of extension, and that this contrast may be linked to westward movement of the [[Sierra Nevada Block]] and southward narrowing of the Great Basin.

## Background & Description

The article is a 1976 paper in *Geology* (vol. 4, pp. 489–494) by Dean Hendricks, based at Pennsylvania State University. It reviews three competing models for late Cenozoic deformation:

- **Megashear model:** Right-lateral faults of the Great Basin, together with the [[San Andreas Fault]], form part of a large right-lateral shear system. Normal faults are second-order, tension-gash features. Cited work includes Becker (1934), Carey (1958), and Wise (1963).
- **Simple extension model:** Normal faults are first-order features that record westward, non-rotational crustal spreading. Strike-slip faults are considered relatively unimportant. Roots are in the work of G. K. Gilbert and W. M. Davis.
- **Conjugate shear model:** Strike-slip faults are conjugate shears produced by north- to northeast-directed compression, and normal faults are release features.

Hendricks notes objections to each model. The simple extension model does not accommodate the complementary strike-slip faults, the megashear model lacks evidence for right-lateral displacement on its northeast margin, and the conjugate shear model would be expected to produce low-angle thrusts rather than high-angle strike-slip faults.

The author proposes a **differential extension model** instead. In this model, the fault pattern records westerly extension of different magnitudes across the Great Basin, with no oblique component.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper concerns tectonic and crustal processes, not ecosystems. Its environmental content is limited to physical geography and geology:

- The Great Basin is roughly 900 km wide at latitude 40°N. The southern part is about 350 km wide.
- The southern Great Basin, including much of Field II, lies on average about 1 km lower than the central Great Basin. Hendricks suggests this may reflect crustal thinning from greater extension.
- The boundary between the two deformational fields coincides with a westerly extension of the [[Intermountain Seismic Belt]].
- The area is bounded by the relatively rigid [[Colorado Plateau]] and [[Middle Rockies]] to the east, the [[Sierra Nevada]] to the west, and the [[Walker Lane]] belt, which contains major right-lateral faults.

## Key Findings

**1. Two deformational fields with contrasting extension**

- **Field I (northern and central Great Basin):** Steeply dipping normal faults and gently tilted blocks, with a preferred north-northeast trend. Estimated extension is about 10 percent. Field I is characterized by horst and graben structure.
- **Field II (between Walker Lane and the Sierra Nevada, extending across the narrow southern Great Basin):** Most complementary strike-slip faults are concentrated here, along with abundant normal faults. Extension of 50 percent or more is indicated in some areas. Palinspastic restoration of steeply tilted blocks along low-angle normal faults gives 30 to 50 percent extension, and possibly more.

**2. Strike-slip faults**

- Northwest-striking right-lateral faults and northeast-striking left-lateral faults are interpreted as conjugate shears in a primarily extensional environment.
- Many fault terminations and irregularities occur where strike-slip faults join normal faults or each other, which supports an extensional origin.
- Reported displacements vary widely:
  - Las Vegas shear zone: as much as 50 km, a figure attributed to Longwell (1971), which apparently dies out rapidly northwestward.
  - Death Valley region: as much as 150 km ascribed to right-lateral faults and hypothetical oroclinal folds (Stewart and Poole, 1974). Hendricks regards this as contradicted by Precambrian stratigraphic evidence.
  - Northern Death Valley-Furnace Creek fault zone: about 48 km of right-lateral offset of Jurassic plutons (McKee, 1968), with only a small part thought to have occurred since mid-Tertiary time.
  - Pahranagat shear system: as much as 14 km of left-lateral displacement (Tschanz and Pampeyan, 1970).
  - Lake Mead area: cumulative left-lateral displacement of as much as 65 km (Anderson, 1973).

**3. Extension estimates from fault geometry**

- Published estimates for Field I are 4 to 16 percent (Gilluly, 1970), 5 to 10 percent (Stewart, 1971), and 10 percent (Thompson and Burke, 1973).
- A 10 percent extension implies an absolute extension of roughly 80 km at latitude 40°N.
- If the southern Great Basin (about 350 km wide) was extended by 30 percent, it would also widen by about 80 km.

**4. Westward movement of the Sierra Nevada block**

- Eaton (1932) first proposed preferential westward movement of the Sierra Nevada block. Smith (1962) estimated 60 to 80 km of left-lateral movement along the [[Garlock Fault]].
- The bend in Walker Lane, where fault strike changes from about N30°W to about N50°W, is interpreted as evidence for right-lateral dislocation along the north margin of the block.
- Taubeneck (1971) estimated about 50 km of Cenozoic offset in a broad belt that includes Walker Lane faults.
- Lawrence (1976) identified four west-northwest-trending right-lateral zones in Oregon (Mount McLoughlin, Eugene-Denio, Brothers, and Likely fault). He proposed that they function as tear faults contributing to a westward bulge of the continent.

**5. Stress field interpretation**

- Using Anderson's (1951) classification, the author proposes that minimum horizontal compressive stress is oriented west-northwest to east-southeast across most of the Great Basin.
- Maximum compressive stress is vertical where normal faults dominate (steeply dipping, about 60° or more) and horizontal where strike-slip and low-angle faults dominate.
- Carr (1974) independently reported extension at the [[Nevada Test Site]] in the same direction as the minimum principal stress, which supports this interpretation.

**6. Unresolved problems**

The author identifies four open questions:

1. Explaining the superposition of strike-slip and normal faults.
2. Determining the origin of low-angle normal faults, and whether they are deep crustal features or superficial, gravity-driven ones.
3. Relating the fault system to the deep crust and upper mantle.
4. Establishing the genetic, spatial, and temporal relation between fault patterns and [[Volcanism]].

**7. Source status**

The paper's conclusions are based on mapped geology, palinspastic reconstruction, and secondary citations. Hendricks states that some assumptions, including that strike-slip displacements east of the Sierra Nevada are modest, are subject to future testing.
