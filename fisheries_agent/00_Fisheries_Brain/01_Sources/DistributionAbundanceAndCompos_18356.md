# Distribution, Abundance, and Composition of Fishes in Bright Angel and Kanab Creeks, Grand Canyon National Park, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #salmonidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 University of Arizona M.S. thesis by Edward (Ted) Osgood Otis IV documents seasonal distribution, abundance, and composition of fishes in two [[Grand Canyon]] tributaries of the [[Colorado River]], [[Bright Angel Creek]] and [[Kanab Creek]], from January 1992 to June 1993. Sampling used snorkel surveys, electrofishing, seining, and drift and minnow traps. The study also documents spawning of [[Bluehead Sucker]] (Chapter 3) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Chapter 4) and makes recommendations for management of native fishes. Bright Angel Creek is dominated by introduced [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], with native suckers present mainly during spawning. Kanab Creek is dominated by native [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]], with non-native species largely confined to the confluence.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River's native fish fauna is largely endemic and has declined sharply since closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963. Miller (1959) recorded 35 native species in the basin; only five native species remained in Grand Canyon between Lakes Powell and Mead: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Humpback chub and razorback sucker are federally listed as endangered. Tributaries had received little study before this work, and the author argues they are essential to the persistence of native fishes.

Study sites:
- **Bright Angel Creek:** drains about 262 km² of the Kaibab Plateau; the lower 14.3 km was studied, with mean width about 5.4 m, cobble-dominated substrate, and temperatures of 5–21 °C. It is one of eight dolomitic tributaries with high calcium and magnesium.
- **Kanab Creek:** drains about 6,013 km² in Utah and Arizona; the lower 10.0 km was studied, with mean width about 5.2 m, slow and shallow flow, and temperatures of 9–30 °C. It is high in sulfate and low in nitrogen and phosphorus.

Methods combined three size classes (young-of-year, juvenile, adult) for each taxon, standard density estimates (SDE, fish per 100 m²), Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney tests, and PIT tagging of 109 native fish (Appendix IV).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dam effects:** Decreased mainstem temperatures from Glen Canyon Dam have made it easier for trout to establish in tributaries, and loss of high spring flows may have limited native fish access for spawning.
- **Predation:** Brown trout appear to have replaced rainbow trout as the dominant trout in Bright Angel Creek since the 1970s, coinciding with a marked decline in speckled dace.
- **Barriers:** Natural barriers at 14.3 km (Bright Angel Creek, waterfall) and 6.2 km (Kanab Creek) limited upstream movement of most fish; a further barrier at 3.2 km on Kanab Creek restricted upstream movement of adult bluehead suckers.
- **Parasitism:** The copepod [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] infected 10.4% of fish handled in Kanab Creek in June 1993 and was associated with deaths of young-of-year and juvenile native fish.
- **Temperature:** Summer temperatures in Kanab Creek exceeded 30 °C; adult bluehead suckers were virtually absent beyond the confluence in summer.
- **Floods:** Two large floods in August and September 1992 caused fish kills and bedload movement in Bright Angel Creek.

## Key Findings

**Bright Angel Creek**
- Brown and rainbow trout dominated throughout the study. Brown trout comprised 80.6% (± 5.77, n = 4 periods) of trout stream-wide and 57.4% in the lower 3.0 km, rising to 66.3% in winter.
- Native flannelmouth suckers were present mainly during spawning in March. Spawning occurred at depths of 19–41 cm (mean 28.2 cm), mean column velocities of 0.23–0.89 m/s (mean 0.57 m/s), and over loosely compacted gravel and rock. Spawning concentrated in the lower 1.2 km; no young-of-year were found.
- Speckled dace were markedly less abundant than in the 1976–1978 studies of Minckley (1978): 13 dace were caught in over 1,000 trap-hours, versus abundant catches earlier. Dace persisted in an isolated spring, Willow Spring.
- Juvenile bluehead suckers were not observed; adults spawned (young-of-year present each June), but rearing did not appear to occur in the stream.
- Trout stomachs (10 fish > 250 mm) contained no vertebrate remains; 25–50% of trout examined contained nematodes.
- A creel census trend shows rainbow trout falling from 97% of catch (1977–1979) to 76.5% (1988), with brown trout rising to 22.5%.

**Kanab Creek**
- Four native and six non-native species were recorded. Non-native species (fathead minnow, carp, channel catfish, green sunfish, and one rainbow trout) were confined to the confluence area.
- Bluehead suckers spawned in April–early May 1993 at 5 sites in the lower 3.2 km, in water < 25 cm deep, currents < 0.25 m/s (mean 0.20 m/s), and loose pebble-gravel substrate. Peak spawning was estimated at 17–23 April 1993.
- Adult bluehead suckers were absent beyond the confluence in summer; juveniles remained year-round.
- Speckled dace were found throughout the lower 10.0 km and above the 6.2 km barrier, and spawned throughout much of the year.
- Lernaea infection reached 10.4% of 335 live fish in June 1993; no infection or dead fish were found above the 6.2 km barrier.
- A single adult [[Humpback Chub]] was observed at night at the mouth in June 1993, the first report of adult humpback chub in Kanab Creek.

**Spawning and conservation**
- Bluehead suckers spawned in Kanab Creek in April–May 1993, with spawning on a receding hydrograph near base flow. Average mean length at spawning sites was 198 mm; males as small as 94 mm and females as small as 102 mm were mature.
- Recommended research: determine whether flannelmouth sucker fry emigrate immediately, the effect of predation and bedload on recruitment, and extent of stock mixing.
- Recommended management: GCNP should ensure rock dams in lower Bright Angel Creek are breached before February, when flannelmouth sucker spawning migrations begin.
- Recommended continued standardized quantitative tributary surveys and further study of trout predation and [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] effects on native fish, particularly given the proposed multi-level intake at Glen Canyon Dam.
