# Fishes of Utah: Annotated Checklist and Bibliography

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a portion of an annotated checklist of the fishes of Utah, authored by William F. Sigler (Department of Wildlife Resources, Utah State University) and Robert Rush Miller (Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan), published in 1963. It covers taxonomic notes for several groups, including whitefishes ([[Bear Lake Whitefish]] and related *Prosopium* forms), [[Arctic Grayling]], and the families Cyprinidae and Catostomidae. It records native ranges, introductions, nomenclatural changes, and conservation concerns, and it closes with extensive general and cited-in-text bibliographies. The main themes are the decline of native [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes, the history of introduced species, and unresolved taxonomy and hybridization.

## Background & Description

The checklist entries follow a consistent format: scientific name, author, common name, and a short note on taxonomy, distribution, or status. Species introduced by people are marked with an asterisk. Several names are recommended revisions, reflecting work by Miller, Hubbs, and others that revised genus assignments and separated species previously grouped together.

Notable taxonomic points include:

- **Whitefishes (*Prosopium*):** Identification is difficult. The [[Bear Lake Whitefish]] occurs only in Bear Lake, and the [[Bonneville Whitefish]] is the most abundant of the forms there. Gill raker counts, body measurements, and sex-related variation are discussed as diagnostic characters, with some specimens remaining unassigned.
- **Leatherside chub:** Previously placed in *Snyderichthys*, it is referred to *Gila copei* based on unpublished osteological studies by Uyeno.
- **Colorado chub complex:** The authors recommend the name Colorado chub for *Gila robusta*, which is divisible into several subspecies, and note confusion with the common name "bonytail" used for *G. r. elegans*.
- **Speckled dace:** One of the most widespread and variable fishes in the western United States, with named Utah subspecies in the Sevier and Colorado basins.
- **Virgin River spinedace:** Formerly listed as *Lepidomeda vittata*, but recognized as a distinct species by Miller and Hubbs (1960).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Utah's fish fauna reflects the region's desert hydrography. Many native species are restricted to particular drainages, and the authors link fish distribution to the hydrographic history of the Great Basin and Colorado River systems. The text emphasizes habitat change as the main threat to native fishes:

- Dams and impoundments are cited as the driving cause of decline in swift-water fishes, including the [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], which the authors describe as doomed or rapidly disappearing.
- Destruction of habitat is named as the reason the Colorado squawfish is declining.
- Hybridization between closely related species, especially among suckers in the genera *Catostomus* and *Xyrauchen*, is documented from nature and linked to the presence of introduced or altered populations.

Introduced species and their effects are a major theme:

- **[[Common Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*), native to Asia, was introduced to Utah about 1881 from Washington, D.C. It is now abundant in warm waters and occurs in many colder ones.
- **[[Arctic Grayling]]** (*Thymallus arcticus*) was introduced near Salt Lake City in 1889 and planted again between 1899 and 1902, but few were taken by fishermen by 1903. Annual plantings from 1934 to 1953 in high lakes and streams of Uintah, Duchesne, and Summit counties were followed by self-sustaining populations in several lakes.
- **Bullhead minnow** (*Pimephales vigilax vigilax*) was accidentally introduced into the Sevier River drainage on October 7, 1953, along with channel catfish shipped from San Angelo, Texas. Whether it has become established is unknown.
- **[[Mosquitofish]]** (*Gambusia affinis*) is documented in Utah through notes by Rees (1934, 1945), and its use in mosquito control is referenced.
- Game and forage introductions are summarized in Popov and Low (1953), which is cited throughout.

Other species with ecological notes include the [[Walleye]] and [[Channel Catfish]] in [[Utah Lake]], and the [[Utah Chub]] studied in several reservoirs and lakes (including Panguitch, Navajo, Fish, and Scofield).

## Key Findings

- **Native Colorado Basin fishes are declining.** The [[Humpback Chub]] persists in only a few swift-water locations, including the bottom of Grand Canyon, the Colorado River near Moab, the White River near Bonanza, and a section of the Green River between Wyoming and Colorado. Dams are the main cause.
- **The [[Woundfin]] is restricted to the Virgin River drainage**, and the [[Virgin River Spinedace]] is recognized as a distinct species within a unique group of Colorado Basin minnows.
- **Several fishes have uncertain or evolving status.** The webug sucker (*Catostomus fecundus*) remains taxonomically uncertain, and the status of the bullhead minnow introduction is unknown.
- **Taxonomic revisions are proposed.** The leatherside chub is moved to *Gila*, the Colorado chub name is recommended for *Gila robusta*, and the Virgin River spinedace is treated as a separate species.
- **Introductions have had mixed results.** Carp became abundant. Grayling plantings produced self-sustaining lake populations only after decades of stocking, and the outcome of several other introductions was not documented.
- **Fish populations are shaped by hybridization and distribution history.** Hybrid suckers from several Utah localities, including La Verkin and Strawberry Reservoir, are described, and the authors call for more study of their parentage.
- **The bibliography is extensive.** It is divided into General references (regional keys and checklists for Iowa, Colorado, Nevada, California, Montana, and Washington, among others) and Cited in Text, which includes many theses and papers on life histories, growth, and ecology of Utah species such as the [[Utah Sucker]], [[Utah Chub]], [[Least Chub]], and sculpins.
