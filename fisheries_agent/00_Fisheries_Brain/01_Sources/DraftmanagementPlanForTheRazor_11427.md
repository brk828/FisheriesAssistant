# Management Plan for Razorback Sucker in California

#ichthyology #native-fish #Catostomidae #endangered-species #Colorado-River #conservation-management

## Executive Summary

This 1985 management plan by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] addresses the critically endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), once abundant throughout the [[Colorado River]] drainage but now reduced to perilously few individuals. The plan presents a 10-year (1986-1995) recovery strategy focused on securing existing populations in [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] and [[West Pond]] (Imperial County) and reestablishing viable populations in the mainstream lower Colorado River through propagation, grow-out facilities, and systematic stocking. The species was state-listed as endangered in January 1974. Without active management intervention, this monotypic genus will continue declining toward extinction in California waters.

## Background & Description

[[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was first described by Abbott (1861) as *Catostomus texanus* from the Colorado and New rivers of California. The species represents a monotypic genus with unique body form, physiology, and life history requirements. Historically, razorback sucker was among the most abundant large fishes in the [[Colorado River Basin]], ranging from Wyoming south to Sonora and Baja California del Norte, Mexico, including major tributaries. The species was sufficiently abundant to be harvested commercially for food and fertilizer, collected by wagon-loads from canals near Phoenix, Arizona, and served as an important food source for various Indian tribes.

The species' historic range extended into the [[Salton Sea]] when it filled in 1905-06 but disappeared as salinity increased. Concentrations were reported in the lower basin until the early 1950s, after which populations declined precipitously. In the upper basin (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming), the species was never considered abundant and recent collections indicate further decline, with populations comprised primarily of large, presumably old individuals.

Current distribution is highly fragmented. In the **upper Colorado River basin**, only 92 razorbacks were collected from extensive 1979-81 surveys of the [[Green River]], with the species considered extremely rare. Two gravel pits near Grand Junction, Colorado (including [[Walker Wildlife Area]]) produced 37 of 52 total razorbacks collected during mainstream surveys. The species has not been taken in Wyoming since closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962.

In the **lower basin** (Arizona, Nevada, California), fishery surveys since the early 1970s confirm continued decline. A 1981 [[California Department of Fish and Game]] netting survey from [[Parker Dam]] south to the U.S.-Mexican boundary caught zero razorbacks in 2,500 hours of effort despite using gear selective for the species. Remnant populations persist in [[Lake Mohave]] (representing the largest known natural population), [[Lake Mead]], and [[Senator Wash Reservoir]]. Young razorbacks were collected in 1973-74 from ponds filled from the [[East Highline Canal]] near Niland, and seven young individuals were collected in 1984 from lateral reservoirs near the [[Coachella Canal]].

The [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] population has been intensively studied since the late 1970s. A mark-recapture population estimate yielded 54±22 adults (95% confidence limits) in August 1980. Although larval razorbacks were collected over three years (1980-83), no young were taken in subsequent sampling, indicating no recruitment beyond the larval stage. An interagency ultrasonic telemetry study (1980-81) documented seasonal and daily movement patterns, depth preferences, and habitat use. Spawning studies documented reproduction, egg predation by exotic fishes, and geologic composition of preferred spawning substrate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Decline of razorback sucker parallels that of other endemic "big river" fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]], including [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail]] (*G. elegans*), all listed as state and/or federally endangered. Multiple factors have contributed to population collapse:

**Physical alterations**: Construction of dams (1930s-1950s) including [[Hoover Dam]], [[Davis Dam]], [[Parker Dam]], [[Imperial Dam]], and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] altered seasonal flow and temperature regimes. Vanicek et al. (1970) reported alterations for 104 kilometers downstream from Flaming Gorge Dam severe enough to exclude native fishes. Mainstream channelization and dredging created deeper, more uniform channels, eliminating quiet backwaters and oxbows potentially necessary as spawning, nursery, and refuge habitats. Dredging creates unstable, shifting channel bottoms.

**Biological alterations**: Introductions of exotic fishes by state and federal agencies, plus incidental releases, have severely impacted native fish communities. Predation by exotic fishes on razorback eggs (documented by multiple researchers including Wydoski et al. 1980, McAda and Wydoski 1980, Ulmer 1981, Minckley 1983) and larvae/fingerlings (Ulmer 1981) is a significant factor depressing lower basin populations. In [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Senator Wash Reservoir]], exotic fishes (principally [[Green Sunfish]]) prey heavily on razorback recruitment, preventing any young from reaching adulthood despite successful larval production.

**Population structure**: Current populations in major lower basin reservoirs consist of senescent individuals with no evidence of recruitment (Minckley 1983; McCarthy 1985). Age determination studies indicate collected specimens are predominantly old adults. Without successful recruitment, mortality of these aging individuals will result in extinction of remaining populations.

**Critical habitat**: Although razorbacks still occur in reservoirs and occasionally in mainstream river reaches, successful recruitment is inadequate to maintain viable populations. Spawning substrate studies by Ulmer (1981) provided data on inferred historic spawning sites, but current conditions do not support recruitment survival.

## Key Findings

**Legal and Conservation Status**:
- Listed as endangered by California in January 1974
- Protected status in Nevada; "Group 3 Classification" in Arizona (1982)
- Proposed for federal endangered status in 1978; withdrawn and dropped in 1980
- Designated federal candidate species December 1982
- [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] recommends threatened listing for upper basin
- Federal listing deferred to accommodate active management and reintroduction program

**Recovery Efforts by State**:

*Arizona*: In 1981, [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region 2 entered Memorandum of Understanding for 10-year reintroduction program into central Arizona streams. Terms specify that if populations have not reestablished by 1991, federal endangered listing would be pursued. [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) initiated propagation program; since 1981, more than 10 million razorback fry and fingerlings have been stocked. [[Paige Springs Hatchery]] (Arizona) provides additional grow-out facilities.

*Nevada*: [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] is rearing razorbacks from Dexter NFH in ponds to assist Arizona and California recovery efforts. Attempting to obtain ESA-Section 6 funding for status survey in [[Lake Mead]].

*New Mexico*: Despite lack of historic records, considering introduction of hatchery stocks into [[San Juan River]] basin as part of overall recovery effort.

*California*: Considerable effort by CDFG Region 5 over past 10 years directed toward life history information and management plan development. Spawning studies at [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] documented reproduction and egg predation. Propagation experiments using aquaria and tanks (purchased through Environmental Protection Program funds) demonstrated excellent growth and low mortality in controlled conditions. Rearing ponds constructed at [[Imperial Valley Warmwater Hatchery]] at Niland for grow-out to appropriate stocking size.

**Management Plan Objectives (1986-1995)**:

1. Obtain adequate numbers of razorback fry and fingerlings from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] to meet stocking requirements

2. Obtain written concurrence from AGFD, [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]], and USFWS to stock razorback in mainstream lower Colorado River

3. Utilize available grow-out habitats (CDFG Niland endangered species pond facilities, [[Blythe Municipal Golf Course]] pond) to rear fry to minimum stocking size of 10-15 cm (4-6 inches)

4. Obtain additional grow-out/maintenance habitats through: cooperative agreement with CRIT; appropriation of funding; construction/acquisition on Colorado River federal wildlife refuges; construction/acquisition on federal lands as mitigation for projects adversely impacting Colorado River habitats

5. Stock appropriate numbers of 25-30 cm (10-12 inch) razorbacks to secure [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] population (large size necessary to increase survivability to sexually mature adult stage)

6. Establish [[West Pond]] as native fishes refugium by: incorporating concept in Bureau of Land Management's Laguna-Martinez Habitat Management Plan; aligning under Department of Interior Natural Research Area Designation; renovating using [[Sikes Act]] or other funding; reestablishing razorback populations through reintroduction

7. Annually stock appropriate numbers of fry and 10-15 cm fingerlings in three river reaches: [[Topock Division]] ([[Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]]), [[Parker Division]] (Parker II), [[Imperial Division]] ([[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]). Stocking will occur behind sandbars, in small backwaters, and as far upstream as possible to enhance survival and retention

8. Conduct annual or biannual monitoring surveys in Senator Wash Reservoir and mainstream Colorado River divisions to document movement, habitat use, recruitment, and predation losses. Annual reports will be prepared. Funding sources: state tax-checkoff funds, ESA-Section 6 funds, General Fund nongame projects, state decal funds. Survey results will update and revise Management Plan

9. Cease stocking of exotic fishes in river reaches, reservoirs, and backwaters targeted for razorback stocking and population reestablishment

**Justifications for Recovery Effort**:
- Legal responsibility under California Endangered Species Act of 1984 and Fish and Game Code Section 1790 to protect species within Public Trust
- Species' unique body form, physiology, and life history of scientific interest with yet-undetermined applications
- Intrinsic value as monotypic genus
- Potential as important forage fish
- Contribution to diversity in lower Colorado River sport fishery

**Collection Records**: Appendix A documents complete record of razorback sucker collections in California from 1966 to 1985, including 100+ individual capture records with dates, localities, sizes (fork length and total length), weights, collection methods, and notes. Records span from [[Lake Havasu]] south to [[Imperial Reservoir]] and associated canals, documenting the species' decline from occasional captures in the 1960s-70s to increasingly rare occurrences by the 1980s.

**Assessment Timeline**: Due to slow maturation of razorback sucker, minimum of 5 years necessary to determine if viable population has been reestablished. In 1991, the Arizona-USFWS reintroduction and monitoring program will be assessed, with results used to modify and update the second five-year period of California Management Plan.
