# Arizona Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Strategic Plan (1988)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This March 1988 strategic plan, prepared by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) under the [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]], sets five-year goals and objectives (to 1992) for nongame and endangered wildlife across Arizona. The document is a policy and planning framework rather than a research study. It covers the legal basis for management, the definition of "nongame," nonconsumptive wildlife use, the history and structure of the Nongame Program, and separate chapters for native fish, amphibians and reptiles, raptors, nongame birds and nongame mammals. Its chapter on [[Native Fish]] is the most detailed fish content, and it documents the loss of one native fish and severe depletion of the rest.

## Background & Description

- **Legal mandate:** The AGFD manages wildlife under Arizona Revised Statutes Title 17. The Commission "establish[es] broad policies and long range programs for the management, preservation and harvest of wildlife" (ARS 17-231).
- **Planning process:** The plan began with questionnaires to wildlife managers, each assigned one of 46 management units, and was drafted by sub-teams covering raptors, nongame birds, amphibians and reptiles, [[Native Fish]], nongame mammals and general topics. Plans are reviewed every five years.
- **Definitions:** "Nongame" is defined by statute (ARS 17-101). The plan notes that many nongame species are hunted, trapped or fished, and that the term is confusing to the public. Nonconsumptive use (e.g., [[Birdwatching]], photography) is distinguished from consumptive use, though collecting can occur under both.
- **Funding:** The Department is largely self-funded through hunting and fishing license revenue. A [[Wildlife Contribution Fund]] (Nongame Checkoff) on the state income tax form was created in 1982.
- **History:** Nongame work began with a nongame biologist position created in 1967. The [[Arizona Natural Heritage Program]] (1979–1983), established with The Nature Conservancy, identified sensitive elements of Arizona's natural resources. Its methods were absorbed into a Nongame Branch in July 1983. The [[Nongame Data Management System]] (NGDMS) held more than 6,000 occurrence records and over 400 species of plants and animals in the mid-1980s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Arizona has more than 800 vertebrate species occurring, or occurring within the past 100 years. Native vertebrates include **31 species (35 forms) of fish**, 24 amphibians, 76 reptiles, 333 birds and 138 mammals. Elevation ranges from 75 feet near Yuma to 12,670 feet on the San Francisco Peaks.
- Habitat loss is identified as the most pervasive cause of decline. Human population growth, recreation, water development and urbanization are the main pressures. Wetlands and riparian zones are singled out as the most threatened and most important habitats for many taxa.
- Management commitments include more than 40 agreements with the Bureau of Land Management, Forest Service and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, including the [[Apache Trout Recovery Plan]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]] reintroduction agreement.
- Nonconsumptive recreation grew rapidly. National Audubon Society membership in Arizona rose 307% (1972–1983, chapter-affiliated), and Sierra Club membership rose 21% (1982–1985).

## Key Findings

**Plan-wide problems and possible solutions (listed by priority, from a 1985 survey):**
1. Habitat manipulation (review projects, expand NGDMS)
2. Habitat ownership and retention (land acquisition, mitigation)
3. Management data (field surveys, prioritization)
4. Law enforcement (poaching, commercial trade of reptiles, raptor vandalism)
5. Other agency interests (cooperation, advocacy)
6. Population management (consistent decision-making)
7. Information and education
8. Access (regulated access, closures for sensitive areas)
9. Public interest
10. Legal problems (state endangered species act)
11. Competition and predation (grazing management, control of exotic species)

**Native Fish (chapter findings):**
- Of the 35 native fish taxa, one is recorded extinct (the [[Monkey Springs Pupfish]]). Five others were extirpated from Arizona after 1967 but still exist elsewhere.
- At least 70 exotic fish species have been introduced, and more than 50 are established. They interact with native species to their detriment.
- As of 1982, 17 of the 35 taxa were federally listed as endangered or threatened. The Department's 1982 list includes 26 native fish taxa.
- Only a few streams support viable native fish communities. The largest purely native fauna is seven species in one stream, and communities of four or more native species are rare.
- Of 63 perennial streams in the Little Colorado River drainage, 37 have no survey records. In the Gila River Basin above the Salt River, 32 of 58 perennial streams are unsurveyed.
- The outlook for native fish is described as "bleak" for all but the Apache trout, Gila trout, desert pupfish and Gila topminnow.
- Native fish goals for 1992 include a statewide stream inventory, a population monitoring plan for all 35 taxa, taxonomic clarification (e.g., *Gila*), reintroductions to historic ranges, and refugia on the Hassayampa River, Sonoita Grasslands and three other sites.

**Amphibians and Reptiles:**
- Arizona has 120 native species: 1 salamander, 23 frogs and toads, 4 turtles (including 1 tortoise), 49 snakes and 43 lizards.
- Over 20 species were listed as threatened in 1982. Only the [[Desert Tortoise]] was under serious consideration for federal listing. A 1985 petition was rejected by the Commission in April 1986, but the Fish and Wildlife Service later found it warranted (finding expected early 1988).
- The leopard frog complex may comprise four species in Arizona, most in some degree of jeopardy.

**Raptors:**
- 43 raptor species are known to have occurred in Arizona. Three are listed as endangered or threatened, and nine are on the Department's threatened list. Two species (aplomado falcon and California condor) are extirpated from the state.
- Falconry permits were limited to fewer than 30 per year, and the state has about 70 licensed falconers.
- More than 150 raptors per year reach the [[Adobe Mountain Wildlife Center]] after being shot.

**Nongame Birds:**
- Arizona has about 450 nongame bird species. Lowland riparian forests and woodlands, which many birds prefer, have declined severely. Approximately 30% of non-raptorial birds on the Department's list are in such habitats.
- Human-associated species (e.g., rock dove, house finch) are expected to increase, while riverine species are expected to decline.

**Nongame Mammals:**
- 134 native mammal species are recognized. Eight are federally listed as endangered or threatened, and five have been extirpated but are not believed extinct (grizzly bear, black-footed ferret, Mexican wolf, ocelot, jaguar).
- The Mount Graham red squirrel is the most intensively studied nongame mammal. The Hualapai Mexican vole is recent and has no recovery plan.
- Future supply of most mammals is expected to decrease, whether or not demand increases.

**Overall plan outcomes:** The Department faces a fundamental dilemma. Demand for nongame information and services is rising, but staffing and funding remain limited, so efforts to increase public support and alternative funding are central to the plan.
