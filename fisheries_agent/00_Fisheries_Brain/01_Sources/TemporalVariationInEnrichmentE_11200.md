# Temporal Variation in Enrichment Effects During Periphyton Succession in a Nitrogen-Limited Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #periphyton #algae #benthic-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study examines how nitrogen (NO₃-N) enrichment and invertebrate grazing interact to shape [[Periphyton]] succession over 89 days in two adjacent reaches (a riffle and a run) of [[Sycamore Creek]], a spatially intermittent [[Sonoran Desert]] stream in central Arizona. Using [[Clay Saucer Substrata]] containing nitrate-enriched or unenriched agar, with or without the insecticide [[Malathion]] to reduce grazing, the authors tracked biomass, productivity, diversity, and taxonomic composition. Early communities on unenriched substrata were dominated by the N₂-fixing diatom [[Epithemia sorex]], which was later replaced by the heterocystous cyanobacterium [[Calothrix]]. Enrichment delayed this shift and raised diversity, and its effect on biomass was strongest early and faded over time. The authors attribute the late convergence of enriched and control communities mainly to autogenic changes within thickening periphyton mats, not to attainment of a maximum biomass.

## Background & Description

Nutrient supply in aquatic systems is patchy at both micro and macro scales, and enrichment alters algal composition, biomass, and productivity. Autogenic feedbacks from periphyton development can modify nutrient availability and dampen enrichment effects. Grazing by invertebrates can reduce algal biomass, alter community structure, or stimulate growth through excretion, and its effects often interact with light and nutrient limitation.

The study site has basaltic parent bedrock, giving high soluble reactive phosphorus (about 30–70 µg/L SRP), while nitrate-N at base flow often falls below 10 µg/L. Nitrogen-limited algal growth has been documented at concentrations ≤55 µg/L. Nitrogen enters the surface channel mainly through upwelling of nitrate-rich [[Hyporheic Zone]] water, and seasonal spates reset the benthic template to bare substrata.

**Study design:**
- Two reaches at 700 m elevation: a 50 m run (pea gravel, 20–30 cm depth, 5–10 cm/s) and a 70 m riffle (mixed gravel, cobbles, boulders; 5–15 cm depth, 10–20 cm/s).
- Four treatments: +N, +M, +N+M, and Control, with substrata introduced 13 April 1989.
- Agar replaced every 2 weeks; sampling on days 8 and 12 (riffle only), then 25, 40, 55, 68, and 89 (three replicate saucers per treatment per date).
- Response variables: net primary productivity (NPP, dissolved oxygen change in closed chambers), chlorophyll a, ash-free dry mass (AFDM), algal biovolume and diversity (H′), and invertebrate densities.
- Analysis: 3-factor ANOVA on ln-transformed data, plus separate 2-way ANOVAs for each riffle date.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** Nitrate-N was 13 µg/L at the start, rose to 54 µg/L on day 25, then fell to 20–34 µg/L over the final six weeks. SRP ranged 19–81 µg/L. Water temperature ranged 19–22°C during measurements.
- **Riparian vegetation:** Dominated by [[Seepwillow]] (*Baccharis salicifolia*) and aquatic macrophytes including *Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum*, *Ranunculus* sp., and *Zanichellia palustris*.
- **Grazing community:** Twelve invertebrate taxa were classified as grazers (scrapers or collector-gatherers). Chironomid larvae were most numerically abundant. The moth larva [[Petrophila jaliscalis]] was the only common grazer clearly reduced by Malathion, and it occurred mainly in the riffle (peak of 26 individuals on one unenriched saucer on day 55). The beetle *Psephenus murvoshi* was also reduced but never exceeded one individual per substratum.
- **Seasonal context:** Temperatures at the site were 14–24°C in April and 19–32°C in July of the following year, relevant to cyanobacterial prevalence.

## Key Findings

**1. Nitrogen fixers versus non-fixers**
- Of 130 algal taxa in 95 samples, seven were presumed N₂-fixers: three heterocystous cyanobacteria (*Anabaena* sp., *Calothrix* spp., *Nostoc* sp.) and four Epithemiaceae diatoms.
- On unenriched substrata, *Epithemia sorex* dominated by day 25 in both reaches and persisted in the riffle to day 55, then was replaced by *Calothrix* (94.8% of biovolume in the run and 78.5% in the riffle by day 89).
- On enriched substrata, the early community was dominated by the stalked diatom *Cymbella affinis* (53.9% in the run and 39.7% in the riffle on day 25). *Calothrix* still dominated by day 89 but to a lesser degree (56.0% run; 53.6% riffle), with *Oscillatoria* at 11.7% and 18.2%.

**2. Biomass and productivity**
- AFDM and chlorophyll a both increased nearly linearly throughout the study and were significantly enhanced by enrichment in both reaches (AFDM: run p < 0.001, riffle p = 0.002).
- Enrichment's effect on AFDM was significant early (days 8, 12, 25) but mostly absent later, except on day 55.
- Riffle biomass was 3–7× higher on enriched than control substrata on days 8 and 12, declining to 1–2× by days 25–89.
- NPP was unaffected by enrichment from day 25 onward. Total algal biovolume was enhanced by enrichment only on day 12.

**3. Diversity**
- Diversity (H′) was greater on enriched substrata overall (p < 0.001) and declined through succession (p < 0.001).
- A significant reach × age interaction (p = 0.004) reflected faster [[Calothrix]] dominance in the run than in the riffle.

**4. Grazer and Malathion effects**
- Malathion significantly raised AFDM and chlorophyll a in the riffle (AFDM p = 0.017 in the three-factor model; the effect was significant only on enriched substrata, AFDM p = 0.010 and chlorophyll a p < 0.001), but not in the run.
- Malathion increased *Achnanthes minutissima* in both reaches and basal cells of *Stigeoclonium* in the riffle. The authors suggest phosphorus release from pesticide degradation may have stimulated these taxa.
- Grazing effects on biomass were detectable only on day 55, coinciding with peak *Petrophila* density, which lagged algal biomass accrual.

**5. Proposed mechanisms**
- Early enrichment effects were attributed to strong N limitation of unenriched communities. The late convergence of enriched and control communities was attributed to autogenic changes: as mats thickened, light penetration fell, nutrient diffusion from the water column and interstitial space declined, microenvironments became hypoxic, and nutrient recycling became dominant.
- Riffle communities shifted to cyanobacteria more slowly than run communities, which the authors attribute to greater turbulence increasing nutrient delivery to riffle mats, and greater light penetration in riffle mats.
- The authors argue that grazing effects depend on grazer identity and timing relative to algal succession, and that short-term enrichment bioassays may give a limited picture of nutrient effects in long-lived algal communities.

**6. Conclusions**
- Spatial heterogeneity in nutrient supply maintains algal diversity, but over long successional sequences diversity declines regardless of enrichment because of autogenic change.
- Early enrichment differences in biomass and community structure were largely eliminated by mid to late succession.
- In Sycamore Creek, long inter-spate periods may favor cyanobacterial dominance, which might shift back toward diatoms after scouring spates.
