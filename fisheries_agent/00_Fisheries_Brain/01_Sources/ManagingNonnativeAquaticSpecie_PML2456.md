# Managing Nonnative Aquatic Species Impacts: Implementation of the 1994 Central Arizona Project Biological Opinion, and a National Stream Monitoring Program for the Western United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from the *Proceedings of the Desert Fishes Council*, Volume XXXIII (2000 Symposium, published November 13, 2001), contains three abstracts (bilingual, English and Spanish). The main document addresses the implementation of the 1994 [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) biological opinion, issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS), which found that CAP threatened four federally listed Gila basin fishes through the spread of nonnative aquatic species. The second abstract describes a four-year [[EMAP]] survey of western U.S. streams. A fragmentary opening abstract reports on monitoring of two bay-associated populations, with larval collection during the February–May 2000 spawning season. The source is a symposium abstract collection, so the findings are qualitative and program-level rather than quantitative.

## Background & Description

**Species of concern.** The 1994 opinion covered four federally listed Gila basin fishes:

- [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*)
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*)
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

**Regulatory context.** The USFWS determined that CAP had the potential to introduce and spread nonnative aquatic species in the Gila River basin in Arizona, which could jeopardize the continued existence of these fishes. To remove jeopardy, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] was assigned a five-part program:

1. Constructing physical barriers on Aravaipa Creek and the San Pedro River
2. Monitoring
3. Information and education
4. Providing funding for management against aquatic nonnatives
5. Providing funding for other recovery actions for the four listed fishes

**Abstract on the EMAP survey.** The [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]] began a four-year survey of western streams under the [[Environmental Monitoring and Assessment Program]] (EMAP). Sites are selected randomly, and biological assemblages are sampled together with physical and chemical measures to estimate biological integrity. Because site selection is random, the data can support regional and statewide estimates of stream condition. Included states are Arizona, California, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, North Dakota, Oregon, South Dakota, Utah, Washington, and Wyoming (12 states). The abstract reports a preliminary account of fishes from sampled sites in Arizona, Nevada, and Utah.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The core ecological concern is competition and predation by nonnative fishes on native fishes in Gila basin streams. The abstracts identify several additional pressures:

- **Changing conditions.** The "big picture" analysis assesses nonnative dispersal over multidecade timescales, accounting for future weather patterns and human water and land use. Critics regard this as too speculative.
- **Recent invasions.** Aquatic invertebrate, disease, and plant invasions have occurred, which some argue make a focus on nonnative fishes insufficient.
- **Detection limits.** Rare species are hard to detect during monitoring, which delays identification of new incursions or population increases.
- **Sport fish stocking.** USFWS and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] were evaluating whether a similar "umbrella" approach should address effects of sport fish stocking on native fishes in the [[Santa Cruz River]] subbasin.
- **Bay populations.** In the fragmentary opening abstract, two bay-associated populations varied their habitat use between autumn/winter and spring/summer seasons, consistent with earlier years. Both populations produced larvae successfully during the spawning season.

## Key Findings

- **Program status.** Implementation of the 1994 CAP opinion has faced political lobbying to block funding and lawsuits seeking to overturn it. The abstract describes the controversy as "winding down," with work moving toward on-the-ground reduction of nonnative/native conflicts.
- **Umbrella approach.** The 1994 opinion addresses nonnatives basin-wide, while physical barriers are placed at the bottom of important native fish habitats. This approach is being extended to the Santa Cruz subbasin.
- **Points of controversy.** The abstract lists several contested elements:
  - Speculative long-term "big picture" analysis, which some consider too pessimistic
  - Reliance on barriers just below native habitats rather than removing nonnatives near their point of entry, which some consider risky
  - Difficulty of detecting rare species during monitoring
  - Focus on nonnative fishes, which may be insufficient given recent invertebrate, disease, and plant invasions
  - Substitution of mitigation for threat removal, which is controversial but consistent with USFWS trends
- **Outcome assessment.** The success of the CAP approach cannot yet be judged because implementation is recent. Nonetheless, many recovery actions are underway that would not have occurred without the 1994 opinion, and these are covered in a separate presentation.
- **EMAP design.** The national survey uses randomly selected sites to produce regional and statewide estimates of stream biological integrity across 12 western states. The abstract reports preliminary fish results for Arizona, Nevada, and Utah only.
