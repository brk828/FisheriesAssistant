# Breeding Behaviour Patterns and Ecological Differences Between Tilapia Species and Their Significance for Evolution Within the Genus Tilapia (Pisces: Cichlidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Rosamary Lowe]] (McConnell), of the East African Fisheries Research Organization, Jinja, Uganda, collates field observations from 1945 to 1966 on Tilapia species in East and Central Africa and compares them with published aquarium and field work from West Africa, the Congo and southern Africa. It classifies Tilapia into three breeding groups: substratum-spawners, paternal-brooders and maternal-brooders. Each group differs in breeding behaviour, feeding and habitat use, and gill raker number. The author argues that maternal-brooding, combined with preadaptation for open water, polygamy and the feeding of phytoplankton, underlies the extensive speciation seen only among maternal-brooders, most notably the species flock of [[Lake Nyasa]].

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Field data from the author's own observations on roughly 20 species, supplemented by literature from across Africa. Identifications were checked at the [[British Museum (Natural History)]] with the help of [[Ethelwynn Trewavas]].
- **Breeding groups:**
  - *Substratum-spawners* (4 known species, including [[Tilapia sparrmanii]], [[Tilapia zillii]] and [[Tilapia melanopleura]]): both sexes guard eggs and young on the substratum; monogamy is likely; eggs are not brooded in the mouth.
  - *Paternal-brooders* (e.g. [[Tilapia macrocephala]]): the male broods eggs and young in his mouth; few species, mainly West Africa and the Congo.
  - *Maternal-brooders*: the female broods eggs and young in her mouth; the general rule for East and Central African Tilapia, including all endemic Great Lakes species.
- **Gill rakers:** Substratum-spawners have fewer than 12 on the lower limb of the anterior gill arch; maternal-brooders have more than 15 (generally 17 or more), and up to 28 in some species; paternal-brooders are intermediate (about 15 to 19 in *T. macrocephala*).
- **Morphology and taxonomy:** Tilapia species are morphologically very similar, and naming confusion in earlier aquarium studies (e.g. [[Seitz]], 1949) is noted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Diet:** Tilapia are primarily vegetable feeders (phytoplankton, epiphytic and bottom algae, aquatic plants), with occasional insect larvae and molluscs. Substratum-spawners are the only group specialised for feeding on aquatic plants. The genus contrasts with the very varied diet and adaptive radiation of the associated cichlid genus [[Haplochromis]].
- **Habitat and movement:** Substratum-spawners are restricted to shallow, plant-bearing water and show little movement. Maternal-brooders move between zones (swamp, shore, open water) as they grow, and adults undertake long movements. In [[Lake Victoria]], marked fish of *T. esculenta* and *T. variabilis* were recaptured up to 150 miles away, and moved up to 4 and 10 miles in a single day, respectively.
- **Spatial partitioning in Lake Nyasa:** Five species at the southern end occupy increasingly offshore zones, from the lagoon-dwelling *T. melanopleura* to the open-water *T. squamipinnis* complex. Spawning depth and timing separate them.
- **Breeding seasons:** Lake Nyasa species have restricted, well-defined breeding seasons, while Lake Victoria species breed year-round. Seasonality follows latitude and hydrology (for example, *T. nilotica* breeds only in the rains in northern Nigeria but year-round near the equator).
- **Predation:** Predatory cichlids (*Lates* and *Hydrocyon*) are thought to restrict Tilapia to littoral zones in Lakes Albert and Rudolf; the author notes this may have affected speciation.
- **Lake age and depth:** Lake Nyasa is much older than Lake Victoria, which is shallow (perhaps under 10 m deep at present) and was repeatedly dried out during pluvial and arid periods.

## Key Findings

1. **Three breeding types** are distinguished in *Tilapia*, with associated differences in colour, courtship, mate fidelity, nest form, feeding and gill raker number.
2. **Only maternal-brooders have speciated extensively.** All endemic Tilapia of the Great Lakes are maternal-brooders, despite the likelihood that substratum-spawners are closest to the ancestral stock.
3. **Maternal-brooding is the general rule** in East and Central African Tilapia. Paternal-brooding is known mainly from West Africa and the Congo, and is intermediate in behaviour and gill raker number.
4. **Speciation constraints in substratum-spawners:** their restriction to shallow, plant-bearing water and their contiguous distribution limit isolation, and their diet and gill rakers restrict their exploitation of open water.
5. **Maternal-brooding advantages:** preadaptation for open water, the ability to use phytoplankton, females carrying eggs during migration (which may isolate new populations), and polygamy with sexual selection.
6. **Breeding colours** advertise spawning grounds and may serve species recognition; in Lake Nyasa, *T. saka* and *T. squamipinnis* differ in male breeding colour, which may act as an isolating mechanism.
7. **Nyasa's unique species flock:** Lake Nyasa has six Tilapia species, four of which form a species flock (*T. squamipinnis*, *T. saka*, *T. lidole*, *T. karongae*). By contrast, Lake Victoria and Lake Jipe have only two endemic species each, and Lake Tanganyika has two species with geographic as well as ecological isolation.
8. **Sympatric speciation debate:** Worthington argued for speciation within lakes, while [[Ernst Mayr]] argued that geographic isolation was required. The author concludes that the Nyasa flock evolved within the lake, supported by ecological, temporal (breeding season) and spatial (depth) isolation.
9. **Lake Nyasa's isolation** combined temporal breeding differences (e.g. *T. saka* breeding before the rains, *T. squamipinnis* during them) with depth-related spawning, and lake-level fluctuations, to maintain species boundaries.
10. **Ecological isolation in Lake Victoria** is weaker, owing to the shallow, uniform lake and year-round breeding, which likely limits speciation in Tilapia compared with the Haplochromis flock.
11. **Limitations:** the author notes that classification of some paternal-brooders and the behaviour of some West African species remain uncertain, and that the causes of rapid, long-distance movements are unknown.
