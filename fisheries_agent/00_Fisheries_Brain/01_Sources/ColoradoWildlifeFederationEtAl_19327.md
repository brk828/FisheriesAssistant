# Declaration of Henry R. Maddux on Razorback Sucker Critical Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This sworn declaration, filed in [[Colorado Wildlife Federation v. Turner]] (U.S. District Court, District of Colorado, Civil Action No. 92-F-884), was made by [[Henry R. Maddux]], Team Leader of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Critical Habitat Designation Team for the endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It explains why the [[Razorback Sucker]] critical habitat designation is complex, why data gaps remain, and why a proposed rule should not be published before the [[Economic Analysis]] is complete. Maddux argues that publishing early would add no protection for the species, would hinder recovery, and would likely produce a rule open to challenge. He projects a draft economic analysis by April 1, 1993, and a possible proposed rule in September 1993. The declaration was executed on September 16, 1992, in Salt Lake City, Utah.

## Background & Description

- **Author and team:** The team comprises 5 fishery biologists and 3 economists (two from the [[University of New Mexico]], one from a private consulting firm in Wyoming). Maddux holds a B.S. (Honors) in Fisheries from the University of Arizona and an M.S. in Fisheries from South Dakota State University. His career includes the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] program, Texas Parks and Wildlife, and the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (Lake Powell work).
- **Species covered:** The declaration focuses on the razorback sucker, listed as endangered, and the other endangered Colorado River fishes: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. An attachment photograph shows all four.
- **Legal framework:** Critical habitat must be defined through primary constituent elements (PCEs), the essential physical and biological features of a species. The declaration cites the [[Endangered Species Act]] (Sections 4(b)(2), 4(b)(8), and 7(a)(2)) and the implementing regulations at 50 C.F.R. §424.12 and §402.02.
- **Habitat requirements:** Regulations require consideration of space, food and water, cover, breeding and rearing sites, and protected or historically representative habitats. Example PCEs include spawning sites, feeding sites, water quality or quantity, vegetation type, and soil types.
- **Survival vs. recovery areas:** Areas needed to prevent extinction cannot be excluded from designation. Areas needed only for recovery may be excluded on economic grounds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic and current range:** The razorback sucker historically occupied over 3,500 miles of the Colorado River and major tributaries across seven states. Over half of that range is no longer occupied. Many reaches are now fragmented by [[Dams and Diversions]].
- **Survey difficulty:** Murky water, rapids, and fast currents make work hard. Only one spawning area above [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has been positively identified. In some areas a single fish may take weeks to capture.
- **Data gaps:** Much of the life-history literature was produced after habitat had already been altered. Many needs must be inferred from other areas or from historic and archaeological records.
- **Young and survival:** Razorback sucker spawn annually, but the young hatch and then disappear. Most remaining wild individuals are 35 to 50 years old.
- **Stressors identified:**
  - [[Water Quality and Contaminants]]: selenium in the [[Green River]] is linked to reproductive failure. Young suckers exposed to water from some Green River tributary drainages showed mortality of 30 to 80 percent.
  - [[Oil and Gas Development]]: aromatic hydrocarbons along the San Juan, Green, White, Duchesne, and Colorado rivers have caused fish kills.
  - [[Hydropower Peaking]]: daily fluctuations strand young fish in nursery shallows. In some areas about 50 percent of spawning nests are dried by peaking releases.
  - [[Non-Native Fishes]]: carp and green sunfish prey on eggs and young. Over 40 introduced species are now established in the river.
  - [[Bottomland Loss]]: channelization, diking, reduced spring runoff, and exotic vegetation have cut off floodplain feeding and nursery habitat.
  - [[Shoreline Development]]: this is mainly a Lower Basin issue, where rock lining has straightened meandering channels.
- **Human uses:** Dependent economies include hydropower, irrigation (about 3 million acres in Basin water), mining, recreation, and municipal supply to cities including Phoenix, Denver, Salt Lake City, Las Vegas, and Los Angeles. Roughly 10 Indian tribes and several national parks and recreation areas are involved.

## Key Findings

1. **Flow alteration is severe.** Dams and diversions have reduced spring peak flows by 50 percent since 1942 and increased fall and winter flows by 21 percent in some reaches. Twenty-three dams in the basin block or impede fish movement; the first was built in 1905.
2. **Flow analysis is slow.** The Service is analyzing daily flows from 10 [[U.S. Geological Survey]] gages against fish needs across 18 years of record. Summarizing took 3 weeks for only 2 gages. The full process was projected to exceed 4 months, including economic modeling.
3. **Over 50 consultations are ongoing.** The Service has identified more than 50 proposed activities within the species' range that are already under formal or informal Section 7(a)(2) consultation. Federal agencies named include the Bureau of Reclamation, Corps of Engineers, Western Area Power Administration, and Federal Energy Regulatory Commission.
4. **Biology must precede economics.** Biologists must quantify each flow element (seasonal volume, rise rates, daily fluctuation limits, temperatures, drought minimums) before economists can value impacts. The two processes must run iteratively until the final rule.
5. **Designation effects are expected to be limited.** Maddux states that Section 7 consultation would rarely prohibit an activity outright. More often an activity would shift location or scale.
6. **Remaining work is substantial.** Outstanding tasks include physical descriptions of areas, contaminant assessments drawing on EPA, USGS, and state data, and flow-versus-need comparisons. The draft proposed rule was targeted for Regional review in January or February 1993.
7. **Timing argument.** Maddux opposes publishing a proposed rule before the economic analysis is complete. He argues that a premature proposal would provide no extra protection, could hinder recovery, invite public condemnation, and require two proposed rules, lengthening the process. He cites the winter-run chinook salmon rule (57 Fed. Reg. 36626, August 14, 1992), which was published with its economic analysis included.
8. **Comparison with the spotted owl.** The [[Northern Spotted Owl]] designation benefited from the [[Interagency Scientific Committee]] report (May 1990) and its conservation strategy. No comparable in-depth study exists for the razorback sucker, and the Colorado River system is more complex.
9. **Schedule.** The draft economic analysis is due April 1, 1993, and a proposed rule could be published in September 1993.
