# Preliminary Report on Fish Remains from the Falcon Hill Sites, Washoe County, Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #archaeoichthyology #Salmonidae #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #LahontanBasin

## Executive Summary
This 1963 report by W. I. Follett describes an archaeozoological analysis of fish skeletal remains recovered from the Falcon Hill archaeological sites near [[Winnemucca Lake]], Washoe County, Nevada. The remains, roughly two-thirds identified at the time of writing (excluding material from Kramer Cave), represent four native fish taxa historically inhabiting the [[Lahontan Basin]]: [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], [[Kuyui]], [[Lahontan Sucker]], and [[Tui-Chub]]. Vertebral counts, normalized against the number of vertebrae per individual fish, were used to reconstruct the relative composition of the prehistoric fish catch. A single anomalous vertebra attributable to Pacific salmon (*Oncorhynchus* sp.) — a genus not native to the closed Lahontan Basin — indicates probable aboriginal long-distance transport of fish material from an external river system.

## Background & Description
- **Skeletal element focus:** Vertebrae dominate the assemblage, vastly outnumbering all other skeletal elements (cranial bones, spines, etc.) combined, making them the primary basis for species identification and quantification.
- **Species-specific vertebral counts recovered:**
  - [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarkii henshawi*): 156 elements
  - [[Kuyui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*): 75 elements
  - [[Lahontan Sucker]] (*Catostomus tahoensis*): 55 elements
  - [[Tui-Chub]] (*Siphateles bicolor*): 27 elements
- **Kuyui size distribution:** Remains include individuals of only about one foot in length and roughly one pound in weight — notably smaller than typical spawning adults, which reach more than two feet in length and five to seven pounds or more.
- **Anomalous salmon vertebra:** A single vertebra recovered from a depth of 126–132 inches is morphologically referable to the genus *Oncorhynchus* (Pacific salmon), though not yet identified to species. This vertebra is morphologically distinct from the local Lahontan taxa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Winnemucca Lake system:** The four native taxa identified are considered highly likely to represent the only sizable fish species that inhabited [[Winnemucca Lake]] prior to European contact ("before the advent of the white man").
- **Kuyui habitat behavior:** Adult Kuyui are typically captured during spawning migrations at the mouth of the [[Truckee River]], entering shallow water only seasonally. Outside of spawning, they are inferred to inhabit intermediate depths of large lakes (e.g., [[Pyramid Lake]], and formerly Winnemucca Lake), feeding on planktonic organisms.
- **Anomaly of half-grown Kuyui:** Half-grown, nonspawning Kuyui are rarely observed in the wild, making their presence in the Falcon Hill assemblage ecologically unusual. The author hypothesizes these individuals may have been captured during an early stage of lake desiccation, when normally inaccessible fish populations became vulnerable to human harvest.
- **Non-native salmon vertebra:** *Oncorhynchus* does not occur naturally in the closed Lahontan Basin (which lacks ocean access). Its presence at Falcon Hill is interpreted as evidence of aboriginal trade or transport from an external anadromous river system — candidates include the [[Columbia River]], [[Klamath River]], or [[Sacramento River]] systems.
- **Comparative regional find:** The only other archaeological salmon remains known from Nevada at the time were recovered by Dr. Shutler at [[Deer Creek Cave]] (Elko County), on the [[Jarbidge River]], a high tributary of the Columbia River system — supporting a pattern of prehistoric long-distance fish transport into the Great Basin.

## Key Findings
1. Four fish taxa — trout, kuyui, sucker, and tui-chub — comprise the identified Falcon Hill assemblage, consistent with the presumed native fish fauna of pre-contact Winnemucca Lake.
2. Vertebral proportional analysis suggests the prehistoric catch composition was approximately: **40% [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], 30% [[Kuyui]], 20% [[Lahontan Sucker]], and 10% [[Tui-Chub]]**.
3. The presence of half-grown, nonspawning Kuyui is ecologically anomalous and may correlate with episodes of lake-level desiccation; radiocarbon dating of the associated strata is recommended to test this hypothesis.
4. A single *Oncorhynchus* (Pacific salmon) vertebra, found at 126–132 inches depth, represents an out-of-range taxon for the Lahontan Basin, implying aboriginal transport from an external river system (Columbia, Klamath, or Sacramento).
5. This finding parallels a similar isolated salmon occurrence at [[Deer Creek Cave]] in Elko County, suggesting broader patterns of prehistoric fish exchange or long-distance travel across the Great Basin.
6. The study remains preliminary; full identification of the Falcon Hill fish remains (excluding Kramer Cave material) was incomplete (~two-thirds finished) at time of publication.
