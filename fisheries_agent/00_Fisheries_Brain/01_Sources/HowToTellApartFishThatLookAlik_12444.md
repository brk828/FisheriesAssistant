## Executive Summary

This 1941 field guide (revised 1947), authored by Louis A. Krumholz and Walter R. Crowe of the Michigan Institute for Fisheries Research, addresses widespread angler confusion in distinguishing morphologically similar Michigan game and commercial fishes. The paper explicitly cautions against using **color** as a reliable diagnostic trait, since coloration is highly variable and unreliable even between species as different as [[Largemouth Black Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Black Bass]]. Instead, the authors provide comparative anatomical keys — focusing on scale coverage, fin spine counts/lengths, mouth size, and structural features — to differentiate 25 species across 7 families, including [[Esocidae|Pikes]], [[Percidae|Perches]], [[Centrarchidae|Sunfishes and Basses]], [[Ictaluridae|Catfishes]], [[Salmonidae|Trouts and Whitefishes]], [[Catostomidae|Suckers]], and [[Cyprinidae|Carp]]. The work also highlights the problem of common-name ambiguity (e.g., "pickerel" applied to multiple unrelated species).

## Background & Description

The guide was created to give sportsmen a practical, non-technical means of identifying their catch, correcting common misconceptions perpetuated by reliance on coloration and inconsistent vernacular naming. Readers seeking full technical keys are referred to Hubbs and Lagler's *Guide to the Fishes of the Great Lakes and Tributary Waters* (Cranbrook Institute of Science). The text is organized around paired or grouped species commonly confused with one another, using diagrams of key anatomical features (scale patterns on cheek/gill cover, dorsal fin structure, mouth size, anal fin spine counts, gill raker morphology).

### Naming Confusion Highlighted
- **One name, multiple species**: "Pickerel" applied to [[Mud Pickerel]], [[Northern Pike]], and even [[Walleye]].
- **Multiple names, one species**: [[Black Crappie]] known as "calico bass," "strawberry bass," "check," "speck," and "crappie."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat range**: Species covered span the southern Lower Peninsula (e.g., [[Mud Pickerel]], restricted to warmer, southern waters) to the Great Lakes proper (e.g., [[Great Lakes Whitefish]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Cisco]], caught commercially and via trolling).
- **Habitat partitioning within Centrarchidae**: [[Smallmouth Black Bass]] favors rocky lake and stream habitats, while [[Largemouth Black Bass]] favors weedier habitats — an ecological distinction reinforcing their morphological differences.
- **Commercial vs. sport importance**: [[Great Lakes Whitefish]], [[Menominee Whitefish]], [[Cisco]], and [[Common Sucker]] are emphasized as commercially significant species netted/speared, particularly in early spring, while [[Bluegill]] is noted as the most frequently caught sport fish in Michigan and [[Brook Trout]] as the most frequently caught trout.
- **Distribution notes**: [[Sauger]] has a more limited Michigan distribution than [[Walleye]], resulting in lower angler catch frequency. [[Mud Pickerel]] is restricted to the southern half of the Lower Peninsula.
- **Hybridization**: Sunfish (Centrarchidae) hybrids occur in nature due to close relationship and overlapping habits, though they rarely constitute a significant portion of angler catch.

## Key Findings

### Pikes (Esocidae) — Cheek/Gill Cover Scale Coverage
| Species | Cheek Scales | Gill Cover Scales | Coloration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[Mud Pickerel]] | Fully scaled | Fully scaled | Brownish bars on yellow-green | Smallest pike, rarely >14 in; dorsal fin set far back |
| [[Northern Pike]] | Fully scaled | Upper half only | Yellow spots (not barred) | Most common pike species caught |
| [[Muskellunge]] | Upper half only | Upper half only | Barred, often bluish tinge | Largest pike family member; 30+ lb individuals common |

### Perches (Percidae) — Dorsal Fin Marking
- **[[Walleye]]** (*Stizostedion v. vitreum*): Single black blotch at posterior end of spiny dorsal fin.
- **[[Sauger]]** (*Stizostedion c. canadense*): Several dark spots distributed across the entire spiny dorsal fin (not one blotch); more limited Michigan distribution than walleye.

### Black Basses (Centrarchidae) — Mouth Size & Dorsal Fin Structure
| Character | [[Smallmouth Black Bass]] | [[Largemouth Black Bass]] |
|---|---|---|
| Maxillary extension | Rarely past mid-eye | Well past eye (adults) |
| Dorsal fin notch | Poorly marked separation | Deep notch between spiny/soft dorsal |
| Longest:shortest spine ratio | ~1.5:1 | ~3:1 |
| Lateral stripe | Usually absent | Usually present (black horizontal stripe) |
| Scale size | Smaller | Larger |
| Preferred habitat | Rocky | Weedy |

### Sunfishes (Centrarchidae, Lepomis & allies)
- **[[Bluegill]]**: Flexible jet-black opercular flap ("ear-flap"); black blotch at posterior dorsal fin base; no red/orange side speckling; dark green body with fading vertical bars. Most-caught fish species in Michigan overall.
- **[[Pumpkinseed]]**: Bright orange-red spot on ear-flap; prominent orange/red side spots; no posterior dorsal blotch.
- **[[Green Sunfish]]**: Large mouth (distinct from bluegill/pumpkinseed); dark coloration with yellow/white fin edgings; small posterior dorsal blotch (like bluegill); short, rounded pectoral fins (vs. long/pointed in bluegill and pumpkinseed).
- **[[Warmouth Bass]]**: Three anal spines (as in all sunfish); brownish coloration and large mouth resemble [[Rock Bass]], but distinguished by small eye and patch of small teeth on tongue.
- **[[Rock Bass]]**: Five anal fin spines (vs. three in true sunfishes); large red eye (source of names "red eye," "goggle-eye"); reaches larger size than typical sunfish.
- **[[Black Crappie]]**: Anal fin as large as dorsal fin — key diagnostic. Rarely confused with white crappie, which is rare in Michigan.

### Catfishes (Ictaluridae)
- **[[Brown Bullhead]]** (and other bullheads): Rounded or slightly forked tail fin; blunt head; small eye.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]**: Deeply forked tail fin; sharper head; large eyes; reaches 3-4 lb commonly.

### Whitefishes & Ciscoes (Salmonidae, Coregoninae)
| Species | Mouth Position/Size | Gill Rakers | Body Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Menominee Whitefish]] | Extremely small, inferior | Stubby | Round body |
| [[Great Lakes Whitefish]] | Larger than Menominee, smaller than Cisco; inferior | Intermediate length | Flattened sides |
| [[Cisco]] | Relatively large, terminal | Extremely long, slender | Flattened sides; variable size across cisco complex (lake herring, chubs, bloaters) |

### Trouts (Salmonidae, Salmoninae)
| Species | Scales | Spots | Tail Fin | Adipose Fin | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[Brook Trout]] | Extremely small | Clear red spots; worm-like back tracings | Very little forked | — | White edges on lower fins; large mouth; most frequently caught trout |
| [[Brown Trout]] | Larger than brook trout | Diffuse brown/pink spots; black spots on back (no wavy lines) | Scarcely spotted | Orange-blotched, no black margin | — |
| [[Rainbow Trout]] | Moderately large | No red/pink spots | Heavily black-spotted | Black spots present + black margin (always) | Relatively small mouth; red/pink lateral stripe in well-colored individuals |
| [[Lake Trout]] | — | Grey/white coloring, no bright colors | Deeply forked | — | Attains 15-20 lb commonly; primarily commercial, growing as sport fish via trolling |

### Suckers & Carp (Catostomidae, Cyprinidae)
- **[[Common Sucker]]**: Rounded body, sucking mouth, small scales crowded on forward body region. Important commercial and speared/netted species in early spring.
- **[[Redhorse]]** ("mullet," 5 species in Michigan): Large silvery scales, flattened sides, no spines or barbels. Some reach 8-10 lb.
- **[[Carp]]**: Long dorsal fin (~20 rays) with coarse sawtoothed anterior spine; short barbels on mouth — distinct from suckers/redhorse.
