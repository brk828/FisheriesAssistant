# Allozymic Variation in Desert Pupfish from Natural and Artificial Habitats: Genetic Conservation in Fluctuating Populations

#ichthyology #conservation-genetics #cyprinodontidae #desert-pupfish

## Executive Summary

This study examines allozymic variation in wild and captive populations of endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*) from the [[Lower Colorado River]] basin and [[Salton Sink]]. Four polymorphic loci were surveyed across 13 populations (9 captive, 4 wild). Few consistent differences exist between wild and captive populations; observed variation is attributed to founder sources, founder effects, bottleneck effects, or combinations thereof. Local isolation promotes differentiation in both wild and captive populations, historically countered by dispersal and population mixing during wet periods—gene flow now precluded by hydrologic control. Recovery requires re-establishment of natural hydrograph conditions, unlikely in the foreseeable future. Authors propose hands-on captive management involving periodic isolation and compositing to mimic natural metapopulation dynamics until natural conditions are restored.

## Background & Description

[[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) comprises two recognized subspecies: *C. m. eremus* from [[Quitobaquito Spring]] (Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona) and *C. m. macularius* throughout most of the remaining range. This study focuses exclusively on *C. m. macularius*. The species was formerly distributed throughout the lower [[Colorado River]] basin of northwestern México (Baja California Norte, Sonora) and southwestern United States (Arizona, California), covering >40,000 km² over altitudinal range of -50 to 1500 m.

The species is part of a declining native fish fauna in the American Southwest. Habitat destruction and exotic species impacts have extirpated and fragmented populations throughout its range, leading to endangered status listing by U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service (1986). The term "captive" refers to translocated populations in quasi-natural outdoor habitats including fish hatcheries, concrete ponds, irrigation ponds, and excavated natural springs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Desert pupfish historically occupied a dynamic metapopulation system characterized by "boom and bust" cycles. Permanent spring and stream refugia served as "mainland" areas periodically connected by flooding. The [[Colorado River]] historically experienced vast fluctuations in annual discharge, causing pupfish populations to wax and wane. Intermittently flooded areas harbored flourishing populations until desiccation again isolated permanent habitats.

In the [[Salton Sink]], pupfish were restricted to isolated springs and seeps prior to 1904. Flooding in 1904-1907 formed the present [[Salton Sea]], and pupfish formed remarkably large shoreline populations from numerous formerly isolated local populations. Populations remained abundant until mid-1960s, followed by dramatic declines in response to exotic fish introductions. Recent rebounds occurred following decimation of exotic fishes due to increasing salinity from irrigation return flow and evaporation.

On the [[Colorado River Delta]], upstream agricultural diversions beginning in early 1900s and river regulation beginning with Hoover Dam (1935) caused increasing desiccation. Rewatering by extensive flooding in 1983-84 resulted in increased distribution and abundance. The species accommodates severe selective arenas, requiring little more than water to survive so long as other species are excluded. Most non-native species cannot survive the severe conditions pupfish tolerate readily.

## Key Findings

**Genetic Variation Patterns:**
- Four polymorphic loci resolved: sAh-A, Gpi-A, Gpi-B, Ldh-A
- Only 2 of 39 chi-square tests showed statistically significant deviation from Hardy-Weinberg expectations
- Considerable genetic variation exists at surveyed loci across populations
- Captive populations from Colorado Delta were fixed for same allele at Gpi-B locus that dominated in [[Salton Sink]] populations (>90% frequency in 6 of 7 populations)
- Dexter and Desert Botanical Garden fish lacked variation at Gpi-A and Ldh-A loci
- Significant frequency divergences in three of four lineages examined

**Hierarchical Distribution of Variation:**
- 88.5% of total genic diversity contained in average site
- 86.3% contained in average site within geographic area
- Genetic variation among sites greater than among lineages, which is greater than variation between geographic areas
- Pattern indicates strong influences of within-population events

**Factors Affecting Captive Population Variation:**
- Strong negative association (P=0.016) between number of founders and difference in heterozygosity between source and descendant populations
- Founder population size most important determinant of variation in captive populations
- No significant association with time since founding, relative habitat size, or hierarchical relationships
- Lack of association with habitat size explained by highly variable nature of pupfish habitat and dramatic population size fluctuations

**Specific Population Comparisons:**
- Dexter population: founded with 280 fish (1983), maintained as few thousand individuals, shows low genetic variation
- WLM population: founded with only 64 fish (1976), persisted in low hundreds, shows greater variability than Dexter despite smaller founder size
- Pattern suggests differentiation of wild source populations despite geographic proximity
- Wild populations in [[Salton Sink]] relatively homogeneous over much larger geographic scale than delta populations

**Management Implications:**
- Desert pupfish fail to demonstrate consistent patterns of interpopulation variation
- Genetic divergence among populations not uncommon
- Drift (via founder/bottleneck effects) most important determinant of variation in captive populations
- Species most closely approximated by mainland-island metapopulation models
- Present recovery efforts focus on maintaining local captive populations, inconsistent with natural history
- Persistent isolation will result in "Death Valley Model" scenario with decline in both among- and within-population genetic diversity

**Proposed Management Strategy:**
- Existing captive populations founded directly from wild sources designated as "primary populations"
- Primary populations should be periodically composited to form pool of "migrants"
- Composited population used as source to create "secondary populations"
- Wild fish should be added to descendant primary populations when available
- Plan represents compromise between logistical and biological considerations
- Requires establishing and maintaining more captive populations with degree of among-population gene flow
- Future recovery efforts should be implemented on experimental basis
- Searches for and study of wild populations should continue to minimize likelihood that "evolutionary significant units" are overlooked
- Possibility of local adaptation should be considered before managing groups as relatively homogeneous units

**Conservation Prognosis:**
- Poor prognosis for continued survival of wild populations
- Unknown but substantial proportion of intraspecific diversity may have been lost with disappearance from entire [[Gila River]] basin
- Human hydrological control precludes gene flow among populations
- Species unlikely to achieve self-sufficiency until natural hydrologic conditions (including major flooding) restored
- Considering demands on [[Colorado River]] water by >20 million people, natural restoration unlikely in foreseeable future
- Hands-on management of captive populations mimicking natural state should be implemented
- Severe selective arenas occupied by desert pupfish not difficult to replicate
- Species needs little more than water to survive so long as other species excluded
