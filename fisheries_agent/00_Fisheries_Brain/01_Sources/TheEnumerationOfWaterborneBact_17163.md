# Enumeration of Waterborne Bacteria by a New Direct Count Method

#bacteria #limnology #freshwater-microbiology

## Executive Summary

This 1957 methods paper by [[Vera G. Collins]] and [[Charlotte Kipling]], from the [[Freshwater Biological Association]], describes a direct microscopic method for counting total bacteria in lake water without cultivation. Samples are stained with [[Gentian Violet]] in a [[Glycerol]]-water base, concentrated in vacuo, and drawn into flattened glass [[Capillary Tubes]] for counting under oil immersion. The authors argue that plate counts on any single [[Culture Media]] miss much of the bacterial population, which motivated the search for a direct method. Direct counts were six to over ten thousand times higher than [[Colony Count]] results. Statistical tests indicated that cells in lake-water capillaries were approximately randomly distributed, and results could be obtained within about 2 hours, compared with 10 days for colony counts.

## Background & Description

The paper's stated motivation is that [[Foot and Taylor (1949)]] showed that media of differing composition select different parts of the bacterial population in water. No single medium could therefore give a reliable total count, so a non-cultural counting technique was sought.

The counting method has four main steps:

- **Staining:** 0.2 ml of a glycerol-based [[Gentian Violet]] stain (0.1% w/v in 50% v/v glycerol) is added to 5 ml water subsamples. Stain is filtered through [[Gradocol Membranes]] (mean pore diameter 1.01 µm) to remove contaminating bacteria. Samples are mixed for 1 min and heated to 80 °C for 30 s to improve stain penetration, then cooled.
- **Concentration:** Drops of about 0.01 ml are placed on a polished [[Stainless Steel Plate]] and held in vacuo over phosphorus pentoxide. The initial 50% glycerol drop (29 mg) lost its water in about 16 min, when it weighed 14.5 mg. The residue is therefore essentially glycerol with stained cells.
- **Capillary filling:** Hand-drawn Pyrex capillaries with internal bores of 20–60 µm are weighed empty (500–1,500 µg), filled by capillary attraction, sealed, and reweighed. Concentrate weight was usually 35–120 µg. Column length is measured from meniscus to meniscus with the microscope stage vernier.
- **Enumeration:** Cells are counted with a 95× fluorite oil-immersion objective. A calibrated ratchet advances the capillary 60 µm per step, which equals one field width. The aim is about 30 organisms per capillary. Volume is derived from concentrate weight divided by the specific gravity of glycerol. Cells per ml are then calculated from mean cells per field, total fields, and the concentration factor.

Organisms for pure-culture tests were recent lake-water isolates grown on nutrient agar slopes with 0.1% Difco Bactotryptone and then in nutrient broth for three days at 20 °C. Lake water was collected with a [[Mortimer Sampler]] into sterile 1 L soda glass bottles and examined within 3 hours, before significant bacterial multiplication could occur. The bottles were shaken by inversion twenty times before a 5 ml subsample was taken.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The method was developed and tested on lake water from [[Windermere North Basin]], with water taken at depths of 1 m, 5 m, 30 m and 50 m during May, June and July 1949. Seventeen lakes were also sampled for the randomness test. The authors stress that bacterial numbers in lakes change quickly with external conditions, so a rapid estimate of cell content matters to limnologists.

Colony counts were made on the medium of [[Taylor (1940)]], adjusted to pH 7.2, with plates incubated at 20 °C for 10 days. Hand-drawn capillaries introduced variation in bore. Because capillary bore was not uniform, the authors noted that the volume estimate depends on capillary geometry, which affects the reliability of the counts.

## Key Findings

**Direct count versus colony count**

- Direct counts were 6 to 11,000 times greater than colony counts (abstract).
- In the replicate Windermere experiment (20 replicates of each type, Table 3), the direct count was much higher than the colony count in all cases. Some direct-count confidence limits did not overlap, indicating significant differences between replicates. Colony counts were more consistent.
- In the depth and date series (Table 4), direct counts ranged from about 38,680 to 4,286,000 cells/ml. Colony counts ranged from about 200 to 13,400 cells/ml. Direct:colony ratios varied greatly with depth and date, but the direct count was always higher.
- Low column weight:length ratios were associated with high bacterial numbers per ml. The authors attribute this to narrow capillaries, which they did not expect.

**Randomness and Poisson distribution**

- Counts were tested for randomness against the Poisson distribution using a chi-square test. Because counts were large, the normal approximation to the chi-square deviate was used.
- Pure culture at a 1:2000 dilution (53 capillaries): mean normal deviate −0.30, standard deviation 1.25. Cells were treated as randomly distributed, although there were more negative values than theory predicts.
- Lake water (112 capillaries from 17 lakes): mean −0.35, standard deviation 0.93. Cell range was 0.17–1.55 cells per microscope field. The authors warn that confidence limits assuming randomness may be too wide.
- Low dilutions (10⁻¹ to 10⁻³) showed clumping, with mostly positive, significantly large deviates. Clumping could not be confidently attributed to pre-concentration aggregation or to crowding in the capillary.

**Stirring experiment**

- Stirring the concentrate with a vibrating platinum wire raised the mean count from 137,000 to 372,000 cells/ml (1:2000 pure culture, Table 2).
- Stirred drops showed significantly negative deviates, implying more even cell distribution. Unstirred drops did not differ significantly from zero.
- Stirring was dropped because it may have drawn detritus from the residue into capillaries, making it unclear whether bacteria or particles were counted.

**Capillary uniformity and precision**

- In a test where one capillary was filled five times from one drop, and in another where a capillary was filled once from each of five drops, weight:length ratios ranged from 1.74 to 2.86 (Table 1). Rising ratios were linked to shorter columns and attributed to irregular bore.
- Each capillary used for counting was checked for bore uniformity.

**Recommended operating conditions**

The authors list these conditions from experience:

- Uniform internal bore, small enough to fit within one field of a 1/12 in. oil immersion lens.
- Liquid column length of 30–50 mm.
- Minimum concentrate weight of 30 µg.
- 20–50 organisms counted per capillary.
- Density between about 2 cells per field and 1 cell in 10 fields.

**Conclusions and practical value**

- The method works for total counts of bacteria in lake water and in low-density pure-culture suspensions. It is less suitable for dense suspensions unless smaller drop volumes are used (not yet tested).
- Standard-bore manufactured capillaries would improve volume control.
- The method gives a confidence interval for each count, so differences between counts can be judged quickly.
- Results are available within 2 hours, compared with 10 days for colony counts, which the authors consider important for limnological monitoring.
