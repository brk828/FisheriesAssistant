# Ecology of Spawning Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*) in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the spawning ecology of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the lower [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) near [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. Over spring (March–June) 1993–1995, the authors combined hoopnet sampling, [[PIT Tagging]] recapture records, discharge and temperature data, and habitat measurements to determine spawning phenology, habitat use, and movement. Spawning began in late March, peaked in mid-April, and waned by mid-May. Ripe males aggregated in complex, gravel-associated habitat, and ripe females joined these aggregations. Evidence indicates that a portion of the adult population migrates from the [[Colorado River]] into the LCR to spawn and then returns.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub was not formally described until 1945 (Miller 1946) because the remote, turbulent canyons of the Colorado River hindered fish surveys before the mid-20th century. The species has been reduced by more than 70% in distributional range over the twentieth century due to anthropogenic activities. It was listed as endangered in 1967, and recovery plans were approved in 1990 for upper Colorado River populations.

Habitats used by the species are typically swift, deep, and turbid, so direct observation is difficult. Researchers have historically relied on nets, traps, and electrofishing, which yield only general habitat descriptions. Phylogenetic evidence predicts that *Gila* species are generalized broadcast spawners. Earlier reports describe spawning over clean rubble and gravel, boulder and sand shoreline eddies, submerged cobble and gravel bars, and shorelines of large angular boulders.

The study area comprised two reaches in the lower 21 km of the perennially flowing LCR. Base flow is about 6.31 m³ s⁻¹, maintained by warm (approx. 20°C), chloride-rich, carbonate-saturated springs, and extensive [[Travertine]] deposits form reefs, terraces, and dams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Snowmelt causes flooding in February–March, with discharge ranging from 1.5 to more than 50 m³ s⁻¹. Flows in the mainstem Colorado River (150–600 m³ s⁻¹) are much higher than in the LCR, which allowed more intensive quantitative sampling in the LCR.
- **Temperature:** Mean water temperature was below 12°C in February and exceeded 20°C by mid-May at base flow. Spawning and egg incubation require temperatures above roughly 14–15°C.
- **Sampling constraints:** High turbidity, boulders, travertine deposits, high conductivity (>5000 µS cm⁻¹), and depths up to 5 m made seines, trammel nets, and electrofishing ineffective. [[Hoopnets]] were used instead.
- **Habitat structure:** Spawning habitat consisted of matrices of large boulders, travertine masses, chutes, runs, and eddies at 0.5–2.0 m depth, with clean gravel deposits.
- **Community:** Humpback chub numerically dominated the LCR fish community. Other native species included [[Speckled Dace]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. Non-native species included [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Plains Killifish]].

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort and population**
- A total of 1,597 net sets (March–June 1991–1995) captured 628 adults (>200 mm TL), 678 older juveniles (100–200 mm TL), and 782 yearlings (80–100 mm TL).
- Most adults (463 of 628, 73.7%) were captured in the upper study reach, which was therefore used for habitat and spatial analyses.
- Adult catches peaked in May. Large adults (>300 mm TL) fell to near zero by August, while smaller adults declined only by half.

**Spawning phenology**
- Spawning commenced between late March and early April, peaked in April as discharge approached base flow, and waned in May.
- Ripe-spawning fish peaked in April at 41%, and non- and near-spawning fish peaked in May at 84%.
- Relative condition (K) declined from a March high (1.22 females, 1.08 males) to June lows (0.99 females, 0.98 males). Winter condition was lower than March, suggesting condition peaked before spawning.

**Sex-specific patterns**
- Females expended their egg supply rapidly in March–April, whereas males maintained ripeness over a longer period.
- Among ripe-spawning fish, 85% were males. Among males, about 46% were ripe; among females, only about 7% were ripe.

**Movement and fidelity**
- Of 603 adults captured in the study reaches, 111 (18.7%) were also captured at the Colorado River–LCR confluence, indicating upstream spawning migration.
- Larger adults and ripe/near-spawning adults were more likely to have been captured at the confluence than smaller or non-spawning adults.
- Overall, 16% (97 of 603) of individuals were recaptured within the study reaches, with mean maximum recapture distances of 172 m within years and 325 m between years. Only 2 of 97 recaptured fish were found in both reaches, indicating fidelity to specific sections.

**Habitat use**
- Ripe-spawning fish were associated with increased angular variation of the bottom profile (SDPVA), higher gravel frequency (FGRAVL), and proximity to emergent edges (lower MLATP).
- Non-spawning fish favored slower currents, finer substrates (mostly sand), and less structural complexity.
- Discriminant analysis classified groups with 74% overall accuracy: 85% for non-spawning fish, 22% for near-spawning fish, and 75% for ripe aggregations.

**Spatial distribution**
- Adult fish were significantly clumped across the upper reach (1.98 fish per net overall). Clumping increased to 3.22 fish per net when ripe fish were present, and to 3.92 when a ripe female was present.
- Of 76 ripe fish captured in multiples, 70% were aggregated, and 86% of these were males.
- Of 21 aggregation sites, 17 lay near or within major travertine–boulder complexes. Three consistent clusters (A below a travertine dam, B at the mouth of Big Canyon, C at the mouth of Salt Trail Canyon) occurred in each of the three years. All observed ripe females caught with ripe males were within these clusters.

**Spawning activity indicators**
- Nearly 80% of ripe-spawning fish were captured during night sampling, suggesting crepuscular or nocturnal spawning.
- Abrasions on the anal and caudal fins (about 15% incidence in April–May, absent in June) suggest spawning contact with gravel substrates.

**Proposed spawning model**
- Winter and spring floods deposit clean gravel behind boulders and travertine structures. As flows recede in March–April, ripe males aggregate at these gravel sites. Gravid females move to these aggregations and may spawn with two or more males. Males do not appear to guard sites. Spawning repeats until gametes are exhausted, usually by mid-May.

**Management implications**
- The authors recommend identifying spawning habitat within the historic range to establish additional spawning stocks, and modifying regulated flows (e.g., in Grand Canyon) to encourage spawning.
- Future work should sample gravel for eggs and embryos, observe spawning behavior directly, and quantify spawning habitat using GIS.
