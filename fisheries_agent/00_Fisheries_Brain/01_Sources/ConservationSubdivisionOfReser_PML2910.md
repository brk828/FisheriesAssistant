# Conservation, Subdivision of Reserves, and the Anti-Dismemberment Hypothesis

#conservation-biology #island-biogeography #ecology

## Executive Summary

This Oikos forum (1984, vol. 42, no. 3) is a multi-author exchange on whether one large nature reserve or several small reserves of equal total area better conserve species, the "SLOSS" question. The lead piece by [[Edwin O. Willis]] defends the [[Anti-Dismemberment Hypothesis]], which holds that cutting a reserve into fragments is as harmful as or more harmful than taking the same area from one edge. Responses by [[Olli Järvinen]] and [[Daniel Simberloff]] with Lawrence Abele challenge this view. Järvinen's plant data from Finnish woodlots show that small woodlots can hold as many species as one large woodlot. Simberloff and Abele argue the debate concerns resource allocation, not literal dismemberment, and that no single optimal strategy exists. A third, unrelated paper on time as a niche axis by [[John H. Carothers]] and [[Fabian M. Jaksić]] is also included.

## Background & Description

The forum format invites concise commentary, viewpoints and theoretical debate, and the editor notes that formal research reports are not accepted. The exchange centres on reserve design in [[Conservation Biology]] and draws on [[Island Biogeography Theory]].

Willis traces the anti-dismemberment idea to the 1975 [[Applied Biogeography]] chapter by [[Edward O. Wilson]] and Willis, which grew from peninsular biogeography. Peninsulas hold fewer species than mainland tracts, and the authors attributed this to impeded immigration on three sides. Willis argued that a single large reserve has a shorter edge-to-area ratio than several small reserves of the same total area and shape. Wilson and Willis originally recommended human settlement in moderate "elliptical" zones within a natural matrix covering about 50% of the earth. Willis states that Wilson later reframed this as favouring large natural "ellipses" to make the proposal more publishable.

Simberloff and Abele state that their work addressed SLOSS ("single large or several small") and never advocated "dismemberment" in the sense of tearing a reserve apart.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Edge effects.** Willis lists edge-related effects that can reduce diversity: fires, hunters, cats, herbicides ("pollutants"), edge species, fencing costs, restricted movement, and predators or diseases concentrated at edges. He also lists possible benefits: outside disturbances reaching only one reserve, faster human access to interiors, added habitat diversity or speciation at edges, and containment of population imbalances. Willis doubts that the benefits are as strong as the detriments, except for increased propagule movement between reserves, which underlies his [[Corridor Zone]] recommendation. He argues that small reserves resist fire and predator spread less well and that forest-interior species cannot use the peripheral 100 m or more of forest.

**Chance and ecological truncation.** Willis calculates that two reserves each holding 30% of a large reserve's species would, by chance alone, retain 0.3 × 0.3 = 9% of the original species. [[Ecological Truncation]], the preferential loss of specialists and large or wide-ranging species, would be expected to preserve 70–91% instead. Willis cites [[Barro Colorado Island]] ant-following birds, all large and lost after isolation, which persisted 20, 56 and 68 years after isolation. He cites Willis (1979) for São Paulo woodlots, where large frugivores and ground-feeding insectivores disappeared more often than small members of their groups.

**Peninsula effect.** Simpson (1964) first described fewer species toward peninsula tips. [[MacArthur and Wilson]] (1967) attributed the Florida, Baja California and Yucatán patterns to reduced immigration. Simberloff and Abele counter that habitat change along peninsulas may explain the pattern. Four studies support this alternative: Taylor and Regal on heteromyid rodents of Baja California (decline toward the tip, with immigration-extinction considered viable), Seib on Baja California reptiles (no decline), Warner on Florida birds (decline toward the Keys), and Means and Simberloff on Florida reptiles and amphibians (decline toward the Keys). None of these studies documented recent extinction. Brouin's survey of archipelagos found shape explained none of the variation in insular species richness for most systems.

**Harpy eagles and area sensitivity.** Willis argues that [[Harpy Eagle]] (*Harpia harpyja*) and similar area-sensitive species would not persist in ten reserves of 20 ha, though they might in one larger reserve. The area figure is ambiguous in the OCR text. Järvinen's triage approach is criticised for accepting the loss of such species.

**Plants and mangroves.** Järvinen's study concerns plants in Finnish woodlots. Willis suspects these species disperse readily and need little area, and that trees may be relicts of formerly continuous woodland. Simberloff notes that Åland woodlots were never connected to the mainland after glaciation. Willis expects few [[Mangrove Arthropods]] to die off in fragmented mangroves because the habitat is naturally patchy.

## Key Findings

- **Järvinen (1982):** Several small Finnish woodlots held about as many plant species as one large woodland of equal area. Total species richness predicted endangered plant species better than area did.
- **Willis's position:** Dismemberment is generally harmful, edge effects dominate, and large undissected reserves should be trialled. Corridors connecting reserves are recommended. Willis also warns that species extinctions may lag reserve isolation by decades.
- **Simberloff and Abele's position:** The real question is SLOSS, how limited conservation funds should be allocated. A single optimal strategy does not exist. Their data and Brouin's analysis do not support a general shape effect.
- **Taxonomic and regional limits:** Willis argues that studies in Europe cannot settle questions for tropical Africa or South America. Simberloff and Abele argue that Willis's own biogeographic "basic procedures" are similarly unqualified.
- **Shared ground:** Both sides agree that local variation in outcomes matters, that small reserves can be valuable when they hold distinct habitats, and that field data are needed.
- **Time as a niche axis (Carothers and Jaksić):** Temporal partitioning is usually treated as a niche axis only in exploitation models. The authors argue that interference competition is more likely to produce temporal partitioning, so time can be an independent niche dimension.
