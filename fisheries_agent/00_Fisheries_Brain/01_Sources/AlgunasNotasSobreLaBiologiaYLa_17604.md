# Algunas notas sobre la biologia y la pescaa de los camarones del Mar de Cortes

## Executive Summary
This 1993 paper (Costa, Almeida, Costa, Assis & Moreira, Instituto de Zoologia "Dr. Augusto Nobre") examines why [[European Eel]] (*Anguilla anguilla*) and mullet species (Fam. [[Mugilidae]]) thrive in the heavily anthropized [[Tagus Estuary]] (Tejo), despite intense pollution, damming, and urban/industrial pressure from a basin populated by ~3.8 million people (41% of mainland Portugal's population). The authors argue that both taxa owe their ecological resilience to **behavioral and dietary plasticity** rather than specialized adaptation: eels exploit individual variability in migratory behavior, diet, and sex determination to reduce intraspecific competition, while mullets exploit broad dietary opportunism (detritivory, herbivory, carnivory, filter-feeding) to reduce both intra- and interspecific competition. The paper also documents how human-made barriers (29 dams in the Tagus basin, many without functional fish passages) disrupt these natural strategies, skewing sex ratios and lowering condition factors in blocked eel populations, and how a 1990 mass poisoning event during the mullet reproductive "critical period" nearly collapsed a *Liza ramada* cohort.

## Background & Description
- **European Eel (*Anguilla anguilla*)**
  - Sexual dimorphism: males are smaller-bodied than females; growth rate variability (fast- vs. slow-growing individuals) increases length dispersion within age classes ([[Tesch 1977]]).
  - No differentiated sex chromosomes in most individuals; sex determination is **metagamic** (environmentally influenced after fertilization) rather than genetically fixed ([[Wiberg 1983]]); early claims of female heteromorphic chromosomes ([[Chiarelli et al. 1969]]) were later refuted ([[Sola et al. 1980]]; [[Wiberg 1983]]).
  - Immature gonads are a "basic" testis-like structure that can differentiate into either testis or ovary depending on expression of an "anti-gene" ([[Colombo et al. 1984]]).
  - **Head width** (normalized to body length) correlates with prey type: narrow-headed eels take smaller/burrowing prey; intermediate heads take larger mobile prey; wide-headed eels take prey indiscriminately (except deeply-burrowed items), reflecting jaw musculature constraints ([[Opuszynski & Leszczynski 1967]]).

- **Mullets (Fam. Mugilidae)**
  - Digestive tract morphology allows filtering of suspended solids ([[Odum 1970a]]), enabling opportunistic feeding on organic matter from sewage outfalls.
  - Diet-related morphology shifts with ontogeny: juveniles (*Liza ramada*, *L. aurata*) show a gradual dietary transition from carnivory to herbivory beginning around **15–20 mm standard length** ([[Albertini-Berhaut 1974]]).
  - Low mercury bioaccumulation in tissues of *L. ramada* and *M. cephalus* despite inhabiting highly mercury-contaminated sites (Lima, pers. comm.).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
### European Eel
- **Reproductive strategy**: single spawning ground (Sargasso Sea) enables cross-fertilization and prevents subspecies formation ([[Helfman et al. 1987]]), while **individual behavioral diversity** compensates for this reproductive uniformity ([[Moriarty 1987]]).
- **Habitat range**: colonizes coastal marine zones, estuaries, lagoons, lakes, and rivers — an ecological breadth unmatched among fish, linked to variable migratory instinct (strongly migratory individuals move upstream; less migratory ones settle downstream) ([[Tesch 1977]]; [[Moriarty 1987]]).
- **Diet**: ~80 prey categories identified from ~1000 stomachs from Tagus estuary and river eels ([[Costa 1989]]), including annelids, molluscs, crustaceans, insect larvae, fish and fish eggs, plus terrestrial insects, arachnids, macrophyte fragments, algae, and mineral matter.
  - Size-based diet partitioning reduces intraspecific competition (Tables I–IV):
    - **Estuary**: small eels → mysids/isopods/amphipods; intermediate → polychaetes; large → bivalves/decapod crustaceans.
    - **River**: small eels → oligochaetes; intermediate → trichopteran larvae; large → gastropods/coleopteran larvae.
  - Head-width-based partitioning mirrors this pattern (narrow heads → small/burrowing prey; wide heads → larger prey).
- **Sex determination & density**: high population density during early freshwater residence favors male development; low density favors female development ([[Sinha & Jones 1966]]; [[Tesch 1977]]; [[Vollestad & Jonsson 1988]]; [[Colombo et al. 1984]]).
  - Estuaries (high density) → male-dominated; upstream reaches (low density) → female-dominated; near-mouth zones (low density due to emigration and *Conger conger* predation/competition) → also female-biased ([[Moriarty 1987]]; [[Tesch 1977]]).
  - Sex-ratio data along the Tagus ([[Costa 1989]]): Alcochete (estuary) 78.0% male; Vila Franca de Xira (transition zone) 40.2% male; Belver Dam (~200 km from mouth) 65.2% male — anomalously high due to a poorly functioning fish pass blocking upstream migration and artificially concentrating eels ([[Alexandrino 1990]]; [[Assis 1990]]).
  - Possible mechanism: density-dependent behavioral/physiological (hormonal/odor) inhibition of female differentiation rather than simple food-limitation ([[Vollestad & Jonsson 1988]]).
  - Female sex is the **limiting sex** for recruitment (egg output determines potential recruitment); density-dependent sex determination acts as a natural population control mechanism; sudden population declines can be buffered by sex reversal of young males ([[Sinha & Jones 1966]]).
- **Impact of dams**: Reduced invertebrate diversity in lentic habitats behind dams ([[Maitland 1978]]) lowers prey availability, producing eels with poor condition factor and reduced reproductive migration success. Condition factors measured: Alcochete 0.18, Vila Franca de Xira 0.19, Belver Dam 0.15 — the Belver value is concerning given that brackish-water eels already show lower baseline condition due to osmotic stress ([[Tesch 1977]]).

### Mullets (Fam. Mugilidae)
- Classified in the literature as detritivorous/herbivorous as adults (Table V), but field observations reveal broad dietary plasticity:
  - *Liza ramada* and *L. aurata* readily consume polychaetes (used as bait) and even bread crumbs; *L. ramada* exhibits predatory strikes on metallic fishing lures.
  - High densities of mullet occur at urban sewage outfalls, exploiting suspended organic material via filter-feeding ([[Odum 1970a]]).
  - Juvenile diet is more carnivorous, transitioning to herbivory with growth ([[Albertini-Berhaut 1974]]); contra this, *M. cephalus* juveniles in Sri Lanka were found to feed mainly on plant particles ([[De Silva & Wijeyaratne 1977]]).
  - Juvenile *M. cephalus* and *M. curema* observed filter-feeding at the air-water interface during a bloom of the dinoflagellate *Kryptoperidinium* sp., switching feeding mode to exploit the most energetically rewarding available resource ([[Odum 1970b]]).
  - Mullets can subsist on low-quality food unusable by other fish ([[Hickling 1970]]), potentially deriving significant energy from ingested bacteria and protozoans ([[Moriarty 1976]]).
  - Reduced interspecific competition with other teleosts due to unique feeding niche ([[Hartley 1940]]; [[Odum 1970a]]); in the Tagus, competition occurs mainly with cyprinids in freshwater reaches.
  - Intrafamilial (Mugilidae) competition is reduced via differential particle-size selectivity among species ([[Marais 1980]]).
- **Critical life-cycle period**: For Tagus *L. ramada*, the reproductive/recruitment critical period spans **September–March** ([[Almeida 1989]]), during which disturbances can severely impact future population.

## Key Findings
- **Behavioral/genetic diversity as adaptive strategy**: In *A. anguilla*, variability in migratory behavior, growth rate, head morphology, and density-dependent sex determination collectively minimize intraspecific competition for food and shelter, enabling successful colonization of degraded and food-poor habitats.
- **Dam-induced sex ratio distortion**: Physical barriers with non-functional or inefficient fish passages (e.g., Belver Dam) trap eels in unnaturally concentrated populations, skewing sex ratios toward males (against the limiting sex) and lowering condition factors — a form of "unnatural variability" that threatens reproductive output.
- **Mullet dietary plasticity as key to pollution tolerance**: Ability to shift between herbivory, detritivory, filter-feeding, and opportunistic carnivory, plus tolerance of low-quality/high-organic-load food sources (e.g., sewage effluents), and low mercury bioaccumulation, explain mullet success in polluted, human-impacted estuarine environments.
- **Vulnerability despite plasticity**: A September–October 1990 mass poisoning event (suspected rodenticide) killed ~100 tonnes of mullet (mainly *L. ramada* and *M. cephalus*) in the Tagus estuary during the reproductive critical period ([[Anonimo 1990a,b,c]]), illustrating that even highly adaptable species remain vulnerable to acute anthropogenic disturbances during sensitive life-history windows; full population recovery was projected to take 5–6 years (time to first maturation of the affected recruitment cohort).
- **Broader ecosystem implication**: Anthropogenic modifications (damming, effluent discharge, water abstraction) selectively favor species with high adaptive plasticity ([[European Eel]], Mugilidae) while likely suppressing less tolerant fish communities, leading to simplified, low-diversity ichthyofaunal assemblages in the Tagus basin.
