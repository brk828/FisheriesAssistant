# Interactions Among Trout and Little Colorado Spinedace

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Technical Guidance Bulletin No. 2 (June 2000), funded under Federal Aid in Sportfish Restoration Project F-14-R, examines how nonnative trout affect the federally threatened [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*). The study addresses habitat use and overlap, diet overlap, predation, health and growth, and the movements and distributions of trout and spinedace in the upper [[Little Colorado River Basin]]. Results show extensive habitat overlap between [[Rainbow Trout]] and spinedace, low dietary overlap, documented but limited piscivory, and behavioral shifts by spinedace in the presence of trout. The authors conclude that trout pose a potential, density-dependent threat, and that trout management should be coordinated with spinedace conservation.

## Background & Description

- **Focal species:** Little Colorado spinedace is a small cyprinid endemic to the Little Colorado River system in Arizona, listed as federally threatened with critical habitat designated in 1987 ([[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]). Its decline is attributed to habitat degradation and loss, pollution, flow alteration, and interactions with nonnative fishes.
- **Nonnative trout:** [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) became the primary trout species stocked in the region and is currently the only nonnative salmonid stocked into spinedace habitat. Brown trout (*Salmo trutta*), [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), and [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarkii apache*) were also studied.
- **Regulatory context:** Following a Section 7 ([[Endangered Species Act]]) consultation, AGFD sportfish stocking practices were adjusted to reduce risk to spinedace.
- **Study objectives:** Six objectives covered habitat use and overlap, predation, diet, health, movements, and distributions, with management recommendations.
- **Study areas:** Rudd Creek (second-order stream, lower 4.5-km reach sampled), Nutrioso Creek (below and above [[Nelson Reservoir]]), and the Little Colorado River at [[Wenima Wildlife Area]] (3.3-km reach).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish assemblages:** Nutrioso Creek below Nelson Reservoir held spinedace, bluehead sucker, rainbow trout, brown trout, fathead minnow, and green sunfish. The Little Colorado River at Wenima included spinedace, roundtail chub, bluehead sucker, fathead minnow, rainbow trout, brown trout, and green sunfish.
- **Hydrological stress:** Annual precipitation was below average during the study. Rudd and Nutrioso creeks became intermittent in summer–autumn 1996–1997. The authors estimated that about 25% of Nutrioso Creek below Nelson Reservoir and more than 50% of lower Rudd Creek were dry in summers 1996 and 1997.
- **Stocking and removal:** Between 16,000 and 20,000 rainbow trout were stocked into Nelson Reservoir annually. Anglers removed roughly 11–18% of stocked fish (Table 12 values), and escapement was minimal.
- **Crayfish:** Crayfish were included as a competing and predatory species in multiple-predator experiments, and spinedace responded more strongly to crayfish than to either trout species.

## Key Findings

**Habitat use and overlap**
- Habitat overlap between 84–177 mm TL rainbow trout and Little Colorado spinedace was extensive (Schoener's index values in Table 1 ranged roughly 60–94%).
- In Rudd Creek (1996), small spinedace occurrence was independent of rainbow trout, while large spinedace and rainbow trout co-occurred positively (χ² = 8.86, P = 0.003, φ = 0.19).
- Rainbow trout tended to use undercut banks more than spinedace. Spinedace occupied deeper, faster-flowing locations than trout in enclosures.
- Spinedace increased cover use (from 57.5% to 100%) and shifted toward the bottom depth zone after rainbow trout were added to enclosures.
- In the artificial streams, low trout density increased spinedace cover use, while high density decreased it.
- Apache trout showed high habitat overlap with spinedace; the species tended to co-occur under cover and in the same zones rather than avoid each other.

**Diet overlap**
- Diet overlap between rainbow trout and spinedace was low in both natural and experimental settings, indicating limited food competition.
- Corixids and terrestrial insects comprised large portions of both species' diets, creating potential for competition.
- Terrestrial insect and corixid frequency in spinedace diets tended to decrease when rainbow trout were present.
- Fish lengths did not overlap; all spinedace were smaller than all trout examined.

**Predation**
- Piscivory was low. Of 54 rainbow trout examined, 3 had consumed fish; brown trout had the highest rate (7 of 24, one of which was a spinedace); brook trout had 1 of 4.
- Only 2 predation events on spinedace by rainbow trout were observed in artificial streams.
- In aquaria, rainbow trout consumed larval fish in 4 of 7 trials, even when alternate prey were available. Larvae were digested rapidly (typically undetectable 30 min after consumption), so wild predation on larvae could not be excluded.

**Health and growth**
- No rainbow trout–related effects on spinedace health or condition were detected in Nutrioso Creek (1996 condition factor 0.94 with trout present vs. 0.97 with trout absent).
- Condition of spinedace over 60 mm TL in Rudd Creek did not differ between trout-present and trout-absent years.
- Laboratory growth and feeding experiments showed no detectable indirect effects of trout on spinedace growth, likely because the fish were held in separate compartments.

**Movements and distributions**
- Mark–recapture data showed high site fidelity (66% of marked fish did not move), with movements of more than 1 km recorded.
- Rainbow trout escapement from Nelson Reservoir was minimal; trout numbers declined in Rudd Creek even without removal, likely due to drought.
- The authors hypothesized that rainbow trout may have historically limited spinedace distribution, though evidence was described as suggestive rather than conclusive.

**Management recommendations (summary)**
- Consider the trade-off between sport fishery benefits and spinedace conservation when planning trout stocking.
- Stock catchable rather than fry or fingerling rainbow trout where possible, and avoid stocking in streams occupied by spinedace.
- Prioritize streams with low gradient, pools, and few crayfish or nonnative fish for spinedace reintroduction.
- Conduct periodic surveys for nonnative trout and remove them from spinedace streams.
- Avoid introducing additional nonnative species; favor native-dominated fish assemblages.
