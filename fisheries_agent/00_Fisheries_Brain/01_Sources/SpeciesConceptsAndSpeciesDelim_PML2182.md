# Species Concepts and Species Delimitation

#systematic-biology #species-delimitation #general-biology

## Executive Summary

This theoretical paper argues that the long-running confusion over [[Species Delimitation]] stems from a failure to separate two problems: [[Species Concept]] (what a species is) and species delimitation (how to determine species boundaries and numbers from empirical data). The author reviews the many incompatible species concepts in use, shows that they share a common core, and proposes a [[Unified Species Concept]] in which a species is a separately evolving metapopulation lineage, or a segment of one. All other properties once treated as necessary for species status are reclassified as contingent properties and as lines of evidence for lineage separation. The paper then outlines consequences for how species should be delimited, including the use of multiple lines of evidence and new methods that move beyond traditional criteria.

## Background & Description

The paper opens by noting that species are among the fundamental units of biology, but because they are large, long-lived, and composed of ephemeral connections among organisms, they are difficult for humans to perceive directly. The author states that for roughly the past half century the species delimitation problem has been obscured by disagreement over the species concept itself.

Mayden (1997) listed 24 named species concepts, and many more alternative definitions exist. The paper notes that many of these are mutually incompatible and can yield different conclusions about the number and boundaries of species. Table 1 groups the alternatives into major classes and lists the property each one is based on, including [[Biological Species Concept]], ecological, evolutionary, [[Phylogenetic Species Concept]] (monophyly and diagnosable versions), phenetic, and genotypic cluster concepts. Properties that represent thresholds crossed by diverging lineages, and that are commonly treated as necessary, are marked with an asterisk. Examples include intrinsic reproductive isolation, same niche or adaptive zone, diagnosability, monophyly, and exclusive coalescence of alleles.

Different biologists emphasize different properties. Reproductive incompatibility matters to hybrid-zone researchers, niche differences to ecologists, diagnosability and monophyly to systematists, and morphology or genetics to paleontologists, museum taxonomists, and population geneticists.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is not an empirical field study, so it contains no site data, sample sizes, or measured environmental conditions. Its ecological content is conceptual. It explains that [[Speciation]] proceeds through general processes (mutation, natural selection, migration or its absence, and genetic drift) acting on diverse characters. As lineages diverge, they acquire properties at different times and in no fixed order. These include phenetic distinguishability, diagnosability, reciprocal monophyly, reproductive incompatibility, distinct ecologies, and passage through polyphyletic and paraphyletic stages in their genes. This variable timing is the root of the disagreement among concepts.

The paper also stresses that geographic information is necessary to separate true lineage discontinuities from differentiation within species, such as clines and [[Isolation By Distance]]. It notes that many traditional criteria do not explicitly incorporate geographic data.

## Key Findings

**1. Common element across concepts.** All contemporary species concepts share the view that species are (segments of) separately evolving metapopulation lineages. Disagreement arises from which secondary property each concept treats as necessary.

**2. Conceptualization versus delimitation.** Under the unified concept, species are defined by the general lineage concept alone. Properties such as intrinsic reproductive isolation, diagnosability, and monophyly become operational criteria (lines of evidence) for delimitation. Disagreements about species numbers should then arise only from differences in method reliability, data relevance, temporal scale, prospective versus retrospective perspective, or cases of incomplete lineage separation.

**3. Relevance of diverse properties.** Any property that provides evidence of lineage separation is relevant to delimitation. The author argues that privileging a single criterion unduly emphasizes one property at the expense of others.

**4. Subcategories of species.** Properties can define subcategories with precise adjectives, such as reproductively isolated, ecologically differentiated, or monophyletic species. The paper gives examples: a study of reinforcement needs reproductively isolated species, while a historical biogeography study may need monophyletic ones.

**5. Asymmetry of evidence.** The presence of a single relevant property is evidence for lineage separation, though it is fallible. The absence of any such property is not evidence against separation, because a lineage may not yet have acquired it. Only the absence of all of them is weak negative evidence. Recognizing a species without any positive evidence is nonetheless inappropriate.

**6. Multiple lines of evidence.** A highly corroborated hypothesis of lineage separation requires multiple lines of evidence. The further lineages have diverged, the more differences they are likely to have acquired, and the easier separation is to detect. The paper illustrates a pitfall: reciprocal monophyly of maternally inherited mitochondrial DNA can arise from low dispersal of females even when nuclear genes are exchanged, so two or more species might be inferred from a single metapopulation lineage.

**7. Moving beyond traditional criteria.** Intrinsic reproductive isolation, diagnosability, and reciprocal monophyly often represent artificial cutoffs along a continuous divergence process, and they are poor at detecting early-stage separation. The paper highlights coalescent-based methods (Knowles and Carstens, 2007), which can support lineage separation even when no sampled locus is monophyletic. It also highlights geographically explicit methods, such as Mantel tests, nested clade analysis, and boundary-detection approaches, as movement toward explicit delimitation.

**8. Limits of threshold methods.** Methods based on a fixed divergence level, whether drawn from prior cases, theoretical models, or arbitrary parsimony thresholds, are questioned. They may still be useful for first approximations when screening large numbers of samples from poorly studied taxa.

**9. Reconciling historical concepts.** Several earlier characterizations, including Mayr's "interbreeding-population concept," and the definitions of Simpson (1951, 1961) and Wiley (1978), are argued to correspond closely to the unified concept. Mayr's operational definition is distinguished from his general biological species concept. Hennig's (1966) extinction criterion is described as a conformity requirement, not an operational criterion.

**10. Conclusion.** The paper concludes that the realization that alternative species concepts are variations on a common theme ends interminable debates about the definition of the species category. It also gives a unified context for integrating the results of different delimitation methods in empirical work.
