# An Ecologic Observation on [[Succinea Avara]]

#malacology #gastropoda #succineidae

## Executive Summary
This short ecological note by Branley A. Branson documents an unusually dense terrestrial/amphibious population of the amber snail [[Succinea Avara]] found associated with colonies of the cyanobacterium [[Nostoc]] on the saturated banks of a small pond near Stillwater, Oklahoma. The observation, made in early April 1958 following heavy rains, demonstrates a strong ecological linkage between snail abundance and algal food availability under moist soil conditions. A follow-up visit eleven weeks later, after the soil had dried, showed the snails had shifted from the terrestrial slope to the pond's mud banks and aquatic vegetation, illustrating the species' ecological plasticity and amphibious tendencies in this region.

## Background & Description
- No detailed morphological description of the shell or soft body is provided in this brief note.
- Shells are noted to be fragile/thin — described as cracking audibly underfoot when the population density was high ("the snail shells cracked audibly as one walked over the area").
- Classified taxonomically as a member of [[Succineidae]] (amber snails), a family within [[Gastropoda]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Primary habitat**: Moist, well-vegetated watershed slopes surrounding a small pond; also found on aquatic vegetation and pond mud banks.
- **Microhabitat association**: Strongly associated with firm, spherical colonies of [[Nostoc]] (a cyanobacterium/blue-green alga), which the snails apparently graze upon as a food source.
- **Density**: Extremely high local density — 31 specimens counted in a single square foot of Nostoc-colonized soil; overall density across the ~30-foot band from the water's edge was estimated to be even higher.
- **Substrate/vegetation context**: The affected zone extended about 30 feet from the water's edge into a covering of Bermuda grass ([[Cynodon Dactylon]], implied).
- **Seasonal/moisture dependence**: Population was tightly linked to soil saturation from heavy rains; the terrestrial slope population collapsed entirely once the soil dried (by July 1, 1958), with both Nostoc and snails disappearing from that zone.
- **Amphibious behavior**: Despite being "generally considered a terrestrial species," S. avara in Oklahoma behaves amphibiously — observed crawling on aquatic macrophytes such as [[Nelumbo Lutea]] (American lotus) and [[Typha Latifolia]] (cattail) within the pond itself, as well as on dead vegetation floating in water.
- **Co-occurring species**: [[Succinea Grosvenori]], another succineid, was maintained alongside S. avara in a long-term aquarium (>1 year) containing [[Elodea]], suggesting both species can tolerate sustained aquatic/semi-aquatic conditions in captivity.
- **General moisture preference**: The species is consistently found in moist microhabitats favorable to cyanophyte algae or molds, whether terrestrial or aquatic.

## Key Findings
- Extraordinarily high localized density of [[Succinea Avara]] (≥31 individuals/ft²) was directly tied to lush growth of [[Nostoc]] following heavy rainfall, supporting a grazer-food source relationship between the snail and the alga.
- The snail population exhibited a rapid habitat shift over an 11-week period: from terrestrial slope dwelling (moist, Nostoc-rich soil) to purely aquatic-margin dwelling (mud banks and emergent vegetation) as the terrestrial habitat dried out — demonstrating short-term ecological plasticity and habitat tracking in response to moisture availability.
- Despite its classification as a terrestrial species, S. avara displays clear amphibious tendencies in Oklahoma populations, regularly using aquatic vegetation and submerged/floating dead plant material as habitat.
- Long-term captive maintenance (>1 year) of S. avara and [[Succinea Grosvenori]] in an aquarium with [[Elodea]] further corroborates the species' tolerance for aquatic conditions.
- The note underscores [[Succinea Avara]] as a "remarkably plastic species" with broad environmental tolerance spanning terrestrial and aquatic ecotones, contingent primarily on moisture and algal food availability rather than strict habitat type.
