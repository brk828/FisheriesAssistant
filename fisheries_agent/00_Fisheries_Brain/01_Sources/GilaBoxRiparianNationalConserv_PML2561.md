# Gila Box Riparian National Conservation Area Management Plan, Environmental Assessment and Decision Record

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1998 Bureau of Land Management (BLM) package from the [[Safford Field Office]] contains the 15-year [[Gila Box Management Plan]] for the [[Gila Box Riparian National Conservation Area]] (RNCA) in Arizona, together with [[Environmental Assessment]] AZ-M0-08-03, a [[Finding of No Significant Impact]], and a [[Decision Record]]. The RNCA was designated by the [[Arizona Desert Wilderness Act of 1990]] to conserve, protect and enhance its riparian areas and aquatic resources. The plan prioritises riparian recovery, which is expected to benefit native fish, through road reduction, livestock deferment from riparian zones, instream flow water rights, and non-native fish control. The Decision Record approves the plan, which is in conformance with the [[Safford District Resource Management Plan]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] biological opinions reviewed in the document.

## Background & Description

- **Legal basis:** Public Law 101-628 (Title II) designated the RNCA, withdrew it from mineral entry (subject to valid existing rights), limited motorized vehicles to designated roads, and required a management plan with "full public participation" and a report to Congress every five years.
- **Area:** About 21,767 acres of public land and 1,720 acres of private land, 180 miles east of Phoenix. It includes a 15-mile segment of [[Bonita Creek]], 23 miles of the [[Gila River]], and 1/8 mile of the lower [[San Francisco River]]. [[Eagle Creek]] flows into the area, giving four perennial waterways. The area is described as one of the most significant riparian zones in the Southwest.
- **Planning process:** A seven-member [[Gila Box Advisory Committee]] was appointed. Public scoping began in 1991; the committee met 19 times between October 1991 and November 1992; the Draft Plan/EA was released in August 1993, with comments through December 1993.
- **Consultation:** Six tribes were contacted, including the [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]], with no responses received. The San Carlos Apache Tribe raised a boundary dispute, which the BLM ruled outside the scope of this planning effort.
- **Governing laws:** The [[Federal Land Policy and Management Act]] (multiple use), the [[National Environmental Policy Act]], the [[Endangered Species Act]], the [[Wild and Scenic Rivers Act]], the [[National Historic Preservation Act]], the [[Clean Water Act]], and Executive Orders 11988 and 11990.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish fauna.** Bonita Creek still supports five native fish species at risk from non-native fishes that come from the Gila River. The plan lists 10 native species considered for reintroduction or range extension: razorback sucker, bonytail chub, Colorado squawfish, roundtail chub, flannelmouth sucker, woundfin, spikedace, loach minnow, desert pupfish and Gila topminnow. The [[Gila Chub]] is the key species for population monitoring.

**Listed and priority species.** The species table includes the [[Razorback Sucker]], which is endangered, with its Gila River critical habitat, plus the threatened spikedace and loach minnow. The table lists the Gila chub as a candidate species, with densities of 150 adults per mile in Segment 1 and 185 adults per mile in Segments 3 and 5. The razorback sucker is probably extirpated from Bonita Creek; no individuals were detected in recent intensive surveys.

**Key threats.** Flooding is naturally important, since native fishes are relatively flood resistant while non-native fishes are not (Meffe and Minckley 1987). Livestock trailing, off-road vehicles, sand rails, recreational pool damming and wading can displace fish. Water withdrawal during drought may stress aquatic organisms.

**Riparian habitat.** The vegetated riparian area totals about 635 acres on the Gila River and about 160 acres on Bonita Creek (795 acres overall). Cottonwood, willow and sycamore are the main woody species. Non-native [[Salt Cedar]] is a management priority.

**Hydrology and water rights.** The [[Gila Decree]] (1935) apportions almost all Gila River flow. The City of Safford holds a water right of 3,240,000 gallons per day from Bonita Creek. The BLM is seeking [[Instream Flow Water Rights]] on the Gila River, San Francisco River, Eagle Creek and Bonita Creek.

**Other context.** Visitor use exceeds 20,000 visitor use days per year. Bonita Creek contains [[Cultural Resources]] (cliff dwellings, pictographs, a Mimbres village and historic cabins). The plan also covers grazing allotments, minerals (the Dorothy B claim), and fire management under Department of the Interior Manual Part 910.

## Key Findings

1. **Riparian roads reduced.** Riparian roads in Bonita Creek are cut from about 15 miles to 2 miles for public access, plus 1.5 miles maintained by the City of Safford. No road is designated in the Gila River floodplain, so motorized use there is prohibited.
2. **Livestock deferred.** Livestock grazing is deferred from riparian areas for the life of the plan. Nine of 11 permittees had voluntarily agreed to this approach over the previous 20 years.
3. **Vegetation objectives.** The plan sets tree-to-sapling and density targets for cottonwood, willow and sycamore in nine segments (five on Bonita Creek, four on the Gila River), to be met within seven years after a major flood.
4. **Native fish protection.** A feasibility study is proposed for a barrier (under eight feet tall) to stop upstream invasion of non-native fish into lower Bonita Creek. The barrier would be built only if it is found effective and does not significantly harm visual resources or wild and scenic river values.
5. **Reintroduction.** The BLM, [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and USFWS will assess 10 native fish species for reintroduction, under a cooperative interagency agreement. Off-channel pools for razorback sucker were rejected as cost-prohibitive and a risk to habitat.
6. **Monitoring.** Fish monitoring is planned twice over the life of the plan, using key sites. Aquatic habitat is to be inventoried and monitored periodically. Tree-to-sapling ratios will be measured every three years and channel cross-sections every six years.
7. **Wild and scenic rivers.** The Record of Decision of May 29, 1996 found 8.1 miles of Bonita Creek suitable as "recreational," 15.2 miles of the Gila River "scenic" and 7.8 miles "recreational," and 1/8 mile of the San Francisco River "recreational." A legislative package went to Congress on April 16, 1997.
8. **Sand rail and river boating.** Sand rail use of the Gila River corridor is prohibited, justified by the House Report language and by unquantifiable impacts on spawning fish and birds. Commercial outfitters are capped at five, and a permit system starts when daily use reaches 80 persons in the canyon.
9. **Consultation outcome.** The USFWS Programmatic Biological Opinion (September 26, 1997) concluded that the grazing program is not likely to jeopardise listed species. A separate memorandum (November 6, 1997) gave conditional concurrence for the [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy-Owl]] and Arizona hedgehog cactus, conditional on updated protocol surveys before vegetation-removing actions.
10. **Decision.** The Safford Field Manager approved the plan for 15 years, finding no significant impacts and that no environmental impact statement is required. Environmental effects were assessed as high positive for soil and water, riparian vegetation, wildlife and fish, and low to moderate negative for dispersed motorized visitation and livestock operations.
