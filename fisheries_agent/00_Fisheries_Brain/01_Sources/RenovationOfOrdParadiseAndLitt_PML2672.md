# Draft Environmental Assessment: Renovation of Ord, Paradise, and Little Bonito Creeks for Apache Trout Restoration

#ichthyology #marine-biology #oncorhynchus

## Executive Summary

This April 1990 draft Environmental Assessment (EA) proposes restoring genetically pure [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*) in three streams on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] in the [[White Mountains]] of Arizona. Apache trout were listed as threatened (previously endangered). The main threats are competition and hybridization with introduced [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Rainbow Trout]], along with habitat degradation and fishing pressure.

[[Genetic Analysis]] by Genean Genetic Laboratories showed that fish in [[Ord Creek]] and [[Paradise Creek]] are hybrids, so the streams must be renovated and restocked. The preferred option, Alternative A, uses the piscicide [[Antimycin A]] to remove all fish, leaves the streams unstocked for at least one year, and then restocks them with pure Apache trout over two years. The EA was prepared for review by the [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]], the [[Apache Trout Recovery Team]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

## Background & Description

**Species status.** The Apache Trout Recovery Team was established in 1975. The recovery plan requires a minimum of thirty genetically pure, self-sustaining populations before the species can be delisted. Apache trout were historically confined to a few isolated streams, and their purity was uncertain after decades of rainbow and cutthroat stocking (40–50 years in the White Mountains).

**Study areas.** Three drainages are covered:
- **Ord Creek**, a headwater of the North Fork of the White River. It is the source stream of the original species description by [[R.R. Miller]] (1972).
- **Paradise Creek**, a North Fork tributary.
- **Little Bonito Creek**, a tributary of Big Bonito Creek and the Black River. It drains Crooked, Boggy, and Loafer Creeks, which hold pure Apache trout and are free of introduced trout in their upper reaches.

**Barriers.** Existing [[Fish Barriers]] are key to the plan:
- Ord Creek has a log barrier on the lower reach (1960s, Bureau of Indian Affairs and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) and a gabion/rock barrier on the upper reach (1976, Tribe and Service).
- Paradise Creek has a gabion/rock barrier near its midpoint, built by the Tribe's Game and Fish Department. It was undermined at the west end, letting brown trout pass. Reinforcement in summer 1989 directed all flow over the spillway.
- Little Bonito Creek received a new gabion/rock barrier in summer 1989, placed about one-half mile above the confluence with Big Bonito Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ord Creek.** In the late 1970s, the Ord Creek Apache trout population was under 400 individuals, while brook and brown trout were extensive and expanding. An earlier antimycin treatment, followed by reintroduction of parent stock, failed. The parent stock was later lost, and Ord Creek was re-treated and restocked with fish from Paradise Creek. Brook trout have since reinvaded and are now dominant.

**Paradise Creek.** Brown trout have expanded to outnumber hybrid Apache trout by at least 10:1. Lower-reach brown trout populations were earlier reduced by toxicant treatment, but the barrier failure allowed reinvasion.

**Little Bonito Creek.** This system holds a large brown trout population and only a very small, possibly hybrid, Apache trout population. Apache trout are rarely found. Without removal, brown trout threaten the pure populations in Crooked, Boggy, and Loafer Creeks. A successful renovation would create the first large, continuous Apache trout drainage.

**Restocking source.** Pure fish from the original Ord Creek strain are in the Coyote/Mamie Creek system on the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]]. Brood stock for Paradise Creek will come from stocks identified by the Recovery Team. Little Bonito Creek will be restocked from Boggy, Crooked, and Loafer Creeks.

**Sensitive species.** Several species of concern may occur in the project areas:
- *Birds:* bald eagle (endangered), osprey, and Mexican spotted owl. Disturbance during the project is the only risk.
- *Mammals:* Occult little brown bat. No potential for damage was identified.
- *Reptiles:* Mexican garter snake and narrow-headed garter snake (both category 2 candidates). Their food supply could be temporarily reduced.
- *Invertebrates:* White Mountain water penny beetle (category 2), which may be affected depending on its tolerance to the chemical.
- *Plants:* Arizona willow (category 1) and Gooding onion (category 1). The main threat is trampling. Pinetop Fishery Assistance Office staff will survey the area before work begins.

## Key Findings

**Recovery goals and alternatives**

- **Alternative A (preferred):** Use antimycin A to remove all fish from Ord, Paradise, and Little Bonito Creeks, leave them unstocked for at least one year, and restock with pure Apache trout over two years. The assessment concludes this option is "clearly" preferred because it is the only one that meets the recovery goal of establishing one of the thirty pure populations.
- **Alternative B:** Remove Apache trout or their hybrids to a holding site, eliminate all brook and brown trout, and then return the Apache trout or hybrids. The Endangered Species Act does not recognize hybrids, so these fish would not count toward delisting. This option does not change the purity of the population.
- **Alternative C:** Stock hatchery-reared Apache trout annually to "swamp" hybrid genes. This is less expensive, but it risks overcrowding in streams that are often at or above carrying capacity. Impurities would persist. It may suit areas that are not sensitive to recovery.
- **Alternative D (no action):** Hybrid Apache trout populations in Ord and Paradise Creeks could be reduced to extirpation. Brown trout in Little Bonito Creek would continue to threaten the nearby pure populations and could cause future revenue losses for the Tribe.

**Selection criteria and evaluation**

- Alternatives were judged on recovery goals, environmental impacts, impacts on other species of concern, economic impacts to the Tribe, and public and tribal acceptance.
- The Tribe's Apache trout fishery provides income and employment that would not exist with only rainbow, brook, and brown trout. Alternatives A and C were the only ones with a positive effect on public and tribal acceptance.
- Alternatives B, C, and D would not meet the recovery goal. Alternative D is the only option expected to cause a long-term negative effect, by eliminating Apache trout from a stream within its historic range.

**Piscicide safety**

- Antimycin A (Fintrol) is applied at parts per billion, is non-toxic to warm-blooded animals at recommended doses, and is only marginally harmful to target-area invertebrates.
- It breaks down naturally through riffle action and organic absorption. It can also be neutralized with one part per million chlorine or potassium permanganate.
- The assessment notes that enough Fintrol is available to save thousands of dollars in recovery costs.

**Planned sequence (Alternative A)**

1. Treat each stream from its origin down to its barrier with antimycin A.
2. Leave renovated reaches unstocked for at least one year to confirm that all introduced salmonids are gone and that barriers are working.
3. Restock with pure Apache trout broodstock over a two-year period, once no introduced species are confirmed.

**Review and coordination.** The EA must be reviewed and approved by the Tribe's Game and Fish Department, the Apache Trout Recovery Team, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service before implementation. Review will also be requested from the University of Arizona, Arizona State University, the U.S. Forest Service, and the Arizona Game and Fish Department.
