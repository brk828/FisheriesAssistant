# Ecological and Geomorphological Concepts for Instream and Out-of-Channel Flow Requirements

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fluvial-geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This paper proposes a conceptual framework that links [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) fish-habitat modelling with the geomorphic and riparian flow needs of river ecosystems. The authors argue that single-purpose instream flows, such as those derived from [[PHABSIM]], protect fish only in the short term, and that multiple flow regimes are needed to maintain channels, riparian vegetation, floodplains and valleys. The framework is demonstrated with [[USGS Gaging Station Data]] from the [[Salmon River]] at [[Whitebird Gaging Station]], Idaho. The paper was published in *Rivers* (Volume 2, Number 3, July 1991, pages 198–210) by Mark T. Hill, William S. Platts and Robert L. Beschta.

## Background & Description

Water withdrawals for power production, irrigation, flood control and other uses alter streamflow and harm aquatic resources. Earlier work set instream flow requirements mainly from fisheries, channel maintenance, or riparian perspectives, but few approaches linked instream and out-of-channel needs. The authors note that streamflow management has typically addressed one or two resource values rather than several at once.

The paper describes four flow regimes, which increase in magnitude in this order:

1. **Fish maintenance flows** (in-channel, meeting critical fish requirements), usually analysed with [[PHABSIM]] within [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]].
2. **Channel maintenance flows** (moderately high in-channel flows that limit vegetation growth in the channel and remove sediment).
3. **Riparian maintenance flows** (overbank flows that sustain riparian habitat, adjacent upland habitat, water tables and soil saturation).
4. **Valley maintenance flows** (flood flows that form floodplain and valley features).

Watersheds are considered in longitudinal, lateral, vertical and temporal dimensions (Ward and Stanford 1989). Valley type, classified with the [[Cupp Valley Segment Classification]], determines which flow regimes apply. For example, steep V-shaped valleys typically lack floodplains, so out-of-channel flows are not needed for riparian or valley maintenance there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Channel and floodplain processes.** Altered flows can cause floodplains to stop flooding, local water tables to stop recharging, bars and channels to stop being scoured, side channels to disconnect, and the ratio of pools to riffles to change (Morisawa 1968; Platts 1979). In [[Gravel-Bed Streams]], high flows are needed to flush fine sediment from spawning gravels. Regulated downstream flows can reduce fine sediment inputs (a benefit) while also reducing gravel recruitment for bank building (a cost).

**Riparian vegetation.** Riparian corridors influence light, temperature, organic input, cover and bank morphology (Larsen et al. 1986). Flooding during the growing season has a larger effect on floodplain productivity than an equal amount of non-growing-season flooding (Junk et al. 1989). Reduced natural flooding can let non-riparian species invade riparian zones.

**Floodplain function.** Floodplains provide cover, nesting, spawning and rearing habitat for fish and wildlife, and they transfer sediments and nutrients that sustain stream productivity (Sedell et al. 1989). They also supply energy to detrital food webs (Vannote et al. 1980).

**Hydrology of the study site.** Mean monthly flows at Whitebird derive from 76 years of daily records. The [[Hydrologic Data Base]] reports monthly and annual mean discharges for 1911–1917 and 1920–1989. Key values from Table 1:

The reach is an alluviated mountain valley, deeply entrenched in mountainous sidewalls, with a relatively wide floodplain and alluvial/colluvial deposition. The hydrology is snowmelt-controlled.

## Key Findings

- **Single-purpose flows are insufficient.** Fish-only instream flows (PHABSIM or similar) give short-term protection. Multiple flow regimes are needed for ecological integrity of streams, riparian zones and valleys.
- **PHABSIM remains useful for base flows.** It is most useful in late summer and fall, when most habitat suitability index curves are developed. It is not the right tool for channel, riparian or valley maintenance flows.
- **Bankfull flow is a benchmark, but variable.** Leopold and Emmett (1983) suggest a 1.5-year recurrence interval. Chorley et al. (1984), studying 36 active floodplains, found recurrence intervals between 1 and 32 years, so bankfull flow must be evaluated for each stream. Andrews (1980) found effective discharge nearly equal to bankfull discharge, which simplifies channel-maintenance estimates.
- **Snowmelt hydrographs have three components.** Rosgen et al. (1986) identify a snowmelt peak (bankfull), a base flow (7-day low flow at the 1.5-year return interval), and rising and recession limbs.
- **Ramping rates matter.** The authors recommend reducing flows by no more than 10% of the previous day's flow. Smaller reductions (less than 10%) are preferred to protect fish from stranding and to extend high flows for vegetation seeding. Rosgen et al. (1986) suggest a 10-day ramp-down from peak to base flow.
- **HEC-2 links discharge to riparian elevation.** The [[HEC-2 Model]] predicts water-surface elevations, which are used to find the discharge that reaches riparian habitat and floodplain elevations. HEC-2 transects can be extensions of IFIM transects, so field effort is minimal.
- **Valley-forming flows are hard to establish.** Discharges above the 10-year recurrence (Q10) are difficult to estimate because of short gaging records, measurement errors, and the infrequency of such flows. Many valleys were formed by glaciers, faults or lava flows, and some are now occupied by roads and homes.
- **Diversion of bankfull and flood flows** in any valley type will alter habitat and reduce fish populations and diversity.
- **Conventional wisdom about excess water is questioned.** The authors argue that substantial alteration of natural flows from snowmelt-controlled broad alluvial valleys will significantly affect fish habitat and abundance.
- **The methods are established, and the analysis is feasible.** The authors demonstrate for the Whitebird site that multiple-flow analysis is possible with methods already in common use, though it is seldom done because of cost and complexity.
- **Key research gaps remain.** Future work should quantify ecological links between in-stream and out-of-stream resources, and examine hydrostatic and hydrodynamic floodplain requirements. Data are often lacking for tributaries, and the duration of out-of-channel flooding needed for plant germination is unknown.
