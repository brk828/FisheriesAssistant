# Laboratory Studies of Fathead Minnow Predation on Catostomid Larvae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #predation-ecology #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This 1993 Klamath Tribes laboratory study by Larry Dunsmoor investigated whether introduced [[Fathead Minnow|fathead minnows]] (*Pimephales promelas*) prey upon larvae of the endangered [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) and [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*) in Upper Klamath Lake, Oregon. Despite no prior literature documenting piscivory in fathead minnows, laboratory trials demonstrated that groups of fathead minnows readily and effectively consumed sucker larvae — with mean predation rates reaching as high as 97% in tanks lacking structural cover. Predation was a **group behavior** (requiring ≥3 minnows), was **not gape-limited** (minnows tore larvae apart piecemeal rather than requiring whole-body ingestion), and was significantly **reduced by structural cover** (Scirpus stems) and, to a lesser degree, by the presence of alternate prey (Daphnia, dry food). These findings raise substantial management concerns regarding the role of introduced fathead minnows in the continued decline of endangered catostomid populations in Upper Klamath Lake, particularly in relation to lake-level management practices that eliminate shoreline emergent vegetation cover during larval sucker recruitment.

## Background & Description

- **Study system**: Upper Klamath Lake, Oregon, and its major tributary, the Williamson River — historically the primary nursery habitat for larval [[Lost River Sucker]] and [[Shortnose Sucker]], both federally listed as endangered in 1988.
- **Invasive species of concern**: [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), first established in Upper Klamath Lake between 1974–1979. By 1992, fathead minnows numerically dominated trap net catches in Upper Klamath and Agency Lakes, having risen from 18% of catch by number in 1983.
- **Reported diet elsewhere**: Fathead minnows are typically described in the literature (Carlander 1969; Scott and Crossman 1973; Held and Peterka 1974) as feeding on algae, aquatic insect larvae, zooplankton, detritus, and mud — with essentially **no documented piscivory** except a single ambiguous record of walleye larvae in trap-net-captured fathead minnow stomachs (Franzin and Harbicht 1992).
- **Motivating observation**: A pre-experimental trial found 5 fathead minnows consumed 39 of 40 Lost River sucker larvae, prompting the formal laboratory study.
- **Experimental setup**: Conducted at the Klamath Tribe Native Fish Hatchery, Chiloquin, OR, using 68 L rectangular tubs in a recirculating system at ~17°C (matching ambient Upper Klamath Lake shoreline temperatures). Sucker larvae were derived from adults captured in the Sprague and Williamson Rivers (May 1992); fathead minnows were seined from Upper Klamath Lake near Modoc Point (June 1992) and treated for columnaris infection prior to use.
- **Six trials** examined effects of: fathead minnow gape width (small/medium/large), fathead minnow group size, alternate prey availability (Daphnia + dry food), and water depth/cover (simulated Scirpus emergent vegetation).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic nursery habitat**: 1941 aerial photography showed 9.7 km of meandering Williamson River channel through 1,930 ha of emergent marsh lined with willows (*Salix* sp.) and cottonwoods (*Populus* sp.), with uninterrupted emergent marsh shorelines extending for kilometers along Upper Klamath Lake.
- **Habitat degradation**: Diking, dredging, and channelization (loss of 4.5 km of river channel) converted much of this marsh to agricultural use, replacing structurally complex shoreline habitat with dikes ranging from narrow bands of emergent macrophytes (*Scirpus*, *Sparganium*, *Typha*) to bare sand or riprap.
- **Water management impact**: Lake levels, regulated since a 1921 dam, are drawn down for irrigation through summer — the exact period larval suckers occupy shoreline nurseries. Field measurements indicate emergent vegetation becomes unavailable as cover once water levels drop ~0.48 m below full pool; this threshold was crossed before mid-July in 9 of 20 years (1970–1990).
- **Predator-prey habitat overlap**: High densities of adult fathead minnows occupy the same shoreline nursery habitats used by larval suckers (Buettner and Scoppettone 1990), heightening the ecological relevance of the laboratory predation findings.
- **General ecological principle invoked**: Complex vegetated habitats are well known to reduce fish predator foraging efficiency (Boyle 1979; Cooper and Crowder 1979; Savino and Stein 1982; Heck and Crowder 1991), and reservoir drawdowns typically increase predation on forage fish by eliminating littoral refugia (Ploskey 1986) — a pattern consistent with this study's cover experiment results.
- **Larval developmental vulnerability**: Sucker larvae used in trials (40–52 days old, late mesolarval to mid metalarval stage per Snyder 1981 terminology) were likely *less* vulnerable than wild larvae, which typically enter Upper Klamath Lake nursery habitats at <40 days old as protolarvae/early mesolarvae — an earlier, more predation-susceptible developmental stage (per Miller et al. 1988's link between larval stage and predator avoidance capability).

## Key Findings

### Group Behavior & Gape Limitation
- **No predation occurred with a single fathead minnow** present (Trial 1, 0% predation), regardless of gape width class.
- Predation only emerged when **≥3 fathead minnows** were grouped together (Trials 3–6), indicating **group/mobbing behavior** is necessary for successful piscivory.
- Fathead minnows were **not gape-limited**: larvae were sometimes swallowed whole, headfirst, but were also **torn apart and eaten piecemeal** by groups of minnows — decoupling maximum prey size from simple predator mouth-width/prey body-depth relationships (contrary to the classic gape-limitation paradigm, e.g., Lawrence 1958).
- No significant differences in predation rates were detected among small, medium, and large gape-width treatments (Trials 3–5), though statistical power was low (0.08–0.17) in all cases.
- No significant shift in larval size-frequency distributions pre- vs. post-trial (Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests), suggesting **no selective targeting of particular larval sizes**.

- Presence of **alternate prey** (Daphnia + dry food, Trial 5) markedly reduced predation (5–43%) relative to Trial 4's no-alternate-prey condition (75–98%), but **did not eliminate** predation on sucker larvae — indicating fathead minnows continue to target sucker larvae even when other food is abundant.

### Effect of Structural Cover (Trial 6)
- Simulated emergent vegetation (*Scirpus* stems, ~565 stems/m²) significantly reduced predation:
  - **Mean predation with cover present: 63.3% (SD 18.4%)**
  - **Mean predation with cover absent: 96.6% (SD 11.3%)** (outlier excluded)
  - Parametric ANOVA: cover effect significant (P<0.06); water depth effect not significant (P>0.15); no significant depth × cover interaction (P>0.22).
- Nonparametric analysis (with outlier retained) found no significant main effects of depth (P>0.75) or cover alone (0.10<P<0.25), but a significant **depth × cover interaction** (0.05<P<0.10) — low water with cover present had significantly lower predation than low water with cover absent.
- The ~33 percentage-point reduction in predation attributable to cover is considered **biologically significant**, with potential implications for sucker year-class formation.

### Methodological Artifacts
- In Trials 3 and 4, slightly taller standpipes in two tanks created a submerged ~1 cm ledge that larvae used as escape cover, likely **biasing predation rate estimates downward** (underestimated means, overestimated SDs) in the medium and large gape-width treatments. Standpipes were corrected before subsequent trials.

## Management Implications

- Results suggest fathead minnow predation could be a meaningful contributor to poor recruitment/year-class formation in [[Lost River Sucker]] and [[Shortnose Sucker]], but **laboratory findings require field validation** before predation can be confirmed as a significant field-level threat (per La Bolle 1981's caution regarding artificial confinement effects).
- Pending field validation, the author recommends:
  - Managing sucker nursery habitats for **structural complexity**.
  - **Maintaining lake water levels** sufficient to keep emergent vegetation inundated through at least mid-July, preserving refugia during the peak larval recruitment window.
  - Long-term restoration of the lower Williamson River and associated Upper Klamath Lake shorelines toward historical, structurally complex marsh conditions.
