# Diagnoses and Key to the Juveniles of Tilapia Species in Lake Victoria

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 paper by R. L. Welcomme, of the East African Freshwater Fisheries Research Organization in Jinja, Uganda, provides diagnoses and a rule-of-thumb key for identifying juvenile [[Tilapia]] (family [[Cichlidae]]) in [[Lake Victoria]]. Recent introductions had placed several morphologically similar species together on nursery beaches, and adult-based taxonomic keys are hard to apply to fry. The paper treats eight species, of which only [[Tilapia Esculenta]] and [[Tilapia Variabilis]] are native. The key relies mainly on the [[Tilapia Mark]], flank bars, body shape and a few meristic counts such as [[Gill Rakers]] and anal spine number.

## Background & Description

- **Source context:** Published in the *East African Agricultural and Forestry Journal* (October 1964); received 26 March 1964.
- **Motivation:** Introduced species had spread unevenly. [[Tilapia Nilotica]] and [[Tilapia Leucosticta]] remained a small share of the population in most regions, while [[Tilapia Zilli]] had increased so much that its catches often exceeded those of the native [[Tilapia Variabilis]]. Introductions began in 1953 (see Welcomme 1964 for the history).
- **Source of material:** Juveniles were collected during a habitat-preference study and from the [[Kajansi Fish Farm]], Uganda. Taxonomic information came from Lowe (1955, 1957) and Boulenger (1915).
- **Separating from Haplochromis:** Young tilapias are distinguished from similar-sized [[Haplochromis]] by:
  - the presence of the Tilapia mark (Haplochromis lacks it);
  - scale type: [[Ctenoid Scales]] (rough, Haplochromis) versus [[Cycloid Scales]] (smooth, Tilapia);
  - absence of coloured spots on the anal fin in Tilapia.
- **Method limits:** Lateral line scale and fin ray counts are similar across most species, so separation relies mainly on the Tilapia mark, flank bars and body shape. Absolute separation by meristic characters alone is achieved in only two cases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Introduced versus native:** Of the eight species, only *T. esculenta* and *T. variabilis* occur naturally in the basin. The others were introduced, mainly through [[Pond Culture]] in ponds and dams around the lake, and could be washed into the lake at any time.
- **Northern versus southern lake:** Five species were at least moderately common at the northern end. At Mwanza in the south, only *T. zilli*, *T. esculenta* and *T. variabilis* were common.
- **Habitat associations:**
  - *T. nilotica*: intermediate, mud-bottomed, slightly exposed beaches, usually with *T. esculenta* and *T. variabilis*. Its natural range is wide in North Africa and the Middle East, but the [[Murchison Falls]] previously blocked its upstream spread from the Nile into Lakes Victoria and [[Lake Kyoga]].
  - *T. zilli*: moderately exposed sandy and rocky beaches, and muddy shores with vegetation. Absent from very sheltered areas with low dissolved oxygen. Previously restricted to [[Lake Albert]] in Uganda.
  - *T. esculenta*: muddy, sheltered areas with weeds, including papyrus swamp channels (Lowe 1956). Recent lake-level rises submerged these sites, and fry are now absent from them. Small shoals have entered rivers.
  - *T. leucosticta*: mostly in lagoons behind fringing swamps at the northern end. Typically in de-oxygenated zones, and occasionally on lake-shore beaches.
  - *T. variabilis*: sandy or gently shelving rock bottoms, and intermediate beaches. Also recorded in rivers (Whitehead 1959).
  - *T. mossambica*: endemic to warmer waters of southern Africa. Not reported from the lake, but heavily stocked in basin ponds, where fish may enter the lake during high rainfall.
- **Possible hybridization:** Fish with characters intermediate between *T. nilotica* and *T. variabilis* have been found, at least in northern waters.

## Key Findings

1. **Species count:** Five *Tilapia* species are certainly present in Lake Victoria. Three more (*T. nigra*, *T. melanopleura*, *T. mossambica*) are considered possibly present or likely to be introduced.
2. **Identification approach:** Juvenile identification uses a key built on one or a few outstanding characters per fish. Within-species juvenile appearance differs markedly from adults.
3. **Key entry point:** Four or more anal spines identify *T. nigra* alone. Definite bars on the caudal fin separate *T. nilotica*.
4. **Closely similar pair:** *T. zilli* and *T. melanopleura* are very hard to separate as fry. The main differences are the opercular spot (usually absent in *melanopleura*), the dark bars behind the Tilapia mark, and pelvic fin length and colour.
5. **Tilapia mark:** It is a diagnostic spot on the dorsal fin that fades with age in most species. In *T. variabilis* it is absent in all but the youngest fish, and in *T. esculenta* it persists to about 7 cm.
6. **Colour variants:** The "Muaradadi" variety of *T. variabilis* (orange and black as adults) can be recognised in juveniles down to about 4 cm.
7. **Discussion and outlook:** Morphological distinctness is still clear, but hybrids and fish with intermediate characters may increase. The author recommends Lowe (1955, 1957) and Greenwood (1958) for adult keys.
