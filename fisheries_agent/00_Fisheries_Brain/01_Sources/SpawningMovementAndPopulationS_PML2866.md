# Spawning, Movement and Population Structure of the Flannelmouth Sucker (Catostomus latipinnis) in the Paria River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 University of Arizona M.S. thesis by Steven Joseph Weiss examines the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) in the lower [[Paria River]] and its confluence with the [[Colorado River]] in Glen Canyon and Grand Canyon. The study combined spawning observations, habitat availability and use measurements, seine and hoop-net catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) sampling, [[PIT Tagging]] and recaptures, and a broader aquatic survey of the Paria basin. Spawning occurred from mid-March to early April, in shallow water over clean gravel, and the population appeared to rely on recruitment from downstream populations rather than local juvenile rearing.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The flannelmouth sucker is the only endemic "big river" catostomid native to the Colorado River that remains common throughout the Grand Canyon. Its eggs (3.8-3.9 mm) are the largest of Colorado Basin catostomids. It is a non-guarding, lithophilic breeder with demersal, initially adhesive eggs.
- **Conservation status:** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service lists it as a Candidate Category 2 species. Hybridization with introduced [[White Sucker]] in the upper basin and with the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] is a documented threat.
- **Diet:** Upper-basin stomach analyses show a diet largely of algae and debris. In the Grand Canyon, [[Amphipoda]] (*Gammarus lacustris*) and [[Chironomidae]] larvae were also significant.
- **Study area:** The Paria River rises from springs at the base of cliffs in Bryce Canyon National Park, Utah. It drains about 3650 km² and enters the Colorado River 25 km below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Its sediment load and water chemistry most closely resemble the pre-dam Colorado.
- **Thesis context:** The work was funded by a Bureau of Reclamation contract through the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and was supervised by O. Eugene Maughan, with committee members William Matter and Bill Mannan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and temperature.** The Paria is perennial, with a base flow of 3.5-35.3 cfs. Instantaneous flows during the study ranged from 2.5 to 1790 cfs, and [[Flash Floods]] (above 50 cfs) were common. Mean daily flows exceeded 100 cfs on 21 days in 1992 and 43 days in 1993, with peak mean flows of 559 and 599 cfs. Water temperatures ranged from 0 to 34°C. Colorado River flows ranged from 5,000 to 20,000 cfs, with temperatures between 6 and 12°C.

**Sediment and chemistry.** The drainage is dominated by gypsiferous sediments. The river is extremely turbid, with a mean discharge-weighted suspended sediment concentration of 114,000 ppm at its mouth, and daily means during floods reached about 280,000 mg/L. Phosphate and nitrate concentrations were the highest among Grand Canyon tributaries.

**Confluence.** High Colorado flows (15,000-20,000 cfs) inundate the lower Paria up to about 150 m from the mouth. The area is a zone of sediment deposition, dominated by fine sand or silt at the 0.0 transect.

**Aquatic community.** Speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*) were present throughout the year. Bluehead sucker, rainbow trout (n = 24), common carp (n = 1) and a single moribund striped bass were also recorded. The invertebrate survey (Appendix B) recorded 12 orders and 54 families; 58 taxa were identified to genus and 17 to species. No fish other than speckled dace occurred upstream of a rockslide about 16 km above the mouth.

**Human and regulatory context.** The lower Paria lies within the Paria River-Vermillion Cliffs Wilderness Area and the Glen Canyon National Recreation Area, and it has numerous upstream water diversions near Tropic and Cannonville.

## Key Findings

**Spawning**
- Spawning occurred mid-March to early April in 1992 and 1993, and in 1981 and 1991 at the same time of year.
- Ripe females were not found until minimum stream temperatures reached about 5°C. Peak spawning (April 9-10, 1993) occurred at temperatures of roughly 9-20°C.
- Spawning fish were observed in shallow water (under 25 cm), with dorsal fins or backs out of water, over gravel and cobble. Mean depth at the 6.0-km site was 15 cm.
- Spawning habitat differed from availability: mean column velocity was higher at the 5.0-km site, and bottom velocity was slower at spawning sites (0.39 m/s vs 0.57 m/s across transects). Substrate categories 3 and 4 were used disproportionately.
- No spawning was observed in reach 1 or in the confluence area.
- Males outnumbered females about 2:1 above the confluence. Peak CPUE reached about 30 fish/100 m².

**Size and population structure**
- Overall, 1,063 flannelmouth suckers were caught (January 1992 to August 1993), with mean total length 495 mm.
- Mean length of spawners in 1993 (478 mm, n = 246) was 53 mm larger than in 1981 (425 mm, n = 285), a significant shift (p < 0.001).
- Females averaged 54 mm longer than males (p < 0.001). Fish at the confluence (mean 501 mm) were larger than upstream spawners (476 mm).
- Twenty-one fish between 580 and 620 mm, with features suggestive of [[Hybridization]] with razorback sucker, were caught at the confluence.

**Recruitment**
- Post-larval fish under 379 mm were not found in 1992 or 1993. Juveniles (22-45 mm) were caught in 1992, and no young-of-the-year (YOY) were caught in 1993.
- The 1991 Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) survey caught 5 juveniles in the lower Paria, but juveniles disappeared by June.
- The authors concluded there is no evidence of successful juvenile rearing in the Paria/Glen Canyon area for about 12 years, and that 380-400 mm fish enter the population each year from downstream.

**Movement and recaptures**
- The cumulative PIT-tag recapture rate was 7.3% at study end, and the per-trip rate ranged from 11 to 17% over the last four trips.
- Some fish originally tagged in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR, about 97 km downstream) and at Kanab Creek (229 km downstream) returned to the Paria to spawn.
- Seven PIT-tagged fish were recaptured in the LCR soon after spawning.

**Growth and longevity**
- PIT-tagged recaptures showed no measurable growth during the study. Floy-tagged sub-adults grew about 0.15 mm/day, and mature fish about 0.1 mm/day.
- Growth after maturity is linear at about 7 mm/year. Longevity may approach 30 years, based on extrapolation.

**Confluence use**
- Catch at the confluence was erratic during daylight and higher after dark. The mean daytime CPUE was 0.6 fish/100 m² (73 of 93 hauls empty), and the mean night CPUE was 2.3 fish/100 m² (p < 0.05).
- The highest individual catches occurred at Colorado flows of 10,000-14,000 cfs. Flows were not significantly related to CPUE (Kruskal-Wallis test).

**Management implications**
- Flash floods and high sediment loads may be detrimental to incubation, hatching and larval survival. Spawning in spring, when flows are relatively stable, may be a timing adaptation.
- The authors suggest that flannelmouth suckers have persisted in the presence of exotic predators (channel catfish, carp, centrarchids), so exotic species alone cannot be blamed for losing juveniles.
- Because of the long lifespan, recruitment need not occur every year to sustain the population.

**Summary**
- The Paria spawning run includes both Glen Canyon residents and migrants from downstream, especially the LCR.
- Growth of pre-spawning fish appears logistic, and mature fish grow slowly (about 7 mm/year).
