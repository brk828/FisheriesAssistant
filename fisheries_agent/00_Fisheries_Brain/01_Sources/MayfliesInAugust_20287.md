# Mayflies on August Dine

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Heptageniidae #aquatic-entomology #Ephemeroptera

## Executive Summary

This article describes the occurrence and life history of late-summer mayflies in Michigan trout streams, with particular focus on [[Stenonema rubrum]] and related species. Written for anglers, it documents the timing, behavior, and identification characteristics of these mayflies that emerge between mid-July and the close of trout season, contrary to the common perception that mayflies are exclusively early-season insects. The article provides detailed morphological descriptions of both nymphal and adult stages and offers practical guidance for fly-fishing applications.

## Background & Description

[[Stenonema rubrum]] is a mayfly species commonly found in Michigan trout streams during late summer. The nymphal stage exhibits distinctive morphological adaptations: greatly flattened body and legs enabling limpet-like attachment to flat surfaces in fast water. Nymphs are dark grayish-olive with lighter color patterns and inhabit areas under stones in the stream bed and on the underside of submerged planks.

The adult emergence follows a two-stage process characteristic of mayflies. The subimago (dun) stage emerges almost entirely white with faint amber yellow on the thorax near wing bases and at the abdominal tip. Following a second molt, the imago stage displays more pronounced coloration: deep purplish brown on top of thorax contrasting with white sides. Males develop translucent white abdomens with dark purplish brown tips, while females show faint creamy-yellow tinge due to eggs. Both sexes possess white legs with faint purple bands, pearly gray eyes (larger in males), and two white tails with narrow dark cross bands that are nearly twice body length.

Related species in Michigan streams include [[Stenonema canadense]], [[Stenonema proximum]], [[Stenonema nepotellum]], and [[Stenonema rubromaculatum]], all similar in size, color, and habits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Stenonema rubrum]] occupies fast-water habitats in Michigan trout streams, specifically fast gravel riffles and partially submerged slab piles. The species demonstrates strong habitat specificity, with nymphs requiring fast-flowing water and flat surfaces for attachment.

Temporal patterns show peak activity between mid-July and mid-August, though related [[Stenonema]] species extend the overall group's flight period from June 15 through the close of trout season. Emergence ("hatch") typically occurs in late afternoon or early evening. Following emergence, duns rest on streamside vegetation, particularly spruces and balsams, through the night and much of the following day. The white coloration provides considerable contrast against dark coniferous foliage.

Mating flights occur about dusk, with egg-laying potentially extending long after dark. Flights are characteristically localized rather than widespread, concentrated around preferred breeding habitats. The species exhibits positive phototaxis, being strongly attracted to artificial lights at night.

## Key Findings

- [[Stenonema rubrum]] provides important late-season mayfly activity in Michigan trout streams, contradicting the perception that mayflies are exclusively early-season insects
- The species can be found on the wing from mid-July through mid-August, with related [[Stenonema]] species extending availability from June 15 through the close of trout season
- Hatches are localized rather than widespread, occurring over restricted stream areas near fast gravel riffles and partially submerged slab piles
- Nymphs exhibit specialized morphology (greatly flattened bodies and legs) for inhabiting fast-water environments
- Adult emergence follows typical mayfly pattern: subimago stage emerges in late afternoon/early evening, rests on streamside conifers, then molts to imago stage the following day
- Mating flights occur at dusk with egg-laying after dark
- For angling applications, the [[Light Cahill]] pattern most closely imitates these mayflies; experimental patterns using transparent white quill body, white and ginger hackle mixture, white tails on No. 12 hook are recommended
- Pale olive materials may substitute for white when imitating closely allied [[Stenonema]] species
- Boat fishermen have advantage over wading anglers in encountering localized flights due to greater water coverage
- Streamside vegetation examination during daytime and observation of insects attracted to lights at night provide indicators of flight timing
