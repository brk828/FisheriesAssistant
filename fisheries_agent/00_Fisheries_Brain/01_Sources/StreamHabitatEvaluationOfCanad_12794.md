# Stream Habitat Evaluation of Canada del Oro Drainage

#macroinvertebrates #riparian-vegetation #stream-habitat

## Executive Summary

This June 1980 report by Carol Demuth, Wildlife Biologist, Santa Catalina Ranger District, [[Coronado National Forest]], evaluates the stream habitat of the Canada del Oro drainage in the [[Santa Catalina Mountains]] near Tucson, Arizona. The work was a two-week study intended to support Land Management Planning. It addressed two objectives: assessing present water quality, and evaluating the resistance of the stream channel to the detachment of bed and bank material. The report found no detrimental effects from nearby mining or cattle grazing, recorded mostly desirable aquatic invertebrates, and documented dense riparian vegetation of high wildlife value. It does not identify any fish species.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The Santa Catalina Mountains span four life zones (Lower Sonoran, Upper Sonoran, Transition, Canadian) and five vegetative types, from desert shrub at 2,000 feet to coniferous forest at 9,150 feet. Many drainages in the range are described as little explored because of terrain and access.
- **Drainage:** Canada del Oro lies west of the Oracle-Mount Lemmon Road and north of the Trico Electric Cooperative powerline, between the Oracle and Samaniego ridges. Within the National Forest it is about 13 miles long, falling from 8,100 feet to 4,100 feet.
- **Water sources:** The drainage is fed by snowmelt and four springs: Shovel Spring, Bill Williams Spring, Cascade Spring, and Walnut Spring.
- **Flow regime:** Flow is intermittent-perennial. The stream has no flow at times during the drier months, specifically May through June and September through November.
- **Human use:** Vehicle access is limited by a locked gate on private land at the Burney Mines. Mine shafts and tailing piles are frequent in the lower drainage. Cattle graze the Canada del Oro allotment.

**Objectives**
1. Assess present water quality to inform future management, research, and resource planning.
2. Evaluate the channel's resistance to detachment of bed and bank materials, rated by bank wasting, cutting and deposition, overbank floods, plant density, obstructions and deflectors, and stability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality and disturbance.** No detrimental effects from mining or cattle activity were apparent. The author states that further testing is needed to confirm whether problems exist. The report contains several pages of water-chemistry and station data, but these are largely illegible in the source scan, so specific values are not reported here. Station locations are shown on a map in the appendix.

**Macroinvertebrates.** The report treats aquatic invertebrates as both fish food and a pollution indicator. Desirable taxa include caddisfly, stonefly, and mayfly larvae, which decline under pollution. Undesirable taxa such as leeches, blackfly larvae, and predaceous diving beetles tend to increase when water is polluted. Nearly all invertebrates recorded in Canada del Oro were classed as desirable. In March, more invertebrates were recorded in the lower drainage than the upper, which the author attributes to warmer temperatures favouring reproduction.

**Riparian vegetation.** Vegetation was very dense, to the point that parts of the channel were effectively inaccessible. Tall trees dominated at higher elevations, while forbs and grasses were more common at lower elevations. The report credits streamside vegetation with:
- shading to control water temperature
- cover for fish
- bank stability through root systems
- habitat for insects that breed in the vegetation and fall into the stream as fish food
- transitional habitat for wildlife

**Vegetation inventory.** Table IV lists 61 genus or species entries. These include conifers (ponderosa pine, white fir, Douglas fir, Arizona cypress), oaks (Gambel, silverleaf, netleaf, Emory, and white oak), and riparian trees (Arizona sycamore, Arizona walnut, Arizona alder, Fremont cottonwood, quaking aspen). Desert taxa include saguaro, ocotillo, prickly pear, and paloverde species, and herbaceous and grass taxa include bracken fern and gramma grasses. The OCR of the table is partly corrupted, so this summary relies on the surviving entries.

**Wildlife.** Dense cover concealed species such as whitetail deer. The author judged the vegetation to provide excellent cover, food, and protection for many observed wildlife species.

## Key Findings

- **Water quality:** No detrimental effects from mining or cattle grazing were detected. Further testing is recommended.
- **Invertebrate community:** Most recorded invertebrates were desirable pollution-sensitive taxa (caddisfly, stonefly, mayfly larvae). Lower-drainage counts exceeded upper-drainage counts in March.
- **Riparian value:** Dense, diverse vegetation (61 entries in Table IV) provides shade, bank stability, insect production, and wildlife cover. Taller and denser vegetation, including grasses and forbs, is rated as more valuable.
- **Elevation gradient:** Tall trees dominate higher elevations, while forbs and grasses are more common lower down.
- **Flow constraints:** Intermittent flow, with dry periods in May–June and September–November, shapes habitat availability and should be considered in management planning.
- **Management context:** The study was designed to inform the Coronado National Forest Land Management Planning process for the Canada del Oro drainage.
