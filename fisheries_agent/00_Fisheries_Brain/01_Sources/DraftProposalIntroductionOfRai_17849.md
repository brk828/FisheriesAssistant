# Introduction of Rainbow Smelt (Osmerus mordax) into Lake Powell, Utah-Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1989 draft proposal and supporting correspondence from the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, prepared by the [[Lake Powell]] Fisheries Project (A. Wayne Gustaveson and Bruce Bonebrake), to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]] as a forage fish. The stated rationale is a critical shortage of pelagic forage, since [[Threadfin Shad]] have been depleted by predation from [[Striped Bass]] and [[Walleye]]. The proposal argues that smelt would be thermally partitioned from shad in the stratified reservoir, would provide deep-water forage, and would be unlikely to harm endangered native fishes of the [[Colorado River]] system. The cover letter (August 30, 1989, from Bruce R. Schmidt, Chief of Fisheries) requests technical review by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the [[Squawfish Recovery Team]], and schedules a September 29 meeting in Salt Lake City. No final decision was sought at that meeting.

## Background & Description

**Proposal context**
- Submitted to the [[Colorado River Fish And Wildlife Council]] (CRFWC), which comprises the seven state wildlife agencies of the Colorado Basin. Its Technical Committee recommended acceptance in spring 1989, and the full Council directed the proponents to contact other agencies in July 1989.
- Format and timing follow the "procedures for introduction of aquatic organisms into the Colorado River basin," adopted in 1987.
- Supporting background is in Appendix I, "Assessment of the Need for Forage Enhancement for the Benefit of Lake Powell Fishes."
- The letter notes that visitor use at Lake Powell exceeds that at Yellowstone Park, with a significant share directed at fishing.

**Species description (as reported)**
- Slender, silver fish, pale green on the back, with iridescent sides.
- Typical length 178-203 mm; landlocked smelt in small lakes often reach a maximum of 102 mm, while 356 mm individuals were taken in Lake Ontario and maritime coastal waters.
- Soft-rayed dorsal fin at mid-body; well-developed adipose fin; deeply forked caudal fin; thin, deciduous cycloid scales; incomplete lateral line.
- Growth: 40-50 mm in the first year. Total length at ages 2, 3, and 4 was 117, 155, and 183 mm in Lake Huron, and 152, 185, and 201 mm in Lake Superior.
- Maturity: shortest mature smelt were 127 mm in Lake Superior. Among two-year-old smelt in Lake Superior, 41% of males and 18% of females were mature.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning runs begin after ice-out (usually March to May). Spawning has been documented at 2-18.3 C.
- Spawning runs began at 4.5 C and peaked at 6.1 C in Quabbin Reservoir, Massachusetts.
- Individual females produce an average of 25,000 eggs (range 10,000-43,000).
- Incubation is 10-60 days depending on temperature. Cooper (1978) found eggs hatched in 8-29 days at 4.4-21 C.
- Egg survival to prolarvae was 1-2% in Maine waters.

**Distribution and introduction history (as reported)**
- Introduced into Lake Michigan in 1906-1912; established throughout the Great Lakes within 25 years.
- Introduced to Lake Sakakawea, ND, in 1971, subsequently established downstream in Lake Oahe, SD.
- Stocked in several Colorado reservoirs (Clear Creek, Twin Lakes, Turquoise Lake, Pueblo, Quincy, Rampart, and Horsetooth) as forage.
- Stocked in Lake McConaughy, NE, 1983-85; no successful recruitment documented.
- Introduced to Pactola Reservoir, SD, in 1982; reproduction documented but population not fully developed.

**Lake Powell fishery history (Appendix I)**
- Impounded in 1963. Early stockings included [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], rainbow trout, and kokanee salmon.
- [[Threadfin Shad]] were introduced in 1968-1969.
- [[Striped Bass]] fingerlings were introduced in 1974, and began contributing to the sport fishery in 1979.
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] were introduced in 1982 and stocked through 1988 across many sites.
- Walleye were never stocked; a population established from stock present at impoundment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and temperature preferences**
- Smelt are schooling, pelagic fishes of lake midwaters or inshore coastal waters. They do not inhabit streams except to spawn, and are sensitive to light and temperature.
- Most smelt in the hypolimnion at 5-14 C. In Lake Sakakawea smelt suspended at 90-125 ft in summer.
- Adult smelt in Lake Michigan occupied 7-8 C by day and 11-16 C at night.
- Smelt avoid water above 15 C, and cannot survive above 21-26 C (Wichers 1980).
- Smelt were not found in Lake Oahe where no hypolimnion existed and bottom waters exceeded 20 C.
- Hypolimnetic waters of Lake Powell range from 7.0-12 C in summer (Merritt 1976), within the preferred smelt range.

**Feeding and food web role**
- Diet is mainly plankton, amphipods, and invertebrate insects, with some predation on small fishes and their own young. Studies in several locations found smelt seldom ate fish.
- Smelt are opportunistic. Calanoid copepods dominated in Lake Superior; *Leptodora kindtii* was important in Lake Sakakawea and Lake Oahe.
- Both smelt and alewives may restructure plankton communities by removing larger zooplankton, potentially allowing algal blooms (Kircheis and Stanley 1981; Mills and Schiavone 1982).

**Lake Powell ecology**
- Lake Powell is oligotrophic (chlorophyll-a near 2 ug/l).
- Threadfin shad occupy the upper 10 m of the stratified reservoir and are not found below the thermocline. Open-water shad populations are cyclical; peaks occurred in 1978, 1981, and 1984.
- Lake Powell has roughly 2000 miles of shoreline, largely talus rockslides.
- Large-scale brush loss and reservoir water fluctuation have reduced recruitment of largemouth bass, black crappie, and other shorebound species.

**Interactions with native and endangered fishes**
- Water temperature is considered the primary factor in the extirpation of native fishes from regulated river reaches.
- [[Humpback Chub]] spawning occurs March-June at 16-20 C; spawning may be triggered near 19 C.
- Main-channel temperatures near 11 C may be lethal to larval chub.
- The [[Little Colorado River]] is generally 9 C warmer than the main channel and is the most important tributary for humpback chub.
- [[Bonytail Chub]] prefers about 20 C and would be least likely to compete with or be preyed upon by smelt.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] spawning reported at 10-18 C across several reservoirs; the proposal argues smelt would be thermally separated from these life stages.

**Competing and related species**
- [[Pygmy Smelt]] (*Osmerus spectrum*) was evaluated as an alternative. It is a non-piscivorous, non-migratory planktivore that grows more slowly, reaches 125 mm TL, and prefers warmer, shallower water. The proposal concludes rainbow smelt is the species of choice.

## Key Findings

**Need for forage**
- The shad population has been impacted by predation to the point it no longer provides adequate forage for pelagic or deep-water predators.
- [[Striped Bass]] are stunted, with few attaining more than 20 inches.
- Juvenile and some adult striped bass maintain condition by foraging on large zooplankton, which the proposal treats as evidence of a missing link in the food chain.
- Condition factor for adult striped bass fell to approximately 1.0 in 1982, and to near 0.90 in 1985. It has since held near 1.0-1.1.

**Striped bass population trends (Appendix I)**
- Average size of sport-caught fish from 1979-1984: 620 mm (24.4 in).
- 1985 average size of all sampled fish: 555 mm (21.8 in).
- Spring 1986 average: 497 mm (19.5 in); fall 1986: 423 mm (16.5 in).
- 1987: 393 mm; 1988: 348 mm.
- A die-off of adult striped bass was observed in winter 1986-1987.

**Shad population**
- Peaks occurred in 1978, 1981, and 1984; each succeeding peak since 1978 has been progressively smaller.
- No peak materialized in 1987; no shad were collected in most trawl areas. Shad were also absent from 1988 pelagic trawl sampling.
- The proposal describes the current state as a "predator impacted resource."

**Angler use**
- Mixed-bag catch rate averaged near 0.4 fish per hour over the reservoir's life; the 1985 rate was still near this average despite increasing fishing pressure.
- Smelt are described as widely used by anglers in New England, the Great Lakes, and Missouri River reservoirs; a spring smelt run could benefit the local economy during a slow period.

**Expected benefits**
- Increased forage species diversity and abundance; smelt thermally partitioned from shad.
- Smelt would provide food for deep-water predators restricted to cool water.
- Larger, more abundant forage would allow striped bass to reach larger size; the proposal projects an immediate dramatic increase in average striped bass size.
- Increased angling could help control the long-lived striped bass population and raise license, boat rental, and visitor revenue.

**Predicted seasonal behavior in Lake Powell**
- Spawning expected February or March, around the time walleye spawn, when surface water is 7-12 C.
- Smelt likely to spawn on talus rockslides in the shallows; silt bars at the San Juan and Escalante rivers would limit upstream movement. Fine silt at Cataract Canyon would limit survival of young.
- Spawning run expected to last about 3 weeks, with most spawning in one week. Larval smelt hatch in about 10 days at 12 C.
- Adult smelt forced from shallows by warming in early April; they avoid temperatures above 15 C, the minimum for centrarchid spawning.
- Larval smelt prefer water below 14 C; larval centrarchids use 19-27 C nursery areas.
- Shad spawn when early morning water temperatures reach 20.5 C, normally in mid-May.
- Fall overturn (by November) allows smelt to interact with all species; striped bass predation is expected to reduce the annual smelt crop substantially.

**Risks and mitigation identified**
- Predation on larval fishes is a concern. The proposal states smelt would be thermally separated from larval shad and game fish, except perhaps striped bass fry.
- Interaction with young-of-year game fish could occur in winter, once they exceed 80 mm.
- Smelt upstream migration is blocked by falls of 1 foot or greater; they seldom move more than a quarter mile upstream.
- Downstream migration is expected. Smelt could likely establish populations in Lake Mead and Lake Mohave; they may not complete life cycles in Lake Havasu.
- Disease: smelt host parasites, notably *Glugea hertwigi*. Examinations of Lake Oahe smelt by Utah and South Dakota found them free of these parasites; histological testing detected no prohibited pathogens. Adult stocking is preferred; fertilized eggs would be used if concerning parasites are later found.
- Hybridization: no potential with any other species in the Colorado River system.
- Bait bucket introductions: the proposal notes this risk and recommends law enforcement and education to control it.

**Management options evaluated (Appendix I)**
- *No action*: shad would remain predator-limited; striped bass growth and condition would stay marginal; walleye recruitment would stay low.
- *Regulation changes*: removing the striped bass limit is not expected to raise harvest significantly. The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources recommends raising the limit to twenty fish for 1990 and plans a similar proposal in Arizona.
- *Reopening the buoy sanctuary* below Glen Canyon Dam during April and May to target pre-spawn striped bass is proposed as a harvest measure.
- *Commercial fishery for striped bass* is rejected. The enabling legislation for Glen Canyon National Recreation Area prohibits commercial fishing, and sale of game fish is illegal in UT and AZ.
- *Deep-water forage fish* is the preferred option; the proposal argues it would have no effect on the remnant adult endangered fish population.
- *Forage complex*: Lake Texoma (35,600 ha, average depth 9.5 m, maximum 28 m), where striped bass fed mainly on schooling forage and 30-50% of striped bass caught between 1983-1986 exceeded 500 mm, and Santee-Cooper Reservoir, are cited as models of diversified forage bases.

**Plankton comparison (Table 2, Paulson 1989; Sollberger et al. 1989)**
- April zooplankton density: Lake Powell (1987-88) 17,613/m³; Lake Mead (1988) 23,085/m³; Great Lakes average 12,434/m³; Beaver Reservoir (1965-66) 22,600/m³; Bull Shoals Reservoir (1965) 24,600/m³.
- Plankton density in Lake Powell ranged between 3,000 and 26,000/m³ in the upper 40 m; plankton is available to 100 m.
- Plankton abundance has not changed dramatically since 1960s work.

**Native fish conclusions**
- Rare and endangered endemic fishes would have minimal interaction with smelt and would not face significant competition for food and space.
- Smelt would be thermally separated from native fish larvae.
- The proposal states the potential consequences of an introduction are less for littoral and warmwater species (such as humpback chub) than for pelagic and coldwater species.

**Proposed monitoring**
- Smelt introduction to be monitored and reported annually under JOB I (Forage Condition Study) of DJ Project F-46-R, administered by the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources.

**Status and next steps**
- The proposal is a draft submitted for technical review. No final decision was requested at the September 29, 1989 meeting.
- The letter requests specific technical information indicating smelt pose a likely threat to endangered fishes, and asks whether the plan will meet its objective of improving forage for striped bass.
- The proposal's concluding statement: rainbow smelt "would seem to be a beneficial addition to the Colorado River system," supported by a Berard quote (North Dakota Fish and Game Dept., personal communication, 1987) that "The rainbow smelt is the salvation of the Missouri River system reservoirs." This is the proponents' opinion, not a demonstrated result for Lake Powell.
