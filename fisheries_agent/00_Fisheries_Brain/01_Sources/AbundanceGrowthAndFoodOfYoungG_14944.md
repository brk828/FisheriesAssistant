# Abundance, Growth and Food of Young Game Fish in Clear Lake, Iowa, 1949–1957

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study analyses nine years (1949–1957) of seine-based surveys of young-of-the-year (YOY) fishes in [[Clear Lake Iowa]], a 3,640-acre eutrophic lake. Year-class success and growth were rated across [[Yellow Perch]], [[Walleye]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Yellow Bass]] and [[White Bass]]. Year-class success was generally good in 1951, 1954 and 1956 and poor in 1949 and 1950. Growth was usually better when [[Aquatic Vegetation]] was abundant. Growth reductions were linked to both [[Intraspecific Competition]] and [[Interspecific Competition]], and the author concluded that food was the primary limiting factor, with space possibly also important.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** [[Clear Lake Iowa]] has a maximum depth of 20 feet. Its wide littoral zone, often extending more than 500 feet from shore, is mainly sand or fine gravel with thin organic deposits in weed beds. Giant bulrush (*Scirpus validus*), pondweeds (*Potamogeton* spp.) and *Chara* spp. are fairly abundant in places.
- **Sampling history:** Surveys were carried out by Iowa Cooperative Research Unit biologists each summer from 1949 by [[Bag Seining]] several littoral areas repeatedly. Some earlier data exist from 1941–1948. A more systematic program began in 1956, using two 75-foot seine hauls at each of 12 stations weekly.
- **Methods of analysis:** [[Year Class Success]] was rated as good, fair or poor on relative abundance, acknowledging that sampling effort was inconsistent before 1956. Growth was measured as total length and compared using straight-line regressions of length on time from July 15 to August 31, following [[Growth Regression Analysis]] methods. Stomach contents were examined by period, and day versus night feeding was compared.
- **Length standardisation:** Earlier standard lengths were converted to total length using species-specific factors from Carlander (1953).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature:** There were no adequate lake water temperature records, so Mason City Airport monthly air temperatures (1949–1957) were used as a proxy. Air temperature did not correlate with year-class success for the eight species. Growth was not clearly related to temperature either, with 13 positive and 11 negative regressions for growth versus July/August temperatures. The coldest summer was 1950.
- **Vegetation:** Aquatic vegetation was not quantified but was ranked as low, moderate or high for each year. [[Yellow Perch]] showed the clearest link: poor success in 1950, when vegetation was low, and good success in most years when it was abundant. Growth of several species also tracked vegetation.
- **Predation:** Predation by larger fish was considered important. Forage fish other than young game and panfish made up less than 20% of the small-fish catch, so young game fish were likely a major forage base. [[Walleye]] predation was implicated in the loss of the 1950 yellow bass year class.
- **Habitat partitioning:** Species fell into groups by habitat. [[Yellow Perch]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Black Crappie]] and [[Black Bullhead]] favoured heavy vegetation. [[Walleye]] and [[White Bass]] favoured moderate vegetation. [[Yellow Bass]] favoured sparse vegetation over sand or gravel. Because of this, species were often found together.
- **Food base:** [[Entomostraca]] dominated early diets, then [[Hyalella]] and immature insects became important, and fish increasingly dominated the diets of larger predators.

## Key Findings

### Year-class success
- Success was good for most species in 1951, 1954 and 1956, but only one species had good success in 1949 and in 1955.
- No year saw complete failure of more than two of the eight species.
- [[Walleye]] year classes were strongly tied to experimental fry stocking begun in 1949. Stocking occurred in even-numbered years, and few or no fish were seined from unstocked years, apart from 1949 and 1955.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] success was aided by stocking in 1956.
- [[Yellow Bass]] reproduced fairly well in recent years, but [[White Bass]] were absent from collections in 1950–1953.
- [[Black Bullhead]] showed cyclic good and poor reproduction (low in 1948–1950 and 1955–1957, good in 1945–1947 and 1951–1954). The author suggested this resembled a "bound" lake.
- [[Yellow Perch]] success was unlikely to match pre-1940 levels, when [[Yellow Bass]] began to increase.

### Growth
- Growth was generally good in 1952, 1953 and 1955, poor in 1950, and poor for most species in 1956 and 1957 relative to earlier years.
- Pooled 1947–1957 mean estimated September 1 lengths (Table 7) were approximately 74 mm for [[Yellow Perch]], 126 mm for [[Walleye]], 77 mm for [[Largemouth Bass]], 68 mm for [[Yellow Bass]], 62 mm for [[Black Crappie]], 33 mm for [[Bluegill]] and [[Pumpkinseed]], 58 mm for [[Black Bullhead]] and 97 mm for [[White Bass]].
- [[Yellow Bass]] was the only species with a clear link between year-class abundance and growth. Growth was slower in abundant years, which suggests intraspecific competition.
- A significant regression was found only for [[Yellow Bass]] size on May temperature (r = 0.68, exceeding the critical value of 0.63 at 8 degrees of freedom).

### Competition
- **[[Yellow Perch]]:** Competed mainly with itself. Its diet differed enough from co-occurring species that interspecific competition was not evident.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]:** Results were erratic, but both intraspecific and interspecific competition appeared likely, especially in 1956 when YOY populations were large.
- **[[Bluegill]] and [[Pumpkinseed]]:** Possibly affected by both types of competition. A late 1957 hatch complicated interpretation.
- **[[Black Crappie]]:** Evidence was limited to possible interspecific competition, probably for [[Entomostraca]].
- **[[Yellow Bass]]:** Consistently showed intraspecific effects, stronger in large schools (Figure 1, Station 4, 1956).
- **[[Black Bullhead]]:** Appeared to suffer both types of competition because it schools and shares habitat with several species.
- **[[White Bass]]:** Low numbers limited any competition effects.
- Overall, slower growth was best explained by competition for food. The author judged environmental effects such as low water levels to be small, though space limitation could not be excluded.

### Food habits (1956)
- Early in summer, most species fed mainly on [[Entomostraca]]. Later, immature insects, [[Hyalella]] and fish became more important.
- [[Walleye]] shifted to fish at about 2–3 inches long.
- [[Black Crappie]] fed almost entirely on [[Entomostraca]].
- [[Yellow Perch]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]] and [[Black Crappie]] fed mainly during the day. [[Walleye]] and [[Yellow Bass]] fed more at night.
- Empty stomachs were most frequent in [[Walleye]] during the day (57%) and in [[Yellow Bass]] during the day (29%).

### Species notes
- **[[Northern Pike]]:** Abundance depended mainly on stocking. Young fish were restricted to weedy areas until about 8 inches long.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]:** Two YOY were seined in 1954, and the species was fairly common in 1956–1957 night seining. Natural reproduction's role in maintaining the population was uncertain.
- **[[Yellow Bullhead]]:** Young were rarely caught before 1956 because seining was mostly daytime. They were common at night, and growth matched [[Black Bullhead]].
- **[[White Bass]]:** Declined after 1950, with the cause unknown. Possible factors included limited spawning habitat, low inflow during drought years, and predation on eggs or fry by [[Yellow Bass]].
- **[[Smallmouth Bass]]:** Only two YOY were collected after 1943. Lake development and boating may have reduced habitat.
- **[[White Crappie]]:** Under-sampled by the 30-foot seine. Lake survey seining with a 500-foot seine found many young, suggesting this species uses deeper open water.
- **[[Pumpkinseed]]:** Indistinguishable from [[Bluegill]] when small. In late August 1956, pumpkinseeds made up about 4% of the bluegill–pumpkinseed catch.

### Conclusions
- Food was the primary limiting factor for growth, though space limitation may also have mattered.
- Vegetation appears to influence year-class success and growth, possibly through cover and food production.
- The study could not identify a clear control on [[Yellow Bass]] population levels.
