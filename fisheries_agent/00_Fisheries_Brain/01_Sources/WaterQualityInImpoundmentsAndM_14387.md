# Water Quality in Impoundments and Modifications from Destratification

#limnology #water-quality #reservoir-management #thermal-stratification #engineering

## Executive Summary

This 1967 research report from the [[Federal Water Pollution Control Administration]] describes engineering methods to overcome water quality deterioration in stratified impoundments through artificial destratification. The study examined two small lakes in northern Kentucky ([[Boltz Lake]] and [[Falmouth Lake]]) during 1965-1966, comparing mechanical pumping versus diffused air systems for mixing stratified waters. Results demonstrated that artificial destratification significantly improved water quality by maintaining uniform temperatures, better dissolved oxygen distribution, and reducing concentrations of iron, manganese, and sulfides. The diffused air method proved more hydraulically efficient than mechanical pumping, circulating 5.48 ac-ft/kw versus 0.72 ac-ft/kw. This work supported streamflow regulation projects requiring good quality impounded water for downstream flow control.

## Background & Description

### Study Context

Research initiated by the [[Cincinnati Water Research Laboratory]] in 1962 to support streamflow augmentation programs authorized under the 1961 Federal Water Pollution Control Act amendments. The project aimed to develop engineering methods for maintaining water quality in impoundments used as sources for downstream dilution water.

### Study Sites

Three impoundments in northern Kentucky were studied:

- **[[Bullock Pen Lake]]** (control): 142 surface acres, 44 ft maximum depth, 3,210 ac-ft volume
- **[[Boltz Lake]]**: 96 surface acres, 62 ft maximum depth, 2,870 ac-ft volume (mixed in 1965 and 1966)
- **[[Falmouth Lake]]**: 225 surface acres, 42 ft maximum depth, 4,630 ac-ft volume (mixed in 1966)

All three lakes are earthen-dam impoundments forming deep-sided basins, generally narrow and protected from strong winds. Waters are clear, clean, but mildly eutrophic with fair to good fish populations.

### Thermal Stratification Patterns

In northern Kentucky, impounded water begins absorbing heat by early March, with strong thermal stratification present by mid-April. The shearing plane (plane between great density differences) remains at 15 ft or less from the surface during summer and early fall. Lakes become isothermal before or during early November when cooler air removes heat from upper waters.

### Destratification Methods

**1965 - Mechanical Pumping ([[Boltz Lake]])**:
- 12-inch diameter mixed-flow pump powered by 26-hp gasoline engine
- Capacity: approximately 13 ac-ft per day (4.2 mgd, 6.4 cfs, 2,880 gpm)
- Water discharge velocity: 8.4 feet per second
- Suction pipe extended to just above lake bottom
- Single mixing operation in August-September

**1966 - Diffused Air System**:
- 30-hp portable electrically driven rotary air compressor
- 16 diffuser stones arranged in cross pattern on lake bottom
- 40 ft diameter circular release pattern
- Air release rates: 112 cfm at 62-ft depth ([[Boltz Lake]]), 115 cfm at 42-ft depth ([[Falmouth Lake]])
- Created 100-ft diameter surface boil with 4-5 inch elevation
- [[Boltz Lake]]: four mixing operations (early June, mid-June, mid-July, mid-August)
- [[Falmouth Lake]]: five mixing operations (mid-May, mid-June, early July, late July-early August, September)

### Analytical Methods

Water samples collected at various depths near mixing sites and analyzed for:
- Physical parameters: temperature, pH, conductivity
- Dissolved gases: dissolved oxygen (DO), carbon dioxide
- Organic content: chemical oxygen demand (COD)
- Major ions: alkalinity, total solids, sulfates, calcium, magnesium
- Reduced species: sulfides, iron, manganese
- Nutrients: four nitrogen forms, total and soluble hydrolyzable phosphorus

Field measurements for temperature, pH, conductivity, DO, CO₂, and alkalinity. Laboratory analyses followed [[Standard Methods]] with modifications for [[Technicon Autoanalyzer]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Water Quality Deterioration in Stratified Impoundments

When impoundments thermally stratify, lower waters become effectively sealed from the atmosphere by light warm water floating on the surface. This prevents atmospheric reaeration, and naturally occurring oxygen-demanding reactions reduce dissolved oxygen concentration to zero. Under anaerobic conditions, reducing reactions occur:

1. **Sulfide production**: Anaerobic organisms produce hydrogen sulfide, methane, and ammonia nitrogen
2. **Metal dissolution**: Iron and manganese that settled in oxidized insoluble form return to solution under reducing conditions
3. **Organic accumulation**: Plankton populations in warm upper waters die and sink, creating tons of organic material that furnish more oxygen demand and chemical reduction substrate

Warmer temperatures and longer stratification periods in the southern United States further aggravate quality deterioration.

### Beneficial Aspects of Impoundments

Despite stratification problems, impounded waters often show better quality than original streams due to:

1. Blending and dilution of influent water with impounded water reduces peak pollutant concentrations
2. Detention and lower velocity permit sedimentation and precipitation of solids
3. Longer exposure to light and air permits greater biodegradation of organic wastes
4. Higher trophic levels result from energy transfer to protoplasmic units, creating greater available fish food (unless eutrophication exceeds optimum)

### Toxicity Concerns

Satellite bioassay studies in 1965-1966 revealed unexpected toxicity in aerated bottom water from stratified [[Bullock Pen Lake]]:

- Test fish died within 12-96 hours of exposure to aerated bottom water (July-October)
- Fish in surface water tanks remained healthy
- Toxicity persisted despite presence of 4-5 mg/L DO
- Investigation revealed sulfides can coexist with DO due to slow oxidation kinetics
- Bottom water contained almost 2 mg/L total sulfide in first bioassay tank
- Toxicity disappeared after natural lake overturn in November 1966

However, no fish toxicity was detected during actual mixing operations in [[Boltz Lake]] and [[Falmouth Lake]], apparently because slow mixing rates prevented sulfide concentrations from rising rapidly to toxic levels at the surface.

## Key Findings

### Water Quality Improvements from Destratification (1966 Data)

Calculated as percentage change in weighted vertical average concentrations before versus after mixing (averaged across all mixing operations):

**[[Boltz Lake]] (4 mixings)**:
- Heat content: +17% (79×10⁶ to 92×10⁶ BTU/ac-ft)
- Dissolved oxygen: -4% (7.6 to 7.3 mg/L) - slight decrease but better vertical distribution
- Chemical oxygen demand: -18% (16 to 13 mg/L)
- Iron: -25% (0.16 to 0.12 mg/L)
- Manganese: -25% (0.51 to 0.38 mg/L)
- Sulfide: -71% (0.14 to 0.04 mg/L)
- Ammonia nitrogen: +31% (0.19 to 0.24 mg/L)
- Organic nitrogen: +71% (0.35 to 0.60 mg/L)
- Soluble hydrolyzable phosphorus: -43% (0.07 to 0.04 mg/L)
- Total hydrolyzable phosphorus: -33% (0.12 to 0.08 mg/L)

**[[Falmouth Lake]] (5 mixings)**:
- Heat content: +17% (80×10⁶ to 94×10⁶ BTU/ac-ft)
- Dissolved oxygen: -23% (3.1 to 3.0 mg/L) - slight decrease but better vertical distribution
- Chemical oxygen demand: -3% (29 to 28 mg/L)
- Iron: -27% (0.52 to 0.38 mg/L)
- Manganese: +25% (0.69 to 0.86 mg/L) - reason unknown
- Sulfide: -86% (1.44 to 0.20 mg/L)
- Ammonia nitrogen: -56% (0.09 to 0.04 mg/L)
- Organic nitrogen: +68% (0.32 to 0.54 mg/L)
- Soluble hydrolyzable phosphorus: -16% (0.51 to 0.43 mg/L)
- Total hydrolyzable phosphorus: -16% (0.62 to 0.52 mg/L)

**Inorganic constituents** (total solids, conductivity, alkalinity, calcium, magnesium, sulfates) showed minimal change (within ±10%) in both lakes.

### Hydraulic Performance Comparison

**Mechanical Pumping (1965)**:
- Time to create 2°C temperature change in deepest portion: 360 hours
- Pumping rate: approximately 2.7 ac-ft/hour
- Total time to achieve isothermal conditions: 912 hours
- Energy efficiency: 0.72 ac-ft/kw (240,000 gallons/kw) or 4.2 kw per million gallons

**Diffused Air System (1966)**:
- [[Boltz Lake]]: One hypolimnion cycle in 25 hours, pumping rate 79 ac-ft/hour
- [[Falmouth Lake]]: One hypolimnion cycle in 25 hours, pumping rate 82 ac-ft/hour
- Time to achieve near-uniform temperature: 72 hours ([[Boltz Lake]]), 134 hours ([[Falmouth Lake]])
- Energy efficiency: 5.48 ac-ft/kw (1,800,000 gallons/kw) or 0.55 kw per million gallons
- **Cost advantage**: At $0.01/kw, diffused air is approximately 7.6 times more cost-effective than mechanical pumping

The diffused air system's superior efficiency results from its larger effective diameter (approximately 100 ft versus 1 ft for mechanical pump), moving large volumes of water at low velocity rather than small volumes at high velocity.

### Vertical Distribution Patterns

Analysis of summer season averages at multiple depths revealed:

**Vertically stratified with highest concentrations near surface**:
- Heat content
- Dissolved oxygen

**Vertically stratified with highest concentrations near bottom**:
- Iron
- Manganese
- Sulfides
- Conductivity
- Ammonia nitrogen
- Total and soluble hydrolyzable phosphorus

**Relatively uniform distribution top to bottom**:
- Chemical oxygen demand
- Total solids
- Calcium
- Magnesium
- Sulfate
- Nitrite nitrogen
- Nitrate nitrogen
- Total organic nitrogen

### Additional Benefits Observed

1. **Algal bloom prevention**: Autumnal blue-green algal blooms did not occur in mixed lakes, likely because uniform water quality favors green algae or diatoms over blue-greens

2. **Evaporation reduction**: Decreased surface temperatures should reduce evaporation (not quantified in this study, but similar work by [[Koberg and Ford]] showed 6% reduction)

3. **Enhanced recreational potential**: Lack of blue-green blooms, absence of anaerobic odors, more uniform temperature, and greater fishing potential improve recreational value

4. **Improved downstream water quality**: More uniform temperature and greater DO content in released water should benefit fish populations in receiving streams

5. **Enhanced biodegradation**: Presence of DO at all levels should enhance biodegradation of organic waste materials flowing into reservoir

### Oxygen Transfer Dynamics

Despite oxygen transfer from diffused air bubbles (evidenced by decreased COD, iron, and sulfides), total dissolved oxygen content decreased slightly during mixing in both lakes. This indicates the reaeration capacity was insufficient to both remove oxygen-demanding materials and increase DO concentration simultaneously. Gas analysis of rising air bubbles showed oxygen and nitrogen contents similar to air, with detectable carbon dioxide and methane.

### Nutrient Dynamics

Nutrient changes showed variable patterns between lakes:
- Organic nitrogen increased in both lakes (+71% [[Boltz Lake]], +68% [[Falmouth Lake]])
- Phosphorus decreased in both lakes
- Inorganic nitrogen forms showed opposite trends between lakes
- Despite percentage increases in some nutrients, absolute concentrations remained low and did not cause algal blooms

### Lake-Wide Mixing Effect

Temperature data from various points (dam to upper end) showed entire lake was stirred as a unit during mixing. As cold water from deep areas was lifted and warmed, cold dense water flowed to the deep point due to greater weight, creating beneficial circulation throughout the impoundment without additional energy expenditure.

### Research Needs Identified

1. **Operational optimization**: Time required to put entire lake in motion and benefits of continuous versus intermittent operation unknown; important for cost savings using off-peak electricity at hydroelectric installations

2. **Equipment design improvements**: Other diffuser arrangements, groupings, and bubble sizes should be studied to potentially increase water lift rate and oxygen transfer efficiency

3. **Biological impacts**: Influence of destratification on aquatic organisms (plankton, fish populations and species composition) requires detailed study

4. **Toxicity investigations**: Presence of toxic compounds beyond sulfides needs investigation; potential for toxic materials or anaerobic conditions to persist at mud-water interface; species-specific sensitivity to sulfides and other toxicants

5. **Bottom fauna recovery**: Whether toxic materials or anaerobic conditions at mud-water interface prevent bottom fauna abundance following destratification
