# Forests Forever: A Plan to Restore Ecological and Economic Integrity to the Southwest's National Forests

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

*Forests Forever!* is a 1996 citizens' policy document issued by the [[Southwest Forest Alliance]], a coalition of more than 50 environmental groups in Arizona and New Mexico. It argues that the region's forests, woodlands and wetlands have been degraded by a century of logging, grazing, mining, [[Road Building]], [[Fire Suppression]] and predator removal, and that these practices are subsidized by taxpayers. The document proposes a restoration plan built on landscape-scale mapping, protection of old-growth, watershed and riparian restoration, reduced livestock use, reintroduction of native species, and restoration jobs for local forest communities.

The document is advocacy material rather than a single research study. Its claims draw on cited scientists (including Russell Balda, Wallace Covington and Margaret Moore of Northern Arizona University, and Norbert DeByle of the U.S. Forest Service) and on agency data from the General Accounting Office and the U.S. Department of Agriculture.

## Background & Description

The Southwest is usually associated with desert and canyon landscapes, yet forests and woodlands cover nearly 45 million acres, including the largest contiguous [[Ponderosa Pine]] forest in the United States, the [[Mogollon Plateau]]. The region holds an unusually high diversity of species, including [[Mexican Spotted Owl]], [[Northern Goshawk]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Apache Trout]], [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]], loach minnows and [[Abert's Squirrel]].

The document describes eight forest communities:

- **Pinyon-juniper woodlands:** more than 150 vertebrate species; pinyon seed dispersal by [[Pinyon Jay]] and [[Clark's Nutcracker]].
- **Ponderosa pine:** covers over 10 million acres, generally at 7,000–8,500 feet; grass cover is central to its fire regime.
- **Mixed-conifer:** old-growth habitat for Mexican spotted owl, black bear, pine marten and native trout.
- **Aspen:** regenerates after fire; some 200 animal species depend on aspen groves.
- **Spruce-fir:** high-elevation forests on the region's major water-producing snowpack.
- **Riparian forests and ciénegas (wetlands):** critical habitat for native fish and birds.

The text also recounts the history of Native American, Hispanic and early scientific engagement with these forests, including [[Merriam and Bailey Life Zones]] mapping in 1889.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document presents several ecological linkages:

- **Ponderosa pine–fungus–squirrel–goshawk web:** pines host about 150 mycorrhizal fungus species; Abert's squirrels disperse truffle spores; northern goshawks nest in old-growth canopy.
- **Fire and aspen chemistry:** fire raises cytokinin (sprout-promoting) and lowers auxin (sprout-suppressing) production; aspens then shade out sprouts and create conditions for fir seedlings.
- **Grass and fire:** native grass cover historically carried frequent low-intensity "ground fires" that thinned young trees; grass loss from overgrazing removed fire from the system.
- **Riparian function:** about 95 percent of Arizona's and 85 percent of New Mexico's riparian forests are said to be destroyed; over half of endangered species are said to have become so due to riparian losses.
- **Wetlands and floods:** ciénegas absorb and slowly release floodwaters; their loss leads to short destructive floods and long dry periods.
- **Exotic species:** tamarisk, non-native bullfrogs (which decimated native fish, amphibians and reptiles), red shiners and cheatgrass are identified as major threats.

Key threats named include overgrazing, fire suppression, road building, old-growth liquidation, predator control and exotic species.

## Key Findings

- **Old-growth loss:** about 90 percent of Southwest old-growth has been liquidated, including 98 percent of old-growth ponderosa pine (as stated in the text).
- **Songbird decline:** breeding bird surveys since 1964 record significant declines in 75 percent of songbirds associated with logged ponderosa pine forests.
- **Fish status:** 28 of Arizona's 32 native fish are threatened, endangered or candidate species. The Apache trout has been downlisted to threatened; the Gila trout continues to decline; the Rio Grande cutthroat trout is described as imperiled and deserving listing.
- **Roads:** the Forest Service is described as the largest road-building agency in the United States; roads are called the leading cause of erosion in the National Forest system.
- **Pinyon-juniper treatment:** more than 600,000 acres were bulldozed, chained and poisoned in the 1950s–1960s; the text states the effort cost taxpayers millions and proved futile.
- **Fire trend:** the text links increasing livestock numbers to decreasing fire frequency and reports unnaturally large fires and rising suppression costs.
- **Public subsidies:** the Forest Service lost nearly $1 billion on its timber program between 1992 and 1994; Arizona and New Mexico timber programs lost $54 million in the same period. The text estimates about $24,000 annual taxpayer subsidy per logging job in 1994.
- **Grazing:** ranching pays $1.35 per month per cow-calf pair on National Forests; taxpayers reportedly pay over $100 million a year to subsidize public lands grazing in the Southwest.
- **Mining:** mining companies are said to have left 50 billion tons of toxic waste ore, polluted 12,000 miles of rivers and streams, and spoiled 180,000 acres of lakes and reservoirs, with cleanup costs estimated at $32–72 billion.
- **Restoration recommendations:** map and inventory every forest ecosystem; protect all old-growth and pines over 16 inches dbh; maintain an average of 7 snags per acre greater than 14 inches dbh in pine-oak stands; thin dense stands to roughly 50–250 trees per acre; exclude livestock from priority streams; prohibit new road construction in priority watersheds; reintroduce beavers and extirpated predators; prohibit non-native species introductions; and direct restoration jobs and redirected subsidies to local communities.
