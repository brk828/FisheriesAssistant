# AFS Guidelines for Introductions of Threatened and Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document presents proposed [[American Fisheries Society]] guidelines for introducing threatened and endangered fishes, circulated for comment before submission for approval at the 1989 Spring meeting. Introductions are a common feature of [[Recovery Plan]] programs, with more than 80% of listed fishes having been subject to introductions to establish new populations, supplement existing ones, create educational exhibits, or support propagation. The authors note that few introductions are published or analyzed, and that poorly planned efforts have sometimes failed or harmed other native organisms. The guidelines are organized into three components: (1) selecting the introduction site, (2) conducting the introduction, and (3) post-introduction monitoring and response.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with the case of the [[Amistad Gambusia]], a fish that was collected in 1968 from a spring about to be flooded, held at the [[Brackenridge Field Laboratory]] in Austin, Texas, and partly transferred to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico in 1974. By 1979 all stocks were found to contain only mosquitofish, which had apparently contaminated the populations. The species was declared extinct.

The [[Endangered Species Act]] requires the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to develop recovery plans for listed species. Recovery plans for 39 endangered or threatened fishes were reviewed, and 32 of them (82%) called for one or more introductions, in addition to habitat protection, research, and monitoring. Introduction purposes in those plans included:

- Information and education
- Genetic reserve
- Biological control
- Artificial propagation
- Supplementing existing populations
- Establishing new populations

Introductions proposed for two cyprinids (the [[Mohave Tui Chub]] and [[Warm Springs Dace]]), two cavefishes, and three darters ([[Snail Darter]] among them) were noted. Where species had restricted ranges and habitats hard to duplicate, the authors suggest focusing on habitat protection rather than introduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several case studies show how habitat and genetics shape introduction outcomes:

- **[[Devils Hole Pupfish]]:** Fish moved from a limestone spring to a cement refuge near [[Hoover Dam]] experienced more daylight, higher productivity, and a greater food supply. Refuge populations became larger, with brighter coloration and differences in several measurements.
- **[[Unarmored Threespine Stickleback]]:** Allozyme differences were documented in an introduced population in Holcomb Creek, which Buth suggested may have originated from the [[Fillmore Hatchery]] stock rather than the lower Santa Clara River.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]:** Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) found that the source population used for restocking was genetically impoverished, with low fecundity.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]:** Reintroductions began in 1981 with millions of young fish, but the first wild-released fish did not survive until 1985. Minckley and Carufel (1985) reported an approximate success rate for Arizona fishes, though the figure is illegible in the scanned text.

Hybridization is another concern. Closely related catostomids readily hybridize, and the authors caution against introductions where hybrids could result, except in limited cases where the hybridization is natural and the population is low.

## Key Findings

**Site selection**
- Introductions should occur within the native or historic range whenever possible. Sites outside historic range should be used only if all historic locations are unsuitable or unrestorable.
- Sites should be secure and preferably on protected land, with landowner agreement.
- Sites must meet all life-history requirements (food, spawning, and rearing habitat), and water quality should resemble undisturbed natural habitat.
- Sites must be large enough to support a viable population. The authors cite an effective breeding population of 200 as a benchmark, and note that habitat supporting hundreds of individuals may be needed.
- Introductions should be prohibited where they could cause hybridization or harm other rare or endemic taxa, and pre-introduction surveys are advised.

**Conducting the introduction**
- Stock should come from an appropriate source. Genetic information can guide choices among wild, hatchery, or geographically close stocks.
- Taxonomic status should be verified before transport, and voucher material preserved.
- Stock should be inspected by a qualified fish pathologist and quarantined for at least two weeks where feasible. Quarantine may be waived in emergencies or for transfers within a drainage.
- Founding stock should reflect the genetic composition of the source. Sex ratio and age structure should be balanced. Salmonid studies suggest at least 25 males and 25 females as an absolute minimum under controlled hatchery conditions, but more fish may be needed where reproduction is uneven.
- Handling and transport should minimize stress, which can suppress immunity and impair learning and homing for weeks.
- Introductions should occur under favorable weather and water conditions, with temperature matched to the receiving habitat and timing chosen to avoid predators.
- Procedures and locations must be documented in the scientific literature, including the people involved, source, sample size, number and sex/age of fish, date, and exact location.

**Post-introduction activities**
- Monitoring should include initial survival, recruitment, and persistence through extreme events, with long-term surveys once the population is established.
- Restocking may be advisable to increase gene flow, with genetic studies used to detect founder effects, bottlenecks, or inbreeding.
- When introductions fail, the causal factors should be identified and eliminated before restocking.

**Regulatory note**
- Experimental population designations can reduce local opposition and relax some Endangered Species Act requirements, but the authors caution that these designations may be used for non-essential populations. Emergency procedures may deviate from the guidelines when the last population is at risk.
