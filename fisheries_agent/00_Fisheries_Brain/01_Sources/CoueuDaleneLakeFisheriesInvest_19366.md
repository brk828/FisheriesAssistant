# Coeur d'Alene Lake Fisheries Investigations: 1967 Creel Census

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 report by Regional Fishery Biologist Jerry L. Mallet, [[Idaho Department of Fish and Game]], evaluates fishery management on [[Coeur d'Alene Lake]] using a [[Boat Count Interview Census]] run from April 29 to November 30, 1967. The survey estimated 37,757 anglers spent 169,908 hours and caught 242,207 [[Kokanee]], 889 [[Cutthroat Trout]], 572 [[Rainbow Trout]], and 2,443 other game fish. Kokanee made up 98.4 percent of the catch, a shift from the trout-dominated fishery of the late 1950s. The report attributes the decline of cutthroat to lost spawning habitat from mine pollution and farming runoff, and it links the kokanee fishery to large, continuous hatchery plantings. It closes with three recommendations on stocking and habitat restoration.

## Background & Description

**Lake setting.** Coeur d'Alene Lake covers 25,072 acres, is about 25 miles long, has 125 miles of shoreline, and lies in Kootenai County. The city of Coeur d'Alene sits on its north shore. The [[Washington Water Power Company]] has regulated lake levels since 1903, when the [[Post Falls Dam]] was built on the Spokane River about 8 miles downstream. The natural low water level is 2119.9 feet, and storage can raise it to 2128 feet. Several neighboring lakes (Round, Hidden, [[Chatcolet Lake]], Benewah, and Coeur d'Alene) became hydrologically connected after the raise.

**Survey design.** The lake was split into census Area A (north, Sections 1 and 2) and Area B (south, Sections 3 and 4). Section 4 is Chatcolet Lake and was reported separately. Key methods:
- The season was divided into two-week segments to reduce seasonal error.
- Boats were counted on one randomly chosen weekday and one weekend day per segment.
- Each day was split into four three-hour periods, plus "buffer" periods covering the remaining daylight.
- Anglers per boat were multiplied by mean boat counts to estimate anglers on the water, then by fishable hours to estimate total fishing hours.
- Species composition and catch per hour were applied to fishing hours to compute total catch.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pollution.** Mining on the South Fork of the [[Coeur d'Alene River]] began after the Bunker Hill strike in about 1885. Pollution eliminated aquatic life in the South Fork and in the main river below the confluence. Ellis (1932) reported mine slimes deposited across the lake floor from Harrison to the outlet, as documented in [[Mine Pollution Reports]].

**Watershed degradation.** Farmed watersheds on the lake's west side have been denuded. Siltation, higher water temperatures, and rapid runoff reduced stream spawning and nursery habitat. Many west-side bays now act as settling basins for soil washed off each spring.

**Competition.** The report notes that large kokanee populations have reduced cutthroat fishing quality in other north Idaho lakes and probably did so at Coeur d'Alene Lake too.

## Key Findings

### Total catch and effort (1967)
- Anglers: 37,757; hours fished: 169,908.
- Catch composition: 98.4% kokanee, 0.6% trout, 1.0% other species.
- Other species counted: 1,810 perch, 598 bullheads, 35 largemouth bass.
- Overall catch rate: 1.4 fish per hour.

### Catch by census section

| Section | Anglers | Hours | Kokanee | Cutthroat | Rainbow | Fish/hour |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (north) | 16,285 | 73,284 | 93,041 | 176 | 123 | 1.3 |
| 2 (north) | 5,477 | 24,648 | 32,114 | 225 | 27 | 1.3 |
| 3 (south) | 15,995 | 71,976 | 117,052 | 488 | 422 | 1.7 |

Section 3 produced the most kokanee, cutthroat, and rainbow. It also accounted for 55.0 percent of cutthroat harvest, while Section 1 accounted for the fewest (19.8 percent).

### Cutthroat decline
- The cutthroat is the only native game fish in the lake. Ellis (1932) recorded its decline as early as 1932.
- Share of catch in the south end (Section 3): 45.0% (1958), 51.0% (1959), 0.7% (1967).
- Share of catch in the north end (Section 1): 36% (1958), 17% (1959), 0.2% (1967).
- Jeppson (1960) reported cutthroat falling from 40% to 15% of the catch between 1957 and 1960.
- 1967 harvested cutthroat ranged roughly 7 to 16 inches, averaging 12 inches.
- Best cutthroat angling ran from May to early July and again in September.
- Causes cited: loss of Coeur d'Alene River spawning grounds, loss of west-side streams, competition from kokanee, and reduced angling pressure on trout as anglers shifted to kokanee.

### Kokanee
- Introduced in 1937. Plantings were small until 1955, then became large and continuous.
- Notable plants: 854,690 in 1942, 832,896 in 1957, 1,128,000 in 1960, 1,304,000 in 1961, and 1,166,422 in 1967 (Table 5). A 1959 plant of 920,000 included 488,000 fish from an early-spawning strain.
- Before 1954, annual plants were 100,000 to 200,000. Kokanee never exceeded 40 percent of the harvest before 1960, but reached 98.4 percent in 1967.
- Fishing timing: small catches from mid-May, good fishing from June 1 through late July, a second peak from September 1 to early October, then anglers lost interest as fish developed spawning characteristics.
- Spawning occurs late November to mid-December, but natural success is believed to be low. Causes include lack of suitable lakeshore sites, the post-spawn drawdown, and late egg deposition with cold water in tributaries such as Wolf Lodge Creek.
- Average spawning sizes (Table 6) declined as plantings and populations rose, then leveled off. Example: 1954 males 16.1 in. and females 15.0 in.; 1967 males 12.5 in. and females 11.9 in.
- Stated management goal: 1,000,000 kokanee fry annually.

### Rainbow trout
- Rainbow were planted annually near Wolf Lodge Bay in the north end.
- In 1967, 11,430 legal-size rainbow were stocked in two plants: 9,720 fish at 3.6 per pound on April 3, and 1,710 fish at 1.9 per pound on April 24.
- Only 572 rainbow were harvested, about 5.0 percent of the plant. Some may have been holdovers, and Section 3 may include fish from Chatcolet Lake.

### Species not fully censused
The census covered only the trout and kokanee season. Bullheads are heavily fished from shore in April in the north (Cougar Bay area) and south ends, often at night by campfire light. Most largemouth bass fishing occurs in April and May and was not surveyed.

### Recommendations
1. Discontinue catchable-size hatchery rainbow plants in the north end of the lake.
2. Continue annual fingerling kokanee stocking at current levels, not allowing it to fall below 500,000.
3. Reestablish a large-lake-run cutthroat population in the Coeur d'Alene River once pollution is removed from the drainage.
