## Executive Summary

This study is a continuation of long-term research (Gammon & Hasler, 1965) examining the ecological consequences of introducing young-of-the-year [[Muskellunge]] into two Wisconsin bog lakes (George Lake and Corrine Lake) devoid of native pike species. Conducted during years 8-9 post-introduction (1963-1964), the research tracks the population recovery of [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] following an initial 5-year period of severe predation-driven decline. Key findings indicate a numerical recovery of age II+ perch, but a persistent failure of prey fish to recruit into larger size classes (>150mm), suggesting a size-selective "cropping" predation dynamic consistent with Ricker's (1952) **Type C** predator-prey model. Concurrently, the muskellunge population itself experienced high mortality (annual survival rate of only 0.76) and exceptionally slow growth, likely due to intraspecific competition for a dwindling forage base.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study lakes represent a closed, low-diversity bog lake ecosystem where [[Muskellunge]] were introduced as an apex predator into a system previously dominated by [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] (with [[Smallmouth Bass]] present in one lake).

- **Trophic Interactions:** The core dynamic is a predator-prey relationship where the growth and survival of the [[Muskellunge]] population is directly coupled to the availability and size of forage fish (perch and bass).
- **Prey Diet Shifts:** Age 0 perch are noted as primarily plankton feeders (per Pycha and Smith, 1955), which explains why growth-rate increases due to reduced competition were only observed in Age I and Age II perch (which rely on food resources subject to intraspecific competition), not in Age 0 fish.
- **Intraspecific Competition:** As the forage base (perch/bass) collapsed, evidence points to significant intraspecific competition *among* the muskellunge themselves for the remaining appropriately-sized prey, leading to stunted predator growth analogous to findings by Muir (1960) in Nogies Creek.
- **Density-Dependent Growth:** The severe reduction in the adult perch population (occurring 1959-60) triggered compensatory increases in growth rates for the remaining young perch, a classic density-dependent growth response.

## Key Findings

1. **Population Recovery, but Size-Capped:** Age II+ perch numbers recovered substantially between 1960 and 1963-64 (Table 1), but the size structure remained skewed small; very few individuals exceeded 150mm TL, contrasting sharply with the pre-treatment 1959 population which included fish up to 240mm.
    - 1963 Population Estimate (Age II+): ~12,100 (95% CI: 8.8k–19.1k) [Schnabel method].
    - 1964 Population Estimate (Age II+): ~2,200 (95% CI: 1.9k–2.5k) [Schnabel method].

2. **Growth Rate Compensation:** Perch aged I and II showed statistically significant increases in growth rate for the 1959-62 year classes compared to pre-treatment (pre-1956) cohorts (Table 3), specifically:
    - Age I: t = 5.88 (p < 0.01)
    - Age II: t = 2.40 (p < 0.05)
    - No significant growth changes were detected in older age classes (III, IV, V) or during the initial years of muskellunge activity prior to the 1959-60 population crash.

3. **Predator Mortality and Survival:** The annual survival rate of muskellunge was calculated at approximately **0.76** (76%), consistent with earlier findings (Gammon & Hasler, 1965) of a 20–25% annual mortality rate.
    - George Lake 1964 Estimate: 23 individuals (95% CI: 19–58).
    - Corrine Lake 1964 Estimate: 35 individuals (95% CI: 24–133).

4. **Slowed Predator Growth:** Muskellunge growth rates plummeted, correlating temporally with the collapse of the age II+ perch forage base around 1959-1960. Growth rates observed in both George and Corrine lakes were comparable to the *slowest* rates documented by Muir (1960) for Ontario populations, and slower than the slowest rates documented by Schloemer (1936) for other Wisconsin muskellunge.

5. **Predation Model Support:** The pattern of eliminating large fish while allowing successful reproduction/recruitment of small fish supports **Ricker's (1952) Type C predation model**—where predators preferentially remove prey above a certain size/safety threshold—rather than a Type B model (fixed fraction/random encounter), which is anticipated to emerge as predator numbers continue to dwindle.
