# Tule Creek Fish Barrier: Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliopsis

## Executive Summary

This 1989 progress report by Curtis Lathan of the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (Arizona Projects Office) evaluates four candidate sites for a fish barrier on [[Tule Creek]]. The barrier would stop non-native reservoir fish from moving upstream during storm events and endangering the introduced topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and other native fish. The commitment arises from the [[New Waddell Dam]] and [[Central Arizona Project]] environmental documents, which require avoiding impacts to native fisheries in perennial streams caused by higher reservoir storage elevations. The report compares the sites using channel sections, peak-flow adjustments, and Manning's-formula depth and velocity estimates. It also reviews barrier construction materials. The design flood frequency had not been chosen at the time of writing, so no final design was made.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Prevent reservoir fish from entering Tule Creek and protect isolated native fish populations, as required by the [[New Waddell Dam]] final EIS and environmental assessment.
- **Study team:** [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] biologists Larry Riley and Eric Swanson, with Curtis Lathan of the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]].
- **Stream context:** Tule Creek is perennial in its upper reaches and intermittent in its lower reaches.

Four candidate sites were identified and described:

| Site | Location (as given) | Channel and geology |
|---|---|---|
| A | About 150 yards south of the lower access road, NW corner of Section 10, T7N, R1E | Narrow channel with exposed, erosion-resistant bedrock; natural drop of 4 to 6 feet |
| B | 1/2 mile north of Site A, south-central Section 3, T7N, R1E | Canyon at least 45 feet wide with high walls; conglomerate bedrock showing considerable erosion |
| C | About 1 mile south of the upper access road, Section 33, T8N, R1E | Downstream of the confluence of spring-fed perennial streams; thin alluvium over bedrock |
| D | About 1/2 mile south of the upper access road, Section 33, T8N, R1E | Thin alluvium over bedrock |

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow analysis.** Peak flows were taken from the 1981 [[Inflow Design Flood]] study for [[New Waddell Dam]] (see Appendix A). Flood frequency was derived from the [[Mayer Gaging Station]] (599.8 square miles). Flows were adjusted to each site by the square root of the ratio of watershed areas. The watershed areas used for the adjustment were:

- Site D: 3.7 sq mi (factor 0.078194)
- Site C: 4.6 sq mi (factor 0.087655)
- Site B: 8.5 sq mi (factor 0.119001)
- Site A: 11.1 sq mi (factor 0.135823)

**Hydraulic assumptions.** Depths and velocities were calculated with [[Manning's Formula]] for each site without a barrier, assuming Manning's n = 0.04. Channel slope was 0.03 at Sites A, B, and C, and 0.06 at Site D. Channel shapes were trapezoidal at Sites A, C, and D, and rectangular at Site B.

**Structural design considerations.** Two construction materials were considered for the [[Barrier Structure]]:
- [[Gabion Barrier]] structures, built from local loose rock in wire baskets. They are low in material cost and aesthetically favorable, but depend on labor costs and need dynamic stability investigation.
- [[Reinforced Concrete]], which may be more stable but is harder to transport to remote sites and offers no aesthetic advantage.

Permanent ponding behind the barrier should be avoided because a large pool could encourage well-meaning people to introduce game fish. Sturdy backfill may be required if the structure ponds water.

**2. Modeled 500-year depths and velocities (without barrier)**
- Site A: 8.81 ft depth, 22.06 fps velocity
- Site B: 7.52 ft depth, 20.30 fps velocity
- Site C: 7.67 ft depth, approximately 19.7 fps velocity
- Site D: 5.75 ft depth, 24.32 fps velocity

Depths decline with each lower return period. For example, Site A falls from 8.81 ft (500-year) to 4.87 ft (25-year).

**3. Site comparison (from the Discussion section)**
- **Site A:** Strong, erosion-resistant bedrock. The natural 4-to-6-foot drop reduces construction needs. The narrow channel requires less material, and road access is easy. Abundant natural rock is available for gabions. Drawbacks are high canyon velocities that may preclude a gabion structure.
- **Site B:** Natural rock is available, and access is easy. Its elevation is below the maximum-flood inundation level. Conglomerate bedrock shows considerable erosion, and high velocities through the canyon may rule out gabions.
- **Site C:** Protects all tributaries with perennial flow. Smaller flood flows mean a smaller barrier is needed. Access is limited, possibly requiring a service road, and gabion rock would have to be hauled in. Its elevation is above the maximum-flood inundation level.
- **Site D:** Also has smaller flood flows, a smaller barrier, and limited access with rock hauled in. One listed drawback is that a perennial stream would not be protected by the barrier.

**4. Inundation note.** A footnote states that a 1-in-500-year flood would be needed to reach elevation 1711.3 ft (±1 ft) at one location, where the barrier crest is set at 1718 ft (±2 ft). A separate elevation of 1716 ft cannot be linked to a flood probability because historical data are lacking.

**5. Barrier height.** As flow increases, the drop between barrier crest and downstream water surface decreases, reducing barrier effectiveness. Higher velocities over the crest, however, can act as a barrier depending on fish size and species. Further study is needed to set the required height.

**6. Open decision.** The design flood frequency (for example, 50- or 100-year) had not been decided and must be settled before final design.

**7. Appendix A: Inflow Design Flood (IDF) for New Waddell Dam**
- Basin: Agua Fria River, 1,450 sq mi, with the site about 1/4 mile below existing Waddell Dam.
- Recommended IDF: peak discharge of 493,400 ft³/s and a 7.3-day volume of 688,500 acre-feet, used for feasibility design.
- Storm comparison (peak inflow / 
volume): winter 388,321 ft³/s / 751,212 AF; summer 493,415 ft³/s / 688,510 AF; winter temporal 364,183 ft³/s / 744,662 AF; summer temporal 455,818 ft³/s / 699,405 AF.
- The summer flood was identified as the critical event.
- Lag time: 9.32 hours for the basin, compared with 10.3 hours in the prior study.
- Historic record: 41-year record (1940 to 1980) at the Mayer gage. Highest recorded discharge was 33,100 ft³/s on February 19, 1980. Estimated Glendale gage peaks were 105,000 ft³/s (January 1916) and 80,000 ft³/s (July 1917).

**8. Appendix B: PVC Gabion Specification**
- Gabions are PVC-coated, double-twisted hexagonal steel wire mesh baskets filled on site with clean, hard stone of 3 to 8 inches.
- Wire core is galvanized, about 0.1063 in (gauge 12), with an overall coated diameter of 0.1363 in. Mesh openings are approximately 3.5 by 4.5 inches.
- Standard sizes range from 6 to 12 feet long, with 3-foot widths and heights of 1 to 3 feet.
- Durability tests include 1,500 hours of salt spray (ASTM B 117) and 2,000 hours of UV exposure. PVC properties must stay within set limits after aging, such as tensile strength changing no more than 25%.
- Assembly requires connecting wires in gabions over 12 inches tall, and filling in stages so no cell is more than one foot deeper than its neighbor.
