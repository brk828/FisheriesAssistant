## Executive Summary

This document is a 1986 expert correspondence from Dr. W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, responding to a proposal to reintroduce the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) into the Gila River drainage, Arizona, under a "nonessential experimental population" designation. Rather than presenting primary morphological or physiological data, the letter is a conservation-policy critique grounded in Minckley's direct experience as the source of the species' captive broodstock. He argues strongly against the "nonessential" designation on biological and genetic grounds, characterizes the species' perilous conservation status, and proposes several alternative reintroduction strategies. The note is structured to extract the ecological, genetic, and population-management data embedded in this policy discussion.

## Background & Description

No morphological data (body form, meristics, coloration) are provided in this source. The document is policy-focused; morphological description of *Gila elegans* is not addressed. The species is referenced within the broader [[Gila (Genus)|Gila]] cyprinid group native to the [[Colorado River Basin]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Historically a big-river fish of the desert Southwest, associated with large, unmodified, highly fluctuating river systems in the [[Chihuahuan Desert]].
- **Co-occurring native fishes**: Documented alongside other large-river endemics, [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), both recorded in the same Fort Thomas, Arizona reach of the Gila River — used as evidence that bonytail chub likely also occupied this range.
- **Hydrology dependency**: Native range experiences extreme discharge fluctuation — from high flows during wet decades to complete drying between the proposed reintroduction area and Yuma, AZ, documented on at least four occasions in the prior decade.
- **Elevational range**: In the upper Colorado River basin, the species ranged into high-elevation habitats in Colorado and Wyoming, suggesting upstream movement in the Gila drainage is plausible despite no historic upstream records — potentially driven by attraction to larger water volumes, which currently decrease downstream in the Gila due to irrigation withdrawal.
- **Remnant wild population**: The only extant wild population resides in [[Lake Mohave]], separated from the proposed Gila River reintroduction site by six dams.
- **Threats in reservoir habitat**: Increasing populations of non-native [[Striped Bass]] in Lake Mohave are identified as a likely driver of extirpation of both bonytail chub and razorback sucker from that system.
- **Predator interactions**: Centrarchids (inshore sunfish/bass) considered more efficient predators on bonytail than trout, informing recommended introduction timing (winter/early spring) to avoid predation pressure.
- **Reproductive habitat**: Demonstrated capacity to reproduce successfully in artificial pond habitats at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], suggesting refuge ponds, oxbow lakes, and borrow pits could support recovery populations.
- **Alternative candidate habitat**: The Gila River reach between [[Coolidge Dam]] and [[Picacho Reservoir]], dependent on San Carlos Reservoir water levels and recent mine-pollution remediation, currently dominated by non-native fish but flagged as a potentially superior short-term reintroduction site compared to the Gila River above Safford.

## Key Findings

- **Conservation status**: *Gila elegans* is characterized as one of the most endangered species in the Colorado River region, comparable in peril to the [[California Condor]] and [[Black-footed Ferret]].
- **Genetic bottleneck**: The entire captive broodstock (~1,400 adults) at Dexter National Fish Hatchery is derived from only 12 wild-caught founder individuals, raising serious concern about limited heterozygosity and risk of losing rare alleles if broodstock numbers are reduced for reintroduction stocking.
- **Single-site risk**: All broodstock is held at one facility (Dexter National Fish Hatchery), creating a security/catastrophic-loss risk; the author recommends establishing a genetically-matched backup stock at a second site.
- **Policy critique — "nonessential" designation**: Minckley argues no population of a species this endangered should be classified "nonessential" until full recovery is achieved, as doing so removes regulatory leverage to protect habitat (e.g., against future river flow manipulation) and sets a precedent undermining the intent of the Endangered Species Act.
- **Monitoring gap**: The original proposal lacked any defined experimental design or monitoring plan for assessing reintroduction success/failure — the author calls this a mandatory missing component given the "experimental" label.
- **Proposed reintroduction site range issue**: The proposed site is located upstream of Fort Thomas, AZ — the most upstream historically confirmed record for the species — meaning it is not strictly "within the historic range," though presence there is inferred from co-occurring big-river fish records.
- **Recommended alternatives to Gila River (above Safford) reintroduction**:
  1. Development/modification of existing National Wildlife Refuge waters (ponds, oxbow lakes, borrow pits) as low-cost, low-disruption recovery habitat.
  2. Survey and use of the Gila River reach between Coolidge Dam and Picacho Reservoir as an alternative "nonessential experimental" site.
  3. Annual augmentation stocking into [[Lake Mohave]] timed to winter/early spring to reduce predation exposure, including use of isolated backwater grow-out habitats.
- **Political dimension**: Cooperation from other state/federal/private stakeholders is characterized as contingent on the population being classified "nonessential," creating tension between practical recovery logistics and legal protection strength.
- **Conditional support**: The author ultimately gives qualified support for the "nonessential experimental" designation only if paired with committed, funded, long-term research on the reintroduced population to generate management protocols essential for species recovery — otherwise the "experimental" designation is scientifically meaningless.
- **Contingency concern**: Raises the scenario that if both the Dexter broodstock and the Lake Mohave wild population were lost, the reintroduced Gila River population would become the last surviving population of the species — questioning whether it would then be redesignated "essential."
