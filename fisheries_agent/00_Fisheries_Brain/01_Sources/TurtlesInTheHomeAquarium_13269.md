## Executive Summary

This 1963 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service leaflet provides practical husbandry guidance for keeping juvenile freshwater turtles in home aquaria, a popular pet trade largely supplied by wild-collected hatchlings and reburied eggs from the southern United States (especially [[Louisiana]]). The document covers species identification, housing requirements, thermal biology, diet, and common ailments of captive baby turtles. It is notable primarily as a historical snapshot of the once-thriving pet turtle trade and its ecological sourcing practices (wild collection and egg reburial) rather than as a source of quantitative ichthyological or herpetological data.

## Background & Description

- **[[Map Turtle]]** (*Graptemys* spp.): Generally has a brown shell with a black ridge (keel) along the carapace midline.
- **[[River Turtle]]** (*Pseudemys* spp.): Has a green shell and a distinctive red blotch on each side of the head.
- Other commercially available varieties mentioned: northern and southern **[[Painted Turtle|painted turtles]]**, **[[Snapping Turtle]]**, **[[Softshell Turtle|soft-shell turtle]]**, and various forms of the "green river turtle."
- Captive turtles are noted to be noiseless and odorless, small enough to require only "a square foot or so" of living space as hatchlings.
- Shell deformation is documented as a pathological morphological change: turtles painted with enamel coatings develop shell deformities that restrict normal growth unless the paint is removed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Source populations**: More than sixty turtle varieties exist in the United States; most commercial baby turtles originate from the wild-caught or captive-hatched populations of southern states, particularly Louisiana, via collector capture of hatchlings or reburial of collected eggs in pens until hatching.
- **Trophic ecology**: Most turtles in this trade are described as carnivorous, with a natural diet inferred to include lean meat, fish, and invertebrates (earthworms) — consistent with the natural predatory ecology of juvenile emydid and chelydrid turtles.
- **Interspecific interactions**: Turtles of different varieties can be co-housed without injury to one another, but cohabitation with [[Goldfish]] or tropical aquarium fish is discouraged, as turtles will readily predate on fish, which serve as "excellent food for turtles" — highlighting the turtles' role as active fish predators even in captivity.
- **Thermal ecology**: As ectotherms, turtles are strongly temperature-dependent for feeding activity; typical household temperatures (below 75°F) are insufficient, leading to feeding cessation, starvation, and death if not corrected. Optimal captive range is 80–90°F, with a lower survival threshold of 78°F.

## Key Findings

- **Two dominant market genera**: *Graptemys* (map turtle) and *Pseudemys* (river turtle) constitute the majority of baby turtles sold in the U.S. pet trade as of 1963.
- **Thermal requirement is critical to survival**: Turtles kept below 75°F in ordinary home conditions frequently refuse food entirely and die of starvation; supplemental heating (40–60 watt bulb with reflector) is recommended, with careful monitoring to avoid overheating.
- **Diet composition**: Effective feeding regimens rely on raw lean meat, raw fish, and earthworms rather than dried/prepared foods, which fail to sustain long-term health. Feeding twice weekly is sufficient under adequate thermal conditions.
- **Predator-prey dynamic in mixed tanks**: Cohousing turtles with fish results in predation losses, underscoring turtles' carnivorous foraging behavior even as juveniles.
- **Common captive ailments**: Malnutrition (from cold-induced anorexia) is the primary cause of mortality; secondary conditions include sore/closed eyelids (treatable with 2% Mercurochrome and warm, moist housing) and shell deformation from decorative paint application.
- **Conservation/sourcing context**: The described collection practice — wild harvesting of hatchlings and reburial of wild-collected eggs for commercial hatching — reflects an extractive, unregulated wildlife trade model typical of mid-20th-century U.S. pet supply chains, predating later conservation-driven regulation of wild turtle harvest (e.g., subsequent size-limit laws targeting *Pseudemys* and related genera).
