# Pollution and Fisheries: Assessing Toxicity Thresholds for Freshwater Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by D. W. M. Herbert of the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] (Stevenage) examines how to set water quality criteria that protect freshwater fisheries from domestic, industrial and mining wastes. Because it is impractical to study every ecological effect of every discharge, the paper relies on field surveys, laboratory [[Toxicity Trials]] using [[Rainbow Trout]] as a standard test species, and methods for predicting the toxicity of mixtures and complex effluents. The author argues that laboratory "safe" concentrations are a useful guide, but that they must be adjusted for environmental variables such as [[Dissolved Oxygen]], [[Water Hardness]], pH, [[Salinity]] and temperature, and that mixtures of poisons can be assessed by summing threshold fractions.

## Background & Description

Waste discharges have made some British and American rivers unable to support fish, while others carry wastes yet still sustain good fisheries. The central aim of research is to find the levels of harmful factors that are critical for freshwater fisheries. The author frames this as an ecological problem: a viable fish stock requires survival of well-grown fish, successful reproduction, adequate food, and water that fish do not avoid. Habitat changes that favour less-valued species, or effects on growth rate and disease resistance, can also reduce fishery value.

Three main approaches are discussed:

- **Extensive surveys** of fish populations relative to water quality.
- **Toxicity trials (bioassays)** on sensitive indicator species.
- **Predictive methods** for mixtures and complex effluents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Field surveys.** Ellis (1937) surveyed fish at 982 places in American streams and concluded that a minimum [[Dissolved Oxygen]] concentration of about 5 ppm in warm weather was needed for a healthy fishery. Herbert & Richards (1963) found that suspended solids up to about 100 ppm were generally compatible with a reasonable fishery in British rivers. The method is limited because it requires many sites and a clear cause of fish absence, so it suits only common, widespread pollutants. Poisons, which are discharged in great variety, require other approaches.

**Choice of test species.** Rainbow trout is used in both North America and Europe because it is generally more sensitive than most regional species. Comparative data include:

- More susceptible than carp, chub, dace, perch, roach and tench to low dissolved oxygen (Downing & Merkens 1957).
- More susceptible than roach to high pH (Jordan & Lloyd 1964), and more sensitive to ammonia than roach, perch and gudgeon (Merkens & Downing 1957).
- More sensitive than char, carp and tench to several heavy metal salts (Schweiger 1957).
- Less resistant than coarse fish to stearylamine benzoate and acetate (Barth 1954).

Salmon smolts are more susceptible than rainbow trout to some poisons, notably [[Zinc Sulphate]] and [[Ammonia]].

**Sensitivity of life stages and other organisms.** Eggs appear more resistant than adults to phenol and fluoride, fry are more resistant to phenol, and small trout tolerate cyanide better than large ones. Several invertebrate food organisms (for example, *Carinogammarus*, *Tubifex*, *Daphnia*, *Anabolia*, *Chironomus*) are more resistant to some poisons than trout, although others are more susceptible. Koeppe (1961) showed that trout exposed to sub-lethal toxaphene died after eating exposed but more resistant prey, so food-chain effects matter.

**Behaviour.** Fish avoid low oxygen concentrations somewhat above lethal levels (Whitmore et al. 1960). Sticklebacks and minnows detect and avoid some poisons, but this ability is lost on dilution before the solution becomes non-toxic, so avoidance is unlikely to protect fish from sub-lethal exposure. Swimming trout at 85 per cent of maximum sustainable velocity for one or two days raised their susceptibility only slightly; the lethal concentration fell to about 70 per cent of the still-water value.

**Environmental modifiers of toxicity.** Key effects include:

- Zinc sulphate is much more toxic in soft water than in hard water.
- In brackish water, zinc toxicity falls with salinity up to roughly the isotonic point with fish blood, then rises.
- Weak acids ([[Cyanide]], hydrogen sulphide) are more poisonous at low pH; weak bases ([[Ammonia]]) at high pH.
- Higher temperature shortens survival time, but threshold concentrations are rarely much affected, except for weak acids and bases.
- Lower dissolved oxygen increases toxicity for all poisons tested, probably because fish ventilate more rapidly; the increase is similar across chemically dissimilar poisons.

Consequently, laboratory dilution water should match the receiving water or be adjusted to resemble it.

## Key Findings

1. **Standardised toxicity trials.** Rainbow trout toxicity trials give a practical estimate of concentrations tolerable in rivers, but one species and one test cannot guarantee protection of all fisheries from all effects, including growth, fecundity and disease resistance.

2. **Permitted discharge rate.** Herbert (1961) proposed that the maximum discharge of a poisonous waste should kill no more than a negligible percentage of rainbow trout within three months when diluted only by dry-weather flow. Allen (1962) reports that after six months, salmonid mortality is commonly 60–85 per cent per year, implying about 20–40 per cent over three months. The author suggests an extra poison-induced mortality of about 5 per cent could be tolerable, applied only in years when flow stays at dry-weather level for three months.

3. **Extrapolating short tests to long exposures.** Routine trials last only weeks. Probit versus log-time plots can be fitted and extrapolated to estimate the concentration causing 5 per cent kill after three months. For a demulsifier used at oil refineries, a tangent to the 5 per cent kill curve extrapolated to 90 days gave 4 ppm. The author notes these methods usually err in favour of fish, if a threshold exists above the extrapolated value.

4. **Thresholds.** Some poisons (for example, [[Sodium Fluoride]] and [[Gammexane]]) show a threshold concentration after about two weeks, where survival time becomes effectively independent of exposure duration.

5. **Mixtures.** Laboratory studies showed that the toxicity of mixtures can be predicted by summing the fractions of individual threshold concentrations ([[Threshold Concentration]] fractions). This relation held for:
   - [[Zinc Sulphate]] and [[Copper Sulphate]] (Lloyd 1961b)
   - [[Ammonia]] and monohydric [[Phenol]]s (Herbert 1962)
   - Zinc and ammonia (Herbert & Shurben 1964)
   - Zinc and phenol (Herbert & Vandyke 1964)

6. **Gasworks liquor.** Toxicity of a stream receiving spent still liquor from a gasworks was predicted from temperature, pH, carbon dioxide and oxygen data using summed ammonia and phenol threshold fractions. Prediction and observation agreed in 83 per cent of trials. Biological treatment of coke-works liquor removed most monohydric phenols, and the reduction in toxicity could be estimated fairly accurately from chemical analysis.

7. **River Cole.** Rainbow trout in an aquarium fed with polluted river water (Warwickshire) showed 50 per cent mortality after about 36 h. Monohydric phenols, ammonia, zinc, copper, nickel, chromium, total cyanide, pH, hardness and oxygen were monitored; the first four were judged most responsible. Predicted and observed toxicity showed reasonable agreement (sum of fractions on average somewhat below unity). Upstream of the sewage works, the summed fractions never exceeded 0.6, and zinc entering upstream was a substantial contributor to toxicity below the works. Complications include metallocyanide complexes, which are less toxic than free metal ions or free HCN (Doudoroff 1956), and copper threshold values varying by a factor of two or more.

8. **Sewage effluent validation.** Lloyd & Jordan (1963, 1964) applied a similar method to 24 sewage effluents; 19 agreed with observed toxicity within ±30 per cent (Fig 18, 3-day toxicity index).

9. **Conclusion.** The predictive method involves unverified assumptions but could be developed into a practical tool for evaluating each poison's contribution to toxicity in fishless rivers affected by multiple pollution.
