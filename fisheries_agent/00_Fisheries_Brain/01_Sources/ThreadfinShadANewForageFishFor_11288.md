# Threadfin Shad: A New Forage Fish for West Virginia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 extension article by Frank J. Schwartz of the [[Chesapeake Biological Laboratory]] reports the first West Virginia record of the [[Threadfin Shad]], captured on July 25, 1957, in [[Bluestone Reservoir]]. The author argues that the species could become a valuable [[Forage Fish]] for the state's reservoirs, because it converts plankton to fish flesh, grows to a size suitable for [[Game Fish]] prey, and spawns on submerged vegetation rather than shallow shore zones. The article contrasts it with the [[Gizzard Shad]], whose rapid growth and shoreline spawning make it a poorer forage species. The author also notes a risk of cold-water mass mortality and closes with an optimistic outlook for West Virginia's fisheries.

## Background & Description

- **Threadfin shad** (*Dorosoma petenense atchafalaya*; the source prints the genus as "Signalosa") is a clupeid with several subspecies native to the Gulf region from Florida to Texas, the lower [[Mississippi River]] drainage and Mexico. The northernmost subspecies is common in mainstream impoundments of the [[Tennessee River]] in Kentucky, Tennessee, Mississippi and Alabama.
- The species feeds mainly on microscopic plants and animals, which it strains from the water through specialized gill structures.
- It lives about two years, rarely exceeding 7 inches in length or 3 inches in height.
- It spawns dense clusters of adhesive eggs that attach to submerged vegetation, so it does not depend on shallow shoreline spawning grounds.
- **Gizzard shad** is a freshwater species of the Mississippi Valley to northern Mexico, and along the Atlantic Coast north to Maryland. Adults reach 20.5 inches, 4 inches in height and 3.5 pounds.
- The article also mentions a [[Herring]] that is "solely an Atlantic" species, but the passage is garbled in the scan, so its details are not reported here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Possible routes of entry.** The [[Ohio River]] route is considered unlikely because the threadfin's range does not include that system, and no threadfin have ever been recorded in the [[Cumberland River]], which enters the Ohio only 16 miles above the Tennessee's mouth. The most probable source is a 1953 Virginia planting in [[Claytor Lake]] (Pulaski County), which spawned in 1956 after earlier failed plantings from 1953. Shad could have drifted down the [[New River]] into the Bluestone Reservoir.

**Reservoir fit.** Bluestone Reservoir has dense weed beds near Bertha, below Bull Falls, that favor spawning, and dense plankton blooms that support rapid growth. The reservoir's fluctuating water levels make shallow-shore spawning risky, which favors a species that spawns on vegetation.

**Temperature tolerance.** Threadfin shad do not tolerate water temperatures below 40°F well. The article warns of possible mass die-offs, similar to those of gizzard shad, which run ashore in spring and litter beaches with dead fish.

**Salinity.** Gizzard shad along the Atlantic Coast tolerate brackish water up to 15 parts per thousand; the threadfin is confined to freshwater in the source.

**Predators.** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Walleye]] and [[Skipjack Herring]] (*Pomolobus chrysochloris*) are known to feed heavily on threadfin shad.

## Key Findings

- **First West Virginia record:** One 2.5-inch specimen, captured July 25, 1957, at Dock 1, Bluestone Reservoir.
- **Forage value:** Threadfin shad can convert plankton into fish flesh and remain available as prey for game fish, with yields of 300 to 500 fish per acre not uncommon.
- **Gizzard shad comparison:** Young gizzard shad reach 4 to 6 inches by their first winter and then fall out of the forage base. Their shoreline spawning in May or June is vulnerable to water-level fluctuations.
- **Translocation history:** In November 1953, 3,150 threadfin shad were taken from the Tennessee River 2.5 miles below Watts Bar Dam and flown to rearing ponds in San Diego County, California. Only 314 arrived alive, but they spawned in 1954 and spread to Lake Havasu, Mexico, the [[Salton Sea]] and the Los Angeles Metropolitan Aqueduct System. A Kansas planting failed.
- **Spread potential:** The author concludes that the threadfin could readily colonize West Virginia streams and reservoirs and other Ohio River watershed waters.
- **Maturity and spawning:** Information from California, Tennessee and Virginia indicates that young fish mature by their first fall and spawn as young-of-the-year, which would increase forage supply.
- **Key uncertainty:** Success depends on how many fish survive the 1957–1958 winter. The author notes that cold-water mortality remains a risk.
- **Conclusion:** The author expects a better forage–game fish relationship and a "bright future" for West Virginia's reservoirs.
