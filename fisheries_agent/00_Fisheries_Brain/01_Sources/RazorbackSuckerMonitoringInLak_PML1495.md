# Razorback Sucker Monitoring in Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada (1995–1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This monitoring report covers the period 01 November 1995 to 31 October 1996 for the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the Arizona–Nevada border. The adult population has declined from an estimated 60,000–75,000 in the late 1980s to roughly 25,000 today, and natural recruitment is blocked by predation on early life stages by non-native fishes. The Lake Mohave population is the largest remaining population of the species. The report describes tag-recapture monitoring of wild adults, a larval-harvest and rearing program for repatriation (the "cove" fish), movement analyses, and an interim evaluation of survival of stocked juveniles, which was estimated at 56.2 percent.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is a critically imperiled catostomid, and the document treats it as a species whose persistence in Lake Mohave depends on active management. The wild adult stock is monitored under a precedent set in the late 1960s, with coordination shifting from [[Arizona State University]] (ASU) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) for spring sampling, under the multi-agency [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG).

The goal of the NFWG is to repatriate 50,000 razorback suckers to replace the ageing adult stock, which is expected to effectively disappear within the next decade. Repatriation uses winter–spring harvest of wild-produced larvae because this best preserves the genetic diversity of the adult population (Dowling et al. 1996a, b); hatchery propagation was judged less able to capture that diversity.

Monitoring methods:
- **Gear:** [[Trammel Net]] sampling is the main method, set near shore on gravel spits, reefs, and embayments at 1–10 m depth. Nets are typically 91 m long, and tended about every 6 hours because catch rates peak between dusk and dawn.
- **Measurements:** Total length (TL) to the nearest mm, optional weight, sex, parasite/health/injury checks, and tag scanning.
- **Tagging:** Carlin tags (UNLV, 1982–1983) and Floy anchor tags (through 1990) were used; since 1991 fish have received internally implanted [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) tags. All data go into a central tagging database maintained by ASU.

Rearing pathway: larvae are reared at [[USBR]] facilities (Boulder City, NV) or the USFWS hatchery at Willow Beach, AZ, then stocked as juveniles into backwaters, ponds, and hatcheries. Juveniles reaching about 30 cm TL (typically late summer–autumn of age 0) are PIT tagged and released as "cove" fish. The 30 cm target was chosen as a size thought to be relatively immune to predation (Marsh and Brooks 1989).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predation:** Non-native fishes prey on early life stages, which is identified as the reason natural recruitment is precluded. Predation by ictalurid catfishes is cited as a deterrent to re-establishment of hatchery-reared fish (Marsh and Brooks 1989).
- **Spatial structure:** The reservoir was divided into six areas (River, Uplake, Arizona Bay, Nevada Side, Arizona Side, Downlake) considered limnologically distinct. Most adults concentrate in [[Arizona Bay]] and along the Nevada shoreline between Six- and Nine-mile coves, where spawning aggregations occur.
- **Growth measurement limits:** Length changes in recaptured adults were indistinguishable from measurement error (about ±2.5% of TL), caused by factors such as snout compressibility and caudal lobe choice.
- **Genetics:** The adult population has exceptional genetic diversity, which the repatriation approach aims to retain.

## Key Findings

**Population and capture results (1995–1996)**
- A total of 1,883 razorback suckers (wild adults plus cove-reared repatriates) were captured.
- There were 534 recaptures, 28.4% of the total sample.
- Of recaptures, 479 (89.7%) were wild adults (25.4% of total captures) and 55 (10.3%) were repatriated juveniles (2.9% of total captures).
- Wild adult recaptures were 479 (26.2%) of the 1,828 wild fish captured.
- Recaptures spanned all five years since PIT tagging began in 1991. Some fish had been at large about 60 months.
- Most recaptures were single events; 26 fish were captured three times, and one was captured four times.

**Wild adult movement**
- Within-area movement accounted for 47% of recaptures; movement between adjacent areas accounted for another 33%, so about 80% of recaptures were within or close to the capture area.
- Movement among Nevada Side and Arizona Bay alone accounted for 60% of records, and 89% when movements to other areas are included.
- One fish carried an inactive sonic tag implanted 07 November 1994 ([[Sonic Telemetry]]; Knowles et al. 1996).
- Three fish first Floy tagged in 1990 had been at large 72 months; their growth over that span (about 1.5–2.2 cm) fell within measurement error.

**Repatriate recaptures and growth**
- The 55 recaptured cove fish came from several backwater sources; Yuma Cove contributed 33 individuals, with others from Arizona Juvenile, Dandy, Nevada Larvae, North Chemehueve, and the Boulder City Golf Course pond. Stocking occurred between November 1992 and November 1995.
- Four fish released at Yuma Cove in November 1992 were recaptured after 40 months, averaging 35.4 cm at stocking and 52.7 cm at recapture (about 5.2 cm per year).
- The largest individual gained 41.5 cm after stocking (23.0 cm to 64.5 cm); the largest recaptured repatriate measured 64.6 cm.
- Most recaptures were from Arizona Bay, with fewer from the Nevada and Arizona sides of Cottonwood Basin.

**Interim evaluation of the rearing program**
- Using the 25,000 adult estimate and the recapture ratio, about 2,575 repatriated fish were estimated to be in the population (25,000 × 0.103).
- A total of 4,580 cove-reared fish were stocked (14 October 1992 to 21 December 1995); 252 more were stocked through September 1996 without recaptures.
- Estimated survivorship of repatriated juveniles is 56.2% (2,575/4,580); about 64% of surviving fish from 1996 were from 1995 releases.
- Neither stocking size nor number stocked reliably predicted survival. Regression on size (23 stocking events) gave r² = 0.1297; regression on number stocked gave r² = 0.4881, which the authors called unsatisfactory.

**Conclusions**
- Authors conclude the results are encouraging, show significant progress, and fully support continuing the repatriation program, while noting much work remains before the goal of replacing the remnant population is reached.
