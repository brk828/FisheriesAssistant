# Thoughts on the Status and Recovery of the Rare Fish of the Upper Colorado River Basin

## Executive Summary

This 1990 internal U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service commentary (authored by D. Osmundson, transmitted by Lynn R. Kaeding) critiques the FWS "road maps" for recovering four endangered fish species of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]: [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The author argues that recovery planning has proceeded on unverified assumptions about population status and decline causes, and calls for a more systematic, hypothesis-driven research approach rather than a "shotgun" strategy of simultaneously addressing all potential limiting factors. Key conclusions: the [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are functionally near extinction in the upper basin and require immediate genetic rescue via refugia populations; the [[Humpback Chub]] appears stable with self-sustaining populations; and the population trend of the [[Colorado Squawfish]] remains scientifically undetermined despite it being the most-studied of the four species.

## Background & Description

- **Historical context**: All four species were once widespread across the lower and upper basins of the [[Colorado River]], including the Gila and Salt River drainages, prior to dam construction and diversions.
- **Primary driver of decline (Assumption 1)**: Disruption of natural hydrological regimes (dams, diversions, cold-water releases) and rapid shifts in fish community composition due to introduced species.
- **Key habitat alterations**:
  - Mainstem dams converted warmwater riverine habitat to cold, unsuitable conditions.
  - Tributary impoundments/diversions eliminated populations in the Gila and Salt River drainages.
  - Specific structures cited as range-reducing/fragmenting: [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] (Gunnison River), Price Stub Dam (Colorado River), [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (Green River), [[Taylor Draw Dam]] (White River).
- **Documented mechanistic evidence**:
  - Marsh (1985): Cold water reduces [[Colorado Squawfish]] egg viability.
  - Black and Bulkley (1985): Cold water reduces young Colorado Squawfish growth rates.
  - Coon (1965): Two young Colorado Squawfish found in channel catfish stomach contents (Dolores River).
  - Taba et al. (1965): Squawfish found in black bullhead stomachs (Colorado River).
  - Minckley (1983): Underwater observations of carp preying on freshly spawned [[Razorback Sucker]] eggs in Lake Mohave.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Refugia and captive stocks**:
  - [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] holds a Bonytail refugia population derived from Lake Mohave stock, and Razorback Sucker adults/progeny from Lake Mohave stock plus three adults captured near Grand Junction.
  - [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] maintains young Razorback Suckers of Green River origin (H. Tyus).
  - Dexter NFH no longer maintains a Humpback Chub refugia population.
- **Population geography**:
  - Humpback Chub: five canyon populations in the Colorado, Green, and Yampa rivers (upper basin) plus a sizable Little Colorado River population (lower basin).
  - Razorback Sucker: adults persist in Lake Mohave (lower basin) and in the Colorado and Green Rivers (upper basin), with declining catch rates in the Colorado over the prior decade.
  - Colorado Squawfish: higher adult and young numbers in the Green River system than in the Colorado River system.
- **Monitoring programs**:
  - [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP), begun 1986, tracks young-of-year (YOY) relative abundance via standardized seining CPUE, and adult relative abundance via standardized spring electrofishing CPUE.
  - Limitations noted: high year-to-year CPUE variance from small catch sizes and environmental effects on gear efficiency, limiting detection to long-term trends or only dramatic population changes.
- **Taxonomic uncertainty**: Ongoing debate over whether [[Humpback Chub]] is genetically/taxonomically distinct from [[Roundtail Chub]], raising concern about introgression and loss of genetic purity.

## Key Findings

### Species Status Summary
| Species | Status (1990) | Key Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| [[Bonytail]] | Functionally extinct in wild; near-total absence in UCRB | No significant recruitment; only old adults persist in Lake Mohave; refugia at Dexter NFH |
| [[Razorback Sucker]] | On brink of extinction in upper basin | Declining adult catch rates in Colorado River over 10 years; reproduction documented (Green River—Tyus 1987; Lake Mohave) but recruitment insufficient |
| [[Humpback Chub]] | Apparently stable / self-sustaining | Successful reproduction/recruitment in 5 canyon populations + Little Colorado River population |
| [[Colorado Squawfish]] | Uncertain trend | Best-studied species; reproduction/recruitment ongoing in Colorado & Green systems; no validated long-term trend data |

### Critique of Recovery Strategy
- The FWS road maps assume population decline for Colorado Squawfish without confirmed evidence, then propose a **"shotgun approach"**: simultaneously (1) enhancing habitat, (2) enhancing flow/temperature regimes, (3) stocking, (4) controlling introduced species, (5) building fish passage structures.
- Author argues this approach is scientifically inefficient, potentially wasteful, and possibly counterproductive if applied without identifying actual limiting factors.

### Proposed Research Framework for Colorado Squawfish
Two core objectives proposed:
1. **Determine population trend** — stable vs. declining in Green and Colorado subsystems (via improved larval sampling, YOY CPUE, and age-structure analysis of adults).
2. **Determine limiting factors**, categorized under three hypotheses:
   - **Habitat/resource limitation** (adult, young, and subadult habitat/food constraints)
   - **Reproduction limitation** (disrupted spawning cues, insufficient adults, poor spawning habitat, contaminant-induced sterility)
   - **Survival limitation** (egg/larval, YOY, subadult, and adult mortality causes including predation, disease, desiccation, angling, and sampling-induced mortality)

### Management Recommendations (Priority Order)
1. **Highest priority**: Rescue/preserve genetic material of [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] via refugia populations, with backup facilities beyond Dexter NFH in case of disaster.
2. **High priority**: Immediate life-history research on Green River Razorback Sucker population before it becomes too rare to study.
3. **Moderate priority**: Develop Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub broodstocks; conduct genetic analysis to determine whether distinct "stocks" exist per locale before broodstock development.
4. **Humpback Chub**: Continue/increase monitoring; gather flow-related life history data to support Aspinall Unit consultation with the Bureau of Reclamation; no active habitat manipulation or stocking recommended absent evidence of decline.
5. **Stocking policy**: All stocking of the four species should remain experimental only, used to help identify limiting factors, not as a presumed recovery mechanism, since underlying causes of decline likely remain unaddressed.

### Core Argument
The author asserts that recovery planning is currently proceeding on **unverified assumptions** rather than confirmed cause-and-effect relationships, and urges the research community to first establish population trends and isolate limiting factors (habitat, reproduction, or survival) via a **stepwise hypothesis-testing framework** before large-scale management interventions are implemented.
