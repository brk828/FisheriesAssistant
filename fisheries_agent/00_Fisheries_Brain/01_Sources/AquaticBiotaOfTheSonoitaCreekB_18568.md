# Aquatic Biota of the Sonoita Creek Basin, Santa Cruz County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 ecological leaflet reports the historical and current status of the aquatic biota of the [[Sonoita Creek]] basin in Santa Cruz County, Arizona. Based on surveys from autumn 1963 and an intensive 1967 survey, the study finds that the basin, though mostly dry for much of the year, retains about 10 linear miles of perennial flow. Its springs and permanent ponds shelter remnants of the region's depleted native aquatic fauna. Fourteen fish species have been recorded, seven of them native. Several native fishes are now restricted to a few sites, and the author warns that planned impoundment and development threaten these remnants. The paper closes with a call for basin-wide planning and a "living museum" approach to conservation.

## Background & Description

**Physical setting and history.** Before 1890, Sonoita Creek likely flowed through a broad, marshy floodplain with multiple channels, slow currents, and cienega habitat. From the 1890s, "arroyo cutting" produced eroding banks, moving sand bottoms, and woody invasion of the floodplain. The author links this erosion to a combination of factors, including severe overgrazing from 1870 to 1890 and a drought in 1891–93 that reportedly killed an estimated 75 percent of the cattle herd. The timing of erosion across a wide region suggests a broad regional driver such as climate.

**Research history.** Early collections in Arizona date to the U.S. and Mexican Boundary Surveys of 1851–54. Later records include Edgar A. Mearns (1892) and F. W. Chamberlain (1904, Sonoita drainage). Other workers, including C. E. Burt, C. L. Hubbs, and R. R. Miller, sampled the stream from 1928 to 1959. These records allow a chronology of faunal change. Fishes are the best-documented group, and the report's interpretations rest mainly on them.

**Fish fauna.** The 14 recorded species are grouped below. Native species are noted with their roles.

- **Cyprinidae (minnows):** [[Goldfish]] (introduced), [[Gila Chub]] (native), [[Speckled Dace]] (native), [[Longfin Dace]] (native, dominant in Sonoita Creek)
- **Catostomidae (suckers):** [[Gila Coarse-Scaled Sucker]] (native, historical record only from Sonoita Creek in 1904), [[Gila Mountain-Sucker]] (native)
- **Ictaluridae (catfishes):** [[Pacific Channel Catfish]] (introduced, apparently extirpated), [[Black Bullhead]] (introduced)
- **Cyprinodontidae (killifishes):** [[Desert Pupfish]] (native, two remnant populations)
- **Poeciliidae (livebearers):** [[Mosquitofish]] (introduced), [[Gila Topminnow]] (native, endemic to the Gila basin)
- **Centrarchidae (sunfishes):** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill Sunfish]], and [[Green Sunfish]] (all introduced, found mainly in artificial tanks)

The author also notes that [[Common Carp]] has not yet been recorded in Santa Cruz County but should be excluded from the drainage.

**Other aquatic organisms.** Plant records (Table 1) include watercress, sedges, filamentous algae, cattails, and several rare species. Notable among them are *Lilaeopsis recurva*, possibly restricted in the United States to Monkey Spring, and *Ludwigia natans*, not previously recorded in Arizona. Invertebrate work is incomplete. Springs support planarians, the amphipod *Hyalella azteca*, a minute undescribed hydrobiid snail, mayflies, and predatory belostomatid and naucorid bugs. Permanent ponds have insect larvae, oligochaetes, leeches, and ostracods. Vegetated ponds are the most diverse. Corixids and notonectids become very abundant at low flow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality and chemistry.**
- Stream and spring waters generally exceed 200 mg/l total hardness and reach as high as 750 mg/l. The waters have relatively high sulfate and low chloride proportions, which the author describes as chemically unusual for Arizona.
- At Monkey Spring, source pH is 6.8–7.0, turbidity is undetectable, and dissolved oxygen is low (2.3–2.6 mg/l) but adequate for fish and invertebrates. Average spring ion concentrations are Ca²⁺ 210 mg/l, Mg²⁺ 50 mg/l, Na⁺ 3 mg/l, CO₃²⁻ 130 mg/l, SO₄²⁻ 350 mg/l, and Cl⁻ 4 mg/l.
- Source water emerges at about 81.5 °F. Carbonates precipitate downstream, and average Ca²⁺ in [[Hill Pond]] falls to about 198 mg/l and CO₃²⁻ to 117 mg/l. Hill Pond pH ranges from 7.2 to 8.1, with a weak, transitory summer thermocline.
- Mainstream Sonoita Creek pH ranged from 7.5 to 8.4 across 11 samples. Dissolved oxygen was near 100 percent saturation in flowing waters, including below Patagonia's domestic wastewater inflow.
- Flood runoff is turbid but low in dissolved solids. Rapid thermal drops occur when cool rain enters warm summer flow. A fish kill in August 1966 may have resulted from such thermal stress.
- Temporal Creek receives seepage from abandoned mines, and inorganic pollutants likely affect its chemistry locally.

**Habitat permanence.** Flow in Sonoita Creek depends on springs, underflow, and sources such as [[Monkey Spring]] and [[Cottonwood Spring]]. Their thermal nature suggests a deep aquifer, so they will likely persist. However, diversion of spring water, pumping from the alluvial aquifer, and impoundment of tributary canyons could reduce creek discharge. Water-holding cienega soils are no longer present along the creek, so water-level fluctuation is expected to increase.

**Threats.**
- **Introduced species.** Mosquitofish, goldfish, and carp are regarded as serious risks, and mosquitofish are linked to the decline of native small fishes elsewhere in Arizona.
- **Impoundment and development.** The proposed lower-creek lake would accelerate human impacts.
- **Enrichment.** Organic enrichment from human sources is increasing.
- **Population growth.** Growing human populations are expected to bring more exotic species and a shift toward species such as [[Longfin Dace]] that tolerate fluctuating conditions.

## Key Findings

1. **Surviving but reduced biota.** Sonoita Creek retains about 10 linear miles of perennial flow, supplemented by two major springs and several earthen tanks. These sites preserve remnants of the basin's native aquatic fauna, which has been widely depleted across southern Arizona.

2. **Native fish status.** Several native fishes have declined sharply:
   - [[Gila Chub]] once ranged widely across the upper Santa Cruz basin and related systems but is now abundant at fewer than 10 places.
   - [[Speckled Dace]] was considered extinct in Sonoita Creek in 1959, but reproduction followed favorable rains in 1966, and it was frequent in suitable habitat in 1967.
   - [[Desert Pupfish]] is restricted in Arizona to two isolated populations, one at Quitovaquito Spring and one at Monkey Spring Pond. The author suggests both may be recognized as distinct taxa.
   - [[Gila Topminnow]] is now restricted to the Sonoita Creek drainage (with healthy populations at Monkey and Cottonwood springs), though it once ranged widely across the Gila basin.

3. **Dominant stream fish.** [[Longfin Dace]] dominates the Sonoita Creek fauna. It is relatively unselective and disperses rapidly into ephemeral pools. Its spawning was observed in April 1967. In 33 apparent spawnings, two involved two males.

4. **Breeding and behavior.** Breeding observations include:
   - Gila chub juveniles (25–75 mm) move to inflow currents, and reproduction may occur year-round in the constant-temperature spring.
   - Pupfish breed from March through September, with territorial males and no parental care.
   - Gila topminnow reproduction is most intense in spring and early summer, but occurs in almost all months.
   - Mountain-suckers and speckled dace were in reproductive condition together in April 1967.

5. **Introduced species impacts.** Most introductions are judged undesirable. Mosquitofish, introduced into Arizona around 1922, now occur in most streams. The author argues that native small fishes in Arizona declined after mosquitofish became established, rather than right after arroyo cutting. Recommended actions include drying the Cottonwood Spring tank to eliminate mosquitofish, and avoiding stocking of other species.

6. **Historical loss.** [[Pacific Channel Catfish]] was stocked in 1899 in Monkey Lake, last taken in the 1950s, and not recovered since 1963 despite extensive netting. [[Gila Coarse-Scaled Sucker]] has been recorded in the basin only once (1904).

7. **Water chemistry as a habitat constraint.** The mineral-rich, carbonate-precipitating spring waters create stable habitats that support specialized flora and fauna. Stream habitats, by contrast, are variable and prone to thermal and flood disturbance.

8. **Management recommendations.** The author recommends:
   - Careful development of the protected portion of the basin to include as many former habitats (especially cienegas) as possible.
   - Immediate planning to offset expected land and water-use changes.
   - A basin-wide approach, including a current file on water and land developments.
   - Public information exchange and a "living museum" approach to conservation.

9. **Overall conclusion.** The relative isolation of the basin, combined with its springs, streams, and artificial ponds, has allowed much of the native fauna to persist. However, projected development makes it unlikely that natural areas including aquatic habitats can be maintained without immediate, coordinated planning.
