# Evaluation of Trout Planting in Lake Tahoe

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 report by [[Almo J. Cordone]] and [[Ted C. Frantz]] evaluates experimental trout stocking in [[Lake Tahoe]] from 1960 to 1963, run as part of the cooperative California-Nevada Lake Tahoe Fisheries Study. The stated goal was to improve fishing, especially for shore and pier anglers in summer, by establishing a [[Premium Fishery]] of large, near-wild trout. None of the tested strains met that goal. [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] contributed least. [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]] showed the most long-range potential but returned few premium fish. [[Domestic Rainbow Trout]] gave the highest immediate returns and supports a [[Put-and-Take Fishery]]. The authors conclude that a premium fishery cannot currently be developed through stocking, and they recommend an annual plant of large catchable domestic rainbow.

## Background & Description

Lake Tahoe is a large, deep, oligotrophic lake straddling the California-Nevada border. Its mean depth is 1,027 feet and its maximum depth is 1,645 feet, and it covers 193 square miles. Its restricted [[Littoral Zone]] and low productivity limit food availability. Lake trout became the dominant game fish after the native Lahontan cutthroat trout disappeared. Lake trout are seldom caught by average anglers, however.

Between 1950 and 1964, about 14 million trout and kokanee salmon were released in the lake (13,934,967 total). Of these, 10,076,313 were domestic rainbow fingerlings, mostly stocked in the first 10 years. The authors judged that this fingerling program apparently did not materially improve fishing.

Trout were classed by size: fingerlings (5.2 inches or smaller), subcatchables (5.3 to 7.3 inches), catchables (7.4 to 9.9 inches) and large catchables (10.0 inches or larger). Trout surviving longer than 180 days were defined as premium fish, since they had likely acquired wild coloration and behavior. Fish were marked by fin and maxillary bone excision under anesthesia (chlorobutanol or MS-222). Marking mortality was negligible, though long-term effects were unknown.

Strains tested included domestic rainbow from hatchery brood stocks, [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]], [[Pyramid Lake Rainbow Trout]] (wild Nevada strain), [[Williams Lake Rainbow Trout]] (wild Idaho strain), Lahontan cutthroat, Yellowstone cutthroat, and small numbers of eastern brook trout and steelhead.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake structure:** Rainbow were largely confined to shoreline areas shallower than 25 feet, preferring shallow rocky habitat and rock-filled piers. Sandy beaches dominate much of the lake, and rocky habitat is scarce. Netting showed some rainbow in the [[Limnetic Zone]], usually within 10 feet of the surface.
- **Tributaries:** The drainage basin covers 326 square miles and contains about 30 to 40 tributaries. Most are small, intermittent, and in poor condition from human activity. The larger meadow streams (Upper Truckee River, Trout, Taylor, Blackwood and Ward creeks) support wild rainbow runs. Mountain whitefish, brown trout, [[Kokanee Salmon]] and eastern brook trout also occur.
- **Regulations:** The bag limit was five fish (trout, kokanee or whitefish in any combination). Fishing was open year-round, with tributary closures and a [[Logan Shoals]] harbor closure from March 1 to October 31. Logan Shoals was the main Nevada release site.
- **Wild fishery:** Roughly 4,000 wild rainbow were caught annually. The authors argued these were naturally reproduced rather than stocked, based on stable catch rates from 1960 to 1965 and observed spawning runs and fingerlings.
- **Predation and competition:** Lake trout predation, competition with abundant nongame fish, tributary mortality, and low food supply were identified as the main causes of poor survival.

## Key Findings

**Contribution to the fishery**
- Lake trout made up about 56% of the total catch. Planted trout contributed 23%, wild rainbow 18%, and other species 3%.
- Planted trout comprised 24.7% of the total catch in 1961 (4,880 fish), 16.8% in 1962 (3,365) and 38.4% in 1963 (9,471).
- Of planted trout caught, 59.1% were taken by shore anglers, 17.1% by pier anglers, 23.4% by topliners and 0.4% by deepliners.
- From June through November, planted trout made up roughly 35 to 80% of the catch per hour, peaking in July, August and October, and falling to about 8% in April and May.

**Harvest by strain**
- [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]: poorest returns. None were checked from the 1961 release of 80,174 fish, and the 1962 release of about 77,667 fish yielded about 0.2%.
- [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]]: 0.1, 0.2 and 0.8% from three plants totaling 40,748 fish.
- [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]]: estimated harvests generally below 4%. Shallow rocky Lake Forest plants returned 1.4% (1961) and 3.9% (1962). Sandy Kiva plants returned 0.1 to 0.8%. The 1963 plant of 98,368 subcatchables returned an estimated 3.0%, probably closer to 5% after late returns.
- [[Pyramid Lake Rainbow Trout]]: 18.7% (1,339 catchables, 1961) and 9.2% (14,742 subcatchables, 1962).
- [[Williams Lake Rainbow Trout]]: 14.0% from the 1963 catchable plant.
- [[Domestic Rainbow Trout]]: large catchables returned 21.0, 29.8 and 7.9% (1960 to 1962). Catchables released at Logan Shoals ranged from 3.5 to 35.8%, averaging 14.8%. The 1960 Hot Creek catchables returned 13.1% (Al Tahoe), 8.0% (Lake Forest) and 2.5% (McKinney Bay limnetic).

**Survival and premium potential**
- Domestic catchables: about 95% of censused fish were taken within six months, and about 70% within two months. Plants at Logan Shoals contributed the few fish surviving 3 to 4 years.
- [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]]: an average of nearly 30% survived beyond six months, 10% beyond one year and 4% beyond two years. Several survived about 3.25 years, the longest recorded for the strain.
- [[Williams Lake Rainbow Trout]]: about 10% returned after six months and 2.5% after one year.
- [[Pyramid Lake Rainbow Trout]]: about 5% after six months, under 2% after one year, and one fish beyond two years. The authors attributed this to adaptation to the alkaline Pyramid Lake.
- Hot Creek catchables had the poorest long-range returns: 99% returned within six months.
- Lahontan and Yellowstone cutthroat yielded too few returns to assess premium potential.

**Growth**
- Planted trout grew about 0.4 inch per month in the first 12 to 18 months, then about 0.2 inch per month, tapering to 0.1 inch per month after 30 to 36 months.
- Mean October lengths for domestic rainbow were about 10.0, 15.5, 18.5 and 20.0 inches in years 1 to 4. For Kamloops they were about 8.5, 13.0, 15.0 and 16.5 inches.
- The 10 largest domestic rainbow from 1961 to 1963 plants measured 17.5 to 21.0 inches. The 10 largest Kamloops measured 16.5 to 17.8 inches.

**Distribution and movement**
- Kamloops and Williams Lake rainbow spread through the limnetic zone. Kamloops commonly entered tributaries and Emerald Bay.
- Domestic and Pyramid Lake rainbow stayed in shallow littoral waters. Trout planted on the rocky east shore tended to remain on that side.
- Of 423 fish caught in monofilament gill nets across 40 limnetic sets, 73 (17%) were planted rainbow. Most were surface-oriented, and 73% were within 10 feet of the surface.
- Kamloops comprised 48% of planted trout caught in the limnetic zone. Domestic rainbow were nearly absent there.
- Kamloops spawners were recorded in Hatchery Creek, and in May and June 1965 six spawners were dip-netted there. In October 1961, 103 planted trout were seined from Taylor Creek, including 93 Kamloops.

**Mortality factors**
- Murphy (1963) found one-month survival of 10.6% for domestic fingerlings and 11.7% for wild trout-of-the-year in a screened Taylor Creek section.
- Condition indices declined from 40 to 45 at release to means of 35.8 (Kamloops, n=149), 38.0 (Williams Lake, n=48) and 38.1 (domestic, n=100) after six months.
- Of 1,150 [[Lake Trout]] stomachs examined, 30 (2.6%) held 123 possible planted trout. Of these, 98 were definitely planted: 75 Lahontan cutthroat, 18 Yellowstone cutthroat, 4 Williams Lake rainbow and 1 Kamloops rainbow. The authors concluded that [[Predation]] by lake trout was the primary cause of the cutthroat failures.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- A premium fishery cannot currently be developed in Lake Tahoe by stocking domestic or wild rainbow or cutthroat trout. Other species tested (eastern brook trout, steelhead, Mt. Whitney strain domestic rainbow, silver salmon) also failed to provide premium fisheries.
- Kamloops rainbow would be retested if [[Mysis Relicta]] or [[Bonneville Cisco]] established dense populations, since these would improve the food supply.
- A put-and-take program using domestic catchables could catch 90% or more of stocked fish when released in accessible shoreline locations. The authors did not recommend this because it would concentrate anglers too heavily.
- A 1964 tagged-release test suggested harvests of about 40% for domestic catchables and about 70% for large catchables released in summer at closed harbors such as Logan Shoals.
- Cost analysis ([[Stocking Cost Analysis]]): hatchery rearing and planting cost $0.874 per pound. A catchable (8.0 inches, 0.23 pounds) cost about $0.20, and a large catchable (12.0 inches, 0.75 pounds) about $0.66. Replacing the 4,000 wild rainbow taken annually by surface anglers would cost $2,000 with 10,000 catchables ($0.50 per creel trout) or $3,771 with 5,714 large catchables ($0.94 per creel trout).
- The authors recommended annual stocking of large catchables. In 1964 the California and Nevada Fish and Game Commissions approved an annual plant of 26,500 large catchable rainbow, which began in 1965 as a routine management program.
