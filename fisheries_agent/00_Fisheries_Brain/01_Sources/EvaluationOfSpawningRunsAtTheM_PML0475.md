# Evaluation of Spawning Runs at the Marble Bluff Fish Facility, Nixon, Nevada, 1978–1985

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service report (Great Basin Complex, Reno, Nevada; author Mark E. Coleman, February 1986) evaluates spawning runs of [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (LCT) and [[Cui-ui]] at the [[Marble Bluff Fish Facility]] (MBFF), with supporting data on [[Tahoe Sucker]] runs. MBFF was built in 1975 to restore access to the lower [[Truckee River]], which had been largely unavailable to these fish for about 50 years. LCT runs were small (42 to 887 fish), attributed mainly to the lack of Truckee River imprinting. Cui-ui runs were larger, reaching 13,804 fish in 1982, and five of the six years from 1980 to 1985 exceeded 5,000 fish. Fishway passage design, lake elevation and river access, and sedimentation were the dominant operational controls. The report concludes that only major facility redesign or a new facility will fully achieve cui-ui recovery goals.

## Background & Description

**Historical context.** [[Pyramid Lake]] and the Truckee River supported large populations of cui-ui and LCT, which were central to the [[Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe]]'s food supply and to a historic commercial and sport fishery. [[Derby Dam]] (1905), built for the [[Newlands Project]], diverted much of the Truckee River flow to the Carson basin. Reduced river flow, delta formation, a poorly designed fish ladder, pollution, channelization, and exotic fish introductions degraded spawning access. The original LCT strain became extinct by 1943, and cui-ui declined considerably (La Rivers 1962).

**Restoration.** Restocking began in the early 1950s through the Nevada Department of Wildlife and hatcheries including [[Lahontan National Fish Hatchery]]. The Tribe assumed fishery management in 1976 and built its own hatcheries, including the Dunn LCT Hatchery (1975), the Dave Koch Cui-ui Hatchery (1978), and the Numana LCT Hatchery (1981).

**Facility.** MBFF was authorized under the Washoe Project Act and comprises four components: Marble Bluff Dam (MB Dam), a fish handling building with a vee-trap and sorting table, a river trap at the dam base, and the Pyramid Lake Fishway. The fishway runs 4.8 km from the dam to the lake through an earth-lined canal with three intermediate ladders and a terminal ladder, based on a modified 1-on-10 slope Ice Harbor design. It was designed for flows of 20 to 50 cfs.

**Biology.**
- *LCT*: Historically two runs (winter and spring). The introduced strain is of Summit Lake origin, matures at 3 to 4 years, and averages 1.5 to 2.5 kg. Lifespan is typically 6 to 7 years, with some reaching 9.
- *Cui-ui*: The last member of the genus *Chasmistes*, endemic to Pyramid Lake. A lacustrine species and obligatory stream spawner. Matures at 6 to 8 years (males) or 10 to 17 years (females); may live to 41 years.
- *Tahoe sucker*: Spawns in the lower Truckee River alongside cui-ui and LCT.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Factors limiting LCT runs.** The report examined accessibility, water temperature, river flow, bird predation, imprinting, lake survival, and water quality. Fishway passage was judged not to be a serious problem for LCT. Bird predation by herons, cormorants, and pelicans was a concern: 20 to 30 percent of fish reaching the building showed bird wounds in recent years. Imprinting on Truckee River odors is considered the main factor, since most stocked fish were imprinted on hatchery water. Water temperature is also important: egg mortality rises sharply above 13 °C, and maximum April–May river temperatures often exceeded this during the peak run.

**Water quality.** Effluent from the [[Reno-Sparks Sewage Treatment Plant]] and other sources may deter fish. Nitrogen gas supersaturation was measured at about 100 percent in 1982 and judged not critically high.

**Sedimentation and operations.** Record 1983 runoff from streambank erosion filled much of the impoundment, which had a mean depth of about 2 m. Sediment entered the fishway canal and building raceway, and sand bars formed at the fishway entrance during spring storms. Lake elevation rose about 7 m in 1982–1984, flooding the delta and restoring river access.

**Cui-ui passage.** Ladder design (velocity and turbulence) was the primary control. Successive ladder modifications from 1978 to 1980 increased passage, but the 1981 run was small with all ladders modified, suggesting other factors also operate. Higher spring river flows (January–April) correlate with larger runs.

## Key Findings

**Lahontan cutthroat trout**
- Runs ranged from 42 fish (1980) to 887 fish (1985). The 1976 run was 560 fish.
- Spawning runs typically began in early March and ended in June, with peaks from mid-April to mid-May.
- Mean annual fork lengths since 1979 ranged from 458 to 568 mm.
- Sex ratio (males:females) averaged about 1.0:1.5 over the period, with females outnumbering males in 6 of 7 years. Ratios were similar to Summit Lake (1.0:1.3 to 1.0:2.2).
- Egg collections were minimal before 1984, then 416,000 (1984) and 475,000 (1985) eggs were taken for hatchery rearing.
- Runs at [[Sutcliffe]] (a lakeside rearing facility effluent stream) were much larger: 4,000 (1981), 9,081 (1982), 7,305 (1983), 6,153 (1984), and 16,560 (1985). Sutcliffe fish came mainly from LNFH, Dunn, and Sutcliffe hatcheries.
- In 1984 and 1985, about 15 to 20 thousand juvenile LCT were penned at MBFF to attract spawners. Runs (641 and 887) were much smaller than at Sutcliffe in the same years, and Sutcliffe rearing trials showed adults entered almost immediately when juveniles were present.
- Post-1983, most LCT were captured in the river trap rather than the fishway.

**Cui-ui**
- The largest run was 13,804 fish in 1982; runs of 5,000 or more occurred in five of the last six years.
- Runs began as early as the second week of April and lasted into the third week of June.
- Mean fork lengths increased yearly for both sexes, driven by dominance of a single year class (1969). Mean male lengths were 471 to 529 mm and female lengths 500 to 567 mm.
- Tagging indicated most cui-ui do not migrate actively: only 7.4 and 5.8 percent of the offshore aggregate reached the building in 1982 and 1983, respectively. Radio tracking of over 50 spawners showed a mean upstream movement of 2.1 km.
- Larval passage over MB Dam caused mortality of 3.1 to 8.9 percent (adjusted), with no clear link to river flow. The report considers this serious given the species' endangered status.
- Hatchery production was substantial: about 7.7 to 11.7 million larvae were stocked annually from 1982 to 1985, nearly equal to all earlier plantings combined.

**Tahoe sucker**
- Run sizes were 36,379 (1982), 7,997 (1983), 28,469 (1984), and 15,800 (1985).
- Runs peaked in the second half of May, at mean daily water temperatures of 12 to 13 °C.
- Ages ranged from 2 to 5 years by Sigler and Kennedy (1978), but opercle aging suggests fish up to 16 years.

**Facility operations and conclusions**
- The by-pass ladder was redesigned several times (1982–1984), with flow and turbulence problems persisting. Blocking the by-pass in 1985 changed how fish were routed.
- River trap modifications in 1984 and 1985 included vee-trap resizing (opening reduced from 14 cm to 11.5 cm) and a deep holding pool. Escapement from the recessed vee-trap was high (28 to 60 percent), and retention improved to about 70 percent when the vee was mounted on the elevator cage.
- The Station Development Plan (November 1984) for a new facility was estimated at over $17 million. A May 1985 design conference proposed alternatives to be evaluated.
- The authors recommend passing as many cui-ui as possible by evaluating MBFF ladder and trap modifications in the interim, and say that only major attention to the facility or a new facility will achieve recovery goals.
