# Humpback Chub Monitoring in Yampa Canyon, 1998-2000

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Colorado River Fish Project (Vernal, Utah) describes a three-year study (1998-2000) of the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) population in [[Yampa Canyon]], a reach of the [[Yampa River]] within [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. The study had three objectives: develop procedures for monitoring population trends as a response measure for management, estimate population size if feasible, and develop procedures for determining reproductive success.

Electrofishing and angling captured 86 humpback chub. Attempts at a precise population estimate using [[Capture-Recapture]] failed because too few marked fish were recaptured. The best estimate was about 400 fish over 150 mm TL, with a likely range between roughly 100 and 2,000 adults. Seining captured juvenile *Gila* and confirmed that the population is recruiting. The authors concluded that an intensive single-reach capture-recapture study is not feasible and recommended expanding the study area to adjacent canyons.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and occurs only in isolated populations in narrow, canyon-bound reaches. The largest population, roughly four to five thousand fish, is in the Grand Canyon and Little Colorado River. The upper basin has five smaller aggregations, including Desolation/Gray, Cataract, Westwater, Black Rocks, and Yampa canyons. The Yampa Canyon population had been poorly studied because of limited access, taxonomic confusion within *Gila*, and low numbers. The first documented capture in the area was in 1948, and the population was recognized as present in the mid-1970s. The standardized monitoring program run from 1987 to 1989 found adults and juveniles, which indicated reproduction.

Field identification separates humpback chub from [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) using several traits (Table 1). Adults are identified by a predorsal hump, subterminal mouth overhung by a fleshy snout, 10 or more anal fin rays, and a narrow caudal peduncle. Juveniles (<150 mm TL) are harder to identify, and the key traits are 10 anal fin rays and an anal fin base extending well onto the upper caudal lobe. Laboratory identification of field-collected juveniles by an expert at the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University supported the field criteria.

The study was funded by the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin, a joint effort of federal agencies, Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming, water users, and environmental groups.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Yampa River flows about 320 km from the Rocky Mountains to its confluence with the Green River at Echo Park. The study covered river kilometers 76 to 17 (about 55 to 75 km of river, as cited in different sections). In Yampa Canyon the river has a relatively high gradient (3.2 m/km) with boulder, cobble, and gravel substrate. Below river km 20 the gradient moderates. The average annual discharge is about 61 m³/s, peaking near 150 m³/s, with a late-summer base flow of about 15 m³/s. The canyon is reachable only by hiking or rafting.

**Habitat use.** Humpback chub concentrated in deep pools and eddies around large boulders. Frequently used sites included Mathers Hole (river km 31), Big Joe (km 39), Five Springs (km 43), and Irvings Hole (km 61). The authors described Yampa Canyon as marginal habitat during base flow, particularly in low-flow years, which may push fish to other reaches seasonally or annually.

**Associated species.** Captured fish included roundtail chub, Colorado pikeminnow, and channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*), the last of which was the target of a removal study in 1999.

**Threats and management.** Non-native fish control is the management activity that the monitoring program was designed to track. The authors noted that repeated electrofishing should be used cautiously, citing published evidence of spinal hemorrhaging in other species.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- Sampling was done in July 1998, June-July 1999, and June 2000 using electrofishing rafts (Smith-Root GPP or Coffelt WP-15), angling (5-9 anglers, using Mormon crickets and earthworms as bait), hoop netting, and seining.
- Each pass lasted 3-5 days, and subsequent passes were 7-10 days apart. Fish were PIT tagged (for fish >150 mm TL) and released.
- Juvenile seining was done in August 1998 from river km 60 to 17.

**Adult captures**
- Electrofishing and angling captured 86 humpback chub. Adults ranged from river km 17 (Laddie Park) to river km 72 (Disappointment Draw), similar to historic records.
- Of the adults, 83 were tagged and released, and 3 were recaptured (one recaptured from 1998, two from earlier passes in the same year).
- Mean TL was 244 mm for electrofished fish (n=69) and 211 mm for angled fish (n=17). Historic mean TL was 278 mm, which the authors attributed partly to different gear mixes.

**Juvenile captures**
- Seining captured 426 *Gila* in 1998: 33 humpback chub, 374 roundtail chub, and 19 unidentified *Gila* spp.
- Humpback chub and most roundtail chub were under 200 mm TL. Length-frequency suggested two age classes: age-1 (70-130 mm) and age-2 (130-190 mm).

**Gear efficiency (CPUE)**

| Gear | Captures | Effort | CPUE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electrofishing rafts | 77 | 85 h | 0.80 fish/h |
| Angling | 9 | 229 h | 0.04 fish/h |
| Seining | 33 | 223 seine-hauls | 0.15 fish/haul |
| Hoop netting | 0 | 72 h | 0.00 fish/h |

- Electrofishing was the most effective method, and the best passes used two rafts (18 and 17 fish captured in the last two 2000 passes).
- Hoop nets caught no humpback chub.

**Population estimate**
- CAPTURE could not produce a usable estimate in 1998-1999 because there were no recaptures. In 2000, 44 fish and 2 recaptures were available, and the model estimates ranged widely (for example, Nhat 242 under M(o) and over 4,700 under other models).
- A likelihood model assuming a stable population from 1998 to 2000 gave N = 391, with a 95% CI of 180-2,750.
- The authors concluded the population is probably between 100 and 2,000 adults, with a best estimate of about 400 fish >150 mm TL. They noted the point estimates were likely biased low.
- The per-pass capture probability was about 0.03. The simulations showed that reducing bias below 10% and CV below 0.2 would require capture probability near 0.10 with a population above 200 fish. The authors judged this infeasible without extraordinary effort.

**CPUE variability and trend detection**
- Electrofishing CPUE for fish >150 mm TL ranged from 0.72 to 1.54 fish/h, with CVs from 0.07 to 0.67 (mean CV 0.31).
- Simulations showed CPUE was unlikely to detect the declines monitoring needs to find. A 1% annual decline over 20 years (an 18% total decline) was detected only 18% of the time. A 5% annual decline over 10 years (40% total) was detected 37% of the time. A 10% annual decline over 10 years (65% total) was detected in most runs, though the exact figure is OCR-ambiguous in the source.

**Population status**
- The population appears persistent and is successfully recruiting, but it is sparse.
- Earlier comparisons with 1987-1989 catch rates were judged unreliable for trend inference because of differences in effort definitions and anglers.
- The authors cited earlier work (Tyus 1998) suggesting possible decline over 50 years, but little supporting data exist.

**Conclusions**
- The three-pass capture-recapture approach did not yield a reasonable estimate. Uncertainty remains about whether low recaptures result from movement out of the canyon, mortality of tagged fish, or low capture efficiency. Humpback chub in Yampa Canyon have been documented to travel long distances.

**Recommendations**
1. Attempt a population estimate across the combined reach of Yampa, Whirlpool, Split Mountain, and Lodore canyons. The larger area should increase captures and recaptures and the chance of recapturing fish that moved between canyons.
2. Conduct a three-year, three-pass electrofishing capture-recapture study in the expanded area, combined with seining to monitor reproductive success.
3. Monitor juvenile abundance, distribution, and length-frequency annually in documented nursery areas (Yampa Canyon, Whirlpool Canyon, and Island Park).
