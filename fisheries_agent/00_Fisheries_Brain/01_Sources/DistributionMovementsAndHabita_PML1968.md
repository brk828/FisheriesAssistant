# Distribution, Movements, and Habitat Use of Razorback Sucker in a Lower Colorado River Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study used sonic telemetry to track the movements, distribution, and habitat use of wild adult [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a mainstem [[Colorado River]] reservoir on the Arizona-Nevada border. Ten wild adults were tagged in November 1994, and data from the 6 females and 4 males tracked for the full 14-month study period were analyzed. Movement rates and ranges were similar to those reported for riverine populations. Fish returned to known spawning sites but also visited other spawning areas, and females moved substantially more between sites during peak spawning. Fish associated strongly with inshore habitat and showed thermal behavior consistent with temperature regulation during reservoir stratification.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is the largest [[Catostomidae|catostomid]] in North America and is endemic to the Colorado River basin. It was historically common but now survives as relict, fragmented populations. Three of four known populations containing more than 100 fish are in reservoirs, the largest in Lake Mohave. The species was listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1991.

Prior telemetry work had focused mainly on riverine settings, where spawning occurs over a short window of roughly 2 to 3 weeks on the ascending limb of spring runoff. Published reports on lentic use were limited, so this study was designed to provide data on spawner movements and the use of multiple spawning sites in a mainstem reservoir.

Lake Mohave was formed by the closure of [[Davis Dam]] in 1954, about 5 km upstream of Laughlin, Nevada. At full pool (197 m elevation) it covers 11,655 ha and extends about 100 km upstream to the tailrace of [[Hoover Dam]]. The only inflow is cold, hypolimnetic release water from Lake Mead. The reservoir population is thought to have originated from recruitment of fish trapped upstream during dam closure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime:** Water upstream was cold (12–16 °C) from hypolimnetic releases. In summer the reservoir stratified downstream, with temperatures from about 30 °C at the surface to lower values at depth. A [[Warm/Cold Water Mixing Zone]] formed where Hoover Dam releases met warmer lake water.
- **Habitat:** Fish used broad, shallow shoreline habitats, and were seldom found in deeper canyon habitats up- or downreservoir. Big Basin and Owl Point were used year-round, while Yuma and Gabbs... (Gaquila) coves and the mixing zone were used seasonally.
- **Visibility and conditions:** Reservoir water visibility exceeded 10 m, permitting direct observation. Depths exceeded 40 m, and summer temperatures spanned a wide gradient.
- **Study methods:** Fish were captured with a trammel net, surgically implanted with 6 g sonic transmitters, and located through weekly, then biweekly, boat surveys along a grid of about 70 GPS way-points spaced at intervals under 1,600 m. Body temperature was logged by the transmitters and compared with a [[Hydrolab Temperature Profiler]] record to estimate depth. Data were analyzed in [[ARC/INFO]] GIS.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and site use**
- Fish distribution was strongly seasonal. Detection numbers peaked between way-points 30 and 37.
- Eleven spawning-associated areas were documented, including [[Gaquila Cove]] (visited by 9 of 10 fish during spawning) and [[Owl Point]] (visited by all spawners).
- After spawning, 7 suckers moved into the area between way-points 12 and 24.
- Fish were seldom detected up- or downreservoir in canyon habitat.
- Fish were found within 50 m of shore (P < 0.001, chi-square test), most strongly during spawning, when distance from shore was under 30 m. Fish moved offshore during summer, possibly to avoid warmer water.

**Movements**
- Mean movement rate was 382 m d⁻¹ (range 242–788 m d⁻¹). Females were slightly more active (582 m d⁻¹) than males (446 m d⁻¹), though the difference was not statistically significant.
- Movement rates for males exceeded 1,700 m d⁻¹ on some occasions, and females reached 1,010 m d⁻¹.
- Average linear range was 39 km (18–72 km) overall, 42 km for females and 35 km for males.
- Spawning females were significantly more active than spawning males (460 vs. 87 m d⁻¹; ANOVA, F₁,₈ = 6.79, P = 0.0314).
- Females roamed among multiple spawning sites during peak spawning (1–28 February 1995 and 1996), while males generally stayed at a single site.
- Some spawning-site movements exceeded 50 km, and 8 of 10 fish crossed the reservoir between Arizona and Nevada spawning groups.
- Post-spawning, 70% of study fish moved up-reservoir to the warm/cold mixing zone, where nutrient-rich hypolimnetic water stimulates algal production. Activity increased in shallower water, possibly reflecting planktonic feeding.
- Post-spawning dispersal was followed by return to previously used summer areas. Distances between spawning and summer areas ranged from 20 to 30 km, with no correlation to sex or size.

**Thermal ecology and vertical position**
- Body temperatures ranged from 12 to 28 °C, with summer values typically 18–22 °C, consistent with reported thermal preferences.
- Vertical movement showed no correlation with depth or time of day.
- Fish were usually found near 9 m depth, though they occasionally went below 30 m. They were nearer the surface in spring and autumn and deeper in midsummer, suggesting behavioral thermoregulation during stratification.

**Conclusions and implications**
- Reservoir conditions allow direct observation and document spawning in ways riverine studies usually cannot.
- Multiple-site spawning may help explain the high genetic diversity of the population ([[Dowling et al. 1996]]), though females' spawning at multiple sites was not directly confirmed.
- Backwater and floodplain habitats, and the feeding opportunities they offer, appear important for post-spawning recovery of body condition, supporting their value in species restoration.
