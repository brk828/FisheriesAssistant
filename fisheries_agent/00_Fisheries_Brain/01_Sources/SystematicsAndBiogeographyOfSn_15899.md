# Systematics and Biogeography of Snubnose Darters, Genus Etheostoma, from the Black Warrior River System, Alabama

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 study in *Biochemical Systematics and Ecology* by Clabaugh, Knott, Wood and [[Mayden]] uses allozyme electrophoresis at 44 presumptive gene loci to test the taxonomy of the [[Etheostoma Chermocki Species Group]], long treated as a single undescribed "Warrior snubnose darter." The authors support recognition of *E. chermocki* and show that *E. bellator* is a composite of three evolutionary species, two of them new and endemic to the upper [[Sipsey Fork]] and upper [[Locust Fork]]. Phylogenetic analyses find the group non-monophyletic, with some members more closely related to *E. simoterum* and *E. ramseyi* from other drainages. The authors link diversification to the breakup of the ancestral Appalachian River System and to drainage exchanges among the Black Warrior, Cahaba and Coosa rivers.

## Background & Description

The *E. chermocki* species group is endemic to the Black Warrior River System in Alabama and includes *E. chermocki* (described by Boschung, Mayden and Tomelleri, 1992) and *E. bellator* (described by Suttkus and Bailey, 1993). The 1993 description of *E. bellator* was based mainly on Murphy Creek (Mulberry Fork) and Valley Creek (lower Black Warrior River) and did not evaluate populations from the Sipsey and Locust forks. Earlier morphological work by Mayden and Kuhajda (unpublished data) revealed divergence in both morphology and coloration in these excluded populations.

The two undescribed forms are referred to as *E.* sp. cf. *bellator* (Sipsey Fork) and *E.* sp. cf. *bellator* (Locust Fork). Full descriptions were deferred to a later publication. The authors recommend detailed biological investigation, status surveys and protection for both.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Range of *E. chermocki*:** restricted to about 3.2 km of stream (Turkey Creek, Locust Fork); listed as endangered (Boschung et al., 1992).
- **Range of *E. bellator* (s.s.):** Mulberry Fork, lower Locust Fork and lower Black Warrior River. Suttkus and Bailey's (1993) Fivemile Creek record was identified as a misidentification.
- **Drainage history:** The Black Warrior system has a complex history of river capture and isolation with the Mobile Basin and the upper [[Tennessee River]]. The upper Sipsey Fork may once have connected to the Tennessee via the Bear Creek system. Eastern tributaries of the middle Locust Fork have headwaters within about a kilometer of Cahaba and Coosa tributaries, in karst terrain with spring and cave habitats.
- **Fish diversity:** The Black Warrior River supports nearly 130 freshwater fish species. Isolated spring and spring-run habitats are cited as drivers of population divergence.
- **Shared fish distributions** suggesting exchange with the Cahaba and Coosa include *Cyprinella trichroistia*, *Notropis chrosomus*, *Percina brevicauda*, and *Cottus carolinae* and *C. bairdi* complexes.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Allozyme electrophoresis of 44 presumptive loci (enzyme systems in Table 1, with buffer conditions A–J).
- Phylogenetic analyses used PAUP (v3.1.1) with discrete and step-matrix coding, and FREQPARS with Manhattan path-length distance. Exhaustive searches covered all possible bifurcating topologies (n = 945 for discrete characters).
- Three outgroups were used: *E. ramseyi*, *E. simoterum* and *E. coosae*. *E. coosae* was used for final rooting.

**Genetic variation**
- 14 of 44 loci were monomorphic across all samples. Of the 30 polymorphic loci, only G3pdh-A in the Murphy Creek sample of *E. bellator* deviated from Hardy-Weinberg expectations (heterozygote excess).
- Genic heterogeneity was significant across all 30 polyallelic loci (χ² = 2557.2, 110 df, P < 0.001).
- Among-sample subdivision was very high (F_ST = 0.782 for the whole group). Within-sample F_IS was low (0.018 for the group), so most variation lies among taxa.
- Diagnostic loci: 11 loci showed fixed or significant frequency differences among the four taxa. *E. chermocki* had fixed differences at 8 loci; *E. bellator* (s.s.) at 5; *E.* sp. cf. *bellator* (Sipsey Fork) at 8; and *E.* sp. cf. *bellator* (Locust Fork) at 8.
- Restricting *E. bellator* to the Mulberry Fork, lower Locust Fork and lower Black Warrior populations brought divergence statistics close to within-species levels (F_ST = 0.311).

**Phylogenetics**
- Discrete-character analysis with step-matrices produced two shortest trees (length 98; range 98–114, significantly skewed). Fitch parsimony gave a single shortest tree (length 63).
- The shortest FREQPARS tree (length ~82.5) placed *E. chermocki* as sister to *E. ramseyi*. *E. bellator* was polyphyletic, with some populations closer to *E. simoterum*.
- *E.* sp. cf. *bellator* (Locust Fork) consistently fell basal to the remaining ingroup and two outgroups.
- Constraining the *E. chermocki* group to be monophyletic added six steps (length 104).
- **Conclusion:** the "Warrior snubnose darter" group is polyphyletic or paraphyletic under all preferred trees.

**Taxonomic conclusions**
- Four distinct species are recognized: *E. chermocki*, *E. bellator* (s.s.), *E.* sp. cf. *bellator* (Sipsey Fork), and *E.* sp. cf. *bellator* (Locust Fork).
- The two undescribed species are restricted to the upper Sipsey and upper Locust forks.

**Biogeographic inferences**
- Speciation within the group may have been linked to the break-up of the ancestral Appalachian River and the separation of the Tennessee and Mobile systems near the end of the Tertiary.
- Later divergence may have involved stream transfer between the Cahaba and a Locust Fork tributary.
- The authors caution that rooting choice could alter some inferred speciation events.

**Sampling**
- Material came from about 10 localities across the Black Warrior system, with 2–20 individuals per sample (UAIC voucher catalogue numbers listed in Appendix 1). The outgroup samples came from the Coosa, Cahaba and Cumberland drainages.
