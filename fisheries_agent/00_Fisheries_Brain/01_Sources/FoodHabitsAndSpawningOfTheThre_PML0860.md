# Food Habits and Spawning of the Threadfin Shad in a Small Desert Impoundment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[John H. Gerdes]] and [[William J. McConnell]] (Arizona Cooperative Wildlife Research Unit, University of Arizona) examines the [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) in [[Pena Blanca Lake]], a small desert impoundment in Arizona. It combines stomach-content analysis of shad from 1959 to early 1960 with ovary-volume monitoring and direct observation to estimate the spawning period. The authors report that threadfin shad fed heavily on animal prey (crustaceans in particular) when these were abundant, and that spawning occurred from late spring into summer, with spawning activity seen on at least three dates. They also raise the question of competition with young [[Game Fish]] and recommend comparative growth trials on plant versus animal diets.

## Background & Description

The threadfin shad is a planktivorous clupeid native to the southeastern United States, introduced into western waters for use as forage fish. The authors note that the food habits of the species had been studied by [[Haskell (1919)]] and [[R. V. Miller (1960)]], but that little was known about its feeding and reproduction in western impoundments. Earlier work by [[Kimsey (1958)]] and others is cited regarding introductions to California waters.

The study was conducted under the Federal Aid to Fisheries project (F-9-R-2, Arkansas), with the first collections made in 1959 and sampling continuing into January 1960. Shad were collected with a gill net, and stomach contents were analyzed by volume. Ovaries were examined to determine spawning condition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Pena Blanca Lake is described as a small impoundment in grassland country, with a shallow littoral region. The authors emphasize that in shallow or small lakes the littoral zone can equal or exceed the pelagic zone in area, which affects how food availability should be interpreted.

**Food resources.** Copepods and [[Cladocera]] were important forage at times. At Pena Blanca Lake, crustaceans were abundant in the seston and near plant beds in the shallow littoral. Average standing crops of littoral and pelagic cladocerans occasionally exceeded 20 pounds per acre (dry weight) during the study.

**Behavior.** Threadfin shad were observed to feed on the bottom and in plant beds of the shallow littoral, so comparing pelagic net seston with stomach contents alone would not demonstrate selectivity. Captive adults were seen to stalk individual cladocerans and strike them with a body movement described as a snake-like S-configuration.

**Competition.** The authors judged it unlikely that young game fish were deprived of forage by shad feeding, because crustaceans were abundant when shad fed on them. Competition could occur if shad selectively fed on crustaceans when these were scarce and invertebrates were needed by young game fish.

## Key Findings

### Food Habits

- Winter food intake was minimal. Previous workers ([[Haskell (1919)]] and [[R. V. Miller (1960)]]) also found little food in stomachs of winter-collected shad.
- Foreguts of shad collected at Pena Blanca Lake on January 24, 1960 held only a few algal fragments. Flies were found only after considerable searching of foregut rinsings.
- In two of three comparable studies, threadfin shad fed extensively on animals. In the Lake Chicot material, animal volume was reported to be several times plant volume, though the authors note that Haskell's volume-conversion method was not described, so the two studies may not be directly comparable.
- The authors state that it has not been shown that threadfin shad cannot grow on a diet made entirely of plant material, fresh or decomposed. They recommend a study of the comparative growth of captive shad on vegetable and animal diets.

### Spawning Timing

Spawning was assessed from ovary condition, the presence of eggs on vegetation, and periodic sampling of [[Chara]] beds and mats of [[Filamentous Algae]].

Ovary volume was measured by water displacement, using four fish from each of eight collections between March 31 and January 23:

| Date (1959–1960) | Observation |
|---|---|
| March 31 | Ovaries too small to measure; modal total length 65 mm |
| April 10 | Average ovary volume 0.13 ml; eggs translucent |
| May 26 | Ovaries increased in volume; eggs opaque |
| June 12 | Average volume dropped; one large fish nearly spent |
| June 24 | Average volume 0.40 ml; modal length had increased |
| November 1 | Average volume 0.30 ml; modal length about 121 mm; fish-of-the-year |
| January 23, 1960 | Average volume 0.30 ml |

(Some OCR-garbled values in the source were not reliable, so the May and June volumes are reported only as trends.)

- The sharp decline in ovary volume between May 26 and June 24 and the spent condition of larger fish indicated that the spawning peak fell between those dates.
- Eggs were abundant on vegetation close to shore near deep water on June 17, when the surface water temperature was 79°F. New eggs declined during July but remained common. By August, eggs were difficult to find.
- Adult schools did not reappear until October. The November and January fish were uniform in size and were judged to be fish-of-the-year, some of which appeared mature by November.
- The authors found no evidence of spawning later than early August. Fall turnover reduced water temperatures to about 60°F by November.

### Spawning Behavior

- Actual spawning was observed on at least three dates, always at sunrise or shortly after.
- Schools swam rapidly back and forth along the margins of plant beds that extended from the shore toward deep water, then suddenly ran up onto the vegetation, apparently stranding themselves.
- The spawning act lasted only a few seconds, after which all fish abruptly returned to deeper water.
- Early developmental stages were always found adhering to the vegetation where splashing had occurred.

### Laboratory Rearing

- Eggs collected during the 1960 and 1961 spawning seasons were hatched in the laboratory. All fry died within a week in containers holding up to 29 gallons.
- Fry survived 30 days in a 660-gallon concrete tank. None exceeded about 11 mm total length at the end of this period.
- The size and appearance of eggs and newly hatched larvae closely matched those described by [[R. V. Miller (1960)]] for the [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*).
- Captive shad fed differently from wild adults, and captive larvae were observed to stalk prey.

### Conclusions

- Threadfin shad in Pena Blanca Lake spawned in late spring and early summer, mainly between late May and late June 1959.
- Shad were opportunistic feeders. They relied heavily on crustaceans when these were abundant and fed little in winter.
- Negative effects on young game fish were considered unlikely under the observed conditions, but could occur if shad selectively fed on crustaceans when invertebrates were scarce.
- Comparative growth trials on plant and animal diets were recommended as the most useful next step.
