# Hatchery Conditions and Survival of Brown Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether the rearing conditions of hatchery brown trout (*Salmo trutta*) affect their survival after stocking in a natural stream. Fish grown at a normal hatchery rate were compared with fish whose growth was deliberately slowed by colder rearing water. Both groups were stocked in [[Fall Creek]], New York, and their [[Natural Mortality]] was estimated from [[Electrical Inventory]] data, [[Creel Census]] data and tag returns. Yearling and 2-year-old retarded trout generally had higher natural mortality than yearling normal trout, and the gap was largest in the second part of each season, when water temperatures were higher. A separate 6-week test in [[Cascadilla Creek]] in 1951 found the same pattern, even though water temperatures never reached critical levels. The author concludes that hatchery water supply characteristics influence post-stocking survival, that [[Thermal Acclimation]] is one mechanism, and that it is not the only one.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The fish were domestic-stock [[Brown Trout]], all hatched and reared on the same diet at the [[Cortland Hatchery]], New York. Because all fish shared one genetic strain, the study isolates rearing environment as the variable of interest.

**Rearing treatments.**
- *Normal growth:* reared in Cortland hatchery water averaging about 54°F, varying annually from 39°F to 60°F.
- *Retarded growth:* reared in a separate water supply held at a nearly constant 47°F year-round, which slowed growth through temperature stunting.

**Hypothesis.** Earlier literature on growth rate and longevity in animals suggested that rapid hatchery growth might reduce trout survival after planting. The experiment was designed to test whether the slower, temperature-retarded fish would survive better in the wild.

**Study area.** The main site was a 5-mile section of Fall Creek. It is a rubble-bottomed stream averaging about 35 feet wide, with alternating pools and riffles. The author classifies it as a marginal trout stream with a heavy population of coarse fish and very few trout of non-hatchery origin.

**Experimental design.**
- *1949 season:* equal weights of yearling normal and yearling retarded trout were stocked. Nearly all yearling retarded trout were below the legal size (7 inches), so anglers could not harvest them. This limited direct comparison with the harvestable normal fish.
- *1950 season:* equal numbers of three groups were stocked: yearling normal, yearling retarded, and 2-year-old retarded. Stocking was confined to the upper 3.5 miles of the study area.
- All fish were fin-clipped for identification. About 20–27% of yearling normal and 2-year-old retarded trout were also tagged to track movement and growth. Yearling retarded trout were considered too small to tag.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mortality estimation.** Total mortality came from [[Electrical Inventory]] surveys using a 230-volt AC shocker, conducted in late June and September of each year. Surveys covered 12–36% of the experimental area. Population totals were estimated with a [[Petersen Estimate]] applied to randomly selected blocked-off sections, then scaled up to the full mile. Angling mortality came from [[Creel Census]] data covering 35% of fishing days in 1949 and 80% in 1950. Catch was recorded through three channels: observed by census takers, reported on postcard returns, and estimated for non-census days. Natural mortality rate (n) was computed from the proportion of fish lost to total mortality after removing angling losses.

**Movement and escapement.** Analysis of 250 tag returns showed a tendency for trout to move short distances downstream. Escapement from the study area before the September inventory was probably under 5%. Movement patterns of normal and 2-year-old retarded trout were similar, so differential escapement did not bias the mortality comparison. Recoveries of fin-clipped fish suggested that yearling retarded trout also did not leave the area more than the other groups.

**Recruitment.** Yearling retarded trout were below legal size when stocked and became exposed to angling as they grew. Growth and mortality models showed that failing to adjust for recruitment into the legal-size class introduced an error too small to matter.

**Temperature.** Surface water temperatures were higher in the second part of each season. The retarded trout's mortality rose most sharply in that period, while normal trout's mortality stayed roughly constant. The author notes that the normal-growth fish had been acclimated to higher and more variable temperatures than the retarded fish.

**Cascadilla Creek test.** On April 5, 1951, 200 yearling normal and 200 yearling retarded trout were stocked in a 2,000-foot section of [[Cascadilla Creek]], a small stream a few miles southwest of Fall Creek. There was no fishing, so total and natural mortality were the same. Mean stocking lengths were 7.2 inches for normal fish and 5.9 inches for retarded fish. The area was inventoried on May 16 after the 6-week study. The estimated population was 109 trout, and 64 were captured.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth rate affects post-stocking survival.**
- Yearling normal trout had similar natural mortality in both years, and in 1949 at least it was considerably lower than that of yearling retarded trout.
- Natural mortality of yearling retarded and 2-year-old retarded trout rose sharply in the second parts of the 1949 and 1950 seasons. Normal groups stayed about the same across both parts of both seasons.
- In the first part of the 1950 season, the 2-year-old retarded trout had much lower mortality than yearling normals' comparison group, but in the second part their mortality was much higher.
- Natural mortality rates for whole seasons were judged unreliable because natural and angling mortality were not spread evenly across each season. Survival into the second season was also too low to yield valid estimates.

**2. Physiological condition differed between treatments.**
- At stocking in 1949, the [[Iodine Number]] of yearling normal fish was 83.4, compared with 100.4 for yearling retarded fish. A lower iodine number indicates harder body fat, so normal fish had harder [[Fat Hardness]].
- Similar data were not collected in 1950 or 1951.

**3. Size did not explain the difference.**
- The mean length difference was 2.2 inches in 1949 and 1.3 inches in 1951. The mortality difference was smaller in 1949 and larger in 1951, which is the inverse of the size difference. The author concludes that initial size probably did not drive the mortality comparison.
- None of the four lots compared in the Cascadilla and Fall Creek tests was shorter than 5.5 inches at stocking.

**4. Temperature acclimation is one plausible mechanism.**
- Laboratory studies show that acclimation can increase tolerance to high temperatures, and that such effects can last at least 30 days or more.
- The retarded fish were reared at a constant temperature, whereas normal fish experienced seasonal variation. The author notes that the hatchery variation was not comparable to the controlled acclimation used in laboratory studies.
- A related study by Schuck and Kingsbury (1948) on brown trout fingerlings in Crystal Creek, New York, reported survival of about 37.6% for fish reared at 60°F and about 16.1% for fish reared at a second, higher temperature (read from a degraded scan). Stream temperatures averaged about 68°F. The authors also noted other factors that might have contributed to the difference.

**5. Other factors beyond temperature are likely involved.**
- Even at Cascadilla Creek, where temperatures never approached critical levels, the normal fish survived much better. This suggests the hatchery environment gave normal fish more resistance to other stresses.
- The author proposes that normal fish may have been better acclimated to variability in temperature, or to the chemical fluctuations in their hatchery water. The normal-growth supply varied seasonally in pH, alkalinity, oxygen, mineral content and metabolic products. The retarded supply was chemically stable (personal communication, A. M. Phillips, Jr., 1962).

**6. Genetics versus environment.**
- Vincent (1960) found that wild-parentage brook trout reared under identical conditions differed from domestic-strain fish in growth, temperature tolerance, survival and fat content.
- The brown trout in this study shared one genetic strain. Their differences therefore came from rearing water alone, which suggests that environmentally induced physiological changes can carry over into survival in the wild.
- Gibson (1954) reported that both rearing-temperature history and genetic strain affected thermal resistance in guppies.

**Conclusions**
1. Characteristics of the hatchery water supply can affect a trout's fat hardness (iodine number) and its natural mortality during the season after spring stocking in a stream.
2. Acclimation to high and/or variable water temperatures may be one mechanism by which the hatchery water supply influences stream survival.
3. Hatchery water characteristics can apparently affect stream survival by means other than high-temperature acclimation.
4. Carefully controlled experiments are needed to determine which specific hatchery water characteristics affect a fish's ability to withstand specific stresses in the natural environment.
5. Survival of artificially propagated trout may be increased by genetic and environmental improvements in the hatchery. In many situations, the relatively cool, constant water temperature traditionally considered ideal for trout hatcheries may reduce the survival of the fish produced.
