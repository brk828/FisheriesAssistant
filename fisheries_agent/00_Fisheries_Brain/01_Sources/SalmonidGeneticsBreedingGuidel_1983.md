## Executive Summary
This 1983 paper by Phelps, Schill, and Simon (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) synthesizes electrophoretic population genetic data, test mating experiments, and computer simulations to develop practical breeding guidelines for hatchery [[Salmonidae|salmonid]] brood stocks. The central problem addressed is **inadvertent genetic change** (via genetic drift, not selection) in hatchery trout strains, which the authors show can exceed the magnitude predicted by classical population genetics for a given number of breeding adults. The paper compares breeding strategies — pair mating, egg pooling, random picking vs. family lot rearing, and overlapping vs. nonoverlapping generations — to identify practical methods that fish culturists can implement to preserve genetic variation and minimize inbreeding in [[Hatchery Brood Stock|hatchery brood stocks]].

## Background & Description
This paper is a population genetics and breeding management study rather than a morphological one; no morphometric or anatomical data on the species are reported. The relevant "characteristics" discussed are genetic rather than morphological:
- **Gene frequency profiles**: Wild trout populations show relative year-to-year stability (Allendorf and Utter 1979; Altukhov et al. 1975; Grant et al. 1980; Ryman 1983), whereas hatchery strains show large, unstable fluctuations between year classes and generations.
- **Genetic distance**: In some hatchery rainbow trout comparisons, [[Genetic Distance (Nei 1975)]] between two temporal samples of the *same* strain equaled or exceeded distances measured between genetically distinct strains — an indicator of anomalously rapid genetic drift in captivity.
- **Heterozygosity/polymorphism loss**: [[Atlantic Salmon]] hatchery populations show decreased average heterozygosity and fewer polymorphic loci compared to wild stocks (Cross and King 1983; Stahl 1983), attributed to founder effects and genetic drift rather than selection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The paper's "ecosystem" is the hatchery brood stock system itself, framed against the natural population genetic backdrop of wild salmonids:
- **Wild vs. hatchery divergence**: Hatchery-derived strains of [[Brown Trout]], [[Atlantic Salmon]], and [[West Slope Cutthroat Trout]] show significant loss of alleles and shifted gene frequencies relative to their wild source populations (Ryman and Stahl 1980; Allendorf and Phelps 1980), which can compromise the suitability of hatchery fish for restoration/reintroduction into native habitats.
- **Brood stock vs. production stock distinction**: The brood stock (fish used to generate the next hatchery generation) is the critical genetic bottleneck point; the production stock (fish raised for harvest/release) does not affect long-term genetic trajectory since it does not reproduce.
- **Year-class ecological variability**: [[Pink Salmon]] (Utter et al. 1980) show greater genetic differences *between year classes within a stock* than *between stocks within a year class*, implying that temporal (cohort) genetic structuring can be as ecologically significant as spatial (stock) structuring.
- **Selection vs. drift**: The authors deliberately scope the paper to *inadvertent* genetic change from genetic drift, excluding natural and intentional selection effects, to isolate breeding-methodology impacts on brood stock genetic health.

## Key Findings

### Population Factors Affecting Genetic Change
Four primary drivers of genetic change in brood stocks were identified:
1. **Number of breeding individuals** — more breeders = less genetic change; often the limiting factor in hatcheries.
2. **Variance in progeny number per parent** — unequal male reproductive contribution is common even with balanced sex ratios; [[Phelps Et Al. Breeding Experiments]] found that typical spawning methods result in a few males fertilizing most eggs despite multiple males' sperm being present.
3. **Fluctuation in effective population size (Ne) over time** — genetic change depends on the *harmonic mean* of Ne across generations, so a single-generation bottleneck causes disproportionately large genetic damage.
4. **Selection** — excluded from this paper's scope; focus restricted to drift-driven inadvertent change.

### Breeding Method Comparisons
- **Pair mating vs. egg pooling**: No significant difference in gene frequency change between pair mating and egg pooling with equal sex ratios (t=0.37, 24 df) when genetic variation is uniformly distributed.
- **Egg pooling advantage**: When crossing multiple strains or maintaining sublines (nonuniform allele distribution), [[Egg Pooling]] outperforms pair mating in reducing gene frequency variance; recommended for 3–4 generations post-outcrossing. Egg pooling also tolerates a lower male:female ratio (down to 40♂:60♀) without markedly reducing Ne.
- **Sperm pooling not advised**: Due to variable sperm viability, unpredictable activation, and seminal potency differences (Ginzberg 1968) — individual male contributions in pooled sperm cannot be tracked.
- **Single-use spawning**: Using each adult only once for brood stock spawning reduces progeny variance and equalizes genetic contribution.

### Random Picking vs. Family Lots
- Constant brood stock size across generations with random pair selection produces a **Poisson distribution of family contribution (mean = 2)**; ~14% of families will have zero representation in the next generation.
- Raising fish in **full-sibling family lots** and picking exactly two per family only marginally increases effective population size (Ne) over random picking for realistic (small) hatchery population sizes — the theoretical doubling of Ne (Wright 1938; Crow 1954) only holds asymptotically as population size → infinity.
- **Practical recommendation**: Simply increasing total brood stock numbers is more effective and practical than the added labor of family-lot rearing.
- Effective population size formula reported: 

  Ne = 4N / (2 + σ²)

  where N = census number of adults, σ² = variance in progeny number.

### Overlapping vs. Nonoverlapping Generations
- Overlapping generations (e.g., breeding 2-year-old males × 3-year-old females) essentially **doubles the effective population size** compared to nonoverlapping breeding.
- This strategy dampens both year-to-year and generation-to-generation gene frequency fluctuations (supported by Table 1 data comparing variance across N=4 to N=100 under Poisson, exact z², and various generation schemes).
- Overlapping generations with differing sexes also incidentally reduces the risk of mating closely related individuals.

### Sampling Throughout the Spawning Cycle / Sublines
- Time of maturity is highly heritable (Caliprice 1969); therefore sampling brood stock **throughout the spawning season**, rather than from a narrow window, better captures genetic diversity and stabilizes gene frequencies.
- Large egg-production hatcheries spawning thousands of adults can achieve good genetic representation by sampling eggs broadly across spawning pans.
- **Subline strategy** (allowing separate lots to drift independently to preserve diversity via fixation of different alleles) is genetically valid in theory but requires impractically large numbers of sublines for real salmonid populations.

### Avoiding Related Matings
- [[Rotational Line Crossing]] (Kincaid 1977) is a circular half-sibling mating scheme that avoids breeding individuals more closely related than half-siblings, but does not reach maximum avoidance of inbreeding until many generations have passed (Senner 1980).
- With an outbred starting brood stock and random mating using the Kincaid-recommended minimum of 300 fish, probability of mating closely related individuals (full-siblings, parent-offspring) is negligible.
- Maintaining multiple isolated lines increases genetic drift beyond that of one large panmictic population — reinforcing the preference for large, well-managed single populations over fragmented line-breeding schemes.

### Summary Breeding Guidelines (Author Recommendations)
1. Use **pair mating** or **egg pooling**; allow each adult to contribute only once.
2. **Randomly sample fish throughout the spawning cycle** for next-generation brood stock.
3. **Overlap generations** to reduce year-to-year and generation-to-generation variation.
4. **Use adequate numbers of breeders** — larger brood stocks reduce the need for strict adherence to specific mating methodologies.
