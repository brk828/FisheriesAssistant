# Experimental Studies on Drift and Upstream Movements of Gammarids in Running Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gammaridae

## Executive Summary

This German-language study by [[Meijering]] (Limnologische Flußstation, Schlitz) experimentally examines **drift** (downstream displacement with the current) and **upstream migration** (positive rheotaxis) in three freshwater amphipods: [[Gammarus Fossarum]], [[Gammarus Pulex]] and [[Gammarus Roeseli]]. Experiments were run in a standardized 5 m flume supplied with Schlitz river water under controlled light, temperature and flow. The author concludes that night-time drift and upstream movement are both driven by rhythmic activity, that light shapes orientation, that temperature drives drift (rising) and upstream migration (falling), and that species differ mainly quantitatively in current resistance, which largely explains their longitudinal zonation.

## Background & Description

The study is the experimental part of a habilitation thesis (Justus Liebig University, Gießen) and is dedicated to the memory of Rudolf H. Fritsch. It reviews the literature on benthic drift, noting that drift curves commonly show a night-time maximum in the first hours of darkness, while some taxa (certain Trichoptera, Hydracarina, possibly Ostracoda) drift mainly by day.

The three species were collected from small side ditches and the Sengelbach near Schlitz. Animals were held in flumes for long periods, and some matings and offspring were observed. Gammarids were fed with alder leaves (*Alnus glutinosa*) and tubificids.

Key methods:
- Flume: 5 m usable test section, 25 cm wide, water depth 1–5 cm, bottom of sand, fine gravel and stones.
- Flow set by pump timing (10 l in 4, 8, 16 or 32 s); standard setting 10 l/16 s, with surface velocities of 16–35 cm/s and near-bed velocities of 8–6 cm/s in the boundary layer.
- Light: five 60 W bulbs giving 220–280 lux at 5 cm above the bed; darkness tests in total darkness.
- Standard test periods: 10–11 h (day) and 22–23 h (night); each test lasted 60 minutes with roughly 300 animals per single-species test.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature**: Schlitz water ranged up to about 20 °C in summer and near 0 °C in winter; daily amplitudes rarely exceeded 2 °C in the river. The laboratory room was held at 10–12 °C.
- **Substrate**: Stable gravel stabilizes benthos. Gammarids tend to hide behind stones in low-velocity pockets. Drift of *G. fossarum* did not increase with flow beyond about 30 cm/s on gravel.
- **Water chemistry**: Conductivity and pH of the test water were stable within test series. Tables compared Schlitz, *pulex* and *fossarum* stream waters (conductivity roughly 127–272 µS/cm, pH 5.4–7.8, temperature maxima up to 15 °C in spring).
- **Field correlates**: Upstream migrations of *G. fossarum* in the Rohrwiesenbach peaked in spring and early summer, were densest in cool nights and after a temperature drop, and were absent on some warm, still days. Downstream drift in winter is linked to *G. pulex* losses, explaining its absence from the epirhithral.
- **Cross-taxon comparison**: The author reviews drift and migration data for mayflies, stoneflies, caddisflies and other invertebrates, many of which show similar light and temperature responses.

## Key Findings

**1. Time of day**
- Drift was stronger at night in all three species.
- Upstream movement was stronger at night (22–23 h) than in the morning (10–11 h), with a higher share of animals reaching the top of the flume at night.

**2. Light**
- Drift in darkness was slightly lower (fewer animals reached the lowest section), while more animals stayed in the upper two sections. Darkness-related increases were largely in the fourth section.
- Upstream migration was slightly more successful in darkness than in light.
- Interpretation: animals in darkness keep closer contact with the substrate because they lose optical orientation cues. The field night-drift maximum is attributed to general locomotor activity, and to the "light-off" effect that disturbs animals on exposed sites.

**3. Temperature**
- Rapid cooling (for example, 11.6 °C to 8.0 °C) produced low drift and strong upstream migration. Warming (4.5 °C to 7.9 °C) produced higher drift and weak upstream movement.
- *G. roeseli* drifted most after cooling (over one-third reached the lowest section); *G. pulex* stayed at the top.
- Near-freezing conditions (0.2 °C field water, 1.6 °C test water, December) caused strong drift in *G. roeseli* and *G. pulex* but little in *G. fossarum*. *G. pulex* held at 10.2 °C over winter did not show elevated drift, confirming that drift results from extreme cold rather than season alone.

**4. Current velocity**
- Higher flow reduced the number of animals reaching the lowest section, but increased short-distance drift (up to about 2 m), probably because animals seek cover.
- Upstream movement was hindered at higher flow; the fastest animal's travel time increased from 6 min to 17 min for *G. pulex* at the highest setting, and from 22 to 32 min for *G. roeseli*, when the lowest setting was compared with the middle one.
- Interspecific ranking: *G. fossarum* and *G. pulex* resist current better than *G. roeseli*.

**5. Foreign water**
- *G. pulex* held for eight days in *G. fossarum* water drifted much less and clustered at the upper screen, some climbing up to 5 cm above the water surface.
- Transplanted *G. pulex* reached the top of the flume in 49.1% of trials, compared with 24.8% for controls. Overall upward passage past the first section was 70.8% versus 44.5%. The study suggests that unidentified chemical factors drive this response.

**6. Sex and size**
- Males drifted slightly more than females, and larger animals drifted more.
- Males migrated upstream faster; field measurements confirmed this for *G. fossarum* (for example, mean 19.7 m/h for males versus 14.8 m/h for females, June 1969).
- Precopula pairs also migrated upstream, so migration is not primarily a mate-search behaviour.

**7. Survival**
- Drifted *G. pulex* (group B) had a 50% mortality point at about 10.5 days versus nearly 14 days for non-drifted animals, indicating that a share of drift consists of weakened animals.
- Upstream migrants showed no increased mortality compared with non-migrants.

**8. Field observations of *G. fossarum***
- Upstream movement of up to about 1 animal per cm along both banks was recorded in strong migrations.
- Speeds of 10.4–38.8 m/h were measured in the Rohrwiesenbach; migrations of more than 40 m/h were recorded in general.
- Migration was localized: it was confined to a turbid stretch below a pond and to an area of cooling water after a fishpond was drained (3 June 1970).

**9. Species-specific conclusions**
- *G. fossarum*: current resistance is good year-round; spring is its peak migration period.
- *G. pulex*: good current resistance, but this declines near 0 °C. It is the strongest upstream migrant and can recover winter losses in the summer.
- *G. roeseli*: weakest current resistance; its occurrence is limited to lowland reaches and slow zones.
- The longitudinal zonation of the three species is largely explained by these resistance differences.

**10. Conclusions**
- Upstream migration is a directed, continuous, positive rheotactic movement; drift is a mixture of undirected searching movements and passive displacement.
- The author states that upstream movement cannot be assumed to compensate drift; this remains an open question, pending full production models.
- Drift and migration both need to be included in ecosystem productivity calculations.
