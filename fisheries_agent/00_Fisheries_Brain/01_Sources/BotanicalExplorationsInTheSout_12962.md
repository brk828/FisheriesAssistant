# Botanical Explorations in Arizona, Sonora, California and Baja California (1905)

#botany #plant-ecology #cactus

## Executive Summary

This 1905 report by [[D. T. MacDougal]], Assistant Director of the [[New York Botanical Garden]], describes a spring botanical expedition down the [[Colorado River]] from northern Arizona to the [[Gulf of California]]. The party collected living cacti and other plants, herbarium specimens and photographs, and recorded topographic observations. The document also includes a short notice on essay prizes funded by the [[Olivia and Caroline Phelps Stokes Fund]]. The text contains no fish data. Its biological content concerns desert and delta vegetation, with brief notes on mammals, birds and reptiles collected by a U.S. Biological Survey member.

## Background & Description

- **Expedition purpose:** Examine the Colorado River valley between northern Arizona and the Gulf of California. Botanists had rarely visited this area since early railway surveys.
- **Personnel:** Dr. N. L. Britton, Director-in-Chief, was the addressee. Mr. G. G. Copp joined en route. Mr. Stanley Sykes, of Flagstaff, built a row-boat and piloted the early stage. Mr. Godfrey Sykes, master of the sloop, joined at Yuma. [[E. A. Goldman]] of the [[U.S. Biological Survey]] joined to collect mammals, birds and reptiles. Miguel Gonzalez, a [[Cucopa Indians|Cucopa]] Indian, guided the party.
- **Timeline:** The party left New York on March 9, 1905, and reached Mellen, Arizona, on March 13. It set off by boat on March 14 for the 300-mile trip to Yuma, arriving March 22. After the reorganization at Yuma, the party left on March 26 by a newly built boat of about one ton, with a small gasoline engine. It reached Calexico on April 28.
- **Vessels:** A 12-foot canvas boat with a collapsible cage for cacti and bulky specimens, a row-boat built locally, and a launch with a gasoline engine.
- **Permissions:** Mexican customs and officials, arranged through the U.S. Embassy and Mexican Ambassador, facilitated passage of equipment and collections across the border.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Desert vegetation near Yuma and Ehrenberg:** Dominant cacti included Opuntia, Mammillaria, Echinocereus, Echinocactus and Cereus. Sandstorms eroded the trunks of the giant cactus (*Cereus giganteus*). The giant cactus extended north to the mouth of [[Bill Williams Fork]] and crossed into the California mountains. The document notes its botanical recognition dated to 1846, and that Spanish explorers likely observed it as early as 1540.
- **Rainfall effects:** Heavy rain in the upper valley produced a deep green cover, caused by a multiplication of a few small annual species. In the delta, the annuals (a small Astragalus, an Oenothera and a Krynitzkia) showed a smaller increase than on sloping mesas 300 miles to the north.
- **Flooding:** Flood stages carried driftwood, changed the channel and cut banks. A rise of a few feet on the Colorado was followed by an 80-mile run in about seven hours. A Gila flood raised the lower Colorado about 10 feet. The entire delta was largely flooded. The Hardy channel flood had not occurred since 1891 and recurs about every 15 to 20 years.
- **Delta and Hardy's Colorado:** Travel up the Hardy, estimated at 125 to 150 miles, proceeded at one to two miles per hour against the current, with dense mesquite, cattail and willow along the banks. Flooding also produced mosquito swarms that were a serious nuisance.
- **Volcano Lake and mud volcanoes:** Volcano Lake was 10 to 15 miles long and about half as wide, very shallow and present only in high water. About two square miles of active mud volcanoes lay on its southern shore, with saline, sulphur-rich soil. Some areas were devoid of vegetation. An alga found in a hot spring had been reported previously only from Algeria. The Cerro Prieto extinct cone was climbed.
- **Cucopa Indians:** The Cucopa build houses of arrow-weed (*Pluchea sericea*) and practice limited agriculture tied to the periodic overflow. They use many plants, including "quelite" (*Amaranthus*), whose tender stems are forage and whose seeds are cooked into a gruel.

## Key Findings

- **Plant collection:** About 75 plant species were secured, one-third of them living cacti. Living specimens were shipped to the glasshouses of the Garden.
- **Expedition scale:** The party travelled about 600 miles on the Colorado and Hardy's Colorado by boat, and about 300 miles by land. Collections of living plants, photographs and herbarium specimens were made in California, Arizona, Sonora and the Cucopa Mountains.
- **Novelty:** Some parts of the region had not previously been visited by a botanist. Topographic records and a sketch map were being prepared for publication.
- **Delta observations:** The effect of maximum rainfall on the delta and adjoining deserts was examined. The work showed only modest increase in annuals compared with northern mesas.
- **Other taxa:** Mammals, birds and reptiles were collected by Goldman. No counts or species lists are given in the text.
- **Fish:** The document contains no fish records.
- **Stokes Fund prizes (separate notice):** Prizes for essays on preserving wild plants were offered for the year, payable December 15:
  - $25.00 for the best essay on local needs near New York City (up to 1,000 words).
  - $15.00 for the best essay on needs in New York parks (up to 1,000 words).
  - $10.00 for the best essay on any locality (up to 500 words).
  - Essays were due by November 1, 1905, to the Director-in-Chief.
