# Development of Propagation Techniques for Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] for the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], describes a 2002–2004 study to develop hatchery maintenance and propagation methods for the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). Wild fish were collected from [[Aravaipa Creek]] and held in a recirculating wetlab at [[Bubbling Ponds Hatchery]]. Captive fish spawned repeatedly under an April photoperiod, and the study produced 7,183 eggs and 979 juveniles that survived to the juvenile stage. Adult mortality, linked to disease, current-velocity changes and nitrite problems, was the main obstacle. The report concludes that captive stocks will need annual replacement from wild sources, and it supplies a propagation protocol (Appendix). A short addendum reports that [[Spikedace]] also bred readily in the same facility.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Loach minnow is a small, secretive member of [[Cyprinidae]], endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]]. Historically locally abundant, its populations have declined drastically and are now sparse tributary populations in Arizona and New Mexico.
- **Status:** Listed as threatened by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1986) and as a species of special concern in Arizona and New Mexico.
- **Prior work:** Early attempts at captive rearing in the 1990s were largely unsuccessful, with fish lost to [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]] infestations. [[David And Wirtanen 2001]] achieved natural spawning in captivity, first observed in April at 12.2 °C, about ten months after collection.
- **Related species:** [[Speckled Dace]] is closely related and was used as a model: earlier work showed that spawning can be induced by manipulating temperature and photoperiod.
- **Life history:** Loach minnow are short-lived (24–36 months) and highly susceptible to chronic systemic bacterial infection. In the wild they spawn February through May in riffles, with some fall spawning recorded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Wild habitat:** Eggs are laid under and on the downstream side of partially embedded cobbles and boulders in riffles of about 3% gradient. Adults feed opportunistically on benthic insects among substrates.
- **Study water:** Artesian well water at 18.6 °C, 110 ppm CaCO3 and pH 7.6, run through [[Recirculating Aquaculture System]] tanks. The laboratory was held at about 21 °C under ambient air temperature.
- **Collection sites:**
  - West end of Aravaipa Canyon (WAC): 18 fish collected 22 August 2002. Fish were emaciated and infected with [[Yellow Grub]]; they were never used for breeding.
  - East end of Aravaipa Canyon (EAC1): 20 adults received 3 September 2002.
  - East end of Aravaipa Canyon (EAC2): 77 fish collected 8 September 2002 (about 20 °C). Of these, 65 were tentatively identified as females and 12 as males.
- **Sex ratio:** Sex ratio at collection sites was estimated at about 1:1. Many fish initially judged female later developed male-type breeding coloration.
- **Environmental settings:** Spawning occurred only in circular tanks or linear raceways with flow of about 10 cm/s (range 5–10 cm/s). No spawning was recorded in the first or second row of 37-L aquaria.

## Key Findings

**Spawning and reproduction**
- Spawning began in October 2002 and continued to April 2004, with multiple events every month under an April photoperiod (12.5 h light : 11.5 h dark).
- Eggs are adhesive and demersal. Clutches averaged 26.1 eggs (±1.6 SE). Clutches of 1–50 eggs were usually found on spawning cobbles.
- Breeding experiments showed male–female pairs spawned more than 50% of the time within five days of being placed together. Groups of three to five females with one male spawned 75% of the time, but only four such tests were run.
- Fl progeny produced 13 spawning events and 148 eggs between September 2003 and March 2004.

**Egg deposition**
- EAC fish deposited 7,183 eggs between 23 October 2002 and April 2004. About 82% (5,814 eggs) were laid by at most 22 breeding pairs after April 2003.
- Each EAC female averaged 267 eggs between May 2003 and April 2004, roughly 10 clutches per year.
- Clutch size did not differ by spawning substrate (ANOVA, F = 1.325, p = 0.251), but loach minnow clearly preferred cobble to clay potsherds.
- Of the eggs, 4,032 were discarded for lack of space.

**Egg incubation and hatching success** ([[Hatching Success]])
- Eggs hatched after about five days at 21 °C.
- Hatching success was highest (69.7%) for eggs incubated inverted in hatching jars with cobbles placed on [[Bio Barrel]] supports.
- Logistic regression ranked the most important factors as incubation location, position and raceway. Substrate was dropped from the model (p = 0.055).
- Five Fl clutches incubated under the best conditions hatched at 90%.

**Larval survival and diet** ([[Larval Survival]])
- Larval loach minnow were difficult to culture. The original diet, Biokyowa B-250, was no longer available in the U.S., and early larvae starved on artificial plankton.
- The highest larval survival (91.1%) came from a combination of ground Silvercup trout starter, powdered egg yolk and 100 µm larval diet, with [[Artemia]] nauplii added at one week post swim-up.
- Of 1,330 eggs hatched in the laboratory, 979 larvae survived to the juvenile stage.
- Larvae placed with adults were cannibalized, so life stages must be separated.

**Survival and mortality**
- Juvenile and adult survival of F1 fish was estimated above 95% in the laboratory and above 99% for fish sent to the University of Arizona.
- Adult mortality was high early in the study. Key contributors were [[Yellow Grub]] infection, [[Nitrite Poisoning]] from failing biofilters, rapid changes in [[Current Velocity]] (near zero to 10 cm/s) and food lost down tank drains.
- [[Aeromonas Hydrophila]] septicemia ([[Motile Aeromonad Septicemia]]) was detected in April 2004 and probably affected many fish, since no systemic antibiotic treatments were given.
- Expected natural [[Senescence]] is 33–50% mortality per year. Otoliths from dead fish were unreadable after formalin preservation, so senescence could not be separated from other causes.
- Mortality of EAC adults declined after March 2003.

**Spawning behavior** ([[Spawning Behavior]])
- A dominant male typically guards the spawning cobble and chases other males and females. Up to four females approach, perform a shuddering display, and one is chosen.
- The female rubs her snout across the male's lower jaw. The pair then aligns vents and broadcasts 1–20 eggs, which the male fertilizes.
- Males (and possibly females) collect displaced eggs in their mouths and deposit them under the cobble. False spawning events were frequent.
- Adults were observed preying on newly hatched larvae, so brood guarding is likely absent.
- Video of spawning was recorded in May 2003.

**Recommendations and protocol** (Appendix)
- Change current velocity slowly and periodically restore low-velocity conditions. Provide low-velocity, off-channel areas if possible.
- Use flow-through artesian water for egg incubation and larval rearing where available. Place plexiglass under spawning cobbles to ease egg recovery.
- Replace captive adults annually from the wild, and separate F1 progeny from adults to prevent backcrossing.
- Sexing wild fish is probably unnecessary when collecting or translocating, given the near 1:1 sex ratio.
- Avoid stacking stressors: spawning, flow changes and an extended April photoperiod should not overlap.

**Addendum: [[Spikedace]] propagation**
- Spikedace from the upper Gila River (New Mexico) and Aravaipa Creek were held in the same wetlab.
- Broadcast spawning placed fertilized eggs into pea gravel, where they were protected from adult predation.
- Spikedace did not appear to eat their own young. Larvae could stay in spawning aquaria and were easy to remove at swim-up.
- Spikedace spawned prolifically under the April photoperiod. Broodstock maintenance will still require annual replacement because the species is also short-lived.
