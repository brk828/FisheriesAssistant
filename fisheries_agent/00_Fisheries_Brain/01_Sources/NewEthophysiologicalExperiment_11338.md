# New Ethophysiological Experiments with Male Three-Spined Stickleback

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gasterosteus-aculeatus

## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by [[Segaar]] and [[Nieuwenhuys]] examines how the [[Telencephalon]] (forebrain) of male [[Three-Spined Stickleback]] regulates three components of reproductive behaviour: aggression, sexual behaviour and parental care. Earlier work had shown that the [[Olfactory Bulb]] and olfactory nerves are not required for nest building or for the balance of these tendencies, whereas the forebrain is. The authors combine a standardized [[Breeding Cycle]] chronology, quantitative behavioural tests and histological mapping of lesions. They report that frontal, bilateral and superficial lesions shift the normal pattern in one direction, bimediocaudal lesions shift it in the opposite direction, and small coagulations within the [[Dorsal Telencephalon]] can separate and disinhibit parental activity while aggression and sexual behaviour stay normal.

## Background & Description

**Model species.** The study uses the male three-spined stickleback, *Gasterosteus aculeatus*. Male sticklebacks build nests, court females with a zigzag dance, fertilize eggs in the nest and fan the eggs until they hatch. This makes them a useful model for linking brain structure to a complete reproductive sequence.

**Forebrain development and anatomy.** The authors describe telencephalon development in bony fishes, which differs from other vertebrates. In teleosts, the dorsal parts of the lateral walls evert, so that the ependymal (ventricular) surface of the adult forebrain far exceeds the meningeal surface. The vascular supply enters through a small basal meningeal area, so superficial operations can be made without damaging blood vessels. The stickleback forebrain is divided into:

- **Area ventralis telencephali (V.):** subdivided into V.v., V.d., V.l., V.s. and V.p. This area receives secondary olfactory fibres from the medial olfactory tract.
- **Area dorsalis telencephali (D.):** subdivided into D.m. (medial), D.d. (dorsal), D.l. (lateral) and D.c. (central). Most of this area receives no olfactory fibres. The exception is the ventrocaudal portion of D.l., designated D.l.p.
- **Other structures:** the [[Nucleus Taeniae]] and the [[Nucleus Entopeduncularis]] lie in the basocaudal telencephalon.

**Connections.** Three tracts link the forebrain to caudal brain regions: the stria medullaris (to the habenular ganglia), the fasciculus medialis telencephali (from V.s., largely ending in the nucleus subrotundus of the thalamus) and the fasciculus lateralis telencephali. The last is the most prominent tract and carries both ascending and descending fibres.

**Breeding cycle and test methods.** Each cycle begins with nest building (Day N). Fertilization occurs about four days after the nest is completed (Day F), followed by days D1 to D6. Hatching (Day H) occurs on D7 or D8, depending on temperature. Three behavioural tests were used:

- **Aggression:** an 8 cm glass cylinder holding a red male was placed 23 cm from the nest. The number of bites was counted over 3 minutes.
- **Sex:** a courting female in a similar cylinder was presented. The number of zigzags was counted over 5 minutes.
- **Parental care:** the total duration of nest fanning was measured over 30 minutes. Values were divided by ten for graphing.

Normal sexual behaviour fell to zero by D5 to D7 and did not recover until the next cycle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a laboratory study, and it does not describe field habitat, distribution or population ecology. Its ecological relevance lies in the functional link between forebrain structure and the timing and balance of reproductive behaviour. The authors frame the three tendencies as a "relative balance," a dynamic equilibrium in which parental care can inhibit or be inhibited by sexual drive (after van Iersel, 1953). They relate their findings to comparative work on other teleosts, including the Cichlid *Tilapia macrocephala*, the platyfish *Xiphophorus maculatus*, the green sunfish *Lepomis cyanellus* and the goldfish *Carassius auratus*. Those studies showed that forebrain lesions affect nest building, sexual behaviour, aggression and visual discrimination. The authors argue that the telencephalon of bony fishes is therefore not merely a centre for smell.

## Key Findings

**1. Normal pattern.** In 13 control males, aggression is high around nest building and fertilization, sexual behaviour peaks before fertilization and then declines to zero, and parental care rises after fertilization and peaks at hatching. These curves are the reference for all operated groups.

**2. Frontal, bilateral or superficial lesions.** Operated males show:
- significantly low aggression between fertilization and hatching, though aggression recovers after hatching;
- sexual behaviour of brief duration that drops to zero early after fertilization;
- significantly high parental care.

**3. Large bimediocaudal lesions.** In 5 animals across 8 breeding cycles, the pattern reverses:
- aggression is constantly high or very high;
- sexual behaviour is flatter and continues after hatching, so one cycle runs into the next without a new nest being built;
- parental care is low, averaging 42 per cent of normal fanning. Eggs did develop into young, so these cycles can be compared with controls.

The authors used the Wilcoxon test to compare 13 non-operated animals with the 5 operated animals. They reported that aggression was significantly higher than normal on most days, sexual behaviour was significantly lower on D0, D1 and D3 and significantly higher on D7 to D9, and parental care was significantly lower on all days.

**4. Linked cycles.** In animal E12, three consecutive breeding cycles (fertilization at 49, 58 and 66 days after operation) occurred without new nest building. Parental care stayed low, aggression high and sexual activity continuous.

**5. Small lateral or mediocaudal coagulations.** In animal F22, observed 65 days after a small coagulation of the lateral mediocaudal area, parental care remained low. Aggression and sexual behaviour moved in counter-phase to parental care. Parental activity appeared as two smoothed waves, and rises in parental activity coincided with troughs in aggression and sex. In a group of three animals (4 breeding cycles) given slightly more central caudomedial coagulations, sexual behaviour was normal, aggression was mostly normal and parental activity showed two waves, with the second higher than normal.

**6. Centromedial coagulations and disinhibited fanning.** In animal F12 (three animals, 7 breeding cycles in total), aggression and sex were normal while parental care was disinhibited across the whole cycle. In animal F31, observed 20 days after removal of a slightly larger medial area, the male began fanning spontaneously with no eggs present. Fanning was high or very high over 13 days, and sex and aggression were present and not unusually low. In animal F12, fanning on the first day reached a peak of 1570 seconds out of 1800 in the 30-minute test. The peak occurred early in the cycle rather than at the end, and the authors offer no explanation for this.

**7. Localization.** Histology indicates that the lesions were centred in specific portions of D.m. and D.d. (for example, the dorsocaudolateral part of D.m. in F22, the caudolateral part of D.m. in F32, and the intermediate parts of D.m. in F12). The authors suggest that the effects of parental care can be isolated by coagulating specific cell groups. They describe this as a first step toward determining whether components of the regulatory mechanism are localized.

**8. Conclusions.** The authors summarize that:
- the normal A-S-P balance can be altered in two opposite directions by lesions of two topographically different forebrain areas, and both alterations remain a relative balance;
- small coagulations can separate the inhibition and facilitation of parental care without changing aggression or sexual behaviour;
- the telencephalon regulates the timing of reproductive behaviour, not just nest building.

**9. Limitations.** The paper reports the sample size for each group, but several results rest on one to three animals. Some histological mapping was incomplete, and the authors state that the localization of components is not yet established.
