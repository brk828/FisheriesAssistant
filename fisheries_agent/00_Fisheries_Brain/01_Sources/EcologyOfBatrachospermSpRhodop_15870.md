# Ecology of Batrachospermum sp. in Doe Run, Kentucky

#phycology #freshwater-ecology #batrachospermum

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by W. L. Minckley and Donald R. Tindall examines the ecology of a [[Batrachospermum]] red alga (Rhodophyta) in [[Doe Run]], a spring-fed stream in [[Meade County]], Kentucky. Observations ran from February 1959 to August 1962. The alga's sexual thalli appear in autumn, grow luxuriantly through winter, and disintegrate in late spring and early summer. The authors attribute this seasonal cycle more to qualitative changes in light under riparian canopy than to water chemistry or temperature, which are nearly constant. Discharge, current velocity, silt and marl deposition, and dissolved free [[Carbon Dioxide]] shape where the alga persists in the stream.

## Background & Description

*Batrachospermum* is a genus of floridean red algae found in cool, clear waters of temperate and subtropical regions, with a few species in Arctic and Antarctic areas. The study focused on a single form that dominated the mainstream near the source of Doe Run. The authors note that material from the drainage likely includes more than one species, and a full analysis was still in progress.

Key life-cycle features reported:

- **[[Chantransia Stage]]:** A perennial, prostrate thallus that produces erect uniseriate filaments. It generates [[Monospores]] most abundantly in summer, after the sexual thalli disintegrate.
- **Attachment:** Monospores and [[Carpospores]] attach to gravel, rubble, twigs, logs, riparian roots, and shells of the snail [[Goniobasis Snail]], which may serve as an accessible research or classroom source.
- **Sexual thalli:** Apparently dioecious in the mainstream, though monoecious forms occur in some tributaries. Male thalli were scarce whenever female-bearing plants were conspicuous, possibly because smaller male thalli were overlooked by collectors.
- **Fertilization:** Follows the sequence of spermatium attachment, nuclear migration to the carpogonium, and fusion to form a diploid zygote. The authors note that the timing of meiosis (haploid carpospore production) remains unresolved.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical setting (near source):**
- Stream about 30 feet wide; rises as a large spring east of Ekron and flows 9.7 miles north-northeast to the [[Ohio River]].
- Late-summer discharge at the source: 10 to 20 cfs; winter and spring discharges greater and more variable.
- Hard water: total alkalinity 200 to 300 mg/l.
- pH near 7.0; dissolved oxygen 7 to 9 mg/l; free carbon dioxide usually above 40 mg/l.
- Temperature constant, 13° to 14° C.
- Chemical and physical variation occurred only during periods of unusual discharge.

**Light:** Growth and development of sexual thalli coincided with maximum winter light. A decrease in April and May came with leaf canopy development by [[Riparian Vegetation]], and light stayed low from July through September. The authors suggest that summer deterioration results from changes in light quality (spectral composition under the leaf canopy), not light intensity alone.

**Current and substrate:** The alga was most common in currents exceeding 12 cm/sec and was rarely found below 0.5 ft/s (12 cm/sec). Thalli take a fusiform shape in laminar flow and are lifted and spread in turbulent flow, increasing breakage risk. Preferred substrates were permanent features (bedrock outcrops, rubble, logs, thick tree roots). After low-flow periods, it also colonized gravel, twigs, and sand or mud, and in autumn 1960-61 it grew epiphytically on [[Fissidens Julianus]], [[Potamogeton Foliosus]], [[Nitella Flexilis]], and [[Vaucheria Germinata]].

**Limiting factors downstream:** Increased variability in discharge, substrate, and temperature; [[Silt Deposition]] and [[Marl Deposition]] (marl was pronounced between Stations III and IV); and possibly insufficient free carbon dioxide. The authors also cite [[Ruttner]] (1960), who showed that *B. boryanum* and *B. moniliforme* cannot use bicarbonate and require free CO2 for photosynthesis.

## Key Findings

1. **Seasonal cycle:** Sexual thalli appeared in September, became luxuriant by February, and disintegrated from mid-May, with deterioration complete by June 20. None were found until mid-August.
2. **Light over chemistry:** Seasonal changes did not match changes in water chemistry or temperature, which were stable. Light conditions, specifically spectral quality under canopy, are the proposed trigger for summer disintegration.
3. **Discharge as main disturbance:** On December 5, 1959, the alga covered 15 percent of available bottom at a discharge of about 25 cfs. On December 19 the discharge reached near 200 cfs and no macroscopic thalli remained. A second extreme flow on January 17, 1960 caused another near-complete washout. Thalli reappeared as short fronds by February 2.
4. **Flood effects:** After the high discharge of March through May 1961, the alga was found farther downstream than previously recorded, likely through transport of Chantransia fragments. The downstream population declined in 1962 but did not recover to pre-flood levels near the springs.
5. **Current preference:** The alga is restricted to areas with low gradient, laminar flow, minimal silt or marl, and clear, cool, CO2-rich water. Silt was deposited on the alga on only 4 occasions and was quickly removed by small discharge increases.
6. **Spatial distribution:** The alga is largely restricted to the upper half mile of the main channel, to short channels of 4 upstream springs, and to the extreme headwaters of 2 downstream springs. It was not collected from the main channel below Station III.
7. **Carbon dioxide hypothesis:** Persistent populations occur only where water is supersaturated with free CO2, which is reduced downstream of the springs. The authors consider this the most likely factor restricting the species to spring-influenced reaches.
8. **Genetic alternative:** The authors caution that seasonal patterns may reflect inherent genetic traits rather than environmental control, and that apparent limiting factors may be artifacts.
