# Proposed Critical Habitat for Spikedace and Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #spikedace #loach-minnow

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed rule (*Federal Register*, December 10, 1999) proposes designating [[Critical Habitat]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] for the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), two threatened freshwater fishes of the Gila River basin. The proposal responds to a court order in *Southwest Center for Biological Diversity v. Clark* (D.N.M., September 20, 1999) requiring completion of the designation by February 2000. The proposed area totals approximately 1,443 km of rivers and streams in Arizona and New Mexico. It includes occupied habitat and unoccupied areas considered essential for restoration, organized into seven habitat complexes.

## Background & Description

**Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)** is a small, slim fish, with very slender sides and spines in the dorsal and anal fins. It occurs in moderate to large streams over sand, gravel, and rubble substrates, and in swift pools. Its habitat includes shear zones, rapid-flow borders, sheet flow, and eddies. It was first collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro and is the only species in the genus *Meda*.

**Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)** is a small, slender, olive-colored fish with a subterminal mouth. It lives in small to large perennial streams, typically in turbulent riffles with cobble substrate and swift flow. It avoids habitats where fine sediments fill interstitial spaces. It was first collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro and described from those specimens by Girard.

**Regulatory history:**
- Both species were proposed for listing in 1985. Spikedace was listed as threatened on July 1, 1986. Loach minnow was listed as threatened on October 28, 1986 (51 FR 39468).
- Critical habitat was finalized on March 8, 1994 (59 FR 10906 and 10898), set aside by the *Catron County* court order (October 13, 1994), and removed from the Code of Federal Regulations on March 25, 1998 (63 FR 74378).
- Recovery plans were completed in 1991 under the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]]. They recommend protecting existing populations, restoring occupied habitat, and reestablishing populations in historical range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Both species depend on natural flooding and a natural hydrograph. These conditions maintain habitat and help the fishes compete with [[Nonnative Aquatic Species]], which, along with habitat destruction, caused severe range reductions.

**Range status:**
- Spikedace now occupies only about 10–15% of its historical range, with about 445 km (276 mi) of stream occupied.
- Loach minnow occupies about 15–20% of its historical range.
- Status within occupied areas ranges from common to very rare.

**Primary constituent elements (PCEs):** The proposal defines PCEs as habitat features essential for foraging, sheltering, and reproduction. For loach minnow, 13 PCEs are listed. They include permanent, unpolluted flowing water; riffle, run, and backwater components; water temperatures of about 1–30°C (39–85°F); an abundant aquatic insect food base; periodic natural flooding; a natural or native-supporting hydrograph; and few or no nonnative predators or competitors.

**Land ownership (occupied areas, per Table 1):**
- Total occupied stream length: 963.2 km (598.5 mi).
- Federal: 594.7 km; private: 353.1 km; state: 15.4 km.
- Largest occupied stream lengths by county: Greenlee County, Arizona, 212.0 km; Yavapai County, Arizona, 169.4 km; Catron County, New Mexico, 233.6 km.

Unoccupied but recoverable areas (Table 2) total 449.9 km (279.4 mi). Ownership is mainly private (177.3 km) and federal (245.8 km).

## Key Findings

**Proposed designation**
- Approximately 1,443 km of rivers and streams are proposed for the two species combined. The entire area is proposed for loach minnow, and approximately 1,325 km (822 mi) is also proposed for spikedace.
- The designation is organized into 7 complexes. It covers 31 reaches within 26 streams for spikedace and 41 reaches within 26 streams for loach minnow.
- Key complexes include:
  - [[Verde River]] complex (Yavapai County, AZ): the only area with a stable thermal and hydrologic regime in the upper river and [[Fossil Creek]]. Spikedace here is genetically and morphologically distinct from other populations.
  - [[Black River]] complex (Apache and Greenlee Counties, AZ): the only remaining public-land population of loach minnow in the Salt River basin.
  - [[Tonto Creek]] complex (Gila County, AZ).
  - Middle Gila/Lower San Pedro/[[Aravaipa Creek]] complex (Pinal and Graham Counties, AZ). Aravaipa Creek has one of the best-protected spikedace populations.
  - Middle-Upper [[San Pedro River]] complex (Cochise, Graham, and Pima Counties, AZ). No habitat is currently occupied, but the San Pedro River is the type locality of both species.
  - [[Gila Box]]/[[San Francisco River]] complex (Graham and Greenlee Counties, AZ; Catron County, NM). This complex has the largest area of habitat suitable for restoration.
  - [[Upper Gila River]] complex (Grant and Catron Counties, NM). This complex holds the largest remaining spikedace population and is considered the "core" of the species.
- Unoccupied areas are proposed because the species' survival requires reestablishing populations in historical range. The recovery plans call for "secure self-reproducing populations." Spikedace has no remnant populations in the Agua Fria, Salt, and San Francisco/Blue subbasins.

**Legal and procedural status**
- Under section 7, federal agencies must consult with the Service on actions that may affect proposed or designated critical habitat.
- Since listing (1986), three consultations ended in a jeopardy finding. Four more received draft jeopardy findings, three of which were withdrawn or resolved, and one remained in progress.
- The Secretarial Order 3206 provisions regarding tribal lands are not implemented, because Public Law 106-113 and H.R. 3423 bar funding for them. Critical habitat is not proposed on the Fort Apache, San Carlos Apache, or Yavapai-Apache reservations at this time.

**Economic and regulatory conclusions**
- The rule is a significant regulatory action under Executive Order 12866. The draft [[Economic Analysis]] found no significant effect on a substantial number of small entities.
- Critical habitat applies only to federal lands and to private lands with a federal nexus.
- The Service may exclude areas if the benefits of exclusion outweigh the benefits of inclusion, but it cannot exclude areas if exclusion would result in the species' extinction.

**Public process**
- Written comments and the draft economic analysis were due by January 14, 2000.
- Public hearings were scheduled for:
  - Thatcher, Arizona (December 15, 1999)
  - Silver City, New Mexico (December 15, 1999)
  - Camp Verde, Arizona (December 16, 1999)
- All hearings ran from 7:00 to 9:00 p.m.
- Peer review by at least three independent specialists was planned.
