# Phylogeny and Stochasticity in the Evolution of Perennibranchiate Troglobitic Salamanders

#caudata #plethodontidae #troglobitic-salamanders

## Executive Summary

This paper re-examines the long-standing assumption that the permanently gilled, sexually mature condition ([[Perennibranchiate Morphology]]) in cave salamanders is a specific adaptation to subterranean life. Using a [[Phylogenetic Systematics]] framework, the authors argue that perennibranchiation is a historical (phylogenetic) inheritance rather than a repeatedly evolved cave adaptation. They propose that epigean ancestors already polymorphic or fixed for the trait were stochastically "entrapped" in caves, and they present a New Mexico population of cave-breeding [[Ambystoma Tigrinum Mavortium]] as a plausible intermediate stage. The paper is a conceptual and comparative argument supported by distribution data, a life-history table, and three cladograms, not a controlled experiment.

## Background & Description

- **Authors:** Thomas R. Jones (Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan) and D. Bruce Thompson (Department of Zoology, Arizona State University).
- **Key terms:** [[Paedomorphosis]] (neoteny or progenesis), perennibranchiate (sexually mature and permanently gilled), facultative vs. obligate perennibranchs, and [[Troglobitic Salamanders]] (obligately cave-restricted forms).
- **Taxonomic scope:** There are six genera of true troglobitic salamanders in two families. The tribe Hemidactyliini (Plethodontidae) includes about ten troglobitic species, all North American endemics. The European [[Proteus Anguinus]] (Proteidae) is the only troglobitic form found elsewhere.
- **Geographic scope:** North American troglobites occur in the Valley and Ridge, Appalachian Plateau, and Dougherty Plain provinces of the southeastern USA, the Ozark Mountains, and the [[Edwards Plateau]] of Texas.
- **Life-history pattern:** Nearly all troglobitic salamanders are perennibranchiate. The exceptions are [[Gyrinophilus Subterraneus]] and [[Typhlotriton Spelaeus]], which metamorphose.
- **Prior hypotheses:** The authors group earlier explanations into two categories:
  1. Active entry, either to escape harsh surface conditions (Wake, 1966; Brandon, 1971; Mitchell and Smith, 1972) or to exploit empty niches (Howarth's adaptive-shift theory).
  2. Passive entry through physical forces such as spring failure or stream capture (Sweet, 1982, 1984).
- **Adaptive explanations reviewed:** Food availability and feeding efficiency in dark aquatic habitats (Brandon, 1971; Culver, 1982), the energetic cost of metamorphosis (Brandon, 1971), a selective disadvantage for metamorphs leaving productive water (Wilbur and Collins, 1973), and prey-resource shifts in [[Gyrinophilus Palleucus]] (Bruce, 1979). [[Eurycea Neotenes]] was also treated in this framework (Bruce, 1976; Sweet, 1977).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Cave climate:** Temperate caves are typically stable, cool, and humid. Amphibians enter them readily, often well beyond the twilight zone, for refuge, feeding, or breeding.
- **Facultative cave users:** Many metamorphosed plethodontids use caves but are not troglobites. Examples include [[Eurycea Lucifuga]], [[Gyrinophilus Porphyriticus]], and European *Hydromantes* spp. The authors list about 20 further facultative plethodontid species, and note that *Ambystoma jeffersonianum* occurs in large numbers in West Virginia caves.
- **Tiger salamander cave records:**
  - Metamorphosed *A. t. nebulosum* occur in about 90% of roughly 50 caves surveyed on the Mogollon Plateau, Arizona (Thompson, unpublished).
  - Metamorphosed *A. t. mavortium* have been recorded in over 40 caves in south-central New Mexico.
  - Reproduction by *A. t. mavortium* has been reported in Oklahoma caves, but no perennibranchs were found there.
- **Escape hypothesis critique:** The authors argue that escape is unnecessary as an explanation. Amphibians already enter caves for many reasons, and the relatively mesic eastern USA has a species-rich epigean salamander fauna that calls for a better explanation.
- **Karst geomorphology:** Gypsum caves have high solubility and low mechanical strength, so they are likely transient on geological time scales (White, 1988).

## Key Findings

1. **Perennibranchiation is historical, not repeatedly adaptive.** The trait is a synapomorphy of the hemidactyliine clade excluding *Hemidactylium*, and it has not evolved independently in obligately troglobitic forms. The restricted distribution, which is absent from other plethodontid lineages, is taken as strong evidence for a significant genetic component.
2. **Proteids cannot show a cave-specific adaptation.** All known fossil and extant proteids are perennibranchiate, so the trait in [[Proteus Anguinus]] cannot be an adaptation to subterranean habitat.
3. **Adaptive predictions are falsified.** *Typhlotriton spelaeus* is an obligately troglobitic, widespread, and relatively abundant salamander with a typical biphasic life history. The authors argue it refutes the prediction that cave salamanders must be perennibranchiate, and they reframe the question as why so few troglobites metamorphose.
4. **Ambystoma pattern.** Mapping perennibranchiation onto a cladogram of ambystomatids (Fig. 2) shows it is the ancestral condition and is restricted to two clades. This also supports a common historical basis, and the authors dispute the claim of multiple independent evolution (Shaffer, 1984).
5. **Stochastic entrapment model.** The authors propose that epigean forms polymorphic (or fixed) for perennibranchiation entered caves, perhaps only for shelter, and bred there. Non-metamorphosing offspring were confined to aquatic cave habitat. Natural selection could later refine larval features already present, and geomorphic isolation (stream capture, erosion of cave openings) could complete the divergence.
6. **Empirical example in New Mexico.** Small breeding populations of perennibranchiate *A. t. mavortium* were found in two isolated cave pools, [[Antelope Gyp Cave]] and [[Flat Rock Cave]], in the [[GYPKAP Survey]] area of south-central New Mexico. These are the only reported cases of cave-dwelling perennibranchs in this species. Branchiate morphology in these cave animals did not differ visibly from surface-breeding populations. The authors suggest this is an intermediate stage in the stochastic model.
7. **Metamorphosis is difficult once established.** In *A. t. stebbinsi*, mature branchiates make up about 90% of the adult population (Collins et al., 1988), and laboratory-induced metamorphosis usually results in death (Jones et al., 1988). The authors suggest that once an Ambystoma becomes perennibranchiate, later metamorphosis may be difficult, though not impossible.
8. **Speciation implication.** If a genetic propensity for perennibranchiation exists within a lineage, ecological isolation in caves may be enough to start further morphological divergence and speciation (sensu West-Eberhard, 1986, 1989). The authors do not claim that the New Mexico populations are speciating.
9. **Research needs.** The authors call for a more robust cladistic analysis of Hemidactyliini, along with studies of the genetic and developmental basis of perennibranchiation in a strong phylogenetic context and ecological tests of the competing hypotheses.
