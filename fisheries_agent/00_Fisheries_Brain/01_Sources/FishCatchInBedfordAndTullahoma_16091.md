# Fish Catch in Bedford and Tullahoma Lakes, Tennessee, with Special Reference to Soil Productivity

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper compares the sport fish catch of two newly impounded lakes in middle Tennessee, [[Bedford Lake]] and [[Tullahoma Lake]], both completed in 1939 by the Tennessee Game and Fish Commission. The lakes have similar size ratios, climate and depth, but drain watersheds of very different fertility. The author argues that lake productivity follows the nutrient status of the drainage soils and geology, and that this explains the higher catch at Bedford. Over 1943–1948, Bedford yielded an average of 86 lb/acre (1.5 lb per fisherman-day) against 29 lb/acre (0.9 lb per fisherman-day) at Tullahoma, despite Bedford being fished about twice as intensively. The author concludes that neither lake is overfished and that the creel census should continue before any regulation change.

## Background & Description

- **Origin of the study:** Construction of the two lakes gave a chance to compare waters from two contrasting drainage areas. Creel records were kept by lake managers under a permit system, and the author compiled them after joining the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]].
- **Physical setting:** Bedford Lake has a surface area of 65 acres (drainage ratio about 1:32) at 880 ft elevation. Tullahoma Lake has 40 acres (ratio 1:33) at 1,010 ft. Maximum depths are 25 ft and 30 ft.
- **Climate:** Forty-year records give annual precipitation of 50.76 in. (Bedford County) and 54.34 in. (Coffee County). January means are 39.7 °F and 40.7 °F; July means are 77.6 °F and 77.1 °F.
- **Water chemistry (July 1941, data collected under Dr. C. S. Shoup, Vanderbilt University):** Tullahoma had lower total alkalinity at depth-surface levels (3.0 ppm at surface versus 63.0 ppm at Bedford) and a lower pH (6.6 at depth versus 7.0 at Bedford). Bottom waters of both lakes were anoxic (0.0 ppm dissolved oxygen).
- **Stocking:** Both lakes were stocked after impoundment with a variety of game fish. Tullahoma received bluegill, black crappie, spotted bass, smallmouth bass and warmouth bass. Bedford received bluegill, Gambusia minnows and largemouth bass.
- **Management:** Fishing was by daily permit. Lake managers kept a [[Creel Census]] and sold permits. Records were judged reasonably accurate, though some were missing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and soils.** Tennessee is divided into eight physiographic provinces. The study concerns the [[Highland Rim]] and [[Central Basin]]. The Tullahoma watershed lies wholly within the Highland Rim, an infertile plateau of about 1,000 ft average elevation. Its surface rocks are Mississippian limestones, and the weathered [[Fort Payne Chert]] produces "Barrens" soils that are low in lime, phosphate, potash and nitrogen. Almost the whole Tullahoma watershed is Barrens. The Bedford watershed is more varied, with Fort Payne chert, Chattanooga shale along the escarpment, and Ordovician formations high in phosphate. Its soils range from low to high fertility.

**Soil productivity.** Table 2 (after the [[Soil Survey of Bedford County]]) gives expected yields without amendments. Basin soils produced 30 bu/acre of corn and 100 cow-acre-days of pasture for Maury silt loam. Rim soils produced 10 bu/acre of corn and only 30 cow-acre-days of pasture. The author concludes that Bedford's watershed is "basically much more productive" than Tullahoma's.

**Nutrient theory.** The author builds on [[Mortimer]] (1941), who described lakes as trapping "chemical potential" from drainage systems, and on [[Fippin]] (1945), who found nutrient losses from Tennessee River watersheds to be large and variable. [[Thompson and Hunt]] (1930) linked soil fertility to fish abundance and growth in Illinois. Alkalinity was treated as a fertility indicator, following [[Moyle]], who reported the greatest walleye yields in ponds where alkalinity exceeded 40 ppm.

**Species composition.** Bluegill, largemouth bass and black crappie dominated the catch throughout. The bass-to-bluegill ratio of about 2:1 (by weight) reported for well-fertilized ponds by [[Davison]] (1947) held at Bedford. At Tullahoma the ratio was about 4:1 when crappie were counted with bass.

## Key Findings

**Catch and effort (1943–1948)**

| Metric | Bedford Lake (65 ac) | Tullahoma Lake (40 ac) |
|---|---|---|
| Total man-days, 6 seasons | 23,526 | 7,972 |
| Man-days per acre per year (avg.) | 60 | 33 |
| Catch per acre per year (avg.) | 86 lb | 29 lb |
| Catch per fisherman-day (avg.) | 1.5 lb | 0.9 lb |
| Catch composition: bass / bluegill / crappie | 17% / 65% / 16% | 11% / 81% / 9% |

- The first, nine-day opening in 1941 gave Tullahoma 39 lb/acre and Bedford 26 lb/acre. The author notes that the Tullahoma figure probably drew on a large part of the standing crop.
- Per-acre yield at Bedford ranged from 54 to 120 lb (1943–1948), against 21 to 42 lb at Tullahoma. Yield per acre varied almost directly with fishing intensity in both lakes.
- Tullahoma black crappie fell from 28% of the catch in 1943 to 1% in 1945, then rose to 4% in 1948. The author attributes this to the ease with which crappie are caught under concentrated effort.

**Benchmarks**

- Rounsefell (1946) reported annual sport fish yields of 9.46 to 74.0 lb/acre for lakes of 1 to 200 acres. Tullahoma falls within this range and Bedford exceeds it.
- Rounsefell's model predicts 17.7 lb/acre for a 40-acre lake and 15.1 lb/acre for a 60-acre lake. Both lakes exceed these predictions, and Bedford by a wide margin.

**Spring fishing experiment.** Bedford was opened to fishing during the normal closed season (April–May) in 1944, 1945, 1947 and 1948. Tullahoma was not included, because of its low productivity.

**Redhorse.** An estimated 19,450 lb of redhorse (*Moxostoma* sp.) were taken from a tributary of Bedford Lake during spawning runs. This catch was not included in the totals. The run has since been largely destroyed by overfishing. No redhorse runs occurred at Tullahoma.

**Growth.** No formal growth study was done. Bluegill growth was similar in both lakes. Largemouth bass grew faster at Bedford: age III bass at Tullahoma averaged 231.5 mm total length, and age II bass at Bedford averaged 233.3 mm.

**Overfishing.** The author cites [[Swingle]] (1947), who suggested that only about 50% of the total fish weight can be removed each year, and [[Eschmeyer]] (1946), who held that lakes are very hard to fish out to the point of losing broodstock. On Fife Lake, Michigan, a 92% increase in fishing intensity raised total catch by only 18%. Tullahoma's catch per trip stayed constant while trips rose from 1,064 (1946) to 2,029 (1948). Bedford's rose from 3,235 to 6,437 with no drop in catch. The author concludes that neither lake is overfished and that there is no justification for a shorter open season, though the creel census should continue.

**Central conclusion.** Bedford's higher yield is attributed mainly to the greater fertility of its drainage basin, since other conditions were largely similar. The study links soil productivity directly to fish production.
